Safety, pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics and antitumor activity of legubicin in patients with advanced solid tumors, including lung cancer: a phase 1 dose-escalation and expansion trial
Authors: Fengjuan Lin, Junli Xue, Qun Li, Wenbo Tang, Wei Zhao, Liqiong Xue, Jiuli Zhou, Yuan Liu, Wenyue Jin, Hui Sun, Cheng Liu, Ye Guo, Jin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis first-in-human, phase 1a/b dose escalation and expansion trial investigated the safety and antitumor activities of legubicin, a novel conjugate of doxorubicin with a legumain-cleavable tetrapeptide linker, for patients with advanced solid tumors who failed or had no applicable standard of care treatment.MethodsEligible patients received legubicin over 7 dose levels from 20 mg/m2 to 360 mg/m2 every 3 weeks (Q3W) in phase 1a and legubicin 270 mg/m2 every 2 weeks (Q2W) on day 1 and 15 of each 4-week cycle or legubicin 270 mg/m2 Q3W on day 1 of each 3-week cycle in phase 1b. The primary endpoints included safety (phase 1a/b), the maximum tolerated dose and recommended phase 2 dose (RP2D) (phase 1a), and independent review committee (IRC)-assessed antitumor activities of legubicin per RECIST version 1.1 (phase 1b).ResultsPhase 1a enrolled 28 patients (median age 57.5 years; men 60.7% [17/28]) and phase 1b enrolled 35 (median age 53.0 years, men 51.4% [18/35]), with 15 in the 270 mg/m2 Q2W cohort and 20 in the 270 mg/m2 Q3W cohort. All patients received prior antitumor therapy and 32 (91.4%) in phase 1b received ≥ 3 prior lines of therapy. All patients in phase 1a had any-grade treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs), including grade 3 or worse TEAEs in 35.7% (10/28). No maximum tolerated dose was established; the RP2D of legubicin was 270 mg/ m2 Q2W or Q3W. Thirty-four patients (97.1%) in phase 1b had any-grade TEAEs. Grade 3 or worse TEAEs occurred in 37.1% (13/35) of the patients, including neutrophil count decreased (25.7% [9/35]), leucocyte count decreased (20.0% [7/35]), and anemia (11.4% [4/35]). At the phase 1b data cutoff, 1 patient with lung cancer receiving legubicin 270 mg/ m2 Q2W and 1 with ovarian cancer receiving legubicin 270 mg/ m2 Q3W had a partial response, and the IRC-confirmed objective response rate was 5.7% (95% CI 0.7%-19.2%).ConclusionsLegubicin is safe and well tolerated at a dose of 270 mg/m2 administered every 2 or 3 weeks, with preliminary signals of antitumor activities in a heterogeneous cohort of patients with pretreated advanced tumors, including heavily pretreated lung cancer patients. These findings support further evaluation in tumor‑specific studies.Trial registrationChinese Clinical Trial Registry (http://www.chictr.org.cn/, ChiCTR2600122142; registered on April 9, 2026).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-026-16261-2.

Background

The clinical use of doxorubicin, an anthracycline drug, is hampered by prominent toxicities such as dose-dependent myelosuppression and cardiotoxicity despite its strong efficacy for the treatment of solid tumors [1–6]. These side effects preclude adequate dosing and rechallenge on relapse. Hitherto, attempts to increase the therapeutic index and enhance the overall clinical benefit of doxorubicin have limited success [7, 8], including aldoxorubicin, a prodrug of doxorubicin, that binds covalently to plasma albumin and is released at the tumor site due to its acid-sensitive hydrazone linker [9, 10]. A promising strategy for improving the benefit-risk profile of doxorubicin is increasing the tumor targetability and hence enhancing the therapeutic efficacy of doxorubicin by taking advantage of the tumor microenvironment (TME).

Legumain, an asparaginyl-specific cysteine endopeptidase in the peptidase family C131 [11], is widely conserved across species and is markedly overexpressed in various solid tumors including tumor cells, the matrix, and endothelial cells, while its expression in normal tissues remains limited. The peptidase specifically hydrolyzes substrate asparaginyl bonds and represents a good candidate molecule for the design of doxorubicin carrier for legumain-based tumor targeting. This design strategy would allow for legumain-cleavable doxorubicin carrier to remain stable in plasma until the complex is recognized and subsequently cleaved by active legumain in the TME. In particular, the high tumor expression and substrate specificity of legumain have allowed investigators to develop novel legumain-cleaved, TME-activated drugs including antibody-drug conjugates containing legumain-sensitive linkers [12, 13].

Legubicin is a novel conjugate of doxorubicin with a legumain-cleavable tetrapeptide linker (Leu-Asn-Ala-Ala) [14]. The peptide chain is capped with a 6-maleimide group at its terminus, forming a stable albumin complex and transforming doxorubicin into a non-toxic, TME-activated albumin-drug conjugate (ALDC) with doxorubicin as payload. The asparaginyl bonds between leucine and asparagine is cleaved by legumain in the TME [15, 16]; the self-immolated moiety readily undergoes elimination to release doxorubicin precursor N-L-leucyl-doxorubicin into the TME under acidic conditions, which is subsequently converted by tissue peptidases to release the free drug [15, 17], doxorubicin. The albumin-binding and legumain-activating properties of legubicin are expected to significantly change the tissue distribution behavior and pharmacokinetics (PK) of legubicin, leading to reduced systemic exposure and increased tumor exposure to doxorubicin. Preclinical studies showed that legubicin was present in plasma predominantly as a protein complex and mainly available as the free base form of legubicin, doxorubicin, in the TME [18]. At equivalent doses, legubicin showed an improved toxicity profile over conventional doxorubicin, particularly cardiotoxicity.

Currently, it remains unknown whether this legumain-based PK-altering strategy could engender favorable PK characteristics, an improved safety profile and enhanced therapeutic efficacy. In this first-in-human, phase 1a/b dose escalation and expansion trial, we examined the safety and antitumor activities of legubicin for patients with advanced solid tumors who failed or had no applicable standard of care (SOC) treatment.

Methods

The study population

This open-label, single center, dose escalation (phase 1a) and expansion (phase 1b) trial enrolled adult patients aged between 18 and 75 years with histologically or cytologically confirmed advanced solid tumors who had failed or had no applicable SOC treatment. Eligible patients in both parts should have at least one measurable lesion per Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1, an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) Performance Status score of 0 or 1, adequate renal, hepatic and bone marrow function, and a life expectancy of at least 3 months. Patients should have received no chemotherapy within the preceding 3 weeks (6 weeks for mitomycin C and nitrosourea), other systemic antitumor therapy within the preceding 4 weeks, oral fluoropyrimidines and targeted small molecule agents within the preceding 2 weeks or 5 half-lives, whichever was longer, and trastuzumab within the preceding 6 months. The main exclusion criteria for both parts were symptomatic or uncontrolled central nervous system (CNS) or leptomeningeal metastasis, a history of severe cardiocerebrovascular disease, active infection requiring systemic anti-infective therapy within one week of enrollment, and prior treatment with doxorubicin or other anthracyclines, and the accumulative dose of anthracyclines exceeding doxorubicin 300 mg/m2 or the clinical equivalent of this dose.

In phase 1b, eligible non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients should have received at least 3 lines of systemic therapy, including targeted therapy or immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy and platinum-containing chemotherapy regimens, and eligible recurrent extensive-stage small cell lung cancer patients should have received at least 2 lines of systemic therapy, including platinum-containing chemotherapy and/or immune therapy. Detailed eligibility requirements are described in the trial protocols.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, International Conference on Harmonisation, and Good Clinical Practice. The protocol and all amendments were reviewed and approved by the institutional review board of Shanghai East Hospital. All patients provided written informed consent before participating in the trial. The trial is registered at Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (https://www.chictr.org.cn, ChiCTR2600122142; registered on April 9, 2026).

Procedures

In the dose escalation phase, legubicin was to be administered over 7 dose levels 21 days apart from 20 mg/m2 to 360 mg/m2. The legubicin 20 mg/m2 and 40 mg/m2 cohorts used an accelerated escalation protocol, with one patient in each cohort, and would transition to the standard 3 + 3 dose-escalation design when any grade 2 or worse treatment-related adverse event (TRAE) was observed during the first cycle, with 3 weeks per cycle. The legubicin 80 mg/m2, 133 mg/m2, 200 mg/m2, 270 mg/m2, and 360 mg/m2 cohorts followed the standard 3 + 3 dose-escalation design, with 3 to 6 patients in each cohort (Fig. 1A). Dose escalation continued until at least 2 patients among a cohort of 3 to 6 patients experienced a dose-limiting toxicity (DLT). The maximum tolerated dose (MTD) was defined as the highest dose level at which no more than 1 of the 6 patients in a cohort had a DLT.

The dose-expansion phase consisted of two cohorts. Cohort A received legubicin 270 mg/m2 Q2W given intravenously on day 1 and 15 of each 4-week cycle, with enrollment being contingent upon the occurrence of a DLT in no more than 1 of the first 6 patients in the cohort during the first cycle. Cohort B, including 6 from the 270 mg/ m2 cohort in the phase 1a portion of the study, received legubicin 270 mg/m2 Q3W given intravenously on day 1 of each 3-week cycle. Treatment beyond the first 6 cycles in both cohorts was allowed until RECIST-defined disease progression or unacceptable toxicities or patient decision to withdraw. Dose interruptions and reductions to 270 mg/m2 Q3W were permitted in Cohort A after the completion of the first 6 cycles.

Assessments and study endpoints

AEs were assessed weekly and graded according to the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (NCI CTCAE) version 5.0. All AEs were described in Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA v24.1) preferred terms and CTCAE grade. The incidences and severities of treatment-emergent AEs (TEAEs) and TRAEs were tabulated.

Tumor response was assessed radiologically every two cycles by investigators (phase 1a) and an independent review committee (IRC; phase 1b) using RECIST version 1.1. Complete (CR) and partial response (PR) were confirmed radiologically at least 4 weeks apart. Progression-free survival (PFS) was calculated from the time of the first dose of legubicin to the date of documented disease progression or death from any cause, whichever occurred earlier, and overall survival (OS) was calculated from the time of the first dose of legubicin to the date of death from any cause.

The primary endpoints included the safety and tolerabilities of legubicin in both the phase 1a and 1b portions of the study, the MTD and the recommended phase 2 dose (RP2D) of legubicin (phase 1a), and IRC-assessed antitumor activities of legubicin (phase 1b). The secondary endpoints included the PK and pharmacodynamics (PD) profile of legubicin (phase 1a/b), investigators-assessed antitumor activities (phase 1a), and OS, total and free leucyl-doxorubicin, and free doxorubicin (phase 1b).

PK and PD

Venous blood was collected from patients in both the phase 1a and 1b portions of the study and urine samples were obtained from patients in phase 1a. Plasma total and free leucyl-doxorubicin, and free doxorubicin contents, and urine doxorubicin and leucyl-doxorubicin levels were measured using a validated LC-MS/MS assay and accumulative urine excretion fraction of total and free leucyl-doxorubicin [19], and free doxorubicin was calculated. The PK parameters were calculated by the non-compartmental analysis (NCA) model using WinNonLin® v8.3 (Certara USA Inc, Princeton, NJ, USA).

Anthracyclines were shown to induce major peripheral T-cell deletion [20]. To investigate whether legubicin adversely affected peripheral T cell dynamics, we analyzed the frequencies of CD3+, CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in the peripheral blood in all patients.

Statistical analysis

No sample size calculation was done. A sample size of 11 to 30 patients was anticipated for phase 1a and 70 patients for Cohort A and 20 for Cohort B in phase 1b. The study followed the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle. Efficacy analysis was based on the full analysis set (FAS), which included all patients who received at least one dose of legubicin. The objective response rate (ORR) and disease control rate (DCR) and their 95% confidence interval (CI) were estimated using Clopper-Pearson method. Missing data was not imputed. The median PFS and OS were estimated, and the corresponding 95% CI were estimated with the Kaplan-Meir method. Patients who had not progressed at the time of the analysis were censored at the time of their last tumor assessment. The safety set included all patients who received at least one dose of legubicin and had at least one safety evaluation. AEs were analyzed mainly using descriptive statistics.

Statistical analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Patient characteristics

Between May 7, 2020 and March 11, 2022, 48 patients with advanced solid tumors were screened for the phase 1a study. Twenty-eight patients, including 8 with lung cancer, 2 with hepatocellular carcinoma and 2 with gastric cancer, were eligible and included in the FAS and the safety set (Fig. 1A). Their median age was 57.5 years (Q1, Q3 53.5, 66.5). Twenty-seven patients (96.4%) had metastatic disease and 25 (89.3%) had recurrent disease.

Between March 2, 2021 and August 24, 2023, 52 patients with advanced solid tumors were screened for the phase 1b study; 15 were included in Cohort A and 20 in Cohort B, including 6 from the 270 mg/m2 cohort in phase 1a, and constituted the FAS and the safety set (Fig. 1B). Their median age was 53.0 years (Q1, Q3 45.0, 60.0). Eight patients had lung cancer and 7 had breast cancer. All patients had recurrent metastatic disease (Table 1). Legumain was highly expressed in solid tumor tissues, including lung cancer tissues, while absent or lowly expressed in normal tissues (Fig. S1).

Patient demographic and baseline characteristics

In phase 1a, patients received a median of 2 doses (Q1, Q3 2, 4) of legubicin, with a median total exposure of 695.6 mg (Q1, Q3 417.80, 1786.40). Three patients had at least one dose adjustment, one each in the 200, 270 and 360 mg/m2 cohorts. Six patients withdrew from the study and one patient in the 360 mg/m2 cohort died of unknown cause. In phase 1b, patients in Cohort A and B received a median of 8 (Q1, Q3 4, 10) and 4 doses (Q1, Q3 2, 6) of legubicin, respectively, with a corresponding median total exposure of 3515.40 mg (Q1, Q3 1792.80, 4681.80) and 1663.20 mg (Q1,Q3 982.80, 2810.70). Eight patients, including 6 in Cohort A and 2 in Cohort B, had at least one dose adjustment. In Cohort A, 4 patients discontinued the study due to disease progression, 1 was lost to follow up and 1 withdrew from the study. In Cohort B, 7 patients withdrew from the study, 2 discontinued the study due to disease progression and 1 was lost to follow up (Fig. 1B).

Safety

In the phase 1a study, among 26 patients who completed at least one cycle of treatment and underwent DLT assessment, 2 (7.7%) experienced DLTs, including grade 3 hyperbilirubinemia in 1 of 6 patients (16.7%) in the 200 mg/m2 cohort and grade 3 febrile neutropenia in 1 of 6 patients (16.7%) in the 360 mg/m2 cohort. No MTD for legubicin was established and legubicin 270 mg/ m2 Q2W or Q3W was recommended for the dose expansion study. All patients (100% [28/28]) had at least a TEAE of any grade; grade 3 or worse TEAEs were less frequent (35.7% [10/28]). The most frequent grade 3 or worse TEAEs were neutrophil count decreased (10.7% [3/28]), anemia (7.1% [2/28]) and vomiting (7.1% [2/28]) (Table 2). Serious AEs occurred in 28.6% (8/28) of the patients (Table S1). TEAEs led to treatment interruptions in 17.9% (5/28) of the patients. No patients discontinued study or died due to TEAEs.

Incidence of adverse events (AEs) occurring in ≥ 10% of patients in either phase of the study and grade 3 or worse AEs

Adverse events were assessed and graded according to the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (NCI CTCAE) version 5.0 and were described in Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA v24.1) preferred terms and CTCAE grade.

In the phase 1b study, 97.1% (34/35) of the patients had at least a TEAE of any grade, including grade 3 or worse TEAEs in 37.1% (13/35). The most frequent grade 3 or worse TEAEs were neutrophil count decreased (25.7% [9/35]), leucocyte count decreased (20.0% [7/35]), and anemia (11.4% [4/35]) (Table 2). Serious AEs occurred in 11.4% (4/35) of the patients. TEAEs led to treatment interruptions in 37.1% (13/35) of the patients. No TEAEs led to study discontinuation or death.

Cardiotoxicities were reported in 50% (14/28) of the patients in phase 1a, which occurred exclusively in the 200, 270 and 360 mg/m2 cohorts, and 60% (12/20) in Cohort A and 80% (12/15) in Cohort B in phase 1b. Cardiotoxicities were mostly grade 1 and required no specific treatment.

TRAEs occurred in 100% (28/28) of the patients in phase 1a and 97.1% (34/35) of the patients in phase 1b (Cohort A, 100% [15/15]; Cohort B, 97.1% [34/35]).

Antitumor activities

Tumor responses

At the phase 1a data cutoff date (March 11, 2022), no patients in the FAS showed an objective response to legubicin, and the investigators-confirmed ORR was 0% (95% CI 0.0%-12.3%). Ten patients had a stable disease and the DCR was 35.7% (95%CI 18.6%-55.9%). Twelve patients had progressive disease (Table 3 and Table S2).

PK

The in vivo exposure (the area under the curve from the first to the last observation [AUC0−t] and from the first observation to the extrapolated time infinity [AUC0−∞]) showed a more than dose-proportional increase at doses between 40 mg/m2 and 133 mg/m2 and a largely less than dose-proportional increase at single ascending doses between 200 mg/m2 and 360 mg/m2 (Fig. 4). The maximum concentration (Cmax) was generally observed within 1 to 2 h following single legubicin infusion and approximately 4 h with repeat doses of legubicin. Legubicin exhibited a biphasic decline in total leucyl-doxorubicin plasma concentrations, with a mean terminal half-life (T1/2) between 6.27 h (40 mg/m2) and 22.62 h (133 mg/m2) at single doses and 21.21 h (270 mg/m2 Q2W) and 22.07 h (270 mg/m2 Q2W) at repeat doses. The cumulative exposure was 3437.82 ± 1563.66 mg and 1729.89 ± 956.67 mg after multiple infusions of legubicin 270 mg/m2 Q2W and Q3W, respectively. Overall, legubicin 270 mg/m2 Q2W or Q3W had similar PK profiles (Table 4).

Discussion

This phase 1a/b dose escalation and expansion trial explored the safety, PK/PD properties and antitumor activities of legubicin in patients with pretreated advanced solid tumors. Preliminary signals of antitumor activities were observed in patients with heavily pretreated advanced lung cancer. Overall, the patients had a manageable toxicity profile. Cardiotoxicities were infrequent and mostly grade 1 and did not require specific treatment. Improved safety and clinical activities of legubicin were supported by its favorable PK/PD characteristics, providing proof of concept for using the legumain-based tumor targeting strategy to achieve a favorable benefit-risk profile of legubicin.

Legubicin demonstrated a tolerable safety profile: only 2 of 26 DLT evaluable patients in phase 1a experienced DLTs, which did not appear to be dose related. Grade 3 or worse TEAEs occurred in 35.7% and 37.1% of the patients in phase 1a and 1b, respectively. The most frequent grade 3 or worse TEAEs were myelosuppression and vomiting, which were expected as they occur with doxorubicin and other anthracyclines [21, 22]. Toxicities were managed with dosing adjustments, and treatment discontinuations were rare. No MTD was established, and no new safety concerns emerged during the study. In comparison, in a phase 1b/2 trial of aldoxorubicin, 2 of 3 patients in the aldoxorubicin 450 mg/m2 cohort experienced DLTs (grade 4 neutropenia and grade 3 febrile neutropenia), and the MTD was established (350 mg/m2 or 260 mg/m2 doxorubicin equivalents) [23]. Legubicin could reduce systemic toxicity in normal tissues as an albumin complex, and in the TME, tumor-targeted activation and localized payload release are achieved through tumor-specific cleavage of legumain under acidic conditions, which distinguish it from other simple prodrugs like aldoxorubicin that rely on passive hydrolysis or non-specific enzymatic cleavage and may explain differential toxicity profiles of legubicin and albumin-binding prodrugs [24].

Based on exposure characteristics, DLTs and toxicities observed at 200 mg/m2 and 360 mg/m2 during dose escalation, legubicin 270 mg/m2 Q2W or Q3W was chosen for the phase 1b dose expansion study and selected as the RP2D. Compared to those treated with legubicin 360 mg/m2, patients treated with legubicin 270 mg/m2 had a longer median duration of exposure (96 days vs. 22 days; mean, 85.2 ± 44.6 days vs. 32.8 ± 43.4 days) and a greater median accumulative dose (2771.55 mg vs. 1090.80 mg; mean 2231.10 ± 979.03 mg vs. 1510.65 ± 1278.80 mg). Nevertheless, grade 3 or worse TEAEs were more frequent with legubicin 360 mg/m2 (16.7% [1/6] vs. 62.5% [5/8]), so were serious AEs (16.7% [1/6] vs. 37.5% [3/8]). In addition, DLT occurred in 1 patient in the legubicin 360 mg/m2 cohort and none in the legubicin 270 mg/m2 cohort. These findings indicated that legubicin 360 mg/m2 was less tolerable in practice than legubicin 270 mg/m2 despite not meeting the formal MTD criteria. These observations supported legubicin 270 mg/m2 as the highest dose that could be delivered consistently over time.

Dose-dependent and cumulative cardiotoxicity appears after doxorubicin administration and may require dose reductions or treatment discontinuations [25, 26]. In preclinical studies, legubicin demonstrated lower incidences and severities of cardiotoxicities in dogs compared to conventional doxorubicin at equivalent doses. The improved cardiac safety profile could be partially attributed to legubicin being present as a protein complex in blood and the extremely low levels of free leucyl doxorubicin and doxorubicin in plasma and the heart [18]. In the current trial, the plasma levels of free leucyl-doxorubicin and free doxorubicin were less than < 0.3% of total leucyl-doxorubicin following repeated doses of legubicin and the Cmax and AUC of total leucyl-doxorubicin were two to three orders of magnitude greater than for leucyl-doxorubicin and free doxorubicin, suggesting markedly reduced systemic exposure to doxorubicin. Unlikely extravasation of albumin-bound legubicin across the non-fenestrated endothelium and basement membrane of normal tissues diminished bioavailability to the heart and other organs. Consistently, cardiotoxicities were mostly grade 1 in both the phase 1a and 1b portions of the study and required no specific treatment.

In addition, the trial showed encouraging preliminary signals of antitumor activities of legubicin based on both investigator and IRC assessment. It is important to highlight that one of 7 efficacy evaluable lung cancer patients had a PR and 4 had a stable disease according to the RECIST criteria. Despite that these patients were heavily pretreated (≥ 3 lines of prior systemic therapy), the median PFS was 7.0 months, and the median OS was 15.5 months. Doxorubicin is a cornerstone chemotherapeutic agent for advanced ovarian cancer [27]. Also of note, 1 of 3 patients with ovarian cancer had a PR and 2 had a stable disease. Nevertheless, given the low number of objective responses, the wide confidence interval around the ORR estimate, and the heterogeneity of the study population, the efficacy findings should be considered exploratory. Stable disease should be interpreted cautiously, as it may reflect factors unrelated to drug activity in a phase 1 setting. The preliminary signals of clinical activity of legubicin require further testing in a larger and more homogenous patient population.

The key rationale for designing legubicin is to lessen the toxicities while enhancing the antitumor activities of legubicin by making use of legumain-cleavable tetrapeptide, thus potentially improving the therapeutic benefit-risk ratio of legubicin. Our trial sheds light on the association between PK, PD and clinical activities of legubicin. CD3+, CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in the peripheral blood showed no apparent trend of changes across different dosing cohorts, indicating that the presence of tetrapeptide in legubicin did not elicit potent immune responses. Legubicin half-life at the RP2D was approximately 21–22 h; this prolonged presence in blood could allow for increased tumor uptake of legubicin. In addition, legubicin had a low clearance and small distribution volume, suggesting enhanced deposition of legubicin in the TME, possibly due to enhanced permeation and retention (EPR) effect as a result of increased microvascular permeability in tumors [28].

In conclusion, this phase 1 study has demonstrated that legubicin is safe and well tolerated at a dose of 270 mg/m2 administered every 2 or 3 weeks, with preliminary signals of antitumor activity in patients with pretreated advanced tumors, including heavily pretreated lung cancer patients, and a favorable PK/PD profile of legubicin. Overall, legubicin has an acceptable risk benefit profile. Additional investigation in cancer-specific populations is required to further explore the clinical activities of legubicin.
