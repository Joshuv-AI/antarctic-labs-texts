Understanding the training need priorities of the Australian allied health workforce: a national survey
Authors: James Bartholomew, Kirby Adams, Chanelle Louwen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAllied health professionals (AHPs) are pivotal to delivering safe, effective, and collaborative healthcare. Yet, there is limited empirical insight into their current training need priorities across core domains of professional practice. In an increasingly digital, team-based health system, identifying profession-specific and shared training needs is essential for designing development strategies that retain and grow a future-ready workforce. This study aimed to identify training needs across the Australian AHP workforce and explore whether demographic factors predict perceived gaps between the importance and performance of key professional tasks, both within and across professional groups.MethodsA national cross-sectional survey of AHPs was conducted using the Hennessy Hicks Training Needs Analysis Questionnaire. Participants self-rated 30 core and 10 emerging tasks by perceived importance and current performance. Gap scores (importance minus performance) were analysed and visualised using an importance-performance matrix to highlight priority areas. Descriptive statistics, multiple regression models and multivariate analysis explored whether variation existed between profession and other demographic variables across seven domains of practice.ResultsFrom 2,436 valid responses a consistent pattern emerged: AHPs rated the importance of all tasks higher than their self-reported performance, highlighting statistically meaningful perceived capability gaps. The most pronounced needs were found in leadership/continuous improvement, and digital health domains. Emerging tasks such as managing work-life balance, achieving efficient results and identifying opportunities for quality improvement also ranked highly, reflecting the current landscape of AHP practice. While significant demographic differences including level of experience, highest qualification, profession and rurality were observed across domains, effect sizes were uniformly small, suggesting limited practical significance.ConclusionThis study provides the first national snapshot of AHP training needs and provides evidence that the future of allied health workforce development lies in shifting from siloed, discipline-specific training to capability-building that supports interdisciplinary, digitally enabled healthcare. Interprofessional education models provide an opportunity to address shared training needs in leadership, digital transformation, and system improvement, particularly for early-career professionals and those in remote roles. These findings highlight potential directions for aligning training with the evolving demands of healthcare delivery.Keywords: Allied health, Training needs analysis, Capability development, Education, Leadership, Work-life balance, Hennessey hicks training needs analysis

Background

In Australia, allied health encompasses a diverse group of more than 25 university-qualified professions [1]. These practitioners provide diagnostic, therapeutic, and preventative services that are distinct from medicine and nursing, and they represent a significant portion of the healthcare workforce across a wide range of settings [2, 3]. The breadth and diversity of allied health professions (AHP), with differences in technical expertise, scopes of practice, and education pathways, highlights the importance of discipline-specific training needs. However, with common areas of professional capability such as digital health, interprofessional practice, leadership, and quality improvement becoming more integral to effective healthcare, identifying and addressing both profession-specific and shared training needs is essential [4, 5]. Doing so not only equips AHPs to meet current and future healthcare challenges but also aligns with key policy and service drivers aimed at improving patient safety, enhancing quality of care and supporting workforce productivity [6, 7].

Training Needs Analysis (TNA) provides a systematic process for identifying gaps between current competencies and required skills within a workforce [8, 9]. The literature demonstrates the utility of TNA across a range of healthcare settings [10]; however, studies adopting a cross-disciplinary approach to examine training needs spanning multiple AHPs are lacking. Training Needs Analysis serves as a foundational step in facilitating the identification of specific areas where training is most needed, enabling the development of tailored educational interventions that enhance service delivery and patient outcomes [11]. A literature review by Gould et al. emphasized that TNA is instrumental in informing the design of continuing education programs, ensuring that they are aligned with the actual needs of practitioners [9]. Moreover, TNA has been linked to improved job satisfaction and retention among healthcare workers, as it fosters a culture of continuous learning and professional growth [12].

Conducting TNA is important in healthcare for several reasons. Firstly, it ensures that educational resources are allocated efficiently, focusing on areas that will yield the most significant impact on patient care [10, 13]. Secondly, it promotes the development of capabilities that are responsive to evolving healthcare demands, such as the integration of digital technologies and interprofessional collaboration. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention advocate for regular TNA to maintain a competent workforce [14]. Similarly, the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control emphasises the role of TNA in strengthening healthcare systems by identifying capacity gaps and informing workforce development strategies [13].

This is particularly relevant in remote and rural Australia, where workforce maldistribution, service access barriers, and broader scopes of practice highlight the need for training that is tailored to local capacity and community needs [15]. These areas are classified under the Modified Monash Model (MMM), a system that defines remoteness from MMM1 (metropolitan areas) to MMM7 (very remote communities) [16]. In the Australian context, the importance of TNA is underscored by the need to address predicted workforce shortages and ensure equitable access to quality care. By systematically identifying capability gaps, TNAs inform the design of targeted training initiatives that are responsive to specific workforce needs, including those unique to rural and remote settings where access to services is more limited [17]. A report by the Australian Government emphasised that such targeted training is essential for building a resilient and adaptable health workforce capable of meeting diverse community needs and adjusting to evolving service demands [18].

Recent studies have identified several key training priorities within the allied health sector [19]. Digital health competencies have become increasingly important, with AHPs expected to navigate electronic health records, telehealth platforms, and data analytic tools [20]. Leadership and management skills are also in demand, as AHPs often take on supervisory roles and migrate into roles that contribute to organisational decision-making with very little training involved [21].

Interprofessional education (IPE) has gained prominence as a strategy to address shared training needs across healthcare professions. By fostering collaborative learning environments, IPE promotes communication, teamwork, and a deeper understanding of professional roles, which in turn can enhance patient outcomes [22, 23]. The World Health Organisation’s Framework for Action on Interprofessional Education and Collaborative Practice [24] advocates for the integration of IPE into health professional curricula, emphasising its role in improving healthcare delivery. Taken together, these developments indicate the need for a comprehensive TNA that captures both common and profession-specific priorities. The aim of this study was to identify and prioritise the training needs of allied health professionals (AHPs) working within the Australian public health sector. The objectives were to examine and rank training needs across the core domains of allied health practice, and to explore variations in these needs according to demographic characteristics to inform the development of targeted training initiatives.

Methods

Design, setting and participants

This national study used an analytical cross-sectional design conducted between May and August 2024. An online, self-administered questionnaire was distributed via email to public sector health districts that chose to participate. In Australia, health districts are local administrative and service delivery units governed by state and territory health departments. The study was promoted through the 2023 National Allied Health Conference and peak allied health bodies in each state and territory. Participation was therefore based on convenience, with some jurisdictions, including Tasmania and the Australian Capital Territory, not represented as no sites expressed interest.

All AHP’s as described by Allied Health Professions Australia [1] were eligible. Allied health assistants, health promotion officers and welfare officers were excluded because their scope of practice, regulation, and education differ substantially, making their training needs not directly comparable to those of AHP’s [25–27].

At the start of the survey, participants viewed an information and consent form describing the study’s purpose and procedures. Only those who confirmed consent could proceed. No directly identifiable or sensitive personal information was collected, and all responses were de-identified. Only participants who completed the survey in full were included in the analysis [28].

Data collection

Training needs of AHP were measured using the Hennessy Hicks Training Needs Analysis Questionnaire (HHTNA-Q), a self-reported, close-ended structured questionnaire [29]. The HHTNA-Q was chosen for its psychometric testing for validity and reliability and its previous use in the healthcare sector [29]. The instrument is semi-opaque, thereby reducing the likelihood of response distortion and ensuring that the data more accurately reflect respondents’ training requirements [10]. The questionnaire included 30 core tasks that are relevant to the role of AHP’s, categorised into five superordinate domains: research/audit, communication/teamwork, clinical tasks, administration and management/supervisory tasks. Respondents rate each task along a 7-point Likert scale according to two criteria: perceived importance to their role and their current performance of the task. The difference between these two ratings (importance minus performance) indicated the relative training need for each task. The original HHTNA-Q allows for the inclusion of up to 10 additional items without compromising its psychometric properties [30]. For this study, 10 new items were developed from thematic reviews of the literature [15, 31–33] and local TNA [34] to capture contemporary priorities in allied health practice. These items were categorised into two additional domains: ‘utilisation of digital health’ and ‘leadership/continuous improvement’, both of which have been identified as core capability areas of the health workforce in recent years [35–37]. Table 1 provides the full list of domains and items, including those added for this study. To enhance face and content validity, the additional items were reviewed for clarity and relevance by experienced AHP’s prior to inclusion. Scale reliability analysis (Cronbach’s alpha >0.90) confirmed that the additional items did not adversely affect the overall internal consistency of the tool, consistent with best practice recommendations for instrument adaptation.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise importance and performance scores across the 40 items. Training needs were initially explored by calculating mean gap scores (importance minus performance) for each item and visualised using an importance-performance matrix. Paired-samples t tests were used to assess whether importance ratings significantly exceeded performance ratings, and effect sizes were calculated using Cohen’s d to assess the magnitude of discrepancy between the two ratings. Although Likert scales are ordinal in nature, prior research indicates that when scales have five or more response options, aggregated means approximate continuous distributions and support the use of parametric methods [38, 39]. This approach is consistent with established applications of the HHTNA-Q and similar training needs surveys [10, 30, 40].

To explore the predictability of demographic variables, multiple linear regression models were constructed using mean performance score as the dependent variable. Each model included demographic variables (profession, age group, years of experience, highest qualification, work setting and rurality). Multivariate analyses of variance (MANOVA) were conducted across all seven domains to test for differences in training need ratings using the same demographic factors. All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 29) with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 2436 AHPs completed the survey from across five Australian states and one territory. The majority of participants were female (83%), aged 28–43 (58%), had 11 years or more profession-based experience (58%), were working in a metropolitan area (76%) and a hospital setting (78%). Priority training needs at task and domain level

Scale reliability analysis demonstrated strong internal consistency for both the original 30-item HHTNA-Q (α = 0.93) and the modified 40-item questionnaire (α = 0.94). Paired-sample t-tests using the mean importance and performance item scores showed all comparisons were statistically significant (p < 0.001) and indicated consistency in the direction (performance < importance) and magnitude of the differences observed. This was further supported by most items scoring a positive Cohens d value, with effect size ranging from 0.06 (T29 – Undertaking administrative activities) to 1.12 (T36 - Managing a healthy work-life balance) suggesting a diverse range of training need priorities across the 40 items (Fig. 1).

To aid interpretation and prioritisation of workforce development needs, an importance–performance matrix was developed (Fig. 2). This method, which has been applied in other studies to illustrate training needs [41], not only enhances visual presentation but also highlights priority areas for action. The matrix plots items against mean importance (y-axis = 5.74) and performance (x-axis = 5.17) scores, with quadrant boundaries used to identify gaps. Items in the high-importance/low-performance quadrant represent the most urgent training needs. In this study, 15 items fell in quadrant one (low importance/low performance), four in quadrant two (high importance/low performance), 20 in quadrant three (high importance/high performance), and one in quadrant four (low importance/high performance). The four items identified as priority training needs (high importance/low performance) were: T12 – Accessing relevant literature for clinical work, T36 – Managing a healthy work-life balance, T37 – Developing more effective ways to get results, and T38 – Identifying an opportunity for quality improvement.

Demographic predictors of training needs

To examine whether demographic characteristics predicted perceived training need priority, separate linear regression models were conducted for each item in the high importance/low performance quadrant, using the mean score difference between importance and performance as the dependent variable. The initial model included age, gender, First Nations status, disability status, highest qualification, profession, years of experience, work setting and rurality (based on the Modified Monash Model - MMM classification). Preliminary analyses indicated that gender, disability status, and First Nations status did not contribute significantly to any model and were excluded to reduce overfitting and improve model parsimony. All four item models were statistically significant (p < 0.001), indicating that demographic variables explained a small but significant proportion of variance in training need scores (Table 3). The strongest model was for item T37 – Developing more effective ways to get results (F = 26.01, p < 0.001, adjusted R² = 0.049), with age, qualification, rurality, and profession emerging as significant predictors. Item T12 – Accessing relevant literature for clinical work also showed a meaningful fit (F = 21.20, p < 0.001, adjusted R² = 0.040), influenced by age, qualification, and rurality. For item T36 – Managing a healthy work-life balance, predictors were age and rurality (F = 19.88, p < 0.001, adjusted R² = 0.037). Item T38 – Identifying an opportunity for quality improvement yielded similar findings (F = 20.78, p < 0.001, adjusted R² = 0.039), with qualification, years of experience, and rurality as significant predictors.

Rurality (based on MMM classification) was the most consistent predictor, with training needs increasing as remoteness increased, highlighting geographic variation in priorities. Age and qualification were also frequent predictors. Effect sizes were modest across all four items (adjusted R² = 0.037–0.049), suggesting that targeted training interventions may be most effective when tailored to demographic characteristics, particularly in remote and very remote communities.

A series of multivariate analyses of variance (MANOVAs) were conducted to examine the influence of demographic characteristics including age group, profession, years of experience, highest qualification level, primary work location, and rurality on training needs across seven professional practice domains. Training needs were calculated using mean importance–performance gap scores for each domain, with higher scores indicating greater perceived need.

Initial two-way MANOVAs tested for interaction effects between key demographic variables (e.g., age × experience), but none were significant (p > 0.05). Accordingly, separate one-way MANOVAs were conducted for each factor. Significant multivariate effects were found for age group (F(21, 6978) = 6.455, partial η² = 0.018), years of experience (F(21, 6978) = 10.566, partial η² = 0.030), profession (F(126, 15857) = 2.296, partial η² = 0.017), qualification (F(42, 11387) = 4.235, partial η² = 0.012), work setting (F(14, 4422) = 3.770, partial η² = 0.012), and rurality (F(42, 11387) = 4.602, partial η² = 0.013). Although all effects were statistically significant (p < 0.001), effect sizes were small, indicating modest subgroup differences. The strongest effect was observed for years of experience.

Follow-up univariate ANOVAs were conducted to explore domain-level effects in more detail. These analyses demonstrated that demographic factors influenced perceived training needs across all seven domains to varying degrees (Table 4). Highest qualification level and years of experience emerged as the most consistent predictors across domains, particularly in the research/audit and communication/teamwork domains. The largest individual effect sizes were observed for rurality in the leadership/continuous improvement domain (partial η² = 0.048) and the management/supervisory domain (partial η² = 0.036). In contrast, administrative tasks and digital health utilisation domains showed relatively minimal variation by demographic subgroup, indicating that training needs in these areas may be more universally experienced across the allied health workforce.

Post hoc comparisons were performed using Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference test where omnibus ANOVA results indicated statistically significant group differences. In the research and audit domain, significant differences were observed across age, qualification, profession, work setting, and rurality. Participants aged 18–27 years reported significantly higher training needs than those aged 44 years and older. Those with highest qualification of diploma and bachelor level reported greater needs than those with doctoral qualifications. Several individual allied health professions reported higher training needs than speech pathologists, who consistently recorded lower scores across all domains. (Fig. 3). This pattern suggests that many of the observed post hoc differences may be attributed to the overall low self-assessments reported by one AHP (speech pathology), rather than notably elevated needs among other professions. Higher training needs were also found among those working in hospital or community settings, compared to those in addiction and mental health services. Professionals located in very remote communities (MMM 7) reported higher needs than those in metropolitan areas (MMM 1) and medium rural towns (MMM 4).

In the communication and teamwork domain, participants aged 18–27 reported higher needs than all other groups, while those aged 28–43 rated their needs lower than those 44 and older. A similar pattern was observed for experience, with those reporting ≤ 5 years’ experience indicating greater needs than those with > 6 years. Differences across qualification levels were modest, though doctoral-qualified professionals consistently reported the lowest needs. Rurality patterns were mixed, likely reflecting sampling variability.

For clinical items, AHPs aged 28–43 years and those with ≤ 5 years’ experience reported greater needs. Bachelor-qualified respondents reported higher needs than doctoral-qualified respondents. Rural differences were also evident: those in large rural towns (MMM 3) reported greater gaps than those in very remote areas (MMM 7), driven by higher importance ratings in MMM 3. Profession-level comparisons were again influenced by lower mean scores among Speech Pathologists.

In the administrative domain, no statistically significant post hoc differences were observed.

For management and supervisory items, AHPs aged 28–59 reported greater needs than those aged 18–27. Qualification continued to differentiate responses, with bachelor-level respondents reporting higher needs than doctoral-level respondents. Participants in regional centres (MMM 2) reported higher needs than those in remote (MMM 6) and very remote (MMM 7) areas.

In digital health utilisation, doctoral-qualified professionals reported lower needs than all other qualification groups. Respondents in large rural towns (MMM 3) reported greater needs than those in very remote areas (MMM 7). Those working in addiction and mental health services also reported higher needs than those working in hospital or community settings.

For leadership and continuous improvement, respondents in very remote communities (MMM 7) reported significantly higher needs than all other MMM groups. Participants aged 18–27 and those with less experience also reported greater needs. Several professions reported higher needs than Speech Pathology, consistent with other domains.

Although many demographic variables reached statistical significance, effect sizes were small. Findings suggest training needs are not strongly or consistently predicted by demographics, with several significant differences driven by outlier groups; notably Speech Pathology, which consistently reported lower scores across domains.

Discussion

This study identified the priority training needs of Australian AHPs working in the public sector and examined whether these differed across demographic groups. Three areas consistently emerged as high-priority needs: managing a heathy work-life balance, identifying an opportunity for quality improvement and developing more effective ways to get results. At the domain level, the largest training gaps were in Leadership/Continuous Improvement and Utilisation of Digital Health. These findings suggest that contemporary training needs extend beyond traditional clinical competencies and are increasingly shaped by systemic, technological and workforce pressures.

Our results align with those of Pang et al. (2024), who applied the HHTNA-Q with AHPs in regional and rural Victoria [34]. They identified research and education delivery as high-priority needs, along with challenges in digital learning and assessment design. Notably, they reported lower satisfaction with continuous professional development among mid-career professionals (6–10 years of experience), indicating that career stage may influence training needs in ways not fully captured in our study. Together, these findings highlight both the utility of the HHTNA-Q and the value of supplementing it with items that address advanced practice and discipline-specific competencies.

While Pang et al. only focused on CPD needs within a regional cohort, their work highlighted the absence of cohesive evidence on workforce-wide capability gaps in AHPs and the extent to which training needs cluster within or between professions [34]. Our study extends on this evidence by examining these questions at a national level and demonstrating that demographic factors such as profession, age, years of experience, work setting, and rurality accounted for only a small proportion of the variance in training needs. Regression models returned adjusted R-squared values below 0.06, and similar patterns were observed at the domain level. This suggests that demographic segmentation has limited predictive value for planning workforce education and training. Workforce development strategies should therefore be guided primarily by common role expectations and identified task-level competencies, while providing targeted support for early-career professionals and those working in rural or remote areas, who consistently identified higher training needs across most domains in this study. Further, other factors not captured in this study, such as organisational culture, access to supervision, leadership opportunities, and time pressures, may also have an influence and should be carefully considered in workforce training and development initiatives [43, 44]. Importantly, aligning training not only with practitioner learning needs but also with service provider priorities strengthens its impact. This reflects the value of authentic professional learning, whereby uncertainties and challenges encountered in practice prompt meaningful, practice-embedded learning opportunities [45].

A consistent pattern across all 40 items was that importance ratings exceeded performance ratings. This discrepancy highlights a perceived gap between what AHPs believe to be important for effective practice and their current confidence in performing those tasks. Such gaps may reflect limited access to training, supervision, or opportunities to apply skills in the workplace. They may also reflect respondents’ recognition of the need for ongoing, career-long professional learning in the context of complex and rapidly changing practice landscapes. Prioritising these identified needs through targeted training can enhance both skill development and confidence, which has been shown to improve job satisfaction and workforce engagement [46]. Moreover, research suggests that planned and responsive professional development initiatives contribute positively to workforce retention, particularly when staff perceive that their individual development needs are acknowledged and addressed [46–48].

Work-life balance was prioritised across all demographic groups and professions, reflecting its recognised impact on staff well-being and healthcare delivery [42, 49]. This finding is consistent with evidence linking workload pressures to reduced well-being and service quality [42, 49]. The high rating underscores the need for training in psychological resilience, stress management, and sustainable work practices [50].

Similarly, value-based healthcare is gaining momentum as systems seek to align outcomes with cost-effectiveness and patient-centred care, requiring new capabilities in economic and strategic thinking [51]. In parallel, the rapid expansion of digital health, including artificial intelligence and data-driven decision-making, highlights the need for digital literacy and ethical reasoning skills [52, 53]. These findings suggest that contemporary training programs must be agile and future-oriented, equipping AHPs with skills to navigate technological, systemic, and personal challenges in a transforming healthcare landscape. Further, this aligns with broader workforce literature, emphasizing the growing need for system-level digital capabilities, including digital transformation, strategic leadership, and quality improvement [8, 54]. Equipping AHPs with these skills is essential for driving service innovation, improving efficiency and ensuring safe, ethical use of technology [55–57].

Leadership and continuous improvement also emerged as a consistently high priority need across all professional groups. This is consistent with existing literature showing that many clinicians are expected to lead teams and influence strategy without formal training [58, 59]. Training in leadership, design thinking and improvement science can strengthen workforce capacity to manage change and deliver quality improvement at scale. Targeted leadership development programs are therefore required to sustaining both high quality patient care and a resilient workforce [60].

The relatively small differences in training needs across professions suggest opportunities for shared learning approaches. However, it is important to distinguish between interprofessional education (IPE) and shared competency training. IPE involves professionals learning with, from, and about each other to improve collaboration and patient outcomes, and is most effective when the learning objectives emphasise teamwork, role understanding, and interprofessional practice [23]. Shared competency training, by contrast, involves different professions learning the same content without necessarily engaging in an interprofessional format [50]. Our findings suggest that both approaches are indicated. Interprofessional education should be considered as one strategy within a broader training framework, most suited to domains where collaboration and shared capability development are essential (e.g., teamwork, communication), while shared competency training models may be more appropriate for areas like leadership and digital health [54, 59, 61]. At the same time, profession-specific training remains essential to maintain technical expertise and discipline-specific competencies.

Limitations

While this study contributes novel insights into AHP training needs, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional, self-reported design may introduce bias, as responses can be influenced by perception, recall, or social desirability [62]. Although the HHTNA-Q was developed with rigorous psychometric validation, pilot testing, and cultural adaptation across settings [10, 30], these processes cannot eliminate such risks. Self-reported performance was also used as a proxy for capability, which may not always reflect actual competence [63].

Second, the sample was not tested for representativeness of the Australian public health sector workforce. Participation was based on health districts opting in, and some jurisdictions did not take part, meaning the results may not fully capture the diversity of allied health experiences across all states, territories, and service settings. Nevertheless, few statistically significant differences in training need priorities were identified within the sample, and where present, effect sizes were modest, suggesting overall consistency in training needs across groups.

Finally, although the HHTNA-Q has been applied internationally, it has not been widely used in allied health research, limiting opportunities for direct comparison. Some items, particularly within the clinical domain (e.g. ‘treating patients’), are broad in scope and may be less sensitive to discipline-specific or advanced clinical training needs. Future studies may strengthen the tool’s utility by adapting or supplementing items to better capture higher-level clinical competencies, advanced practice roles, and profession-specific requirements. Mixed methods approaches, including qualitative interviews, longitudinal studies, and triangulation of self-reported data with objective measures such as workplace assessments or supervisor ratings, are also recommended to provide a more comprehensive understanding of training needs and the impact of interventions on workforce capability [13, 34, 64].

Conclusion

This TNA offers a timely and comprehensive examination of the evolving development priorities for Australia’s allied health workforce. The findings highlight a clear need to modernise professional development approaches; shifting from traditional, discipline-specific models towards capability-building that reflects the realities of interdisciplinary, digitally enabled, and rapidly transforming healthcare environments.

Although some demographic factors, such as experience, age, and qualification level, were statistically associated with training needs, effect sizes were small and inconsistent. No demographic variable emerged as a strong predictor, reinforcing that workforce development should focus on shared training priorities rather than demographic segmentation. This was particularly true in the domains of leadership/continuous improvement and utilisation of digital health. These areas reflect contemporary system-wide shifts in healthcare priorities, including the need for adaptive leadership, data-informed practice, and cross-disciplinary collaboration.

The consistent gap between the perceived importance and self-rated performance across tasks underscores a need for targeted training. Interprofessional education (IPE) models are well-suited to address shared learning needs in domains where collaboration and role understanding are central, offering efficiency, consistency, and team-based benefits. However, shared competency training may be more appropriate for areas such as digital health or leadership, where common content can be delivered without requiring an interprofessional format. Training strategies should therefore retain flexibility to incorporate both shared and discipline-specific approaches to ensure capability development meets the diverse needs of the allied health workforce.

Taken together, these insights provide an evidence-informed foundation for allied health workforce development that aligns with the broader goals of healthcare reform. Investing in targeted, domain specific, and scalable training across all sectors and experience levels will not only enhance allied health workforce capability, but also contribute to professional satisfaction, service quality, and system sustainability.
