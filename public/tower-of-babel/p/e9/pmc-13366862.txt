Long-term preservation strategy for legume root nodule phenotypes coupled with a comprehensive evaluation method
Authors: Ya Zhao, Can Guo, Wen Zhang, Du Wang, Jun Jiang, Ao Liu, Wei Wang, Qin Han, Qi Zhang, Peiwu Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Nodule color and morphology are key readouts of legume symbiotic performance. However, long-term preservation of post-excavation nodules with intact morphology, color, and microbial cleanliness remains a major challenge. This study developed a two-stage aqueous-phase preservation method (TAPP) that enables rapid structural fixation and long-term chemical stabilization. Peanut and soybean nodules from multiple regions and cultivars were tracked for 24 months under five preservation methods: TAPP, FormalinCu, TAPP-Resin, Resin, and AirDry. TAPP showed the best overall preservation, with composite morphological scores of 4.65 ± 0.14 for peanut and 4.63 ± 0.22 for soybean at 24 months, and no visible mold. In addition, this study further proposes a stage-wise workflow that integrates on-site pre-fixation, long-distance transport, and long-term storage to enable cross-regional circulation and collaborative phenomics of oxidation-prone, dehydration-sensitive nodules. Introduction

Legume crops are key components of global agroecosystems and the terrestrial nitrogen cycle [1, 2]. Through symbiosis with rhizobia, soybean and peanut plants enable the fixation of atmospheric N2 into bioavailable nitrogen, reducing fertilizer inputs and mitigating the environmental impacts of agricultural production [3, 4]. As the primary site of symbiotic nitrogen fixation, the root nodule serves as a widely used phenotypic indicator of symbiotic performance, reflected by nodule number, size, morphology, spatial distribution, and internal coloration [5]. Nodule number and biomass reflect the extent of symbiosis establishment [6], spatial distribution is closely linked to root system architecture and carbon–nitrogen allocation strategies [7]. The gradual shift of internal nodule color from pink to brown provides a readily interpretable signal of declining nitrogen-fixation activity [8]. Moreover, nodule phenotypes are highly sensitive to agronomic management and environmental stresses such as drought and salinity, providing informative entry points for investigating nitrogen-fixation capacity and environmental adaptation in legumes [9].

Current legume nodule phenotyping largely relies on morphology-based evaluation. Standardized image acquisition and annotation procedures have improved the consistency and cross-experiment comparability of nodule traits, including nodule number, size, and spatial distribution [10]. For color, the field has progressed from subjective visual grading to objective quantification based on standardized imaging and spectroscopic approaches, enabling quantitative color phenotyping using signals such as leghemoglobin [11, 12]. With advances in high-throughput phenotyping, automated analysis pipelines that integrate computer vision and machine learning have further enabled high-throughput extraction of these traits [10, 13]. In addition, for data standardization and traceability, minimum-information metadata standards such as the Minimum Information About a Plant Phenotyping Experiment (MIAPPE) define structured metadata descriptors that facilitate cross-study and cross-platform data integration and comparison [14, 15]. However, most existing frameworks target in situ or short-term assays and do not adequately account for nodule-specific physicochemical properties or post-harvest phenotypic deterioration, thereby limiting standardized assessment of color and structural changes during long-term storage or long-distance transport.

In addition, whereas genetic information can be preserved for the long term in DNA libraries and germplasm collections, root nodule phenotypes are inherently transient [16]. Highly hydrated nodule tissues are enriched in polyphenolics, heme proteins such as leghemoglobin, and metal ions [17]. After plant excavation and shoot removal, air exposure rapidly promotes enzymatic browning and oxidative reactions, resulting in distorted nodule coloration [18, 19]. In parallel, water loss induces cellular collapse, leading to pronounced shrinkage and deformation of the three-dimensional structure [20]. Moreover, microbes commonly colonize nodule surfaces, and mold readily develops under humid or semi-enclosed conditions, further compromising tissue integrity [21]. Therefore, root nodule samples should be processed and preserved in a standardized manner as soon as possible after collection to maintain the original fidelity of color and spatial structure. Current presevation of plant samples relies mainly on press-drying and general-purpose wet preservation, yet neither is suitable for preserving root nodules [22, 23]. Press-drying is appropriate for planar organs such as leaves but has major limitations for root nodule systems: fine roots readily tangle and break, nodules dehydrate and collapse or detach, and three-dimensional phenotypic information is irreversibly lost. General-purpose wet preservation typically employs conventional fixatives such as formalin, ethanol, or FAA for long-term specimen storage [24]. However, extended storage of root nodules in these fixatives rarely preserves phenotypic fidelity. Existing nodule-preservation approaches have not been optimized for nodule-specific physicochemical properties, making it difficult to maintain stable morphology and color and thereby failing to meet the consistency requirements of phenotypic analyses [25, 26].

Within this problem, we pose two key scientific questions. First, how can a stage-wise handling and processing strategy, aligned with nodule vulnerabilities to dehydration-induced shrinkage, oxidative browning, and microbial growth, enable rapid three-dimensional fixation and long-term stable preservation while maintaining morphological and color fidelity during long-distance transport? Second, how can a reproducible, quantitative quality-evaluation framework be established to yield traceable and comparable phenotypic measurements across cultivars, treatments, and years, thereby supporting cross-source integration, repository construction, and digital phenotyping of symbiotic nitrogen fixation in legumes?

Accordingly, we developed a two-stage aqueous-phase preservation method (TAPP) for oxidation-prone, dehydration-sensitive legume nodules that couples rapid stabilization of three-dimensional architecture with sustained physicochemical stabilization during storage. We further establish a standardized multidimensional evaluation framework integrating morphology grading, color stability quantification, and visible contamination assessment for systematic benchmarking of preservation performance. In addition, we define a stage-wise operational workflow that enables on-site pre-fixation, long-distance transport, collaborative analysis, and long-term archiving of nodule samples. Together, these components provide a traceable and cross-laboratory comparable basis for preserving nodule phenotypes in longitudinal and cross-source phenomics studies, with potential utility for germplasm identification and precision breeding for enhanced nitrogen fixation in legumes.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and sample preparation

Root nodule samples were collected from representative peanut- and soybean-growing regions of China. The field collection covered eight site–cultivar combinations from seven field sites: Huayu 23 from Fuxin, Liaoning; Yuhua 37 from Kaifeng, Henan; Zhonghua 34 from Wuhan, Hubei; Jihua 21 from Handan, Hebei; Mengdou 170 and Xingdou 8 from Hinggan League, Inner Mongolia; Heinong 46 from Harbin, Heilongjiang; and Qiandou 12 from Bijie, Guizhou. Details of cultivars, sowing dates, and sampling dates are provided in Supplementary Table S-1. Crop management followed local conventional practices at each site.

Representative plants were randomly sampled using a five-point method. Peanut samples were collected from the pegging stage through the maturity stage, and soybean samples were collected at the seed filling stage. Plants were excavated carefully to preserve the root system and attached nodules, minimizing breakage and detachment. Bulk soil was removed in water, followed by gentle rinsing and soft-brush cleaning to remove residual soil from roots and nodules. Mechanical damage during washing was carefully avoided. After washing, residual surface moisture was removed with filter paper. Root systems bearing nodules were then immersed for 15 min in 75% (v/v) ethanol supplemented with potassium sorbate (2 g·L-1), propylene glycol (10 g·L-1), and polyoxyethylene lauryl ether (15 g·L-1) to reduce surface microbial load. This common surface antimicrobial pre-treatment was applied uniformly to all samples before any preservation workflow to reduce site-dependent variation in initial surface microbial load and standardize the starting conditions across treatments.

Development of the two-stage nodule preservation method

To prevent dehydration, discoloration, and mold after sampling, we developed the TAPP method by integrating polymer infiltration and crosslinking, osmotic control, surface protection, and antimicrobial preservation. Stage Ⅰ was the polymer crosslinking and fixation stage. The fixative was prepared in deionized water and contained 20% (v/v) formaldehyde, 5% (w/v) H2SO3, 20% (v/v) polyethylene glycol 200 (PEG-200), polysorbate 80 (350 g·L-1), copper(II) acetate (25 g·L-1), and boric acid (5 g·L-1). Pre-treated samples were immersed in fixative (≥ 20:1, v/m) and kept at room temperature in the dark for 72 h. Samples were manually inverted every 12 h to ensure uniform penetration and exchange of the fixative within nodule tissues. This stage rapidly stabilized nodule three-dimensional structure and volume via formaldehyde cross-linking and polymer filling, while slowing early color deterioration. Stage II comprised an antioxidative stabilization phase for long-term storage. The storage solution was prepared in deionized water and contained 5% (w/v) H2SO3, 20% (v/v) PEG-200, and MgSO4·7H2O (2 g·L-1). After stage Ⅰ fixation, samples were transferred to the storage solution at the same liquid-to-fresh-mass ratio (≥ 20:1, v/m) and kept for 48 h at room temperature, followed by long-term storage in the same solution. This stage continuously suppressed oxidative deterioration by establishing a reducing microenvironment and maintained water balance through osmotic regulation. Overall, the two-stage workflow couples rapid structural fixation with long-term chemical stabilization to preserve nodule volume, morphology, and color during extended storage.

Preservation treatments for fresh root nodule samples

Four conventional preservation methods were included as controls alongside TAPP. In the long-term preservation experiment, five preservation methods were evaluated for each crop: TAPP, FormalinCu, TAPP-Resin, Resin, and AirDry. For each crop and preservation method, four independent biological replicates were included (n = 4), corresponding to four site–cultivar combinations within that crop. Each biological replicate consisted of one intact root system with attached nodules collected from an individual plant; thus, the individual plant served as the experimental unit. At each assessment time point, 20 plant units were included within each crop species, corresponding to five preservation methods with four biological replicates each. Samples collected from the same field site and within the same sampling batch were randomly assigned to preservation treatments after the common surface antimicrobial pre-treatment described in Sect. 2.1. This design reduced potential confounding from site-, cultivar-, batch-, and initial microbial-load differences, allowing preservation methods to be compared primarily within each crop species. Site–cultivar combinations were included as biological sources of variation but were not analyzed as independent fixed effects because the primary objective was to compare preservation methods over time.

Samples were assessed non-destructively at 0, 1, 3, 6, 12, and 24 months, with the same plant units followed longitudinally across all time points. The 0-month assessment was performed immediately after completion of each preservation treatment and before long-term storage. Therefore, the 0-month measurements represented a post-treatment baseline rather than the original freshly harvested untreated state. After each assessment, samples were returned to their original storage conditions.

Conventional wet preservation (FormalinCu)

After pre-treatment, samples were fixed for 24 h at room temperature in a formalin–copper solution containing 5% (v/v) formaldehyde and 0.5% (w/v) CuSO4, and then transferred to 5% (v/v) formalin for long-term storage, as previously described [27]. Solution volume was sufficient to fully cover the entire root system and attached nodules, and containers were tightly capped to minimize evaporation.

Air drying (AirDry)

Following pre-treatment, samples were gently blotted with sterile filter paper to remove surface water. Whole root systems were then spread in a single layer on sterile filter paper, with no overlap. This prevented localized moisture retention and tangling of fine roots. Then, samples were air-dried under controlled conditions (20 ± 2 °C, 40 ± 5% RH) with continuous ventilation until a constant weight was reached. Filter paper was replaced as needed to prevent rewetting at contact points. Dried samples were subsequently stored at room temperature [22].

Direct epoxy resin encapsulation (Resin)

Epoxy resin A adhesive (Phoenix epoxy resin E51; Nantong Xingchen Synthetic Materials Co., Ltd.) and epoxy resin curing agent B adhesive (curing agent D230; Kunlun Jiumei Electronic Equipment Co., Ltd.) were used for encapsulation. Components A and B were mixed at an A:B ratio of 3:1 (w/w) immediately before use [28].

The pre-treated samples were removed from the solution and gently blotted with sterile filter paper to remove excess surface moisture while avoiding compression of nodules. A small volume of freshly mixed epoxy was then poured into a clean silicone mold to form a thin base layer. The root system was positioned in the mold, and epoxy was poured slowly along the mold wall to minimize bubble entrapment and to fully cover the sample. The resin level was adjusted to ensure complete encapsulation, with at least 5 mm of resin above the highest point of the sample. After casting, the molds were left undisturbed. The blocks were cured at room temperature for approximately 48 h until fully hardened, then demolded and stored at room temperature until evaluation [29].

Two-stage TAPP followed by epoxy resin encapsulation (TAPP-Resin)

Samples were first preserved following the complete TAPP workflow (Sect. 2.2). After completion of Stage II, they were retrieved from the storage solution and surface liquid was gently removed by blotting with sterile filter paper. Encapsulation was then performed using the same epoxy system as in the Resin group (Sect. 2.3.3), with an A: B mixing ratio of 3:1 (w/w), identical casting procedures, and curing at room temperature for 48 h. The cured blocks were stored at room temperature until evaluation.

Peanut samples collected from Fuxin, Liaoning, and soybean samples collected from Hinggan League, Inner Mongolia, were used as representative materials. For each crop, samples collected from the same field site and within the same sampling batch were randomly assigned to three transport groups: B-T, SI-T, and SI-NT. Each group contained four independent biological replicates (n = 4), and each biological replicate consisted of one intact root system with attached nodules collected from an individual plant. Thus, 24 plant units contributed to the transport experiment, corresponding to two crop species × three transport groups × four biological replicates.

Composite evaluation of nodule phenotypes

To compare long-term preservation performance across methods, we developed an integrated evaluation framework combining morphology, microbial contamination, and color stability. Morphology was evaluated using a 0–5 multi-criteria grading system across four dimensions: nodule fullness, collapse control, surface crack control, and root system integrity. Detailed grading criteria and representative examples are provided in Supplementary Table S-2. Nodule fullness captured volume retention and surface smoothness; collapse control quantified tissue shrinkage and surface indentation; surface crack control considered crack number, length, and severity; and root system integrity assessed the preservation of primary, lateral, and fine roots and the overall root architecture [30]. A composite morphology score was calculated as the arithmetic mean of the four dimension scores. Visible contamination was graded on a 0–3 scale based on visible mold incidence and severity. Detailed grading criteria are provided in Supplementary Table S-3.

All assessments were performed independently by three trained evaluators using a standardized, blinded workflow (Supplementary Protocol S-1). For each sample at each time point, the final morphology score used for downstream analyses was the mean of the three evaluators’ scores. Assessments were non-destructive, and samples were returned to their original storage conditions immediately after evaluation.

Color stability was quantified using CIE L*a*b*. Images were acquired using our previously developed root nodule imaging and analysis system [13], with the focus on extracting color measurements from specific regions of interest (ROIs) on the external surface of the liquid-preserved samples. This approach enabled the assessment of color changes over time while maintaining the sample’s preservation integrity. Because images were acquired from preserved samples through storage liquid and transparent containers, L*, a*, and b* values were interpreted as relative color measurements obtained under a standardized imaging setup rather than as absolute colorimetric measurements of nodule tissue. All samples were imaged using the same imaging system, illumination conditions, background, container type, and ROI selection procedure to ensure within-experiment comparability. L*, a*, and b* values were extracted in ImageJ (v1.53) [31], and color was summarized by averaging these values across all visible nodules for each sample. Data processing and statistical analysis

Data processing and visualization were conducted in R (v4.2.0) using ggplot2 (v3.4.0). Linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) were fitted using the lme4 package, and post hoc comparisons were performed using estimated marginal means from the emmeans package. For each sample at each time point, scores from three independent evaluators were averaged to obtain one value per biological replicate. Data were presented as mean ± SD.

Because the same plant units were evaluated repeatedly across storage time, longitudinal data were analyzed using LMMs to account for within-plant correlation. Composite morphology score and ΔE were analyzed separately for each crop. Preservation method, storage time, and their interaction were included as fixed effects. Storage time was treated as a categorical factor because the assessment intervals were unequal and the primary objective was to compare preservation methods at defined storage stages. Plant identity was included as a random intercept to account for repeated measurements of the same biological replicate over time. For composite morphology score, all six assessment time points were included in the LMM analysis. For ΔE, the 0-month records were retained for descriptive trajectory visualization but excluded from the LMM analysis because ΔE was defined relative to the 0-month post-treatment baseline and therefore equaled zero at baseline. Post hoc comparisons between TAPP and each comparator method at each storage time point were performed using estimated marginal means, with P values adjusted using the Holm method. Two-sided adjusted P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Visible contamination grade was summarized descriptively across preservation methods and time points because the ordinal scores were highly zero-inflated, with no visible contamination observed in TAPP and FormalinCu samples throughout the 24-month storage period. Inter-rater reliability of morphology grading was quantified using the intraclass correlation coefficient under a two-way random-effects model with absolute agreement and was reported as the average-measure reliability across three raters, ICC(2,3).

Results

Baseline-referenced quantitative phenotyping metrics for nodule preservation

During storage after sampling, legume root nodules underwent progressive and irreversible deterioration. As a result, purely qualitative descriptions were insufficient for objective comparisons across preservation methods, time points, and sampling batches. To address this, we established a quantitative phenotyping framework encompassing morphology, color stability, and visible contamination, using explicit and standardized metrics and scoring scales.

Because samples collected from different cultivars and regions inherently varied in root architecture, nodule size, nodule distribution, and baseline color, direct comparison of absolute values across all samples would have been strongly influenced by background biological heterogeneity. We therefore adopted a baseline-referenced evaluation strategy. For each preservation treatment, measurements obtained at 0 months were used as the internal reference baseline, and subsequent observations were expressed as deviations relative to that baseline. Therefore, the longitudinal analyses described preservation stability after treatment completion and did not quantify immediate fixation-induced changes relative to freshly harvested untreated nodules. In addition, to further reduce confounding from site-specific and batch-specific variation, samples collected from the same site within the same sampling batch were randomly assigned to the different preservation methods, and treatment effects were evaluated primarily within batch. Longitudinal changes were thus interpreted relative to each sample’s own 0-month post-processing baseline. This normalization framework improved comparability across heterogeneous sampling backgrounds and supported harmonized integration of longitudinal, cross-source phenotypic datasets. Across the site–cultivar combinations included in this study, the preservation methods showed generally consistent relative performance, supporting the applicability of TAPP beyond a single genotype or sampling source. Long-term morphological preservation across methods

Using the above framework, we systematically compared the ability of different preservation methods to maintain nodule morphology. Quantitative results are summarized in Fig. 2. Fig. 2A and B presented the longitudinal trajectories of composite morphology scores for peanut and soybean, respectively, showing that TAPP provided the strongest long-term morphological preservation among the tested methods. Linear mixed-effects model-based post hoc comparisons between TAPP and each comparator method for composite morphology score at each storage time point are provided in Table S-4. At 24 months, TAPP maintained significantly higher composite morphology scores than all comparator methods in both peanut and soybean after Holm adjustment. For peanut, the composite score was 4.92 ± 0.12 at 12 months and remained high at 4.65 ± 0.14 at 24 months. The heatmap of morphological sub-scores (Fig. 2C) further showed that, after 24 months, peanut nodules retained high scores for nodule fullness (4.42 ± 0.51) and collapse control (4.33 ± 0.49), with particularly strong surface crack control (5.00) and root system integrity (4.83 ± 0.40). These scores indicated that nodule volume and surface morphology were well preserved, and the spatial architecture among primary, lateral, and fine roots remained largely unchanged. Soybean showed a similar pattern: the composite score was 4.63 ± 0.22 at 24 months, and sub-scores remained high in the heatmap (Fig. 2D), indicating good applicability across legume species.

In contrast, conventional methods generally exhibited more pronounced morphological degradation, with composite scores declining rapidly as storage duration increased. In peanut, the FormalinCu treatment decreased to 3.58 ± 0.18 at 24 months and was associated with moderate volume shrinkage and surface wrinkling. The Resin treatment yielded a score of 2.33 ± 0.12, likely due to dehydration and resin hardening, and was characterized by widespread collapse and surface cracking. AirDry showed the most pronounced deterioration, with a composite score of 1.23 ± 0.04, characterized by extensive nodule shrinkage and detachment and severe loss of root-system integrity. Notably, samples processed with TAPP-Resin showed intermediate performance between TAPP and Resin alone. After 24 months, peanut samples in the TAPP-Resin group retained a nodule fullness score of 3.83 ± 0.39 and a composite score of 3.35 ± 0.08, indicating that TAPP pretreatment mitigated morphology loss associated with resin embedding. Soybean showed a broadly similar pattern of morphological changes across treatments, and detailed scores are provided in Supplementary Table S-6. A related, but distinct, trend was observed for attached nodule retention. As shown in Fig. 1, attached nodule retention at 24 months remained high in the TAPP, FormalinCu, TAPP-Resin, and Resin treatments, but declined markedly under AirDry to 74.15 ± 1.91% in peanut and 75.48 ± 4.72% in soybean (Table S-7). Overall, compared with conventional plant-preservation approaches, TAPP provided the strongest preservation of nodule morphology and root structural features during long-term storage. TAPP pretreatment also improved resin-embedded samples relative to direct resin encapsulation, yielding structurally more stable specimens suitable for downstream phenotyping and display.

Color deviation and color-drift kinetics during storage

Using the CIE L*a*b* color space, we systematically evaluated long-term color preservation across different methods. Because ΔE was calculated relative to the 0-month post-treatment baseline under a standardized imaging setup, it was interpreted as a relative indicator of color drift during storage. In peanut, color drift occurred in all treatments, but the magnitude and rate of change differed markedly among methods. Visible mold incidence and contamination severity during storage

Figure 4 showed visible contamination levels in peanut and soybean nodules over 0–24 months. TAPP and FormalinCu exhibited strong antimicrobial performance. Across 24 months, no visible mold was observed on either peanut or soybean samples in these two groups, yielding a 0% incidence and a mold severity score of 0 throughout. TAPP-Resin was largely stable, showing only sporadic, mild edge mold in a few soybean samples with negligible impact on observation. Other methods controlled contamination poorly. In the Resin group, mold became detectable after 3 months and severity increased with storage duration. In peanut, mold severity reached 2.42 ± 0.52 at 12 months and generally increased to 3.00 by 24 months. Mold progression was more rapid in soybean, reaching 2.92 ± 0.29 at 12 months and remaining at 3.00 during months 12–24, substantially compromising sample integrity. Although AirDry showed no obvious mold at early stages, the incidence reached 100% by 24 months, indicating that dehydration alone was insufficient to suppress microbial growth during long-term storage.

Cross-source applicability and operational feasibility of TAPP

Building on the superior long-term preservation performance of TAPP, we further evaluated its operational feasibility for cross-site applications, focusing on cross-crop robustness, compatibility with different preservation formats, and the standardization/reusability of the evaluation framework. First, longitudinal monitoring of peanut and soybean nodules representing multiple site–cultivar combinations showed that TAPP maintained generally consistent preservation outcomes across crops. After 24 months of storage, the mean composite morphology scores differed by only 0.02 between peanut and soybean, while terminal ΔE differed by 0.21. No visible mold growth was observed in either species. These results indicated that TAPP maintained stable preservation performance across the crop species and site–cultivar combinations included in this study and supported standardized phenotyping of legume root nodules. Second, TAPP-treated nodules could be archived either as liquid samples that preserved three-dimensional root-nodule architecture, or as solid epoxy-embedded samples that provided enhanced mechanical stability for repeated handling, transport, and display. Importantly, completing the full two-stage TAPP workflow prior to resin embedding markedly improved phenotypic fidelity relative to direct resin encapsulation. After 24 months, for peanut, the composite morphology score increased from 2.33 ± 0.12 (Resin) to 3.35 ± 0.08 (TAPP-Resin), while ΔE decreased from 30.21 ± 2.86 to 13.55 ± 1.96. For soybean, the composite morphology score increased from 2.33 ± 0.00 to 3.48 ± 0.10, and ΔE decreased from 24.27 ± 3.00 to 16.41 ± 2.07. Together, these results demonstrated that TAPP pretreatment mitigated resin-associated shrinkage and color drift and enabled flexible deployment in both liquid and solid specimen formats. In addition, we quantified the reproducibility of the morphology grading system using the ICC under a two-way random-effects model with absolute agreement, reported as the average-measure reliability across three raters (ICC(2,3)). Inter-rater reliability was consistently high for both crops across the full set of scored samples (Table 1). For composite morphology scores, ICC(2,3) was 0.9923 (95% CI: 0.9891–0.9945) for peanut and 0.9908 (95% CI: 0.9868–0.9935) for soybean, indicating excellent agreement. High reliability was also observed for each individual morphological dimension (Table 1), supporting reproducible morphology grading across time-point scoring units and providing a basis for data standardization, algorithm calibration, and retrospective validation.

Across time points, composite morphology scores of SI-T and SI-NT differed by no more than 0.25 points. At 24 months, SI-T and SI-NT differed by 0.13 points in peanut and by 0.09 points in soybean. Discussion

Mechanistic basis for improved morphological retention under TAPP

The two-stage method was designed for nodules with high water content, thin cell walls, and well-developed intercellular spaces that are prone to rapid dehydration and collapse after excision, integrating cross-linking fixation, polymer-based filling, and osmotic regulation. Stage Ⅰ constitutes a rapid fixation and early stabilization phase. Deionized water served as the dispersion medium, providing a uniform and stable environment for the delivery of the preservation components. A high formaldehyde concentration promotes cross-linking with tissue proteins and polysaccharides, rapidly forming a stable three-dimensional network that suppresses post-excision autolysis and tissue softening, thereby increasing mechanical strength and morphological retention [33]. Hydrophilic polymers such as PEG-200 increase viscosity and osmotic pressure, reduce the activity of free water, and provide volumetric support within intercellular spaces, thereby slowing dehydration and structural collapse [34]. Polysorbate 80, a non-ionic surfactant, lowers surface tension, improving uniform wetting and coverage on nodule and fine-root surfaces and reducing heterogeneous shrinkage caused by locally insufficient penetration [35]. The sulfite-based system may also contribute to early morphological stabilization by reducing oxidative damage, delaying tissue degradation, and partially suppressing microbial activity during the initial preservation phase. In addition, its reaction with formaldehyde may help maintain a relatively stable chemical environment during early fixation. Copper acetate and boric acid jointly establish a mildly acidic, antimicrobial environment. Cu2+ coordination and borate-mediated cross-linking may further inhibit tissue degradation and structural loosening, helping maintain morphological and structural stability during early storage [36]. Stage Ⅱ constitutes a long-term stabilization and osmotic-balancing phase. At this stage, the main preservation focus shifts from rapid structural fixation to sustained regulation of water migration and osmotic balance during long-term storage. After Stage I treatment, the spatial structure of the nodules is largely fixed. In the reducing microenvironment provided by sulfite, low-molecular-weight PEG-200 together with MgSO4·7H2O regulates water migration and redistribution during storage, reducing secondary shrinkage or structural relaxation of nodules [32, 37]. Overall, this staged strategy couples rapid structural fixation with long-term stabilization, preserving nodule volume, surface morphology, and overall root architecture during extended storage.

Mechanisms of sustained contamination control under TAPP

Microbial contamination is a major constraint on long-term sample integrity and downstream usability of root nodule samples. Mold growth can obscure surface morphological features and impede imaging and visual inspection, while also accelerating tissue degradation, thereby reducing sample utility for morphological analysis, digital phenotyping, and long-term archiving. The TAPP method may reduce visible microbial contamination during long-term storage through three layers of protection: pre-treatment microbial load reduction, rapid inactivation coupled with nutrient sequestration, and sustained suppression under a controlled microenvironment [41]. During pre-treatment, high-concentration ethanol and potassium sorbate effectively reduce surface microbial load on roots and nodules, delaying fungal and bacterial colonization and establishing favorable initial conditions for subsequent preservation. In the fixation stage, high-concentration formaldehyde rapidly cross-links and inactivates microbial proteins; meanwhile, by immobilizing soluble proteins and low-molecular-weight nutrients within the tissue, it deprives microbes of substrates required for proliferation. The non-ionic surfactant polysorbate 80 enhances wetting and penetration of antimicrobial components, thereby reducing localized protection gaps caused by uneven infiltration. More specifically, PEG-200 may provide mechanical filling and water-activity regulation within intercellular spaces, which helps reinforce tissue structure and reduce microenvironments favorable for microbial colonization [42, 43].

During storage, sustained contamination control arises from the combined effects of chemical and physical constraints. The sulfite-based chemical environment maintains reducing conditions that are unfavorable for aerobic microorganisms, particularly molds [44, 45]. This reducing microenvironment may constrain mold proliferation by limiting oxidative processes and energy acquisition associated with aerobic growth. At the same time, elevated osmotic pressure and reduced water activity limit the conditions required for microbial growth and reproduction [46]. These effects are closely linked to preservation of tissue structure. Polymer cross-linking and filling help reinforce tissue architecture, reduce microcrack formation, and lower the risk of endogenous nutrient leakage, while storage-phase physicochemical stability also mitigates oxidation and hydrolysis. Together, this coupling between structural stabilization and contamination control may help explain the long-term preservation performance of TAPP. As a common upstream handling step, the ethanol-based surface antimicrobial pretreatment primarily served to standardize initial surface bioburden across groups. The divergence observed during storage is therefore more plausibly attributable to the distinct physicochemical environments established by the downstream preservation regimens. Further clarification of how initial decontamination interacts with storage-phase regulation may help refine the mechanistic framework underlying long-term preservation performance.

Implications and future perspectives

The TAPP method provides a practical basis for high-fidelity temporal archiving and spatially standardized handling of root nodule samples, while minimizing morphological distortion, color drift, and microbial contamination during long-term storage. These features can facilitate broad applications across disciplines. In crop genetic improvement and phenomics, TAPP enables a physically archived, retrievable reference collection in which nodule morphology remains stable after treatment and suitable for quantitative comparisons across samples. This supports high-throughput screening of nitrogen-fixation-related genotypes [47]. In crop physiology and stress biology, TAPP provides standardized preservation of nodules exposed to contrasting environments and stressors. It reduces confounding from sampling-time differences and supplies stable samples for investigating stress-resilience mechanisms [48]. For agroecosystem studies and sustainability assessments, the framework enables standardized collection and long-term storage of nodule samples across regions and years. It provides both methodological consistency and a physical sample base for evaluating how management practices influence biological nitrogen fixation across broad spatial scales and extended time horizons [49]. In addition, TAPP is highly compatible with modern imaging and analytical pipelines. Coupled with micro-computed tomography and other high-resolution three-dimensional imaging modalities, TAPP enables multi-scale digital reconstruction and quantitative analysis of nodule structure. This supports a shift in nodule phenotyping from qualitative descriptions toward computable and reusable digital phenotypes [50]. Critically, the standardized preservation workflow improves comparability among samples collected at different time points and under different experimental conditions. It provides key technical support for building large-scale, high-quality, and traceable nodule phenotyping databases.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be considered. First, the 0-month assessment in this study was performed after completion of each preservation treatment. Therefore, the baseline used for longitudinal comparison represented a post-treatment state and did not fully capture immediate changes that may have occurred during fixation or early processing relative to freshly harvested untreated nodules. Accordingly, the morphology and color results in this study should be interpreted primarily as indicators of long-term preservation stability after treatment completion. Future studies should include freshly harvested untreated nodules as an initial reference to quantify early treatment-induced changes in nodule morphology, color, and tissue structure. Another limitation concerns color measurement. Because color values were extracted from images of samples stored in liquid and transparent containers, the resulting L*, a*, and b* values should be interpreted as relative measurements under standardized imaging conditions rather than absolute tissue colorimetric values. Although this approach was suitable for comparing color drift within the same imaging workflow, future studies should incorporate calibrated color standards, container-specific correction procedures, or spectrophotometric validation to improve the absolute precision of color measurements. Third, the applicability of the TAPP method to other legume species and distinct nodule types remains to be further validated. In addition, the current evaluation framework primarily focuses on macroscopic morphological integrity and color stability, while preservation performance at the cellular and subcellular levels warrants systematic assessment using histological sectioning and advanced microscopic imaging. Future work may therefore extend applicability testing across a broader range of legume crops and nodule types, establish corresponding parameter-optimization criteria, and integrate multi-scale imaging approaches to quantitatively evaluate tissue micro-architecture. Furthermore, the inclusion of fully quantitative measurements of nodule size and shape using calibrated digital imaging and analysis platforms will be explored to complement the ordinal morphological grading system and further reduce evaluator subjectivity. Standardized whole-root imaging could also support future analysis of attached nodule number and spatial distribution, further enriching the assessment of preservation performance at the whole-root level. Further optimization of fixative and storage solutions could also improve environmental compatibility and cost-effectiveness. Ultimately, deeper integration of this preservation framework with digital phenotyping and multi-omics analyses is expected to advance the understanding of genotype–phenotype–environment interactions and support genetic improvement and ecological management of nitrogen-fixation traits in legumes.

Conclusion

This study presents TAPP, a two-stage aqueous-phase preservation method for legume root nodules, enabling long-term retention of key phenotypic traits after excavation. By rapidly stabilizing nodule structure and providing sustained protection during storage, TAPP preserves key visual phenotypes (morphology and color) while limiting microbial contamination, addressing a major bottleneck in archiving oxidation-prone and dehydration-sensitive nodule samples. In parallel, we establish a quantitative, traceable evaluation framework to standardize phenotypic assessment across preservation strategies, time points, and sample sources. We further propose a deployable, stage-wise workflow supporting on-site pre-fixation, sealed transport, and centralized long-term storage. Collectively, these advances provide a practical and reproducible foundation for long-term phenotypic archiving of legume root nodules and cross-regional phenomics, enabling retrospective analyses and data reuse in studies of symbiotic nitrogen fixation.
