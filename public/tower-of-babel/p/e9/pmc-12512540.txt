Prevalence of chronic non-communicable diseases and multimorbidity among adults living in slums in Rio de Janeiro, Brazil: a population-based survey
Authors: Amanda Echeverría-Guevara, Paula M Luz, Débora C Pires, Emilia M Jalil, Hugo Perazzo, Thiago S Torres, Sandra W Cardoso, Guilherme T Goedert, Daniel Csillag, Eduardo M Peixoto, Breno Augusto Bormann de Souza Filho, Carlos A M Costa, Rodrigo T Amancio, Cleber V B D Santos, Nadia C P Rodrigues, Beatriz Grinsztejn, Valdilea G Veloso, Claudio J Struchiner, Lara E Coelho
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMultimorbidity accounts for 77% of overall mortality in low- and middle-income countries, with scarce prevalence estimates among populations living in slums. This study aims to estimate the prevalence and factors associated with chronic non-communicable diseases (CNCD) and multimorbidity among adults living in a large slum complex in the city of Rio de Janeiro, Brazil.MethodsAdults aged 18 or older who resided in the Manguinhos slum complex and participated in a cross-sectional study conducted between September 15, 2020, and February 10, 2021 were included in the analyses. We estimated the prevalence of self-reported CNCD by gender and age strata. Multimorbidity was defined as the presence of at least two CNCD. Logistic regression models were used to assess the association of demographic and behavioral variables with multimorbidity.ResultsOf 3,119 participants, 37% were cisgender men and 63% cisgender women, most participants were aged between 45–64 years (36.4%). High blood pressure was the most common CNCD reported by men (29.7%) and women (36.3%), followed by obesity (men: 19%, women: 25.6%) and diabetes mellitus (men: 13.1%, women: 12.4%). We identified multimorbidity in 21.5% of men and 31% of women, older participants and those self-identified as Pardo or Black had higher odds of multimorbidity regardless of gender, women with private health insurance and reporting tobacco smoking had higher odds of multimorbidity.ConclusionOur findings showed a high burden of CNCD and multimorbidity among people living in slums in a large metropolitan area in Latin America, highlighting an ongoing “neglected epidemic” that affect a substantial part of the urban population exposed to precarious living conditions and socioeconomic vulnerability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12939-025-02599-9.Keywords: Chronic non-communicable diseases, Multimorbidity, Poverty, High blood pressure, Obesity, Diabetes mellitus

Background

Multimorbidity has been defined as the presence of two or more chronic non-communicable diseases (CNCD) [1, 2]. This definition takes into consideration all health conditions experienced by an individual throughout their life with equivalent weights, focusing on the individual as a whole [3]. CNCD are characterized by persisting for more than three months, requiring medical care and affecting the individual's quality of life [4]. In the last report by the World Health Organization (WHO) the CNCD are responsible for 75% of overall mortality globally (43 million deaths annually), of which 73% are in low- and middle-income countries [5].

Brazil, with 210 million inhabitants, is a middle-income country with the 10th largest gross domestic product in the world [6]. Yet, it remains the eight most unequal country globally, with women and Black people disproportionately affected [7]. National estimates have documented an increase in the prevalence of CNCD over the past 30 years, from 59% in 1990 to 69% in 2019 [8]. This trend persisted toward the end of this period with data from the Brazilian National Health Survey (NHS) showing that between 2013 and 2019 there was an increase in the prevalence of high blood pressure (HBP, from 21.4% to 23.9%), diabetes mellitus (DM, 6.2% to 7.7%), obesity (20.8 to 25.9%) and depression (7.6 to 10.2%) [9]. More recent data from the 2023 Vigitel Survey, Brazil's annual telephone-based health monitoring system, revealed continued increases in prevalence: HBP was found in 27.9%, diabetes in 20.2%, obesity in 24.3% and depression in 12.3% of adults in urban areas [10]. However, multimorbidity disproportionately burdens certain subpopulations. While the estimated prevalence of multimorbidity in the general Brazilian population is 24.2% [11], it affects 41.4% of the population living in rural areas, and 64.6% in elders (60 years or older) [12, 13].

In Brazil, unplanned urbanization and rapid rural-to-urban migration between the 1950 s and the 1980 s, led to overcrowded cities with insufficient infrastructure, resulting in the formation of slums, where currently 8% of the urban population resides [14]. These densely populated areas are marked by inadequate access to essential services, including potable water, sanitation, and poor structural quality of housing [4]. The intersection of limited healthcare access, low educational attainment, socioeconomic vulnerabilities, inadequate nutrition, social segregation, psychological stress, exposure to violence, and high rates of alcohol and tobacco use perpetuate a cycle of disadvantage that potentially drive the occurrence of CNCD and multimorbidity [15].

Data on CNCD in the population living in slums are scarce, originating from indirect statistics from hospital and general mortality data [15], with little or no field knowledge of the impact of multimorbidity on these populations. Thus, this study aimed to investigate the prevalence of CNCD and of multimorbidity, along with associated factors, among cisgender men and women living in a large slum complex in Rio de Janeiro city.

Methods

Study population

The Manguinhos slum complex is composed of 16 slums located in the North Zone of Rio de Janeiro city. With nearly 36,000 inhabitants residing in an area of over 1,618,400 square meters, Manguinhos slum complex is characterized by concentrated poverty, overcrowding, poor housing conditions and has the fifth worst human development index for Rio de Janeiro city [16].

The COmVIDa-1 study was a population-based survey conducted in the Manguinhos slum complex from September 15, 2020 to February 10, 2021, that included a probabilistic sample of participants aged ≥ 1 year old. The study design and primary results have been published elsewhere [17]. In this secondary analysis, we included adults aged 18 or older who participated in the COmVIDa-1 study.

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the local ethics committees (INI/Fiocruz, ENSP/Fiocruz, and IOC/Fiocruz, CAAE 3555558920.6.0000.5262). All participants provided a written informed consent, subsequently, structured questionnaires were administrated using REDCap software on cell phones.

Chronic non-communicable diseases (CNCD) and multimorbidity

The presence of self-report CNCD was accessed during the interview via the following question: “Has a doctor ever said that you have:” with the following conditions assessed separately: HBP, DM, asthma/bronchitis, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease or emphysema, cancer of any type, chronic kidney disease, heart disease, stroke, arthritis and depression. Each participant informed their weight (in kilograms) and height (in meters) to calculate the body mass index (BMI), and obesity was defined as BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 [18]. Multimorbidity (yes or no) was defined as the coexistence of two or more CNCD in an individual [2].

Explanatory variables

Exploratory variables included: gender dichotomized (men, women); age in years (age group categories included 18–24, 25–44, 45–64, and > 65 years, as used in the Brazilian National Health Surveys) [19]; self-reported race/ethnicity (White, Black, Pardo [Black-mixed], Indigenous, and Asian, as defined by the Brazilian Geography and Statistics Institute) [20]; formal education level was categorized in complete years of education as 0–4, 5–9, 10–12, and > 13.

Monthly family income was measured by the number of minimum wages (in 2020 the monthly minimum wage was 1,045 Brazilian reais or 199 USD), categorized: no income; ≤ 1, > 1 and ≤ 2, > 2 and ≤ 3, > 3 minimum wages. Access to federal cash transfer benefit programs including Bolsa Família benefit and Emergency aid were ascertained and dichotomized (yes, no). The Bolsa Família benefit, created in 2003, is a nationwide conditional cash transfer program that aims to reduce income poverty and expand access to basic services such as health and education [21]. Emergency aid was a Brazilian federal government program that provided financial assistance to informal workers, individual microentrepreneurs, self-employed workers and the unemployed during the COVID-19 pandemic (April 2020 through December 2021) [22].

Current employment status was ascertained (e.g. formal employment, informal work or freelance work) and dichotomized (yes, no). Participants were asked about having private health insurance (yes or no). Information on household composition was also collected (e.g., household size [number of people dwelling in the same household] and number of bedrooms). Household density was calculated as persons per bedroom (PPB) dividing the household size by the number of rooms used as bedrooms and categorized as 1.1–1.5; 1.6–3.0; > 3.0 [23].

Hazardous drinking (yes or no) was measured using the gender-specific AUDIT-C score cut-offs and was considered positive if the sum score was ≥ 3 points for women and ≥ 4 points for men [24]. Participants were questioned about tobacco smoking (former, current, never). Tobacco smoking was categorized in yes (responses “former” or “current”) or no (response “never”).

Statistical analysis

He characteristics of the study population were described using absolute and relative frequencies for men and women, and comparisons were made using the chi-squared test. The prevalence and 95% confidence interval (CI) of each CNCD and multimorbidity were estimated by gender and age strata using Poisson regression models.

Logistic regression models with a binomial distribution were used to assess the association between demographic, socioeconomic and behavioral variables and multimorbidity (participants with one or no CNCD were coded as “0” and participants with two or more CNCD were coded as “1”)). Separate models were fitted for men and women. Unadjusted regression models were used to quantify the association of exploratory variables and the outcome. An initial adjusted regression model included all exploratory variables, while the final adjusted model retained only variables with p-values < 0.05. Multiple imputation was used to fill in the missing data using Multivariate Imputation by Chained Equations from MICE library in R [25]. Logistic regression models using complete data were also fitted and are available in the supplementary material. We assessed multicollinearity in the final adjusted regression models using Variance Inflation Factor (VIF), with values below 5 indicating acceptable levels. All analyses were performed in R Version 4.3.3.

Results

Between September 15, 2020 and February 10, 2021, 4,033 participants were included in the original COmVIDa-1 study of which 762 participants aged < 18 years and were excluded from the present analysis. Additionally, 152 participants were excluded because they did not answer the questionnaire section about comorbidities. The final study population of the present analysis included 3,119 participants, of whom 1,142 (37%) were men and 1,977 (63%) women (Fig. 1). The age distribution of the population by gender was similar (Table 1), with most participants aged between 45–64 years (36.4%). Most participants self-identified as Pardo (men: 44.9%, women: 40.8%), while 30.8% self-identified as White and 24.2% as Black. The number of years of formal education was similar between genders, with almost 85% of participants reporting 12 years or less (i.e. secondary education or less). Almost 90% of participants reported family monthly income < 3 minimum wages, the distribution of income differed by gender with men reporting relatively higher income than women. More women reported receiving Bolsa Familia benefit (men: 14.0%, women: 22.2%), whereas Emergency aid was more evenly distributed between genders (men: 45%, women: 50.3%). Household density (PPB) did not differ much by gender with most (44%) participants residing in households with 1.6–3.0 PPB. Private health insurance was reported by 13% of the participants and was similar between genders. Hazardous drinking use was more frequent among men (32.5%) than women (22.3%), with a similar pattern for tobacco smoking (men: 36.1%, women: 25.6%).

HBP was the most common CNCD reported by men (29.7%) and women (36.3%), followed by obesity (men: 19%, women: 25.6%) and DM (men: 13.1%, women: 12.4%). Heart diseases were the fourth most frequent CNCD in men (3.5%) whereas depression was the fourth most frequent in women (10.3%). Multimorbidity was more prevalent among women (31.0%) than men (21.5%) (Table 1).

Figure 2 shows the prevalences of the five most prevalent CNCD by age strata in men and women. In both genders, the prevalence of HBP and DM increased sharply with age, reaching almost two-thirds of men and women aged 65 years or older. In contrast, the prevalence of asthma showed a slight decrease by age groups in both genders. The prevalence of obesity was highest among men and women aged 25–44 years. Among men, logistic regression model results showed that age was positively associated with multimorbidity (Table 2 and Table S4). Compared to men aged 18–24 years, the final adjusted odds ratio [aOR] of multimorbidity were 2.48 (95%CI 1.22–5.60) for those aged 25–44 years, 7.10 (95%CI 3.67–15.50) for those aged 45–64 years, and 10.28 (95%CI 5.10–23.08) among men 65 years or older. Race/ethnicity was also associated with multimorbidity, with Pardo men having higher odds of multimorbidity compared to White men (aOR 1.59, 95%CI 1.10–2.31). Higher family income was also associated with higher odds of multimorbidity. Finally, being currently employed (aOR 0.50, 95%CI 0.36–0.70) and residing in households with PPB 1.6–3.0 (aOR 0.67, 95%CI 0.47–0.96 [reference PPB < 1.0]) were associated with lower odds of multimorbidity among men.

Among women age was also positively associated with multimorbidity (Table 3 and Table S4). Compared to women aged 18–24 years, the final aORs of multimorbidity were 2.23 (95%CI 1.41–3.67) for those aged 25–44 years, 6.16 (95%CI 3.97–9.99) for those aged 45–64 years, and 10.96 (95%CI 6.83–18.29) among women 65 years or older. Black women were found to have higher odds of multimorbidity (aOR 1.36, 95%CI 1.03–1.79) compared to White women. Having private health insurance (aOR 1.58, 95%CI 1.18–2.11) and tobacco smoking (aOR 1.62, 95%CI 1.30–2.03) were also associated with higher odds of multimorbidity. Similar to men, women residing in households with PPB of 1.6–3.0 had lower odds of multimorbidity (aOR 0.75, 95%CI 0.58–0.96 [reference PPB < 1.0]).

Discussion

The present study investigated multimorbidity in a highly vulnerable socioeconomic adult population in Rio de Janeiro, Brazil, during the COVID-19 pandemic. Our results indicate that 27.5% of the population had multimorbidity. Older age groups, Pardo race, and higher monthly family income were associated with higher odds of multimorbidity in men. On the other hand, among women, older age, Black race, having private health insurance, and tobacco smoking were associated with higher odds of multimorbidity. Interestingly, living in households with 1.6–3.0 PPB showed lower odds of multimorbidity, as did current employment status in men. Our findings indicate that the prevalence of multimorbidity among adults living in slums was higher than that observed in the Brazilian general population [19]. This elevated prevalence may be attributable to the chronic stress associated with poverty, precarious living conditions and exposure to high levels of violence, factors known to increase susceptibility to CNCD [26]. The underlying mechanism involves chronic systemic inflammation driven by oxidative stress and a neuroimmune pathway that can lead to systemic endothelial damage and increased risk to major cardiovascular adverse events [27]. Additionally, chronic systemic inflammation is associated with a higher risk of metabolic diseases, and trigger bio behavioral responses such as fatigue, sleep disturbance, and social withdrawal [28].

In our study, the top three most prevalent CNCD in both genders (HBP, obesity and DM) were also the ones with the highest prevalence globally [29] and in the adult Brazilian population [19]. Notably, we found higher prevalences of HBP (33.9%) and DM (12.7%) than the ones reported for the adult Brazilian population (23.9% and 7.7%, respectively). The observed obesity prevalence (23.2%) was similar to the Brazilian estimate (25.9%) [19]. Importantly, while most of the CNCD burden increased with age (as expected), obesity prevalence peaked in individuals aged between 25 and 44 years in both genders suggesting an ongoing obesity transition in the most vulnerable population in Brazil. Over the past 50 years, Brazil has experienced a significant obesity transition, shifting from undernutrition to a rapid rise in overweight and obesity rates, driven by urbanization, changes in dietary patterns, increased access to processed foods, and reduced physical activity levels [30]. Moreover, socioeconomic vulnerable populations are disproportionately affected by obesity due to limited access to nutritious food, higher exposure to low-cost, calorie-dense options and restricted access and opportunities for physical activities in their communities [30].

Depression was the fourth most prevalent CNCD in our population (7.4%), with higher estimates in women (10.3%) compared to men (2.4%). The observed prevalences in both genders were lower than the 2019 Brazilian national estimates (14.7% among women and 5.1% among men) [9]. It seems unlikely that the prevalence of depression would be lower in our study population compared to the general population. That said, this lower prevalence may be due to barriers in access to screening and diagnosis of mental health diseases [31]. As for the difference in prevalence by gender, it may result from the lower social support network available for women [32] and/or from the low health care seeking behavior associated with stoicism, particularly for mental health services, often observed among men [31].

Individual level factors were associated with higher odds of multimorbidity in our study population. First, our study reproduces findings observed in longitudinal studies, which show that as age increases, the prevalence of multimorbidity increases concomitantly in both genders, which can be explained by general senescence in multiple organs caused by aging [33]. Second, we found that Pardo adults were more likely to have multimorbidity, an association previously reported in a large multicentric longitudinal study in Brazil (ELSA-BRASIL) [34]. This study found that Pardo and Black individuals had a higher incidence rate of multimorbidity, 16% and 29% respectively, when compared to White individuals. The authors speculated that the racial disparities were attributable to the fact that both Black and Pardo individuals included in the study lived in segregated and low-income neighborhoods. Moreover, racial discrimination and microaggressions have been shown to increase stress levels, a biological pathway that could further explain the disproportionate burden of multimorbidity among Black individuals [35].

We found that the highest family income stratum was associated with higher odds of multimorbidity in men. Contrastingly, current employment reduced the odds of multimorbidity in men. These findings can be somewhat contradictory and might reflect the complex economic and employment conditions of the study population. People living in socioeconomic vulnerability frequently depend on unstable jobs or freelancing to support their living such that they are excluded from the usual benefits that favor formal sector employees [15, 36]. Although our “current employment” variable included those reporting formal employment, informal work or freelancing, it is not unreasonable to assume that men with unsteady and precarious work would describe themselves as unemployed while earning income that would classify them in the highest income stratum. An additional source of complexity is that we captured family income and not individual income. Also intriguing was the positive association between household density and the odds of multimorbidity. Overcrowding (PPB > 3.0) [23] has historically been associated with poor living conditions, economic deprivation, increased exposure to mold and allergens, and a consequently increased risk of developing infectious diseases and mental health problems [23, 37]. In our study, most of the participants (45.4%) reported living in households with 1.6–3.0 PPB, which was associated with lower odds of multimorbidity among both genders. We hypothesize that unmeasured confounding factors such as access to health care and missed opportunities for CNCD screening and diagnosis may explain some of these unexpected associations [15].

We found a positive association between having private health insurance and the odds of multimorbidity in women, which might suggest facilitated access to health care, screening and diagnosis of CNCD [38]. In Brazil, access to private health insurance is mainly through a company benefit offered to the employed individual and their family [39]. Smoking was associated with higher odds of multimorbidity in women. Smoking, a known risk factor for several CNCD, is more prevalent in low- and middle-income countries, where almost 80% of the world's smokers live [40]. Our findings reveal a much higher prevalence of smoking (men: 31.6%; women: 25.6%) compared to the Brazilian urban population in the 2023 Vigitel national survey (men: 11.7%; women: 7.2%), corroborating findings that suggest that vulnerable populations are at greater risk of smoking [9].

Our study has strengths and limitations worth mentioning. Limitations include the cross-sectional design, which precludes establishing definitive causal relationships, and a potential classification/diagnosis bias related to barriers to healthcare access, screening and diagnosis that may have led to underestimation of CNCD prevalence. Moreover, those barriers, as well as the unawareness of CNCD likely vary by age, gender, race/ethnicity, education level, income, and employment. Furthermore, CNCD prevalence underestimation could also result from self-reported responses during the study interview, as shown by another study from Brazil where almost 20% of people with HBP were unaware of their diagnosis [41]. Additionally, our analyses were likely affected by unmeasured potential confounding variables, such as access to health care, including screening and diagnosis assessments and stigma associated with health care. Although our study population is not necessarily representative of other Brazilian communities living in slums, the findings of our study could be reasonably similar to other populations living in urban precarious conditions and slums in Brazil and other countries in Latin America. Moreover, our study is one of few field studies that investigated the burden of CNDC in a population-based sample of a socioeconomic vulnerable population.

Conclusion

Our study showed a high burden of CNCD and multimorbidity among people living in slums in a large metropolitan area in Latin America, which sheds light on a relevant “neglected epidemic” that affect a substantial part of the urban population exposed to precarious living conditions and socioeconomic vulnerability. Targeted health policies focusing on health promotion, primary care, prevention, screening and treatment of CNCD are urgently needed to reduce morbimortality and improve quality of life and productivity among people living in slums.
