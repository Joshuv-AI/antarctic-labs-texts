Lac-Phe: a central metabolic regulator and biomarker
Authors: Taikang Yao, Guolin Alexander Wen, Zhenchao Wu, Ning Shen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractN-lactoyl-phenylalanine (Lac-Phe), an exercise-induced metabolite that suppresses appetite, has quickly emerged as a key molecule in metabolic signaling networks. Lac-Phe, following its CNDP2-mediated synthesis, mediates key appetite- and weight-modulating effects of metformin, which acts primarily by mitochondrial inhibition in gut epithelial cells. Both Lac-Phe and related N-lactoyl-amino acids serve as potent biomarkers of mitochondrial dysfunction. Elevated levels of these metabolites are found in genetic mitochondrial diseases, offering potentially superior prognostic value compared to lactate in conditions alike. Despite uncertainties regarding its specific receptor(s) and signaling mechanisms, the expanding roles of Lac-Phe underscore its critical position at the intersection of exercise physiology, pharmacology, energy metabolism, and disease pathology, suggesting significant potential for future diagnostics and therapeutics in mitochondrial and metabolic disorders.

The discovery of N-lactoyl-phenylalanine (Lac-Phe) as a novel metabolite induced by intense exercise has attracted significant attention [1]. Early studies demonstrated that Lac-Phe, synthesized from lactate and phenylalanine by the enzyme CNDP2, suppressed feeding and reduced obesity in mice. In humans, acute exercise-induced levels of Lac-Phe predicted subsequent fat loss [2]. Initially, this appeared to be a straightforward case of an exercise-induced signaling molecule. However, further research has revealed that the biological context and functional significance of Lac-Phe are much more complex, positioning it at the crossroads of physiology, pharmacology, and disease management (Figure 1).

N-lactoyl-phenylalanine (Lac-Phe). Lac-Phe is a signaling metabolite induced by exercise, metformin, and feeding. It mediates metformin’s weight-loss effects and is associated with mitochondrial diseases, sepsis, diabetes, obesity, and cancer metabolism.

Studies have shown that metformin significantly increases circulating Lac-Phe in both mice and humans, including non-diabetic individuals [3], 4]. The underlying mechanism differs from the exercise-induced pathway; instead of relying on circulating lactate from muscle, metformin inhibits mitochondrial complex I, predominantly in gut epithelial cells, leading to localized energy stress. This increases intracellular lactate levels, which fuels CNDP2-mediated Lac-Phe synthesis and secretion. Critically, mice lacking CNDP2 were resistant to metformin’s effects on weight reduction and appetite suppression, suggesting that Lac-Phe is a key mediator of these effects, independent of metformin’s primary glucose-lowering action. It remains unclear whether gut-derived Lac-Phe signals differ by targeting distinct circuits from exercise-induced Lac-Phe, which may originate from a broader range of cellular sources.

The complexity deepened with the discovery that CNDP2 is not solely dependent on lactate. During ketosis, CNDP2 can utilize β-hydroxybutyrate (BHB) to generate BHB-amino acids, with BHB-Phe also exhibiting appetite-suppressing effects [5]. This positions CNDP2 as a versatile metabolic integrator, linking the production of anorexigenic signals to the states of high fuel availability, either high glycolytic flux (lactate) or high fat oxidation/ketogenesis (BHB) in real clinical scenarios.

Further expanding the scope, N-lactoyl-amino acids (Lac-AAs), including Lac-Phe, have been identified as robust plasma biomarkers in patients with mitochondrial encephalomyopathy, lactic acidosis, and stroke-like episodes (MELAS), a genetic mitochondrial disease characterized by NADH-reductive stress and elevated lactate levels [6]. The accumulation of Lac-AAs in mitochondrial diseases is mechanistically linked to elevated NADH/NAD+ ratios, a hallmark of NADH-reductive stress. Unlike classical markers (e.g., lactate), Lac-AAs remain stable during physical exertion, making them promising chronic indicators of mitochondrial redox imbalance.

The relevance of Lac-AAs extends into acute critical illness. Rogers et al. examined circulating markers in septic shock in a controlled study, where comparators included cardiogenic shock without infection, bacteremia without sepsis, and ambulatory controls [7]. Lac-AAs (represented by Lac-Phe) and N-formyl-methionine (f-Met, a marker of disturbed mitochondrial protein synthesis) were significantly elevated in septic shock patients compared to all other groups. In contrast, tryptophan level was uniquely decreased with an increase in kynurenate: a metabolite of tryptophan consistent with known inflammatory pathway activation in sepsis. Importantly, early measurements of Lac-AAs and f-Met after ICU admission showed greater differentiation between survivors and non-survivors of septic shock compared to lactate, the current clinical standard. These findings suggest that Lac-AAs and f-Met could serve as valuable biomarkers for mitochondrial dysfunction and outcome prediction in septic shock [7].

Future directions are promising but fundamental questions remain unanswered. The roles of other lactoyl-amino acids warrant further investigation. Receptors for Lac-Phe, BHB-Phe, and other molecules are ongoing topics, though a probability of G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) involvement is expected given the nature of being sensors to other metabolites. It is unclear whether their primary action is based on an endocrine mechanism to act on distant brain centers, or on a paracrine mechanism to trigger local appetite-regulating neurons. A paracrine mechanism could explain the effectiveness of signaling despite relatively low circulating concentrations. As a comparison, high levels in circulation is typically required to activate metabolite-sensing GPCRs.

In clinical practice, beyond its potential to predict mortality in the ICU and serve as a biomarker for rare mitochondrial diseases, could Lac-Phe become a useful tool for broader disease management? Could understanding Lac-Phe as a key mediator of metformin’s weight-modulating effects reveal new pharmacological mechanisms for other drugs? Overcoming challenges such as the poor oral bioavailability of peptide-like molecules will be essential. Strategies could involve developing stable analogs or modulating CNDP2 activity, though the enzyme’s ability to utilize multiple substrates requires careful consideration.

While direct evidence linking Lac-Phe to cancer growth or treatment monitoring is currently limited, its metabolic functions provide a basis for potential investigation. Cancer cells often exhibit altered metabolism, including increased glycolysis and lactate production, known as the Warburg effect [8]. Lactate itself has been shown to influence the tumor microenvironment and cancer progression. Given Lac-Phe’s derivation from lactate, it is plausible to hypothesize that Lac-Phe may play a role in cancer metabolism or signaling pathways.

Lac-Phe serves as a crucial link among physical activity, drug action, and fundamental energy metabolism. Its progression from an exercise-induced metabolite to a mediator of metformin action and a potential integrator of glycolytic and ketogenic states, underscores the dynamic nature of metabolic signaling. The potential of lactoyl-amino acids as biomarkers and therapeutic targets for mitochondrial and metabolic diseases is substantial, but fully realizing their therapeutic value will require interdisciplinary collaboration, spanning biochemistry, physiology, pharmacology, and clinical research.
