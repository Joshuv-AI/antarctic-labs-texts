Intercellular mosaic methylation in fast-growing Mycobacterium tuberculosis clinical isolates
Authors: Maryam Ahmadi Jeshvaghane, Samuel J Modlin, Derek Conkle-Gutierrez, Monica E Espinoza, Faramarz Valafar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractOrphan DNA adenine methyltransferases (MTases) of Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb) exhibit diversity across clinical isolates, but the forces driving this variation are not entirely clear. Recently, we observed several isolates exhibiting anomalous hypomethylation by Type I MTase Mycobacterial Adenine Methyltransferase C (MamC) despite a wild-type mamC genotype (‘MamC-anomalous’ isolates). Investigating this hypomethylation through multiple analyses revealed three key findings. First, heterogeneity analysis revealed intercellular mosaic methylation (IMM) in MamC-anomalous isolates. While they often exhibit phase-variable heterogeneity, this is the first report of IMM by a prokaryotic Type I MTase. Second, MamC-anomalous isolates exhibited a large, stable difference in chromosome copy number along the replication axis (a proxy for bacterial growth rate), suggesting a distinct growth phase accompanied by MamC hypomethylation. Third, MamC methylation efficiency decreased progressively with distance from the origin of replication on both strands, with a marked exaggeration in MamC-anomalous isolates. In contrast, other Mtb MTases (MamA and MamB) exhibited lower methylation levels away from the origin only on the lagging strand, and without exaggeration in MamC-anomalous isolates. We conclude that, among Mtb MTases, MamC DNA methylation is uniquely linked to growth dynamics.

Introduction

Tuberculosis is a deadly and widespread infectious disease, killing 1.6 million people globally in 2021 alone (1). Its causative bacterial agent, Mycobacterium tuberculosis, switches between highly virulent states and persistent dormant states, making relapse after treatment common (2–5). The mechanisms operating these switches in M. tuberculosis are largely unknown. In several other bacterial pathogens, DNA methylation serves as a regulatory switch controlling virulence phenotypes. Characterized examples include growth rate (6–8), invasion of host cells (9–11), cytotoxicity (12–14) and influence on clinical outcomes such as bacterial burden (7,9,12) and host mortality (7,12,15,16). DNA methylation similarly regulates survival phenotypes in other bacterial species, including antibiotic resistance (8,17), oxidative stress tolerance (8,16) and survival within immune cells (12,17,18). Heterogeneity in DNA methylation such as phase variation allows these switches to maintain simultaneous subpopulations with distinct virulence and survival phenotypes (11,17,19,20). The scope of virulence and survival phenotype regulation by DNA methylation is less well known in M. tuberculosis, with hypoxia tolerance (21) and transcriptional regulation by DNA adenine methylation for several M. tuberculosis genes (22,23) as the only implicated roles.

M. tuberculosis encodes three known DNA methyltransferases (MTases) (24): Rv2756c [MamC, also known as HsdM (22)], Rv3263 (MamA) and Rv2024c (MamB). MamC and MamA are what is known as orphan MTases (24), as they each lack a partner restriction endonuclease targeting the same motif (24) and are thus not part of a functional restriction–modification system. MamA is a Type II MTase with a methyltransferase domain which catalyzes the methyl transfer, and a specificity domain which determines the target motif (24). Rv2756c (mamC) encodes a Type I MTase subunit, which forms an MTase complex with two Rv2756c subunits and one specificity subunit, encoded by Rv2761c (MamS, also known as HsdS) (22). While Type I MTase subunits such as Rv2756c are often named HsdM, Rv2756 was renamed MamC due to its orphan status, as the term ‘hsd’ is traditionally reserved for the gene encoding the MTase component of a complete restriction–modification system (22). Orphan MTases such as MamA and MamC mediate various physiological and regulatory processes in other prokaryotic species, including transcriptional regulation, chromosomal replication, phase variation (25) and DNA mismatch repair (26). However, the precise function(s) of MamA and MamC remains to be discovered.

We previously demonstrated that missense mutations drive heterogeneous MamA methylation in Beijing sublineage isolates harboring the MamAE270A allele and several Latin American–Mediterranean sublineage isolates harboring the MamAG152S allele (22). Read-level heterogeneity analysis revealed that heterogeneous MamA methylation is not phase variable [some strands are fully methylated while others are entirely unmethylated (22)], but rather stochastic, generating a mosaic of methylation states across cells, a phenomenon known as intercellular mosaic methylation (IMM) (22).

In the same study, we identified four isolates with modestly undermethylated MamC sites globally and seemingly coordinately hypomethylated sites at a subset of motif sites across the genome (22). The four isolates exhibiting this pattern comprised two distinct Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTBC) lineages: three were East-Asian (Lineage 2) and one was M. africanum West-African 2 (Figure 1A). We refer to these four isolates as ‘MamC-anomalous’ isolates. Surprisingly—as all other isolates with wild-type MTase protein sequences exhibited nearly complete methylation at all motif sites—three of the four MamC-anomalous isolates had the wild-type mamC sequence (MamCWT). We refer to this subset with wild-type MamC as ‘MamCWT-anomalous’ isolates.

DNA adenine methylation can be directly and accurately inferred from the sequencing kinetics of Pacific Biosciences’ single-molecule real-time sequencing technology (SMRT-sequencing) (27). SMRT-sequencing kinetics are expressed as the ratio of expected to observed time between the fluorescent pulses emitted upon base incorporation by DNA polymerase for a given sequence, known as the ‘inter pulse duration’ ratio (IPD ratio). In this study, we more closely examine the nature of MamC methylation heterogeneity in the four MamC-anomalous isolates to discern what process(es) underpin it. We focus on a subset of MamC motif sites (22) with a highly variable IPD ratio across MamCWT isolates (‘hypervariable MamC motif sites’). We successively assess evidence for technical, batch effect and biological phenomena, and investigate four hypotheses: competitive antagonism with DNA-binding proteins (28,29), altered MamC motif specificity (30), phase-variable MamC activity (31) and differential growth phase (32–34). Evidence supporting the fourth hypothesis led us to conduct methylome variability analyses over the origin–terminus axis to contrast how this relationship differs between the three known DNA adenine MTases of M. tuberculosis.

Materials and methods

Data pre-processing

Sample preparation, DNA extraction, sequencing, genome assembly, annotation, genotyping, MamC motif site determination, locus assignment, promoter identification and transcription factor-binding motif scanning were performed as described in detail previously (22). In summary, sample preparation and extraction were performed at the World Health Organization Supranational Reference Laboratory in Stockholm, Sweden and at the University of California, San Diego (San Diego, CA, USA). DNA was extracted during the stationary phase of culturing and sequenced using PacBio SMRT-sequencing with P6C4 sequencing chemistry. Following read pre-processing steps, reads were assembled using hierarchical genome assembly process (HGAP) version 2 (RS_HGAP_Assembly.2 protocol). Canu (35) (v1.6) was used if HGAP was not able to successfully assemble the reads. The assembled genomes were then circularized using Circlator (36). Finally, the assemblies were polished (37) using an iterative polishing process with Quiver in SMRTAnalysis Softwarev2.3.0 from Pacific Biosciences (https://www.pacb.com/support/software-downloads). Lineages were determined using TBInsight (38) and MiruHero (https://gitlab.com/LPCDRP/miru-hero). Gene annotation was performed using Hybran software (https://gitlab.com/LPCDRP/hybran) with H37Rv version NC_000962.3 as the reference genome. MTase genotyping was done with the most common allele in clinical isolates considered as the wild-type sequence. IPD ratios were determined using SMRTAnalysis and previously characterized (21,24) DNA adenine methylation target motifs were used to extract the IPD ratio of targeted adenines in motif-matching sites. A custom Python script was used to assign locus names to motif sites based on the nearest gene boundary. A phylogenetic tree was constructed using RAxMLv8.2 with the genomes of Mycobacterium canettii CIPT 140070010 (NC_019951.1) and Mycobacterium bovis BCG Pasteur 1173P2 (AM408590.1) as the outgroups. Promoters were identified using a custom Python script that scans for surrounding sigma factor-binding site motifs previously characterized (39) and uses transferred annotations of empirically determined transcription start sites (40,41). Empirically characterized transcription factor-binding sites (TFBSs) (42) were found using motif scanner FIMOv4.12.0 (43).

Phenotypic and drug susceptibility testing

Drug susceptibility testing (DST) for isoniazid (INH), rifampicin (RIF), moxifloxacin (MOX), ofloxacin (OFX), amikacin (AMK), kanamycin (KAN) and capreomycin (CAP) was performed as previously described (44) with the mycobacterial growth indicator tube (MGIT) 960 platform using EpiCenter Software (BD Diagnostic Systems, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) and WHO-suggested critical concentrations used for MGIT DST.

Hierarchical clustering

An unsupervised clustering method was used to establish a hierarchy of clusters and group the isolates based on the IPD ratio value of MamC motif sites. For this purpose, first, a distance matrix that shows the pair-wise dissimilarity measure was calculated using the Euclidean method. Second, the hclust algorithm of the stats package in Rv4.2.1 was used with default parameter values (method = ‘complete’). The hierarchical clustering dendrograms were created using the same function. A second hierarchical clustering on a Spearman-based distance metric with average linkage was performed to assess the robustness of the MamC-anomalous dendrogram grouping. A heatmap was generated using Complex Heatmap v3.16, and isolate lineage, sequencing site and MamC behavior was overlaid.

k-means clustering

k-means clustering based on the IPD ratio value of MamC motif sites was performed as a second method of identifying clusters of isolates. R’s k-means function of stats library with number of clusters parameter (centers) 2 and 30 as random sets to be chosen (nstart) were used. Default values were used for all other parameters. The k-means function was also used to determine which of the MamC motif sites individually recapitulate the same clustering of isolates.

Principal component analysis (PCA)

The PCA function in the FactoMiner package was used to run PCA on the IPD ratio of MamC motif sites. The output was then visualized using the fviz_pca_ind function in R.

Structural variant analyses

Consensus structural variation

Mummerv.4.2 (45) was used to call variants in the isolates compared with H37Rv version NC_000962.3 as a reference genome. Gene functional annotations were drawn from an updated H37Rv reference genome functional annotation (46). The variant call format (VCF) files were further used to find indels and single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) present in the MamC-anomalous but not MamC-typical isolates. R’s tidyverse and data.table libraries were used in this analysis. In addition to Mummer, Assemblyticsv.1.2.1 and Syriv1.7.0 (47) were used to detect unique structural variants overlapping mamC, mamS, mamS.1 and proximally flanking genes in MamC-anomalous isolates. Assemblytics parameter values used were 1000 as unique sequence length required and 50 and 10 000 as minimum and maximum variant size. To detect structural variants (SVs) using Syri, first minimap2 (48) was used to align each isolate's whole genome to the H37Rv reference sequence using recommended -ax asm5 (genome to genome alignment) -eqx (output CIGAR strings with E, X and = operations) parameters. The sam alignment outputs were then used to run Syri with -k (keep intermediate files) -F S (file format) flags.

Heterogeneous structural variation

Heterogeneous structural variations were identified with Snifflesv1.0.11 (49). Raw sequencing reads were mapped back onto each isolate's consensus de novo assembly using NGMLR, and Sniffles was used to call SVs (scripts can be found at https://gitlab.com/LPCDRP/convergent-het-sv/). Sniffles was used with the following parameters -genotype (genotyping) -min_support 6 (minimum number of supporting reads to call an SV) -report_seq (include sequence of the SV in the output) -num_reads_report 500 (number of reads included in the output for each SV). The output was then cross-referenced with genomic coordinates of mamC, mamS and mamS.1.

Genomic heterogeneity analysis

Heterogeneous small variant calls were made using PBHooverv1.1.0 (50). The gene start and end points were determined using the Hybran v1.7 (51) functional annotation tool, and 1000 bp upstream of each gene were added to include potential cis-regulatory regions. Three genes were included in this analysis: mamC, mamS and mamS.1. Reads with variant frequency (VF) ≤ 2 × standard deviation (VF) + mean (VF) were filtered out to remove variants spuriously called due to noisy long reads.

Methylation heterogeneity analysis

We used the software SMALRv2.0.1 to calculate each read's native IPD value (52), the mean IPD across sites matching the MamC target motif in the read (excluding reads with fewer than three MamC motif sites). The native values of reads from the MamC-anomalous isolates were compared with the distribution of native values in four MamC-typical isolates with normal MamC methylation activity and four fully unmethylated MamC-inactive isolates. The four MamC-inactive isolates with the longest read lengths were chosen for this comparison, along with the four MamC wild-type isolates with typical methylation activity with the smallest phylogenetic distance to the MamC-anomalous isolates.

The sparseness of MamC motifs across the M. tuberculosis chromosome increased the noise from SMALR, as there were fewer MamC motif sites in each read to average. SMALR was run on the isolates with a threshold number of motif sites per read set to 3, two fewer sites per read than the default threshold. To account for the extra noise, statistical tests were applied to determine if the partially methylated mosaic reads could be explained by mixtures of noisy native values from fully unmethylated and fully methylated distributions.

A custom R script classified reads as fully methylated, fully unmethylated or partially methylated, based on probability density functions of mean native values of methylated and unmethylated reads, estimated from the mean and standard deviation (SD) of native values from the MamC-typical and MamC-inactive isolates, respectively. Reads were classified as methylated if their native value had a probability of > 0.05 within the methylated probability density function. Similarly, reads were classified as unmethylated if their native value had a probability > 0.05 within the unmethylated probability density function, and were classified as ‘partial’ if their native value had a probability > 0.05 in both probability density functions.

To determine if the partially methylated reads in the MamC-anomalous isolates were an artifact of the noisy native values from the sparse MamC sites, we tested whether the MamC-anomalous isolates had more reads classified as ‘partial’ than would be expected from a mixture of native values from MamC-typical and MamC-inactive subpopulations. For this test, 400 000 mixtures were made of reads randomly subsampled from the MamC-typical and MamC-inactive isolates. There were 100 000 mixtures sampled for each MamC-anomalous isolate, using a different random seed for every 1000 mixtures. The proportions of reads sampled from MamC-typical and MamC-inactive isolates were determined based on the proportion of methylated and unmethylated reads in the MamC-anomalous isolates. The sampled reads in each mixture were then classified using the same method as the MamC-anomalous isolates, and the number of ‘partial’ reads in each mixture were compared with the number of ‘partial’ reads in the MamC-anomalous isolates.

SMALR (52) was also run on the MamC-anomalous, MamC-typical and MamC-inactive isolates with a putative alternative MamC target motif identified in the ‘Motif refinement tool section’. A custom R script compared the native values of these runs with a t-test.

Motif refinement tool

To investigate motif specificity changes in MamC, we used a similar approach to the Methylation Motif Refiner (MeMoRe) tool (53). Here, we specifically looked at the known bipartite MamC motif GATNNNNRTAC/GTAYNNNNATC. The adenine bases in bold and underlined shown in the motif are targeted by MamC for methylation. When methylated, they have a relatively higher IPD ratio than when unmethylated. To identify motif specificity changes in these motifs, we examined all possible alternatives of the motif made by changing it one unambiguous base at a time in each relative position (keeping the targeted adenine base the same in all alternative motifs). Then, for each alternative motif, we measured and plotted the IPD ratios at the targeted adenines in each instance in the genome matching the alternative motif. This approach helped us to investigate MamC motif specificity differences between the MamC-anomalous and MamC-typical isolates. To determine whether differences in motif specificity between MamC-anomalous and -typical isolates were statistically significant, we compared each MamC-anomalous isolate and its most similar MamC-typical isolate, pairwise. This ensures any reported differences in motif specificity are statistically significant with respect to all MamC-typical isolates. Here, the most similar MamC-typical isolate is defined for each MamC-anomalous isolate as the MamC-typical isolate with the greatest overlap in adenine IPD ratio distribution. To find pair-wise distribution overlaps, we used the overlapping, lattice and lawstat packages of the R statistical programming language. A two-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to determine whether each pair of distributions differed to a statistically significant degree (Supplementary Tables S1 and S2).

Protein modeling

The MamC MTase protein complex was visualized using Pymol (https://pymol.org/2/) software. SWISSMODEL (54) was utilized to visualize the 3D structure of isolate N0091’s MamC modification subunit. This structure was constructed using the AlphaFold-predicted structural model of MamC from M. tuberculosis virulent type strain H37Rv (ATCC 25618) as a template. To visualize how the MamC modification subunit binds to DNA and its specificity subunit, we used EcoR1241-DNA in the restriction-alleviation state model structure resolved by electron microscopy (55) (PDB ID: 7BTQ). The predicted MamC modification subunit was then structurally aligned to the 7BTQ restriction-alleviation state model. The previously reported (55) functionally important residues in 7BTQ protein and the mutations in isolate N0091’s MamC modification subunit were visualized using Pymol to identify putatively functionally important effects of MamC mutations in N0091.

Transcription factor-binding site analyses

Overlap between TFBS motifs identified by Minch and colleagues (42) and the 40 nucleotides flanking MTase motif sites were identified as previously described (22). A custom R script was utilized to find MamC motif sites overlapping a TFBS. Overlaps were then summarized in a contingency table, and a Fisher's exact test was carried out to determine statistical significance of the overlap.

Differential growth analysis

To calculate the growth rate for each isolate using the peak-to-trough ratio (PTR), we adapted previous short read growth estimation pipelines (56) by adjusting the following steps: first, as the length of the reads artifactually diminishes coverage at the beginning and end of the genome (reads spanning the beginning and end of the genome only map to one or the other in the linear representation of the genome), we made a synthetic genome where the break in the circular genome was artificially placed at the genome midpoint. We then took the maximum coverage at each position using this genome representation and the conventional genome representation.

Second, since there are fewer measurements contributing to the overall coverage given equivalent coverage depth, we adjusted the hyperparameters as follows (see Supplementary Figure S1 for a flowchart detailing the process).

Coverage pre-processing. A moving mean coverage depth and GC skew ratio were calculated using different window and window slide sizes (step) to determine the origin (ori) and terminus (ter) of replication as described previously (57). Windows with a median value of ± 4.25 SDs were filtered out for quality control; 4.25 is an approximate value for the SD at which an extreme value is expected to appear by chance of 50% of the time in our dataset.

Origin and terminus detection. With these smoothened coverage depth data in hand, we next applied a signal baseline correction method implemented by Scipy's ndimage.uniform_filter function using different mean filter widths as a hyperparameter. This step reduces low-frequency noise. The ori and ter coordinates were first estimated using the maximum and minimum GC skew ratio (58), defined as (G – C)/(G + C) in each window. The minimum GC skew ratio corresponds to the ori and the maximum value corresponds to the ter. This was determined using the lmfit() function to find the optimized ori and ter based on the smoothed, baseline-corrected coverage depth data.

Peak-to-trough ratio calculation. The PTR is then calculated using coverage depth of ori divided by coverage depth of ter found by the piece-wise linear function.

Hyperparameter selection. Hyperparameters are defined as coverage smoothing window size, slide size and Scipy's ndimage.uniform.filter window size (for coverage baseline correction). To calculate the best performing hyperparameters, we evaluated two metrics. First, we evaluated how many isolates for which a plausible origin and terminus were predicted. Second, we used the strength of correlations between pairwise genetic distance and absolute PTRs. For calculating the PTR difference and genetic distance correlations, we excluded all pairwise comparisons that included any of the MamC-anomalous isolates (since we expect them to behave differently, and thus they would confound the method of assessing hyperparameter combinations through this method). Genetic distance was defined as the mash distance between isolates. We selected the hyperparameter combination that jointly maximized the number of isolates with a plausible ori and ter, and a correlation coefficient of pairwise genetic distance and pairwise PTR difference (Supplementary Table S9).

Results

Anomalous DNA adenine methylation in M. tuberculosis complex clinical isolates is MamC specific

To follow up on our previous observation of anomalous methylation (22), we clustered all MamC-active isolates from 93 M. tuberculosis clinical isolates (22) using all sites invariably present across isolates with an active copy of MamC. Hierarchical clustering of the MamC-active isolates (n = 71) on the fraction of reads methylated at each MamC motif site (Figure 1C, D) yielded two clusters: one with four isolates—the same four that were observed previously to have similar methylation patterns (22)—and a second cluster comprising the remaining isolates with active MamC (n = 67). We refer to these four distinctly clustered isolates as ‘MamC-anomalous’ isolates and the others as ‘MamC-typical’. k-means clustering (k = 2) generated the same cluster memberships (Figure 1A, k-means clustering labeled in blue), suggesting that the clusters are separated by a general feature, rather than a hierarchical one. Noting that the larger distance between N0091 and the other three MamC-anomalous isolates (mean mash distance = 0.0009, mean mash distance between MamCWT-anomalous isolates = 0.00015), we applied PCA to evaluate whether N0091 should be grouped with the other three MamC-anomalous isolates. PCA (Figure 1E, F) showed that N0091 is distinctly methylated. The other three MamC-anomalous isolates remained distinctly clustered from MamC-typical isolates after removing N0091 (Supplementary Figure S2). Moreover, N0091 has two MamC missense mutations (K458N and E481A) that might explain its MamC-anomalousness. Therefore, we refer to the other three MamC-anomalous isolates as ‘MamCWT-anomalous’ and group them separately from N0091 where appropriate.

Next, we scrutinized the MamC-anomalous isolates for potential artifactual sources causing their distinct clustering before moving onto hypothesizing potential biological sources. We investigated the possibility of run-specific differences driving altered kinetics at MamC motif sites in the MamC-anomalous isolates. Two observations limit the plausibility of this scenario a priori. The first observation is that the three MamCWT-anomalous isolates are proximal in the phylogenetic tree (Figure 1A), consistent with an inherited feature driving the phenomenon in these three isolates. The second observation is that many isolates that do not cluster with these four were sequenced in two distinct laboratories. MamC sequencing kinetics clustered neither according to sequencing center, DNA isolation site (Brooklyn, San Diego or Stockholm, Figure 1D), drug resistance phenotype (Supplementary Table S3) nor isolate lineage (except for Indo-Oceanic isolates, Figure 1D). To examine the possibility of run-specific, technical effects driving our observations, we compared how sequencing kinetics distributed at MamA and MamB motif sites for the four MamC-anomalous isolates (Figure 1B). If the source were technical in nature, it should affect sequencing kinetics at motif sites for all three M. tuberculosis MTases. Yet in MamC-anomalous isolates, sequencing kinetics at MamA and MamB motif sites distributed similarly to those in MamC-typical isolates with active MamC (Figure 1B). This effectively rules out technical idiosyncrasies that broadly alter sequencing kinetics in a run-specific manner and confirms that the anomalous methylation is MamC specific.

Distinct methylation in MamC-anomalous isolates is exaggerated at particular MamC motif sites

Examining how sequencing kinetics distributed among MamC motif sites revealed differences between MamC-typical and MamC-anomalous isolates. In MamC-anomalous isolates, sequencing kinetics distribute bimodally among MamC motif sites across the genome (Figure 1B). This contrasts with the invariably unimodal distributions (each isolate centered around either ∼0 or ∼2.5) observed for MamC-anomalous isolates at motif sites of other MTases and with MamC-typical isolates at MamC motif sites. Together, these observations are consistent with a driver of altered MamC activity in MamC-anomalous isolates with an outsized effect on a subset of MamC motif sites.

Next, to find a subset of MamC motif sites that are driving the clusters, we tested each site with k-means clustering individually to see which sites reproduced the same clustering on their own. (Supplementary Figure S3A, B), using the three MamCWT-anomalous isolates versus the MamC-typical isolates (n = 67) as the classes to recapitulate. A total of 26/492 MamC motif sites individually recapitulated the same partitioning of k-means clustering of MamCWT-anomalous and MamC-typical isolates applied to all sites simultaneously. We refer to these 26 sites as the ‘cluster-driving sites’ (Supplementary Table S4; Supplementary Figure S3A, B).

Hypotheses for factors driving anomalous MamC methylation of M. tuberculosis

Having ruled out technical artifacts as the cause of MamC-anomalous isolates and having identified N0091 as distinct from the cluster of three MamCWT-anomalous strains (Figure 1E, F), we next asked what might be driving the effect. We considered factors shown previously to alter DNA MTase activity for a subset of sites (rather than globally) in other prokaryotes and formulated four hypotheses to investigate (Figure 2).

Hypothesized phenomena driving anomalous MamC methylation. (A) Hypothesis 1: competitive antagonism. MamC motif sites can be physically occluded by DNA-binding proteins, preventing access to its target adenine (28,29). (B) Hypothesis 2: altered motif specificity. MamC motif specificity can be changed due to mutations (30). (C) Hypothesis 3: phase-variable MamC. Mutations in MamC subpopulations can ablate or alter MTase activity in one phase of the colony, resulting in heterogeneous DNA methylation (31). (D) Hypothesis 4: differential growth phase. prokaryotic DNA methylation is often carried out at certain cell cycle periods and can thus be biased by the number of cells in different cell cycle stages (32–34).

Mutated MamC-anomalous isolate N0091 exhibits reduced methylation probably due to mutations in MamC affecting its processivity

Our second hypothesis was that the cluster-driving MTase motifs are less methylated in MamC-anomalous isolates than in MamC-typical isolates due to altered MamC motif specificity (Figure 2B). While MamCWT-anomalous isolates did not carry consensus mutations in MamC or other proteins of the complex (MamS and MamS.1), mutations in protein(s) with an unknown role in determining MTase specificity or some non-genetic factor could drive altered motif specificity in these isolates. Some MamC-anomalous isolates carried heterogeneous mutations in their MTases (Supplementary Figure S4; Supplementary Table S5), which may have altered the target motif of the MamC complex in a subpopulation of the bacterial cells.

To test the altered motif specificity hypothesis, we utilized a motif refinement strategy, comparing MamC-typical isolates with those exhibiting MamC-anomalous sites (Figure 3). We used a scheme similar to MeMoRe (53), but adapted for comparison across multiple samples (see the Materials and methods). For each position in the MamC motif, we altered the base to each alternative nucleotide, and tested whether each MamC-anomalous isolate was differentially methylated at the MamC targeted within matches to the alternative motif (Figure 3A, B; Supplementary Tables S1 and S2) compared with its most similar MamC-typical counterpart (two-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, Supplementary Text). Some MamC-anomalous isolates presented evidence of altered motif specificity (Supplementary Text) including the mutant MamC-anomalous isolate N0091 and MamCWT-anomalous isolate 1-0071 which both had increased IPD ratios at sites matching the alternative MamC motif GATNNNNRTCC/GGAYNNNNATC (Figure 3A, B). At all alternative motifs where the MamC-anomalous isolates significantly departed in methylation level from their MamC-typical counterparts, they had reduced methylation efficiency (Figure 3A, B). The altered methylation of N0091 may be due to the two known missense mutations in its MamC gene, K458N and E481A. Missense mutation K458N changes the amino acid residue from the highly reactive, positively charged lysine to the less reactive, neutrally charged asparagine. In addition, E481A missense mutation changes the amino acid residue from hydrophilic, polar and charged glutamic acid to hydrophobic, non-polar and uncharged alanine. These mutations may reduce MamC processivity in N0091, potentially accounting for its reduced methylation levels. Alternatively, the heterogeneous mutations observed in mamS, mamC or mamS.1 may have changed the target motif in subpopulations of MamC-anomalous isolates (Supplementary Figure S4; Supplementary Table S5). However, the consensus mutations of N0091 are more likely to impact MamC methylation substantively.

To investigate the possibility of an alternative target motif in a subpopulation of the MamC-anomalous isolates, we tested for statistically significant IPD ratio increases at sites matching the alternative bipartite MamC motif GATNNNNRTCC resulting from a minority subpopulation with MamC specificity altered accordingly. We selected this alternative motif among all alternative motifs as it had the most statistically significant IPD difference from MamC-typical isolates (Supplementary Text; Materials and methods). The IPDs averaged across sites matching the alternative bipartite MamC motif GATNNNNRTCC within each sequencing read did not exhibit a bimodal distribution across sequencing reads characteristic of phase variation (Figure 3F) for any of the four MamC-anomalous isolates. We thus refute the existence of a minority population with altered motif specificity.

Differential methylation in MamC-anomalous isolates is probably not explained by TF competitive antagonism

Occlusion of DNA motif sites by DNA-binding proteins is a well-characterized driver of hypomethylation in prokaryotes (25,59,60). This informed our first hypothesis: transcription factors (TFs) occlude the targeted adenine within a MamC motif in the cluster-driving MamC motif sites (Figure 2A) and do so specifically in the MamC-anomalous isolates. We observed no enrichment between cluster-driving MamC sites and MamC sites that overlapped with empirically determined TFBSs (42) [Supplementary Table S6; Supplementary Figure S3D, P = 0.6913, odds ratio (OR) = 0.836, 95% confidence interval (CI) = 0.345–1.993]. Moreover, no mutations in TFs or other genes were common to MamCWT-anomalous isolates, apart from a few missense mutations shared between two of the three MamCWT-anomalous isolates (Supplementary Table S7). We conclude that mutation-driven competitive antagonism of MamC by TFs is unlikely to explain the methylation patterns observed in MamC-anomalous isolates.

MamC-anomalous isolates display intercellular mosaic methylation

Phase variability in MTases is a common phenomenon driving heterogeneous methylation (11,31). While it is typically driven by homopolymer frameshifts (31,52)—which would delete MamC function entirely—a missense mutation or heterogeneous mechanism in MamC or MamS could affect a subset of sites by altering MTase specificity. This informs our third hypothesis, that heterogeneous mutation creates subpopulations of MamCOFF bacilli in the MamC-anomalous isolates (Figure 2C). Two of the MamCWT-anomalous isolates carried heterogeneous frameshift mutations in mamC (Supplementary Figure S4; Supplementary Table S5), which could have resulted in subpopulations of MamCOFF bacilli, contributing to the lower methylation signals at MamC motif sites. Given this apparent genetic heterogeneity, we hypothesized that the MamCWT-anomalous isolates had bimodal methylation patterns from a mixture of MamCOFF and MamCON bacilli, as seen in phase variation (11,31).

Based on the genetic evidence of a mixture of MamCOFF and MamCON bacilli in the MamCWT-anomalous isolates, we next looked for signatures of phase-variable MamC activity in their methylomes. The lower mean IPD ratios in the MamC-anomalous isolates compared with MamC-typical isolates implies heterogeneous methylation. Considering a particular MamC motif site, the IPD ratio is the average calculated across multiple reads. Therefore, the intermediate IPD ratios observed at many sites in the MamC-anomalous isolates is caused by a mix of sequencing reads that are methylated at the site of interest and reads unmethylated at that site. However, which type of heterogeneous methylation underpins this remained unclear. Heterogeneous methylation comes in two forms: phase-variable and intracellular stochastic methylation (22,52) (Figure 4A). In phase-variable methylation, some sequencing reads have all MTase sites methylated (those from MTase-active bacilli) while the remaining sequencing reads have no MamC sites methylated (those from MTase-inactive bacilli) (52). In intracellular stochastic methylation, individual sequencing reads have a subset of MamC motif sites methylated (52). This results in a mosaic of unmethylated/methylated combinations across cells, known as IMM (22).

To determine whether the heterogeneous MamC methylation in MamC-anomalous isolates is stochastic or phase variable, we assessed the distribution of MamC methylation at the read level (Figure 4). We used SMALR to calculate the mean IPD across MamC motif sites for each sequencing read. The normalized methylated fraction values of MamC-anomalous isolates were lower than those of MamC-typical isolates (mean 0.789 versus 1, P= 2.56e-20, Wilcoxon rank-sum test), and greater than those of MamC-inactive isolates (mean 0.789 versus 0, P=6.66e-217, Wilcoxon rank-sum test), consistent with mosaic methylation (Figure 4B). Moreover, the MamC-anomalous isolates had more partially methylated reads than expected from randomly permuted mixture of reads from MamC-inactive and MamC-typical subpopulations (Figure 4C). When compared with 100 000 mixtures of randomly sampled MamC-typical and MamC-inactive reads (in proportions based on the MamC-anomalous isolate), < 2.67% of simulated mixtures had as many partially methylated reads as the MamC-anomalous isolates (N0091, 0.367%; 1-0039, 0%; 1-0071, 0.021%; 1-0069, 2.667%; Figure 4C; Supplementary Table S8). Moreover, methylated fraction of reads from MamC-anomalous isolates occupied a greater area under the curve in partially methylated space than simulated mixtures of wholly methylated and unmethylated reads that would equate to the same mean. From these observations, the methylomes of MamC-mutated N0091 and MamCWT-anomalous isolates are not consistent with a mixture of MamCOFF and MamCON bacilli. Instead, they exhibit intracellular stochastic methylation resulting in intercellular mosaic methylation (22), as previously observed for MamA in numerous Modern Beijing and several Latin American–Mediterranean sublineage M. tuberculosis isolates with specific MamA genotypes (22).

Partially active DNA methyltransferase alleles exhibit methylation bias along the axis of replication

While the restriction of anomalous methylation to MamC motifs (Figure 1E) rules out technical run-specific effects, it does not rule out genuine biological sample-specific effects, such as differential growth phase. Bacterial DNA adenine MTases are often involved in coordinating replication (32,34,61,62), which results in some MTases, such as CcrM of Caulobacter crescentus (34), being expressed and enzymatically active only during a brief window of chromosome replication. Other MTases are expressed and active constitutively. MTase patterns specific to a particular growth phase could thus affect only some MTases as we see here with MamC-specific anomalies. This motivates our fourth hypothesis, that MamC-anomalous isolates were in different growth phases at the time of DNA extraction, conferring distinct methylation patterns (Figure 2D).

Due to the polar directionality of bacterial chromosomal replication, growth rate can be inferred from the slope of DNA sequencing coverage depth along the axis of replication (from the replication origin to its terminus) (56). To evaluate our hypothesis, we take advantage of this phenomenon, associating DNA methylation patterns with the axis of replication and the growth rate implied by the coverage depth slope along it. This relies on having a single ori and ter on a circular chromosome [which is consistent with M. tuberculosis (63)], where the cell division time can be shorter than the chromosome replication time due to the presence of multiple replication forks (64,65)

We first contrasted how sequencing kinetics varied along the ori–ter axis for the three known DNA MTases encoded by M. tuberculosis: MamA, MamB and MamC. Strikingly, in isolates with alleles encoding partially active MamA or MamB, the methylated fraction of their target motifs decreases slightly as a function of distance from the origin of replication (Figure 5A). The crossing over of the strands’ curves approximately at the genome midpoint is consistent with a chromosome replication-dependent effect on the methylation level. This pattern appears for all MamB genotypes, but is exaggerated in the partially active MamB allele. Like the MamB alleles (Figure 5A), in the fully active MamA alleles the strands’ curves intersect around the genome midpoint, again consistent with a chromosome replication-dependent effect on relative methylation levels (Figure 5B). However, unlike MamB and fully active MamA alleles, partially active MamA alleles lose strand specificity of the pattern (note that the partially active MamA exhibits comparatively lower MTase activity compared with MamBK1033T). Instead, the methylation level in partially active MamA alleles is more influenced by the absolute proximity to the ori, irrespective of strand and to a greater extent than fully active MamA alleles.

Fully active MamC alleles behave differently from fully active MamA or MamB alleles, resembling more closely partially active MamA alleles (particularly MamAG152S, Figure 5B). The partially inactive MamC allele behaves similarly, but with a stronger ori-proximity effect and exaggerated right-of-center peak, creating a ‘w-like’ shape (Figure 5C). MamC-anomalous isolates exhibit MamC-specific methylation bias along the axis of replication

Considering these findings, we hypothesized that the three MamCWT-anomalous isolates exhibit exaggerated methylation bias along the ori–ter axis due to methylation changes that emerge in particular growth phases, and that the other isolates were not in this growth phase when DNA was extracted for sequencing.

Unlike MamA and MamB (Figure 6B, C), the pattern along the ori–ter axis for fully active MamC alleles is not entirely predictable by genotype (Figure 6A, D). Rather, the magnitude of methylation bias along the ori–ter axis depends on MamC behavior (typical versus anomalous) (Figure 6A, E). All four MamC-anomalous isolates appear to exhibit this behavior. One of the four MamC-anomalous isolates (N0091) has a partially active MamC allele, which may explain the behavior of this isolate. The other three, however, have a fully active, wild-type MamC allele. Contrasting the relationship of the ori–ter axis and MamC methylation across isolates shows that the three MamCWT-anomalous isolates are dissimilar to all the wild-type MamC-typical isolates, yet are quite similar to the mutated MamC-anomalous isolate (N0091). While the MamC-anomalous isolates have a similar shape to MamC-typical isolates, it is invariably of greater magnitude in MamC-anomalous isolates (Figure 6A).

The growth rate of MamCWT-anomalous isolates is hyperstable and fastest among Beijing sublineage isolates

Next, we evaluated the degree of coverage bias along the ori–ter axis to see whether it is consistent with MamCWT-anomalous isolates being in a different growth phase from MamC-typical isolates. This approach is motivated by the well-characterized relationship between the strength of ori–ter axis coverage bias and bacterial growth rate or phase (56). The more rapidly a bacterial population replicates, the larger the discrepancy in coverage depth as a function of position on the ori–ter axis. Estimating the growth rate using this method, however, requires determination of the origin and terminus. Recent methods have been developed to infer the origin and terminus of replication directly from coverage depth. However, these methods were developed for short reads. We adapted the pipeline developed for short reads to work with long-read sequencing data (Supplementary Figure S1; Materials and methods) and identified suitable hyperparameters (Supplementary Text; Supplementary Figures S5–S8; Supplementary Table S10).

We used the PTR of coverage depth to infer growth rate and deviation with respect to the fitted regression line as a proxy for growth phase homogeneity (Supplementary Text; Materials and methods). MamCWT-anomalous isolates exhibited highly stable coverage bias along the ori–ter axis (lower mean-squared error of residuals) relative to MamCWT-typical isolates (Figure 7D) and high growth rate (as inferred by PTR) for their genetic background (Figure 7E). All three MamCWT-anomalous isolates belong to the Modern Beijing sublineage (L2.2.1) of M. tuberculosis lineage 2 (East-Asian). Consistent with prior observations (66), we found that East-Asian isolates were growing at a slower rate than other lineages, except for the Euro-American lineage. East-Asian lineage isolates averaged a significantly lower PTR than Indo-Oceanic lineage isolates (P= 0.0011, means 1.05 versus 1.082, Student's t-test, Figure 7E).

From the PTR-inferred growth rates, we make three conclusions. First, M. tuberculosis clinical isolates indeed exhibit coverage depth bias along the axis of replication, and this bias is recoverable from long reads (Figures 6 and 7). Second, consistent with the alternative growth phase hypothesis, the three MamCWT-anomalous isolates exhibit an exceptionally strong (Figure 7D) and stable (Figure 7E) coverage depth bias, consistent with more rapid growth at the time of DNA extraction than other isolates. PTR-inferred growth is significantly higher than isolates of their lineage for all three MamCWT-anomalous isolates (2.72 × 10−8 ≤ P ≤ 6.56 × 10−5, one-sample t-test). Third, the effect of more rapid growth on DNA adenine methylation is MamC specific; in the MamCWT-anomalous isolates, MamA and MamB exhibited neither site-specific hypomethylation (Figure 1B) nor the exaggerated relationship between methylation level and position along the ori–ter axis for motif sites that we observed for MamC (Figures 5 and 6).

Discussion

Primary conclusions

We investigated four hypothesized causes of the anomalous clustering observed in four M. tuberculosis clinical isolates. The anomalous methylation pattern observed in isolate N0091 can be attributed to two specific mutations in its MamC gene, unlike the other three MamC-anomalous isolates. These three MamCWT-anomalous isolates exhibit instead an exceptionally strong coverage depth bias as a function of position along the axis of chromosome replication (ori–ter axis), particularly for their genetic background. This extensive coverage depth bias in MamCWT-anomalous isolates suggests that a greater fraction of bacilli were undergoing chromosome replication at the time of DNA extraction than other MamC-typical isolates. Our analyses uncovered three primary discoveries. First, MamC-anomalous isolates exhibit heterogeneous MamC methylation not in the form of phase variation, but rather through stochastic methylation—the first report of this phenomenon by a Type I MTase. Second, all three MamCWT-anomalous isolates belong to the globally successful Modern Beijing sublineage and exhibit faster and more rapid growth than MamC-typical Modern Beijing isolates. Third, the association between PTR-inferred growth rate and methylated fraction is specific to MamC among M. tuberculosis DNA adenine MTases; MamA and MamB exhibit typical methylation profiles in MamC-anomalous isolates.

Stochastic DNA methylation in the context of previous findings

Heterogeneous methylation by Type I MTases has been observed in other bacteria, but not the type of heterogeneous methylation we observe here. Previous examples in other human pathogens Streptococcus pneumoniae and Listeria monocytogenes are caused by homologous recombination between the MTase specificity subunit and a pseudogene copy of the specificity subunit (20,67). This results in phase-variable MTase target motif specificity, with cells in different phases having different sites methylated (20,67). In other characterized examples, phase-variable target motif specificity in Type I MTases has been attributed to reversible truncations in its specificity subunit due to a variable length homopolymer tract (19). The form of heterogeneity we observed here in Type I MTase MamC, however, is a distinct phenomenon; the heterogeneous methylation was intracellularly stochastic rather than phase variable (Figure 4). While there was putative evidence of heterogeneous methylation at sites of alternative motifs, with a slight increase in IPD ratio in the MamC-anomalous isolates at alternative motif sites (Figure 3), investigation of the strongest of these signals for phase-variable methylation at the alternative motif sites unturned no evidence of a phase with reads methylated at the alternative motif (Figure 3F). Meanwhile, at the known MamC motif, we observed reads with a mix of methylated and unmethylated motif sites in the MamC-anomalous isolates, rather than distinct phases of reads fully methylated and unmethylated across sites (Figure 4). This observed intracellular stochastic methylation (22,52) is a form of heterogeneous methylation distinct from the phase-variable motif specificity previously observed in Type I MTases (19,20,67).

Intracellular stochastic methylation by M. tuberculosis MamC is the third reported instance of the phenomenon in any bacterial MTase. The first instance was reported in Chromohalobacter salexigens (52). The second reported instance was by the M. tuberculosis DNA MTase MamA, creating IMM (22). This IMM was constitutive in some isolates with particular MamA alleles and also emerged in response to methionine starvation in MamAWT isolates. Considering the recent evidence of direct transcriptional regulation by promoter MamC methylation (22), the MamC IMM observed here probably creates heterogeneous gene expression, in turn creating a multitude of phenotypically diverse subpopulations. Importantly, this additional layer of phenotypic plasticity is specific to strains with active MamC. Thus, strains with active MamC may have greater adaptivity than those with inactive MamC. Notably, most East-Asian strains possess both an active copy of MamC and, as previously reported, a constitutively heterogeneous copy of MamA. We speculate that this dual capacity for IMM further contributes to the adaptivity and rapid global spread of the Modern Beijing sublineage by conferring a rapid, non-genetic mechanism of phenotypic plasticity. This enhanced phenotypic plasticity could help the infection flourish in a greater variety of immunological backgrounds driven by host population genetic backgrounds or by social and environmental factors.

Directionality and periodicity of DNA methylation along the ori–ter axis

Our analysis of MamC methylation efficiency along the ori–ter axis reveals intriguing patterns that raise questions about the underlying forces that give rise to these patterns. First, the increased methylation efficiency near the origin (Figure 6) contrasts with what one might expect from stochastic methylation and with the behavior of Escherichia coli Dam MTase, where transient hemimethylation is more frequent behind the replication fork (68) (which would occur at a relatively higher frequency at origin-proximal DNA in fast-growing populations). Many genes in M. tuberculosis are expressed coordinately with the various processes of growth, including chromosome replication (69). One explanation for this higher origin-proximal methylated fraction is that MamC expression is up-regulated at the initiation of replication, leading to higher MamC levels as origin-proximal DNA is synthesized. This could result in a decay in methylation efficiency as replication progresses toward the terminus, resulting in a greater methylated fraction near the origin, as we observed. Another contributing factor could be competition for motif site access by DNA-binding proteins. In E. coli, and in other species with orphan MTases, hypomethylated sites are often the result of competition with DNA-binding proteins (70). This is consistent with the overlap of TF-binding motifs and the small set of consistently hypomethylated sites observed in MamC-active clinical isolates (22,24).

Second, it is curious that methylation efficiency does not decrease monotonically along the replication axis but instead exhibits periodicity (Figure 6C–E). The consistent spacing of local maxima and minima suggests that periodicity might arise from forces involved in chromosomal replication, several of which could plausibly engender periodic methylation efficiency. The size of the observed periods—ranging from hundreds of kilobases to > 1 Mb—points to features that organize chromosomal topology as potential explanative forces, such as macrodomains and nucleoid-associated proteins (NAPs). Macrodomains are physically separated chromosomal regions in bacteria (71) and often span ∼1 Mb (72), consistent with the period size observed in MamC methylation efficiency (Figure 6E). M. tuberculosis encodes homologs of the well-characterized NAPs HupB and Lsr2 (73), which organize the chromosome into large domains (74). NAP abundance also varies according to growth phase (72), which is consistent with the exaggerated periodicity of methylation efficiency we observe in the fast-growing MamCWT-anomalous isolates (Figure 6E).

These chromosomal topology features could affect methylation efficiency through physical separation from the locus of MTase expression on the parent chromosome or through increased antagonism by the DNA-binding proteins that orchestrate chromosomal topological organization. The period size difference between MamA and MamC methylation efficiency along the ori–ter axis suggests that the MTases interact differentially with the periodicity-driving forces. These distinctions provide a potentially useful framework for interrogating the interplay between DNA methylation, chromosomal topology and chromosome replication in Mycobacteria, the details of which remain largely unknown.

Link between DNA methylation and growth rate

What links anomalous MamC methylation and bacterial growth rate remains unclear. We observed hypomethylation in MamC-anomalous isolates in two forms: the marked site-specific hypomethylation that originally drew our attention to these isolates, and the general hypomethylation that increases from chromosome origin to terminus. Potential causes include chromatin conformation (75–77), the binding state of DNA-binding proteins (42) and physicochemical properties intrinsic to sequence composition features that vary along the ori–ter axis (78). The former two causes may also influence site-specific hypomethylation and could be consequences of gene expression programs associated with more rapid growth, while the latter cause may explain the general trend of greater hypomethylation from origin to terminus. MamC interaction with nascent DNA during replication could also be involved, but these dynamics are essentially unknown. Other explanations include shorter accessible regions of the genome closer to the terminus, as accessible oligonucleotide length has a strong impact on DNA adenine methylation kinetics in other prokaryotic MTases (79), and increased prevalence of natural inhibitors of methylation as a function of the ori–ter axis, such as S-adenosylhomocysteine (80). A final possibility is that pools of S-adenosylmethionine (SAM)—the methyl donor for DNA MTase reaction—are under greater demand during more rapid growth and that MamC is more sensitive to SAM concentration than either MamA or MamB, which would explain the MamC-specific hypomethylation in the more rapidly growing MamCWT-anomalous isolates. SAM is the methyl donor for hundreds of methylation reactions catalyzed by enzymes encoded by M. tuberculosis (81). Many of these MTase reactions are essential for M. tuberculosis growth—such as ribosome maturation (82) and mycolic acid biosynthesis (83)—and take place more frequently in actively dividing cells, creating competition for available SAM between all the M. tuberculosis MTases.

Methylation strand specificity

The top 30 most differentially methylated sites between MamC-anomalous and MamC-typical isolates (Supplementary Table S10) exhibit two noteworthy patterns. First, out of 30 differentially methylated MamC motif sites, 24 were pairs of the same motif locus (targeted adenine on both strands were hypomethylated), far more than expected by chance. Since methylation is preserved in the daughter strand but not in the newly synthesized strand, the strong correlation between hypomethylation in bipartite pairs suggests either that the differential methylation is not strand specific or that hemimethylation potentiates remethylation, which is consistent with the resistance of hemimethylated sites to cleavage by the HsdR subunit in Type I restriction–modification systems (84). Even in the absence of the hsdR gene and its product, the mechanism remains unchanged, implying that hemimethylation potentiates remethylation (85). Moreover, some prokaryotic MTases have a demonstrated preference for hemimetylated sites (86), consistent with the strand-independent trends observed genome-wide as a function of position along the axis of replication (Figures 6 and 7)

Potential phenotypic implications of hypomethylated MamC motif sites

We observed differential methylation of motif sites in several genes with potential clinical and phenotypic implications. These may indicate what is inhibiting methylation, or potentially modulate their transcription (22,21). Rv1219c and guaA have differentially methylated MamC sites in their promoter. MamC methylation status has a cis-regulatory effect on gene expression for many of its target motifs (22), though this effect does not necessarily operate at every MamC-targeted promoter. GuaA is involved in guanine nucleotide biosynthesis, and is essential for in vitro growth and a potential candidate for new drug development (87). Determining whether MamC methylation materially influences GuaA expression may thus be a prudent step in evaluating GuaA-targeting therapeutics. A MamC motif is also located in the promoter (–25 bp) of Rv1219c (known as raaS). RaaS is a transcriptional regulator that controls expression of ABC efflux pump proteins Rv1217c–Rv1218c, overexpression of which increases the minimum inhibitory concentration to anti-tuberculosis first-line drugs rifampicin and isoniazid (88). Critically, raaS was one of the genes that harbors promoter MamC motif sites and significantly changed transcriptional levels upon MamC knockout (22), suggesting a direct interaction between raaS promoter methylation by MamC and RaaS expression, ostensibly affecting drug tolerance through altered efflux pump activity by Rv1217c–Rv1218c. Other noteworthy genes harboring intragenic MamC sites are discussed in the Supplementary Text.

Challenges and advantages associated with the PTR method for inferring growth rate

Our use of the long-read PTR (lrPTR) to infer growth rate presented some challenges. First, compared with short reads, long reads provide fewer measurements of genome multiplicity at a given coverage depth; since reads are longer, fewer comprise the total sequenced nucleotides. This, combined with the relatively slow growth of M. tuberculosis, makes recovering lrPTR challenging, particularly for slower growing isolates. GC skew reliably correlates with position along the ori–ter axis and is thus often used to estimate origin and terminus location (58). However, evolutionarily recent events such as large, heterogeneous variants affecting genome structure—which are known to emerge in M. tuberculosis (37,89,90)—present a second challenge, as they can distort the relationship between coverage depth and position over the heterogeneously inverted portions of the ori–ter axis, potentially confounding the PTR metric. We observed some evidence of this phenomenon in a small subset of our studied isolates, highlighted in Supplementary Figure S8B and C. However, most isolates did not display these signatures of heterogeneous structural variants, and we implemented quality control strategies to identify and exclude such isolates from our analysis (see the Materials and methods). Despite these challenges, our adaptation of the PTR method (56) for long reads recovered changes in coverage depth slope across the genome and variability (Figure 7; Supplementary Figure S9), both of which reflect features of the growth dynamics of the culture from which the DNA of these strains was isolated.

Our lrPTR approach offers a key advantage over traditional wet lab methods for measuring bacterial growth rate. lrPTR allows for the direct association between DNA methylation and growth rate within the same subpopulation of cells. In contrast, common wet lab techniques (e.g. time-course optical density or fluorescence intensity) measure population-level growth rate, decoupling them from the subpopulations from which DNA methylation is derived. lrPTR enables assessment of DNA methylation (via sequencing kinetics) and growth rate from the same physical DNA molecules. This enabled us to demonstrate that the sequenced subpopulation had anomalous MamC methylation patterns and also originated from a population with a higher fraction of cells actively replicating their chromosome at the time of DNA isolation compared with their slower growing, MamC-typical counterparts.

lrPTR holds significant potential for broader applications in clinical and environmental samples. It enables comprehensive analysis of bacterial growth dynamics, genome structure variation and DNA methylation patterns, all from a single sequencing run. This capability may prove invaluable in elucidating microbial interactions, bacterial correlates of clinical variables and ecological adaptations. lrPTR can be applied prospectively in designing future experiments, or retrospectively to existing long-read datasets, unveiling previously unrecognized associations between bacterial growth rate, genetic variation and epigenetic markers.

Alternative interpretations and remaining open questions

While our findings provide evidence that is likely to explain the atypical MamC behavior of the four MamC-anomalous isolates, alternative causes cannot be ruled out. First, DNA replication machinery mutations could theoretically cause differential coverage depth across the chromosome. In addition to the MamC mutations in the MamC-anomalous isolate N0091, isolate 1-0039 has a missense K894N mutation dnaA, a replication initiator that is essential for DNA replication initiation (91). In theory, this could explain the differential growth pattern in this isolate. However, this would leave the other two MamCWT-anomalous isolates unexplained, rendering this an unlikely cause. Second, is the possibility of DNA demethylation in MamC-anomalous isolates. While there is not a known enzyme dedicated to N6-methyladenine demethylation, its existence cannot be ruled out. Demethylation could be catalyzed by an unknown demethylase, or by promiscuity from other nucleic acid demethylases, such as those involved in DNA repair systems like E. coli alkB (92). M. tuberculosis alkB (Rv3252c) is involved in alkylation repair but has no characterized adenine demethylation activity. This second alternative, however, would require an explanation for higher levels of demethylation in the MamC-anomalous isolates.

It remains unknown whether the atypicality of MamC activity and anomalous growth dynamics of the three MamCWT-anomalous isolates are strain-level features or were particular to this instance of their growth and timing of DNA extraction. One the one hand, the absence of a genetic mechanism common and unique to the three MamCWT-anomalous isolates suggests that the phenomenon is not a strain-level feature. On the other hand, the phylogenetic proximity of the three MamCWT-anomalous isolates is consistent with the MamC-patterning idiosyncrasies of these isolates being a strain-level feature. Potential explanations for how these features would be strain level are complex epistasis, or some ontogenetic niche (93) unique to these isolates that perpetuated from isolation through culturing. This question remains an intriguing focus for future research. Without clear evidence that the observed anomalous MamC methylation is a persistent feature of the isolated stains, we characterize our central finding as features of fast-growing clinical ‘isolates’ rather than of fast-growing clinical ‘strains’.
