Consonant Proficiency in Children With and Without Hearing Impairment Assessed With Profiles of Early Expressive Phonological Skills – Swedish (PEEPS‐SE)
Authors: Anna Persson, Dyveke Christensen Karlström, Traci Flynn, Ulrika Marklund, Anette Lohmander
Published: 2025

Introduction

Children with hearing impairment (HI) are at risk for early speech‐language difficulties, which can persist during childhood. There is a lack of clinical tests adapted for young preschool children, enabling early identification of speech‐language delays. The focus of this article is to present the Swedish version of the Profiles of Early Expressive Phonological skills (Williams and Stoel‐Gammon 2023; Marklund et al. 2024) and to use the test to explore consonant proficiency in young children with HI.

The early consonant inventory is an important aspect of early speech‐language development, particularly the so‐called true consonants, that is, all consonants except glottals and glides (Stoel‐Gammon 2011). The absence or low frequency of anterior oral stops (d/t, b/p) is common in children with known medical conditions, such as HI (Moeller et al. 2007; Persson et al. 2022) cleft palate (Lohmander et al. 2011), cerebral palsy (Nyman and Lohmander 2018), and extreme prematurity (Strandberg et al. 2024). Presence of anterior oral stop consonants at 18 months and consonant proficiency at 3 years of age are related in children with HI and children with cleft palate (Lohmander et al. 2011; Klintö et al. 2014). Furthermore, a strong relationship between the size of the consonant inventory and expressive vocabulary has been shown in children with cleft palate and in typically developing children without a clinical condition (Stoel‐Gammon 2011; Willadsen 2013).

Hearing constitutes a fundamental basis for developing speech. This is particularly evident in studies on early phonological development in children born with HI. Children with HI have a later onset of canonical babbling (CB), despite their similar level of volubility to children with normal hearing (NH) (Iyer and Oller 2008; Moeller et al. 2007). They also present with a significantly lower number of different (true) consonants, fewer oral stops, and alveolar stops (Moeller et al. 2007; Persson et al. 2022), even with hearing amplification by six months of age. Fricative production has also been found to be significantly less in the early speech development of children with HI, most likely due to the limitations in auditory access of high frequency information caused by the HI (Ambrose et al. 2014). Consequently, these delays may not only impact later articulation skills but also vocabulary growth (Moeller et al. 2007; Persson et al. 2022).

There are many factors impacting the speech and language development of children with HI. First is the challenge of consistent use of hearing aids (HAs). Hours of HA use vary greatly in the pediatric population, and more hours of use relate to better speech and language performance (Persson et al. 2020; Walker et al. 2015). Second, around 30–40% of children with HI also have an additional disability (Cupples et al. 2014). Therefore, investigating their early phonological skills is critical to planning intervention services appropriately. A crucial factor to examining early phonological skills is the availability of reliable and valid measures. Until recently, speech and language pathologists in Sweden have lacked age‐appropriate tests to measure phonological development in children under 3 years of age. Therefore, the American test, Profiles of Early Expressive Phonological Skills (PEEPS), created by Stoel‐Gammon and Williams (2013) (Williams and Stoel‐Gammon 2023), was adapted to Swedish (Marklund et al. 2024), PEEPS‐Swedish (PEEPS‐SE) (Marklund et al. 2024).

PEEPS‐SE (Marklund et al. 2024) assesses consonant use, including phonological complexity of words, word forms and syllable structures in Swedish children aged 18–36 months. PEEPS‐SE enables independent and relational analyses of child speech production and is suitable for clinic and research use. Independent analysis, where child production is analysed without comparisons to the mature production of the target language, is particularly suitable for early and/or atypical production (e.g., if the child´s speech is difficult to understand). An independent analysis includes descriptions of the child's production of consonants in different word positions, use of syllable structures and word forms. With a relational analysis, the child's production can be compared to a mature production of the target sounds in terms of consonant inventory, word patterns and syllable structures (Stoel‐Gammon and Williams 2013). A Percentage of Consonants Correct (PCC) can be calculated from the correct use of consonants divided by the number of correctly and incorrectly produced consonants/segments multiplied by 100 (Shriberg et al. 1997). A relational analysis can also form the base for descriptions of specific deviant phonological patterns (or phonological processes) to detect “red flags” or warning signs of atypical development that should be investigated and, if necessary, followed up with suitable intervention.

PEEPS‐SE BAS (Eng. Basic) is comprised of 40 words, and PEEPS‐SE PLUS (Eng. Expanded) contains the 40 words along with an additional 20 words (i.e., a total of 60 words). The selection of the words for PEEPS‐SE was based on the same three criteria as PEEPS: word acquisition age, phonological complexity of the words, and the consonants are represented in different word positions. The criterion for age of acquisition is used to increase the probability that the children in the targeted age group are familiar with the test words, minimising the possibility that test results would be impacted by insufficient vocabulary knowledge. For PEEPS‐SE, the age of acquisition was based on vocabulary data obtained with the Swedish version of the McArthur Bates Communicative Developmental Inventory, SECDI (Eriksson and Berglund 1999), for 282 children (157 boys) aged 17–24 months (Marklund et al. 2018). Words with varying phonological complexity levels were selected, allowing for expressive phonology production to be described in terms of complexity (Stoel‐Gammon 2010). The phonological complexity of the words was calculated with the Swedish version of the Word Complexity Measure (Word Complexity Measure for Swedish, WCM‐SE (Marklund et al. 2018). The words were also selected to represent the majority of Swedish consonants in different word positions.

The purpose of this study was to compare the phonological development in terms of consonant proficiency of children with HI at 24 and 30 months to children with NH using the PEEPS‐SE. The hypothesis was that the children with HI would perform lower on the PCC and consonant number of different established consonants, and fricatives in particular, than the control group of children with NH. In addition, the study also aimed to describe the use of PEEPS‐SE on Swedish‐speaking children with and without HI and thereby contribute to the clinical application of this new test.

Materials and Methods

Participants

The participating children were part of a longitudinal project that utilised the PEEPS‐SE (Persson et al. 2020). Inclusionary criteria for all children were that at least one of the parents had Swedish as their native language and spoke it with the child, as well as the absence of additional disabilities known at the timepoint of enrolment in the study. The project was approved by the Regional Ethics Board in Stockholm, with ethical permit no XXX. Parents were informed about the study verbally as well as in writing, and signed a consent form before study participation.

The participants of this study consisted of two groups of children: (1) children with HI (n = 9) and (2) typically developing children with NH (n = 21). The children with HI were recruited from the Hearing Habilitation Centre for Children and Youth at Karolinska University Hospital, where they were clients. All children had a congenital bilateral sensorineural hearing loss of moderate degree (41–60 dB HL) and a mean age of diagnosis of 2.7 months (SD = 1.1 months). Mean pure‐tone average thresholds measured at four frequencies (PTA4F) at 24 months were (PTA4F) 44 dB HL (SD = 7.25) and at 30 months of age (PTA4F) 48.89 dB HL (SD = 7.56). All children were fitted bilaterally with behind‐the‐ear hearing aids before 6 months, mean age of 4.5 months (SD = 1.4 months). The hours of hearing aid use were obtained from datalogging at each time point of testing with the PEEPS‐SE.

The children with NH were recruited through two Child Health Care Centres in Stockholm near the hospital. To control for hearing status, all children in the typically developing group participated in hearing assessments before the PEEPS‐SE assessment. As part of the longitudinal project, the two groups of children were assessed with the Bayley Scales of Development at 3 years of age. The results from the cognitive scale showed scores within norms for all children in this study.

Material and Assessment Procedures

The PEEPS‐SE material used in testing includes worksheets, analysis forms, and toys and objects. All target words are listed on the worksheets, enabling the examiner to keep track of which words are elicited during the assessment. The worksheets are also used for transcriptions and analysis of child production. The results are compiled on the analysis forms. The toys and other objects represent the target words of the test, that are also selected to be easily elicited during testing. There is a hierarchy of elicitation strategies to encourage spontaneous productions and minimise imitations. If the child does not spontaneously produce the target word, the examiner asks, “What is this?”. If the child does not produce the target word, the examiner says a sentence ending with the target word but not saying it. For example, “you eat porridge with a…(spoon)”. …. If the child still has not produced the target word, the examiner asks the child to repeat it. In this study, all children were assessed with a preliminary version of PEEPS‐SE PLUS (expanded set), using the appropriate toys, objects, and elicitation strategies. For a list of toys and objects used, see Appendix 1.

All children were assessed individually using the PEEPS‐SE at the Hearing Habilitation Centre where the children with HI were enrolled. The test was performed in a room furnished for testing young children and was carried out on the floor on a mat. Parent(s) were present during testing. An experienced speech and language pathologist trained in PEEPS‐SE administration assessed the child. The testing took approximately 20–30 min and was recorded with a video camera Panasonic HC‐V750, external microphone Røde NT4 and audio recorder TASCAM DR‐22WL. The assessment was transcribed from the video recordings by a graduate student in speech and language pathology (2nd author) trained in phonetic transcription and repeated for reliability (see below).

Data Analysis

Based on transcriptions of target word productions, i.e., utterances that referred to the target words in the respective word list, each child's results were transferred to the analysis PLUS form. The results of the groups were compared separately at 24 and 30 months of age control for language development. All test recordings were grouped by hearing status and age at testing. According to the original PEEPS version, to ensure valid results, participants who produced fewer than 50% of the target words were excluded. In the current study, 15 children out of a total of 59 assessments (25%) were excluded due to less than 50% of the target words being produced during the session. In the current study, three measures were chosen for analysis. The percentage of consonants correct (PCC), which was based on target consonants in the PEEPS‐SE single words (Shriberg et al. 1997). When the target word contained a consonant cluster, the consonant phonemes were counted separately in the analysis. No distortions were accepted in the measure. The second measure was the total number of different established true consonants, which included consonants that were not glides or glottals and produced more than once in the test but not in a cluster. This is in agreement with the instructions in the original PEEPS (Williams and Stoel‐Gammon 2023). To be considered established, at least two productions of a consonant in the elicited words should have been performed. Finally, the number of established different fricatives was included since the study group were children with HI who may have specific problems to hear and produce the high frequency fricative sounds. The fricatives were thus included both in the main measure of different established true consonants and as a separate measure. The other PEEPS‐SE measures were not included in the analysis.

Reliability

Intra‐ and inter‐observer reliability were determined after the analysis using a random selection of 10.7% of the audio‐video recordings of the assessment. The intra‐observer reliability was calculated with the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), two‐way mixed with absolute agreement and a 95% confidence interval (CI). This demonstrated a very high ICC of 0.99 [CI 0.92–0.99] for PCC and 0.99 [CI 0.97–0.99] for the number of established consonants. The inter‐observer reliability was calculated with the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), two‐way random with absolute agreement. The ICC was high for PCC 0.98 [CI 0.79–0.99] and for the number of established consonants 0.96 [CI 0.74–0.99].

Statistical Analysis

The percentage of consonants correct (PCC) is presented at a group level. The number of established consonants and the number of established fricatives are presented in a descriptive manner. Individual data are presented for the children with HI and compared to the group mean score for the children with NH. As data were non‐parametric, the Mann‐Whitney U‐test was utilised to compare group mean scores for the HI and NH groups on all variables.

RESULTS

At 24 months, the children with HI presented with a mean PCC score of M = 45.95% (range 29.48%–57.27%) and the children with NH M = 62.71% (range 41.43%–87.69%), respectively. The difference between mean scores was statistically significant (Z = −2.612, p = 0.009). At 30 months of age, the mean PPC score of the children with HI was 59.72% (range 51.26%–83.59%) and children with NH 78.17% (range 47.73%–79.56%). The difference in mean PCC scores at 30 months between the groups was not statistically significant (Z = −1.763, p = 0.078). PCC scores of both groups at both ages are presented in Figure 1.

Discussion

Results Discussion

The aim of this study was to compare the early expressive phonological skills in children with HI and a group of children with NH using the PEEPS‐SE. Based on previous research (Persson et al. 2022; Lohmander et al. 2011; Moeller et al. 2007; Persson et al. 2022), the hypothesis was that the children with HI would score lower on the outcome variables at both 24 and 30 months of age, in comparison to the control group.

This study examined the PCC, number of different established consonants, and number of different fricatives produced. In terms of PCC, the difference between the groups were statistically significant at 24 months but not at 30 months. The closed gap between groups by 30 months indicates that children with HI have the possibility to catch up, despite a slower start. The slower development may be due a lower amount of exposure for children with HI. This lower exposure may include quantity as well as quality of spoken language stimulation due to limitations in hearing and variations in hours of hearing aid use (Walker et al. 2015).

While differences in PCC between children with and without HI can be expected to have diminished by 30 months of age, the results indicate that the size of the consonant inventory could be a significant indicator of language deviation. The number of different established consonants was significantly lower for children with HI than for children with NH at both ages. This finding is consistent with previous literature concerning the consonant inventories of children with HI (Persson et al. 2022; Lohmander et al. 2011). The results of the children with HI were significantly higher at 30 months than at 24 months of age, but still significantly lower than the NH group's results at 30 months of age. The significant difference at both ages may indicate that the number of consonants may be a more sensitive measure to demonstrate consonant proficiency as compared to the PCC. However, the results should be interpreted with caution due to the small groups and the large variation. Similarly, PCC‐values at 30–36 months have been shown to vary between 1 and 4 standard deviations below norms in different groups with CP ± L and/or OME‐related HI (Scherer et al. 2012; Raud Westberg et al. 2019). However, even though the PCC‐value in these studies was related to hearing sensitivity in both the group with and without CP ± L, the mean value for the latter was in agreement with peers (Lohmander et al. 2021).

Thus, children with HI may catch up a low PCC‐value, whereas the number of established consonants seems to be a clinically appropriate measure of phonological ability in toddlers and young children with HI. With that in mind, it is interesting to notice that children with HI in the current study did not produce significantly fewer fricatives than children with NH. These findings are not in line with previous studies (Persson et al. 2022). However, the previous studies were conducted more than 10 years ago, and children with HI were not fitted with hearing aids before six months of age. Advancements in hearing aid technology and early intervention services (e.g., guiding parents on how to stimulate early language development) may also have contributed to this result. The result may also be due to the variation in the number of established fricatives in both the HI and NH groups at 24 and 30 months of age. It is not unusual for children at these ages not to have acquired fricatives. Less than 50% of 102 three‐year‐olds assessed with SVANTE had the two sibilant fricatives /s/ and /ɕ/ established, whereas the fricative /f/ was established in 90% (Lohmander et al. 2017). This effect may be due to fricatives, and particularly sibilants, which are generally acquired somewhat later and are the phonemes most often affected by substitutions or distortions (Lohmander et al. 2017; Nettelbladt and Salameh 2007). This may indicate that fricatives are not a clinically sensitive or appropriate measure of language abnormalities in the early preschool age, as they may not be expected to be established in children with NH at either 24 or 30 months of age.

Method Discussion

The outcomes of this study may have been impacted by methodological issues. Since children's early expressive phonology is typically unstable, productions can be difficult to categorise with respect to isolated segments and on other phonological levels, such as in consonant clusters and syllables. Even though the manual prescribed a maximum of five listenings of each of the child's produced words during the analysis, it could be difficult to make categorisations, for example, to decide whether an atypical pronunciation of a segment should be classified as the target phoneme or not. Some phonemes may be distorted but still perceived as the target phoneme. A target phoneme may also be omitted or substituted by another phoneme. If a verb is expressed in another tense than in the test, that may lead to representation of a syllable or word form that was not intended as the target. Although perceptual judgements always carry a risk for misinterpretations, the inter‐rater reliability was high (0.96–0.98 for the different measures) in the current study.

The selection of words for PEEPS‐SE was based on age of acquisition and phonological complexity, analogous to the word selection for original PEEPS (Stoel‐Gammon and Williams 2013; Marklund et al. 2018). For PEEPS‐SE, Swedish vocabulary data from the SPRINT project was used to identify appropriate words 21]. The composition of words in the PEEPS‐SE has proven to function well, although some children during testing produced very few words. This type of missing data raised the question regarding a cut‐off for inclusion in the normative data. After discussions with the authors of original PEEPS, this cut‐off was set to 50%, which means that norm data is based on results from children producing at a minimum of 50% of the words in the test. Sixty‐seven per cent of the 24‐month‐olds and 90% of the 30‐month‐olds managed to produce 50% or more of the test words (Marklund et al. 2018). For future test development, updated word lists according to semantics should be considered, which also implies that SECDI might be in need of a re‐evaluation.

Strengths and Limitations

The PEEPS‐SE was found to be an age‐appropriate assessment to use with young children. The test material and setting are clinically feasible, and the methodology takes several aspects of young children's developmental needs into consideration (e.g., testing takes place on a mat in the corner of a smaller room with few visual distractors, and the test administrator has prepared boxes and bags to sort, present and get rid of toys for a fluent, yet flexible assessment procedure).

The main limitation of the current study is the small number of participants. To expand the amount of normative data to justify the prognostic reliability of PEEPS‐SE, future studies using this test are critical.

The original American version of PEEPS includes a screening version and has been used with children as young as 18 months. This is an aim for the Swedish version. Since one‐third of the children at 24 months did not produce enough words of the PEEPS‐SE wordlist to be included in the analysis, the development of a screener is indicated.

Conclusion and Clinical Implications

The results from this study demonstrate that PEEPS‐SE seems to be a thorough and developmentally appropriate method for assessing the phonological skills of toddlers. It can allow for early intervention for phonological disorders in children with HI and other clinical groups. The small number of participants calls for future studies to include larger samples and to investigate the feasibility of using the PEEPS‐SE with children from 18 months of age.
