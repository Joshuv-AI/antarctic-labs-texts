Deep learning technique for plant disease classification and pest detection and model explainability elevating agricultural sustainability
Authors: Wasswa Shafik, Ali Tufail, Liyanage Chandratilak De Silva, Rosyzie Anna Awg Haji Mohd Apong, Ki-Hyung Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The rapid advancement of technologies such as artificial intelligence (AI), deep learning, and precision agriculture tools is driving the development of efficient, data-driven crop management solutions. These innovations are increasingly critical in modern agriculture, where early and accurate detection of plant diseases plays a vital role in securing crop yields and sustainability. Agronomists, agriculturists, and local farmers continue to face significant economic losses due to delayed diagnosis or misclassification of diseases affecting high-value crops, key contributors to the global market. Failure to identify and manage such diseases in time can severely impact both agricultural productivity and global food supply chains. To achieve the United Nations’ sustainable development goals of zero hunger, climate change, good health, and well-being, early and timely disease detection is critical to ensure increased apple-related production, damage control, and reduced application of inappropriate herbicides that pollute the environment. Despite the availability of various methods for early disease detection and classification, how early signs of green attacks can be identified remains uncertain. Using the Turkey Plant Pests and Diseases (TPPD) dataset with 4,447 images categorized into 15 diverse classes, this research implements ResNet-9 to detect and classify the commonly known pests and diseases of six plants, including Malus pumila, Prunus armeniaca, Prunus padus, Prunus persica L. Batsch., Pyrus communis L., and Juglans regia. A laborious hyperparameter tuning, hyperparameter optimization, and augmentation procedure on the training set was done for some imbalanced dataset classes. Testing results of the proposed model demonstrated accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score values of 97.4%, 96.4%, 97.09%, And 95.7%, respectively, which is a significant leap in comparison to other existing research. This study further elucidates and enhances the interpretability of the proposed model by making saliency maps available using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) that efficiently illustrate the rationale behind the model’s prediction capabilities. The study further tested the statistical significance of the model, the Area Under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC), and the confidence interval (CI). Critical observations revealed that the model uses several visual cues for disease detection and classification, including (i) edge contours and shape structures that help define lesion boundaries, (ii) texture and color variations that signal symptom type and severity, and (iii) high-activation regions that indicate areas of strong feature relevance. These cues collectively guide the model in distinguishing between visually similar disease patterns across different plant parts. The application of SHAP saliency maps further enabled interpretation by visually localizing and quantifying the influence of these features on the model’s predictions.

Introduction

The United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs1) address several global issues, particularly SDG 2: Zero Hunger2, SDG 12: Responsible Consumption and Production3, And SDG 13: Climate Action4. Developing an artificial intelligence5 (AI)-based vision system for early And accurate detection of apple plant diseases using real-world data, the research directly supports SDG 2.3, which aims to double the agricultural productivity and incomes of small-scale food producers. Furthermore, the use of explainable AI6 (XAI) enhances decision-making for sustainable crop management, contributing to SDG 12.2 by promoting more resource-efficient agricultural practices And reducing unnecessary pesticide usage. The deployment of the system under uncontrolled, real-field conditions fosters resilience in agricultural systems, aligning with SDG 13.1, which emphasizes strengthening adaptive capacity to climate-related hazards. Unlike prior work limited to controlled datasets, this study ensures real-world applicability, making it a pragmatic and analytically grounded contribution to global sustainability objectives [1].

The Rosaceae family is a botanically, economically significant, diverse flowering plant that includes several ornamental plants and fruit-bearing crops that are incredibly edible. However, plants encounter a multitude of diseases, mainly fifteen, that pose a threat to both their health and productivity. If these diseases are not detected in a timely manner, they result in substantial reductions in output [2]. Plant disease’s impact on the environment, mostly in developing nations, is less well-known And recognized. These plant diseases greatly affect crop output, global food production, And security. However, projections indicate that by the year 2030, there is a necessity for a 70% increase in food production in relation to SDG 27, specifically aimed at achieving “Zero Hunger” and ensuring comprehensive food security. The international community can Help achieve SDG 2 by investing in plant disease detection and classification research and innovation [3]. New technologies such as deep learning8 (DL) are being used to detect and treat plant illnesses, Helping to sustain agriculture And reduce hunger globally. SDG 15, “Life on Land9” stresses safeguarding ecological and earthbound communities such as orchards and woodlands. These conditions can damage ecological communities, minimizing biodiversity, the environment, and financial advantages for locals [4]. Enduring plant condition discovery methods assist in the early discovery And control of plant conditions, sustaining SDG 15.

The standard methods for plant symptom diagnosis in plants include difficult and lengthy treatments consisting of aesthetic examination and laboratory assessment carried out by plant scientists, and the use of analysis tools in the field. To properly take on these concerns and improve the top quality of plant farming, the unification of modern technology into farming techniques has become an important demand [5]. For example, some hyperspectral remote sensing approaches have made it feasible to keep track of disease intensity, anxiety discovery, spatial resolution, and ecological influence. DL, an essential element of AI, has become an extremely considerable source in the field of plant illness recognition because of the increased information schedule. Because of sustainable farming advancement and AI, the ingenious DL design method uses semantic networks to examine large collections of plant images methodically. This enables rapid and accurate detection of diseases impacting apple plants [6]. By effectively employing DL algorithms, it becomes possible to train models to differentiate between the health and disease of apple leaves, fruits, or even the entire tree.

Due to promising results in agriculture and large data applications, transfer learning10 (TL) based research is on the rise. For example, in a study considering 54,000 images of fourteen plant diseases And twenty-six disease classes, a 99.35% accuracy was achieved when a deep convolutional neural network11 (DCNN) was applied. However, several web-centered applications have not overlooked end-user devices (like a smartphone a farmer can use to solve the problem of identifying the apple leaf or fruit disease), which have been identified as affecting end-user application performance. Another study illustrated An accuracy of over 90% in a parallel study with 7,000 images of 25 plant clusters [4]. The research showed that using the model for real-time plant disease identification without discussing the scale complexity of the model made the implementation of end-user devices more impractical. These applications can be categorized into two main groups: first, stress detection, where a particular crop is imposed on the stress evident during on-plant growth by abiotic or biotic influences, and these influences can be either external or internal. One of these is water stress, which causes a change in the color of the leaf and reduces the amount of available water. This results in the stomata shutting, which impedes both photosynthesis and transpiration, leading to pest stress; also, plants are affected by three principal stresses, including pests, water, and nutrient deficiency.

Recent studies comparing convolutional neural network12 (CNN) models such as ResNet50, Inception version 4, Xception, DenseNet121, EfficientNet version 2_m, and Visual Geometry Group13 (VGG) 13 have demonstrated the superior performance of EfficientNet version 2_m, achieving 100% accuracy and F1 score in detecting diseases in apple leaves [7]. However, despite these impressive results, Limitations such as small dataset size And the lack of environmental variability suggest that further validation under real-world agricultural conditions is necessary for robust And scalable deployment. A study fine-tuned 14 CNN And 17 vision transformer models to classify grape leaf diseases And grape varieties using the PlantVillage And Grapevine datasets, achieving up to 100% accuracy with models like Swinv2-Base [8]. While the results highlight the promise of DL in precision agriculture14, further validation on larger and more diverse real-world datasets is needed to ensure robustness and generalizability. This study evaluated DL models for tomato leaf disease detection, highlighting the superior performance of Res2Next50 and Res2Net50d, which achieved accuracy rates of 99.85% And 99.78%, respectively. While these results demonstrate strong potential for automated disease diagnosis in agriculture, the models were tested on curated datasets, and further validation under diverse, real-world conditions is needed to ensure robustness and generalizability in practical field applications [9]. In Another study, the author applied EfficientNet And Inception version 4 models for sugarcane leaf disease detection, with EfficientNet-b6 achieving the highest accuracy of 93.39% on an 11-class dataset, but the validation in real-world conditions did not confirm practical effectiveness [10].

Research motivation

The exponential advancement of DL architectures, particularly in CNNs and transformer-based vision models, has redefined the landscape of automated visual recognition. These innovations offer unprecedented capabilities in modeling complex, high-dimensional image data, making them ideal for tasks requiring fine-grained classification and real-time decision support. In agricultural domains, such technologies hold transformative potential for addressing longstanding challenges in plant disease detection, where traditional methods are constrained by subjectivity, limited scalability, and delayed intervention. This study is technologically motivated by the need to leverage computationally efficient yet powerful models, such as ResNet variants and EfficientNet architectures, coupled with XAI frameworks such as SHAP, to deliver interpretable and reliable disease diagnostics. Applied to plant pathology, the approach enhances early detection and classification of multiple disease types with high precision. Throughout this research, it is demonstrated to support the United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goals, specifically SDG 215, 916, 1217, 1318, And 1519, the research explicitly links technological innovation to real-world outcomes: promoting sustainable food systems, optimizing resource use, and enhancing agricultural resilience. In this way, the work not only advances algorithmic performance but also demonstrates tangible societal and environmental impact.

Problem statement

Timely and accurate identification of plant diseases and pests remains a critical challenge in modern agriculture, directly influencing crop yield, economic stability, and global food security. Traditional diagnostic methods, whether visual inspections by experts or basic imaging systems, are often hindered by subjectivity, inconsistent accuracy, and limited scalability, especially in large-scale farming contexts. Existing automated approaches have shown promise, but they frequently struggle with (i) distinguishing between visually similar disease symptoms across species, (ii) handling class imbalance in datasets, and (iii) providing interpretable predictions that agronomists and farmers can trust. DL models, while powerful, often operate as “black boxes,” limiting adoption in high-stakes agricultural decision-making where understanding why a prediction is made is as important as its accuracy. Furthermore, many current studies focus narrowly on performance metrics without robust statistical validation or explainability mechanisms, making real-world deployment risky. Addressing these limitations requires an integrated approach that combines computationally efficient DL architectures with XAI techniques, enabling not only high-precision classification but also transparent model reasoning. This is particularly urgent in the context of sustainable agriculture and the UN SDGs, where early intervention can minimize chemical usage, reduce environmental impact, and enhance resilience in crop production systems.

Explicit contribution to the sustainable development goals

To summarize the research And its contribution to SDGs, the proposed DL-based vision system contributes concretely to SDG 2: Zero Hunger by enabling early And accurate detection of apple crop diseases, which are a major cause of yield loss globally. By achieving a classification accuracy of 97.4% on real-field images, the model facilitates timely intervention, thereby reducing crop losses caused by late or incorrect diagnosis. This supports SDG Target 2.3, which calls for doubling the productivity of small-scale food producers, particularly in developing regions. The interpretability layer, provided through SHAP20 saliency maps, empowers agronomists And local farmers with actionable insights, fostering data-informed agricultural decisions. Additionally, the ability to distinguish between diseases with high precision contributes to SDG 13: Climate Action by minimizing the excessive use of chemical pesticides. Traditional disease control often relies on broad-spectrum treatments due to uncertain diagnoses, leading to environmental degradation And increased greenhouse gas emissions through agrochemical production And application. By offering class-specific visual feedback, the proposed model promotes targeted treatment, which can significantly reduce unnecessary pesticide applications, thereby supporting SDG Target 13.1, strengthening resilience and adaptive capacity in agriculture under changing climate conditions.

The utilization of these emerging technologies facilitates the rapid detection of illnesses, specifically those that impact apple plants. This process is crucial in preventing agricultural yield losses, mitigating the dissemination of infections, and fostering the adoption of sustainable farming methods. The synergy between timely disease detection and the comprehensibility of models enhances crop preservation and the acceptance of cutting-edge agricultural technologies to achieve sustainable and efficient farming practices. The original ResNet architectures vary in terms of depth, starting with the shallowest ResNet-18, 34, 50, 101, and deepest ResNet-125. Other proposed ResNet versions include Wide ResNet, which usually uses a wider set of filters. ResNet launched a “cardinality” parameter that regulates the number of parallel paths inside a block. Pre-activated ResNet customizes the initial ResNet design, applying activation and batch normalization prior to the convolution procedure, which assists in alleviating the gradient issue [35]. ResNet Version 2 includes modifications like the use of pre-activation blocks and changing the shortcut connections. Residual Networks with Nested Skip Connections pioneered nested skip connections in representational capacity enhancement. ResNet with Stochastic Depth integrates a stochastic depth training method where an arbitrary part of the layers is missed throughout training [36]. An expansion of the ResNet style that integrates the ideas of depthwise separable convolutions and ResNet to accomplish enhanced efficiency and computational effectiveness.

Nonetheless, the proposed ResNet-9 model tackles a variety of concerns, such as reducing the gradient problem and allowing the gradient to flow easily throughout backpropagation. The addition of recurring links (residual connections) assists in the training of much deeper networks. The model further mitigates the degradation problem using identification mappings discovered, stopping the deterioration of precision by boosting depth, and advances enhanced feature reuse using skip connections within the network. When it comes to reduced computational speed, the architecture’s reliable training qualities enable faster merging throughout the training procedure. ResNet-9 streamlines learning due to the residual blocks, making it simpler for the model to discover and adjust to detailed information patterns without becoming bewildered by the intricacy of the depiction, and model explainability has not been presented in the summarized studies.

Data processing

Within this section, we present all data processing steps that were done before model implementation, including data splitting, augmentation, and image resizing.

Data splitting

The dataset, consisting of 4,447 images fixed with leaves, stems, and fruits, was divided into three distinct sets using custom functions: a training set, which was used to train the deep learning model; a validation set, which was used to validate the deep learning model; and a testing set, which was used to test the deep learning model. This approach was used to ensure that there were no data leakages; details on how images were split are presented in Table 4.

Dataset augmentation

Some classes within the used dataset had considerably low image counts in comparison to other classes. To intensify those dataset class sizes, which will yield benefits in the later modeling phase, data augmentation techniques are utilized on the images contained in the training set. These techniques are executed through the utilization of MATLAB22 Transform class. These consist of center cropping, the application of Gaussian blur, and the introduction of random rotations. The center crop technique involves the application of a cropping operation to an image, specifically targeting its central region. The dimensions of this central crop are controlled by the parameter provided to the center crop function. The size is selected randomly from a predetermined List of alternatives, namely 180, 200, And 240. To illustrate this, when the size chosen is 180, it indicates that An initial image with dimensions of 300× 300 would be enlarged to emphasize the central portion, yielding a revised image size of 180 × 180. The original dataset consisted of 4,447 labeled images across 15 distinct classes of fruit-related diseases And symptoms, with 3,084 allocated for training, 619 for validation, And 744 for testing. To enhance model generalization And improve robustness, data augmentation techniques, such as random rotation, scaling, horizontal flipping, And contrast adjustments, were applied exclusively to the training set. Following augmentation, the number of training images increased from 3,084 to 9,252, representing a 3x augmentation factor, while the validation (619) and testing (744) sets were preserved without augmentation to maintain evaluation integrity. Thus, the total number of images used in the experiment was 10,615, comprising 9,252 training, 619 validation, And 744 testing samples. This augmentation strategy was crucial in addressing class imbalance and improving the model’s ability to generalize across varying image conditions.

Resizing of dataset images

To ensure compatibility with the ResNet deep learning model, all input images must conform to a fixed size of 256 × 256 pixels, as required by the network’s architecture. However, the original dataset consisted of images sized 300 × 300 pixels, necessitating a preprocessing step to resize all images accordingly. This was accomplished using MATLAB’s “imresize” function23, which is designed to alter image dimensions while maintaining visual fidelity efficiently. A custom MATLAB script was developed to automate the resizing of the entire dataset. The script traverses the dataset directory, reads each image file using “imread”, resizes it to 256 × 256 using “imresize,” and saves the modified image to a new output folder using “imwrite”. Importantly, the class structure of the dataset (for instance, folder names representing different categories) was preserved, ensuring that the resized dataset remained properly organized for supervised learning tasks. This preprocessing step was critical not only for satisfying the input size requirement of ResNet but also for promoting uniformity and efficiency in the model training process. Without consistent image dimensions, the model would encounter input shape mismatches, resulting in errors during training or degraded performance.

Model architecture and label mapping

The proposed model is based on a ResNet architecture tailored for multi-class classification. Given that the TPPD dataset comprises 15 distinct classes representing various fruit diseases And symptoms, the final classification layer of the model was modified to output a vector of 15 probability scores using a fully connected layer followed by a softmax activation function. Each neuron in this output layer corresponds directly to one of the class labels, ensuring strict consistency between the dataset annotations and the model’s structural design. During preprocessing, categorical labels were transformed into one-hot encoded vectors, And all classification predictions were computed from this 15-dimensional output. We confirm that the number of class labels and the output dimensionality of the model are fully aligned, and the reported classification results are derived using this verified architecture.

Proposed model and the explainability

This section details the proposed model and approaches used to provide insights into the model results presented.

Proposed network architecture

MATLAB tools were utilized in this research experiment to implement the ResNet-9 model, and the training process was carried out with NVIDIA GPUs24. The architectural design of the proposed ResNet-9, depicted in Fig. 2, the model consists of a classifier, two residual blocks, and four convolutional blocks, each composed of two residual layers, resulting in a total of nine layers. Hence, the name “ResNet-9” is coined based on the cumulative count of layers present in the proposed model. Convolutional Block 1 functions as the initial layer, tasked with receiving input data, specifically the image of the apple fruit, leaf, or stem in this context. The data is subsequently transmitted to the hidden layer, which includes Convolutional Layer 2 up to Linear Layer within the Classifier. The ultimate layer within the proposed model is the output layer, which is implemented as a Softmax layer. The output layer of the model generates class probabilities, and the predicted class for the input image is determined by choosing the class with the highest possibility.

Operations of the proposed model

The input images have dimensions represented as (3, 256, 256), where “3” signifies the number of color channels, which typically correspond to the colors red, green, and blue (RGB) commonly found in colored images. The “256” and “256” values represent the Height And width of the image. Thus, each input is composed of three RGB color channels And possesses a resolution of 256 pixels in width And 256 pixels in height. When the data goes across the different layers of the ResNet-9, the outcomes undergo significant form alterations. The process described is an ultimate characteristic of neural networks, in which each layer carries out procedures on the input, leading to changes to the information’s depiction as it passes through the network. These makeovers often include procedures like convolutions, pooling, and non-linear activations, which have the potential to customize the dimensionality of the data. The network is particularly designed to attain knowledge and gradually extract many more theoretical and essential attributes. At the same time, it goes through input handling, ultimately allowing it to create specific projections or classifications.

Within the ResNet-9 design, the first phase entails the Convolutional Block 1. The following is a delineation of the sequential operations within this specific block in two dimensions (2D). The input image undergoes a 2D convolutional layer procedure. The procedure is liable for the removal of numerous features from the input, consisting of, but not limited to, color, shape, texture, and edges; the procedure requires the application of convolution bits to the input image to recognize and highlight these attributes. The batch normalization layer is responsible for stabilizing the result of the convolutional layer. This is an extensively utilized strategy in artificial intelligence that intends to stabilize or systematize the inputs stemming from the layers before their transmission to the succeeding layer. This approach adds to improving the reliability and efficiency of the network and rate of training by minimizing the impacts of inner covariate change, henceforth promoting faster merging and boosting the general efficiency of the version.

The last step of Convolutional Block 1 is the application of the Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activation function. The ReLU activation function is a non-linear function that serves to incorporate non-linearity into the model. The presence of non-linearity plays a vital role in enhancing the data’s suitability for acquiring intricate patterns And relationships. Within the hidden layer, the image performs a series of essential actions, including 2D convolutional layer, batch normalization, ReLU activation, and MaxPool2d Operation (in, max pooling, via a kernel size of 4, trims the spatial dimensions (width and height) of the input image. This focuses on the most critical features in the image). The same structure is maintained for convolutional block 3 And convolutional block 4. As the data navigates within this layer, it undergoes a reduction in spatial dimensions, as demonstrated in Table 6 . In the final residual block of the hidden layer, known as Residual Block 2.1, the channel count is increased to 512, while the Height And width dimensions are decreased to 4.

The last 2D max pooling operation conducted in the hidden layer results in a further reduction of both the height and breadth dimensions to a single unit each. Subsequently, a layer referred to as “flatten” is used to reconfigure input tensors into a one-dimensional structure, hence facilitating the subsequent application of the SoftMax function. The SoftMax layer, situated in the output layer, applies a scaling operation on input tensors, restricting their values to the range of [0, 1] and ensuring that the sum of all tensors is equal to one.

The softmax activation function is widely employed as an activation function in neural networks, specifically in problems involving multi-class classification. The process involves converting the initial output scores (logits) generated by a neural network into a probability distribution that encompasses many classes. It then calculates the probability of classifying the input image into one of the three distinct classes using four primary processes.

Raw scores

Prior to the application of the softmax function, the neural network produces unprocessed scores or logits for every class. Optimization of hyperparameter

The training process of the proposed ResNet-9 model encompassed meticulous utilization of many crucial hyperparameters, for instance, the selection of the optimizer, determination of the learning rate, weight decay, momentum, gradient clipping, and the specification of the number of training epochs. The hyperparameters have a significant impact on both the training process and the overall performance of the proposed model.

The search for the most efficient combination of hyperparameter values was expedited using Optuna, a deep learning package. The achievement was realized by the utilization of a sampler that is based on the Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE) algorithm, which is a Bayesian optimization technique. Additionally, the sampler was enhanced by the incorporation of An integrated pruning mechanism. The utilization of the pruning method effectively reduced time consumption throughout the optimization process by terminating trials that exhibited unfavorable outcomes. In the context of hyperparameter optimization, we conducted a series of 100 trials to develop and implement an objective function. This scheduler dynamically modified the learning rate throughout the training process based on the number of epochs, hence improving the efficiency and effectiveness of the training.

The scheduler that has been implemented guarantees a systematic decrease in the values of the learning rate for the subsequent epochs of the training process [4]. MATLAB implementation details

In this study, SHAP-based interpretability was integrated into the ResNet-9 model workflow using MATLAB’s Deep Learning Toolbox. Given the lack of direct SHAP support in MATLAB, a custom implementation was developed that approximates SHAP values through a gradient-based approach compatible with deep neural networks. The model was first trained using the “trainNetwork” function on preprocessed input images resized to 256 × 256. For SHAP value estimation, we constructed a surrogate method combining “dlgradient” for sensitivity analysis and a reference background dataset composed of healthy apple leaves to approximate the expected model behavior in the absence of specific disease features. Layer-wise gradients were computed to trace feature attributions from the output back to the input layer, essentially capturing how variations in pixel intensities influenced the final class prediction. The resulting SHAP values were visualized as saliency maps using “imshowpair” and “heatmap,” overlaid on the original input images for enhanced interpretability. These maps provided pixel-level visual explanations, enabling domain experts to identify which regions (for instance, spots, edges, discolored patches) the model relied on to make classification decisions.

Quantitative validation of interpretability

To validate the reliability of the DeepSHAP saliency maps, a quantitative assessment was conducted by comparing model-explained regions against expert-labeled or visually identifiable symptomatic features. The evaluation employed two primary metrics: Intersection over Union (IoU) and Pearson correlation. IoU was computed by binarizing the saliency maps using a fixed threshold and calculating the overlap with annotated ground-truth disease regions, implemented via logical operations in MATLAB. For correlation analysis, SHAP intensity matrices were compared with expert-provided lesion heatmaps using the “corr2” function, yielding similarity scores that quantify the alignment between the model’s attention And biologically significant features. Across the 15 disease classes, the average IoU exceeded 85%, while the mean Pearson correlation coefficient surpassed 0.90, indicating a high degree of alignment between model interpretation and domain knowledge. This quantitative validation substantiates the claim that SHAP-based visualizations not only enhance model transparency but also serve as a trustworthy diagnostic aid in precision agriculture.

SHAP interpretation methodology and visual feature analysis

To interpret the SHAP saliency maps, we followed a systematic visual inspection approach, wherein each pixel’s contribution to a class prediction is quantified and overlaid onto the original image. SHAP values represent the magnitude and direction of influence that a particular image region (or feature) exerts on the classification outcome. Red regions indicate positive contribution (for instance, features that support the predicted class), while blue regions reflect negative contribution (for example, features that push the prediction away from that class). Each saliency overlay was examined in relation to the corresponding original image to identify biologically or visually meaningful features, such as:

(i)Lesion spots, rot zones, or aphid clusters (as blob-like patterns or edge-broken textures),(ii)Curved leaf tips or stem branches (highlighted in edge-like regions),(iii)Color transitions, wilted patches, or gall structures (captured via shaded areas).

Notably, while Grad-CAM and LIME both offer valuable visualization capabilities, their limitations in stability, quantitative precision, or architectural flexibility reduce their applicability in high-stakes agricultural contexts. SHAP, by contrast, provides consistent, model-agnostic, and fine-grained attributions that capture both supportive and opposing feature influences. This level of interpretability is critical for building agronomist trust, validating predictions against expert knowledge, and supporting data-driven, sustainable crop management decisions.

Deployment feasibility and edge device considerations

While this study demonstrates the high classification accuracy and explainability of the proposed ResNet-9 model using SHAP, the practical deployment on edge devices such as mobile phones, drones, or IoT-based field sensors remains underexplored. Real-world applications in resource-constrained environments require models to not only be accurate but also computationally efficient in terms of latency, throughput (frames per second), and memory usage. Although ResNet-9 is a lightweight architecture compared to deeper networks such as EfficientNet-B6, a comparative evaluation of inference time and memory footprint on representative edge platforms (for instance, NVIDIA Jetson Nano, Raspberry Pi, or ARM Cortex-based systems) is essential. Future work should benchmark ResNet-9 against models like EfficientNet (V1/V2) to quantify trade-offs between performance and resource efficiency. Metrics such as model size (MB), inference latency (ms), and energy consumption per inference (J/frame) would offer a deeper understanding of model suitability for on-device deployment in precision agriculture applications.

Limitations and future directions

While the proposed ResNet-9-based model demonstrated high classification accuracy and interpretability using SHAP, it is not without limitations. First, the model’s performance may be constrained by class imbalance in the dataset, particularly for underrepresented disease categories, which can affect generalization. Additionally, although the model performed well on the TPPD dataset captured in uncontrolled environments, it may still be sensitive to extreme variations in lighting, occlusion, or background clutter, which are common in field conditions. Another limitation is that the current framework focuses solely on image-based features, without incorporating temporal, environmental, or multispectral data that could further enhance prediction robustness. Future work will explore domain adaptation and transfer learning to improve performance across diverse crop types and agro-climatic zones. Incorporating temporal monitoring using video data, multimodal data fusion (for instance, weather or soil data), and active learning techniques to optimize labeling for rare disease instances are also promising directions. Finally, expanding the explainability framework beyond SHAP, such as integrating Grad-CAM or attention-based insights, could enrich the interpretive capacity for real-time, in-field applications.

Conclusion

This study presents a technically robust and interpretable deep learning framework for the early detection and classification of plant diseases, addressing a critical challenge in precision agriculture and sustainable crop management. Leveraging a fine-tuned ResNet-9 architecture, trained on a curated dataset of 4,447 leaf images spanning 15 disease categories, the proposed model achieved state-of-the-art performance across all key evaluation metrics, accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score, demonstrating its diagnostic reliability in complex, multi-class classification tasks. Importantly, the incorporation of SHAP-based saliency maps enhanced the model’s transparency by identifying spatially relevant features that influenced each decision, thereby contributing to the interpretability and trustworthiness of AI-driven diagnostics. The research underscores the utility of lightweight CNN architectures for scalable deployment in real-world agricultural environments where computational resources may be constrained. Future work will explore real-time deployment pipelines, as well as the integration of hyperspectral or multispectral imaging data to analyze early phenotypic and spectral deviations between healthy and diseased leaves. These advancements are essential for enabling adaptive, data-informed crop interventions and advancing the global agenda for sustainable food systems and climate-resilient agriculture.
