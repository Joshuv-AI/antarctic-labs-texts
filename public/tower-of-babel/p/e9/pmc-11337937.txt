Placement of 2 cages using navigation-guided biportal endoscopic transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion: illustrative case
Authors: Siravich Suvithayasiri, Young-Jin Kim, Yanting Liu, Changik Lee, Ashwinkumar Vasant Khandge, Jin-Sung Kim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDIn recent developments, full endoscopic and unilateral biportal endoscopic (UBE) spine surgery have emerged to aid the transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (TLIF) procedure. Yet, both approaches present a challenge due to limited space for cage insertion, potentially leading to complications such as cage subsidence or nonfusion in long-term assessments. Utilizing double cages may mitigate these concerns. This paper presents a unique case in which a patient successfully underwent computed tomography (CT) navigation–guided UBE-TLIF with 2 converging cages, highlighting the potential benefits and feasibility of this innovative approach.OBSERVATIONSA 59-year-old female diagnosed with degenerative spondylolisthesis at the L4–5 level underwent a UBE-TLIF. The operation is detailed step by step and supported by illustrative figures and surgical videos. Postsurgery results revealed a significant improvement in the patient’s condition, with the visual analog scale score decreasing from 7 to 3 on the first day, leading to a satisfaction rate of 90% at the last follow-up.LESSONSUtilizing endoscopic visualization complemented by contrast medium has substantially elevated the quality of disc preparation. From their observations, the authors affirm that the integration of intraoperative CT navigation systems significantly augments safety and pinpoint accuracy in UBE-TLIF procedures. The strategy of employing 2 converging cages through a unilateral technique stands as a practical solution, potentially optimizing the fusion outcomes of UBE-TLIF surgery. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE23512

Endoscopic spine surgery (ESS) has a long history dating back several decades and is considered one of the significant advancements in minimally invasive spinal surgery (MISS).1, 2 It has proven its safety and efficacy for treating various spine pathologies and fits nicely into the MISS armamentarium.3–9 There are 2 main techniques in the use of ESS: the full endoscopic (FE), or uniportal, approach and the unilateral biportal endoscopic (UBE) technique. They were recently developed to assist the transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (TLIF) procedure, and both approaches (FE-TLIF or UBE-TLIF) have been reported.10 However, both methods usually have a small corridor for cage insertion, which could negatively impact the size of the inserted cage. Theoretically, cage subsidence or nonfusion rate incidence could be increased in the long-term follow-up.11 To overcome this, as reported in previous studies, double cages may decrease those cage-related complications.12 Herein, we describe a patient who underwent a computed tomography (CT) navigation–guided UBE-TLIF using 2 cages placed in a convergent fashion via a one-sided approach. Our objective was to report the efficacy and feasibility of our novel technique. The CARE guidelines were followed.13

Illustrative Case

A 59-year-old female with underlying osteoporosis, which had been treated with a bone antiresorptive agent for more than 1 year, presented with persistent, worsening low-back pain and intermittent claudication radiating to both legs, predominantly on the right side, despite conservative treatments. Her pain was rated at 2 for the back and 7 for the leg on the visual analog scale (VAS). Diagnostic tests, including radiography and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), confirmed grade I degenerative spondylolisthesis at the L4–5 level, with instability revealed by flexion and extension studies, disc bulging causing right lateral recess and central canal stenosis, and widening of the L4–5 facet joints (Fig. 1). Given her condition, she underwent a right-sided UBE-TLIF with unilateral laminectomy for bilateral decompression (ULBD) at the L4–5 level, guided by an intraoperative CT navigation system (O-arm system, Medtronic Sofamor Danek). Surgical Technique

Operating Room Setup

The O-arm and endoscopic systems were used simultaneously throughout the procedure (Video 1). Therefore, the room had to be spacious enough to accommodate all the monitors and devices. The operating table was situated at the center of the room, where all the surgeons were standing on the operating side of the patient. The O-arm and the endoscopy monitors were placed on the opposite side of the surgeon to facilitate visualization during the procedure (Fig. 1G).

Patient Positioning

Standard positioning of the patient was used as in the general spinal procedure. The patient was placed in the prone position with chest and abdominal rolls applied after general anesthesia. Soft padding should be used at both orbits and all bony prominences to prevent pressure-related or nerve compression injuries. Both shoulder girdles should be relaxed and not excessively abducted to avoid brachial plexus traction injuries. The knees should also be slightly flexed. Intraoperative intermittent pneumatic compression devices could also prevent deep vein thrombosis. The saline irrigation bag was placed approximately 50–80 mm above the patient’s level.

O-arm Navigation System Setup

The navigation reference was placed on the patient’s skin surface. Its placement was in accordance with the operating side and the surgeon’s dominant hand, as mentioned above. A surgical adhesive drape tightened the navigation reference and made it more stable. Intraoperative CT imaging was performed using the O-arm imaging system, and the navigation probe was registered. From this point, we could use the probe to navigate the operating site in all 3 anatomical planes (Fig. 2). FIG. 2.A: The navigation probe was used to register and plan the incision for the UBE. Incision Planning and Application of the Biportal Endoscope

Our first goal was to insert the cages before the percutaneous screws. The advantage of initially placing the cages is that there is no concern regarding the leakage of irrigation fluid via the nearby incisions of those screws, and the maneuverability of the biportal instruments will not be impeded by the already inserted screws. From the surgeon’s perspective, the left portal (or caudal portal in this patient) was used as an endoscopic portal, whereas the right portal was used as a working one. The portals should be around 2.5–3.0 cm apart from each other. Navigation probes were used to target the right L4–5 facet joint, and skin incisions were made deep into the muscular fascia layer. Sequential soft tissue dilators were placed. Dilator guides from both incisions could be used to palpate each other. This could facilitate the device triangulation maneuver and create the working space above the corresponding facet joint. During the operation, saline irrigation flow had to maintain proper flow at all times. This could be enhanced by opening up the fascia and the skin incision more.

Ipsilateral Facet Joint Removal

After clearing the soft tissue and identifying the facet joint, a high-speed burr was used to remove the facet joint, starting from the medial to the lateral side, as well as from the more superficial inferior articular process of L4 and deep down to the superior articular process of L5. Small osteotomes could also be used to enhance the speed of the bone removal process. Then, the edge of the ligamentum flavum could be identified. The navigation system could identify and recheck the correct anatomical position throughout the procedure.

Bilateral Spinal Canal Decompression

Following identification of the interlaminar space and lamina, a high-speed burr was used to remove the borders of the corresponding level’s lamina until the flavum’s insertion edge was clearly visible. The flavum was then detached from the epidural space using a dissector or small Kerrison rongeur. To continue with the ULBD procedure, additional bone removal at the spinous process base and contralateral lamina was necessary. Upon identifying the contralateral facet joint, decompression was performed as required. Once the flavum was detached from the contralateral lamina and facet joint, it was removed en bloc, exposing the epidural space and dural sac. The endoscope and navigation system then allowed us to verify the adequacy of decompression (Fig. 3). Discectomy and Endplate Preparation

Following comprehensive spinal canal decompression and facet joint removal, the ipsilateral Kambin’s triangle14 is typically identified. After the coagulation of obstructing epidural vessels via a radiofrequency probe, annulus identification and access were possible. The navigation system reaffirmed the anatomical location and disc space trajectory, leading to the execution of an annulotomy procedure and a subsequent total discectomy. The disc was then removed using a variety of blunt disc shavers and ring curettes. The navigation probe was employed to verify the contralateral annulus position. For enhanced endplate preparation, direct endoscope visualization of the endplate surface and the application of radiopaque contrast medium are suggested. The latter is achieved by temporarily halting saline irrigation and infusing contrast into the intervertebral disc space. Any detected filling defects on the endplate surface in intraoperative C-arm fluoroscopic images indicate inadequate preparation and suggest the risk of pseudarthrosis (Fig. 4). Cage Insertion and Bone Grafting

A series of cage trials were performed. The first titanium cage (a height of 10 mm was selected for our patient) was packed with an autogenous bone graft. It was inserted using intraoperative C-arm guidance. Then, using a curette and a small hammer, the first cage was repositioned so that its proximal part lay toward the medial side. Therefore, the ipsilateral side was again available for inserting the next cage. Another titanium cage packed with grafts was inserted in the same fashion. As a result, the 2 cages aligned nicely in a convergent fashion (Fig. 5). After the intraoperative C-arm images confirmed the cage positioning, the rest of the bone graft was packed into the space between the cages. Percutaneous Screw Fixation and Wound Closure

Percutaneous screw fixation was performed in a standard fashion at the L4 and L5 vertebral bodies. The incision for the screw can be extended from the existing endoscopic wound or separated, according to surgeon preference. Because this patient also had an underlying problem of poor bone quality, bone cement augmentation was used for all the pedicle screws. The rod was applied percutaneously, and the screw-rod system could be compressed to enhance the stability of the cage as appropriate. A small drain (diameters of 0.8–1.0 mm) was placed in the cage-inserted wound deeply adjacent to the spinal canal and could be removed 1 or 2 days after the surgery if there were no related complications. Skin closure was done in a simple fashion.

Results

The total operative time and estimated blood loss were 220 minutes and 100 mL, respectively. After the surgery, the patient’s back and leg pain were promptly relieved compared to before the surgery. The VAS score decreased to 3 out of 10 points on postoperative day 3, and she could be discharged. Immediate postoperative radiographs indicated that L4 was reduced and that the L4–5 intervertebral disc height was restored (Fig. 5). Three months postsurgery, the patient reported less back pain with no leg pain (VAS score 1) and could walk for more than 30 minutes without rest. A satisfaction rate of 70% was achieved. At the latest follow-up, 6 months postsurgery, she had continued to perform routine activities without back or leg pain (VAS score 0). A follow-up CT scan of her lumbar spine showed successful fusion at the L4–5 intervertebral space without cage subsidence. The overall sagittal alignment on the standing radiograph was still maintained (Fig. 5). Her latest satisfaction rate was increased to 90%.

Discussion

Observations

Several studies have already proven the safety and efficacy of ESS in the TLIF procedure, as it can achieve comparable or better results than the traditional microscopic or tubular minimally invasive TLIF (MI-TLIF).10, 15, 16 Its potential advantages include minimal tissue injury and better visualization, which can facilitate better endplate preparation, as shown in our case. Lin et al. reported the preliminary results of a pilot study of FE-TLIF.17 The patient’s symptoms improved without reported cage subsidence. The authors concluded that this technique is safe and could improve visualization during discectomy and endplate preparation. As a result, this could enhance bony fusion. A recent meta-analysis by Heo et al. included 13 articles integrating outcomes from all endoscope-assisted TLIFs.10 They found that the preoperative Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) and VAS score were significantly improved after all the procedures and concluded that all these ESS techniques yielded favorable outcomes.

Two main ESS techniques used are FE-TLIF and UBE-TLIF. Even though both are considered ultra minimalistic and provide optimal visualization, some surgeons may believe that the FE procedure has the disadvantage of safety concerns, as it may not properly visualize the surrounding neural structures during the cage insertion steps.18–20 Others may consider the UBE procedure to involve much more collateral damage to the surrounding structures than the FE technique.21 Therefore, the matter is still largely under debate. Kim et al. compared the outcomes between FE-TLIF and MI-TLIF across 55 fusion levels performed.15 Interestingly, they found that the FE-TLIF cohort had better clinical outcomes, a better fusion rate, and a lower subsidence rate than the MI-TLIF group. Similar outcomes were also acquired from a UBE-TLIF study. Kang et al. reviewed the difference in outcomes in 79 patients who had undergone UBE-TLIF and MI-TLIF. They found that both techniques provided similar favorable clinical statuses and fusion rate outcomes.16

Despite the results from previous studies, limitations do exist for all endoscope-assisted TLIF procedures. Many of the studies are considered low-level evidence. Moreover, this technique may have a longer learning curve to overcome, more radiation exposure, and a limited working space to operate.11 The latter may cause cage positioning to be more difficult, limit the cage size that can be used, and ultimately negatively impact the overall fusion rate.11 Furthermore, because of its posterior-based approach, smaller cages are typically used, which may lead to a minimal loss of lumbar lordosis compared to other lateral or anterior-based MI fusion techniques. This makes it less suitable for patients requiring significant lumbar lordosis restoration or sagittal imbalance correction. Therefore, while endoscopic TLIF could be an effective alternative to tubular-based MI-TLIF, it may not be feasible in patients with significant sagittal imbalance or loss of lordosis. Interestingly, compared to the MI-TLIF procedure, much soft tissue destruction and facet joint removal were mandatory to facilitate cage insertion in both MI-TLIF and all endoscopic approaches. Therefore, the ability to preserve the surrounding structures and achieve significant outcome differences is still under debate. Nevertheless, more high-quality evidence would be needed to confirm the actual value and ability of ESS in achieving fusion goals.

Regarding the types of cages used in endoscope-assisted fusion, other options, such as the banana-type cage, are available but come with additional concerns. While the larger banana-type cage could potentially enhance the overall fusion rate, it may require an extended incision for insertion. Additionally, manipulating this type of cage under endoscopic visualization demands advanced skills, presenting a significant learning curve. Consequently, we believe that using 2 small cages, as described in our technique, is easier and more reproducible. The recent availability of expandable cages (both vertically and laterally) in endoscope-assisted fusion procedures offers benefits, as they allow for the positioning of larger cages despite the smaller incision. This could potentially enhance the overall fusion rate and address concerns about loss of lordosis. However, since this technique is relatively new and has limited evidence in the literature, it remains much under debate.

Intraoperative CT-based navigation has often been used with ESS. Its notable advantages are the real-time orientation of intraoperative anatomy, improvement in the accuracy of pedicle screw placement, and reduction in radiation exposure. Chen et al. reported a technical note using O-arm navigation during the interlaminar contralateral endoscopic lumbar foraminotomy.22 It showed the potential for safety and efficiency in treating lumbar foraminal stenosis. Therefore, they concluded that this could enhance the surgeon’s learning curve, lessen the risk of facet joint violation, and reduce overall radiation exposure. Another study by Quillo-Olvera et al. reported the outcomes of 7 patients who had undergone UBE-TLIF under O-arm navigation guidance.23 They found that the navigation system provided safety and accuracy during the operation. The mean length of stay for all patients was 1 day, and all the ODI and VAS scores for back and leg pain significantly improved after the surgery. Compared to our novel modified technique, our dual-cage approach has demonstrated its feasibility, which is a substantial progression in the context of the navigation-guided UBE-TLIF operation. This method offers potential benefits over the traditional small single-cage insertion reported in a previous study and underscores the ongoing evolution of surgical practices for enhanced patient outcomes.

The cage-related complication (e.g., cage migration, cage subsidence, or pseudarthrosis) is one of the significant concerns when performing the TLIF procedure. It can negatively impact the overall fusion rate, and the number of cages is considered a main contributing risk factor.24–26 Using intraoperative fluoroscopic guidance and contrast medium to assist with the disc preparation, as demonstrated in our case, can help to improve the fusion rate.27 Moreover, using a small single cage usually results in less construct stability than using 2 cages.12 Lee et al. reported a technical note using a unilateral approach for 2-cage insertion during the FE-TLIF procedure. Their cages were aligned parallel to each other in their final position.28 This was modified in our case, as we repositioned the first cage and laid its proximal part toward the medial side. Therefore, the final positioning of both cages was aligned in a convergent fashion. This could share the load to the endplate more equally and lessen the cage subsidence or migration rate.29 A study by Pao recently demonstrated promising clinical and radiographic results in using double cage insertion in UBE-TLIF surgeries.30 His 1-year postoperative follow-up using CT scans yielded a great fusion success rate of 93.3%, with the incidence of significant cage subsidence (more than 2 mm) limited to only 5% of the cases. However, in contrast to Pao’s technique, which requires a customized cannulated dural anchor for oblique cage insertion, our method avoids the need for specialized equipment, making it more accessible and reproducible. By adjusting the tightness of the initial cage to facilitate lateral repositioning, we allow for the second cage insertion with minimal medial retraction of the dural sac, thereby theoretically reducing the risk of nerve root injury. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that our approach’s difference in cage height can pose a risk for the smaller cage to migrate and can subsequently affect the overall fusion rate. Further long-term studies are essential to thoroughly evaluate the validity and effectiveness of both techniques.

Although not encountered in our report, incidental durotomy with cerebrospinal fluid leakage is a potential complication during the procedure. Various management techniques are employed, tailored to the size of the tear. For small to medium-sized tears (less than 5 to 10 mm), effective management ranges from postoperative observation with a brief period of bed rest to the application of a collagen matrix inlay technique or mechanical compression using gel foam and sealants, often resulting in excellent outcomes without further complications.31 However, for large tears (more than 10 mm), primary closure via endoscopy or conversion to open repair is recommended, especially for complex tears that are associated with fair to poor outcomes.32 Based on the size of the tear, this approach is crucial in optimizing patient outcomes and reducing the risk of further complications.

Lessons

This technique may present certain limitations due to the lack of percutaneous screw distraction before cage insertion, potentially restricting cage size. Even with prior percutaneous pedicle screws and distraction to enlarge the intervertebral disc space, it remains challenging to place optimal-sized cages in patients with severely collapsed discs. Similar to the traditional tubular-assisted MI-TLIF technique, the procedure becomes more challenging in patients with higher grades of spondylolisthesis, necessitating careful patient selection. Another point is that outcomes from our report are derived from a single patient with a brief follow-up period. A comparative analysis between a control group and the various techniques employed in endoscope-assisted TLIF—such as FE or UBE approaches, the use of contrast medium, the types of cages used, and the cage positioning technique—could augment our understanding and refine the optimal procedure. Cost is another critical factor to consider when optimizing options for patients undergoing fusion. Conducting a cost-effectiveness analysis comparing the use of 2 cages with the use of 1 cage or other MI techniques is essential to better describe the impact of clinical benefits and the effectiveness of our approach. Such analyses would also aim to maximize the potential benefits of endoscope-assisted TLIF and minimize fusion-related complications, thereby providing further insight into the continual optimization of this surgical approach.

Our innovative approach to navigation-assisted UBE-TLIF, which incorporates a dual cage application, is both feasible and can be readily implemented. This technique presents a viable alternative for patients requiring lumbar interbody fusion procedures, offering the potential for promising outcomes. The utilization of an intraoperative CT navigation system could facilitate enhanced anatomical structure identification for surgeons, thereby accelerating the learning process. Additionally, this method may reduce the incidence of facet joint violation and diminish radiation exposure, further enhancing its clinical utility and overall patient safety.
