Attitude and Involvement Regarding Sexual and Reproductive Health Among University Students: A Cross‐Sectional Study in Bangladesh
Authors: Ishita Akhter, Quazi Maksudur Rahman, Md Jamil Hossain, Tareq Mahmud, ATM Foysal Rabbi, Shahnaz Parvin, Lubaina Khanom, Mamun Ur Rashid Pranta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsSexual and reproductive health (SRH) is a crucial aspect of overall health and well‐being, especially among young adults. However, in many low‐ and middle‐income countries, including Bangladesh, SRH issues remain underexplored due to sociocultural taboos, stigma, and inadequate education. This lack of attention can adversely affect both sexual practices and reproductive health in the future. This study aimed to assess the attitudes and practices regarding SRH issues and their associated sociodemographic factors among university students in Bangladesh.MethodsA cross‐sectional study was conducted among 422 undergraduate students at a public university in Bangladesh using convenience sampling. Data were collected using a semi‐structured questionnaire that assessed sociodemographic characteristics, attitudes, and practices related to SRH. Moreover, descriptive statistics, chi‐square tests, and logistic regression analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.ResultsApproximately half of the participants demonstrated negative attitudes and poor SRH practices. Factors such as maternal education, family income, residential location, and family structure were significantly associated with SRH attitudes and practices (p < 0.05). Students from urban areas, nuclear families, and higher socioeconomic backgrounds were more likely to report positive attitudes and practices. Male participants were significantly less likely to engage in SRH practices compared to females (AOR = 0.31, 95% CI: 0.18–0.52, p < 0.001). Participants aged less than 22 years had significantly lower odds of a positive attitude toward SRH (AOR = 0.45, 95% CI: 0.24–0.85, p = 0.013).ConclusionSociodemographic disparities significantly influence attitudes and practices toward SRH issues among university students in Bangladesh. Younger male students were less likely to practice good SRH. These findings highlight the urgent need for inclusive, gender‐sensitive, and culturally appropriate SRH education programs in academic settings to promote informed decision‐making and healthier behaviors among young adults.Keywords: Attitudes, Bangladesh, practices, sexual and reproductive health, SRH, university students

Introduction

Sexual and Reproductive Health (SRH) is defined as a wide range of services, including birth control, care for mothers and babies, care for infertility and fertility, treatment and prevention of STIs, protection from sexual and gender‐based violence, and education on healthy relationships [1]. SRH is a vital component of overall health and well‐being. The Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) emphasize universal access to SRH services and recognize it as a human right [2]. Moreover, access to comprehensive sexual and reproductive health services is essential for advancing universal health coverage (UHC) [3]. Adolescence is a critical period for developing sexual and reproductive behaviors [4], as young people undergo biological and psychological changes that enable them for sexual maturity [5, 6]. Without accurate knowledge, positive attitudes, and access to SRH services can lead to adverse health outcomes and risky behaviors [7]. Studies found that a lack of understanding about reproductive health negatively impacts their health [8].

Globally, adolescents remain one of the most vulnerable groups to SRH issues [9]. Each year, an estimated 340 million new cases of sexually transmitted infections (STIs) are reported, with nearly one‐third affecting young people aged 15–24 [10]. Adolescent pregnancy is another major public health concern, with over 10 million unintended pregnancies annually among adolescents aged 15–19 in developing countries [11]. Beyond health consequences, poor SRH contributes to unsafe abortions and early parenthood, which can disrupt education, reduce employment opportunities, and perpetuate cycles of poverty and social disadvantage [12, 13].

In Bangladesh, SRH remains a culturally sensitive and often stigmatized topic, particularly among adolescents and young adults. Limited access to accurate SRH information and services increases adolescents’ vulnerability to SRH‐related health problems [14]. Moreover, parents feel discomfort discussing these topics, while educational institutions also do not provide sufficient information [15]. As a result, young people often rely on peers, mass media, or other informal sources, many of which may offer inaccurate or misleading information, adversely shaping their attitudes and behaviors [16, 17]. Additionally, rapid urbanization, global exposure, and the growing influence of digital media are reshaping traditional values and creating conflicting messages about gender roles, relationships, and sexual behavior [12].

Despite some national efforts to address SRH issues, Bangladesh continues to face substantial challenges, including limited access to contraception, STI prevention, family planning, and safe abortion services [18]. Urban poor women experience disproportionately higher SRH risks than their rural counterparts due to overcrowding, high fertility rates, unintended pregnancies, and inadequate access to healthcare [19, 20, 21]. Additionally, adolescents living in slums, migrants, and internally displaced populations face substantial gaps in access to comprehensive SRH information and services [22]. Therefore, research focusing on adolescents’ attitudes and involvement regarding SRH is critical for informing comprehensive sexuality education and improving service delivery. Globally, the importance of advancing SRH is increasingly recognized for improving health outcomes, promoting gender equity, and empowering individuals, particularly women and girls, to make informed choices about their bodies and reproductive lives [2]. Moreover, SRH research is vital for combating gender inequality and advancing women's rights.

In Bangladesh, adolescents make up a significant portion of the population, with more than 32 million individuals [18], and these young adults have exhibited health risk behaviors [23]. The Government of Bangladesh has implemented several initiatives, such as the National Strategy for Adolescent Health (2017–2030) and the National Plan of Action for Adolescent Health Strategy (2017–2030) [18, 24]. However, these initiatives have not been sufficient to ensure comprehensive SRH education for all adults. Moreover, limited evidence exists on SRH knowledge, attitudes, and practices among university students, which is essential for designing timely and effective interventions. Therefore, this study aims to assess university students’ attitudes and practices regarding SRH issues in Bangladesh and identify key predictor factors. The findings are expected to inform the development of more effective, comprehensive SRH education programs and service strategies for the needs of young adults.

Methodology

Study Design and Setting

This study was characterized by a quantitative approach and a cross‐sectional design. This investigation was conducted with university students across different faculties and institutes at Jahangirnagar University in Bangladesh, spanning from September 2023 to April 2024. Male and female students from Jahangirnagar University's several faculties and institutes, as well as their willingness to participate in the study, met the inclusion requirements. Additionally, participants who failed to meet the established criteria were excluded.

Sampling and Sample Size

The study employed convenience sampling (which may limit the extent to which conclusions can be generalized because of potential sampling bias) to collect relevant data from participants. The study followed the Cochran formula, which determines an optimal sample size based on a specified level of precision, confidence, and the estimated proportion of the attribute in the population. Allowing for a 10% non‐response rate, the final targeted sample size was 422. To account for a 10% non‐response rate, the adjusted sample size was calculated as: 384 ÷ (1 − 0.10) = 426, rounded to 422.

Data Collection Tools and Methods

Following an extensive review of the pertinent literature, a structured questionnaire and a consent form were developed. The first page of the questionnaire contained a brief introduction to the study's background, objectives, eligibility criteria, and a declaration of confidentiality. The questionnaire included (i) sociodemographic details, (ii) attitudes on sexual and reproductive health, and (iii) level of involvement in SRH‐related issues. Additionally, Cronbach's alpha value of 0.81 was found for this questionnaire, indicating that it was used as a valid and reliable instrument for evaluating the study's objectives. A pilot study (ten interviews) was conducted before data collection to assess the clarity of the questionnaires for university students and to evaluate the effectiveness of the data collection methods in efficiently obtaining information without placing unreasonable expectations on participants. Following the completion of the pilot study, we revised the wording of certain questions to better align with the needs of the respondents. By ensuring that every participant understood the questionnaire and the study's objectives, overall credibility was enhanced. Following the pre‐testing phase, data were collected through face‐to‐face interviews (interviewer‐administered), during which the significance of the study was clarified, and consent was taken. Following the communication, it was noted that the objective data had been gathered in their native language. During the investigation, ethical standards were upheld to the utmost degree. The investigation was conducted in accordance with the Institutional Research Ethics and the Declaration of Helsinki or similar ethical guidelines.

Additionally, all participants reviewed the consent form and agreed to take part in the study. A total of 422 data points were included in the final analysis, in accordance with the established sample size estimation guidelines. Participants in the study were not provided with any incentives for their involvement. At every stage, data privacy and confidentiality were rigorously upheld.

Data Analysis

Data was meticulously cleaned, sorted, and coded using Microsoft Excel 2010. Descriptive statistics (e.g., frequency, percentage, mean, and standard deviation) and logistic regression analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 27. The odds ratio (OR), with a 95% confidence interval (CI) was used to convey the results of the regression analyses, and the adjusted odds ratio (AOR) was computed, taking into account all the study variables. When the p‐values were 0.05 or lower, the association between the variables was considered significant. This study followed the Statistical Analyses and Methods in the SAMPL guidelines in statistical procedures.

Ethical Statement

The study received approval from the Biosafety, Biosecurity, and Ethical Review Board of Jahangirnagar University, Savar, Dhaka‐1342, Bangladesh (Ref. No: BBEC, JU/M 2023/09 [60]). Informed consent was obtained from each participant before the interview began. All the participants were informed about the type and purpose of the study and the confidentiality of their data. They had the right to withdraw from the study at any time during the interview.

Result

SocioDemographic Information of the Participants

Out of 422 participants, the majority (42.2%) were aged between 22 and 23 years old. The mean age of the participants was 22.28 years (SD = 1.65). Among them, 55.2% were female, and 44.8% were male. A significant portion of respondents (84.1%) identified as Muslim. Additionally, 35.2% of the participants were enrolled in the social science faculty. Regarding their mothers’ education qualifications, 51.2% had completed a Secondary School Certificate (SSC) or below. The majority of participants (94.3%) were unmarried, and 37.9% reported a monthly income ranging from 20,000 to 35,000 BDT. Most participants (97.1%) came from nuclear families. In terms of ethnicity, 98.8% identified as Bengali. Furthermore, 45.3% of the participants were from rural areas, while 29.1% were from urban areas (Table 1).

Attitudes Regarding Sexual and Reproductive Health Issues

Table 4 demonstrates the binary logistic regression analysis examining factors associated with attitudes toward sexual and reproductive health (SRH) issues. Participants under 22 years had significantly lower odds of exhibiting a positive attitude toward SRH issues than those over 23 years old (AOR = 0.45, 95% CI: 0.24–0.85, p = 0.013). Males were significantly less likely than females to have a positive attitude toward SRH issues (AOR = 0.43, 95% CI: 0.26–0.71, p = 0.001). Participants with a monthly income of less than 20,000 BDT were significantly less likely to have a positive attitude than those with family income > 50,000 BDT (AOR = 0.44, 95% CI: 0.19–0.99, p = 0.048). Similarly, those with family incomes of 20,000–35,000 BDT had significantly lower odds of a positive attitude than those with income > 50,000 BDT (AOR = 0.43, 95% CI: 0.198–0.95, p = 0.037). Interestingly, students from nuclear families were significantly more likely to have a positive attitude toward SRH issues compared to those from combined families (AOR = 1.79, 95% CI: 1.05–3.1, p = 0.034). Overall, these findings underscore the multifaceted influences of age, gender, socioeconomic status, and family structure on university students’ attitudes toward SRH (Table 4).

Practices Regarding Sexual and Reproductive Health Issues

Table 5 demonstrates the binary logistic regression analysis identifying the factors influencing the practice level of sexual and reproductive health (SRH) issues. The study revealed that male participants were significantly less likely to engage in SRH‐related practices than females (AOR = 0.31, 95% CI: 0.18–0.52, p < 0.001). Participants whose mothers had SSC or below education were also less likely to engage in SRH practices than those whose mothers had an SSC or lower level of education (AOR = 0.51, 95% CI: 0.28–0.93, p = 0.028). Similarly, participants whose mothers had completed HSC education were significantly less likely to practice SRH behaviors than the reference group (AOR = 0.48, 95% CI: 0.25–0.94, p = 0.033). Furthermore, participants with a monthly family income of less than 20000 BDT were significantly less likely to practice SRH behaviors compared to those from families with an income of higher than 50000 BDT (AOR = 0.38, 95% CI: 0.16–0.86, p = 0.021). Finally, a residential setting was associated with SRH practices. Students living in rural areas were significantly less likely to engage in good SRH‐related behaviors than those in urban areas (AOR = 0.53, 95% CI: 0.30–0.92, p = 0.024). Overall, these results highlight that SRH practices among university students are influenced by a combination of gender, maternal education, family income, and place of residence (Table 5).

Discussion

The findings of our study highlighted attitudes and practices related to sexual and reproductive health among university students in Bangladesh. The investigation showed notable sociodemographic differences in attitudes towards SRH issues, especially in sex, maternal education, family income, family size, and residence. Similarly, SRH‐related practices were significantly associated with sex, field of study, maternal education, family income, and residence.

The study revealed that female students demonstrated both more positive attitudes and greater engagement in sexual and reproductive health (SRH) practices than their male counterparts. The regression model of this study demonstrated that being a female student was associated with a threefold higher likelihood of engaging in sexual and reproductive health (SRH) practices compared to male students. Similarly, females were twice as likely to report a positive attitude toward SRH matters, even after adjusting for other variables. A previous study conducted among Bangladeshi men reveals that they regard reproductive issues as exclusively women's concerns, indicating limited male involvement in SRH [25].

Studies in Bangladesh and Vietnam similarly report higher SRH literacy among females, suggesting that women are more exposed to reproductive health education and communication opportunities [26, 27]. Understanding SRH issues impacts the attitudes and practices of female students. However, male students may encounter social stigma and cultural expectations that discourage them from seeking SRH information or expressing interest in such topics. Institutional barriers, including the absence of male‐focused SRH education and limited access to confidential support services, may further reinforce these disparities. Therefore, the observed gender differences in both attitude and practice likely reflect broader social and structural dynamics, including gendered socialization, societal stigma, and unequal access to SRH resources.

The present investigation reveals that maternal education plays a significant role in fostering a positive attitude towards sexual and reproductive health issues. Students whose mothers have graduated tend to exhibit more positive attitudes and greater involvement in SRH practices compared to those whose mothers have secondary or lower education. Evidence from Bangladesh indicates that mothers serve as the primary source of such information for adolescent girls [28]. Females tend to depend more on their mothers for sexual and reproductive health information and often feel more comfortable discussing these matters with them due to gender similarity [29]. A study in India pointed out that educated mothers are open to discussing sexual and reproductive health issues with their children [30]. This openness and knowledge help foster positive attitudes and greater engagement in SRH practices.

Students from wealthy families are more likely to possess favorable attitudes toward sexual and reproductive health issues. The logistic regression analysis indicated that students with an income below 20,000 BDT exhibited significantly lower odds of maintaining a positive attitude than those from higher‐income families. They are also significantly engaged in sexual and reproductive health practices. Research on Pakistani women indicates that those from lower socioeconomic strata exhibit lower awareness and attitudes on sexual and reproductive health issues, specifically contraception and family planning [31]. The present study revealed that the nuclear family is more likely to impact the attitude toward SRH issues than the combined family. This could be attributed to the societal values surrounding extended families discussing topics related to SRH with children, which in turn influences their attitudes towards these matters. Closer parent–child interactions or increased openness in communication within smaller family units may influence this positive attitude. In contrast, extended or joint families may have cultural or social norms that discourage discussion of SRH topics, which limit positive attitude formation. Interestingly, previous research indicates that Bangladeshi female university students from joint families sometimes demonstrate greater knowledge and understanding of SRH topics [32]. However, factors such as cultural differences in families, household size, and composition may shape attitudes differently, accounting for the observed contrasts.

The findings of our study indicate a notable association between students’ residence and their attitudes toward and practices regarding SRH issues. In this study, rural residents are almost twice as unlikely to possess a positive attitude towards sexual and reproductive health issues compared to their urban counterparts, which is consistent with earlier studies [28, 33, 34]. However, it is important to note that urban students are not uniformly better informed, and individual knowledge and engagement may vary depending on factors such as socioeconomic status, parental support, and access to education. A study reported that urban females in Bangladesh exhibit more involvement in sexual and reproductive health practices and a more positive attitude towards SRH issues compared to their rural counterparts [28]. Another study found that urban university students in Southeast Ethiopia have a better understanding of SRH issues than students from rural areas [33]. Furthermore, it has been indicated that urban Indian boys tend to exhibit a more favorable attitude towards sexual and reproductive health issues [34]. Rural females have limited access to SRH services, lack sex education, and face different societal and cultural norms [35, 36], which contribute to the attitudes toward and practices regarding sexual and reproductive health.

Students in the Biological Science faculty demonstrated higher engagement and more positive attitudes toward sexual and reproductive health (SRH) issues compared to students in other faculties. Although exposure to SRH‐related topics was not directly measured in this study, students in the Biological Science faculty are likely to encounter more SRH content through their curriculum, which may contribute to their higher levels of knowledge, attitudes, and engagement. Previous research supports our observation that students in science or health‐oriented faculties may have greater SRH knowledge or more positive attitudes. For example, in Serbia, medical students significantly outperformed non‐medical students on STI knowledge and sexual behavior indicators [37]. These findings suggest that sexual and reproductive health‐related content should be added to other faculties and subjects to increase attitudes and practices. Further research should be conducted in this regard to get a deeper understanding.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. First, the study used convenience sampling, which may introduce bias and limit its representativeness of the broader population. As a result, social desirability bias may have influenced responses, and non‐response bias may have had an impact on the findings. Third, the cross‐sectional design prevents the determination of causal relationships between factors and SRH attitudes or practices. In addition, this study did not examine other potentially influential factors, such as fathers’ education, peer influence, or exposure to information from teachers, which have been shown to affect SRH attitudes and practices. Their omission may limit the comprehensiveness of the findings. Finally, it is a single‐centered study with a limited sample size, which limits its generalizability. A mixed‐methods study with a large sample size will be more suitable for identifying the underlying cause because of this attitude and practice.

Conclusion

This study highlighted significant gaps in awareness and practices among university students. Approximately half of the participants demonstrated limited knowledge and lower engagement in SRH‐related behaviors. Attitudes and practices were significantly associated with sex, maternal education, family income, family structure, and place of residence. Female students, those from higher‐income households, urban residents, and participants with more educated mothers exhibited better SRH knowledge and more responsible behaviors.

These findings underscore the necessity for targeted and inclusive interventions, particularly for male students, individuals from rural areas, and those from socioeconomically disadvantaged backgrounds, to enhance SRH awareness and behaviors. Integrating comprehensive SRH education into university curriculum and offering confidential counseling services at university health centers can promote informed decision‐making and responsible SRH behaviors. The study's insights support national priorities under the National Strategy for Adolescent Health (2017–2030) and global commitments, including the SDG to ensure universal access to SRH services by 2030. Overall, the findings provide evidence to guide policymakers and academic institutions in developing gender‐sensitive, culturally appropriate SRH programs at the tertiary education level.
