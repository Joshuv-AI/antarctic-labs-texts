Circulating T-lymphocyte subsets as promising biomarkers for the identification of sepsis-induced acute kidney injury
Authors: Xue-Ling Guo, Cheng-Xiang Lu, Yan Luo, Ping-Ping Wang, Wen-Song Su, Si-Jiu Yang, Ling-Hui Zhan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:This retrospective study investigated whether disturbances in circulating T-lymphocyte subsets could predict the incidence of acute kidney injury (AKI) and in-hospital mortality in patients with sepsis.Methods:Clinical data from patients with sepsis admitted to the intensive care unit were reviewed. Logistic regression analyses were used to identify independent predictors of in-hospital mortality and the development of AKI.Results:Of 81 patients with sepsis, 50 developed AKI. Both nonsurvivors and patients with septic AKI exhibited higher Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) and Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE) II scores. Nonsurvivors exhibited more organ damage, with significantly lower levels of peripheral T-lymphocyte subsets, including total circulating lymphocytes, and CD3+, CD3+CD4+, and CD3+CD8+ T-lymphocytes. Patients with septic AKI exhibited fewer total peripheral lymphocytes and fewer CD3+, CD3+CD4+, and CD3+CD8+ T-lymphocytes, with higher serum lactate levels and lower nadir platelet counts. Independent predictors of 30-day hospital mortality included maximum SOFA and APACHE II scores, occurrence of encephalopathy, and peripheral CD3+ and CD3+CD8+ T-lymphocyte counts. Moreover, the maximum SOFA score and CD3+ and CD3+CD8+ T-lymphocyte counts demonstrated good predictive power for AKI in receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses, with an area under the ROC curve of 0.810 (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.712-0.908) for SOFA score, 0.849 (95% CI, 0.764-0.934) for CD3+ T-lymphocytes, and 0.856 (95% CI, 0.772-0.941) for CD3+CD8+ T-lymphocytes.Conclusion:Patients with sepsis-induced AKI experienced T lymphopenia and increased in-hospital mortality. Higher maximum SOFA scores and reduced peripheral CD3+ and CD3+CD8+ T-lymphocyte levels were associated with in-hospital mortality and the development of AKI in patients with sepsis.Keywords: Acute kidney injury, CD3+ T-lymphocyte, CD3+CD4+ T-lymphocyte, CD3+CD8+ T-lymphocyte, Sepsis

1. INTRODUCTION

Sepsis caused by uncontrolled systemic inflammation due to infection can lead to life-threatening multiorgan dysfunction.1 Despite advances in critical care management, sepsis remains a major cause of death worldwide. The kidney is one of the earliest organs to exhibit impairment in sepsis, and acute kidney injury (AKI) is a hallmark. More than 45% of patients with sepsis experience AKI in the intensive care unit (ICU), exhibit poor prognostic outcomes, and have an increased risk of developing chronic kidney disease (CKD) and cardiovascular disease(s).2,3 Possible molecular mechanisms involved in sepsis-induced AKI are complex and not completely understood, but include endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, exposure of renal cells to a surge of inflammatory markers, microcirculatory dysfunction, disruption of energy metabolism, and disordered peritubular capillary oxygen consumption.3–6 Recent studies have reported that sepsis-associated immunosuppression, characterized mainly by impaired lymphocyte function, can contribute to sepsis-induced AKI.7–9

Sepsis can trigger a complex, dysregulated host response involving both innate and adaptive immune responses. Sepsis-induced proinflammatory responses, characterized by intense cell activation and a “cytokine storm”, often result in a prolonged compensatory anti-inflammatory process. After the initial hyperinflammatory stage, patients gradually enter an immunosuppressive state, which is associated with increased secondary organ failure and acquired infections, thus exacerbating morbidity and mortality.10,11 Sepsis-triggered immunosuppression is characterized by both a disrupted innate immune response and an abnormal adaptive immune response, manifesting as diminished immune cell numbers and disturbances in cell functions, including macrophage inactivation, inhibited antigen presentation ability, decreased lymphocyte proliferation, suppression of proinflammatory factor release, and an increase in anti-inflammatory factor release.12,13 Cumulative evidence has reflected the immunosuppressive features of innate immune cells, including neutrophils, monocytes, macrophages, and natural killer cells.12,14,15 The number, phenotype, and functions of neutrophils are altered during sepsis.10 Loss of monocyte human leukocyte antigen-DR (mHLA-DR), a gold standard biomarker of altered innate immunity, reflects defects in monocyte functionality and is associated with increased mortality and secondary nosocomial infection rates in both adults and children with sepsis.11,16,17 With regard to adaptive immunity, T and B lymphocyte levels often diminish at the onset of sepsis but are less impacted and corrected faster than mHLA-DR alterations. Qualitative and functional alterations in adaptive cells significantly contribute to sepsis-related immune paralysis and subsequent poor outcomes.10,18,19

T lymphopenia, defined as extensive T-cell depletion that occurs either in immune organs or in blood circulation due to decreased lymphocyte proliferation or increased cell death, contributes to immune suppression, organ injury, and poor prognosis in patients with sepsis.13,17,18 T-lymphocytes are mainly divided into helper cluster differentiation (CD)3+CD4+ T-helper cells (Th) and killer CD3+CD8+ cells. After being released from the thymus, naive CD3+CD4+ T cells further differentiate into effector T cells and CD4+CD25+ regulatory T-lymphocytes (Tregs). Based on the distinct cytokines secreted, effector T cells can be divided into several subsets, including Th1, Th2, and Th17 cells. Th1 and Th17 cells produce proinflammatory mediators, whereas Th2 cells generate anti-inflammatory factors. Moreover, T cells can exhibit an immune memory phenotype.9,10,18 Based on the available evidence, the reduction in lymphocyte count during sepsis is mainly attributed to apoptosis, mediated by the upregulation of proapoptotic proteins and downregulation of antiapoptotic proteins.9,10,13 Different T cell subsets execute different functions that are regulated by multiple costimulatory signals. Lymphocyte activation is carefully balanced by positive and negative costimulatory molecules. The imbalanced expression of cosignaling molecules on lymphocytes, also known as immune checkpoint molecules, including the abnormal expression of CD28, CD86, programmed death-1 (PD-1), PD-ligand 1 (PD-L1), B- and T-lymphocyte attenuator (BTLA), CD27, OX40, and 4-1BB, is closely associated with immune malfunction and can strongly predict the prognosis of patients with sepsis.17,20–23

AKI is a common complication in patients with sepsis and is strongly associated with poor prognosis.3 As demonstrated in animal models, apoptosis of T-lymphocytes in the peripheral circulation is associated with the incidence of septic AKI.7,8 The early prediction of AKI in critically ill patients with sepsis would facilitate timely and effective interventions. Flow cytometric analysis of circulating T-lymphocyte subsets is available in routine clinical practice and can facilitate the assessment of patient immune status. Although previous data have revealed that sepsis-induced lymphopenia is associated with deleterious clinical outcomes, little is known about the possible association between AKI and changes in circulating T-lymphocyte subsets in patients with sepsis. As such, the main objective of this observational retrospective study was to investigate whether alterations in circulating T-lymphocyte subsets could predict septic AKI and in-hospital mortality in patients with sepsis.

2. METHODS

2.1. Study population

This observational retrospective study was conducted in the ICU of Zhongshan Hospital of Xiamen University, between January 2018 and July 2021. Patients >18 years of age with sepsis or septic shock, who fulfilled the criteria for the current definition from the Third International Consensus Definitions for Sepsis and Septic Shock (ie, “Sepsis-3”)1 were included in the study. Patients <18 years of age, those with an ICU stay <24 hours, repeated admission to the same department, patients without measurement data for T-lymphocyte subsets, pregnant or breastfeeding women, and patients with a history of chronic heart disease, pre-existing CKD, autoimmune disease, or oncohematological disease (eg, lymphoma, leukemia), and immunodeficiency syndrome (eg, HIV or AIDS) or malignant tumors, and those undergoing long-term corticosteroid therapy, immunosuppressant treatment, or transplantation, were excluded. All patients in the study were followed up for >30 days.

Based on current clinical and pathophysiological understanding, sepsis-associated AKI is defined as a clinical syndrome characterized by an abrupt deterioration of renal function in the presence of sepsis without other meaningful explanatory factors.4 Patients with sepsis, but without pre-existing kidney disease, were enrolled. AKI was defined according to criteria from the Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) classification based on an increase in serum creatinine (Scr) level and/or a decrease in urine output during the ICU stay (Supplementary Table 1, http://links.lww.com/JCMA/A287).24 The nadir Scr recorded between 1 year and 7 days previously was considered to be the baseline Scr value.25 This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Zhongshan Hospital of Xiamen University (Xiamen, Fujian, China). The present study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki, and all patient data were protected and maintained with confidentiality.

2.2. Clinical data collection

Clinical data were obtained from electronic medical records, including demographics (age and sex), Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) score, illness severity index, comorbidities, sources of infection, organism culture results, cumulative fluid balance in the first 24 hours, presence of organ damage, and primary outcomes (use of continuous renal replacement therapy [CRRT], length of ICU stay, total hospitalization days, and 30-mortality). The severity of illness was evaluated according to Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE) II (Supplementary Table 2, http://links.lww.com/JCMA/A287) and Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) scores (Supplementary Table 3, http://links.lww.com/JCMA/A287). Both APACHE II and SOFA scores within 24 hours of admission and the highest score during the ICU stay were collected. Laboratory data, including routine blood tests, lactate level at admission, maximum lactate level, kidney function parameters, serum N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) level at admission, highest NT-proBNP level, serum procalcitonin (PCT) level at admission, maximum PCT level, C-reactive protein (CRP) level at admission, highest CRP level, and peripheral lymphocyte subset examination within 72 hours of admission were recorded. Peripheral lymphocyte subset parameters included total peripheral lymphocytes and CD3+, CD3+CD4+, and CD3+CD8+ T-lymphocyte counts.

2.3. Statistical analysis

Normally distributed continuous variables are expressed as mean ± SD and were compared using the two-sample t test. Non-normally distributed measurement data are expressed as median (interquartile range [IQR]) and were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Enumeration data are expressed as number (percentage). Proportions were compared using the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Univariate analysis was used to calculate the unadjusted odds ratio (OR) and corresponding 95% CI. A collinearity diagnosis was performed to evaluate correlations among the significant variables before establishing the regression models. When the variance inflation factor cutoff point was <10 or tolerance >0.1, variables were considered to be potential candidates for multivariate logistic regression analyses.26 Binary logistic regression using stepwise selection was performed to identify independent risk factors for mortality and the incidence of septic AKI. Six adjusted models were generated to determine the predictors of mortality. Model 1 included age, leukocyte count at admission, minimum platelet count, maximum lactate level, septic encephalopathy, AKI, and total lymphocyte and CD3+ T-lymphocyte counts. Model 2 included age, leukocyte count on admission, minimum platelet count, maximum lactate level, septic encephalopathy, AKI, and CD3+CD4+ and CD3+CD8+ T cell counts. Model 3 included maximum SOFA score, leukocyte count at admission, maximum lactate level, total lymphocyte count, and CD3+ T cell count. Model 4 included the maximum SOFA score, leukocyte count at admission, maximum lactate level, and CD3+CD4+ and CD3+CD8+ T cell counts. Model 5 included maximum APACHE II score, leukocyte count at admission, maximum lactate level, and total and CD3+ T cell counts. Model 6 included maximum APACHE II score, leukocyte count at admission, maximum lactate level, and CD3+CD4+ and CD3+CD8+ T cell counts. Six additional adjusted models were created to determine predictors of septic AKI. Model 1 included age, septic shock occurrence, leukocyte count at admission, minimum platelet count, lactate level at admission, maximum lactate level, total lymphocyte count, and CD3+ T cell count. Model 2 included age, septic shock occurrence, leukocyte count at admission, minimum platelet count, lactate level at admission, maximum lactate level, CD3+CD4+ T cells, and CD3+CD8+ T cells. Model 3 included the maximum SOFA score, leukocyte count at admission, lactate level at admission, total lymphocyte count, and CD3+ T cell count. Model 4 included the maximum SOFA score, leukocyte count at admission, lactate level at admission, maximum lactate level, CD3+CD4+ T cell count, and CD3+CD8+ T cell count. Model 5 included the maximum APACHE II score, leukocyte count at admission, lactate level at admission, maximum lactate level, total lymphocyte count, and CD3+ T cell count. Model 6 included the maximum APACHE II score, leukocyte count at admission, lactate level at admission, maximum lactate level, CD3+CD4+ T cell count, and CD3+CD8+ T cell count. Finally, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and the area under the ROC curve (AUC) were used to analyze the predictive value of independent variables for the development of AKI during sepsis. SPSS version 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) was used for data analysis. Differences with p < 0.05 were considered to be statistically significant.

3. RESULTS

3.1. Study population and baseline characteristics

The 30-day mortality rate was 23.46%. Patients who died were older or experienced longer hospital stays than those who survived. There were no significant differences between survivors and nonsurvivors in terms of sex, CCI score, infection sources, causative pathogens, underlying diseases, or length of ICU stay. Regarding the disease severity index, nonsurvivors exhibited higher admission and peak APACHE II and SOFA scores than survivors. Accordingly, nonsurvivors exhibited more organ damage, including AKI, septic encephalopathy, coagulation system disturbance(s), and liver injury. The incidence of AKI in patients with sepsis was approximately 61.73%. CRRT was more common among nonsurvivors than among survivors (Supplementary Table 4, http://links.lww.com/JCMA/A287).

Patients with sepsis and AKI exhibited no differences in ICU hospitalization or length of hospital stay. Sex distribution, cumulative fluid balance, infection sources, and pathogen distribution were similar between the two groups. The sepsis-associated AKI group did not have a significantly different number of patients with bloodstream infection. However, admission and peak SOFA and APACHE II scores were significantly higher in patients with AKI. Patients with sepsis and AKI were older and more likely to undergo CRRT, with increased mortality. Accordingly, patients with AKI had a higher rate of septic shock and were more prone to encephalopathy and hematological system disturbances (Supplementary Table 5, http://links.lww.com/JCMA/A287).

3.2. Laboratory investigations

Laboratory data from survivors and nonsurvivors are summarized in Table 1. There were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of leukocyte counts, platelet counts, or lactate levels at the time of admission. Nonsurvivors exhibited significantly lower levels of peripheral blood lymphocytes examined during routine blood tests at admission. Similarly, compared with the survivor group, a significant decrease in nadir platelet and lymphocyte counts was observed in nonsurvivors. In addition, nonsurvivors exhibited higher peak lactate levels. Regarding inflammatory markers, CRP and PCT in both groups did not exhibit statistically significant differences. Regarding immune parameters, the numbers of total circulating lymphocytes, and CD3+, CD3+CD4+, and CD3+CD8+ T-lymphocytes were lower in nonsurvivors than in survivors. Moreover, nonsurvivors exhibited a significantly higher CD4+/CD8+ ratio.

3.3. Independent risk factors of mortality among patients with sepsis

Potential risk factors associated with increased mortality were evaluated. Univariate analysis revealed that age, SOFA score at admission, APACHE II score at admission, maximum SOFA score, maximum APACHE II score, minimum platelet level, lymphocyte count in routine blood at admission, minimum lymphocyte count in routine blood, maximum lactate level, septic encephalopathy, AKI, total lymphocytes, CD3+ T cells, CD3+CD4+ T cells, CD3+CD8+ T cells, and CD4+/CD8+ ratio were all associated with an enhanced mortality rate (p < 0.05, data not shown). To reveal the possible association between innate immunity (leukocytes) and sepsis outcomes, leukocytes at admission were also included. A multicollinearity test was then performed to determine the inter-influence among the potential variables. Due to severe collinearity, the SOFA and APACHE II scores at admission, routine blood lymphocyte count at admission, minimum routine blood lymphocyte count, and CD4+/CD8+ ratio were not included in the multivariable logistic regression. The final list of potential risk factors is presented in Table 3. After adjustment for age, leukocyte count at admission, minimum platelet level, maximum lactate level, septic encephalopathy, AKI, total lymphocyte count, and CD3+ T cell count in model 1, septic encephalopathy (OR, 11.256 [95% CI, 2.341-54.110]; p = 0.003) and CD3+ T cells (OR, 0.992 [95% CI, 0.987-0.998]; p = 0.004) were independently associated with in-hospital mortality. After adjustment for age, leukocyte count at admission, minimum platelet level, maximum lactate level, septic encephalopathy, AKI, CD3+CD4+ and CD3+CD8+ T cells in model 2, septic encephalopathy (OR, 11.362 [95% CI, 2.636-48.971]; p = 0.001), and CD3+CD8+ T cells (OR, 0.983 [95% CI, 0.971-0.994]; p = 0.004) were significantly associated with poor outcomes. After adjustment for model 3, maximum SOFA score (OR, 1.435 [95% CI, 1.160-1.776]; p = 0.001) and CD3+ T cells (OR, 0.993 [95% CI, 0.989-0.998]; p = 0.004) were independently associated with in-hospital mortality. After adjustment for model 4, maximum SOFA score (OR, 1.410 [95% CI, 1.142-1.740]; p = 0.001) and CD3+CD8+ T cells (OR, 0.983 [95% CI, 0.971-0.996]; p = 0.010) were independently associated with in-hospital mortality. After adjustment for model 5, maximum APACHE II score (OR, 1.140 [95% CI, 1.040-1.249]; p = 0.005) and CD3+ T cells (OR, 0.993 [95% CI, 0.988-0.998]; p = 0.002) were independently associated with in-hospital mortality. After adjustment for model 6, maximum APACHE II score (OR, 1.133 [95% CI, 1.032-1.244]; p = 0.009) and CD3+CD8+ T cells (OR, 0.984 [95% CI, 0.973-0.996]; p = 0.009) were independently associated with in-hospital mortality. Among the models, maximum SOFA and APACHE II score, septic encephalopathy, and CD3+ and CD3+CD8+ T cells were revealed to be important risk factors for in-hospital mortality.

3.4. Relationship between peripheral T-lymphocyte subset parameters and sepsis-induced AKI

Univariate analysis revealed that patients with sepsis who developed AKI exhibited more severe disease status and dramatically higher lactate levels, with an increased shock rate. In addition, decreased peripheral blood lymphocyte subset parameters and platelet counts were associated with the development of AKI. Similarly, a multicollinearity test was performed to assess inter-influence among the potential variables. Due to severe collinearity, SOFA and APACHE II scores at admission, routine blood lymphocyte counts at admission, minimum routine blood lymphocyte count, and CD4+/CD8+ ratio were not included in multivariable logistic regression (data not shown). The final enrollment of potential risk factors is presented in Table 4. After adjusting for age, shock occurrence, leukocyte count at admission, minimum platelet level, lactate level at admission, maximum lactate level, total lymphocyte count, and CD3+ T cell count in Model 1, CD3+ T cells (OR, 0.993 [95% CI, 0.990-0.996]; p < 0.001) were independently associated with the development of AKI in patients with sepsis. After adjusting for age, shock occurrence, leukocyte count at admission, minimum platelet level, lactate level at admission, maximum lactate level, and CD3+CD4+ and CD3+CD8+ T cells in model 2, CD3+CD8+ T cells (OR, 0.989 [95% CI, 0.984-0.994]; p < 0.001) were significantly associated with the development of AKI in patients with sepsis. After adjustment for model 3, the maximum SOFA score (OR, 1.300 [95% CI, 1.077-1.569]; p = 0.006) and CD3+ T cells (OR, 0.994 [95% CI, 0.992-0.997]; p < 0.001) were independently associated with the development of AKI. 4. DISCUSSION

In the present study, we investigated the potential role of circulating T-lymphocyte subsets in predicting mortality and the development of AKI in patients with sepsis. We found that total peripheral lymphocytes, and CD3+, CD3+CD4+, and CD3+CD8+ T-lymphocyte levels were markedly diminished in nonsurvivors and patients with sepsis who developed AKI. Using multivariate logistic regression analysis, we determined that maximum SOFA and APACHE II scores, incidence of septic encephalopathy, and CD3+ and CD3+CD8+ T-lymphocyte count were independently associated with increased mortality in patients with sepsis. Moreover, maximum SOFA score, and peripheral CD3+ and CD3+CD8+ T-lymphocyte counts were independent risk factors for septic AKI. Consistently, it has been previously demonstrated that lymphocyte subsets yield information about sepsis-induced acute organ dysfunction and sepsis prognosis.8,18,27,28

Sepsis is a systemic inflammatory response syndrome caused by infection. Host immunity plays a principal role in determining the recovery and outcomes of patients with sepsis. High absolute T cell counts, alongside diminished CD8+ T cell counts at ICU admission, were associated with significantly decreased mortality, independent of patient age. The rapid restoration of immune defects, as indicated by an increase in T-lymphocyte count, has been confirmed to be beneficial for the general outcomes of patients with sepsis.27 Recently, Polilli et al29 suggested that CD3+CD4+ T-lymphocyte count measured at hospital admission may be useful for identifying patients at a higher risk for in-hospital mortality. Consistent with previous studies, we found that nonsurvivors exhibited dramatically lower levels of T-lymphocyte subsets, including total CD3+, CD3+CD4+, and CD3+CD8+ T-lymphocytes. Furthermore, both circulating CD3+ and CD3+CD8+ T-lymphocyte counts were considered to be independent predictors of 30-day mortality. The mechanisms underlying the predictive value of diminished T-lymphocyte counts in sepsis and sepsis-associated multiple organ dysfunction are not fully understood. Previous evidence has shown that the imbalance of cosignaling molecules expressed on lymphocytes, such as elevated expression of PD-1 in Tregs, one of several T-cell subsets, could strongly indicate an immunosuppressive status.20,21 PD-1 on the surface of Tregs can bind to receptors on T-lymphocytes, transmit inhibitory signals, and subsequently inhibit T-cell proliferation.30 A possible mechanism is that defective T-cell proliferation and cytokine secretion impair the antimicrobial phagocytic or cytotoxic activity of immune cells. In patients in whom invading pathogens are not eradicated and those susceptible to opportunistic organisms, a sustained systemic inflammatory response would induce tissue damage and organ failure and, ultimately, mortality.18,31 In the present study, we also found that organ damage was more common in nonsurvivors with higher disease severity indices, including APACHE II and SOFA scores. Subsequent binary logistic regression analysis revealed that the maximum SOFA score was an important prognostic indicator. Consistent with previous findings, the SOFA score is a valuable tool for predicting the prognosis of patients with sepsis according to the Sepsis-3.0 criteria.21,32 In addition, a shift from Th2 to Th1 cytokine balance during the hypoinflammatory phase could decrease lymphocyte apoptosis, relieve the severity of organ damage and, ultimately, improve survival.31 Thus, these findings support the concept that the restoration of immune homeostasis provides promising targets for sepsis management and better prognosis.10

In the present study, we also found that organ damage involving the kidney, coagulation system, encephalopathy, and liver was more common in nonsurvivors who exhibited remarkably lower levels of T-lymphocyte subsets. AKI is one of the most common complications of sepsis. Patients with sepsis-associated AKI experience poor outcomes.3–5 We consistently found that patients with septic AKI experienced higher in-hospital mortality. However, to date, there are still no recognized biomarkers that can predict the occurrence of AKI in patients with sepsis and guide early interventions to reduce the exacerbation of renal function.33 Previous studies have focused on the relationship between disease severity, as assessed using SOFA and APACHE II scores, and organ impairment, including septic encephalopathy.32,34 Based on regression analysis, the maximum SOFA score was shown to be an important indicator for predicting septic AKI. Previous studies have highlighted the role of platelets as immune mediators and evaluated the relationship between platelet activation and multiple organ failure in sepsis.35,36 In the present study, we found that platelet counts dropped to a lower level in sepsis patients with AKI than in those without AKI. In addition, because of the role of coagulopathy in microvascular thrombosis and subsequent organ injury, coagulation biomarkers, including activated partial thromboplastin time, prothrombin time, and D-dimer levels on admission to the ICU, have been reported to be significant risk factors for sepsis-induced AKI.37 Similar to previous evidence, that immune imbalance significantly impacts the development of septic encephalopathy, and that the proportion of CD4+ T-lymphocytes represents a powerful predictor of septic encephalopathy in patients with severe sepsis,34 we found that CD3+ and CD3+CD8+ T cell counts were not only associated with in-hospital mortality but also to the incidence of AKI in patients with sepsis. These findings provide further evidence supporting the utility of CD3+ and CD3+CD8+ T-lymphocyte counts as good predictors of septic AKI.

However, the role of lymphopenia in sepsis-induced AKI kidney injury remains unclear. In sepsis, lymphocyte dysfunction is present not only in peripheral blood cells but also locally in target organs.18 During the immunosuppression phase, a large number of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), including high-mobility group box 1 protein (HMGB1), cell-free DNA (cfDNA), histones, and neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), released by extensive immune cell death, trigger endothelial damage and microcirculatory impairment and, thus, mediate the development of multiple organ failure in sepsis.10,38 In addition, disturbance of T-lymphocytes induces an abnormal release of immunoregulatory molecules, such as excessive inflammatory cytokines, leading to aggravation of tissue inflammatory injury.9,10 The anti-inflammatory agent curcumin can effectively alleviate tissue inflammatory injury in both the kidney and lungs by enhancing the suppressive activity of Tregs.8 The administration of glutamine can alleviate kidney impairment by achieving balanced T cell polarization and reducing T cell apoptosis.9 Immune checkpoint molecules expressed on lymphocytes and antigen-presenting cells, such as PD-1 and PD-L1, CD40 and CD40L, CD28, cytotoxic T-lymphocyte antigen 4 (CTLA-4), BTLA, and the T-cell immunoglobulin mucin (Tim) family, serve as the second signal to activate T cells and control immune responses.10 Recent in vitro studies have revealed that immunosuppression resulting from apoptosis in lymphocytes through PD-1/PD-L1 over-interaction could contribute to renal tubular epithelial cell damage and subsequent septic AKI. Treatment with anti-PD-L1 or blocking the lactate receptor could be a potentially beneficial therapy for septic AKI by restoring lymphocyte function.7 A similar study showed that T-lymphocytes can regulate renal function during sepsis via the CD28 pathway.39 Based on this evidence, it is reasonable to speculate that restoring the function of immune cells, particularly T and B lymphocytes, is a critical strategy for reducing sepsis-associated organ damage and improving patient outcomes.38

However, the present investigation had several limitations, the first of which was the inherent biases of a retrospective, single-center study design and limited number of patients. Nevertheless, this study yielded important positive results. Future studies should aim to validate these findings in larger, prospective, multicenter studies. Second, we limited the records of T-lymphocytes to those tested within the first 72 hours of ICU admission. Future studies should investigate whether alterations in peripheral T-lymphocyte subsets during different time points of ICU stay are associated with patient outcomes and the incidence of AKI. Finally, we did not investigate the underlying molecular mechanisms of T-lymphocyte subsets in the pathogenesis of septic AKI; however, our future study will explore the potential role of specific T-lymphocyte subsets in this disease process. Despite these limitations, we believe that our results are significant and should prompt further studies to verify our findings.

In conclusion, this study provides new insights into patients with septic AKI. Sepsis-induced immunosuppression, particularly T lymphopenia, was significantly associated with increased mortality in patients with sepsis and the incidence of septic AKI. Monitoring circulating T-lymphocyte subset alterations appears to be a potential biomarker for predicting not only the clinical outcome of patients with sepsis but also the incidence of septic AKI.
