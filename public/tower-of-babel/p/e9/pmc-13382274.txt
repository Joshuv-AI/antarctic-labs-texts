A visualizable mitochondrial-targeted Chlorambucil prodrug for enhancing anti-hepatoma efficacy
Authors: Liyuan Lin, Guojun Pan, Ying Zhi, Hanxiao Zhu, Rulin Zheng, Yiwei Li, Shan Gao, Yuhan Guo, Haoyan Zhang, Qingsheng Wang, Yuhan Li, Ziying Zhang, Mengjun Xue, Xinmiao Cui, Jianing Wang, Junrui Yang, Shuaiguang Li, Wei Xu, Liwei Shao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mitochondria play essential roles in tumor biology, influencing apoptosis, redox homeostasis, ion balance, and genetic stability; disruptions in these processes often dictate the balance between tumor progression and suppression. Consequently, mitochondria-targeted cancer therapy has emerged as a promising strategy for improving therapeutic selectivity. Chlorambucil (CLB), a bifunctional alkylating agent, is clinically limited by its lack of tumor specificity and substantial toxicity to normal tissues, including the risk of inducing secondary malignancies. To overcome these limitations, we designed a structurally derivatized CLB prodrug-BPA-CLB that incorporates fluorescence for visualization and a mitochondrial-targeting moiety to enhance intratumoral selectivity. It was constructed by modifying the 1,8-naphthalimide scaffold to improve delocalization and lipophilicity and subsequently conjugating it to CLB. This design leverages organelle-specific distribution and real-time imaging to enable precise drug delivery at the subcellular level. Using advanced confocal imaging and LC-MS analysis, we confirmed that BPA-CLB accumulates selectively in mitochondria. In vitro and in vivo evaluations demonstrated that BPA-CLB markedly reduces toxicity to normal cells while maintaining potent antitumor activity. Mechanistically, BPA-CLB disrupts mitochondrial morphology and function, increases reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, induces apoptosis, and suppresses tumor-cell migration. This process is related to the combination of VDAC2. In conclusion, BPA-CLB provides a visually traceable and mitochondria-specific delivery strategy that minimizes off-target interactions and enhances CLB's therapeutic selectivity. By precisely perturbing mitochondrial function, BPA-CLB significantly improves antitumor efficacy against hepatocellular carcinoma, highlighting its potential as a new class of subcellularly targeted chemotherapeutics.

Introduction

Chlorambucil (CLB) remains a clinically relevant alkylating agent used in chemotherapy [1,2]. Its mechanism of action involves the formation of DNA interstrand cross-links [3,4]. Thereby disrupting DNA replication and inhibiting tumor-cell proliferation [5]. Despite its therapeutic benefits, the clinical utility of CLB is severely limited by high systemic toxicity and poor tumor selectivity. Prolonged administration may lead to irreversible bone-marrow suppression [6,7], severe hematological toxicity, and even secondary malignancies [8]. These limitations underscore the urgent need for structural optimization of CLB to improve the targeted delivery and safety profile of this established chemotherapeutic.

Subcellular organelle-targeted drug delivery has recently emerged as a paradigm-shifting for enhancing therapeutic efficacy by concentrating their action at specific intracellular sites, thereby minimizing off-target toxicity [9,10]. In this context, mitochondria are particularly attractive targets. As the primary site of aerobic respiration [11], mitochondria not only generate energy (ATP) but also regulate membrane potential, apoptosis, biosynthetic pathways, and metabolic homeostasis [[12], [13], [14]]. Increasing evidence links mitochondrial dysfunction-abnormal metabolism [15], elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS), gene mutations, calcium imbalance, and impaired apoptosis-to tumor initiation, progression, invasion, and metastasis [[16], [17], [18], [19]]. Moreover, the heightened metabolic activity and dependence of tumor cells render their mitochondria uniquely vulnerable to pharmacological disruption [20]. Therefore, engineering small molecules that selectively accumulate in mitochondria represents a promising anticancer strategy [21,22]. Guided by this rationale, we sought to modify CLB to obtain a mitochondria-targeted derivative with improved selectivity and reduced toxicity.

The implementation of this strategy leverages two key physicochemical features of tumor mitochondria. During rapid tumor proliferation, aberrant metabolism results in excessive ROS generation [23], among which peroxynitrite (ONOO−) plays a pivotal role in tumor-associated oxidative stress. ONOO− involves in a variety of physiological and pathological processes [[24], [25], [26]]. Its levels are significantly higher in tumor-cell mitochondria than in normal cells. This elevated ONOO− provides a biochemical trigger for the controlled activation of prodrugs specifically within tumor mitochondria [23,27]. Furthermore, the strong mitochondrial membrane potential-negative inside and positive outside-enables delocalized lipophilic cations (DLCs) to penetrate cellular membranes and accumulate preferentially within mitochondria [28,29]. This unique property has made DLCs valuable carriers for covalently delivering small-molecule therapeutics to mitochondria [30,31]. As shown in Scheme 1, in this study, we selected 1,8-naphthalimide as the fluorescent scaffold due to its favorable photophysical properties. Although native 1,8-naphthalimide is neutral [32], the aromatic skeleton of the naphthylimide itself is lipophilic [33]. We enhanced its delocalization and lipophilicity by introducing a positively charged 2-(2-aminoethyl) pyridine side chain at the imide position and replacing the pyridine hydrogen with an alkyl-substituted quaternary ammonium group. This structure can freely traverse the cell membrane and the mitochondrial membrane, and harnesses the elevated mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm, approximately −220 mV to −240 mV) in cancer cells as the driving force to achieve selective mitochondrial accumulation (with an enrichment factor of up to 100-1000 fold). This mechanism is consistent with that of the classical TPP (triphenylphosphonium) targeting moiety. Previous reports have all employed TPP as the mitochondrial targeting moiety. Although TPP exhibits high targeting efficiency, it lacks fluorescence tracing capability. Consequently, for intracellular distribution studies, an additional fluorescent probe must be conjugated, which increases synthetic complexity and the risk of steric hindrance [[34], [35], [36]]. Guided by this rationale, we developed a theranostic prodrug, BPA-CLB, which embodies this integrated design. A customized mitochondrial vector, BPA, was first synthesized based on a 1,8-naphthalimide fluorescent scaffold. Strategic structural modifications, including the introduction of a cationic side chain, endowed BPA with optimal lipophilicity and delocalized positive charge for mitochondrial targeting. CLB was then conjugated to BPA via an ONOO−-sensitive ester bond. This molecular architecture is designed to achieve three synergistic functions: (i) preferential mitochondrial accumulation facilitated by the BPA vector, (ii) real-time, fluorescence-based visualization of drug distribution, and (iii) ONOO−-triggered cleavage of the linker to release the active CLB payload directly within the target organelle.

Experimental section

Biology

Mice and cell lines

Five-week-old male Balb/c nude mice were purchased from Beijing HFK Bioscience Co., Ltd. (SYXK 2023 0012). Mice were acclimatized for one week prior to experimentation.

This research protocol was reviewed and approved by the School of Pharmaceutical Sciences & Institute of Materia Medical, Shandong First Medical University & Shandong Academy of Medical Sciences Medical Ethics Committee, approval number 202211030006.

All animal procedures were carried out in accordance with the protocols approved by the Institute of Materia Medical, Shandong Academy of Medical Sciences, under application number.

The human hepatocellular carcinoma cell line HepG2, the mouse hepatocyte cell line AML-12, the human fetal lung fibroblast line HFL-1, the human normal hepatocyte cell line QSG-7701, and the human normal bronchial epithelial cell line Beas-2B were purchased from ATCC (America). The immortalized human gastric epithelial cell line GES-1 (a non-neoplastic cell line) were obtained from the China Center for Type Culture Collection (CCTCC, China). The normal rat gastric epithelial cell line RGM-1 was obtained from RIKEN BRC (Japan).

HepG2 and HFL-1 cells were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). AML-12 cells were maintained in DMEM/F-12 supplemented with 10% FBS, insulin-transferrin-selenium (ITS), and dexamethasone. QSG-7701 and GES-1 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium containing 10% FBS. Beas-2B cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. RGM-1 cells were maintained in DMEM containing 10% FBS and 2 mM L-glutamine.

All cell lines were cultured at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator, authenticated by STR profiling, and confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination.

Cytotoxicity test

Cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 104 cells/well and treated with BPA, CLB, or BPA-CLB at concentrations of 0, 0.2, 1, 5, 10, 20, 40, and 80 μM for 24 h. After treatment, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution (Beyotime, C0037, China) was added to each well. Cell viability was then quantified using a microplate spectrophotometer (Synergy™ H1, USA) by measuring absorbance at 450 nm.

Colony formation

A colony formation assay was performed to evaluate clonogenic survival. Exponentially growing cells were trypsinized, counted, and seeded at a low density (1000 cells/well) in 6-well plates. Cells were cultured in complete medium for 7-14 days, with medium replacement every 3-4 days, to allow colony development. Colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15-20 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 15-30 min at room temperature. After rinsing with distilled water and air-drying, visible colonies (defined as clusters containing >50 cells) were manually counted. Clonogenic survival was calculated as: (number of colonies formed/number of cells seeded) × 100%.

Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM)

The intracellular localization of BPA-CLB was visualized using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Zeiss 980, USA) with a 405 nm excitation source. HepG2 cells were cultured and treated with BPA-CLB (5 μM) for 15 min, 3 h, or 6 h. After incubation, cells were washed and maintained in DMEM without phenol red for imaging. For colocalization analysis, cells were co stained with organelle specific probes: mitochondria were labeled with MitoTracker Deep Red (50 nM, 30 min), lysosomes with LysoTracker Deep Red (50 nM, 30 min), the nucleus with NucRed™ Live 647 ReadyProbes® Reagent (2 drops/mL, 15-30 min), and the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) with ER Tracker Red (1 μM, 30 min). The latter is a BODIPY™ TR glibenclamide conjugate (excitation/emission maxima approximately 587/615 nm) and is suitable for live cell staining only. All stainings were performed in HBSS or serum free medium according to the manufacturers’ instructions. After staining, cells were washed with PBS and immediately imaged. Confocal images were acquired and processed using ZEISS ZEN software.

Isolation and purification of mitochondria

Mitochondria were isolated from HepG2 cells using differential centrifugation. Briefly, cells were homogenized in ice-cold isolation buffer containing 0.25 M sucrose, 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), and 1 mM EDTA. The homogenate was centrifuged at 1000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove nuclei and cellular debris. The resulting supernatant was then centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C to obtain the crude mitochondrial pellet. This pellet was washed once by resuspension in fresh isolation buffer and re-centrifuged under identical conditions. The final mitochondrial fraction was resuspended in the appropriate assay buffer, and BPA-CLB concentration was quantified using liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (SCIEX Triple Quad™ 5500, China).

Immunofluorescence (IF)

Ki67 expression was detected by immunofluorescence staining. Cells grown on glass coverslips were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, and blocked with bovine serum albumin (BSA). Cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with a primary antibody against Ki67 (Abcam, ab16667, USA), followed by incubation with an Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (Abcam, ab150080, USA). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Coverslips were mounted, and images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope. Ki67-positive cells displayed distinct nuclear staining.

Sphere-formation assay

Tumor sphere formation was assessed using 96-well ultra-low attachment plates (Corning, 4520, USA). Each well was seeded with 8000 cells in 100 μL of medium and cultured at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. After overnight incubation, sphere formation was confirmed microscopically. Drug treatments were initiated by gently removing 50 μL of medium and replacing it with 50 μL of fresh medium containing BPA, CLB, or BPA-CLB. Following an additional 48 h of incubation, spheres were imaged using an Olympus IX83 inverted microscope. Tumor sphere diameters were subsequently measured for quantitative analysis.

Cell apoptosis analysis

HepG2 cells were seeded at a density of 2 × 105 cells per dish and treated with BPA (10 μM), CLB (10 μM), or BPA-CLB (10 μM) for 24 h. For apoptosis detection, cells were stained with Annexin V-PE and 7-AAD (Yeasen, 40310 ES, China). Apoptotic populations were analyzed using an Attune NxT flow cytometer (Thermo Fisher, USA).

Western blot

HepG2 cells were treated with 10 μM BPA, 10 μM CLB, or 10 μM BPA-CLB for 24 h, harvested by trypsinization, centrifuged, and lysed in RIPA buffer. Cell lysates were clarified by centrifugation, and protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein quantification kit. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein signals were developed using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) and imaged with a Clinx ChemiScope 6000 gel documentation system (JET BIOFIL, China). All experiments were independently repeated three times. Primary antibodies against Bak (12105), Bcl-xl (2764), VDAC2 (9412), and β-actin (4970) were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (New England BioLabs Inc., USA). β-actin served as the loading control. The antibodies were diluted at a ratio of 1:1000.

Real-time quantitative PCR (RT q-PCR)

Following treatment with 10 μM BPA, 10 μM CLB, or 10 μM BPA-CLB, total RNA was extracted using the SPARKeasy Tissue/Cell RNA Kit (SPARK Jade, AC0202-A, China). cDNA was synthesized using the SPARKscript II 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit with gDNA Eraser (SPARK Jade, AG0302-A, China). The mRNA expression levels of Ki67 and VDAC2 were quantified by RT-qPCR using FastStart Universal SYBR Green (Roche, 4913914001, Switzerland). β-actin served as the internal reference gene, and relative expression levels were calculated using the 2–ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences are shown in Table S1.

Transwell assay

HepG2 cells were seeded into the upper chamber of Transwell inserts (NEST, 725122, China). The lower chamber was filled with medium containing 10% FBS to serve as a chemoattractant. A total of 8 × 104 cells, resuspended in 100 μL of low-serum medium (1% FBS), were added to the upper chamber and allowed to adhere for 12 h. Cells were then treated with BPA (10 μM), CLB (10 μM), or BPA-CLB (10 μM). After 24 h, cells on the upper surface of the membrane (non-migrated cells) were gently removed with a cotton swab. Migrated cells on the lower surface were fixed, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and visualized using an Olympus IX83 inverted microscope (USA).

Wound-healing assay

The migratory capacity of HepG2 cells was assessed using a wound-healing assay. Once a confluent monolayer was established, a uniform scratch was created using a sterile 200 μL pipette tip. Cells were then treated with BPA (10 μM), CLB (10 μM), or BPA-CLB (10 μM) for 24 h. Wound closure was monitored and imaged at 0, 24, and 48 h using an Olympus IX83 inverted microscope. The migration rate was quantified by measuring the remaining wound area at each time point using ImageJ software.

In vivo experiments

HepG2 cells (5.0 × 106 in 200 μL) were subcutaneously injected into the upper left flank of 5-week-old mice. Once tumor volume reached approximately 50 mm3, the tumor-bearing mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 6 per group). Drug treatment was then initiated, with mice receiving daily intratumoral injections of PBS (control), CLB (5 mg/kg), BPA (5 mg/kg), or BPA-CLB (5 mg/kg) for 14 days. Tumor volumes were monitored throughout the treatment period. Mice were subsequently sacrificed, and tumor weights were measured. Harvested tumors were processed for histological evaluation, including hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP Nick-End Labeling (TUNEL) assay to assess tissue morphology and apoptosis. Liver, stomach, and lung tissues were also examined via H&E staining. Survival time was recorded, and animals were euthanized when tumor volume exceeded 1500 mm3.

HE staining

For histological analysis, tissue samples from the tumor, liver, stomach, and lungs were collected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. Samples were embedded in paraffin, sectioned at a thickness of 5 μm, and subjected to standard H&E staining (Beyotime, C0105S, China). After staining, the slides were coverslipped, and tissue morphology was examined and imaged using an Olympus VS120 high-definition panoramic scanning and analysis system.

TUNEL

Apoptotic cells in paraffin-embedded tissue sections were detected using a TUNEL assay kit (Beyotime, C1090, China), following the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, after deparaffinization, rehydration, and antigen retrieval, sections were digested with Proteinase K, permeabilized, and incubated at 37 °C with a reaction mixture containing terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (TdT) and Cy3-labeled dUTP. Following reaction termination, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. TUNEL-positive apoptotic cells were visualized and imaged using an Olympus VS120 whole-slide scanning and image analysis system.

Measurement of cellular ATP

Cellular ATP levels were quantified using a commercial ATP assay kit (MCE, HY-K0314, USA). After treatment, cells were washed with PBS, lysed, and centrifuged to remove insoluble material. Supernatants were transferred to a white opaque microplate and mixed with ATP detection reagent. Luminescence was measured immediately using a microplate reader, and ATP concentrations were calculated using a standard curve generated in parallel.

Mitochondrial membrane potential assay

To assess mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm), cells were seeded in confocal dishes and treated for 30 min with CCCP (10 μM; positive control), BPA (10 μM), CLB (10 μM), or BPA-CLB (10 μM). After treatment, cells were rinsed and incubated with TMRE working solution (Beyotime, C2001S, China) at 37 °C for 30 min. Following staining and washing, cells were maintained in fresh medium for imaging. TMRE fluorescence was detected using a Zeiss LSM 980 confocal microscope (λex/λem: 550/575 nm).

ROS assay

Intracellular ROS levels were measured using the fluorescent probe DCFH-DA. After removing the culture medium, cells were incubated with 1 mL of 10.0 μM DCFH-DA (λex = 488 nm, λem = 525 nm) for 20 min. Following incubation, cells were washed with serum-free medium to remove excess probe and then treated for 30 min with Rosup (positive control, 10 μM), BPA (10 μM), CLB (10 μM), or BPA-CLB (10 μM); an untreated control group was included. After treatment and washing, cells were immediately imaged using a Zeiss LSM 980 confocal laser scanning microscope. The intensity of green fluorescence was used as an indicator of intracellular ROS levels.

Seahorse test

HepG2 cells were diluted to 1 × 106 cells/mL, and 80 μL of the suspension was seeded into each well of a Seahorse XF plate (Agilent, Seahorse XF Pro M, China) containing assay medium without buffering agents. After loading mitochondrial modulators-oligomycin, FCCP, and rotenone/antimycin A-into the corresponding drug ports, the plate was calibrated within the analyzer. The assay proceeded through automated mix-wait-measure cycles with sequential injections, enabling real-time monitoring of oxygen consumption rate (OCR). Basal respiration, ATP-linked respiration, maximal respiration, and spare respiratory capacity were calculated using the Seahorse software according to manufacturer guidelines.

Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA) coupled with Western blot

HepG2 cells were treated with DMSO (control) or 10 μM BPA-CLB for 2 h at 37 °C. Cells were harvested, lysed in PBS containing 0.4% NP-40 and protease inhibitors (EDTA-free), and subjected to three freeze–thaw cycles. The lysate was centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected. Aliquots were heated in a gradient PCR machine at indicated temperatures (37-64 °C, 3 min each). After heating, samples were centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the soluble fractions were analyzed by Western blot using an anti-VDAC2 antibody. β-Actin served as loading control. Band intensities were quantified by Image J. The melting temperature (Tm) was derived by fitting the data to a Boltzmann equation. ΔTm >2 °C was considered indicative of direct binding.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 8 and Origin 2021. For datasets meeting normality assumptions, one-way analysis of variance (one-way ANOVA) was used for group comparisons. Data are presented as mean ± SD. Statistical significance thresholds were set as follows: ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001; non-significant differences were not labeled.

Molecular docking

The structure of VDAC2 was obtained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB). Docking simulations between VDAC2 and BPA-CLB were performed using AutoDock Vina 1.1.2 to predict binding affinity and interaction modes.

Synthesis and characterization of BPA-CLB

The complete synthetic route of BPA-CLB is provided in the Supporting Information (Fiure S1). The synthesis and characterization of Compounds 1, 2, and 3 are also detailed therein, and the synthetic procedures for Compounds 1 and 2 follow previously reported methods [37].

Synthesis of BPA-CLB: Compound 3 (604.5 mg, 1 mmol) was dissolved in anhydrous acetonitrile (25 mL), and the solution was cooled in an ice bath. Iodomethane (CH3I, 74.7 μL, 1.2 mmol) was added dropwise slowly under cooling. The reaction mixture was then warmed to 60 °C and stirred overnight, with progress monitored by thin-layer chromatography (TLC). After completion, the solvent was removed under reduced pressure to afford a pale yellow solid. The crude product was washed several times with anhydrous diethyl ether and dried under vacuum, yielding BPA-CLB as a pale yellow powder (480.9 mg, 79% yield).

Results and discussion

Synthesis and optical properties of BPA-CLB

Although CLB possesses broad-spectrum antitumor activity, its therapeutic potential is limited by poor intracellular targeting [2]. In addition, the absence of intrinsic fluorescence prevents CLB from being utilized for real-time visualization or mechanistic studies. To overcome these limitations, we rationally designed and synthesized a mitochondria-targeted small-molecule conjugate, BPA-CLB, following the design paradigm of organelle-specific therapeutics. Using CLB as the core pharmacophore and mitochondria as the delivery target, we strategically incorporated a fluorescent, positively charged BPA moiety to endow the conjugate with mitochondrial tropism. The synthetic route is illustrated in Fig. S1: Compound 3 was first obtained through the condensation of Compound 2 with CLB, followed by methylation with methyl iodide to produce the final conjugate, BPA-CLB. Structural characterization of BPA-CLB and its intermediates by 1H NMR, 13C NMR, and high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) demonstrated excellent agreement with the expected molecular structures (Figs. S3 and S4).

As intended by design, BPA-CLB undergoes ONOO−-triggered ester bond cleavage in the presence of ROS, leading to the simultaneous release of BPA and CLB and the emergence of a new red-shifted emission band at approximately 560 nm (Fig. 1A). The optical properties of BPA-CLB were systematically evaluated using UV-Vis absorption and fluorescence spectroscopy. As shown in Fig. 1B, BPA-CLB (10 μM in PBS) exhibited a maximum absorption peak at 345 nm. Upon addition of ONOO−, the absorbance at 345 nm decreased significantly, accompanied by the appearance of a new absorption peak at 450 nm, characteristic of free BPA. The fluorescence emission spectrum analysis (Fig. 1C), revealed that BPA-CLB displayed a strong emission at 457 nm with a weak shoulder near 550 nm. Following ONOO− treatment, the emission intensity at 457 nm decreased markedly, whereas a substantial increase in emission at 550 nm resulted in a distinct peak at 564 nm. This spectroscopic shift confirms the hydrolytic release of BPA and CLB and the associated enhancement of green fluorescence. To verify the selective response of BPA-CLB to ONOO−, competitive assays were conducted with various biologically relevant analytes. In the presence of competing species, the fluorescence ratio F564/F457 remained below 1, and the 564 nm emission remained weaker than that at 457 nm, indicating the absence of green fluorescence activation. In stark contrast, the addition of ONOO− produced a rapid increase in F564/F457, accompanied by strong fluorescence at 564 nm and the activation of green fluorescence (Fig. 1D). To corroborate the CCK-8 results, a colony formation assay was performed. Both CLB and BPA-CLB reduced colony numbers relative to the control, with BPA-CLB showing the most pronounced suppression (Fig. S7). Consistent with this, expression of the proliferation marker Ki67 [38] was significantly downregulated at both the mRNA and protein levels by CLB and, to a greater extent, by BPA-CLB (Fig. 3D–E and Fig. S8).

We next assessed the inhibitory activity of BPA-CLB in a 3D culture system using a tumor sphere assay. Compared with the control group, CLB-treated spheres displayed peripheral dead-cell detachment and had a diameter of 1078 ± 46.13 μm. BPA-CLB treatment resulted in more extensive cell detachment and a significantly smaller sphere diameter of 777.74 ± 67.44 μm (Fig. 3F–G). These findings collectively demonstrate that BPA-CLB exhibits stronger cytotoxicity against HepG2 hepatocellular carcinoma cells than CLB.

Given that evasion of apoptosis is a hallmark of cancer [39], we next investigated whether the enhanced cytotoxicity of BPA-CLB involves the induction of programmed cell death. Annexin V-PE/7-AAD staining revealed significantly increased apoptosis in both CLB- and BPA-CLB-treated cells, with BPA-CLB producing the highest apoptotic rate (Fig. 3H–I). Since apoptosis is regulated by the synergistic effects of pro-apoptotic and anti-apoptotic molecules [40]. Apoptotic pathways are classified into the mitochondrial pathway (intrinsic) and the death receptor pathway (extrinsic), both of which ultimately lead to the activation of Caspase-3. Therefore, detecting changes in the level of cleaved Caspase-3 is a key gold standard for confirming the occurrence of apoptosis and assessing the activity of the mitochondrial pathway [41]. We further investigated the expression of key apoptotic regulators which is related to the Caspase-3 pathway, including the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 and Bcl-xl, the pro-apoptotic protein Caspase-3, Bax and Bak. Both BPA-CLB and CLB treatments increased the protein expression levels of Caspase, Bax and Bak, and decreased the expression of Bcl-2 and Bcl-xl. Among them, BPA-CLB led to more significant changes (Fig. 3J–O). These results confirm that BPA-CLB effectively promotes apoptosis in HepG2 cells.

The metastatic capacity of tumor cells is one of the most dangerous characteristics of malignant tumors, leading to a series of severe consequences [42,43]. Therefore, inhibiting tumor cell metastasis is a critical therapeutic objective. We therefore examined whether BPA-CLB could suppress HepG2 cell migration using wound healing and Transwell assays. At 48 h, the migration rate of the control group was 76.30% ± 6.20%, similar to that of the BPA group (76.95% ± 9.86%). CLB reduced migration to 51.09% ± 4.33%, whereas BPA-CLB drastically reduced migration to 7.82% ± 1.14% (Fig. 3P–Q). This potent anti-migratory effect was confirmed by Transwell assays, where BPA-CLB treatment resulted in the fewest migrated cells (Fig. S9). These results indicate that BPA-CLB possesses superior anti-migratory activity compared with CLB.

Because CLB is known to cause toxicity in normal cells and tissues [3], we next evaluated whether BPA-CLB reduces this undesired toxicity by mitochondrial targeting. We evaluated the toxicity of BPA-CLB against a panel of normal cell lines, including murine and human hepatocytes (AML-12, QSG7701), gastric epithelial cells (GES-1, RGM-1), and lung cells (HFL-1, Beas2b). CLB exhibited substantial toxicity across all lines, whereas BPA-CLB showed no obvious cytotoxicity (Fig. S10). This improved selectivity is attributed to the positively charged, lipophilic structure of BPA-CLB, which promotes preferential accumulation in tumor cells. In addition, BPA-CLB activates the pro-apoptotic protein Bak while inhibiting anti-apoptotic proteins such as Bcl-xl, further enhancing selective tumor cell apoptosis.

Since Δψm directly affects the activity of the respiratory chain, mitochondrial respiration reflected by the oxygen consumption rate (OCR) serves as a critical indicator of mitochondrial function [47]. Clinically, reduced OCR is often indicative of mitochondrial dysfunction. Given the pivotal role of mitochondrial metabolism in tumor growth and metastasis [48], we measured cellular OCR using a Seahorse XFe24 Extracellular Flux Analyzer. Consistent with its effect on ΔΨm, BPA-CLB potently suppressed OCR, indicating severe impairment of mitochondrial electron transport chain activity. CLB also reduced OCR, but to a lesser extent, while BPA had no significant effect (Fig. 5C–E).

As the unique “powerhouse” of eukaryotic cells, mitochondria serve as the core hub of cellular energy metabolism [49,50]. A chemiluminescent ATP assay showed that ATP production remained unchanged in BPA-treated cells, while both CLB and BPA-CLB significantly reduced ATP levels. Again, BPA-CLB produced the most pronounced decrease, consistent with severe mitochondrial impairment (Fig. 5F).

The disruption of electron transport frequently leads to electron leakage and excessive generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [51]. To evaluate BPA-CLB mediated ROS generation, we used the DCFH-DA probe. Rosup, a ROS-inducing reagent, served as a positive control. We found that BPA-CLB induced a significant increase in intracellular ROS levels, greater than that induced by CLB alone (Fig. 5G and H). Collectively, these data demonstrate that BPA-CLB induces a cascade of mitochondrial dysfunction: collapse of ΔΨm, inhibition of respiration, depletion of ATP, and accumulation of ROS, culminating in an apoptotic stimulation.

To elucidate the molecular target underlying these effects, we focused on the voltage-dependent anion channel 2 (VDAC2). VDAC2is a major channel in the outer mitochondrial membrane. It regulates the exchange of metabolites such as ATP, ADP, pyruvate, and ions between the cytoplasm and mitochondria, thereby maintaining normal cellular energy metabolism [52,53]. VDAC2 also binds tightly to Bak, which keeps it in an inactive state. When a strong apoptotic stimulus is applied, Bak is released and undergoes conformational activation. This activated Bak then oligomerizes to form pores, which promote cytochrome c release and initiate apoptosis [54,55]. VDAC2 additionally interacts with anti-apoptotic proteins such as Bcl-xl, further modulating apoptotic thresholds [56]. Thus, VDAC2 serves as a key molecular link between mitochondrial function and apoptosis.

Based on this, we hypothesized that BPA-CLB might directly bind to VDAC2 to induce mitochondrial dysfunction. Molecular docking analysis supported this hypothesis, revealing strong predicted binding affinity (−7.07 kcal/mol). The ligand's hydrophobic domains were deeply embedded within a hydrophobic pocket of VDAC2, forming extensive van der Waals interactions with residues such as Leu123, Val256, and Phe330, resulting in excellent shape complementarity and a favorable binding interface. Although no classical hydrogen bonds were observed, π–π stacking between the ligand's benzene ring and Tyr205 contributed additional stabilization. The bis (2-chloroethyl) amine moiety may further stabilize binding through hydrophobic and halogen-bond/electrostatic interactions (Fig. 5I). To further verify whether BPA-CLB directly interacts with VDAC2, we employed classic cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA) to assess the thermal stability of VDAC2 protein in HepG2 cells. As shown in Fig. 5J and K, in the control (DMSO) group, the melting curve of VDAC2 exhibited typical thermal denaturation characteristics, with soluble protein gradually decreasing as the temperature increased. The calculated melting temperature (Tm, the temperature at which 50% of the protein is denatured) was approximately 48.9 ± 1.2 °C. In contrast, in the BPA-CLB-treated group, the thermal stability of VDAC2 was significantly enhanced, with its melting curve markedly shifted to the right, and the Tm value increased to 54.4 ± 1.1°C (ΔTm ≈ 5.5 °C). These results demonstrate that BPA-CLB directly binds to VDAC2 in cells and significantly enhances the thermal stability of VDAC2. This finding provides independent direct evidence from a thermodynamic perspective for the physical interaction between BPA-CLB and VDAC2.

To experimentally evaluate whether BPA-CLB regulates VDAC2, we measured VDAC2 mRNA and protein expression via RT-qPCR and Western blot. Both levels were significantly reduced following BPA-CLB treatment (Fig. 5L and M, Figure S12). These findings demonstrate that BPA-CLB not only potentially binds to VDAC2 but also regulates its expression. This interaction is proposed to disrupt VDAC2's normal function, contributing to the observed mitochondrial dysfunction and initiation of apoptosis, aligning with the known role of VDAC2 in cell death regulation [57].

Limitation

Although this study demonstrated that BPA-CLB exerts favorable anti-hepatocellular carcinoma efficacy, several limitations of the present study still exist.

Firstly, the mechanistic evidence is not fully established. This study indirectly verified the ONOO− responsive release behavior of the prodrug based on fluorescence spectral analysis and antitumor pharmacological evaluation. Nevertheless, direct experimental evidence is still lacking. In follow-up investigations, LC-MS/MS will be applied to quantify cleavage products and determine the relevant kinetic parameters.

Secondly, systematic evaluations of in vivo biosafety and pharmacokinetics have not been conducted. This study adopted intratumoral injection only for proof-of-concept validation. Systematic assessments of the pharmacokinetic parameters including half-life, bioavailability and tissue distribution of BPA-CLB, chemical stability under physiological conditions such as serum environment and pH 7.4, as well as comprehensive biosafety involving serum biochemical markers, hematological parameters and hemolysis tests were not performed. Subsequent research will develop an active targeted nano-delivery system to achieve intravenous administration. LC-MS/MS will be used to determine plasma pharmacokinetic parameters including half-life, area under the curve and clearance, as well as tissue distribution profiles after intravenous administration. Taking advantage of the intrinsic fluorescence property of BPA-CLB, small animal in vivo imaging technology will be applied to visually validate its tumor targeting and accumulation ability.

These aforementioned limitations remain to be addressed in further investigations. Resolving these issues will facilitate the clinical translation of BPA-CLB.

Conclusion

In this study, we conjugated CLB with the positively charged fluorophore BPA to generate the mitochondria-targeted conjugate BPA-CLB, which efficiently accumulates in mitochondria and releases active CLB while preserving antitumor efficacy. And importantly, this conjugate significantly minimizes the off-target toxicity that has long limited the clinical utility of CLB. Our comprehensive experiments demonstrate that BPA-CLB acts as a pro-apoptotic agent that selectively disrupts mitochondrial function, as evidenced by increased ROS generation, inhibited proliferation and migration, and activation of intrinsic apoptotic pathways. Notably, we propose that these effects may involve direct suppression of VDAC2, a key regulator linking mitochondrial function to apoptosis. Collectively, this work establishes a proof of concept for enhancing the precision of conventional chemotherapeutics through rational subcellular engineering. By redirecting drug action to a critical organelle and exploiting its pathological microenvironment, we achieve a paradigm shift from nonspecific cytotoxicity to targeted, mechanism-based therapy. This strategy offers a promising blueprint for developing safer and more effective anticancer agents.
