Study protocol of a sequential, multiple assignment, randomised trial using an adaptive intervention to reduce loneliness among Chinese older adults in Hong Kong
Authors: Vivien Foong Yee Tang, Da Jiang, Jojo Yan Yan Kwok, Min Qian, Dannii Yuen-lan Yeung, Esther Oi Wah Chow, Namkee G Choi, Kee Lee Chou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLoneliness is a prevalent and alarming issue among older adults that requires effective interventions. While randomised controlled trials have been commonly undertaken to explore reduction in loneliness, there is a growing recognition that a comprehensive treatment strategy involving multiple interventions may yield better outcomes. Therefore, this study aims to develop and identify a two-stage adaptive intervention that combines telephone-delivered behavioural activation (Tele-BA) and mindfulness techniques (Tele-MF) to reduce loneliness in older adults in Hong Kong.MethodsA Sequential, Multiple Assignment, Randomised Trial (SMART) design will be adopted. 244 lonely older adults will be recruited from the community and randomly assigned to either one of the 4-week two-stage, embedded adaptive interventions involving different sequences and dosages of Tele-BA and Tele-MF (eg, 4 weeks of Tele-BA followed by booster sessions for responders or 4 weeks of Tele-BA followed by 4 weeks of Tele-BA with motivational component or 4 weeks of Tele-MF for non-responders) based on a set of decision rules. The primary outcome will be loneliness measured by the Revised University of California Los Angeles (UCLA) Loneliness Scale. Secondary outcomes, such as De Jong Gierveld Loneliness Scale, perceived stress, sleep quality and depressive and anxiety symptoms, will be assessed. Adhering to the intention-to-treat principles, the data will be analysed using linear mixed models. The findings may have implications for the development of psychosocial adaptive interventions involving BA and MF to reduce loneliness and improve well-being among older adults in Hong Kong using the SMART design.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval was obtained by the Human Research Ethics Committee at The Education University of Hong Kong (reference: 2022-2023-0117). The findings from this study will be presented in academic conferences and submitted for publication.Trial registration numberChiCTR2300077472.Keywords: Psychosocial Intervention, Aging, MENTAL HEALTH, Clinical Trial

Introduction

Loneliness is a widespread phenomenon, especially in community-dwelling older adults, with a noticeable rise in its prevalence during the COVID-19 pandemic.1 In Hong Kong, a significant number of older adults experience loneliness. Studies reported that 46% of community-dwelling older adults felt lonely sometimes or often in 2018, and 70% from the public primary care clinics felt moderate and severe loneliness in 2020.2 3 Loneliness is characterised as social distress arising from a discrepancy between the desired and actual social relationships.4 This disparity can lead to feelings of loneliness even in social interaction.5 Loneliness in older adults has been associated with a wide range of age-related detrimental effects on physical and mental health (ie, mortality, cardiovascular diseases, lower capacity of self-regulation, impairment of cognitive functioning and presence of mental health issues).6,9 Interventions have highlighted the benefits of adopting psychosocial intervention to lessen loneliness, given the growing concerns and its detrimental effects on an individual’s health. Recent systematic reviews have found that psychosocial interventions such as behavioural activation (BA) and mindfulness (MF) have been shown to effectively reduce loneliness in older individuals.10 11

BA is a psychosocial approach to treating depression from the perspective of the link between behaviours and mood, that is, scheduling and engaging in value-based, rewarding activities and improving self-efficacy.12 Due to its benefits in alleviating depressive symptoms, studies have shown that BA as a single-modality intervention is viable, scalable and cost-effective for reducing loneliness in older persons.13 14 A meta-analysis found that BA significantly alleviated loneliness (Hedges' g = −0.27) among older adults.10 However, a recent study using telephone-delivered BA (Tele-BA) intervention spanning 8 weeks for older adults residing in England and Wales reported lower levels of depressive symptoms and emotional loneliness but not social and overall loneliness.13 MF is a psychosocial intervention that aims to enhance an individual’s mental and physical well-being by encouraging MF practice and awareness of the present moment.15 A review revealed that MF meditation significantly reduced the perception of loneliness in older adults (Mean Differences = −6.03, 95% CI −9.33 to –2.73).16 A randomised controlled trial using an 8- week MF intervention compared with a health enhancement programme found that MF could protect and reverse the effect of loneliness on inflammatory gene expression.17 Given the importance of BA and MF intervention, a recent dual-RCT study, Helping Alleviate Loneliness in Hong Kong Older Adults (HEAL-HOA) conducted in Hong Kong consisting of 8-biweekly, 30-min Tele-BA or MF sessions against an active control group found that BA and MF have a significant impact in reducing loneliness, stress and depressive symptoms among community-dwelling Chinese older adults.18 Although BA and MF have shown to be effective in reducing loneliness, they have different foci.19,21 BA focuses on the link between behaviour, mood, and affect, and reinforcing engagement in life activities13 22; whereas MF focuses on regulating negative affect and monitoring and accepting present-moment experiences in life.16 23

Interventions aimed at reducing loneliness in older adults often involve one-off randomised controlled trial (RCT) that primarily focus on testing the effectiveness of the intervention.24 However, a recent systematic review highlighted that while these interventions may have short-term benefits, the long-term effects tend to diminish.25 Considering that loneliness can be experienced by older adults for varying durations and not all individuals respond to the same intervention, it is crucial to develop comprehensive treatment strategies that incorporate various interventions to effectively support older adults.24 An adaptive intervention consists of a set of guidelines that specify when, how and what indicators to use when making decisions about adjusting the dosage, treatment type or delivery of treatment during different decision-making phases.26 The use of a Sequential, Multiple Assignment, Randomised Trial (SMART) design allows researchers to assess the effectiveness of adaptative interventions that can be modified over time to better address the changing needs of the older adults.27 SMART designs have been used in relation to health and physical research mostly among adolescents and adults (eg, depression, anxiety, cancer and obsessive-compulsive disorder).28 One study used the SMART approach in examining the effectiveness of a two-stage intervention using cognitive behavioural therapy and physical therapy in reducing depressive symptoms in older adults who have knee osteoarthrosis. This study highlighted the importance of dosage instead of the type of interventions for responders and non-responders to extend the intervention period further before switching to another.29 Therefore, this study will be the first to identify an effective two-stage adaptive intervention that can effectively reduce loneliness among older adults and sustain its effects over time.

Methods

Study design

This is a prospective study using SMART, where a two-stage adaptive intervention will be used. In this study, we propose to use a SMART design by deciding which intervention to provide initially, how to determine whether the first-stage intervention is effective or not and whether to extend the first-stage intervention or switch out to another intervention if the first-stage intervention is not working. This study will span across 2.5 years starting from January 2024 to June 2026, including the preparation phase (ie, finalising assessment and training research personnel) from January 2024 to April 2024, followed by the implementation phase from May 2024 to June 2026. Study objectives and hypothesis

The overarching goal of this study is to develop and identify an effective two-stage adaptive intervention that could reduce loneliness among Chinese older adults in Hong Kong using a SMART design. In our study, participants will be randomly assigned for the first-stage intervention. Depending on the response status, participants who did not respond (defined as individuals who do not show a decrease of 2.86 or more in their scores on the Revised UCLA Loneliness Scale from T1 to T2) in the first-stage intervention, will be re-randomised to either Tele-BA or Tele-MF in the second-stage intervention, whereas for participants who responded to the first-stage intervention will be assigned to the booster group. Ethical approval and informed consent

Participants will be requested to provide a signed written consent to participate in this study. Ethical approval for this study has been obtained from the Human Research Ethics Committee of The Education University of Hong Kong (reference number: 2022-2023-0117). The study has also been prospectively registered at the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry in the WHO Registry Network (registration number: ChiCTR 2300077472).

Participant recruitment and eligibility

Participants will be recruited via convenience sampling from community centres, academies and public housing estates across the geographical area of Hong Kong.

Inclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria are (1) community-dwelling Chinese older adults aged 65 years and above, (2) Cantonese speaking and communicable over the telephone, (3) no cognitive impairment, psychiatric disorder, learning disability or active suicidal ideation, (4) no prior engagement in any form of psychosocial interventions or MF-related practices in the past 6 months and (5) feeling lonely and have a score of ≥6 on the 3-item UCLA Loneliness scale.31

Sample size consideration

A power analysis was conducted to estimate the required sample size for this study, which uses a SMART design to detect the main effect of loneliness. Previous interventions targeting loneliness in older adults have shown a medium effect size of 0.5.14 To be conservative, for the first-stage intervention, a sample size of 122 is estimated based on an effect size of 0.5, a power of 85% and an alpha level of 0.05, which resulted in 61 participants in each group. Assuming an average of 50% non-response rate in the first-stage intervention, a sample size of 244 will be required for the second-stage intervention, resulting in 61 participants in each group (ie, subgroup B+F and subgroup C+E, as shown in Figure 1). To compare the effect between the four embedded adaptive interventions, a sample size of 154 is required to identify the best embedded adaptive interventions with at least 95% probability, an effect size of 0.5 and assuming an attrition rate of 10% over a period of 6 months. Hence, after careful consideration of the sample sizes needed at various stages, a conservative sample size of 244 will be used for this study. This sample size will allow for sufficient power to detect the main effect of loneliness and to compare the four embedded adaptive interventions effectively.

SMART design, randomisation and procedure

Prior to randomisation, all participants will undergo a baseline assessment. All participants will receive universal psychoeducation, which includes assessing their needs related to financial assistance (eg, applying for welfare schemes), housing (eg, public housing and rental subsidy) and referrals to medical and social services. Participants will be randomly assigned to either the Tele-BA or Tele-MF group for the first-stage intervention using an online random number generated by an independent research assistant. The same research assistant who generated the random assignment at both stages will inform all participants about their assigned intervention and instruct them not to disclose their assignment. Both groups will receive a total of eight telephone-delivered sessions over a span of 4 weeks, with two sessions per week. Each session will last approximately 30 min. For the second stage of the intervention, participants who responded positively in the first stage will be assigned to the booster group and receive two biweekly booster sessions. Non-responders will be re-randomised to either Tele-BA or Tele-MF. Non-responders assigned to the same intervention as the first stage will receive an additional motivational component, which includes information about the positive effects of the intervention and a 10-min video featuring older adults practising the intervention in their daily lives. To maintain the treatment effect, all participants will receive monthly booster sessions before T4 and T5 follow-up assessments. Responders who received both types of interventions in the earlier stage will indicate their preference for the type of booster they prefer. Non-responders in the second stage will receive treatment recommendations and referrals after the study is completed. Due to the distinct activities in each intervention group, participants cannot be blinded. However, research assistants who are responsible to assess the outcome assessments of the participants will be blinded to the intervention assignment. On completion of all interventions and assessments, participants will receive a financial incentive of HK$500 (approximately US$65) as a token of appreciation for their participation.

Intervention

Behavioural activation

The goal of BA is to encourage participants to engage in meaningful activities, limit activities that contribute to loneliness and identify and overcome obstacles that hinder participation in meaningful activities.32 The BA intervention will be adapted based on the Telehealth Behavioural Activation Treatment Manual for Homebound Older Adults,33 which has been tested in a recent dual-RCT, HEAL-HOA.18 The BA intervention will be delivered by trained volunteers over the telephone for eight sessions, each lasting 30 min, spread across 4 weeks.18 34 Participants will be asked to track their daily activities, set goals for their activities, implement their activities while finding ways to overcome any barriers and review their accomplishments. Participants will also be encouraged to complete a daily monitoring form, which involves documenting their activities, the importance and enjoyment associated with each activity and ensuring that the tasks are feasible. In cases where participants are illiterate, the trained volunteers will assist them in completing the form. In sum, the trained volunteers will play a crucial role in guiding participants to learn about the link between behaviour, mood, and affect, change negative thinking patterns, identify and schedule activities that align with their values and provide a sense of reward, develop strategies to reduce loneliness and overcome barriers to engage in meaningful, healthy and enjoyable activities.

Mindfulness

The goal of MF aims to reduce loneliness by increasing participants’ awareness in the present moment, experience non-judgmentally and redefine their stress and negative emotions.23 The MF intervention will be adapted from a smartphone-delivered intervention developed by Lindsay et al23 which has been effectively implemented in the HEAL-HOA dual-RCT study.18 Using the same training procedures, MF intervention will be delivered by trained volunteers with eight sessions, each lasting 30 min, spread across 4 weeks, and participants will be asked to practice 20 min of MF per week.18 34 To support the participants, volunteers will provide weekly telephone support, answering questions, addressing difficulties and encouraging adherence to the intervention. If volunteers are unable to answer participants’ questions, they can consult a therapist for additional guidance.

Volunteer training and intervention fidelity monitoring

A pool of volunteers between 50 years and 70 years old will be recruited via community centres, academies and public housing estates in Hong Kong to deliver the interventions. Volunteers will be required to attend training sessions conducted by either an experienced social worker for Tele-BA or an experienced MF therapist for Tele-MF. The study design will not be disclosed to the volunteers. During the training sessions, volunteers will be provided with hands-on practices and materials to familiarise themselves with the intervention. Each volunteer will only deliver one type of intervention (eg, either Tele-BA or Tele-MF). The volunteers will be required to deliver the intervention for at least 2 months and up to 5 hours per week. All training, feedback and intervention sessions will be recorded, and at least 20% of the training and intervention sessions will be monitored for fidelity. Each volunteer will be reimbursed HK$500 (approximately US$65) for their time and commitment towards the delivery of each cycle (eg, 4 weeks of Tele-BA or Tele-MF) of intervention.

Patient and public involvement

No patient and public will be involved.

Measures

Primary outcomes

Loneliness will be measured using The Revised UCLA Loneliness Scale comprising of 20 items, which has been validated with the Chinese context.35 36 Responses are made on a 4-point Likert scale (1=never to 4=always). The scale reported an acceptable level of internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.90.35 Higher summed scores would indicate higher levels of loneliness.

Secondary outcomes

The validated Chinese version of De Jong Gierveld 6-item Loneliness Scale will also be used to measure loneliness.37 38 Participants will be asked to indicate their level of agreement for each statement on a 3-point Likert scale (1=Yes, 2=More or Less and 3=No). The scale has demonstrated good psychometric properties, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.76.38 Responses will be compiled into an overall score, as well as subscale scores. A higher score indicates a greater perception of loneliness.

Stress level will be assessed by the validated Chinese version of the 14-item Perceived Stress Scale.39 40 The scale seeks to understand the feelings and thoughts in the past month. Participants will be asked to specify the frequency of each statement on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 0 (never) to 4 (very often). The scale has an acceptable internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.85 among the Chinese population.40 Prior to summing the items, some items will need to be reverse-coded, and a higher overall score represents a greater level of perceived stress.

Sleep quality will be measured using the 19-item Chinese version of the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index,41 42 which assesses overall sleep quality, duration, latency, efficiency, disturbance, daytime dysfunction and usage of sleep medication within the past month. Each item will be rated from 0 to 3, with higher scores indicating a poorer level of sleep quality and greater level of sleep disturbance. A Cronbach’s alpha of 0.75 has been reported among the Hong Kong Chinese population, indicating good internal consistency.42

Depressive symptoms will be assessed using the 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire Chinese version.43 44 Participants will be asked to report the frequency of cognitive, somatic and affective symptoms of depression felt over the past 2 weeks on a 4-point Likert scale, ranging from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day). The scale has reported a satisfactory level of psychometric properties, with Cronbach’s alpha of 0.82 and test-retest reliability of 0.76 within the Hong Kong context.43 All items will be calculated cumulatively, with higher scores indicating higher symptom severities.

Anxiety disorder will be measured using the 7-item Chinese version of the Generalised Anxiety Disorder scale,45 46 which assesses the anxiety symptom severity in the past 2 weeks. Each statement is rated on a 4-point Likert scale, from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day), with higher scores indicating the severity of anxiety symptoms. A Cronbach’s alpha of 0.89 has been demonstrated in the Chinese population, which suggests an acceptable level of internal consistency.46

Participants will be asked to rate the frequency on a scale ranging from 1 (not at all) to 5 (daily) and indicate the duration (number of hours) where they engaged in positive and meaningful daily life activities in the past 2 weeks. Positive and meaningful activity is defined as ‘involving in various aspects of subjective well-being, such as deriving pleasure and enjoyment, engaging in activities and satisfying basic needs through a wide range of daily living tasks’.47,49

Engagement in activities will be assessed using the 10-item Social Engagement and Activities Questionnaire (SEAQ).50 Participants will be asked to report their activity level (ie, social-group, interpersonal interaction and solitary) in different settings in the past month, on a 6-point Likert scale, ranging from 0 (not at all) to 5 (everyday). All items will be summed, with higher scores indicating a high level of daily engagement in activities.

MF will be evaluated using the 20-item Chinese version of the Five Facet Mindfulness Questionnaire Short Form (FFMQ-SF).51 52 The FFMQ-SF consists of items measuring the five facets of MF: observing, describing, acting with awareness, non-judging to inner experience and non-reacting to inner experience. Participants will rate the questions on a 5-point Likert scale, from 0 (never or very rarely true) to 5 (very often or always true). Questions on two facets (ie, awareness and non-judging to inner experience) will be reserve-coded. The total MF score is the average of all items, and each facet can be calculated by averaging the corresponding items, with a higher score indicating a higher level of MF. Among colorectal cancer patients in Hong Kong, a satisfactory omega coefficient (ω=0.77–0.85) for four of the five facets (except non-judging to inner experience) and test-retest reliability (Intraclass Correlation Coefficient = 0.75–0.87) was reported.53

Sociodemographic and other covariates

The following sociodemographic and other characteristics will be collected: (1) age, gender, marital status, living arrangements, education attainment, employment status, household income and financial status; (2) health-related indicators (ie, functional health—activities of daily living and instructional activities of daily living, physical symptoms inventory and the presence of chronic illnesses) and (3) the use of social and healthcare services within the past 4 weeks.

Planned statistical analysis

Preliminary analysis

Descriptive statistics, correlation analysis, attrition analysis (comparing the sociodemographic and outcome variables for those who have completed and dropped out from the intervention using t-tests) and randomisation tests will be conducted to test for differences in covariates and outcome variables between conditions using SPSS V.29. All analyses will follow the intention-to-treat principle.

Analysis for hypotheses 1, 2 and 3

Linear mixed models will be used to address the following main effects on loneliness and secondary outcomes:

To determine if Tele-BA or Tele-MF is a better intervention option in the first-stage intervention by comparing subgroups A+B+C versus subgroups D+E+F at T2.

To determine if Tele-BA or Tele-MF is a better intervention option in the first-stage intervention by comparing subgroups A+B+C versus subgroups D+E+F at T2.

To determine if it is better to extend the original intervention or switch to an alternative intervention for non-responders at first-stage intervention by comparing subgroup B (Tele-BA and Tele-BA combination) versus subgroup C (Tele-BA and Tele-MF combination) and subgroup E (Tele-MF and Tele-BA combination) versus subgroup F (Tele-MF and Tele-MF combination) at T3, T4 and T5.

To determine if it is better to extend the original intervention or switch to an alternative intervention for non-responders at first-stage intervention by comparing subgroup B (Tele-BA and Tele-BA combination) versus subgroup C (Tele-BA and Tele-MF combination) and subgroup E (Tele-MF and Tele-BA combination) versus subgroup F (Tele-MF and Tele-MF combination) at T3, T4 and T5.

To determine which sequence Tele-BA and Tele-MF or Tele-MF and Tele-BA is more effective in reducing loneliness by comparing adaptive intervention #2 (Tele-BA and Tele-MF) versus adaptive intervention #2 (Tele-MF and Tele-BA) at T3, T4 and T5.

To determine which sequence Tele-BA and Tele-MF or Tele-MF and Tele-BA is more effective in reducing loneliness by comparing adaptive intervention #2 (Tele-BA and Tele-MF) versus adaptive intervention #2 (Tele-MF and Tele-BA) at T3, T4 and T5.

Analysis for hypothesis 4

To determine the changes in loneliness between the four embedded adaptive interventions, a linear mixed model will be employed. The analysis will compare the four subgroups: (1) subgroups A+B, (2) subgroups A+C, (3) subgroups D+E and (4) subgroups D+F. The probability of non-responders and responders adhering to the sequence of interventions they are offered is 25% and 50%, respectively. This is because non-responders will be randomised twice, while responders will be randomised once. To account for the under-representation of non-responders in the first-stage intervention compared with responders, weights of 4 and 2 will be assigned to non-responders and responders, respectively.54 These weights are inversely correlated with the probability of being offered a specific sequence of interventions.54 To compare the four embedded adaptive interventions and determine which one reduces loneliness and other secondary outcomes at T3, T4 and T5, the mean outcome and SE will be estimated for each of the four interventions.

Discussion

Loneliness has been warranted as a pressing health threat according to WHO55 and poses further impacts to older adults who are living alone with inadequate social support or social interactions.56 Previous studies have examined the impact of interventions on reducing loneliness in older adults, but the results have been inconsistent.10 57 Therefore, this proposed study aims to be the first to identify an effective adaptive intervention using a SMART design to address and reduce loneliness in older adults. Specifically, the study will focus on developing decision rules that involve switching, extending or intensifying two psychosocial interventions, BA or MF based on the responses of older adults to the initial intervention they receive.

As lonely older adults are unwilling to participate in social activities due to health concerns or a lack of motivation, face-to-face interventions will not be a feasible approach.58 Due to the COVID-19 pandemic, researchers have adapted more accessible and convenient intervention options to meet the needs of older adults who are restricted by their circumstances. Recent reviews have highlighted the potential benefits of remote-delivered intervention compared with face-to-face interventions.59 Telephone-based interventions have shown promising benefits of cost-effectiveness and accessibility, and older adults are familiar with telephone usage.60 This study will adopt a telephone-delivered intervention to provide participants with the flexibility to engage in the intervention in their own comfort. The goal is to address loneliness effectively by tailoring interventions to individual responses. Clinical judgement will guide decisions on whether to extend or intensify the original intervention or transit to a different intervention for older adults who do not respond to the initial approach. This highlights the importance of employing a series of interventions and using a SMART design to gain insights into the most effective strategies for managing loneliness.26 By adopting this approach, the study aims to provide valuable insights into the design and implementation of interventions that can effectively address loneliness among older adults.

This study has potential limitations. Participants cannot be blinded due to the nature of the intervention, which could introduce bias when reporting the treatment’s efficacy. To reduce any potential bias, the outcome assessment was evaluated by research assistants who are blinded to the treatment allocation. Our study is also limited to Chinese older adults in Hong Kong, which may restrict the generalisability of the findings to other populations.

In summary, the main purpose of this study is to develop and identify an effective two-stage adaptive intervention for reducing loneliness among older adults in Hong Kong using a SMART design. The two-stage adaptive intervention is tailored to an individual’s ongoing performance. This then informs a sequence of decision rules that specify whether, how, when and on the basis of which measures, to alter the dosage, type or delivery of treatment at key decision stages.26 If this study is found to be effective, the findings will facilitate further development of large-scale adaptive interventions to reduce loneliness and promote better well-being among older adults in Hong Kong. Future research could consider comparing the effects of a two-stage adaptive intervention using the SMART approach against a traditional RCT in reducing loneliness among older adults.

Ethics and dissemination

Ethical approval for this study was obtained by the Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC) of The Education University of Hong Kong (reference number: 2022-2023-0117). The study was prospectively registered at the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry in the WHO Registry Network (registration number: ChiCTR 2300077472). Participant confidentiality will be ensured through stringent access controls to the research database, as well as by coding participant identifiers within the primary data set and securely storing any nominative information separately. A data monitoring committee comprising social worker, MF therapist and academic experts will be established to safeguard participants from potential harm arising from the intervention. This committee will regularly review participants’ experiences of discomfort and determine whether the intervention should continue, be terminated or if referrals are necessary. The study findings will be disseminated through academic conferences and peer-reviewed journal publications.
