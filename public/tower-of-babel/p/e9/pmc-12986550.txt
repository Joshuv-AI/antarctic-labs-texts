Ecosystem Multifunctionality in Semi-Arid Wetlands Is Jointly Driven by Soil Environmental Factors and Microorganisms
Authors: Yunhao Wen, Gaofan Liu, Huamin Liu, Shan Jiang, Zhichao Xu, Rui Zhang, Feng Gao, Wenxin Li, Lixin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Biodiversity promotes ecosystem multifunctionality (EMF), yet it remains unclear how wetland types mediate the biodiversity–EMF relationship. This study investigated the differences in maintaining ecosystem functionality between lacustrine (lake) and riverine (river) wetlands in a semi-arid region. We examined how multiple soil environmental variables individually influence plant communities, soil enzyme activities, and microbial community composition and diversity, and we further explored how these factors drive EMF via interactions with microbial communities. Results showed that both individual ecosystem functions and EMF were significantly lower in lacustrine wetlands compared to riverine wetlands. Plant community attributes were the primary drivers of spatial heterogeneity in bacterial, fungal, and archaeal communities; conversely, soil enzyme activities were more strongly correlated with soil structure. Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) revealed distinct regulatory mechanisms: riverine wetlands were primarily subject to direct linkage between ecosystem multifunctionality and microbial diversity, whereas lacustrine wetlands exhibited a regulatory paradigm dominated by environmental filtering, where abiotic stressors (e.g., salinity and soil moisture) indirectly drove EMF by reshaping plant communities. These findings provide critical theoretical and technical insights for the conservation and restoration of wetland ecosystems.

1. Introduction

The relationship between biodiversity and ecosystem functioning (BEF) has long been a central focus of ecological research [1,2]. Therefore, understanding how ecosystem functioning responds to key ecological processes is critical for predicting ecosystem functions and the goods and services they provide [3]. Soil biodiversity, accounting for one-quarter of the Earth’s total biodiversity, plays a crucial role in driving global biogeochemical cycles, decomposing organic matter, suppressing soil-borne diseases, and promoting plant growth [4]. This has garnered increasing attention regarding how soil microbial diversity influences ecosystem functioning [5]. First, the loss of soil microbial diversity may impair multiple ecosystem functions and services, such as soil carbon storage, biogeochemical cycling, biomass accumulation, and food production. Indeed, the growth, activities, and environmental interactions of these microorganisms largely drive ecosystem energy flow and material cycling [6]. Secondly, global environmental changes (such as climate warming and increased nitrogen deposition) are significantly reshaping soil microbial community structure and diversity, thereby regulating the direction of ecosystem feedbacks by altering microbial metabolic activities [7]. Ecosystem functioning is closely linked not only to belowground soil microbial diversity but also to aboveground plant richness. Indeed, numerous studies have demonstrated that higher plant species richness leads to stronger ecosystem functioning [8]. Furthermore, acting as the proximate agents of soil biochemical processes, soil enzymes are considered a critical link bridging environmental factors, biotic communities, and ecosystem functions [9]. Soil enzyme activities are not only highly sensitive to changes in abiotic environmental factors (e.g., temperature, moisture, and pH) but are also strongly regulated by substrate availability (such as plant litter inputs) [10]. According to the theory of ecoenzymatic stoichiometry, the ratios of C-, N-, and P-acquiring enzyme activities reflect microbial nutrient limitation, thereby determining elemental cycling efficiency and the ecosystem carbon balance [11].

Beyond the study of specific single functions, there is growing academic interest in the integrated performance of multiple functions, known as ecosystem multifunctionality (EMF). EMF is defined as the ability to maintain multiple ecosystem functions and services simultaneously, or the simultaneous performance of multiple ecosystem functions [12,13,14]. However, belowground microbial communities and the ecosystem multifunctionality they drive do not evolve in isolation; rather, they are subject to strong filtering and reshaping by abiotic environmental factors [15]. Acting as a fundamental driver, soil habitat heterogeneity directly constrains the assembly processes and metabolic activities of belowground communities by altering resource availability and niche space [16]. Concurrently, microbial community composition and diversity are also influenced by soil nutrient content [17]. A wide range of soil variables—including pH, texture, moisture, temperature, organic carbon, and nutrient content—collectively influence the composition and diversity of soil microbial communities [18,19], Microbial diversity and community structure also exhibit significant distance-decay patterns [20]. Therefore, it is essential to investigate the soil environment, microbial community structure, richness, and diversity, as well as enzyme activities and specific soil functional processes under plant communities.

Wetlands are unique ecosystems characterized by frequent or permanent inundation. They provide a wide range of ecosystem functions and services to humanity, including climate regulation, biodiversity maintenance, and habitat protection [21]. Plants and soil microorganisms, acting as the two primary drivers of ecosystem functioning, serve as the primary producers and decomposers, respectively, in wetland ecosystems [22]. Some studies indicate that plant species richness and community structure underpin the maintenance of wetland ecosystem functions, yet these effects are strongly influenced by spatial scale [23]. To date, whether soil microbial diversity is synchronized with aboveground plant diversity remains a subject of debate/remains controversial. While traditional theories suggest a tight coupling between aboveground and belowground biodiversity, recent evidence indicates that they can be decoupled due to their distinct responses to environmental drivers [24]. Most studies have been predominantly conducted in zonal ecosystems. In contrast, for azonal ecosystems such as wetlands, the driving mechanisms underlying their ecosystem functioning remain inconclusive.

In this study, we aimed to investigate the effects of soil drivers, plant diversity, and soil microbial community structure and diversity on ecosystem multifunctionality (EMF). To this end, we surveyed plant diversity across identical plant communities in typical semi-arid riparian and lacustrine wetlands. Subsequently, we employed DNA-based sequencing (Illumina MiSeq) to characterize bacterial, fungal, and archaeal community compositions. These biological data were then linked with a suite of soil environmental variables and a broad range of soil functions to unravel the driving mechanisms of EMF in different wetland types. These findings hold significant implications for revealing the potential impacts of biodiversity shifts on EMF and for the conservation of degraded wetlands. We tested the following hypotheses: (1) Plant communities are the primary drivers of the spatial distribution patterns of wetland soil microbial communities; (2) EMF levels differ between the two wetland types, as do their underlying maintenance mechanisms.

2. Results

2.1. Variations in Ecosystem Functions Across Wetland Types

Results regarding the response characteristics of individual ecosystem functions indicated that riverine wetlands exhibited a significant ecological advantage over lacustrine wetlands across all three plant communities (Figure S1). The response range of ecosystem function indicators was concentrated between −40% and 100%, with the majority showing positive responses (Figure 1a). These findings support our first hypothesis. Specifically, the average increase in individual ecosystem functions in riparian wetlands reached 29.2% ± 11.7%. In terms of biomass, the response value of plant productivity (AGB) reached as high as 45%, indicating that the riparian habitat significantly promoted the accumulation of aboveground biomass. Regarding nutrient pools, carbon (C) and phosphorus (P) cycling exhibited synergistic positive responses. Specifically, soil organic carbon (SOC), microbial biomass carbon (MBC), total phosphorus (TP), and microbial biomass phosphorus (MBP) were all significantly higher in riparian wetlands than in lacustrine wetlands. In contrast, total carbon (TC) showed no significant difference, suggesting that the labile fractions of the carbon pool are more sensitive to habitat changes. In sharp contrast to the aforementioned trends, the majority of indicators related to the nitrogen (N) pool (TN, NH4+-N, NO3−-N, and MBN) exhibited negative responses. This indicates that lacustrine wetlands possess an advantage in terms of inorganic nitrogen and microbial nitrogen storage. This imbalance in elemental stoichiometry was further reflected in enzyme activities: urease (S-Ure) and β-glucosidase (S-β-GC) predominantly exhibited positive responses, whereas alkaline phosphatase (S-AKP) and the C:N ratio showed negative responses. Calculations based on the averaging method [25] further confirmed that ecosystem multifunctionality (EMF) was significantly higher in riparian wetlands (0.42 ± 0.05) than in lacustrine wetlands (0.32 ± 0.02, p = 0.0008; Figure 1b). The significant decline in EMF in lacustrine wetlands may be attributed to the combined effects of hydrodynamics and environmental stress. First, differences in hydrological connectivity serve as a key driver. As typical lentic (static) systems, lakes are characterized by weak water fluidity and slow exchange rates. Consequently, their self-purification capacity and nutrient cycling efficiency are significantly lower than those of lotic (flowing) riparian wetlands [26,27]. Secondly, environmental filtering pressures exacerbated functional degradation. Lacustrine wetlands exhibit high sensitivity to climate change and anthropogenic disturbances. This is particularly evident in arid and semi-arid regions, where drastic water level fluctuations and volume shrinkage result in severe habitat fragmentation and degradation [28]. This habitat instability constrains community assembly and functional performance, thereby leading to the overall decline in ecosystem multifunctionality (EMF) in lacustrine wetlands.

2.2. Variations in Microbial Alpha Diversity Across Wetland Types

Alpha diversity analysis revealed that, distinct from plants, different wetland types exhibited differentiated characteristics in shaping microbial communities (Figure S2). In general, bacterial communities exhibited a trend where the Chao1 and Shannon indices were higher in lacustrine wetlands than in riparian wetlands (Figure 2a). Inferred from the community structure (Figure S3a), this discrepancy may be related to community evenness. In lacustrine habitats, dominant phyla such as Proteobacteria and Actinobacteriota showed a relatively balanced distribution, and nitrogen-cycling taxa like Nitrospirota tended to be enriched, which contributes to maintaining higher diversity. Conversely, riparian habitats were often characterized by the dominance of a few select phyla, which likely limited overall diversity to some extent. In contrast, the diversity patterns of fungi and archaea appeared to be significantly regulated by spatial location (Figure 2b,c). At the Phragmites australis and Typha orientalis sites, lacustrine wetlands likely maintained higher diversity than riparian wetlands, possibly due to the ‘co-dominance’ distribution of fungal classes such as Sordariomycetes and higher archaeal evenness. However, at the Argentina anserina site, this trend was reversed, with riparian wetlands exhibiting higher fungal diversity and archaeal richness. Despite the aforementioned fluctuations in archaeal diversity across habitats, the core community composition remained generally conservative. Specifically, Nitrososphaeraceae and Bathyarchaeia dominated across all sampling sites (Figure S3c).

2.3. Drivers of Microbial Community Composition in Wetlands

The associations between soil variables and microbial community composition were visualized in the NMDS ordination plot (Figure 3). Bacterial (ANOSIM: R = 0.656, p < 0.001), fungal (ANOSIM: R = 0.581, p < 0.001), and archaeal (ANOSIM: R = 0.514, p < 0.001) communities exhibited significant clustering by site. The spatial distribution of bacterial communities was primarily influenced by soil factors such as TN, TP, SOC, and EC, while also showing significant correlations with microbial biomass indicators (MBN, MBP, MBC) and pH (Figure 3a). Specifically, the gradients of TN and TP corresponded to the separation of the RA community. SOC and soil clay content (Clay, Fine) jointly drove the leftward shift in the LT community, whereas higher salinity (EC) was the primary factor causing the distinct clustering of the RT community in the ordination space. The spatial distribution of fungal communities was primarily driven by soil factors such as SOC, TN, and EC. Additionally, significant correlations were observed with microbial biomass indicators including MBN, MBP, and MBC (Figure 3b). Specifically, the gradients of SOC and TN corresponded to the separation of the RA community, suggesting that Potentilla anserina in the riparian zone shapes a characteristic fungal community under high organic carbon and nitrogen conditions. Factors such as TC, TP, and pH generally aligned with the distribution vectors of the LP and RP communities. In contrast, higher salinity (EC) and microbial biomass (MBC, MBP) were the primary drivers causing the rightward shift and distinct clustering of the RT community in the ordination space. Furthermore, SOC, EC, TN, and pH were identified as the dominant factors influencing the spatial variation in archaeal communities. Specifically, the gradients of TN and MBC generally aligned with the shifting direction of the LE community, suggesting that Potentilla anserina in lacustrine wetlands (LE) harbors a distinct archaeal community characterized by high nitrogen and microbial biomass carbon conditions. Meanwhile, SOC, pH, and soil clay content (Clay) jointly drove the clustering of RA, RP, and RT sites toward the right side, indicating that high organic carbon, elevated pH, and fine-textured soil favor the enrichment of characteristic archaeal taxa in these sites. In contrast, higher salinity (EC) corresponded to the negative shift in LP and LT communities along NMDS1, serving as the primary cause for their separation from other sites in the ordination space.

2.4. Relationships Between Soil Factors, Microbial Community Structure, and Soil Enzyme Activities

Based on Mantel tests, the responses of different microbial taxa and extracellular enzymes to soil factors varied significantly (Table 1). Bacterial community structure was primarily influenced by soil moisture (SM, R = 0.37 *) and clay content (Clay, R = 0.43 *), suggesting that water status and fine-grained structure are key drivers of bacterial spatial heterogeneity. Fungal communities were significantly correlated only with soil salinity (EC, R = 0.42 *), indicating their high sensitivity to salinity and soluble ion gradients. Archaeal communities were jointly controlled by pH (R = 0.60 *) and EC (R = 0.35 **), highlighting that acidity/alkalinity and salinity serve as the core chemical environments determining archaeal composition. In contrast, enzyme activities were significantly correlated with SM (R = 0.27 *), Fine (R = 0.30 *), and Gravel (R = 0.29 *), appearing to be more regulated by physical structural characteristics such as soil moisture, fine particles, and gravel. Overall, bacterial communities align with the ‘Moisture–Clay’ gradient, while fungi and archaea are driven by the ‘Salinity–pH’ gradient. In contrast, enzyme activity patterns primarily respond to soil physical structure, reflecting the differential sensitivity of distinct functional groups to environmental gradients within the same wetland system.

2.5. Biodiversity-Ecosystem Multifunctionality Relationships Across Wetland Types

Structural equation modeling (SEM) showed that the model accounted for 74% of the variation in EMF in riparian wetlands, demonstrating a good model fit (Figure 4a). Salinity (EC) had a significant positive effect on plant diversity, whereas soil moisture significantly inhibited enzyme activity. This suggests that abiotic factors influence microbial systems primarily by altering vegetation and enzymatic processes. Among all biotic factors, only microbial diversity emerged as the strongest positive predictor on EMF, with the highest path coefficient (0.85 *). In contrast, the direct effects of plant diversity and enzyme activity were insignificant; instead, they primarily exerted indirect effects mediated by microbial diversity. Therefore, riparian wetlands exhibit a typical ‘biologically associated’ pattern, wherein microbial diversity serves as the key biological indicator closely associated with EMF intensity. The SEM for lacustrine wetlands (Figure 4b) demonstrated a higher explained variance for EMF (R2 = 0.85), yet the control pathways differed significantly from those in the riparian zone. Both salinity (EC) and soil moisture exerted significant positive effects on plant diversity. Consequently, plant diversity emerged as a pivotal node for mediating abiotic signals, whereas the direct impacts of abiotic factors on microbial diversity and enzyme activity were relatively weak. In contrast to riparian wetlands, the pathways from plant diversity, microbial diversity, and enzyme activity to EMF were all statistically insignificant. This indicates that EMF in lacustrine wetlands has a lower dependence on these biotic factors. Instead, it is co-regulated by comprehensive abiotic factors (e.g., soil structure) and multiple indirect effects, exhibiting a typical ‘abiotic-context-dominated’ regulation pattern.

3. Discussion

3.1. Effects of Soil Factors on Soil Microbial Community Composition and Diversity

Plant diversity and soil (micro-) environmental heterogeneity have long been recognized as key factors in maintaining highly diverse microbial communities [29]. Our study reveals that lacustrine and riparian wetlands drive soil microbial alpha diversity patterns by reshaping dominant taxa and evenness. Specifically, bacterial diversity was consistently higher in lacustrine wetlands than in riparian wetlands across the three plant communities. In contrast, fungal and archaeal communities exhibited spatial heterogeneity characterized by Argentina anserina precedence in the riparian zone and Phragmites australis/Typha orientalis dominance in lacustrine wetlands. In lacustrine wetlands, relatively stable hydrological processes, combined with higher nutrient deposition and microhabitat heterogeneity [30], facilitate the synergistic coexistence and functional complementarity of multi-phyla/multi-class taxa (e.g., Proteobacteria, Sordariomycetes, and Nitrososphaeraceae), thereby enhancing species richness and evenness [31]. Alternatively, the observed pattern may be attributed to stronger hydrological disturbance and environmental filtering in riverine wetlands [32], where such disturbances tend to result in the ‘monopolization’ of the community by a few tolerant or unclassified taxa, consequently constraining diversity and functional differentiation [33]. However, microorganisms from different kingdoms face distinct limiting factors along this landscape gradient. The bacterial response, primarily driven by resource availability and micro-environmental heterogeneity [34], is superimposed upon the responses of fungi and archaea, which are more heavily constrained by salinity (EC), pH, and substrate type. This interplay ultimately shapes the observed comprehensive pattern of ‘asynchronous diversity responses among different microbial kingdoms within the same landscape gradient’ [35,36].

The results indicate that soil environmental factors had significant effects on microbial communities and extracellular enzyme activities (Table 1). Bacterial community structure was primarily influenced by soil moisture (SM) and clay content (Clay) (R = 0.37 * and R = 0.43 *, respectively). Consistent with these findings, Guo et al. and Li et al., in their analyses of soil physical structure and microbial communities, observed that soil structure (e.g., particle size distribution) shapes bacterial composition by affecting habitat stability and resource supply [37,38]. In contrast, fungal community composition was primarily influenced by soil salinity (EC) (R = 0.42 *). Numerous studies have found that increased salinity (EC) exerts selective pressure on fungal communities, leading to the enrichment of certain salt-tolerant taxa while simultaneously reducing community diversity [39]. Archaeal community structure was primarily influenced by pH (R = 0.60 **) and salinity (EC, R = 0.35 **), indicating that soil salinity and alkalinity represent the core chemical environment determining archaeal composition [40]. Archaea typically inhabit relatively extreme environments [41], primarily due to their strong adaptability to soil chemical environments, particularly pH and salinity [42]. In contrast, extracellular enzyme activities (EEAs) were susceptible to regulation by a wider range of factors, showing significant correlations with soil moisture (R = 0.27 *), fine particles (R = 0.30 *), and gravel (R = 0.29 *). This indicates that water availability and soil particle composition are critical factors controlling enzyme activity. Similarly, Gao et al. and Wang et al., in their investigations of soil environmental factors and EEAs, observed that variations in moisture and soil texture directly affect microbial metabolic activities, particularly the enzymatic activities of bacteria and fungi [43,44]. Similarly, Huang and Długosz et al. found that variations in the proportions of fine particles and gravel affect the diffusion and adsorption of enzymes, thereby influencing organic matter decomposition and nutrient cycling in the soil [45].

NMDS ordination analysis revealed that bacterial, fungal, and archaeal communities exhibited distinct spatial heterogeneity across different wetland types or plant communities (Figure 3). The ANOSIM results further corroborated the spatial differentiation patterns of microorganisms under the same plant communities across different wetlands. Specifically, bacterial (R = 0.656, p < 0.001), fungal (R = 0.581, p < 0.001), and archaeal (R = 0.514, p < 0.001) communities all exhibited significant between-group differences and within-group clustering. Soil physicochemical properties serve as key factors driving the spatial heterogeneity of microbial communities [46]. Specifically, variations in water status, nutrient availability, and soil texture collectively constitute the primary environmental filtering mechanism shaping the spatial distribution of these communities [47]. In this study, the spatial distribution of bacterial communities was primarily driven by environmental factors including total nitrogen (TN), total phosphorus (TP), soil organic carbon (SOC), and salinity (EC), and it exhibited significant positive correlations with microbial biomass indices (MBN, MBP, and MBC) (Figure 3a). Consistent with these findings, Shu and Wang et al. observed in farmland and grassland ecosystems that soil nutrient status and physicochemical properties shape the spatial heterogeneity of bacterial communities by regulating microbial biomass [48]. The results indicate that TN and TP gradients correspond to the spatial separation of RA communities, suggesting that bacterial communities exhibit characteristic spatial distribution patterns under conditions of high nitrogen and phosphorus [20]. Furthermore, bacterial communities may generate high species richness and diversity through mechanisms of resource competition and niche differentiation, which in turn influence their distributional differences [49]. In contrast, the RT communities clustered independently in the ordination space, influenced by high salinity (EC). This is primarily attributed to the selective filtering effect of salinity on bacterial communities—a conclusion also reached by Rath and Zhang et al. in their investigations of microbial community differentiation and soil environmental factors [50]. The spatial distribution patterns of fungal communities were primarily driven synergistically by environmental factors such as SOC, TN, and EC (Figure 3b). Specifically, SOC and TN gradients corresponded significantly to the spatial separation of RA communities. This suggests that the rhizosphere in the riparian Argentina anserina zone, characterized by high carbon and nitrogen levels, fosters specific fungal community compositions [51]. Consistent with this, Chen et al. observed in temperate and alpine shrub ecosystems that SOC and TN significantly influence the large-scale distribution patterns of soil fungal communities [52]. This also aligns with the laboratory simulation experiments conducted by Zhang et al. regarding nitrogen removal efficiency in riparian zones [52,53]. It is noteworthy that, compared to bacterial communities, fungal and archaeal communities exhibited more complex response patterns in high-salinity and high-organic-carbon environments [54]. This is primarily attributed to the stronger osmoregulation capabilities and physiological adaptation mechanisms to long-term water stress possessed by fungi and archaea [55], which allow for higher potential for niche differentiation under extreme environmental conditions [56].

3.2. Effects of Soil Environmental Variables, Microbial Community Structure, and Diversity on Ecosystem Functions

Compared to lacustrine wetlands, the ecosystem functioning of riverine wetlands is 29.2% ± 11.7% higher, with particularly prominent contributions from aboveground plant productivity and soil carbon and phosphorus pools (Figure 1a). This may be primarily attributed to the effects of hydrological heterogeneity on soil C–N–P stocks and multifunctionality patterns [57]. The riverine zone undergoes seasonal flooding [32], which accelerates the turnover of carbon and phosphorus biogeochemical cycles [48], thereby maintaining a “high nutrient–high output” functional pattern in the wetland [58,59]. In contrast, TN, NH4+-N, NO3−-N, and MBN generally exhibited negative responses in riverine wetlands, characterized by inorganic nitrogen pools and microbial biomass nitrogen being significantly lower than those in lacustrine wetlands. This difference stems from the high mobility and vulnerability of nitrogen caused by stronger runoff leaching [60], denitrification, and groundwater–surface water exchange in riverine wetlands [61].

The results of the ecosystem multifunctionality index (EMF), calculated using the averaging method [25], showed that the EMF of riverine wetlands (0.42 ± 0.05) was significantly higher than that of lacustrine wetlands (0.32 ± 0.02, p = 0.0008). This indicates that riverine wetlands are capable of simultaneously maintaining multiple soil and vegetation functions at higher levels to a greater extent. The averaging method essentially represents the arithmetic mean of standardized individual functions. Therefore, an increase in EMF signifies not the isolated enhancement of a single function [25], but rather the overall ‘elevation’ of multiple key functions. This aligns with the conceptual definition of multifunctionality as ‘multiple functions simultaneously reaching moderate-to-high levels’ [14]. Examining the responses of individual functions reveals that indicators related primarily to carbon and phosphorus pools—such as AGB, SOC, MBC, TP, and MBP—were generally higher in riverine wetlands than in lacustrine wetlands. In contrast, only nitrogen-related functions (TN, NH4+-N, NO3−-N and MBN) exhibited negative responses. This implies that in the calculation of EMF, the substantial enhancements in carbon pools, phosphorus pools, and plant productivity in riverine wetlands were sufficient to offset the negative effects of declined nitrogen functions, thereby resulting in a higher overall level of multifunctionality [62]. Similarly, in a controlled nitrogen addition experiment on the semi-arid Loess Plateau, Zhang et al. found that trade-offs among individual functions jointly maintain the relative balance of grassland ecosystem multifunctionality [63]. A similar pattern of ‘partial functional decline but overall EMF increase’ has also been observed in dryland, grassland, and wetland systems [64]. This phenomenon is interpreted as the result of ecosystems offsetting local functional limitations by enhancing carbon storage, productivity, and specific microbial functions [65]. In this study, hydrodynamic forces and flooding-recession processes in riverine wetlands facilitated the deposition of suspended organic matter and fine particles from upstream channels [66], thereby enhancing the accumulation of soil organic carbon (SOC), total phosphorus (TP), and microbial biomass C and P [67]. Simultaneously, periodic hydrological disturbances and nutrient pulse inputs stimulated higher vegetation productivity and specific enzyme activities [68], collectively elevating the foundation of ecosystem multifunctionality across carbon, phosphorus, and productivity dimensions [69]. In contrast, lacustrine wetlands were characterized by relatively stable hydrology and stronger matter retention [70], which favored nutrient ‘conservation’ and environmental homogenization [71]. However, at the scale of this study, these traits did not translate into higher multifunctionality compared to riverine wetlands. Instead, they presented a pattern of relatively balanced functional structure but with a lower average magnitude [72]. These results align with recent research on wetland and riparian multifunctionality: multifunctionality does not simply peak in ‘stable environments,’ but rather reaches its maximum in spatial units characterized by moderate hydrological disturbance, material inputs, and high micro-environment heterogeneity [73].

3.3. Mechanisms Underlying Ecosystem Multifunctionality Across Wetland Types

The SEM model effectively revealed the driving mechanisms of ecosystem multifunctionality (EMF) in riverine wetlands, accounting for 74% of the variance. Path analysis indicated that abiotic factors indirectly regulated EMF through multiple cascading effects: salinity acted as a key regulator of plant diversity, while soil moisture significantly inhibited enzyme activity. In contrast, Qu et al., in their investigation of drought effects on soil microbes and extracellular enzyme activities, found that drought significantly reduced soil enzyme activity [74]. This is primarily attributed to the significant sensitivity of enzyme activity to soil moisture. Enzymes typically exhibit high catalytic activity within an optimal range of soil moisture, whereas excessively high or low moisture levels exert inhibitory effects on their activity [75]. Crucially, this study confirmed the mediating role of plant communities, whereby environmental factors further determine the distribution patterns of microbial communities by reshaping plant diversity [5]. This finding strongly supports the hypothesis that abiotic factors indirectly regulate microbial diversity and EMF via plant–enzyme processes [57]. Notably, microbial diversity exerted an exceptionally strong direct correlation on EMF (0.85 **), emerging as the major biological predictor. In contrast, plant diversity and enzyme activity primarily played indirect roles by regulating microbial communities. This further corroborates that ecosystem functioning in riparian wetlands exhibits a typical ‘biologically associated’ pattern [76].

In lacustrine wetland ecosystems, salinity (EC) and soil water content served as critical abiotic factors, exerting a significant positive drive on plant diversity. Conversely, the influence of abiotic factors on microbial diversity and enzyme activity was relatively weak. This suggests that plant diversity functions as a key hub, receiving abiotic environmental signals and transmitting them upwards [77]. Compared to riverine ecosystems, EMF in lacustrine wetlands was not solely dependent on the direct environmental responses of microorganisms. Instead, it exhibited a typical ‘abiotic context-dominated’ pattern: environmental factors first filtered plant functional traits or reshaped community structure, subsequently regulating belowground ecosystem functions through cascading effects [5]. Jayaramaiah et al. and Ma et al., through both laboratory controlled experiments and field observations, support the notion that ‘community structure and functional traits influence ecosystem multifunctionality via interactions with microorganisms and functional groups’ [5,78].

Fundamental divergences exist in EMF regulatory pathways between wetland types: riverine EMF is strongly associated with microbial diversity, while lacustrine systems follow an environmental filtering paradigm where abiotic factors indirectly modulate EMF by reshaping plant communities. Although our assessment relied on taxonomic richness, the selected species (Argentina anserina, Phragmites australis, and Typha orientalis) represent distinct functional groups whose varied traits likely influenced the observed soil-microbial patterns. Furthermore, we prioritized 14 empirical indicators, including rate-limiting enzymes for C, N, and P cycling (S-β-GC, S-Ure, and S-AKP), to reflect realized ecosystem performance. While functional gene analysis was not performed, these measured outputs provide robust evidence of functional integrity. Future research integrating trait-based metrics and metagenomic functional profiles will further disentangle the molecular mechanisms driving wetland EMF across diverse ecosystem types.

4. Materials and Methods

4.1. Study Area, Experimental Design, and Sample Collection

In June 2024, soil samples were collected from three plant communities (Argentina anserina, Phragmites australis, and Typha orientalis) in two wetlands located in the central Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region: (1) a lacustrine wetland (121°18′34.26″ E, 44°22′50.33″ N) and (2) a riparian wetland (121°51′48″ E, 44°37′40″ N). Both wetlands are situated in central Inner Mongolia and share similar climatic conditions, characterized by a mean annual precipitation of 350–470 mm (concentrated mainly in summer) and a mean annual temperature of −2 to 2 °C. While direct quantitative measurements of water residence time and sediment transport were limited by field conditions, soil moisture (SM) and salinity (EC) were adopted as integrative proxies for hydrological regimes and environmental filtering pressures, as they effectively reflect the cumulative impacts of hydrological connectivity and evaporation in these semi-arid systems.

For each plant community, a large plot (10 m × 10 m) was established, with three subplots (0.5 m × 0.5 m) set along the diagonal as replicates. Site characteristics, including sampling time, location, altitude, coordinates, and habitat type, were recorded. Within each subplot, we measured species composition, cluster abundance, reproductive branch height, and vegetative branch height [26]. For biomass determination, wetland plants were harvested, oven-dried at 105 °C for 2 h to deactivate enzymes, and subsequently dried at 65 °C to a constant weight to obtain aboveground biomass. Plant community composition was determined based on the importance value (IV), calculated using relative dry weight, relative height, and relative cluster abundance. After clearing the surface vegetation, soil samples were collected from a depth of 0–10 cm. Roots and stones were removed. For microbial analysis, soil subsamples were collected in sterile centrifuge tubes, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen in the field, and stored at −80 °C in the laboratory for microbial amplicon sequencing. The remaining soil was divided into two portions: one was air-dried for the analysis of soil physicochemical properties and enzyme activities, while the other was stored at 4 °C and transported to the laboratory for the determination of available nitrogen.

4.2. Determination of Soil Physicochemical Properties

Soil moisture content was determined gravimetrically by oven-drying. Soil pH and salinity (EC) were measured using a benchtop pH meter (FE28, Mettler Toledo, Switzerland) and a conductivity meter (FE38, Mettler Toledo, Switzerland), respectively. Soil samples were pre-treated with boiling hydrogen peroxide H2O2 and hydrochloric acid HCl solutions to remove organic matter and carbonates, respectively. Subsequently, soil particle size distribution was determined using a laser particle size analyzer (Analysette 22 NanoTec, Fritsch, Germany). Relevant indices related to the cycling and sequestration of soil carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus were determined. Soil total carbon (TC) and total nitrogen (TN) contents were determined using an elemental analyzer (Vario MACRO cube, Elementar, Germany). For soil total phosphorus (TP) determination, samples were digested with a mixture of HNO3, HCl, and HF using a microwave digestion system (Multiwave 3000, Anton Paar, Austria), and subsequently analyzed using Inductively Coupled Plasma Optical Emission Spectroscopy (ICP-OES; Optima 8000, PerkinElmer, USA). Soil ammonium nitrogen NH4+-N and nitrate nitrogen NO3−-N were extracted from fresh soil samples with a KCl solution and quantified using a continuous flow analyzer (AA3, SEAL Analytical, Germany). The activities of soil β-glucosidase (S-β-GC), urease (S-Ure), and alkaline phosphatase (S-AKP) were assayed using commercial kits (Solarbio, Beijing, China). Following color development, absorbance was measured using a multi-mode microplate reader (Epoch 2, BioTek, USA). Soil microbial biomass carbon (MBC), nitrogen (MBN), and phosphorus (MBP) were determined using the chloroform fumigation-extraction method.

4.3. Determination of Soil Microbial Properties

Total genomic DNA of the microbial community was extracted from 0.5 g of soil samples using the FastDNA® Spin Kit (MP Biomedicals, Santa Ana, CA, USA). The quality, concentration, and purity of the extracted DNA were subsequently determined. PCR amplification was performed in triplicate for each sample. The bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified using primers 338F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′). For archaea, the 16S rRNA gene was targeted using primers 349F (5′-GYGCASCAGKCGMGAAW-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACVSGGGTATCTAAT-3′). The fungal internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region was amplified using primers ITS1F (5′-CTTGGTCATTTAGAGGAAGTAA-3′) and ITS2R (5′-GCTGCGTTC TTCATCGATGC-3′). PCR products from triplicate reactions were pooled, purified, and quantified. Subsequently, the purified amplicons were pooled in equimolar amounts based on sequencing requirements. After library construction, sequencing was performed on the Illumina MiSeq PE300 platform by Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) to characterize the community composition and diversity distribution of each sampling site.

4.4. Assessment of Ecosystem Multifunctionality Index

Drawing upon previous studies and considering the specific characteristics of wetlands, 14 ecosystem function indicators were selected. These indicators are closely associated with plant productivity, carbon (C), nitrogen (N), and phosphorus (P) pools, and nutrient transformation. Specifically, plant aboveground biomass (AGB) served as the proxy for plant productivity. Soil carbon pools were represented by total carbon (TC), soil organic carbon (SOC), and microbial biomass carbon (MBC). Soil nitrogen pools included total nitrogen (TN), microbial biomass nitrogen (MBN), ammonium nitrogen (NH4+-N), and nitrate nitrogen (NO3−-N). Soil phosphorus pools consisted of total phosphorus (TP) and microbial biomass phosphorus (MBP). Finally, soil urease (S-Ure), alkaline phosphatase (S-AKP), β-glucosidase (S-β-GC), and the carbon-to-nitrogen ratio (C:N) were selected as indicators of soil nutrient transformation rates. We quantified the ecosystem multifunctionality (EMF) index using the averaging approach, which computes the arithmetic mean of the standardized Z-scores for all measured ecosystem functions [14]. This metric was prioritized to maintain comparability with foundational studies in dryland and wetland ecology [13,14] and to preserve the continuous resolution of functional variations, avoiding the potential biases and information loss inherent in arbitrary threshold selection [79].

4.5. Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.2). To evaluate the effects of wetland type and plant community on soil properties, enzyme activities, and ecosystem functions, two-way analyses of variance (ANOVAs) were conducted, followed by Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) post hoc tests. Specifically, partial eta-squared (η2) was calculated to quantify effect sizes, and Tukey’s HSD was employed to rigorously control the family-wise Type I error rate during multiple comparisons. For microbial community analysis, alpha diversity (Chao1 and Shannon indices) was assessed using the phyloseq package, while beta diversity was analyzed via the vegan package. Non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarity visualized variations in ASV composition. The significance of environmental vectors (p < 0.05) fitted onto the NMDS space was tested using 999 permutations via the envfit function. Differences in community composition were identified using Analysis of Similarities (ANOSIM) and Similarity Percentages (SIMPER) analysis (999 permutations). Mantel and partial Mantel tests evaluated Spearman rank correlations between distance matrices and environmental factors while controlling for covariates. Finally, Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) was constructed to disentangle the pathways by which soil factors influence EMF via changes in plant species richness and soil microbial communities. To ensure model robustness and parsimony, we pre-screened variables for multicollinearity (VIF < 10) [80] and constructed an a priori structural equation model based on established ecological frameworks, selecting the final topology with the lowest Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and satisfactory goodness-of-fit indices [81]. To prevent overfitting due to the finite sample size, we strictly controlled model complexity by aggregating collinear soil variables into composite indices. This simplification maintained a sample-size-to-parameter ratio > 5:1, satisfying the recommended threshold for robust SEM estimation [82].

5. Conclusions

In summary, our study reveals divergent EMF regulatory pathways between riverine and lacustrine wetlands, driven by microbial diversity and environmental filtering through plant communities, respectively. While these findings provide critical mechanistic insights, it should be noted that they are based on two representative models (riparian vs. lacustrine) within a specific semi-arid context. Therefore, our conclusions should be interpreted with caution regarding their broader generalizability. Future research spanning more diverse wetland categories and wider geographic gradients is essential to further validate the universality of these ecological patterns across the semi-arid landscape.
