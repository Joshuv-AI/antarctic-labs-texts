Reconstructing calcium conductance in MCUb illuminates its molecular design for tuning the mitochondrial calcium uniporter
Authors: I-Chi Lee, Po-Hsuan Lai, Chen-Wei Tsai, Yung-Chi Tu, Yu-Chen Huang, Wei-Chen Chen, Ming-Feng Tsai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe mitochondrial Ca2+ uniporter mediates mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake to regulate cellular bioenergetics, Ca2+ signaling and survival, but excessive activity triggers Ca2+ overload and tissue injury. Cells counter this threat by expressing MCUb, a paralog of the uniporter’s pore-forming MCU subunit, to attenuate uniporter activity. Despite harboring the conserved Ca2+-coordinating DIME motif, MCUb paradoxically lacks conductance, a defining yet enigmatic feature underlying its uniporter-inhibitory role. Here, we demonstrate that MCUb’s non-conductivity stems from its inability to bind EMRE, a subunit essential for uniporter function, and that its N-terminal domain (NTD) exerts autoinhibition. Reinstating EMRE binding and relieving NTD-mediated inhibition rebuild Ca2+ conductance in MCUb, reaching ~80% of MCU activity. Wild-type MCUb exhibits ~30% of the inhibitory capacity of a pore-disrupting E249A variant, indicating that MCUb is a modest, rather than potent, negative regulator. These findings reveal how MCU-MCUb paralog divergence endows the uniporter with regulatory plasticity to fine-tune mitochondrial Ca2+ homeostasis.Subject terms: Permeation and transport, Ion channels, Membrane biophysics, Structural biology, Biophysics

Introduction

The mitochondrial Ca2+ transport system, located in the inner mitochondrial membrane (IMM), plays crucial roles in shaping intracellular Ca2+ signaling, regulating oxidative phosphorylation, and controlling cell death pathways1–5. In most tissues, its core components include the mitochondrial Ca2+ uniporter (hereafter, the “uniporter”)1–5, which mediates Ca2+ uptake from the intermembrane space (IMS) into the matrix, and the mitochondrial Na+/Ca2+ exchanger, TMEM656–9, which returns Ca2+ to the IMS. The physiological importance of this system is underscored by evidence that its dysregulation contributes to a wide range of pathological conditions, including neurodegeneration, heart failure, ischemia-reperfusion injury, and cancer2,3,10.

The uniporter is a multi-subunit, highly selective Ca2+ channel complex whose activity is tightly regulated by cytosolic Ca2+ signals11–17. Over the past decade, biochemical, functional, and structural studies18–28 have elucidated the molecular mechanisms underlying this Ca2+-dependent regulation (Fig. 1A). Under resting cellular conditions, the regulatory subunit MICU1, either as a homodimer or in complex with MICU2 within the IMS, closes the uniporter by physically occluding the IMS entrance of the pore, which is formed by a tetrameric assembly of MCU subunits spanning the IMM. Upon cytosolic Ca2+ elevation, Ca2+ binding to the MICU dimer induces conformational changes that displace MICU1 from the pore entrance, thereby opening the channel and permitting Ca2+ influx into the matrix.

Throughout this gating process, the auxiliary subunit EMRE (also known as SMDT1) plays a critical dual role28 (Fig. 1A). First, EMRE remains tightly bound to both MCU and MICU1, serving as a molecular tether that prevents MICU1 dissociation from the uniporter complex when Ca2+ disrupts MICU1–MCU interactions. This tethering enables MICU1 to rapidly reclose the uniporter once cytosolic Ca2+ signals subside. Second, EMRE binding to MCU is required for maintaining MCU in a transport-competent conformation. This ensures that EMRE-free uniporter subcomplexes, potentially lacking MICUs and proper Ca2+ regulation, remain inactive and unable to mediate unregulated Ca2+ uptake that could otherwise lead to mitochondrial Ca2+ overload and subsequent cell death.

This study focuses on another layer of uniporter regulation mediated by the MCUb subunit, a paralog of MCU that shares ~50% sequence identity (Fig. 1B) but lacks Ca2+-conducting activity29. When incorporated into the uniporter complex, MCUb acts as a negative regulator of Ca2+ uptake29–31. It plays important regulatory roles in cardiac and skeletal muscle metabolism30,32,33, platelet activation34, and macrophage differentiation35. Moreover, MCUb expression is acutely upregulated during myocardial infarction30,36, where it protects against cardiac injury by limiting excessive mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake under pathological elevations of cytosolic Ca2+. Despite its physiological importance across multiple tissues, the molecular properties and mechanisms underlying MCUb function remain poorly understood.

A critical knowledge gap concerns how MCUb prevents Ca2+ conduction despite retaining the conserved DIME motif (MCU residues D261–E264 in transmembrane helix 2, TM2, corresponding to MCUb residues D246–E249; residue numbering refers to the human sequence throughout), which forms the Ca2+ selectivity filter of MCU14,15,20,21. Addressing this question is fundamental to understanding how MCUb suppresses uniporter activity. A few mechanisms have been proposed. First, because two charged residues in MCU’s TM1 (R252 and E257) near the DIME motif are replaced by neutral residues in MCUb (W237 and V242, respectively; red dots in Fig. 1B), it was suggested that these substitutions create altered surface electrostatics that disfavor Ca2+ permeation29. Second, homology modeling predicts that MCUb forms a pore with a widened IMS entrance, potentially disrupting Ca2+ coordination and permeation2,37. Despite these proposals, attempts to engineer a Ca2+-conducting MCUb variant have thus far been unsuccessful, indicating that the key molecular determinants underlying its non-conductive nature remain unidentified.

Beyond its non-conductivity, two additional aspects of MCUb’s molecular properties warrant further investigation. First, the interactions of MCUb with other uniporter subunits are not well defined. Although MCUb clearly associates with MCU29,30, whether it interacts with EMRE is uncertain, as conflicting results have been reported30,36. Moreover, one study30 proposed that MCUb does not bind MICU1 or MICU2, but this is unexpected given that MCUb retains the conserved MICU1-interacting residues present on the IMS surface of MCU (MCUb residues Y243, S244, D246, and I247, corresponding to MCU residues Y258, S259, D261, and I262)20. Second, the degree to which MCUb inhibits uniporter activity also remains unresolved. Early work suggested that MCUb completely abolishes uniporter activity29, whereas a more recent study31 reported that MCUb reduces activity by roughly half when present in the complex at a 2:2 MCU:MCUb stoichiometry.

In this work, through systematic construction of MCU-MCUb chimeras, we uncover that MCUb’s lack of Ca2+ conductance stems from the absence of EMRE interaction. Engineering EMRE binding capability converts MCUb into a Ca2+-conducting channel. Additional modifications to the N-terminal domain (NTD), which we find to exert autoinhibition, yield an MCUb variant exhibiting ~80% of wild-type (WT) MCU activity. Biochemical and functional analyses reveal how MCUb assembles with other uniporter subunits and demonstrate that MCUb serves as a modest negative regulator, attenuating overall uniporter activity by ~30% relative to a potent, pore-disrupting mutant. Collectively, these findings elucidate the molecular principles by which MCUb fine-tunes the physiologically critical process of mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake.

Results

Chimeric analysis identifies MCUb structural elements underlying its non-conductive property

MCU and MCUb can be divided into eight distinct structural regions (Table 1), highlighted in different colors in the MCU-MCUb sequence alignment (Fig. 1B) and in the human MCU structure21 (Fig. 1C). The NTR encompasses both the NTD and a mitochondrial targeting sequence (MTS, boxed in Fig. 1B), which are examined in later sections. To identify which regions of MCUb are responsible for its lack of Ca2+ conductance, we generated six chimeric constructs (designated chimeras 1–1 through 1–6; Fig. 2A) in which individual structural regions of MCU were replaced with the corresponding segments from MCUb. Because JML and CTR are short segments (6 and 10 residues, respectively), we combined them with adjacent elements to create chimeras 1–4 (TM2 + JML) and 1–6 (CC2b + CTR), each containing two MCUb-derived regions.

The chimeric constructs were expressed in MCU-knockout (KO) HEK293 cells, and mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake was measured using a standard fluorescence-based assay (Fig. 2B). Briefly, cells were permeabilized with digitonin in the presence of calcium green-5N (CG5N), which reports extramitochondrial Ca2+ concentration ([Ca2+]ex). Upon Ca2+ addition, the fluorescence signal rose sharply, then declined as mitochondria took up Ca2+ via the uniporter, thereby reducing [Ca2+]ex. This uptake was abolished by the potent uniporter inhibitor Ru360. (For clarity, all subsequent traces display only the uptake phase following Ca2+ addition, as boxed in Fig. 2B). As expected, MCU exhibited robust Ca2+ uptake, whereas MCUb showed no detectable uptake. Chimeras 1–3 and 1–5, harboring TM1 and CC2a from MCUb, respectively, lost >95% of activity. Chimera 1–1, containing MCUb’s NTR, exhibited ~50% reduced activity, while the remaining chimeras (1–2, 1–4, and 1–6) retained near-MCU activity. These results demonstrate that sequence differences between MCU and MCUb within TM1 and CC2a are the primary determinants underlying MCUb’s non-conductivity.

We next analyzed the expression levels of the chimeric constructs using Western blotting (Fig. 2C). Transiently expressed MCU, MCUb, and the chimeras all appeared as two bands, with the upper band likely corresponding to MTS-unprocessed proteins. Both bands were detected in whole-cell and mitochondrial lysates, confirming mitochondrial localization (Fig. S1). Accordingly, protein expression (here and throughout) was quantified using the combined intensity of both bands from whole-cell lysates. Although MCUb has been reported to require MCU for expression31, we consistently observed robust MCUb expression in MCU-KO HEK cells at ~50% of MCU levels. This lower expression cannot account for MCUb’s lack of Ca2+ conductance, as chimera 1–6, which exhibited similar expression levels, mediated MCU-level Ca2+ uptake (Fig. 2B). Chimeras 1–1, 1–2, and 1–4 expressed at levels ranging from 0.5 to 1.5-fold of MCU. Importantly, chimeras 1–3 and 1–5 displayed comparable expression to MCU, confirming that their functional deficits (Fig. 2B) are not caused by a lack of expression.

Engineered EMRE interaction reconstitutes Ca2+ conductance in MCUb

To understand how the TM1 and CC2a regions of MCUb prevent channel activity, we generated three MCUb-based chimeras (2–1 through 2–3) in which MCUb’s TM1 (2–1), CC2a (2–2), or both (2–3) were replaced with the corresponding regions from MCU (Fig. 3A). Similar to MCUb, chimeras 2–1 and 2–2 were inactive (Fig. 3B). Strikingly, chimera 2–3 produced measurable Ca2+ uptake, reaching ~12.5% of MCU activity (Fig. 3B). All three chimeras were expressed at levels comparable to MCUb (~50% of MCU; Fig. 3C), demonstrating that the gained Ca2+ conductance in chimera 2–3 is not attributable to increased expression.

What mechanism underlies the gain of conductance in chimera 2–3? Previous structural and functional studies revealed that EMRE contacts MCU primarily through TM1 and CC2a21,38 (Fig. 3D), raising the possibility that substitution of these two segments into MCUb might influence MCUb function by altering EMRE binding. To test this, we first employed co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) experiments to examine how MCUb assembles with other uniporter subunits (Fig. 3E). Consistent with previous reports29,30, immobilized MCUb pulled down both MCU and MCUb (panel i in Fig. 3E), confirming its ability to form homo- and hetero-oligomers. Like MCU20,28, MCUb interacted with MICU1 but not MICU2 (panels ii–iii in Fig. 3E). However, no MCUb-EMRE association was detected (panel iv in Fig. 3E, see also Fig. S2A).

Because EMRE binding is required for MCU to conduct Ca2+, we reasoned that the absence of EMRE interaction might underlie MCUb’s lack of Ca2+ conductance and that MCU’s TM1 and CC2a might restore conductance by conferring EMRE binding. Supporting this hypothesis, chimeras 2–1 and 2–2, which lacked Ca2+ transport activity, also failed to bind EMRE, whereas chimera 2–3, which gained conductance, exhibited robust EMRE association comparable to MCU (Fig. 3F). Further experiments (Fig. S2B) showed that chimera 2–3 failed to mediate mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake in MCU/EMRE double-KO cells, but this deficit was rescued by EMRE co-expression, demonstrating that chimera 2–3 indeed acquires Ca2+ uptake capability through EMRE binding.

Mechanisms by which EMRE binding confers Ca2+ conductance to MCUb

Having established that MCU’s TM1 and CC2a together confer Ca2+-transport capability to MCUb by enabling EMRE binding, we next sought to delineate the individual contributions of these two segments. We previously established that TM1 and CC2a play distinct roles in mediating EMRE binding and MCU activation28,38. CC2a acts primarily as a docking site that anchors the N-terminal soluble region of EMRE (box 1 in Fig. 3D), thereby maintaining tight EMRE association with MCU. TM1 forms extensive hydrophobic contacts with the central portion of EMRE’s transmembrane helix through residues L240, A241, A244, and G248 in MCU and F77 and G81 in EMRE (box 2 in Fig. 3D). This transmembrane interface serves dual functions: it facilitates EMRE binding and enables EMRE to stabilize MCU in a transport-competent conformation (Fig. 1A).

Based on these mechanistic insights, we hypothesized that MCU’s CC2a, when introduced into MCUb, anchors EMRE to MCUb. If this is correct, the requirement for MCU’s CC2a to induce Ca2+ conductance in chimera 2–3 (Fig. 3A, B) should be bypassed by directly fusing EMRE to MCUb. To test this, we generated MCUb-EMRE fusion constructs 3–1 through 3–4 (Fig. 4A). Fusion 3–1 consisted of EMRE fused to the C-terminus of MCUb, while fusions 3–2 to 3–4 were derived from 3–1, with TM1 (3–2), CC2a (3–3), or both (3–4) replaced by the corresponding MCU segments.

Fusions 3–1 and 3–3 exhibited no detectable Ca2+ uptake (Fig. 4B), consistent with the view that MCU’s TM1 is essential not only for EMRE binding but also for EMRE-induced conformational change required for Ca2+ conductance, and therefore cannot be functionally replaced by EMRE tethering alone. In agreement with our hypothesis, fusion 3–2, despite lacking MCU’s CC2a, displayed activity comparable to chimera 2–3, confirming that MCU’s CC2a indeed serves to anchor EMRE to MCUb and suggesting that MCUb’s native CC2a lacks this EMRE-anchoring capability. Notably, fusion 3–4 exhibited higher activity (~20% of MCU levels) than chimera 2–3 (~12.5%) and fusion 3–2 (~10%). Two non-exclusive explanations may account for this enhancement: (1) the combined effects of EMRE fusion and EMRE-CC2a interactions may further strengthen MCUb-EMRE association (Fig. S2C), and (2) fusion 3–4 was expressed at higher levels than 3–2 (Fig. 4C).

We next turned our attention to TM1. MCUb’s TM1 retains hydrophobic residues that form the MCU-EMRE transmembrane interface (box 2 in Fig. 3D), raising the question of why MCUb still requires TM1 from MCU to support EMRE binding. Close examination of the human MCU-EMRE structure (Fig. 3D) revealed previously overlooked molecular interactions near the C-terminal end of TM1: MCU residue R252 forms a hydrogen bond with EMRE residue T82, while MCU residue E257 forms a salt bridge with EMRE residue K86 (box 3 in Fig. 3D). Introducing the R252W/E257V double mutation into MCU abolished both EMRE binding and Ca2+ transport (Fig. S3).

As previously noted29, the residues corresponding to MCU’s R252 and E257 are W237 and V242 in MCUb, respectively. These substitutions cannot support the stabilizing interactions with EMRE described above. To assess the functional significance of these residue differences, we generated fusion constructs 3–5 through 3–7 (Fig. 4A), introducing V242E (3–5), W237R (3–6), or both mutations (3–7) into the 3–1 background. Fusions 3–5 and 3–6 showed no activity, whereas fusion 3–7 mediated Ca2+ uptake comparable to fusion 3–2 (Fig. 4B), with both fusions 3–2 and 3–7 sharing similar expression levels (Fig. 4C). These results demonstrate that MCUb’s TM1 fails to bind EMRE because it lacks the newly identified MCU-EMRE contact surface (box 3 in Fig. 3D).

Further NTD modifications generate MCUb variants with near-MCU Ca2+-transport activity

Chimeric analysis in Fig. 2 showed that replacing MCU’s NTR with the corresponding segment from MCUb (chimera 1–1) reduced MCU-mediated Ca2+ uptake by ~50%. To further investigate the role of this region, which can be divided into the MTS (residues 1–71 in MCU, 1–56 in MCUb) and the NTD (residues 72–195 in MCU, 57–180 in MCUb), we generated chimeras 4–1 through 4–3 (Fig. 5A), in which the NTR (4–1), MTS (4–2), or NTD (4–3) from MCU was introduced into chimera 2–3 (MCUb containing MCU’s TM1 and CC2a; Fig. 3A). Strikingly, chimera 4–1 mediated strong mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake, reaching ~80% of MCU levels (Fig. 5B). This enhancement could be fully attributed to the inclusion of MCU’s NTD, as chimera 4–2 (containing MCU’s MTS) showed activity resembling chimera 2–3, whereas chimera 4–3 (containing MCU’s NTD) recapitulated the high activity of chimera 4–1 (Fig. 5B). All three chimeras were expressed at similar levels (Fig. 5C).

Engineering MCU’s NTD into chimera 2–3 could enhance its activity through two possible mechanisms: (1) MCU’s NTD exerts a potentiating effect, or (2) MCUb’s NTD exerts an inhibitory effect that MCU’s NTD relieves. The latter mechanism seemed more likely, as previous studies showed that deletion of the NTD from MCU does not affect channel function21, indicating that MCU’s NTD is functionally dispensable and therefore unlikely to provide a potentiating effect. To test this, we deleted the NTD of MCUb from chimera 4–2, generating chimera 4–4 (Fig. 5A). This deletion increased activity by ~3-fold to ~40% of MCU levels (Fig. 5B), demonstrating that MCUb’s NTD plays an autoinhibitory role that suppresses channel function. Of note, chimera 4–4 was less active than chimeras 4–1 or 4–3, probably because its expression level was >50% lower (Fig. 5C), presumably as a result of the large NTD truncation.

Finally, we replaced the NTR of MCUb with the corresponding region from MCU, yielding chimera 4–5 (Fig. 5A). This construct exhibited no detectable activity (Fig. 5B), indicating that removal of NTD-mediated autoinhibition alone is insufficient to render MCUb conductive. Thus, the inability to bind EMRE remains the single most critical molecular determinant underlying MCUb’s non-conductive nature. Two additional observations emerged from these experiments. First, chimera 4–5 expressed at levels comparable to MCU (Fig. 5C), in contrast to MCUb, which expressed at ~50% of MCU levels (Fig. 2C), consistent with recent findings that the NTD regulates MCUb expression31. Second, the robust activity of chimera 4–1 enabled us to assess whether its activity is subject to MICU1 regulation. We found that this chimera bound MICU1 in a Ca2+-dependent manner (Fig. S4A), with elevated Ca2+ disrupting this interaction, and that its activity was potentiated by spermine (Fig. S4B), which enhances uniporter function by relieving MICU1-mediated pore occlusion39,40. These results are consistent with MCUb retaining intact MICU1 gating.

MCUb functions as a modest negative regulator of mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake

Having established the molecular basis for MCUb’s non-conductive property, we next sought to determine the extent to which MCUb suppresses uniporter activity. To this end, we compared the inhibitory effect of WT MCUb with that of a pore-disrupting E249A MCUb variant, in which the critical Ca2+-coordinating glutamate within the DIME motif was replaced by alanine. The analogous E264A mutation in MCU abolishes channel function15,24, underscoring the essential role of this residue. We reasoned that E249A MCUb would maximally inhibit mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake, since its incorporation into MCU-MCUb hetero-oligomers would not only disrupt Ca2+ coordination at the selectivity filter but also introduce a hydrophobic side chain into the narrowest region of the aqueous Ca2+ permeation pathway20,21. Thus, comparing WT with E249A MCUb would reveal the relative magnitude of WT MCUb’s inhibitory effect as a fraction of maximal inhibition (see also Discussion).

Accordingly, we transiently expressed WT MCUb, E249A MCUb, or E264A MCU in WT HEK cells. Unexpectedly, mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake remained unchanged two days after transfection (Fig. 6A). This lack of effect was not due to insufficient expression, as quantitative Western blot analysis showed that all constructs were expressed at >2.5-fold molecular excess over endogenous MCU (Fig. 6B and see Fig. S5 for quantification). We reasoned that two intrinsic properties of endogenous MCU homotetramers could account for this observation. First, these tetramers are highly stable and undergo minimal subunit exchange20,21, providing few opportunities for incorporation of newly synthesized MCU or MCUb subunits. Second, they exhibit slow turnover41–43, such that two days after transient expression, pre-existing homotetramers remain largely intact and continue to mediate normal mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake.

To overcome this limitation, we established stable WT HEK cell lines expressing WT MCUb, E249A MCUb, or E264A MCU using lentiviral transduction. Two weeks after viral infection, mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake stabilized at levels reduced by ~15% and ~50% in the presence of WT MCUb and E249A MCUb, respectively (Fig. 6C). Importantly, E264A MCU acts as a dominant-negative mutant, eliminating >95% of mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake (Fig. 6C), confirming that our system achieved the expected range of inhibition. All variants were expressed ~4-fold above endogenous MCU (Fig. 6D), indicating that the stronger inhibitory effects of E249A MCUb and E264A MCU compared to WT MCUb were not due to differential expression. Moreover, expression levels were comparable between transient and stable expression (Fig. 6B, D). Thus, the observed inhibition under stable expression arose not from increased protein abundance but from the extended timeframe, which permits turnover of pre-existing MCU homotetramers and incorporation of WT MCUb, E249A MCUb, or E264A MCU into newly synthesized, endogenous MCU-containing heterotetramers. Given that WT MCUb exerted only ~30% of the inhibitory effect of E249A MCUb (the ratio of ~15% vs. ~50% inhibition of mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake), these results demonstrate that MCUb functions as a modest, rather than potent, negative regulator of mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake.

Discussion

MCUb acts as an important negative regulator of mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake, protecting tissues from injury following ischemic events30,36,44. This physiological function depends on MCUb’s non-conductive property, whose molecular basis has remained unresolved. Here, we elucidate how MCUb prevents channel activity despite harboring the Ca2+-coordinating DIME motif that defines its conductive paralog, MCU. Through systematic protein engineering, we dissected the structural features responsible for MCUb’s silence and, in doing so, learned how to reconstruct channel activity—rebuilding Ca2+ transport to nearly 80% of MCU’s level. Our first major finding is that the inability to bind EMRE constitutes the principal molecular determinant underlying MCUb’s non-conductivity (Fig. 7). Introducing the critical EMRE-interacting regions (TM1 and CC2a) from MCU into MCUb produced the first Ca2+-conducting MCUb variant (chimera 2–3), reaching ~12.5% of MCU activity. We previously demonstrated that the EMRE-CC2a interaction functions primarily to maintain stable EMRE association with MCU38. Consistent with this anchoring role, direct EMRE fusion enabled chimera 3–2, an MCUb-based construct containing MCU’s TM1, to bypass the requirement for MCU’s CC2a, exhibiting activity comparable to 2–3. Why MCUb’s CC2a cannot support EMRE binding remains unclear and will require systematic mutagenesis comparing the CC2a regions of MCU and MCUb to pinpoint the responsible residues.

Mechanism of MCUb non-conductivity and model for uniporter gating.

The schematic at the top summarizes two key molecular properties of MCUb identified in this study: (1) MCUb does not conduct Ca2+ due to its inability to bind EMRE, and (2) the N-terminal domain (NTD) of MCUb exerts an autoinhibitory effect. The schematic at the bottom illustrates a gating model for uniporter channels containing MCU and MCUb with two key features: (1) similar to the uniporter gating model depicted in Fig. 1A, these MCUb-containing channels remain properly gated by MICU1 homodimers or MICU1/MICU2 heterodimers in a Ca2+-dependent manner, and (2) incorporation of MCUb into the uniporter reduces channel activity by ~30%. This model further proposes that MCUb may inhibit the uniporter by decreasing EMRE occupancy within the complex and/or through NTD-mediated autoinhibition. Although uniporter pores comprise four MCU/MCUb subunits, only two are shown for clarity. The pore of MCUb is depicted as closed near the matrix exit, based on previous structural work suggesting that EMRE binding to MCU promotes opening of a luminal gate to permit Ca2+ permeation21.

EMRE plays a critical role in stabilizing MCU in a transport-competent state (Fig. 1A), a function mediated through their transmembrane contacts38. Here, we found that MCUb lacks a previously overlooked transmembrane contact surface required for EMRE binding, formed by MCU residues R252 and E257 engaging EMRE residues T82 and K86 (box 3 in Fig. 3D). The absence of these interactions, due to W237 and V242 occupying the corresponding positions in MCUb, explains why MCUb’s TM1 cannot associate with EMRE. Introducing W237R and V242E mutations into MCUb was sufficient to functionally substitute for MCU’s TM1 in chimera 3–2, yielding a comparably active variant, chimera 3–7. Remarkably, chimera 3–7, containing only these two point mutations and a C-terminal EMRE fusion, appears to represent the minimal protein engineering required to reconstitute Ca2+ transport activity in MCUb.

Unlike MCU’s NTD, which can be deleted without discernible functional consequences21, the NTD of MCUb exerts a pronounced autoinhibitory effect (Fig. 7). This conclusion is supported by three lines of evidence: (1) replacing MCU’s NTD with the corresponding region from MCUb reduces activity by half (Fig. 2B), (2) substituting the NTD of chimera 2–3 with that from MCU increases activity from 12.5 to 80% of MCU levels (chimera 4–3), and (3) deleting MCUb’s NTD from chimera 4–2 (yielding chimera 4–4) elevates activity by ~3-fold to 40% of MCU levels despite markedly reduced expression. MCUb’s NTD inhibits MCU less strongly (by 50%) than it inhibits the MCUb-based chimera 2–3 (reducing activity from 80 to 12.5%). This likely reflects the co-evolution of MCUb’s NTD with other MCUb regions, rendering it more effective at suppressing activity within MCUb-based constructs. Notably, chimera 4–5, in which MCUb’s NTR was replaced by that of MCU, showed no transport activity, indicating that relieving NTD autoinhibition alone, without restoring EMRE binding, is insufficient to confer Ca2+ conductance.

What could underlie this autoinhibition at the molecular level? Structural studies have shown that in the human MCU tetramer, the transmembrane region maintains fourfold symmetry around the pore axis, but this symmetry breaks at the NTDs, which assemble laterally into an arc on one side of the channel (Fig. 1C)18–21. As a result, the linkage between the NTD and CC1 differs among protomers. We speculate that in an MCUb tetramer, the NTDs adopt an analogous asymmetric arrangement but with certain NTDs positioned at greater distances from CC1, generating tension on CC1 and the adjoining TM1 segment. Such mechanical strain could shift the transmembrane helices into a conformation less favorable for ion permeation, thereby producing autoinhibition. Future structural studies will be crucial for elucidating the mechanism underlying this effect.

Having identified the molecular basis of MCUb non-conductance, we next sought to assess the extent to which MCUb suppresses mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake. Several strategies have been employed previously to address this question. One approach involved co-expressing MCUb with MCU, followed by purification and reconstitution into planar lipid bilayers29. Electrical recordings from these preparations showed only ~15% of the activity observed with MCU alone, leading to the conclusion that MCUb inhibits most uniporter function. However, these recordings may have captured non-physiological channel-like events rather than authentic uniporter currents, as they were performed in the absence of EMRE, an obligatory component of functional uniporter channels.

A second line of investigation has examined how MCUb depletion or overexpression affects mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake in various cell lines and tissues. However, a consistent picture has not emerged, with reported effects ranging from minimal (<25% change)36, to moderate (25–75% change)29,45, to pronounced (>75% change)30,33,46. These discrepancies likely arise from differences in cellular context and experimental conditions across studies.

Lastly, a recent study employed an elegant strategy using tetrameric MCU-MCUb concatemers containing defined numbers of MCUb subunits31. As the number of MCUb units per tetramer increased from one to two to three—and MCU dropped from three to two to one—uniporter activity progressively declined to approximately 75%, 50%, and 25% of control levels, respectively. Certain technical limitations remain: although the concatemer containing 3 MCUb and 1 MCU was reported to retain only ~25% of the activity of the all-MCU construct, the extent of MCUb inhibition may have been overestimated, given that the Western blot showed drastically reduced expression. Moreover, concatemeric constructs are well documented not to always yield the intended stoichiometry or assemble correctly47–49. Nonetheless, these findings challenge the earlier view29 that MCUb can nearly abolish mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake.

To quantify MCUb’s inhibitory effect, we assessed how its overexpression modulates mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake in cell lines. This experimental setting closely mirrors MCUb’s physiological role, in which ischemia induces its expression to limit mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake and protect tissues from injury. A key distinction from previous work is that, instead of comparing cells with or without MCUb overexpression, we compared cells overexpressing WT MCUb versus an E249A variant. The former comparison (presence vs. absence of MCUb) is inherently variable, as key cellular factors influencing the magnitude of MCUb inhibition—endogenous MCU and MCUb levels, their synthesis and turnover kinetics, and the dynamics of tetramer assembly, among others—can differ widely across cell types and experimental conditions. These differences likely contributed to the broad range of inhibitory effects reported previously. By quantifying WT MCUb’s inhibition relative to that of E249A MCUb, we effectively control for these confounding variables. Because both constructs were expressed at similar levels within the same cellular context, this design normalized for cell-specific variability, allowing us to isolate MCUb’s intrinsic inhibitory capacity.

We selected E249A MCUb as the reference because E249 is the conserved Glu residue within the signature DIME motif shared by MCU and MCUb. In MCU, the corresponding residue, E264, forms the Ca2+-binding site within the selectivity filter at the narrowest region of the pore20,21. Mutating E264 to any other residue, even the chemically similar Asp, abolishes channel function24, underscoring the stringent geometric and chemical requirements for Ca2+ coordination. Incorporation of an E249A MCUb subunit into an MCU-MCUb tetramer, which not only disrupts proper Ca2+ coordination but also introduces a hydrophobic methyl side chain into the selectivity filter, would therefore be expected to eliminate permeation entirely. Hence, E249A MCUb likely represents the maximal inhibitory potential achievable among MCUb variants. Our data indicate that WT MCUb exerts ~30% of E249A MCUb-induced maximal inhibition (reducing Ca2+ uptake by ~15%, compared with ~50% by E249A), demonstrating that WT MCUb functions as a modest, rather than potent, negative regulator of mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake (Fig. 7).

We note, however, that when E249A MCUb was stably expressed in WT cells, it reduced the rate of mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake by half rather than abolishing it completely (Fig. 6C). This observation does not contradict our argument that E249A MCUb represents the maximal inhibition achievable among MCUb variants. The partial inhibition could reflect the continued formation of endogenous MCU homotetramers, which could persist despite MCUb overexpression due to cellular regulatory mechanisms or intrinsic molecular preferences of MCU that favor homotetramerization over hetero-assembly with MCUb. The latter explanation is supported by the observation that stable expression of E264A MCU, a bona fide dominant-negative MCU variant, eliminates mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake (Fig. 6C). Nevertheless, if E249A MCUb indeed fails to abolish all uniporter activity when incorporated into an MCU-MCUb heterotetramer, the true inhibitory capacity of WT MCUb would be even weaker than our current estimate of ~30% of maximal inhibition. Thus, our conclusion remains fully supported.

Our assay carries an inherent uncertainty: the distribution of pore-forming tetramers containing 0, 1, 2, 3, or 4 MCUb subunits is unknown. Given that incorporation of a single E249A MCUb is likely sufficient to disrupt Ca2+ coordination, all heterotetramers containing E249A MCUb are expected to lose Ca2+ permeability completely. By contrast, WT MCUb may impose progressively greater inhibition as it occupies additional positions within the tetramer31. Therefore, the observed ~30% overall inhibition by WT MCUb relative to E249A MCUb, when considered at the single-molecule level, implies that each WT MCUb is less than 30% as inhibitory as an E249A MCUb subunit. This underscores that our conclusion, namely that MCUb acts as a modest rather than potent negative regulator, represents a conservative interpretation.

If an E249A mutation can so effectively enhance MCUb’s inhibitory power, why did nature not evolve MCUb to contain this substitution? We speculate that an excessively strong negative regulator would be maladaptive, as mitochondria require Ca2+ uptake to sustain normal metabolism. Indeed, ischemia-induced upregulation of MCUb in the heart, while protective against cell injury from mitochondrial Ca2+ overload, comes at the cost of impaired mitochondrial metabolism and reduced contractile function30. In this light, the evolution of MCUb as a modest—rather than absolute—suppressor of mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake may reflect a finely tuned balance: providing protection under stress while preserving essential mitochondrial function under physiological conditions.

To inhibit uniporter function, MCUb must assemble into the pore-forming MCU tetramer. Our results show that mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake remains unchanged two days after transient expression of E264A MCU or E249A MCUb (Fig. 6A), but is strongly reduced after two weeks of stable expression (Fig. 6C). This pattern suggests that pre-existing MCU tetramers are highly stable, characterized by (1) slow turnover, and (2) minimal subunit exchange, which limits incorporation of newly synthesized MCU or MCUb. Such stability likely explains why ischemia-induced MCUb expression confers protection primarily during chronic stress rather than the acute phase of myocardial infarction30. It also highlights an important methodological consideration: transient expression of MCUb is unsuitable for evaluating its effects on mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake.

A fundamental question that remains unresolved is how MCUb’s non-conductive molecular property enables it to inhibit uniporter function. Based on our findings and current structural insights, we propose two non-mutually exclusive mechanisms (Fig. 7). First, structural studies have shown that within the uniporter complex, MCU and EMRE assemble in a 4:4 stoichiometry20,21. Because MCUb cannot bind EMRE, its incorporation into the pore-forming tetramer would lower the number of associated EMRE molecules (e.g., a tetramer with 1 MCUb and 3 MCU subunits would bind only 3 EMRE molecules; see also Fig. S2A), thereby reducing channel activity. Notably, our finding that MCUb causes modest rather than complete inhibition supports a previous report showing that the uniporter can remain partially active with fewer than 4 EMRE subunits50. Second, the autoinhibitory NTD of MCUb may further attenuate channel function, potentially through long-range conformational coupling that destabilizes the conductive configuration of the transmembrane region.

This study establishes a detailed interaction map of MCUb within the uniporter complex: it associates with MCU, MCUb, and MICU1, but not with EMRE or MICU2. Although a previous report suggested that MCUb fails to bind MICU130, our Co-IP demonstrates a direct interaction between these two subunits (Fig. 3E), consistent with the fact that MCUb possesses key MICU1-interacting residues present in MCU within the uniporter holocomplex structure20. We speculate that prior studies30 may have failed to detect MCUb–MICU1 interactions because their Co-IP used RIPA buffer, whose harsh detergents (such as deoxycholate) can denature membrane proteins and disrupt protein–protein interactions. Finally, the lack of MCUb binding with MICU2 aligns with the current molecular understanding that MICU2 does not directly occlude MCU but instead modulates MICU1’s Ca2+ sensitivity20,23,26–28.

It is well established that MICU1 serves as a gatekeeper of the uniporter by interacting with MCU to close the channel under resting, low-Ca2+ conditions12,13,28. The ability of MCUb to engage MICU1 suggests that pore tetramers containing MCUb remain subject to MICU1 gatekeeping (Fig. 7). Consistent with this, we found that MICU1 binding to MCUb is perturbed by elevated Ca2+, mirroring Ca2+ disruption of MCU-MICU1 interactions (Fig. S4A). Moreover, spermine, known to potentiate uniporter activity by dislodging MICU1 from the pore entrance39,40, enhanced the activity of a Ca2+-conducting MCUb-based chimera (Fig. S4B), indicating preserved MICU1 regulation. This preservation of MICU1-dependent gating is likely crucial for enabling MCUb to exert a protective role, rather than paradoxically promoting mitochondrial Ca2+ overload.

The constructs generated in this study could serve as useful tools for exploring MCUb’s therapeutic potential. For example, in mouse models, endogenous MCUb could be replaced with the more inhibitory E249A variant to enhance suppression of uniporter activity, or with Ca2+-conducting chimeras to attenuate inhibition. Comparing the effects of these genetic manipulations on pathological processes, such as cardiac ischemia-reperfusion injury, may help determine whether strengthening or weakening MCUb-mediated regulation improves disease outcomes. Such insights could inform strategies for fine-tuning mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake in disease contexts.

The evolutionary emergence of MCUb represents a remarkable refinement: nature transformed what was once a Ca2+ channel into a tempered regulator that curbs mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake without abolishing it, preserving the delicate balance between metabolic demand and protection from injury. Our study elucidates how this evolutionary innovation arose by tracing MCUb’s non-conductive property to its inability to engage EMRE. Through systematic protein engineering, we uncovered an autoinhibitory function of its NTD and reconstructed Ca2+ conductance in MCUb to near-MCU levels. These findings illuminate how two homologous proteins sharing 50% sequence identity and a conserved Ca2+-binding architecture can diverge so profoundly in function. Moreover, our demonstration that MCUb’s inhibitory capacity can be rationally tuned through targeted mutations suggests potential therapeutic avenues for disorders driven by mitochondrial Ca2+ dysregulation, including ischemic injury, heart failure, and neurodegenerative diseases.

Methods

Cell culture and molecular biology

HEK293 cells (ATCC, CRL-11268), authenticated via short tandem repeat profiling, were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum at 37 °C in 5% CO2. All CRISPR-Cas9 engineered KO cell lines used in this study, including MCU-KO, MCU/EMRE-KO, and MCU/EMRE/MICU1-KO cells, have been previously described and validated20,28,41. All cell lines were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination, with no contamination detected.

Site-directed mutagenesis was performed using two-step PCR. Chimeric constructs and MCUb-EMRE fusions were generated by overlap extension PCR. Epitope tags, including 1D4 (TESTQVAPA), C8 (PRGPDRPEGIEE), or FLAG (DYKDDDDK), were added to the C terminus of constructs. All sequences were confirmed by Sanger sequencing. Constructs were cloned into the pcDNA3.1(+) vector for transient expression and the pLJM1-puro vector for stable expression. Transient expression was carried out using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and cells were harvested 48 h post-transfection for experiments. Stable expression was achieved via lentiviral transduction41, and cells were used for experiments 2 weeks after transduction.

Mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake assays

For each mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake measurement, 2 × 107 HEK cells were used. Cells were washed with 10 mL of wash buffer (120 mM KCl, 25 mM HEPES, 2 mM K2HPO4, 1 mM MgCl2, 50 μM EGTA, pH 7.2-KOH), centrifuged at 1000 × g for 3 min, and then resuspended in 2.2 mL of recording buffer (120 mM KCl, 25 mM HEPES, 2 mM K2HPO4, 1 mM MgCl2, 5 mM succinate, pH 7.2-KOH). 2 mL of the cell suspension was loaded into a stirred quartz cuvette in a Hitachi F-7100 spectrofluorometer (excitation 508 nm, slit 2.5 nm; emission 531 nm, slit 5 nm; sampling rate 2 Hz), temperature controlled at 25 °C. Extramitochondrial Ca2+ concentrations were monitored using 0.5 μM calcium green 5N (CG5N; Thermo Fisher, C3737). After CG5N addition, 30 µM digitonin (Sigma, D141) was applied to selectively permeabilize the plasma membrane without impacting the integrity of the mitochondrial inner membrane. Mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake was initiated by adding 15 µM CaCl2 and inhibited by 0.6 µM Ru360 (synthesized in-house). In all figures, except Fig. 2B, only the traces after Ca2+ addition were shown for clear presentation.

Mitochondrial extraction, Co-IP, expression analysis, and Western blotting

For mitochondrial extraction, HEK cells were pelleted and resuspended in 500 μL of mitochondrial resuspension buffer (MRB; 250 mM sucrose, 5 mM HEPES, 1 mM EGTA, pH 7.2-KOH) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher, A32965). Cells were lysed by passing the suspension through a 27-gauge needle 40 times on ice. The lysate was centrifuged at 1000 × g for 10 min, and the resulting supernatant was centrifuged at 13,000 × g for 10 min. The pellet was washed twice by resuspending in 500 μL of MRB followed by centrifugation at 13,000 × g for 10 min, yielding the final mitochondrial sample for Western blotting.

All Co-IP experiments were performed at 4 °C. Transfected HEK cells were lysed in 0.5 mL of solubilization buffer (SB, 150 mM NaCl, 50 mM HEPES, 4 mM n-dodecyl-β-D-maltoside) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail. After incubating for 10 min on ice, the lysate was clarified by centrifugation at 13,000 × g for 15 min. Then, 10% of the supernatant was reserved for whole-cell lysate (WCL) analysis. The remaining supernatant was incubated with 25 μL of 1D4- or FLAG-antibody-conjugated beads (1D4: homemade 50% slurry; FLAG: Sigma-Aldrich, A2220) on a rotating wheel at 4 °C for 1 h. Beads were collected on spin columns, washed six times with 1 mL of SB, and eluted with 140 μL of SDS loading buffer. Both WCL and eluted fractions (IP samples labeled in Figs. 3E, F, S3B, and S4A) were analyzed by Western blotting.

To analyze protein expression levels (e.g., Figs. 2C and 3C), cell pellets were lysed in RIPA buffer (Santa Cruz, sc-24948) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail. The lysate was clarified by centrifugation at 17,000 × g for 10 min and the supernatant was used for protein quantification by the bicinchoninic acid assay. A total of 50 µg of protein was loaded per lane for SDS-PAGE.

To perform Western blotting, proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with Intercept Blocking Buffer (LI-COR, 927-60001) for 1 h at room temperature and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by fluorescent secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Fluorescent signals were acquired using a LI-COR Odyssey CLx imaging system. Uncropped and unprocessed scans are provided in the accompanying Source Data file.

Primary antibodies and dilutions were as follows: anti-1D4 (homemade, 0.1 μg/mL), anti-C8 (homemade, 0.1 μg/mL), anti-FLAG (Sigma, F1804, 1:10,000), anti-MCU (Cell Signaling, 14997, 1:10,000), anti-EMRE (Santa Cruz, sc-86337, 1:1000), anti-MICU1 (Sigma, HPA037480, 1:5000), and anti-COX2 (Abcam, ab110258, 0.1 μg/mL). Secondary antibodies were goat anti-rabbit IRDye 680RD (LI-COR, 926-68071, 1:10,000), goat anti-mouse IRDye 680RD (LI-COR, 925-68070, 1:15,000), goat anti-mouse IRDye 800CW (LI-COR, 926-32210, 1:15,000), and goat anti-rabbit IRDye 800CW (LI-COR, 926-32211, 1:10,000). All antibodies were diluted in TBST (140 mM NaCl, 3 mM KCl, 25 mM Tris, 0.075% Tween-20, pH 7.4-HCl).

Blue native PAGE (BN-PAGE)

BN-PAGE was performed using the NativePAGE Bis-Tris gel system (Life Technologies). Mitochondria (30–100 µg total protein) were solubilized in 24 µL BN-PAGE lysis buffer, containing 3.75 µL NativePAGE Sample Buffer, 1.9 µL glycerol (50% in water), 4.5 µL digitonin stock (20% in MRB), 2.4 µL 5% G-250, and 11.45 µL MRB. Samples were loaded onto 4–16% Bis-Tris gels, and then subjected to electrophoresis at 4 °C according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes, which were destained with 25% methanol and 10% acetic acid, followed by 100% methanol. Membranes were then rinsed with water, blocked in 5% milk in TBS for 1 h, and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies diluted in TBST (anti-MCU, Cell Signaling, 14997, 1:10,000; anti-EMRE, Santa Cruz, sc-86337, 1:1000; anti-1D4, homemade, 1:1000). After washing, membranes were incubated with alkaline phosphatase-conjugated secondary antibodies (anti-mouse, Promega, S3721; anti-rabbit, Promega, S3731; 1:5000 in TBST) at room temperature for 1 h. Signals were developed colorimetrically using nitro blue tetrazolium (Thermo Fisher, N6495) and 5-bromo-4-chloro-3′-indolylphosphate substrates (Thermo Fisher, 34040).

Data analysis and statistics

The number of independent replicates for each experiment is indicated as individual data points in bar charts, and/or provided in the corresponding figure legends. All data are presented as mean ± SEM. Mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake rates were analyzed using Igor Pro (WaveMetrics, version 8). Western-blot intensities were quantified using Image Studio (LI-COR, version 5.2). Statistical analyses were performed using unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t-tests in Microsoft Excel (version 16.99), with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Structure prediction in Fig. S3C–E was performed using the AlphaFold3 server51. Structural images were generated in PyMOL (Schrödinger, version 2.3.4) or UCSF Chimera (version 1.19)52.

All statistical comparisons were performed between each experimental construct and a predefined reference group (e.g., WT MCU or MCUb; leftmost bar in all bar graphs unless otherwise specified). Statistical methods for comparing means across multiple groups (e.g., ANOVA) were not used, because our conclusions are based solely on pairwise comparisons rather than on comparisons across all constructs. For example, in Fig. 2A, B, our goal is to identify structural elements responsible for MCUb’s non-conductive behavior by determining how replacing individual regions in MCU with the corresponding regions from MCUb affects activity relative to WT MCU, rather than by assessing differences among all constructs. Accordingly, statistical tests were intentionally limited to these predefined comparisons using unpaired, two-tailed t-tests.
