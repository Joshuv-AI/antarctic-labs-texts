Comparison status and factors of pregnancy stress among pregnant women with second child between advanced maternal age and reproductive age: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Lijuan Zhang, Tingting Shi, Haiyan Liu, Bing Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWith the implementation of China’s two-child policy and the rising trend of advanced maternal age, pregnancy stress in women with a second child has become a significant concern.ObjectiveTo investigate the status and factors of pregnancy stress in women with second child between advanced maternal age and reproductive age in China.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted in Changsha. A total of 767 pregnant women with second child, including 237 advanced maternal age and 530 reproductive age pregnant women, agreed to participate in this study. It was collected using a socio-demographic and obstetric characteristics questionnaire and the Pregnancy Stress Scale. We conducted a descriptive analysis to characterize pregnancy stress levels and related factors. Univariate analysis was performed to examine the associations between socio-demographic/obstetrical characteristics and pregnancy stress. Additionally, linear regression was utilized to identify significant factors associated with pregnancy stress.ResultsThe pregnancy stress levels of both groups were at a mild to moderate, and there was no significant difference in the total stress score and factor 1 (“parenthood recognition”), factor 2 (“assurance of the health and safety of mother and fetus”), and factor 3 (“changes of body shape and physical activities of mother”) between two groups (p > 0.05). However, there was a significant difference in the factor 4 (“other”) (p < 0.05). The primary source of stress for both groups was factor 2 (“assurance of the health and safety of mother and fetus”) (1.8 ± 0.6); The factors influencing stress for advanced maternal age pregnant women with second child were “relationship with their spouse”, “family financial burden”, and “fertility desire”. For reproductive age pregnant women with second child, the factors were “family financial burden”, “fertility desire”, “relationship with parents-in-law”, “gender of their first child”, “gender preference”, and “relationship with their spouse”.ConclusionThere were both the same and different factors of stress for advanced maternal age and reproductive age pregnant women with second child, targeted interventions should be taken to alleviate pregnancy stress for them.Keywords: Pregnancy Stress, Factors, China’s two-child policy, Advanced Maternal Age, Reproductive Age

Introduction

The past two decades have witnessed a sustained decline in global fertility rates, accompanied by a prevalent postponement of the childbearing age [1, 2]. In China, the universal two-child policy introduced in 2016 has further prolonged the reproductive timeline. Women are increasingly giving birth to their second child at an advanced maternal age. It is noteworthy that more than 60% of women expecting their second child belong to the category of Advanced Maternal Age (AMA), which is defined as by the FIGO pregnancy in women aged 35 years or older [3, 4].

AMA pregnancy is no longer an exception but an emergent public-health reality. Studies have consistently demonstrated that a wide range of adverse pregnancy outcomes are associated with women of AMA. Compared with mothers aged 20–34 years, mothers aged 35 years and higher had a significantly higher risk of gestational diabetes, preeclampsia, placenta abnormalities, rate of cesarean section, postpartum hemorrhage, low birth weight, preterm birth, and stillbirth etc [5–9]. These somatic risks are paralleled by a heightened susceptibility to psychological stress. The higher burden of medical complications intensifies mental stress, while familial and societal pressures surrounding later-life childbearing further amplify the strain [10]. Consequently, delayed motherhood is associated with higher risks of psychological conditions such as autism, bipolar disorder, and symptoms of depression, anxiety, and stress, along with impaired social functioning [11].

Pregnancy is a natural and physiological event in a woman’s life. However, it is also a challenge that will be accompanied by different levels of stress, that is pregnancy stress. Pregnancy stress [12] refers to an imbalanced state of physical and mental well-being during pregnancy, caused by the mismatch between various demands and physiological and psychological responses of pregnant women. A couple of studies [13, 14] revealed that 13.6% to 78% of pregnant women experience mild to moderate pregnancy stress, while 6% to 16.7% experience severe pregnancy stress. Previous Chinese studies [15, 16] have explored that the percentage of pregnant women with mild to moderate pregnancy stress is higher than 71.53%, while the percentage of those with severe pregnancy stress is as high as 26.74%. Appropriate stress has positive effects on individuals, but the accumulation of severe stress can lead to numerous detrimental impacts [17]. Based on previous research findings [7, 8, 18–20], pregnancy stress is a risk factor for adverse pregnancy outcomes, which can have both short-term and long-term detrimental effects on both the mother and the fetus. In the short-term, it can impact maternal and fetal psychological well-being, alter the uterine environment, induce microbial changes, disrupt maternal physiological functions, and potentially lead to adverse pregnancy outcomes such as low birth weight, preterm birth, miscarriage, stillbirth, fetal malformation or neurodevelopmental defects. Severe pregnancy stress can have long-term effects on both infants and mothers. It may alter spatial learning and memory abilities in young children, potentially contributing to adolescent depression [19, 20]. Additionally, it can disrupt maternal attachment, the emotional bond between mother and infant. Studies show that high maternal stress during pregnancy is linked to reduced prenatal attachment. For example, Keten Edis et al. found that prenatal anxiety is correlated with prenatal attachment in high-risk pregnant women [21]. Şanlı et al. also demonstrated that pregnancy stress is a significant factor affecting prenatal attachment [22].

Previous studies have converged on a consistent set of factors of pregnancy stress. Cross-sectional work shows that being unmarried, carrying a high-risk pregnancy, and reporting low income or limited education each independently raise perceived stress levels [23, 25]. Prospective data from Korea add that marital satisfaction and perceived social support are among the strongest modifiable buffers [24]. Age further moderates these effects. Women at Reproductive Age (less than 35 years old, RA) in the first trimester report the sharpest rise in stress, whereas AMA mother benefit from stable partnerships and higher household income; both factors attenuate stress, especially in second pregnancies [25]. Conversely, AMA mothers more often enter pregnancy with chronic hypertension, diabetes, or thyroid disease—conditions that themselves heighten psychological distress [5]. Finally, sex preference for the second child continues to shape late-pregnancy stress, with discordance between desired and actual fetal sex amplifying anxiety even among otherwise low-risk AMA women [26].

Previous studies have examined various factors influencing pregnancy stress among pregnant women, such as the stress experienced by second-time mothers or at different stages of pregnancy. However, the comparative analysis of stress profiles and their influencing factors between AMA and RA women with a second child remains underexplored, particularly in the context of China’s sociocultural environment. Therefore, the present study aims to:

1) Examine pregnancy stress levels in AMA and RA women with a second child in Changsha, Hunan, China;

1) Examine pregnancy stress levels in AMA and RA women with a second child in Changsha, Hunan, China;

2) Compare differences in stress between AMA and RA women;

2) Compare differences in stress between AMA and RA women;

3) Identify the key influencing factors associated with their stress levels.

Methods

Participants and settings

This cross-sectional study recruited pregnant women with second child receiving prenatal care at a tertiary hospital in Changsha, from March 2017 to July 2017. The participants completed questionnaires of socio-demographics, obstetric characteristics, as well as a Pregnancy Stress Scale within approximately 10 min face to face. The research personnel followed a standardized protocol in order to elucidate the purpose and significance of the study, obtained informed consent from the participants, and encouraged them to independently participate. The completeness and accuracy of the questionnaires were checked on the spot, and any missing items were promptly addressed. The questionnaire included 4 dimensions of Pregnancy Stress Scale and 12 independent variables. The sample size was determined by 10 participants per variable [27], and considered a 20% loss to follow-up rate. Based on the sample content estimation formula (max (number of variables) ×10) / (1 − 20%), at least 200 participants were needed in each group. A total of 780 questionnaires were distributed, and 767 valid questionnaires were collected, including 237 AMA and 530 RA pregnant women, 13 questionnaires were excluded with incomplete required fields or missing key information. The inclusion criteria for this study were as follows: (1) pregnant women with second child, (2) viable and singleton pregnancies and (3) voluntary participation in the questionnaire survey. The exclusion criteria included: (1) previous history of psychiatric disorders or mental illness and (2) cognitive impairments.

Instruments

The questionnaire consisted of two sections on pregnant women’s socio-demographic and obstetric characteristics questionnaire, as well as measuring pregnancy stress levels using the Pregnancy Stress Scale (PSS).

Socio-demographic and Obstetric Characteristics questionnaire

This section of the questionnaire is designed by integrating insights from a variety of sources, including epidemiological studies, sociological and demographic research, and clinical practice. It covers a wide range of characteristics, such as ethnicity, gestational week, personality traits, relationship with parents-in-law, history of abnormal gestation (including natural miscarriage, premature birth, stillbirth, and difficult childbirth), pregnancy complications (such as thyroid disease, hypertension, diabetes, and chronic hepatitis), gender of the first child, fertility desires, and gender preferences, relationship with their spouse. Additionally, it includes the mode of delivery for the second child and the family’s financial burden.

Pregnancy stress scale

This scale, a measurement for investigating psychological stress in pregnant women, was developed by Taiwanese scholars Chen [28]. It consists of 30 items, the first 27 items are divided into three factors: Factor 1: “parenthood recognition” (15 items), Factor 2: “assurance of the health and safety of mother and fetus” (8 items), and Factor 3: “changes of body shape and physical activities of mother” (4 items). The remaining 3 items are classified as Factor 4 “other” (3 items), 3 items mean “concern about the ability to rear child properly”, “concern about spouses’ mutual affection after having a baby”, and “concern about inability to provide the child with good support”. The scale uses a four-point likert-style: no stress at all = 1, mild stress = 2, moderate stress = 3, severe stress = 4. The total score of the scale was calculated as the actual total score for pregnancy stress divided by the number of items, and factor score was calculated as the actual score for each factor divided by the number of items. A total or factor score of “1” indicates no stress at all; 1.001 to 2.000 indicates mild stress; 2.001 to 3.000 indicates moderate stress; 3.001 to 4.000 indicates severe stress. A higher score was indicative of more pregnancy stress. It has been shown that the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for this scale is 0.84 [6]. The Cronbach’s α was 0.916 in the current study.

Data analysis

The database was established using Epidata 3.1, and carried out with IBM SPSS version 22.0 (Statistical Package for Social science Software). Scores of pregnancy stress both two groups exhibited a normal distribution. Measurement data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and analyzed using two independent samples t-test and one-way analysis of variance. Count data were represented by frequency and percentage, and analyzed using chi-square test. Univariate analysis and Multiple Linear Regression were used to identify factors of pregnancy stress for two groups. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistical significance.

The gestational weeks ranged from 5 to 42 weeks, with an average of (23.80 ± 10.47) weeks. The RA pregnant women with second-child are between 22 and 34 years old, with an average age of (30.63 ± 2.63) years. The gestational weeks ranged from 4 to 40 weeks, with an average of (23.21 ± 10.36) weeks. Other sociodemographic data are shown in Table 1. A univariate analysis was performed to explore the associations between socio-demographic and obstetrical characteristics and pregnancy stress, with the findings summarized in Table 1.

Among AMA pregnant women, several significant differences in stress scores were observed. Women who reported a good relationship with their spouse experienced lower pregnancy stress (F = 9.89, p = 0.002). Similarly, those with a good relationship with their parents-in-law had lower stress levels (F = 4.10, p = 0.008). Non-voluntary pregnancy was associated with higher stress levels compared to voluntary pregnancy (t = -5.92, p < 0.001). Additionally, in terms of family financial burden, women who found the burden unbearable had higher stress scores than those who found it basic allowed, while those who felt the burden was completely allowed had the lowest stress levels (F = 13.10, p < 0.001).

For RA pregnant women, similar trends were observed in some aspects. For example, those with a good relationship with their spouse had lower pregnancy stress (F = 18.98, p < 0.001), and women with poor relationships with their parents-in-law had higher stress compared to those with average relationships, while those with good relationships had the lowest stress scores (F = 15.57, p < 0.001). Non-voluntary pregnancy was also linked to higher stress levels compared to voluntary pregnancy (t = -6.44, p < 0.001). In terms of family financial burden, women who found it unbearable had higher stress compared to those who found it basic allowed, while those who felt it was completely allowed had the lowest stress scores (F = 21.40, p < 0.001).

However, there were also distinct differences observed among RA pregnant women compared to AMA pregnant women. Specifically, introverted personality traits were associated with higher stress scores compared to other personality traits (F = 2.43, p = 0.047). Additionally, pregnant women whose first child was a girl had higher pregnancy stress than those whose first child was a boy (t = -2.96, p = 0.003). Furthermore, women who had a gender preference for a boy experienced higher pregnancy stress (F = 5.57, p = 0.004). These three factors did not show significant differences among AMA pregnant women.

Based on the results presented in Table 1, a Multiple Linear Regression analysis was conducted to identify the key factors influencing pregnancy stress among two distinct groups: AMA and RA pregnant women who are expecting their second child.

For AMA pregnant women, the Multiple Linear Regression model included three variables: relationship with spouse, fertility desire, and family financial burden. Collectively, these variables accounted for 19% of the variance in pregnancy stress among AMA pregnant women, as detailed in Table 3. Specifically, fertility desire had a positive regression coefficient (0.40), indicating that involuntary fertility desire was associated with higher levels of pregnancy stress. Family financial burden had a negative regression coefficient (-0.15), suggesting that a greater financial burden was linked to increased stress. Additionally, the relationship with the spouse had a positive coefficient (0.24), implying that a less favorable relationship with the spouse contributed to higher stress levels.

Discussion

Our study report on the socio-demographic and obstetrical characteristics and influencing factors of pregnancy stress in AMA and RA women with second child in China. To explore the similarities and differences of influencing factors of pregnancy stress between the two groups, targeted interventions should be taken to reduce the pregnancy stress between the two groups and reduce the incidence of adverse pregnancy outcomes.

The results of this paper demonstrate that both groups of pregnant women with second child experience mild to moderate levels of pregnancy stress, which is consistent with previous researches conducted by Lijuan [29], Liqing [30]. A previous study [31] also reported that pregnancy stress can have direct or indirect impacts on an individual’s psychological well-being, potentially leading to depression. Even mild to moderate levels of pregnancy stress pose a significant risk factor for depression. Therefore, it is crucial for obstetric healthcare professionals and family members to be attentive to the pregnancy stress experienced by pregnant women with second child.

Table 1 showed that the highest score dimension of pregnancy stress in both groups was factor 2, “assurance of the health and safety of mother and fetus”, indicating that the pregnant women with second child spend more attention on the health and safety of both mother and fetus during pregnancy. This is consistent with the results from other studies [32, 33]. With the growth of age, ovarian function gradually declines, physical function, fertility and birth quality decline, pregnancy hypertension, diabetes and other complications, abortion, and other high probability of occurrence, and most of them have experienced the peak of cesarean Sects [34–36]. Consequently, some of the scarred uterus pregnant women with second child in the choice of delivery mode there is confusion and worry. Worried about the occurrence of massive bleeding during childbirth or postpartum, therefore, obstetrical medical personnel should strengthen the perinatal health knowledge education of second child pregnant women, improve their awareness of perinatal adverse pregnancy or delivery outcomes, reduce unnecessary worry or fear, and relieve their pregnancy stress.

In addition, the results showed that there was no statistical significance in the total score of pregnancy stress and the scores of factor 1, factor 2 and factor 3 between two groups (p>0.05), that is the same level of pregnancy stress in both groups. The scores of factor 4 “other” (including three items, “concern about the ability to rear child properly”, “concern about spouses’ mutual affection after having a baby”, and “concern about inability to provide the child with good support”) of AMA pregnant women were higher than those of RA pregnant women, the difference was statistically significant (p<0.05), indicating that AMA pregnant women pay more attention to factor 4 “other” than RA pregnant women. The reason is that compared with RA pregnant women, AMA pregnant women have a longer time from the first pregnancy, and the experience of how to take care of children is unfamiliar, and they need to spend more time and energy to re-learn the knowledge of parenting. In addition, the AMA pregnant women are 35 years old and above, and this stage may be experiencing various barriers and crises such as career, health, family and marriage, so the pressure of life and work is relatively large. Therefore, obstetrical medical staff can add some childcare “scenario simulation” links when carrying out pregnant women’s classes to help AMA pregnant women with second child regain their parenting knowledge and learn new parenting concepts, reduce their stress in parenting, and improve the quality of life during pregnancy.

The same influencing factors of pregnancy stress in two groups are: “relationship with their spouse, fertility desire, and family financial burden”. Among them, “relationship with their spouse” is an independent factor affecting the pregnancy stress of two groups. One study [37] found AMA women had a satisfying relationship with the spouses is advantageous during to mother-hood. Psychological support or material support from spouses during pregnancy can effectively reduce the psychological stress of pregnant women with second child, which is consistent with latest research [38]. Second child pregnant women in addition to bear the stress of pregnancy itself, at the same time to spend a lot of time and energy to take care of their first child, and may also bear the stress from work, if the spouse can understand and accompany pregnant women, give pregnant women support, will be able to reduce the psychological stress. Fertility desire is the influencing factor of pregnancy stress of second child pregnant women. Having a second child is affected by many factors. Some women choose to have a “second child” due to the pressure of their spouses or elders, and the expectation of their spouses or elders increases their psychological pressure to a certain extent, and even affects the diet and sleep of pregnant women [39], leading to anxiety and depression. Therefore, pregnant women who involuntarily give birth to a “second child” should strengthen their communication with their families and promote mutual understanding, so as to relieve their psychological stress. The influence of having “second child” on family financial burden is the factor of pregnancy stress of “second child” pregnant women. The reason is that giving birth to a “second child” will increase the family economic burden for some families, especially considering the cost of future support and education, which will bring lasting stress to second child pregnant women. Therefore, for the “two-child” families with economic difficulties, it is recommended that relevant departments offer economic support and future education support to alleviate their family financial burden.

In addition to the above common influencing factors, the influencing factors of pregnancy stress of RA pregnant women with second child also include: relationship with parents-in-law, gender of first child, gender preference. The relationship with parents-in-law is an influential factor in the pregnancy stress of this group. In many Chinese families, it is common to live with parents, thus, the relationship with the parents-in-law is also important for pregnant women in China [40]. AMA pregnant women with second child spend long time with parents in-laws, are already familiar with each other’s personalities, know how to communicate or avoid contradictions, and reduce conflicts, while RA pregnant women with second child spend less time with parents in-laws than AMA pregnant women, may be in a run-in period with their parents in-law, prone to conflicts, therefore, the bed relationship with parents in-laws was a significant factor for RA pregnant women with second child. Hence, medical staff can communicate with them and their families respectively, so that they can tolerate and understand each other, shorten the run-in period, and then reduce the occurrence of adverse pregnancy outcomes caused by stress. The gender of first child and gender preference for “second child” influence the pregnancy stress them. The liberalization of the birth policy undoubtedly comes as a surprise to AMA women who meet the fertility conditions, resulting in reduced emphasis on the gender of the first child as well as the gender of subsequent children [41]. RA women choose to have a second child most of the consideration is to expect both sex of children, so they are more concerned about the gender of the first child and the second child, for example, parents now have the opportunity to have a second child, and if their first child is a girl, it may be a boy. Therefore, we should help pregnant women with second child to establish a correct concept of fertility, so that they pay more attention to maternal and child health.

Strengths and limitations

This study boasts several notable strengths. Firstly, it uniquely focuses on the status of pregnancy stress among AMA and RA pregnant women with second child. It delves into the similarities and differences influenced by socio-demographic and obstetric factors, an aspect that has been scarcely explored in previous research. Secondly, the study design thoughtfully considers the impact of traditional Chinese reproductive culture in the context of the liberalized birth policy. Lastly, the cross-sectional survey was conducted through face-to-face interviews, allowing participants to promptly address any questionnaire-related questions, thereby ensuring the authenticity and comprehensiveness of the data collected.

However, the study also had some limitations. Firstly, as a cross-sectional survey, it was unable to establish causal relationships between pregnancy stress and its influencing factors in the two groups. Secondly, the study’s participants were exclusively recruited from the obstetric outpatient department of a tertiary hospital in Changsha, Hunan Province, which may limit the generalizability of the findings.

Conclusion

This study extracted the pregnancy stress level, and the factors influencing pregnancy stress of AMA and RA pregnant women with second child. The variables of relationship with their spouse, fertility desire, and family financial burden were the common factors for two group pregnant women, additionally, for RA pregnant women with second child, relationship with parents-in-law, gender of first child, gender preference were also important affecting factors. Therefore, it is crucial to monitor pregnancy stress levels of those pregnant women with “second child”, and take the need for appropriate interventions to address their pregnancy stress.
