Self-advocacy experiences in coping with dysphagia among patients with oral cancer: A qualitative study
Authors: Yuanyuan Li, Lin Cheng, Yuxi Xie, Lan Wang, Guanmian Liang, Yan Lou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to explore the experiences of patients with oral cancer in coping with dysphagia from the perspective of self-advocacy.MethodsA descriptive qualitative research design was adopted. From March to May 2024, face-to-face, in-depth semi-structured interviews were conducted with 21 patients with oral cancer at a tertiary cancer hospital. The interview guide was developed based on the self-advocacy framework, and data were analyzed using the directed content analysis method.ResultsFindings were organized within the self-advocacy framework, yielding three main categories and eight sub-categories: 1) Thoughts and cognition — cognitive misunderstanding, realignment of expectations and goals, and loss of control; 2) Actions for self — communication barriers with medical professionals, limited reliance on physicians, and information seeking via the internet; 3) Utilization of resources — two-way tension of family support, dual effects of peer support, and structural economic exclusion.ConclusionsPatients with oral cancer face multiple barriers in coping with dysphagia, including cognitive limitations, insufficient medical coping strategies, and challenges in resource utilization. These factors interact with one another, influencing patients’ self-advocacy and coping status.

Introduction

Oral cancer is the most common type of head and neck cancer and refers to malignant tumors occurring in the oral cavity and adjacent anatomical structures, including different types of lip cancer, gingival cancer, tongue cancer, jawbone cancer, and cancer of the floor of the mouth.1 Worldwide, oral cancer is becoming an alarming problem, ranking as the 11th most common carcinoma.2 More importantly, its incidence continues to increase annually.3 Current treatment modalities for oral cancer include surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and multidisciplinary cooperative treatment. Diversifying and continuous advancements in therapeutic methods have greatly improved the survival rate of patients with oral cancer.4

Nevertheless, the prolonged survival of patients with oral cancer is not accompanied by better quality of life. Patients undergoing various treatment modalities may experience a range of complications, of which dysphagia is the most common, with an incidence that can reach 80%.5 Shah et al.6 reported that 66%–90% of patients with oral cancer could experience dysphagia. As treatment progresses, dysphagia becomes more severe over time, resulting in advanced side effects or complications such as malnutrition,7 lung infections,8 and negative emotions such as anxiety and depression.9 These statistics highlight the urgent need to address the challenges associated with dysphagia. Most previous studies have focused on the coping experiences of patients with neurologic dysphagia such as Parkinson's patients and stroke patients,10, 11, 12 or explored the obstacles to coping with dysphagia or the safety of swallowing rehabilitation from the perspective of hospital professionals and patients' caregivers.13,14 Nund et al.15 and Crowder et al.16 explored experiences related to dysphagia in the oral phase in cancer survivors. However, their studies were conducted in patients with head and neck tumors, which may not be generalizable to all patients with oral cancer. Shinn et al.17 showed that the patient's own role in coping with dysphagia was not sufficiently activated. As many as 81% of patients with oral cancer were unwilling to comply with oral care recommendations18 and 87% were unwilling to actively participate in swallowing exercise recommendations.19

In cancer, momentum toward greater patient-centered care and patient engagement has resulted in calls to improve patient self-advocacy.20 Self-advocacy is defined as a process of internalizing resources and activating actions to overcome barriers related to cancer and treatment.21 Self-advocacy has been widely endorsed as a necessary part of being a cancer patient by oncology patients and providers,22 researchers,23 etc. These encourage self-advocacy as a means for patients to exert control over complex treatment decisions and uncertain health outcomes, address their unmet needs, and improve their health care experience. Yet efforts to encourage self-advocacy may not always result in these intended outcomes. Asking patients to advocate for their needs may place exceptionally high demands on individuals who lack communication skills, decision-making skills, and social connections.21 Patients with oral cancer seem to be one such group that faces a continuous variety of challenges. They have a high symptom burden24 and suffer from communication difficulties due to lesions in the oral cavity and treatment modalities.25 In addition, patients with oral cancer are predominantly male. Traditional male gender roles emphasize control, stoicism, and strength which may impact men's help-seeking behaviors.26,27 Hagan et al.21 proposed a self-advocacy framework to explain how self-advocacy, as a process of internalizing skills and resources, can help survivors cope with cancer-related barriers and support them in accessing their self-needs and achieving their self-goals. Thus, this study aims to explore the experiences of patients with oral cancer coping with dysphagia through self-advocacy, to provide new insights into the development of appropriate coping strategies.

Methods

Study design

This study used a qualitative descriptive approach to dissect the self-advocacy process of patients with oral cancer coping with dysphagia. Face-to-face, semi-structured, in-depth interviews were conducted with patients with oral cancer with dysphagia to gather information on their experiences and feelings.

Theoretical framework

The self-advocacy framework21 describes the process of how cancer survivors internalize and act on resources to overcome barriers related to cancer and treatment in order to achieve proximal and long-term beneficial outcomes (Fig. 1). To realize internalization, antecedents including personal characteristics, learned skills and attainablity of support, may influence the extent to which a person becomes a self-advocate. Defining characteristics, which is the patients’ ability to internalize personal characteristics, skills and support resources into actions to support personally relevant goals, are the most core behavioral and cognitive manifestation of self-advocacy, and it directly corresponds to the dynamic process in which patients cope with challenges. As such, the current study, which mainly focuses on the experience of patients with oral cancer in dealing with dysphagia through self-advocacy, applied the part of defining characteristics in the framework to guide the design of interview questions. The three major elements of defining characteristics include.(1)“Thoughts and cognition”: it refers to how patients integrate cancer into a part of their lives through cognitive reconstruction and form an active coping thinking pattern. Specifically, it is manifested as creation of 'new normal' (accepting cancer as the norm of life rather than a temporary deviation), prioritization of needs and wants (dynamically identifying core needs at different survival stages (such as the treatment period and the rehabilitation period), and sense of empowerment (the belief in taking control of cancer-related challenges).(2)“Actions for self”: it describes how cancer patients take the initiative to act to control the medical experience and maintain personal goals. This kind of action is defined in the interaction process between patients and the medical environment and is driven by values. Specifically manifested as navigating health care system (effectively obtaining medical resources, such as proactively contacting doctors to handle side effects), teamwork with health care providers (open communication with the medical team), make informed decisions (integrate multi-source information to select treatment plans), be mindful non-adherence (rationally adjust medical orders based on personal values, such as refusing drugs with excessive side effects).(3)“Utilization of resources”: it is the connection and use of internal and external resources to achieve personal goals and benefit others. Specifically manifested as seeking/providing support (sharing experiences through support groups), membership in cancer groups (identifying with the identity of the cancer community and obtaining collective support), and advancing cancer awareness (participating in policy advocacy, public education, or cancer research).Fig. 1Self-advocacy framework. Setting and sample

In a qualitative study, a relatively small and purposively selected sample may be employed, with the aim of increasing the depth (as opposed to breadth) of understanding.28 This study therefore used purposive sampling, a method of identifying and effectively utilizing limited research resources, to select respondents most likely to provide appropriate and useful information.29 Before conduct sampling, the research group discussed the representativeness of samples, such as age, gender and oral communication ability, to achieve the research purpose. Prior to approaching potential participants, the major researcher read the patients' medical records and tried to balance the age distribution of the selected patients and increase the number of female patients. To ensure coverage of patients with varied oral communication ability, the researcher observed patients’ wearing of the tracheostomy tube first and then initiated communication to include those who are willing to participate through pen and paper. Participants were recruited between March and May 2024 at the Head and Neck Surgery Department of Zhejiang Provincial Cancer Hospital. Inclusion criteria for the participants were as follows: 1) aged ≥ 18 years, 2) diagnosed with oral cancer on the basis of pathological examination results, 3) had Water Swallow Test (WST)30 results assessed by clinicians as Grade II or above, 4) were conscious and could communicate in Mandarin or with pen and paper, and 5) voluntarily participated in this study. Exclusion criteria were: 1) a previous or existing stroke, cerebral neuromedullary lesions, or other diseases that could cause dysphagia or 2) metastatic tumors, psychiatric disorders, or comorbidities with other serious life-threatening illnesses (e.g., heart failure and renal failure).

Data collection

Participants have been recruited from wards in a provincial cancer hospital. Using the hospital's in-house electronic medical record system, the researchers reviewed the medical records of all patients in the hospital and identified potential patients with oral cancer with dysphagia. The researcher then entered the ward with a specialized clinician, who administered the WST to potential study subjects to determine if the patient met the inclusion criteria. Participants were informed of the purpose of conducting the trial, which was used to assess subjects' eligibility to participate in the study, and agreed to undergo the WST. Those who met the inclusion criteria were given a detailed verbal explanatory statement by the researcher about the content of the study. This statement emphasized that participation was voluntary, allowing individuals to withdraw at any time without needing to provide a reason. Participants were assured that their personal information would remain confidential and anonymous. Before the interviews, each participant signed an informed consent form.

To collect data, a semi-structured interview guide was developed based on the study's aim and the self-advocacy framework. The guide was subsequently revised through discussion with a research group composed of academicians and clinicians. For example, the term “self-advocacy” is not well understood by Chinese elderly patients. To facilitate understanding, the team agreed to explore self-advocacy from three aspects: thoughts and cognition, actions for the self and utilization of resources. Furthermore, the revised version was refined through trial interviews with three patients. We modified the content and order of the questions in the interview guide to make them more helpful for patients in understanding and expressing their innermost thoughts. Interview guide.

The interviews were conducted after face-to-face communication with the participants, most often in the afternoon, when patients had finished daily treatment. Data were collected through semi-structured interviews in a face-to-face format, which were conducted in the conversation room of the ward. No one other than the questioner, recorder, and participant was present during the interview. Due to physical limitations, some patients’ interviews were arranged in the ward after they provided informed consent. They did not mind other patients in the room overhearing their conversations. Considering the participants' physical status and the fact that too much talking may cause distress and fatigue for patients with oral cancer, each interview lasted for 15–60 min. All the interviews were conducted in Mandarin. Based on the guidance of the theoretical framework, during the data analysis process, the codes or categories have been roughly determined. In order to develop a richer and deeper understanding of categories, saturation is achieved when no new codes or categories emerge during the analysis process on the premise of obtaining relatively sufficient data.31

Data analysis

After the interviews, the researcher listened to the recordings repeatedly and transcribed them within 24 hours. Two researchers (RY and LC) cross-checked the audio recordings with the transcriptions and returned the collated results to the participants to confirm that the interview records were accurate. In addition, we conducted data compilation and analysis using written memos for documentation to increase the credibility and confirmability of the study. Guided by the directed content analysis method32 and using NVivo11 plus software, the transcription data were stored, analyzed, and encoded. The researchers read the interview materials to obtain a sense of immersion and integrity of the data to better understand what was going on in the data. The purpose of this study was to identify and categorize the processes used by patients with oral cancer to cope with dysphagia based on the defining attributes in the self-advocacy framework. In this study, from the aspect of “thoughts and cognition”, we should focus on the interpretation of the meaning of dysphagia by patients with oral cancer, pay attention to whether they have undergone cognitive reconstruction, and whether there is a goal ranking based on personal values when dealing with dysphagia, etc. The “actions for self” focused on the behavioral strategies and obstacles adopted by patients with oral cancer in the medical interaction environment. The “utilization of resources” focused on understanding the social resources available to patients with oral cancer with dysphagia and whether they can promote self-adaptation to the environment. Then, we read and highlighted all the texts that at first impression appear to represent “thoughts and cognition”, “actions for self” and “utilization of resources”. The next step, in the highlighted paragraphs, important statements that fit the patients’ process of self-advocacy in coping with dysphagia were coded according to the specifics in each of the three categories. Any text that could not be categorized with the initial coding scheme would be given a new code. All the codes were pooled, compared repeatedly, categorized according to the three core elements of self-advocacy, and codes with the same attributes were classified and included in the same category. These processes of coding and categorizing were performed by the contribution of two researchers independently. The researchers discussed the preliminary findings with the corresponding author. The final results of the study were decided through discussions at regular Friday group meetings.

Rigor

Several strategies were implemented to ensure the scientific rigor of the findings by guaranteeing credibility, transferability, dependability and confirmability.33 To establish credibility, two researchers independently performed the data analysis, and conducted a thorough review process led by the corresponding author. The first author of this article conducted all the interviews. Her contact with patients with oral cancer during her internship in a head and neck surgical ward facilitated building trust with the participants, thus increasing the credibility of the data. Transferability was strengthened by providing a detailed and comprehensive description of the study's context, including specifics about the setting, participants, and research process. To enhance dependability, the recordings were transcribed verbatim and verified with the participants. Additionally, the main corresponding author of this article is a PhD and professor in nursing with extensive experience in qualitative research and postgraduate mentorship. She actively participated in the entire process of this study and ensured quality in each step, including multiple revisions to the interview guide, the guide for the recruitment of participants, and interview skill training to ensure the authenticity and comprehensiveness of the interview information. Confirmability was addressed through writing reflective notes before and during the analysis process by the authors, all of whom possess significant knowledge and training in qualitative research methodologies. This reflective approach, along with the authors' strict adherence to qualitative research standards, ensured the confirmability of the study's findings.

Results

Demographic characteristics

A total of 21 patients completed the interviews, including 11 patients with oropharynx cancer, 5 with tongue cancer, 3 with mouth floor cancer, and one each with cancer of the buccal mucosa and gingivae. The participants' ages ranged from 31 to 78 years, and the majority were male (85.7%). Most of the patients were married, with only 2 patients being divorced. Most were initially treated with surgery (71.4%), 5 with chemotherapy and 1 with radiotherapy. Semi-structured interview results

The patient presentation was nested and summarized based on the theoretical framework. A coding tree illustrating the main categories, generic categories, codes, and representative quotes is provided in the Supplementary material.

Category 1: thoughts and cognition

Cognitive understanding bias

When participants were asked about their understanding of dysphagia, the majority described symptoms they had experienced, such as coughing, food retention, and pain. P5: “I don't know why I can't swallow, it is easy to get stuck when eating”. However, quite a few participants demonstrated a limited understanding of dysphagia and were unaware that the occurrence of these symptoms might be related to dysphagia. P17: “I don't think I can't swallow it, I just choke”. They perceived dysphagia as a minor issue or a transient episode in life that can be resolved with general treatment and did not recognize the severity of symptoms. P19 stated: “I thought my symptoms would resolve with I.V. infusion”. Some patients also exhibited learned helplessness, which cuts down on positive cognitive adjustments to symptoms in favor of thoughts of resignation. As participant P21 stated: “I think it's just a matter of fate. It's not something you think will improve (showing a negative attitude of not caring and deliberate avoidance).”

Realigning expectations and goals

When interviewees were asked about their most pressing needs and wants in the face of dysphagia, a significant proportion of the patients with oral cancer defined what they wanted to achieve at this stage is to maintaining physiological function. For example P11 said, “I think what I need most right now is to be able to eat, of course.” P12 refused to use a nasogastric tube, “To intubate is to admit defeat, and the fact that I can now swallow on my own is a victory.” In addition to survival needs, developmental needs imply better survival. However, patients seem to have difficulty establishing new goals or thinking about deeper needs. P13: “Because I have trouble swallowing, so of course I want to swallow now. Other things... I don't know what I need.” Some patients mentioned that they no longer focused on the compression and extension of future time, but needed to make sure to cherish the fragmented time at hand. P20: “For me, the calendar is blank except for the treatment day, and the speed and length of future time no longer matter. The only thing that matters at this moment is to cherish and safely get through every fragmented moment at hand - like this meal right now. I must make sure I don't choke.”

Loss of control

In the self-advocacy framework, a sense of empowerment is defined as a sense of command over the obstacles related to cancer. Some interviewees expressed feeling overwhelmed in managing dysphagia, believing that they were incapable of coping with the condition and they had attempted certain methods (e.g., forced swallowing or device-assisted swallowing training) to address dysphagia. P17: “I have attempted to practice independently before, but the effect was not satisfactory, so I lack the motivation to persist.” Some of the patients, due to the influence of symptoms, lead to emotional loss of control. P12: “The inability to eat is highly uncomfortable, resulting in a lack of satiety and taste. Life becomes uninteresting, yet one feels helpless.” Some interviewees indicated that they had experienced situations such as despair and insomnia when managing dysphagia, which they were unable to cope with. For instance, as stated by P3: “When you're faced with a situation like this, you just realize that one's abilities are really limited and I don't know what to do about it.” P16 added: “Swallowing difficulties plunge me into crippling anxiety and insomnia, leading to pre-dawn walks and uncontrollable anger. This loss of control feels overwhelming and beyond my power to manage.”

Category 2: actions for self

Communication barriers with medical professionals

Most of the interviewees reported experiencing communication difficulties with health care professionals during their medical encounters. The embarrassment associated with certain symptoms, such as drooling, slowed speech rate, and unclear pronunciation impedes communication. For example P15 stated, “I did not talk with health care providers, mainly because I cannot speak clearly. Sometimes my drool comes out of my mouth when I talk. I was afraid that they (health care providers) would hate me.”

Some patients also expressed their views from a medical system perspective. In China, especially in some famous hospitals, the health care system is overloaded, clinical work is busy, doctors' time is limited, and doctor-patient communication time is compressed. P18 said, “Sometimes, they are too busy to pay attention to what I am saying. Once I repeat it many times without a response, I will not express it again.” Alternatively, it may be due to the medical staff's tendency to use professional terminology, which results in poor comprehension among interviewees. P10: “Doctors sometimes use professional words, but I do not understand that.”

Limited dependence on physician and internet information searching

The majority of interviewees reported that their knowledge about dysphagia was obtained from the hospital, particularly from their physicians. P10: “I acquire information from doctors.” Some interviewees placed complete trust in the expertise and authority of physicians, leading to a phenomenon of limited autonomous decision-making. P2: “We're all subject to the doctor's judgment, and we do what the doctor tells us to do.” This also reflected the current situation of insufficient supply of medical information. P5: “We don't have access to additional information.”

However, information from health care professionals is relatively limited due to communication barriers. A small number of interviewees indicated that when unable to obtain sufficient information from health care professionals, they would utilize the internet for research. Baidu is the most frequently used search engine for accessing medical information resources. Interviewee P6 stated: “I will also search for relevant information on Baidu. You can know more about treatments and effects.”

Category 3: utilization of resources

Two-way tension of family support

Some participants required familial support and expressed a desire to communicate with their family members. They predominantly selected their spouse as their primary confidant. P10: “I will share my inner thoughts with my wife.” However, utilizing family support presented certain challenges. Participants reported that they often inhibited their expression with their families. P1 articulated this perspective, “I don't talk to my family as much when I have this symptom. My mouth always dries if I talk too much. Sometimes my family does not understand what I'm talking about.” P11 added: “Whenever my family takes good care of me and urges me to exercise, that sense of helplessness always wells up in my heart, but I always habitually keep it silent and don't let it show the slightest bit.” Conversely, family members demonstrate a willingness to assist patients but encounter difficulties in doing so. Participants opted to manage their condition independently, taking into consideration the physical condition or occupation of their family members. P12 responded, “I do sometimes have to bear a little more by myself, such as making food by myself. Since my husband is not well either, I don't want to bother him.”

Dual effects of peer support

Patients with oral cancer' perceptions of peer support were polarized between “potential resources” and “ineffective resources”. Some found it somewhat beneficial. P5 said, “I think I really need this kind of (sharing) experience.” Others felt that peer support did not make a significant difference and that few individuals in their vicinity experienced dysphagia. P10: “I don't have strong ideas about this. I don't believe in others. And I am not sure what kind of peer groups are there.” Each participant had their own specific method of obtaining peer support. As a result of identity symbolization, some people can identify their peers directly from the patient's disease epiphenomena. P8: “When I saw them, I knew we were in the same boat.” Furthermore, those who experienced improved symptoms expressed a sense of relative fortune and gratitude when comparing themselves to patients with other types of cancer, particularly those with high mortality rates. P3: “Compared to those patients with liver cancer and lung cancer, I am lucky.”

Structural economic exclusion

The participants expressed strong hope for refinement of national health insurance policies, particularly improvements in coverage. P20: “Our country's health insurance policy still needs to be improved. For example, swallowing function training devices are not included in the reimbursement catalog, and the design of the policy is out of touch with patients' needs.” For some individuals, financial constraints were a significant barrier to managing dysphagia “it is too expensive for us” [P13]. This direct financial burden forces patients to choose inefficient alternatives. The absence of health insurance coverage for certain treatments led some patients to consider discontinuing treatment and rehabilitation. Other patients terminated their rehabilitation program early due to a lack of health insurance coverage for certain treatments. P14: “We have been through a lot and there is no financial support, and we really cannot stand it.”

Discussion

Main findings

This study explored the experiences of patients with oral cancer coping with dysphagia in terms of the three defining characteristics: “thoughts and cognition”, “actions for self”, and “utilization of resources” of self-advocacy as demonstrated by the self-advocacy framework. The results revealed that Chinese patients with oral cancer lack a clear perception of dysphagia, an insufficient sense of empowerment leads to a lack of close synergistic cooperation with health care professionals, and some objective conditions, such as individual characteristics and economic conditions, affect the acquisition and utilization of social resources. All of the above factors affect patients' ability to cope, and poorer results further counteract patients' autonomy, leading to a vicious cycle, which ultimately threatens the integrity of the swallowing rehabilitation pathway and affects patients’ quality of life.

First of all, in this study, the results of patients with oral cancer in terms of “thoughts and cognition” do not conform to the ideal state proposed by the framework. Participants struggled with cognitive biases due to incorrect symptom attribution, exhibited learned helplessness, and were unable to prioritize adaptive goals other than immediate survival. “Thoughts and cognition” was proposed as the basis for actions for self and utilization of resources. It determines whether patients can correctly identify their own needs and form the willingness to act.21 Thus, cognitive reconstruction and adaptation to the new normal life are the keys for patients to deal with dysphagia. Interviews in the study revealed that patients with oral cancer have limited knowledge of dysphagia. The absence of this may be related to inadequate health education, which was also reported by Llewellyn et al.34 The cognitive understanding bias shown in this study reflects both the medical knowledge divide and cultural cognitive stereotypes. Crossing the medical knowledge divide requires more efficient generalization and contextualization of knowledge transfer.35 It has been suggested that health professionals should improve the methods of knowledge dissemination to help patients deconstruct physiological misconceptions and rebuild their understanding of chronic diseases. For example, Govender et al.36 utilized animated videos to stimulate patient curiosity, enabling patients to enhance their understanding by visualizing the swallowing process depicted in the animation. Pathways for shifting cultural cognitive stereotypes can be achieved through culturally appropriate intervention designs or improving patient health literacy.37,38 Furthermore, regarding the issue of patients' difficulty in creating a new normal in the results, it might be influenced by traditional Chinese fatalism, which regards dysphagia as fate rather than a situation that can be self-dealt with, leading to the collapse of the belief in empowerment.39 In the future, swallowing rehabilitation education should take cultural cognition into consideration and integrate Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) notion that are acceptable to the public. For example, TCM claimed that tongue muscle training strengthens the “qi” of the spleen and stomach and helps food transportation.40 This can help Chinese cancer patients better understand rehabilitation methods and break cultural cognitive stereotype that only medication/infusion is considered as treatment.

From the “actions for self” dimension of the self-advocacy framework, the occurrence of patient behaviors is significantly related to personal characteristics and learned skills, representing the externalization of intrinsic competence. The results of this study indicated that in the medical interaction environment, patients with oral cancer exhibited inadequate medical coping strategies when managing dysphagia and exhibited situations of doctor-patient communication breakdown and information dependence. Poor doctor-patient communication was not only due to the physiological disorders in the oral and maxillofacial regions caused by surgery or tracheostomy, but also influenced by the self-stigmatization of patients caused by accompanying symptoms of dysphagia, such as salivation and slurred speech.41 Koo's team developed a card-based communication tool that patients with head and neck tumors could use quickly to express ideas.42 This provides a reference for solving the current problems in clinical practice and developing communication tools that are suitable for Chinese culture and daily work, particularly a postoperative communication checklist, to guide communication between physicians and patients or patients' families.43 In the results of this study, the alternative method of searching for information through the Internet has the risk of uncontrollable information quality. Some false information may lead to the failure of patient empowerment, which in turn affects the reconstruction of patients' thoughts and cognition.44 In the future, in order to promote the generation of patient active rehabilitation and other behaviors, we can draw on the core elements of international patient communication and decision support models, incorporate the characteristics of Chinese culture, and enhance patients' participation and experience in the medical interactive environment.45

Finally, utilization of resources is an extension of thought and cognition, and action for self, and its effects are influenced by both. Different from the self-advocacy framework focus on external resource, the current study found that patients with oral cancer consider the important impact of family members in their coping with dysphagia. This coincides with traditional Chinese family culture, which regarded the family as the foundation and the most important source of support.11 This notion still plays an important role nowadays. When patients take certain actions, they consciously consider the impact and consequences of their behaviors on their families and family members, such as the altruistic mentality exhibited by the patients in this study, who opted to isolate themselves and refrain from placing pressure or burdens on their families. In addition, owing to the taboo nature of the word “cancer” in China, the traditional feudal ideology discourages the discussion of death and cancer publicly.46 When a patient requires assistance, the family's honor and reputation are meticulously preserved. This sense of “face” may cause a patient to reduce contact with other patients as a means of avoiding external judgment.47 This explains why patients in this study mentioned family support more frequently than they did peer support. In addition, patients in this study raised the objective environmental resource pertaining to the consistent and payable swallowing training program, which could hardly be resolved in a short period but requires long-term efforts.48 Financial considerations are a significant objective factor influencing the initiation of self-advocacy behavior in cancer patients.49 At present, Chinese government endeavors to promote community health care services and family doctor contract models through national policies, which could help to allieviate patients' financial constraints by shorter distance medical care. The obstacle for patients with oral cancer’ utilization of the resource is the deficient referral system with clear responsibilities.50,51 Health care providers should also actively engage with community health care service centers to provide comprehensive and multidimensional social support for patients with oral cancer with dysphagia, from early assessment to swallowing rehabilitation management, through continuous care and other methods to promote the accessibility of public welfare social organizations and encourage symptom-seeking behavior.52

Implications for nursing practice and research

This study highlights the critical need to foster self-advocacy in patients with oral cancer managing dysphagia. Nursing practice should integrate empowerment strategies, such as skill-building interventions, patient-provider partnerships, and culturally tailored education, to enhance patients’ ability to navigate care challenges. Future research must develop and evaluate self-advocacy training programs, explore digital tools for symptom management, and investigate how patient empowerment influences long-term outcomes. By bridging gaps in health literacy and systemic barriers, these efforts can transform patients from passive recipients into active agents in their dysphagia care, ultimately improving quality of life and rehabilitation success.

Limitations

This study had several limitations. First, all the participants were recruited from a large hospital in Zhejiang Province, China, which may have biased the results. Second, some patients with relatively severe dysphagia may have had large secretions in the throat or experienced pain that limited their ability to talk much. These objective factors may have contributed to the shorter duration of some of the patient interviews. Therefore, tools such as a pen and paper should be used to assist in interviews or structured interviews to encourage patients to communicate their feelings more during the procedure. We occasionally involved family members in communication, which could have resulted in inadequate descriptions of some participants’ experiences. Finally, fewer female patients were included in this study, and there may be some limitations in the results.

Conclusions

This study used a qualitative approach to explore the self-advocacy experience of patients with oral cancer when dealing with dysphagia. The results reveal that when swallowing becomes a battlefield, self-advocacy is not only an individual skill but also a multi-party game among patients, their families, the medical system, and social policies. These findings can help health care professionals better understand patients' challenges in coping with dysphagia and provide appropriate support. Future research could build on this study to develop a swallowing rehabilitation support program for patients with oral cancer that promotes patient self-coping and effective symptom management.
