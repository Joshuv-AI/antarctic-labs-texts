Children with recurrent urinary tract infections: who are they and why do we need better prevention options?
Authors: Virginie Boulanger, Hubert Vincent, Zineb Laghdir, Diego Barrieras, Julie Franc-Guimond, Elyse Potvin, Caroline Quach
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRecurrent UTIs (rUTIs) in children can lead to renal scarring and chronic renal failure, if not managed early. The current standard of care involves antibiotic prophylaxis, which benefits remain controversial. There is a need for new interventions, other than antimicrobial use, to reduce the occurrence of rUTIs as well as the identification of groups to target. Our objective was to describe the pediatric population most susceptible to rUTIs, including renal scarring and adverse events associated with prolonged antibiotic use.MethodWe conducted a single-centre retrospective chart review of patients with rUTIs, diagnosed with urinary tract abnormalities or functional disorders, followed at the Centre hospitalier universitaire (CHU) Sainte-Justine urology clinic between January 2015 and December 2020. We described the population and evaluated the antimicrobial usage and uropathogens resistance patterns.ResultsIdentified patients with rUTI (n = 107) had underlying medical conditions such as Spina Bifida or neurogenic bladder (31%), double collecting system (22%), vesicoureteral reflux (42%) and ureteropelvic junction obstruction (7%). Almost all patients (87%) were prescribed antimicrobial prophylaxis, and a significant proportion developed resistance, with 70% of breakthrough UTIs being resistant to at least one drug, and 41% demonstrating multi-drug resistance.ConclusionWhile most patients received prophylaxis, it was not universally effective, leading to persistent concerns like renal scarring and adverse events associated with prolonged antimicrobial use. Background

Urinary tract infections (UTIs) are common infections in children, and those with congenital malformations of the urinary tract are at risk of recurrent UTIs (rUTIs), which can lead to renal scarring, chronic renal failure, reduced renal perfusion, and hypertension [1–4]. Vesicoureteral reflux (VUR), which is present in 30% to 40% of children with UTIs, has been associated with rUTI [5]. Other risk factors include the absence of circumcision, bladder and bowel dysfunction (BBD), and fecal retention [6–8].

The standard of care for children at risk of rUTIs includes antimicrobial prophylaxis, whose effectiveness in preventing rUTIs and renal scarring remains controversial [9]. Prolonged antibiotics use can lead to adverse events [10], and some studies suggested that prophylaxis does not reduce renal scarring and may increase antimicrobial resistance (AMR) [7, 11]. Moreover, UTIs are often treated empirically, with treatment adjusted once the uropathogen’s susceptibility profile is available [7, 8, 12].

Interventions aimed at reducing the occurrence of rUTIs without the reliance on antibiotics are imperative, but the populations that would most benefit from this intervention remain uncertain. This study aimed to identify the pediatric population most susceptible to rUTIs in a urology clinic, evaluate antibiotic use and assess the risk of AMR.

Method

Study design and patient population

We conducted a retrospective chart review to identify patients with rUTI followed at the CHU Sainte-Justine urology clinic between January 2015 and December 2020. The CHU Sainte-Justine is a standalone tertiary mother-child academic hospital. The study population included (1) all children under 2 years of age and (2) children of any age who underwent imaging and were diagnosed with risk factors for rUTIs (i.e., VUR, ureteropelvic junction obstruction (UPJO), double collecting system (DCS), posterior ureteral valve or megaureter), functional urinary tract disorder (e.g.dysfunctional elimination syndrome, neurogenic bladder), spina bifida or myelomeningocele. Patients with renal calculi were excluded to prevent interference with rUTI outcomes.

Definition of variables

At our hospital, UTI is defined as a positive urine culture with (1) any growth of a pathogenic bacteria from a suprapubic aspiration, (2) a colony forming unit (CFU) count of 50 × 10^6/L from a catheter sample, or (3) 100 × 10^6/L from a midstream voided urine sample [13]. Recurrent UTI was defined as at least two episodes of UTI over a six-month period or three within a year [14].

Data collection

We categorized patients based on their anatomical or functional groups and included variables such as sex, age, presence of hydronephrosis, bladder dysfunction, constipation, circumcision, and renal scarring. We identified the number of UTIs, the uropathogen and susceptibility profile for each infection, and details on antibiotics used for prophylaxis or treatment. Data were retrieved through a review of patients’ charts using our hospital Chartmaxx®/Quanum® software, which contains all medical records since 2014.

Data analysis

We described patients’ clinical and demographic characteristics stratified by medical condition, calculated UTI incidence and recurrence rates, analyzed antimicrobial susceptibility for each infection, using antimicrobial categories created by Magiorakos et al. [15] and assessed correlation with antibiotic usage.

Findings were summarized using frequencies and percentages for categorical data, and medians and interquartile ranges for continuous non-normally distributed data. Incidence was calculated using the number of infections recorded in the period between the first and last infection for each patient. We compared clinical and demographic data between medical conditions using χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical data, and ANOVA or the Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous data, as appropriate. We considered p values ≤ 0.05 as statistically significant. We performed analysis using RStudio version 2023.09.0 + 463.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by CHU Sainte-Justine Research Ethics Board (Approval no. 2021–3030). As this was of retrospective nature, CHU Sainte-Justine Research Ethics Board waived the requirement to obtain parental or guardian informed consent to conduct this study.

Results

A total of 1215 patient charts were reviewed and 123 (10%) met the criteria for rUTI. However, we decided to include only patients with four conditions: VUR, spina bifida/neurogenic bladder (Sb/Nb), DCS and UPJO, as there were insufficient patients with other conditions.

Description of sample

Table 1 describes the patient population (n = 107, 74% female), with a median age at the end of the follow-up period of 6.4 years (IQR 4.2, 10.7; range: 1.29, 19.2 years. Median age was higher for Sb/Nb patients (11.1 years) (p < 0.001). We observed a higher proportion of females in all groups, except for UPJO. Overall, 67% of the cohort had VUR and 36% of patients had renal scars, with a higher percentage in severe VUR cases (68%) (p = 0.003).

Compared with patients without the medical condition, patients with UPJO (p < 0.01) and severe VUR (p = 0.02) had more hydronephrosis while patients with Sb/Nb had more bladder dysfunction (p < 0.001), more constipation (p = 0.04) and less renal scar detected (p = 0.05). Nearly half of patients with Sb/Nb, DCS or UPJO also had VUR, including 63% of patients with DCS. Among DCS patients, 38% had a ureterocele, and 17% an ectopic ureter. 70% of Sb/Nb patients used clean intermittent catheterization (CIC), while 24% used diapers and 6% had a vesicostomy.

Most patients received prophylaxis (87%), including all those with VUR (Table 1). The median age for initiating prophylaxis was 1.2 years (IQR 0.4, 4.1), with Trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole (TMP/SMX) being the most prescribed (89%), followed by cephalexin (31%), nitrofurantoin (26%), and amoxicillin (17%), depending on doctor’s preference or patient’s tolerance to the antimicrobial. Furthermore, 53% of patients received more than one prophylactic antimicrobial, as physicians prescribed cycling of classes. This approach was used in 60% of patients with severe VUR. The median age at first infection was 0.4 years (IQR 0.2, 0.7).

Infection characteristics and antibiotic resistance

Incidence and recurrence of infections

The incidence of UTIs was 3.0 per patient-years, with the highest rates observed in patients with severe VUR (Table 2). Of a total of 523 positive cultures detected, 89% met the rUTI definition. The median time between two rUTIs was 50 days (IQR 27, 92); patients with severe VUR showing the shortest duration (median: 43 days) while patients with VUR grade I-III, the longest (median: 62 days) (Table 2). Of note, incidence of infections for Sb/Nb patients without CIC was 8.67 (95% CI: 6.9, 10.8), compared to 2.33 (95% CI: 2.0, 2.7) for patients with CIC. Patients without CIC were often those with more complex conditions or non-cooperative behavior, whose parents have refused CIC.

Microbiology and antibiotic resistance

54% of infections identified in our cohort were due to E. coli, 12% to Enterococcus sp., 9% to Klebsiella pneumoniae and 6% to both Enterobacter cloacae complex and P. aeruginosa (Table 3). At least 50% of UTIs were caused by E. coli for all medical conditions, except for UPJO (38%), which presented more infection caused by P. mirabilis than in the other conditions (p < 0.01). On average, bacterial isolates were resistant to 2.3 antibiotic classes, with 70% of bacteria displaying resistance to at least one class and 41% showing resistance to three classes or more (Table 3). Additionally, more than 20% of E. coli, Klebsiella sp., M. morganii and Acinetobacter sp. and 11% of Enterobacter complex were resistant to at least five classes of antibiotics (Table 3).

When stratified by medical condition, we noted that UTIs in patients with UPJO were significantly more resistant to at least one class of antibiotics (p = 0.02) while Sb/Nb had more resistance to at least three classes of antibiotics (p = 0.01).

Prevention and treatment of rUTIs

Description of prophylaxis in the sample population

Eighteen patients (23%) were on prophylaxis before their first documented infection, 18 began immediately after the first infection, and 5 patients (6%) did not receive prophylaxis. Notably, no patient with isolated low-grade VUR received prophylaxis before their first rUTI (Supplementary material, Table S1).

Prophylaxis was initiated after an average of 2.4 infections, although it was initiated more promptly in children with UPJO and severe VUR cases (Supplementary material, Table S1). All patients with VUR received prophylaxis. At the end of the follow-up period, 41% of patients were still on prophylaxis (> 73% of children with DCS, 44% of those other conditions, but 0% for those with UPJO). Prophylaxis was discontinued because of absence of UTI (e.g., resolution of the underlying medical issue after surgery), or at the parent’s request (11%). In these cases, parental concerns included possible side effects (n = 8), lack of efficacy and resistance (n = 3), or reluctance to use more antibiotics (n = 1). However, most patients resumed prophylaxis after developing new infections (n = 10).

Association of prophylaxis with antimicrobial resistance

Nighty-1% of patients on TMP/SMX prophylaxis developed resistance, 52% upon their first infection after initiating prophylaxis and 64% within one year of initiating prophylaxis, with a median time of 162 days (IQR 69, 259) (Table 4). Of note, all patients had some resistance upon their fourth infections following the start of TMP/SMX prophylaxis.

Treatment of UTI

Antimicrobials used for the treatment of 343 UTIs were analyzed (Table 5). The predominant treatment prescribed was IV antimicrobials (ampicillin, tobramycin, piperacillin/tazobactam), administered in 36% of cases. Additionally, cefixime was prescribed in 27% of infections. Cephalexin was used significantly more in Sb/Nb patients (p = 0.04) and less in DCS patients (p < 0.01), while IV medication was used more in patients with DCS and severe VUR (p < 0.01) and less in Sb/Nb patients (p < 0.001). In patients with CIC, 21% of positive cultures were asymptomatic and untreated. Overall, 30% of empiric treatment needed to be adjusted because of resistance, once the susceptibility profile was available, particularly for ampicillin.

Surgery

Almost all patients with DCS and severe VUR underwent one or multiple surgeries. DCS patients mostly had surgeries around 1.7 year (median, IQR 0.9, 2.1) and patients with severe VUR, around 2.8 years of age (IQR 2.1, 3.7). 68% of patients with severe VUR without other conditions underwent surgery or had planned surgery (data not available for three patients). All surgeries were successful (resolution of rUTI) except one (Deflux injection). Among DCS patients, eleven had surgery for DCS (46%), 9 of 15 with VUR had VUR correction and six underwent both procedures. 44% of those with ureterocele had surgery (two complete excision, two heminephrectomy). Six UPJO patients (75%) underwent surgery (median age 0.6 years, IQR 0.1, 0.9), one also had VUR correction. One developed UTIs afterwards, while the others saw a reduction in their UTIs. Only one patient with Sb/Nb and severe VUR had VUR surgery. Among mild VUR cases (n = 20), seven had surgery (35%), all with bilateral VUR, while five had spontaneous resolution. Surgery resolved hydronephrosis in all affected patients.

45% of circumcisions were performed due to phimosis. Almost all male patients with severe VUR without other condition underwent circumcision (88%). No patients with DCS or Sb/Nb were circumcised, except for one with phimosis.

Discussion

This study aimed to characterize subgroups of children followed in a pediatric tertiary care urology clinic, at high risk of developing rUTIs, and assess antimicrobial resistance of breakthrough infections. Rather than restricting our analysis to a single etiology, we sought to reflect the real-world population encountered in everyday practice, identifying a range of underlying conditions associated with rUTIs and describing their prophylactic and therapeutic pathways. We identified four high-risk populations: Sb/Nb, DCS, UPJO, and VUR.

Consistent with previous studies [9, 16–19], patients with VUR had the highest prevalence of renal scars (68% for severe VUR, 40% for low-grades VUR), while severe VUR was also associated with the highest rUTIs incidence. Patients with Sb/Nb had the highest number of infections overall, although the incidence was not higher than the average, likely because 70% of these patients were born before 2014, before which we had no data. Consequently, there were significant gaps in information regarding their early infections. In fact, their first infection documented in our system was around the age of 6 years. In the literature, more than 50% of children with Spina Bifida develop a UTI by 15 months of age, with 44% having more than 5 UTIs, and 9% more than 20 in their childhood years [20]. Despite our limited data, we observed that 54% of patients experienced at least eight infections since this study onset (after 2014 or from birth). Moreover, these patients demonstrated the highest rates of multi-drug resistant (MDR) isolates (non-susceptible to three or more antibiotic classes), consistent with Ortiz et al.’s findings [21]. Patients with Sb/Nb were the oldest in our cohort, thus had longer exposure to antibiotics. Moreover, 97% had bladder dysfunction, with 70% using CIC.

Duplication abnormalities were the most common urinary tract anomaly reported [22], but few studies reported on risk of resistance associated with these conditions. DCS was associated with high resistance to multiple antibiotic classes and had the shortest interval between recurrent infections. Most patients also had VUR (62%), consistent with the literature [23, 24], and 50% presented with hydronephrosis. Surgery for DCS was typically performed around age two. As our hospital is a tertiary referral center managing more complex urological cases, several patients required multiple surgeries, including procedures for DCS itself or for associated anomalies such as ectopic ureter, VUR, or ureterocele, and some still experienced rUTIs after surgery, all of whom had ureterocele. As previously reported [25], bladder dysfunction associated with ureterocele may persist despite surgical correction, leading to rUTIs.

Patients with the most resistance to at least one antibiotic were patients with UPJO. These patients developed infections early, generally around the age of 5 months and initiated prophylaxis typically around the age of 4 months. However, most patients underwent surgery within their first year of life, and all but one discontinued prophylaxis following surgery, as they were infection-free thereafter. A study found that antibiotic prophylaxis was not indicated for infants with UPJO, due to minimal UTI risk, regardless of the severity of hydronephrosis [26]. However, other studies reported a positive association with UTIs and the need for surgical intervention, with rates of UTIs and related hospitalizations notably decreasing after UPJO repair [27, 28].

Antibiotics use and resistance

At CHU Sainte-Justine, the recommendations were to give antibiotic prophylaxis to patients with severe VUR or significant urological anomalies, and to consider prophylaxis for certain patients with low-grade reflux based on the presence of specific risk factors (age < 1 year, not toilet trained, history of rUTI). TMP-SMX and nitrofurantoin were the preferred agents, but amoxicillin was favored in infants aged 2 months and younger [13].

In our cohort, almost all patients received prophylaxis, primarily TMP/SMX. Cephalexin seemed preferred to nitrofurantoin. A few children experienced side effects from nitrofurantoin, prompting parents to request a change.

Most patients on TMP/SMX became resistant at some point. Studies show that resistance to TMP/SMX is very common in UTIs [29, 30]. In our cohort, 42% of patients receiving TMP/SMX required a change to another antibiotic at some point, mainly due to resistance.

We also noted significant resistance to ampicillin, which was frequently used empirically but had to be changed. A study found ampicillin insufficient for most infections due to high resistance rates, and similar conclusions were drawn for infections treated with TMP/SMX [30]. Our study aligns with these findings, as 30% of patients received at one point a treatment with an ineffective antibiotic. Knowledge of the local antimicrobial susceptibility profiles is key in developing treatment guidelines.

Ultimately, our study found that 70% of isolated bacteria were resistant to at least one antibiotic, with 41% showing multi-drug resistance. While 87% of patients received prophylaxis, almost none resolved their rUTIs with prophylaxis alone. In cases where surgical intervention successfully addressed the rUTIs, patients had already developed antibiotic resistance by that time. Among them, patients with VUR additionally developed a higher incidence of renal scarring. It is noteworthy that, in our cohort, children with DCS or Sb/Nb were not always circumcised, even when this may contribute to UTI recurrence, highlighting the variability in management approaches and the complexity of decision-making in everyday clinical settings.

Given the controversy surrounding antibiotic prophylaxis, with its mixed effectiveness and its association with adverse events, and the lack of new antibiotics, particularly in the pediatric population, we must identify alternative strategies to prevent rUTIs. A recent randomized study of Uromune® (MV140), a sublingual immunotherapy made of inactivated whole cell bacteria, showed promising results in adult women [31]. With no reported side effects, the UTI-free rate over the nine-month efficacy period was 40.6%, a 75.3% reduction compared to the infection rate in the year prior to vaccination. This innovative strategy could become a vital tool for preventing rUTIs in at-risk pediatric population.

Limitation

Our study has certain limitations. Based on a retrospective chart review, it included missing or incomplete data. Although patients attended annual follow-up visits at our center, infections were often managed at their local hospital, leaving the available information dependent on parental recall or external reports. For instance, details on UTIs symptoms (including febrile status), voiding dysfunction and toilet training status were often imprecise, limiting reliable analysis of these factors. However, given this cohort’s susceptibility to UTIs, it is unlikely that an infection would be treated without a urine culture, minimizing sampling bias. Additionally, the electronic system used only contained data from 2014 onwards. Furthermore, we did not assess the effectiveness of antibiotic prophylaxis, as we lacked a comparison group; nearly all patients in our cohort were on prophylaxis. Finally, we included all types of UTIs, including asymptomatic cases such as in patients with CIC, to capture broader patterns of recurrent UTIs and management practices in a real-world pediatric urology setting.

Conclusion

In summary, patient with rUTIs were identified with underlying medical conditions such as Sb\Nb, DCS, VUR and UPJO. Most were prescribed antibiotic prophylaxis, and a significant proportion developed resistance. Surgery successfully addressed rUTI for certain conditions. However, this was not universally effective, leading to persistent concerns such as renal scarring and adverse events associated with prolonged antibiotic use. There is a critical need for the development of new strategies to prevent rUTI and to minimize reliance on antibiotic prophylaxis.
