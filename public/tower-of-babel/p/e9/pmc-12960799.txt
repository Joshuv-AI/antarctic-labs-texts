Systematic rise in flight cancellations during space weather events
Authors: R D Luo, Y Wang, F S Wei, X S Feng, M H Bo, H W Tang, D S Wang, L Bian, B Y Wang, Z Li, P B Zuo, C W Jiang, X J Xu, Z L Zhou, Y X Tong, D Gao, Y X Cao, W Q Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSolar activity can significantly influence the performance and reliability of both space-borne and ground-based technological systems on Earth. This phenomenon, known as space weather, can impact modern aviation operations through complex and highly interconnected mechanisms, presenting a challenging area of study. Until now, no systematic investigation has explored the influence of space weather on flight cancellations outside of specific polar routes. By analyzing ~ 5 million flight departure records from 5 hub airports in China between 2015 and 2019, a period spanning the declining phase and subsequent minimum of Solar Cycle 24, we provide the first evidence of a systematic increase in flight cancellations during space weather events. Our comparative analysis reveals that the average cancellation rate during space weather events, specifically Solar Flares, Interplanetary Coronal Mass Ejections, and Solar Proton Events, was 96.96% higher than during quiet periods. Counter-intuitively, Stream Interaction Regions showed no increase and even a slight decrease in cancellation rates. Additionally, investigations on geomagnetic-ionospheric disturbances reveal a quasi-linear relation between the rate of flight cancellations and the magnitude of perturbations. Such increased flight cancellations persist even when accounting for other factors, such as the seasonal effects. These findings imply that disruptions in communication and navigation systems during space weather events can significantly impact modern aviation operations, leading to more frequent flight cancellations. The superposed epoch analysis uncovers a well-defined temporal response curve of cancellation rates to independent space weather events. The correlations observed suggest that prolonged flight delays induced by space weather may be another important driver of the increased cancellation rates. This research expands the conventional understanding of space weather’s impact on aviation and it implies the importance of integrating space weather as a systematic factor in flight operations and planning, which may also help improve aviation efficiency and safety.Keywords: Space Weather, Solar Flare, Interplanetary Coronal Mass Ejection, Solar Proton Event, Flight CancellationsSubject terms: Solar physics, Transient astrophysical phenomena

Introduction

The role of high-tech devices in human society is becoming increasingly significant, especially in the realms of communication and navigation. These systems are deeply integrated into daily life, yet they are highly vulnerable to solar activity. Space Weather Events (SWEs) such as Solar Flares (SFs), Interplanetary Coronal Mass Ejections (ICMEs), and Solar Proton Events (SPEs) can disrupt the near-Earth space environment, leading to interference or even disruption of communication and navigation systems1. SFs are sudden brightening events on the Sun’s surface that release broadband electromagnetic radiation, impacting the geospace environment2,3. ICMEs are dense plasma clouds ejected from the Sun that can trigger geomagnetic storms and ionospheric disturbances when they interact with Earth’s magnetosphere4–6. SPEs are highly energetic charged particles capable of penetrating Earth’s atmospheric shield, posing risks to satellites, astronauts, and avionics7.

Modern aviation operations are fundamentally reliant on the integrity of their Communications, Navigation, and Surveillance (CNS) systems, the technological backbone of Air Traffic Management (ATM)8,9. Any degradation within this trifecta can cascade into severe operational disruptions. A loss of reliable communication severs the vital link between pilots and ground control, rendering safe separation and traffic flow instructions impossible10. Simultaneously, errors in Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS) positioning can corrupt flight path and altitude data, creating unacceptable safety risks11. When surveillance systems fail, the real-time situational awareness of the entire airspace is lost12,13. In such scenarios where the foundational elements of flight safety are jeopardized, standard procedure often dictates the most conservative response: proactive flight rescheduling and, in many cases, outright cancellations to prevent potential incidents.

The link between SWEs and CNS interference is involved within the Earth’s magnetosphere-ionosphere system, where distinct types of SWEs trigger specific physical phenomena. SFs, particularly those of X/M-class, unleash intense radiation that causes Sudden Ionospheric Disturbances (SIDs) across Earth’s sunlit hemisphere. These SIDs can degrade, or entirely black out, High-Frequency (HF) radio communications14,15,72. In parallel, Earth-directed ICMEs, especially those carrying a significant southward magnetic field, could provoke global-scale geomagnetic storms. Such storms restructure the entire ionosphere, simultaneously inducing signal scintillations that compromise GNSS services and causing large-scale deviations in electron density that can severely disrupt HF communications worldwide16–21. SPEs, in turn, funnel energetic particles into the polar caps, causing intense Polar Cap Absorption (PCA) that can cripple HF communications along critical transpolar routes22. Collectively, these phenomena manifest as degraded communication quality, increased transmission latency, and significant positioning errors, fundamentally undermining the integrity of the CNS architecture19,23,24.

Therefore, space weather has the potential to significantly affect flight operations and cause increased flight cancellations. However, the systemic impact of SWEs on flight cancellations has largely been overlooked25. From a theoretical perspective, the correlation between the two is not straightforward but involves a complex multi-coupled effect, making the impact of space weather on flight cancellations not intuitively obvious26. Additionally, traditional space weather theories do not yet adequately address the systematic impact on flight cancellations27. While some studies have documented the effects of SWEs on specific polar flights, where increased radiation and disrupted HF communications can lead to rerouting and occasional cancellations28,29, these cases represent only a small fraction of global air traffic30. This has fostered a conventional view that space weather has no systematic impact or only a minor impact on flight cancellations.

This perception endures despite significant efforts by organizations like the International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO) to raise awareness31–34. To address the challenges imposed by space weather, ICAO specifies a set of parameters to be monitored by space weather centers and provides also thresholds of advisory issuances for two intensity levels: moderate and severe19. Indeed, this framework has spurred international collaboration to create operational services like PECASUS, which showcases the current pinnacle of translating complex space weather science into practical tools for enhancing global aviation safety. Yet, even with these state-of-the-art services, a fundamental limitation persists. The existing framework successfully categorizes the physical severity of an event, but it does not provide a quantitative assessment of its operational impact, such as the expected increase in flight cancellation rates under specific conditions. A huge gap has led mainstream research on flight cancellations to remain focused on traditional factors like air traffic control, operational schedules, tropospheric weather, and the associated economic and temporal losses for airlines8,13,35–37. Consequently, a comprehensive analysis to quantify the systemic impact of space weather on flight cancellations has, to date, been conspicuously absent25.

Quantifying the impact of space weather on aviation operations, particularly on flight delays and cancellations, represents a significant research challenge26. A combination of factors, including the intrinsic complexity of the phenomena, a traditional research focus confined to polar routes or specified events, and the absence of a theoretical framework to connect space weather related drivers with operational outcomes, has historically hindered rigorous quantitative analysis. It was not until our own recent work, analyzing a large dataset of flight records from five major Chinese hub airports, that the first statistically significant evidence emerged showing that flight delays systematically increase during SWEs38–40. This finding challenged the conventional view by demonstrating that the influence of space weather is not confined to extreme events or high-latitude regions, but is a pervasive factor affecting mainstream aviation operations. Meanwhile, delving into the physical drivers, we found that flight delay time increased monotonically with the intensity of magnetosphere-ionosphere disturbances, as quantified by indices such as the change of the Dst index (|dDst/dt|), the Rate of Total Electron Content (TEC) Index (ROTI), and the deviation of the F2-layer critical frequency (ΔfoF2).

While these findings strongly suggested a correlation between SWEs and flight delays, they focused solely on delays, excluding flight cancellation data. This left us uncertain about whether space weather could systematically impact flight cancellations. Additionally, other confounding variables, such as seasonal effects, which are known to significantly influence both flight operations41, were not adequately accounted for in our previous studies. Furthermore, the lack of detailed investigations into the temporal relationships between space weather effects and aviation operations limited our ability to establish causality between the two.

To address these limitations and contribute to the growing body of knowledge on space weather’s impact on aviation operations, this letter will comprehensively analyze flight cancellations during SWEs and compare them to quiet time periods (QTPs). We will also account for seasonal effects and employ superposed epoch analysis to reveal temporal causal relationships. Furthermore, to elucidate the potential mechanisms accounting for flight cancellations, we correlate the flight cancellations with 4 key geomagnetic-ionospheric indices previously linked to flight delays38,39, hypothesizing that they are also catalysts for cancellations1,19,42.

The Ap index, derived from the quasi-logarithmic Kp scale, serves as a standardized indicator of global geomagnetic activity43. While not strictly a linear measure, Ap provides a well-established proxy for the energy input from the solar wind that can disrupt the entire magnetosphere-ionosphere system. To capture the dynamic aspects of perturbation process, we also use |dDst/dt44. This index is particularly sensitive to the rapid intensification or decay of the ring current during a geomagnetic storm45, a process associated with the injection of energy that can fuel severe ionospheric irregularities. Besides, the ROTI is a widely used metric for quantifying small-to-medium scale irregularities in the ionosphere46,47. High ROTI values are correlated with the occurrence of ionospheric scintillation, a phenomenon known to degrade GNSS signal quality, potentially leading to increased positioning errors or even loss-of-lock events that can compromise navigation system reliability. Concurrently, ΔfoF2 offers a crucial insight into the state of the ionosphere’s dominant layer, which can affect both communication and navigation systems48–50. A significant deviation in foF2 from its quiet-time median indicates a profoundly disturbed ionospheric state. Such deviations not only directly lead to the degradation or blackout of HF communications but also signify large-scale changes in the total electron content, a key factor contributing to errors in GNSS positioning18–21. By examining the relationship between the cancellations and these specific physical drivers, these results will help us better understand potential causal mechanisms and explore the underlying causes of flight cancellations during space weather events, thereby providing a more nuanced and robust analysis of this complex phenomenon to improve the safety and efficiency of aviation industry.

Methods and data

The dataset utilized in this study spans from January 1, 2015, to December 31, 2019. It is important to note that this period covers the declining phase of Solar Cycle 24 and includes the solar minimum between Cycles 24 and 25, which occurred in December 2019.

To quantify the impact of space weather during this period, a comparative analysis was employed, a widely used method for studying factors that influence flight delays and cancellations. This approach involves comparing key metrics, such as delay time and probability density distribution, across different groups based on varying conditions or other potentially confounding factors51. By analyzing the differences in results, researchers can identify specific factors affecting flight delays. For example, a study examining the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on flight delays compared probability density distributions of flight delays at UK major airports from 2018 to 2020 during pandemic and non-pandemic periods. The analysis revealed a decrease in arrival delays by approximately 20 min during the COVID-19 pandemic52. Similarly, our previous research on SWEs found an 81.34% increase in flight arrival delays during the 24 h following the onset of SWEs compared to QTPs.

However, previous studies on the impacts of space weather on flight delays have underestimated these effects due to the limited duration considered for SWEs. The 24-hour period following the onset of an SWE is obviously insufficient to capture the full impact, as magnetospheric-ionospheric disturbances associated with SWEs can persist for 3–5 days2,6. To provide a more accurate assessment of the impact of space weather on flight cancellations, we have redefined SWEs in the current study to encompass the period from the onset of an SWE reaching Earth to the subsequent 72 h. Consequently, any flight departing during this period is considered to be potentially affected by SWEs. Correspondingly, QTPs are defined as complete days (00:00 to 24:00 local time) without SWEs, with flights departing during these periods serving as the comparison group. To enhance the robustness of our results, we exclude flight records near SWEs that do not fall within complete days, thereby reducing potential confounding effects from partial exposure to SWEs and avoiding the “24-hour intrinsic period”53 effect of flight delays. Detailed definitions of SWEs and QTPs are illustrated in Fig. 1 (a).

Schematic diagram of selected space weather events (a) and independent space weather events (b).

Similar to our previous study, 103 typical SWEs, including 52 SFs, 43 ICMEs, and 8 SPEs are selected by considering their potential effects on geospace environment. Only M-class and X-class SFs listed on the NOAA website are used54. ICMEs are selected by using the list compiled by Richardson and Cane55,56. SPEs are selected based on lists provided by NOAA Space Environment Services Center57. The final list is shown in the Supplementary Table S1.

Our analysis period was dominated by the declining phase of the solar cycle, during which a significant driver of geomagnetic disturbances could be the Stream Interaction Regions (SIRs)58. To account for this potential confounding factor, we also compiled a comprehensive list of SIR events for our study period and identified 45 significant events characterized by a maximum solar wind velocity ≥ 600 km/s and a Kp index ≥ 5 (see Supplementary Table S2)59,60. However, as our results show that the cancellation rates during SIRs differ from those during SFs, ICMEs, and SPEs, we will treat SIRs as a separate category and discuss their unique effects in the final section.

The superposed epoch analysis aligns and organizes multiple instances of the similar type of flight events along a timeline to construct a response curve, facilitating the examination of specific factors’ influences. For example, a comparative superposed epoch analysis on the impact of abrupt versus gradual weather changes on departure delays reveals that departure delays could extend by up to 23 min, depending on the type of weather and the extent of the change35. In this letter, to analyze the time response of flight cancellations following SWE outbreaks and to prevent overlapping effects of SWEs within a particular timeframe, 28 independent SWEs (2 SFs and 26 ICMEs marked in the Supplementary Table S1) are selected from the total 103 SWEs. The criteria for independent SWEs are illustrated in Fig. 1 (b); an SWE is classified as independent if no other SWE occurs within 3 days before (t=-3) and 5 days (t = 6) after its occurrence with its onset of occurrence designated as the key time (t = 0).

Flight Data and geomagnetic-ionospheric indices

The selection of flight data is crucial in aviation research, directly impacting study results. To ensure credible outcomes and mitigate incidental effects, datasets should be substantial and span at least one year to account for long-term influences such as seasonal variations in flight delays and cancellations41. Additionally, maintaining homogeneity among research subjects requires selecting airports of the same category (e.g., all hub airports in the same country with comparable flight volumes). Previous studies related to flight operations typically analyze 1–10 airports61 over periods ranging from 1 month to 5 years, involving 103 to 105 flights52,62,63. In this study, we utilize data provided by Travelsky Mobile Technology Limited, an affiliate of the Civil Aviation Administration of China (CAAC). Our dataset comprises 5,333,353 departure records from five major Chinese international hub airports: Beijing Capital (PEK), Shanghai Pudong (PVG), Shanghai Hongqiao (SHA), Guangzhou Baiyun (CAN), and Shenzhen Bao’An (SZX). The data spans from January 1, 2015, to December 31, 2019, chosen to maximize data availability while avoiding COVID-19-related disruptions. This comprehensive dataset al.lows for a thorough examination of flight delay patterns across multiple major Chinese hub airports over a 5-year period, providing a robust foundation for analysis while accounting for seasonal and long-term trends in the aviation industry. The relatively centralized characteristics of departure data in hub airports minimizes the complexity of the delay network often caused by the variety of connecting routes and diverse subsequent flight destinations26, enabling a more accurate assessment of the impact of SWEs on cancellation rate. Detailed information about these airports and the dataset is presented in Supplementary Table S3.

Geomagnetic-ionospheric indices are key indicators for analyzing the impact of SWEs on flight cancellations. This study utilizes several key indices: the Ap index, derived from global geomagnetic station observations43; the disturbance storm time (Dst) index, obtained from the International Service of Geomagnetic Indices44; the total electron content (TEC) data, sourced from the Madrigal database46; and the critical frequency of the ionospheric F2 layer (foF2), provided by the Meridian Project Data Center48. Details of the related data processing and spatiotemporal matching method are provided in the Supplementary Material (Data Processing and Spatiotemporal Matching).

Results

After a rigorous data screening process, records that fell within incomplete daily windows (as illustrated by the gray regions in Fig. 1) or contained missing or erroneous entries were excluded, resulting in 4,220,101 valid records. An analysis of the curated dataset, encompassing flights during 103 SWEs and corresponding Quiet Time Periods (QTPs), reveals a marked increase in the flight cancellation rate (CR) during all types of SWEs. ICMEs, despite showing the smallest relative increase, still resulted in a 88.26% rise in CR compared to QTPs. While SPEs exhibited the most substantial impact with a 137.39% rise. Overall, SWEs collectively caused a 96.96% increase in CR compared to QTPs, with the rate rising from 2.30% to 4.53%. Considering the investigated flight number and the 5-year timespan, the results shown in Table 1 highlight the considerable impact of space weather on aviation operations. This finding also aligns with previous research38,39, indicating that space weather can significantly disrupt flight schedules.

As established in the introduction, SWEs can trigger disturbances within the Earth’s magnetosphere-ionosphere system, which could in turn interfere with the CNS systems critical to aviation. This cascade of effects has the potential to disrupt flight operations and ultimately lead to cancellations. To quantitatively investigate this effect, we now examine the relationship between the flight cancellation rate and 4 key geomagnetic-ionospheric indices selected to explore how space weather related disturbances correlate with flight cancellations.

Figure 2 illustrates the distributions of CR as a function of these geomagnetic-ionospheric indices. It can be seen in Fig. 2 (a) that a clear monotonically increasing relationship existed between CR and the Ap index, indicating that stronger geomagnetic activity leads to higher flight cancellation. Figure 2 (b) shows that while CR increases slightly during stable geomagnetic conditions (dDst < ~ 10 nT/hour), it rises notably as fluctuations intensify, especially when dDst exceeds ~ 20 nT/hour. The ionospheric indices in Fig. 2 (c) and (d), ROTI and ΔfoF2, exhibit comparable trends with apparent thresholds: CR increases marginally when disturbances are mild (ROTI < 0.1 TECU/min, ΔfoF2 < 10%) but escalates significantly under more severe conditions (ROTI > 0.2 TECU/min, ΔfoF2 > 15%). These results reveal significant correlations between space weather induced geomagnetic-ionospheric disturbances and flight cancellations, which align well with our previous results38–40.

Flight cancellations can be attributed to various factors, including severe weather conditions and operational issues35. Thus, it is crucial to identify the specific impact of SWEs on aviation operations. To address the potential confounding factors, such as seasonal variations in weather patterns (e.g., summer thunderstorms and typhoons) that significantly affect flight schedules35,61, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of CR across different months. Figure 3 illustrates that CR during SWEs consistently remains higher than during QTPs throughout the year, with both showing similar seasonal trends of increased cancellations in summer and decreases during other seasons. This parallel pattern suggests that while seasonal effects do contribute, they do not fully explain the observed differences. Moreover, the trend demonstrates that even after accounting for seasonal variations, SWEs continue to have a significant impact on aviation operations. Considering the intrinsic short-term and long-term cyclicality of flight cancellations and the random distribution of 103 SWEs in days, weekdays, and months, these findings strongly indicate that the influence of SWEs on flight cancellations is a unique and important factor, independent of long-term seasonal effects, and should be considered a key variable in aviation operations.

To establish a potential causal relationship between SWEs and flight cancellations, we conducted a superposed epoch analysis on 28 independent SWEs, eliminating potential reciprocal effects caused by overlapping SWEs. Figure 4 (a) illustrates the time responses of independent SWEs on flight cancellations. It is evident that CR remains consistent with QTP levels and relatively stable 3 days prior to SWE onset. However, immediately following an SWE occurrence, CR increases notably. As geomagnetic-ionospheric disturbances caused by SWEs intensify over time, CR peaks on the second day post-event. Subsequently, as SWE effects diminish, CR decreases to QTP levels. It should be noted that a single SF has relatively short influence on Earth, while most of the independent SWEs analyzed here are ICMEs, which have a more prolonged impact. Hence, the results shown in Fig. 4 (a) essentially reflect the temporal response curve of CR to ICMEs.

(a) The superposed epoch analysis on the flight cancellation rate responding to 28 independent space weather events. (b) Correlations between time-dependent delay rate and cancellation rate.

Previous research has indicated that SWEs can lead to significant flight delays38, and it is known that long-time flight delays may ultimately result in cancellations64. To explore this potential mechanism, we conducted a superposed epoch analysis on various time-dependent delay rates (DR90 to DR240) by using the same method as the CR. We examined the time response curves of these delay rates from 3 days before to 6 days after the occurrence of independent SWEs. Subsequently, we correlated these curves with CR to derive Pearson’s correlation coefficients between time-dependent delay rates and CR, as illustrated in Fig. 4(b). The results reveal a clear trend that longer flight delays exhibit stronger correlations with CR compared to shorter delays. This pattern in correlation coefficients suggests that the long-time flight delays may be another important contributor for the increased cancellation rates observed during SWEs.

The results shown in Fig. 4, along with the seasonal effect shown in Fig. 3, and the observed correlations between geomagnetic-ionospheric indices and CR shown in Fig. 2, provides a crucial link in elucidating the causal chain from SWE-associated geomagnetic-ionospheric disturbances to flight cancellations, bridging the gap between space weather phenomena and their practical impacts on aviation operations.

Discussions and conclusions

Flight operations are inherently complex systems influenced by multi-interconnected factors, including meteorological conditions, air traffic congestion, security protocols, airline operations, and airport management8,53. These variables collectively contribute to the likelihood of flight cancellations and exhibit highly non-linear behavior with temporal and spatial dependencies that can propagate across the entire aviation network26. Given this complexity, isolating the specific impact of a single factor, such as SWEs, presents a significant challenge65. Although isolating the ‘pure’ effect of SWEs from a ‘quiet’ background might be theoretically desirable, it is impractical to verify such an effect in real-world scenarios where factors like air traffic congestion, weather conditions, security issues, and operational management are always present and changing53. Instead, quantifying the impact of SWEs within the context of these persistent factors offers a more practical approach, since it could provide actionable insights that can lead to more effective strategies for optimizing aviation operations in the face of space weather challenges. Therefore, to accurately determine the effect of SWEs, it is crucial to analyze a comprehensive dataset spanning sufficiently long durations, ensuring temporal and spatial uniformity in the research samples to enhance the validity of the analysis. This methodology, which aligns with previous studies conducted by our team38,39 and other scholars66,67, has also been adapted in this study. Notably, the number of airports, flights, and the duration of data used in this study exceed those of previous similar studies, making our results more reliable and convincing.

To ensure data homogeneity and control for variations in national aviation policies, we deliberately focused on airports within a single country68. Furthermore, the selection of five major hub airports for this study was strategically motivated. These airports, while situated at similar longitudes, span a wide range of latitudes, thereby providing an optimal framework for examining the systematic effects of SWEs on aviation activities across different latitudes28,36. Additionally, hub airports, given their pivotal role in air traffic networks and their adherence to stringent operational protocols, offer a more standardized environment for study compared to smaller, regional airports with potentially more variable cancellation policies69. This methodological choice enhances the robustness of our findings and provides a solid foundation for extrapolating the results to broader aviation contexts.

It is noteworthy that although the study period of 2015–2019 coincided with a relatively quiescent phase of solar activity, our analysis still revealed a remarkable increase in flight cancellations during SWEs. As we approach the peak of Solar Cycle 25, it is reasonable to anticipate that the impact of SWEs on aviation operations will become even more pronounced. In addition, our methodology may have actually underestimated the full extent of SWE impacts. The chosen 72-hour analysis window following SWE onset potentially missed capturing the complete effects. Firstly, there is an inherent delay in the magnetosphere and ionosphere’s response to SWEs, meaning flights occurring shortly after SWE onset might not show immediate effects2,70,71. Secondly, as also demonstrated by other authors and revealed in Fig. 4(a), SWE impacts can persist beyond the 72-hour duration, but such phenomenon is not fully accounted for in our current analysis. Furthermore, our conservative approach of including these extended periods in QTPs calculations conversely increased the CR for QTPs, thereby reducing the apparent contrast between SWEs and QTPs.

This conservative estimate of SWE impacts seems to become more significant when considering the background solar conditions of our study. Given that our analysis period falls within the declining phase of the solar cycle, a period during which SIRs are a significant driver of geomagnetic disturbances, it is crucial to account for their effects58. To refine our baseline for a more accurate comparison, we excluded these 45 SIR-related periods from our original Quiet Time Periods, thereby deduced a more stringently defined “Refined QTPs” (RQTPs) baseline. Furthermore, to examine the temporal evolution of the impacts, we calculated the flight cancellation rates for each event type (SFs, ICMEs, SIRs, and SPEs) across three distinct 24-hour windows post-onset: 0–24 h, 24–48 h, and 48–72 h. As shown in Table 2, the impact of SFs, ICMEs, and SPEs is not instantaneous but evolves over time. For all three types, the CR and its relative increase are most pronounced in the 24–48 h window following the event onset. This delayed peak aligns well with our independent event analysis (Fig. 4a), reinforcing the conclusion that the most significant operational disruptions manifest approximately one to two days after the initial solar trigger. It is also crucial to note that most of the analyzed SFs were not independent events but precursors to subsequent ICMEs. This compounding effect likely explains the sustained high cancellation rates observed across all three 24-hour windows for SF-associated periods. However, very counter-intuitively, SIRs do not appear to elevate flight cancellation rates. In fact, the rate during SIR periods remains at or slightly below the baseline RQTP level. This striking difference implies that while SIRs are important drivers of geomagnetic activity, the specific geophysical effects they produce related to flight cancellations may differ from those of SFs, ICMEs and SPEs.

To further investigate this conjecture, we analyzed the probability density distributions of |dDst/dt| during the 72-hour period for each event type, as shown in Fig. 5. This analysis reveals that |dDst/dt| during SIRs is similar to that of the RQTPs. The probability density for SIRs (red) is similar to the RQTPs (black), and its mean value is also very close to the RQTPs mean, although revealing a marginally broader tail. This stands in stark contrast to the distribution for the SWEs (blue dashed line), which is remarkably right shifted and exhibits a significantly flatter tail, indicating a much higher probability of large, rapid geomagnetic fluctuations. Considering the strong positive correlation between |dDst/dt| and the flight cancellation rate established in Fig. 2b, the geomagnetic variability during SIRs provides a potential explanation for their negligible impact on flight cancellations. While a detailed discussion of the nuanced differences in how SIRs and other SWEs disturb the magnetosphere-ionosphere system is beyond the scope of this paper, this intriguing finding warrants more dedicated investigations in future work.

A particularly notable result is the high cancellation rate observed during SPEs. At first sight, this may appear inconsistent with our proposed mechanism, since SPEs themselves are not primary drivers of strong geomagnetic fluctuations. However, this apparent discrepancy can likely be reconciled by considering the compound nature of these events, the unique operational hazards they introduce, and the limited number of statistical samples. In our dataset, the identified SPEs are often associated with preceding SFs and subsequent ICMEs. The elevated cancellation rates observed during SPEs may therefore reflect the cumulative impact of this entire event chain. Furthermore, SPEs pose direct and severe operational risks that differ fundamentally from other SWEs22. The influx of high-energy protons can enhance radiation exposure for passengers and crew and may increase the likelihood of avionics malfunctions. These severe conditions often necessitate immediate and conservative operational responses, such as the rerouting or outright cancellation of polar flights to mitigate risks. While our study focuses on non-polar hubs, such major disruptions can propagate as downstream ripple effects across the aviation network, contributing to the elevated cancellation rates observed. Besides, we also note that the small number of SPEs in our sample limits the statistical strength of this particular conclusion, and that disentangling the relative contributions of the compounded effects warrants further study.

Considering that the relationship between SWEs and flight cancellations can be viewed as a binary opposition: either a causal relationship exists or it does not, thus positing that SWEs do have a causal impact on flight cancellations appears to be a more reasonable conclusion. The above results imply that SWEs can play a significant role in causing flight cancellations by disrupting the critical communication and navigation systems that aviation operations rely on. When communication between Air Traffic Control (ATC) and flight crews is compromised, ATC may be unable to provide timely clearances for takeoff or landing, leading to operational delays. Moreover, if the communication link breaks down, dispatch centers might not receive up-to-date flight information, resulting in flawed flight planning and further complications. These disruptions tend to create a ripple effect throughout the aviation system, with delays accumulating at various stages, which can eventually lead to flight cancellations. Navigation system malfunctions, another consequence of SWEs, can also delay flights, as aircraft may require rerouting, ground holds, or additional inspections. Aviation regulations, such as those from the CAAC, require continuous two-way communication with ATC and multiple independent navigation systems onboard aircraft. In cases of communication failure during SWEs, ATC may be forced to temporarily halt takeoffs or reroute aircraft to alternate airports. The root of these issues often lies in geomagnetic field fluctuations and ionospheric disturbances caused by SWEs, which directly affect communication and navigation systems, making them frequent contributors to flight delays and cancellations.

In summary, this study presents the first evidence of a systematic increase in CR during SWEs, a phenomenon previously overlooked beyond polar routes or extreme SWE occurrences28. Our investigation reveals a mono-increase relationship between CR and the intensity of geomagnetic-ionospheric disruptions associated with SWEs. The increased CR can persist even when accounting for other factors that typically influence cancellations, such as seasonal variations41. By employing superposed epoch analysis on independent SWEs, the study unveils a well-defined temporal response curve of CR to SWEs. Further exploration indicates that prolonged flight delays induced by SWEs may serve as an important catalyst for the observed rise in CR. These results suggest that disturbances in communication and navigation systems during SWEs can notably affect aviation operations, resulting in increased flight cancellations. This research broadens the conventional understanding of space weather impacts on aviation, extending beyond the well-documented effects on polar flights. Nevertheless, it should be acknowledged that the influence of space weather on aviation operations is a complex domain. The four geomagnetic-ionospheric indices used here, while demonstrating a strong statistical link, may not be the best optimal predictors for cancellation rates in all scenarios. Future research could benefit from exploring regional geomagnetic indices, which might better capture localized disturbances compared to the global indices that average effects across the planet. Moreover, the development of composite parameters, integrating multiple indices, could be more powerful for future operational forecasting of flight cancellations during space weather events. The urgency and relevance of such work are underscored by recent regulatory advancements. For instance, the Civil Aviation Administration of China’s (CAAC) 2024 revision of its meteorological information regulations, which significantly expanded the inclusion of space weather data, exemplifies a growing recognition within the industry of the need to incorporate space weather considerations into operational decision-making. This complex field, bridging fundamental science with pressing operational needs, necessitates the continued, collective efforts of numerous researchers and studies25. A single paper cannot fully address or resolve all questions, and this work only opens up a small breakthrough in the relevant field. However, it indicates the significance of incorporating space weather as a systematic factor in flight operations and planning, which might lay a groundwork for future investigations and operational enhancements in the aviation industry.
