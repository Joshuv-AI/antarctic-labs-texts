Differential role for hepatic glucuronidation and sulfonation in controlling the 24S-hydroxycholesterol ability to activate LXR
Authors: Valérie Brousseau, Eva Rettenmeier, Mélanie Verreault, Jocelyn Trottier, Shujuan Chen, Robert H. Tukey, Olivier Barbier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
24S-hydroxycholesterol (24SOH-Chol) is a bioactive cholesterol metabolite formed in the brain. This endogenous activator of the cholesterol sensor, liver X receptor (LXR) is abundantly found as a sulfate-glucuronide diconjugate in the human plasma. The present study characterizes the human sulfonating (SULT) and glucuronidating (UGT) enzymes; and evaluates how these enzymes impact its ability to bind to and activate LXR. In vitro enzymatic assays identified the human SULT2A1 and UGT1A4 as the major isoforms for hepatic 24SOH-Chol sulfonation and glucuronidation, respectively. Additional assays demonstrated that 24SOH-Chol-3Sulfate,24Glucuronide formation requires the successive involvement of UGT1A4 and SULT2A1. TR-FRET and transient transfection experiments revealed that glucuronidation, but not sulfonation, inactivates 24SOH-Chol. Exposure of human liver cells and humanized UGT1 mice to LXR ligands identified UGT1A4, but not SULT2A1, as a positively regulated LXR target gene, while chromatin immunoprecipitation assays, luciferase reporter and siRNA knock down assays demonstrated the ability of LXR to bind to and activate the human UGT1A4 gene promoter. The present study establishes the complementary roles played by SULT2A1 and UGT1A4 in 24SOH-Chol conjugation. We also identify glucuronidation as a mechanism allowing this cholesterol derivative to self-stimulate its own inactivation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-61280-5.

Introduction

The brain is the most cholesterol-rich organ and contains about 25% of the total amount of cholesterol in the body1. In the adult brain, cholesterol is mainly synthesized in astrocytes and delivered to the neurons. The blood brain barrier prevents cholesterol transport between the brain and the blood1. Thus, excess cholesterol from the brain is eliminated through 2 complementary mechanisms: i) in the cerebrospinal fluid after binding to apolipoprotein E1, and ii) after biotransformation into 24S-hydroxycholesterol (24SOH-Chol, also called cerebrosterol) prior secretion into the blood2. The cholesterol to 24SOH-Chol conversion is catalyzed by the neuronal 24-hydroxylase (CYP46A1) enzyme1. With a 6 mg/day plasma secretion rate, 24SOH-Chol formation represents the major cholesterol elimination pathway from the human brain1. CYP46A1 is the key enzyme that ensures brain cholesterol turnover, due to its dual action of initiating cholesterol efflux and activating the cholesterol synthesis pathway. Altered CYP46A1 activity has been observed in human patients with Alzheimer’s and Huntington’s diseases, spinocerebellar ataxias, glioblastoma, and autism spectrum disorders. Modulating CYP46A1 activity in mouse models mitigates the manifestations of Alzheimer’s disease, Huntington’s disease, Niemann-Pick disease type C, Machado-Joseph disease (spinocerebellar ataxia type 3), amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, epilepsy, glioblastoma and prion infection (reviewed in3). Plasmatic levels of 24SOH-Chol are increased in high brain cholesterol turnover and low in neurodegenerative processes. Another important function of 24SOH-Chol is modulating N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptors, contributing to synaptic plasticity and learning, of particular importance in hippocampal neurons (reviewed in4).

Beyond its role as a cholesterol elimination form, 24SOH-Chol is also a bioactive molecule involved in the control of gene expression and several physiological function5. Indeed, 24SOH-Chol acts as an endogenous ligand of the liver X-receptors (LXR). The LXR family includes two isoforms: LXRα, which is prominently expressed in the liver and other tissues critical for peripheral lipid metabolism such as the kidney, small intestine, spleen, and adipose tissue, and LXRβ a more ubiquitously isoform, but prominent expression in the liver and the brain6–8. Upon activation by their endogenous (24SOH-Chol and other oxysterols) or synthetic (GW3965, T0901317) activators, LXRs form heterodimers with their RXRα partner, and bind to sequence-specific binding elements within the promoters of target genes to initiate transcription (reviewed in6–8). These 2 receptors are usually viewed as cholesterol sensors, and play major roles in the control of cholesterol synthesis and metabolism5.

In blood, 24SOH-Chol is transported as unbound and bound fractions; the latter being associated to lipoproteins such as the very low density lipoproteins (VLDL), intermediate density lipoproteins (IDL), low density lipoproteins (LDL) and high density lipoproteins (HDL)9,10. Beyond brain secretion, the circulating levels of 24SOH-Chol are also influenced by its hepatic catabolism. Indeed, 24SOH-Chol is efficiently captured by liver cells, where additional metabolic reactions favor its elimination11,12: half of the circulating 24SOH-Chol is transformed into bile acids, while the remaining sustains conjugation reactions such as sulfonation and/or glucuronidation11,13,14. These phase II metabolic processes are respectively catalyzed by sulfotransferase (SULT) and UDP-glucuronosyltransferase (UGT) enzymes. Both reactions convert hydrophobic molecules into water-soluble derivatives that are easily excreted into the bile or urine11,15,16. SULT enzymes transfer the sulfate group from the 3’-phosphoadenosine-5’-phosphosulfate (PAPS) co-substrate17,18, while UGTs use the uridine diphospho-glucuronic acid (UDPGA) as a donor for the glucuronide moiety15,16. Both sulfonation and glucuronidation use similar acceptor groups on the candidate molecules, namely hydroxyl, carboxyl, amine or thiol groups15. Those of the human UGTs reactive with 24SOH-Chol have not yet been identified, while SULT2A1, SULT2B1b and SULT1E1 are known to sulfate this oxysterol18. A recent targeted metabolomics profiling revealed that in addition of 24SOH-Chol, the human plasma also contains detectable levels of 24SOH-Chol-3 sulfate (3S), 24SOH-Chol-24 glucuronide (24G) and 24SOH-Chol-3S,24G19. The last diconjugated derivatives is the most abundant metabolite and is mostly found in solution and not bound to plasma lipoproteins19. The present study aimed at i) identifying the human UGT and SULT enzymes involved in the formation of this abundant metabolite, ii) evaluating how glucuronidation and sulfonation influence the ability of 24SOH-Chol to bind to and activate LXRs; and iii) determining whether LXR control gene expression of the 24SOH-Chol-conjugating SULT(s) and UGT(s).

Materials and methods

Ethic statement

All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Of particular interest, all research involving human subjects were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and were approved by the appropriate ethics and institutional review committees. Indeed, experiments using human liver samples, obtained as previously and extensively described20 were approved by the clinical study review board at the CHU de Québec Research Center («Comité de la recherche clinique du CHUL» Québec, QC, Canada: projects #95.05.14 and #97.05.14)19. All mouse experiments and procedures were performed in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by both the University of California San Diego Animal Care Committee21 and the “Comité de protection des animaux de l’Université Laval” (Project #16-039-4). All methods are reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines.

Materials

24S-hydroxycholesterol, 24S-hydroxycholesterol-3sulfate (3S), 24S-hydroxycholesterol-24glucuronide (24G) and 24S-hydroxycholesterol-3S,24G were synthesized by the organic synthesis service at the CHU de Québec Research Center (http://pfchem.crchul.ulaval.ca/en/index.html) (Québec, Canada). [2H7]-24S-hydroxycholesterol (24SOH-Chol-d7) was purchased from C/D/N Isotopes (Montréal, Canada). [2H7]-24S-hydroxycholesterol-3S (24SOH-Chol-3S-d7), [2H7]-24S-hydroxycholesterol-24G (24SOH-Chol-24G-d7) and [2H7]-24S-hydroxycholesterol-3S,24G (24SOH-Chol-3S,24G-d7) were prepared through in vitro enzymatic assays using liver homogenates or microsomes as reported19,22. UDP-glucuronic acid (UDPGA), 3′-phosphoadenosine-5′-phosphosulfate (PAPS), Trifluoperazine (TFP), lithocholic acid (LCA), lmipramine hydrochloride, puromycine, GW3965 and geranylgeranyl pyrophosphate (GGPP) were from Sigma-Aldrich (Oakville, Canada). T0901317 was from Cayman Chemical (Ann Arbor, MI). Serotonin was purchased from Biochemika (Neu-Ulm, Germany). 17β-Estradiol (E2) and cholesterol were from Steraloids (Newport, RI). Methanol, ethanol, isopropyl alcohol and hexane were purchased from VWR (Montréal, Canada). Ammonium formate was from Laboratoire Mat (Québec, Canada). Sodium hydroxide and all chemicals required for phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. All the reagents were of the highest grade commercially available. Protein assay reagents were obtained from Bio-Rad Laboratories Inc. (Marnes-la-Coquette, France).

Animal experiments

Mice were housed in a pathogen-free animal care facility of the University of California San Diego (UCSD) and received food and water ad libitum. Humanized UGT1 (hUGT1) mice were previously generated by introducing a human UGT1 transgene into mice with a Ugt1-null background23. Ten-day old neonatal hUGT1 mice were treated with 50 mg/kg T0901317 or vehicle (propylene glycol/ Tween 80 4:1) by oral gavage. Mice were sacrificed 48 h after the treatment by cardiac puncture under isoflurane, and livers were collected and immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored in − 80 °C.

Cell culture

Human hepatoma HepG2 cells and human embryonic kidney 293 (HEK293) were from the American Type Culture Collection (Rockville, MD). HepG2 and HEK293 cells were grown as reported24. HepG2 cells (3 × 105 per well) were plated in 12-well plates and treated with DMSO (vehicle; 0.1%, v/v) or LXR agonists as indicated. HEK293 cells expressing human UGTs and/or SULTs were engineered using a previously described procedure24.

Human liver samples, cell/tissue homogenate preparation, microsome isolation and in vitro assays

Human liver samples were as previously and extensively described20. Homogenates were prepared from tissues and UGT/SULT-HEK293 cells using a potter-glass-col (Terre-Haute, IN, USA) type homogenizer with a Teflon pestle in a phosphate-buffered saline solution with dithiothreitol (0.5 mM) and diluted at a final concentration of 5 µg/µL. Microsome pellets were extracted by differential centrifugation as previously described25 and resuspended at a 5 µg/µL concentration.

Enzymatic assays were performed at 37 °C for the indicated duration in a final volume of 100 µL of the indicated assay buffer. Glucuronidation assays were conducted using an assay buffer composed of 50 mM Tris–HCl pH7.5, 10 mM MgCl2, 10 mg/mL phosphatidylcholine, 2.5 mg/mL pepstatine, 0.5 mg/mL leupeptine, 0.025 mg/mL alamethicin in the presence of 1 mM UDPGA19 (Supplemental Fig. 1A). Sulfonation assays were performed in the presence of 25 mM PAPS, 50 mM Tris–HCl pH7.5, 1 mM MgCl2, 1 mg/mL bovin serum albumine (BSA) and 7.5 mM dithiotritol (DTT)19 (Supplemental Fig. 1A). Quantification of glucuronide and sulfate conjugates by Liquid Chromatography coupled to mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS)

Levels of 24SOH-Chol, 24SOH-Chol-3S, 24SOH-Chol-24G and/or 24SOH-Chol-3S,24G in cell culture media, in vitro enzymatic assays or TR-FRET assays were quantified through liquid chromatography coupled to tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) as extensively described elsewhere19.

Time resolved-fluorescence resonance energy transfer (TR-FRET) assay

TR-FRET assays were performed using the LanthaScreen TR-FRET LXRα Coactivator assay from Invitrogen (WI, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The 515/485 nm wavelenght ratio was analyzed using a Infinite M1000 instrument (Tecan, San Jose, CA), with the respective wavelengths of excitation, emission 1 and emission 2 set at 330, 485 and 515 nm.

Plasmid cloning and transient transfection

The pGL3 reporter construct comprising the 0.5 kb proximal promoter region of the UGT1A4 gene was obtained by amplifying the corresponding DNA sequence using the Taq polymerase enzyme (Roche Biodiagnostic, Mannheim, Germany) and 100 pmol of sense (5′-TTCACATATGAGAAGAAAGAG-3′) and antisense (5′-CAGAAGATGTTCTGTTTCAAAGAACCC-3′) primers. Purified PCR products were cloned into a Sma I/Xho I–digested pGL3 basic using the T4 DNA ligase (Roche Biodiagnostic). HepG2 cells (75,000 cells/well) weretransfected in 24-well plates using the Turbofect transfection reagent with 100 ng of pGL3 luciferase reporter vector containing or not 0.5 kb of the proximal UGT1A4 promoter sequence in the presence of LXRα and RXR expression plasmids (10 ng) and 30 ng of the pRL-NULL expression plasmid. Cells were then exposed to T0901317 (1 µM) or GW3965 (1 µM) for 24 h. Cells were lyzed and luciferase and renilla activities were determined. HEK293 cells stably transfected with the pcDNA6 vector (negative control), UGT1A4 and/or SULT2A1 expression plasmids were transiently transfected for 2 h with 100 ng of a LXRE-TK-pGL3 reporter plasmid24 in the presence of LXR and RXR and pRL-NULL as described above. Cells were subsequently incubated in the presence of DMSO (vehicle), T0901317 (1 µM) or 24SOH-Chol (1 µM) for 24 h. Luciferase and renilla activities were quantified and culture media were collected for 24SOH-Chol-3S, 24SOH-Chol-24G and 24SOH-Chol-3S,24G determination.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays

The ChIP assay was performed as previously described24. Briefly, 20 × 107 HepG2 cells were grown to 60% confluence and serum-starved for 16 h. Cells were subsequently treated with either DMSO (vehicle) or T0901317 (5 µM) for 90 min. Protein-DNA cross-linking, nuclear extracts preparation and sonication were performed as reported24. A volume of lysate equivalent of 20 × 106 cells was immunoprecipitated using 4 µg of anti-LXR (Santa-Cruz, sc1201) or anti-RXR (Santa-Cruz, sc553) antibodies, or with 4 µg of an anti-UGT2B antibody as negative control. The same lysate volume was either kept without immunoprecipitation for subsequent purification of input genomic DNA or was incubated in the presence of protein A-sepharose alone. 1/10 of the immunoprecipitated DNA was PCR amplified for 35 cycles (30 s at 95 °C, 30 s at 55 °C and 30 s at 72 °C). The UGT1A4-specific primers (1A4 LXRE − 359/− 149) used for PCR amplification were: forward: 5′-AGCTGCAGCCTGATAAATATGGTTA and reverse: 5′-CCATTAATCTATAACTGCCTTTCG. Other primer pairs were as previously reported24.

Small interfering RNA knock-down

HepG2 cells (500,000 cells/well of 6-well plates) were infected with non-target or LXR-siRNA probes (30 nM, Custom Select #4,390,829, Life Technologies) for 48 h using the SiPORT NeoFX transfection agent (Life Technologies). Cells were then treated with T0901317 (1 µM) for another 48 h prior to mRNA extraction and analysis as described below.

Total RNA extraction, reverse transcriptase and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was isolated according to the Tri-Reagent acid:phenol protocol, as specified by the supplier (Molecular Research Center Inc. OH). The reverse transcriptase protocol was performed as previously described24. RT-qPCR analyses of UGT1A4 and SULT2A1 were determined using the UGT1A4- (Forward: 5′-CCTGTCCTACATTTGCCATA; Reverse: 5′-TCAAATTCCTGAGATAGTGGCTTCCC) and SULT2A1- (Forward: 5′-ACTGGTTGGCTGAGATTCTCT; Reverse: 5′-TCCGTTTCACTGAGTGCTGTA) specific primers. Conditions for RT-qPCR were as previously described24, with melting temperature of 60 °C (UGT1A4) and 62 °C (SULT2A1) using Fast SYBR green master mix from Life Technologies (MA, USA). PUM-1 was used as the housekeeping gene.

Statistical analysis

Correlation studies and parametric and non-parametric test used to analyze for significant differences between experimental groups were performed using JMP 11.1.1 program (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

Results

UGT1A4 and SULT2A1 catalyze the hepatic conjugation of 24S-hydroxycholesterol

A screening assay performed with microsomes from UGT-HEK293 cells revealed that among the 16 human UGT enzymes tested, UGT1A4 is the most efficient isoform to glucuronidate 24S-hydroxycholesterol (Fig. 1A). Similar results were obtained when assays were performed with commercially available UGT supersomes (BD Gentest, Mississauga, ON, Canada) instead of homemade engineered UGT-HEK293 cells (data not shown). Subsequent time-course (Supplemental Fig. 2) and dose–response (Fig. 1B) experiments were performed to fully establish the enzymatic parameters of 24SOH-Chol glucuronidation by homogenates from UGT1A4-HEK293 cells. Since SULT2A1, 1E1 and 2B1b enzymes were previously identified as the human 24SOH-Chol-sulfonating enzymes18, homogenates from HEK293 cells over expressing each of these enzymes were also included in these analyses (Supplemental Fig. 2 and Fig. 1C). Time course experiments performed for up to 24 h showed that the glucuronidation and sulfonation reactions were linear for at least 1 h (Supplemental Fig. 2). This incubation duration was therefore selected for all subsequent in vitro assay experiments. Results from dose–response experiments were used to determine kinetic parameters of 24SOH-Chol conjugation in liver and UGT/SULT-HEK293 cells (Fig. 1B, C). For this purpose, Eadie-Hoftee plots were drawn to identify the kinetic model providing the best estimation for the kinetic constants of affinity (KM). Human liver homogenates and extracts from UGT1A4-HEK293 cells exhibited similar KM values for 24SOH-Chol glucuronidation (2.33 ± 0.21 and 8.31 ± 2.52 µM, respectively) (Fig. 1B). Sulfonation experiments revealed that extracts from SULT2A1-HEK193 cells also resemble to liver homogenates in terms of KM values (1.14 ± 0.68 and 1.98 ± 0.76 µM, respectively), while cells expressing SULT2B1b (KM = 3.55 ± 0.45 µM) and SULT1E1 (24.68 ± 4.98 µM) exhibited lower affinities (Fig. 1C). 24S-hydroxycholesterol-3S,24G is formed from the sulfonation of 24S-hydroxycholesterol-24G

We recently observed that the most abundant circulating conjugate of 24S-hydroxycholesterol in human plasma corresponds to the diconjugated 24SOH-Chol-3S,24G molecule19. Therefore, we sought to investigate the potential role of the UGT1A4 and SULT2A1 enzymes in the hepatic formation of this derivative (Fig. 3). Similar assays as above were performed in the presence of 24SOH-Chol-3S and 24SOH-Chol-24G as substrates for UGT1A4 and SULT2A1, respectively. Neither liver extracts nor HEK293-UGT1A4 cell homogenates were able to glucuronidate 24SOH-Chol-3S (Supplemental Fig. 4). By contrast, HEK293-SULT2A1 extracts and liver homogenates were able to efficiently form 24SOH-Chol-3S,24G when incubated in the presence of 24SOH-Chol-24G (Fig. 3A). This indicates that in the human liver 24SOH-Chol is first glucuronidated and then sulfonated to form 24SOH-Chol-3S,24G. As illustrated on Fig. 3B, both UGT1A4 (TFP and imipramine) and SULT2A1 (LCA) competitors caused a reduction in the conversion of 24SOH-Chol into 24SOH-Chol-3S,24G by liver homogenates. Glucuronidation-but not sulfonation- inactivates 24S-hydroxycholesterol

24SOH-Chol is an endogenous activator of the cholesterol sensors LXRs18, and previous investigations suggested that instead of an agonist, 24SOH-Chol-3S may act as an LXRα antagonist18. Thus, we next tested how glucuronidated and/or sulfated 24SOH-Chol would behave in front of LXRα using a transfer fluorescence assays (Fig. 4). As expected, the synthetic (T0901317) and endogenous (24SOH-Chol) LXRα agonists activated fluorescence transfers (Fig. 4A). By contrast, both 24SOH-Chol-24G and 24SOH-Chol-3S,24G failed to bind to the receptor and activate florescence transfer. Surprisingly, these experiments also evidenced the agonistic activity of 24SOH-Chol-3S (Fig. 4A). Additional competition experiments were then performed to test whether conjugated derivatives of 24SOH-Chol can act as LXR antagonists using T0901317 as the agonist (Fig. 4B). As expected, the positive control GGPP caused a 48% reduction (p < 0.001) of the T091317-dependent LXR activation. By contrast, while the 6% reduction observed with 24SOH-Chol-3S,24G failed to reach statistical significance, the significant (p < 0.05) reduction observed in the presence of 24SOH-Chol-3S and 24SOH-Chol-24G was limited to 7 and 8%, respectively (Fig. 4B). These observations suggest that conjugated forms of 24SOH-Chol are only weak LXR antagonists.

Based on the agonistic activity detected in the screening assay (Fig. 4A), we next determined the binding constant (i.e. EC50) value for T0901317, 24SOH-Chol and 24SOH-Chol-3S (Fig. 4C). While the EC50 values obtained with T0901317 and 24SOH-Chol (16.9 ± 7.1 nM and 101.8 ± 29.6 nM, respectively) were similar to those previously reported18, the value obtained for 24SOH-Chol-3S (72.2 ± 19.9 nM) was lower than for its unconjugated precursor (Fig. 4C). This last observation suggests that sulfonation does not inactivate 24SOH-Chol. Such a possibility was further confirmed with transient transfection assays using a LXRE-TK luciferase reporter gene and HEK293 stably expressing the pcDNA6 vector (negative control), SULT2A1 and/or UGT1A4 (Fig. 4D). When compared to vehicle- (DMSO) treated cells, control cells exposed to 24SOH-Chol displayed a 83% increase in luciferase activity (Fig. 4D). By contrast, the 24SOH-Chol-dependent luciferase activation was limited to 57% and 15% in cells expressing either UGT1A4 alone or UGT1A4 + SULT2A1, respectively. Interestingly, the 24SOH-Chol-dependent activation of LXR was higher (107%) in HEK293-SULT2A1 cells than in control ones, thus further reinforcing the idea that 24SOH-Chol-3S act as an activator of the cholesterol sensor. Measurement of conjugated forms of 24SOH-Chol released in the corresponding cell media confirmed the formation of glucuronide and/or sulfate derivatives in cells expressing UGT1A4 and/or SULT2A1 (Fig. 4D).

UGT1A4, but not SULT2A1, is a positively regulated LXRα target gene

Previous studies indicated that endogenous substrates can modulate their own glucuronidation/sulfonation35. To evaluate whether a similar mechanism also takes place in the context of the 24SOH-Chol/LXR pathway, we next analyzed how LXR ligands affect SULT2A1 and UGT1A4 gene expression in human liver cells and hepatic tissue of humanized hUGT1 mice (Fig. 5). Interestingly, both synthetic (Fig. 5A) and endogenous (Fig. 5B) LXR ligands caused a significant accumulation of UGT1A4 transcripts, while SULT2A1 mRNA levels remained either unaffected (Fig. 5A) or were even reduced (Fig. 5B), thus indicating that only the UGT is a positively regulated LXR target gene. The functional relevance of these in vitro observations was ensured by the demonstration that T0901317 increases the ability of livers from hUGT1 mice to convert 24SOH-Chol into 24SOH-Chol-3S,24G (Fig. 5C). The transcriptional, direct and LXR-dependent nature of these regulatory events were subsequently assessed using i) transient transfection of a luciferase reporter gene containing 0.5 kb of the proximal UGT1A4 promoter (Fig. 5D), chromatin immunoprecipitation (Fig. 5E & Supplemental Fig. 5) and siRNA-mediated LXR knock down (Fig. 5F). Indeed, a 0.5 kb fragment of the UGT1A4 promoter was transfected in HepG2 cells in the presence of LXR and RXR expression plasmids, and activation of these cells with T0901317 (1 µM) and GW3965 (1 µM) significantly increased the UGT1A4 promoter activity (Fig. 5D). Discussion

The present study identifies the human UGT1A4 and SULT2A1 proteins as the predominant enzymes involved in hepatic conjugation of 24SOH-Chol, and demonstrates that glucuronidation, but not sulfonation, acts as an inactivation process for the 24SOH-Chol/LXR regulatory pathway.

Results from UGT screening, competition and correlation analyses clearly point out the UGT1A4 enzyme as the most important human isoform for 24SOH-Chol glucuronidation in the human liver. While this UGT has mainly been investigated in regards to drug metabolism36, previous studies reported its ability to glucuronidate various endogenous substances including structurally related molecules to 24SOH-Chol, such as androgens, estrogens and bile acids37. For example, UGT1A4 has been identified as the major contributor for the hepatic conversion of the primary bile acid, chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA)38, which, similarly to 24SOH-Chol is also an oxidized derivative of cholesterol. Interestingly, for CDCA glucuronide conjugation, UGT1A4 preferentially use the 3-hydroxyl position of the substrate leading to CDCA-3G38, while the present study indicates the preference of the enzyme for the 24-carboxyl position of the 24S-hydroxycholesterol molecule. These observations illustrate the ability of the UGT to use different acceptor groups on structurally related substrates. Although additional structure–activity relationship studies are required to fully understand the molecular mechanisms that allow for such versatility, it is possible that the length of the cholesterol derivative’s side chain may play a role. Indeed, CDCA has a 4-carbon side chain, whereas 24SOH-Chol has a 6-carbon side chain (Supplemental Fig. 6)39.Fig. 6While SULT2A1 and UGT1A4 play complementary roles in 24SOH-Chol metabolism, the LXR-dependent induction of UGT1A4 expression is a feed-forward mechanism allowing 24SOH-Chol to inactivate its own activity as a LXR activator.

While SULT2A1 and UGT1A4 play complementary roles in 24SOH-Chol metabolism, the LXR-dependent induction of UGT1A4 expression is a feed-forward mechanism allowing 24SOH-Chol to inactivate its own activity as a LXR activator.

On the other hand, another interesting structure–activity observation issuing from the present study relates to the differential ability of SULT2A1 and UGT1A4 to take in charge the primo-conjugated forms of 24SOH-Chol. Indeed, the present investigations indicate that if UGT1A4 is unable to glucuronidate 24SOH-Chol-3S, SULT2A1 possesses an equivalent efficiency to conjugate both the oxysterol and its 24glucuronide derivative. While the physiological relevance of such an observation is unclear, ones can envision that, by improving 24SOHChol solubility, glucuronidation may facilitate its access to the cytosolic SULT enzymes. On the other hand, these data also illustrate that the presence of the sulfate group may impair the binding of 24SOH-Chol to the catalytic site of the UGT, while in contrast the glucuronidated molecule can still bind the sulfotransferase enzyme. Because addition of the glucuronide group is generally thought to cause a steric hindrance impairing the substrate molecules to bind proteins40, such observation was unexpected. Nevertheless, previous studies evidenced that, as other cholesterol derivatives41, the 24SOH-Chol enter the SULT2A1 binding pocket through its 3-OH end18. It is therefore probable that the glucuronide on position 24 is sufficiently far from the SULT2A1 catalytic domain to minimally impact the binding and the subsequent sulfate group transfer.

Another intriguing observation issuing from these experiments relates to the differential effects that sulfonation and glucuronidation exert on the ability of 24S-hydroxycholesterol to bind to and activate LXR. Indeed, we evidence that while glucuronidation completely abolishes this activity, the sulfated form still conserve -and has even better- an agonistic activity. These data therefore indicate that only glucuronidation can be considered as an inactivation mechanism for 24SOH-Chol. Furthermore, we also evidence that the ligand-activated LXR receptor activate the transcription of the UGT1A4 gene in human liver cells. In sum, these observations demonstrate that glucuronidation serves as a feed-forward mechanism by which 24SOH-Chol stimulates its own inactivation (Fig. 6). Similar mechanisms allowing endogenous nuclear receptor activators to self-stimulate their own glucuronidation, and by so, limit their biological activities, were previously reported for various molecules42. Less anticipated was the observation that 24SOH-Chol-3S display an improved agonistic activity for LXR, when compared to the native 24SOH-Chol molecule. Indeed, Cook I.T. and collaborators18 previously reported that not only 24SOH-Chol-3S failed to agonize LXR, but even act as an antagonist. While we cannot provide clear explanation to these conflicting results, ones can assume that between 2009 and 2025, the TR-FRET kit has evolved to become more sensitive, which may lead to different results when used. This idea is supported by the fact that the IC₅₀ values for binding to the LXRa LBD from T0901317 (16.9 ± 7.1 nM) and 24SOH-Chol (101.8 ± 29.6 nM) observed in the present study are 2 and 20 times lower, respectively, than those obtained in 2009 (30 nM and 1.99 μM)18. Another possible explanation may lie in variations in the purity of the molecules used in these experiments. To test this hypothesis, we sought to evaluate if our preparation of 24SOH-Chol-3S could be contaminated with sufficient levels of the 24SOH-Chol or if the 24SOH-Chol-3S could sustain sulfate hydrolysis during TR-FRET. Post-assay TR-FRET solutions were then analyzed through LC–MS/MS to search for the unconjugated molecule. These analyses revealed that only a small (and insufficient amount of 24SOH-Chol to activate LXR) could be detected in solution from assays performed with 24SOH-Chol-3S (data not shown). Nevertheless, our observation that 24SOH-Chol-3S behaves as a LXR agonist in TR-FRET assays is supported by results from transient transfection assays. Indeed, these assays revealed that 24SOH-Chol is less efficient to activate the LXRE-governed luciferase activity in the presence of UGT1A4, while in SULT2A1-expressing cells the molecule exhibit similar luciferase gene activation than in control cells. Nevertheless, our results suggest that glucuronidation inhibit the ability of 24SOH-CHol to act as a LXR ligand, while sulfonation has only a minor impact.

In conclusion, the present study confirms the role of SULT2A1 in 24S-hydroxycholesterol sulfonation and identifies UGT1A4 as the most prominent human UGT enzyme for its glucuronidation. We evidence that glucuronidation, but not sulfonation, inactivates 24SOH-Chol, and finally we identify a feed-forward mechanisms by which 24S-hydroxycholesterol self-modulate its inactivation through a LXR-UGT1A4 regulatory pathway (Fig. 6).
