Uveitis in pediatric idiopathic orbital inflammatory syndrome
Authors: Ashley Choi, Matthew Fonte, Mennatullah Elfwwal, Erin P Herlihy, Kristina Tarczy-Hornoch, Kabita Nanda, Erin Balay-Dustrude, Laura C Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo report the clinical presentation, multimodal imaging, and long-term management of two children with idiopathic orbital inflammatory syndrome and associated uveitis.MethodsObservational case series of two pediatric patients with idiopathic orbital inflammatory syndrome and associated uveitis.ResultsBoth patients presented with severe anterior uveitis with posterior segment manifestations including optic nerve papillitis and macular edema in addition to orbital inflammation with ptosis, eyelid swelling, painful extraocular muscle dysmotility, and optic neuropathy. Due to recurrent orbital and uveitis flares with inability to taper off steroids, both children required systemic immunomodulatory treatment to maintain disease quiescence.ConclusionPediatric idiopathic orbital inflammatory syndrome may present with orbital inflammation associated with uveitis requiring systemic immunomodulatory therapy. Orbital inflammation must be considered in the differential diagnosis for pediatric patients presenting with uveitis to determine need for intracranial imaging and systemic treatment. Long-term follow-up is recommended due to risk of recurrence of both orbital and intraocular inflammation.Keywords: Uveitis, Pediatric ophthalmology, Orbital inflammation, Orbital pseudotumor

Introduction

Idiopathic Orbital Inflammatory Syndrome (IOIS) (also known as orbital pseudotumor, non-specific orbital inflammation, or inflammatory orbital pseudotumor) is an inflammatory condition characterized by significant pain, proptosis, eyelid swelling, chemosis, and limitations of eye movement with double vision.1,2 Management includes oral and/or intravenous corticosteroids and immunomodulatory agents for chronic or recurrent disease. Diagnosis of IOIS requires exclusion of secondary causes of orbital inflammation such as systemic lupus erythematosus, rheumatoid arthritis, sarcoidosis, granulomatosis with polyangiitis, and IgG-4 related disease.3

Despite many reports of IOIS in the adult population, there are fewer studies describing disease manifestations in the pediatric population.4,5 Children are more likely to demonstrate constitutional symptoms such as fever, malaise, headaches, lethargy, and weight loss.2,6,7 Additionally, pediatric IOIS is more often associated with uveitis although it is thought to be under-diagnosed.5 We present two cases of children with IOIS and concurrent uveitis with multimodal imaging. Both required systemic immunomodulatory treatment to manage recurrent flares of inflammation.

Case summaries

Case 1

A 12-year-old male presented to a local ophthalmologist with left eye pain, headaches, and blurred vision. He reported a prior history of 3 episodes of pain and blurred vision in the left eye over the past 3 years. He had previously received treatment with topical prednisolone acetate eyedrops which resolved the symptoms. At the initial visit with the local ophthalmologist, he had count fingers vision with diffuse stellate keratic precipitates, 4+ anterior chamber cell, optic nerve edema, subretinal fluid, and cystoid macular edema in a macular star formation in the left eye. He was treated with topical steroids including prednisolone acetate and difluprednate followed by an anterior sub-tenon triamcinolone injection. Three weeks after symptom onset, he developed worsening papillitis, new onset diplopia, and persistent headaches with vomiting. He was transferred to our hospital emergency department for further evaluation.

Visual acuity was 20/70 in the left eye with a relative afferent pupillary defect (RAPD). There was −2 abduction and −3.5 supraduction of the left eye. External exam of the left eye showed ptosis of the upper eyelid with edema and erythema of the upper and lower eyelids. Slit-lamp examination showed 3+ cells in the anterior chamber and anterior vitreous cell. Fundus exam demonstrated severe disc edema, a macular star, and retinal vessel tortuosity (Fig. 1A). Fluorescein angiography demonstrated optic nerve leakage (Fig. 1B) and optical coherence tomography (OCT) of the retina demonstrated hyperreflective exudates concentrated within the outer plexiform layer (Fig. 1C). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the orbits with contrast revealed left periorbital and orbital inflammation including the intra- and extraconal spaces, extraocular muscles, lacrimal gland, optic nerve, and posterior globe/retina (Fig. 2A). Exam of the right eye was normal.

The patient was admitted for further work-up and management. Rheumatologic evaluation included elevated inflammatory markers with an erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) of 58 mm/h and a C-reactive protein (CRP) of 4.7 mg/L. All other laboratory testing and computed tomography (CT) of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis were not suggestive of other autoimmune or infectious conditions including sarcoidosis, IgG4-related disease, syphilis, tuberculosis, and bartonella. The patient was diagnosed with idiopathic orbital inflammatory syndrome (IOIS) and treated with prednisone 60mg daily (1.3mg/kg weight-based dosing) with immediate improvement in his eye pain, diplopia, and orbital inflammation. Due to the rapid improvement with steroids as well as a history of possible recurrent episodes in the past, the patient was started on weekly oral methotrexate of 20mg. Maximal dosing of 25mg as calculated by weight and body surface area was deferred to decrease side effects and improve tolerance of oral methotrexate. Oral prednisone was tapered over 3 months. Consultation with pediatric oculoplastics occurred with decision to defer biopsy given clinical improvement with oral steroids.

During the oral prednisone taper following discharge, the patient experienced two additional orbital flares manifested by eyelid swelling and eye pain which necessitated increasing the dose and extending the systemic steroid taper (Fig. 2B). He also developed steroid response ocular hypertension during this time which was treated with topical antihypertensive drops. At 5 months after discharge, the patient began treatment with adalimumab 40mg injections (standard weight-based dosing for children ≥30 kg) every two weeks in addition to methotrexate due to continued orbital flares on methotrexate monotherapy. While awaiting adalimumab efficacy, he continued to experience orbital flares manifesting as painful eyelid swelling and ptosis (Fig. 2C), including one which required a second hospital admission. CT imaging of the head demonstrated asymmetric enlargement and hyperenhancement of the left lacrimal gland with inflammatory changes within the left lateral and superior orbit. He again initiated treatment with oral prednisone in addition to methotrexate and adalimumab. He subsequently was able to fully taper off steroids without recurrence 3 months after initiating adalimumab.

The patient eventually developed intolerance of methotrexate and continued adalimumab monotherapy, remaining quiescent of orbital and uveitis flares for 1.5 years following his second hospitalization. He was also able to stop all topical antihypertensive drops with normal intraocular pressures. He then underwent cataract extraction with intraocular lens placement with resolution of postoperative anterior chamber inflammation by 3 months after surgery. Best-corrected visual acuity was 20/20 in the affected eye. After more than two years of quiescence from the last orbital flare of IOIS, the patient stopped adalimumab therapy. He remained off treatment until he presented four months later with a symptomatic anterior uveitis flare. MRI was deferred due to lack of orbital signs including no ptosis, eyelid swelling, or extraocular muscle dysmotility. The patient was treated with oral prednisone and restarted on adalimumab injections every two weeks. Final visual acuity remained 20/20 in the affected eye at last follow-up 3.5 years after initial presentation.

Case 2

A 9-year-old female presented to the pediatric ophthalmology clinic with left eye redness, blurry vision, photophobia, and mild drooping of the left upper eyelid.

Visual acuity was 20/200 in the left eye. Examination revealed mild ptosis of the left upper eyelid and full extraocular motility without pain. Slit-lamp examination demonstrated 2+ conjunctival injection, non-granulomatous inflammation with 4+ cells in the anterior chamber with 1mm hypopyon, and anterior vitreous cell in the left eye. Fundus examination showed optic disc edema, vitreous haze, and an early macular star (Fig. 3A). Fluorescein angiography demonstrated late optic nerve leakage (Fig. 3B) and OCT retina showed hyperreflective exudates within the inner nuclear, outer plexiform, and outer nuclear layer (Fig. 3C). Exam of the right eye was normal. Topical prednisolone acetate 1% six times daily and atropine 1% once daily were initiated while awaiting laboratory work-up. Results for HLA-B27 associated disease, sarcoidosis, granulomatosis with polyangiitis, and infectious etiologies including syphilis, tuberculosis, and bartonella returned negative. Two days later, the patient developed worsening headache and new pain with eye movements. Exam revealed a RAPD in the left eye. Urgent MRI of the orbits revealed significant enhancement of the preseptal and post-septal orbital soft tissues, including the ciliary body, chorioretinal layer, and optic sheath, with asymmetric enhancement of the left cavernous sinus and superior orbital fissure (Fig. 4A). The patient was admitted for intravenous methylprednisolone therapy with rapid improvement in her symptoms. Further rheumatologic work-up showed an elevated serum IgG4; subsequent work-up for systemic IgG4 disease including pulmonary function testing, echocardiogram, abdominal ultrasound, and CT chest were normal. The patient was discharged on oral prednisone 40mg daily (1.75 mg/kg daily) with a gradual taper over five months.

During the oral prednisone treatment following discharge, the patient was unable to taper below 15mg daily of prednisone due to recurrence of anterior chamber inflammation (Fig. 4B). Repeat MRI brain imaging 3 months after initial hospitalization demonstrated resolution of the previously seen inflammatory findings within the orbit. The patient was evaluated by pediatric oculoplastics, who deferred orbital biopsy given the clinical and radiologic resolution of orbital inflammation with oral steroid treatment. Four months after initial presentation, the patient started methotrexate injections 25mg weekly due to recurrent uveitis flares. Four months after starting methotrexate she experienced an additional uveitis flare, at which time the addition of adalimumab injections were recommended. This episode of uveitis resolved with topical steroid drops, and adalimumab injections were ultimately deferred by the family. The patient also self-discontinued methotrexate shortly after this visit.

Seven months after discontinuing methotrexate, the patient presented with recurrent symptoms of left eye pain exacerbated by eye movement, ptosis, and eyelid swelling consistent with an orbital flare without intraocular inflammation (Fig. 4C). MRI imaging was discussed, which family deferred. The patient was treated with oral prednisone 30mg daily with a taper over 4 months. She experienced recurrent orbital symptoms of left eye pain, ptosis, and eyelid swelling after stopping oral prednisone. Methotrexate 20mg by mouth and a repeat oral prednisone taper were initiated. She continued to experience recurrent orbital flares as well as a uveitis flare while on methotrexate monotherapy, and adalimumab injections were again recommended. The patient started adalimumab 40mg injections (standard weight-based dosing for children ≥30 kg) every two weeks with methotrexate 20mg by mouth weekly. Within two months of initiating adalimumab, the patient was able to taper off oral steroids, and her disease has been quiescent for the past 9 months. Final visual acuity was 20/20 in the affected eye at last follow-up 3 years after initial presentation.

Discussion

Pediatric IOIS cases are less common than in the adult population, and cases with concurrent uveitis are rarely reported in the literature.4,5,8, 9, 10 Children with concurrent iritis and IOIS comprised 8 of 29 patients reported by Mottow and Jakobiec et al.,8 1 of 30 patients reported by Spindle et al.,4 and 2 cases reported by Bloom et al.9 Anterior uveitis and papillitis were described in one child by Uddin et al.10 and two children by Crowell et al.5 Patel and colleagues also described one child with orbital inflammation, bilateral panuveitis, and macular edema.11 Our case series reports multimodal imaging including fundus photos, fluorescein angiogram, OCT, and MRI findings for pediatric patients with IOIS and uveitis. Additionally, both of our patients demonstrate the risk of recurrent orbital and uveitis flares requiring the use of systemic immunomodulatory treatment for pediatric IOIS.

Our series describes the clinical presentation and course for two children with concurrent uveitis and IOIS, who presented with findings including macular star and hypopyon. Both patients presented with significant uveitis before developing signs more indicative of IOIS such as ptosis, painful extraocular motility, and optic neuropathy. The detection of these findings led to prompt evaluation and management including MRI to diagnose orbital inflammation followed by intravenous corticosteroids. Case 1 demonstrated more significant orbital signs with extraocular muscle dysmotility and optic neuropathy, likely due to a longer period before initiation of systemic corticosteroids. Case 2 presented with hypopyon uveitis and no distinct orbital signs other than mild ptosis, until two days later when optic neuropathy and pain with extraocular movements developed.

Current literature suggests that pediatric IOIS with uveitis may be underreported.4 This could be due to several factors. Uveitis may be undiagnosed in cases of pediatric IOIS with primarily orbital manifestations, as slit lamp examination is not always performed in the emergency setting. Alternatively, subtle orbital signs such as ptosis may be deemed reactive secondary to severe uveitis, highlighting the importance of careful pupillary and extraocular motility examination. IOIS should be considered in children presenting with uveitis along with significant pain and headache. Promptly recognizing the combination of orbital inflammation and uveitis can expedite intracranial imaging and systemic treatment. Alternatively, for children presenting initially with orbital signs, a thorough slit-lamp examination and dilated fundus exam to assess for intraocular inflammation should be performed regularly to prevent vision-threatening complications.

Recurrence of IOIS is well documented in pediatric patients although reports on immunomodulatory therapy for this condition in children is more limited.4,9 Bilateral disease and/or iritis at time of presentation are risk factors for recurrence.4,8,9 In Spindle et al.’s report of 30 pediatric IOIS cases, 37% showed recurrence post-treatment.4 While systemic corticosteroids are established as the first-line treatment of IOIS, long-term treatment carries significant side effects such as glucose intolerance, cataract formation, growth suppression, and weight gain.4,8,9,11 Both of our patients treated with systemic corticosteroids eventually required additional immunomodulatory treatment due to recurrent orbital and anterior uveitis flares. Furthermore, both patients required a disease-modifying antirheumatic drug (DMARD) and a tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-alpha) inhibitor for inflammation to become quiescent. Our cases demonstrate that pediatric patients with IOIS and concomitant uveitis carry a higher risk of recurrence, highlighting the potential need for early use of immunomodulatory therapy to decrease the systemic and ocular morbidities seen with prolonged corticosteroid treatment. Prompt initiation of steroid-sparing therapy, with consideration of escalating to combined therapy with a DMARD and a TNF-alpha inhibitor at onset, could result in efficacious control of inflammation.

Given the limited reports of concurrent IOIS and uveitis in the pediatric population, our cases demonstrate the complexity in diagnosing and managing children with this condition. Key orbital signs suspicious for IOIS in a child with uveitis include severe pain, ptosis, extraocular muscle dysmotility or pain with eye movement, optic neuropathy and papillitis, as well as constitutional symptoms such as headache, nausea, vomiting, and lethargy. These presenting symptoms should prompt consideration of orbital imaging in a pediatric patient presenting with intraocular inflammation. Additionally, children with IOIS and concurrent uveitis are at risk of recurrent orbital and uveitis flares, thus early initiation of steroid-sparing therapy including a TNF-alpha inhibitor may be required to prevent vision-threatening disease.
