Incidence and risk factors for early transient intraocular pressure elevation after penetrating canaloplasty in patients with glaucoma
Authors: Lijuan Xu, Juan Gu, Wenqing Ye, Xinyao Zhang, Yin Zhao, Tao Zhou, Jialing Han, Ruiyi Ren, Yuanbo Liang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate the incidence and risk factors for early transient intraocular pressure (IOP) elevation after penetrating canaloplasty (PCP) in patients with primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG), primary angle-closure glaucoma (PACG) and secondary glaucoma (SG).MethodsThis retrospective study included 198 eyes of 198 patients with glaucoma. Participants were classified as normal IOP, early transient high IOP (E-HIOP [Tst]), early persistent IOP elevation, late transient high IOP, and late persistent IOP elevation, based on the period and persistence of postoperative IOP elevation.ResultsE-HIOP (Tst) developed in 34.8%, 33.3%, and 40.9% of eyes with POAG, PACG, and SG, respectively. In comparison with normal IOP group, we observed the following variations in E-HIOP (Tst) cohorts: 8 − 10 years younger in patients with POAG and PACG; higher prevalence of hyphema (45.5% vs. 20.5%) in POAG; higher baseline IOP in PACG and SG (7 − 8 mmHg); higher preoperative IOP maximum (IOPpre−max) in SG (all p < 0.05). Higher IOPpre−max was a risk factor for E-HIOP (Tst) in PACG and SG (p < 0.05).ConclusionsNearly 30 − 40% of eyes developed E-HIOP (Tst) after PCP. E-HIOP (Tst) tended to develop in younger participants and those with higher preoperative IOP. Background

Glaucoma is an optic neuropathy characterized by progressive retinal ganglion cell damage and visual field loss [1]. Although glaucoma drainage surgeries, most notably trabeculectomies, are effective in lowering intraocular pressure (IOP), they are associated with potentially serious complications [2]. Dr. YB Liang introduced penetrating canaloplasty (PCP), a novel bleb-independent surgery with good efficacy and safety profiles in primary angle-closure glaucoma (PACG) [3, 4], primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG) [5], congenital glaucoma [6], secondary glaucoma (SG) due to traumatic angle recession [7], iridocorneal endothelial syndrome (ICE) [8], and steroid induction [9]. PCP had a 1-year complete success rate of 75.9 − 90.0%, with an IOP reduction of 55%−66% [3–9]. However, frequently occurring early transient postoperative IOP elevations (defined as an IOP > 21 mmHg decreasing to ≤ 21 mmHg within 1 week to 3 months postoperatively) caught our attention. It occurs in approximately 40% of cases and is the most common complication of PCP [6–9].

This phenomenon was commonly observed in other internal glaucoma drainage surgeries [10], such as gonioscopy-assisted transluminal trabeculotomy (8.0 − 37.3%) [11, 12], iStent (6 − 16%) [13, 14], and canaloplasty (6%) [15]. It inevitably imposes financial and mental burdens on patients and requires intervention. Postoperative inflammatory, viscoelastic, or steroid medications may be contributing factors [3, 16]. However, this cannot account for the late onset and spontaneous drops in IOP that occur between 1 week and 3 months. It is necessary to clarify the underlying mechanism to aid surgical optimization and reduce the risk factors for surgical failure.

The trabecular meshwork (TM) and the inner wall of Schlemm’s canal (SC) have the highest resistance to aqueous humor outflow (AHO), and glaucomatous eyes have even higher resistance in these areas [17, 18]. Theoretically, surgical removal of the main resistant sites in conjunction with SC dilation results in direct communication between the anterior chamber and distal AHO. If the distal AHO is healthy, IOP should be controlled. Conversely, the presence of early transient postoperative IOP elevation is an indication of a diseased distal AHO. The pathological sites were considered the TM and its distal pathways in primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG) and the peripheral anterior angles in PACG. However, owing to technical constraints, the investigations into functional and structural differences in the distal AHO among types of glaucoma are limited. The aim of this study was to analyze the risk factors for postoperative early transient IOP elevation in POAG, PACG, and SG groups after PCP.

Methods

This study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki, and the protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Eye Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University (2022-129-K-98). The IRB waived the requirement for informed consent because this was a retrospective analysis based on information from patients’ medical records.

Patients

Patients were enrolled at the Eye Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University between August 2015 and March 2020. Inclusion criteria were: (1) patients with POAG, PACG, and SG indicated for PCP due to insufficient IOP control with the maximum tolerated medical therapy (defined as IOP > 21 mmHg despite receiving two or more anti-glaucoma medications or insufficient IOP control with tolerated drops in use); (2) without severe systemic diseases; and (3) regular follow-up records for ≥ 1 year. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) history of SC destruction; (2) failure of 360° catheterization; and (3) untrustworthy or incomplete data from the follow-up records.

Preoperative examinations

The following ocular phenotypic data were collected: best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) (decimal), intraocular pressure (IOP) measurement (Goldmann Applanation Tonometer, Haag-Streit, Koenitz, Switzerland), slit-lamp biomicroscopy, gonioscopy, ultrasound biomicroscopy (UBM), non-mydriatic fundus photography, and retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thickness evaluated by optical coherence tomography (OCT), axial length (AL) measured by IOL Master (Carl-Zeiss Meditec) and Humphery 24− 2 visual field test (Humphrey Field Analyzer 750i, Carl-Zeiss Meditec AG, California, USA). According to the Hodapp-Anderson-Parrish criteria, glaucomatous eyes were classified as mild, moderate, or severe [19]. Data on preoperative IOPs (including baseline IOP and highest recorded IOP value [IOPpre−max]) and anti-glaucoma medications were collected.

Surgical technique and postoperative evaluation

Dr. YB Liang performed PCP on all participants using a standardized technique [3, 8]. Briefly, the external wall of the SC was opened and unroofed, and the SC was catheterized 360° using a flexible microcatheter (iTrack; iScience Intervention Inc., Menlo Park, CA, USA). To maintain sustained SC dilation, a single 10 − 0 polypropylene suture was knotted with tension. A 1 × 0.5 mm TM tissue block was excised using a combination of peripheral iridectomy, resulting in direct communication between the posterior chamber and SC. Following surgery, all patients were administered Levofloxacin and dexamethasone eye drops four times daily for 2− 4 weeks.

At each postoperative visit (1 day, 1 week, 1, 3, and 6 months, 1 Year, 2 years, and last visit), the following information was collected: IOP, number of anti-glaucoma medications, duration, maximum IOP (IOPmax), and initial onset of postoperative IOP elevation. The lowest recorded IOP value was obtained (IOPmin−1w; usually measured on days 0, 1, 2, and 7).

Early transient IOP elevation was defined as IOP > 21 mmHg that decreased to ≤ 21 mmHg within 1 week to 3 months after surgery. Insufficient IOP regulation (persistent IOP > 21 mmHg despite maximal medication or IOP ≤ 5 mmHg during the first 3 months postoperatively) or the need for additional glaucoma surgery was defined as early surgical failure. At each postoperative visit, surgical success was defined as a postoperative IOP (5 < IOP ≤ 21 mmHg) with (qualified success) or without (complete success) the use of anti-glaucoma medication and additional surgical therapy. The hypotony was referred to as IOP ≤ 5 mmHg that returned to normal within 3 months postoperatively. Based on the presence of early (within 3 months) or late (> 3 months) transient or persistent IOP elevation, patients were divided into five subgroups: normal IOP (NIOP), early transient high IOP [E-HIOP (Tst)], early persistent IOP elevation [E-HIOP (Pst)], late transient high IOP [L-HIOP (Tst)], and late persistent IOP elevation [L-HIOP (Pst)] (detailed definitions were shown in Table S1).

Statistical analysis

SPSS was used for statistical analysis (version 22.0; IBM Corp., NY, USA). A paired t-test was used to compare differences in IOP and decimal visual acuity at baseline and each postoperative visit, and the non-parametric Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed-rank test was used to compare differences in the number of anti-glaucoma medications at baseline and each postoperative visit. To compare variable differences between groups, Student’s t-test (continuous data) or χ2 test (categorical data) was used. Potential factors and variables with p ≤ 0.15 were chosen for further linear logistic regression analysis to assess risk factors for E-HIOP. The threshold for statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Participants selection

Of 307 consecutive patients who underwent PCP, 109 (35.5%) eyes were excluded for the following reasons (Table 1): 56 (18.2%) eyes failed to complete the 360° catheterization, seven (2.3%) had a history of Schlemm’s canal destruction; data of 16 eyes (5.2%) were incomplete or unreliable; and records of 11 eyes (3.6%) were lost at 1-year follow-up. Both eyes of 19 (6.2%) patients met the inclusion criteria, and one eye was chosen randomly. Finally, 198 eyes of 198 patients were studied: 66 with POAG, 39 with PACG, and 93 with SG.

The POAG cohort had slightly more glaucoma years (2.99 ± 3.19 vs. 1.50 ± 1.79 and 2.29 ± 4.44 years), lower preoperative IOP values, and higher 1- and 2-year conditional success rates than the other two cohorts. The PACG group had a mean peripheral angle closure of 10.0 ± 2.3 clock hours, with 94.4% of eyes with closed angles ≥ 180°. Compared with the other two groups, patients in the PACG group showed the following traits: (i) older than the other two cohorts (nearly 10 years); (ii) shorter AL; and (iii) less number of Years with glaucoma. The peripheral closed angles of SG eyes were 3.3 ± 4.6 clock hours, of which 29.3% of eyes had closed angles ≥ 180°. Compared with the other two cohorts, the SG cohort had the following characteristics: (i) less severely damaged optic nerves; (ii) higher preoperative IOP values (baseline and peak); (iii) more hyphema (43.0% vs. 30.3% in POAG and 20.5% in PACG); (iv) higher E-HIOP rates (61.3% vs. 40.9% in POAG and 43.6% in PACG), and (v) lower 1- and 2-year conditional success rates.

Ocular presentations

During the first 3 months post-surgery, four (6.1%) POAG, four (10.3%) PACG, and 19 (20.4%) SG eyes showed persistently elevated IOP despite medical treatment. This was termed early surgical failure. The occurrence of E-HIOP (Tst) was similar between the POAG and PACG groups (34.8% vs. 33.3%, respectively), while it was slightly higher in the SG group (40.9%). Additionally, two eyes each (2.2%) exhibited transient and persistent IOP elevation beyond 3 months in the SG groups. This was absent in the POAG and PACG groups.

Tables 3, 4 and 5 compare the baseline and ocular characteristics of NIOP and E-HIOP/E-HIOP (Tst) in glaucoma subtypes. It should be noted that E-HIOP (Tst) patients in the POAG and PACG groups were significantly younger than the NIOP, with nearly 8 − 10 years (both p < 0.05). In E-HIOP (Tst) eyes with primary glaucoma, postoperative IOPmin−1w was lower (POAG: 7.72 ± 4.00 vs. 8.18 ± 3.61 mmHg, PACG: 8.36 ± 3.34 vs. 9.22 ± 4.58 mmHg), along with more hypotony (POAG: 27.3% vs. 15.4%, PACG: 23.1% vs. 9.1%) than the NIOP, though the difference was not statistically significant. This indicates the possibility of hypoperfusion of AHO in E-HIOP (Tst) eyes.

In comparison with the NIOP, E-HIOP (Tst) eyes showed variable clinical presentations in different glaucoma subtypes: more hyphema (45.5% vs. 20.5%) in the POAG group and higher baseline IOP (43.46 ± 10.71 mmHg vs. 35.00 ± 11.10 mmHg) in the PACG group. These two traits were present in SG E-HIOP (Tst) eyes (all p < 0.05). Additionally, the occurrence of hyphema was positively correlated with hypotony in the entire cohort (r = 0.32, p < 0.01) and the correlation coefficients were 0.22 (p = 0.07), 0.19 (p = 0.25) and 0.41 (p < 0.01) in POAG, PACG and SG, respectively.

E-HIOP eyes had significantly lower success rates than NIOP eyes (POAG: qualified success rates at 1 and 2 Years, and complete success at 2 years; PACG:1 year complete success; SG:1 and 2 year qualified and complete success, all p < 0.05); however, there was no difference in surgical outcomes between E-HIOP (Tst) and NIOP eyes.

E-HIOP characteristics

The E-HIOP cohort comprised eyes with E-HIOP (Pst) and E-HIOP (Tst), both of which exhibited similarly high IOP in the early postoperative period. To distinguish between them, we compared the baseline and ocular parameters. As shown in Table S2, the E-HIOP (Pst) cohort had the following characteristics compared with the E-HIOP (Tst): (i) older age (52.22 ± 13.98 vs. 43.53 ± 12.45 y, p < 0.01); (ii) more DM (nine [33.3%] vs. three [4.1%], p < 0.01); (iii) the median initial onset time of high IOP was later (2.93 vs. 1.00 weeks, p < 0.01), lasted longer (5.28 vs. 3.00 weeks), along with a higher peak value (39.67 ± 8.67 vs. 31.43 ± 7.16 mmHg, p < 0.01), and (iv) more eyes having postoperative IOPmax≥ 30 mmHg (83.3% vs. 54.2%, p = 0.01).

Though there was no statistical significance, E-HIOP (Pst) eyes showed higher IOPpre−max (48.10 ± 7.83 vs. 44.22 ± 9.26 mmHg, p = 0.06) and fewer hyphemas (8 [29.6%] vs. 36 [48.6%], p = 0.09) than E-HIOP (Tst). Further, 96.6% of the high IOP onset and persistent periods in E-HIOP (Tst) occurred within 4 weeks postoperatively, while 67.5% and 45.0% of both characteristics occurred within the same period in E-HIOP (Pst) eyes, respectively.

Risk factors associated with E-HIOP and surgical outcomes

After adjusting for age, gender and laterality of the eyes, hyphema (OR = 16.27, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.58 − 167.08, p = 0.02) and longer glaucoma years (OR = 2.07, 95% CI: 1.29 − 3.34, p < 0.01) were the identified risk factors for E-HIOP in POAG. Higher IOPpre−max was a common risk factor for E-HIOP in both the PACG (OR = 1.25, 95% CI: 1.05 − 1.50, p = 0.01) and SG (OR = 1.12, 95% CI: 1.03 − 1.22, p = 0.01) groups. Older age (OR = 0.76, 95% CI: 0.62 − 0.92, p = 0.01) was a protective factor against E-HIOP in PACG. Longer AL (OR = 1.92, 95% CI: 1.10 − 3.36, p = 0.02) and higher postoperative IOPmin−1w (OR = 1.20, 95% CI: 1.08 − 1.34, p < 0.01) were the identified risk factors for E-HIOP in SG.

The risk factors for E-HIOP (Tst) were similar to those of E-HIOP: More preoperative IOP lowering medicines (OR = 4.05, 95% CI: 1.04 − 15.74, p = 0.04) and longer glaucoma years (OR = 1.57, 95% CI: 1.07 − 2.29, p= 0.02) were the risk factors for E-HIOP (Tst) in POAG. Higher IOPpre−max was a common independent risk factor in the PACG (OR = 1.21, 95% CI: 1.03 − 1.42, p = 0.01) and SG (OR = 1.13, 95% CI: 1.02 − 1.24, p = 0.02) groups. Aging (OR = 0.79, 95% CI: 0.66 − 0.94, p = 0.01) was also a protective factor against E-HIOP (Tst) in PACG. Longer AL (OR = 2.09, 95% CI: 1.12 − 3.90, p = 0.02) and higher postoperative IOPmin−1w (OR = 1.23, 95% CI: 1.08 − 1.40, p < 0.01) were the risk factors for E-HIOP (Tst) in the SG group.

We further analyzed the correlation between E-HIOP/E-HIOP (Tst) and success rates. As shown in Table S3, there were mild-to-moderate negative correlations between E-HIOP occurrence and the 1-year complete success rate in the PACG (r=−0.40, p = 0.01) and SG (r=−0.38, p < 0.01) groups. However, no significant correlation was found between the E-HIOP (Tst) and complete success at 1-year in all subgroups. Additionally, no significant correlation was found between the duration of E-HIOP and the 1-year surgical success rate (all p > 0.05). These results suggest that E-HIOP (Tst) after PCP may not be related to surgical prognosis.

Discussion

In this study, we compared the characteristics and risk factors for postoperative early transient IOP elevation in POAG, PACG, and SG after PCP. The 1-year qualified and complete success rates were 90.9% and 74.2% in the POAG, 89.7% and 82.1% in the PACG, and 77.4% and 74.2% in the SG groups, respectively. This is a promising glaucoma treatment strategy; however, early transient IOP elevation is common and should not be ignored. Although E-HIOP (Tst) rarely results in surgical failure, it can serve as a surrogate for dynamic changes in outflow facilities. However, studies on the underlying mechanisms and associated factors are limited.

PCP combines the techniques of trabeculectomy and canaloplasty, drains aqueous humor through a trabeculectomy-based fistula and then through a dilated SC, and has been shown to have the advantage of not interfering with future filtration or drainage implant [3, 8]. Our previous and current studies show that this procedure is useful for glaucoma treatment because of its remarkable safety and efficacy [4, 6–9].

A major finding of our study was the higher incidence of E-HIOP (Tst) (33.3%−40.9%) than previously reported TM-SC based internal drainage surgeries, such as Hydrus (4.1 − 6.5%) [10], iStent (1.2-6.0%) [20, 21], GATT (24%) [22], Ab-interno canaloplasty (22.2%) [23], and XEN45 gel stent (13%) [24]. (detailed information was presented in Table S4). The outflow facility is partially dependent on the energy-dependent cellular metabolism of the TM and SC [25] The most advanced glaucomatous eyes enrolled in the current study may have had more damaged TM and its distal pathways (including the SC, collector channels, and intrascleral plexus sclerosis), contributing to a higher detected E-HIOP (Tst) rate compared to previous data [26].

The occurrence of E-HIOP (Tst) indicates temporary AHO dysfunction. Some scholars [27] suggest that this may be due to the presence of residual viscoelastic agents or inflammatory factors. However, these substances are usually absorbed within 1 week and cannot explain the later onset or longer persistence of E-HIOP (Tst). Corticosteroids are a potential cause of E-HIOP (Tst). According to our data on corticosteroid-induced glaucomatous eyes after PCP [9] with continued steroid use, the complete success rate at 1 Year was 90%, and postoperative transient IOP elevation developed in two out of 10 eyes, implying that E-HIOP (Tst) may extend beyond steroid use.

AHO structural deformation may be responsible for the E-HIOP (Tst). QI et al. [28] reported that among myopic eyes that underwent cataract surgery, those who developed early postoperative IOP elevation had a significantly smaller SC area, thinner TM, and shorter TM width. Yoshikawa et al. [29] confirmed morphological alterations in AHO after trabecular meshwork surgery using OCT scanning. These results imply that anatomical structural changes during disease progression or surgical intervention, as well as disturbance of the cellular microenvironment, may lead to E-HIOP (Tst) onset.

Healthy TM maintains AHO patency through phagocytosis and pump functions [18, 30], and SC plays an important role in IOP regulation [18]. Mildly damaged AHO may be reversible, and reperfusion probably promotes recovery; thus, IOP decreases. However, in cases where the AHO is severely damaged, mechanical SC reopening may not drain sufficient aqueous humor. Accordingly, we speculate that in the early postoperative period (< 3 months), E-HIOP (Tst) may be mainly attributed to reversible AHO obstruction with the diseased lesion located in the SC and its distal pathway. E-HIOP (Pst) eyes may have lesions in the TM. Further studies are required to verify these hypotheses.

In POAG and SG, hyphema occurs more frequently in eyes with E-HIOP (Tst) than in those with NIOP. Although it resolved spontaneously in all cases in our study, it reflected a pressure reversal between the SC and the episclera, indicating that distal AHO hypoperfusion may contribute to E-HIOP (Tst). Furthermore, patients with E-HIOP (Tst) after PCP had more hypotony than those with NIOP in POAG and PACG eyes. Moreover, the occurrence of hyphema was significantly correlated with hypotony in the entire cohort. The precise relationship between hyphema, hypoperfusion, and E-HIOP (Tst) remains unknown. Further research is required in this area.

In the entire cohort, E-HIOP (Tst) participants were significantly younger than E-HIOP (Pst) patients and younger than NIOP. Furthermore, younger age was identified as a risk factor for E-HIOP (Tst) in the PACG group, and several studies provided consistent supporting evidence [31–33]. Younger patients were expected to have more visible fibrotic responses around the incision site. Age-related metabolic factors may be responsible for this phenomenon.

In both the PACG and SG, a higher IOPpre−max was a significant risk factor for E-HIOP (Tst). According to Johnstone et al., [18] persistently elevated IOP may result in chronic compression of the TM against the external wall of the SC and an undesirable cycle of further IOP elevation. Furthermore, there is a tendency toward ocular rigidity, which may make them vulnerable to surgical attacks and structural deformities. Therefore, eyes with a higher IOPpre−max had poorer reserve or post-trabecular outflow system deformation. This predisposes such eyes to a higher risk of E-HIOP (Tst).

An intriguing finding that merits further investigation was that a significant proportion of E-HIOP (Tst) occurred in eyes with PACG, indicating a pathological condition in distal AHO. We hypothesize that the aqueous humor, which supplies nutrients to cells or tissues along the AHO to maintain a healthy pump function, is reduced for long-term high IOP mechanical pressing, resulting in hypoperfusion of the distal outflow channels. Therefore, we propose a U-shaped relationship between IOP and distal AHO perfusion. In this model, both high preoperative and low postoperative IOP (lower than episcleral venous pressure, 8 − 11 mmHg) in POAG and PACG results in distal AHO hypoperfusion and subsequent structural and functional pathological changes. Depending on the severity of the distal AHO, there may be a time window for the accumulated SC pressure to breach the distal resistance. Therefore, the E-HIOP (Tst) following PCP may represent a process of AHO recirculation. However, further research is required to elucidate this mechanism.

Although manifested as a similar phenomenon of early postoperative IOP elevation, the E-HIOP (Pst) cohort had higher pre- and postoperative IOPmax, older age, later onset, and longer duration of high IOP than the E-HIOP (Tst) cohort. These results indicate different underlying mechanisms. Notably, E-HIOP (Pst) patients had significantly more DM cases than E-HIOP (Tst) patients (28.2% vs. 4.5%), with mean ages of 52.22 and 43.53 years, respectively. Wang et al. [34] reported that the estimated prevalence of DM was 10.9% in 2013 (median age: 55.8 years) and 12.4% in 2018 (median age: 51.3 years). We speculate that the age imbalance may be attributed to the prevalence of DM differences and that microcirculation disorders may correlate with E-HIOP (Pst).

This study had several limitations. The study’s retrospective design prevented consistent follow-up, which was responsible for missing data. Another limitation was selection bias, which was exacerbated by the single-center design. Only Chinese patients were included, and most participants had advanced glaucoma. This may limit the applicability of the results. Third, we only analyzed data up to 2 years postoperatively; thus, long-term AHO changes were undetermined.

Conclusions

In conclusion, following PCP, approximately 40–60% of eyes experienced early postoperative IOP elevation. Of these eyes with high IOP, approximately 70 − 85% exhibited transiently elevated IOP, while the remaining 15 − 30% had persistently high IOP and were considered surgical failures. E-HIOP (Tst) was more likely to occur in patients with these characteristics: younger age, higher preoperative IOP, and hyphema. Ongoing and further research will be critical in understanding the underlying mechanisms.
