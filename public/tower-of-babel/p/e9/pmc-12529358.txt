Kir5.1-modulated potassium flux stimulates an anabolic kidney phenotype
Authors: Aihua Wu, Masa-Ki Inoue, Yahua Zhang, Clinton M. Hasenour, Juan Pablo Arroyo, Fabian Bock, Alina Yu, Mohammed Z. Ferdaus, Yvette Blackwell, Roman M. Lazarenko, Jamey D. Young, Jerod S. Denton, Eric Delpire, Ming-Zhi Zhang, Raymond C. Harris, Andrew S. Terker
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe kidney maintains systemic potassium (K+) balance through energy-intensive epithelial transport processes. Under K+-restricted conditions, kidney epithelial cells proliferate to accommodate profound increases in transport. Tissue reorganization to minimize systemic K+ loss is essential for survival, yet metabolic details remain obscure. Here, we demonstrate that the most-activated kidney pathways under low K+ conditions are those governing metabolism, including carbohydrate- and glutamine-based processes and fatty acid synthesis. We identify that reduced K+ intake stimulates glycolytic flux in the renal cortex to increase amino acid abundance, de novo fatty acid synthesis, and organ expansion. Using a novel mouse model harboring a dominant-negative Kir5.1 channel, we show that each of these steps is dependent on intact basolateral Kir channel flux to initiate rapid kidney growth. Results identify details of a low K+-simulated anabolic kidney program requiring Kir channel function and highlight a therapeutic role for targeting Kir channels to modulate cell physiology and metabolism.

Introduction

Maintenance of potassium (K+) homeostasis is a systemic burden placed on the kidneys. This energy-intensive process is mediated by transport conduits, channels and transporters, expressed along renal epithelial cell membranes. Potassium excursions from the normal physiological range can have profound effects on all cells in the body. Acutely, K+ abnormalities are most concerning for their ability to affect life-threatening cardiac arrhythmias. Chronically inadequate K+ intake can have sustained cardiovascular and metabolic health consequences, as demonstrated by epidemiological and animal studies, including hypertension,1,2,3,4 chronic kidney disease,5,6,7,8,9 cardiovascular disease,10 and stroke.11 Understanding how reduced K+ intake causes this constellation of pathophysiology is essential, given the reduced K+ content in mass-consumed diets today.

We and others have identified both proximal tubule (PT) and distal convoluted tubule (DCT) epithelial cells as being sensitive to changes in K+ intake. Reduced K+ intake profoundly increases epithelial sodium (Na+) reabsorption along these segments; this serves to reduce distal Na+ delivery to the K+-secretory connecting segment (CNT), minimizing urine K+ losses. We have identified the family of inwardly rectifying K+ (Kir) channels as coordinators of this process. Expressed in nephron-specific patterns, basolateral Kir channel flux increases in the setting of reduced blood K+ levels, initiating cytosolic signaling programs that ultimately increase apical Na+ reabsorption.3,8 Kir4.1 and Kir4.2, both of which heterotetramerize with Kir5.1 to form functional channels, are essential along the DCT and PT, respectively, to stimulate Na+ reabsorption under low K+ conditions.7,8,12 Increased Na+ reabsorption is concomitant with PT and DCT segment expansion, leading to an overall increase in kidney mass.7,13 Both low K+-stimulated Na+ transport and kidney expansion are dependent on Kir channel signaling, as their genetic deletion reduces these effects.7,12

Robust energy sources are required for these processes to occur. Na+ transport is dependent on electrochemical gradients established by the Na/K ATPase, and cell growth and proliferation require substantial energetic investments. While we have made progress in understanding the signaling pathways driving increased transport in low K+ states, the underlying metabolism supporting kidney enlargement and increased Na+ reabsorption has not been investigated. Under normal conditions, the PT, which comprises the bulk of the renal cortex, primarily sources energy via β-oxidation of fatty acids.14 Basal glycolysis rates in these cells are minimal, although they do have the capacity to exhibit a more glycolytic phenotype under certain conditions, including pathological states.15

In this study, we sought to identify how underlying renal energetics are linked with the kidney and cardiovascular phenotypes observed in the setting of reduced K+ consumption. We tested the hypothesis that reduced K+ intake signals kidney epithelial cell metabolism through basolateral Kir channel signaling. To test this, we used a novel mouse harboring a mutant Kir5.1 channel that exerts a dominant negative function on its heteromeric partners, including Kir4.1 and Kir4.2, in vivo. We demonstrate that the most activated kidney pathways under low K+ conditions are those governing kidney metabolism, including carbohydrate- and glutamine-based processes and fatty acid synthesis. We observed that reduced K+ intake rapidly and potently stimulates glycolysis to fuel a prosynthetic phenotype supported by increased amino acid availability and fatty acid synthesis. Findings highlight the key role Kir5.1 plays in the profound renal metabolic changes that occur in response to reduced K+ consumption. Absent Kir5.1-dependent K+ flux, kidney metabolism fails to support requisite organ expansion and transport activation, leading to systemic electrolyte derangements.

Results

Reduced K+ intake strongly affects the renal metabolic proteome

To determine the effects of low K+ consumption on the kidney proteome, we performed a mass spectrometry-based proteomics analysis on kidneys from mice consuming either a normal K+ (NK) or K+-free diet for four days. Following LC-MS/MS analysis, we identified over 2500 proteins, 559 of which were significantly increased or decreased in abundance in the K+-free group relative to NK-fed mice (Figure 1A). A principal component analysis (PCA) demonstrated distinct clustering of the two groups with nonoverlapping confidence ellipses (Figure 1B). Ingenuity pathway analysis (IPA) based on the dataset indicated an activation of multiple metabolic and cell proliferation pathways (Figure 1C). Of the 310 proteins that were increased in the K+-free group relative to NK, 43 (∼14%) increased by at least 50%. Of these, 27% were enzymes involved in the regulation of carbohydrate, glutamine, and fatty acid metabolism, including six of the top 10 proteins (Figures 1D and 1E). We confirmed increased kidney abundance of several of these key enzymes by Western blot in a separate group of animals that were treated in an identical manner. These included pyruvate kinase (PK), phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (PEPCK), fatty acid synthase (FAS), ATP citrate lyase (ACLY), and glutaminase (GLS1) (Figures 1F and S1). K+ effects on metabolic pathways occur rapidly with modestly decreased dietary K+ intake

Results suggested that low K+ intake has wide-reaching effects on renal metabolic processes. Because these observations were made under K+-deficient conditions, we postulated that they may not translate to less extreme dietary K+ manipulation. To test this hypothesis, we treated another group of wild-type animals with either a normal diet or a 0.08% K+ diet, rather than a K+-free diet. This dietary K+ concentration was chosen because it maximally activated the renal K+ conservation response as indicated by reduced urine K+ to nearly undetectable levels (Figures 2A and 2B). Urine Na+ concentrations were reduced, yet urine Na+ normalized to creatinine excretion was unchanged with the 0.08% K+ diet (Figures 2C and 2D). Following the consumption of the diets for nine days, we did not detect any differences in blood K+, Na+, or Cl− (Figures 2E–2G). These results indicated that the consumption of a 0.08% K+ diet induces renal K+ conservation to minimize urine K+ losses, resulting in the homeostatic maintenance of normal blood K+ levels. Western blots were performed to determine if alterations in metabolic pathways occurred, as we observed with K+ depletion. We observed increased PEPCK, FAS, PK, and GLS1 abundances under 0.08% K+ conditions relative to normal K+ consumption (Figures 2H, 2I, and S3). A novel mouse model harbors a dominant negative Kir5.1 channel

We hypothesized that these K+ signaling effects on the renal epithelium are mediated by basolateral K+ channels, including the Kir members Kir4.2, along the PT, and Kir4.1, along the DCT.3,7,8,12 These Kir family members both form functional heterotetrametric channels with Kir5.1.16 To study effects of Kir signaling along multiple nephron segments, we used a novel mouse model harboring a mutation in the gene, Kcnj16, which encodes Kir5.1. Our new mouse had a cytosine deletion, as demonstrated by genomic and cDNA sequencing, leading to an F163Sfs∗30 frameshift mutation and a C-terminal deletion (Figures 4A–4C). To identify the functional consequences of this novel mutant Kir5.1 in vitro, we performed patch clamp electrophysiology on HEK cells expressing Kir4.1 along with either wild-type Kir5.1 or the Kir5.1 mutant channel. Cells expressing the mutant channel had significantly reduced Ba2+-sensitive currents relative to wild-type, consistent with a dominant-negative effect of the mutant Kir5.1 channel on Kir4.1/5.1 heteromers (Figure 4D). Cells expressing the mutant Kir5.1 channel also reduced Ba2-sensitive currents in cells expressing Kir4.2 relative to those expressing wild-type Kir4.2/5.1 (Figure 4E). A dominant-negative phenotype was corroborated via quantitative thallium flux assays as described previously.17,18 Heterologous expression of Kir4.1 homomeric channels gave rise to robust thallium flux that was approximately 8-fold greater than that observed in parental HEK cells alone (Figure S6A). This flux was further increased when wild-type Kir5.1 was expressed with Kir4.1 to form heteromeric channels. Similarly, the expression of homomeric Kir4.2 or co-expression with wild-type Kir5.1 produced channel-dependent thallium flux that was greater in the heteromer (Figure S6B). However, the co-expression of the Kir5.1 mutant induced a dominant-negative effect on both Kir4.1- and Kir4.2-mediated thallium fluxes (Figures S6A and S6B). We observed that the total kidney Kir5.1 transcript abundance in Kir5.1DN mice was not different from that of Kir5.1WT controls (Figure 4F). Kir5.1DN animals had reduced protein abundances of both Kir4.1 and Kir4.2 relative to Kir5.1WT controls (Figures 4G and S7). To test the effects of the novel mutation on K+ channel flux in vivo, we performed an 83Rb+ flux analysis on kidney slices from Kir5.1WT and Kir5.1DN mice. We measured ex vivo K+ flux in the absence or presence of Ba2+ to isolate the flux component that is dependent on K+ channels. Kir5.1DN kidney slices had a reduced Ba2+-sensitive 83Rb+ flux compared with those obtained from Kir5.1WT animals, indicating reduced native renal K+ channel flux in mutants (Figure 4H). It has been proposed that basolateral Kir channels are essential for the maintenance of the Na/K ATPase through their role in the “pump-leak” mechanism that facilitates basolateral membrane K+ recycling.19 To test if Kir5.1DN mice have reduced Na/K ATPase activity due to reduced Kir-mediated K+ recycling, we used the pump inhibitor, ouabain, to measure the ouabain-sensitive component of total kidney 83Rb+ flux. Kir5.1DN mice had decreased ouabain-sensitive 83Rb+ flux compared with Kir5.1WT animals, indicating reduced basolateral Na/K ATPase activity, likely secondary to reduced Kir channel flux (Figure 4I).

Baseline phenotypic analysis did not reveal differences in blood Na+ levels, but Kir5.1DN animals did have lower blood K+ and higher blood Cl− than their Kir5.1WT counterparts (Figures 4J–4l). Differences in kidney weight or GFR between genotypes were not observed (Figures 4M and 4N).

The Kir5.1DN channel inhibits the kidney response to decreased K+ intake in vivo

After changing both genotypes to a K+-deficient diet, Kir5.1DN animals had higher daily urine K+ excretion relative to wild-type controls and had greater cumulative urine K+ losses over time (Figures 5A–5C). Genotype differences in urine Na+ were not observed, yielding a lower urine Na+-to-K+ ratio in the Kir5.1DN group (Figures 5D–5F). A failure to adequately respond to K+ deficiency along the PT of Kir5.1DN animals was further supported by reduced abundance of the PT apical and basolateral transporters NHE3 and NBCe1, which have been shown to increase under low K+ conditions (Figures 5L and S8A). Indicative of a thick ascending limb (TAL) compensatory response, abundance of the Na-K-2Cl cotransporter (NKCC2) was significantly increased in mutant animals relative to wild-types following dietary K+ depletion (Figures 5L and S8B). Total and phosphorylated NCC abundance, expressed along the DCT, were not statistically different between genotypes, though they trended toward being reduced. Notably, we did not detect differences in NHE3, NBCe1, NKCC2, and total and phosphorylated NCC abundances on a normal diet (Figure S9).

Kir5.1DN mice also exhibited an impaired response to acid loading. After four days of consuming 0.28 M NH4Cl, they showed reduced urinary ammonia excretion and lower blood bicarbonate levels, consistent with more pronounced metabolic acidosis compared to wild-type controls (Figures S10A and S10B). Reduced kidney GLS abundance in Kir5.1DN animals suggests a failure to upregulate glutaminolysis in support of ammoniagenesis under acid-loaded conditions (Figure S10C).

Inhibition of Kir5.1-mediated flux prevents a low K+-induced prosynthetic kidney phenotype

Prior studies have clearly demonstrated that reduced K+ intake stimulates cell proliferation and expansion along both the PT and DCT.8,13 We next tested if Kir5.1-mediated K+ flux is required for the stimulation of these processes in vivo. Kir5.1DN animals had reduced kidney weights relative to controls on a K+-free diet (Figures 6A and 6B). The abundance of proliferating PT cells was also significantly reduced in Kir5.1DN mice undergoing dietary K+ depletion (Figure 6C). To determine if the abundance of glycolytic intermediates reflected these data in vivo, we measured kidney glycerol-3-phosphate (G3P) and phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP). Both were increased in Kir5.1WT mice under low K+ conditions, consistent with increased glycolytic activity following low K+ feeding (Figure 7F). However, in Kir5.1DN mice, the low K+ response was inhibited for G3P, and entirely absent for PEP, consistent with a deficiency in glycolysis as observed in our glycolytic flux measurements. We hypothesized that increased kidney glycolytic flux supported epithelial cell growth and proliferation along the nephron caused by reduced K+ intake.13 Increased substrates, including amino acids, are required to fuel such a prosynthetic phenotype. This was reflected in the kidneys of Kir5.1WT, which had increased kidney abundances of alanine, serine, aspartic acid, valine, and isoleucine under low K+ conditions (Figure 7F). The low K+-stimulated increases of all measured amino acids were either reduced or absent in Kir5.1DN animals.

Organ expansion also depends on de novo lipogenesis, which can receive carbon substrates downstream of glycolysis to support cell growth and proliferation. Key enzymes in the conversion of glycolytic intermediates to fatty acids (PK, FAS, and ACLY) were some of the most increased kidney proteins under low K+-fed conditions (Figure 1). We performed an isotope-labeling study to determine if intact Kir5.1 signaling is required to drive de novo fatty acid synthesis under low K+-fed conditions. Kir5.1WT and Kir5.1DN animals were treated with 2H2O along with a K+-free diet, and using GC-MS, we determined 2H incorporation into palmitate as a measure of de novo rates of kidney fatty acid synthesis (Figure 7G). Kir5.1DN animals had reduced rates of newly synthesized palmitate relative to the Kir5.1WT group, underscoring a defect in the low K+-induced renal anabolic phenotype in the absence of normal Kir5.1 channel function (Figure 7H).

Discussion

Kir channels are required for low K+-induced kidney metabolic reprogramming

Reduced dietary K+ intake profoundly affects renal epithelial cell function. The kidney response to low K+ intake includes increased Na+ reabsorption, cell proliferation, and nephron segment remodeling,3,7,8,12,13 all of which require large energetic inputs. Our understanding of low K+-driven epithelial signaling pathways has expanded in recent years; however, the requisite metabolism supporting these vast changes has not been identified.

In this report, we identify a renal metabolic program activated in response to reduced K+ consumption. Enzymes involved in glycolysis, glutaminolysis, and fatty acid synthesis were among the most increased kidney proteins under low K+ conditions, highlighting their importance in the response to dietary K+ deficiency. We identified the dependence of these changes on basolateral Kir channel signaling, as the introduction of a Kir5.1 dominant-negative mutant prevented alterations and caused a failure to mount an appropriate K+ conservation response. Urine K+ wasting and a hypokalemic metabolic acidosis consistent with a proximal RTA ensued. Increased PK protein abundance under low K+ conditions suggested increased glycolysis in the low K+ kidney, an organ that typically has low levels of glycolytic flux. Because alterations in protein abundance do not universally imply increased enzymatic flux, we confirmed higher glycolysis rates in the kidney cortex under K+-deficient conditions via Seahorse analysis. Increased glycolysis supported a prosynthetic phenotype demonstrated by increased kidney amino acid abundance and stimulation of de novo lipogenesis, each of which expands the requisite substrate pool for nephron segment growth and kidney hypertrophy (Figure 8). The metabolic switch to a prosynthetic phenotype was dependent upon intact Kir5.1-mediated K+ fluxes. Reduced channel function in the novel Kir5.1DN mouse line prevented the stimulation of glycolysis, the expansion of the amino acid and fatty acid pool, and kidney growth, indicating the essential role of this pathway in the renal response to inadequate dietary K+ intake.Figure 8Model describing low dietary K+-induced renal metabolic changes requiring Kir5.1Reduced K+ intake requires K+ flux through basolateral Kir5.1-containing channels to induce downstream metabolism and transport changes. These changes result in higher glycolytic flux, expansion of the kidney amino acid pool, increased de novo lipogenesis (DNL), and kidney enlargement to support greater levels of epithelial transport. In turn, urine K+ losses are minimized to protect systemic K+ stores.

Low K+ consumption supports anabolism through increased kidney metabolic flexibility

Increased abundance of both carbohydrate and glutamine metabolic enzymes suggested an expanded capacity of the kidney to source energy from a diverse array of substrates under low K+ conditions. This newfound metabolic capacity appears to give the kidney multiple options to obtain energy in support of maximal transport function, thereby minimizing systemic K+ losses. PK, a glycolytic enzyme, was the most increased kidney protein in our low K+-fed mice. We identified increased renal glycolysis under low K+-fed conditions through measurements of kidney glycolytic intermediates and ex vivo determination of glycolytic flux. Without functional Kir5.1 subunits, we observed reduced kidney PK abundance following dietary K+ restriction, leading to reduced glycolytic flux. These findings highlight the essential role of Kir channel signaling in reprogramming kidney carbohydrate metabolism. Although hypokalemia-induced vasoconstriction may limit renal oxygen delivery20 and potentially contribute to elevated glycolytic activity under low K+ conditions, our ex vivo glycolytic flux data indicate a direct effect of reduced extracellular K+ on glycolysis. Nevertheless, potential oxygen limitation as well as secondary hormonal alterations, such as changes in aldosterone or insulin signaling, remain important factors that could influence metabolic reprogramming in our low K+ model.

The third-most increased protein in our dataset was PEPCK, a key gluconeogenic enzyme. Simultaneously increased glycolytic and gluconeogenic enzymes reflect what has been reported in the cancer field. In this context, PEPCK activity reportedly maintains citric acid cycle (CAC) cataplerosis, supporting rapid cell growth and proliferation via carbohydrate and glutamine-fueled metabolism.21 A shift toward a prosynthetic phenotype in cancer cells is essential for neoplastic expansion. In these studies, PEPCK inhibition greatly reduced glycolysis and glutamine-fueled CAC flux in addition to de novo lipogenesis, suggesting it is critical for maintaining carbon flow through these pathways. Our observations in the low K+ kidney may be an analogous metabolic program in a non-neoplastic tissue that is undergoing a rapid rate of cell growth and controlled proliferation.

The increased PEPCK abundance may also represent another facet of low K+ kidney priming for metabolic flexibility. While our data are consistent with increased glycolysis and glutamine-based processes, the kidney may additionally be ready to perform gluconeogenesis as needed, even more so than under standard dietary conditions. While we did not study our animals in a state where we would expect there to be high levels of gluconeogenesis (e.g., fasting), future studies will investigate if the low K+ kidney is also able to perform higher levels of gluconeogenesis in addition to having higher glycolytic flux rates, permitting a higher degree of metabolic agility. Genetic models targeting PEPCK in the kidney will be essential to determine its role.

Metabolic pathways respond to less extreme reductions in dietary K+ intake

Past studies and our present work have made use of K+-free diets, although K+ intake in human populations does not approach such extreme levels of K+ deficiency. We observed that many metabolism enzymes increased in the setting of 0.08% K+ intake, a dietary manipulation that did not chronically reduce blood K+. These findings indicate that more modest reductions in dietary K+ intake, which do not cause frank hypokalemia, are sufficient to stimulate kidney metabolic changes. While small reductions in blood K+ after 24 h of 0.08% K+ consumption do suggest changes in blood K+ may serve as the initiating signal for this system, K+ levels were returned to normal levels after nine days on this diet. We suspect the return to baseline levels is a combination of activated kidney K+ retention pathways and blood K+ buffering from internal stores, such as skeletal muscle.22,23 This raises the possibility that a secondary signal, other than reduced blood K+, maintains the sustained alterations in metabolic pathways. Reduced extracellular K+ not only alters the membrane potential, but also affects intracellular pH, Na+, Cl−, and other heretofore unmeasured cytosolic changes.24,25 One or more of these may serve in this capacity.

Kir5.1 is essential for proximal and distal epithelial cell function

Our results support the existing literature indicating a critical function of Kir5.1 in epithelial kidney biology both proximally and distally.26,27,28,29 Findings are consistent with these studies in that they clearly demonstrate an inability of the kidney to respond to dietary K+ challenges without normal Kir5.1 function. Along the PT, this is evidenced by a hypokalemic proximal RTA along with reduced NBCe1 and NHE3 abundances. Along the DCT, we did not observe a statistically significant reduction in pNCC or total NCC abundances, although they were trending in this direction. This contrasts with the physiological low K+ response in a wild-type kidney, which is a robust increase in pNCC and NCC activity.3 The contrast with the Kir5.1 knockout mouse is even more striking - Kir5.1 deletion is reported to increase basolateral DCT Kir conductance28 effecting increased NCC-mediated transport, which is the opposite of the trend toward a suppressed distal response in our Kir5.1DN animals. These findings suggest that the pathogenesis in patients with dominant-negative Kir5.130 mutations may similarly disrupt heteromeric channel interactions, thereby affecting function along both PT and DCT segments.

The novel Kir5.1DN mutant provides additional insight into the function of this channel and its heteromeric partners in vivo. Results indicate Kir5.1 heteromerizes with both Kir4.1 and Kir4.2 along the nephron. While it is known these channels heteromerize in vitro, it is difficult to demonstrate this in intact kidneys, particularly for Kir4.2. Our mice have reduced kidney Kir4.1 and Kir4.2 abundances, suggesting that Kir5.1 forms oligomers and plays a key role in Kir4.x channel stabilization. Our current work does not identify the underlying dominant-negative mechanisms at play, but possibilities include effects on heteromeric channel stability, trafficking, degradation, or intrinsic function.

The Kir5.1DN animal phenotype resembles that of our Kir4.2 knockout animals,8 although there are key differences. Both models appear to have PT dysfunction leading to distal K+ wasting as indicated by studies using ENaC blockers in both models, but the Kir4.2 null animals have significant DCT activation as a compensatory response.8 Our Kir5.1DN mice, as already mentioned, do not have this DCT response given the inhibitory activity of the mutant channel along this segment. Simultaneous inhibition of PT and DCT K+ responses likely explains the more severe K+ wasting in Kir5.1DN mice relative to Kir4.2 knockouts, including a difference in urine and blood K+ levels under normal dietary conditions. Kir5.1DN kidneys also demonstrated a large compensatory increase in NKCC2 abundance to a greater degree than what we observed in Kir4.2 knockout mice previously. This TAL response may account for an absence of observed Na+ wasting in the Kir5.1DN model and indicate that Kir5.1 does not significantly contribute to TAL Na+ transport. While we did not observe a water-wasting phenotype, Kir5.1 is expressed along the collecting duct and effects along this segment in our Kir5.1DN model require future investigation.

In conclusion, we have identified a novel metabolic program that occurs in response to reduced dietary K+ intake. These metabolic changes include the activation of carbohydrate- and glutamine-based metabolic processes. Under low K+ conditions, increased glycolytic flux fuels de novo fatty acid synthesis and amino acid availability to sustain a prosynthetic phenotype. These fundamental kidney changes are mediated by basolateral Kir5.1-facilitated K+ flux, and they did not occur in the setting of abnormal channel activity. Results have important implications for our understanding of basic kidney epithelial function and highlight a potential therapeutic role for targeting Kir channels to pharmacologically modulate cell function and metabolism in kidney disease.

Limitations of the study

Several important questions remain. Reduced dietary K+ intake exerts broad effects on systemic and hormonal pathways, including insulin signaling, adrenergic tone, and the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system. Although we did not perform extensive hormonal measurements, future studies should investigate how dietary K+ modulates these circulating factors and whether they influence Kᵢᵣ channel activity and epithelial cell function.

While we assessed multiple metabolic and histological parameters, we did not evaluate the impact of K+ intake on mitochondrial function. It is plausible that increased glycolysis and glutamine metabolism support enhanced mitochondrial activity; however, without comprehensive metabolic flux analysis and direct measurements of mitochondrial function and biomass, this remains speculative.

Finally, we identified a central role for Kᵢᵣ5.1-mediated K+ flux in the proximal tubule response to both K+ deficiency and acidosis. The substantial mechanistic overlap in the response to these two physiological perturbations raises the question of whether K+ itself is the lone driver of the observed changes, or if secondary intracellular acidosis contributes to downstream signaling in our low-K+ model, as we have previously reported.7 Ongoing studies within our group aim to provide answers to these remaining questions.

Experimental model and study participant details

All animal experiments were performed in accordance with the guidelines and with the approval of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Vanderbilt University Medical Center. Male mice were used for experimentation aged 8–12 weeks on a C57Bl/6 background. All mice were genotyped by PCR on tail snip DNA before and after experiments by Transnetyx automated genotyping services. Kir5.1 dominant negative mice were created unexpectedly during the targeting of the Kcnj16 gene while attempting to create a floxed animal model. Using guide RNAs located in the 5′ UTR (GACTCCTCTGAATGCCCTAAAGG) and intron 1 (TCTGGCCCTTAGATAAGGGTCGG). Guide RNAs, recombinant cas9, and a 1765 base single stranded repair oligonucleotide were injected into 344 x 0.5-day C57BL/6J embryos. Surviving embryos (257) were implanted into 11 pseudo-pregnant females who delivered 21 pups. Out of 21 pups, 11 were wild-type, 4 had deletions at the cut sites, 3 demonstrated the presence of only one loxP site, and 3 pups (#5, #13, and #16) demonstrated the presence of both loxP sites. Sequences of few hundred bp around each loxP site was performed, including from primers located outside the arms of recombination to ensure recombination in proper locus. Line #5 was backcrossed for 2–3 generations to eliminate possible off target events before crossing to obtain homozygotes, while the two other lines were discarded. Sequencing of the genomic DNA encoding the entire open reading frame revealed deletion of a single nucleotide (c.C486del) that resulted in a frameshift and a truncated protein (p.Phe163Serfs∗30).

Method details

Spot urine collection and electrolyte measurements

Spot urine was collected in the morning between 0800 and 1000 on indicated days. Urine Na+ and K+ were measured on a Diamond Diagnostics CareLyte Plus analyzer (Holliston, Ma), and normalized to creatinine that was measured with The Mouse Creatinine Assay Kit (Crystal Chem).

Metabolic cage studies

Metabolic cage studies were performed as reported previously.3 Briefly, mice were individually housed in metabolic cages from 1700 until 0800 the following day. When urine was collected for consecutive days, animals remained in their home cages between collections from 0800 until 1700 when they were returned to metabolic cages. Urine was collected under water-saturated light mineral oil to avoid evaporation. Urine Na+ and K+ were either measured immediately following collection on a Diamond Diagnostics Carelyte Plus analyzer (Holliston, Ma), or frozen at −20°C until measurements were performed. For urine ammonia measurements, urine was diluted 1:50, measured as previously described (Pointe Scientific), and amount per 16-h collection period was calculated.7

Dietary manipulation

All animals were maintained on standard chow (1.21% K+ and 0.99% NaCl) unless noted otherwise. All experiments with wild-type mice on 0.08% K+ diet used a normal-salt, K+ restricted diet (0.08% K+, 0.75% NaCl, Inotiv) for 9 days or 24 h. All experiments citing a K+-free diet used a normal-salt, K+-depleted diet (TD.88239, 15–30 ppm K+, 0.75% NaCl, Inotiv) for 4 days. For acid-loading, animals were fed a gel diet made with 0.28M NH4Cl in water for four days. This diet contained 0.8% K+ and 0.75% NaCl. Triamterene was supplemented in diet at a dose of 0.5 mg per g of K+-depleted diet as previously reported.8

Blood electrolyte measurements

Blood was collected via cardiac puncture following anesthesia with ketamine and xylazine. Electrolytes were measured on samples obtained by cardiac puncture with an iSTAT analyzer using Chem8+ cartridges (Abbott, Abbot Park, Il) or with a Diamond Diagnostics Carelyte Plus analyzer (Holliston, Ma).

Immunofluorescence imaging

Staining on all sections embedded in paraffin was performed as previously reported.31,32 Briefly, following euthanasia, kidneys were immediately placed in fixative as in prior reports and placed on a rocker overnight at room temperature. Tissues were then dehydrated in a series of ethanols, embedded in paraffin, sectioned (5 μm), and mounted on glass slides for staining. Antibodies used are listed in key resources table. For each experiment, all kidneys were embedded in the same block and mounted on the same slide to reduce sample-to-sample variability. Images were obtaines with a Nikon TE300 fluorescence microscope and spot-cam digital camera (Diagnostic Instruments). For Ki67 quantification, five random 20x images were taken per section and the number of LTL+Ki67+ cells were quantified in each image. The counts for the five images were averaged to obtain a single value per animal. Cortical thickness and tubule diameter measurements were performed using the straight line tool in ImageJ.33 For cortical thickness measurements, 3 images were taken for each mouse with 4 measurements performed in each image. Measurements were averaged within each image and then averaged between images to obtain a single data point for each animal as presented. For tubule diameters, 5 images were taken per mouse with a minimum of two PT and DCT measured for each image. Measurements for each tubule subsegments were averaged for each image and then averaged between images to obtain a single data point for each animal as presented.

Western blotting

As described previously.9 Briefly, kidneys were snap frozen in liquid nitrogen at the time of euthanasia and subsequently transferred to −80°C for storage. For tissue lysate preparation, tissue was homogenized with a Tissue-Tearor homogenizer (Biospec Products) in lysis buffer containing (0.3 M sucrose, 50 mMTris-HCl pH 7.6, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM PMSF, 1 mM DTT, 1 mM EGTA). One PhosSTOP tablet (Roche) and 100 μL Halt protease inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher) was added to each 10 mL lysis buffer. Lysate was then centrifuged at 3300 x g for 15 min. Protein concentration was measured by BCA protein assay (Pierce) followed by gel electrophoresis on a 4–20% Bis–Tris gel (Bio-Rad). Analysis was performed using ImageJ software and protein density was normalized to Ponceau staining for quantification. Antibodies used are listed in key resources table.

Quantitative real-time PCR

As reported previously.34 Kidneys were snap frozen in Trizol reagent (Invitrogen) and RNA was subsequently isolated according to the manufacturer’s protocol. SuperScript IV First-Strand Synthesis System kit (Invitrogen) was used to synthesize cDNA from equal amounts of total RNA from each sample. Quantitative RT-PCR was performed using TaqMan real-time PCR (7900HT, Applied Biosystems). The Master Mix and gene probes for kcnj16 and rps18 were purchased from Applied Biosystems. Relative quantification of specific PCR products was determined by the 2–ΔΔCT method. Data were normalized to RPS18. This kidney cDNA was also used for sequencing to confirm the Kir5.1DN point mutation.

Kir5.1 structure modeling

The pdb file associated with Kir5.1 (AF-Q8R440-F1-v4) was obtained from the AlphaFold Protein structure database. We used PyMOL™ 2.5.3 to color the different domains, rendered (ray trace) the full-length protein model as a cartoon, copied it to the clipboard, and pasted in CorelDraw for addition of labels. Amino acid residues past Phe163 were deleted to depict the truncated protein that we also rendered and copied alongside the wild-type model.

Kidney proteomics

Samples were prepared from whole kidney homogenates. Protein samples for mass spectrometric analysis were prepared by S-Trap™ Midi Spin Columns (ProtiFi LLC) according to manufacturer’s protocol. To each of the protein lysates (1.5 mg), SDS (sodium dodecyl sulfate; Millipore Sigma) was added at a final concentration of 5% followed by addition of DTT (Dithiothreitol; Millipore Sigma) at a final concentration of 20 mM. Samples were heated at 95°C for 10 min. After cooling samples to room temperature, alkylation of cysteine residues was carried out using Iodoacetamide (IAA; Millipore Sigma) at a final concentration of 40 mM for 30 min in the dark. Aqueous phosphoric acid (Fisher Scientific) was added to the samples at a final concentration of 1.2% ensuring the pH is ≤1, followed by S-trap buffer (90% methanol, 100 mM TEAB buffer) at 6.6 times the volume of the sample. The samples were then loaded on Midi spin columns and centrifuged at 4000 x g until the entire sample volume was passed through completely. The spin columns were washed a total of four times with 3 mL of S-Trap buffer by centrifuging at 4000 x g. The trapped proteins on spin columns were digested with Trypsin Protease, MS Grade (Pierce) at 1:20 enzyme to protein ratio in 350uL of 50 mM TEAB pH 8, for 1 h at 47°C without shaking. Peptides were eluted by serial addition of 500 μL each of 50 mM TEAB, 0.2% formic acid, and 0.2% formic acid in 50% acetonitrile. Peptide concentrations of the eluates were measured using a NanoDrop™ 2000 Spectrophotometer with the A205 application. An aliquot of the peptide eluate (5ug) was dried separately in a speed-vac concentrator for total proteome analyses.

The total proteome aliquots were resuspended in 0.2% aqueous formic acid and analyzed by LC-coupled tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). An analytical column (360 μm O.D. x 100 μm I.D.) was packed with 20 cm of C18 reverse phase material (Jupiter, 3 μm beads, 300 Å; Phenomenex) directly into a laser-pulled emitter tip. Samples were analyzed on Orbitrap Exploris™ 480 mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific), using a nanoelectrospray ionization source. Peptides were loaded on the reverse phase column using a Dionex Ultimate 3000 nanoLC and autosampler. The mobile phase solvents consisted of 0.1% formic acid, 99.9% water (solvent A) and 0.1% formic acid, and 99.9% acetonitrile (solvent B). Peptides were gradient-eluted at a flow rate of 350 nL/min, using a 120-min gradient. For total proteome analyses, the gradient consisted of the following: 1–100 min, 2–38 %B; 100–108 min, 38–90 %B; 108–110 min, 90 %B; 110–111 min, 90-2 %B; 111–120 min (column re-equilibration), 2 %B. The instrument method consisted of MS1 using an AGC target of 3e6, followed by up to 20 MS/MS scans of the most abundant ions detected in the preceding MS scan. The intensity threshold for triggering data-dependent MS2 scan event was set to 1e4, the MS2 AGC target was set to 1e5, dynamic exclusion was enabled (20 s exclusion duration), and HCD collision energy was set to 30% nce.

For identification of peptides, tandem mass spectra were searched with Maxquant35 version 2.0.3.0, using a mouse database (downloaded 3 January 2023) created from the UniprotKB protein database (www.uniprot.org). MS/MS spectra were searched with the Andromeda search engine36 against a target decoy mouse database containing forward and reverse sequences. Maxquant parameters included precursor mass and fragment mass tolerances of 4.5 ppm and 20 ppm, respectively. A maximum of two missed cleavages was allowed. The false discovery rate (FDR) was set to 0.01 for peptide and protein identifications. Variable modifications included methionine oxidation and N-terminal acetylation. Carbamidomethyl cysteine was selected as a fixed modification for analysis. Label-free quantitative (LFQ) analysis of identified proteins from the total proteome dataset was performed with the MSstats R package using default parameters,37 including equalize medians for the normalization method, log 2 transformation, Tukey’s median polish as the summary method, and model-based imputation. Protein fold changes with p-value ≤0.05 were considered significant.

Tubule suspensions

As performed previously.8 Briefly, whole kidneys were minced on ice for 30–60 s with a blade. Homogenates were incubated in 1 mL RPMI medium supplemented with collagenase D (Roche) and DNAse I (Biorad) for 5 min at 37°C with intermittent agitation. Sample was pipetted up and down 5–10 times and larger pieces were allowed to settle to the bottom. Overlying tubule suspension was removed and transferred to a new tube. Process was repeated with remaining larger pieces and resulting suspensions were combined, resuspended in RPMI, and centrifuged for 10 min at 300 x g in a tabletop centrifuge. Tubules were plated in DMEM with 10% FBS for 1 h in a 37°C incubator with a CO2 buffering system (5% CO2) prior to use. After 1 h, tubules were collected and centrifuged again for 10 min at 300 x g. After aspiration of the supernatant, the pellet was resuspended in the indicated condition and treated for 30 min prior to collection. Following treatment, suspensions were collected and pelleted by centrifugation in a microfuge for 5 min at 300 x g followed by resuspension in 1x sample buffer. Samples were then heated for 10 min at 65°C followed by gel electrophoresis and Western blot.

Kidney metabolite extraction, derivatization, and gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS)

Performed in collaboration with the Vanderbilt Mouse Metabolic Phenotyping Center similar to a previous report.38 Tissue metabolites (40–70 mg tissue) were isolated using a biphasic methanol/water/chloroform extraction. Norvaline (20 μL of 5 mM) was added to each sample as an internal standard for metabolite quantification. The polar layer of the extract was isolated using a fine-tipped pipette and air dried overnight for storage at −80°C prior to derivatization. Polar metabolites from tissue extracts were converted to their methoxime tert-butyldimethylsilyl derivatives (TBDMS) using MtBSTFA+1% TBDMCS (Regis Technologies 1-270144-200). Calibration standards with known amounts of each metabolite were prepared and derivatized simultaneously with the extracted samples for absolute quantification of metabolite abundances. Derivatized samples were injected onto an HP-5ms column (Agilent Technologies 19091S-433) in an Agilent 7890B gas chromatograph paired with an Agilent 5977A mass spectrometer. Data were acquired in scan mode.

Heavy water (2H2O) treatment and kidney de novo lipogenesis analysis

Animals were fed a K+-free diet for four days as performed in other experiments. On day two of this protocol, animals were given an intraperitoneal injection of isotonic 2H2O (20 μL per g body weight of 99% 2H2O in 0.9% NaCl). Animals were then allowed free access to water containing 4% 2H2O in drinking water for the final two days of the protocol. Blood and kidneys were obtained following euthanasia. To determine 2H2O in plasma, samples were spiked with acetone and NaOH. After exchange, 2H-enriched acetone was extracted with chloroform and assayed by GC-MS.39 Palmitate mole percent enrichments (MPE) and content were assessed in saponified extracts spiked with internal standard (heptadecanoic acid).40,41 De novo lipogenesis was calculated from the incorporation of 2H in palmitate. The contribution of newly synthesized palmitate was determined using the following equations: %newly made palmitate = Palmitate M+1 MPE/(plasma 2H2O MPE x n) × 100% where “n” represents the number of exchangeable hydrogens, which was assumed to be 22.39

Kidney cortex glycolytic flux studies

Mice were maintained on either normal or K+-free diets for four days. Following euthanasia, kidneys were sectioned on a Leica VT 1000S vibratome at 350 μm. Cortical punches were obtained by punch biopsy (1 mm) and placed in serum-free custom DMEM containing either 5 mM K+ (for punches from normal K+-fed mice) or 0 mM K+ (for punches from K+-free-fed mice) adjusted per the manufacturer’s recommendations (Agilent) to include 10 mM glucose, 1 mM pyruvate, and 2 mM glutamine. Three punches were placed in each well of a 24-well plate (Agilent Seahorse Islet Capture Microplate) and placed in a non-CO2 incubator for 1 h prior to analysis on a Seahorse XFe Extracellular Flux Analyzer. Five ECAR readings were obtained before and after the addition of 2-deoxyglucose (2-DG) to the medium at a final concentration of 50 mM. Five readings were averaged under each condition for use in calculating the ΔECAR values used in analysis. ΔECAR was defined as the average ECAR value after the addition of value 2-DG minus the average ECAR value before the addition of 2-DG as outlined in Figure 7E.

Kidney cortex K+ flux studies

Following euthanasia, kidneys from Kir5.1WT and Kir5.1DN were sectioned on a Leica VT 1000S vibratome at 350 μm. Cortical punches were obtained by punch biopsy (4 mm) and placed in serum-free custom DMEM containing either 5 mM K+ or 0 mM K+. To obtain unidirectional K+ influx measurements, two punches were placed in individual wells in a 24-well plate and preincubated for 15 min in 400 μL isosmotic solution containing 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM KCl, 2 mM CaCl2, 1 mM MgSO4, 1 mM glucose, 10 mM HEPES (pH 7.4), and in the presence or absence of 5 mM barium (Ba2+) (or 1 mM oubain). Following the preincubation period, the solution was aspirated and replaced with 400 μL identical solution containing 0.25 μCi/mL 83Rb (Brookhaven National Laboratory, Upton, Ny). Aliquots (5 μL) of the radioactive solutions were sampled and added to small glass vials containing 5 mL scintillation fluid and used as standards. After a 20 min 83Rb uptake period, the radioactive solution was aspirated, and the punches were rapidly washed thrice with 500 μL ice-cold saline. Tissue was then lysed with 400 μL 0.25N NaOH for 2 h, neutralized with 200 μL acetic acid glacial and 150 μL (1/4 of the sample) was used to count 83Rb radioactivity. Another aliquot of 10 μL was used for protein determination. K+ influx was then calculated and normalized to Kir5.1WT data without Ba2+ or ouabain for each experiment.

HEK thallium flux assays

Thallium flux assays were performed essentially as described previously.18 Briefly, HEK293 cells were transfected with expression plasmids (5 μg for each construct per T25 flask) encoding Kir4.1, Kir4.2, Kir5.1, or Kir5.1DN using Lipofectamine LTX according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The following day, the transfected cells were dissociated, re-plated at a density of 20,000 cells/well in black-walled, clear-bottomed, amine-coated 384-well assay plates (Corning) and cultured overnight in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium containing 10% FBS and 1.1% penicillin and streptomycin at 37°C and 5% CO2. Approximately 48-h post-transfection, the cells were incubated with dye-loading assay buffer (Hank’s balanced salt solutions, 20 mM of HEPES, pH 7.3) containing 2% (w/v) Dysolv and 2 μM of Brilliant Thallium (Ion Biosciences, Austin, TX) dye in ambient conditions for 1 h washed with Hanks’ balanced salt solution/20 mM HEPES (assay buffer), and transferred to a Panoptic Kinetic Imaging Plate Reader (Wavefront Bioscience, Franklin, TN) to collect measurements at 1 Hz (482/35 nm excitation and 536/40 nm emission). Thallium flux was initiated by the addition of gluconate-based stimulus buffer containing 1 mM final Tl2S04 (Ion Biosciences). Fluorescence versus time values for each well were normalized to the initial fluorescence values. Slope values were calculated between 5 and 12 s following stimulus buffer.

Patch-clamp electrophysiology

Whole-cell patch-clamp electrophysiology was used to record Kir4.1/5.1 and Kir4.2/5.1 currents from HEK-293 cells transfected with plasmids encoding wild-type Kir4.1 or Kir4.2 channels, co-transfected with wild-type or mutant Kir5.1 using Lipofectamine LTX. 0.5 μg of each construct was used per 35 mm dish. Transfected cells were visualized through GFP fluorescence. Cells were cultured in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37°C, dissociated using 0.25% Trypsin-EDTA, and seeded onto 12 mm circular coverslips (Electron Microscopy Sciences, PA, USA) for same-day whole-cell patch-clamp recordings. Currents were recorded at room temperature using an AxoPatch 200B amplifier with Clampex 10.7 software (Molecular Devices, USA). The voltage-clamp protocol consisted of a 1-s step to −120 mV from a −80 mV holding potential, followed by a 2-s ramp to +40 mV, repeated every 10 s. Data were sampled at 5 kHz and filtered at 1 kHz. Patch pipettes, pulled from 1.5 mm OD thin-walled capillaries (Warner Instruments, MA, USA) using a Sutter P-1000 puller (Sutter Instrument, CA, USA), had resistances of 2–3 MΩ when filled with intracellular solution containing (mM): 135 KCl, 10 HEPES, 1 EGTA, 2 MgCl2, 2 Na2ATP (pH 7.35, adjusted with KOH). The extracellular solution contained (mM): 135 NaCl, 5 KCl, 2 CaCl2, 1 MgCl2, 5 Glucose, 10 HEPES, 10 Sucrose (pH 7.35, adjusted with NaOH). After establishing stable baseline currents, cells were perfused with a 2 mM Ba2+ solution to isolate Ba2+-sensitive currents. Current amplitudes at −120 mV were analyzed using Clampfit 11.2 (Molecular Devices).

Transdermal glomerular filtration rate (GFR) measurements

The FITC-sinistrin method was used as previously reported.32 FITC-sinistrin half-life was calculated using a three-compartment model with linear fit using MPD Studio software (MediBeacon, Mannheim, Germany). The FITC-sinistrin half-life (min) was converted to GFR (in μL/min/100g BW), as previously described.42

Quantification and statistical analysis

Performed using Prism v10.3.1. Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical tests used are indicated in figure legends. Comparisons were made with unpaired Student’s t test, Mann-Whitney, or one/two-way ANOVA with or without repeated measures as appropriate and indicated in the figure legends. Corrections for multiple comparisons were performed as indicated. All tests were two-sided.
