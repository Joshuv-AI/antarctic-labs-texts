Quality of life after pulmonary embolism: psychometric validation of the Danish electronic version of the PEmb-QoL questionnaire
Authors: Stine Foged Lindegaard, Jan Brink Valentin, Nanna Rolving, Stian Ingemann-Molden, Anette Arbjerg Højen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivePatient-reported outcome measures are essential for assessing health-related quality of life (HRQoL) in both research and clinical practice. The Pulmonary Embolism Quality of Life (PEmb-QoL) questionnaire is a disease-specific instrument designed to measure HRQoL in patients with pulmonary embolism (PE). This study aimed to evaluate the psychometric properties of the Danish electronic version of the PEmb-QoL questionnaire.DesignPsychometric validation study.SettingEligible patients received a digital mail 6 months after their diagnosis, with an invitation to complete an online questionnaire. A subset of patients completed a second time 7–10 days following the initial completion.ParticipantsAdult patients diagnosed with PE in Denmark between March 2022 and May 2023 were included in the study.Main outcome measureThe main outcome measure was the PEmb-QoL summary score. Validation procedures included assessments of targeting, internal reliability (IR), test–retest reliability, differential item functioning (DIF) and concurrent validity.ResultsThe study included 1017 patients. The Danish PEmb-QoL demonstrated robust IR (IR=0.95 for the total score) and adequate test–retest reliability for most domains (relative G-coefficients>0.7). However, DIF analysis revealed subgroup differences for some items, particularly those related to daily activity limitations and pain. Targeting analysis indicated moderate alignment between latent score distributions and item response distributions, with ceiling effects noted in several domains. The PEmb-QoL exhibited strong correlations with the Post-Venous Thromboembolism Functional Status Scale (r=0.82) and EuroQol-5 Domain (r=0.73), and moderate correlations with Generalised Anxiety Disorder Questionnaire (r=0.47) and Patient Health Questionnaire (r=0.57), confirming its concurrent validity.ConclusionWhile some items in the PEmb-QoL instrument performed inadequately on DIF and targeting, the expected overall score and the concurrent validity did not seem to be compromised by these issues. Thus, it is likely that a reduced version of the electronic Danish PEmb-QoL will possess suitable psychometric properties for measuring HRQoL in patients with PE.Keywords: Thromboembolism, Patient Reported Outcome Measures, Quality of Life

Introduction

Venous thromboembolism (VTE), including deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism (PE), belongs to the most common cardiovascular conditions globally.1,4 PE is the most severe condition leading to significant morbidity and mortality, and the incidence rate of PE ranges from 39 to 115 per 100 000 person years.3,5 Due to improved treatment and disease management, more patients survive an acute PE event, paradoxically leading to more patients experiencing chronic symptoms.4 5 More than half of patients develop persistent symptoms of dyspnoea and functional limitations, and a significant proportion of patients will experience recurrence of PE.26,8 These complications have been shown to negatively affect patients, manifesting as fatigue, anxiety, fear-avoidance behaviour and reduced activity levels, ultimately resulting in lower quality of life.9,13

Considering the complexity of the consequences patients face after a PE event, it is important that patient-reported outcome measures (PROM), used in both research and clinical practice, are correspondingly multifaceted.13 This will ensure that interventions aimed at improving patients’ well-being are evaluated on a measure of what is important to the patients. The disease-specific Pulmonary Embolism Quality of Life (PEmb-QoL) questionnaire was originally developed in Dutch14 and has subsequently been successfully translated and validated in English,15 Norwegian,16 French,17 Chinese,18 German19 and Persian.20 The PEmb-QoL covers six domains of health-related quality of life (HRQoL).15 21 Recently, we performed an electronic adaptation and cross-cultural translation of the PEmb-QoL questionnaire in a cohort of Danish patients with PE.22 Because the performance of a questionnaire may differ between populations and cultures, as well as between paper and electronic versions, this process included cognitive interviews with 19 patients, aged 19–73, to ensure cultural relevance and understanding.22 However, beyond face validity, a thorough psychometric validation of the PEmb-QoL is essential to ensure that the questionnaire measures the intended traits accurately and is not influenced by unrelated patient characteristics. Therefore, the aim of this study was to conduct a psychometric validation of the Danish electronic version of the PEmb-QoL, including assessments of targeting, inter-item consistency (IIC), test–retest reliability, differential item functioning (DIF) and concurrent validity, to establish both the reliability and validity of the instrument.

Methods

Data were collected as a part of a nationwide study, where A Structured Integrated Post Pulmonary Embolism care model (the Attend-PE model) was developed.23 The main study was designed to evaluate patients’ recovery and return to everyday life after a diagnosis of PE, before and after implementation of the Attend-PE follow-up care model across Danish hospitals.24

Design

This study is a psychometric validation study assessing the validity of the Danish version of the PEmb-QoL using item response theory (IRT). IRT constitutes a set of models, which can be used to evaluate each item’s performance based on the probability of a certain response pattern.25 One of the benefits of applying IRT models is the ability to evaluate the presence of DIF, which identifies if certain patient subgroups have a predisposition for particular responses to individual items.26 In addition to IRT, the COSMIN (COnsensus-based Standards for the selection of health Measurement Instruments) checklist recommends assessing targeting, internal reliability (IR) and concurrent validity, to ensure that the scale items under investigation possess unidimensionality, monotonicity and local independence.27

Instrument

The PEmb-QoL questionnaire

The PEmb-QoL is a PROM designed for self-completion, with content and format of the items and response scales modelled after the 36-Item Short-Form Survey.15 21 It measures the impact of PE within the past 4 weeks for most questions. The PEmb-QoL consists of nine questions with 40 subquestions (items), covering six domains: frequency of complaints (FC), daily activity limitations (AL), work-related problems (WP), social limitations (SL), intensity of complaints (IC) and emotional complaints (EC).15 Responses are rated on Likert response scales and summary scores are calculated for each domain and in total.

The PEmb-QoL summary score provides an estimate of the overall impact of PE on the patient’s quality of life.15 As higher scores reflect worse HRQoL, the response scales of all items in questions 1, 4, 5 and 9 are reversed in the summary calculation. Item 4a was considered missing if the answer was ‘I do not work’. Questions 2 and 3 are not included in the summary scores. The PEmb-QoL summary score was calculated by transforming all items score to a scale ranging from 0 to 100 to make items with different response scale comparable. Then an average of these transformed scores was calculated to obtain an overall summary score.15

Study setting

The study was conducted in Denmark, with a tax-funded healthcare system with services freely available for all citizens, independent of income.

Population

All Danish patients having received a CT-verified diagnosis of PE (International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10 code: DI26) in the period March 2022 to May 2023, aged ≥18 years at the time of diagnosis, who had access to digital mail from the Danish public authorities (≈90% of the population) and were willing to answer the questionnaire were eligible for inclusion in the study. Eligible patients with PE were identified from the Danish National Patient Registry (DNPR).28

Data collection

Eligible patients received a digital mail 6 months after their diagnosis, with an invitation to complete an online questionnaire through a secure digital mail system which is linked to all Danes’ social security numbers. Besides the PEmb-QoL, the patients were asked about their functional status using the Post-VTE Functional Status Scale (PVFS)29; HRQoL using a generic measure EuroQol 5 Domain 5 Levels (EQ-5D-5L)30; symptoms of anxiety and depression using the General Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7)31 and the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9).32 In addition, the patients’ height, weight, level of education and civil status was recorded. Data on the patients’ age, sex, date of PE diagnosis and previous cancer diagnosis (ICD-10: C0-9 except C44) was retrieved from the DNPR.28

A subset of patients who received a diagnosis of PE between August 2022 and January 2023 were sent an additional invitation to complete the PEmb-QoL questionnaire a second time, 7–10 days following the initial completion, to establish test–retest reliability.

Statistical analyses

For the full psychometric validation, several analyses were conducted investigating model fit, local dependency, targeting, IR including a D-study on number of items,33 34 test–retest, DIF25 26 35 and concurrent validity. These are described in detail below. Initially, baseline demographics for the total population and the population used for the test–retest analysis were summarised, together with the baseline responses of all items in the PEmb-QoL questionnaire.

For all analysis, we excluded measurements where all items in the PEmb-QoL were missing. For the test–retest analysis, only patients with complete responses or complete domain responses at both baseline and follow-up were included. Only complete responses were included in the total score analysis. For analysis on individual domains, we included responses that were complete within each domain, except for the AL domain, where item 4a was ignored if missing. For the D-study, we included all available data. Results were presented with 95% CIs. Analysis was conducted in Stata 18 (StataCorp 2023. Stata Statistical Software: Release V.18. College Station, Texas, USA: StataCorp LLC.) and R (R Core Team (2018)).

Model fit and local dependency

For most IRT analyses, we applied the graded response model (GRM); however, two of the software packages used in the study did not support the GRM, and we instead applied the partial credit model (PCM), thus constraining discrimination to one in these two cases.36 37 Item fit statistics were conducted for the GRM using the generalised S-X2 index.38 The PCM and GRM were compared using a likelihood ratio test. Item fit was visualised for selected items using empirical plots comparing expected and observed latent score probability based on the GRM. Tests for local dependence were conducted using the partial Gamma coefficient adjusted for the rest score.39 The magnitude and direction of the local dependence were investigated using the Q3 fit statistic inferred from the PCM.40 Significance tests were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg approach. The following packages were used for the analyses of model fit and local dependency; mirt (Multidimensional Item Response Theory V.1.44.0 for R), RASCHplot (Plots for investigating Rasch model fit V.0.1.0 for R) and iarm (Item Analysis in Rasch Models V.0.4.3 for R).

Targeting

The targeting analysis was conducted using person-item maps for each domain that contained more than two items. Targeting is used to assess how well the population under investigation covers the scale of the PEmb-QoL instrument and its domains. We applied the PCM to estimate the latent score distribution, the item-wise average cut-point locations on the latent score scale and the test-information function (TIF). Item 4a was ignored in the targeting analysis because of the large number of missing on account of the ‘I do not work’ category. The RMX package was used for the targeting analyses.41

Internal and test–retest reliability

Generalisability theory was applied to estimate the measures of reliability. For IR, we calculated relative inter-item reliability for the total score as well as domains. In addition, we estimated the inter-item reliability with a reduced number of items, varying the number of items from one to the corresponding number of items in each domain. This investigation is referred to as a D-study. These analyses were based on a fully crossed random effects model with three facets for the test–retest analysis and two facets for IR. The model assumes additive and normally distributed random effects and local dependence. We considered inter-item reliability to be adequate at a minimal value of 0.8. Moreover, we estimated average inter-item correlation (AIIC) for the total score and each domain. The AIIC is the average correlation estimated using Pearson’s correlation coefficient of all possible item pairs. We considered the AIIC to be optimal in the range 0.2–0.4, which gives a trade-off between consistency and local independence.

For the test–retest analysis, we estimated the relative and absolute G-coefficient for the total score and all domains. The relative G-coefficient is the proportion of random error attributable to between patient variance, while absolute G-coefficient is a proportion of both random and systematic error attributable to between patient variances. We consider the instrument and domains to possess adequate retest reliability if the relative G-coefficient is above 0.7, while the absolute G-coefficient should not be statistically significantly lower than the relative G-coefficient. Both G-coefficients are presented as percentages. The following packages were used for the reliability analyses; glmmTMB (Generalised Linear Mixed Models using Template Model Builder V.1.1.11 for R), and msm (Multi-state modelling V.1.8.2 for R)

Differential item functioning

For the DIF analysis, the GRM was used to estimate the difference in difficulty and discrimination between the subgroups defined in the covariates section. A difference of zero in both difficulty and discrimination means that the item and subgroup under investigation was not subject to DIF. For items with more than two categories, the difference in difficulty was estimated as the maximum separation between two corresponding cut-point locations. In addition, we plotted the boundary characteristics curves (BCCs), and conditional item characteristic curves (CICCs) for selected items and comparisons.42 The CICCs were based on the PCM and compared expected item scores with observed scores stratified by the DIF groups. Moreover, we estimated the expected outcome as a function of the latent score for selected items and comparisons as well as domains. Item 4a is ignored in all DIF analyses. The following packages were used for the DIF analyses; irt (StataCorp 2023. Stata 18 Item Response Theory Reference Manual. College Station, Texas, USA: Stata Press), mirt (Multidimensional Item Response Theory V.1.44.0 for R), RASCHplot (Plots for investigating Rasch model fit V.0.1.0 for R) and eRm (Extended Rasch Modelling V.1.0.10 for R)

Concurrent validity

Concurrent validity refers to the correlation of measures with similar traits. For the analyses, we hypothesised that the PEmb-QoL would have a moderate correlation with functional ability status measured using the PVFS questionnaire (>0.5). Moreover, we hypothesised that the Pemb-QoL would exhibit a positive correlation with both the GAD-7 and the PHQ-9 questionnaire (>0.4), while the strongest positive correlation would be found in comparison with the EQ-5D-5L (>0.7).

Results

A total of 1952 persons were registered with a PE diagnosis during the survey period and were able to receive digital mail. Of those, 1017 (52.1%) unique patients were included at baseline with 957 (49.0%) complete responses. A total of 426 patients received test–retest, where 279 (65.5%) unique patients were included in the analysis, with 261 (61.3%) patients having complete responses at both follow-up and baseline. The mean age of included patients was 68 (58–76) years and 56.5% were male. Baseline characteristics are shown in table 1, and distribution of item responses is shown in online supplemental figure S1 to S8. A total of 477 (46.9%) responded ‘not working’ to item 4a in the PEmb-QoL at baseline and 141 (50.5%) at test–retest follow-up.

Model fit and local dependency

Several items displayed p values less than 0.05 even after Benjamini-Hochberg correction. However, the largest root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) for the S-X2 measure was 0.056 (p<0.001) and shown for item 5d, followed by an RMSEA of 0.049 (p<0.001) and 0.041 (p<0.001) for item 5c and 4d. The domain-specific item-fit statistics showed only two statistically significant results with an RMSEA of 0.022 (p=0.036) and 0.038 (p=0.003) for item 9j and 4d. Empirical plots for item 4d, 5c and 5d are shown in online supplemental figures S9–S11. These plots do not raise any concern for poor item fit.

The likelihood ratio test displayed a statistically significant difference between the fit of the PCM and the GRM with a p value less than 0.001 (test statistic: 1792.879). This is supported by Bayes information criterion of 62 105.78 for the PCM compared with 60 565.42 for the GRM showing significantly better fit of the GRM even after adjusting for the increased number of model parameters.

Likewise, several item pairs displayed statistically significant local dependency for the PEmb-QoL summary score as well as the domain specific local dependencies (results not shown). However, the Q3 fit statistics were adequate for the domain wise local dependencies (see online supplemental figures S12–S15), while for the PEmb-QoL summary score there were indications of local dependencies within domains (see online supplemental figure S16), which may be an indication of multidimensionality despite good model fit.

Targeting

For the domains FC, AL and EC in the Pemb-QoL, the person-item map did not exhibit complete overlap between the distribution of responses on the latent scale and the TIF, and was, thus, only moderately on target (see figure 1, and online supplemental figures S17 and S19). This coincides with the item-wise response distributions of the corresponding domains shown in online supplemental figures S1, S4 and S8, where the majority of items were dominated by positive responses showing a tendency towards a ceiling effect. For the WP domain, the item-wise responses were likewise dominated by positive responses (see online supplemental figure S5); however, since these items were binary (yes/no) questions, the targeting analysis showed adequate overlap between the latent-space distribution of responses and the TIF for this domain (see online supplemental figure S18). The item-wise response distributions for items 2, 3, 6, 7 and 8 are shown in online supplemental figures S2, S3, S6 and S7.

Differential item functioning

Table 4 shows the maximum difference in item functioning found for each comparison and measure of DIF. For comparisons of male and female, item 4b ‘Do your lung symptoms now limit you in daily activities at home’ was responsible for the largest difference in both discrimination and difficulty, where males were more likely to answer ‘no’ compared with females with the same latent score. The items responsible for the largest difference in item functioning across all comparisons were item 7 ‘How much pain around your shoulder blades/pain in your chest have you experienced during the past 4 weeks?’. Moreover, item 7 displayed the largest difference in difficulty for the BMI and cancer group comparisons. Item 7 further showed a large difference in difficulty for the age group comparison (see online supplemental table S3). Figure 3 shows the BCC for item 7 of the cancer and age group comparisons. The BCC for item 4l ‘Do your lung symptoms now limit you in walking about one hundred yards’ of the age group comparison is also depicted in figure 3. This item was responsible for the largest difference in difficulty for this comparison and the related item 4j ‘Do your lung symptoms now limit you in walking more than half a mile’ was responsible for the largest difference in discrimination for the same comparison (see table 4). These results were supported by the CICC which displayed similar DIF (see online supplemental figures S20–S22). However, accounting for differences in responses between age groups on item 4l and 7 as well as the related items 4j and 4k did not affect either the total score nor the AL domain score in any significant way (see figure 4). The DIF measures for all items and all comparisons are shown in online supplemental table S3. These results suggest that while certain items exhibit subgroup-specific variations, the overall scores remain robust, indicating that the PEmb-QoL is generally resilient to DIF across key patient characteristics.

Concurrent validity

The correlation between the PEmb-QoL and the PVFS questionnaire was very strong, with a statistically significant coefficient of 0.82 (95% CI: 0.79 to 0.84) which was higher than expected. As hypothesised, the correlation between PEmb-QoL and the EQ-5D-5L questionnaire was also high, with a coefficient of 0.73 (95% CI: 0.68 to 0.78). Similarly, the correlations between the PEmb-QoL and the GAD-7 and PHQ-9 questionnaires were fair, with coefficients of 0.47 (95% CI: 0.40 to 0.53) and 0.57 (95% CI: 0.51 to 0.62), respectively. These findings confirm the strong concurrent validity of the Danish PEmb-QoL, supporting its use in assessing both physical and psychological aspects of HRQL in patients with PE.

Discussion

This study provides a psychometric validation of the Danish electronic version of PEmb-QoL indicating its validity and utility in assessing HRQoL among patients recovering from PE. The findings highlight strengths and limitations in the instrument’s performance, as well as opportunities for refinement.

The targeting analysis showed moderate alignment between the TIF and the latent score distribution for several domains, including the domains FC, AL and EC in the PEmb-QoL. This suggests that the questionnaire captures the general trait distribution but may lack precision for differentiating individuals with higher functional status or better quality of life. Ceiling effects were evident in many items, as responses tended to cluster positively. This likely reflects the timing of administration, which occurred 6 months after PE diagnosis. This may coincide with the natural recovery timeline for most patients with PE, suggesting that the questionnaire might be more sensitive when applied earlier in the PE recovery.43 Additionally, discrepancies in the ordering of some items on the latent scale suggest that the items were either phrased too generally and not directly associated with PE, or that the items were difficult for the patients to understand. Therefore, advantage is gained from including many items, as the individual items that are problematic have less importance for the overall score. However, the high Cronbach’s Alpha for the internal consistency analysis indicates that there might be some redundancy to the questions, supported by the relatively high AIIC ratios found in this study, also highlighted by other authors.15 16 Therefore, the instrument would benefit from a reduced version where problematic items are removed rather than increasing the number of items.

The IR of the PEmb-QoL was acceptable, with an AIIC of 0.41 for the total score. However, pairwise inter-item correlations were high for certain item pairs, especially for item 4l and 4k and 9d and 9e, indicating possible violations of local independence. These correlations are not unexpected since 4l and 4k both reflect walking ability and 9d and 9e both reflect emotionality.

Test–retest reliability was adequate for most domains, with relative G-coefficients exceeding 0.7. The WP and SL domains showed lower reliability, reflecting the established relationship between limited number of items and domain reliability. Test–retest reliability is generally found to be good to excellent for most domains of the PEmb-QoL.15,2044 45 The only exception found was for the domain SL, which the present study and three other studies found to be moderate19 44 45 compared with five studies15,1820 evaluating test-reliability to be good. Age difference in the different study populations could explain these differences in test–retest reliability.

In general, the domains and total scores showed high consistency and little systematic variation for the included covariates, indicating that the instrument is robust to subgroup variations. However, there were notable subgroup differences in difficulty for item 4b and item 7.

Concurrent validity of PEmb-QoL was supported by strong correlations with established measures, including PVFS, EQ-5D-5L and moderate correlation with GAD-7 and PHQ-9. These findings align with the hypothesised relationships and demonstrate the PEmb-QoL’s ability to measure HRQoL. This study found a higher correlation between the PEmb-QoL and the PVFS than expected based on previous findings.46 One explanation for the difference could be that the previous study administered the questionnaires 21 days after diagnosis, compared with 6 months in the current study. The improved correlation between the two questionnaires at follow-up indicates that an improvement in function lowers the difference in scoring which might be due to significant improvement in function over time.46

The DIF analysis in the present study found some clear differences in responses not explored in previous validation studies on the PEmb-QoL. The sex difference in the perceived effect on limits on daily activities in the home could be due to more traditional-based division of labour in the household. The difference in perceived pain corresponds well with other findings showing that people with unhealthy BMI and cancer survivors have a decreased sensitivity to pain.47 48 The differences in the difficulty with walking between the age groups are also expected as there is a clear decrease in walking distance between groups of people aged 60–69 and 70–79.49

Compared with another disease-specific PROM, for example, the Heart QoL questionnaire,50 the current study showed better internal consistency, which is likely a result of fewer items in the Heart QoL questionnaire compared with PEmb-QoL. On other psychometric properties, the PEmb-QoL and the Heart-QoL show similar convergent validity. However, PEmb-QoL also covers the social and mental impacts of PE compared with only physical and emotional aspects assessed by the Heart QoL, giving PEmb-QoL a wider applicability than the Heart QoL.

This study found the electronic version of the PEmb-QoL to be valid and reliable in a Danish healthcare setting, providing a tool for healthcare practitioners to assess and monitor HRQoL in patients with PE. However, it is important to bear in mind that ongoing symptoms following PE can be complex and, as has been previously shown, the PEmb-QoL does not cover all areas that might be important.13

The electronic version of the PEmb-QoL, investigated in this study, is being implemented in routine PE care in Denmark in the period 2025–2026, as part of a national quality improvement study.23 24 Thus, patients scheduled for outpatient visits in thrombosis clinics as part of a structured follow-up care model in Danish hospitals will receive the questionnaire digitally prior to follow-up consultations. The patient’s reply will be directly integrated into the medical record and is therefore available for the physician to use in the consultation. This model has been tested in a feasibility study, showing that both patients and physicians found good value in the use of PROs as part of routine care.51 Routine collection of PRO-data in clinical practice also allows for monitoring of a patient population and the treatment quality on a national level. There is presently no clinical quality database for this patient population in Denmark, but should this be established, the integration of PEmb-QoL in the medical records systems in all Danish regions will allow for monitoring of HRQoL on a population level.

Strengths and limitations

The study is strengthened by its large sample size and large proportion of complete responses; however, there are limitations. The study was conducted as part of a survey, and while the response rate was high in this context, we cannot declare the study sample to be representative of the entire population. Moreover, no effort was made to assess less literate patients or patients not answering. This may explain the ceiling effect observed, as there may exist an association between low life quality and low response rate. While IRT is the current state of the art methodology for criterion validity with no gold standard, we cannot rule out that there exists differential functioning across the entire scale.

The fact that we found little to no within measurement variance in the test–retest analysis could be attributed to the lack of responsiveness. However, we were not able to investigate responsiveness in the current study. Such analysis is warranted.

Finally, the concurrent validity was conducted employing other PROMs. The evidence of concurrent validity would have been stronger, had we used other forms of measurement, such as rater-based scales of daily functioning.

Conclusion

Despite previously having undergone content validation, the PEmb-QoL measure fails on important criteria for psychometric instruments, for example, some items set limits for other items. These issues are reflected in the DIF and targeting analyses. Additionally, there are considerable ceiling effects for some items, which may indicate that these items are irrelevant for the current population. However, the expected overall score and the concurrent validity did not seem to be compromised by these issues. Thus, it is likely that a reduced version of the electronic Danish PEmb-QoL will possess suitable psychometric properties for measuring HRQoL in patients with PE.
