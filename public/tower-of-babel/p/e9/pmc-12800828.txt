Pacing-induced cardiomyopathy following leadless and transvenous pacemaker implantation: A multicenter retrospective study
Authors: Kevin Lee, Mashaal Ikram, Madhu Reddy, Jason Meyers, Alan Cheng, Kurt Stromberg, Dedra Fagan, Mark Metzl
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPacing-induced cardiomyopathy (PICM) has been reported among patients with transvenous pacemakers (TVP). Recent reports have suggested that the incidence may be lower among patients with leadless pacemakers (LP). We performed a multicenter retrospective study to better define these rates and to identify any potential risk factors.ObjectiveTo determine the rates of PICM between LP and TVP and risk factors for development of PICM in each cohort.MethodsPatients implanted with either a Micra transcatheter LP or TVP between November 2015 and January 2022 were identified from medical records at 3 large medical centers. PICM was defined as a decrease in left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) from pre-implant in patients with a baseline LVEF of ≥50% with a resulting post-implant LVEF ≤40% who received at least 20% ventricular pacing. Logistic regression was used to determine univariate and multivariable predictors of PICM.ResultsA total of 176 patients with LP and 522 patients with TVP implantations were included for analysis. Of these, 6.3% of patients with LP and 7.9% of patients with TVP developed PICM (odds ratio [OR]: 0.78, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.39–1.56, P = .480). Rates of PICM were not significantly different between the 3 centers. Multivariable analysis revealed prolonged paced QRS as a predictor of PICM (OR 1.02 [1.00–1.03], P = .009) and higher pre-implantation LVEF as protective against its development (OR 0.92 [0.88–0.97], P = .001).ConclusionPICM rates are comparable between LP and TVP implantation. Prolonged paced QRS is a predictor for development of PICM and higher pre-implantation LVEF is protective.

Key Findings ▪There is no significant difference in pacing-induced cardiomyopathy (PICM) rates between patients receiving leadless pacemakers and patients receiving transvenous pacemakers.▪Prolonged paced QRS is a predictor of PICM.▪Higher pre-implant left ventricular ejection fraction is protective against the development of PICM.

Introduction

Pacing-induced cardiomyopathy (PICM) is characterized by the deterioration of cardiac function attributed to chronic right ventricular pacing, thought to be due to asynchronous activation of the ventricles.1,2 Specific definitions of PICM vary; in a meta-analysis of 26 studies, Somma et al3 found 15 unique definitions of PICM in patients implanted with transvenous pacemakers (TVP) with a pooled prevalence of 12%, predicted by certain risk factors including comorbidities (history of myocardial infarction [MI], chronic kidney disease, and atrial fibrillation [AF]), pacing characteristics (pacing percentage and paced QRS duration), and pre-implant left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF).

Leadless pacemakers (LP) have been introduced over the past decade as an alternative to TVP in certain patient populations with an indication for pacing.4 They avoid potential complications of TVPs, such as pocket infection, hematoma, lead dislodgement, and lead fracture, and are placed through femoral venous access without the need for a chest incision.5,6 Despite 10 years of worldwide availability, there are limited reports on the prevalence of PICM in patients with LP, and little known about the risk factors associated with PICM development. One single-center study found lower rates of PICM, while another found no difference in rates of PICM between LP and TVP.7,8 Given the limited size and single-center experience, we sought to better understand the rates of PICM across multiple centers over time, and if there are risk factors associated with an increased incidence.

Methods

Patients implanted with a Micra transcatheter LP system (models MC1VR01 or MC1AVR1, Medtronic, Inc) between November 2015 and January 2022 were identified from medical records at the Iowa Heart Center, NorthShore University HealthSystem, and The University of Kansas Health System. Similarly, patients implanted with a TVP were identified from the same 3 centers, implanted between November 2015 and December 2021. Patients with coronary sinus lead implantation and conduction system pacing (CSP) were excluded. Patient demographics, medical history, ventricular pacing percentage, and pre-implant and post-implant LVEF were extracted from medical records. LVEF was measured using modified Simpson’s biplane from transthoracic echocardiograms. Patients who received at least 20% ventricular pacing and had both a pre-implant and post-implant LVEF measurement were included for analysis. We defined PICM as a decrease in LVEF from pre-implant in patients with a baseline LVEF of ≥50% with a resultant post-implant LVEF ≤40% who received at least 20% ventricular pacing.

This study was approved by the institutional review boards (IRB) of the 3 involved institutions (Iowa Heart Center, NorthShore University HealthSystem, and The University of Kansas Health System). The research adhered to ethical guidelines outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Written informed consent was waived by the IRB because of the use of retrospective and deidentified data.

Statistical methods

Baseline and medical history were compared between patients receiving an LP and those receiving a TVP system using either the Fisher exact test (categorical variables) or t-tests or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (continuous variables). Prior to model building, non-parametric LOWESS regression was used to identify the presence of any non-linear associations between the continuous variables (eg, age, paced QRS) and PICM status. Logistic regression was used to determine univariate and multivariable predictors of PICM. Specifically, candidate variables with a univariate association with PICM at the P < .15 level were entered into a multivariable model. The final model which included all variables in the multivariable model significant at the <.05 level was used to predict the rate of PICM for each patient in the study cohort.

As a sensitivity analysis to account for differences in baseline characteristics between patients with leadless and TVPs, propensity score overlap weights were used to derive adjusted PICM rates. To compute the propensity scores, a logistic regression model was used to model the likelihood of receiving a leadless device based on age, sex, AF status, coronary artery disease status, diabetes status, pre-implant LVEF, paced QRS, ventricular pacing percentage, and time between follow-up and baseline echo. Thus, patients missing values for these variables or transvenous patients with echo follow-up time exceeding 72 months (the maximum observed for leadless patients) were excluded. The resulting propensity scores were used to derive the overlap weight for each patient. Overlap weights place more emphasis on patients considered most comparable and less emphasis on patients considered less comparable and induce exact balance on the means of the study population in the variables used to construct the propensity scores. The overlap weights were used in a weighted logistic regression model in this sensitivity analysis to estimate PICM rates for patients with LP and TVP adjusted for the variables listed above.

All analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Cary, North Carolina) or R (www.r-project.org).

Results

A total of 506 patients with LP were identified from medical records. Among them, 324 were paced ≥20% with 270 having a pre-implant LVEF of ≥50%. Of these 270, 176 patients had a post-implant LVEF measurement within a median of 5.3 (interquartile range [IQR]: 1–11) months post-implant (Figure 1A) and were evaluated for PICM. TVPs were implanted in 1295 patients during the data collection period. Of these, 824 received >20% ventricular pacing with 708 patients having a pre-implant LVEF of ≥50%. Since pre-implant LVEF and paced QRS were independent predictors of PICM status, a final logistic regression model was used to quantify the relationship between these 2 variables and the probability of developing PICM. This model included PICM status as the response and pre-implant LVEF and paced QRS as predictors. Figure 2 shows the relationship between paced QRS duration and PICM for patients with baseline LVEF of 50%–60%, >60%–65%, and >65% based on the fitted model. Notably, within both pacemaker types, the risk for PICM increased with increasing paced QRS width (OR: 1.02; 95% CI: 1.01–1.03, P = .003) with most PICM events associated with paced QRS exceeding 150 ms. Additionally, PICM rates decreased with increasing pre-implant LVEF for both device types (OR: 0.92; 95% CI: 0.88–0.96, P < .001). Discussion

PICM rates

Our analysis found a 6.3% PICM rate in patients with LPs and a 7.9% PICM rate in patients with TVPs across 3 study centers, which was not significantly different on a univariate (P = .481) or multivariable basis (P = .420) or in a sensitivity analysis using a propensity score adjusted cohort (P = .836). There was no significant difference in rates of PICM between the study centers.

Single-center reports of PICM in LP populations have varied widely, being as low as 3% and ranging from 4% up to 16.5% depending on implant location, and up to 44% at certain centers.7, 8, 9, 10 Sanchez et al7 noted a significantly lower rate of PICM with LP (3%) compared with TVP (13.7%). In contrast, Saeed Al-Asad et al8 noted similar rates of PICM between LP (44%) and TVP (37%), but at drastically higher rates. Given our larger sample size and multicenter patient population, we suspect that our overall rates of PICM are closer to overall incidence.

Differences in PICM rates can also be attributed in part to differences in methodology and definitions of PICM. We defined PICM as patients with an initial normal LVEF (≥50%) with the development of reduced LVEF (≤40%), in order to capture the most clinically relevant LVEF decreases that would require changes in management with increased risks of morbidity and mortality.11,12 We also only required a 20% ventricular pacing burden, as it has been shown that PICM can occur with lower rates of ventricular pacing.13

Risks for development of PICM

In a multivariable model of pacemaker type, sex, baseline HF status, pre-implant QRS duration, paced QRS duration, and pre-implant LVEF, both paced QRS duration and pre-implant LVEF were independent predictors of PICM, with an OR of 1.02 and 0.92, respectively. These results are concordant with studies in TVP, such as Khurshid et al,13 which reported that a longer paced QRS duration was associated with a multivariate OR of 1.34 per 10 ms increase in paced QRS duration.

We show no difference between the 2 types of pacemakers, with similar multivariable risk factors of longer paced QRS duration and lower pre-implant LVEF for the development of PICM.

Avoidance of PICM

For patients with an LVEF <65%, a cutoff of a paced QRS duration >150 ms determined a higher risk of PICM (Figure 2). Similarly, the heatmap in Figure 3 shows rates of PICM based on paced QRS duration and pre-implant LVEF, with patients with lower pre-implant LVEF and higher paced QRS duration with the highest rates of PICM development. This data suggests that operators can determine the optimal paced QRS duration based on pre-implant LVEF to minimize the rates of PICM.

CSP and cardiac resynchronization therapy

Patients that are believed to be at high risk for development of PICM may benefit from an alternative pacing methodology, such as CSP or CRT. Multicenter findings from Sharma et al14 have shown significant decreases in QRS duration and improvement in LVEF with CSP for patients who have developed PICM. Similarly, Khurshid et al13 showed reversal of PICM and improvement in LVEF with CRT upgrade. However, patients who are undergoing LP may have contraindications for the implantation of pacemaker leads required for CSP and CRT, such as active infection or significant tricuspid regurgitation. In patients at risk for PICM and relative or absolute contraindication for a TVP, our findings suggest a benefit at the time of implantation to obtaining a paced QRS duration <150 sec.

Our study showed that, overall, fewer patients with LP underwent CRT upgrade compared to TVP, possibly due to patient selection of LP, including patients in whom TVPs were contraindicated. This finding is consistent with low reports of reintervention in the Medicare population.15

Limitations and future directions

Although the present study provides valuable insights, certain limitations need consideration. The relatively small number of PICM events, particularly in patients with LP, may limit the generalizability of the findings. Although there was no significant difference in PICM rates between patients with LP and TVP in our study, there were significant differences in baseline characteristics of the 2 groups, as patients with LP were more likely to have AF, had a lower pre-implant LVEF, and had lower rates of ventricular pacing than their TVP counterparts in our cohort. However, sensitivity analysis also showed no significant difference in PICM rates when propensity score overlap weighting were used to balance the 2 groups. Another difference was the longer follow-up in the TVP group (median 19.8 months) vs the LP group (median 5.3 months). Given that PICM can develop 2–4 years after pacemaker implantation, we may be underestimating the incidence of PICM in our LP group when compared to the TVP group.9

This was also a retrospective analysis and inherent biases for which patients were selected for which device exist. There was also inherent bias on which patients received pre-implant and post-implant transthoracic echocardiograms, as there was no standard protocol at the study centers, resulting in differences in long-term follow-up. The indications for pacing, patients’ functional status, and symptoms related to PICM were also not reported.

Conclusion

Our study is the first multicenter report comparing the incidence of PICM in patients with both LP and TVP. We observed a 6.3% PICM rate in patients with LPs and a 7.9% PICM rate in patients with TVPs across 3 study centers. On an adjusted basis PICM rates were 7.7% for patients with LP and 6.9% for patients with TVP after accounting for differences in comorbidities and follow-up time between groups. There were no significant difference in rates between the 2 pacemaker types on an unadjusted or adjusted basis. Risks for the development of PICM include lower pre-implant LVEF and higher paced QRS duration.
