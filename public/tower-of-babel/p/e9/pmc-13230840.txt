Relationship between facet joint osteoarthritis and multifidus fat infiltration in patients with lumbar spondylolisthesis
Authors: Shiyong Wang, Xiaojin Wu, Hong Ma, Haitao Hu, Rudong Chen, Honglai Zhang, Zemin Wang, Wanzhong Yang, Rong Ma, Kaishun Yang, Zhaohui Ge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Facet joint osteoarthritis (FJOA) represents an early and prevalent indicator of lumbar degeneration, potentially impacting the adjacent multifidus muscle through mechanical and inflammatory mechanisms. Nevertheless, the association between FJOA and multifidus fat infiltration (FI) in the context of spondylolisthesis remains inadequately understood. This study retrospectively analyzed 243 patients diagnosed with either isthmic or degenerative lumbar spondylolisthesis (L2–S1) from two specialized spine centers. The severity of FJOA was assessed using Weishaupt’s criteria. Parameters of the multifidus muscle, including FI, total muscle cross-sectional area (TMCSA), functional cross-sectional area (fCSA), and muscle height index (Muscle HI), were quantified utilizing ImageJ software. Spearman correlation and multivariate regression analyses were conducted to explore the relationships between FJOA and muscle parameters, adjusting for confounding variables such as age, sex, body mass index (BMI), and radiographic factors (including disc degeneration, stenosis, slippage level, and Meyerding classification etc.). The study found a significant increase in multifidus FI associated with advancing lumbar FJOA at the L2/3, L3/4, and L5/S1 levels (correlation coefficient r = 0.29–0.47, P < 0.001), which was accompanied by a decrease in fCSA (P < 0.05). FJOA emerged as an independent predictor of multifidus FI at L2/3 (β = 0.32–0.40, P < 0.001), L3/4 (β = 0.15–0.16, P < 0.05), and L5/S1 (β = 0.22–0.23, P < 0.001), whereas disc degeneration, central canal stenosis, and foraminal stenosis did not significantly contribute to multifidus FI. Additionally, age and female sex were consistently identified as positive predictors of FI (P ≤ 0.001). Although the association was less pronounced at the L4/5 level, a positive correlation was observed between the Meyerding grade and multifidus fat infiltration (P < 0.01). Lumbar FJOA is significantly and independently correlated with multifidus FI and a decrease in fCAS, a relationship that persists even in patients with spondylolisthesis. Conversely, BMI, disc degeneration, and central canal or foraminal stenosis did not exhibit a significant association with multifidus fat infiltration.

Introduction

Lumbar facet joint osteoarthritis (FJOA) represents a significant aspect of zygapophyseal joint degeneration1. Evidence indicates that facet degeneration may occur early, sometimes even preceding intervertebral disc degeneration, and can be present during adolescence2. Community-based studies suggest that approximately 10–20% of spinal degenerative changes may originate from the facet joints3. The pathological process of FJOA extends beyond the articular cartilage and subchondral bone, involving structural remodeling of the facet capsule with neovascularization, inflammatory cell infiltration, and nociceptive neuropeptide enrichment4. Mechanical loading can readily compromise the integrity of the capsule, allowing inflammatory mediators to diffuse into the perispinal microenvironment and potentially affect adjacent structures5.

The multifidus muscle serves as the primary deep stabilizer of the lumbar spine6. Increasing evidence indicates that multifidus atrophy and fat infiltration (FI) are associated with spinal instability6,7, deformity8, adjacent-segment degeneration9, implant failure10, and sagittal imbalance11. Multiple factors contribute to paraspinal muscle FI, including age, sex, neural compression, local inflammation, spinal stenosis, disc degeneration, and facet degeneration12–20. Notably, the facet joint is anatomically and functionally closely associated with the multifidus; its deep fibers lie in direct proximity to the facet capsule6,21. This spatial relationship suggests the hypothesis that facet joint pathology may influence the composition of the multifidus muscle through mechanical coupling or paracrine signaling mechanisms.

Indahl et al.22 reported that intra-facet saline injection inhibited multifidus muscle activation, indicating potential neuromuscular modulation originating from the facet joint. Nevertheless, existing clinical studies have yielded inconsistent findings regarding the relationship between FJOA and multifidus FI12,18,23. These inconsistencies are likely due to heterogeneity in study design and the common focus on a single lumbar level, typically L4/5. Hodges et al.24 suggested that alterations in the multifidus muscle along the lumbar spine are not uniform, and averaging data across different levels may obscure segment-specific biological associations. Therefore, further investigations stratified by disease subtype and different lumbar segment are warranted.

Lumbar spondylolisthesis represents a distinct subtype of degenerative lumbar disease, characterized by unique segment anatomical and biomechanical changes that differ from those observed in isolated disc degeneration or lumbar spinal stenosis. However, whether an association between FJOA and multifidus FI exists within this distinct pathological context has not yet been systematically investigated. To address this research gap, the study retrospectively analyzed surgical patients with lumbar spondylolisthesis and examined the association between FJOA severity and multifidus FI across multiple lumbar levels. This investigation aims to elucidate segment-specific facet–muscle relationships in lumbar spondylolisthesis and inform earlier clinical evaluation.

Methods

Study design

This dual-center retrospective study included patients who underwent lumbar surgery for symptomatic lumbar spondylolisthesis from January 2021 to December 2022. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Ningxia Medical University General Hospital (approval No. KYLL-2025-2398) and the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Dali University (approval No. DFY20250528001). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. Due to the retrospective design of the study and the use of anonymized patient data, the requirement for informed consent was waived by both institutional ethics committees.

Sample size estimation

Sample size estimation was conducted using G*Power 3.1.97 (Heinrich-Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany; https://www.gpower.hhu.de)25. Based on an F-test for a fixed-effects multiple linear regression model, with an assumed effect size of f2 = 0.15, α = 0.05, statistical power (1 − β) = 0.90, and 10 predictor variables, determined that a minimum sample size of approximately 147 participants was necessary.

Data collection

Inclusion criteria: (1) Patients with a confirmed diagnosis of lumbar spondylolisthesis; (2) Both degenerative and isthmic types of spondylolisthesis were included; (3) Complete and high-quality imaging data available (computed tomography, CT and magnetic resonance imaging, MRI); (4) Age ≥ 20 years. Exclusion criteria: (1) Incomplete or poor-quality MRI or CT data; (2) Presence of spinal deformities, including scoliosis (Cobb angle ≥ 20°), lumbar kyphosis, or traumatic spondylolisthesis; (3) History of prior spinal interventions; (4)Systemic disorders (e.g., ankylosing spondylitis, metabolic diseases); (5) Upper motor neuron diseases, including spinal cord tumors or cerebral palsy; (6) Duplicate cases or overlapping records (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Strobe diagram for the study.

Strobe diagram for the study.

Clinical parameters

Demographic data, including sex, age, height, weight, and body mass index (BMI), were extracted from the electronic medical record system.

Radiographic parameters

Lumbar CT (Siemens, 64-slice, Germany) and MRI (1.5 Tesla, GE Healthcare) scans were obtained for all participants. The parameters evaluated included Pfirrmann grading of the intervertebral discs from L2 to S126, lumbar central canal stenosis (LCCS) grading27, foraminal stenosis grading28, as well as the slippage level, type, and Meyerding classification29. These imaging parameters have all demonstrated acceptable inter-rater and intra-rater reliability in previous studies. Due to the absence of axial MRI images at the L1/2 level in most cases, this segment was excluded from the analysis. To maintain consistency, measurements were performed at the axial plane parallel to the superior endplate of the lower vertebral body within each motion segment, as this level provides optimal imaging consistency and a larger facet joint dimension, enabling more reliable identification and comparison of facet degeneration on both CT and MRI30.

Assessment of facet joint degeneration

Facet joint degeneration was assessed using the Weishaupt classification31, which ranges from 0 to 3 (Fig. 2). Previous research has indicated that both CT and MRI assessments exhibit moderate accuracy and reliability compared with histopathological findings, while the combined use of CT and MRI enhances diagnostic consistency beyond the capabilities of either modality alone32.Fig. 2Combined CT (A1–D1) and MRI (A2–D2) evaluation of lumbar facet joint degeneration (grades 0–3).

Combined CT (A1–D1) and MRI (A2–D2) evaluation of lumbar facet joint degeneration (grades 0–3).

In this study, facet joint degeneration was graded by a spine surgeon with 6 years of experience in spinal imaging assessment, who was blinded to clinical information and other imaging assessments, and bilateral measurements were performed separately at each lumbar segment. The evaluation process consisted of two independent grading sessions: the examiner first reviewed the Weishaupt criteria and completed the initial grading based on both CT and MRI images, and then repeated the grading 30 days later. Any discrepancies between the two sessions were re-evaluated, and the final consensus grade was determined after review.

Measurement of multifidus cross-sectional area and fat infiltration

All quantitative measurements of the multifidus muscle were performed using Image J software (version 1.54f., NIH, USA; http://imagej.org). The threshold-based segmentation method, which distinguishes tissues according to signal intensity differences, was applied to quantify the muscle and fat components33. The reliability of this method has been previously validated34.

Using pseudo-color segmentation (Fig. 3), both the fat infiltration percentage (FI%) and the total muscle cross-sectional area (TMCSA) were obtained. The measurement procedure followed the standardized protocol recommended by Hodges et al.24, and was applied consistently across all lumbar levels and both sides. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.0.1.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\overline{\mathrm{x}}}$$\end{document} ± SD, and categorical variables as percentages. The correlation between facet joint degeneration grade and multifidus fat infiltration (FI) was assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation. To identify independent predictors of multifidus fat infiltration, multivariate linear regression analysis was performed, incorporating potential confounding factors. When the assumptions of normality were not met, a generalized linear model was used instead. For reliability analysis, intra- and interobserver agreement for continuous data was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) based on a two-way random-effects model with absolute agreement, while ordinal data were analyzed using Cohen’s weighted kappa. All tests were two-tailed, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Reliability analysis

To assess measurement reliability, 30 randomly selected patients were analyzed. The intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) for multifidus cross-sectional area measurements demonstrated excellent reliability, ranging from 0.88 to 0.97 on the left side and 0.90 to 0.95 on the right side. For categorical variables, Cohen’s weighted kappa (κ) indicated good to excellent agreement across parameters: Degree of spondylolisthesis 0.90; Disc Pfirrmann grading 0.72–0.89; Central canal stenosis 0.81–0.87; Foraminal stenosis: left 0.77–0.80, right 0.80–1.00; Facet joint degeneration: left 0.66–0.76; right 0.67–0.72; These results confirm high intra- and interobserver reliability for all imaging measurements.

A total of 243 patients were included in the final analysis. The number of analyzed intervertebral levels was L2/3: 168, L3/4: 234, L4/5: 243, and L5/S1: 234. Among them, 175 patients (72%) were female, with a mean age of 55.44 ± 9.12 years and a mean BMI of 23.62 ± 3.69 kg/m2. The L4 and L5 levels accounted for the highest proportions of spondylolisthesis (60.9% and 34.2%, respectively). Degenerative spondylolisthesis was observed in 60.9% of patients, and Grade I slippage in 80.2% (Table 1). Trend of multifidus FI across FJOA

At the L2/3 level, the FI of the multifidus increased progressively with the severity of FJOA, showing a marked rise from grade 0 to grade 3 on both sides (Fig. 5A–A′). A similar upward trend was observed at L3/4 and L5/S1 (Fig. 5B–B′, D–D′), where the transition from grade 0 to grade 1 represented the most pronounced increase. In contrast, at the L4/5 level (Fig. 5C–C′), the increase in FI tended to plateau across degeneration grades, suggesting a less consistent relationship between FJOA and multifidus FI. Multiple linear regression analysis

The results of the multiple linear regression analysis (Table 4) showed that, across all analyzed lumbar levels, age and female sex were significant positive predictors of multifidus fat infiltration (P ≤ 0.001). At the L2/3 level, FJOA emerged as an independent positive predictor of fat infiltration (β ≈ 0.32–0.40, P < 0.001). At L3/4, both FJOA (β ≈ 0.15–0.16, P ≈ 0.01) and Meyerding of slippage (β ≈ 0.21, P = 0.001) were significant positive predictors, whereas LCCS was identified as a negative predictor (P ≤ 0.05). At the L4/5 level, the Meyerding of slippage was positively correlated with fat infiltration (P < 0.01), while FJOA on the left side showed a weak negative association (P = 0.046). At the L5/S1 level, both FJOA (β ≈ 0.22–0.23, P < 0.001) and location of the slipped vertebra (P < 0.01) were the main independent positive predictors of multifidus FI. Discussion

This study systematically evaluated the relationship between lumbar degenerative structures and multifidus fat infiltration (FI), and found that facet joint osteoarthritis (FJOA) was significantly and independently associated with multifidus FI, even in patients with spondylolisthesis. Conversely, no significant associations were found between multifidus FI and disc degeneration, central canal stenosis, or foraminal stenosis, suggesting that the facet joint may serve as a more pertinent structural correlate of multifidus muscle degeneration in this.

Anatomically, the multifidus muscle is situated in close proximity to and attaches directly to the facet joint capsule, sharing its vascular and neural supply. Emerging evidence from human histopathology and molecular profiling studies indicates that degenerative facet capsule tissue exhibits inflammatory cytokine upregulation, neovascularization, nociceptive neuropeptide enrichment, and synovial remodeling4,5. Friedrich et al.36 reported that facet joint edema was positively associated with low back pain, with fat-suppressed MRI demonstrating marked edema in the tissues surrounding the facet joint. Arthrographic findings indicated that in non-degenerated lumbar facet joints, the joint contour was preserved without evidence of contrast leakage into adjacent tissues. Conversely, in degenerated facet joints, contrast medium showed irregular extravasation into the pericapsular region and could extend through the Okada gap to the contralateral facet joint space37. These findings may support a biologically plausible mechanism for paracrine inflammatory signaling to permeate the multifidus–facet microenvironment. Therefore, degenerative changes in the facet joint may be associated with muscle alterations through inflammatory, biomechanical, or neuromuscular pathways. Our study identified significant positive correlations between FJOA and multifidus FI at L2/3, L3/4, and L5/S1 levels, with degeneration severity independently predicting reductions in the fCSA. These findings support the hypothesis an interactive relationship between the facet joint and muscle, rather than a mere coincidental degenerative co-progression. Our results align with those of Guven et al.12, who reported facet degeneration as an independent predictor of multifidus fatty atrophy, even after controlling for age, sex, and BMI. Notably, this association was not observed at L4/5, where a weak negative correlation was noted on the left side, differing from the findings of Liu et al.20. The L4/5 level, being the apex of lumbar lordosis and subject to maximal shear load transfer, is the predominantnsite for spondylolisthesis in the lumbar spine; Consequently, local loading characteristics at this level may overshadow or obscure facet-mediated effects, resulting in a distinct microenvironmental degenerative pattern. Due to its distinctive segmental biomechanics, the L4/5 level may exhibit a spectrum of muscle adaptive responses that differs from those observed at the superior and inferior lumbar levels. This segmental variability supports the assertion by Hodges et al.24 that analyses single-level may not adequately capture the overall degenerative trends of the lumbar spine.

Clinically, these findings underscore the necessity of evaluating both facet joint degeneration and the quality of multifidus muscle when assessing lumbar spine pathology. The severity of FJOA may serve as an imaging marker indicative of multifidus FI replacement. Interventions aimed at preserving facet joint integrity or reducing mechanical load may potentially delay the degeneration of the multifidus muscle and its associated biomechanical consequences. Future research should aim to elucidate whether facet joint degeneration directly induces muscle fat infiltration through inflammatory or neuromuscular signaling pathways, or whether both processes share a common etiologic mechanism. Mechanistic substudies incorporating facet capsule cytokine quantification, neuromuscular activation mapping, or capsule–muscle spatial transcriptomics–building upon previous work on inflammatory capsule profiling4,5–would enhance our understanding of the underlying biological processes.

Age and sex are independent determinants of paraspinal muscle quality. Aging is associated with paraspinal atrophy and fat infiltration14,20,38, and women exhibit greater multifidus fat infiltration compared to men14,20,39. In this study, age and sex were correlated with increased multifidus FI and fCSA across lumbar segments, indicating a systemic susceptibility. However, multifidus fat infiltration is predominantly localized in the deep layers adjacent to the facet joints and laminae. This distribution pattern cannot be entirely attributed to age or sex differences, suggesting that local structural factors may significantly contribute to the promotion of multifidus FI.

The relationship between LCCS and paraspinal muscle degeneration remains a subject of debate. Some researchers propose a vicious cycle between lumbar canal stenosis and muscle atrophy16,40, yet there is no high-quality evidence supporting a direct link with pain severity or stenosis grade40. Conversely, others studies argue that atrophy is not directly associated with the degree of denervation15. In our multivariate analysis, we found no significant correlation between LCCS and multifidus FI, indicating that neural compression–mediated denervation is unlikely to be the predominant mechanism in this cohort. Theoretically, foraminal stenosis could induce denervation of the medial branch and consequently lead to paraspinal muscle degeneration at the same23 or adjacent41 spinal levels. However, our findings did not reveal a significant association between foraminal stenosis and multifidus FI. Given that most cases in this cohort exhibited mild (grade 0–2) stenosis without definitive neural compression, these results further imply that foraminal stenosis has only a minimal impact on multifidus degeneration. Therefore, isolated central canal or foraminal stenosis alone may not sufficiently explain the observed degree of multifidus fat infiltration.

Previous studies have also reported an association between lumbar disc degeneration and multifidus FI17–19,38. Although disc injury has been demonstrated to induce local multifidus atrophy in animal models42, histologic data indicate that acute disc herniation alone does not cause substantial muscle degeneration within a short time window43. In alignment with these observations, our study found no significant correlation between disc Pfirrmann grade and multifidus FI, even at the most severely degenerated L4/5 and L5/S1 levels. These results imply that, in chronic degenerative conditions such as spondylolisthesis, disc degeneration may not be the primary contributor to multifidus FI. Although disc-related effects cannot be entirely ruled out, their influence may be more indirect compared to that of facet joint osteoarthritis.

Interestingly, the Meyerding classification of spondylolisthesis showed a weak but consistent positive association with multifidus FI at the L3/4–L5/S1 levels, implying that altered spinal alignment and sagittal imbalance may contribute to reduce muscle quality. However, this effect was relatively minor in multivariate analysis, suggesting that vertebral slippage is likely a secondary mechanical factor rather than a primary determinant of multifidus degeneration.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. Firstly, the assessment of facet joint osteoarthritis was conducted using imaging techniques rather than histopathological analysis, which may lead to an underestimation of the true severity of degeneration. Secondly, the cross-sectional design of the study limits the ability to infer causality; thus, the observed relationships represent associations rather than direct causative links. Despite these limitations, they highlight potential directions for future mechanistic validation. Subsequent studies should incorporate targeted mechanistic approaches, such as cytokine profiling of facet capsule tissue and spatial transcriptomics of capsule-muscle interactions, to directly investigate whether facet degeneration biologically initiates remodeling of the multifidus muscle. Finally, muscle degeneration and fat infiltration are influenced by multiple factors, including diabetes, alcohol consumption, and occupational physical workload et al.. Further in-depth investigations, particularly longitudinal cohort studies, are required to elucidate the individual contributions of these factors. In addition, the present study included only hospitalized patients with lumbar spondylolisthesis, which may introduce selection bias and limit the representativeness of the study population. Future studies employing prospective, multicenter designs with larger cohorts are warranted to validate and extend the present findings.

Conclusion

Lumbar facet joint osteoarthritis (FJOA) is significantly and independently correlated with multifidus muscle fat infiltration and a decrease in functional cross-sectional area, a relationship that remains evident even in individuals with spondylolisthesis. Conversely, BMI, disc degeneration, central canal and foraminal stenosis did not exhibit a significant association with multifidus fat infiltration. These findings indicate that FJOA may serve as a critical structural correlate of multifidus muscle deterioration, rather than merely a coincidental degenerative change.
