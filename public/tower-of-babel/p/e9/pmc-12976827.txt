Effects of gonadectomy on brain sex hormone levels and amyloid pathology in male and female App NL‐G‐F and App NL‐F mice
Authors: Patricia Muñoz, Heba G. Ali, Aphrodite Demetriou, Maria Latorre‐Leal, Makoto Shimozawa, Ljerka Delac, Tudor‐Fabian Troncea‐Sandu, Jose Inzunza, Per Nilsson, Silvia Maioli, Ivan Nalvarte
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMore women than men are diagnosed with Alzheimer's disease (AD). Sex hormones have been ascribed neuroprotective properties, and their decline, particularly the reduction of estrogen during menopause, has been implicated in AD risk. In this study, we examined how loss of circulating sex hormones affects cognitive performance and amyloid pathology in two mouse models of AD, the aggressive App NL‐G‐F and the slower App NL‐F models of brain amyloidosis. Bilateral gonadectomy was induced in both male and female App NL‐G‐F and App NL‐F mice. Pathology was assessed using cognitive tests and histological evaluations of amyloid depositions and neuroinflammation. Serum and brain estrogen and testosterone levels were measured by ELISA, and the expression of key estrogenic signaling genes was evaluated using qPCR. We report that female gonadectomy had little impact on behavior or pathology in the App NL‐G‐F model, whereas male gonadectomy improved learning and reduced hippocampal amyloid depositions. In the App NL‐F model, gonadectomy worsened amyloid pathology in both sexes. Hormone analysis revealed that significant levels of estrogen in females, but not testosterone in males, remain partly preserved in the brain after gonadectomy, and that low testosterone levels associate with increased insulin‐like growth factor 1 (IGF‐1) expression which may play a compensatory role in maintaining estrogenic signaling. Our study provides new insights into how the loss of circulating sex hormones influences brain sex hormone levels and AD pathology and contributes to a better understanding of the sex differences observed in this disease.

INTRODUCTION

More women than men are diagnosed with Alzheimer's disease (AD), and the prevalence of AD is higher for women throughout the aging spectrum, suggesting that other factors in addition to age contribute to women's AD risk. 1 The female sex hormone estrogen (E2, 17β‐estradiol) has been ascribed potent neuroprotective properties, and its loss at menopause is suggested to contribute to the sex difference in AD risk. 2 , 3 , 4 Similarly, bilateral oophorectomy, especially if performed at younger ages, has been associated with increased risk of dementia. 5 , 6 In aging males, testosterone is decreased in a less drastic manner, and it is considered that a significant portion of testosterone can be converted to E2 in the brain through aromatization by the aromatase (CYP19A1) enzyme. 7 , 8 Beyond peripheral tissues, sex hormone levels can also be produced locally in the brain through in situ steroidogenesis, by the cholesterol side‐chain cleavage enzyme (CYP11A1), catalyzing the conversion of cholesterol to pregnenolone, the first step of this process. 9 Although emerging evidence suggests that sex hormones play important roles in brain function and neuroprotection, there is little knowledge on the sex differences by which sex hormones elicit neuroprotection and how much of E2 or testosterone is left in the brain during normal aging or after their systemic depletion by removal of the gonads (gonadectomy).

In this study, we compare the effects of gonadectomy on brain amyloid beta (Aβ) deposition and cognition in two mouse models of AD, the App NL‐G‐F and the App NL‐F models. The App NL‐G‐F model displays an aggressive amyloid pathology and neuroinflammation with cognitive decline starting from 6 months of age, while the App NL‐F model has slower AD pathogenesis with cognitive decline at 18 months of age. 10 Using these two models, we compare the effect of male and female gonadectomy at an early adult stage with respect to amyloid pathology, neuroinflammation, and maintenance of brain sex hormone levels at 6 months or 18 months of age in App NL‐G‐F and App NL‐F mice, respectively. We show that removal of systemic E2 or testosterone at 2.5 months of age results in surprisingly mild effects on pathology in 6 months old App NL‐G‐F mice, while gonadectomy at the same age in the App NL‐F model displays memory deficits and aggravated amyloid pathology at 18 months. We also provide evidence that E2 levels are maintained in the brain at a low basal level upon ovariectomy, and that a portion of circulating testosterone may be converted to E2 in aged male mice. This study provides a new understanding of how sex hormones are maintained in the brain and how they contribute to the sex differences observed in AD.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

Male and female App NL‐G‐F and App NL‐F knock‐in mice (carrying the Swedish [NL], Iberian [F], and/or Arctic [G] mutations in the humanized Aβ peptide 10 ) were obtained from local breeding using the C57BL/6J strain background. At 2.5 months of age, male and female mice were randomly selected for gonadectomy, bilateral ovariectomy in females and removal of testes and epididymides in males, or sham surgery. The sham‐operated mice underwent the same procedure, but without removal of gonads. Wild‐type (WT) C57BL/6J mice without any surgery were included as reference. At 6 or 18 months of age, App NL‐G‐F and App NL‐F mice were subjected to behavioral tests and then sacrificed. For brain dissection, animals were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane followed by intracardial ice‐cold 0.1M PBS perfusion. Prior to perfusion, cardiac puncture of the right ventricle with a syringe was performed to collect approximately 200–300 μL blood into serum gel tubes (Multivette 600 Serum Gel tubes, Sarstedt, #15.1674). Half of the brain was fixed in cold 4% paraformaldehyde and the hippocampus, cerebral cortex, and rest of the other half (excluding cerebellum) were snap‐frozen for biochemical assays. All procedures were performed in accordance with approved ethical permits (ethical approval ID 407 and ID 2199‐2021, Linköping's animal ethical board).

Behavioral tests

Y‐maze

Hippocampal‐dependent spatial working memory and reference memory were analyzed using the standard Y‐maze test. The Y‐maze consisted of 3 arms (35 × 7 × 15 cm, made of non‐reflective gray plastic, Noldus Wageningen, the Netherlands) at a 120° angle to each other. A random mouse from each test group was placed in the center of the maze, and the 5 min trial started when the experimenter was out of the room of the maze to allow uninterrupted movement of the animal. Automated recording (using EthoVision XT, Noldus, Wageningen, the Netherlands) of the number of entries into each arm was used to calculate the percent spontaneous alterations. Alternations were considered completed when a mouse performed successive entries into three different arms. The threshold for the number of arm visits for data to be included was set to 10, and the threshold for considering an arm entry was set to when at least half of the mouse's body (excluding the tail) crossed the arm entry border. Percentage alternations were calculated as [total alterations/(# arm entries – 2)]. The Y‐maze was cleaned with 70% ethanol before each individual mouse test.

Contextual fear conditioning

A conditioning semi‐transparent plexiglass chamber of 17 × 17 × 25 cm (l × w × h) with a stainless‐steel grid floor (grid spaced 0.5 cm apart, Ugo Basile, Gemonio, Italy) surrounded by sound‐attenuating gray chest was used for training and conditioning tests under a constant light (50 Lux) and background white noise (77 db). The chest was fitted with a light‐sensitive camera over the chamber. The chamber was cleaned with 70% ethanol before each individual mouse test. The contextual fear conditioning test was performed over a span of 3 days, as previously described. 11 Briefly, on the conditioning day, mice were individually and randomly placed in the chamber and allowed to explore for 2 min before the onset of the conditional stimuli in the form of two sound exposures (65 db, 2000 Hz) 1 min apart lasting for 30 s each. During the last 2 s of each conditioning stimulus, the mice received a mild electric foot shock (0.5 mA). The conditioning ended 1 min after the last shock. The next day, the mice were subjected to the contextual test, where they were placed back in the same chamber (context A) for 3 min, but were not subjected to any sound stimuli or foot shock. On the third day, the mice were subjected to the cued test in which they were placed back in the chamber that had been fitted with different environment (checkered wall patterns and white bottom, context B). The mice were free to explore the chamber for 2 min (baseline) before the onset of the sound stimuli but without any foot shock (cue tone, 65 db, 2000 Hz) for the remaining 2 min (cue test). Mouse movement was traced by a computer‐based video tracking system (ANY‐Maze 6.3 software, Stoelting, Dublin, Ireland). The freezing response was defined as the percentage of time a mouse remained motionless (divided into 30 s intervals). Mice that had ≥80% baseline freezing in context B of the fear conditioning cue test were removed from the test.

Immunostaining

4 μm thick paraffin‐embedded sagittal mouse brain sections were fixed on glass slides, hydrated, followed by heat‐induced antigen retrieval in 10 mM sodium citrate solution (pH 6.0) at 121°C for 20 min using a programmable pressure steamer (Biocare Medical, Pacheco, CA, USA). Tissues were permeabilized for 15 min with 0.5% Triton‐X 100 (Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA) and blocked using 10% Horse Serum (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), 0.1% Tween‐20 (Millipore) in 0.1 M PBS for 1 h at 37°C. Following blocking the slides were immunostained over‐night at 4°C with antibodies specific to Aβ (1:2000 dilution, 82E1, IBL‐Tecan, Männedorf, Switzerland), Iba1 (1:300, ab178846, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), and GFAP (1:300, GA5 Alexa Fluor488‐labeled, Millipore). For antibodies raised in mice, we used 1x mouse‐on‐mouse IgG blocking solution (ThermoFisher Scientific) prior to antibody incubation. Secondary antibodies were Alexa Fluor488 and Alexa Fluor568 (both from ThemoFisher Scientific). To reduce autofluorescence, the sections were incubated in 1 mM CuSO4 diluted in 50 mM ammonium acetate for 15 min. Nuclei were stained with 300 nM DAPI (ThermoFischer Scientific) for 10 min, prior to mounting. ABC‐horseradish peroxidase (HRP) kit, Vector Red Alkaline Phosphatase Substrate kit, and Impact‐diaminobenzidine (DAB) (all from Vector Laboratories, Newark, CA, USA) were used for immunohistochemical staining according to manufacturer's recommendations. Immunofluorescence images were captured using an AxioPlan‐2 fluorescent microscope (Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) and Nikon Eclipse E800 (Nikon, Amstelveen, The Netherlands). Image analysis was performed on at least 3 sections per mouse using the ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA) and QuPath open source software 12 and image setting threshold and quantifications were as described previously according to software's own object detection and classification protocols. 13 In brief, regions of interest (ROIs) were defined, and color estimation (deconvolution) was adjusted to separate the colors (e.g., red and brown staining). Cell/object detection was done by defining appropriate parameters (size, intensity thresholds, and separation) to extract object measurements (e.g., mean DAB intensity, area, and circularity). Using a set of annotated examples, a classifier was trained in QuPath to automatically label objects as positive or negative (e.g., Iba1 or Aβ depending on the channel/marker). Finally, the quantification was obtained as the number/density of positive objects and/or positive area within each ROI. For each acquired image, the image lookup table (LUT) was kept linear and covered the whole image data.

Sex hormone measurements

Collected blood from the mice was centrifuged at 10000 × g for 5 min at +4°C. Supernatant containing serum was kept at −80°C. Brain extracts were obtained by homogenization of whole left brain hemisphere (pre‐weighed) in 50 mM Tris–HCl, 150 mM NaCl (1 × TBS) buffer, supplemented with 1 × protease inhibitor cocktail (Merck‐Millipore, #11836153001), using a bead homogenizer (MP FastPrep24, MP Biomedicals, Irvine, CA, USA). The serum and the brain extract samples were diluted 4‐fold in methanol, mixed and incubated at room temperature for 10 min under agitation, followed by centrifugation at 2000 × g for 5 min. Supernatant was transferred to a new tube and evaporated to dryness, followed by reconstitution in ELISA assay buffer according to manufacturer's recommendations (Cayman Chemical, Ann Arbor, MI, USA). Free estrogen and free testosterone were assayed using the estradiol and testosterone competitive ELISA kits according to manufacturer's instructions (Cayman Chemical, assay# 501890 and #582701, respectively), taking dilution factors into consideration. Percent coefficient of variation (%CV) for E2 measurements is listed in Supplemental Table S2.

Real‐time qPCR analysis

Total RNA from cells or tissue was extracted using the RNeasy plus mini kit, RNeasy plus micro kit or Allprep DNA/RNA kit (Qiagen) according to manufacturer's instructions, and RNA concentrations and quality were determined with NanoDrop (ThermoFisher Scientific). Complementary DNA was synthesized using SuperScript IV VILO Master Mix cDNA synthesis kit (ThermoFisher Scientific). PCRs were performed on cDNA samples using TaqMan Gene Expression assays and TaqMan Fast Advanced Master Mix (Applied Biosystems; #4444556) or and KAPA SYBR Fast qPCR master mix (Sigma‐Aldrich; #SF1UKB) according to the manufacturer's protocols. All primers and TaqMan assays used in our experiments are listed in Supplementary Table I. TaqMan qPCR was set up and run on 7500 fast real‐time PCR system (Applied Biosystems; Foster City, CA, USA). Relative mRNA expression of target genes was analyzed after normalization to the reference gene RPLP0 using the ∆∆Ct method (calculated as fold change compared to WT samples). All qPCR analyses are based on at least 3 experimental replicates, with at least 2 technical replicates for each.

Statistical analyses

Results are expressed as means ±SD or ± SEM (for qPCR analyses). The statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.02 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, USA). Unpaired two‐tailed Student's t‐tests were used for comparisons between two groups. Unless stated otherwise, multiple group analyses were performed by one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's post‐hoc test for multiple comparisons, or two‐way ANOVA followed by uncorrected Fisher's LSD test as indicated in figure legends. Significance level was set at <0.05 (*p<.05, **p<.01, ***p<.001, ****p<.0001). All analyses are based on at least 3 biological replicates. Mouse behavior analyses were based on at least 6 mice, and mice that showed extreme behavioral performance compared to other participants in the test (i.e., less than 10 arm entries in Y‐maze, or over 80% baseline freezing in context B of the fear conditioning cue test) were excluded from analysis. Furthermore, low quality RNA samples, as well as outliers that failed Grubb's outlier test, were excluded from analyses. Pre‐planned main effect factors in two‐way ANOVAs are presented when significant in the figures, and all pre‐planned main effects F statistics are provided in Supplemental Table S3.

Artificial intelligence generated content

No artificial intelligence, other than imaging analyses using QuPath described above, was used in developing this study or writing this paper.

RESULTS

Gonadectomy moderately improves cognitive performance in App NL ‐G‐F mice

Memory deficits start to appear in App NL‐G‐F mice at around 6 months of age when cortical and hippocampal amyloid depositions are already evident. 10 , 14 To test if gonadectomy (GX) affects memory at 6 months of age, surgical GX was performed in males (orchiectomy) and females (ovariectomy) at 2.5 months of age (Figure 1A), a timepoint corresponding to the onset of amyloid depositions. 10 Sham‐operated male and female App NL‐G‐F mice were used as control group and 6 months old intact WT mice were used as reference. Spatial working memory, assessed in the Y‐maze test (Figure 1B), revealed no difference among the groups (Figure 1C). However, App NL‐G‐F mice overall showed fewer arm entries than WT mice (Figure 1D), indicative of reduced exploration. Next, associative learning was assessed using the contextual fear conditioning test (FC, Figure 1E). Surprisingly, we observed increased freezing in App NL‐G‐F mice in context A (24 h after conditioning), and GX further increased freezing behavior especially in males (Figure 1F). This effect of GX was also observed in the cue test in males (Figure 1G), and females (Figure 1H) although latter did not reach significance. These observations suggest that GX could protect against associative learning deficits in males but less in females.

Gonadectomy has a protective effect on amyloid pathology in App NL ‐G‐F males and promotes astrogliosis

We next assessed amyloid beta load in the cortex and hippocampus of App NL‐G‐F mice. In line with the better performance in the fear conditioning test of GX male App NL‐G‐F mice, they also displayed fewer amyloid depositions in the cortex and hippocampus, while no effects of GX on amyloid load could be observed in female App NL‐G‐F mice (Figure 2A–C). Similarly, GX resulted in less microgliosis in the hippocampus of male App NL‐G‐F mice, while in females GX slightly increased microgliosis in the hippocampus but had little effect in the cortex (Figure 2A,D,E, Supplemental Figure 1). On the other hand, we could observe a significant increase in hippocampal astrogliosis in both male and female gonadectomized App NL‐G‐F mice (Figure 2F,G). Such effects of GX could also be observed in the female cortex but not in the male cortex (Figure 2H). Combined, these data suggest that GX has a protective effect especially in the App NL‐G‐F male hippocampus with less amyloid load and less microgliosis, but with increased astrogliosis.

Impact of GX on brain sex hormone levels and sex hormone signaling in App NL ‐G‐F mice

GX results in depletion of circulating E2 or testosterone. The brain is however a steroidogenic organ where key enzymes such as Cyp11a1 (cholesterol side‐chain enzyme) and Cyp19a1 (aromatase enzyme) can de novo synthesize sex hormones from cholesterol. 15 Indeed, GX in App NL‐G‐F females drastically decreased circulating E2 levels (Figure 3A), while this decrease was not as drastic in the brain (Figure 3B,C). Male E2 levels were overall much lower, and we could not observe any difference in circulating or brain E2 levels upon GX (Figure 3D,E). On the other hand, male testosterone levels were at the detection limit upon GX in App NL‐G‐F male serum and brain (Figure 3F,G), suggesting that de novo synthesis of testosterone does not occur in the male mouse brain.

Since the largest effects of GX on amyloid depositions and inflammatory response were in hippocampus (Figure 2), we analyzed gene expression of key enzymes involved in steroidogenesis in male and female brain. Cyp11a1 expression was overall higher in male App NL‐G‐F brain but with minor effect of GX (Figure 3H), while Cyp19a1 expression was slightly increased in sham App NL‐G‐F females (Figure 3I). Thus, GX did not result in major differences in the expression of these genes overall in the brain. We next evaluated estrogen receptor expression. Whole‐brain expression of Esr1 (estrogen receptor alpha, ERα) was decreased in male App NL‐G‐F mice, while increased in GX females (Supplemental Figure 2A). We could not observe any significant effects on Esr2 (estrogen receptor beta, ERβ) expression in the brain (Supplemental Figure 2B). However, in hippocampus we observed minor effects on Esr1 expression while Esr2 expression was highly increased in male App NL‐G‐F mice while significantly decreased in gonadectomized female App NL‐G‐F mice (Figure 3K). This suggests that hippocampal Esr2 may be more sensitive to GX than Esr1 in females.

Aberrant IGF‐1 signaling has been suggested to be associated with AD development, 16 , 17 and studies have shown that in the absence of estrogen, IGF‐1 can promote estrogen receptor phosphorylation to maintain estrogenic activity in different tissues, including the brain, which may possibly prevent cognitive decline. 18 However, we could only detect strongly increased hippocampal Igf1 levels upon male GX (Figure 3L), suggesting that IGF‐1 may have a compensatory effect on androgen receptor signaling in the male hippocampus.

Gonadectomy in male and female App NL ‐F mice impacts spatial memory

While the App NL‐G‐F model represents a very aggressive model of brain amyloidosis, the App NL‐F model displays slower, more clinically relevant amyloid accumulation in the brain, with memory impairments starting at around 18 months of age. 10 To compare the effect of GX in both models, App NL‐F mice were gonadectomized at 2.5 months of age and pathology was monitored at 18 months of age (Figure 4A). Both male and female GX App NL‐F mice showed a significant reduction in the spatial working memory, as indicated by fewer alternations in the Y‐maze test (Figure 4B). Additionally, only female GX App NL‐F mice displayed a significant decrease in the number of arm entries, showing a reduced exploratory activity (Figure 4C). When associative learning was evaluated in Context A of the FC test, only male App NL‐F mice showed less freezing (Figure 4D). However, in the FC cue test there was little difference between the male groups (Figure 4E). Female App NL‐F mice showed less freezing upon GX, irrespective of cue in context B of the FC test (Figure 4F). This suggests that GX can affect spatial working memory in both sexes in the App NL‐F mice, while effects on associative learning are less pronounced in the App NL‐F model.

Gonadectomy aggravates amyloid pathology in App NL ‐F males and females, and has sex‐specific effects on microglia and astrocyte levels

In contrast to App NL‐G‐F mice, GX in the App NL‐F mice resulted in increased amyloid depositions in the hippocampus (Figure 5A–C). However, similarly to the App NL‐G‐F model, Iba1+ microglia were decreased in gonadectomized male App NL‐F mice compared to gonadectomized female App NL‐F mice in cortex and hippocampus (Figure 5A,D,E, Supplemental Figure 3). Astrocyte levels were increased in female GX App NL‐F mice in the hippocampus but not in the cortex, while in males GX had no major effect on astrocyte levels (Figure 5F–H). Interestingly, there were significant sex differences in astrocyte and microglia levels, where GX had a more pronounced effect on microglia in male compared to female App NL‐F mice.

Impact of GX on brain sex hormone levels and sex hormone signaling in App NL ‐F mice

We next measured circulating and brain E2 levels in 18 months old WT, sham‐operated, and gonadectomized App NL‐F mice. Similar to the 6 months old App NL‐G‐F mice, GX resulted in severely depleted circulating E2 levels in female App NL‐F mice, while some E2 levels were maintained in the brain (Figure 6A–C). GX in male App NL‐F mice also decreased both circulating and brain E2 levels (Figure 6D,E), as well as decreasing circulating and brain testosterone levels to detection limit (Figure 6F,G). This suggests that E2 levels can be de‐novo synthesized even in the 18 months old female App NL‐F brain in the absence of circulating E2, and that circulating testosterone can to some extent be converted to E2 in the aged male App NL‐F brain. We could detect an increase in whole‐brain Cyp11a1 mRNA expression in App NL‐F brain relative to WT mice (Figure 6H), while Cyp19a1 was increased in the App NL‐F male brain especially after GX (Figure 6I). Overall brain Esr1 mRNA expression was decreased in App NL‐F mice (Supplemental Figure 4A), while brain Esr2 mRNA expression was lower only in GX App NL‐F males, but increased upon GX in App NL‐F females relative to sham App NL‐F mice (Supplemental Figure 4B). In the hippocampus, we could not detect any difference in Esr1 expression upon GX (Figure 6J), which is similar to the App NL‐G‐F data. However, hippocampal Esr2 levels were significantly increased upon GX in App NL‐F females (Figure 6K). Finally, analysis of hippocampal IGF‐1 levels revealed increased expression upon GX in male App NL‐F mice, while decreased levels in gonadectomized female App NL‐F mice (Figure 6L).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we investigate the loss of circulating sex hormones upon surgical removal of gonads in two mouse models of AD: App NL‐G‐F and App NL‐F that reflect early aggressive and late brain amyloid pathology, respectively. While App NL‐G‐F mice have been reported to display memory deterioration from 6 months of age, the App NL‐F model shows spatial memory impairment from 18 months of age. 10 , 19 Our data suggest that loss of circulating E2 in the early, more aggressive App NL‐G‐F model does not worsen pathology in females and that basic E2 levels can be maintained after GX in the brain. However, early E2 loss in aged App NL‐F female mice worsens performance in the Y‐maze test and increases amyloid pathology in the hippocampus. On the other hand, loss of circulating testosterone in App NL‐G‐F and App NL‐F males totally depleted brain testosterone levels, with no or slightly positive effects on memory in App NL‐G‐F males, concomitant with decreased hippocampal amyloid levels and less microgliosis but increased astrogliosis. Opposite to GX in App NL‐G‐F males, GX in App NL‐F males resulted in increased amyloid levels, but not in any major effects on microglia and astrocyte levels.

A recent study showed no increased amyloid pathology from androgen deprivation therapy in App NL‐G‐F mice (bearing prostate tumors), 20 which is in line with our study, but different from other studies showing that male gonadectomy increases amyloid pathology in the 3 × Tg AD mouse model. 21 , 22 Thus, the use of animal AD model seems to greatly influence the effect of sex hormones on pathology.

Although performed in App‐knock‐in mice, this study is the first to our knowledge to compare circulating serum and brain sex hormone levels between males and females with or without prior gonadectomy. It is well known that local E2 levels can be produced in the brain since all necessary steroidogenic enzymes are present there. 15 , 23 , 24 Our study brings important confirmation by demonstrating that basic E2 levels are maintained in the brain after ovariectomy (at approx. 20%) in both App NL‐G‐F and App NL‐F models at 6 and 18 months of age, respectively. In contrast, no testosterone was detected in the brain after male gonadectomy. On the other hand, and very interestingly, E2 levels were decreased in male App NL‐F GX brains (Figure 6E), suggesting that during aging, a proportion of circulating testosterone can indeed be converted to E2 in the brain, which was further supported by increased Cyp19a1 levels in male App NL‐F GX hippocampus.

In contrast to ERα (Esr1), ERβ (Esr2) expression was lower in App NL‐G‐F females than in males and was further reduced upon GX, whereas in App NL‐F females Esr2 mRNA expression was increased, particularly upon GX. This suggests that hippocampal Esr2 expression differs in a sex‐dependent manner in these AD models and is sensitive to systemic E2 levels at least in females. We have recently shown that ERβ activation in the App NL‐G‐F model is neuroprotective, 11 and since Esr2 expression appears to be maintained upon GX in App NL‐G‐F males (Figure 3K), which coincides with better associative memory (Figure 1F,G) and less hippocampal and cortical plaques (Figure 2B,C), it is tempting to speculate that it may exert neuroprotective effects, possibly through ligand‐independent mechanisms in GX males.

IGF‐1 has been implicated in enabling ligand‐independent activation of sex steroid nuclear receptors, likely through promoting estrogen and androgen receptor phosphorylation, as well as through abolishing FOXO1 occupancy to promoter regions of sex hormone receptor target genes. 25 , 26 , 27 Previous studies have associated lower serum IGF‐1 levels with increased risk of developing AD while higher IGF‐1 levels appeared to be neuroprotective. 16 , 17 In our study, gonadectomized App NL‐G‐F males show much higher Igf1 expression compared to all other groups. This could possibly lead to ligand‐independent neuroprotective activation of ERβ via IGF‐1 crosstalk that could potentially contribute to improved cognitive performance and reduced plaque burden in gonadectomized App NL‐G‐F males. In the aged App NL‐F males, the change in Igf1 mRNA expression upon GX is not as drastic. Instead, there is a significant downregulation of Igf1 in gonadectomized App NL‐F females (with a seemingly compensatory increase in ERβ expression), which is consistent with the more aggravated effects on associative memory in these mice. Astrocytes are major producers of IGF‐1 and mediators of its neuroprotective functions in the brain. 28 , 29 We observed elevated astrogliosis in the 6 months‐old male App NL‐G‐F hippocampus (but not in 18 months‐old App NL‐F hippocampus) upon GX, which could indicate that astrocytic IGF‐1 production may be linked to neuroprotection in the gonadectomized App NL‐G‐F males. However, the causal relationship between loss of circulating androgens and age‐ and model‐specific neuroprotection via the ERβ–IGF‐1 crosstalk remains to be elucidated.

In this study we noted that the WT mice showed similar cognitive behavior to the App‐knock‐in mice, especially in the spatial memory test. Although this was unexpected, it could reflect the reported subtle cognitive deficits displayed in the App‐knock‐in mice, despite significant amyloid depositions. 30 , 31 , 32 Additionally, the 6 months and 18 months timepoints in App NL‐G‐F and App NL‐F mice, respectively, reflect the onset period of behavioral deficits which are therefore expected to be mild. We also noted high basal movement, especially in the 18 months old mice, before sound in the fear conditioning cue test. This could mask memory deficits in this group and suggests that age and genotype effects observed on behavior are test‐dependent, warranting caution in overinterpreting these results.

Another point of discussion is the selection of timepoint for gonadectomy. In the App NL‐G‐F model, GX at 2.5 months of age is reasonable to allow for possible changes on AD pathology by 6 months of age. However, we also chose GX at 2.5 months of age for the App NL‐F model that displays memory deficits from 18 months of age. Since female C57BL/6 mice (the background of our App‐knock‐in mice) show a significant drop in reproductive capacity as early as from 9 months of age, 33 we decided to also perform GX at 2.5 months for the App NL‐F mice to avoid controlling for secondary reproductive effects (and to facilitate comparisons between the models).

Finally, we observed stronger sex differences in App NL‐F compared to App NL‐G‐F mice, especially in microglia and astrocyte levels, where App NL‐F males displayed similar astrocyte levels as WT mice in the hippocampus. This could be due to the aged phenotype of 18 months old mice.

Some limitations of this study include moderate effects on cognition in comparison to WT mice, as mentioned above. Possibly, a later age at analysis could have given clearer differences. Nevertheless, we detect significant differences at the molecular levels. Another limitation is that WT mice were not gonadectomized, preventing full comparisons to this group. However, the focus of this study was on AD pathology and therefore, the WT mice are included as a reference for the non‐AD condition rather than as a control group for gonadectomy. Furthermore, potential age‐related effects cannot be ruled out. Indeed, age seems to interfere with behavior, such as results of fear conditioning tests in our study. Thus, despite including age‐matched WT mice as reference, the contribution of potential age‐related effects between the models cannot be ruled out. Finally, the effects on IGF‐1 are interesting and would require functional studies to link IGF‐1 crosstalk with the estrogen signaling in the two AD models used. This will be an exciting task to address in the future.

In summary, we compare the effect of gonadectomy on pathology in male and female App NL‐G‐F and App NL‐F mice. We show that GX in App NL‐G‐F mice does not worsen pathology, while it does in aged male and female App NL‐F mice. We also show that a significant proportion of E2 is maintained in the female brain after GX and that testosterone indeed can be converted to E2 in the brain. Furthermore, we propose that decreased sex hormone levels can induce IGF‐1 expression, which is a mechanism for ligand‐independent activation of sex hormone receptors, such as the neuroprotective ERβ. 11 Although this study is largely descriptive, it is the first to examine the sex‐specific effects of GX in two mouse models of AD, including effects on brain sex hormone levels, and bring important new knowledge to understand the implication of menopause on AD risk, as well as the sex differences observed in this devastating disease.
