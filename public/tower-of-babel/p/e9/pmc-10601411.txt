Experimental Study on the Impact of Hydrogen Injection Strategy on Combustion Performance in Internal Combustion Engines
Authors: Zhaoming Huang, Liangmo Wang, Hao Pan, Jianping Li, Tao Wang, Li Wang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractHydrogen is an ideal alternative fuel for internal combustion engines due to its fast combustion rate and near-zero carbon emissions. To further investigate the impact of the injection strategy on combustion performance under various excess air coefficients and loads, experimental methods were employed to systematically study injection timing, injection pressure, and dual injection. The results demonstrate that appropriately delaying hydrogen injection timing can improve the thermal efficiency by up to 2.6% and reduce NOx emissions of equivalent combustion by nearly 88%. While increasing the hydrogen pressure to 8 MPa does not directly enhance the thermal efficiency and emissions, it can reduce the injection pulse width by approximately 75%, allowing for more flexibility in delaying the injection timing. Delaying the secondary end of injection (SEOI) leads to a reduction of over 47% in NOx emissions for λ (excess air coefficient) values of 1.5 and 1.0. The combustion duration and ignition delay initially increase and then decrease with the delay in SEOI, depending on the movement state of the fuel jet. With the increase in secondary injection proportion (SIP), brake thermal efficiency increases by no more than 1%, but NOx is reduced by more than 43% for λ values of 1.5 and 1.0. In the case of ultralean conditions (λ = 2.3), increasing the SIP from 0 to 30% results in a nearly 14% increase in the peak heat release rate (HRR) and a 19% reduction in combustion duration.

Introduction

In the context of global energy conservation and emissions reduction, the Internal Combustion Engine (ICE) industry is facing a severe challenge and is urgently seeking clean and renewable energy sources. On one hand, hydrogen, regarded as an ideal clean energy source,1,2 can be derived from a wide range of sources and does not produce carbon emissions during combustion. On the other hand, hydrogen possesses characteristics that make it an excellent alternative fuel for ICE, as shown in Table 1. Due to its faster combustion speed, which brings it closer to constant-volume combustion, hydrogen can achieve higher thermal efficiency. Its wide flammability limit gives it the advantage of lean combustion. Moreover, its higher auto-ignition temperature enables it to adapt to a larger compression ratio, resulting in higher thermal efficiency. With a high mass-based calorific value, hydrogen theoretically has the potential to achieve a higher power density. Additionally, its wider flammability limit and lower ignition energy allow for greater flexibility in injection strategies.

The combustion process directly determines the thermal efficiency of an engine, and the formation of the mixture plays a crucial role in combustion.7 Therefore, similar to traditional internal combustion engines,8−10 the study of mixture formation is also an important aspect of hydrogen internal combustion engines (HICE). The excess air coefficient has a significant impact on the combustion rate and NOx emissions in HICE. During lean combustion, the combustion rate is much lower compared to the equivalence ratio. Therefore, it is vital to implement a control strategy in hydrogen ICE that increases the combustion rate during lean combustion to achieve higher thermal efficiency while reducing the combustion rate during equivalent combustion to minimize combustion roughness and NOx emissions. Consequently, a reasonable injection strategy holds immense significance in effectively controlling the combustion process in hydrogen ICE.

The mixture distribution is influenced by the injection timing and nozzle structure. Scarcelli et al.11 tested the hydrogen distribution with planar laser-induced fluorescence technology in a single-cylinder optical engine at Santiago National Laboratory. The experimental results verified the accuracy of numerical simulations in predicting the hydrogen mixing process, including jet penetration, wall impingement, and overall evolution. Fu et al.12 conducted numerical research on the impact of injection timing and nozzle diameter on ultralean combustion. The results showed that delaying injection increased the concentration near the spark plug and combustion rate. Increasing the nozzle diameter resulted in a shorter injection pulse width and a more concentrated mixture distribution. Unlike gasoline and diesel, hydrogen does not undergo an atomization process and has a larger diffusion coefficient. Therefore, later injection timing can achieve a higher thermal efficiency. Late spray lean combustion can also reduce NOx emissions, especially under heavy load.13 Furthermore, the injection strategy and spray direction affect the flow field near the spark plug. When the spray direction is toward the spark plug, an early injection timing leads to low turbulent kinetic energy near the spark plug due to a long dissipation period. Conversely, late injection increases the turbulent kinetic energy and concentration around the spark plug,14,15 which is crucial for the combustion stability of lean combustion. Lee et al.16 studied the effect of injection and ignition timing on hydrogen-lean stratified charge combustion in a single-cylinder engine. Adjusting injection timing could realize different combustion modes: hard and mild combustion, and combustion stability was controlled by ignition timing. Wang et al.17 studied numerically the effects of injection timing, nozzle diameter, and nozzle position on mixture formation in a port fuel injection hydrogen engine. The results showed that the injection timing greatly influenced the remaining hydrogen content in the intake port. Advancing the injection timing gradually decreased the hydrogen remaining in the intake port, thereby reducing the possibility of backfire and pre-ignition. Conversely, excessively delayed injection would result in incomplete injection before the intake valves closed. Fan et al.18 studied the influence of injection strategies on mixture formation in a Wankel engine using numerical simulation. The study found that advancing the Start of Injection (SOI) had little effect on the distribution of hydrogen. However, with delayed SOI, the mixture distribution became increasingly sensitive to injection timing and stratification became progressively obvious. When the injection process occurs during the intake stroke, the interaction between the inlet airflow and hydrogen jet can be fully utilized to accelerate the mixing process, promoting the formation of a more uniform mixture. However, proper arrangement of nozzle position and direction is necessary.19 Hamzehloo et al.20 observed that earlier injection timing resulted in a more uniform mixture due to increased mixing time. Although late injection led to a nonhomogeneous mixture, stratified combustion could be achieved by optimizing the injection strategy and combustion system. The mixture near the spark plug was enriched to ensure the formation and development of the flame kernel, while the mixture near the cylinder wall was leaner to reduce heat transfer loss and knocking tendency.21

Injection timing is closely related to the injection pressure. Increasing the injection pressure can shorten the injection duration, providing more flexibility in adjusting the injection timing. Cheng et al.22 recorded the high-pressure hydrogen injection process with a high-speed camera in a constant-volume combustion bomb. It was found that with the increase of injection pressure, the permeability and penetration distance of hydrogen gradually increased, but the diffusion capacity of the hydrogen jet in the radial direction decreased. Keskinen et al.23 studied mixture formation from the perspectives of nozzle type, injection pressure, and injection timing. They found that the mixing rate was more sensitive to injection timing than to injection pressure. Late injection significantly enhanced the mixing rate through the interaction between the hydrogen jet and the wall or piston top surface. Tsujimura24 et al. found that lower injection pressure resulted in a prolonged injection pulse width, leading to an uneven mixture and accelerated combustion. This inhomogeneity made the combustion performance less sensitive to the exhaust gas recirculation (EGR) rate, enabling the use of EGR to reduce NOx emissions with minimal power loss.

Multiple injection strategies have been studied to achieve different combustion modes. Wallner et al.25 investigated the effects of injection direction and strategy on combustion in a Hydrogen Direct Injection (HDI) engine. The results showed that the dual injection strategy could reduce NOx emissions by up to 85% compared with single injection while maintaining the same engine efficiency. NOx is primarily generated under high-temperature, oxygen-rich conditions. Adjusting the dual injection strategy allows for the formation of locally too-rich or too-lean mixtures, reducing the level of NOx generation. Li et al.26,27 and Biffiger et al.28 studied the effects of the ratio and timing of the dual injection on combustion and emissions. The results showed that a dual injection could form the stratified mixture. The mixture near the central area was richer, accelerating the combustion speed, and the mixture near the cylinder wall was leaner, reducing the wall heat loss and suppressing knocking, thus improving the thermal efficiency. In addition, the proportion and timing of the secondary injection significantly influenced the torque and emissions. Pan et al.29 studied the effect of different mixture distributions on combustion and emissions in a gasoline/hydrogen engine. The results showed a partially premixed hydrogen mixture distribution organized by two injectors in the intake port and cylinder, respectively, achieved the fastest combustion rate, while a premixed hydrogen mixture distribution organized by hydrogen port injection achieved the lowest emissions for its homogeneous mixture. Yu et al.30 conducted similar research in a hydrogen/n-butanol combined injection engine and found that split HDI achieved a balance between dynamics and emissions. Li et al.31 numerically analyzed the effects of different injection parameters on the formation of hydrogen-air mixture. The results showed that a uniform mixture could be achieved with an SOI before 88 °CA BTDC and jet-room coordination was employed to increase tumble strength. In addition to reciprocating engines, the injection strategy also plays an important role in the mixture distribution and combustion performance of rotary engines.32,33 Optimized injection timing and angle significantly improve emissions, and split injection can create stratified mixtures, thereby enhancing thermal efficiency and reducing emissions.

Based on the literature reviewed, it is evident that although there have been numerous studies on the injection strategy of HDI engines, most of them rely on theoretical analysis or numerical simulations, lacking practical engineering guidance and comprehensive systematic research. Furthermore, there is a lack of optimization studies on the injection strategy under different operating conditions and mixture concentrations. For having a comprehensive understanding of the impact of injection strategy on the combustion characteristics of hydrogen engines under varying loads and excess air coefficients (λ), this paper aims to systematically investigate the influences of injection timing, injection pressure, and dual injection strategies on the combustion characteristics of HDI engines under different loads and λ by experimental methods and to provide guidance for the engineering development of high-efficiency, low-emission hydrogen engines.

Methodology

The hydrogen engine utilized in this paper has been modified from a 4-cylinder gasoline direct injection engine. A hydrogen rail and hydrogen injectors were installed for hydrogen injecting. Hydrogen is supplied from a group of hydrogen gas bottles (40 L × 16, 10 MPa). Experimental Method

In this study, experimental studies were conducted to investigate the influences of different injection strategies on the combustion performance of hydrogen engines. The specific parameters for each study are listed in Table 3. The first two sets of experiments focused on studying the effects of different timings and pressure of injection on the combustion performance of hydrogen engines under different λ. The third set of experiments explored the impact of the dual injection strategy, specifically the Secondary End of Injection (SEOI) and Secondary Injection Proportion (SIP), on combustion performance under different λ values. Under heavy loads, the injection pulse is relatively wide due to low injection pressure, and this wide injection pulse results in a restricted range for adjusting injection strategies. Besides, because the combustion rate is already quite fast at heavy loads, the improvement of combustion performance by optimizing the injection strategy is limited. Therefore, the experimental operating conditions primarily range from 2 to 8 bar in Brake Mean Effective Pressure (BMEP) and the engine speed is fixed at 1200 rpm. CA10, CA50, and CA90 denote the crank angles corresponding to the cumulative heat release reaching 10, 50, and 90% of the overall heat release, respectively. In the paper, the combustion duration is defined as the crank angle interval between CA10 and CA90, and the ignition delay is defined as the crank angle interval between the spark timing and CA10. CA50 represents the combustion phase, which is used to determine whether the combustion phase is appropriate. For the safety and validity of test results, the COV of BMEP was controlled to within 3% during the experiment. If the COV went beyond 3% under an injection strategy, that injection strategy was considered inappropriate, and the result was not recorded.

Results and Discussion

Effect of Injection Timing on Combustion

Experimental studies were conducted on different SOIs under different λ values at BMEP values of 2 and 5 bar. As shown in Figure 2, for a BMEP of 2 bar, the Brake Thermal Efficiency (BTE) exhibits an initial increase followed by a subsequent decrease as the SOI is delayed. Delaying the injection timing improves the thermal efficiency by at least 1.8% and up to 2.6%. The postponement of the injection timing reduces the negative work when the piston moves upward, which is one of the main factors contributing to the improved thermal efficiency. This can be observed from the cylinder pressure curve depicted in Figure 3. Moreover, appropriately delaying the SOI facilitates the formation of a stratified mixture in the cylinder, reducing heat transfer losses and further enhancing the thermal efficiency. However, when the SOI is later than 80 °CA BTDC, the BTE gradually decreases. The short duration of mixture formation prevents its diffusion into the center of the cylinder, resulting in an increased hydrogen concentration near the cylinder wall and increased heat transfer losses. For a BMEP of 5 bar, the BTE is increased by at least 2.0% and up to 2.35%. However, the pulse width of the injection gradually increases with an increasing load. To prevent injection blockage due to increased back pressure as hydrogen injection approaches top dead center, the SOI cannot be later than 80 °CA BTDC. Therefore, for BMEP of 5 bar, there is no decrease in thermal efficiency when delaying the SOI. The combustion stability is pretty good during the SOI ranging from 180 to 30 °CA BTDC attributed to the relatively fast combustion speed of hydrogen. Therefore, COVs are not listed and analyzed in this paper.

Variation of BTE with SOI under different λ and BMEP, (a) BMEP = 2 bar, (b) BMEP = 5 bar.

In-cylinder pressure curves under different SOIs.

Figure 4 illustrates the combustion duration and ignition delay under different SOI and λ. It can be seen that with the delay of the SOI, the combustion duration and ignition delay increase first and then decrease. When the SOI is before 100 °CA BTDC, the combustion duration and ignition delay change slightly because the mixture is relatively uniform. When the SOI is after 80 °CA BTDC, as the injection is delayed, the injection process gradually approaches the ignition timing. The turbulence intensity in the cylinder gradually increases with the effect of the hydrogen jet. Therefore, the flame propagation speed increases, and the formation time of the flame kernel shortens. When SOI is postponed from 100 to 80 °CA BTDC, hydrogen has not diffused to the spark plug area at the ignition time, and a large amount of hydrogen is distributed near the wall far from the spark plug, so the ignition delay and combustion duration are significantly increased. The results of NOx emissions, as depicted in Figure 5, demonstrate an overall downward trend as the injection is delayed, particularly for equivalent combustion (λ = 1.0). The NOx emissions decrease from 4576 to 560 ppm. High temperatures and oxygen enrichment are favorable conditions for the generation of NOx generation. For equivalent combustion (λ = 1.0), when the uniformity of the mixture is poor, there is a large area where the lambda is much smaller than 1 or much larger than 1, which means it is extremely hypoxic or has a low combustion temperature. Hence, NOx emissions decrease as the uniformity of the mixture deteriorates due to the injection delay. However, for lean combustion (λ = 1.5 and 2.0), when the mixture uniformity worsens, there are some areas where λ is close to 1, indicating relatively high combustion temperature, promoting NOx production. Therefore, there is no considerable drop in the level of NOx with SOI delaying.

Impact of Hydrogen Injection Pressure on Combustion

Since gaseous hydrogen does not undergo an atomization process, the injection pressure theoretically has a limited influence on the mixing process and combustion performance of hydrogen engines. As shown in Figure 6, there is a negligible difference in BTE and NOx emissions for different injection pressures. Similarly, the results of the combustion duration and ignition delay, shown in Figure 7, indicate that injection pressure has a slight effect on the combustion process. However, there is a strong correlation between injection pressure and the injection pulse width. Figure 8 demonstrates that higher injection pressures result in smaller pulse widths, providing more flexibility to delay injection timing. This has the potential to enhance thermal efficiency and reduce the likelihood of pre-ignition.

In summary, the impact of the hydrogen injection pressure on the performance is limited. Higher injection pressure results in a shorter injection pulse width, facilitating greater hydrogen flow and allowing for a delayed EOI. This, in turn, can lead to higher BTE and help mitigate the risk of pre-ignition.

Effect of Parameters of Secondary Injection on Performance of Hydrogen Engines

Secondary injection timing and proportion are studied to reveal the effect of secondary injection parameters on the combustion of hydrogen ICE. The experimental operating condition in the section is controlled at BMEP of 5 bar, and the engine speed is fixed at 1200 rpm.

Effect of End of Secondary Injection on Combustion Performance

In the experiment, the Primary Start of Injection (PSOI) is fixed at 180 °CA BTDC, and the Secondary Injection Proportion (SIP) is fixed at 50%. The Secondary End of Injection (SEOI) is then progressively delayed from 100 °CA BTDC until combustion becomes unstable.

The baseline in Figure 9 represents the single injection with the SOI at 180 °CA BTDC. As shown in Figure 9, BTE exhibits an initial increase followed by a subsequent decrease as the SEOI is delayed, while NOx emissions decrease first and then increase. Besides, for leaner cases (λ = 2.3, 1.8), the BTE increases by up to about 1.5% by delaying the SEOI, whereas for richer cases (λ = 1.5, 1.0), the maximum BTE increase is about 0.8%. This difference can be attributed to the greater sensitivity of combustion rate to hydrogen concentration in leaner cases affected by SEOI. Reasonable delay of SOI can create a stratified mixture with a rich inner region and a lean outer region inside the cylinder, thereby increasing the combustion rate while reducing heat transfer loss. Conversely, when SEOI is delayed, the reduction in NOx emissions is more pronounced in richer cases (λ = 1.5, 1.0) compared to leaner cases (λ = 2.3, 1.8). For leaner cases, due to the low combustion temperature, NOx emissions are not significantly reduced and even increase. For richer cases, as the SEOI is delayed, the uniformity of the mixture gradually deteriorates, resulting in larger areas with λ values significantly below 1, known as over-rich regions. Although the combustion temperature is high in the over-rich area, NOx production is reduced due to lack of oxygen. Therefore, NOx emissions decrease with the delay of SEOI.

Variation of BTE (a) and NOx (b) with SEOI under different λ.

Furthermore, with the delaying of SEOI, turbulent kinetic energy increases, which contributes to a faster combustion rate, as shown in Figure 10. It is worth noting that with the delay of SEOI, the ignition delay and combustion duration first decrease and then increase, and finally decrease. When the SEOI is before 80 °CA BTDC, the hydrogen jet follows a clockwise path guided by the cylinder wall, as shown in Figure 11. With the delay of SEOI, the turbulent intensity increases, leading to a decrease in ignition delay and combustion duration for leaner cases (λ = 2.3, 1.8). However, turbulent intensity is relatively low since SEOI is relatively early. Therefore, for richer cases (λ = 1.5, 1.0), ignition delay and combustion show less sensitivity to SEOI. When SEOI is further delayed from 80 °CA BTDC to 40 °CA BTDC, the hydrogen jet drop point approaches the junction between the piston top surface and cylinder wall. This results in two effects: first, a significant loss of kinetic energy for the hydrogen jet, leading to a decrease in turbulent intensity during the ignition timing, and second, the challenge for hydrogen to diffuse toward the spark plug, resulting in higher concentration near the spark plug. As the SEOI continues to be delayed, the movement of the hydrogen jet gradually transitions to piston-guided counterclockwise movement, shown in Figure 11. The turbulent intensity increases once again, leading to a decrease in ignition delay and combustion duration with further SEOI delay. Overall, as the mixture becomes richer, there is a decreasing sensitivity of ignition delay and combustion duration to SEOI.

Effect of Secondary Injection Proportion on Combustion Performance

The PSOI and SEOI are fixed at 180 °CA and 80 °CA BTDC, respectively. The SIP increases from 0 to 50%, while the base case represents a single injection with an SOI of 180 °CA BTDC. Figure 12(a) illustrates that increasing SIP results in a slight improvement in BTE, with the maximum increase not exceeding 1%. For leaner cases, as SIP increases, more hydrogen is distributed near the spark plug through secondary injection, leading to an increase of the combustion rate, as shown in Figure 13. However, for richer cases, the influence of mixture concentration on the combustion rate is slightly so the improvement of combustion rate by increasing SIP is limited. NOx values of richer cases decrease by more than 43% with SIP increasing, while the change of NOx for leaner cases is negligible, as shown in Figure 12(b). For richer cases, the increase of SIP considerably enlarges the over-rich area, which is detrimental to NOx generation. For leaner cases, because of the lower combustion temperature, the mixture distribution affected by SIP has a slight influence on NOx production.

BTE (a) and NOx (b) for different SIP under different λ.

Combustion duration for different SIP under different λ.

The in-cylinder pressure and HRR curves for different SIPs are shown in Figure 14. As we can see from Figure 14(a), the difference between pressure curves and HRR curves is negligible, indicating that the dual injection has little influence on cylinder pressure and HRR for equivalent combustion. Consequently, the difference in BTE is also slight for richer cases. However, for leaner cases, as depicted in Figure 14(b), dual injection can significantly increase the peak of cylinder pressure and HRR. Moreover, the peak HRR rises from 103 to 117 kJ/(m3·deg) as SIP increases from 0 to 30%. Nevertheless, when SIP increases from 30 to 50%, the change in the cylinder pressure and HRR becomes insignificant. This suggests that excessively increasing SIP cannot indefinitely improve the combustion rate, and a SIP of 30% is sufficient, as also observed in Figure 13.

Conclusions

This paper investigates the impacts of various injection strategies on the combustion characteristics of HDI engines through experimental analysis. The key findings can be summarized as follows:

For BMEP of 2 and 5 bar, properly delaying injection can improve thermal efficiency by up to 2.6% because of reducing the negative work of compression stroke and heat transfer loss. However, premature SOI decreases thermal efficiency due to increased heat transfer losses resulting from higher hydrogen concentration near the cylinder wall. For equivalent combustion, NOx emissions can be reduced from 4574 to 560 ppm, nearly 88%. But the effect of SOI on NOx of lean combustion is limited.

However, premature SOI decreases thermal efficiency due to increased heat transfer losses resulting from higher hydrogen concentration near the cylinder wall. For equivalent combustion, NOx emissions can be reduced from 4574 to 560 ppm, nearly 88%. But the effect of SOI on NOx of lean combustion is limited.

Since gaseous hydrogen has no atomization process and has a strong diffusion capacity, hydrogen injection pressure has a slight influence on the combustion rate and emissions. Therefore, blindly increasing the injection pressure is not meaningful and may result in a reduced cruising range due to decreased available hydrogen. However, when the injection pressure is increased from 2 to 8 MPa, the injection pulse width decreases by approximately 75%, respectively, which allows for greater flexibility in delaying the injection timing to achieve higher thermal efficiency and lower pre-ignition probabilities.
