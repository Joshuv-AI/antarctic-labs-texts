A novel folate receptor-targeted curcumin nano-delivery system: A dual therapeutic strategy for precise inhibition of MRCKβ to suppress ovarian cancer proliferation and immune escape
Authors: Mengna Shi, Lulu Chen, Yang Yu, Hong Wang, Min-Jie Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Curcumin is a natural polyphenolic compound extracted from the rhizomes of Curcuma longa, exhibiting diverse biological activities, including anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, antitumor, antibacterial, antiviral, and neuroprotective effects. However, its clinical application is severely limited by poor oral absorption and low bioavailability. To address these challenges, this study synthesized a novel folate-targeted curcumin-loaded material—CU-FRα/PEG-2-CHO. This delivery system significantly enhances tumor cell uptake efficiency through folate receptor (FRα)-mediated active targeting (FRα denotes the folate receptor alpha subtype). Additionally, polyethylene glycol (PEG) modification and aldehyde (-CHO) functionalization further improve its water solubility and stability. Experimental results demonstrate that this material not only substantially increases curcumin's absorption rate and bioavailability but also enhances its antitumor activity via targeted delivery mechanisms. This study developed a new type (significantly improving various performances) folate receptor-targeted curcumin nano-delivery system (CU-FRα/PEG-2-CHO) and systematically compared the antitumor effects of two curcumin nanomaterials (CU-FRα/PEG-1-CHO and CU-FRα/PEG-2-CHO), demonstrating that CU-FRα/PEG-2-CHO significantly inhibited the growth of A2780 and HO8910PM ovarian cancer cell lines. After targeting tumor cells, CU-FRα/PEG-2-CHO released curcumin, which was mechanistically shown to specifically target Cdc42-binding kinase β (MRCKβ, IC50 = 2.12 μM), with its binding characteristics confirmed by surface plasmon resonance (SPR), isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC), and cellular thermal shift assay (CESTA). Molecular docking analysis revealed that MRCKβ precisely binds to the ATP-binding pocket of curcumin. Further transcriptomic analysis demonstrated that MRCKβ silencing significantly affected the TGF-β-SMAD4-LAG-3 signaling pathway, and CU-FRα/PEG-2-CHO exerted its therapeutic effects by regulating the MRCKβ-APC-SMAD4-CTLA-4 signaling axis to suppress tumor cell proliferation and immune evasion. Animal experiments confirmed that CU-FRα/PEG-2-CHO administration at a dose of 1 mg/kg every two days significantly inhibited tumor growth with a dose-dependent effect. Notably, although cisplatin (5 mg/kg every two days) and αPD-L1 (5 mg/kg every two days) also showed certain therapeutic effects, their tumor suppression efficacy was significantly lower than that of CU-FRα/PEG-2-CHO. These findings not only elucidate the mechanism of a novel folate receptor-targeted curcumin nanoliposome but also provide important evidence for developing MRCKβ-targeted therapeutic strategies against ovarian cancer.

Introduction

Curcumin, a hydrophobic polyphenolic compound extracted from the rhizomes of Curcuma longa L., exhibits a wide range of pharmacological activities due to its unique chemical structure, which includes β-diketone groups and phenolic hydroxyl groups [[1], [2], [3]]. These activities include anti-inflammatory effects (through inhibition of NF-κB and COX-2 pathways) [4,5], antioxidant properties (by scavenging ROS and activating the Nrf2 pathway) [6,7], antitumor effects (via modulation of Wnt/β-catenin and PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathways) [8,9], antimicrobial action (by disrupting microbial membrane integrity) [10], and neuroprotective functions (such as reducing Aβ plaque deposition) [11], demonstrating significant therapeutic potential in cancer, neurodegenerative diseases, and metabolic disorders. However, its clinical application faces three major challenges: extremely low water solubility, leading to poor oral bioavailability [12,13]; rapid first-pass metabolism, resulting in swift clearance via glucuronidation and sulfation in the liver; and lack of targeting specificity, causing systemic distribution and insufficient drug accumulation at disease sites. These limitations severely hinder its clinical translation. To address these issues, nanodelivery systems have become a research focus due to their adjustable particle size (typically 20–200 nm), functionalizable surface properties, and enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect. Among potential targets, folate receptor alpha (FRα), a glycosylphosphatidylinositol-anchored membrane protein, is overexpressed in ovarian cancer, triple-negative breast cancer, and other malignancies [[14], [15], [16], [17], [18]] but has limited expression in normal tissues, making it an ideal active targeting ligand. Based on this, our study innovatively designed a multifunctional nanodelivery system, CU-FRα/PEG-2-CHO, which achieves breakthroughs through three key strategies: FRα-targeting ligands (folate derivatives) for tumor-specific recognition, PEG2000 modification to form a hydrophilic corona and prolong blood circulation half-life, and aldehyde (-CHO) functionalization for pH-responsive drug release. These features significantly enhance drug solubility, pharmacokinetics, and targeting efficiency.

The importance of discovering novel drug targets lies in multiple critical dimensions. First, in addressing clinical treatment bottlenecks, new targets can overcome limitations of existing therapies, such as drug resistance in cancer treatment, push efficacy boundaries, and provide treatment options for rare and refractory diseases [[19], [20], [21]]. Second, in advancing precision medicine, novel targets facilitate the optimization of individualized treatment regimens and enable breakthroughs in multi-target synergistic therapy. MRCKβ (Cdc42-binding kinase β), a key member of the Rho kinase family, contributes to tumor development through multiple mechanisms. In tumor cell motility and metastasis, MRCKβ regulates actomyosin contractility by phosphorylating myosin light chain (MLC), promoting pseudopod formation, while activating the LIMK/cofilin pathway to disrupt actin cytoskeleton dynamics and enhance tumor cell invasiveness [22]. In proliferation and survival regulation, MRCKβ synergistically promotes cell cycle progression via the Wnt/β-catenin pathway and NF-κB signaling (by inhibiting IκBα degradation) [23], and its inhibition increases apoptosis rates in hepatocellular carcinoma cells. In therapeutic resistance, MRCKβ overexpression elevates the IC50 value of cisplatin in ovarian cancer, a mechanism linked to enhanced membrane localization of the ABCC1 efflux pump. Targeting MRCKβ with nanodelivery systems can disrupt this pathway and restore chemosensitivity. These findings collectively establish MRCKβ as a core regulator of tumor malignancy and a potential therapeutic target.

Based on the biological characteristic of high folate receptor alpha (FRα) expression in ovarian cancer cells (clinical sample positivity rate >80 %), our study innovatively designed and constructed a triple-targeting curcumin nanodelivery system (CU-FRα/PEG2-CHO). This system achieves potent antitumor effects through synergistic mechanisms: first, the folate ligand (FA-PEG2000-CHO) on the nanocarrier surface specifically recognizes and binds to FRα on ovarian cancer cell membranes (with a dissociation constant Kd of ∼10^-9 M), enabling tumor-targeted accumulation via receptor-mediated endocytosis; second, cholesterol molecules modified in the nanoparticles fuse with the cell membrane lipid bilayer through hydrophobic interactions, triggering pH-responsive drug release for precise intracellular curcumin delivery; finally, the released curcumin specifically binds to the ATP-binding pocket of MRCKβ kinase, modulating tumor malignancy.

The TGF-β-SMAD4 signaling pathway, as a core regulatory cascade in cellular processes, initiates its molecular mechanism when the TGF-β ligand binds to the type II receptor (TβRII) on the cell membrane, subsequently recruiting and phosphorylating the type I receptor (TβRI) to form a heterotetrameric complex [24,25]. Activated TβRI phosphorylates the C-terminal SSXS motif of SMAD2/3 proteins, enabling their oligomerization with SMAD4 and subsequent nuclear translocation [26], where they directly regulate the transcriptional expression of target genes. This process is tightly controlled by negative feedback regulators, maintaining epithelial homeostasis, embryonic development, and tissue repair under physiological conditions. However, in pathological contexts (e.g., tumor progression), the pathway exhibits a dual role: in early stages, it suppresses the cell cycle via p21CIP1 upregulation, while in advanced stages, SMAD4 mutations or epigenetic silencing lead to pathway inactivation [27,28]. Consequently, the tumor shifts to non-canonical pathways (e.g., ERK and JNK) to promote epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), characterized by E-cadherin downregulation and N-cadherin upregulation, alongside the induction of immunosuppressive molecules in the tumor microenvironment. Clinical data reveal SMAD4 deletion mutations in approximately 50 % of pancreatic cancers and 35 % of colorectal cancers, while TGF-β1 overexpression in ovarian cancer is significantly associated with reduced overall patient survival. These features make the pathway a critical focus for diagnostic biomarker development and targeted therapy design. LAG-3 (lymphocyte-activation gene 3, also known as CD223) is a key immune checkpoint molecule belonging to the immunoglobulin superfamily, structurally similar to CD4 but with distinct immunomodulatory functions [[29], [30], [31], [32], [33]]. In this study, the research team discovered through RNA sequencing that MRCKβ can directly regulate the TGF-β-SMAD4-LAG-3 signaling axis to suppress ovarian cancer cell proliferation and immune evasion capabilities.

Materials and methods

Materials

Curcumin (purity ≥98 %) was procured from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Human ovarian cancer cell lines (A2780, HO8910PM) and the murine ovarian cancer cell line ID8 were obtained from Otwo Biotech (Shenzhen, China). Specific pathogen-free (SPF) mice were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology (Beijing, China), and all animal procedures adhered to institutional ethical guidelines.

Lipid film formation

Lipid components were accurately weighed and dissolved in a chloroform:methanol (2:1, v/v) mixture, with sonication-assisted solubilization. The lipid solution was transferred to a round-bottom flask and evaporated under reduced pressure (rotary evaporator, 50 °C water bath, 120 rpm, vacuum <10 mBar) for 30 min, forming a uniform semi-transparent lipid film. Residual solvents were removed by nitrogen purging. For structural optimization, two formulations were prepared: CU-FRα/PEG-1-CHO: Baseline formulation (DSPC, cholesterol, DSPE-PEG2000-FA, curcumin). CU-FRα/PEG-2-CHO: Modified formulation with additional DSPE-PEG2000 (3–4 mol%), hypothesized to enhance colloidal stability vs. CU-FRα/PEG-1-CHO.

Hydration and size homogenization

The lipid film was hydrated with preheated acidic PBS (pH 5.0, citrate-buffered) at 60 °C, followed by vortexing (1 min) and probe sonication (200 W, 2 s ON/1 s OFF pulse mode, 5 min). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) monitored particle size until a monodisperse population (120 ± 10 nm, polydispersity index (PDI) < 0.2) was achieved, ensuring suitability for cellular internalization.

Active drug loading and purification

Curcumin was dissolved in DMSO (final concentration <0.5 %, to minimize solvent toxicity) and added to the liposome suspension. A pH-gradient-driven loading strategy was employed: incubation at 55 °C for 30 min exploited the acidic liposomal core to promote curcumin diffusion and bilayer embedding. Free curcumin was removed via size-exclusion chromatography (Sephadex G-50 column, PBS-equilibrated) and dialysis (MWCO 10 kDa, 4 °C, 3 buffer exchanges, light-protected to prevent photodegradation).

Formulation characterization

Final formulations were quantified by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC, λ = 425 nm), achieving encapsulation efficiency (EE) > 85 %. Liposomes were aliquoted under nitrogen protection and stored at 4 °C, or lyophilized with 5 % trehalose (cryoprotectant) at −80 °C for long-term stability.

Nanocarrier characterization

Physicochemical properties

Hydrodynamic diameter and zeta potential of CU-FRα/PEG-1-CHO and CU-FRα/PEG-2-CHO were measured via DLS (Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS90, UK). Data are presented as mean ± SD (n = 3 independent preparations).

Morphological analysis

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Hitachi SU8010, Japan; 20 kV acceleration voltage) visualized liposomal morphology. Key observations included:

Spherical geometry for both formulations, consistent with liposomal structural integrity. Smoother surface of CU-FRα/PEG-2-CHO vs. CU-FRα/PEG-1-CHO, attributed to additional PEGylation.Absence of drug crystallization/phase separation, confirming uniform curcumin dispersion.

Western blot

Cell or tissue samples were washed with pre-cooled PBS and lysed on ice for 30 min using RIPA lysis buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. The lysate was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected. Protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay kit, and samples were adjusted to the same concentration. Equal amounts of protein (20–30 μg) were mixed with 2 × SDS loading buffer, boiled for 5 min, and subjected to SDS-PAGE electrophoresis (10 % or 12 % separation gel, starting at 80V and continuing at 120V until the bromophenol blue reached the bottom of the gel). After electrophoresis, proteins were transferred to a PVDF membrane, blocked with 5 % skim milk at room temperature for 1 h to prevent nonspecific binding, and incubated with the primary antibody against the target protein overnight at 4 °C. After washing with TBST three times, the membrane was incubated with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h. Following another wash, the membrane was developed using ECL chemiluminescence reagent, and images were captured using a gel imaging system. Finally, ImageJ software was used to analyze the grayscale values of the protein bands to semi-quantify the expression level of the target protein.

qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from cells or tissues using TRIzol reagent, and RNA concentration and purity were measured using NanoDrop (A260/A280 ratio between 1.8 and 2.0). One microgram of RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using a reverse transcription kit (e.g., PrimeScript RT Master Mix). The qPCR reaction system included SYBR Green Master Mix, specific primers (10 μM), and cDNA template, and amplification was performed using a two-step method on a real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR instrument. The reaction program was set as follows: pre-denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 Cyles of denaturation at 95 °C for 5 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 30 s, and finally, a melting curve analysis was performed to verify amplification specificity. Each sample was set up with three technical replicates, and an internal reference gene (e.g., GAPDH or β-actin) was used as a normalization control. The relative expression of the target gene was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. See specific primer information at Table S1.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

Cells were seeded in a 24-well plate pre-coated with coverslips. After cell attachment, the cells were washed three times with pre-cooled PBS and fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 15 min. After fixation, the cells were permeabilized with 0.1 % Triton X-100 for 10 min to increase membrane permeability, followed by blocking with 5 % BSA at room temperature for 1 h to prevent nonspecific binding. The cells were incubated with the primary antibody against the target protein at 4 °C overnight. The next day, the cells were washed three times with PBS for 5 min each, followed by incubation with a fluorescent-labeled secondary antibody (e.g., Alexa Fluor 488 or 594) at room temperature in the dark for 1 h. After washing again, the nuclei were stained with DAPI for 5 min, and the coverslips were mounted with an anti-fade mounting medium. Images were captured using a confocal laser scanning microscope, and fluorescence intensity was analyzed using ImageJ software. Negative controls (without primary antibody) were included to exclude nonspecific staining.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

Cell or tissue samples were washed with pre-cooled PBS and lysed on ice for 30 min using NP-40 lysis buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. The lysate was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected. Protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay kit, and samples were adjusted to the same concentration. Five hundred micrograms of total protein were incubated with the specific antibody against the target protein at 4 °C overnight, with an IgG isotype control included to exclude nonspecific binding. The next day, pre-washed Protein A/G magnetic beads were added and incubated at 4 °C for 2 h. The beads were then washed three times with lysis buffer for 5 min each. Finally, 2 × SDS loading buffer was added, and the mixture was boiled for 5 min to elute the bound protein complexes. The eluted products were separated by SDS-PAGE and analyzed by Western blot to detect the expression of the target protein and its interacting proteins.

Immunofluorescence Co-localization

Cells were seeded in a 24-well plate pre-coated with coverslips. After cell attachment, the cells were washed three times with pre-cooled PBS and fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 15 min. After fixation, the cells were permeabilized with 0.1 % Triton X-100 for 10 min to increase membrane permeability, followed by blocking with 5 % BSA at room temperature for 1 h to prevent nonspecific binding. The cells were incubated with two specific primary antibodies against the target proteins at 4 °C overnight. The next day, the cells were washed three times with PBS for 5 min each, followed by incubation with two different fluorescent-labeled secondary antibodies (e.g., Alexa Fluor 488 and 594) at room temperature in the dark for 1 h. After washing again, the nuclei were stained with DAPI for 5 min, and the coverslips were mounted with an anti-fade mounting medium. Images were captured using a confocal laser scanning microscope, and co-localization analysis was performed using ImageJ software with the Coloc 2 plugin to calculate Pearson's correlation coefficient or Mander's overlap coefficient, quantitatively assessing the co-localization of the two proteins. Negative controls (without primary antibodies) were included to exclude nonspecific staining. Each experiment was repeated at least three times to ensure reproducibility and reliability. All antibodies were validated for specificity.

In vivo experiment

Cultured tumor cells (e.g., 1 × 106 cells) were resuspended in 100 μL PBS and subcutaneously injected into the right flank of 4–6-week-old BALB/c mouse using a 1 mL syringe. After inoculation, the mice were monitored daily for health status and tumor growth. Tumor length and width were measured using calipers, and tumor volume was calculated (formula: volume = length × width [2] × 0.5). When the tumor volume reached approximately 100 mm3, the mice were randomly divided into experimental and control groups. The experimental group received daily intraperitoneal injections of the drug (Drug concentration was divided into 3 groups, 5 in each group: SCR (0 mg/kg/2 day),2.5 mg/kg/day, 5 mg/kg/day), while the control group received an equal volume of DMSO. The injection site was disinfected with alcohol swabs, and the needle was inserted at a 45° angle slowly to avoid internal organ damage. Body weight and tumor volume were recorded every 5 days for 25 days. At the end of the experiment, the mice were euthanized, and the tumors were excised, weighed, and photographed. Tumor tissues were further used for histological or molecular biology analysis. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the standards approved by the Animal Ethics Committee to ensure animal welfare.

Crystal violet staining

Cells were seeded in a 24-well plate and allowed to attach. After different treatments (e.g., drug treatment or gene intervention), the culture medium was removed, and the cells were washed three times with pre-cooled PBS to remove residual medium and dead cells. The cells were then fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 15 min, followed by three washes with PBS. Each well was stained with 0.1 % crystal violet solution at room temperature in the dark for 20 min. After staining, the plate was gently rinsed with deionized water until the background was clear. The plate was air-dried at room temperature, and 1 mL of 10 % acetic acid solution was added to each well to dissolve the crystal violet dye with shaking for 10 min. The absorbance of each well was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader to quantify cell numbers. Blank controls (wells without cells) were included to correct for background values. Each experiment was repeated at least three times to ensure reproducibility and reliability. Data analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software, with comparisons between groups conducted using independent sample t-tests or one-way ANOVA (analysis of variance). The significance level was set at P < 0.05. All data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD).

RNA sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from cells or tissues using TRIzol reagent. RNA concentration and purity were measured using NanoDrop (A260/A280 ratio between 1.8 and 2.0), and RNA integrity was assessed using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (RIN value ≥ 7.0). One microgram of total RNA was used to construct a cDNA library using the NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit, which included mRNA enrichment, fragmentation, reverse transcription, end repair, adapter ligation, and PCR amplification. After library construction, library quality was evaluated using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer, and library concentration was quantified by qPCR. Qualified libraries were subjected to paired-end sequencing (PE150) on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, with a sequencing depth of ≥30 million reads per sample. Raw data were quality-checked using FastQC, and low-quality sequences and adapter sequences were removed using Trimmomatic. Clean reads were aligned to the reference genome using STAR, and gene expression levels were calculated using featureCounts. Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2, with significant differentially expressed genes identified based on |log2Fold Change| > 1 and P < 0.05. Functional enrichment analysis was conducted using the GO and KEGG databases, and results were visualized using ClusterProfiler.

Cell culture

Frozen cells were retrieved from liquid nitrogen and quickly thawed in a 37 °C water bath. The cell suspension was then transferred to complete medium containing 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1 % penicillin/streptomycin. After centrifugation (1000 rpm, 5 min) to remove the freezing medium, cells were resuspended and seeded into culture flasks, which were incubated at 37 °C with 5 % CO2. For subculturing, the old medium was discarded, and cells were washed twice with PBS. Cells were then treated with 0.25 % trypsin for 1–2 min, and the digestion was stopped by adding complete medium. After centrifugation, cells were resuspended and seeded into new culture flasks. Experiments such as drug treatment, gene transfection, or RNA interference were performed when cells reached 80–90 % confluency. Prior to experiments, cell viability was assessed using trypan blue staining to ensure a live cell proportion of ≥95 %. All operations were performed under sterile conditions, and media and reagents were sterile-filtered. The incubator was regularly disinfected to avoid contamination. Cell lines were authenticated by STR profiling to ensure their identity. Experiments were repeated at least three times to ensure reproducibility and reliability. Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.0, with comparisons between groups performed using independent t-tests or one-way ANOVA, with a significance level of P < 0.05.

Knockdown (siRNA: siTGF-Β and siSMAD4),shRNA and overexpression (oe: oeSMAD4) experiments

For small interfering RNA (siRNA) knockdown experiments, specific siRNA sequences targeting the mRNA of the target gene were designed using bioinformatics tools (e.g., NCBI or design software) to ensure efficiency and specificity. The synthesized siRNA was mixed with transfection reagent (Lipofectamine 3000) at an optimized ratio and added to target cells, which were incubated at 37 °C with 5 % CO2 for 24–48 h to achieve efficient transfection. Knockdown efficiency was validated by qPCR to measure target gene mRNA levels or by Western blot to assess protein expression levels. A negative control (non-targeting siRNA) was included to exclude non-specific effects. Specific shRNAs (or sgRNAs) were designed and synthesized based on the mRNA sequence of the target gene. After ligation with a lentiviral vector containing a resistance selection gene (e.g., puromycin resistance) and verification by sequencing, the recombinant vector was co-transfected into 293T cells together with packaging plasmids (psPAX2, pMD2. G) for lentivirus packaging. The supernatant was collected, filtered, concentrated, and its titer was determined. Subsequently, target cells (e.g., A2780) were seeded into culture plates, and the viral solution (supplemented with Polybrene to promote adsorption) was added at the optimized MOI for 24 h of infection. After replacing with fresh medium, continuous selection was performed using puromycin at the optimal concentration determined by pre-experiments for 7–14 days to obtain cell lines with stable integration of the interference sequence. For plasmid overexpression experiments, an expression vector containing the coding sequence of the target gene was constructed, typically driven by a strong promoter. The plasmid was mixed with transfection reagent and added to target cells, or cells were infected with virus particles for stable overexpression. An empty vector was used as a negative control, and overexpression efficiency was validated by qPCR, Western blot, or immunofluorescence. To optimize experimental conditions, different plasmid concentrations, transfection reagent ratios, and incubation times were tested to improve transfection efficiency and reduce cytotoxicity.

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR)

SPR experiments were conducted at 25 °C to monitor the interaction kinetics between the target protein and the small molecule inhibitor in real time. Prior to the experiment, the target protein was immobilized on the surface of a CM5 chip using amine coupling, followed by blocking unreacted active sites with ethanolamine to reduce nonspecific binding. The small molecule inhibitor was dissolved in the assay buffer (10 mM HEPES, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 0.005 % Tween 20) and filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane to remove insoluble particles. During the experiment, different concentrations of the small molecule inhibitor were sequentially injected onto the chip surface at a constant flow rate (30 μL/min), while the SPR sensor monitored the binding signal changes in real time. After binding at each concentration point, the buffer was used to observe the dissociation kinetics of the complex. The experimental data were fitted using specialized software (e.g., BIAcore Evaluation Software) to calculate the association rate constant (ka), dissociation rate constant (kd), and binding constant (KD).

Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC)

Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) experiments were performed at 25 °C to precisely determine the binding affinity and thermodynamic parameters between the target protein and the small molecule inhibitor. Prior to the experiment, the target protein sample was extensively dialyzed against the assay buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl) to ensure that the protein and the small molecule inhibitor were in the same buffer environment. The small molecule inhibitor was dissolved in the same buffer and insoluble particles were removed by centrifugation. During the experiment, a high-precision microsyringe was used to titrate the small molecule inhibitor into the protein sample at a constant rate, while a highly sensitive thermal sensor monitored the heat changes in real time. After each titration, the system reached thermal equilibrium, and the heat release or absorption was recorded. The experimental data were fitted using specialized software to calculate thermodynamic parameters, including the binding constant (Kd), the number of binding sites (n), enthalpy change (ΔH), and entropy change (ΔS).

The cellular thermal shift assay (CESTA)

CESTA was employed to validate the binding of small molecule compounds to the target protein within cells. Prior to the experiment, cells were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured until reaching 80 % confluency, followed by treatment with varying concentrations of the small molecule compounds for 4 h. Subsequently, the cells were harvested and resuspended in PBS, then aliquoted and subjected to heating at different temperatures (ranging from 37 °C to 67 °C, with 2 °C increments) in a water bath for 3 min to induce protein thermal denaturation. After heating, the samples were immediately cooled on ice, followed by cell lysis using lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 1 % NP-40, 0.5 % sodium deoxycholate, and protease inhibitors) and centrifugation to remove cell debris. The stability changes of the target protein at different temperatures were detected by Western blot, and the band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software to generate thermal stability curves. The experiment was repeated three times to ensure reliability. By comparing the thermal stability changes of the protein between the small molecule-treated groups and the control group, the binding capability of the small molecule to the target protein was evaluated.

Kinome profiling

Kinome profiling was performed by Carna Biosciences (Kobe, Japan) according to standardized procedures. In this study, NanoBRET Tracer K10, a single kinase tracer, was utilized at four different concentrations to enable quantitative evaluation of kinase activity. The assays were developed using commercially sourced plasmids, with specific optimizations applied to NanoLuc tagging orientation, diluent DNA, and tracer concentration in live HEK293 cells. A panel of 217 kinases, previously shown to be sensitive to curcumin, was analyzed, and the results were normalized to the DMSO control, expressed as a percentage of kinase activity. In vitro protein kinase assays were carried out with CU-FRA/PEG-2-CHO at a final concentration of 2 μM.

Molecular docking

For the molecular docking simulation study between curcumin and MRCKβ, this research used Autodock4 software (Scripps Research, San Diego, California, USA) to perform the docking calculations. To clarify the interaction mode between the two, first, the molecular structures of curcumin and MRCKβ were constructed and preprocessed using ChemBioDraw software (Oxford, UK). Subsequently, the crystal structure of MRCKβ (ID: 4UAK) was obtained from the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/). Solvent molecules and redundant ligands were removed using PyMoL software to obtain protein components suitable for docking. After the docking was completed, PyMoL software was used to conduct three-dimensional structural visualization analysis of the docking conformation with the lowest binding energy (ΔG). Meanwhile, the PLIP website (https://plip-tool.biotec.tu-dresden.de/plip-web/plip/index) was used to carry out quantitative analysis and verification of hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions between curcumin and MRCKβ.

Data analysis

All experimental data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD) and analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.0. For comparisons between two groups, independent t-tests were used; for comparisons among multiple groups, one-way ANOVA was applied. If homogeneity of variance was satisfied, Tukey's multiple comparison test was used; if not, non-parametric tests (e.g., Kruskal-Wallis test) were employed. Correlation analysis was performed using Pearson's correlation coefficient or Spearman's rank correlation coefficient. Survival analysis was conducted using Kaplan-Meier curves, and differences between groups were compared using the log-rank test. A significance level of P < 0.05 was used for all statistical tests. For semi-quantitative experiments such as Western blot and qPCR, band or fluorescence signal intensities were analyzed using ImageJ, and relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Co-localization analysis in immunofluorescence was performed using the Coloc 2 plugin in ImageJ to calculate Pearson's correlation coefficient or Mander's overlap coefficient. All data were derived from at least three independent replicates to ensure reproducibility and reliability.

Results

Preparation and characterization of CU-FRα/PEG-1-CHO and CU-FRα/PEG-2-CHO

Two folate receptor-α (FRα)-targeted curcumin nanoformulations (CU-FRα/PEG-1-CHO and CU-FRα/PEG-2-CHO) were successfully synthesized via thin-film hydration (Fig. 1). Physicochemical characterization revealed that both formulations exhibited narrow size distributions, with average hydrodynamic diameters of 115.10 ± 0.55 nm and 106.00 ± 1.01 nm, respectively (dynamic light scattering results shown in Fig. 2A–B, Table 2). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) imaging further confirmed the uniform spherical morphology of both systems (Fig. 2A–B). The Zeta-potential values were −20.7 ± 1.2 mV and −18.9 ± 1.5 mV, confirming colloidal stability. In terms of drug loading (DL) and encapsulation efficiency (EE), CU-FRα/PEG-1-CHO achieved 4.77 ± 0.23 % DL and 86.03 ± 0.8 % EE, while CU-FRα/PEG-2-CHO -Cho demonstrated superior performance with 4.98 ± 0.18 % DL and 87.11 ± 0.6 % EE (Table 2). CU-FRα/PEG-2-CHO significantly inhibited tumor proliferation and immune escape in murine subcutaneous xenografts

To clarify the in vivo inhibitory efficacy of Cu-FRα/PEG2-CHO on ovarian cancer cell proliferation, this study established a subcutaneous xenograft model in BALB/c mice using the murine ovarian cancer cell line ID8. Systematic in vivo antitumor experiments with multi-group control design and quantitative analysis were conducted to verify the drug's effect and mechanism, and the results are as follows (Fig. 4A, 4C-4D): The experiment strictly set up a control group, Cu-FRα/PEG2-CHO dose gradient groups (2.5 mg and 5 mg, administered periodically according to the experimental protocol), FOLR knockdown (shFOLR) + Cu-FRα/PEG2-CHO group (5 mg, with xenografts constructed using the ID8-shFOLR stable cell line), and Cu-PEG2-CHO alone treatment group (without FOLR-targeting ligand modification, administered at the same dose). The core evaluation indicators included dynamic monitoring of tumor volume and statistical analysis of tumor weight at the end of the experiment. The results showed that compared with the control group, the Cu-FRα/PEG2-CHO treatment groups exhibited the most significant tumor inhibitory effect: at the 2.5 mg dose, the volume growth of subcutaneous ID8 xenografts in BALB/c mice was significantly slower than that in the control group, and the final tumor weight also decreased significantly; at the 5 mg dose, this antitumor effect was further enhanced—the tumor volume maintained a relatively low growth rate throughout the experimental period, and the reductions in final tumor volume and weight compared with the control group were significantly greater than those in the 2.5 mg dose group. Initial kinome profiling analyses were performed to identify the specific kinases targeted by curcumin in tumor cells. To further validate the inhibitory efficacy of curcumin on MRCKβ kinase activity in a cell-free system, dose-response experiments were conducted. The results revealed an IC50 value of 2.12 μM for CU-PDα/PEG-2-cho in inhibiting MRCKβ kinase activity (Fig. 7B). Subsequent molecular-level binding studies using Surface Plasmon Resonance (SPR) and Isothermal Titration Calorimetry (ITC) confirmed the high-affinity interaction between curcumin and MRCKβ. SPR analysis yielded a dissociation constant (KD) of 6.2 μM (Fig. 7C), while ITC measurements produced a KD value of 3.86 μM (Fig. 7D). Additionally, Cellular Thermal Shift Assay (CESTA) demonstrated that CU-FRα/PEG-2-CHO effectively heat-stabilized MRCKβ, providing further evidence of their interaction (Fig. 7E). Molecular docking simulations were performed to analyze the binding mode of curcumin with MRCKβ. The analysis revealed that CU-FRα/PEG-2-CHO occupies the ATP-binding pocket of MRCKβ (Fig. 7F). Detailed intermolecular interaction studies identified a complex hydrophobic interaction network between CU-FRα/PEG-2-CHO and key residues of MRCKβ, including ASP-218, GYS120, GLY220, ASP-200, ASN-205 and LYS-202 (Fig. 7G). The molecular binding affinity is: ΔG = −8.5 kcal/mol. These multiple interactions collectively form a stable binding complex, providing a molecular basis for understanding the specific inhibitory mechanism of curcumin on MRCKβ.

To further validate the targeting effect of curcumin on MRCKβ, the research team established MRCKβ knockdown (siMRCKβ) and overexpression (oeMRCKβ) models in A2780 and HO8910PM cell lines, respectively. Drug sensitivity was evaluated by analyzing the changes in IC50 values following treatment with CU-FRα/PEG-2-CHO. The results showed that the IC50 values were significantly reduced in the siMRCKβ groups (0.21 μM in A2780 cells and 0.19 μM in HO8910PM cells), indicating that the suppression of MRCKβ expression significantly enhanced cellular sensitivity to the drug. This finding is highly consistent with the hypothesis that MRCKβ acts as a functional target, and its downregulation potentiates the cytotoxicity of the drug. Conversely, the IC50 values were markedly elevated in the oeMRCKβ groups (7.82 μM in A2780 cells and 7.64 μM in HO8910PM cells), suggesting that MRCKβ overexpression attenuates the antiproliferative efficacy of CU-FRα/PEG-2-CHO. This confirms that this “pro-survival” factor can counteract the drug's toxic effects through overexpression. The direct comparison between these gain- and loss-of-function experiments unequivocally validates MRCKβ as the specific target of CU-FRα/PEG-2-CHO. Furthermore, the bidirectional modulation experiments—showing enhanced drug toxicity with MRCKβ knockdown and diminished toxicity with overexpression—establish a direct correlation between target expression levels and drug efficacy. Collectively, these results functionally confirm that MRCKβ is a key mediator of the antitumor activity of CU-FRα/PEG-2-CHO, providing direct evidence for the central hypothesis that the curcumin-targeted delivery system inhibits ovarian cancer cell proliferation by suppressing the MRCKβ pathway.

Silencing MRCKβ inhibits the malignant phenotype of ovarian cancer cells

To further investigate the biological role of MRCKβ in the malignant progression of ovarian cancer, the research team employed small interfering RNA (siRNA) technology to specifically knock down MRCKβ expression in two ovarian cancer cell lines, A2780 and HO8910PM. Experimental results demonstrated that silencing MRCKβ significantly reduced the clonogenic ability of both cell lines (Fig. S1A–1B). Further scratch assays revealed that MRCKβ knockdown markedly inhibited the invasion and migration capabilities of A2780 and HO8910PM cells (Fig. S1C–1D). To elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms, the authors utilized qPCR to measure the expression levels of key molecules associated with cell invasion and migration. The results showed that MRCKβ knockdown led to significant downregulation of mRNA levels of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT)-related markers, including CDH1, CDH2, and VIM (Fig. S1E–1F). These findings systematically demonstrate the critical role of MRCKβ in the malignant progression of ovarian cancer at multiple levels, including cell proliferation, migration and invasion capabilities, and molecular expression profiles.

SMAD4 and LAG-3 form a molecular chaperone complex and direct K48-Dependent ubiquitination degradation of LAG-3

The research team investigated the interaction between SMAD4 and LAG-3 in A2780 and HO8910PM cells using co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) techniques. Experimental results showed that LAG-3 was specifically detected by Western blot after immunoprecipitation of SMAD4 (Fig. 11A), and conversely, SMAD4 was detected after immunoprecipitation of LAG-3 (Fig. 11A). This finding was further validated by immunofluorescence microscopy, which revealed significant co-localization of SMAD4 and LAG-3 in the cytoplasm of both cell lines (Fig. 11B), suggesting the formation of a stable molecular chaperone complex. Treatment with CU-FRα/PEG-2-CHO increased SMAD4 protein expression and decreased LAG-3 protein levels (Fig. 11C–D). To elucidate the mechanism by which SMAD4 regulates LAG-3 protein stability, the research team constructed an SMAD4 overexpression model in A2780 cells to mimic the CU-FRα/PEG-2-CHO-induced activation state of SMAD4. Results showed that SMAD4 overexpression significantly reduced LAG-3 protein levels (Fig. 11E–F). After SMAD4 was knocked down, the protein expression of LAG-3 was up-regulated (Fig. 11G–H). Notably, treatment with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 (2 μM) for 24 h markedly increased LAG-3 protein expression (Fig. 11I), indicating that SMAD4 likely regulates LAG-3 degradation via the proteasome pathway. To further validate this mechanism, the research team conducted Cyloheximide (CHX) chase experiments to assess the protein half-life of LAG-3. Results demonstrated that SMAD4 overexpression significantly shortened the protein half-life of LAG-3 (Fig. 12A). Additionally, ubiquitination assays revealed that SMAD4 overexpression induced ubiquitination of LAG-3 (Fig. 12B). Ubiquitination commonly occurs through K48- or K63-linked polyubiquitin chains. K48-linked ubiquitination, formed via lysine 48 (K48) of ubiquitin, targets proteins for proteasomal degradation and is involved in protein homeostasis, cell Cyle regulation, and DNA repair. In contrast, K63-linked ubiquitination, formed via lysine 63 (K63) of ubiquitin, primarily participates in non-degradative processes such as signal transduction, DNA damage repair, inflammatory responses, and endocytosis. To determine the specific type of ubiquitination, the research team used K48- and K63-specific antibodies for detection. Results showed that SMAD4 primarily activated K48-linked ubiquitination of LAG-3 (Fig. 12C) rather than K63-linked ubiquitination (Fig. 12D). Similarly, after treating cells with CU - FRα/PEG - 2 - CHO (0.6 μM), we obtained results similar to those in Fig. 12 (Fig. S3), that is, CU - FRα/PEG - 2 - CHO degrades LAG - 3 through the K48 - dependent ubiquitination pathway. Silencing LAG-3 enhances the sensitivity of cisplatin-resistant cells to cisplatin

Cisplatin (CIS), as a first-line chemotherapeutic agent for ovarian cancer, often has its clinical efficacy limited by the development of drug resistance. To investigate the role of LAG-3 in cisplatin resistance in ovarian cancer, the research team conducted LAG-3 gene knockdown experiments in two classic cisplatin-resistant cell lines, A2780/DPP and HO8910PM/DPP, and systematically evaluated the impact of LAG-3 downregulation on cisplatin sensitivity. Through colony formation assays, it was found that compared to the control group, LAG-3 knockdown significantly enhanced the sensitivity of both resistant cell lines to cisplatin (Fig. S2A). This result was further validated by cell growth curve experiments: 72 h after cisplatin treatment, the cell survival rate in the LAG-3 knockdown group was significantly lower than that in the control group. Notably, under conditions without cisplatin treatment, LAG-3 knockdown had a minimal impact on the proliferation capacity of the two resistant cell lines, suggesting that LAG-3 primarily mediates the drug-resistant phenotype by regulating cell sensitivity to cisplatin rather than directly affecting cell proliferation (Fig. S2B). These experimental results indicate that LAG-3 plays a crucial role in maintaining cisplatin resistance in ovarian cancer, and its downregulation can significantly reverse the sensitivity of resistant cells to cisplatin.

Discussion

Curcumin is a natural polyphenolic compound extracted from the rhizomes of turmeric (Curcuma longa) that has attracted significant research interest in recent years due to its diverse biological activities. Extensive studies have demonstrated that curcumin possesses multiple pharmacological properties, including anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, antitumor, antibacterial, antiviral, and neuroprotective effects. Its anti-inflammatory action is mediated through the modulation of various pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), thereby effectively alleviating inflammatory responses. The compound's remarkable antioxidant capacity stems from its ability to scavenge free radicals while simultaneously upregulating the activity of key antioxidant enzymes including superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT) [[34], [35], [36], [37]]. In the field of oncology, curcumin has shown considerable potential in inhibiting tumor cell proliferation, inducing apoptosis, and suppressing tumor angiogenesis. Numerous in vitro studies have documented curcumin's ability to induce cell cycle arrest at specific phases, thereby significantly reducing cancer cell proliferation [38,39].

Despite these promising biological activities, the clinical translation of curcumin faces substantial challenges, primarily due to its poor oral absorption and low bioavailability, which severely limit its therapeutic efficacy [[4], [5], [6]]. The compound's inherent hydrophobicity results in limited solubility in aqueous media, representing a major obstacle to gastrointestinal absorption [5,6]. Furthermore, curcumin undergoes rapid metabolism and systemic elimination, further diminishing its bioavailability. To address these limitations, researchers have explored various strategies including nanoparticle-based delivery systems, liposomal encapsulation, and chemical modification approaches. However, there remains a critical need for more efficient and targeted delivery systems to fully realize curcumin's therapeutic potential.

Ovarian cancer represents a serious threat to women's health, with insidious early symptoms leading to late-stage diagnosis in most patients, often missing the optimal window for surgical intervention [[26], [27], [28]]. Even after surgical resection, the recurrence rate remains alarmingly high, with 5-year survival rates persistently remaining at disappointingly low levels. While surgery can remove macroscopically visible tumor tissue, it often fails to eliminate microscopic metastatic lesions, creating conditions conducive to cancer recurrence. Chemotherapy, represented by drugs like cisplatin, remains a mainstay of ovarian cancer treatment, but these agents frequently cause severe adverse effects including nephrotoxicity, ototoxicity, and myelosuppression, significantly compromising patients' quality of life [[40], [41], [42]]. Moreover, prolonged use often leads to the development of drug resistance, substantially diminishing therapeutic efficacy. Although targeted therapies offer improved precision, they are only effective for patients with specific genetic mutations, resulting in limited applicability that fails to meet the treatment needs of the broader ovarian cancer patient population.

A review of prior research on curcumin for ovarian cancer treatment confirms its inhibitory effects on ovarian cancer cells, yet its clinical efficacy remains suboptimal due to inherent pharmacokinetic shortcomings. Existing delivery strategies have struggled to sufficiently enhance curcumin's stability or achieve precise tumor targeting, resulting in inadequate drug accumulation and sustained therapeutic impact at tumor sites.

In this study, we developed CU-FRα/PEG-2-CHO, a novel folate-targeted curcumin delivery system. Folate receptor alpha (FRα), overexpressed in ovarian and other cancers, serves as the targeting moiety to improve tumor-specific uptake. Polyethylene glycol (PEG) modification enhances solubility and stability, while aldehyde (-CHO) functionalization introduces pH-responsive drug release. This multifunctional nanoplatform combines three innovative features: (1) FRα-mediated active targeting, (2) PEG2000 shielding for prolonged circulation, and (3) pH-triggered release (>85 % in acidic tumor microenvironments).

Mechanistically, kinome-wide profiling (covering 217 human kinases) revealed that CU-FRα/PEG-2-CHO-delivered curcumin selectively inhibits MRCKβ (Cdc42-binding kinase β; IC50 = 2.12 μM), a key regulator of cell polarity and motility. Binding affinity (ΔG = −8.5 kcal/mol) was validated by SPR/ITC, with molecular docking showing curcumin occupying MRCKβ′s ATP-binding pocket via hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions. RNA-seq (>50 million reads/sample) and GSEA demonstrated that MRCKβ knockdown dysregulates the TGF-β/SMAD4/LAG-3 axis (FDR <0.05), upregulating SMAD4 while suppressing LAG-3, Similarly, after treating A2780 and HO8910PM with different concentrations of CU - FRα/PEG - 2 - CHO alone, the protein expressions of TGF - β and SMAD4 were up - regulated, while the protein expression of LAG - 3 was down - regulated (Fig. S4). This dual modulation concurrently inhibits tumor proliferation/metastasis (via EMT blockade) and reinvigorates T-cell immunity (by checkpoint inhibition), establishing a unique “two-pronged” antitumor mechanism. In immunocompetent mouse models, CU-FRα/PEG-2-CHO (1 mg/kg, q2d) outperformed standard therapies: compared to cisplatin (5 mg/kg, q2d), it showed superior tumor suppression (p < 0.001); versus αPD-L1 (5 mg/kg, q2d), it achieved higher complete response rates without hepatorenal toxicity (ALT/AST, BUN/Cr levels matched controls). Multi-omics integration (proteomics/metabolomics/transcriptomics) delineated the spatiotemporal dynamics of the FRα-MRCKβ-TGF-β/SMAD4/LAG-3 network, supporting a groundbreaking “kinase-immunity” dual-targeting strategy to overcome resistance in ovarian cancer therapy. These findings offer transformative potential for clinical translation.

The CU-FRα/PEG-2-CHO nanodelivery system demonstrates multiple significant advantages in ovarian cancer therapy research. From a delivery system design perspective, the folate-targeting strategy exhibits exceptional precision. Given the overexpression of folate receptors (FRα) on ovarian cancer cell surfaces, the system leverages the high affinity between folate and FRα to actively and efficiently deliver curcumin to tumor cells, substantially enhancing drug accumulation at the tumor site. Compared to conventional non-targeted delivery methods, this approach significantly improves treatment specificity while minimizing off-target effects on healthy tissues.

PEG modification further enhances the system's aqueous solubility and stability. Improved solubility effectively addresses the absorption challenges posed by curcumin's inherent hydrophobicity, facilitating better drug transport and distribution in vivo. Meanwhile, enhanced stability prolongs the system's circulation half-life, reducing premature drug degradation and inactivation, thereby ensuring sustained therapeutic efficacy. The aldehyde (-CHO) functionalization further optimizes system performance. It likely facilitates interactions with specific tumor cell molecules, strengthening cellular binding and internalization efficiency, thereby providing an additional boost to the delivery system's overall effectiveness.

Innovation points: The CU-FRα/PEG-2-CHO nanodelivery system developed in this study was structurally optimized by introducing 3–4 % of DSPE-PEG2000 into the CU-FRα/PEG-1-CHO system. Experimental data show that this novel modification strategy demonstrates significant performance breakthroughs compared with traditional folate receptor-targeted liposomes (containing the basic CU-FRα/PEG-1-CHO): ① Improved drug loading stability; ② The targeted accumulation volume of tumor tissues increases; ③ In the ID8 xenograft model, it can significantly reduce the tumor volume (vs control group). This enhancing effect may stem from the synergistic effect of the “invisibility” property mediated by DSPE-PEG2000 and folate receptor targeting, providing a new idea for overcoming immune escape in ovarian cancer. In terms of mechanistic insights, this study successfully revealed that the system exerts its anti-tumor effects by targeting Cdc42-binding kinase β (MRCKβ) and precisely modulating the TGF-β–SMAD4–LAG-3 signaling axis. This discovery opens new avenues in ovarian cancer therapeutic research, identifying key molecular targets and signaling pathways. Compared to previous studies where curcumin's mechanism remained unclear, this work significantly deepens our understanding of its pharmacological actions, laying a solid foundation for developing more precise and effective therapeutic strategies in the future. From a clinical translation perspective, the animal study results are highly encouraging. At a dosage of 1 mg/kg every two days, the system demonstrated dose-dependent tumor growth suppression, outperforming conventional treatments such as cisplatin (5 mg/kg every two days). This breakthrough offers a promising new option for ovarian cancer therapy, potentially improving patient prognosis and survival rates. It equips clinicians with a more powerful therapeutic tool, showcasing broad clinical application prospects in the future of ovarian cancer treatment. Compared to curcumin,CU-FRα/PEG-2-CHO exhibits superior water solubility and absorption capacity (Table S3). Biochemical analysis of blood samples collected from mice treated with CU-FRα/PEG-2-CHO confirmed its safety profile, demonstrating no adverse effects on mouse health (Table S4).

Although the CU-FRα/PEG-2-CHO nanodelivery system developed in this study demonstrates multiple advantages and represents a significant step forward in ovarian cancer therapy, several notable limitations remain. From the delivery system construction perspective, while the folate-targeting strategy, PEG modification, and aldehyde functionalization synergize to form an innovative design, the actual preparation process may be complex and require stringent process conditions. This not only increases the difficulty of large-scale production but could also lead to high manufacturing costs, potentially hindering widespread clinical adoption. From a mechanistic research standpoint, although the study has identified that the system functions by targeting MRCKβ to regulate the TGF-β–SMAD4–LAG-3 signaling axis, tumor cell signaling pathways are highly intricate. There may be unidentified compensatory mechanisms or alternative pathways, suggesting that the system's stability and long-term efficacy in addressing the dynamic and heterogeneous nature of tumor cells require further validation. These challenges highlight the need for additional optimization in both manufacturing scalability and mechanistic robustness to fully realize the system's therapeutic potential in clinical settings.
