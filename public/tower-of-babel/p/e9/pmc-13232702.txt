Cognitive Neuroelectrical Activity During Preparation for Exercise Under Hyperthermia
Authors: Manabu Shibasaki, Hiroki Nakata
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Exercise in a hot environment can impair performance due to not only impaired coordination of major physiological systems but also reduced cognitive processing related to the exercise strategy. In this review, we discuss potential factors contributing to reduced exercise performance during excessive brain temperature elevation, with a focus on the functional localization of brain activity.

KEY POINTS

Effective exercise strategies require adjustments in work output to compensate for heat-induced physiological changes. Heat stress promotes central fatigue and disrupts physiological balance, impairing cognitive processes involved in exercise strategy.

INTRODUCTION

Exercise in the heat imposes competing physiological demands related to thermoregulation, fluid balance, and cardiorespiratory control, which collectively impair the ability to maintain exercise performance (1–3). Excessive sweating reduces the blood volume, thereby restricting blood flow to the brain and active skeletal muscles, which facilitates the development of both central and peripheral fatigue (4). Central fatigue reduces the ability to sustain maximal muscle activation during prolonged contractions (5). Todd et al. (6) demonstrated that hyperthermia increases central fatigue, as indicated by a greater transcranial magnetic stimulation–evoked twitch amplitude during sustained maximal voluntary contractions (MVCs) when force production declines. Whole-body hyperthermia is associated with reduced voluntary activation during sustained or repeated contractions, supporting its role in central fatigue. Moreover, impairment of skeletal muscle perfusion sufficient to induce peripheral fatigue further limits oxygen delivery and alters muscle metabolism, thereby modulating afferent feedback and influencing the perception of fatigue (7). For example, under such physiological constraints, individuals performing self-paced exercise are likely to consciously or unconsciously reduce their exercise intensity to optimize performance. Périard et al. (8) showed that prolonged high-intensity self-paced exercise under heat stress was associated with alterations in cerebrocortical activity, as assessed by electroencephalography (EEG) and source localization analyses. Specifically, reductions in activity within prefrontal and cingulate regions were observed during exercise in the heat, which the authors interpreted, based on the known functional roles of these regions, as reflecting reduced attentional control and motivational drive. These findings suggest that maintaining high-level performance in hot environments depends critically on the integrity of neural networks involved in the regulation of exercise intensity.

Cortical perturbations within brain regions associated with motor control under heat stress can be assessed indirectly through changes in cognitive performance, particularly in domains such as decision-making and working memory. Over the past few decades, many research groups have attempted to elucidate the mechanisms underlying heat-related cognitive impairment using a variety of experimental approaches. The effects of heat stress on cognitive function are considered to be task-dependent (9–12). However, the mechanisms responsible for this task dependency remain incompletely understood. In addition, many earlier studies relied primarily on behavioral or self-reported indices, such as reaction time, error rate, perceived exertion, or subjective fatigue, which are susceptible to influences from individual differences in the skill level, task strategy, and motivation. Considering these limitations, along with methodological differences, such as variations in environmental and physiological conditions among studies, the reported effects of hyperthermia on cognition remain inconsistent (13). To overcome these limitations, neurophysiological measurements that directly reflect brain activity are required to better elucidate the neural basis of heat-induced cognitive impairments. Several approaches are available to assess brain function objectively; in this review, we focused on studies using EEG, which is relatively inexpensive and practical to implement. Among the various EEG-based approaches for evaluating cognitive function, such as event-related potentials (ERPs), spectral (frequency) analysis, and functional connectivity between brain regions, we have employed EEG-ERPs because they can be measured in a relatively short time and are readily applicable to exercise-related tasks. Although ERP components can also be influenced by factors such as task engagement and motivation, ERPs provide time-locked indices of specific cognitive processes and allow assessment of neural activity underlying cognitive function with greater specificity than behavioral measures alone. This review examines how hyperthermia influences cognitive processing related to exercise. We propose an integrative concept: the regulation of exercise intensity and performance in the heat critically depends on the balance between thermoregulatory and circulatory control systems and neural processes underlying executive function. Specifically, hyperthermia may suppress prefrontal cortical (PFC) function involved in attentional control, motivation, and decision-making, thereby impairing exercise strategies independently of cerebral blood flow reduction.

EVALUATION OF BRAIN ACTIVITY USING EEG-ERPS

ERPs, obtained by time-locked averaging of EEG signals with high temporal resolution, are widely used to evaluate the reception and processing of sensory information, as well as higher-level processing that involves selective attention, memory updating, semantic comprehension, and other types of cognitive activity (14). ERPs can be recorded when a participant receives sensory stimuli such as visual, auditory, or somatosensory inputs and consciously responds to specific stimuli, such as pressing a button or counting. ERPs consist of several components, and this review primarily focuses on the P3 component, which is elicited during cognitive processes such as attention, stimulus discrimination, and response selection (15–17). In addition, to provide a broader context for understanding cognitive and preparatory processes related to exercise under heat stress, we also describe movement-related ERPs, including the movement-related cortical potential (MRCP) and contingent negative variation (CNV), which assess motor preparation with differing cognitive demands. In a typical experimental paradigm, two types of stimuli are presented, such as high- and low-pitched tones, and participants are asked to press a response button immediately after perceiving the high-frequency auditory cue. Following the stimulus, a positive deflection appears approximately 300 milliseconds after onset. This waveform is called the P3 component, or often the P300 component, because it typically exhibits three positive peaks, occurring approximately 300 ms after the stimulus. The latency from stimulus presentation to the P3 peak reflects the speed of cognitive processing, while the amplitude represents the amount of neural activity involved. Therefore, a larger P3 amplitude indicates that a greater degree of neural activity is engaged in the cognitive processing stage. The P3 component is generated by multiple brain regions, including PFC, parietal association areas, and the temporoparietal junction, which interact dynamically to generate this complex potential (17). There are several methods for presenting stimuli to elicit the P3 component. These include the oddball paradigm, in which two types of stimuli are presented in unequal proportions (e.g., 2:8), making the target stimulus relatively infrequent; the Go/No-go task, which presents stimuli in equal proportions (e.g., 5:5) to evaluate motor execution and inhibition; and the flanker task, which includes irrelevant information to induce interference and assess selective attention and inhibitory control. The amplitude and latency of P300 show partial correlations with behavioral indices, such as reaction time and error rate; however, they primarily reflect higher-order cognitive processes unrelated to response selection processes (18,19). Thus, the P300 provides information about cognitive processing that is qualitatively distinct from that obtained through behavioral performance measures alone. In addition, near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) is a useful tool for evaluating human cognitive function; however, caution is required when it is applied under hyperthermic conditions. As body temperature increases, blood is preferentially redistributed to cutaneous capillaries to facilitate heat dissipation. Because near-infrared light penetrates extracerebral tissues, NIRS signals may be influenced by changes in oxyhemoglobin and deoxyhemoglobin within scalp capillaries, rather than accurately reflecting true cortical hemodynamics (20). Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) is a valuable tool for assessing cognitive function. However, because metallic devices cannot be brought into the scanner environment, core body temperature cannot be continuously monitored during measurements. As a result, controlling and ensuring participant safety with respect to body temperature and physical condition under heat stress may involve inherent risks.

ERPs can also be observed during the motor preparation period, and brain activity that occurs prior to movement can be evaluated using the MRCP or CNV. Although it is not impossible to record EEG signals during movement, muscle activity and body motion generate noise that interferes with the EEG signal. Therefore, this review does not address EEG activity recorded during actual movement but rather focuses on brain activity during the motor preparation phase associated with exercise strategies. In the following section, we describe MRCP in more detail, focusing on how preparatory brain activity contributes to the regulation of exercise strategies under heat stress.

MOVEMENT PREPARATION UNDER HYPERTHERMIC CONDITIONS

Using EEG, motor planning can be detected in both self-initiated and externally cued movements (21). The MRCP is described as the Bereitschaftspotential (BP) or readiness potential when the action is self-initiated, and as CNV when triggered by an external cue (22,23). Before and at the onset of voluntary movement, MRCPs are recorded across multiple centroparietal areas (23). Typically, the waveform begins with a slow, progressive negativity about 1.5–2 s before movement initiation, reaches maximal negativity around the onset, and is succeeded by a rebound-positive deflection following execution (22–24). The bilateral supplementary motor area (SMA), pre-SMA, anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), premotor cortex, and contralateral primary motor cortex (M1) are considered the main cortical sources, although some evidence also points to involvement of the ipsilateral M1 (23,25). Therefore, MRCP reflects motor preparation independent of cognitive processing related to an imperative stimulus (23). In contrast, CNV is associated with both motor preparations and cognitive processes, including expectancy, motivation, attention, and arousal (26,27). In the CNV task, subjects were given a warning stimulus (S1)—imperative stimulus (S2) paradigm. CNV develops during the interval between the warning (S1) and imperative (S2) stimuli, which are typically separated by approximately 2 s. Beyond motor preparation for an upcoming event, CNV has been linked to additional cognitive processes. CNV is mainly generated in the PFC, SMA, the premotor cortex, M1, cingulate gyrus, and subcortical structures, including the basal ganglia (27–31).

Using the different generation mechanisms of these two ERPs, we examined the effects of hyperthermia on motor preparation (32). In the MRCP task, subjects performed the movement at their own pace, whereas in the CNV task, they were instructed to respond immediately after S2. The exercise load was set at 20% MVC. MRCP begins with a slowly increasing negativity, called BP, and progresses to a steeper, later negativity starting approximately 500 ms before movement onset, called the negativity slope (NS′). CNV was divided into three phases: early-CNV (from 0.5 to 1 s after the onset of S1), middle-CNV (from 1 to 1.5 s), and late-CNV (from 1.5 to 2 s), based on previous reports (33,34). The experiments were conducted on two separate days, and the order of MRCP and CNV trials was randomized to minimize order effects. Although 18 male subjects were initially recruited, several withdrew after the first trial, resulting in different numbers of participants completing each task. Eleven subjects completed both trials, with at least 4 d between sessions and no more than 2 wk. The Go/No-go task was performed on both days to assess the effects of heat stress on cognitive function. Heat stress increased esophageal temperature by 1.5°C ± 0.1°C during the MRCP trial and 1.5°C ± 0.2°C during the CNV trial. The Go/No-go task was performed on both days, and the results were similar between the two trials. In the MRCP trial, the amplitudes of BP and NS′ did not change before or after heat stress (Fig. 1A). In contrast, in the CNV trial, CNV amplitudes in the middle and late phases were significantly reduced during heat stress (Fig. 1B). Behavioral performance did not differ between MRCP and CNV tasks. In both tasks, participants were instructed to exert grip force with their right hand as quickly and accurately as possible to match the target force trajectory once they initiated squeezing. Because the motor demands and performance requirements were essentially identical, behavioral performance did not differ between MRCP and CNV tasks. Nevertheless, despite comparable behavioral output, CNV amplitude was selectively reduced during heat stress. These findings indicate that hyperthermia does not affect brain activity during self-initiated movement but does influence it during externally triggered movement. When considering our EEG observations, it becomes evident that CNV generation engages a wider cortico-subcortical network than MRCP. Studies involving individuals with cerebellar damage have shown a complete loss of BP while CNV remains preserved (35). In contrast, Parkinson’s disease patients exhibit the opposite pattern: intact BP but attenuated CNV responses (36). Moreover, in a CNV paradigm lacking a motor response to the imperative cue (S2), a clear negative potential still appears prior to S2 (27). Overall, these findings indicate that CNV and MRCP are functionally distinct (37), even though CNV shares some cortical origins and BP-like characteristics. Hence, the influence of passive heat stress is more plausibly linked to cognitive activity in the PFC rather than motor processing in M1. As a limitation of MRCP and CNV tasks, the movement intensity was set at 20% MVC because CNV requires about 40 repetitions, and MRCP requires about 70 repetitions to improve the S/N ratio. Therefore, it is possible that the effect of hyperthermia was less marked in the low-intensity movement.

COGNITIVE PROCESSING UNDER HYPERTHERMIC CONDITIONS

Voluntary movements, including self-initiated and externally cued actions, consist of two components: planning and execution. Execution refers to the actual movement, whereas planning is generally considered to precede it (38). Based on the aforementioned results regarding the effects of hyperthermia on MRCP and CNV, it was shown that functional impairment was greater in the planning than the execution component. PFC allows for formulating and enacting plans of behavioral, linguistic, and cognitive action (39). Motor inhibition is an essential executive function required for adaptive motor control. However, it is difficult to evaluate such stopping actions because the underlying processes of inhibition are not directly observable. Using the Go/No-go task with EEG-ERPs is a useful paradigm for investigating executive and inhibitory processes. As mentioned earlier, the P300 amplitude reflects the degree of neural activity during the cognitive processing stage. We conducted Go/No-go task experiments during passive heat stress using a water-perfused suit and during active heat stress induced by cycling exercise in a hot environment (40,41). In the experiment of passive heating, the neural activities of response execution (i.e., amplitude of Go-P300 component) and response inhibition (i.e., amplitude of No-go-P300 component) were decreased when the core temperature increased by ~1.2°C (Fig. 1C and D) (42). In the exercise experiment, subjects performed four bouts of 15-min moderate cycling (at a room temperature of 20°C, ~130 bpm) under both 20°C and 35°C conditions on separate days (40). Under the 20°C condition, both executive and inhibitory cognitive processing (i.e., amplitude of Go-P300 and No-go-P300 components, respectively) did not change throughout the four sessions of moderate exercise. However, both peak amplitudes were significantly smaller in post-third and post-fourth exercise bouts under the 35°C than 20°C condition (Fig. 2). The neural network associated with executive and inhibitory processing has been examined using fMRI, and these processes include the dorsolateral (DLPFC) and ventrolateral (VLPFC) prefrontal cortices, SMA, primary sensorimotor area (SMI), ACC, temporoparietal junction, temporal and parietal lobes, and thalamus (42–44). Based upon these findings, hyperthermia may reduce neural activities in these regions. Notably, the strength of neural activity is greater in No-go than Go trials at DLPFC, VLPFC, ACC, the inferior parietal lobule, and caudate (44), which is consistent with our results.

Hyperthermia induces multiple physiological responses, many of which are directed toward heat dissipation to maintain thermal homeostasis. Therefore, not only an excessive increase in brain temperature but also other physiological responses to heat stress may contribute to reduced cognitive processing. When the core temperature increases approximately 1.0°C or more, it reduces cerebral blood velocity and cerebral vascular conductance (45–47). The reduced cerebral perfusion results from a redistribution of blood toward cutaneous circulation and hyperventilation-induced hypocapnia (48). The brain has a high metabolic rate and, therefore, requires continuous blood flow to supply oxygen and nutrients while removing heat. Consequently, the combination of increased cerebral metabolic rate of oxygen due to cognitive tasks and restricted cerebral blood flow due to hyperthermia further elevates the brain temperature (49). Using pseudo-continuous arterial spin labeling magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), Qian et al. (50) reported that after 1 h of environmental heat stress, perfusion in certain regions, including PFC, somatosensory areas, and the limbic system, was significantly reduced, while global cerebral blood flow tended to decrease. Similarly, Tan et al. (51) demonstrated that during passive heating using a water-perfused suit, global gray matter perfusion and regional perfusion in the sensorimotor cortex were reduced. Reduced cerebral perfusion, as well as increased brain temperature, may contribute to impairments in the brain neural network and cognitive processing. In addition, thermal comfort and arousal are impaired under hyperthermic conditions. Thermal perception is known to influence exercise performance in the heat (52), although the magnitude of this effect may depend on the type of exercise and intervention. Périard et al. (8) suggested that the rise in rate of perceived exertion and thermal discomfort during prolonged high-intensity self-paced exercise, along with an increase in core temperature, are associated with changes in cerebrocortical activity in areas linked to the inhibition of somatosensory processing, execution of motor tasks, and cognitive function. Cooling the face and/or head is a natural behavioral response to improve thermal comfort and arousal. Based upon these natural behaviors, coupled with heat stress increasing facial and head skin blood flow for heat dissipation (53), facial and head cooling may recover brain function by restoring cerebral perfusion. Then, we restored both cerebral perfusion and thermal discomfort to resting levels by cooling the face and head while maintaining an elevated core temperature (41). However, the amplitudes of both Go- and No-go trials remained decreased (Fig. 3). These findings suggest that the decline in cognitive function in hot environments is not due to reduced cerebral blood flow or increased thermal discomfort, but rather to elevated brain temperature, which disrupts neural networks within the brain. We also examined potential learning effects on the amplitude of the P300 component by employing a repeated-task design (54). In this study, participants performed the same experimental tasks under both heat stress and normothermic control conditions. Under the normothermic control condition, neither the P300 amplitude nor behavioral performance showed significant changes across repeated sessions, whereas under heat stress, the P300 amplitude decreased and reaction time was shortened. These findings indicate that the observed changes in P300 amplitude cannot be attributed to learning effects but are instead associated with heat stress.

AFTEREFFECTS OF HYPERTHERMIA ON EXECUTIVE AND INHIBITORY FUNCTIONS

The effects of cooling interventions in skill-based sports remain underexplored, even though athletes in these disciplines often experience significant thermal strain, particularly during recovery after exercise in the heat. Cold or mildly cool stimulation applied to the skin is typically experienced as pleasant and promotes wakefulness when the body temperature is markedly elevated (55). Previous studies reported mixed findings, with some showing improvements in working memory and visual processing following head cooling, while others found no benefits or effects limited to specific cognitive domains (11,56,57). As mentioned earlier, face and head cooling while maintaining hyperthermia did not restore executive or inhibitory functions (41). Body cooling is also effective for recovery from heat exhaustion. We conducted whole-body cooling after passive heat exposure to examine the recovery of cognitive function. In the first experiment, the core body temperature was elevated in two stages, by 0.8°C and 2.0°C above the resting level (54). We confirmed that cognitive function was maintained at a 0.8°C increase but declined at a 2.0°C increase. After cooling, the mean body temperature returned to the resting level, whereas the core temperature remained approximately 1.0°C higher than the baseline. Under this condition, the amplitude of the P300 component, an index of cognitive processing, did not fully return to its resting level. In the second experiment, the core temperature was increased by 1.2°C, which caused a decline in cognitive function (41). Although the mean body temperature was restored to the resting level following cooling, the core temperature remained about 0.8°C higher. Under this condition, the amplitude of the Go-P3 component had mostly recovered to the baseline except for Pz, whereas the amplitude of the No-go-P3 component, which reflects motor inhibition, remained impaired (Figs. 3 and 4). However, facial and head cooling recovered the internal carotid artery blood flow and psychological comfort toward pre-heat baseline levels (Fig. 5). Finally, in the third experiment, after confirming that cognitive function had declined with a 1.2°C elevation in core temperature, we conducted measurements once the core temperature had completely returned to the resting level (58). Under this condition, cognitive function fully returned to the baseline. These results indicate that cognitive function does not recover unless the core body temperature is sufficiently lowered to the resting level, demonstrating that partial or inadequate cooling after heat exhaustion is insufficient to fully restore cognitive function. As mentioned earlier, the P3 component is generated by multiple brain regions, including PFC, parietal association areas, and the temporoparietal junction (17). Our data suggest that neural activities within these regions are affected by heat stress and can recover following sufficient cooling. Although the precise neural mechanisms underlying these changes remain unclear, several studies have reported that the amplitude of the P3 component is associated with GABAergic and glutamatergic neurotransmission (59,60). We therefore hypothesize that alterations in these neurotransmitter systems may be related to changes in core body temperature, but further studies are required to elucidate the detailed mechanisms involved.

ADVANTAGES OF EEG-ERP MEASUREMENTS WITH BEHAVIORAL RESPONSES

Although behavioral indices such as reaction time and error rate are commonly used for simple assessments of cognitive function, behavioral measures alone may not fully capture underlying neural processes, particularly under heat stress, because these measures do not necessarily reflect underlying neural function, especially inhibitory processing. Figure 6 provides a schematic illustration of the relationship between cognitive and response processing, based on previous studies (18,19). Cognitive processing includes stimulus detection, evaluation, and categorization, which are reflected in P300 amplitude and latency. In contrast, response processing involves response selection, preparation, and execution, which are reflected in reaction time. McCarthy and Donchin (18) clearly distinguished between these processing stages. Our previous experiments consistently showed shortened reaction times during passive heat stress, a finding that could misleadingly be interpreted as enhanced cognitive performance. However, this shortening is more plausibly explained by heat-induced increases in peripheral nerve conduction velocity rather than improvements in cognitive efficiency. Indeed, our previous studies demonstrated that passive heat stress influenced neural processing at both cortical and subcortical levels (61,62) (Fig. 3). In particular, ascending afferent signals from the periphery to primary somatosensory cortex may be transmitted more rapidly under heat stress, consistent with evidence from animal studies (63,64) (Fig. 7). In our previous work, reaction times in the Flanker task were shorter during the heat stress (second session) than pre-stress (first session) session, but lengthened again during the recovery (third session) compared with heat stress session (58). This prolongation during recovery is more likely explained by peripheral nerve cooling rather than task repetition or learning effects. Furthermore, in our previous studies using the Go/No-go task, error rates showed task- and condition-dependent changes. For example, in Shibasaki et al. (41), the error rate was significantly higher during heat stress compared with the recovery session, suggesting that inhibitory control was adversely affected despite faster responses. This pattern is consistent with a speed-accuracy trade-off, in which accelerated peripheral neural conduction under heat stress coexists with impaired cognitive control processes. Taken together, these findings indicate that the shortened reaction times observed under passive heat stress primarily reflect accelerated neural conduction, rather than improvements in cognitive efficiency or practice-related facilitation, underscoring the advantage of combining EEG-ERP indices with behavioral responses for evaluating cognitive function and detecting subtle cognitive impairment under hyperthermic conditions.

The significance of the present study lies in its potential application to the prevention of heat-related illness in sports settings. Specifically, shortened reaction times may create the impression, both to observers and athletes themselves, that motor performance is enhanced. However, our findings indicate that cognitive function may be concurrently impaired under such conditions, highlighting a critical and often overlooked risk associated with heat-related illness.

CONCLUSION

Collective evidence indicates that hyperthermia impairs the cortical mechanisms responsible for executive, inhibitory, and preparatory processes that govern voluntary movement. These effects primarily arise from heat-induced neural inhibition and reduced cerebral perfusion within temperature-sensitive brain regions, rather than peripheral factors alone. Among the cortical structures involved, PFC and ACC are considered to be particularly vulnerable to heat stress, whereas SMA maintains its activity even with an elevated brain temperature. Importantly, the use of EEG-ERP provides novel insight into heat-induced cognitive dysfunction that cannot be obtained from behavioral measures or hemodynamic imaging alone. Understanding how hyperthermia modulates these neural circuits provides important mechanistic insights into heat-induced cognitive–motor dysfunction. Such knowledge may help identify vulnerable neural processes and critical thermal thresholds, thereby providing evidence to improve the timing and evaluation of interventions aimed at maintaining cognitive and motor function during exercise in hot environments.
