The mechanoresponsive chromosomal passenger complex sustains furrow ingression under confinement
Authors: Chenxin Wang, Jingjing Ding, Chao Wang, Maiyong Zhang, Junjie Wu, Bowen Chen, Hui Yang, Ting Gang Chew, Yaming Jiu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCells sense and respond to forces from neighbouring cells and the extracellular matrix during growth and division. When cells undergo mitosis in a confined environment like in the tumour environment, high compressive stress causes unstable cell cortex and prolonged mitosis. Confined mitotic cells frequently experience chromosome loss and multipolar division. How the cortical instability affects cytokinesis under confinement is unclear. Here, we show that confined mitotic cells undergo furrow ingression comparable to unconfined mitotic cells but are strongly reliant on Aurora B kinase, a catalytic subunit of the chromosomal passenger complex (CPC) for its completion. Mechanistically, the cortical pool of CPC via the scaffolding protein INCENP sustains Aurora B at the equatorial cortex to drive furrow ingression under confinement. We identified mechanoresponsive elements within the single alpha-helix domain of INCENP that maintain the cortical CPC at the equatorial cortex to promote furrow ingression in response to high compressive stress. Thus, the cortical INCENP not only binds to actin filaments but also mechanically responds to forces at the equatorial cortex to regulate the CPC during confined cytokinesis.Keywords: cytokinesis, mitosis, confinement, mechanobiology

Introduction

During cytokinesis, cells undergo an actomyosin-driven cleavage furrow ingression to partition one cell into two after chromosome segregation (Green et al., 2012; D'Avino et al., 2015; Chew et al., 2017; Leite et al., 2019; Mangione and Gould, 2019). The chromosomal passenger complex (CPC) consists of INCENP that serves as a scaffolding protein to bind two non-enzymatic subunits, Survivin and Borealin and the protein kinase Aurora B, plays an important role in coordinating mitosis and cytokinesis (Carmena et al., 2012; van der Horst and Lens, 2014; Pollard and O'Shaughnessy, 2019). The CPC, by interacting with the plus-end kinesin MKLP2, relocalizes from centromeres to the spindle midzone and the equatorial cortex, to regulate central spindle assembly and cleavage furrow ingression (Gruneberg et al., 2004; Hummer and Mayer, 2009; Kitagawa et al., 2013; van der Horst et al., 2015; Adriaans et al., 2020; Serena et al., 2020; Hadders and Lens, 2022). The CPC phosphorylates the kinesin subunit of the centralspindlin complex MKLP1, to promote the formation of centralspindlin oligomers that associate with the plasma membrane (Guse et al., 2005; Douglas et al., 2010; Basant et al., 2015; Mishima, 2016). This in turn leads to local activation of a small GTPase RhoA at the equatorial cortex to promote actomyosin contraction during cleavage furrow ingression (Yuce et al., 2005; Kamijo et al., 2006; Nishimura and Yonemura, 2006; Wagner and Glotzer, 2016).

Cells grow and divide in a confined tissue environment, which is exacerbated in tumour microenvironment dominated by cell overgrowth and extracellular matrix stiffening (Sorce et al., 2015; Nia et al., 2020; Gupta et al., 2021; Gupta and Chaudhuri, 2022; Devany et al., 2023). Breast tumours, for example have a stiffer and confining tissue environment than normal breast epithelium and experience high compressive stress ranging from 20 kPa to 200 kPa (Plodinec et al., 2012; Stylianopoulos et al., 2012; Munne et al., 2021; Zeng et al., 2024). Under confinement, elevated compressive stress derived from the cellular environment perturbs mitotic spindle morphogenesis and increases the frequency of multipolar spindle formation, leading to chromosome mis-segregation and aneuploidy during mitosis (Dumont and Mitchison, 2009; Lancaster et al., 2013; Cattin et al., 2015; Guild et al., 2017; Neahring et al., 2021; Hayes et al., 2023). To avoid deleterious aneuploidy, cancer cells engage oncogenic signaling to stiffen the cell cortex during mitosis under confinement, so that mitotic cells could maintain a cell geometry that is feasible and reliable for chromosome segregation (Hosseini et al., 2020; Matthews et al., 2020, 2021; Ganguli et al., 2023; Mouelhi et al., 2024). In cells undergoing cytokinesis, high compressive stress in a confined space also destabilizes the cell cortex, leading to extensive cell membrane blebbing or drastic cell shape change (Lancaster et al., 2013; Xi et al., 2016). How cells respond to high compressive stress during confined cytokinesis remains largely elusive. We here demonstrate that under confinement, cells could still undergo cleavage furrow ingression but rely on a mechanoresponsive mechanism involving INCENP and Aurora B at the equatorial cortex.

Results

Identification of a 57-amino-acid-motif involved in the spindle midzone localization of INCENP

While analyzing the genome structure of INCENP, we identified an INCENP isoform lacking four amino acid residues (aa) compared to the widely characterized INCENP that has 918 aa. The missing 4 aa (CSFV) are within the aa 500–680 of the single alpha-helix (SAH) domain of INCENP, which was previously shown to localize to the mitotic spindle and the cell cortex (Samejima et al., 2015; van der Horst et al., 2015; Landino and Ohi, 2016). Since the aa 500–680 of INCENP are associated with cytoskeletal structures, we named this region as the cytoskeleton-binding domain (CBD) of INCENP (INCENP-CBD) (Supplementary Figure S1A). Further computational analysis of the secondary structure of INCENP-CBD led us to divide this domain into six sub-helices (Figure 1A; Supplementary Figure S1A and B). Interestingly, when INCENP-CBD lacking CSFV (INCENP-CBDΔCSFV) was expressed, the fluorescent fusion protein failed to localize to the cell cortex while retaining its localization at the mitotic spindle (Figure 1B). To identify the key residues regulating the subcellular localizations, we generated INCENP-CBD mutants containing each of the CSFV mutated to alanines (Figure 1C). Mutations of F534 and V535 to alanine, respectively, abolished the cortical localization of INCENP-CBD, indicating the roles of these two non-polar residues in regulating cortical localization of INCENP-CBD (Figure 1D).

Next, we examined the effects of lacking CSFV in the full-length INCENP. To prevent the influence of endogenous INCENP, we used shRNA technology targeting the 3′ untranslated region of INCENP to knock down INCENP (Supplementary Figure S1C and D). In all subsequent experiments involving complementation of INCENP proteins with or without certain deletions/mutations (Supplementary Figure S1E), this shRNA knockdown strategy was employed to ensure depletion of endogenous INCENP. Our experiment showed that in contrast to INCENP-CBDΔCSFV, full-length INCENP lacking these four residues (INCENPΔCSFV) localized to the spindle midzone and the equatorial cortex in dividing cells (Figure 1E). Thus, the CSFV residues present in the full-length INCENP are not involved in the localizations of INCENP during cell division.

To test whether the sub-helices of CBD predicted computationally were involved in INCENP localization, we first prepared truncated proteins of INCENP-CBD lacking different sub-helices (Figure 1F). Interestingly, removal of sub-helices 4, 5, and 6, consisting of 57 aa (aa 623–680) of INCENP (INCENP-CBDΔ4.5.6, also known as INCENP-ΔSLM when subsequently mentioned) resulted in weaker localization at the mitotic spindle while retaining its cortical localization (Figure 1G; Supplementary Figure S1E). When we expressed INCENP proteins lacking these 57 aa, we observed an absence of INCENP at the spindle midzone while retaining cortical association albeit with less enrichment at the equatorial cortex as compared to controls (Figure 1H–J). Thus, we named this 57-aa-motif in CBD as the spindle-localization motif (SLM) (Figure 1K; Supplementary Figure S1E). INCENP protein lacking SLM (INCENP-ΔSLM) (Figure 1K) mimicked INCENP-ΔCC (Δ539–747) described previously (van der Horst et al., 2015), which contains a much larger deletion of SAH (∼208 aa), in terms of the loss of spindle midzone localization of INCENP. Taken together, we found that INCENP-ΔSLM lacking the 57-aa-motif in the SAH domain is impaired in spindle midzone localization and has a more uniform distribution at the cell cortex during cell division.

INCENP-ΔSLM expressing cells have lower efficiency in driving furrow ingression

Since INCENP-ΔSLM does not localize to the spindle midzone, which is important in the function of INCENP during mitosis and cytokinesis, we tested whether INCENP-ΔSLM expressing cells have any defects during cell division. Notably, the stably expressed fluorescently tagged INCENP were present on the chromosomes when cells entered anaphase, which was conveniently used as an indicator for chromosome segregation. Our later experiments also showed retention of Aurora B on the chromosomes in normal MCF10A cells in anaphase, consistent with our stable expression of INCENP (Supplementary Figure S3D). Whereas most cells expressing wild-type INCENP underwent furrow ingression competently, INCENP-ΔSLM expressing cells have more severe blebbing and ∼20% of mitotic cells failed to initiate or complete furrow ingression after depletion of endogenous INCENP (Figure 2A, C, and D). Crucially, majority of mitotic INCENP-ΔSLM expressing cells underwent furrow ingression. These cells have INCENP-ΔSLM uniformly distributed at the cell cortex and accumulated punctate structures near the cleavage plane and were able to complete furrow ingression (Figure 2A). Thus, our data suggested that the cortical or near-cortical INCENP-ΔSLM is capable of supporting furrow ingression despite no spindle midzone localization during anaphase.

Next, we examined the localizations of Aurora B in cells expressing INCENP-ΔSLM using immunostaining. We found that Aurora B followed the localizations of INCENP-ΔSLM in which Aurora B was absent in the spindle midzone and distributed along the cell cortex with punctate structures accumulated near the cleavage plane, suggesting that INCENP-ΔSLM still functions as a scaffold protein to regulate the CPC localization during cell division (Figure 2B and E; Supplementary Figure S2). The centralspindlin subunit MKLP1, which is a substrate of Aurora B was detected by immunostaining at the spindle midzone although the distribution at the midzone was broader and less intense in the INCENP-ΔSLM expressing cells compared to that of in cells expressing wild-type INCENP (Figure 2B, F, and G; Supplementary Figure S2). The weak but detectable MKLP1 signal at the equatorial cortex in control cells was however absent in INCENP-ΔSLM expressing cells. The detection of MKLP1 at the spindle midzone is consistent with most INCENP-ΔSLM expressing cells retaining the ability to undergo furrow ingression.

Immunostaining of MKLP2, which is a kinesin transporting CPC from centromeres to the spindle midzone and to the equatorial cortex, showed that the spindle midzone localization of MKLP2 was largely absent in cells expressing INCENP-ΔSLM while the localization at the cell cortex near the cleavage plane was retained (Figure 2B and H; Supplementary Figure S2). This is consistent with the mutual dependency of INCENP and MKLP2 for their spindle midzone localizations (Hummer and Mayer, 2009). The absence of MKLP2 and CPC at the spindle midzone in cells expressing INCENP-ΔSLM also resulted in a more disorganized microtubule network and a less well-structured bipolar spindle during mitosis (Figure 2B). Taken together, our data showed that INCENP-ΔSLM expressing cells failed to organize a proper spindle midzone but were able to undergo furrow ingression in majority of mitotic cells, possibly relying on the cortical or near-cortical CPC during cell division.

Inhibition of MKLP2 in cells expressing INCENP-ΔSLM leads to complete furrow ingression failure

We speculated that INCENP-ΔSLM retains its ability to associate with the cell cortex to drive furrow ingression in majority of cells lacking CPC at the spindle midzone. Since MKLP2 is responsible for the localizations of CPC at the spindle midzone and the equatorial cortex, we speculated that the cortical INCENP-ΔSLM depends on MKLP2’s activity to drive furrow ingression. To test whether this was the case, we used a specific ATPase inhibitor of MKLP2, Paprotrain, to inhibit MKLP2 in cells at mitosis (Tcherniuk et al., 2010). Paprotrain treatment abolished the localization of INCENP at the spindle midzone in anaphase and resulted in ∼60% of mitotic cells failing in furrow ingression (Figure 3A and B). While majority of INCENP-ΔSLM expressing cells (∼80%) in DMSO treatment underwent furrow ingression, most mitotic INCENP-ΔSLM expressing cells upon Paprotrain treatment completely failed to initiate the furrow ingression and exited mitosis as binucleated cells (Figure 3A and B). Consistently, mitotic cells expressing INCENP-ΔSLM lacking single or double sub-helices (INCENP-ΔSLMΔ4, INCENP-ΔSLMΔ6, INCENP-ΔSLMΔ4.6) instead of three sub-helices in INCENP-ΔSLM were also defective in furrow ingression upon Paprotrain treatment (Supplementary Figure S3A–C). This indicates a strong dependency of MKLP2 activity by the cortical INCENP-ΔSLM to drive furrow ingression.

Closer examination of INCENP and INCENP-ΔSLM localizations in cells treated with Paprotrain showed that INCENP localized to a broader region of the cleavage plane, which was less well-focused at the equatorial cortex as compared to cells in DMSO treatment (Figure 3C, left panel). INCENP-ΔSLM was either weakly distributed or nearly absent at the cell cortex upon Paprotrain treatment (Figure 3C, right panel). Immunostaining of MKLP1 revealed that the localizations of MKLP1 at the spindle midzone and the equatorial cortex were significantly impaired when INCENP-ΔSLM expressing cells were treated with Paprotrain, which is consistent with the high penetrant furrow ingression failure phenotype (Figure 3C and D). The Paprotrain treatment used in our experiments abolished MKLP2’s localization at the spindle midzone and weakened its localization at the equatorial cortex (Figure 3E, left panel, and F). This effect was exacerbated in INCENP-ΔSLM expressing cells, possibly due to the mutual dependency of MKLP2 and INCENP (Figure 3E, right panel, and F).

Since we observed additive effects of MKLP2 inhibition in cells expressing INCENP-ΔSLM, we next determined whether simultaneous inhibition of Aurora B and MKLP2 would lead to a similar cytokinetic defect. Treatment of cells with Aurora B inhibitor, AZD1152, and Paprotrain perturbed MKLP1 localizations at the spindle midzone and the equatorial cortex and led to ingression failure, which is similar to INCENP-ΔSLM treated with Paprotrain (Supplementary Figure S3D). Taken together, our data suggested that the cortical INCENP-ΔSLM scaffolds Aurora B at the cell cortex and depends on MKLP2 activity to drive furrow ingression.

Identification of mechanoresponsive elements in INCENP-CBD

INCENP binds actin filaments and associates with the cell cortex during cell division (Chen et al., 2007; Hu et al., 2008; Landino and Ohi, 2016). During furrow ingression, actin filaments at the cleavage plane are under tension produced by non-muscle myosin IIs (NMIIs) (Cheffings et al., 2016). It is possible that INCENP senses the tensed actin filaments and enriches CPC at the equatorial cortex to drive furrow ingression. Consistently, a previous study showed that Dictyostelium INCENP was mechanoresponsive and showed accumulation at the cell cortex under tension (Kee et al., 2012). However, the detailed mechanism and the physiological significance of the INCENP mechanoresponse remained unclear. Since INCENP-ΔSLM localizes to the cell cortex but does not enrich at the equatorial cortex, we speculated that INCENP-ΔSLM was able to bind actin filaments but was defective in responding to tension in actin filaments during cytokinesis.

INCENP-CBD contains residues that bind actin filaments, so it is possible that this protein domain contains elements that enable INCENP to respond to forces after association with actin filaments. To test whether this was the case, we employed cell compression assay developed previously and exerted compressive forces on mitotic cells expressing EGFP-INCENP-CBD (Wang et al., 2024). Our data showed that EGFP-INCENP-CBD showed an increased accumulation at the cell cortex in response to compression (Figure 4A and B). Next, we treated mitotic cells expressing EGFP-INCENP-CBD with the myosin light chain kinase (MLCK) inhibitor, ML-7, to inactivate NMIIs’ contractility and to decrease cell surface tension. Interestingly, we found that ML-7 treatment decreased cell surface tension and abolished the cortical localization of EGFP-INCENP-CBD in mitotic cells, suggesting a tension-dependent localization of INCENP-CBD during mitosis (Figure 4C–E).

To identify regions of INCENP-CBD conferring the mechanoresponse, we subjected the INCENP-CBD containing different truncations of sub-helices to cell compression assay. Quantification of the cortical EGFP-INCENP-CBD containing different sub-helix truncations in mitotic cells showed that INCENP-CBDΔ4.5.6, which was impaired in mitotic spindle localization, was also weaker in accumulating at the cell cortex after compression compared to INCENP-CBD (Figure 4F–H; Supplementary Figure S4). Shorter truncations including INCENP-CBDΔ6 and INCENP-CBDΔ5.6 were also accumulated weakly to the cell cortex after compression, indicating the involvement of these regions in conferring INCENP-CBD's mechanoresponse (Figure 4F–H; Supplementary Figure S4).

Next, we further identified residues in the sub-helices 4, 5, and 6 of INCENP-CBD that might be important in regulating the mechanoresponse of INCENP-CBD. Inspired by previous studies that showed enhanced binding of actin-binding proteins to tensed actin filaments via hydrophobic interactions (Mei et al., 2020; Sun et al., 2020), we speculated that aliphatic residues in sub-helices 4, 5, and 6 of INCENP-CBD may be involved in enhancing binding to actin filaments upon cell compression. We point-mutagenized leucine and valine residues present in sub-helices 4, 5, and 6 of INCENP-CBD to alanine residues and found that INCENP-CBD containing L676A in sub-helix 6 (numbering based on the full-length INCENP) was defective to accumulate at the mitotic cell cortex in response to compression (Figure 4I and J; Supplementary Figure S4). Taken together, we showed that INCENP-CBD is mechanoresponsive and contains regions that are involved in enhancing association with the cell cortex in response to forces.

INCENP-ΔSLM expressing cells fail to support furrow ingression when dividing in a highly confined environment

We found that sub-helices 4, 5, and 6 of INCENP-CBD are involved in the mechanoresponse of INCENP-CBD. Next, we tested whether the lack of these three helices in the full-length INCENP, which is INCENP-ΔSLM, was defective in responding to compression. To this end, we compressed cells expressing INCENP and INCENP-ΔSLM fluorescent fusion proteins, respectively, and examined the fluorescence intensity changes of INCENP and INCENP-ΔSLM fusion proteins at the chromosomes and the cell cortex. While cell compression has pushed the chromosomes closer to the imaging plane and resulted in higher intensity after compression (fold change >1), there was no significant difference between the increased chromosomal signals of INCENP and INCENP-ΔSLM (Figure 5A and B). Importantly, INCENP-ΔSLM did not increase at the cleavage plane as much as INCENP at the cleavage plane in response to compression, suggesting that SLM confers mechanoresponsivity to the cortical INCENP during cell division (Figure 5A and B).

Next, we speculated that the mechanoresponse of INCENP was important to promote furrow ingression when cells are dividing in a highly confined space. To test whether this was the case, we confined mitotic cells to half of their original cell heights using stiff gels of 30 kPa, mimicking stiffness and confining environment experienced by cells in the breast tumours (Supplementary Figure S5A; Matthews et al., 2020; Munne et al., 2021). It is to note that this contrasts with the cell compression assay we used to assess the mechanoresponsivity of a protein described in previous experiments in which we reduced the cell height only by ∼20% using a soft gel of 5 kPa.

When dividing in a confined space, mitotic cells exhibited cortical instability by producing blebs, initiated furrow ingression during anaphase and completed furrow ingression with comparable ingression speed as unconfined cells (Supplementary Figure S5B). Consistently, active RhoA was enriched at the cleavage plane in dividing cells under confinement as revealed by the localization reporter for active RhoA, mCherry-AHPH (Supplementary Figure S5B; Wang et al., 2023).

Under confinement, INCENP localized to the spindle midzone and the equatorial cortex during cell division (Figure 5D). While only ∼20% of unconfined INCENP-ΔSLM cells failed in furrow ingression, majority of mitotic INCENP-ΔSLM expressing cells (∼60%) failed to initiate or complete furrow ingression in the confined space (Figure 5C and D). Our data showed that cells dividing in a confined environment have an unstable cell cortex but were able to complete furrow ingression when the cortical INCENP mechanically responds to the compressive forces. Thus, the mechanoresponse of the cortical INCENP is essential to support furrow ingression when dividing in a highly confined environment.

We further tested mitotic cells containing INCENP proteins with different smaller deletions of the mechanoresponsive elements in the INCENP-CBD, which are INCENP-ΔSLMΔ4, INCENP-ΔSLMΔ6, INCENP-ΔSLMΔ4.6, respectively, for their ability to drive furrow ingression in the presence or absence of confinement. Without confinement, a small population of mitotic cells expressing INCENP-ΔSLMΔ4 or INCENP-ΔSLMΔ6 failed in furrow ingression (Supplementary Figure S6A and B). These two deletions (INCENP-ΔSLMΔ4.6) have an additive effect on the ability of cells to drive furrow ingression (Supplementary Figure S6A and B). Interestingly, confinement exacerbated the failure of furrow ingression in these cells containing smaller deletion in the INCENP-CBD, particularly when sub-helix 6 was deleted, suggesting that the mechanoresponsive elements in INCENP-CBD are necessary for efficient furrow ingression, especially under confinement (Supplementary Figure S6A and B). Next, we tested whether mitotic cells expressing INCENP-CBD (not the full-length INCENP) were sufficient to drive furrow ingression. To ensure recruitment of Aurora B by the INCENP-CBD, we have fused the IN box (aa 833–897) and the C-terminus (aa 898–918) of INCENP to the INCENP-CBD and a linker in between them (Supplementary Figure S6C). We observed that INCENP-CBD with the IN box localized to the mitotic spindle and weakly at the cell cortex in the absence of endogenous INCENP. These cells entered mitosis and elongated their mitotic spindle abnormally without forming obvious spindle midzone or enriching at the equatorial cortex and eventually failed in furrow ingression (Supplementary Figure S6C). Thus, INCENP-CBD is not sufficient to drive furrow ingression and other components of INCENP are required for the full activity of CPC during mitosis and cytokinesis.

Aurora B is required to drive furrow ingression in cortical-unstable cells

The actin cortex is essential in maintaining the cell cortex stability during cell division (Carreno et al., 2008; Chugh and Paluch, 2018). Previous studies showed that mild perturbations of the actin cortex by the low dosage of actin polymerization inhibitors led to destabilization of the cell cortex characterized by excessive blebbing (Paluch et al., 2005; Sedzinski et al., 2011). We found that cells treated with low doses of latrunculin A (latA), an actin polymerization inhibitor, were mechanically unstable especially during cell division, but were able to complete furrow ingression, similar to cells dividing in a confined environment (Figure 6D). To identify signalling pathways that might be responsible to support furrow ingression in cortical-unstable cells, we performed phosphoproteomic analysis on a cell population enriched in dividing cells and treated with low doses of latA. We found that after treatment by low doses of latA, protein substrates of basophilic kinases with (R/K)XX(S/T) motif predominated the phosphoproteome (Figure 6A). Interestingly, the phosphorylated form of Vimentin S72, which is a downstream substrate of Aurora B during cell division (Goto et al., 2003), was enriched in our phosphoproteomic screen. We further verified the phosphorylation of Vimentin S72 by performing protein blotting using cell lysates prepared from cells treated with low doses of latA. Consistent with our phosphoproteomics, the protein level of phosphorylated Vimentin S72 was higher in cells treated with low doses of latA, and the increased phosphorylation of Vimentin S72 was diminished upon Aurora B inhibition by AZD1152 treatment, suggesting that Aurora B is activated in cortical-unstable cells (Figure 6B).

Next, we tested whether Aurora B activity is required to support division of cortical-unstable cells. Whereas mitotic cells treated with either low doses of latA or low doses of AZD1152 completed furrow ingression, co-treatment of both low doses of latA and AZD1152 led to ingression failure (Figure 6C and D). Thus, our data showed that inhibition of Aurora B in cortical-unstable cells leads to ingression failure, indicating an essential role of Aurora B in driving furrow ingression when the cell cortex is mechanically unstable during cell division.

Essential requirements of Aurora B activity in driving furrow ingression when dividing in a confined environment

Since cortical-unstable cells up-regulate Aurora B activity to drive furrow ingression, we speculated that dividing cells under confinement engaged Aurora B to sustain furrow ingression. While mitotic cells treated with low doses of AZD1152 that partially inhibit Aurora B underwent furrow ingression, AZD1152-treated cells failed to complete their furrow ingression under confinement (Figure 7A and B). Thus, confined mitotic cells are highly sensitive to mild perturbation of Aurora B and require full Aurora B activity to sustain furrow ingression. Taken together, our data suggested that INCENP mechanically responds to compressive stress and enhances Aurora B's localization at the equatorial cortex to support furrow ingression when cells are dividing in a confined environment.

Discussion

INCENP functions as a scaffold protein to localize CPC to the spindle midzone and the equatorial cortex during anaphase. INCENP binds to Survivin and Borealin at the CEN domain of its N-terminal to assemble a heterotrimeric protein complex that associates with MKLP2 (Carmena et al., 2012). MKLP2 transports CPC to the spindle midzone and the equatorial cortex for Aurora B, which binds to INCENP C-terminal, to phosphorylate substrates involved in mitosis and cytokinesis (Hadders and Lens, 2022). Between these two protein-binding domains, INCENP contains a SAH domain that is first shown to be involved in binding to microtubules and is required to localize CPC to the spindle midzone (Samejima et al., 2015). Human INCENP without SAH (also known as INCENP-ΔCC) does not localize to the spindle midzone while retaining cortical localization with punctate structures near the cell cortex (van der Horst et al., 2015). Further deletion experiments of the SAH domain by Landino and Ohi (2016) identified aa 500–680 of human INCENP to contain elements that bind actin filaments in addition to microtubules, which is named as CBD for brevity in our study. Consistent with the role of CBD in cytoskeletal binding, INCENP containing reversed charged aa in the CBD does not localize to the spindle midzone and the equatorial cortex (Landino and Ohi, 2016). Our study further showed that the cytoskeletal binding functions of CBD can be dissected to bind microtubules or actin filaments independently. Through computational analysis of INCENP-CBD and deletion experiments, we identified a SLM of 57aa in CBD, which is required by INCENP to localize to the spindle midzone in cells at anaphase. INCENP lacking SLM mimics INCENP-ΔCC and localizes to the cell cortex or near the cortex as a punctate structure without strong enrichment at the equatorial cortex. Importantly, the majority of INCENP-ΔSLM and INCENP-ΔCC mutant cells undergo furrow ingression, suggesting that the cortical INCENP drives furrow ingression in the absence of INCENP at the spindle midzone, but has a strong dependency on MKLP2 activity to support furrow ingression.

Both actin and microtubule networks are involved in CPC localization at the equatorial cortex (Chen et al., 2007; Landino et al., 2017). MKLP2 functions as a plus-end directed motor to transport CPC along microtubule networks to relocate CPC from centromeres to the spindle midzone and to the equatorial cortex (Adriaans et al., 2020). Parallel to the microtubule-dependent pathway, INCENP associates with actin networks to scaffold CPC at the cell cortex, which functions in the absence of microtubule networks or upon MKLP2 depletion (Landino et al., 2017). Consistently, INCENP mutants like INCENP-ΔSLM and INCENP-ΔCC, which retain association with the cell cortex but are defective in localization to the microtubule-based spindle midzone, undergo furrow ingression (van der Horst et al., 2015).

INCENP not only associates with actin filaments, we also showed that INCENP contains mechanoresponsive elements enabling INCENP to respond to forces present in tensed actin filaments. Similar to actin-binding proteins such as LIM-domain containing protein FHL2 and α-catenin that respond to forces, INCENP responds to forces via aliphatic residues, possibly to enhance hydrophobic interactions with exposed residues when actin filaments are under tension (Mei et al., 2020; Sun et al., 2020; Winkelman et al., 2020; Zsolnay et al., 2024). It is possible that under confinement, the tensed actin filaments ‘crack’ and expose residues to interact with the aliphatic residues within the mechanoresponsive elements of INCENP (Zsolnay et al., 2024). This leads to tighter binding between INCENP and actin filaments and to sustain CPC at the equatorial cortex. Future experiments using single-molecule force spectroscopy such as optical tweezers or atomic force microscopy to exert tension on actin filaments and to test interactions between tensed actin filaments and INCENP-CBD would strengthen the mechanosensing mechanism. In addition, computational modelling as developed by Zsolnay et al. (2024) for cracked actin filaments could potentially be used to test interactions with INCENP-CBD. Stronger maintenance of INCENP at the equatorial cortex in response to forces could result in a full and sustained activation of Aurora B at the equatorial cortex through a positive feedback loop involving phosphorylation of INCENP by Aurora B (Bishop and Schumacher, 2002; Honda et al., 2003; Sessa et al., 2005). As a result, the mechanoresponsiveness of INCENP enables Aurora B to respond to forces during cell division. Consistently, in Xenopus embryos subjected to higher centrifugal forces, basophilic kinases including Aurora B were found to have higher kinase activity (Hashimoto et al., 2019). Thus, through interaction with actin filaments and enhanced interaction with actin filaments under tension, INCENP scaffolds Aurora B to the cell cortex and sustains Aurora B at the equatorial cortex in response to forces during cell division. The mechanoresponse of INCENP allows sustained CPC's activity at the equatorial cortex to drive furrow ingression, particularly when cells are dividing in a crowded and confined environment commonly present in tumours (Nia et al., 2020). Furrow ingression of the dividing cells is constrained by the neighbouring cell stiffness in an epithelium (Landino et al., 2023), further supporting the importance of mechanoresponsive mechanisms to drive furrow ingression in a mechanically-constrained environment.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

Cells were incubated in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 at 37°C. Human mammary epithelial cell line MCF10A was cultured in DMEM/F-12 (Sigma) supplemented with Glutamax (Gibco), 5% horse serum (Biological Industries), 20 ng/ml epidermal growth factor (Gibco), 0.5 mg/ml Hydrocortisone (MedChemExpress), 100 ng/ml Cholera toxin (Sigma), 10 μg/ml Insulin (Biological Industries), 1% Pen/Strep (Biological Industries). The 293Ta lentivirus packaging cell line (Genecopoeia, LT008) was cultured in DMEM (Sigma) supplemented with Glutamax, 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Excel Bio), 1% Pen/Strep.

Chemicals

Chemicals used in this study include: CDK1 inhibitor: RO-3306 (Selleckchem S7747); Aurora B inhibitor: AZD1152-HQPA (Selleckchem, S1147); MKLP2 inhibitor: Paprotrain (MedChemExpress, HY-101298); and MLCK inhibitor: ML-7 (MedChemExpress, 110448-33-4). These chemicals were dissolved in DMSO. Treatment conditions were described in figure legends. To stain and visualize DNA in live cells, SiR-DNA (Cytoskeleton, CY-SC007) was added to cells prior to imaging. DAPI (Sangon Biotech, E607303; 5 mg/L) was used to stain DNA in fixed cells.

Quantitative and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis and graphs were done using Prism 9 (GraphPad). Datasets were analyzed by Student's t-test. Experiments were repeated three times to generate data for statistical analysis.

More detailed assay methods are described in Supplementary Methods.
