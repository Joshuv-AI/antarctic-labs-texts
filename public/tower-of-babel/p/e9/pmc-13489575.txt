EBUS-guided Cryobiopsy-Proven Anthracotic Mediastinal Lymph Node Mimicking Malignancy on FDG PET-CT: A Case Report
Authors: Trilok Chand, Hussa M. A. Alshehhi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Anthracotic mediastinal lymph nodes are an uncommon cause of intense fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) uptake on positron-emission tomography-computed tomography (PET-CT), often mimicking malignancy and leading to diagnostic uncertainty. Here, we report the case of a 67-year-old Asian male with FDG-avid mediastinal lymphadenopathy that was highly suspicious for metastatic disease. Subsequent endobronchial ultrasound-guided lymph node sampling demonstrated anthracosis without evidence of malignancy or infection. Recognition of this rare benign entity prevented cancer upstaging and unnecessary treatment. This case highlights the importance of histopathological confirmation of PET-positive mediastinal lymph nodes, particularly in patients with suspected or known malignancy, to ensure accurate diagnosis and appropriate clinical management.

INTRODUCTION

Anthracosis is a benign condition affecting the lungs and mediastinum, arising from the accumulation of carbon particles within lymph nodes as a result of chronic exposure to sources such as biomass fuel, tobacco smoke, or environmental pollution.[12] Anthracotic lymph nodes are often identified as an incidental finding in imaging or biopsy, particularly in countries where biomass fuel is in widespread use or where smoking is prevalent.[3]

The imaging appearance of anthracotic lymphadenopathy are diagnostically challenging. On positron emission tomography (PET) with fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG), these nodes may show increased metabolic activity that often mimic malignant disease, tuberculosis, or sarcoidosis.[45] This significant uncertainty affects the diagnosis, more specifically, in patients already known to have malignancy and undergoing staging or surveillance.[6]

In this regard, histopathological confirmation is of great importance for separating benign anthracosis from other possible conditions causing FDG-avid lymphadenopathy.[78] The minimally invasive approaches ensure that diagnostic tissue can be safely and effectively obtained.[91011] Here, we present a case of a 67-year-old male with bladder carcinoma in whom FDG-avid mediastinal lymphadenopathy was later diagnosed as anthracosis, a benign condition, following histopathological examination. This case highlights the limited specificity of FDG PET-CT for distinguishing benign from malignant lymphadenopathy and emphasizes on the need of tissue sampling to avoid inappropriate management.

CASE REPORT

A 67-year-old Asian male, who was a chronic smoker, was referred to the pulmonology department from the oncology department after FDG-avid lymph nodes were found on PET scan. The patient was otherwise healthy until recently, when he was diagnosed with urinary bladder carcinoma (specifically, invasive high-grade papillary and large nested urothelial carcinoma). During presentation, the patient had no respiratory symptoms, and chest examination revealed normal bilateral breath sounds.

Multiple hypermetabolic lymph nodes were seen with paratracheal, subcarinal, and hilar distributions that were roughly symmetrical on PET-CT imaging. The most intensely FDG-avid lymph node was located in the right hilar region (station 11R), lateral to the bifurcation of the bronchus intermedius. It measured 1.4 × 1.2 cm and demonstrated a maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax) of 7.7 [Figure 1]. The demonstrated lymph nodes were round, moderately enlarged, with well-defined margins, and showed evidence of necrosis or calcification.

Laboratory investigations revealed that both inflammatory markers and serum angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) levels were normal. The Quantiferon-TB Gold test proved positive. Later, bronchoscopy with endobronchial ultrasound (EBUS) revealed enlarged lymph nodes in the right lower paratracheal, subcarinal, and bilateral hilar stations in a non-homogeneous manner. Subsequently, EBUS-guided transbronchial needle aspiration (EBUS-TBNA) and cryobiopsy were performed in these stations, and both the aspirated and biopsy materials were blackish in color [Figure 2].

Sample bottle showing cryobiopsy specimens stained with black pigments

Histopathological examination of the submitted samples diagnosed lymph node anthracosis, characterized with the presence of numerous pigment-laden macrophages within a fibrotic stroma [Figure 3]. Acid-fast bacilli (AFB) smear, Mycobacterium tuberculosis PCR, and AFB culture results from aspirated lymph node samples after 6 weeks were all negative and thus excluded tuberculosis as the underlying etiology. At the 6-month follow up, contrast-enhanced CT of the chest showed no changes in the size of lymph nodes, and the patients remained continued to have no respiratory symptoms.

DISCUSSION

FDG-PET scans are usually done as part of staging for patients with malignancy. In clinical practice, FDG-avid mediastinal lymph nodes raise suspicion of metastasis in patients with known primary malignancy. However, to avoid misinterpretation and over-treatment, benign entities, especially anthracosis, must also be ruled out.[14]

FDG uptake is non-malignancy specific, as it shows increased glucose metabolism from inflammatory, infectious, or benign fibrotic processes. Anthracotic lymph nodes, which contain carbon pigments and reactive macrophages, may have overlapping SUV with metastatic nodes [Table 1].[567] In our patient, the SUV max of 7.7 was raised high suspicion of malignancy; however, histopathological examination confirmed benign anthracosis. This finding shows that imaging findings alone are insufficient for definitive diagnosis.[79]

While EBUS-TBNA is the conventional method of mediastinal lymph node sampling, EBUS-guided lymph node cryobiopsy is increasingly being considered as a minimally invasive technique with a high diagnostic yield in benign nodes (91.2%).[910] In addition, EBUS-TBNA specimens may sometimes yield limited cytological material for detailed histopathological assessment, and thus complimentary EBUS-guided cryobiopsy can provide larger specimens with better preservation and representation of the nodal architecture.[1011]

Recent studies have shown better diagnostic yield with the combined use of TBNA and cryobiopsy, particularly in cases with sarcoidosis, lymphoma, and anthracosis.[12131415] In this case, the presence of black-pigmented aspirates from EBUS immediately suggested anthracosis, which was then confirmed on histopathology.[8] The combined use of TBNA and cryobiopsy helped avoid unnecessary thoracotomy/mediastinoscopy.

Anthracotic lymphadenopathy has been reported to drastically differ in prevalence geographically, and is more commonly reported in Asian populations, particularly in rural regions where biomass fuel exposure is widespread.[23] In smokers, chronic inhalation of carbon particles could lead to the same node changes.[1] Clinically, anthracosis is mostly asymptomatic and detected incidentally during evaluation for unrelated diseases.

Persistent exposure to anthracotic material may continuously activate macrophages through ongoing antigenic stimulation, leading to inflammatory metabolic activity visible on PET-CT. Anthracotic lymphadenopathy, although benign, remains a diagnostic dilemma owing to its radiologic overlap with tuberculosis, sarcoidosis, or malignancy.[456] This has resulted in reports of cases where patients had been misdiagnosed and unnecessarily initiated on anti-tubercular therapy or invasive surgical procedures before the histological diagnosis of anthracosis was established.[78]

To treat oncology patients, it is very important to differentiate between anthracosis and nodal metastasis, as misinterpretations could lead to inappropriate upstaging, unnecessary chemotherapy, and loss of surgical eligibility. The current case underscored the principle that, whenever PET-positive lymph nodes are encountered, histopathological confirmation should be done before initiating definitive management. Clinicians should also consider population-specific risk factors, such as biomass fuel exposure and smoking history, which increase the possibility of benign anthracotic nodes.[23] Thus, combining clinical context with radiologic data would improve diagnostic accuracy and reduce uncertainty.

Although we present a single case, this report highlights that anthracotic lymphadenopathy may lack distinctive clinical or radiologic features and that not all FDG-avid mediastinal lymph nodes are malignant.[456] The presence of black pigmentation on aspirated and/or biopsy may serve as an important diagnostic clue that could facilitate confirmation through histopathology.[789]

CONCLUSION

Anthracotic lymphadenopathy is a benign condition that may present with deceptively high fluorodeoxyglucose uptake on positron emission tomography imaging, thereby mimicking malignant nodal disease. Therefore, histopathologic confirmation through EBUS-TBNA and/or cryobiopsy is essential to prevent misdiagnosis, particularly in oncology patients where accurate nodal staging is critical for management.
