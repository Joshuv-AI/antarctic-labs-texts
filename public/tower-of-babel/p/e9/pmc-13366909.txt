Therapy-induced tumor regression heterogeneity for early prediction of response and prognosis in HER2-positive breast cancer
Authors: Zewen Liu, Qin Li, Juntao Zhang, Xiaomei Jiang, Xiqing Wu, Haitong Yu, Shasha Wu, Chengsheng Li, Ying Chen, Peng Dong, Qingliang Niu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEarly prediction of pathologic complete response (pCR) during neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) in HER2-positive breast cancer remains challenging using conventional whole-tumor imaging approaches, particularly when substantial tumor shrinkage occurs on mid-NAC MRI (acquired after two cycles of NAC).MethodsThis retrospective study included 163 patients with HER2-positive breast cancer who underwent dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI at pre-NAC and mid-NAC. A therapy-induced tumor regression region (TRR) was defined as the regressed tumor volume between pre- and mid-NAC MRI. Tumor regression heterogeneity (TRH) was quantified within this region using a habitat-based radiomic approach. Logistic regression models were constructed and evaluated in training (n = 115) and validation (n = 48) cohorts.ResultspCR was achieved in 71 patients (43.6%). The TRH model demonstrated superior predictive performance compared with pre-NAC, mid-NAC, tumor regression region–based, and delta models, achieving an AUC of 0.83 (95% CI: 0.70–0.97) in the validation cohort. An integrated nomogram combining TRH with clinicopathologic factors further improved predictive performance.ConclusionsQuantification of spatial heterogeneity within therapy-induced tumor regression regions on mid-NAC MRI may enable improved prediction of treatment response during neoadjuvant therapy.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12880-026-02423-y.

Background

Human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) status is a critical molecular determinant in breast cancer, guiding therapeutic decision-making and prognostic stratification [1]. Through homodimerization or heterodimerization, HER2 activates multiple downstream signaling pathways that promote tumor cell proliferation, invasion, and survival [2]. Accordingly, the development and clinical implementation of HER2-targeted therapies have markedly improved treatment outcomes in patients with HER2-positive breast cancer, particularly in the neoadjuvant setting. Pathologic complete response (pCR) serves as a key surrogate endpoint for neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) efficacy; however, treatment response remains highly variable, partly due to intratumoral heterogeneity.

Quantitative imaging analysis allows noninvasive assessment of tumor heterogeneity [3–5]. Habitat-based models further enhance this by capturing spatially distinct subregions within tumors [6, 7]. However, many existing habitat analyses focus on the whole tumor at a single time point, with limited consideration of dynamic intratumoral changes during treatment.

Delta-based imaging biomarkers have been proposed to assess longitudinal response, but reliable feature extraction becomes challenging in cases of marked tumor shrinkage, particularly when residual lesions become indistinguishable on mid-NAC MRI (acquired after two cycles of NAC). Importantly, minimal or absent residual enhancement at mid-NAC does not necessarily indicate pCR, and viable tumor may persist at pathology, limiting the utility of conventional delta-based approaches for early response assessment [8].

To address this challenge, we introduced the concept of the therapy-induced tumor regression region (TRR), defined as the subvolume corresponding to measurable tumor shrinkage between pre- and mid-NAC imaging. Building on this region, we developed an MRI-based framework to quantify tumor regression heterogeneity (TRH) using spatial habitat analysis. This study aimed to evaluate the value of TRH derived from mid-NAC MRI for the early prediction of pCR in patients with HER2-positive breast cancer.

Methods

Study population and inclusion criteria

This retrospective study was approved by the institutional review board of the participating institution (approval number: No. 2025Y×378; approved on 26 March 2025). The requirement for written informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. No clinical trial registration was applicable.

The inclusion criteria were: (1) female patients with histopathologically confirmed HER2-positive breast cancer; (2) completion of standard NAC; (3) availability of dynamic contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (DCE-MRI) both pre-NAC and mid-NAC; (4) surgical resection following NAC with clearly documented pCR status; and (5) high-quality MRI suitable for analysis. PCR was defined as the absence of any residual invasive cancer in the breast and axillary lymph nodes, corresponding to Miller–Payne grade 5 (MP5) on histopathologic evaluation after completion of neoadjuvant chemotherapy(pathologic assessment details are described in Supplementary Methods S1). HER2-positive was defined according to the 2018 ASCO/CAP guidelines (IHC 3+ or IHC 2+ with positive FISH results).

Exclusion criteria included: (1) prior anticancer treatments; (2) presence of other malignancies and/or recurrent or metastatic breast cancer. Figure 1 provides a cohort construction flowchart.Fig. 1Flowchart of patient enrollment and cohort allocation for the study

Flowchart of patient enrollment and cohort allocation for the study

Consecutive patients meeting the inclusion criteria were enrolled. Between July 2020 and January 2024, 163 patients were included and randomly assigned to a training cohort (n = 115) and a validation cohort (n = 48) at a 7:3 ratio. All patients with HER2-positive breast cancer received standard neoadjuvant chemotherapy combined with anti-HER2–targeted therapy, including anthracycline- or taxane-based regimens with trastuzumab, with or without pertuzumab (detailed regimens are provided in Supplementary Methods S2).

MRI image acquisition

MRI examinations were performed at pre-NAC and mid-NAC as part of routine clinical care using a 3.0T scanner. The imaging protocol included dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI, T2-weighted imaging, and diffusion-weighted imaging. Detailed acquisition parameters are provided in Supplementary Methods S3.

MRI preprocessing and extraction of tumor regression heterogeneity (TRH) features

All MRI data underwent standardized preprocessing, including N4 bias field correction, resampling to an isotropic voxel size of 1 mm3, and histogram intensity normalization. Image registration was performed using the Elastix platform with B-spline–based nonrigid alignment.

Tumor volumes of interest (VOIs) were manually segmented on contrast-enhanced T1-weighted MRI (T1C) using ITK-SNAP (version 3.8.0; University of Pennsylvania, Philadelphia, PA, USA) by two radiologists with 5 and 10 years of experience, respectively, who were blinded to clinical data. Interobserver reproducibility was assessed using the independently segmented VOIs prior to consensus, based on intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) derived from a two-way random-effects model with absolute agreement. Discrepancies were subsequently resolved by consensus to obtain the final VOIs for analysis. The TRR was defined as the subvolume corresponding to tumor shrinkage, calculated by subtracting the overlapping region (“∩”) of pre- and mid-NAC VOIs from the pre-NAC VOI. This definition enables spatial delineation of regions exhibiting treatment-related regression between imaging time points, effectively acting as a spatial filtering step that isolates regressed tumor components while reducing the confounding effects of simultaneous tumor progression and shrinkage within the same lesion. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${V_{TRR}} = {V_{Pre}} - \left( {{V_{Pre}} \cap \,{V_{Post}}} \right)$$\end{document}

TRH was derived by subdividing the TRR into grayscale-coherent voxel clusters using SLIC, followed by habitat-level feature extraction and clustering (details in Supplementary Methods S4).

To complement the TRH analysis, tumor volume reduction rate (TVRR) was calculated as a morphometric indicator of tumor shrinkage. TVRR was calculated as the ratio of the TRR volume to the pre-NAC tumor volume, expressed as a percentage:

TVRR = TRR volume/pre-NAC tumor volume × 100%.

Model development and performance evaluation

Five MRI-based predictive models were constructed: (1) a pre-NAC model using baseline whole-tumor features, (2) a mid-NAC model based on residual tumor features, (3) a TRR model utilizing features from the tumor regression region, (4) a TRH model characterizing subregional heterogeneity within the TRR, and (5) a delta model quantifying longitudinal changes between pre- and mid-NAC imaging.

Quantitative features were extracted using PyRadiomics, including first-order statistics, shape descriptors, and texture features derived from the gray level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM), gray level run length matrix (GLRLM), gray level size zone matrix (GLSZM), gray level dependence matrix (GLDM), and neighborhood gray tone difference matrix (NGTDM). All features were z-score normalized prior to modeling.

Feature selection was performed using a two-step approach. First, the minimum redundancy maximum relevance (mRMR) algorithm was applied to eliminate redundant and irrelevant features, and the top 30 features were retained based on their relevance to the outcome and minimal redundancy. Subsequently, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) logistic regression was used to select the optimal subset of features for model construction. The regularization parameter (λ) was determined using 10-fold cross-validation, and the value corresponding to the minimum mean cross-validated error (λ_min) was selected. Features with non-zero coefficients were retained in the final model. All feature selection procedures were conducted using the training cohort only.

A Tumor Regression Heterogeneity score (TRH score) was calculated for each patient by linearly combining the LASSO-selected TRH features weighted by their respective coefficients. This composite score was used as the input for building the TRH model.

A clinical model was constructed by incorporating clinicopathologic variables that demonstrated significant associations with pCR in univariate logistic regression analysis (p < 0.05). These variables were then combined with TRH features in a multivariable logistic regression framework. The regression coefficients from this final model were used to construct a nomogram for individualized probability estimation.

Statistical analysis

Baseline differences within cohorts (pCR vs. non-pCR) and between cohorts (training vs. validation) were assessed using χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables and independent-samples t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables. Continuous variables were first tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Variables with a normal distribution and homogeneity of variance were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using the independent-samples t-test; variables with a non-normal distribution were presented as median [interquartile range, IQR] and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Univariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to identify clinical factors associated with pCR. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated. Multicollinearity was assessed, and backward stepwise selection was used in multivariable modeling. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to assess model performance, and the area under the curve (AUC) with 95% CI was calculated. Model comparisons were conducted using DeLong’s test, with Bonferroni correction applied for multiple comparisons. Additional performance metrics included sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV). Model calibration was evaluated using calibration curves, and clinical utility was assessed via decision curve analysis (DCA).

TRR habitat clustering and imaging feature characterization

The extracted radiomic features demonstrated good interobserver reproducibility, with the majority of ICC values exceeding 0.75, indicating robust feature stability across independent segmentations. The generation of TRRs and their corresponding habitats is illustrated in Fig. 2. Based on subtraction MRI images, TRRs were initially segmented into clusters of voxels with highly self-correlated gray-level intensities using the SLIC algorithm. From each cluster, six first-order histogram features were extracted, and t-SNE was applied for dimensionality reduction. The resulting low-dimensional representations were clustered using K-means. The optimal number of habitats was determined by evaluating the Silhouette Coefficient across a range of cluster numbers (k = 2–15), with the highest coefficient observed at k = 3, indicating optimal cluster separability and structural stability (Figure S1). A total of 3,396 TRH features were extracted through quantitative analysis of the clustered tumor subregions. In addition, 1,132 quantitative imaging features were independently extracted from pre-NAC, mid-NAC, and TRR MRI images. Selection of key imaging features and clinical predictors

Imaging features were further screened using mRMR and LASSO regression. Through this process, 26 key TRH features with predictive relevance were identified. The selected TRH quantitative features are shown in Table S1. We applied the same selection pipeline to obtain predictive features from pre-NAC, mid-NAC, TRR, and delta-imaging datasets (details provided in Supplementary Table S2-5). Among the selected TRH features, GLCM Cluster Shade (wavelet_HHH_glcm_ClusterShade) exhibited the highest regression coefficient, indicating the strongest predictive contribution. The TRH score for each patient was calculated using a linear combination of selected features weighted by LASSO coefficients.

In univariate logistic regression analysis, N stage, non-mass enhancement (NME), and multifocality were significantly associated with pCR (Table 2). In multivariable logistic regression analysis, lesion morphology remained independently associated with pCR in the clinical model. After incorporation of TRH-derived radiomic features, the TRH score emerged as the only independent predictor of pCR, whereas the associations of clinicopathologic variables were attenuated (Table 3).

Separately, TVRR was significantly higher in the pCR group than in the non-pCR group (p =0.005).

Model construction, performance comparison, and integrated nomogram evaluation

Based on the selected imaging features and significant clinicopathologic variables, five MR-based models were developed: the pre-NAC model, the mid-NAC model, the delta model, the TRR model, and the TRH model. In addition, a standalone clinical model was constructed using significant clinical predictors.

ROC curve analyses for all models in the training and validation cohorts are shown in Fig. 3. The TRH model demonstrated strong discriminative performance in the validation cohort, with an AUC of 0.83 (95% CI: 0.70–0.97), outperforming the pre-NAC, mid-NAC, and TRR models in terms of AUC. However, pairwise comparisons using the DeLong test did not remain statistically significant after adjustment for multiple comparisons. In addition, the TRH model demonstrated relatively high specificity, suggesting potential value in reducing false-positive predictions. Discussion

This study proposed a novel treatment-related imaging strategy centered on the TRR, a spatially defined subvolume reflecting early tumor shrinkage induced by NAC. Based on this region, the concept of TRH was introduced to quantify internal spatial variation within areas of therapy-induced regression. The TRH-based model demonstrated superior performance in predicting pCR compared with conventional approaches based on pre-NAC, mid-NAC, or simple delta-based features, and retained robust predictive capability in patients with marked tumor shrinkage at mid-NAC, where residual lesions became difficult to delineate on MRI. Incorporation of TRH features with key clinicopathologic factors (N stage, enhancement type, and multifocality) into an integrated nomogram further improved predictive performance and enhanced its potential clinical utility.

While prior studies have highlighted intratumoral heterogeneity (ITH) as a valuable imaging biomarker for guiding therapy in breast cancer, most have focused on static, baseline imaging [9]. Shi et al. [7] first introduced the concept of ecological diversity quantification based on MRI and showed that habitat-based radiomic features could effectively predict pCR. Compared with conventional whole-tumor analyses, habitat-level approaches are more sensitive in capturing spatial heterogeneity within tumors. Moreover, other studies have linked ITH to HER2 expression patterns [10], suggesting its broader potential in tumor stratification. However, these studies largely overlook the dynamic evolution of tumor heterogeneity during NAC. To address this limitation, delta-based approaches have been proposed to capture longitudinal changes in tumor characteristics [11–13]. These methods typically incorporate pre-NAC and mid-NAC imaging, allowing early assessment of tumor response while providing an opportunity for subsequent treatment adaptation. Huang et al. [14] proposed a habitat-based delta model that integrates temporal changes with spatial subregion analysis, leading to partial improvements in predictive performance. Nevertheless, most delta approaches rely on whole-tumor segmentation and may be prone to information loss when substantial tumor regression occurs, particularly when mid-NAC lesions become indistinguishable or invisible on MRI. To overcome this limitation, we introduced the concept of the TRR, defined as the spatial extent of tumor shrinkage during therapy, enabling the isolation of treatment-responsive regions from areas with mixed or inconsistent changes. Histopathologically, the TRR corresponds to treatment-induced tissue remodeling. Following neoadjuvant therapy, tumor regions do not regress uniformly but instead undergo complex structural changes, including fibrosis, necrosis, inflammatory infiltration, and the persistence of residual tumor cells [15]. These components are heterogeneously distributed within the affected region, reflecting spatial variation in treatment sensitivity. Regions of necrosis and fibrosis are generally associated with effective tumor cell eradication, whereas residual viable tumor cells may indicate relative resistance to therapy. Based on the TRR, we developed a TRH-based framework to quantify spatial heterogeneity within treatment-responsive regions. The TRH model demonstrated favorable predictive performance, supporting our hypothesis that ecological diversity within the TRR may better reflect treatment responsiveness in breast cancer. A higher TRH score may reflect a broader and more heterogeneous pattern of response across multiple tumor subregions, indicating varying degrees of sensitivity to therapy within the tumor. Such a pattern may correspond to a more effective overall therapeutic response and is therefore associated with a higher likelihood of achieving pCR. Unlike previous studies in which greater ITH is typically associated with poorer outcomes [7], this discrepancy may be attributed to differences in the biological interpretation of heterogeneity: ITH in prior studies primarily reflects intrinsic tumor complexity and aggressiveness, whereas TRH captures treatment-induced spatial heterogeneity in therapeutic response. Our analysis demonstrated a statistically significant difference in TVRR between the pCR and non-pCR groups (p = 0.0051), indicating that greater tumor volume reduction was associated with a more favorable treatment response, consistent with previous reports by Xu et al. [16]. Unlike RECIST criteria, which rely solely on changes in the longest tumor diameter [17], TVRR captures volumetric tumor shrinkage and may better reflect overall treatment-induced shrinkage, while being less affected by localized progression. In addition, TVRR showed a positive correlation with Ki-67 expression, suggesting that tumors with higher proliferative activity may exhibit a greater extent of therapy-induced volume reduction. We further observed that, after incorporating TRH into the multivariable model, the clinicopathologic variables were no longer statistically significant. Although several clinical factors showed significant associations with pCR in univariable analyses, their effects were attenuated after inclusion of TRH-derived radiomic features, with the TRH score emerging as the dominant contributor to response prediction. As an imaging biomarker derived from therapy-induced tumor regression, TRH may reflect underlying tumor aggressiveness and treatment sensitivity, which are also partially captured by clinicopathologic factors. Such overlap in predictive information has been reported in prior radiomics studies and may lead to collinearity [18], thereby attenuating the apparent contribution of clinical variables in the multivariable model.

Our study has several limitations. First, as a single-center retrospective analysis with a relatively small sample size, the generalizability and robustness of the TRH model require validation in larger, multicenter cohorts. The relatively small validation cohort may also limit statistical power, potentially affecting the statistical significance of some model comparisons despite observable differences in performance. Second, despite applying standardized preprocessing protocols, MRI acquisition remains subject to variability in scanners and imaging parameters, which may affect radiomic feature stability. Future research may incorporate AI-based harmonization techniques or multimodal imaging fusion strategies to improve feature robustness and reproducibility. Third, manual segmentation of TRRs—although performed by experienced radiologists under blinded conditions—may introduce interobserver variability; automated, AI-driven segmentation could enhance efficiency and consistency [19, 20]. Finally, this study utilized only the first post-contrast phase of DCE-MRI without exploring the potential of multiphase or multimodal imaging. Emerging studies suggest that techniques such as isochronous proportional sampling (IPS) may further enhance the diagnostic performance of DCE-MRI [21]. Future studies should further explore its value in characterizing treatment response heterogeneity, profiling the tumor immune microenvironment, and supporting personalized therapeutic decision-making.

Conclusion

In this retrospective single-center study, we proposed a tumor regression region–based imaging strategy to characterize therapy-induced regression heterogeneity during neoadjuvant chemotherapy in HER2-positive breast cancer. Quantification of tumor regression heterogeneity showed promising performance for early prediction of pathologic complete response and provided incremental information beyond conventional whole-tumor and delta-based approaches. Although further validation in larger, multicenter cohorts is warranted, this framework offers a potential imaging biomarker for capturing treatment-related spatial heterogeneity and may contribute to response-adapted assessment strategies.
