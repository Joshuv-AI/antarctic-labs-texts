Specific monitoring of viable plant growth-promoting Bacillus velezensis PH-023 in the rhizosphere of tomato
Authors: Núria Daranas, Laura Montesinos, Nuria Sierras-Serra, Javier Velasco-Alvarez, Emilio Montesinos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPlant growth-promoting bacteria (PGPB) must be able to successfully colonize the plant rhizosphere and remain viable after their release into the field to exert beneficial effects on crop production. Therefore, understanding the behavioral dynamics of PGPB in the rhizosphere is a key challenge to evaluate their persistence and fate under field conditions. This study aimed to develop and validate a strain-specific viability quantitative PCR (v-qPCR) assay for monitoring the viable population dynamics of the PGPB B. velezensis PH-023 in the rhizosphere.ResultsA v-qPCR method was developed for the unambiguous detection and quantification of B. velezensis PH-023 viable cells and spores in the rhizosphere of tomato. The TaqMan-based qPCR assay was designed within a PH-023-specific molecular marker. The nucleic acid-binding dye PMAxx was used in combination with qPCR to selectively amplify DNA of only viable cells. The v-qPCR showed good linearity over a 4-log range and good amplification efficiency. The performance of the v-qPCR method was assessed in the rhizosphere of tomato plants treated with a commercial biostimulant product based on B. velezensis PH-023 under greenhouse conditions and high and stable survival rates of PH-023 were confirmed by v-qPCR. Finally, confocal microscopy of stained roots of tomato plants inoculated with B. velezensis PH-023-based biostimulant product using a viability test showed preliminary evidence of association of the viable inoculated bacteria with the root surface, forming large cell aggregates in comparison with untreated plants.ConclusionsThis study offers a strategy to develop and validate a strain-specific viability qPCR method for monitoring the viable population of B. velezensis PH-023 and provides evidence for the colonization ability of this strain formulated as a commercial microbial biostimulant in the rhizosphere. The method will allow further studies of ecological fate, including colonization and survival under field conditions, as well as improvements in formulations and delivery strategies for consistent agricultural benefits. It is expected that the development of the v-qPCR presented here provides a reference for similar studies with other beneficial bacterial strains in different environments. As far as we know, the application of strain-specific v-qPCR approaches for the quantification of the viable population of Bacillus sp. strains in soil or rhizosphere samples have not been reported yet.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-05209-y.

Background

Global crop production faces significant challenges in enhancing yields to meet future demands [1]. Although agrochemicals have contributed to boost crop yields, their overuse causes environmental pollution and harms living organisms [2]. In the context of the transition toward more sustainable agricultural practices, biostimulants have emerged as a promising and environmentally friendly strategy [3]. Plant biostimulants are substances or microorganisms that enhance plant nutrition efficiency, abiotic stress tolerance, and/or crop quality traits, regardless of its nutrients content [4]. A relevant group of microbial biostimulants is the plant growth-promoting bacteria (PGPB) that are plant-associated bacteria with a positive influence on the growth and development of plants [5, 6]. Bacillus spp. have been extensively reported as root-associated PGPB due to the multifaceted features of selected strains, including nutrient solubilization, nitrogen fixation, synthesis of phytohormones, production of volatile organic compounds (VOCs), hydrolytic enzymes, siderophores, antibiotics, and induction of systemic resistance in plants (ISR) [5, 7]. In addition, Bacillus spp. form resilient spores with high resistance to harsh environmental conditions, which makes them good candidates for formulating products and contributes to their ubiquitous presence in a wide range of habitats, including rhizosphere and soil [5]. Bacillus velezensis is recognized as a notable representative of this genus that belong to the “operational group B. amyloliquefaciens” [8]. This species has been reported to establish beneficial interactions with plants and selected for formulation of biostimulant and biocontrol products [9, 10].

However, to exert its beneficial effects on crop production, PGPB must be able to colonize the plant rhizosphere and remain viable after their release into the field [11]. Under changing and complex field conditions, introduced PGPB usually fail to establish or quickly decline in the rhizosphere since they are exposed to adverse conditions and intense competition with indigenous rhizosphere microbial communities [12, 13]. Therefore, understanding the behavioral dynamics of PGPB in the rhizosphere is a key challenge and also essential for optimizing formulations and inoculation strategies [13]. Consequently, the development of reliable, strain-specific monitoring methods capable of quantifying viable, and not only culturable and total cells, of the introduced microorganism is highly necessary. In parallel, in regulatory frameworks governing microbial pesticides [e.g. Regulation (EU) 1107/2009], data on post-application population dynamics and background environmental levels of microbial biological control agents is required to support assessments of environmental fate and potential non-target effects [14–16], that may suffer of similar regulatory requirements for microbial biofertilizers and biostimulants [17].

Bacillus spp. are widespread in the soil and rhizosphere [18]. Therefore, the discrimination of an introduced Bacillus strain from autochthonous wild populations of the same species or genus is required. The quantitative PCR (qPCR) has been successfully applied as strain-specific monitoring method of microbial inoculants. Within this framework, unique molecular markers for the candidate strain must be identified by next-generation sequencing (NGS) technologies [19], genome mining and sequencing [20], whole-genome comparison between strains [21], or DNA fingerprinting techniques [22]. The development of strain-specific qPCR has been widely reported for monitoring plant beneficial bacteria (Pantoea spp. Pseudomonas spp., Lactobacillus spp., and Bacillus spp. strains) in different environments, such as phyllosphere [23–26], fruit surfaces after harvesting [27], and rhizosphere [28, 29]. However, qPCR amplifies DNA from both viable and dead cells [30]. DNA and the corresponding gene markers can remain in the environment for some time after cell death, resulting in an overestimation of viable cells as reported in previous studies [24, 25, 31]. To overcome this limitation, viability qPCR (v-qPCR), which is based on the use of nucleic acid-binding dyes, such as propidium monoazide (PMA), in combination with qPCR, selectively amplify DNA of only viable cells [32]. Nevertheless, the efficiency and reliability of the v-qPCR are strongly dependent on the carefully optimization of dye type and concentration for a particular matrix [33, 34]. The v-qPCR has been well-documented in the literature for several bacterial foodborne pathogens [35–38] but its use in plant pathogenic or beneficial bacteria is still limited. The v-qPCR has been used to evaluate antimicrobial effects of peptide compounds on Xylella fastidiosa [31] and the efficacy of seed sanitation treatments on Ca. Liberibacter solanacearum [39] as well as the colonization dynamics of the biocontrol strains Pantoea agglomerans CPA-2 [40], Lactobacillus plantarum PM411, and B. velezensis A17 [24, 25] in greenhouse and field trials. However, few reports have focused on the quantification of viable microorganisms in soil or rhizosphere using PMA coupled with qPCR [41, 42] but none of them involved plant-beneficial bacteria, including Bacillus sp. strains.

The biostimulant strain B. velezensis PH-023 was chosen in the present study because it was previously reported to enhance pepper and lettuce yield [43]. The aim of the present work was to develop and validate a strain-specific v-qPCR assay for monitoring the dynamics of B. velezensis PH-023 viable cells in the rhizosphere of tomato plants.

Methods

Bacterial strain and growth conditions

The present study was focused on B. velezensis PH-023 that was isolated from the mouth of the Vélez River in Málaga (Spain) and deposited in the Colección Española de Cultivos Tipo (CECT30677). PH-023 was cultured on Luria–Bertani (LB) agar at 28 °C for 24 h and stored in 20% glycerol at − 80 °C.

Plant material and conditions for the greenhouse experiments

Tomato seeds cv. Rio Grande (Rocalba S.A., Girona, Spain; batch number 309513CN) were germinated in kokofina: perlite (5:1) substrate (Burés, S.A.U., Girona, Spain), fertilized with 2 g L− 1 osmocote. Plantlets were transplanted into 500 mL plastic pots with the same substrate in the greenhouse under controlled environmental conditions at 26 ± 2 °C, 60 ± 10% relative humidity and a 16:8 h light: dark photoperiod. Standard nitrogen-phosphate-potassium (NPK) fertilization and irrigation, as well as insecticide and miticide sprays were applied. Plants were regularly watered (100–200 mL plant− 1 every 2–3 days).

Strain-specific qPCR and viability qPCR (v-qPCR) for B. velezensis PH-023

Identification of a strain-specific molecular marker

The whole-genome comparison strategy was carried out to identify PH-023-strain specific regions using Mauve [44] and Artemis [45] softwares. The whole genome sequence of PH-023 was compared with the B. velezensis JS25R (CP009679.1), FZB42 (CP000560.2), JP3144 (CP082283.1), and MOH1-5b (CP061853.1) genomes deposited in GenBank database. The strain-specificity of the putative unique regions was ensured in silico using the BLAST program at National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database. Then, forward and reverse primers were designed within three selected unique regions for qPCR assays (Table 1). To check the strain-specificity of the three new primer pairs, qPCR was carried out in a total volume of 20 µL containing 1× SYBR® Green PCR Master Mix (Invitrogen, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA), 0.5 µM of forward and reverse primer, and 2 µL of genomic DNA. Bacterial species listed in Table 2 that included 24 Bacillus sp., 16 Pseudomonas sp., and 4 Pantoea sp. strains were tested. The thermal cycling conditions for the amplification were as follows: 10 min at 95 °C for initial denaturation; 50 cycles of 15 s at 95 °C, and 1 min at 60 °C; and a final melting curve program of 60 to 95 °C with a heating rate of 0.5 °C s− 1. All reactions were performed in triplicate and were carried out in a QuantStudio™ 5 Real-Time PCR System (Applied biosystems, Foster City, USA). A non-template control (NTC), using water instead of genomic DNA, and positive control with PH-023 DNA were included in all qPCR runs.

Design of B. velezensis PH-023 strain-specific TaqMan-based qPCR

A TaqMan-based qPCR assay was designed using the forward (A-For) and reverse (A-Rev) primers and a TaqMan Probe (A-Probe) (Table 1). The nucleotide sequence of the TaqMan probe was designed by PrimerQuest tool and was labelled with the 6-carboxyfluoresceine (FAM) reporter dye at 5’ end and with the 6-carboxytetramethylrhodamine (TAMRA) quencher dye at 3’ end. The specificity of the TaqMan qPCR design for PH-023 was tested using a collection of Bacillus sp., Pseudomonas sp., and Pantoea sp. strains (Table 2) after optimization of the concentration of the primers and probes. qPCR was carried out in a total volume of 20 µL containing 1x TaqMan Universal PCR Master Mix (Invitrogen), 0.3 µM A-For and A-Rev primers, 0.2 µM of probe, and 2 µL DNA sample. The thermal cycling conditions for the amplification were as follows: 2 min at 50 °C, 10 min at 95 °C, and 50 cycles of 15 s at 95 °C, and 1 min at 60 °C. NTC, using water instead of genomic DNA, and positive control with PH-023 DNA were included in all qPCR runs. All reactions were performed in triplicate and were carried out in a QuantStudio 5 Real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystem).

Standard curves for qPCR in rhizosphere homogenates

The sensitivity and amplification efficiency of the qPCR assay for PH-023 were evaluated by developing standard curves in rhizosphere homogenates. Cell suspensions of PH-023 (mixture of vegetative cells and spores from 72-h old solid culture) were prepared in sterile distilled water at high concentration (1–5·108 CFU mL− 1) and diluted to appropriate concentrations in rhizosphere homogenate. Previously, the cell concentration was checked by OD600 measure, considering that 0.6 correspond to 108 CFU mL− 1 and was confirmed by colony counts. To obtain rhizosphere homogenate, roots and rhizosphere from tomato plants were infused with 50 mM sterile phosphate buffer (PBS, pH 7.0) and 0.1% tween 80 (at the proportion of 1 g / 10 mL) in a 100 mL sterile bottle and mixed in an orbital shaker (KS501 digital; IKA Labortechnik, Staufen, Germany) at 175 rpm for 1 h. The concentrations covered a 6-log range, from 4.2·102 to 4.2·108 CFU g− 1. Standard curves were prepared in triplicate. DNA of vegetative cells and spores from 1 mL of rhizosphere homogenate samples was isolated following the manufacture instructions of the DNeasy Powersoil Pro kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) and qPCR was carried out as described above.

Viability qPCR assay

The concentration of PMAxx reagent (Biotium, Fremont, California, USA), which can only penetrate cells with damaged cellular membrane and covalently bind to DNA after photolysis, inhibiting the PCR amplification of dead cell DNA, was optimized according to the procedure described previously by Daranas et al. [25]. Briefly, five concentrations were evaluated (15, 20, 25, 35, or 50 µM) and an appropriate volume of PMAxx stock solution at 2000 µM (7.5, 10, 12.5, 17.5, or 25 µL) was added into 1 mL of viable or dead vegetative cell suspension of PH-023 strain at 107 CFU mL− 1 both in water and in rhizosphere homogenate. PH-023 vegetative cells were harvested from a 3.5-hour-old liquid culture at log-phase, where sporulation was not initiated. Dead cells were obtained by heating a cell suspension at 100 °C for 30 min (ThermoMixer F1.5; Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany). The loss of viability of dead vegetative cells was checked by plating on LB agar followed by incubation for 48 h at 28 °C. After PMAxx addition, cell suspensions were incubated for 10 min in the dark at 20 °C, photo-activated for 15 min with the PhAST Blue photoactivation system (GenIUL, Barcelona, Spain), collected by centrifugation at 13200xg for 10 min, and washed with 50 mM sterile PBS (pH 7.0) as described previously [25]. Samples of viable and dead cells without being treated with PMAxx were also used. DNA extraction of PMAxx and non-PMAxx treated samples in water was performed according to the method described by Schmidt et al. [49] with some modifications [25], whereas DNA from rhizosphere homogenate samples was isolated following the manufacture instructions of the DNeasy powersoil Pro kit (Qiagen). Each experimental condition was assayed in duplicate. The TaqMan-based qPCR was performed in triplicate as described above. The effect of PMAxx on DNA amplification suppression was tested in viable and dead cells and expressed as “signal reduction”. Signal reduction was calculated by subtracting Ct values between non-PMAxx and PMAxx-treated samples.

To check the v-qPCR method as a strain-specific detection and quantification tool of viable cells, the sensitivity and amplification efficiency of the v-qPCR assay for PH-023 were evaluated by developing standard curves in rhizosphere homogenates. Cell suspensions of viable and dead PH-023 vegetative cells were prepared in rhizosphere homogenate. Samples were prepared from covering a 5-log range from 2·103 to 2·108 CFU g− 1 of viable or dead cells. Also, cell suspension of a mixture of viable cells (7·107 − 7·103 CFU g− 1) with a constant number of dead cells (7·107 CFU g− 1) were prepared in rhizosphere homogenate. From each sample, 1 mL was treated with PMAxx at 35 µM following the procedure described above. DNA extraction of PMAxx and non-PMAxx treated samples were performed using the DNeasy powersoil Pro kit (Qiagen) as described above. The strain-specific TaqMan qPCR assay for PH-023 was performed, and two NTC were included in all PCR runs: (i) using water instead of genomic DNA, and (ii) using DNA isolated from rhizosphere homogenates without PH-023 cells. All reactions were performed in triplicate as described above. Ct values were plotted against the logarithm of the initial number of CFU mL− 1 and standard curves were generated by a linear regression of the plotted points. Population dynamics of B. velezensis PH-023 in the rhizosphere of tomato plants

The population level of B. velezensis PH-023 in the rhizosphere of tomato plants under greenhouse conditions was evaluated. The experimental design consisted of two theses: the B. velezensis PH-023-based formulated product (BFP) (Terra-Sorb® Symbiotic, Bioiberica, S.A.U) [43, 50] with three biological replicates and an untreated control (UTC) with one biological replicate. Preliminary work confirmed qPCR strain specificity (no amplification in untreated tomato rhizosphere samples; data not shown); therefore, a single biological replicate was used for UTC. Each biological replicate included 2 tomato plants. The concentration of spores and viable vegetative cells of PH-023 contained in the BFP was determined by plate counting on LB agar and v-qPCR.

The treatment application was performed by root irrigation to the substrate with 1 mL of the BFP (2–3 · 108 CFU mL− 1) per plant during the watering with 150–200 mL of water. Treatments were applied at intervals of 7 days over four weeks, and the first one was carried out just after transplanting plants. The population levels of PH-023 in the rhizosphere of tomato plants were tracked at 7 days after each application (four sampling time points). Rhizosphere samples from two tomato plants were collected for each biological replicate and rhizosphere homogenates were obtained as described above. The population levels of PH-023 in the rhizosphere were determined by three different approaches: strain-specific TaqMan-based qPCR (spores and viable and dead vegetative cells), v-qPCR (spores and viable vegetative cells), and non-selective plate counting (germinated spores).

For strain-specific TaqMan-based qPCR and v-qPCR assays, DNA of rhizosphere homogenates was isolated using the DNeasy Powersoil Pro kit (Qiagen) as described above. For the v-qPCR analysis, before DNA isolation, samples were treated with PMAxx® at 35 µM, according to the optimized procedure described above. For both qPCR and v-qPCR analysis, TaqMan-based qPCR using the strain-specific primers (A-For and A-Rev) and TaqMan probe (A-Probe) (Table 1) was carried out in triplicate as described above. Two NTC, using water instead of genomic DNA and DNA isolated from rhizosphere homogenate without PH-023, and two positive controls, using PH-023 DNA and DNA isolated from rhizosphere homogenate supplemented with BFP, were included in all qPCR runs. The number of total and viable cells estimated by qPCR and v-qPCR, respectively, was obtained by interpolating the Ct values from the unknown samples against the corresponding developed standard curve and expressed as log10 CFU per gram of rhizosphere. For non-selective plate counting, rhizosphere homogenates were 10-fold serially diluted, and appropriate dilutions were seeded by 20 µL drops onto agar plates. To count presumptive PH-023 spores, LB agar supplemented with 50 µg mL− 1 cycloheximide (Sigma, St. Louis, Missouri) to avoid fungal growth was used, and samples were incubated at 80 °C for 10 min before being seeded onto LB plates to inactivate vegetative cells and count only spores. Plates were incubated at 28 °C for 24–48 h. The culturable population levels of PH-023 were expressed as the log10 CFU per gram of rhizosphere.

B. velezensis PH-023 - tomato root association

Microscopy was carried out to determine the interaction of B. velezensis PH-023 with tomato roots under greenhouse conditions. The experimental design consisted of two theses, the BFP and UTC, with three tomato plants each thesis. The product application was performed by root irrigation to the substrate with 4 mL of BFP per plant during the watering with 150–200 mL of water. Treatments were applied at intervals of 7 days over three weeks and the first one was carried out just after the transplanting. Root sampling was performed 7 days after the last application. Root samples from each tomato plant were collected for both BFP and UTC theses. Single roots were stained with a bacterial viability kit (LIVE/DEAD® BacLight™ Molecular Probes ref. L7012, Invitrogen) at 12 µM SYTO9 and 30 µM propidium iodide (PI) for a period of 15 min at room temperature in the dark. SYTO9 stains all viable bacteria in green, while PI stains in red the bacteria whose membranes were damaged (non-viable bacteria). The root-bacteria association was observed via spectral confocal laser microscopy (CLSM) (NIKON A1R) under 60x objective using 488 nm (blue laser; green fluorescence) and 543.5 nm (green laser; red fluorescence) channels. The images were processed using NIS-Elements AR v 4.10 program (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Statistical analysis

A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to test the significance of (i) the effect of PMAxx concentration in the suppression of DNA amplification (signal reduction) on dead and viable cells of PH-023, and (ii) the effect of the quantification method to estimate the PH-023 population levels in the rhizosphere for each sampling time point. Means of the ∆Ct (signal reduction) and log10 of CFU g− 1 were separated according to Waller-Duncan’s test at a p value of ≤ 0.05 (IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 25.0 released in 2017 by IBM Corp, Armonk, New York, USA).

Results

Strain-specific qPCR and viability qPCR (v-qPCR) for B. velezensis PH-023

Three putative strain-specific molecular markers (A, 360 bp; B, 341 bp; C, 518 bp) were found in the PH-023 genome (Supplementary Table S1). Their specificity was confirmed both in silico, since no NCBI homologies were reported, and by SYBR Green-based qPCR using the three designed primer-pairs. No amplification or random fluorescence signal at Ct values higher than 35 was observed with the bacterial strains listed in Table 2 different from PH-023 (Supplementary Table S2). Eventually, molecular marker A was selected to design the PH-023-specific TaqMan-based qPCR assay. The 171 bp-length sequence amplified was not annotated by RASTk pipeline. The in silico analysis using BLAST to align the complete genome of PH-023 and the 171 bp-length sequence revealed that this was a single-copy sequence.

The standard curve of the TaqMan-based qPCR assay, which was prepared with a mixture of vegetative cells and spores of PH-023 in rhizosphere homogenate, showed good linearity over a 5-log range (from 4.2·103 to 4.2·108 CFU g− 1), and the lowest limit of detection was 4.2·103 CFU g− 1 (Ct value = 37–38). The equation of regression curve (three replicate means) was Ct = -3.6936 · log CFU g− 1 + 50.827 with high correlation coefficient value (R2 = 0.99) and an amplification efficiency (E) of 86.5%.

The effect of different PMAxx concentrations on the signal reduction (SR) (difference of Ct value [∆Ct] between non-PMAxx-treated and PMAxx-treated samples) was determined on dead and viable cell suspensions, both in water and rhizosphere (Fig. 1). The PMAxx at 20 µM was the lowest concentration showing good results as inhibited the DNA amplification of dead cells at 2 × 107 CFU mL− 1 (SR = 21) and viable cells were not affected (SR < 2). In addition, no significant differences were observed among PMAxx concentrations in PH-023 suspensions in water. However, in rhizosphere homogenate samples 20 µM PMAxx showed only a SR of 8 and then, higher concentrations of PMAxx were tested. Significant differences were observed among PMAxx concentrations and 35 µM was the lowest concentration showing a SR higher than 14 in dead cell suspensions (2·107 CFU mL− 1), while viable cells were not affected (SR < 2). Based on these results, the PMAxx concentration of 35 µM was chosen for further experiments.

Standard curves were developed in rhizosphere homogenates to check the v-qPCR method as a specific bacterial detection and quantification tool (Fig. 2). The equations of regression curves, as well as the amplification efficiencies and sensitivities, are presented in Supplementary Table S3. Standard curves of viable cells (with or without PMAxx treatment) showed high correlation coefficient values (R2 = 0.97–0.99) and amplification efficiencies above 83%. The relationship was linear over the range of 2·103 to 2·108 CFU g− 1. On dead cells treated with PMAxx, Ct values higher than 35 were obtained over the range from 2·103 to 2·107 CFU g− 1, meaning that the amplification was inhibited. Only, at concentrations of 2·108 CFU g− 1 lower Ct values were reached, namely around 25. In mixtures of viable cells (from 7·107 − 7·103 CFU g− 1) and a fixed quantity of dead cells (7·107 CFU g− 1) treated with PMAxx, the standard curve achieved a high correlation coefficient (R2 = 0.99), with an amplification efficiency of 98.1%. On the contrary, without PMAxx treatment, the qPCR assay was unable to differentiate between DNA from viable and dead cells. The detection and quantification limits of the v-qPCR were 2·103 CFU g− 1.

Population dynamics of B. velezensis PH-023 in the rhizosphere of tomato plants

PH-023 was monitored in the rhizosphere of tomato plants under greenhouse conditions by the strain-specific TaqMan-based qPCR and v-qPCR, and non-selective plate counting (Fig. 3, Supplementary Table S4). Despite the absence of the sampling point immediately after the first inoculation, this initial value was estimated based on the known concentration of B. velezensis PH-023 in the BFP (8.3 log CFU mL− 1) and the corresponding inoculum dose applied to the plants (5.6 log CFU mL− 1 of substrate). B. velezensis PH-023 was detected by both qPCR and v-qPCR methods in all rhizosphere samples from inoculated tomato plants, whereas it was not detected in UTC samples. However, a population level between 4.5 and 4.9 log CFU g− 1 was quantified by non-selective plate counting in UTC samples along the experiment, indicating the presence of native spores different from PH-023 in the rhizosphere of tomato plants. Indeed, the native spores represented the 8–12% of the total germinated spores quantified in the BFP rhizosphere samples.

Discussion

In the present study, we developed and validated a strain-specific v-qPCR for the unambiguous detection and quantification of B. velezensis PH-023 viable cells and spores in the rhizosphere of tomato plants. The development and use of strain-specific v-qPCR has been scarcely reported for monitoring plant beneficial Bacillus sp., especially in the rhizosphere. For example, a v-qPCR was developed for the biocontrol agent B. velezensis B-9987, despite the method was not strain-specific and was only validated in pure culture and biopesticide formulation but not in plant material [51]. Also, the ecological fitness of the biocontrol strain B. velezensis A17 was studied using a strain-specific v-qPCR but in the phyllosphere of different crops [25].

Considering that other strains of Bacillus spp. may be native inhabitants of soil [18], the strain-specificity is a key factor in monitoring PH-023. Therefore, in the present study, the primer pair and TaqMan probe set was successfully designed within a PH-023-specific molecular marker identified by whole genome comparison in line with other studies [25, 28]. In addition, its specificity was both confirmed in silico and by the absence of amplification signal by qPCR in several plant-associated bacterial strains. The designed qPCR assay fulfills the requirements for satisfactory analysis of PH-023 in the rhizosphere of tomato plants with a range of quantification from 4.2 103 to 4.2 108 CFU g− 1. Similar results were reported in other qPCR assays designed to quantify microbial inoculants, including Bacillus sp., in flowers, fruits, leaves, and rhizosphere [24, 25, 28, 40, 52].

The PMAxx reagent (upgraded version of PMA) was used to set up the v-qPCR method. PMAxx was reported to have higher sensitivity to differentiate viable and dead cells and less false binding to the DNA of viable cells than PMA [53]. The optimized PMAxx concentration of 35 µM allowed selectively detect viable cells of B. velezensis PH-023 in the rhizosphere, avoiding both the qPCR amplification of heat-killed vegetative cells and the cytotoxic effect on viable cells. In accordance with this result, previous studies reported between 20 and 60 µM as the optimized dye concentration for Bacillus sp [25, 38, 51, 54]. and such variability was likely associated with the type and composition of the matrix [33, 34]. Indeed, higher dye concentrations were needed when complex sample matrices, including soil, were analyzed because some dye may bind to contaminants in the sample [54–56]. The developed v-qPCR assay worked properly for a precise quantification of only viable B. velezensis PH-023 in the range from 2·103 to 2·107 CFU g− 1 in tomato rhizosphere. Previous studies have reported similar results using v-qPCR to detect and quantify Bacillus spp. in milk, biopesticide formulations, and the phyllosphere [25, 51, 54]. In addition, the developed v-qPCR assay proved to be suitable despite the presence of dead PH-023 cells, which is particularly relevant given the adverse and fluctuating environmental conditions in the rhizosphere that can reduce microbial survival [13].

High and stable survival rates of PH-023 were confirmed in the rhizosphere of tomato plants after PH-023 root inoculation under greenhouse conditions. The population dynamics of PH-023 was consistent with those previously reported for other Bacillus spp. inoculated on aerial plant surfaces and in the rhizosphere [25, 29]. The stable abundance of PH-023 in the rhizosphere may positively be correlated with the plant growth-promoting effects of the microbial biostimulant as reported previously [43]. Although previous studies also highlighted the ability of Bacillus sp. to effectively colonize the rhizosphere of maize, lettuce, tomato, and pepper plants, antibiotic-selecting plate counting or conventional qPCR approaches were used for assessing the population dynamics [28, 52, 57–59]. Interestingly, the total population level of PH-023 estimated by qPCR was higher than the viable population level (v-qPCR). Taking into account that spores are considered alive in a dormant state, capable of germinating under favorable conditions and becoming vegetative cells [60], the entire population of PH-023 spores was estimated by both qPCR and v-qPCR. In addition, the qPCR also amplified DNA from PH-023 vegetative cells being culturable, viable but non-culturable, or dead, as well as non-degraded DNA released from dead PH-023 cells, which could be present in the rhizosphere. These results are in line with other studies in which the population level of the biocontrol strain L. plantarum PM411 was overestimated by qPCR [24, 25]. However, spores and only viable vegetative cells of PH-023 were quantified by v-qPCR since PMAxx pretreatment avoided the amplification of DNA from dead vegetative cells. Although the proportion of spores and viable vegetative cells within the v-qPCR quantification remains unknown, both contribute to the biostimulant effect since spores can germinate into viable vegetative cells. Moreover, the population level of germinated spores in B. velezensis PH-023-treated plants, estimated by non-selective plate counting after a heat-shock treatment to inactivate vegetative cells, was higher than the viable population level (v-qPCR). These results suggest that other native spores different from PH-023 but probably belonging to Bacillus genus may be present in the rhizosphere of the tomato plants [5]. Therefore, they could grow in the medium with similar morphology to PH-023 hindering differentiation between them. Consistent with these results, the population of B. velezensis A17 spores on aerial plant surfaces was also overestimated by non-selective plate counting in a previous study [25]. Given the overestimation associated with non-specific plate counting and the amplification of DNA from dead cells by conventional qPCR, the use of accurate, strain-specific and viability-based quantification methods is essential for the reliable determination of target microorganism population levels.

Microscopy analyses to study biofilm formation and root association by plant-beneficial bacteria in their natural environments are essential for elucidating their functional roles during plant-microbe interactions [61, 62]. Confocal microscopy of stained roots from BFP-treated tomato plants showed an association of viable B. velezensis PH-023 with the root surface, forming large cells aggregates. These results are in accordance with other studies focused on PGPB Bacillus sp. strains and are potentially indicative of biofilm formation [61–63]. Although endophytic colonization in the stem of tomato plants was previously reported for B. amyloliquefaciens FZB42 [59], PH-023 cells did not enter tomato roots as it was reported for the biocontrol strain B. velezensis S4 [63]. The staining of root samples with a viability kit allowed discrimination between root-associated viable and dead bacterial cells despite lacking strain-specificity. However, considering that in the present study PH-023-inoculated roots showed higher numbers of alive cell aggregates than non-inoculated roots, it was suggestive of the strain interaction with the tomato roots. Given that these findings remain preliminary, further studies using strain-specific imaging approaches, ideally performed under greenhouse conditions, are required to confirm and accurately track the colonization pattern of PH-023 in the rhizosphere. Reported systems based on auto fluorescent proteins have been used for PGPB to track specifically their colonization abilities [59]. However, this approach presents an inherent limitation, as the genetic modification may affect bacterial physiology, growth, and fitness [64]. Consequently, reporter-tagged strains may not fully reflect the behavior of the wild-type, potentially biasing colonization assessments. Therefore, validation through comparative analyses between tagged and non-tagged strains is recommended to ensure that the reporter system does not significantly alter bacterial performance.

Conclusions

In summary, this study offers a strategy based on a strain-specific viability qPCR method for directly monitoring the viable population of B. velezensis PH-023 and provides preliminary evidence for the colonization ability of this strain formulated as a commercial microbial biostimulant in the rhizosphere of tomato plants under greenhouse conditions. It is expected that v-qPCR provides a reference for the quantitative study of other beneficial bacterial strains in different environments. The developed method will allow further studies of ecological fate, including colonization and survival under field conditions, the impact of the long-term application of B. velezensis PH-023 on the biodiversity of rhizosphere microbial community, as well as improvements in formulations and delivery strategies for ensuring its environmental friendliness and sustainable use and consistent agricultural benefits.
