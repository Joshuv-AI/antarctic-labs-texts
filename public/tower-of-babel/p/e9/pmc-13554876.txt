Abdominal Aortic Pseudoaneurysm
Authors: Maraida L Serrant Hernández, Utkarsh Kohli, Daniel Duarte, Luisa Cervantes, Ana Maria Rodriguez Barreto, Juan Bolivar, Danyal Khan, David Kalfa, Michele Taubman, Ignacio Rua
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVascular Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (vEDS) is a rare connective tissue disorder characterized by arterial fragility and life-threatening vascular complications. Presentation in pediatric age group patients remains rare and is therefore not well characterized.Case SummaryA 14-year-old man presented with severe abdominal and back pain. Imaging revealed a posterior abdominal aortic pseudoaneurysm with retroperitoneal hemorrhage and progressive aortic wall thickening. He underwent urgent open repair with Dacron interposition grafting and visceral artery reconstruction. Postoperative course was complicated by transverse colon perforation requiring partial colectomy and ostomy, as well as left nephrectomy due to renal infarct. Genetic testing identified a novel likely pathogenic collagen type III alpha 1 chain sequence variation, confirming vEDS.DiscussionThis case highlights a severe early-onset phenotype of vEDS and underscores the importance of early recognition, serial imaging, and multidisciplinary management.Take-Home MessageUnexplained aneurysms/pseudoaneurysms in young patients should raise suspicion for vEDS; early surgery and multidisciplinary care are essential.Key Words: aortic pseudoaneurysm, child, Ehlers-Danlos syndrome

Clinical Course and Case Highlights

History of Presentation

A 14-year-old boy presented with an acute onset of severe abdominal and bilateral lower back pain, which was associated with pallor, diaphoresis, and an episode of acute dysuria. There was no history of trauma, fever, recent illness, chest pain, or gastrointestinal symptoms. On arrival at the emergency department, he was hemodynamically stable. Physical examination revealed diffuse abdominal tenderness without peritoneal signs.

Past Medical History

History was notable only for attention deficit hyperactivity disorder and orchiopexy in infancy. There was no known family history of connective tissue disease, aneurysm, or sudden cardiac death.

Differential Diagnosis

Initial considerations included infectious aortitis, large-vessel vasculitis such as Takayasu arteritis, IgG4-related periaortitis, retroperitoneal fibrosis, and connective tissue disorder–associated vascular pathology. The absence of trauma and the patient’s age broadened the differential to include inflammatory and genetic causes of aortic wall instability.

Investigations

Initial computed tomography angiography revealed a pseudoaneurysm arising from the posterior abdominal aorta at the level of the superior mesenteric artery measuring approximately 5 × 5 × 8 mm (Figure 1). Hypodensity was seen surrounding the descending thoracic and the abdominal aorta, corresponding to periaortic hematoma. Follow-up magnetic resonance imaging performed 5 days later demonstrated upper abdominal aortic wall thickening, edema-like signal, and enhancement consistent with aortitis or periaortitis, with interval enlargement of the pseudoaneurysm to 9 × 12 × 14 mm (Figure 2). Repeat computed tomography angiography obtained 2 days afterward showed continued rapid enlargement to 9 × 14 × 17 mm (Figure 3). Echocardiography revealed no structural abnormalities and fair left ventricular systolic function without pericardial effusion. Electrocardiography demonstrated a sinus rhythm with probable left ventricular hypertrophy and normal axis and intervals. Laboratory evaluation showed mild leukocytosis and elevated inflammatory markers, prompting infectious and rheumatologic evaluation. Rapid duo whole genome sequencing identified a heterozygous likely pathogenic variant in collagen type III alpha 1 chain (COL3A1) (c.629G>A, p.G210D), consistent with autosomal dominant vascular Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (vEDS); maternal testing was negative, and inheritance from the father could not be assessed.

Management

Given rapid pseudoaneurysm expansion and consequent risk of rupture, the patient underwent urgent open repair for posterior perforation of the visceral abdominal aorta. The procedure included placement of an 18-mm Dacron interposition graft with renal and superior mesenteric artery Carrel patches, temporary supraceliac to infrarenal aortic bypass, and left hemidiaphragm repair (Figure 4). The pathology report of the resected specimen demonstrated fragments of arterial wall with adjacent fibrosis, organizing hemorrhage, and focal acute and chronic inflammatory infiltrates, consistent with a pseudoaneurysm. The postoperative course was complicated by transverse colon perforation requiring partial colectomy with end colostomy and Hartmann’s pouch formation. He subsequently developed intra-abdominal collections requiring washout and reinforcement of the Dacron graft. Renal artery compromise led to left renal infarction and nephrectomy. Postoperatively, pain was managed with a multimodal regimen. He required prolonged antimicrobial therapy for Candida and extended-spectrum β-lactamase-producing bacterial infections. Blood pressure was strictly controlled using intravenous nicardipine, which was transitioned to angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibition. Serial vascular imaging and close multidisciplinary management were maintained.

Outcome and Follow-Up

Over the subsequent weeks, the patient demonstrated gradual clinical recovery, with progressive improvement in pain control that allowed for stepwise de-escalation of analgesic therapy. He tolerated the advancement of oral intake and is engaged in physical therapy. Blood pressures remained controlled on oral metoprolol and amlodipine. Renal function stabilized with a solitary kidney. Plans were established for lifelong vascular surveillance imaging from the head to pelvis at regular intervals in accordance with current guidelines. Ongoing multidisciplinary follow-up with cardiology, vascular surgery, nephrology, genetics, and infectious disease has been arranged.

Discussion

vEDS is a rare, autosomal dominant connective tissue disorder caused by pathogenic sequence variation in COL3A1, resulting in defective or reduced type III collagen production, causing marked arterial, intestinal, and tissue fragility.1, 2, 3

The c.629G>A(p.G210D) variant reported in our patient has not been described in ClinVar and has not been observed in the gnomAD v4 (non-UK Biobank) data set. This variant occurs at a residue with high evolutionary conservation and is predicted to be deleterious (REVEL = 0.997; CADD = 28.5). An allelic variant disrupting this residue, c.629 G>T(p.G210V), has been described as pathogenic in ClinVar (ID: 101215) and has been reported in individuals with vEDS.4

vEDS affects approximately 1 in 50,000 to 150,000 individuals, accounting for approximately 5% of all patients with EDS.1, 2, 3,5 vEDS morbidity is quite high as it commonly presents with spontaneous arterial rupture or dissection, and arterial rupture represents the leading cause of mortality.3,5,6 Although events remain uncommon in early childhood, 25% of patients have had a medical or surgical complication by 20 years and 80% by 40 years.2 These events most frequently involve medium-sized abdominal arteries, including the renal, iliac, femoral, mesenteric, and hepatic vessels, as well as the abdominal aorta.3,6 Arterial events are associated with a high rate of fatality.7,8 In cases of unexplained arterial or bowel rupture, the diagnosis of vEDS should be considered.6 As seen in our patient, early recognition remains challenging, particularly in pediatric patients without classic phenotypic features.5 Diagnosis during childhood is most often prompted by a positive family history.5 In our patient, rapid pseudoaneurysm enlargement over several days despite a small initial size highlights the unpredictable and aggressive behavior of vascular complications in vEDS. Given the rarity of this presentation in pediatric patients, management is particularly challenging and often requires coordinated care by a multidisciplinary team.6,7 In the absence of life-threatening complications, a conservative strategy is generally preferred, emphasizing strict blood pressure control to minimize hemodynamic stress and arterial wall stress, high-dose vitamin C to promote collagen cross-linking, and regular noninvasive vascular imaging to detect new or evolving lesions.8,9 Both surgical and catheter-based interventions are associated with substantial risk in vEDS due to extreme tissue fragility and impaired wound healing.9, 10, 11 Given the rapid enlargement of the pseudoaneurysm and the imminent risk of rupture, our patient underwent urgent surgical repair to prevent a catastrophic vascular event. Even after successful repair, patients remain at lifelong risk for the development of new vascular lesions and recurrent arterial events.9, 10, 11 Current American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association guidelines recommend baseline head-to-pelvis vascular imaging, strict blood pressure control, and longitudinal surveillance.7 The findings in our patient reinforce the importance of considering connective tissue disorders in young patients with unexplained vascular pathology and underscore the critical role of coordinated multidisciplinary care in optimizing outcomes.

Conclusions

Given its substantial morbidity and mortality, vEDS should be included in the differential diagnosis of otherwise unexplained spontaneous vascular aneurysms, pseudoaneurysms, or rupture. Serial imaging, aggressive hemodynamic control, and timely surgical intervention are critical to preventing fatal rupture. Early diagnosis enables structured lifelong vascular surveillance and risk modification strategies.

Take-Home Messages

•Rapidly evolving pseudoaneurysms in otherwise healthy pediatric patients, particularly in the absence of trauma, should be considered a vascular emergency and prompt immediate evaluation for underlying connective tissue disorders such as vascular Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, even in the absence of classic phenotypic features.
