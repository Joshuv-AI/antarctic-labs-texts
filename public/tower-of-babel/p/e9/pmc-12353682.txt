HIV-related microaggressions and depressive symptoms among people living with HIV: the mediating roles of internalised stigma and coping with shame
Authors: Aliye Canan Taşlıoğlu Sayıner, Bulent Turan, Arzu Nazlı, Deniz Özer, Hamed Abdollahpour Ranjbar, Salih Atakan Nemli, Ahmad Nejat Ghaffari, Merve Mert Vahabi, Nur Miray Ayhan, Deniz Gokengin, Andrea Norcini Pala
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesOvertly experienced HIV-related stigma is associated with depressive symptoms for people living with HIV (PLWH). Research suggests that an important mediating mechanism in this association is internalised stigma, where PLWH accept negative societal views about HIV, apply them to themselves and develop a negative view of themselves. Microaggressions are subtle and indirect discriminatory behaviours and, like overtly experienced stigma, may also contribute to depressive symptoms. We tested this hypothesis and examined the mediating roles of internalised stigma and maladaptive ways of coping with shame in the association between microaggressions and depressive symptoms among PLWH in Türkiye.MethodsThis study examines the serial mediating roles of internalised HIV stigma and shame-related coping strategies (ie, withdrawal and self-attack) in the association between HIV-related microaggressions and depressive symptoms among PLWH. PLWH in Izmir, Türkiye (N=308) completed validated scales measuring experiences of microaggressions, internalised HIV stigma, coping strategies related to shame and depression symptoms.ResultsStructural equation modelling results suggest that internalised HIV stigma significantly mediates the relationship between HIV-related microaggressions and depression symptoms. Furthermore, serial mediation analysis suggests that withdrawal and self-attack strategies of coping with shame mediate the association between internalised HIV stigma and depressive symptoms. Thus, internalised HIV stigma and shame-related coping strategies may partially stem from experiencing HIV-related microaggressions and may negatively affect the mental health of PLWH.ConclusionsFindings highlight the importance of assessing subtle forms of discrimination (ie, microaggressions) given their negative impact on the self-concept of PLWH. In addition to addressing negative attitudes and behaviours against PLWH in society, interventions that help PLWH cope with shame-related experiences might improve their mental health.

Introduction

Stigma remains a significant challenge for people living with HIV (PLWH), adversely impacting their quality of life and treatment outcomes.1 HIV-related stigma consists of a range of negative behaviours towards PLWH, including avoidance behaviours, verbal abuse and social rejection. Among these negative behaviours are microaggressions: subtle, sometimes unintentional behaviours or statements that give negative or dismissive messages to individuals belonging to a marginalised group. Unlike more overt forms of discrimination (eg, enacted or experienced stigma), microaggressions are usually covert.2

Initially conceptualised within the framework of racism, the phenomenon of microaggressions has been increasingly identified across other stigmatised identities and conditions. Limited research on microaggressions related to HIV suggests that the level of experienced HIV-related microaggressions is associated with lower viral suppression,3 reduced access to healthcare, difficulties in disclosing HIV status, diminished self-efficacy in managing HIV care and increased depressive symptoms.4 HIV-related microaggressions frequently intersect with other forms of discrimination, such as racism and homophobia, creating a cumulative burden that may increase psychosocial challenges.3

Theoretically, an important mechanism through which experienced stigma or microaggressions may affect PLWH may be internalised stigma. Individuals belonging to a stigmatised group may internalise negative attitudes originating in society, leading to internalised stigma, and acknowledge, agree with and apply the public’s negative perceptions and attitudes to themselves.1 Research on the internalisation of stigma as a mechanism through which microaggressions affect the well-being of people facing them is scarce. This is especially the case with HIV-related microaggressions. On the other hand, these mechanisms have been studied for more direct and blatant forms of enacted or experienced HIV-related stigma. For example, a recent study5 identified two interconnected pathways through which experienced and perceived HIV stigma affects the self-concept and mental health of PLWH: the intraindividual and interpersonal pathways. The intraindividual pathway links perceived HIV stigma in the community and experienced stigma from the community to higher internalised stigma and self-blame, which in turn are associated with higher depression symptoms. On the other hand, the interpersonal pathway, which involves one’s perceptions and interactions with others, negatively affects the fundamental social needs of belonging and positive social evaluation. Furthermore, the interpersonal pathway is associated with depression symptoms via intrapersonal mechanisms. Other studies also suggest that internalised HIV-related stigma mediates the relationship between community stigma and quality-of-life indicators for individuals living with HIV, such as disclosure concerns, depression, anxiety, self-esteem, and treatment adherence.1 6

When discrimination is subtle and ambiguous (ie, microaggressions), people may be even more likely to internalise others’ negative attitudes, which can be more detrimental to health.2 Given the limited research on the internalisation of HIV-related microaggressions, this study aims to be one of the first to investigate this crucial issue.

Moreover, research on HIV-related stigma remains sparse in Türkiye. The HIV prevalence in Türkiye remains relatively low at 0.1%–0.3%.7 Türkiye’s relatively collectivistic culture, emphasising social reputation, may increase stigma by negatively judging behaviours seen as socially deviant.8 In countries with low prevalence and limited public awareness of HIV, stereotypes may be stronger.9 On the other hand, Türkiye’s relatively homogeneous racial population may reduce the influence of intersectional stigma present in Western contexts, providing an opportunity to explore how HIV-related stigmatisation operates in a non-Western setting with low HIV prevalence. Therefore, Türkiye’s distinctive sociocultural and economic context might offer new valuable insights and cross-cultural comparisons.

A concept theoretically related to stigma is shame. Internalised stigma may lead to feelings of inadequacy and shame. Accordingly, research has identified a link between shame and depressive symptoms for PLWH.10 Findings from a network analysis suggest that the feeling of being ‘ashamed of having HIV’ stands out as the most crucial component in the network of internalised HIV stigma, underscoring its role in stigma-related experiences.11

While previous research has explored shame as an emotion,12 how people cope with shame needs further investigation. Coping with stressors is particularly relevant considering the psychological mediation framework,13 which posits that marginalised groups experience higher stress due to stigma, which challenges individuals’ healthy coping resources and leads to maladaptive coping strategies and emotion regulation problems. These factors then act as mediators, linking stigma-related stress to worse mental health.14 Theoretically, internalised stigma may lead to feelings of shame, which can trigger various coping strategies with shame. Research identified four primary coping responses to shame: self-attack, attacking others, avoidance and withdrawal.15 Withdrawal and self-attack are considered internalising strategies for coping with shame, where individuals either retreat from the situation or direct anger towards themselves, intensifying the feeling of shame.16 Such intense negative engagement with shame may lead to a perception where individuals view their entire selves as responsible for negative events, further intensifying their negative psychological effects. Therefore, when shame leads to self-attack or withdrawal, it can have a profound impact on depressive symptoms and other internalising problems.16 17

In line with this hypothesis, coping with shame through self-attack was found to be linked to higher self-criticism,18 and research suggests a consistent connection between shame, self-criticism and depression.19 On the other hand, attacking others and avoidance are externalising strategies,15 where individuals deflect the discomfort of shame by blaming others or disengaging emotionally from the situation. In attacking others, individuals typically do not acknowledge their shame but instead direct their efforts towards inducing negative emotions in others. With avoidance, people generally do not confront their shame directly but rather remain indifferent or use humour to minimise the situation’s significance.16 Although avoidance behaviour may contribute to depression in general,20 avoidance coping with shame specifically, as positioned in the compass of shame, is similar to the concept of distraction and may provide temporary relief from intense negative emotions.21 Therefore, both the avoidance and attacking others strategies of coping with shame generally show weaker and sometimes non-significant associations with internalising problems, such as depression and anxiety symptoms,17 and rather may lead to externalising problems.16

The present study aims to examine the association between HIV-related microaggressions and depressive symptoms, as well as the mediating mechanisms in this association, among PLWH in Türkiye. Does experiencing these subtle yet harmful discriminatory actions lead to internalised stigma and specific shame coping strategies, with downstream adverse effects on depressive symptoms? Given that theory and research suggest stronger links with depression symptoms for withdrawal and self-attack strategies of coping with shame, we will focus on these shame coping strategies. Methods

Procedure

Participants were adults aged 18 years or older living with HIV and receiving treatment. Recruitment took place during routine HIV care visits at the infectious diseases departments of Dokuz Eylül University, İzmir Kâtip Çelebi University and Ege University. Medical staff at these institutions identified eligible participants during their scheduled visits. Those who expressed interest were provided with detailed information about the study and their rights and were asked to provide written informed consent. No identifying information was collected to maintain anonymity. Data collection occurred between 1 June 2022 and 1 June 2023, with no compensation offered to participants.

Measures

HIV-related microaggressions were assessed using a 14-item scale,4 which was translated into Turkish using the back-translation method. Participants rated each item (eg, ‘You heard someone say, but he or she doesn’t look HIV positive’) on a 4-point Likert scale. The ordinal alpha was 0.92.

Internalised HIV stigma was assessed using the Turkish version22 of the negative self-image subscale of the revised HIV Stigma Scale,23 comprising seven items. One sample item is ‘Having HIV makes me feel unclean/dirty’. Participants expressed their level of agreement with each item using a 4-point Likert scale. The ordinal alpha was 0.94.

The Turkish version17 of the Compass of Shame Scale,15 containing 28 items, was used to assess participants’ coping strategies with shame (eg, ‘I blame myself for not being more careful’). It assesses four coping strategies (withdrawal, self-attack, attacking others and avoidance) on a 5-point Likert scale. Higher scores indicate a greater reliance on each coping strategy for dealing with shame. In this study, the ordinal alpha for the self-attack and withdrawal subscales was 0.86, and 0.74, respectively.

The Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression24 was used to assess depressive symptom severity (eg, ‘I was bothered by things that usually don’t bother me’). It was previously adapted into Turkish25 and requires participants to rate their responses on a 4-point Likert scale. Due to low factor loadings of the reversed items (items 5 and 8), instead of the original 10-item scale, we used an eight-item version in our study. We conducted a sensitivity analysis using both the original 10-item scale and the modified eight-item scale to ensure the robustness of our findings. The results from the two versions were highly similar and the key conclusions were the same. The ordinal alpha was 0.79.

Statistical analyses

To examine the direct and indirect paths between experienced HIV microaggressions and depressive symptoms, we used structural equation modelling in the R statistical software.26 The models were estimated using the diagonally weighted least squares method, with parametric bootstrapping with 1000 samples. Bootstrapped CIs were calculated for standardised solutions. A statistically significant indirect path is indicated when the CI excludes 0, demonstrating mediation. Covariates of medical centre, age and gender were included in all models. The fit of the models was assessed using standard goodness-of-fit indices, including χ2, comparative fit index (CFI), Tucker-Lewis index (TLI), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR). Acceptable model fit was determined based on commonly accepted cut-offs (CFI and TLI ≥0.95; RMSEA and SRMR ≤0.08).27 We adhered to the AGReMA (A Guideline for Reporting Mediation Analyses) statement to ensure comprehensive and transparent reporting of mediation analyses conducted in this study.28

Results

Participants who failed to complete 50% or more of any given questionnaire (incomplete data) were excluded from analyses, resulting in the removal of data for 73 participants. The remaining data set of 308 participants, which included some missing responses, was tested for randomness. The non-parametric test for missing data indicated a missing completely at random pattern (p=0.280), which does not require multiple imputations.29 Power analysis for the current study, with a sample size of 308 participants, an assumed small-to-medium effect size (d=0.3) and a significance level of α=0.05, indicates a power of 1−β=0.96. Depressive symptoms were significantly correlated with all other variables; higher severity of depressive symptoms was associated with higher levels of microaggression (r=0.47, p<0.01), internalised HIV stigma (r=0.53, p<0.01) and the two coping strategies related to shame, namely self-attack (r=0.50, p<0.01) and withdrawal (r=0.44, p<0.05).

In the first model (figure 1; CFI=0.97, TLI=0.98, RMSEA=0.06, SRMR=0.08), internalised HIV stigma was a significant mediator in the association between microaggression and depressive symptoms, with a significant indirect effect (β=0.12, SE=0.03; 95% CI 0.06, 0.19).

The second model (figure 2; CFI=0.98, TLI=0.98, RMSEA=0.05, SRMR=0.08) indicated that the serial indirect effect of microaggression on depressive symptoms through first internalised HIV stigma and then shame coping strategy of self-attack is significant (β=0.08, SE=0.03; 95% CI 0.03, 0.14).

Another model (figure 3; CFI=0.98, TLI=0.98, RMSEA=0.05, SRMR=0.08) indicated that the serial mediating roles of internalised HIV stigma and shame coping using withdrawal in the relationship between microaggression and depression were significant (β=0.05, SE=0.00; 95% CI 0.02, 0.09).

Conclusions

HIV-related stigma continues to be a significant barrier to the well-being and effective treatment of PLWH. Our findings suggest that experiencing HIV-related microaggressions is associated with higher levels of internalised HIV stigma. In turn, higher levels of internalised stigma are associated with maladaptive shame coping strategies, such as self-attack and withdrawal, which are further linked to higher depressive symptoms. These findings are consistent with previous research suggesting that higher levels of overtly experienced stigma lead to higher internalised stigma, with downstream effects on greater depressive symptoms.6 Microaggressions may intensify intraindividual mechanisms, such as internalised stigma and self-blame, and interpersonal mechanisms, such as diminished social belonging and positive social evaluation, through which perceived HIV-related stigma in the community contributes to higher depressive symptoms.5

Building on these findings and the literature, our second model incorporated both internalised HIV stigma and coping with shame using self-attack as serial mediators. The third model extended the analysis to include the withdrawal strategy of shame coping alongside internalised HIV stigma as serial mediators. Both models revealed significant serial pathways from microaggressions to depression severity through these mediators, suggesting a complex interplay of cognitive and emotional processes underlying the adverse effects of microaggressions.

The mediating effects of shame coping strategies highlight the significant role of dealing with shame. Those who experience shame about their HIV status, partly due to internalised stigma, may find it difficult to maintain positive beliefs about their condition. Internalised stigma can lead to disengagement from optimal health behaviours due to feelings of undeserving care or a desire to avoid thinking about their HIV status. Additionally, societal perceptions linking HIV to poor lifestyle choices can cause PLWH to believe they deserve their diagnosis. This may manifest not only in suboptimal healthcare behaviours but also psychosocially, such as withdrawing from social interactions or engaging in self-criticism during moments of introspection. Thus, withdrawal can be understood as a shame-related coping strategy in the interpersonal pathway. It may help decrease intrapersonal stress by avoiding social interactions and perceived judgement, which might temporarily relieve stigma-induced stress, but may result in increased isolation, loneliness and depressive symptoms. Conversely, self-attack is a coping mechanism in the intrapersonal pathway, where individuals internalise negative societal judgements and direct criticism inwardly, potentially leading to decreased self-esteem and greater depressive symptoms.

Initially conceptualised within the framework of racism, broadening the scope of microaggression research to examine HIV-related microaggressions (and other contexts) is important, as studies suggest that distress associated with microaggressions varies depending on the social context.3 Our findings on HIV-related microaggressions suggest implications for clinical practice, public health initiatives and systemic approaches to HIV care, offering valuable insights not only for stakeholders in Türkiye, but also for global efforts to address HIV stigma and discrimination. The interplay of stigma, shame and depression of PLWH underscores the need for multifaceted and targeted interventions to address these issues in clinical settings. Understanding the mechanism behind coping strategies with shame in PLWH and factors that minimise internalisation of stigma could be useful in creating prevention models. Furthermore, reducing depressive symptoms is essential in promoting healthcare practices for PLWH, as people experiencing depressive symptoms are less likely to achieve optimal treatment adherence. The importance of targeted interventions is also highlighted in light of previous findings suggesting that cumulative HIV stigma experiences are associated with suboptimal treatment adherence.1

From a public health perspective, these findings underscore the importance of addressing stigma at both community and systemic levels. Educational campaigns aimed at reducing misconceptions and stereotypes about HIV could help create inclusive environments, potentially decreasing the negative impact of stigma on PLWH. Programmes focusing on social stigma reduction may not only support improved mental health and healthcare engagement among affected individuals, but also address depression and lower quality of life, which are critical barriers to achieving better individual health outcomes and advancing public health efforts to reduce HIV transmission and improve population-level outcomes.30

One limitation of our study is that the sample included individuals who were already engaged in HIV care. Thus, findings might not be generalisable to PLWH not in care. Furthermore, the survey was self-reported, and findings could be susceptible to reporting bias. The cross-sectional and correlational nature of the study limits our ability to establish causal relationships, even though the causal pathways we examined were derived from theory and previous research in other fields. Despite these limitations, findings suggest important mechanisms in the association between microaggressions and depressive symptoms for PLWH.

In conclusion, these findings underscore the importance of integrating psychological support into routine care, especially in resource-constrained settings, such as Türkiye’s HIV clinics. Current barriers in Türkiye and similar contexts, such as the absence of dedicated psychologists, long wait times for psychiatric services and frequent turnover of medical trainees, should be addressed to enable the development and implementation of comprehensive care models. Incorporating mental health professionals into multidisciplinary HIV care teams can ensure that psychological factors such as stigma-related distress, which influence health behaviours, are adequately addressed.
