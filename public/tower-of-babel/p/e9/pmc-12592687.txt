Helicobacter pylori Infection Increases Thrombocytopenia and Inflammation in Patients With Hepatitis B and/or C: An Analytical Study in Cameroon
Authors: Romaric Tuono De Manfouo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and Aims Helicobacter pylori infection is associated with increased intestinal permeability. It promotes the passage of bacterial endotoxin through the portal vein and reaches the liver, constituting a factor in the aggravation of hepatitis. This study aims to investigate the impact of H. pylori coinfection with HBV/HCV on the progression of hepatic pathophysiology, specifically the inflammatory system and hematological parameters, with the goal of enhancing the care of patients with hepatitis.MethodsAn analytical cross‐sectional study was carried out for 6 months in 2023. This study was carried out in people infected with the hepatitis B and/or C virus who were being monitored at Laquintinie Hospital. After having informed the participants about the objective of the study and obtaining their consent, we collected sociodemographic data and clinicobiological history. Subsequently, the diagnosis of H. pylori infection was made by testing for immunoglobulins G and M using the ELISA method. Then, inflammation was explored through the determination of C‐reactive protein (CRP) and the cytokines Interleukin‐6 and Interleukin‐8 (IL‐6, and IL‐8). Finally, the hemogram was carried out by the flow cytometry method. The data obtained were analyzed using SPSS statistical software.ResultsThe study population consisted of 184 patients with hepatitis, including 113 (61.4%) cases of hepatitis B and 71 (38.6%) cases of hepatitis C. A total of 94 (51.1%) patients were male and 90 (48.9%) female for a mean age of 41.55 ± 16.90 years. The frequency of coinfection was 37.5%. The mean CRP was 3.79 ± 3.5 mg/dL and 2.4 ± 2.1 mg/dL in coinfected and non‐coinfected patients, respectively (p = 0.057). IL‐6 was significantly associated with coinfection (p = 0.001). Furthermore, elevated IL‐6 was a predictive sign of H. pylori infection in patients with viral hepatitis B/C (OR = 2.88 [0.82–10.11]; p = 0.01). Furthermore, 22 (12.0%) patients presented thrombocytopenia, including 21 (30.4%) in the coinfected group and 1 (0.9%) in the non‐coinfected group (p = 0.01). Moreover, the reduction in platelets is a predictive factor of H. pylori infection (OR = 2.34[0.11–7.6]; p = 0.09).ConclusionThese results suggest that hepatitis B/C and H. pylori coinfection is a factor in worsening inflammation and thrombocytopenia in patients. Hence, the a need to prevent H. pylori coinfection and control the progression of the disease in patients with hepatitis.

Introduction

Helicobacter pylori is a spiral‐shaped, microaerophilic, flagellate Gram‐negative bacterium known to colonize the gastric mucosa [1]. This bacteria can survive in the stomach's acidic environment through the secretion of an enzyme called urease, which hydrolyzes urea into ammonia; this creates the alkaline environment necessary for survival and impairs host defense mechanisms [2]. Among the factors favoring its transmission, we find living in a community, sharing cutlery, or the habit of chewing food given to infants [3]. However, the duration of exposure to H. pylori causes chronic inflammation of the gastric mucosa, which leads to atrophy and metaphase changes [4, 5]. Worldwide, 50% of the population is infected with H. pylori, while in European countries, 20% of the population is infected and in African countries, and in Cameroon in particular 80% are infected although some patients are asymptomatic carriers [6]. More specific in Cameroon, Ankouane et al. reported 72.5% [7], Agbor et al. reported 43.4% [8]. In developing countries, its prevalence varies widely depending on geography, ethnicity, age, and socioeconomic factors [9].

H. pylori causes a local infection (limited to the gastric mucosa) and promotes a general increase in proinflammatory cytokines, including interleukin IL‐1, IL‐2, IL‐4, IL‐6, IL‐8, IL‐10, IL‐17, interferon‐β, and tumor necrosis factor‐α [10]. This phenomenon not only leads to the stimulation of a local inflammatory reaction but also exacerbates various inflammatory reactions in the body [11]. H. pylori infection is associated with the progression and exacerbation of diseases other than extra‐gastric diseases, such as hematological diseases, cardiovascular diseases, and especially hepatic diseases [1].

Indeed, hepatitis is defined as inflammation of the liver, characterized by the presence of inflammatory cells in the liver parenchyma [12]. There are several forms of hepatitis including viral, autoimmune, fatty liver, alcoholic, drug‐induced, and toxin‐induced hepatitis [13]. It is assumed that most cases of hepatitis are due to viral infections worldwide [14]. Indeed, there are five classes of hepatitis viruses (A, B, C, D, and E) which are described according to their modes of transmission, their incubation period, and their evolutionary patterns [13]. According to estimates from the World Health Organization (WHO), viral hepatitis is responsible for the deaths of approximately 1.45 million people per year [15]. More precisely, hepatitis B represents 53% of primary liver cancers worldwide [16]. In Africa, 91 million people live with hepatitis B/C [17]. In Cameroon, they are the main cause of chronic liver diseases and hepatocellular carcinoma in approximately 90% of cases. Furthermore, hepatitis constitutes a real public health problem and can also worsen in the event of coinfection with H. pylori [18]. Indeed, studies have demonstrated that invasion of the intestinal mucosa by H. pylori can increase intestinal permeability and promote the passage of bacterial endotoxin through the portal vein and reach the liver [1] and that in the circulation enterohepatic, the reduction of bile pH by cholestasis creates a favorable environment for the growth of H. pylori in the liver and biliary tree [19]; hence H. pylori coinfection and hepatitis may be associated with an increased risk of gastric and extra‐gastric malignancies [11].

One of the hematological complications of coinfection is damage to the platelet lineage predisposing patients to a risk of bleeding [20]. However, information on the susceptibility of patients during coinfections to develop platelet disorders diverges and remains poorly understood. The question of H. pylori coinfection and viral hepatitis and its influence on inflammation and platelets remains a question among researchers in general, and those in sub‐Saharan Africa and Cameroon in particular. This study aims to study the influence of HBV/HCV—H. pylori coinfection on the worsening of hepatic pathophysiology, particularly on the inflammatory system and hematological parameters; to improve the care of patients suffering from hepatitis, in par,t it would allow a better understanding of the hematological and platelet complications associated with coinfection and improve the treatment of the associated complications.

Methods

Ethical Considerations

This study was carried out after obtaining ethical clearance issued by the ethics and research committee of the Université des Montagnes (Authorization N°2023/172/UdM/PR/CEAQ) and authorization from the administration of the Laquintinie Hospital of Douala (Authorization N°178/AR/MINSANTE/DHL). This study was carried out in strict compliance with the principles of research according to the Declaration of Helsinki [21]. The study population was previously informed about the benefits and risks of the research, the progress of the research, and the expected objectives through a technical sheet that was given to them. Eligible patients gave their free and informed consent by signature. The data obtained from the participants were kept confidential [22].

Design and Recruitment of Study Participants

An analytical cross‐sectional study was carried out for 6 months in the year 2023. The study population consisted of patients with hepatitis B and/or C recruited from the Gastroenterology department of Laquintie Hospital in Douala. The sample size was determined using the arithmetic formula n = t 2 × p × (1–p)/m 2 [23] where n = sample size; et = 95% confidence level; p = prevalence of H. pylori and hepatitis coinfection; and m = error fixed at 5%; having taken into account the prevalence of H. pylori infection (43.4%) obtained in a study conducted in Cameroon by Agbor et al. [8].

Regarding the selection criteria, for each participant, clinical, biological, and sociodemographic data were obtained from a pre‐established form including the type of hepatitis, duration of infection, lifestyle habits, and comorbidities/coinfections. To ensure smooth communication and good understanding by participants during discussions, local language translators, simplified forms were used when necessary. The patients included were those interned in the said hospital and were able to provide the information necessary and useful for the study. Those unable to provide it, those with coinfections, and lifestyle factors (alcohol consumption, tobacco) that are factors in inflammation and hepatitis were not included; also, patients on antiretroviral therapy were not included due to potential confounding effects on the liver function. In total, 184 patients with hepatitis B and/or C were included. Subsequently, the search for coinfection was confirmed, and then the population was grouped into those with coinfection and those without it.

Blood Samples and Biological Analyses

A volume of 04 mL of blood was obtained from each participant in 02 tubes. On the one hand, the dry tube for serological tests, and on the other hand, the EDTA anticoagulant tube for hematological analysis. Once the samples were taken, they were sent to the hematology and biochemistry/immuno‐sérology laboratories to prevent possible hemolysis.

Beforehand, once in the immuno‐sérology laboratory, confirmation of hepatitis B and C infection was carried out in each of the participants by immunoenzymatic method using the indirect ELISA principle from HBsAg, HBeAg, and Anti‐HBc on the one hand and Anti‐HCV on the other hand. Subsequently, in hematology, the hemogram was carried out on each of the EDTA tubes on a hematological counter Mindray BC 30 using a principle based on flow cytometry to make the number of cells and interpretation according to the values defined by the WHO. Subsequently, the appreciation of the form and the quality of the cells, the number of reticulocytes for the classification of anemia, if necessary, was made from a colored blood smear according to the principle of Rovanowsky [24]; and reading the blades made using an Irmerco brand optical microscope.

Subsequently, the research and diagnosis of the H. pylori infection were carried out on each of the blood samples according to the competitive ELISA method based on the competitive reaction between the sample antigen and the antigen linked to Wells of a micro‐tilting plate with the primary antibodies to allow the detection of immunoglobulins M (IgM) and immunoglobulins G (IgG) of H. pylori. The questionable cases were confirmed by a new analysis and a new blood sampling. Determination was according to the Protocol of the Reagent “ElabscienceR” of the Batch number ER020N407977 and on the System Mindray MR‐96A Elisa Chain.

The assessment of the inflammatory status of the participants was made from the determination of the C‐reactive protein (CRP) (Genrui brand reagent kits [manufacturer Lotus NL B.V. The Hague, Netherlands]) by ultra‐sensitive method from a specific analyzer of semi‐automatic Protein Analyzer PA54 whose operating principle is based on nephelometry. Then the determination of the interleukins, in particular, interleukin 6 and interleukin 8 (“Elabscience R” kits [manufacturer Elabscience biotech Inc, United States]), was dosed by the ELISA Sandwich method using the ELISA Chain (Elx50TM Automated Strip Washer Biotek). CRPs, interleukin 6, and interleukin 8 were interpreted from the reference values defined by the present–present kits as follows: HS‐CRP cut‐off values < 6 mg/L; IL‐6, a normal range 2.2–12.1 pg/mL, and IL‐8 with a normal range was 10.2–34.3 pg/mL.

The evaluation of the liver function of the patients was made from the determination of the enzymatic activity of the transaminases according to the dosage protocol described by the company manufacturing of reactive “SGM Italia” and reading to the spectrophotometer by the kinetic method.

Data Management and Statistical Analysis

The positivity of IGMs witnesses a recent infection because it appears 10–12 days after the infection and disappears in 2–3 months. IgG appears offbeat from 2 to 3 days compared with IgM and persists for many years. The data obtained was recorded using an Excel spreadsheet and analyzed by statistical software R version 4.1.1. The qualitative variables studied were the socio‐demographic and clinicobiological characteristics such as the lifestyle and clinical characteristics of patients, sex, and profession. The quantitative variables were age, IgG and IgM concentration, hematological parameters, in particular the rate of red blood cells, white blood cells, platelets, VGM, TCMH, CCMH, enzymatic activity of ALAT and ASAT, CRP concentration, IL6, and IL8. Each of the quantitative variables has been dichotomized and grouped into “low, normal, or high” depending on the reference intervals defined by the reactive manufacturer or according to the WHO. The quantitative variables were presented in the form of average and standard deviation, while the qualitative variables were presented in the form of a frequency. The comparison of the quantitative variables was made from the Student T test and the qualitative variables from the χ 2 test. Logistical regression analyses have made it possible to identify the factors associated with infection in participants from the selection and integration into the model of the main factors reported by the literature as being able to constitute confounding factors. The level of confidence for this study has been set for a value of p < 5%.

Discussion

The present study aimed to determine the frequency of H. pylori‐Hepatitis B and/or C coinfection and study its association with inflammation and hematological and mainly platelet parameters. Indeed, coinfection can be associated with the progression of diseases such as liver disease by increasing intestinal permeability and promoting the passage of bacterial endotoxin through the portal vein to reach the liver and then become an etiological agent, independent factor causing inflammation [1]. Additionally, H. pylori exerts cytopathic effects on hepatocytes in vitro [25]. Coinfection with H. pylori and hepatitis can lead to an increased risk of gastric and extra‐gastric malignancies [3]. Madala et al. in a meta‐analysis reports that coinfection of H. pylori and Hepatitis C Virus promotes the Development of Hepatocellular Carcinoma (2021) [1]; they suggest that H. pylori is known to be a strong initiator for inflammatory cascade, whereas HCV can create a chronic inflammatory state through secretion of interleukins 1, 6 and tumor necrosis factors, which together can help build a strong inflammatory state in the liver tissue potentiating the development of HCC. Understanding the pathophysiology of H. pylori and hepatitis coinfection would constitute a key factor in patient management; moreover, few studies have focused on this subject, and this is also the first to highlight the influence of this coinfection and the worsening of inflammation and thrombocytopenia in patients.

Thus, the frequency of coinfection reported was 37.5%. Alexander Gutwerk et al. (2018) reported 38% coinfection [26]. Furthermore, Mahmoud et al. (2019) reported 56.04% coinfection [27]. These differences could be explained by the contexts and geographical situations in which these studies were carried out, which differ from each other. Coinfection was associated with thrombocytopenia in patients (30.4%) and differed significantly from the non‐coinfected group (p = 0.01). Attallah et al. (2022) also reported cases of thrombocytopenia in coinfected patients [25]. Umemura et al. (2007) reported lower platelet counts in coinfected patients compared with the other group (128 vs. 158 × 103/µL; p = 0.009) [28]. Indeed, patients during coinfection have a greater susceptibility to developing thrombocytopenia [29, 30] to the point that Zhang et al. (2015) report a history of platelet transfusion during their study in these patients [30]. HBV/HCV‐H. pylori coinfection has specific effects on platelet activation and turnover [20]. Splenic sequestration of platelets, failure of platelet secretion as a result of insufficient secretion of thrombopoietin, and platelet destruction mediated by anti‐GPIIb‐IIIa autoantibodies have been proposed to be associated with thrombocytopenia in patients with hepatic cirrhosis [31, 32, 33, 34]. These observations made during this study raise the possibility that H. pylori‐HBV/HCV coinfection may contribute to thrombocytopenia, thereby predisposing patients to a risk of bleeding [35, 36]. Also, the decrease in platelets was reported during this study as a predictive factor of H. pylori infection during hepatitis (OR = 2.34[0.11–7.6]; p = 0.09). Other authors also report an association of thrombocytopenia with H. pylori infection in patients infected with HBV or HCV [30]. Also, it would be important to note that cirrhotic thrombocytopenia is a multifactorial condition in which thrombopoietin deficiency is not the main factor, because it is possible that thrombocytopenias are an indirect consequence of autoimmune reaction in patients infected with HBV/HCV and do not necessarily indicate the presence of Helicobacter species in the liver [37, 38]. Regarding other hematological parameters, this study reports 46.2% of anemias in patients and mainly regenerative. Indeed, the latter would be the result of hemorrhage due to coagulation disorders associated with liver disease, peripheral hemolysis; several authors also report this during their studies [39, 40].

Furthermore, the exploration of inflammation during this study reported a significant association between coinfection and CRP (p = 0.057). This observation was also made by Rahmani et al. (2016) [41]. Moreover, we report a statistically higher average IL‐6 in coinfected patients compared with others (p < 0.001). Wu et al. (1996) reported an average IL‐6 of 10.0 ± 9.5 pg/mL and 2.6 ± 0.5 pg/mL (p < 0.001), respectively, in coinfected and non‐coinfected patients [42]. Furthermore, IL‐8 during this study was not significantly associated with coinfection. Abbas et al. (2014) also report no significant difference in IL‐8 levels in patients with coinfection and those with a single infection [43]. Indeed, H. pylori is a major etiological factor of gastric ulcers, activating factors of cytokines and growth factors, such as interleukins [43]. CRP serum level increases and causes an acute inflammatory reaction [41]. Colonization of the gastric mucosa by H. pylori could be indirectly associated with inflammation. Furthermore, the virulence factors of H. pylori induce proinflammatory cytokines such as IL‐1, IL‐6, and IL‐8, which influence mucosal inflammation and/or gastric acid secretion [44]. This study reports in particular that a high IL‐6 level is negatively associated with a risk of H. pylori infection in patients with viral hepatitis B/C (OR = 2.88 [0.82–10.11]; p = 0.01). As previously reported by several authors, H. pylori and hepatitis coinfection is an aggravating factor in inflammation [1, 45, 46, 47].

This study was carried out on 184 patients including 113 (61.4%) with HBV infection and 71 (38.6%) with HCV infection. This difference could be explained by the structure of HBV giving it greater resistance to environmental conditions, thus increasing the probability of infection compared with HCV [16, 48, 49]. The study population was mainly male (51.1%) with a sex ratio of 1.04 for an average age of 41.55 ± 16.90 years, which corresponds to the most exposed age group to STIs linked to more frequent sexual practices [16, 50]. This study reports an association of hepatic hyperactivity with coinfection characterized by an elevation of transaminases. Indeed, 71.01% of coinfected patients presented ASAT hyperactivity compared with 59.13% of those not coinfected (p = 0.013). A total of 89.86% of coinfected patients presented ALT hyperactivity compared with 81.74% of those not coinfected (p = 0.02). Attallah et al. (2022) also reported higher transaminase activity in coinfected patients compared with non‐coinfected patients (p < 0.05), which would indicate that coinfection is an aggravating factor in transaminases activity [25]; observations also reported by other authors [30].

One of the limitations of this study lies in its transversal nature because a cohort or longitudinal study would provide more conclusive results. Also, the design of the study is made could expose to confusion factors not controllable by the examiner despite all the precautions that may have been taken. Faming studies according to the multicenter model would make it possible to have more general results and would allow more generalizable conclusions to all the population of patients presenting this coinfection. The use of more specific tests (e.g., stool antigen or breath test) during future research would improve accuracy. Nevertheless, the results obtained provide us with information on the predisposition of patients throughout hepatitis has worsening inflammation and thrombocytopenia. This study also relieves the prospect of the use of probiotics, which could act in the repair and the slowdown of the progression to a hepatocarcinoma [51].

Conclusion

Patients with hepatitis are subject to coinfection with H. pylori for which this study reported 37.5%. During coinfection, patients are subject to worsening inflammation characterized by a significant elevation of CRP, IL‐6, and IL‐8. Moreover, the latter are subject to hematological disorders mainly characterized by thrombocytopenia predisposing patients to bleeding risks. This study raises the need to prevent H. pylori infection in patients with hepatitis because it is an aggravating factor of inflammation and disorders of hematological parameters. Remedial measures such as the use of probiotics may alleviate HP infection and the consequences of viral infection in patients and could be the subject of future studies.
