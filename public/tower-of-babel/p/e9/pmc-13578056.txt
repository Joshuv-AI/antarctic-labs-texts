Longitudinal change in anterior chamber depth and its determinants: a median 4.9-year follow-up of 2,247 Japanese adults
Authors: Taiga Inooka, Ryo Tomita, Taro Kominami, Shota Fujikawa, Tetsuhito Kojima, Shinji Ueno, Yasuki Ito, Koji M Nishiguchi, Kenya Yuki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo quantify anterior chamber depth (ACD) changes and identify associated determinants in individuals with good-corrected visual acuity.MethodsIn a retrospective single‑centre cohort, participants attending a combined health and eye screening programme underwent same‑day biometry, anthropometry and routine haematology. For each participant, the annual ACD change (mm/year) was estimated as the individual-linear regression slope. The cross-sectional age–ACD relationship was characterised with restricted cubic splines and a single-knot piecewise model. Associations between annual ACD change and baseline characteristics were evaluated using ordinal logistic regression across quartiles of the slopes.ResultsA total of 2,247 participants (median age at baseline visit 52.7 years) with 10,869 visits over a median follow-up of 4.9 years were analysed. The median annual ACD change was − 1.11 × 10⁻² mm/year (interquartile range − 2.04 × 10⁻² to − 0.24 × 10⁻²). The piecewise model identified a knot at 51.3 years, with estimated ACD narrowing of − 1.52 × 10⁻² mm/year at ages ≤ 51.3 and − 0.91 × 10⁻² mm/year at > 51.3 (both P < 0.001). Ordinal models showed that younger age and shorter body height were associated with faster ACD narrowing (both P < 0.05).ConclusionACD narrowing progressed more steeply until approximately 51 years of age, with a trend toward relatively faster narrowing in shorter individuals. These findings suggest that structural predisposition to angle-closure is largely established by early middle age and support targeted monitoring and preventive strategies in middle-aged, shorter adults.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00417-026-07181-9.Keywords: Anterior chamber depth, Eye, Risk factors, Longitudinal study

Introduction

Primary angle-closure disease (PACD) is the leading cause of blindness in the Asian population [1–3], and a shallow anterior chamber depth (ACD) is its most consistent anatomical risk factor [4–7]. Various factors, including female sex [8–10], short body height [11–15], advanced age [8–11, 16, 17], high intraocular pressure (IOP) [18], uncontrolled blood sugar levels [19, 20], and obesity [13, 21] have been associated with shallow ACD, although the correlation strengths vary by race and region. Despite the clinical significance of identifying cases with faster ACD narrowing, most prior studies were cross-sectional [8, 9, 11–18, 20, 21] and did not examine longitudinal ACD changes or comprehensively assess contributing factors to ACD [22].

Determining the longitudinal ACD change rate and identifying parameters associated with this change in individuals with good corrected visual acuity (VA) could facilitate early intervention and reduce the risk of sudden vision loss. This study aimed to quantify ACD changes in individuals with good corrected VA and examine these changes in association with ocular examination findings, haematological tests, and anthropometric data in healthy check-up participants in mainland Japan.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective single-centre study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Nagoya University Graduate School of Medicine (Nagoya, Aichi, Japan) (Approval No. 2025 − 0237 37403). Informed consent was obtained using an opt‑out approach and data were anonymised before analyses to ensure confidentiality.

Participants

The participants underwent health and eye screening between April 3, 2018 and March 31, 2024 at the Aichi Health Promotion Foundation (Aichi, Japan). All the participants underwent ophthalmic examinations, anthropometric measurements, and haematological tests. The inclusion criteria were assessed at the baseline visit for items (1)–(3) and over follow-up for items (4)–(5): (1) Asian (Japanese) adults aged ≥ 20 years, (2) VA of 20/20 or better, (3) refractive errors with cylinder power within ± 3 dioptres, to exclude moderate-to-high astigmatism that may affect ACD measurements; no restriction was applied to spherical refractive error or spherical equivalent, (4) history of at least 3 check-up visits, and (5) a follow-up period of ≥ 1 year.

The exclusion criteria were: (1) history of intraocular surgery including cataract and vitreous surgery, either before baseline or during the follow-up period, (2) history of ocular disease or trauma as obtained from a medical questionnaire, and (3) individuals with any missing values in the examination protocols data described in the subsection Examination protocols. Based on these criteria, 1 eye per participant was included; if both eyes qualified, the right eye was designated as the study eye.

Examination protocols

All participants underwent comprehensive ophthalmologic assessments on a single day, including anthropometric and haematological tests and medical history collection. VA was measured using an automatic vision tester (CA-1000, Tomey, Nagoya, Aichi, Japan), and refractive error was assessed with an auto Ref/keratometer (Canon, RK-F2, Tokyo, Japan). ACD and axial length were measured automatically by partial coherence interferometry (Tomey, OA-2000, Nagoya, Aichi, Japan). ACD was defined as the distance between the anterior corneal surface and the anterior lens surface, whereas the axial length was measured from the anterior corneal surface to the retinal pigment epithelium. IOP was measured using a non-contact tonometer (Canon, TX-20P, Tokyo, Japan). Fasting blood samples were collected by venipuncture, and the serums were analysed for routine biochemical parameters in our laboratory. Anthropometric measurements were recorded during screening. All data were evaluated independently by two ophthalmologists (TI and KY) for post-acquisition quality control and any discrepancies were resolved by consensus.

Statistical analyses

All analyses were performed with R version 4.2.2. (https://www.r-project.org/). Parametric variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation, nonparametric variables as median (interquartile range [IQR]: 25th–75th percentile), and categorical variables as a number (%).

Visualization of the association between age and ACD using restricted cubic splines (RCS) was conducted with all participant data, which suggested a single inflection point with approximately linear relationships on either side of the age range. Accordingly, a piecewise regression model with a single knot was applied to characterise the age–ACD relationship. The knot location was estimated in a data-driven manner by minimising the residual sum of squares, based on the pattern observed in the RCS analysis. Sensitivity analyses in which the knot location was varied within ± 5 years of the estimated value yielded similar slopes and model fit, indicating that the findings were robust to the precise knot position. In these analyses, participants were treated as random effects.

For each participant, a univariable regression analysis was performed with ACD as the dependent variable and years since baseline as the independent variable, with the regression coefficient representing the annual ACD change rate (mm/year). The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test indicated that the ACD change rates were not normally distributed (P < 0.001); thus, nonparametric statistics were applied. Accordingly, annual ACD change rates were categorised into quartiles to enable robust, distribution-free stratification of participants according to the relative rate of ACD narrowing, and an ordinal logistic regression based on a proportional-odds model was applied with the quartile category as the outcome. Extreme slope values were not excluded from the analysis; robustness of the findings was further confirmed in complementary mixed-effects models treating ACD as a continuous outcome. The proportional-odds assumption underlying the ordinal logistic regression was formally assessed using the Brant test. A prespecified core model (Model 1) including age, sex, body height, baseline ACD, axial length, and IOP was fitted without variable selection. For exploratory analyses, additional baseline covariates were considered, including age, sex, height, blood pressure, baseline ACD, axial length, and intraocular pressure, white blood cell count, haemoglobin, platelet count, triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), fasting blood sugar, haemoglobin A1c, creatinine, estimated glomerular filtration rate, aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, and γ-glutamyl transpeptidase. Because HDL-C and LDL-C are strongly correlated, they were evaluated in separate models (Model 2 with HDL-C and Model 3 with LDL-C) and not entered simultaneously. For the exploratory models, final covariates were selected by Akaike information criterion–guided stepwise procedures. Results are reported as odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), expressed per unit (1 year for age, 1 cm for height, 1 mg/dL for HDL-C/LDL-C). The variance inflation factor was computed to assess multicollinearity, ensuring that no variable exceeded a variance inflation factor of 5. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

As a sensitivity analysis, we fitted a one-stage linear mixed-effects model with random intercepts and random slopes to assess whether the associations identified in the primary two-step analysis were robust to an alternative modelling strategy. In this model, ACD was treated as a continuous outcome across all visits, with time since baseline as the longitudinal variable. Fixed effects included baseline age, sex, body height, axial length, IOP, and their interactions with time, while random intercepts and slopes were specified at the participant level to account for between-individual heterogeneity in baseline ACD and longitudinal change.

Results

Demographic information

A total of 9,374 individuals with 27,101 healthy check-up visits participated. After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, including exclusion of individuals with a history of intraocular surgery (n = 944) either before baseline or during follow-up, data from 2,247 individuals with 10,869 visits were included in the statistical analyses. The key demographic characteristics from the participant baseline visits included in the statistical analyses are presented in Table 1. The median number of healthy check-up visits attended by participants was 5 (IQR: 4, 6) (range 3 to 7), and they were followed up for a median of 4.9 (IQR: 3.6, 5.0) (range 1.5 to 5.7) years.

ACD changes and correlated factors

The median annual rate of ACD changes (IQR) was − 1.11 × 10⁻² (− 2.04 × 10⁻², − 0.24 × 10⁻²) mm/year (range: −0.17 to + 0.14 mm/year). A visualization of the RCS model depicting the age-dependent relationship of ACD is shown in Fig. 1. Piecewise regression models with a single knot indicated that the rate of ACD change was − 1.52 × 10⁻² mm/year (95% CI: −1.60 × 10⁻², − 1.43 × 10⁻², P < 0.001) in individuals aged 51.3 years or younger and − 0.91 × 10⁻² mm/year (95% CI: −0.98 × 10⁻², − 0.85 × 10⁻², P < 0.001) in those older than 51.3 years.

Ordinal logistic regression showed that younger age and shorter body height were associated with faster ACD narrowing (Table 2). Per 1-year increase in age, the odds of belonging to a slower-narrowing quartile increased by approximately 4% across all models. Per 1-cm increase in body height, the corresponding increase was approximately 1% in the prespecified core model and the LDL-C–included model, and approximately 2% in the HDL-C–included model. Serum lipid parameters (HDL-C or LDL-C cholesterol) showed a weak negative association with faster ACD narrowing but did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.056 and P = 0.064, respectively). The proportional-odds assumption was satisfied in the prespecified core model (Brant test, omnibus P = 0.13) as well as in the lipid-specific ordinal logistic regression models (LDL-C model, omnibus P = 0.37; HDL-C model, omnibus P = 0.23).

Sensitivity analysis

In sensitivity analyses using the one-stage mixed-effects model with random intercepts and random slopes, the direction and significance of the key associations were consistent with those observed in the primary two-step analysis (see Online Resource 1). The residual standard deviation of the model was 0.035 mm, indicating limited unexplained within-individual variability.

Discussion

We evaluated the annual rate of ACD narrowing over a median 4.9‑year follow‑up in 2,247 participants from mainland Japan. Piecewise models showed that ACD narrows more steeply until approximately age 51 years and more slowly thereafter, and ordinal logistic regression indicated faster narrowing at younger ages and in shorter individuals. This study has three main strengths demonstrating rigorous methodology: (i) each participant contributed at least three measurements, enabling slope‑based individual rates; (ii) all examinations were performed at a single centre using the same partial coherence interferometry device, ensuring highly consistent measurement conditions across visits; and (iii) the cross‑sectional age–ACD pattern (via RCS and piecewise) agreed with the longitudinal individual slopes, lending robustness to the interpretation that the observed age-dependent pattern reflects genuine natural-history of ACD narrowing.

Previous studies have shown a negative correlation between ACD and age [8–11, 16, 17, 23]. However, those studies were primarily cross-sectional [8, 10, 11, 16, 17, 23]. This is the first study to analyse longitudinal data collected using a partial coherence interferometry-based device in more than 2,000 participants. In this longitudinal cohort study, the median ACD change rate (IQR: 25th − 75th percentile) was − 1.11 × 10⁻² (− 2.04 × 10⁻², − 0.24 × 10⁻²) mm/year. The ACD change rate in this study was relatively similar to that reported in a Korean cross-sectional study [24] (− 1.19 × 10⁻² mm/year), although it differed from that in a Chinese cross-sectional study [16] (− 0.9 × 10⁻² mm/year). The similarity between our cohort study and the Korean cross-sectional study may be attributed to the comparable racial backgrounds of individuals from Japan and Republic of Korea. Notably, the change rate tended to be faster than that reported in China [16], which may have been due to differences in the age groups studied as the Chinese study included only individuals aged 50 years and older. Supporting this, our piecewise regression model of the age-dependent relationships identified a single knot at age 51.3 years, estimating the ACD change rate at −0.91 × 10⁻² mm/year in individuals older than 51.3 years, which is a value comparable to that reported in the Chinese study [16]. Several factors may explain the relatively steeper narrowing of ACD at approximately age 51 years. Anatomically, ACD is influenced by lens thickness and posterior corneal arc length [25]. One possible explanation is that younger individuals retain a greater capacity for lens vault thickening. For instance, a study in Singapore suggested that lens vault does not increase with age in women with narrow angles and hypothesised that lens vault reaches an upper threshold at approximately age 50 years [26]. It should be noted, however, that although ACD can be measured with high repeatability using the OA-2000, the reported test–retest variability for ACD measurements ranges from approximately 0.06 to 0.12 mm in previous studies [27, 28]. Therefore, the magnitude of the annual ACD change observed in this study is modest in absolute terms. Accordingly, the present findings should be interpreted primarily at the population level, and caution is warranted when inferring clinically meaningful change at the individual level over relatively short intervals.

This longitudinal analysis quantified how the anatomical substrate for narrow anterior chamber develops over time. Our finding suggests that the structural predisposition is largely established by the early fifties. This is noteworthy because previous epidemiological studies have shown that the highest risk for PACD occurs in the sixth or seventh decade of life [29, 30]. The temporal gap implies that, in addition to structural predisposition, non‑anatomical triggers are required for disease onset. Indeed, a previous review has emphasised that acute angle closure cannot be fully predicted by anatomical factors alone and that non‑anatomical precipitants—such as autonomic imbalance, posterior choroidal expansion, or physiological fluctuations—may act as triggers in susceptible eyes [31]. Furthermore, as suggested by previous reports [32, 33], identifying a uniform set of risk factors in the narrow‑angle population may be challenging because of the low incidence of immediately vision-threatening outcomes.

Interestingly, this longitudinal study also suggests a trend toward faster ACD narrowing in individuals with shorter stature. Previous cross-sectional reports have consistently shown a positive correlation between ACD and body height [11–15, 23]; however, none of those studies examined the correlation between body height and the longitudinal ACD change rate. Importantly, this finding refers to the rate of narrowing over time rather than merely a shallower baseline ACD—an aspect that cross-sectional designs cannot capture. One proposed explanation for this trend is that shorter stature has been associated with an increased likelihood of cataract maturation, which may contribute to ACD shallowing [34]. Another potential mechanism involves resistance to aqueous humour flow from the posterior to anterior chambers at the pupil [35], which may lead to a chronic reduction in anterior chamber volume, accelerating ACD narrowing. Nonetheless, the results of this study suggest that ACD narrowing progresses more steeply in individuals of shorter stature despite the well-established association between short body height and a shallow anterior chamber. Careful monitoring or preventive strategies through early intervention may help reduce the prevalence of progressive ACD narrowing and its complications because individuals with shorter stature face a higher risk of developing PACD, even when the baseline ACD is similar to other adults.

This study has a few limitations. First, medical history was obtained through a questionnaire and prior therapeutic interventions (including medications) could not be fully identified; given that the associations of younger age and shorter stature with faster ACD narrowing were modest (approximately 4% per year; approximately 1–2% per cm), residual confounding from unmeasured or uncontrolled factors cannot be excluded. Second, older participants may have exited the study due to cataract surgery. Cataract is a major determinant of ACD, becomes more pronounced with age, and tends to be removed relatively early through surgery in mainland Japan, as suggested by previous reports [36, 37]. Third, the age–ACD curve presented in this study reflects a cross-sectional association between age and ACD rather than a direct within-person trajectory. Although this cross-sectional pattern was consistent with the longitudinal individual slopes observed in this cohort, it may also be influenced by cohort effects, as younger generations tend to have deeper baseline ACD and a higher prevalence of myopia [38]. Accordingly, the age–ACD curve should be interpreted as contextual information, while the longitudinal within-person change rates represent the primary evidence for age-related ACD narrowing in this study. However, given the presence of measurement variability, caution is warranted when interpreting statistically significant change at the individual level over relatively short intervals. Finally, the study population was racially homogeneous, consisting solely of Japanese participants residing in a single city and the inclusion of corporate health check-up participants resulted in a predominance of male participants; therefore, the results may not be generalisable. Further studies in diverse racial and regional populations are needed to validate the trends observed in this study.

In conclusion, the results of this study suggest that ACD continues to narrow until approximately age 51 years in individuals with good corrected VA. Risk factors for ACD narrowing, such as shorter stature, may not be modifiable. Therefore, careful monitoring and early intervention are essential strategies to reduce the prevalence of pathologic ACD narrowing and its complications.
