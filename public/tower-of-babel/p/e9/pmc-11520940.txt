Effects of Blood Collection Tubes on the Quantification of Catecholamines and Their O‐Methylated Metabolites by Liquid Chromatography Coupled With Tandem Mass Spectrometry
Authors: Rong Su, Yanjie Wu, Qiuyi Huang, Shengchun Huang, Yao Li, Caifeng Chen, Wenchun Xie, Yan Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundCatecholamines (epinephrine; norepinephrine; and dopamine) and their O‐methylated metabolites (metanephrine; normetanephrine; and 3‐methoxytyramine) are biomarkers for pheochromocytoma and paraganglioma. Liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (LC‐MS/MS) was recommended by Endocrine Society for detecting these compounds. The influence of blood collection tubes on the analysis of the six analytes by LC‐MS/MS was not thoroughly investigated, which we want to clarify in our study.MethodsBlood samples of healthy individuals were collected into serum, lithium heparin, and K2EDTA plasma tubes separately. Samples were subjected to solid phase extraction and then analyzed by LC‐MS/MS. The retention behavior and assay performance of the six analytes were assessed for samples from different collection containers. The impacts of potassium and sodium as the counter ions of EDTA on the retention time and matrix effect were also studied.ResultsCompared with O‐methylated metabolites, the results for catecholamines were more affected by the collection tubes, especially for norepinephrine, which displayed severely suppressed signal and very low extraction efficiency in K2EDTA plasma. Changing the counter ion of EDTA from potassium to sodium dramatically changed the retention behavior and matrix effect of norepinephrine.ConclusionsIt is necessary to evaluate blood collection tubes for catecholamines and their O‐methylated metabolites analyzed by LC‐MS/MS. In addition, attention should also be paid when the anticoagulant counter ion was changed.Keywords: blood collection tubes, catecholamines, LC‐MS/MS, matrix effect, metanephrines, pheochromocytomas and paraganglioma

Introduction

Pheochromocytomas and Paraganglioma (PPGL) are neuroendocrine tumors arising from chromaffin cells located at the adrenal medulla or extra‐adrenal paraganglia. PPGL produce excess amounts of catecholamines, including epinephrine (E), norepinephrine (NE), and dopamine (DA). If left untreated, this hypersecretion of catecholamines ultimately causes severe hypertension, resulting in fatal cardiovascular events. Therefore, it is important to detect this tumor so that further action can be taken [1, 2, 3]. Liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (LC‐MS/MS) has been widely used for analyzing biomarkers of various diseases because of its high analytical specificity and sensitivity. Many laboratories have developed LC‐MS/MS assays to analyze plasma catecholamines and metanephrines, the o‐methylated metabolites of catecholamines, containing metanephrine (MN), normetanephrine (NMN) and 3‐methoxytyramine (3‐MT) for biochemical assessment of PPGL [4, 5]. We also established a LC‐MS/MS method using off‐line solid phase extraction (SPE) and hydrophilic interaction chromatography (HILIC) for sample preparation and analytes separation respectively to measure concentrations of plasma catecholamines and their o‐methylated metabolites [6].

The diagnostic process used for measuring various disease markers can be divided into three phases: preanalytical, analytical, and postanalytical phases. While the testing is performed during the analytical phase, a considerable number of errors occur during the preanalytical phase. Preanalytical variables such as sample collection, processing, and transportation greatly affect the quality of test results. More specifically, the effects of preanalytical factors on the analysis of catecholamines and the metabolites in blood has been studied in detail. One study compared how the concentrations of six compounds in blood differed when collected either by venipuncture or by indwelling intravenous cannula [7]. In addition, several reports showed that plasma catecholamines and metanephrines varied in their concentrations, depending on the seasons [8, 9, 10, 11]. Other studies showed that the stability of catecholamines and the metabolites in whole blood and plasma is a function of storage temperature and time, as well as the conditions for centrifuging the blood [12, 13, 14, 15]. Among all the preanalytical factors, blood collection tubes are considered to constitute a critical parameter that affects the matrix properties of the specimens, which may in turn affect the performance of assays and the reliability of their results [16, 17, 18].

A thorough literature search by us found that there were few studies assessing the effects of blood collection containers on the LC‐MS/MS analysis of catecholamines and their o‐methylated metabolites. One study investigated the effect of blood collection tubes on the levels of plasma MN and NMN by LC‐MS/MS including EDTA, lithium heparin, heparin‐containing plasma separator (PST), serum, and clot activator‐containing serum separator (SST) tubes [4]. However, their study lacked an assessment of how the collection tube materials affect the measured levels of 3‐MT, DA, E, and NE in blood samples.

The aim of our study was to systematically evaluate how different blood collection tubes affect the measurement of catecholamines and their O‐methylated metabolites by LC–MS/MS and help to identify which collection tubes is most suitable for this type of assay.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals and Reagents

All reagents for LC‐MS/MS analysis were of chromatography grade. Formic acid, methanol, isopropanol, and acetonitrile were purchased from Fisher Chemical (Pittsburgh, PA, USA) and water was purchased from Wahaha (Hangzhou, Zhejiang, China). All standards, namely E, NE, DA, MN, NMN, and 3‐MT were purchased from Sigma‐Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). The deuterated internal standards (IS), α, α, β, β‐d4‐3‐methoxytyramine: HCl, α, β, β‐d3‐metanephrine, α, β, β‐d3‐normetanephrine, α, α, β, β‐d4‐dopamine, ring‐d3, α, β, β‐d3—epinephrine and ring‐d3, α, β, β‐d3‐norepinephrine were purchased from Cambridge Isotope Laboratories (Tewksbury, MA, USA). Both K2EDTA and lithium heparin vacuum blood collection tubes were purchased from Becton, Dickinson and Company (Franklin lakes, NJ, USA), and serum tubes were purchased from YULI (Taizhou, Jiangsu, China).

Preparation of Calibrators

Stock solutions of E, NE, DA, MN, NMN, and 3‐MT were prepared separately at 1 μg/mL in methanol and then diluted with water to produce mixed working solutions of catecholamines and metanephrines at 20 and 10 ng/mL, respectively. Eight calibrators ranging from 10 to 2500 pg/mL for E, NE and DA, and ranging from 10 to 1250 pg/mL for MN, NMN, and 3‐MT were prepared in water from working solution.

Preparation of Blood Samples From Different Collection Containers

10 mL venous blood was collected into serum, lithium heparin, and K2EDTA plasma tubes respectively from seven healthy volunteers (three males and four females) aged 25–35 years, all of whom fasted for at least 8 h and did not take any medicines or supplements for two weeks. All the volunteers gave informed consent for this study, which has been approved by The Research Ethics Committee at Institute of Biophysics, Chinese Academy of Sciences (ethics approval number: XDB38010200). Except for serum tubes, all tubes were inverted 10 times to ensure that blood was mixed well with the inner coating anticoagulants. The blood samples were centrifuged at 1500 g for 10 min at 4°C within 30 min of collection and 4 mL of serum or plasma was obtained and removed from each of the collection containers. To minimize inter‐individual variation, all serum and plasma collected in the same type of collection tubes were pooled together, yielding approximately 28 mL of serum, heparin, and K2EDTA plasma respectively. The concentrations of catecholamines and their o‐methylated metabolites in the pooled samples were determined by LC‐MS/MS as follows: in serum, 3‐MT, MN, NMN, DA, E, NE were 3.5, 32.4, 64.3, 13.6, 82.8, 324.8 pg/mL, respectively; in heparin plasma, 3‐MT, MN, NMN, DA, E, NE were 2.0, 31.0, 60.9, 17.8, 69.1, 307.6 pg/mL, respectively; in K2EDTA plasma, 3‐MT, MN, NMN, DA, E, NE were 2.9, 33.2, 61.0, 12.6, 70.8, 282.6 pg/mL, respectively. We prepared low‐ and high‐quality control (QC) samples by adding 200 μL of mixed standard solutions in water containing either low (3‐MT/MN/NMN, 2000 pg/mL; DA/E/NE, 10,000 pg/mL) or high (3‐MT/MN/NMN, 10,000 pg/mL; DA/E/NE, 20,000 pg/mL) concentrations of catecholamines and their O‐methylated metabolites to 3.8 mL of serum, heparin plasma, and K2EDTA plasma, respectively. As a result, the spiked concentrations were set at 100 pg/mL for 3‐MT/MN/NMN and 500 pg/mL for DA/E/NE in the low‐QC samples, and 500 pg/mL for 3‐MT/MN/NMN and 1000 pg/mL for DA/E/NE in the high‐QC samples. The low QC, high QC, and remaining mixed native samples were dispensed into 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes in aliquots of 200 μL each. All samples remained stable through at least three freeze–thaw cycles and for a duration of a year at −80°C and were stored at −80°C until further use.

Analytical Design

Sample Preparation

200 μL of plasma, serum, and calibrators were pre‐treated with 350 μL of IS working solution (1 ng/mL). The mixed samples were subjected to SPE on a Waters Oasis weak cation exchange 96‐well μElution plate (MA, USA). The plate was placed in a vacuum manifold and preconditioned with 200 μL of methanol and water, and then the samples were loaded onto the plates and wells were washed with 200 μL of water followed by 200 μL of 50:50 acetonitrile: isopropanol. The target compounds were eluted from the plate with 2 × 50 μL aliquots of 98:2 acetonitrile: water containing 1.5% formic acid. 10 μL of the eluate was injected onto the LC‐MS/MS system.

LC‐MS/MS Conditions

Sample analysis was performed on a Thermo U3000/TSQ Endura LC‐MS/MS system (Milford, MA, USA). The mobile phase was delivered at a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min onto a hydrophilic column; Waters XBridge BEH Amide XP column (2.5 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm, Milford, MA, USA) kept in a 35°C column chamber. Mobile phase A consisted of 0.5% formic acid in water and mobile phase B consisted of 85% acetonitrile in water with 0.5% formic acid. The target compounds were separated on the column by binary gradient. Mobile phase A changed from 0% to 50% in 2.5 min, and then the mobile phase composition returned to the initial condition followed by equilibration for 2.5 min. The mass spectrometer was equipped with an electrospray ionization probe and the analytes were scanned in positive ion mode using selected reaction monitoring (SRM). Data were acquired using the following instrument settings: spray voltage = 3600 V, ion transfer tube temperature = 350°C, vaporizer temperature = 330°C. Effects of Collection Containers on Assay Imprecision, Accuracy, Matrix Effect and Extraction Efficiency

Imprecision and Accuracy

Duplicates of low‐ and high‐QC samples were subject to SPE process and assayed by HILIC‐MS/MS system across three separated batches. The concentrations of the analytes in each injection were calculated according to the calibration curves, which were constructed by plotting the peak area ratio of the analytes to the corresponding IS against the calibrators' concentrations. The imprecision of the test for the six analytes in the low‐ and high‐QC samples from different collection tubes was calculated as the coefficient of variation (CV% = (standard deviation/mean) × 100%), which was based on variations in concentrations of catecholamines and their o‐methylated metabolites across the three batches for each sample type. The accuracy of the assay was determined by the following equation: (final concentration‐initial concentration)/added concentration.

Matrix Effect

To investigate effects of collection tubes on the matrix effect, a post‐extraction spiking assay was performed on the native samples collected from different tubes. Plasma and serum samples were first subject to SPE and then mixed standard solutions containing low and high concentrations of 3‐MT, MN, NMN, DA, E, and NE, and their corresponding IS were added to the eluates from SPE, respectively. Matrix‐free solvents were also spiked with the same solutions used above. The final spiked concentrations of 3‐MT, MN, and NMN were 100 pg/mL and DA, E and NE were 500 pg/mL in the low‐concentration samples. The final spiked concentrations of 3‐MT, MN, and NMN were 500 pg/mL and DA, E and NE were 1000 pg/mL in the high‐concentration samples. The concentrations of IS were 5 ng/mL in both low‐ and high‐concentration samples. The above low‐ and high‐concentration samples were run in duplicates for each of three separate batches. The absolute matrix effects (ME%) were calculated as the ratio of the peak areas of the analytes in the presence of matrix to those in neat solvents, −1, then multiplied by 100%. The IS‐normalized matrix effects were determined by the ratio of peak area ratio (analyte/IS) in the presence of matrix ions to the peak area ratio (analyte/IS) in neat solvent, −1, then multiplied by 100%.

Extraction Efficiency

The influence of blood collection tubes on extraction efficiency of the SPE procedure was also assessed. Briefly, one set of native samples from different collection tubes were spiked with mixed standard solutions of 3‐MT, MN, NMN, DA, E and NE at low (3‐MT, MN, NMN: 100 pg/mL; DA, E, NE: 500 pg/mL) and high concentrations (3‐MT, MN, NMN: 500 pg/mL; DA, E, NE: 1000 pg/mL) and then applied to SPE procedure. Another set of the native samples were first subject to SPE, before the same amount of analytes was added to the eluates. The extraction efficiency of each analyte was calculated by comparing the peak area of the analytes between samples spiked before‐extraction and post‐extraction.

Effects of Counter ion of EDTA on the Matrix Effect and Retention Behavior of Catecholamines and metanephrines' Internal Standard

To study how counter ion of EDTA affects the retention behavior and response of the six analytes, the final concentrations of both anticoagulants 5.5 mM were adjusted by adding either 275 mM K2EDTA or Na2EDTA in physiological saline to the serum. These samples as well as the plain serum were then subject to the SPE process in duplicates. The resulting eluates were spiked with IS of the six analytes at 5 ng/mL, and then injected onto the column twice. The peak area of the IS in the matrix was compared with that of the IS prepared at the same concentration in the eluent.

Data Analysis

Comparison of the assay performance of the 6 analytes between different collection tubes were obtained by ANOVA test. p‐Values lower than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. The SPSS for Windows package, release 21.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), was used for statistical analysis.

Results

Effects of Collection Tubes on the Retention Behavior of Catecholamines and Metanephrines

The SRM chromatograms of catecholamines and metanephrines in high‐concentration QC samples are shown in Figure 1. We found that all compounds except NE were characterized by nearly identical retention times as well as peak shapes in serum, lithium heparin, and K2EDTA plasma. We also found that the retention time of NE in K2EDTA plasma was much longer, and the peak shape became much narrower. The peak area of 3‐MT, MN, NMN in K2EDTA plasma was slightly smaller compared with those in serum and lithium heparin plasma. In comparison, the peak area of DA, E, and NE in heparin plasma slightly decreased compared to that in serum, while the peak area of DA, E, and NE in K2EDTA plasma decreased almost three‐, four‐ and 23‐fold respectively than that in serum.

Extraction Efficiencies

As shown in Figure 2, under all sample conditions, the average extraction efficiencies for 3‐MT, MN, and NMN varied from 54.6% to 72.9%, 59.0% to 79.1%, and 44.8% to 65.1%, respectively. Unlike O‐methylated metabolites, catecholamines displayed a clear downward trend in extraction efficiencies when compared between serum, heparin, and K2EDTA plasma. More specifically, low extraction efficiencies of the catecholamines were observed in low and high K2EDTA plasma control, ranging from 20.2% to 27.6% and 13.1% to 19.0%, respectively.

Matrix Effect

3‐MT and MN displayed moderate to slightly ion suppression (ME%: −30.5% to −5.9%) in all the sample conditions, while NMN showed a little ion enhancement (ME%: 13.1% and 4.8%) in heparin samples (Table 3). While the absolute matrix effects of DA exhibited a little difference between different sample conditions (ME%: 1.9%–19.2%), those of E and NE changed dramatically from ion enhancement in serum and heparin samples to severe ion suppression in K2EDTA plasma. Importantly, the ANOVA test for catecholamines and metanephrines indicated that unlike the absolute matrix effects, there were almost no statistical differences between different collection tubes for the IS‐normalized matrix effects, which all fell in the range between −15% and 15%.

Discussion

In our study, we investigated the impact of blood collection tubes on the retention behavior and test performance of the catecholamines and metanephrines and found that K2EDTA tubes showed great influence on the catecholamines especially for NE.

The effect of collection tubes on the quantification of endogenous metabolites has been explored on different methodology. One earlier study found that an organosilicone surfactant used to coat the inner surfaces of BD Vacutainer SST blood collection tubes may cause erroneous results for analyzing total triiodothyronine by some immunoassays [19]. Another report compared four types of blood collection tubes for analysis of dozens of analytes in routine chemistry tests and their study showed that the test results of several analytes including potassium, total protein, aspartate aminotransferase, were affected by different tube types [20]. Although several studies showed that sample type like serum, plasma EDTA, heparin, and citrate affected the quantitative analysis of various metabolites in human blood by LC‐MS/MS [21, 22, 23], studies on the catecholamines and their O‐methylated metabolites were few. One study found that only EDTA and heparin plasma yield acceptable results for LC‐MS/MS analysis of MN and NMN [4]. Our study showed that for metanephrines, the retention behavior was similar among three collection tubes and the quantitative analysis were satisfactory for serum, heparin, and EDTA plasma sample, while the assay performance of catecholamines were more likely influenced by the collection tubes, especially for NE.

The retention time of DA and E in K2EDTA plasma was nearly identical to that of serum and heparin, whereas the peak of NE in K2EDTA plasma shifted forward for 0.4 min, compared with that in the other two types of samples. This result suggested that NE in K2EDTA plasma appeared to be more hydrophilic and thus interacted more strongly with the stationary phase (Figure 1). Unlike the retention time, the peak areas of DA, E, and NE in different sample types decreased in the order of serum, heparin, and K2EDTA plasma. Two reasons might be responsible for this finding. First, the extraction efficiencies for catecholamines displayed a declined trend in the order mentioned above. Second, the matrix effect may differ in the three sample types. For example, change of peak areas for DA from the three collection tubes was mainly caused by the different extraction efficiency (Figure 2) because no matter in which kind of matrix, DA exhibited similar ion‐enhancement effect (Table 3). In contrast, the combination of ion‐suppression effect and reduced extraction efficiency for E and NE in K2EDTA plasma resulted in much smaller peak area of these two analytes in comparison with that of the other two sample type (Figure 2 and Table 3). Notably, the severely suppressed signal of NE in K2EDTA plasma also led to a highly imprecise result in low‐QC sample (CV% = 28.7%, Table 2), which indicated that the loss in sensitivity may cause problem in other aspects of the assay performance.

The SPE plated used in our study retained the analytes primarily via two interactions: hydrophobic interactions and ionic interactions. The catecholamines were less hydrophobic than their corresponding metabolites because one of the two phenolic hydroxyl groups in catecholamines was metabolized to a methoxy group. (LogP (Human Metabolome Database): DA, 0.03; E, −0.43; NE, −0.68; 3‐MT: 0.53; MN, −0.00068; NMN, −0.39) and more prone to ionic interaction. We hypothesized that the differences in the pH value and ionic strength (serum < lithium heparin < K2EDTA) of the matrix in the three collection containers resulted in an increased loss of the catecholamines during the SPE process; namely when the sample type changed from serum to lithium heparin to K2EDTA plasma (Figure 2).

The matrix effect is caused by the influence of co‐eluting component on the desolvation and ionization of the target analytes. Here, we found that the matrix effect of E and NE was affected primarily by the collection tubes. The absolute matrix effect changed from ion enhancement in serum and heparin plasma to moderate and severe ion suppression in K2EDTA plasma for E and NE respectively (Table 3). The noticeable ion enhancement for E and NE in serum may be due to two reasons. First, during the clotting process, some small molecules and factor were released into serum and may coelute with E and NE from the column, enhancing their ionization. Second, the protein composition in serum were changed because of the clotting process, which may also affect the ionization of E and NE. The severe ion suppression in K2EDTA plasma for NE may be the result of the anticoagulant and/or the difference in the environment in which the analyte was eluted from the column as the retention time of NE in K2EDTA plasma changed considerably compared with that in the other two sample types (Figure 1). When we assessed the IS‐normalized matrix effect, we found that the above‐mentioned difference between varied sample type disappeared, which clearly indicated that usage of isotope internal standard compensated for the matrix effect on the ionization of the analytes.

We also investigated the effect of EDTA's counter ions on the response of the six biomarkers, because K2EDTA plasma displayed the most influence on the assay performance. The European Bioanalysis Forum suggested that plasma samples with same anticoagulants, but different counter ions were considered to be equal matrix [24, 25]. However, we found that when the counter ion of EDTA was changed from K to Na, the impact of K2EDTA on the retention time and absolute matrix effect was almost eliminated (Table 4). One possible explanation is that using K as the counter ion changed the plasma concentration of K (endogenous blood concentration of K: 4 mM) considerably. In contrast, plasma concentration of Na remained almost unchanged when Na was used as the counter ion (endogenous blood concentration of Na: 150 mM). Therefore, the two counter ions may introduce different environment for the target analytes.

One thing needs to mention is that although we do not recommend the K2EDTA tube due to the heavily suppressed signal of NE, it may not be the case if the HPLC separation mode and/or SPE process are changed, because the results for the matrix effect and extraction efficiency are closely related to the chosen HPLC column and SPE sorbents.

Here, we investigated the impact of collection tubes on the analysis of catecholamines and their o‐methylated metabolites using LC‐MS/MS, an area that, according to our knowledge, has received limited attention in published research. Nevertheless, there were several limitations in our study. First, due to the availability of native positive sample in different collection tubes, we used spiked samples in our imprecision study, which may impact our findings. Second, to assess the matrix effect caused solely by different collection containers, we used pooled plasma from the seven healthy volunteers to minimize inter‐individual variation, which did not display the matrix effect of different sample type for each individual volunteer. Third, we used internal standards that sometimes may behave differently from the native analytes to study the effects of counter ions of EDTA. Thus, further investigation including native positive samples will be required. Additionally, studies on individual matrix effect in samples collected from different collection tubes for several individuals, as well as the effects of counter ions of various anticoagulants using both native analytes and IS, need to be performed.

In conclusion, according to the findings outlined in our research, we strongly recommend a proper evaluation of blood collection tubes. Such step should ensure that LC‐MS/MS analysis of catecholamines and metanephrines results in reproducible and meaningful data. In case where the counter ion of the anticoagulant is changed, it might be necessary to do partial validation.
