The relationship between medical workers’ presence of meaning in life and turnover intention: the mediating role of searching for meaning in life and professional happiness
Authors: Zihan Zheng, Huilin Zhang, Yuting Huang, Siyu Su, Zuming Qin, Jiao Cheng, Zhiling Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe high turnover rate of medical workers has become a global problem. This study aims to explore the relationship between the presence of meaning in life and turnover intention among medical workers, and to examine the mediating role of the search for meaning in life and professional happiness. The study conducted a cross-sectional survey involving 1125 Chinese medical workers. The data were collected through an online survey, including medical workers’ demographic information, sense of meaning in life, professional happiness, and turnover intention. Data analysis and structural equation modeling were performed using SPSS 27.0 and smartPLS 4.0 software. The results of the study showed a good model fit. The presence of meaning in life had a direct negative predictive association with turnover intention (β = − 0.128, p < 0. 01). Importantly, the presence of meaning in life significantly positively predicted the search for meaning in life (β = 0.692, p < 0.001), a finding counter to Western Presence-to-Search Model. The presence of meaning in life also showed an indirect negative predictive association with turnover intention through the mediation of professional happiness (β = − 0.278, p < 0. 001); and it showed an indirect positive predictive association with turnover intention through the mediation of the search for meaning in life (β = 0.071, p < 0.01). This study used the structural equation model to reveal the complex relationship among the presence of meaning in life, the search for meaning in life, professional happiness and turnover intention of medical workers. Notably, contrary to Western‑based models, we found a positive association between the presence of meaning in life and the search for meaning in life, suggesting that in collectivist cultural contexts, a stronger sense of meaning may be positively linked to further search for meaning in life. These findings suggest that presence of meaning in life and professional happiness are negatively associated with turnover intention among medical workers, which may inform human resource management and the development of more humane work systems and incentive mechanisms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54195-8.Keywords: Medical workers, Turnover intention, The presence of meaning in life, The search for meaning in life, Professional happinessSubject terms: Health care, Health occupations, Psychology, Psychology, Risk factors

Introduction

Medical worker turnover represents a significant challenge within the healthcare sector. A global shortage of healthcare personnel, particularly in developing countries1, encompasses significant deficits in doctors, nurses, midwives, and pharmacists2. With the burgeoning global population, the aging demographic trends, and a rise in chronic noncommunicable diseases, the demand for healthcare services is escalating3. Forecasts from a global health workforce labor market report indicated that by 2030, demand for medical workers will surge to approximately 80 million, yet the supply is projected to reach only about 65 million. The most pronounced net shortages are anticipated in East Asia and the Pacific, notably in China4. This underscores the imperative for researchers to delve into the turnover issue of medical workers. Turnover intention (TI) is a principal predictor of actual turnover behavior5–7. It is defined as an employee’s readiness to depart from the current organization or institution, representing the probability of leaving a job within a specific timeframe8. Despite the widespread discussion of turnover intention among medical workers in numerous studies, researchers often focus on the impact of psychological issues and negative emotions on turnover intention, seeking solutions to these psychological problems while neglecting the positive aspects of mental health. Most existing turnover research relies on dominant frameworks such as the Job Demands Resources model and Conservation of Resources theory9,10. These frameworks tend to emphasize job stressors, burnout, resource depletion, and external workplace resources, focusing heavily on negative psychological outcomes while underemphasizing the role of positive, higher-order inner psychological resources. This represents a theoretical gap: mainstream turnover models lack a positive psychology-oriented account of intrinsic psychological resources that can explain turnover intention beyond stress and burnout. Yet even in high-pressure work environments, medical workers can experience positive psychological feelings that are also significantly related to turnover intention. Therefore, to fully understand the factors associated with retention intention of medical workers, it is necessary to emphasize the role of positive psychological factors. Positive psychology is a scientific study that focuses on positive qualities and strengths, aiming to promote the flourishing of individuals and society11. In recent years, substantial evidence has shown that positive psychological resources are related to better physical and mental health among medical workers and predict positive work attitudes and behaviors12. The sense of meaning in life and professional happiness are important components of positive psychology.

As an important positive psychological quality, the sense of meaning in life of medical workers is receiving increasing attention. Frankl13 posited that work serves as a primary source of individual sense of meaning in life. Work holds significant importance in individuals’ lives, particularly for medical workers14,15. Besides earning an income, medical workers encounter diverse challenges that enhance their skills and competencies. Simultaneously, they derive fulfillment and professional identity from establishing social relationships through their work16. The sense of meaning in life is one of the factors influencing the turnover of medical workers. If medical workers do not feel the meaning and value of life at work, they may not develop a sense of organizational commitment and thus leave their current jobs17. The sense of meaning in life comprises two components—the Presence of Meaning in Life (PM) and the Search for Meaning in Life (SM). The former one reflects an individual’s stable, internal realization and understanding of life’s meaning, coupled with the ability to perceive a sense of purpose or mission in daily activities. The latter one involves dynamic, positive efforts toward realizing life goals, representing an individual’s active pursuit of life’s value18. PM emphasizes an individual’s current perceptions and experiences, focusing on the valuable outcome of perceiving one’s life as meaningful. In contrast, SM underscores the individual’s proactive endeavor to render life more meaningful, highlighting a critical process19. Research indicates that PM and SM have distinct impacts on individuals’ psychological health and behavioral outcomes. PM typically correlates with positive mental health outcomes, while SM is often associated with negative ones20,21. However, as one of the factors affecting the turnover among medical workers, the sense of meaning in life currently tends to be explored as a whole, without the separate consideration of its two dimensions—the presence of meaning in life and the search for meaning in life22.

Professional happiness (PH) emerges as a pivotal factor in turnover. Professional happiness, a positive energy effect of employees in their work, is a feeling about the occupation generated by realizing their own value and emotion. It is the psychological satisfaction of employees directly obtained from work-related perspectives, such as work content, environment, and remuneration23. Professional happiness refers to the positive emotional experiences that individuals have in their work, which persist throughout their career. As the main bearers of healthcare, medical workers gain a sense of achievement and satisfaction by treating patients’ diseases and maintaining their health. However, their professional happiness was greatly depleted by the high-intensity, high-pressure, and high-risk nature of their profession. Previous studies have shown that presence of meaning in life can mitigate negative outcomes such as psychological distress and job burnout among medical workers, thereby enhancing their happiness20. Mobley et al.24 argued that unhappiness at work leads individuals to first think about leaving their job, then intend to leave their job. Meanwhile, they actively search for another job, and then decide to leave their job if an intriguing job opportunity arises.

There may be a complex relationship among medical workers’ sense of meaning in life, professional happiness, and turnover intention. This study therefore measured medical workers’ sense of meaning in life, professional happiness, and turnover intention in three hospitals in Hunan, China, to better understand the complex associations among these variables so that relevant measures can be taken to lower turnover.

Literature review

Turnover intention has long been a focus of numerous studies. March and Simon first introduced the concept of turnover intention in 1958, defining it as the strength of an employee’s inclination to leave their current job and seek other opportunities. They also proposed the well-known decision to participate model, which posits that the two key factors influencing turnover intention are job satisfaction and the opportunities and abilities to obtain new employment25.

The turnover intention of medical workers is not only related to individual career development but also directly affects the quality of medical services, as well as the stability and efficiency of the entire healthcare system. A systematic review showed that the turnover intention rate of medical workers ranges from 12% to 64%, with over two-thirds of nurses in Turkey expressing such intention26. A survey conducted in 12 countries found that 26.1% of medical workers have turnover intention27. In a survey of 15,890 doctors, 32.6% reported moderate or higher levels of turnover intention28. In China, 41.3% of nurses have turnover intention, with 19% showing strong or relatively strong intention to leave29,30. These findings indicate that, despite variations in reported levels of turnover intention among medical workers across different studies, the overall turnover intention among medical workers is relatively high.

The factors influencing the turnover intention of medical workers can be categorized into personal, job-related, and organizational factors26. Personal factors include age, gender, education level, and marital status31,32. Job-related factors encompass job stress, occupational burnout, and job satisfaction33,34. Organizational factors involve compensation and benefits, human resource allocation, and leadership style31,35,36. Current research on above-mentioned job-related factors tends to focus more on negative psychological factors, such as occupational burnout, job stress, and emotional exhaustion33,34,37,38. However, focusing solely on negative factors may not provide a comprehensive understanding of the causes of turnover intention. The role of positive psychological factors, such as meaning in life and professional happiness, in the turnover intention of medical workers has not been fully explored.

Conceptual framework and development of hypotheses

To sharpen the theoretical contribution and align with positive psychology perspectives, the present study frames presence of meaning in life, search for meaning in life, and professional happiness as a coherent configuration of higher-order inner psychological resources, termed the meaning resource profile. We thus conceptualize them as an integrated set of intrinsic resources that extend beyond dominant turnover frameworks focused on stress, burnout, and resource depletion. This unified profile allows us to interpret direct and indirect effects as a systemic inner resource mechanism rather than isolated associations.

The presence of meaning in life, the search for meaning in life and turnover intention

Meaning in life plays a crucial role in maintaining an individual’s physiological, psychological, and spiritual health. It provides individuals with life goals, values and standards for behavior, a sense of control over life events, and a sense of self-worth. In contrast, the absence of meaning in life is related to higher levels of depression and anxiety, and even a higher tendency towards suicide7. Employees want to engage in work that gives their sense of meaning in life39. Those who feel meaningful in their work have a lower tendency to leave40. Medical workers are engaged in a unique profession that is closely tied to health and life, carrying significant responsibilities. Therefore, it is particularly important to focus on their understanding of the meaning of life. As medical workers, if they believe that their work contributes to the realization of the meaning of their lives, they will practice patient-centeredness in their daily work, ensure the quality of their work and maintain their dedication to their work, and thus be less likely to leave their jobs41. Therefore, the first hypothesis of this study is:

Hypothesis 1

The presence of meaning in life is negatively related to turnover intention.

According to the research by Steger et al.18, the presence of meaning in life and the search for meaning in life are two distinct dimensions of meaning in life, which are psychometrically distinguishable. Steger’s “Presence-to-Search Model” posited that the absence of meaning in life is related to a greater search for it, indicating that individuals lacking meaningful experiences will seek meaning42. Subsequent research has validated this model43. Therefore, the second hypothesis of this study is:

Hypothesis 2

The presence of meaning in life is negatively related to the search for meaning in life.

The Meaning Maintenance Model (MMM) posits that individuals’ sense of meaning in life largely depends on their relationship with their surroundings. If this relationship is disrupted, their sense of meaning in life may be compromised. In such cases, individuals may seek to compensate by obtaining meaning in life through alternative means44. This suggests that when medical workers can derive a sense of meaning in life from their work environment, they are more likely to experience professional happiness. Conversely, when medical workers face threats to their sense of meaning in their work, they may seek to compensate by pursuing new sources of meaning, such as leaving their current positions to establish new social relationships or finding satisfaction and happiness outside of work, so as to regain and affirm their sense of meaning in life. The pursuit of meaning in life is one of the fundamental human motivations. People seek meaning to discover the purpose of life and the value of existence, and the absence of meaning is the root of all psychological suffering and discomfort13. Frankl13 noted that one can seek meaning in life by taking a job; if one is unable to find meaning in one’s current job, then he/she can change a job that enables a sense of fulfillment, to find a sense of meaning in his/her work. Accordingly, this study hypothesizes the search for meaning in life is positively associated with turnover intention. This reasoning is further supported by Schneider’s attraction–selection–attrition (ASA) framework45, which suggests that individuals are naturally attracted to organizations consistent with their personal values and sense of meaning, whereas those who perceive a poor fit between their needs and the current work environment are more likely to withdraw from their jobs. Building on this, we further propose that the search for meaning in life mediates the relationship between the presence of meaning in life and turnover intention:

Hypothesis 3

The search for meaning in life is positively related to turnover intention.

Hypothesis 4

The search for meaning in life mediates the relationship between the presence of meaning in life and turnover intention.

The presence of meaning in life, professional happiness and turnover intention

Personal happiness is positively correlated with the presence of meaning in life46. Individuals have a high level of professional happiness when the job is in line with their values and sense of meaning. In that case, they will be highly motivated and satisfied with the work environment47. For one thing, having a clear sense of meaning in life can stimulate the intrinsic motivation of medical workers. They are more likely to be highly engaged in their work and derive a strong sense of intrinsic satisfaction and achievement from it. For instance, successfully treating a critically ill patient not only saves a life but also fulfills their value as medical workers. This sense of achievement is incomparable to material compensation16. For another, medical workers with a high sense of meaning in life are more likely to maintain a positive attitude when facing work-related pressures and challenges. They view difficulties as necessary steps towards realizing their life’s purpose, rather than insurmountable obstacles. This positive mindset helps them better regulate their emotions, weakening the impact of negative emotions and thereby relating to higher professional happiness. Therefore, this study hypothesizes that the presence of meaning in life among medical workers is positively related to their professional happiness:

Hypothesis 5

The presence of meaning in life is positively related to professional happiness.

Meaningful work and professional happiness are key factors in enhancing employee productivity and retention in the workplace. Employees who feel meaningful and valued at work have higher levels of professional happiness16, and those who experience higher levels of happiness at work change jobs less frequently48. Psychological Contract Theory (PCT) addresses the unspoken psychological expectations that individuals hold for organizations49. Individuals’ professional happiness can be enhanced if their jobs enable them to achieve personal goals, realize the meaning of their lives, and fully utilize their potential. Conversely, a job lacking in expected recognition or support may relate to lower professional happiness. If their psychological contracts are not fulfilled, employees are more likely to choose to resign50. Complementing this perspective, Self-Determination Theory (SDT) posits that individuals are more inclined to stay in roles where their basic psychological needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness are satisfied51. Professional happiness, as a reflection of intrinsic need fulfillment, therefore serves as a key protective factor against turnover intention52.

Thus, drawing upon PCT and SDT, we suggest that medical workers’ professional happiness is negatively related to turnover intention, and it may mediate the relationship between medical workers’ presence of meaning in life and turnover intention.

Hypothesis 6

Professional happiness is negatively related to turnover intention.

Hypothesis 7

Professional happiness mediates the relationship between the presence of meaning in life and turnover intention.

Research methodology and data analysis

Research design

This cross-sectional study used an online survey as the research method. Its purpose was to evaluate the complex associations among the presence of meaning in life, the search for meaning in life, professional happiness, and turnover intention of medical workers.

Setting

This study adopted a two-stage mixed sampling design. In the first stage of site selection, simple random sampling was used. Computer-generated random numbers were assigned to all 57 tertiary-level hospitals in Hunan Province, and the three hospitals with the smallest random numbers were selected as the research sites. In the second stage of participant recruitment, convenience sampling was used to recruit voluntary participants from clinical and medical technology departments within each selected hospital. All surveys were completed via anonymous self-administered questionnaires. The data collection period was from March 2023 to April 2023.

Participants

The inclusion criteria of participants were as follows: (1) working in a tertiary hospital; (2) medical workers; (3) normal physical and mental conditions; and (4) informed consent and voluntary participation in this survey. The exclusion criteria included: (1) medical workers who were unable to fill out the questionnaire because their absence from their positions due to vacation, further training, or going out; and (2) interns who had not yet obtained their practicing certificates. Before completing the questionnaire, each participant received a 15–25-min online training on the purpose, content, process, and precautions of the survey. During the survey period, 1214 questionnaires were eventually collected, of which 1125 questionnaires were valid, resulting in a return rate of 92.67%.

Our study tested 46 independent variables in each hospital. And according to the recommendation, the sample size should be at least 20 times larger than the number of questions in the questionnaire, thus the minimum sample size for this study was 92053. Considering the 15% proportion of invalid data, a larger sample size was estimated, i.e. a minimum sample size of 1083 cases was required for this study. Therefore, the sample size of this study exceeds the minimum requirement.

Data collection tools

The measurement included four parts: demographic information, the Meaning of Life Questionnaire (MLQ), the Professional Happiness Scale for Medical Workers, and the Turnover Intention Scale.

Demographic information

Designed by the research team, this section collects respondents’ gender, age, occupation, years of working experience, education level, marital status, and the number of night shifts per month.

Meaning of life questionnaire (MLQ)

The MLQ was initially developed by Steger et al.18 and later adapted for Chinese by Wang and Dai54. The scale consists of two dimensions: the presence of meaning in life and the search for meaning in life, with a total of 10 items. The questionnaire items were scored on a 7-point Likert scale, with scores ranging from 1 to 7 indicating “not compliant at all” to “fully compliant”. And the total score ranged from 10 to 70. The higher the score, the higher the level of meaning in life of the respondents. The Cronbach’s coefficients for each dimension of the scale and the total scale ranged from 0.82 to 0.85.

Professional happiness scale for medical workers

This scale was compiled by Hu et al.55 in 2011. It contains 5 dimensions with a total of 24 items, including 6 items related to physical and mental health, 6 items to the expression of values/capabilities, 5 items to social support, 3 items to economic income, and 4 items to the working environment. The questionnaire items were scored using the Likert 5-point scale, with scores ranging from 1 to 5 indicating “not compliant at all” to “fully compliant”. Positive items were scored directly, and negative items were scored in reverse, with total scores ranging from 24 to 120. Higher scores suggested higher professional happiness level of the respondents. The Cronbach’s coefficients for each dimension of the scale and the total scale were 0.84–0.97.

Turnover intention scale

This scale was compiled by Michael et al.56 in 1982. It was translated and revised into a Chinese version by Lee et al.57, and then adapted again by Fan58 for use in the medical staff group. The scale consists of three items and uses a Likert 4-point scoring method, ranging from 1 to 4, where 1 indicates “never” and 4 indicates “always.” The total score ranges from 3 to 12, with higher scores indicating a greater likelihood of leaving the current work organization. The scale takes into account the specific context of China and thus has been widely accepted by researchers as an authoritative tool for measuring the strength of turnover intention among Chinese medical staff. The Cronbach’s coefficient for the scale was 0.878. However, in this study, the second item was removed due to low factor loading in the PLS‑SEM analysis. The final 2-item version yielded a Cronbach’s α of 0.65 and has restricted content coverage, as it mainly assesses thoughts of leaving and occupational switching rather than comprehensive job-search behavior. This limitation may lead to attenuated or biased effect size estimates for paths involving turnover intention. Future studies are encouraged to incorporate alternative validated and widely adopted turnover intention scales to enable a more comprehensive and robust measurement of this construct.

Consideration of ethical principles

This study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Central South University (approval NO. E202361). All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, and the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their involvement in the study. The data collection procedure adhered strictly to the approved protocol. Before participating, all respondents were fully informed about the study’s purpose and procedures, and provided their informed consent electronically. Participation was entirely voluntary. All collected data were anonymized and kept confidential; no personal identifiers (such as identity card numbers, cell phone numbers, or names) were collected. This study was a cross-sectional survey and did not involve human trials.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistical analysis of demographic information was conducted using SPSS version 27.0, utilizing frequencies and percentages for description. The Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM) approach was employed with smartPLS version 4.0 software. In our study, we opted for PLS-SEM for several reasons. Firstly, the raw data we collected were not normally distributed, which aligns with the recommendation by Hair et al.59 that PLS-SEM is preferable for non-normally distributed data. Secondly, PLS-SEM is versatile, being applicable to both exploratory and confirmatory research59. Lastly, across various scientific disciplines such as computer science, environmental science, medicine, psychology, sociology, and political science, researchers are increasingly favoring PLS-SEM over CB-SEM due to its advanced analytical techniques and supplementary methods that facilitate complex analysis tasks60. In the course of the specific analysis, the first step evaluated the measurement model with Cronbach’s alpha, factor loadings, average extracted variance, composite reliability, and discriminant validity. Subsequently, the hypothesized model was tested to examine the relationships among the presence of meaning in life, search for meaning in life, professional happiness, and turnover intention. Statistical significance was established at p < 0.05.

Given the cross-sectional self-report design of this study, Harman’s single-factor test was performed to assess potential common method variance (CMV). All items from the four scales were subjected to an unrotated exploratory factor analysis with one fixed factor extracted. The results showed that the first unrotated factor accounted for 34.71% of the total variance, which is well below the 50% threshold, indicating that common method bias was not a significant concern in the present study.

To control for potential confounding effects, we conducted a robustness check by incorporating key demographic control variables in the structural equation model. The detailed results are presented in Additional file 1.

Results

Characteristics of participants

In the demographics of this study, the respondents were predominantly female (84.71%). The age of respondents mainly ranged from 30 to 40 years old (58.40%), with fewer over the age of 40 (1.07%). A larger proportion of the respondents had a work experience of less than 10 years (76.98%). Respondents’ general level of education was a bachelor’s degree (68.18%). Their occupations were nurses (69.24%), medical technicians (19.29%), and physicians (11.47%). The percentage of married respondents amounted to 60.98%. Their number of night shifts was mostly 1–5 (28.00%) and 5–9 (31.82%).

Measurement model

The initial phase of PLS-SEM analysis was to evaluate the measurement model. Before evaluating the measurement model, we assessed the factor loadings of the indicators61. According to the literature62, they should be more than 0.7. Therefore, the fifth item of the presence of meaning in life subscale and the second item of the turnover intention scale were deleted for further analysis. For PM, the dropped Item 5 represents the purpose facet of meaning in life; however, this facet was still sufficiently represented by Item 2, so the conceptual coverage of PM was not narrowed. For TI, the dropped second item measured whether participants would seek similar employment after resignation. The remaining two items mainly reflect the frequency of turnover thoughts and the tendency to switch to a different type of occupation, rather than the full scope of job search behavior. Thus, the multidimensional representation of turnover intention was somewhat limited. These two items were deleted because they affected the internal consistency of the data. Structural model

The analysis of the structural model constituted the second stage of the PLS-SEM analysis. Firstly, it revealed that the presence of meaning in life (β = − 0.128, p < 0.01) and professional happiness (β = − 0.544, p < 0.001) were negatively associated with turnover intention, corroborating Hypotheses H1 and H6. Subsequently, the search for meaning in life was found to be positively associated with turnover intention (β = 0.102, p < 0.01), supporting H3. Contrary to H2, the presence of meaning in life was significantly positively associated with the search for meaning in life (β = 0.692, p < 0.001). And it was significantly positively associated with professional happiness (β = 0.511, p < 0.001), affirming H5. Discussion

Medical workers, especially the nursing community, are central to the delivery of comprehensive and high-quality patient care. They are indispensable for the effective operation of the global health system. As an important part of the health care system, nurses accounted for nearly 70% of the participants in this study. Therefore, maintaining a sufficient healthcare workforce is essential for ensuring the delivery of high-quality healthcare services to patients68. Moreover, rising turnover rates among medical workers compromise both the quality and continuity of healthcare services. In recent years, leaders of medical institutions have become increasingly concerned about how to attract and retain employees. Moreover, many researchers have proposed ways to reduce the turnover intention of medical workers, such as improving the working environment, providing better remuneration, optimizing the performance appraisal system, and reducing the intensity of work. However, relying solely on extrinsic forms of incentives may not exert long-term effects, while the intrinsic motivation to ensure the loyalty and dedication of medical workers comes from the sense of meaning in life and professional happiness gained from their work. This study employed structural equation modeling to examine how a unified meaning resource profile shapes turnover intention among medical workers. This profile comprises presence of meaning in life, search for meaning in life, and professional happiness as a cohesive set of higher-order inner positive psychological resources. This integrated framework extends beyond conventional turnover models centered on job stress, burnout, and resource depletion.

Our findings intriguingly revealed a positive relationship between the presence of meaning in life and the search for meaning in life, contradicting our initial Hypothesis 2 and the “Presence-to-Search Model”42. This discrepancy is particularly fascinating given Steger et al.’s findings of a negative correlation between these variables in the United States, contrasted with a positive correlation in Japan19. Such variations underscored the potential influence of cultural contexts on the relationship between the presence of meaning in life and the search for meaning in life69. We therefore propose that individualism versus collectivism, as well as independent versus interdependent self-construal, can shape how presence of meaning in life relates to subsequent search for meaning in life. In individualist contexts, higher presence tends to reduce search, consistent with the Western model; in collectivist contexts such as China, higher presence predicts greater search. In Western societies, the pursuit of individual happiness, freedom and independence is often more emphasized, and the search for the meaning of life is often regarded as a process of pursuing personal fulfillment. In these contexts, individuals with a substantial sense of life meaning might not actively seek further meaning. Whereas Asian countries such as China, South Korea, and the Philippines70,71 emphasize more on collectivism, social harmony, teamwork and the fulfillment of social roles, people may perceive the search for meaning in life and the presence of meaning in life as interrelated. When an individual’s presence of meaning in life is no longer able to provide him or her with sustained fulfillment, at that point, they may begin to seek new and higher levels of meaning in life. To advance organizational behavior research on meaning dynamics, future cross-cultural studies are encouraged to explicitly test this cultural moderation effect and clarify how cultural values shape the interplay between the two meaning components.

This study explored the dynamics between meaning in life and turnover intention among medical workers. It revealed that the presence of meaning in life is negatively associated with turnover intention, corroborating H1. Conversely, actively searching for meaning in life was positively associated with turnover intention, supporting H3. These findings combined with research suggested that meaningful work is related to lower inclination to leave among medical workers16,72. When people participate in meaningful work, they are more engaged17 and may even perform work beyond their contractual obligations. This concept is known as “extra-role performance”16. The presence of meaning in life allows people to devote more enthusiasm, time, and energy to their work. Conversely, when medical workers believe that their current job does not allow them to realize their self-worth, or to obtain sufficient space and opportunities for development, they gradually lose their motivation and enthusiasm for work, and they will seek a job that better matches their values and leave their current jobs. Another influential factor is professional happiness, which is negatively associated with turnover intention (supporting H6). This is consistent with previous research that professional happiness is related to lower propensity to leave among employees16. Lack of happiness at work has been associated with medical workers’ intention to leave their jobs73. High professional happiness in medical workers correlates with greater job satisfaction, favorable work conditions, adequate compensation, leader support, work-life balance, and a strong sense of achievement and organizational belonging—factors that collectively associated with lower propensity to leave. Moreover, professional happiness was positively associated with the presence of meaning in life, affirming H5. Meaningful work is a significant predictor of professional happiness16 and is associated with well-being indicators like job satisfaction74. The presence of meaning in life is associated with individuals’ realization of their values and beliefs at work, enhancing identification with their work when it resonates with their values. Additionally, a strong sense of meaning in life among medical workers may foster positive work relationships and organizational support, contributing to professional happiness.

The search for meaning in life positively mediated the association between the presence of meaning in life and turnover intention, supporting H4. However, rather than viewing the search for meaning in life solely as a negative force, it should be recognized as a potentially double-edged resource: adaptive at moderate levels, but associated with higher turnover intention when not matched by opportunities for meaningful work. Schneider’s ASA model, namely the attraction-selection-attrition model suggested that employees were attracted to matched organizations in terms of personality, values, beliefs, interests, etc. And over time, those who do not fit in with the organization would eventually choose to leave75. Therefore, when the organization can provide medical workers with meaningful work, it will make them feel that their goals, self-efficacy, etc. are highly aligned with their work. This alignment may motivate them to commit to their current job for a longer period. In that case, their sense of meaning in their existential life will be higher, and thus they will be less likely to leave their current organization76. However, if medical workers attempt to consistently seek higher meaning in life in their work, but find failures of their current job for adequately fulfilling this need, they may perceive their current job as contrary to their professional goals and values, which is associated with a greater likelihood of leaving16. While medical workers may choose a particular organization because they are seeking meaning in their lives, they may also choose to resign for the same reason if they do not receive a sustained sense of fulfillment. The search for meaning in life is a positive psychological mechanism and motivation18, and when individuals actively seek meaning in life, they tend to focus more on the intrinsic value of their work than on extrinsic rewards such as pay and promotion. Thus, when the search for meaning in life is chronically unmet, it can relate to a stronger desire to leave. However, over-seeking of meaning in life may result in ignoring the value of the job at the moment and decreasing work engagement, which may weaken the effect of the presence of meaning in life on turnover intention, thus creating a tendency to leave the job. This suggests that over-seeking of meaning in life may upset the balance between the presence of meaning in life and the search for meaning in life. We further suggest that search for meaning in life can take two functionally distinct forms. Exploratory search refers to a proactive, growth-oriented pursuit of deeper or higher-order meaning, often observed among individuals with strong presence of meaning in life in collectivist settings. Restorative search, by contrast, reflects a reactive, deficit-driven effort to repair unfulfilled or threatened meaning, and is more common among those with low presence of meaning in life and psychological distress. In high-pressure medical settings, these two types of search may show divergent links to presence of meaning in life and turnover intention, which helps explain the positive indirect effect of search on turnover intention observed in this study. Therefore, in addition to enhancing medical workers’ presence of meaning in life, both individual level and organizational level interventions may help align the search for meaning in life with retention. At the individual level, hospitals can organize reflective activities and peer sharing sessions on meaning in life. These help medical workers recognize how their daily work connects to personal and social values, reducing frustration from unfulfilled meaning seeking. At the organizational level, grounded in Self-Determination Theory51, these managerial levers can be explicitly mapped to three basic psychological needs: autonomy, competence, and relatedness. First, autonomy is supported by flexible and participatory staffing and scheduling, especially night shift allocation, which grants employees control over their work arrangements and mitigates burnout. Second, competence is strengthened by clear and accessible career development pathways, including specialty training, skill upgrading, and transparent promotion criteria that enable professional growth. Third, relatedness is fostered by participative leadership, constructive feedback, peer recognition systems, and team-based supportive practices that build social connection and shared values. Hospitals can also provide onboarding training, job redesign, recognition systems, and altruistic activities such as free medical assistance. For resource‑constrained public hospitals, the most actionable and low‑cost practices include flexible participatory scheduling, peer sharing and reflective sessions, informal recognition, and clear communication of career paths. These practices require minimal funding but generate strong retention benefits77,78. Given that nurses constituted nearly 70% of the study sample, several of the aforementioned human resource strategies carry particular relevance for nursing management, especially flexible and participatory night‑shift scheduling, participative leadership, and reflective activities on meaning in life. For physicians and medical technicians, dissatisfaction with career development prospects is significantly positively related to turnover intention79,80; thus, clear and accessible career development pathways, such as specialty training and promotion criteria, deserve greater emphasis. By combining these tailored approaches, healthcare organizations can better support meaning in life and lower turnover intention.

This study corroborated the H7 that professional happiness partially mediates the association between the presence of meaning in life and turnover intention. Self-Determination Theory (SDT) posited that understanding human motivation necessitates considering intrinsic motivations such as competence, autonomy, and relatedness81. These are pivotal for reducing turnover intention, and enhancing individual well-being, emotional commitment, and job satisfaction82. Intrinsic motivations have been identified as a critical determinant of job satisfaction and turnover intention among medical professionals52. When medical workers’ intrinsic motivations are fulfilled, they find their work more meaningful, leading to higher work engagement and professional happiness83. Conversely, unmet intrinsic needs and a lack of feeling valued diminish professional happiness and increase turnover intention. Research has shown that meaning at work was positively correlated with well-being; higher degree of meaning at work subsequently reduced the risk of leaving the job84. This highlights the importance for nursing managers and healthcare administrators to develop interventions aiming at higher professional happiness among healthcare staff.

Compared with the searching for meaning in life, we found that professional happiness shows a stronger association with turnover intention. We quantified the relative magnitude of the direct and indirect pathways to clarify their substantive contributions. The total effect of presence of meaning in life on turnover intention was β = − 0.335, with the direct effect accounting for 38.2% of this association. The indirect effect via professional happiness constituted the dominant protective pathway, explaining 83.0% of the total negative association, whereas the indirect effect via search for meaning in life represented a smaller positive countereffect, accounting for 21.2% of the total offsetting impact. These numerical findings confirm that professional happiness serves as the primary protective mechanism, whereas search for meaning in life operates as a context‑dependent, double‑edged factor with a weaker opposing influence, which is consistent with our interpretive framework. Professional happiness is the result of a combination of factors that are often closely related to the practical needs of medical professionals, such as job stress, burnout, job satisfaction, income, and organizational commitment85. If a person’s work environment does not fulfill basic livelihood needs, they may be more likely to leave their job because of practical issues, instead of considering whether the job is in line with the meaning of their life in the first place. Professional happiness is a sense of spiritual happiness and fulfillment86, which is usually associated with transient satisfaction and pleasure at work. A person’s positive experience at work may have a more direct relation with short-term well-being, and such positive experience is negatively associated with their tendency to leave. The search for meaning in life, on the other hand, is usually a much longer and deeper process. In the search for meaning in life, individuals need to develop a deeper understanding of themselves and the world, and continually strive to make their current experiences meaningful21. Therefore, professional happiness plays a more critical role in the factors related to medical professionals’ turnover intention.

Taken together, these findings illustrate how the meaning resource profile operates as an integrated inner system that shapes turnover intention among medical workers. As a cohesive configuration of higher-order positive psychological resources, presence of meaning in life serves as the core foundation that promotes both search for meaning in life and professional happiness. In turn, professional happiness acts as the primary protective pathway against turnover intention, whereas search for meaning in life functions as a double-edged mechanism that slightly increases turnover tendency. This integrated pattern underscores the value of moving beyond stress‑ and burnout‑centered turnover models and highlights the importance of cultivating a holistic meaning resource profile to retain medical staff.

Recommendations for employees

Theoretical recommendations

This study enriches the theoretical framework of positive psychology in healthcare workforce retention by verifying the dual mediating mechanism of searching for meaning in life and professional happiness. Findings challenge the Western “Presence-to-Search Model” and highlight the critical role of collectivist cultural contexts in the relationship between meaning in life and turnover intention. Researchers are encouraged to incorporate cultural values as moderators in future models to improve cross‑cultural generalizability. This study also expands the application of Self‑Determination Theory and Meaning Maintenance Model in explaining medical workers’ career behaviors, providing a new perspective for exploring positive psychological protective factors against turnover.

Practical recommendations

For medical workers themselves, actively cultivating the presence of meaning in life may be negatively associated with turnover intention. Employees can reflect on the value and social contribution of clinical work to strengthen internal motivation. Meanwhile, moderately pursuing meaning in life rather than over‑searching helps maintain work engagement and reduce job-hopping tendency. Improving professional happiness by adjusting work attitudes, enhancing work‑life balance, and gaining a sense of achievement from clinical practice is also negatively associated with the willingness to leave. Medical workers are suggested to communicate psychological needs with managers proactively to obtain more organizational support and resource guarantee.

Limitations and future directions

There are still some limitations of this study. First, this study used a cross-sectional design with single‑source self‑reported data, which may carry common method variance. Although Harman’s single-factor test showed the first factor accounted for only 34.71% of variance, indicating no severe CMV concern, Harman’s test alone is a relatively weak diagnostic for common method bias. Future studies are encouraged to adopt more rigorous approaches such as marker variables or temporal separation to further control for CMV. Second, this study adopted whole-cluster random sampling to select a partial sample from Hunan Province, China, which may not be representative of all medical workers in mainland China, thus limiting the generalizability of the findings. Future multi-center studies should be conducted to address this limitation. In this study, the Turnover Intention Scale used was reduced to two items from three after PLS-SEM analysis, with a Cronbach’s α value of 0.65. Although this value meets the acceptable threshold for reliability, it also suggests room for improvement in the scale’s internal consistency. This scale is widely accepted by researchers as an authoritative measure of the strength of turnover intention among Chinese medical workers. It is selected due to its feasibility for data collection and the consideration of reducing the participation time of participants. However, we acknowledge that the limited number of items may restrict the scale’s ability to capture the multidimensional characteristics of TI. We recommend that future studies employ more comprehensive and widely used turnover intention scales to improve measurement precision and enable replicable results.

Further research can be carried out in the following areas in the future. First, as most studies currently use cross-sectional surveys, longitudinal studies can be conducted in the future to track changes in medical workers’ turnover intention over different periods and to explore the dynamic trajectories between different variables. Second, medical workers’ sense of meaning in life and professional happiness may be affected by cultural contexts, and future studies can be conducted in different countries to explore the impact and the model applicability in different cultural contexts. Third, qualitative interview studies can be continued on the original basis to more comprehensively understand the intrinsic correlation mechanisms and influence paths among different variables. Finally, in addition to the several variables currently studied, the influence of other potential variables, such as job satisfaction, organizational atmosphere, etc., on turnover intention can be explored, so as to improve the model.

Conclusions

The reduction of turnover rates among medical workers holds critical importance for maintaining the quality and continuity of healthcare services and mitigating organizational costs associated with recruitment and training. This study illuminated the significant roles played by meaning in life and professional happiness in influencing medical workers’ turnover intention. Specifically, it was discovered that both the presence of meaning in life and professional happiness are inversely related to turnover intention, whereas the search for meaning in life exhibits a positive correlation with turnover intention. These findings suggest that a work environment imbued with meaning and support for medical workers’ well-being are negatively associated with turnover intention. By prioritizing these aspects, healthcare organizations can not only protect their human resources but elevate the level of patient care and ensure the healthcare system’s stability. Moreover, such initiatives are instrumental in fostering the high-quality advancement of healthcare in China.
