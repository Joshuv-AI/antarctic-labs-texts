Integrative single-cell and bulk transcriptomic analysis reveals the landscape of T cell mitotic catastrophe associated genes in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Shuang Li, Zheng Tao, Nan Wang, Yazhou Liu, Kai Xie, Haitao Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMitotic catastrophe (MC) is a well-recognized endogenous mechanism of tumor cell death, characterized as a delayed cell death process associated with aberrant mitosis. However, its prognostic significance in the context of intratumoral heterogeneity in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) remains largely unexplored.MethodsWe performed an in-depth analysis of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from ESCC obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. MC scores for individual cells were calculated using the AddModuleScore function, and T cell specific gene modules were identified via the high-dimensional weighted gene co-expression network analysis (hdWGCNA) framework. To further elucidate the developmental trajectories and intercellular interactions of T cells, pseudotime analysis and cell-cell communication inference were conducted. A prognostic risk model was then constructed using three machine learning algorithms combined with multivariate Cox regression analysis. Following risk stratification, we performed immune infiltration profiling, drug sensitivity analysis, and molecular docking to comprehensively assess the functional implications of the risk model in ESCC. Based on preliminary results from quantitative Real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) and Western blotting (WB), we selected the hub gene SLF2 for functional validation using wound healing, Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay, Transwell, and colony formation assays.ResultsBased on T cell mitotic catastrophe associated genes (MCAGs) and utilizing machine learning algorithms, we established a robust prognostic risk model for ESCC. The model demonstrated excellent stratification capability in predicting patient outcomes and effectively revealed the heterogeneity of the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) and drug sensitivity. Furthermore, functional experiments confirmed that knockdown of the hub gene SLF2 significantly inhibited the migration, invasion, and proliferation of ESCC cells.ConclusionThe prognostic model based on MCAGs we developed serves as an effective tool for predicting outcomes in ESCC.T cell-specific MCAGs drive intratumoral heterogeneity in ESCC, serving as potential prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40246-025-00815-9.Keywords: ESCC, T cell, Single cell, Mitotic catastrophe, Molecular docking

Introduction

Esophageal cancer (EC) is one of the most prevalent malignancies of the upper gastrointestinal tract, primarily comprising ESCC and esophageal adenocarcinoma (EAC). ESCC predominates in Southeast Asia, accounting for approximately 90% of EC cases globally, and is associated with a generally poor prognosis [1]. EC ranks as the eighth most common cancer worldwide but the sixth leading cause of cancer-related mortality [2–3]. If current trends persist, projections indicate 957,000 new EC cases and 880,000 EC related deaths by the year 2040 [4]. Despite advances in multimodal therapies, including surgery, chemoradiotherapy, and immunotherapy, the 5-year survival rate remains low, largely due to early metastasis and the development of treatment resistance [5]. Consequently, identifying key molecular drivers and developing novel therapeutic targets are crucial for improving patient outcomes.

Mitosis is the process by which replicated genetic material is segregated into two daughter nuclei during cell division [6]. Mounting evidence implicates mitotic processes and their key regulators in the pathogenesis of cancer and other diseases [7–9]. The fidelity of late mitotic events, in particularly, is critical for maintaining genomic integrity; dysfunction at this stage can promote malignant transformation. MC represents a delayed cell death mechanism triggered by aberrant mitotic entry or progression, often induced by chemical or physical stressors. This process encompasses a series of events culminating in cell death following failed or inappropriate mitosis. While cancer cells frequently exhibit hyperactivated mitotic cycles, MC serves as a safeguard mechanism that senses mitotic failure and eliminates potentially malignant cells through cell death [10–11]. MC can be considered a “point of no return” preceding other cell death pathways like apoptosis or necrosis [12].

Recent studies have explored the roles of mitotic catastrophe associated genes (MCAGs) in various cancers. For instance, Dai et al. combined machine learning with multi-omics analyses to delineate the prognostic and immunotherapeutic relevance of MCAGs in bladder cancer [13]. Similarly, MCAGs signatures have been successfully utilized to construct prognostic models demonstrating robust predictive performance in hepatocellular carcinoma, colon cancer, and breast cancer [14–16]. Notably, Duan et al. reported that phosphorylated histidine inorganic pyrophosphatase induces MC and suppresses ESCC cell growth via the P27/cyclin A/CDK2 signaling pathway [17]. These findings suggest MC as a potential therapeutic target in EC. However, a prognostic risk model based on MCAGs specifically for ESCC remains unexplored.

In this study, we hypothesize that T cell-specific MCAGs may drive intratumoral heterogeneity in ESCC and hold prognostic and therapeutic relevance. This hypothesis is based on the observed enrichment of mitotic catastrophe signatures within the T cell compartment of the ESCC tumor microenvironment (TME), a phenomenon that remains uncharacterized in this cancer type. To our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive analysis exploring MCAGs at both the single-cell and bulk transcriptomic levels in ESCC. Previous studies have identified a range of prognostic gene signatures in ESCC, including those related to hypoxia [18], mitophagy [19], and intracellular communication [20]; however, MC-related gene signatures, particularly those involving T cells, have not been reported. This study aims to explore our understanding of T cell-mediated mitotic dysfunction in ESCC and its implications for prognosis, immune escape, and therapeutic targeting.We initiated our investigation with scRNA-seq to identify core MCAGs. Subsequently, we leveraged bulk transcriptomic data to construct a prognostic model and analyze immune heterogeneity. Finally, key findings were validated through in vitro cellular experiments. Methods

Data acquisition

ScRNA-seq datasets GSE196756, GSE145370 and bulk transcriptomic dataset GSE53625 were obtained from the GEO database(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). GSE196756 includes scRNA-seq profiles from three ESCC tumors and matched adjacent normal tissues, while GSE145370 comprises seven tumor-normal pairs. A total of 10 tumor samples were selected for downstream analyses. GSE53625, consisting of transcriptomic and clinicopathological data from 179 ESCC patients, was used as the training cohort. An independent validation cohort of 96 ESCC patients with RNA sequencing and survival data was obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA)(https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). A list of 900 MCAGs was curated from GeneCards (Supplementary Table. S1), based on prior literature [15–16].

Preprocessing of scRNA-seq data

Data preprocessing was conducted using the “Seurat” package [21]. Low-quality genes and cells were filtered out, and the top 3,000 highly variable genes were used for principal component analysis (PCA). Batch effects were corrected via the “Harmony” algorithm. Cell types were annotated using “SingleR” in conjunction with the CellMarker 2.0 database for manual curation. Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) was applied for dimensionality reduction and visualization. A MC score was calculated for each cell using the AddModuleScore function. Based on the median score, cells were stratified into high and low score groups. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the two groups were identified using FindMarkers with thresholds of Fold Change (FC) > 1.2 and adjusted p-value < 0.05.

HdWGCNA analysis

To construct a co-expression network in single-cell data, we employed the “hdWGCNA” package [22]. Metacells were generated using the “MetacellsByGroups” function with 50 cells per metacell. T cells were extracted from the integrated Seurat object and used for network construction. The following functions were executed sequentially: TestSoftPowers, ConstructNetwork, ModuleEigengenes, ModuleConnectivity, and RunModuleUMAP.

Pseudotime and Cell-Cell communication analysis

Cellular trajectory inference was performed using the “Monocle2” package [23] to elucidate T cell differentiation dynamics during ESCC progression. Intercellular communication was inferred using the “CellChat” package [24], leveraging the human ligand–receptor database CellChatDB. Communication probability and interaction strength among cell clusters were computed and visualized.

Functional enrichment analysis

Key genes were defined as the intersection of DEGs and T cell-associated module genes. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were performed using the “ClusterProfiler” package [25]. GO terms were categorized into biological process (BP), molecular function (MF), and cellular component (CC). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Prognostic gene selection via machine learning

Candidate prognostic genes were initially screened via univariate Cox regression using the GEO-ESCC cohort. Subsequently, three machine learning algorithms—least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO), support vector machine (SVM), and random forest (RF)—were applied to refine gene selection. LASSO regression was performed with 10-fold cross-validation to determine the optimal regularization parameter (λ). A sequence of 100 logarithmically spaced λ values was generated on the training set. During each iteration, the model was trained on 9 folds and evaluated on the remaining fold by computing the partial likelihood deviance. This process was repeated 10 times, and the average deviance was calculated for each λ. The λ value corresponding to the minimum average deviance (λ. min = 0.024997) was selected as the optimal regularization strength. To evaluate the performance of the support vector machine (SVM) classifier and to avoid overfitting, 5-fold cross-validation was performed. The entire dataset was randomly partitioned into five equally sized subsets. In each iteration, four subsets were used for model training, and the remaining one was used for validation. This process was repeated five times, with each subset used exactly once as the validation set. The average performance metrics across the five folds were computed to assess the model’s stability and generalizability. The TCGA-ESCC cohort served as the validation set.

Construction and validation of a prognostic risk model

A multivariate Cox proportional hazards model was developed based on selected prognostic genes. The risk score was calculated as: Risk Score = ∑ (βi × expressioni) where βi is the regression coefficient and expressioni is the expression level of genei. Patients were dichotomized into high and low risk groups based on the median risk score. Model performance was evaluated via time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and area under the curve (AUC). Kaplan-Meier (KM)survival analysis was used to assess prognostic stratification.

Nomogram construction and evaluation

To assess clinical utility, a nomogram was constructed by integrating the risk score and relevant clinicopathological variables. Univariate and multivariate Cox analyses were performed to evaluate the independence of the risk score. Calibration plots and decision curve analysis (DCA) were used to assess predictive accuracy and clinical benefit, respectively. A circle diagram was generated to visualize correlations between risk scores and clinical features.

Immune infiltration and immunotherapy response analysis

The CIBERSORT algorithm [26] was used to estimate the infiltration of 22 immune cell subtypes in each sample. Spearman correlation was applied to assess associations among risk scores, gene expression and immune infiltrating abundance. The Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) algorithm (http://tide.dfci.harvard.edu/) was employed to predict response to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs).

Drug sensitivity prediction and molecular Docking

The “OncoPredict” package was used to predict the half-maximal inhibitory concentrations (IC50) of 198 drugs for ESCC patients based on the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) database. Drugs with the smallest p-values were selected for molecular docking analysis with the model genes. Small-molecule structures were obtained from the PubChem database (http://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), and protein structures were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) (http://www.rcsb.org/). When unavailable in PDB, protein structures were downloaded from the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database. Molecular docking was performed using Cb-Dock2 by removing hydrogen atoms and water molecules. Blind docking was conducted in undefined binding pockets. Docking results were visualized to show the ligand conformation within the binding site.

Cell culture and SiRNA transfection

Human ESCC cell lines (Kyse-30 and TE-1) and a normal esophageal epithelial cell line (HET-1 A) were obtained from the Shanghai Institute of Biological Sciences. Cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. SLF2-specific siRNAs (si-SLF2-1, si-SLF2-2,si-SLF2-3) and negative control (NC) were transfected using Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Western blot analysis

Proteins were extracted in PMSF-containing lysis buffer and quantified via BCA assay. Equal protein amounts were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes. After blocking with 5% non-fat milk, membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence reagents.

Wound healing assay

Cells were seeded in six-well plates and grown to confluence. A linear scratch was introduced using a sterile 200-µL pipette tip. Wound closure was monitored at 0, 24, and 48 h, and images were captured under a microscope. The wound width was measured using ImageJ.

CCK-8 cell viability assay

Cell viability was measured using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (Beyotime). Cells (5 × 10³/well) were seeded in 96-well plates, and absorbance at 450 nm was measured at 24, 48, and 72 h following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Transwell invasion assay

Cell invasion was assessed using Matrigel-coated Transwell chambers (8-µm pore size, Corning). Cells (2 × 10⁴) were seeded in the upper chamber with serum-free medium, and 10% FBS-containing medium was added to the lower chamber. After 36 h, non-invading cells were removed, and invaded cells were fixed with paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.1% crystal violet.

Colony formation assay

Cells were seeded at 1 × 10³ cells/well in six-well plates and cultured for 10–14 days. Colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.5% crystal violet, and counted using ImageJ.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.4.2) and GraphPad Prism (version 8.0). If the data followed a normal distribution, comparisons between groups were conducted using the t-test or one-way ANOVA, as appropriate. For data that did not meet the assumptions of normality, the non-parametric Mann–Whitney U test was applied. Statistical significance was defined as follows: p < 0.05 (*), p < 0.01 (**), p < 0.001 (***), p < 0.0001 (****), ns indicates no significance.

Results

Single-cell analysis of ESCC and HdWGCNA

Our study encompassed 31,499 cells derived from 10 ESCC samples. Following PCA for batch effect removal, normalization was performed using the Harmony R package. Subsequent dimensionality reduction and clustering identified 15 distinct cell clusters, visualized via UMAP (Fig. 2A). These clusters were annotated into 7 major cell types: T cells, monocyte/macrophages, B cells, fibroblasts, mast cells, epithelial cells and endothelial cells—based on canonical marker genes (Fig. 2B).To assess MC activity across cell types, the AddModuleScore function was employed, incorporating a set of 900 MCAGs to generate an MC score for each cell (Fig. 2C). Cells were stratified into high and low MC score groups based on the median score (Fig. 2D), yielding 6,690 DEGs between these groups (Supplementary Table. S2). Furthermore, we compared MC scores among the different cell clusters (Fig. 2E). Notably, higher MC scores were observed in T cells, monocyte/macrophages, mast cells, epithelial cells, and endothelial cells, while B cells and fibroblasts exhibited lower scores. This suggests a potentially more active MC process in the higher-scoring clusters, which may contribute to tumorigenesis.

Analysis of cellular composition revealed T cells as the predominant population (53.69%), followed by monocyte/macrophages (24.46%) and B cells (11.78%) (Fig. 2F). Supplementary Fig. S1A and Supplementary Fig. S1B depict the cluster composition per sample and sample composition per cell type, respectively, consistently showing T cells as the most abundant population across most samples. Given these findings and the high MC scores in T cells, we selected T cells for subsequent in-depth analysis, hypothesizing a significant role for MC within this compartment in ESCC pathogenesis.

To identify key gene modules associated with T cells, we performed hdWGCNA. A power of β = 6 was selected to achieve a scale-free network topology (Supplementary Fig. S1C), resulting in the identification of 10 gene modules (Supplementary Fig.S1D). Bubble plot analysis revealed the expression of each module in different cell types (Fig. 2G). The brown, red, green, pink, and turquoise modules showed strong expression in T cells, while the blue module showed weak expression in T cells. Expression patterns of module genes across cell types (Fig. 2H) corroborated the correlation findings: blue module genes were scarcely expressed in T cells but highly expressed in monocyte/macrophages, whereas the brown, red, green, pink, and turquoise modules showed high expression in T cells (Supplementary Table. S3). Module-module correlation analysis confirmed significant negative correlation between the blue module and others, while positive correlations prevailed among the brown, red, green, pink, and turquoise modules (Fig. 2I). Consequently, genes within the brown, red, green, pink, and turquoise modules were designated as T cell-associated signature genes, with top hub genes ranked by Connectivity to the Module Eigengene (kME) displayed (Fig. 2J-N).

Pseudotime trajectory analysis and Cell-Cell communication

T cells were subsetted and re-clustered, yielding 12 subpopulations (Fig. 3A). Based on established marker genes, these were annotated into four major subtypes: CD4 + T cells (“SELL”, “CD4"and"ITK”; primarily helper and regulatory T cells), CD8 + T cells (“CD8A”, “CD8B"and"CRTAM”; primarily cytotoxic T cells), Double-Positive T cells(DP T cells) (“MKI67”, “STMN1”; indicative of activated/exhausted states), and Double-Negative T cells(DN T cells) (“TBX21"and"TRDC”; encompassing γδT, MAIT, NKT, and other unconventional T cells) (Fig. 3B).

Pseudotime trajectory analysis delineated the developmental path of T cells (Fig. 3C-E). Cells progressed from the lower-left to the upper-right along the pseudotime axis, partitioned into 8 distinct states. CD4 + and CD8 + T cells were distributed relatively evenly across pseudotime, suggesting their involvement throughout tumor progression. DP T cells were more prevalent in later pseudotime stages, implying heightened activity in advanced disease.

Cell-cell communication analysis indicated that T cells primarily interact with monocyte/macrophages, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells (Fig. 3F-G). Both the number and strength of interactions were most prominent between T cells and monocyte/macrophages, highlighting a potentially critical crosstalk in ESCC development.

Identification of prognostic core genes and functional enrichment in transcriptomics

Intersection of the previously identified DEGs from scRNA-seq MC score comparison and the T cell signature genes from hdWGCNA yielded 335 core genes (Fig. 4A). GO and KEGG analysis revealed these genes were primarily involved in protein folding, chaperone-mediated protein folding, unfolded protein binding, actin cytoskeleton regulation, and shigellosis pathways (Fig. 4B-C). Univariate Cox regression analysis in the GEO-ESCC cohort identified 13 core genes significantly associated with patient prognosis (Fig. 4D). To refine this set, three machine learning algorithms were applied: LASSO regression: Identified 10 important genes (Fig. 4E-F). SVM: Achieved minimal error and maximal accuracy with 13 genes (Fig. 4G-H). RF: Ranked the 13 genes by importance; the top 10 were selected (Fig. 4I). Intersection of the genes selected by all three methods yielded 9 core prognostic genes (Fig. 4J).

Construction the prognostic risk model, development and evaluation of a clinical nomogram

Multivariate Cox regression analysis on the 9 core genes within the GEO-ESCC cohort identified three genes as independent prognostic factors (Fig. 5A): ARAP2[HR = 0.735,95%CI(0.584–0.925)], SLF2[HR = 1.429,95%CI(1.062–1.922)] and AKAP13:[HR = 1.641, 95% CI (1.032–2.609)].The prognostic risk score was calculated as: Risk Score = ARAP2×(-0.281) + SLF2 × 0.258 + AKAP13 × 0.374.Patients were stratified into high-risk (n = 89 in GEO-ESCC; n = 48 in TCGA-ESCC) and low-risk (n = 90 in GEO-ESCC; n = 48 in TCGA-ESCC) groups based on the median risk score. KM survival curves demonstrated significantly worse overall survival in the high-risk group for both cohorts (p < 0.05) (Fig. 5B-C). In the GEO-ESCC cohort, the risk score achieved AUC values of 0.709, 0.724, and 0.724 for predicting 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year survival, respectively (Fig. 5D). In the TCGA-ESCC cohort, the risk score achieved AUC values of 0.662, 0.746, and 0.582 for predicting 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year survival, respectively (Fig. 5E). The risk score distribution plot for GEO-ESCC cohort (Fig. 5F) showed increasing risk scores correlated with rising expression of the risk genes SLF2 and AKAP13, decreasing expression of the protective gene ARAP2, and a higher number of deaths. A similar pattern was observed in the TCGA-ESCC cohort (Fig. 5G).

A circos plot illustrated differences in clinicopathological features between high and low risk groups within GEO-ESCC cohort (Fig. 5H). While age, sex, tumor location, and grade showed no significant differences, the high-risk group exhibited more advanced T stage, N stage, and TNM stage, indicating an association between higher risk scores and later disease stage and poorer prognosis. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses incorporating the risk score and key clinicopathological features confirmed both age and the risk score as independent prognostic factors (Fig. 5I-J). Consequently, a clinical nomogram was constructed incorporating age, grade, N stage, TNM stage, and the risk score to predict 1-year, 3-year and 5-year survival probabilities (Fig. 5K). Calibration curves demonstrated good agreement between nomogram-predicted and actual survival probabilities at 1, 3, and 5 years (Fig. 5L). DCA indicated favorable clinical net benefit for the nomogram at 3 and 5 years, though net benefit was slightly lower at 1 year (Fig. 5M-O).

Localization and dynamic expression of model genes

UMAP plots and multi-group violin plots visualized the expression of the three model genes (ARAP2,SLF2 and AKAP13) across the major cell clusters (Supplementary Fig. S2A-B). ARAP2 and AKAP13 were broadly expressed in ESCC, with ARAP2 predominantly in T cells and AKAP13 in T cells, monocyte/macrophages, B cells, mast cells, and endothelial cells. SLF2 expression was largely restricted to T cells.Analysis within T cell subpopulations revealed widespread expression of AKAP13 across all subtypes (Supplementary Fig. S2C). ARAP2 and SLF2 expression levels were comparatively lower. Pseudotime trajectory analysis of T cells showed dynamic expression patterns (Supplementary Fig. S2D): AKAP13 expression started high and gradually decreased; ARAP2 began low, peaked midway, then declined; SLF2 expression remained relatively stable. This highlights the heterogeneous roles of these genes during ESCC progression.

Immune microenvironment analysis, drug sensitivity analysis and molecular Docking

The CIBERSORT algorithm was used to quantify the relative abundances of 22 immune cell types in the GEO-ESCC cohort. A stacked bar chart detailed the immune landscape for each patient (Fig. 6A). Boxplots comparing immune infiltration between risk groups revealed significant heterogeneity (Fig. 6B). The high-risk group exhibited significantly higher infiltration of immunosuppressive cell types like Regulatory T cells (Tregs) and M2 macrophages, which promote tumor progression. Conversely, infiltration of pro-inflammatory/anti-tumor cell types like plasma cells, monocytes, M0 macrophages, M1 macrophages, and CD8 + T cells was significantly lower in the high-risk group. Correlation analysis revealed significant associations, Risk Score (Fig. 6C-E): Positive with M2 macrophages; Negative with plasma cells and T helper cells. ARAP2 (Supplementary Fig. S3A): Positive with activated dendritic cells. SLF2 (Supplementary Fig. S3B-E): Positive with activated memory CD4 + T cells and resting mast cells; Negative with activated NK cells and activated mast cells. AKAP13 (Supplementary Fig. S3F-G): Negative with M1 macrophages; Positive with resting dendritic cells. (All p < 0.05). These findings suggest a more immunosuppressive TME in high-risk patients. Furthermore, analysis using the TIDE algorithm showed a positive correlation between the risk score and TIDE score (Fig. 6F), with significantly higher TIDE scores in the high-risk group, indicating a greater likelihood of immune evasion and potentially poorer response to immunotherapy, consistent with the CIBERSORT results.

Drug sensitivity analysis (based on estimated IC50 values) identified significant differences (p < 0.05) between risk groups for 37 compounds. Supplementary Fig. S3H displays the top 15 clinically relevant chemotherapeutic and targeted agents with p < 0.001. Agents like Buparlisib, Cytarabine, Dactolisib, Daporinad, Foretinib, Staurosporine, Talazoparib, and Tozasertib showed lower IC50 (higher sensitivity) in the high-risk group. Conversely, Afatinib, Entinostat, Erlotinib, Gefitinib, Lapatinib, Sapitinib, and Selumetinib exhibited higher IC50 (lower sensitivity) in the high-risk group, indicating distinct therapeutic vulnerabilities.Molecular docking was performed between Talazoparib (selected due to its low p-value and significant IC50 difference) and the protein products of the three model genes (Fig. 6G-H show Talazoparib structure). Docking results demonstrated favorable binding interactions between Talazoparib and all three proteins (Fig. 6I-K), with low binding energies: -12.4 kcal/mol (ARAP2), -9.2 kcal/mol (SLF2) and − 8.1 kcal/mol (AKAP13). This provides a structural rationale supporting the observed association between the risk score and drug sensitivity, offering insights for potential therapeutic strategies.

Discussion

Despite surgical resection being the conventional treatment for esophageal cancer, the 5-year survival rate for advanced-stage patients remains dismal, at 15–25% [27]. Given the incomplete understanding of ESCC pathogenesis, identifying novel signaling pathways or molecular mechanisms is crucial. As previously mentioned, MCAGs have been utilized for prognostic prediction in multiple cancers [14–16] and to reveal immune heterogeneity. However, their role in ESCC remains largely unexplored. Our study pioneers the construction of a prognostic risk model based on T cell MC-associated genes in ESCC. This model effectively stratifies patients, delineates immune microenvironment heterogeneity, predicts drug sensitivity, and experimentally validates that the key model gene SLF2 promotes migration, invasion, and proliferation in ESCC cell lines. These findings provide novel insights into ESCC pathogenesis and potential therapeutic avenues.

We began by integrating scRNA-seq data from 10 ESCC samples. Following dimensionality reduction, clustering, and annotation, we identified 7 major cell types. MC scores were calculated for each cell, and cells were stratified into high and low MC score groups based on the median score, yielding DEGs between these groups. Notably, T cells constituted the predominant cell population and exhibited significantly higher MC scores compared to B cells and fibroblasts. This observation suggests that T cells may influence ESCC pathogenesis through MC associated processes. Consequently, we employed hdWGCNA to identify T cell associated module genes. The intersection of these module genes with the DEGs yielded core genes used for subsequent prognostic screening in bulk transcriptomic data.

Pseudotime trajectory analysis revealed that DP T cells were predominantly located in the mid-to-late stages of the pseudotime axis, potentially implicating them in the immunosuppressive microenvironment characteristic of advanced tumor progression. This aligns with the documented complex role of DP T cells in cancer [28]. Cell-cell communication analysis highlighted prominent interactions between T cells and monocyte/macrophages, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells. T cells secrete cytokines such as interleukin-2(IL-2), interferon-γ(IFN-γ), granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), and interleukin-3(IL-3). Collectively, these cytokines efficiently activate macrophages, bolstering immune defense responses and playing a pivotal role in the early recognition and initiation of tumor immunity. Furthermore, T cells secrete cytokines including IL-3, B cell stimulating factor 1 (BSF-1/IL-4) and interleukin-5(IL-5), deeply engaging in humoral immune regulation and contributing to the maintenance of immune microenvironment homeostasis during tumor progression [29].

Leveraging bulk transcriptomic data and clinicopathological information from the GEO-ESCC cohort, univariate Cox regression analysis on the 335 MCAGs identified 13 prognostic genes. Subsequent refinement using machine learning algorithms-LASSO regression, SVM and RF-yielded 9 genes. Finally, multivariate Cox regression analysis established a 3-gene (ARAP2, SLF2 and AKAP13) prognostic risk model. This model demonstrated robust prognostic stratification capability in both the GEO-ESCC and TCGA-ESCC cohorts, particularly for predicting 3-year survival (AUC = 0.724 and 0.746, respectively). To enhance clinical utility, we integrated the risk score with key clinicopathological features to construct a nomogram, further augmenting individualized prognostic prediction.ARAP2: An ADP-ribosylation factor GTPase-activating protein [30]. While its specific role in cancer is not extensively documented, Waleed S Al Amri et al. identified ARAP2 as a potential mediator of chemotherapy response and a prognostic indicator in breast cancer [31]. Our findings position ARAP2 as a protective factor in ESCC, with higher expression correlating with better prognosis. Consistently, we observed significantly reduced ARAP2 expression in Kyse-30 and TE-1 ESCC cell lines compared to the normal HET-1 A cell line.SLF2 (SMC5-SMC6 Complex Localization Factor 2): Involved in processes like positive regulation of cellular component organization, double-strand break repair, and protein localization to DNA damage sites. SLF2 functions in the DNA damage response (DDR) pathway by regulating post-replication repair of UV-damaged DNA and maintaining genomic stability [32]. It forms a complex with SLMC1, essential for recruiting the SMC5-SMC6 complex to PML nuclear bodies and viral replication sites [33]. While prior links to cancer are limited, our study identifies SLF2 as a risk factor in ESCC, where higher expression correlates with increased risk scores and poorer prognosis. This suggests SLF2 may promote tumor progression, potentially by impeding MC in T cells and altering the immune microenvironment. Functional validation confirmed that SLF2 knockdown significantly impaired ESCC cell migration, invasion, and proliferation.AKAP13 (A-Kinase Anchoring Protein 13): A protein-coding gene associated with diseases like autism spectrum disorder and long QT syndrome 8. It participates in pathways such as p75 NTR receptor-mediated signaling and activation of cAMP-dependent PKA, with GO annotations including obsolete signal transducer activity and molecular adaptor activity. Yangzhou Liu et al. reported that miR-629-5p promotes prostate cancer occurrence and metastasis by targeting AKAP13 [34], and Jianlan Liu et al. included AKAP13 in a glycosylation-related gene prognostic model for uveal melanoma [35]. Our results identify AKAP13 as a risk factor in ESCC. However, we detected no significant difference in AKAP13 expression (mRNA or protein) between ESCC cell lines (KYSE-30, TE-1) and the normal HET-1 A cell line. This discrepancy may stem from heterogeneity between cell lines and clinical tumor samples, warranting further validation using immunohistochemistry on patient tissues.

Immune infiltration analysis demonstrated that our risk model effectively captures the heterogeneity of the immune microenvironment in ESCC. As shown in the results, the high-risk group exhibited significantly higher infiltration of immunosuppressive cell types like Tregs and M2 macrophages (which promote tumor progression), and significantly lower infiltration of pro-inflammatory/anti-tumor cell types like plasma cells, monocytes, M0 macrophages, M1 macrophages, and CD8 + T cells. Tregs suppress anti-tumor immunity by inhibiting dendritic cell antigen presentation, CD4 + T helper (Th) cell function, and the generation of tumor-specific CD8 + cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) [36–37]. Furthermore, the risk score showed a significant positive correlation with M2 macrophage infiltration, a known promoter of tumor progression and immune suppression, consistent with studies linking M2 macrophage infiltration to esophageal cancer advancement [38].

Drug sensitivity analysis revealed significant differences between risk groups for numerous chemotherapeutic and targeted agents. Molecular docking was performed with Talazoparib, the compound showing the smallest P-value for IC5₀ difference. Intriguingly, Talazoparib, a poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitor known to selectively kill cancer cells with DNA repair defects, demonstrated favorable binding interactions with all three model gene proteins (ARAP2, SLF2 and AKAP13). This structural finding suggests a potential mechanism whereby Talazoparib might exert therapeutic effects in high-risk ESCC, possibly by correcting aberrant MC processes associated with these genes. Collectively, these results underscore the significant role of MC-associated genes in ESCC.

Our study has several limitations: (1) In the process of identifying core genes, we did not directly use the initial set of 900 MCRGs to construct the prognostic model. Instead, we based our selection on the DEGs between high and low MC score cells. This approach may introduce a potential double-dipping issue, possibly leading to biased identification of core genes. (2) The inconsistency in AKAP13 expression levels (lack of difference in cell lines vs. its role as a risk factor in cohorts) necessitates validation using clinical tumor samples; (3) The limited number of patients with 5-year survival data in the TCGA cohort restricts the validation of long-term predictive performance, requiring larger studies; (4) The functional validation focused on SLF2’s phenotypic effects, leaving its precise mechanistic role in MC and ESCC progression unexplored.

Conclusion

This study pioneers the construction of a T cell MC-associated prognostic signature for ESCC. We identified key driver genes, including SLF2, and unveiled novel insights into their roles in promoting ESCC progression, potentially via modulating the TME and mitotic processes. Our findings not only deepen the understanding of molecular heterogeneity in ESCC but also provide a foundation for developing precision therapeutic strategies targeting the mitotic catastrophe pathway.
