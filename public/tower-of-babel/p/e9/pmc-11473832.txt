Percutaneous transluminal angioplasty is safe and feasible for reinsertion of tunneled cuffed catheters in the right internal jugular vein
Authors: Daoyang Wang, Yanchun Guan, Li Zhuo, Zheng Chen, Xuxiang Ma, Yuping Huang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study explores the feasibility, safety, and efficacy of percutaneous transluminal angioplasty (PTA) for reinserting tunneled cuffed catheters (TCC) with a Dacron sheath in the right internal jugular vein (RIJV) in hemodialysis patients with a history of prior RIJV catheterization and subsequent stenosis or occlusion of the RIJV, right innominate vein, and superior vena cava. Clinical data from 21 hemodialysis patients with dysfunctional vascular access who underwent PTA for reinsertion of TCC in the RIJVs from July 2020 to July 2023 at the First and Second Affiliated Hospitals of Bengbu Medical College were retrospectively analyzed. Clinical efficacy during hospitalization, postoperative TCC blood flow, and related complications during follow-up were observed. The procedure was successful in all 21 patients, with postoperative TCC blood flow meeting daily hemodialysis requirements. Only one case experienced acute bleeding with contrast agent extravasation at the intersection of the left and right innominate veins during sharp recanalization. No severe complications, such as arrhythmias, vascular rupture, pneumothorax, mediastinal hematoma, or pericardial tamponade, occurred during the procedures. Upon discharge, all patients exhibited satisfactory TCC blood flow (247.14 ± 11.46 ml/min). Postoperatively, TCC blood flow ranged between 200 and 260 ml/min, meeting the demands of regular hemodialysis. For patients with a history of repeated TCC or non-tunneled catheter (NTC) placement in the RIJV, reinserting TCC in the RIJVs through PTA is a safe and reliable technique. It effectively utilizes vascular resources and prevents vascular resource depletion associated with changing the venous catheter placement location. Introduction

A well-functioning vascular access is a lifeline for patients undergoing maintenance hemodialysis (MHD) to ensure their long-term survival. With the rapid increase in elderly, diabetic, and obese dialysis patients, coupled with a significant extension in the duration of MHD treatment, the depletion of vascular access resources has become a common cause of hospitalization and death among MHD patients. The challenges faced by medical professionals in maintaining vascular access for these patients are formidable.

Balloon dilation, or percutaneous transluminal angioplasty (PTA), restores the patency of narrowed or occluded veins in hemodialysis patients, crucial for effective treatment1. During the procedure, a balloon catheter is inserted into the vein under ultrasound digital subtraction angiography (DSA) guidance and inflated at the stenosis site to restore blood flow2. This is vital for end-stage renal disease patients dependent on hemodialysis1. If satisfactory blood flow is not achieved, additional procedures like further dilation or stent placement may be needed. Strict aseptic conditions are maintained to prevent infections3.

This procedure plays a critical role in managing vascular access in hemodialysis patients, ensuring they can continue receiving the necessary treatments. It’s essential to consider the patient’s condition and the specific characteristics of the stenosis or occlusion to determine the appropriate approach and techniques. While the balloon dilation procedure in hemodialysis is generally considered low-risk and complication-free4, as with any medical procedure, there are potential risks, including injuries to the venous of arterial wall during catheter insertion, detachment of deposits leading to vascular obstruction, and, in rare cases, the need for further intervention such as a bypass operation to restore blood flow5. Patients with compromised vascular access often have a history of repeated catheter placements in the left or right internal jugular vein (RIJV), or even the common femoral vein. Combined with stenosis or occlusive lesions in the internal jugular vein, innominate vein, or vena cava, clinicians may choose to avoid using the RIJV for catheter placement, especially in cases where there is a history of repeated placements of temporary dialysis catheters or catheters with cuffs and tunnels in the RIJV. Instead, they may opt for catheter placement in the left internal jugular vein or even the common femoral vein, leading to new vascular damage and severe complications such as central venous stenosis (CVS) or occlusion6–8.

With the continuous development of intravascular intervention techniques, repairing narrowed or occluded central veins through PTA has emerged as a novel treatment approach9. A tunneled cuffed catheter (TCC) is a specific type of central venous access device (CVAD) utilized for long-term vascular access, particularly in patients undergoing hemodialysis8. It is inserted into a vein in the neck and exits through the chest10. A segment of the TCC remains beneath the skin and is tunneled through the chest wall10. The TCC, typically 19–23 cm in length, features two lumens. Unlike a temporary catheter, the tube of a TCC passes under the skin for approximately 8–10 cm and emerges over the chest8. The TCC serves to deliver treatments, blood transfusions, and is also capable of facilitating blood sample collection11. It proves especially beneficial for patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) requiring regular hemodialysis4.

Reinsertion of a TCC into a vein with a history of catheter placement using endovascular interventional techniques not only addresses vascular access dysfunction in dialysis patients, but also protects the central venous vascular resource in these patients. This article summarizes and follows a case series of PTA-assisted reinsertion of a TCC into the RIJV for patients with vascular access dysfunction in the Department of Nephrology of the First and Second Affiliated Hospital of Bengbu Medical College. They provide a new therapeutic approach for reinsertion of TCC catheters by reusing “discarded” right jugular vein resources, which can help to effectively solve the vascular access challenges in hemodialysis patients. Details of these cases are reported below.

Materials and methods

General information

The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Bengbu Hospital of Shanghai General Hospital, the Second Affiliated Hospital of Bengbu Medical University. The research has been performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Each patient has provided written informed consent before participating in this study.

Clinical data of patients who underwent the placement of TCC in the RIJV due to dysfunction of the hemodialysis vascular access at The First and Second Affiliated Hospital of Bengbu Medical College from July 2020 to July 2023 were collected and analyzed. The study included 13 female and 8 male patients, aged 33 to 85 years, with an average age of (63.86 ± 13.86) years. The duration of dialysis ranged from 2 to 192 months, with an average duration of (64.57 ± 56.71) months. The average number of TCCs placed in the RIJV (including non-tunneled catheters [NTC] and TCC) was (2.71 ± 0.90) per patient. The primary diseases leading to hemodialysis were diabetic nephropathy in 11 cases, chronic nephritis in 5 cases, hypertensive nephropathy in 1 case, lupus nephritis in 1 case, obstructive nephropathy due to urolithiasis in 1 case, membranous nephropathy in 1 case, and Goodpasture’s syndrome in 1 case. Preoperative dialysis access included 10 cases with TCC (including 7 cases of dislodgement of [RIJV]/left internal jugular vein [LIJV]-TCC and 3 cases of malfunction of RIJV/LIJV-TCC), and 10 cases with inadequate blood flow, difficult puncture, or failure of arteriovenous fistula (AVF)/arteriovenous graft (AVG). One case involved the placement of NTC in the right common femoral vein due to multiple failures in creating autologous AVF. General patient information is summarized in Table 1.

Inclusion criteria

History of NTC or TCC placement in RIJV, with no current TCC in the right jugular vein. Exhaustion of bilateral forearm vascular resources, preventing the establishment of autologous AVF or AVG. Preoperative ultrasound, computed tomography venography (CTV), or intraoperative DSA showing stenosis or occlusive lesions in the RIJV, the connection between the RIJV and innominate vein, stenosis of the right innominate vein, or occlusive lesions in the transition area between the right innominate vein and the superior vena cava.

Exclusion criteria

No history of catheter placement in the RIJV.

Replacement of TCC in situ in the RIJV or replacement of NTC with TCC. Duration of RIJV catheter placement not exceeding 1 week. Contrast agent allergy. Severe heart failure incompatible with surgery.

Coagulation disorders.

A total of 21 patients were enrolled in the study. Preoperative examinations, including neck vein ultrasound, chest CT scans, superior vena cava CTV, and intraoperative DSA, revealed stenotic lesions in 4 cases of RIJV, 5 cases of stenosis in the right innominate vein, 4 cases of stenosis in the connection between the RIJV and innominate vein, 1 case of stenosis in the right innominate vein and the superior vena cava, 1 case of bilateral innominate vein stenosis, and 1 case of stenosis in the transition area between the RIJV and right innominate vein, as well as left internal jugular vein stenosis. Occlusive lesions included 2 cases of RIJV occlusion, 2 cases of right innominate vein occlusion, and 1 case of occlusion in the transition area between the RIJV and right innominate vein (see Table 1).

Treatment methods

Patients were positioned in the supine position. After local anesthesia of the inferior, ultrasound guidance was used for caudal puncture of the RIJV, inserting a 6 F vascular sheath (RS*A60K10SQ, Terumo, 2 Chome-44-1 Hatagaya, Shibuya City, Tokyo 151 − 0072, Japan). The guidewire and catheter were advanced until reaching the site of stenosis or occlusive lesions. If the guidewire couldn’t smoothly pass through the stenosis or occlusive lesions to reach the inferior vena cava, ultrasound-guided puncture of the right common femoral vein was performed, and a 6 F vascular sheath (RS*A60K10SQ, Terumo) was inserted to establish a new access. Digital subtraction angiography (DSA) through the sheath or a single-curve catheter was used to visualize the location, length, and morphology of stenotic or occlusive lesions in the internal jugular vein, innominate vein, and superior vena cava.

For stenotic lesions, a 0.035 guidewire (RF*GA35153, Terumo, 44 − 1,2-Chome, Hatagaya, Shibuya-Ku, Tokyo 151 − 0072, Japan) or 0.018 guidewire (Inno Hydrowire SW-18-180-A-3-D, Suzhou, China) was used through the common femoral vein or RIJV access, along with a contrast-guided catheter, attempting to pass through the stenosis. With appropriate force, the guidewire was twisted and pushed through the stenotic segment until it smoothly progressed without resistance. A single-curve catheter was then advanced, and after confirming no contrast agent extravasation through catheter angiography, the catheter was withdrawn. A dilation balloon catheter (Conquest, Bard, USA or Gladiator, Boston Scientific, USA) was introduced along the guidewire and performed balloon angioplasty, gradually inflating from 4 to 20 atm in three steps, each lasting 30 s. Post-dilation angiography showed the opening of stenotic lesions, and a TCC catheter was smoothly placed through the RIJV, as shown in Fig. 1. After confirming no contrast agent extravasation, PTA was performed. Post-PTA angiography demonstrated unobstructed central veins, and a TCC catheter was then placed, as shown in Fig. 2. After successful TCC catheter placement, a 10 ml syringe was used to repeatedly test the withdrawal of blood from the TCC at the arteriovenous end, ensuring smooth and unobstructed flow, indicating successful catheter placement. Hemodialysis treatment was initiated on the same day or the next day, with a target blood flow rate of 220–260 ml/min. Post-discharge, maintenance hemodialysis treatment continued, and patients were followed up to monitor the hemodialysis blood flow, with the follow-up endpoint being December 2023 or in the event of kidney transplantation, conversion to AVF/AVG, conversion to peritoneal dialysis, or death.

Criteria for evaluating successful surgery

After percutaneous endovascular angioplasty, there is no contrast agent retention in the RIJV catheter insertion route. The TCC catheter is successfully inserted through the RIJV. A 10 ml syringe is used to repeatedly test the arterial and venous ends of the TCC catheter, which are smooth and without stagnation, indicating successful placement of the TCC catheter. Hemodialysis treatment is performed on the day of surgery or the next day, with a dialysis blood flow rate of 220-260 ml/min. After discharge, maintenance hemodialysis treatment continues. The dialysis blood flow rate of the TCC catheter is followed up by phone after surgery, still reaching 200-260 ml.

Result

Surgical outcomes

Twenty-one patients underwent successful surgeries, with 5 cases of chronic total occlusion (CTO) and 16 cases of CVS. In all cases, balloon assistance was used to open the occluded or narrow lesions. During PTA, vascular sheaths were concurrently inserted into the right common femoral vein and internal jugular vein, especially for the opening of occlusive lesions. Balloons of three sizes 6 × 40 mm, 8 × 40 mm, and 10 × 40 mm, were used during PTA. Only one case experienced acute bleeding with contrast agent extravasation at the intersection of the left and right innominate veins during sharp recanalization. No other severe complications, such as arrhythmia, vascular rupture, pneumothorax, mediastinal hematoma, or pericardial tamponade, occurred during the procedures. After PTA, TCC was routinely inserted, mainly using the US Covidien Tal Palindrome or Bard Hemosplit TCC catheters with lengths of 19 and 23 cm, as shown in Table 1. The treatment details of a typical patient are illustrated in Figs. 1 and 2.

Follow-up

All cases were followed up for an average of (16.86 ± 9.36) months. During the follow-up period, one patient died due to rectal cancer, one due to heart failure and cachexia, one due to cerebral hemorrhage, and one shifted to a left high-position AVF. Currently, all 17 patients with dialysis catheters can use them normally and undergo routine hemodialysis treatment. The initial average blood flow rate using the dialysis catheter postoperatively was (247.14 ± 11.46) ml/min. At 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively, the average blood flow rates were (244.29 ± 13.63) ml/min, (240.95 ± 11.36) ml/min, (235.33 ± 14.57)ml/min, respectively. Except for three patients who died and one who shifted to a high-position AVF, none of the remaining patients experienced malfunction of the TCC again during the follow-up period, as shown in Table 2.

Discussion

All 21 patients included in this study underwent the placement of TCC in the RIJV again. Only one case experienced sharp bleeding with contrast agent extravasation at the intersection of the left and right innominate veins during sharp recanalization. No other severe complications such as arrhythmia, vascular rupture, pneumothorax, mediastinal hematoma, or pericardial tamponade occurred in the remaining cases. Regular telephone follow-ups were conducted for 7 to 37 months, and currently, the dialysis catheters of the patients have blood flow rates mostly ranging between 200 and 260 ml/min, meeting their daily dialysis needs.

It is observed that PTA followed by the reinsertion of TCC into the RIJV is associated with a high success rate and satisfactory treatment outcomes, while also resulting in a relatively lower economic burden for dialysis patients during hospitalization. Therefore, reinserting TCC catheters via the RIJV with PTA assistance provides a new method for re-establishing dialysis access in patients with vascular exhaustion. In our study, 21 patients underwent the placement of TCC catheters in the RIJV after the successful dilation of narrowed or occluded central veins under PTA assistance. Subsequent complications may include dialysis catheter-related vascular stenosis or worsened occlusive lesions. However, the successfully placed TCC catheters offer patients the opportunity for central vein treatment in the future, including TCC in situ replacement, complete central vein patency restoration with covered stent placement, and re-establishment of AVF. Through continuous follow-ups and patient education, we have emphasized the importance of the reinserted TCC not only as the current dialysis access but also as a pathway for future treatment of central vein disorders. This collaborative effort between healthcare providers and patients aims to preserve this vital lifeline, ultimately prolonging the survival time of dialysis patients as much as possible.

The fundamental principles for establishing vascular access in maintenance hemodialysis patients include preferring autogenous AVF or AVG as the second option, while central venous catheter placement should be avoided as much as possible. However, depending on individual circumstances, it is advisable to select a reasonable dialysis vascular access14,15. Due to economic, cultural, and differences in medical technology levels, approximately 60% of first-time hemodialysis patients in China currently rely on central venous catheters16. Under the guidance of healthcare professionals, a significant portion of these patients shift to AVF or AVG upon entering maintenance hemodialysis, simultaneously removing RIJV NTC and TCC catheters. However, patients with a history of previous catheter insertion may develop vascular stenosis or occlusive lesions17. As the duration of dialysis increases, various factors contribute to functional obstruction of the dialysis access, and it becomes challenging to establish AVF or AVG again. Faced with the need for reinsertion of TCC catheters, clinical decisions may sometimes lead to the avoidance of RIJV placement due to a history of repeated catheter insertions, opting instead for left internal jugular vein or common femoral vein placement. The history of catheter placement in both left and RIJVs increases the likelihood of CVS or occlusion in dialysis patients. Once superior vena cava stenosis or occlusive lesions occur, it can have catastrophic consequences for dialysis patients, directly impacting their quality of life and long-term survival. Therefore, in clinical practice, it is crucial to minimize the occurrence of bilateral internal jugular vein catheter placements4,18. In our study, all selected dialysis patients had a history of repeated RIJV catheter insertions and subsequently underwent TCC reinsertion in the RIJV to avoid depletion of central venous resources.

Accurate placement of TCC and vascular complications

Accurate placement of the TCC is paramount, as incorrect positioning may lead to blood clots and induce central vein thrombosis19. The success of treatments involving a TCC heavily relies on the proper functioning of the catheter20 The RIJV, which forms a straight line with the right innominate vein and superior vena cava, is the preferred location for the insertion of hemodialysis catheters. However, repetitive catheter placements in the RIJV can lead to subsequent stenosis or occlusive lesions in the RIJV, right innominate vein, and superior vena cava21.

As our understanding of catheter-related vascular stenosis or occlusive lesions continues to deepen with advancements in dialysis catheter-related research, factors such as catheter dwell time, insertion frequency, the presence of AVF, and vascular calcification have been implicated in TCC-related central vein stenosis and occlusive lesions22. Pathological and anatomical studies reveal that the insertion of TCC into venous vessels causes endothelial cell damage, proliferation of smooth muscle cells and fibroblasts, and the formation of fibrin sheaths encapsulating the catheter. These fibrin sheaths extend continuously around the catheter and its tip, resulting in catheter dysfunction and central vein stenosis or occlusion23. Currently, surgical and intravascular techniques are employed to address stenosis or occlusion in the internal jugular vein, innominate vein, and superior vena cava. These techniques include open surgery to remove central vein thrombosis, patch formation, or interposition of synthetic grafts to relieve vascular stenosis or occlusive lesions. However, surgical interventions are more invasive. Interventional techniques, such as percutaneous intravascular angioplasty, stent placement, and central vein direct puncture, are recommended as the first choice for treating dialysis access-related central vein stenosis or occlusion, minimizing the need for open surgeries. Percutaneous intravascular angioplasty is favored because it is often performed under local anesthesia, resulting in minimal blood loss, shorter operation times, simplicity of operation, and higher success rates. In contrast, surgical treatment places higher demands on patients and operators, and there is an increased risk of complications. For the treatment of central vein stenosis, PTA is conducted with guide wire passage through the narrowed lesion under digital subtraction angiography (DSA) guidance. Following dilation with balloons of varying sizes (6–10 mm), stents are implanted to establish AVFs or TCC catheters are directly placed. This type of treatment is relatively straightforward. However, for occlusive central vein lesions, the obstruction must be opened first. Occlusive lesions caused by dialysis catheters differ from typical arterial and venous vessel obstructions. Catheter-induced occlusions in central veins are often CTO. The chronic occlusive lesion is a result of catheter-induced endothelial cell damage, proliferation of smooth muscle cells and fibroblasts, and fibrin sheath formation encapsulating the catheter. This continuous extension around the catheter leads to a chronic complete occlusive lesion in the central vein. Therefore, the success factors for opening this type of occlusive lesion are diverse, including the duration of occlusion, residual morphology of the occlusion, location of the occlusion, length of the lesion, degree of calcification, bridging vessels, vascular angulation and distortion, and the presence of local thrombus formation.

Management of central vein occlusions and technical considerations

In clinical practice, it has been observed that factors such as short occlusion duration, short lesion length, mild calcification, conical or mouse-tail-shaped residual occlusion ends, occlusion position behind vascular branches, small vascular angles, absence of twists, and no local thrombus presence are associated with higher success rates in opening occlusions. Conversely, long occlusion duration, long lesion length, severe calcification, absence of residual ends, occlusion position at vascular branching points, complete occlusion, large vascular angles, high distortion degree, and the presence of local thrombus make intervention treatments challenging. Therefore, preoperative central venous computed tomography (CTV) examination and intraoperative evaluation through multiple approaches, angles, contrast imaging, and guide wire markings are crucial for a detailed assessment of the lesion type. In the treatment process of vascular CTO, the selection of operation techniques and equipment is equally important. Instruments with strong pushability, good torque control, low resistance, and ease of passage through occlusive lesions should be chosen. The use of single-curve catheters is maximized to provide support for the guide wire to pass through the lesion, and techniques like using a “mother-child” coaxial dual-catheter technique can facilitate the opening of complex vascular stenosis or occlusions, resulting in higher long-term patency rates24,25.

In this study, due to the alignment of the RIJV, right innominate vein, and superior vena cava, most lesions could be traversed using catheter and guidewire techniques. If crossing failed, sharp recanalization was performed, placing a 4 F angiographic catheter at the distal end of the occluded segment to serve as a target for impinging the occlusion. Given the proximity of the RIJV, a 6 F vascular sheath (RSA60K10SQ, Terumo) was placed, and the inner cannula of the sheath was used for support. This cannula was positioned against the CTO lesion, and the posterior end of a 0.035 guidewire (RFGA35153, Terumo) was employed to impinge the occluded segment until successful passage was achieved13.

Various surgical techniques have been reported in the literature. Cui et al. performed sharp recanalization of the occluded superior vena cava and then placed a TCC. This approach requires multiple instruments and angles to check the position of the puncture needle to avoid damaging arteries or lung tissues26. Other scholars have reported the use of guide wire balloon trapping technique, placing a balloon at the proximal end of the severely narrowed vessel through the common femoral vein, and using a micro-puncture needle system (21G with 0.018 guide wire) to puncture the balloon. This allows the guide wire to be passed through the severely stenotic or occluded lesion27,28. During the procedure, it is crucial to maintain patience, gentle movements, and avoid violent operations. If treatment becomes challenging or complications arise, timely cessation of treatment is necessary to minimize the risk of severe complications such as vascular rupture or dissection. In cases where conventional methods fail to open the occluded central vein, advanced techniques, such as direct puncture of the superior vena cava, may be considered26.

In this study, one case of CTO involved vessel perforation during the procedure. Upon identifying the perforation, the authors quickly assessed its severity using imaging techniques to evaluate contrast extravasation. If active bleeding was present, balloon tamponade was performed by inflating a balloon distal to the perforation site to compress the vessel walls and control hemorrhage. A covered stent or standard stent was then deployed at the perforation site to seal the defect and restore vessel integrity. The patient was closely monitored for complications, and post-procedure care included fluid management and possible transfusions to ensure stability. These measures effectively managed the vessel perforation, ensuring patient safety and recovery29.

If the guidewire passes through the CTO lesion while the catheter does not, the medical team first employs a single-curve catheter. They may switch to a reinforced guidewire or establish a balloon-dilating guidewire. A balloon dilation catheter is then advanced along the guidewire, selecting an appropriate balloon size (typically 6–10 mm) based on the diameters of the vessels proximal and distal to the occlusion. After dilation, follow-up angiography confirms the recanalization of the lesion, followed by the placement of a TCC. The team typically establishes two operational channels: one using the guidewire and catheter in tandem to navigate stenosis, while the other may require “impingement” on the occluded segment. Central venous lesions are predominantly venous, and follow-up angiography is performed to check for contrast agent extravasation30.

The RIJV, right innominate vein, and superior vena cava are typically aligned, allowing most stenotic or occlusive lesions to be navigated using a catheter and guidewire. If passage is not achieved, techniques such as sharp recanalization, the mother-child sheath technique, and blunt impingement can be employed. Sharp recanalization is essential for recanalizing occlusions, often using septostomy needles, RUPS100, or Chiba needles, with a target placed distally. In patient NO12, the posterior end of a 0.035 guidewire was used to repeatedly impact the occluded segment. Although there was initial extravasation, hemostasis was quickly achieved, allowing continued attempts to breach the lesion, proving safer than direct sharp recanalization. Central venous lesions are primarily venous, characterized by localized hyperplasia and thickening, with arterial dissection being rare. Follow-up angiography is conducted to check for contrast agent extravasation after navigating through the lesion31.

Limitation

This study is retrospective in nature and includes a limited number of cases, accompanied by a relatively short follow-up duration. These factors present certain inherent limitations that must be acknowledged. The small sample size raises concerns regarding the statistical robustness of the conclusions drawn from the data. Specifically, insufficient statistical power may hinder the ability to detect meaningful differences or effects, potentially leading to inconclusive or misleading results. Therefore, it is imperative to address these limitations by expanding the sample size in future research. Additionally, extending the follow-up period will provide a more thorough understanding of the long-term effectiveness and safety of placing TCC in the right neck vessels under DSA-assisted balloon dilation. A larger, more diverse cohort would enhance the reliability of the findings and strengthen the overall conclusions. Furthermore, longer follow-up may yield insights into complications and the durability of the treatment effect over time. By addressing these issues, future studies can offer a more comprehensive evaluation of this important intervention and contribute valuable data to the existing literature on the management of vascular access in patients requiring TCC placement.

Conclusion

For maintenance hemodialysis patients with peripheral vascular exhaustion, a thorough assessment of central vein vascular resources is crucial when considering the reinsertion of a central vein TCC. It is essential to select an appropriate central vein for TCC placement, and clinicians should not easily abandon vessels with a history of central venous catheterization. Replacing TCC in the right neck vessels under PTA assistance is a safe and reliable technique that preserves central vein resources and provides a conduit for subsequent treatments of central vein disorders, such as stent placement. Moreover, follow-up results suggest that the newly inserted TCC catheter’s blood flow can meet daily dialysis requirements.
