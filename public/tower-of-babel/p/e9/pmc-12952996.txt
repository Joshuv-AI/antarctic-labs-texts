Prevalence of Therapeutic Inertia in the Management of Overweight and Obesity in Patients With a Diagnosis of Type 2 Diabetes Who Are Followed up by Primary Care Physicians
Authors: Pablo Aschner, Diana Henao‐Carrillo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionInformation on therapeutic inertia (TI) in weight loss in people with type 2 diabetes (T2D) is limited.ObjectiveTo describe the prevalence of TI in the management of overweight/obesity in people with T2D undergoing primary care program (PCP) follow‐up and weight changes according to the prescribed treatment.MethodsA retrospective longitudinal study was conducted in patients with T2D with body mass index (BMI) ≥ 25 kg/m2 followed by PCP. The following data were obtained from medical records at the first assessment, 6 and 12 months: anthropometric data, HbA1c, medications added according to their effect on weight, and specialist referrals (nutrition and metabolic and bariatric surgery).ResultsA total of 128 patients were included (age 65.5 ± 11.7 years, HbA1c 7.8 ± 1.7%, BMI 33.1 ± 5.7 kg/m2). The mean HbA1c at the end of follow‐up was 7.4% ± 1.4%. Twenty‐five percent achieved ≥ 5% weight loss after 12 months. The proportion of patients without weight‐loss interventions increased from 59% in the first assessment to 86% and 89% after 6 and 12 months of follow‐up. Patients who lost ≥ 5% of their weight were more likely to be referred to dietary counseling at program entry (37.5% vs. 23.4%) and at 6 months (18.8% vs. 6.4%) compared to patients who gained weight. In addition, the prescription of SGLT2 inhibitors/GLP1‐receptor agonist was higher in this group at 6 months (49.3% vs. 10.7%).ConclusionsAlthough there was a reduction in HbA1c, the TI for prescribing weight‐loss interventions was high. This suggests the importance of including weight loss as a therapeutic goal alongside lowering HbA1c in primary care.Keywords: obesity, therapeutic inertia, type 2 diabetes

Introduction

In people with type 2 diabetes (T2D) and obesity, excess dysfunctional adipose tissue is associated with inflammation and oxidative stress. This can worsen insulin resistance and promote the development of hypertension, dyslipidemia, and kidney disease [1]. Obesity has become a major health problem in Latin America, affecting nearly one‐third of the population [2]. When combined with T2D, it results in a high disease burden with a significant economic impact on health systems in the region [3, 4]. Therefore, in Colombia the Basic Health Plan includes assessment by a nutritionist, the use of medications such as SGLT2 inhibitors (SGLT2i), GLP1 receptor agonist (GLP1‐RA) and Orlistat in patients with a BMI ≥ 27 kg/m2 [5] and referral for metabolic and bariatric surgery in patients with a BMI ≥ 35 kg/m2 [6], as part of the comprehensive management of patients with T2D [7, 8].

After the United Kingdom Prospective Diabetes Study (UKPDS) was published, reducing HbA1c was considered the main treatment goal, which promoted the use of insulin and sulfonylureas in all care settings, including primary care [9, 10, 11]. However, multiple studies have shown that a 1% reduction in HbA1c levels, alongside a 1‐kg weight loss, significantly decreases the risk of complications and all‐cause mortality in people with diabetes [5]. In contrast, a similar reduction in HbA1c levels associated with weight gain has been linked to increased mortality [5]. Therefore, weight control should be a primary treatment goal alongside glycemic control [6, 7].

Therapeutic inertia in the treatment of obesity is defined as the delay or lack of initiation, intensification, or modification of treatment, whether pharmacological, behavioral, or surgical, despite the patient not reaching weight loss goals [12, 13, 14]. It is multifactorial and occurs at the provider, patient, and system levels, accounting for 50%, 30%, and 20% of therapeutic inertia causes, respectively [12, 13]. Also, it contributes to more than one‐third of people with T2D not having adequate glycemic control, and in the subgroup of patients with obesity, more than 50% have poor metabolic control [15]. However, there is a lack of unified criteria for evaluating it in clinical practice.

Diabetes and overweight/obesity are two conditions that primary care physicians play a key role in diagnosing and treating. However, although weight loss is a treatment goal in this population, information on therapeutic inertia in weight loss in patients diagnosed with T2D followed by a structured primary care group is scarce, and there are no parameters to define or stage it. The objective of this study was to describe the prevalence of therapeutic inertia in the management of overweight/obesity in patients diagnosed with T2D followed for 1 year by primary care physicians and to describe weight changes according to the prescribed treatment strategy.

Methods

This was a retrospective longitudinal study that included adults with T2D who were ≥ 35 years with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 and anthropometric data available from the time of admission through 12 months of follow‐up at the Cafam Primary Care Group in Bogotá, Colombia, between January 2010 and September 2023. Patients with type 1 diabetes, a history of metabolic and bariatric surgery prior to entering the primary care program, or conditions associated with weight loss (active neoplastic disease, chronic pancreatitis, HIV infection) were excluded from the study. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Pontificia Universidad Javeriana (FM‐CIE‐1321‐23). Data collection was approved by the medical director of Cafam.

The structured primary care program “VITAL” consists of a team of general practitioners who receive short training courses on chronic diseases, including T2D, through a virtual platform and must be certified every 2 years. Each group of physicians is supervised by a primary care specialist who evaluates the most complex cases. The request for this collaborative review is at the discretion of the primary care physician. Patients are referred to the program from a primary care consultation, a specialty consultation, or after hospitalization. Upon admission to the program (first assessment), a comprehensive assessment is performed, studies are ordered, a prescription is written based on the underlying pathology, a referral is made to the dietician or nurse for education on insulin administration according to medical criteria, and a quarterly follow‐up appointment is scheduled.

Baseline demographics, hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), and anthropometric data were obtained from the electronic medical records before patients were included in the primary care program. A calibrated electronic scale (Seca 813) was used to measure the weight of all patients at each visit. The addition of antidiabetic medications for metabolic control, some of which are associated with weight loss (SGLT2i, orlistat, GLP1‐RA), and referrals for nutrition or metabolic and bariatric surgery during the follow‐up period were recorded at each visit. HbA1c data were obtained from the ENTERPRISE platform. Information on HbA1c, anthropometric data, addition of medications, referral to nutrition and metabolic and bariatric surgery were measured at the first assessment and 6 and 12 months of follow‐up.

Patients were classified according to Body Mass Index (BMI) according to the World Health Organization (WHO) [5]. Therapeutic inertia in the treatment of overweight or obesity in people with T2D was defined as meeting one or more of the following five criteria at both the baseline and 6‐month assessments:

The failure rate of referring patients with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2, to nutrition services was calculated as follows: Number of patients referred to nutrition services/Total number of patients with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 × 100.

The failure rate of referring patients with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2, to nutrition services was calculated as follows: Number of patients referred to nutrition services/Total number of patients with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 × 100.

Failure to prescribe a GLP1‐RA receptor agonist or other anti‐obesity medication (with orlistat being the only available option) to patients with BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2. This was calculated as follows: number of patients prescribed anti‐obesity medications/total number of patients with a BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 × 100.

Failure to prescribe a GLP1‐RA receptor agonist or other anti‐obesity medication (with orlistat being the only available option) to patients with BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2. This was calculated as follows: number of patients prescribed anti‐obesity medications/total number of patients with a BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 × 100.

The failure to refer patients with diabetes and a BMI ≥ 35 kg/m2 for metabolic and bariatric surgery was calculated as follows: Number of patients referred for metabolic and bariatric surgery/Total number of patients with a BMI ≥ 35 kg/m2 × 100.

The failure to refer patients with diabetes and a BMI ≥ 35 kg/m2 for metabolic and bariatric surgery was calculated as follows: Number of patients referred for metabolic and bariatric surgery/Total number of patients with a BMI ≥ 35 kg/m2 × 100.

Failure to discontinue or replace sulfonylureas was assessed by the persistence of sulfonylurea formulations at 6 and 12 months of follow‐up and was calculated as follows: Number of patients who continued sulfonylureas/Number of patients treated with sulfonylureas × 100.

Unnecessary maintenance of insulin in patients who had a BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 and achieved an HbA1c ≤ 7.0%: Number of patients who continued insulin with HbA1c ≤ 7.0%/Number of patients treated with insulin with BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 × 100.

Unnecessary maintenance of insulin in patients who had a BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 and achieved an HbA1c ≤ 7.0%: Number of patients who continued insulin with HbA1c ≤ 7.0%/Number of patients treated with insulin with BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 × 100.

The sample size was calculated based on a prevalence of therapeutic inertia of 80% and a desired margin of error of ± 10%. A minimum of 232 consultations was estimated, assuming an average of two follow‐up medical consultations per subject over the semester. The recruitment target was 116 subjects.

For statistical analysis, each of the quantitative and qualitative variables was described using descriptive statistics. Quantitative variables were described using means and standard deviations or medians and interquartile ranges, as appropriate. Categorical variables were described as proportions. Variables were compared between the two groups identified by weight loss. Means were compared using the t‐student test, and proportions were compared using the chi‐squared test. The association of variables with therapeutic inertia was assessed using logistic regression. The statistical package STATA 16.0 was used for the analyses.

Results

Of those patients, 10 achieved a weight loss of at least 10%, and only one patient achieved a weight loss of > 20% during follow‐up. This only patient was a male under 45 years of age who was treated with nutritional assessment, use of SGLT2i upon entering the program, and GLP1‐RA at 6 months of follow‐up. The primary treatment for diabetes management prior to enrollment was metformin (71.5%) and insulin (39.2%). The SGLT2i was the most commonly prescribed medication in combination with metformin (16.2% and 6.2% at 6 and 12 months, respectively). Following entry into the primary care program, prescriptions for interventions to manage overweight/obesity remained modest (41%), exhibiting a subsequent decline during the 6‐ and 12‐month follow‐ups, reaching 14% and 11%, respectively. Treatment for T2D remained unchanged in 46% and 81.2% of cases during the 6‐ and 12‐month follow‐ups, respectively (Figure 1). Weight gain occurred in 50 patients at 6 months; of these, 22% had a weight gain of ≥ 5% and 21.3% had a weight gain of ≥ 10%. Weight gain persisted in 47 patients at 12 months; of these, 21.3% had a weight gain of ≥ 5% and 10.6% had a weight gain of ≥ 10%. The mean age of these patients was 66.1 ± 11.9 years, mean HbA1c 8.1% ± 2.1% and mean BMI 32.5 ± 5.1 kg/m2. During follow‐up, mean HbA1c was 7.6 ± 1.8% and 7.4 ± 1.2% at 3 and 6 months, respectively. After 6 months, prescriptions were higher for non‐weight‐loss drugs such as metformin (8.5%) and DPP4i (17%). Only, 23.4% of patients had been referred to a dietitian in the first assessment.

Discussion

The global burden of overweight and obesity in low‐ and middle‐income countries is well documented and quantified [16]. However, the worldwide prescription of weight control medications is low even in people with T2D [17]. Patients evaluated in this study achieved a clinically significant reduction in HbA1c levels after 1 year of follow‐up. However, only 25% of patients achieved a ≥ 5% weight loss in the same time frame. Furthermore, those patients who gained more than 5% of their body weight during the follow‐up period had higher HbA1c levels. Despite the cardiovascular, renal, and weight‐loss benefits of SGLT2i/GLP‐1RA and strategies to achieve weight loss (nutrition assessment and orlistat), primary care physicians did not prescribe them often.

Both diabetes and clinical inertia combined with obesity appear to be the perfect combination for the development and progression of cardiometabolic and renal complications of T2D in all age groups [6, 18, 19]. A study of people with T2D and obesity with HbA1c > 8.0% at 4 years of follow‐up found that 60% of patients did not have treatment intensification in the first year and 28% had persistent therapeutic inertia at 3 years [14]. In this study, the glycemia‐targeted changes were implemented since the initial assessment by the primary care physician; however, weight loss treatment prescription was low from the time of entry into the primary care program, decreasing further during the first year of follow‐up. This highlights the importance of promoting active weight loss treatment as a strategy for helping people with T2D achieve the recommended HbA1c target from their first primary care physician consultation.

Intensive lifestyle interventions consisting of dietary modification, increased physical activity, stress management and behavioral modification strategies such as problem solving and counseling have a significant impact on weight loss, cardiometabolic risk factor control and even T2D remission [20, 21]. In primary care, recommendations for specific dietary interventions, particularly dietician‐led carbohydrate restriction, are available and are associated with modest weight loss in the first 6 months [21]. In the first assessment of patients with significant weight loss, referral to dietary counseling was more frequent (37.5% vs. 23.4%) and at 6 months (18.8% vs. 6.4%) compared with patients who gained weight during follow‐up. However, in this study, only a third of patients were referred for nutritional assessment, even though it was covered by the Basic Health Plan.

Therapeutic inertia is common in the management of patients with T2D and obesity, and most available treatments have an impact on weight [22]. The population with significant weight loss ≥ 5% had a higher prescription of weight loss‐associated therapies such as SGLT2i/GLP1‐RA 6 months after program entry compared to the population that gained weight during follow‐up (49.3% vs. 10.7%), with no differences in HbA1c between the two groups, possibly related to the higher use of insulin/sulfonylureas at program entry in patients with significant weight gain. A study found that people with T2D who were treated with GLP1‐RA and participated in a weight loss program lost more weight [23]. This reinforces the importance of structured care in encouraging lifestyle changes and medication use.

The results of this study suggest that reducing HbA1c levels remains the primary objective in the primary care setting. Consequently, insulin prescriptions remain common; among patients with weight gain during follow‐up, half were on insulin; of these, only six had HbA1c > 9.0%. This is consistent with previous reports in the literature, which found that all insulin regimens were associated with a 30% increase in the likelihood of reaching the HbA1c target, as well as weight gain of more than 1 kg and an elevated risk of severe hypoglycemia [9] Although the benefits of weight loss in patients with T2D are clear, this study suggests the importance of emphasizing significant weight loss as a therapeutic goal in conjunction with HbA1c reduction. In addition, pharmacological therapies that contribute to weight gain should be avoided or minimized in patients with T2D and obesity according to current guidelines [24].

The findings of this study reflect the regional trend of a higher percentage of women with obesity and T2D. In many middle‐income countries in Latin America, obesity is more prevalent among women [2]. Although sociodemographic factors have been identified, hormonal changes throughout a woman’s life, such as menopause, pregnancy, and conditions like polycystic ovary syndrome, significantly contribute to higher rates of overweight and obesity [2]. Compared with national data from the ENSIN survey, this study found a much higher prevalence of obesity in women, possibly due to the fact that the average age of the sample is approximately 20 years older than most people seeking treatment for obesity and frequent insulin use.

At the time of publication, this is the first study to propose criteria and evaluate therapeutic inertia in weight management for patients with T2D and obesity who were part of a primary care program. It is also the first study to include the lack of de‐escalation of insulin and withdrawal of sulfonylurea therapy as part of the criteria. Strengths of this study include the sample size, where approximately 384 visits were analyzed (three assessments per individual) with a standardized follow‐up schedule that reflects usual clinical practice in a primary care group. Limitations of the study, given its retrospective nature, include factors such as lifestyle changes, referral to sports medicine, education received, risk or history of hypoglycemia, and other factors that may influence decisions to intensify treatment during medical care were not available for analysis. The average age of the sample was approximately 20 years older than that of most people seeking treatment for obesity; however, the main reason for entering the primary care program was for the treatment of diabetes. Similarly, inertia was only observed at the provider level, and other patient‐ and payer‐level components were not analyzed. In addition, these findings cannot be generalized given the great diversity of health care systems in Latin America.

Conclusion

Weight loss in patients with T2D and obesity promotes metabolic control and reduces complications. However, the therapeutic inertia to achieve weight loss was high and only a quarter of the subjects included in the study achieved significant weight loss. Prescribing insulin and antidiabetic drugs without significantly affecting weight loss was common. Therefore, a key goal in primary care populations should be to avoid or minimize the risk of weight gain by prioritizing interventions that promote weight loss. These findings suggest that metabolic control goals in primary care settings should include significant weight loss as one of the therapeutic goals.
