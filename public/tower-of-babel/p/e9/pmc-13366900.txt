Psychometric properties of the Night Eating Questionnaire (NEQ) in a clinical sample of Turkish adolescents
Authors: Mehmet Akif Akıncı, Bahadır Turan, Kübra Bayındır, Furkan Can Tuzcu, Esen Yıldırım Demirdöğen, Abdullah Bozkurt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to examine the psychometric properties of the Turkish version of the Night Eating Questionnaire (NEQ) in an adolescent clinical sample.MethodsThe sample consisted of 267 adolescents aged 13–17 years (64.8% girls; mean age = 15.23 ± 1.29 years) recruited from child and adolescent psychiatry outpatient clinics of two university hospitals in Türkiye. The NEQ, Eating Attitudes Test–26 (EAT-26), Revised Child Anxiety and Depression Scale – Child Version (RCADS-CV), and Sleep Quality Scale and Sleep Variables Questionnaire (SQS-SVQ) were administered. Exploratory factor analysis (EFA), confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), internal consistency, and convergent validity were examined.ResultsEFA identified a four-factor structure accounting for 62.39% of the total variance, consistent with the original factor solution. CFA supported the four-factor structure of the NEQ (CMIN/df = 1.606, RMSEA = 0.048, CFI = 0.964, TLI = 0.951), consisting of nocturnal ingestions, evening hyperphagia, morning anorexia, and mood/sleep disturbances. Internal consistency was acceptable for the total scale (Cronbach’s α = 0.73). The NEQ demonstrated meaningful convergent validity through significant positive correlations with sleep quality, anxiety, depression, and disordered eating attitudes. Adolescents in the overweight group reported significantly higher NEQ total scores, particularly on the evening hyperphagia and mood/sleep disturbances subscales. Girls showed higher scores on morning anorexia and mood/sleep disturbances compared with boys.ConclusionsThe Turkish NEQ demonstrated satisfactory psychometric properties in an adolescent clinical sample and appears to be a valid and reliable instrument for assessing night eating symptoms in Turkish adolescent clinical populations.

Introduction

Night eating syndrome (NES) is characterized by a delayed circadian pattern of food intake in which a substantial proportion of daily caloric consumption occurs during the evening or nighttime hours [1]. Within the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision (DSM-5-TR), NES is categorized under Other Specified Feeding or Eating Disorders (OSFED) [2]. The proposed research diagnostic criteria for NES include the consumption of at least 25% of daily food intake after the evening meal or the occurrence of at least two nocturnal ingestions per week, accompanied by awareness of these eating episodes. In addition, the presence of at least three associated features has been suggested, including morning anorexia, a strong urge to eat between dinner and sleep or during the night, insomnia, the belief that eating is necessary to initiate or return to sleep, and/or evening mood worsening [1]. Although the estimated prevalence of NES in the general population is around 1.5%, it appears to be substantially higher in psychiatric populations [3]. In addition, although NES can occur across the weight spectrum, the risk of NES has been reported to increase with higher body mass index (BMI) [4]. Assessing the prevalence and severity of NES has important public health implications because the syndrome is associated with metabolic conditions such as obesity and type 2 diabetes [5]. However, epidemiological research on NES remains relatively limited, particularly in adolescent populations. To date, only a small number of studies have examined the prevalence of NES in adolescent samples, with reported rates ranging from approximately 3.7% to 7.1% [6–8], and no epidemiological study has specifically investigated NES in the Turkish adolescent population. Given that maladaptive eating behaviors emerging during adolescence may be associated with adverse outcomes later in life [9], understanding night eating during this developmental period becomes especially relevant.

Although NES was initially described in adult populations, increasing evidence suggests that night eating symptoms also emerge during adolescence [10]. Adolescence is characterized by substantial biological and behavioral changes that may influence eating patterns, including a shift toward a delayed circadian rhythm, irregular meal timing, and increased evening wakefulness [11]. These changes, together with environmental factors such as increased evening screen use and altered sleep patterns, may predispose adolescents to night eating symptoms [12, 13]. Emerging research has also indicated that night eating symptoms in adolescents are associated with emotional difficulties, including anxiety and depressive symptoms, as well as problematic eating attitudes [14]. Therefore, identifying night eating symptoms during adolescence may be important for the early detection of disordered eating patterns and related psychological difficulties, and may also help recognize those at increased risk during a developmental period when vulnerability to eating disorders increases substantially [9].

Reliable and valid assessment tools would be useful for identifying night eating symptoms in both clinical and research settings. The Night Eating Questionnaire (NEQ), developed by Allison et al. [15], is the most widely used self-report instrument for assessing the severity of NES. The scale consists of 14 items designed to evaluate the core features of NES, including evening hyperphagia and nocturnal ingestions, as well as related symptoms such as morning anorexia, sleep disturbances, and mood changes. In the original study, the NEQ demonstrated acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.70), and factor analysis identified a four-factor structure comprising nocturnal ingestions, evening hyperphagia, morning anorexia, and mood/sleep disturbances. Internal consistency coefficients for the subscales ranged from 0.30 to 0.94, with the highest alpha observed for nocturnal ingestions, although several subscales showed Cronbach’s alpha values below 0.70. Variations in eating behaviors across cultural contexts and developmental stages emphasize the need for the cross-cultural adaptation and validation of assessment instruments [16]. Since its development, the NEQ has been translated and validated into several languages in adult populations [17, 18], supporting its cross-cultural applicability. Although the NEQ has been used in adolescent samples [14, 19], its psychometric properties have largely been examined in adults. In Türkiye, a study was conducted to assess the validity and reliability of the NEQ; however, this study was limited to the adult population [20]. To date, the psychometric properties of the NEQ have not been validated among Turkish adolescents. Given that night eating symptoms are also observed during adolescence, it is important to examine how well the NEQ performs in younger individuals in clinical samples.

The present study aimed to validate the Turkish version of the NEQ in an adolescent clinical sample. We hypothesized that the Turkish NEQ would: (1) demonstrate structural equivalence by replicating the original four-factor model (morning anorexia, evening hyperphagia, mood/sleep disturbances, and nocturnal ingestions), (2) exhibit robust psychometric integrity, including adequate internal consistency and construct validity, and (3) establish convergent validity through statistically significant associations with BMI percentiles, eating attitudes, sleep quality, and symptoms of anxiety and depression.

Method

Participants

Participants were recruited from child and adolescent psychiatry outpatient clinics of two university hospitals in Türkiye: Atatürk University and Karadeniz Technical University. Data collection was conducted between March 2024 and August 2024. Inclusion criteria were: (1) age between 13 and 17 years, and (2) presenting to the outpatient clinic during the study period. Exclusion criteria included: (1) a confirmed diagnosis of autism spectrum disorder or intellectual disability, (2) an active manic or psychotic episode at the time of assessment, and (3) a diagnosed endocrine or gastrointestinal system disease. Participants with other psychiatric diagnoses (including anxiety disorders, major depressive disorder, obsessive-compulsive disorder, conduct disorder, tic disorders, eating disorders, and other DSM-5 diagnoses) and somatic conditions (including allergy, asthma, acute rheumatic fever, familial Mediterranean fever, and epilepsy) were not excluded.

Procedure

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Atatürk University Faculty of Medicine (approval no: B.30.2.ATA.0.01.00/33; date: 21.02.2024). The study procedures were conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Permission to use the NEQ was obtained from the developers of the original scale prior to the initiation of the study. Adolescents who presented to the child and adolescent psychiatry outpatient clinics during the study period were invited to participate in the study. Written informed consent was obtained from both the adolescents and their parents before participation. Following consent, all participants underwent a face-to-face psychiatric interview conducted by a child and adolescent psychiatrist, based on DSM-5 diagnostic criteria, to establish psychiatric diagnoses. Participants then completed a set of self-report questionnaires during their outpatient visit.

Translation and cross-cultural adaptation

The translation and cultural adaptation of the NEQ were carried out using a forward–backward translation procedure. First, the original English version of the scale was independently translated into Turkish by two child and adolescent psychiatrists who were fluent in English. The two translations were then compared and synthesized into a single preliminary Turkish version. Subsequently, this Turkish version was translated back into English by an independent bilingual translator who was blinded to the original scale. The back-translated version was compared with the original English version to identify potential discrepancies and ensure conceptual equivalence. Subsequently, the preliminary Turkish version of the scale was administered to ten randomly selected adolescents to assess clarity and comprehensibility. Based on the feedback obtained, minor revisions were made to items that were considered unclear. Following this process, the research team reviewed all items and finalized the Turkish adolescent version of the NEQ, ensuring linguistic clarity and cultural appropriateness for adolescents.

Measures

Sociodemographic data form

A sociodemographic data form developed by the researchers was used to obtain descriptive and clinical information about the participants. This included age, gender, body mass index (BMI), family income, daily screen time, time of screen use, number of daily meals, meal skipping behavior, nutritional supplement use, and history of dieting. Body weight (kg) and height (m) of the participants were measured during the clinical assessment. BMI was calculated using the standard formula (kg/m²). BMI percentiles were then determined according to the age and sex of the participants using the World Health Organization (WHO) growth reference charts. Based on the recommended BMI percentile cut-off values for Turkish adolescents [21], participants were categorized as underweight (< 5th percentile), normal weight (5th–85th percentile), or overweight/obese (> 85th percentile).

Night Eating Questionnaire (NEQ)

The NEQ is a 14-item self-report instrument developed by Allison et al. [15] to assess the severity of NES. The original scale demonstrated solid psychometric properties with a reported Cronbach’s alpha of 0.70 and a four-factor structure: morning anorexia, evening hyperphagia, mood/sleep disturbances, and nocturnal ingestions. Items are rated on a five-point Likert-type scale with response options ranging from 0 (never/not at all) to 4 (always/very severe). In the scoring system of the questionnaire, items 1, 4, and 14 are reverse scored. Item 13 assesses awareness during nocturnal eating episodes and is used to differentiate night eating syndrome from sleep-related eating disorders; therefore, it is not included in the calculation of the total score. The total NEQ score ranges from 0 to 52, with higher scores indicating greater severity of night eating symptoms. A total score of 25 or higher indicates the presence of night eating symptoms and is considered the screening threshold for NES.

Eating attitudes test–26 (EAT-26)

The EAT-26 is a self-report measure developed by Garner and Garfinkel [22] to assess disordered eating attitudes and behaviors. The scale consists of 26 items and provides a total score ranging from 0 to 53, with higher scores indicating more disturbed eating attitudes. A total score of 20 or higher is considered indicative of abnormal eating behaviors and suggests an increased risk for eating disorders. The Turkish validity and reliability study of the EAT-26 was conducted by Ergüney-Okumuş and Sertel-Berk [23], which demonstrated high internal consistency and test–retest stability.

Revised Child Anxiety and Depression Scale–child version (RCADS-CV)

The RCADS-CV, developed by Chorpita et al. [24], is a self-report measure designed to assess symptoms of anxiety and depression in children and adolescents according to DSM-based diagnostic categories. The scale consists of 47 items rated on a 4-point Likert-type scale. The RCADS-CV includes several subscales assessing generalized anxiety disorder, social phobia, separation anxiety disorder, panic disorder, obsessive–compulsive disorder, and major depressive disorder. Higher scores indicate greater severity of anxiety and depressive symptoms. The Turkish validity and reliability study of the RCADS-CV was conducted by Görmez et al. [25], demonstrating satisfactory psychometric properties in clinical samples.

Sleep Quality Scale and Sleep Variables Questionnaire (SQS-SVQ)

The SQS-SVQ is a self-report instrument developed by Meijer and van den Wittenboer [26] to assess sleep characteristics. The scale consists of 15 items and evaluates several aspects of sleep behavior, including sleep quality, parental control, total sleep time, mid-point of sleep, and sleep efficiency. Items are rated using Likert-type response options, and higher scores indicate poorer sleep quality and greater sleep-related difficulties. The Turkish validity and reliability study of the SQS-SVQ was conducted by Önder et al. [27].

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 24.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were presented as means and standard deviations (mean ± SD), while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages (n, %). The normality of the distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Group differences in the NEQ scores according to gender were examined using independent samples t-tests. Differences across BMI percentile categories were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). When significant differences were detected across BMI groups, post hoc comparisons were conducted using the Bonferroni to determine the source of the group differences. Differences between categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test.

Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was conducted prior to confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) to explore the underlying factor structure of the Turkish NEQ. Principal components analysis with varimax rotation was applied. Components with eigenvalues greater than 1.0 were retained, and item loadings of ≥ 0.40 were considered indicative of meaningful factor association. Construct validity of the NEQ was examined using CFA. CFA was conducted using AMOS 24.0 software. Model fit was evaluated using several goodness-of-fit indices, including the Chi-Square Minimum/Degrees of Freedom (CMIN/df), the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), the Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR), the Comparative Fit Index (CFI), and the Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI). CMIN/df values lower than 2 were considered indicative of excellent model fit [28]. RMSEA and SRMR values below 0.05 were considered good fit [29], while CFI and TLI values greater than 0.95 were considered indicative of good fit [30].

Internal consistency reliability of the scale and its subscales was evaluated using Cronbach’s α coefficients, item-total correlations, and “alpha if item deleted” procedure. Criterion-related validity was assessed using correlation analyses between the NEQ total and subscale scores and related variables, including BMI, EAT-26, SQS-SVQ, and RCADS-CV. Depending on the distribution of the variables, Pearson’s or Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients were calculated. A two-tailed p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Sociodemographic and clinical findings

The study included 267 adolescents with a mean age of 15.23 years (SD = 1.29), of whom 173 (64.8%) were girls and 94 (35.2%) were boys. Of these, 172 (64.4%) were recruited from Atatürk University Hospital and 95 (35.6%) from Karadeniz Technical University Hospital. Based on the established cut-off score of 25 for the NEQ [15], the prevalence of clinically significant night eating symptoms was 17.2% (n = 46). Regarding BMI percentile categories, 44 participants (16.5%) were classified as underweight, 162 (60.7%) as normal weight, and 61 (22.8%) as overweight. Socioeconomic status was relatively balanced; most participants were from a middle-income background (35.2%). A majority of the sample exhibited a predominant nocturnal screen-use pattern (66.3%). In terms of dietary habits, 53.2% of the participants consumed three or four meals per day, meal-skipping behaviors were prevalent in more than half of the participants (56.6%). Regarding clinical characteristics, the most common psychiatric diagnoses were anxiety disorder (28.8%), attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) (25.8%), and major depressive disorder (12.0%). Among the participants with eating disorder diagnoses (n = 2, 0.7%), one was diagnosed with bulimia nervosa and one with avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder (ARFID) and none had a prior diagnosis of NES. A total of 12 participants (4.5%) had a comorbid somatic disease. The demographic and clinical characteristics of the study sample are presented in Table 1.

Exploratory factor analysis (EFA)

Prior to EFA, the suitability of the data for factor analysis was assessed. The Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin measure of sampling adequacy was 0.79, indicating good sampling adequacy, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was statistically significant (χ² (78) = 1091.96, p <.001), confirming that the correlation matrix was appropriate for factor analysis. An EFA was then conducted using principal components analysis with varimax rotation. Four components with eigenvalues greater than 1.0 were extracted (eigenvalues = 3.86, 1.77, 1.36, and 1.12, respectively), together accounting for 62.39% of the total variance. The first factor (Nocturnal Ingestions) comprised items 9, 10, 11, 12, and 14, with loadings ranging from 0.64 to 0.90. The second factor (Evening Hyperphagia) comprised items 3, 4, and 5, with loadings ranging from 0.55 to 0.80. The third factor (Morning Anorexia) comprised items 1 and 2, with loadings of 0.75 and 0.59, respectively. The fourth factor (Mood/Sleep Disturbances) comprised items 6, 7, and 8, with loadings ranging from 0.60 to 0.79. This four-factor structure is consistent with the original factor solution reported by Allison et al. [15]. Detailed factor loadings and item-total correlations are presented in Table 2.

Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA)

The structural validity of the Turkish NEQ was confirmed using CFA. The results supported the four-factor structure of the scale, consisting of morning anorexia, evening hyperphagia, mood/sleep disturbances, and nocturnal ingestions. The model demonstrated excellent fit indices (χ² = 91.570, df = 57, p <.001, CMIN/df = 1.606, RMSEA = 0.048, SRMR = 0.031, CFI = 0.964, TLI = 0.951). Standardized factor loadings of the items ranged from 0.31 to 0.91, indicating acceptable item representation within their respective factors (see Table 4). Reliability

Internal consistency of the Turkish NEQ was evaluated using Cronbach’s α coefficients. Descriptive statistics, Cronbach’s α coefficients, item-total correlations, and “alpha if item deleted” values of the NEQ are provided in Table 4. The mean total NEQ score was 17.73 (SD = 7.13). The mean scores of the subscales were 4.94 (SD = 1.76) for morning anorexia, 5.49 (SD = 2.14) for evening hyperphagia, 5.78 (SD = 3.21) for mood/sleep disturbances, and 3.21 (SD = 4.17) for nocturnal ingestions. The Cronbach’s alpha value for the total scale was 0.73. For the subscales, Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were 0.31 for morning anorexia, 0.49 for evening hyperphagia, 0.62 for mood/sleep disturbances, and 0.86 for nocturnal ingestions. These results indicate good internal consistency for the nocturnal ingestions subscale and acceptable internal consistency for the total scale and mood/sleep disturbances subscale, whereas the evening hyperphagia and morning anorexia subscales demonstrated lower internal consistency. The “alpha if item deleted” analysis indicated that removing any item did not substantially improve the internal consistency of the scale. Item analysis showed that item–total correlation coefficients ranged between 0.22 and 0.65 across the scale items, indicating that the items generally contributed adequately to the measurement of the construct.

Convergent validity

The convergent validity of the Turkish NEQ was evaluated by examining its associations with BMI, EAT-26, SQS-SVQ, and RCADS-CV anxiety and depression scores. The NEQ total score showed significant positive correlations with its subscales, including morning anorexia (r =.515, p <.001), evening hyperphagia (r =.537, p <.001), mood/sleep disturbances (r =.675, p <.001), and nocturnal ingestions (r =.790, p <.001). In addition, the NEQ total score was positively correlated with BMI (r =.191, p <.001), EAT-26 (r =.256, p <.001), SQS-SVQ (r =.533, p <.001), RCADS-CV anxiety (r =.459, p <.001), and RCADS-CV depression (r =.486, p <.001). Evening hyperphagia showed a small but significant positive correlation with BMI, whereas morning anorexia, mood/sleep disturbances, and nocturnal ingestions were not associated with BMI. Mood/sleep disturbances and nocturnal ingestions demonstrated significant positive correlations with EAT-26 scores, whereas morning anorexia and evening hyperphagia were not related to eating attitudes. All NEQ subscales were positively associated with SQS-SVQ scores, with the strongest relationship observed for the mood/sleep disturbances subscale. Mood/sleep disturbances, nocturnal ingestions, and morning anorexia showed significant positive correlations with both RCADS-CV anxiety and depression scores, with mood/sleep disturbances demonstrating the strongest associations. Evening hyperphagia was not associated with anxiety symptoms and showed only a weak positive correlation with depression symptoms. Convergent validity results of the NEQ are presented in Table 5.

Discussion

In this study, the psychometric properties of the Turkish version of the NEQ were examined in a clinical sample of adolescents aged 13–17 years. In addition, the associations between NEQ scores and BMI, eating attitudes, sleep quality, anxiety, and depression symptoms were investigated. The findings indicated that the Turkish NEQ demonstrated a satisfactory four-factor structure, acceptable internal consistency, and meaningful convergent validity with related psychological and behavioral measures, suggesting that it is a psychometrically sound instrument for assessing night eating symptoms in Turkish adolescent clinical populations. To our knowledge, this is the first study to validate the NEQ in a Turkish adolescent clinical sample, addressing an important gap in the literature, as the existing Turkish validation study was conducted exclusively in adults.

One of the key findings of the present study is that the four-factor structure of the NEQ was supported in this adolescent clinical sample. Prior to CFA, EFA using principal components analysis with varimax rotation yielded a four-factor solution consistent with the original factor structure, together accounting for 62.39% of the total variance. The convergence of EFA and CFA results across both exploratory and confirmatory approaches strengthens confidence in the structural validity of the Turkish NEQ. CFA demonstrated a satisfactory model fit, replicating the factor structure originally reported by Allison et al. [15], which includes nocturnal ingestions, evening hyperphagia, morning anorexia, and mood/sleep disturbances. The replication of the four-factor structure across cultural and developmental contexts supports the multidimensional conceptualization of NES and indicates the cross-cultural applicability of the NEQ. Moreover, the confirmation of this structure in the current adolescent clinical sample is consistent with validation studies conducted in other cultural contexts [17, 18], suggesting that the underlying factor structure of the NEQ remains stable across populations and developmental groups. Taken together, these results provide evidence that the NEQ can be reliably used to assess night eating symptoms in adolescent clinical populations.

Another important finding of the present study concerns the internal consistency of the NEQ. The Turkish NEQ demonstrated acceptable reliability in the present study (Cronbach’s α = 0.73), which is comparable to the original development study (α = 0.70) [15]. Consistent with this, the Turkish validation study conducted in adults reported a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.69 for the total NEQ score [20]. Similar reliability estimates have also been reported in adolescent samples; for example, a Brazilian adaptation study reported a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.73 for the total scale [31]. These findings indicate that the internal consistency of the NEQ total score is relatively stable across different cultural contexts and age groups. Notably, whereas the nocturnal ingestions subscale demonstrated the highest internal consistency in both the present study and the original validation by Allison et al. [15], the subscale with the lowest internal consistency differed across the two samples. In the original study, the mood/sleep disturbances subscale yielded the lowest alpha coefficient (α = 0.30), whereas in the present study, this subscale demonstrated acceptable consistency (α = 0.62), and it was instead the morning anorexia subscale that showed the poorest reliability (α = 0.31). Similar variability in the reliability of NEQ subscales has also been reported in other cross-cultural adaptations [17, 18]. This divergence may reflect meaningful developmental and contextual differences between adult and adolescent samples. In adolescence, mood disturbances and sleep difficulties are closely intertwined with normative biological changes, including a shift toward a delayed circadian rhythm, as well as environmental demands such as early school start times and increased evening social engagement [12]. These factors may render the co-occurrence of mood and sleep symptoms more consistent and homogeneous among adolescents, thereby enhancing the internal coherence of the mood/sleep disturbances subscale in this age group. Conversely, morning anorexia, while a core feature of NES in adults, may be a more heterogeneous construct in adolescents, as reduced morning appetite can arise from a variety of developmentally normative factors unrelated to night eating, including habitual breakfast skipping, sleep deprivation, and irregular meal timing [32]. Overall, these findings suggest that the psychometric performance of NEQ subscales may vary depending on developmental stage and sample characteristics.

The Turkish NEQ total score demonstrated significant positive correlations with all four subscales, supporting the internal coherence of the scale as a unified measure of night eating symptom severity. The strongest association with the total score was observed with the nocturnal ingestions’ subscale, this pattern is conceptually consistent with the clinical features of night eating syndrome, in which nocturnal ingestions represent a central behavioral component of the disorder [1]. This finding suggests that the total NEQ score is most strongly driven by the core behavioral dimension of the syndrome, namely the occurrence of eating episodes during the night, while still capturing related dimensions such as mood and sleep disturbances, evening hyperphagia, and morning anorexia.

The Turkish NEQ demonstrated robust convergent validity through its significant associations with disordered eating attitudes, sleep quality, and both anxiety and depression symptoms, with the strongest association observed with sleep quality. These findings are broadly consistent with prior research linking night eating to disordered eating, sleep disturbances, and emotional difficulties in adolescent samples [14, 19]. At the subscale level, the mood/sleep disturbances subscale demonstrated the strongest associations across all four external variables, with particularly robust correlations with anxiety and depression symptoms. This pattern suggests that the mood/sleep disturbances dimension of night eating may be especially sensitive to emotional dysregulation in adolescents, and supports the conceptual overlap between night eating, affective symptoms, and sleep difficulties during this developmental period. These findings further strengthen the convergent validity of the Turkish NEQ and highlight the mood/sleep disturbances subscale as a clinically meaningful indicator of psychological distress in adolescent night eating presentations.

Comparisons of NEQ scores across BMI percentile categories revealed that adolescents in the overweight group scored significantly higher than those in the underweight and normal weight groups on the NEQ total score, as well as on the evening hyperphagia, and mood/sleep disturbances subscales, while no significant differences were observed for morning anorexia or nocturnal ingestions. Previous studies have reported that the severity of NES in clinical populations tends to increase with body weight and that individuals with obesity are substantially more likely to meet criteria for NES than those of normal weight [33]. Consistent with this literature, our findings suggest that excess weight in adolescence is associated with a broader pattern of night eating symptomatology, particularly characterized by increased evening food consumption and greater mood and sleep disturbances. The specific elevation of the evening hyperphagia subscale in the overweight group is noteworthy, as it supports the proposed mechanism whereby a disproportionate shift of caloric intake toward the evening hours may contribute to positive energy balance and weight gain over time [34]. In contrast, the absence of group differences in nocturnal ingestions suggests that excessive evening caloric intake, rather than waking to eat during the night, may represent the more prominent behavioral pathway linking night eating symptoms with increased body weight in this clinical sample. The concurrent elevation of mood/sleep disturbances scores in this group further indicates that overweight adolescents may be particularly vulnerable to the emotional and sleep-related dimensions of night eating, consistent with prior research linking excess weight to sleep difficulties and emotional dysregulation in adolescent populations [35]. Furthermore, the elevated mood and sleep disturbances in the overweight group align with the hypothesis that metabolic dysregulation and affective distress often co-occur in NES [36], potentially creating a vicious cycle where poor sleep and negative affect trigger compensatory overeating during the night. These results reinforce the clinical utility of the NEQ in identifying weight-related eating patterns and suggest that weight management interventions for adolescents should specifically screen for and address evening hyperphagia.

Gender differences in NEQ scores were also examined. Girls scored significantly higher than boys on the morning anorexia and mood/sleep disturbances subscales, with small-to-moderate effect sizes, whereas no significant differences were observed for the total NEQ score, evening hyperphagia, or nocturnal ingestions. These findings are consistent with the literature indicating that female adolescents often exhibit higher rates of internalizing symptoms [37], subjective sleep complaints, and emotional eating patterns during the mid-to-late adolescence [38]. The moderate effect size observed in the mood/sleep dimension suggests that the affective component of NES may be more prominent in girls, potentially reflecting a greater susceptibility to the emotional dysregulation that frequently accompanies circadian disruptions. Interestingly, the absence of gender differences in evening hyperphagia and nocturnal ingestions suggests that the core behavioral manifestations of night eating are relatively uniform across genders in this clinical sample. This pattern implies that while the primary eating behaviors of NES do not vary by sex, the subjective experience of morning anorexia and psychological distress may be more pronounced in female adolescents.

Several limitations of the present study warrant consideration. First, the sample was recruited exclusively from child and adolescent psychiatry outpatient clinics of two university hospitals in Türkiye, which limits the generalizability of the findings to other regions of the country as well as to community-based adolescent populations. Second, although all participants underwent a face-to-face DSM-5-based psychiatric interview, NES diagnoses were not formally established through a structured clinical interview, as no validated diagnostic interview for NES currently exists for adolescent populations. Consequently, the presence of NES was inferred from NEQ scores rather than established through a formal clinical assessment, which may affect the accuracy of prevalence estimates. Third, the cross-sectional design of the study precludes causal inferences regarding the observed associations between night eating symptoms and related psychological and behavioral variables. Fourth, the internal consistency of the morning anorexia and evening hyperphagia subscales was relatively low, which may limit the precision of subscale-level interpretations. Fifth, the sample included a higher proportion of girls than boys, which may limit the representativeness of the gender-based comparisons. Sixth, the appropriateness of the established NEQ cut-off score of ≥ 25 for Turkish adolescent clinical populations has not been empirically validated, and future research should examine optimal cut-off values for this specific age group. Finally, ROC analysis could not be conducted in the present study, as formal NES diagnoses were not established through diagnostic interviews. Consequently, the discriminatory capacity of the NEQ and the optimal cut-off score for Turkish adolescent clinical populations could not be determined. Future studies incorporating diagnostic interviews for NES are needed to enable cut-off validation and ROC analyses in this population.

Conclusion

The present study provides evidence supporting the validity and reliability of the Turkish version of the NEQ in an adolescent clinical sample. The four-factor structure of the scale was confirmed, and the total score demonstrated acceptable internal consistency along with meaningful convergent validity with theoretically related constructs, including sleep quality, emotional symptoms, disordered eating attitudes, and BMI. The findings also showed that overweight adolescents reported greater night eating symptom severity, particularly in the domains of evening hyperphagia and mood/sleep disturbances, while girls exhibited more pronounced morning anorexia and mood-related symptoms compared with boys. Overall, these results indicate that the Turkish NEQ is a reliable and clinically useful instrument for assessing night eating symptoms in adolescent clinical populations. The relatively high estimated prevalence of clinically significant night eating symptoms observed in this sample further highlights the potential value of routine screening for night eating behaviors in child and adolescent psychiatry settings. Future research should further examine the performance of the scale in community samples and explore its utility in longitudinal and diagnostic studies of NES during adolescence.
