ANKRD49 promotes immune evasion in lung adenocarcinoma by activating the MEF2A/PD‑L1 axis
Authors: Tian-qi Sun, Shuo Wu, Jing Qiao, Gang Liang, Chao-feng Liu, Hui-yu Jiang, Kai-yuan Liu, Hai-long Wang, Min Pang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Non‑small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), particularly lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), exhibits a high rate of primary resistance to immune checkpoint inhibitors, underscoring the need to decipher the molecular mechanisms of immune evasion. Here, we identify ANKRD49 as a critical regulator of PD‑L1 expression and immunosuppression in LUAD. Immunohistochemical analysis of clinical LUAD specimens revealed that high ANKRD49 expression correlates with reduced CD4+ and CD8+ T cell infiltration, increased FOXP3+ regulatory T cells, and elevated PD‑L1 levels. Functional experiments demonstrated that ANKRD49 overexpression in LUAD cells suppresses T cell cytotoxicity, promotes T cell apoptosis, and impairs granzyme B and IFN‑γ production in vitro. Mechanistically, ANKRD49 directly binds the transcription factor MEF2A via its second ankyrin repeat domain, upregulates MEF2A expression, and promotes its transcriptional activity at two specific sites within the PD‑L1 promoter. Chromatin immunoprecipitation and luciferase reporter assays confirmed that MEF2A directly activates PD‑L1 transcription in an ANKRD49‑dependent manner. In subcutaneous and metastatic mouse models, ANKRD49 overexpression accelerates tumor growth and metastasis, while PD‑L1 blockade reverses these effects and restores antitumor immunity. Collectively, our findings establish the ANKRD49–MEF2A–PD‑L1 axis as a novel driver of immune evasion in LUAD, and identify ANKRD49 ankyrin repeat domain 2 as a potential therapeutic target for overcoming immunotherapy resistance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13062-026-00904-2.

Introduction

Lung cancer stands as the most commonly diagnosed malignancy and the foremost cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [1]. Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for roughly 80–85% of all lung cancer cases, with adenocarcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma as its primary histological subtypes [2]. In recent years, the therapeutic landscape for NSCLC has witnessed a profound shift, fueled by progress in molecularly targeted agents and immunotherapies [3, 4]. Among these, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) targeting pathways such as programmed cell death protein 1 / programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-1/PD-L1) have emerged as a mainstay of clinical management [5, 6]. Nevertheless, the overall response rate to current immunotherapies stays constrained, with objective responses in solid tumors typically hovering around 15–20% [7]. This highlights an urgent necessity to elucidate the complex regulatory networks orchestrating immune checkpoint expression, since deeper insight is vital not only to unravel NSCLC pathogenesis but also to pinpoint novel therapeutic targets capable of enhancing the efficacy of existing immunotherapies.

Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) represents the predominant subtype of NSCLC, comprising roughly 40–50% of all lung cancer cases globally [8]. It features distinct pathological characteristics, such as glandular differentiation, mucin production, and a frequent peripheral origin [9]. Clinically, LUAD is frequently diagnosed at advanced stages owing to its indolent early progression and pronounced metastatic propensity, thereby imposing a markedly greater mortality burden than other NSCLC subtypes. Despite therapeutic strides, treatment responses in LUAD – especially to immune checkpoint inhibitors – stay suboptimal [10]. This restricted efficacy underscores the intricacy of its immune landscape and accentuates the pressing need to explore the underlying mechanisms of immune escape specific to LUAD.

A pivotal determinant of LUAD progression and therapeutic resistance resides in the tumor microenvironment (TME) [11]. Elements including immune cell infiltration, local inflammatory responses, and dynamic tumor-stromal interactions are progressively acknowledged as critical in dictating prognosis and immunotherapy outcomes [12–14]. Given the expanding clinical application of anti-PD-1/PD-L1 antibodies – encompassing unresectable locally advanced and perioperative contexts – the need to comprehend and manipulate the TME grows increasingly urgent [15, 16]. Therefore, advancing our comprehension of LUAD-specific immune evasion mechanisms is indispensable for devising more effective therapeutic strategies.

The ankyrin repeat domain 49 (ANKRD49) gene, situated on chromosome 11 (11q21), encodes a member of the ankyrin repeat domain (ANKRD)-containing protein family [17]. Owing to their structural adaptability, ANKRD domains facilitate versatile protein-protein interactions and rank among the most prevalent interaction modules in humans, participating in a broad spectrum of biological processes [18]. Our prior work demonstrated that ANKRD49 is highly expressed in NSCLC tissues, where it drives tumor cell epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), invasion, and migration through activation of the PKNOX1/TGF-β1/SMAD, p38/ATF-2, and JNK-ATF2/c-Jun-MMP-2/9 signaling pathways [19–21], thereby positioning it as a potentially crucial molecule in LUAD pathogenesis and a promising therapeutic target.

Myocyte enhancer factor 2 A (MEF2A) is a member of the MADS-box superfamily of eukaryotic transcription factors and has been implicated in the tumorigenesis and prognosis of multiple human cancers, including pancreatic cancer, colorectal cancer (CRC), hepatocellular carcinoma, and gastric cancer [22–24]. High MEF2A expression is associated with poor overall survival and relapse‑free survival in pancreatic adenocarcinoma [24]. Mechanistically, MEF2A transcriptionally upregulates ZEB2 and CTNNB1 to promote EMT in CRC [23]. Furthermore, MEF2A binds to the PD‑L1 promoter to enhance PD‑L1 transcription, which in turn stabilizes SOX12 mRNA and reduces cetuximab sensitivity in CRC [25]. Although several transcription factors (e.g., IRF, STAT3, NF‑κB, and MYC) are known regulators of PD‑L1 expression [26–29], the role of MEF2A in PD‑L1‑mediated immune evasion remains largely unexplored, particularly in LUAD. Notably, bioinformatic analysis using RNA‑seq data from the TCGA‑LUAD/LUSC cohort and the GEPIA2 platform revealed that MEF2 family members are associated with immune cell receptor function and regulation of immunoglobulin complexes, and MEF2A itself has been proposed as a potential diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic target in NSCLC [30].

Given that ANKRD49 promotes EMT in NSCLC and MEF2A promotes EMT in CRC, along with the established transcriptional regulation of PD‑L1 by MEF2A, we hypothesized that ANKRD49 may modulate PD‑L1 expression via MEF2A in LUAD. Therefore, the present study aims to investigate the role of the ANKRD49/MEF2A/PD‑L1 axis in LUAD immune evasion. Using a series of in vitro and in vivo functional and mechanistic assays, we demonstrate that ANKRD49 contributes to LUAD progression and modulates tumor‑associated immune cell functions, at least in part, through MEF2A‑dependent PD‑L1 regulation. Our findings not only expand the mechanistic understanding of LUAD pathogenesis but also reveal novel potential therapeutic targets.

Materials and methods

Human tissue samples and ethics statement

Commercially acquired LUAD tissue microarrays (TMAs) containing 89 human LUAD samples were obtained from YiBeiRuibiotek Inc. (Shanghai, China). The clinicopathological characteristics of the patient cohort are summarized in a previous report [19]. This study was carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Ethics Committee of the First Hospital, Shanxi Medical University (Approval No. KYYJ-2023-198). Written informed consent was secured from all participants or their legal representatives/attending clinicians prior to tissue collection.

Cell culture and generation of stable cell lines

Human LUAD cell lines (A549, H2228, PC-9, and H1975), HEK 293T cells, and mouse LUAD cell lines (LLC, M109, LA795) were sourced from Fenghui Biotechnology (China). These cells were cultured as follows: A549 cells in McCoy’s 5 A medium (Cat# PYG0025); 293T, M109, and LA795 cells in DMEM (Cat# PYG0073, Boster Biological Technology, China); and H2228, PC-9, H1975, and LLC cells in RPMI 1640 medium (Cat# BCM017, Bio-Channel, China). All media were supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (Cat# SA301.03 H, Cellmax, China), and cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. Human bronchial epithelial BEAS-2B cells and their corresponding complete culture medium were purchased from iCell Bioscience (Cat# iCell-0001, Shanghai, China).

Stable ANKRD49-overexpressing (ANKRD49-OE) and ANKRD49-knockdown (ANKRD49-KD) cell lines were generated via lentiviral transduction. Stable ANKRD49-OE or vector control A549 cells were constructed following previously published protocols [21]. For knockdown, H2228 cells were transduced with lentivirus expressing ANKRD49 shRNA (LV3-ANKRD49-KD) or empty vector (LV3; GenePharma, Shanghai, China) at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10, generating ANKRD49-KD H2228 and vector H2228 cells, respectively. For overexpression in mouse cells, LLC cells were transduced with lentivirus encoding mouse ANKRD49 (LV17(EF-1a/Luciferase17&Puro-ANKRD49) or empty vector (LV17; GenePharma, Shanghai, China) at an MOI of 200, yielding ANKRD49-OE LLC and vector LLC cells. After transduction, cells were incubated for > 48 h and subsequently selected with 2 µg/mL puromycin to establish stable polyclonal populations. The resulting cells were maintained under standard culture conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂). Successful construction of stable cell lines was confirmed by Western blot and quantitative real‑time PCR.

Co-culture experiments

Peripheral venous blood from healthy volunteers was collected into heparinized tubes. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated by Ficoll-Paque density gradient centrifugation. The isolated PBMCs were counted and activated by adding 25 µL/mL ImmunoCult™ Human CD3/CD28/CD2 T Cell Activator (Cat# 10970, STEMCELL Technologies, Canada) and 0.2 µL/mL recombinant human IL-2 (Cat# PIL201, Cell-regen, China) to expand T cells.

For co-culture assays, ANKRD49-OE or vector A549 cells were replenished with fresh medium and then co-incubated with activated cytokine-induced killer (CIK) cells at an effector-to-target ratio of 6:1 (CIK cells : A549 cells) in 12-well plate for 48 h. Following incubation, plates were centrifuged at 250 × g for 10 min, and the supernatants were collected for subsequent analyses.

LDH release assay

Cytotoxicity was evaluated by measuring lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release in the co-culture supernatants using a commercial kit (Cat# A020-2-2, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The specific cytotoxicity percentage was calculated as: [(Experimental LDH release – Spontaneous LDH release) / (Maximum LDH release – Spontaneous LDH release)] × 100%.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted using the RNAsimple Total RNA Kit (Cat# DP419, TIANGEN, China). cDNA was synthesized with the Fast Two‑Step RT&qPCR Kit (Cat# SRQ-01, SEVEN BIOTECH, China). mRNA expression levels of ANKRD49, PD‑L1, CD24, CD112, PD‑L2, and MEF2A were quantified by qPCR using the same kit on a QuantStudio 3 real‑time PCR system, with all reactions performed in triplicate. The thermocycling protocol comprised an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 30 s. To verify amplification specificity, a melting curve analysis was performed by gradually raising the temperature from 60 °C to 95 °C at a rate of 0.5 °C/s. β‑Actin served as the endogenous control. Relative mRNA expression was calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Cytoplasmic and nuclear protein extraction

Cytoplasmic and nuclear proteins were extracted from A549 or H2228 cells using the Subcellular Proteome Extraction Kit (Cat# AR0106, Boster Biological Technology, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Western blot analysis

Cells were lysed in SDS lysis buffer (50 mM Tris‑HCl [pH 6.8], 10% glycerol, 2% SDS) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Total protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay kit (Cat# PA115, TIANGEN, China). Equal amounts of protein (25 µg per lane) were resolved by 10% SDS‑PAGE and subsequently transferred onto PVDF membranes (0.22 μm pore size) via standard wet transfer. After blocking with 5% skimmed milk, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the indicated primary antibodies, followed by incubation with HRP‑labeled anti‑mouse or anti‑rabbit secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. The primary antibodies used were as follows: ANKRD49 (Cat# 25034-1-AP, 1:1000, Proteintech, USA), PD‑L1 (Cat# A1645, 1:1000), GAPDH (Cat# A19056, 1:20,000), MEF2A (Cat# A12059, 1:1000, ABclonal, China), Flag‑tag (Cat# CPA9001, 1:3000), β‑tubulin (Cat# A12289/CPA9126, 1:5000, Cohesion Biosciences, UK), and Myc‑tag (Cat# R1208-1, 1:3000, Huabio, China). Protein bands were visualized using an ECL detection system (Cat# MA0186, Meilunbio), and band intensities were quantified with ImageJ software, normalized to GAPDH or tubulin.

Animal studies

All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Shanxi Medical University (Approval No. SYDL2025037) and performed in compliance with institutional and national ethical guidelines for animal research. Four‑week‑old female C57BL/6 mice (n = 5 per group) were obtained from Beijing HFK Bioscience Co., Ltd. Mice were randomly divided into three groups: vector, ANKRD49‑OE, and ANKRD49‑OE + PD‑L1.

For the subcutaneous xenograft model, 1 × 10⁶ vector‑LLC or ANKRD49‑OE LLC cells resuspended in 100 µL of serum‑free medium were injected subcutaneously into the inguinal region of the hind limb of each mouse. On days 17, 20, 23, and 26 post‑inoculation, five mice bearing ANKRD49‑OE LLC tumors received intratumoral injections of a PD‑L1 monoclonal antibody diluted in normal saline (Cat# S0B0593, Clone No. 10 F.9G2, STARTER, China) at 2 mg/kg body weight. Tumor growth was regularly monitored by caliper measurements, and tumor volume was calculated as V = 0.5 × length × width². On day 26 post‑inoculation, mice were euthanized by CO₂ inhalation followed by cervical dislocation. Blood was collected via the orbital route, spleen tissues were harvested for splenic lymphocyte isolation, and subcutaneous tumors were excised for immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis.

For the lung metastasis assay, 1 × 10⁶ vector‑LLC or ANKRD49‑OE LLC cells resuspended in 100 µL of serum‑free medium were injected via the caudal vein. Two weeks later, five mice inoculated with ANKRD49‑OE LLC cells received intratumoral PD‑L1 monoclonal antibody (2 mg/kg) on days 3, 6, 9, and 12. At the endpoint, mice were euthanized similarly; blood and spleen tissues were collected as described, and lungs were harvested for metastatic nodule counting and IHC analysis. Liver tissues were also collected to evaluate metastatic nodules.

All harvested subcutaneous tumors and lung tissues (n = 5 per group for all tissues, except tumor tissues in the subcutaneous xenograft vector group, where n = 3) were fixed in 4% neutral buffered formalin for 24–48 h, then embedded in paraffin and sectioned at 4 μm thickness for subsequent histological examination.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Immunohistochemical staining was conducted on deparaffinized sections of human LUDA tissue microarrays, mouse subcutaneous tumors, and mouse lung tissues. Primary antibodies used were: ANKRD49 (1:100), MEF2A (1:100), PD‑L1 (1:100), CD4 (Cat# A26036PM, 1:100), CD8 (Cat# A23305PM, 1:100), and Foxp3 (Cat# A27744, 1:50, ABclonal, China). Detection was performed using the appropriate Mouse/Rabbit IgG‑SABC Ready‑to‑use Immunohistochemical Kit (Cat# SA1020, Boster Biological Technology, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin. Protein expression levels were quantified using the H‑score method: H‑score = (percentage of unstained tumor cells × 0) + (weakly stained × 1) + (moderately stained × 2) + (strongly stained × 3) [20]. The antibodies were sourced from the same vendors as those used for Western blot.

Mouse peripheral blood lymphocyte isolation

Mouse whole blood (approximately 500–1000 µL) was collected via retro‑orbital venous plexus puncture or cardiac puncture into an EDTA‑K2‑containing anticoagulant tube. Blood was diluted with an equal volume of PBS. An equal volume of mouse lymphocyte separation medium was carefully added to a sterile centrifuge tube, and the diluted blood was gently layered onto the separation medium. After centrifugation (800 × g, 20 min, room temperature, minimal acceleration, no brake), the white buffy coat layer (containing lymphocytes) was carefully aspirated and transferred to a new tube. Cells were washed with five volumes of PBS and centrifuged (400 × g, 10 min). The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in 5 mL of red blood cell lysis buffer (Cat# R1010, Solarbio, China) and incubated on ice for 3–5 min to lyse erythrocytes. Lysis was stopped by adding 10 mL PBS, followed by centrifugation (400 × g, 5 min).

Mouse splenic lymphocyte isolation

Mice were euthanized, and spleens were aseptically removed and placed in PBS containing 2% fetal bovine serum. Each spleen was gently mashed using a syringe plunger on a 70 μm cell strainer, and the strainer was rinsed with PBS. The cell suspension was collected and centrifuged (400 × g, 5 min). The pellet was resuspended in 5 mL of red blood cell lysis buffer and incubated on ice for 3–5 min. Lysis was terminated by adding 10 mL PBS, followed by centrifugation (400 × g, 5 min). If the pellet still appeared red, the lysis step was repeated once.

Flow cytometry

For surface PD‑L1 staining, cells were washed twice with cold PBS, resuspended, and incubated with 1 µg/mL ABflo® 488 Rabbit anti‑Human PD‑L1/CD274 mAb (Cat# A22304, ABclonal, China) for 1 h at 4 °C. After washing with PBS, samples were analyzed on a BD FACSCelesta flow cytometer. Data were processed using FlowJo v10 software.

Human PBMCs were co‑cultured with vector or ANKRD49‑OE A549 cells for 48 h. Subsequently, vector and ANKRD49‑OE A549 cells were collected to assess apoptosis using the Annexin V‑FITC/PI apoptosis detection kit (KGA1101, KeyGEN BioTECH, China) via flow cytometry, following the manufacturer’s instructions. For immune phenotyping, human PBMCs were stained with the following antibodies: PE anti‑human CD3 (Cat# 130-113-701), FITC anti‑human CD4 (Cat# 130-114-722), FITC anti‑human CD8 (Cat# 130-110-815), APC anti‑human CD25 (Cat# 130-113-846), PE anti‑human Foxp3 (Cat# 130-125-857, 1:50 dilution, Miltenyi, Germany), PE anti‑human IFN‑γ (Cat# E-AB-F1196D, Elabscience, China, 5 µL/mL), and PE anti‑human Granzyme B (Cat# 396406, BioLegend, USA, 5 µL/mL). In a separate set, ANKRD49‑OE A549 cells co‑cultured with PBMCs were also treated with 18 nM BMS‑202 (a PD‑L1 inhibitor, Cat# B276435, Aladdin, China) diluted in DMSO for 48 h, then stained with the above antibodies.

In our study, we adopted a hierarchical gating strategy to analyze the various T‑cell subpopulations in human peripheral blood. Briefly, cells were first gated on lymphocytes based on forward scatter (FSC) and side scatter (SSC), followed by exclusion of doublets using FSC‑area versus FSC‑height, and selection of viable cells using a fixable viability dye. Within the live, single‑cell population, total T lymphocytes were identified as CD3+ cells. From this gate, the percentages of CD3+CD4+ and CD3+CD8+ T cells were determined. For regulatory T‑cell analysis, the CD3+CD4+ population was further examined for CD25 and Foxp3 expression; since Foxp3 is an intranuclear transcription factor, cells were fixed and permeabilized with a Foxp3/transcription factor staining buffer kit, and the positive gate was set using a fluorescence minus one (FMO) control. To evaluate cytotoxic T‑cell function, CD3+CD8+ cells were assessed for intracellular IFN‑γ and Granzyme B production. For this, cells were stimulated with PMA/ionomycin in the presence of brefeldin A for 4–6 h, followed by fixation, permeabilization, and staining with anti‑IFN‑γ and anti‑Granzyme B antibodies; FMO or isotype controls were used to define the positivity thresholds. All data were acquired on a BD FACSCelesta cytometer, and data were processed with FlowJo v10.

Purified mouse peripheral blood lymphocytes and splenic lymphocytes were stained with: FITC anti‑mouse CD3 (Cat# E-AB-F1013C), Elab Fluor® Violet 450 anti‑mouse CD4 (Cat# E-AB-F1097Q), PerCP/Cyanine5.5 anti‑mouse CD8a (Cat# E-AB-F1104UJ), APC anti‑mouse CD25 (Cat# E-AB-F1102UE), PE anti‑mouse Foxp3 (Cat# E-AB-F1238D), and APC anti‑mouse IFN‑γ (Cat# E-AB-F1101E, Elabscience, China, 5 µL/mL). Flow cytometry analysis and data processing were performed as above.

Cycloheximide (CHX) chase assay

For the CHX chase assay, cells were treated with CHX (Cat# HY-12320, MCE, USA) at a final concentration of 20 µg/mL dissolved in ddH₂O. At indicated time points (0, 6, 12, 24, 48 h) post‑treatment, cells were harvested and lysed in RIPA buffer (Cat# P0013B, Beyotime, China). Protein samples were subsequently analyzed by Western blot.

Luciferase reporter assay

The pGL3‑basic‑Luc reporter plasmid (Cat# BioVector105817-1) was kindly provided by Prof. Wei Song (Peking Union Medical College). The PD‑L1 promoter sequence was amplified by PCR, and truncated versions carrying deletions of either − 1122 to − 1113 or − 568 to − 559, or both, were generated via overlap PCR. These fragments were cloned into the pGL3‑basic vector, yielding pGL3‑basic‑PD‑L1‑promoter (wild‑type, − 2000 to + 50), pGL3‑basic‑PD‑L1‑promoter‑Mut1 (Δ − 1122 to − 1113), pGL3‑basic‑PD‑L1‑promoter‑Mut2 (Δ − 568 to − 559), and pGL3‑basic‑PD‑L1‑promoter‑Mut1 + Mut2 (Δ − 1122 to − 1113 and Δ − 568 to − 559). The coding sequence (CDS) of human MEF2A was inserted into the pcDNA3.1‑3×Myc‑C vector (Cat# ZT911, Hunan Fenghui Biotechnology, China), generating pcDNA3.1‑3×Myc‑MEF2A.

For luciferase reporter assays, HEK 293T cells were transiently co‑transfected with 0.5 µg of reporter plasmid (wild‑type/mutant PD‑L1 promoter or empty pGL3‑basic), 0.25 µg of pcDNA3.1‑3×Myc‑MEF2A or empty vector, and 0.05 µg of pRL‑TK using LipoFiter 3.0 transfection reagent (Cat# RLF3-1000, HANBIO, China). After 48 h, cells were lysed and analyzed with the Dual‑Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (Cat# RG027, Beyotime, China). Firefly luciferase activity was normalized to renilla luminescence. Data represent three independent experiments (mean ± SD). All cloning primers – including those for PD‑L1 promoter amplification, deletion mutant construction, and PD‑L1 CDS cloning – are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay

ChIP was performed using a commercial kit (Cat# P2078, Beyotime, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Vector A549 or ANKRD49‑OE A549 cells transfected with pcDNA3.1‑3×Myc‑MEF2A for 48 h were fixed with 1% formaldehyde in PBS to crosslink chromatin. Cell lysates were prepared and digested with micrococcal nuclease to obtain chromatin fragments of approximately 150–900 bp. After pre‑clearing, immunoprecipitation was conducted overnight at 4 °C using rabbit anti‑Myc antibody (1:100; positive IP) or normal rabbit IgG (negative control, Cat# AC005, ABclonal, China). Immune complexes were captured with Protein A/G magnetic beads for 1 h at 4 °C, followed by elution and reversal of crosslinks by incubation at 65 °C for 2 h. DNA was purified via proteinase K digestion and collected using a DNA clean‑up column. To detect MEF2A binding to the PD‑L1 promoter region, quantitative real‑time PCR was carried out using primers listed in Supplementary Table S1, with input DNA (1% of total lysate) serving as the internal control.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay

ANKRD49 truncated versions lacking ankyrin repeat domain 1, domain 2, domain 3, domain 4, or the C‑terminus were generated via overlap PCR. These fragments were cloned into the p3×Flag‑CMV‑14 vector (Cat# E4901, Sigma, USA), producing p3×Flag‑ANKRD49 ΔDomain 1, ΔDomain 2, ΔDomain 3, ΔDomain 4, and ΔC‑terminal. The N‑terminal region and ankyrin repeat domain 2 of ANKRD49 were separately amplified by PCR and inserted into the same vector, yielding p3×Flag‑ANKRD49 N‑terminal and p3×Flag‑ANKRD49 Domain 2.

HEK 293T cells were co‑transfected with p3×Flag‑ANKRD49 or the various truncation mutants together with pcDNA3.1‑3×Myc‑MEF2A for 48 h. Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (Cat# P0040, Beyotime, China) and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was subjected to protein quantification by BCA assay. For each immunoprecipitation reaction, 500 µg of total protein was incubated overnight at 4 °C with gentle rotation, together with 2 µL of rabbit anti‑Flag antibody, rabbit anti‑Myc antibody, or control rabbit IgG. Then, 20 µL of Protein A/G PLUS‑Agarose beads was added, and the mixture was incubated for an additional 4 h at 4 °C with rotation. After incubation, the beads were washed three times with ice‑cold IP wash buffer. Bound proteins were eluted, separated by SDS‑PAGE, and analyzed by immunoblotting using the indicated primary antibodies.

Immunofluorescence

The pDsRed2‑N1‑ANKRD49 plasmid was constructed by inserting the human ANKRD49 CDS into the pDsRed2‑N1 vector (Cat# 632406, Clontech, USA). Similarly, the pEGFP‑N1‑MEF2A plasmid was generated by inserting the human MEF2A CDS into the pEGFP‑N1 vector (Cat# 6085-1, Clontech, USA). For colocalization analysis, HEK 293T cells were co‑transfected with 1.5 µg of pDsRed2‑N1‑ANKRD49 and 1.5 µg of pEGFP‑N1‑MEF2A using LipoFiter 3.0 transfection reagent for 48 h. Live cells were imaged without fixation or staining using an inverted fluorescence microscope (TS2-S-SM, Nikon, Japan). GFP was excited at 488 nm, with emission collected through a 505–530 nm filter; DsRed2 was excited at 563 nm and detected using a 585–620 nm filter. Overlaying the red and green channel signals allowed assessment of protein colocalization.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), unless otherwise indicated. Western blot band intensities were analyzed using ImageJ software, and flow cytometry data were processed with FlowJo software. Statistical analyses and graph generation were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0. Differences between two groups were compared using the independent t‑test, while comparisons among multiple groups were conducted using one‑way ANOVA. A P‑value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Correlations between ANKRD49 and CD4, CD8, FOXP3, MEF2A, or PD‑L1, as well as between MEF2A and PD‑L1, were assessed by Pearson correlation analysis.

Results

ANKRD49 levels correlate with an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment in LUAD tissues

To evaluate the clinical relevance of ANKRD49 in tumor immune regulation, we performed IHC staining on a LUAD tissue microarray (Fig. S1). Tissues displaying high ANKRD49 expression exhibited a distinct immunosuppressive profile: the proportion of FOXP3+ regulatory T cells (Tregs) was markedly elevated, whereas the infiltration of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells was substantially reduced. Conversely, tissues with low ANKRD49 expression showed a modest decline in FOXP3+ Tregs but a increase in CD4+ and CD8+ T cell infiltration. Correlation analyses further revealed a positive association between ANKRD49 and FOXP3+ Tregs, and negative correlations with CD4+ and CD8+ T cells (Fig. 1A–C). Additionally, six paired tumor and adjacent normal tissues were assessed for ANKRD49 protein levels by Western blot, which were significantly higher in the tumor tissues than in their normal counterparts (Fig. 1D).

ANKRD49 impairs T cell function in vitro

To investigate the functional role of ANKRD49 in tumor immune evasion, we established a co‑culture system using human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (hPBMCs) together with vector‑ or ANKRD49‑OE A549 cells (Fig. S3C). T cell‑mediated cytotoxicity was assessed by measuring lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release. Compared with vector controls, ANKRD49‑OE A549 cells exhibited significantly reduced susceptibility to T cell killing at an effector‑to‑target ratio of 6:1 (Fig. 2A).

Flow cytometric analysis of the co‑culture system revealed that ANKRD49‑OE A549 cells induced substantial alterations in immune cell composition. Specifically, we observed a higher rate of T cell apoptosis (5.89% vs. 3.84%; Fig. 2B and C) and an increased proportion of Tregs, along with concomitant decreases in both CD4+ and CD8+ T cell populations (Fig. 2D). ANKRD49 elevates PD-L1 expression in LUAD cells

Overexpression of immune checkpoint molecules within the tumor microenvironment is a well‑established mechanism of immune evasion and a major therapeutic target [31]. Immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy has revolutionized treatment for multiple advanced cancers, including LUAD, by significantly improving patient survival [32]. Our above findings suggested that ANKRD49 impairs T cell function and may therefore be involved in immune evasion. To explore which immune checkpoint molecules might mediate this regulation, we profiled the mRNA expression of 12 clinically relevant checkpoints (PD‑L1, PD‑L2, CD155, CD47, TSPO, CD112, MHC‑I, DKK1, CD24, CD39, CD73, GD2) in ANKRD49‑OE A549 cells. RT‑qPCR analysis revealed that ANKRD49 overexpression transcriptionally upregulated several checkpoints, including PD‑L1, PD‑L2, CD24 and CD112. These expression patterns were further validated in ANKRD49‑knockdown H2228 cells, where ANKRD49 silencing decreased the mRNA levels of these molecules (Fig. 3A and S4A). Subsequent protein analysis in ANKRD49‑OE A549 cells and ANKRD49‑KD H2228 cells confirmed a consistent regulatory trend for PD‑L1, CD24, and CD112, with the exception of PD‑L2 (Fig. 3B and S4B).

Given that PD‑L1 acts as a potent immunosuppressive factor in various cancers [33–35], we selected PD‑L1 for further mechanistic investigation. Flow cytometry demonstrated that ANKRD49 overexpression significantly increased cell‑surface PD‑L1 protein levels, whereas ANKRD49 knockdown obviously decreased them (Fig. 3C). IHC analysis of LUAD tissue microarrays showed that tissues with high ANKRD49 expression also exhibited high PD‑L1 levels, and the two correlated positively (Fig. 1A–C). Furthermore, Western blot of six pairs of fresh LUAD tumors and matched adjacent normal tissues showed that PD‑L1 protein levels were significantly elevated in tumors with high ANKRD49 expression compared to normal tissues, which expressed low levels of ANKRD49 (Fig. 1D).

Collectively, these results establish that ANKRD49 positively regulates PD‑L1 expression in LUAD tissues and cells, supporting the hypothesis that ANKRD49 promotes immune evasion at least in part through the PD‑1/PD‑L1 axis.

MEF2A serves as a transcriptional mediator of ANKRD49 induced PD-L1 upregulation

To elucidate the mechanism underlying ANKRD49‑mediated PD‑L1 upregulation, we performed bioinformatic screening for transcription factors potentially regulating the PD‑L1 promoter using the JASPAR database (https://jaspar.elixir.no). This analysis identified two high‑confidence binding sites for MEF2A within the PD‑L1 promoter region (positions − 1122 to − 1113 and − 568 to − 559), suggesting MEF2A as a candidate transcriptional regulator (Fig. 3D and E). In agreement with this prediction, analysis of the GEPIA database (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn) revealed a significant positive correlation between ANKRD49 and MEF2A expression, as well as between MEF2A and PD-L1 expression, in LUAD clinical samples (Fig. 3F).

We therefore hypothesized that MEF2A mediates the regulatory effect of ANKRD49 on PD‑L1. Western blot and real‑time PCR assays demonstrated that ANKRD49 overexpression increased, whereas its knockdown decreased, MEF2A protein and mRNA levels in both A549 and H2228 cells (Fig. 4A and B). To directly assess whether ANKRD49 regulates MEF2A at the post‑transcriptional level, we first performed an mRNA decay experiment using the transcription inhibitor actinomycin D (5 µg/mL, Cat# HY-17559, MCE). As shown in the Fig. 4C, actinomycin D treatment revealed that ANKRD49 overexpression did not significantly alter the decay rate of MEF2A mRNA compared to the vector control. This indicates that ANKRD49 does not stabilize MEF2A mRNA. Consequently, the observed increase in MEF2A mRNA levels is likely attributable to enhanced transcription rather than reduced decay. We next examined the effect of ANKRD49 on MEF2A protein turnover using cycloheximide (CHX) chase assays. The results showed a modest deceleration of MEF2A protein degradation upon ANKRD49 overexpression (Fig. 4D and Fig. S5A). However, given the concomitant increase in MEF2A mRNA levels, the predominant mechanism by which ANKRD49 upregulates MEF2A appears to operate at the transcriptional level. Whether ANKRD49 contributes to MEF2A protein stability, and to what extent, requires further investigation using approaches such as pulse‑chase or ubiquitination assays. As a transcription factor, MEF2A must enter the nucleus to function. Subcellular fractionation followed by Western blot analysis showed that ANKRD49 overexpression increased MEF2A levels in both the cytoplasmic and nuclear fractions, with a more pronounced increase in the nuclear fraction (Fig. 4E). To functionally validate this regulatory axis, we transfected MEF2A‑targeting siRNA into ANKRD49‑OE A549 cells. Knockdown of MEF2A significantly attenuated ANKRD49‑induced PD‑L1 upregulation at the protein level (Fig. 4F and Fig. S5B).

Additionally, IHC analysis of LUAD tissue microarrays showed that tissues with high ANKRD49 expression also exhibited high MEF2A levels, with positive correlations both between ANKRD49 and MEF2A and between MEF2A and PD‑L1 (Fig. 1A–C). In six fresh LUAD tumor tissues with high ANKRD49 and PD‑L1 expression, MEF2A levels were also elevated compared to matched adjacent normal tissues with low expression of both proteins (Fig. 1D). Notably, analysis of the GEPIA database revealed weak positive correlations between ANKRD49 and MEF2A (r = 0.16) and between MEF2A and PD‑L1 (r = 0.11) at the mRNA level (Fig. 3F), in contrast to the strong protein‑level correlations observed in our tissue microarray (Fig. 1C, r > 0.5). This discrepancy may be attributed to post‑transcriptional regulation (mRNA vs. protein levels), differences in sample sources and sample sizes (GEPIA database vs. our tissue microarray), and context‑dependent regulation in LUAD. Future studies with larger sample sizes and multi‑omics approaches are warranted to further validate our findings.

ChIP‑qPCR analysis showed specific enrichment of both regions in Myc‑MEF2A pull‑downs from ANKRD49‑OE cells compared to controls (Fig. 5A and B), confirming that MEF2A directly binds these sites and that ANKRD49 strengthens this interaction.

To further identify the functional MEF2A binding sites, luciferase assays were performed in HEK 293T cells co‑transfected with p3×Flag‑ANKRD49, pcDNA3.1‑3×Myc‑MEF2A, and either the wild‑type PD‑L1 promoter (WT) or the mutant constructs. In contrast, deletion of the − 1122 to − 1113 site (Mut 1) or the − 568 to − 559 site (Mut 2), or both (Mut 1 + Mut 2), markedly reduced luciferase activity, identifying these regions as critical for MEF2A‑dependent PD‑L1 transcriptional activation (Fig. 5C). Moreover, the enhancing effect of ANKRD49 on MEF2A transcriptional activity exhibited dose dependence (Fig. 5D).

To further uncover how ANKRD49 regulates MEF2A, co‑immunoprecipitation (Co‑IP) assays in 293T cells co‑transfected with Flag‑ANKRD49 and Myc‑MEF2A confirmed a direct interaction between the two proteins (Fig. 6A). Immunofluorescence microscopy revealed the co‑localization of ANKRD49 and MEF2A in both the cytoplasm and the nucleus of HEK 293T cells (Fig. 6B). Given that ANKRD49 contains four ankyrin repeat domains – structural motifs known to mediate protein‑protein interactions in oncogenesis [36] – we systematically mapped the interaction interface. We constructed ANKRD49 expression plasmids with deletions of individual domains (N‑terminal, ankyrin domain 1, domain 2, domain 3, domain 4, and C‑terminal). A schematic diagram of the ANKRD49 truncation mutants is shown in Fig. S6A, S6B. These constructs, along with full‑length ANKRD49, were co‑transfected with Myc‑MEF2A into 293T cells. Co‑IP assays identified ankyrin domain 2 as essential for binding to MEF2A (Fig. 6C). Based on this finding, we generated a plasmid expressing only ankyrin domain 2. Functionally, deletion of ankyrin domain 2 completely abrogated the ANKRD49‑induced upregulation of MEF2A and PD‑L1 at both protein and mRNA levels, whereas overexpression of this domain alone partially restored their expression in A549 cells (Fig. 6D and Fig. S7). These results collectively demonstrate that the specific interaction between ANKRD49’s ankyrin domain 2 and MEF2A is necessary but not sufficient for full PD‑L1 upregulation, although it contributes partially to the restoration of MEF2A and PD‑L1 levels.

Together, these data indicate that ANKRD49 binds MEF2A via its ankyrin domain 2, and that this interaction enhances MEF2A occupancy at the − 1122 to − 1113 and − 568 to − 559 regions of the PD‑L1 promoter, thereby amplifying its transcriptional activity. ANKRD49 overexpression attenuates T cell immunity and promotes tumor growth in a subcutaneous xenograft model

To functionally validate the role of ANKRD49 in PD‑L1‑mediated immune evasion, we established subcutaneous xenograft models in immunocompetent C57BL/6 mice. We first examined ANKRD49 expression in three murine LUAD cell lines (LLC, M109 and LA795). Western blot and RT‑qPCR analyses revealed that ANKRD49 was expressed at the lowest level in LLC cells and the highest in M109 cells (Fig. S8A, S8B). Accordingly, we generated LLC cells stably overexpressing ANKRD49 (ANKRD49‑OE) for subsequent in vivo studies (Fig. S8C, S8D). Additionally, ANKRD49 overexpression led to elevated protein levels of PD‑L1 and MEF2A in LLC cells (Fig. S8C).

In the subcutaneous tumor model, mice engrafted with ANKRD49‑OE LLC cells were randomly assigned to receive intraperitoneal injections of either saline or anti‑PD‑L1 monoclonal antibody every three days (Fig. S8E). ANKRD49 overexpression significantly accelerated subcutaneous tumor growth compared to controls (in which two mice showed complete tumor regression by the end of the experiment), whereas anti‑PD‑L1 treatment effectively reversed this pro‑tumor effect (Fig. 7A and B and Fig. S8F ). Flow cytometric analysis of peripheral blood and splenic immune cells revealed that ANKRD49 overexpression markedly increased the proportion of regulatory T cells (Tregs), while reducing the proportions of CD4+/CD3+ and CD8+/CD3+ T cells, and impaired cytotoxic T cell activity (as indicated by a decreased proportion of IFN‑γ+ T cells). Importantly, these immunosuppressive alterations were substantially reversed upon PD‑L1 blockade (Fig. 7C and Fig. S9).

Importantly, PD‑L1 blockade substantially reversed these immunosuppressive alterations (Fig. 9B and S11). These findings demonstrate that ANKRD49 overexpression suppresses systemic antitumor immunity, while PD‑L1 inhibitor treatment reverses this effect.

Collectively, these in vivo findings demonstrate that ANKRD49 activates the transcription factor MEF2A, which transcriptionally upregulates PD‑L1. These results establish ANKRD49 as a critical modulator of the tumor immune microenvironment and further validate the PD‑1/PD‑L1 axis as a key downstream effector of ANKRD49‑driven immune evasion.

Discussion

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounting for approximately 85% of all cases [1, 37]. Given the pressing need for effective and safe therapeutic strategies, immunotherapy – particularly immune checkpoint blockade (ICB)—has emerged as a first-line treatment for advanced NSCLC [38–40]. By reactivating the host immune system to recognize and eliminate tumor cells, ICB has achieved notable clinical success in a subset of lung adenocarcinoma patients [41, 42]. Nevertheless, over half of LUAD patients still fail to derive durable benefit from current immunotherapies, highlighting a critical clinical challenge [43]. Elucidating the key molecular regulators and underlying mechanisms of tumor immune escape therefore holds substantial scientific and translational value, with the potential to uncover novel therapeutic targets and enhance the efficacy of existing treatments. Our previous work demonstrated that ANKRD49 promotes invasion and migration in LUAD cells [19, 21], prompting us to investigate whether this molecule also contributes to immune evasion – the central question of the present study.

The tumor microenvironment (TME) constitutes a dynamic ecosystem comprising tumor cells, stromal components, immune cells, and endothelial cells [44]. Its pivotal role in cancer initiation, progression, metastasis, and therapeutic response is now firmly established [45, 46]. Among the hallmark features of the TME, immunosuppression is particularly prominent [47, 48]. Immune evasion – the process by which tumors circumvent immune surveillance – is widely recognized as a fundamental driver of treatment failure. Within this context, immunosuppressive subsets such as CD4+CD25+FOXP3+ regulatory T (Treg) cells, myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), and tumor-associated macrophages promote an immunosuppressive pre-metastatic niche, whereas Th1-polarized CD4+ and CD8+ T cells exert opposing, tumor-suppressive functions [49, 50].

To explore whether ANKRD49 shapes the LUAD immune microenvironment, we first assessed its correlation with immune cell infiltration using clinical tissue specimens. Immunohistochemical analyses revealed that high ANKRD49 expression was significantly associated with reduced infiltration of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells within the TME, suggesting that ANKRD49 may contribute to establishing an immunosuppressive milieu. This observation raised a key mechanistic question: does ANKRD49 mediate immune escape indirectly, through modulation of other immunosuppressive factors, or directly, via regulation of canonical immune checkpoint molecules? To address this, we performed functional validation experiments in LUAD cell lines. Our results demonstrated that ANKRD49 overexpression markedly upregulates programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1). PD-L1, a critical inhibitory immune checkpoint protein, maintains immune homeostasis by engaging PD-1 on activated T cells, delivering negative regulatory signals that promote T cell exhaustion and prevent overactivation [51–53]. Aberrant PD-L1 upregulation in cancer cells disrupts this physiological feedback, enabling tumors to evade cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL)-mediated killing – a phenomenon frequently observed across multiple malignancies [54–56]. Our findings thus establish a direct mechanistic link between ANKRD49 and PD-L1, identifying ANKRD49 as a novel positive regulator of this key immune checkpoint.

We next sought to delineate the molecular basis underlying ANKRD49‑mediated PD‑L1 regulation. Bioinformatic screening of the PD‑L1 promoter using the JASPAR database identified the transcription factor MEF2A as a high‑scoring candidate, with two putative binding sites located at positions − 1122 to − 1113 and − 568 to − 559. Although the MEF2 family is best known for its role in muscle differentiation, the gene programs governed by individual members in non‑muscle contexts remain incompletely defined [57]. Using chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays, we confirmed that MEF2A directly occupies both identified sites within the PD‑L1 promoter. Dual‑luciferase reporter assays further demonstrated that ANKRD49 overexpression significantly enhances MEF2A‑driven transcriptional activity at this promoter in a dose‑dependent manner. To clarify the nature of the ANKRD49‑MEF2A interaction, we performed co‑immunoprecipitation (Co‑IP) and immunofluorescence colocalization experiments. Co‑IP confirmed a direct physical association between ANKRD49 and MEF2A, while immunofluorescence revealed their predominant colocalization within the nucleus, providing critical subcellular context for their functional cooperation in transcriptional regulation.

Given that ANKRD49 contains four tandem ankyrin repeat domains – well‑characterized protein interaction motifs [58] – we sought to identify the specific domain responsible for MEF2A binding. Using a panel of ANKRD49 truncation mutants in co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays, we determined that deletion of the second ankyrin repeat domain (Domain 2) completely abolished the interaction with MEF2A, whereas deletion of other domains had minimal effect. This finding establishes Domain 2 as the essential structural module mediating the ANKRD49-MEF2A interaction in LUAD cells. Functionally, deletion of Domain 2 abrogated ANKRD49-induced PD-L1 upregulation, whereas overexpression of Domain 2 alone could partially elevate PD-L1 expression, demonstrating that the specific interaction between ANKRD49’s ankyrin domain 2 and MEF2A is necessary for the observed regulatory effect. Proper protein function relies on correct polypeptide folding to ensure appropriate topological structure and stability [59]. In this study, expression of ankyrin repeat Domain 2 alone only partially recapitulated the increase in PD-L1 and MEF2A expression (Fig. 6D). We therefore speculate that the absence of Domains 1, 3 and 4 prevents ANKRD49 from adopting its correct spatial conformation, thereby compromising its full regulatory capacity on PD-L1 expression. Nevertheless, this finding further demonstrates that the ankyrin repeat domain not only serves as a protein-protein interaction motif but can also function as an independent functional unit [60].

Our findings show that ANKRD49 upregulates not only PD‑L1 but also PD‑L2, CD24, and CD112. Although we focused on the MEF2A/PD‑L1 axis for mechanistic studies due to the well‑established role of PD‑1/PD‑L1 in immune evasion, the potential contribution of other ANKRD49‑regulated checkpoints cannot be ruled out. Therefore, ANKRD49 may reshape the tumor immune microenvironment by synergistically upregulating multiple immune checkpoint molecules, with PD‑L1 being a key but not the only effector molecule. Accordingly, we conclude that ANKRD49 promotes immune evasion at least in part through the MEF2A/PD‑L1 axis, and may coordinately regulate other immune checkpoints to suppress T‑cell function. Future studies using combinatorial blockade are warranted to dissect the relative contributions of each molecule.

Collectively, our study elucidates a previously unrecognized signaling axis – the ANKRD49–MEF2A–PD‑L1 pathway – through which ANKRD49 promotes immune evasion in LUAD. Mechanistically, ANKRD49 directly binds MEF2A via its second ankyrin repeat domain, upregulates MEF2A expression, and potentiates its transcriptional activity at the PD‑L1 promoter. This cooperative interaction drives PD‑L1 upregulation, leading to impaired T cell function and immune evasion (Fig. 11). The identification of this axis provides a novel mechanistic framework for understanding aberrant immune checkpoint activation within the LUAD TME and offers a compelling rationale for combinatorial therapeutic strategies that simultaneously target this axis and established immune checkpoint pathways. Importantly, the essential functional domain we have mapped – ANKRD49 Domain 2 – represents a structurally defined and potentially druggable target for future development of competitive peptide inhibitors or small molecules designed to disrupt this oncogenic protein‑protein interaction [61]. These insights not only deepen our understanding of LUAD immunopathogenesis but also open new avenues for therapeutic intervention in this refractory malignancy.

Despite these novel findings, several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, our correlation analyses between ANKRD49 expression and immune cell infiltration were derived from a relatively small cohort of LUAD tissue microarrays. Although statistically significant, these results warrant validation in larger, independent patient cohorts with more comprehensive clinical annotations. Second, the mechanistic experiments were largely conducted in established LUAD cell lines (A549, H2228, LLC), which may not fully recapitulate the heterogeneity and complexity of patient tumors. Future studies using patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models or primary LUAD cells would strengthen the translational relevance of our findings. Third, while we demonstrated that ANKRD49 regulates PD‑L1 at both transcriptional and post‑translational levels (by affecting MEF2A mRNA expression), we did not exhaustively investigate other potential post‑translational modifications (e.g., ubiquitination, phosphorylation) that might influence PD‑L1 expression. Fourth, although our in vivo experiments employed two distinct routes of tumor inoculation (subcutaneous and tail vein injection) to model both primary tumor growth and metastatic dissemination, they were restricted to a single mouse strain (C57BL/6) and a single syngeneic tumor cell line (LLC). The immunophenotype and tumor–host interactions may differ substantially across different genetic backgrounds or with other murine LUAD models (e.g., M109, LA795). Therefore, the generalizability of our observations to more diverse immunocompetent backgrounds or orthotopic lung cancer models remains to be further validated. Finally, the therapeutic potential of targeting the ANKRD49‑MEF2A interaction has not yet been directly tested. Future work should focus on developing specific inhibitors (e.g., cell‑penetrating peptides disrupting Domain 2‑MEF2A binding) and evaluating their efficacy alone or in combination with existing ICBs in preclinical models. Addressing these limitations will be essential to translate our mechanistic insights into clinically actionable strategies for LUAD patients who are resistant to current immunotherapies.
