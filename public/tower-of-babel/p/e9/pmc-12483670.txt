Protocol for Hi-C-based identification of chromosomal inversions in mosquitoes
Authors: Varvara Lukyanchikova, Ilya I. Brusentsov, Dmitry A. Karagodin, Maria V. Sharakhova, Igor V. Sharakhov
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryChromosomal inversions play an important role in the genomic evolution and adaptation of mosquito species. Here, we present a protocol for detecting chromosomal inversions in mosquitoes using Hi-C technology and chromatin contact heatmaps. We describe the steps for Hi-C library preparation using the Hi-C Arima+ kit, along with the Arima Library Prep Module, and provide several adaptations for mosquito samples. We then outline procedures for Hi-C data analysis, Hi-C heatmap generation, and the identification of polymorphic and fixed inversions.For additional details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Lukyanchikova et al.1

Before you begin

Chromosomal inversions are structural variations in the genome in which a segment of a chromosome is reversed 180 degrees, with no gain or loss of genetic material. These inversions can have significant implications for the evolution and adaptation of plant and animal species, including mosquitoes.1,2 By altering the arrangement of genes, inversions can influence gene expression, create reproductive isolation, and enhance local adaptation. In malaria mosquito species, chromosomal inversions are associated with specific ecological niches, allowing populations to thrive in different environments. Additionally, these genomic rearrangements can facilitate the spread of beneficial alleles through populations, contributing to the persistence and evolution of Anopheles vectors. However, studying chromosomal inversions in some mosquito species (e.g., Aedes and Culex) is complicated by the abundance of repetitive elements in their genomes, making it challenging to identify and characterize these inversions without advanced genomic techniques. The increasing availability of genomic sequences for multiple mosquito species offers new opportunities for detecting chromosomal rearrangements using genomics-based methods.2 One such method is the genome-wide chromosome conformation capture (Hi-C) approach that detects chromosomal rearrangements by measuring the frequency of physical contacts between specific genomic loci in the nuclear space.3,4 The Juicebox software allows visualization of different types of chromatin interactions on Hi-C heatmaps.5 Here, the Hi-C proximity ligation approach is used to identify chromosomal inversions in various mosquito species. Inversions are detected based on a distinctive butterfly-like pattern outside the major diagonal in the Hi-C heatmap. The butterfly wing triangles connect at a point that marks the breakpoints, with the color intensity increasing as a gradient from the base to the center of the triangles (Figure 1). This protocol can be employed for different developmental stages of mosquitoes (eggs, larvae, adults) as well as for various tissues (such as ovaries, testes, midgut, heads, antennae, etc.). We have successfully used this protocol to identify known and novel chromosomal inversions in species of the Anopheles genus1 and in Aedes aegypti.6Figure 1Hi-C-based identification of chromosomal inversions in mosquitoes(A) Schematic (left) and polytene chromosome (right) image of an inversion in a heterozygous mosquito individual.(B) A procedure for Hi-C-based identification of the standard (left) and inverted (right) chromosomal arrangement. Innovation

The current protocol provides the detailed guide to discover chromosomal inversions in various mosquito species and, for the first time, describes the complete workflow starting from raising and collecting mosquito material all the way through inversion breakpoints detection. The protocol outlines multiple steps through Hi-C library preparation, sequencing data analysis, Hi-C data visualization and, finally, inversion detection and assessment. Technical innovations include an alternative crosslinking step that does not involve tissue homogenization in liquid nitrogen, which allows to utilize Arima Hi-C+ kit protocol even for small mosquito tissues or limited cell types and it prevents significant material loss during the fixation step. We also provide technical instructions for detecting and assessing polymorphic and fixed inversions. The protocol details the possible limitations of the suggested workflow and includes an itemized troubleshooting section.

Raising mosquito colony for the experiment

Maintain mosquito colonies under clean and optimal conditions as temperature and humidity are critical for their well-being. Keep adult specimens at 27°C and 75% humidity with a 12-hour light/dark cycle. A healthy mosquito colony has low larval mortality and at least 75% of females successfully feed on blood. Purchased blood should be used no later than three days before the expiration date. Maintain the required parameters, such as temperature and humidity, while keeping the adult mosquitoes. The cage should be clean, and fresh sugar water and blood should be provided regularly. There should be no dead adult mosquitoes or parasites present during the first seven to ten days. For Hi-C, use males, females or a mixture of both sexes, as it will not affect the results. However, if you require a specific sex, separate males and females at the pupal stage and keep in different containers. Male and female mosquitoes can easily be distinguished under a dissecting microscope based on the appearance of their genital lobes, which differ by sex. If no specific age is required, we recommend using 5-7-day-old mosquitoes for the flash freezing step.

Flash freezing mosquito material

1.Allow the mosquito cage to cool to +4°C for 5 min.2.Using tweezers, transfer the required number of mosquitoes into a 1.5 mL Eppendorf tube, close the cap, and place the tube on ice prior to flash freezing.3.When ready, use long forceps to place the tube in liquid nitrogen and hold it there until the boiling stops. Note: Ensure that the entire tube is fully immersed in liquid nitrogen. 4.Once frozen, transfer the tube to a long-term freezer and store it at −80°C for up to several months. Optional: If earlier developmental stages (larvae or pupae) are required, collect the required number of specimens, rinse mosquito larvae or pupae 2–3 times in nuclease-free water, then aspirate any residual water before freezing in liquid nitrogen. Use filter paper to remove the last drops of water.

Hi-C library preparation using an Arima kit

There are several existing Hi-C protocols that can reliably be used to generate Hi-C libraries. Here, we describe library preparation using the Arima HiC+ kit and highlight some technical adaptations including alternative crosslinking protocol that may be useful when working with mosquito samples or low-input materials.

#!/bin/bash

#SBATCH -p amd_256M

#SBATCH -t 2:00:00

#SBATCH -c 1

#SBATCH --mem=32G

#SBATCH -o genome.out

#SBATCH -e genome.err

# Set default values

genomeDir="./genome"

genome=""

# Function to display help

help() {

echo "Usage: sbatch genome.sh -f <filename> [-g <dir>]"

echo "-g Output directory for the prepared genome [default: ./genome]"

echo " -f Input reference genome file (required)"

echo " -h Show this help message"

exit "$1"

}

# Parse command-line arguments

while getopts "g:f:h" opt; do

case $opt in

g) genomeDir="$OPTARG" ;;

f) genome="$OPTARG" ;;

h) help 0 ;;

∗) help 1 ;;

esac

done

# Check if the required argument is provided

if [ -z "$genome" ]; then

echo "Error: Input genome file not specified."

help 1

fi

# Check if the genome file exists

if [ ! -f "$genome" ]; then

echo "Error: File '$genome' not found."

help 1

fi

# Uncompress the file if it is gzipped

if [[ "$genome" == ∗.gz ]]; then

echo "Uncompressing file $genome..."

gunzip "$genome" || { echo "Error: Failed to uncompress $genome"; exit 1; }

genome="${genome%.gz}"

fi

# Create the genome directory

echo "Creating genome directory: $genomeDir"

mkdir -p "$genomeDir" || { echo "Error: Failed to create directory $genomeDir"; exit 1; }

# Move the genome file to the target directory

echo "Moving genome file to $genomeDir..."

mv "$genome" "$genomeDir/" || { echo "Error: Failed to move file $genome"; exit 1; }

# Change to the genome directory

cd "$genomeDir" || { echo "Error: Failed to change to directory $genomeDir"; exit 1; }

# Create the genome index using BWA

echo "Creating genome index using BWA..."

bwa index "$genome" || { echo "Error: Failed to create index using BWA"; exit 1; }

# Generate the restriction site map

echo "Generating restriction site map..."

../juicer/misc/generate_site_positions.py Arima "${genome%.∗}" "$genome" || { echo "Error: Failed to generate restriction site map"; exit 1; }

# Create the contig size file

echo "Creating contig size file..."

awk 'BEGIN{OFS="\t"}{print $1, $NF}' "${genome%.∗}_Arima.txt" > "${genome%.∗}.genome" || { echo "Error: Failed to create contig size file"; exit 1; }

echo "Done! All steps completed successfully."

chmod +x genome.sh

Expected outcomes

The detailed protocol described above provides key steps for Hi-C library preparation, sequencing results processing, and chromatin heat map analysis for the detection of chromosomal inversions in mosquitoes. This approach allows the identification of new undescribed chromosomal inversions as well as the detection and confirmation of breakpoints for previously known chromosomal rearrangements. Chromosomal inversions are emerging as a key driver of the adaptation and evolution of insect species, including mosquitoes. Polymorphic inversions are the most common type of structural variation in natural mosquito populations, while fixed inversions are the most common feature of mosquito genome evolution.2 Based on our experience, the method described here is reliable for de novo inversion identification in different mosquito species when using well-assembled, chromosome-level genome references validated by FISH.1,6 Using the Hi-C approach in the Anophelinae subfamily, we were able to detect the known 2La inversion in An. coluzzii and 2Rb inversion in An. stephensi, as well as a new inversion in An. atroparvus we named 2L1.1 In the subfamily Culicidae, we identified 21 new inversions in several Aedes strains6 and new inversions in several Culex strains (unpublished data).

A Juicer map (./juicer-Acol/aligned/inter.hic) for An. coluzzii is shown in Figure 3A. The An. gambiae PEST genome,7 which differ from An. coluzzii by the 2L arrangement, was used as a reference. The NT_078265.2 chromosome clearly shows an inversion of ∼22 Mb in length, which has a characteristic butterfly-like appearance and a sharp decrease in contact density on the diagonal in the areas of the breakpoints. Figure 3B shows the common quality control statistics relevant to a Hi-C experiment (in the ./juicer-Acol/aligned/inter.txt). The key parameters are the percentage of uniquely mapped reads (the higher percentage the better, although a low percentage does not necessarily reflect a failure of the experiment, but rather the presence of many repeats in the genome), the library complexity estimate (the higher the better), and the number of Hi-C contacts (expected to be >20%, best >50%). Another critical metric is the ratio of long-range (>20kb) to short-range (<20kb) intra-chromosomal contacts.Note: Successful Hi-C libraries prepared for mosquitoes are expected to have a percentage of long-range intra-chromosomal contacts of ∼35%–50% and a percentage of short-range intra-chromosomal contacts of ∼20%–35%.14 For example, a high-quality Hi-C library would have approximately 80% Hi-C contact reads (mappable true interactions). Within this number, intra-chromosomal contacts would account for ∼75%, while inter-chromosomal contacts would account for ∼5%. Of the intra-chromosomal contacts, 30% would be short and 45% would be long. It is important to remember that these parameters can vary widely depending on the cell type and species involved in the experiment. Figure 3C shows identification of the 2La inversion breakpoints.

Limitations

Overall, the presented protocol works successfully for a wide range of mosquitoes and mosquito tissues. Sometimes the amount of input material can be so low, that intermediate Qubit dsDNA HS quantification and Agilent TapeStation/Bioanalyzer runs are not informative. This is especially true when working with single mosquito cells or single organs. In this case, we recommend following the protocol until the very end, set up the PCR for 12 cycles and estimate concentration using the Qubit dsDNA HS assay. In most cases, you will be able to successfully create a Hi-C library suitable for further sequencing.

The approach described in the manuscript allows to identify inversions from 1 Mb to 55 Mb. Smaller inversions may still be visible on the heatmap if a high-quality genome assembly was used as reference genome, but the results are not guaranteed. Another limitation is the accuracy of the breakpoint coordinates, which is closely related to the quality of the genome. A high-quality genome assembly will provide more accurate breakpoint coordinates than a low-quality assembly. The Hi-C-based inversion detection method is a good approach to start the research. However, this approach can’t determine the inversion frequency (only an approximate estimate), so FISH or PCR-based verification may still be suggested.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Unsuccessful crosslinking step.

Potential solution

After processing the sequencing data, you get chromatin heatmaps that reflect the 3D structure of the target genome. If the 3D structure is missing except for the main diagonal (e.g. loops, compartments), you may suspect that the cross-linking step has failed. Unsuccessful crosslinking step may occur due to improper storage conditions of flash-frozen samples. In this regard, it is strongly recommended to prepare fresh TC buffer for each experiment, not to store flash-frozen samples at −80°C for more than 2–4 weeks, and to avoid repeated thawing/freezing.

Problem 2

This warning only occurs with the latest versions of Python. Despite the warning, the script works correctly.

Potential solution

Correct line 233 in the ./juicer/misc/generate_site_positions.py from “firststr=re.split('\s+',line[1:])” to “firststr=re.split(r'\s+',line[1:])”

Problem 3

In the AgamP3 genome assembly the order of chromosome arms (2R and 2L; 3R and 3L) is mixed up.

Potential solution

To get the correct heatmap, you need to edit manually the ./AgamP3/GCF_000005575.2_AgamP3_genomic.genome file. Swap lines 2 and 3, as well as 4 and 5.

Problem 4

Script juicer.sh specifies 150 GB of RAM.

Potential solution

Change the amount of RAM to the amount available on your PC (N):sed -i 's/Xms[0-9]\+m/XmsNm/g; s/Xmx[0-9]\+m/XmxNm/g' ./juicer/CPU/juicer.sh

sed -i 's/Xms[0-9]\+m/XmsNm/g; s/Xmx[0-9]\+m/XmxNm/g' ./juicer/CPU/juicer.sh

Problem 5

High background noise/weak 3D pattern, can’t detect any inversion.

Potential solution

Some heatmaps may show high background noise and/or weak 3D chromatin structure. One possible solution is to try a different developmental stage. In our experience, using embryonic material for Hi-C library preparation provides a clearer inversion pattern compared to adult tissue. In addition, a similar heatmap view may reflect problems in the crosslinking step (see troubleshooting 1).

You may also consider repeating an experiment by preparing a high-quality library. Start by harvesting new material and performing new fixations according to the instructions above. Monitor the integrity of the genomic DNA prior to the actual Hi-C experiment by extracting a small amount of DNA and performing gel electrophoresis. If no DNA degradation has occurred at this step, proceed with Hi-C library preparation.

Problem 6

The pattern of detected inversion is not a clear “butterfly”: only one “wing” is visible, another one is completely or partially missing; the “butterfly” pattern is noisy.

Potential solution

While it is known that tandem duplications have a “one-wing” pattern on Hi-C heatmap,15 it has been demonstrated that overlapping chromosomal inversions would also result in a similar “one-wing” pattern.6 For instance, there is evidence of a complex rearrangement in Ae. aegypti when breakpoints overlap (e.g. inversions 1qE and 1qF6). In this case, we can see a “broken butterfly” pattern where one “wing” is present, but the other “wing” is partially missing. Furthermore, poor genome quality near inversion breakpoints may affect the detection of chromosomal rearrangements. Some genomic fragments near the breakpoints may be missing, which can result in the loss of one “wing” or a noisy inversion pattern. First, improve the quality of the genome, and then repeat the experiment or use previous sequencing data with the improved genome as a reference. Additionally, the pronounced pattern of compartments in some tissues can significantly affect the inversion pattern and detection accuracy. Therefore, preparing a Hi-C library from a different tissue may be a potential solution.

Problem 8

Is it a polymorphic inversion, a fixed inversion or a misassembly?

Potential solution

We consider an inversion to be polymorphic in a population if there are individuals who carry the inverted variant and individuals with a standard chromosomal arrangement. If a polymorphic inversion is indeed present on the chromatin heatmap, flipping the inverted fragment in Juicebox (as described in checking whether an inversion is polymorphic or fixed using 3d-dna) will not change or will slightly change the inversion pattern on the map. On the other hand, we consider an inversion to be fixed in the population if inversion carriers are the only ones present. In that case, flipping the inverted fragment in Juicebox, as described in “checking whether an inversion is polymorphic or fixed using 3d-dna,” will cause the inversion pattern to disappear from the heatmap.2 There is no way to distinguish between misassembly and fixed inversion, as both events look the same on the Hi-C map. However, in case of misassembly, the butterfly pattern will appear in all examined strains or populations.6 It can be also confirmed by fluorescence in situ hybridization.
