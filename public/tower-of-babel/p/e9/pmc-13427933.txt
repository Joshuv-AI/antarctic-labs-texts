Precision Medicine in Pancreatic Cancer: Targeting KRAS and Beyond
Authors: Hendrik Schürmann, Wungki Park, Grainne M. O’Kane, Carolina Sciortino, Monica Niger, Maximilian Reichert, Kenneth P. Olive, Ina Pretzell, C. Benedikt Westphalen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
For the past decades, chemotherapy constituted the therapeutic foundation in advanced or metastatic pancreatic cancer. Despite significant advances in the molecular understanding, translation into tangible patient benefit has remained modest. Until recently, mutant KRAS, the dominant oncogenic driver, was considered undruggable, and only a small subgroup of patients potentially benefited from targeted therapies. With the emergence of KRAS inhibitors, most patients with pancreatic cancer in theory qualify for targeted therapeutics. Final results from the RASolute 302 trial showed clinically meaningful activity of RAS inhibition in patients with metastatic pancreatic cancer and paved the way for approval. Ongoing preclinical and coclinical studies have documented both intrinsic and acquired mechanisms of resistance to KRAS inhibition. Given the cellular plasticity seen in pancreatic cancer, the identification and anticipation of resistance mechanisms will be critical to exploit emerging therapeutic vulnerabilities through novel combination strategies. In view of the increasing number of trials and the growing body of evidence for targeted therapies, pancreatic cancer is entering a transitional phase in which precision oncology strategies must be redefined beyond rare molecular subgroups. In this review, we will briefly revisit targeted therapeutic approaches in pancreatic cancer to then discuss the clinical implications of genomic and transcriptomic heterogeneity in KRAS-mutant and KRAS wild-type disease. We will outline how our expanding biological insights into pancreatic cancer could inform combination and sequential therapeutic approaches.

Introduction

Basic Characteristics of Pancreatic Cancer and Clinical Challenges

Pancreatic cancer, most commonly pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), remains one of the biggest challenges in medical oncology. Despite substantial advances in our understanding of the molecular foundations of PDAC, translation of this understanding into effective systemic therapeutic strategies has remained modest at best. Consequently, 5-year overall survival has stagnated at approximately 10% across stages, and long-term survival remains rare, particularly in metastatic disease [1].

Evolution of Standard-of-Care Treatment

The therapeutic landscape in PDAC has evolved incrementally over the past three decades and can be conceptualized as three overlapping phases: (1) cytotoxic chemotherapy, (2) targeted therapy for biomarker-driven subgroups, and (3) novel targeted treatment against the major oncogene, the “KRAS era” (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Conceptual epochs in pancreatic cancer therapy. Cytotoxic chemotherapy emerged at the end of the twentieth century. First, monotherapy with gemcitabine replaced 5-FU [2], then the introduction of combinatorial chemotherapy with FOLFIRINOX set a clinical standard that still influences today’s clinical practice [3]. From this foundation, the therapeutic landscape evolved across settings: adjuvant [4], maintenance [5], and second-line [6] therapy [7, 8]. Reformulations such as nab-paclitaxel and nal-irinotecan [9, 10], as well as sequential concepts [11, 12], further expanded options. In the preoperative setting, the taxane- and platinum-based PAXG regimen demonstrated improved efficacy compared with mFOLFIRINOX in the CASSANDRA trial [13]. While these developments broadened the therapeutic toolkit, the prognosis of patients with advanced pancreatic cancer has remained dismal.

Over the years, numerous targeted agents have been evaluated in pancreatic cancer, often in combination with chemotherapy. Most trials were not biomarker-guided, and the vast majority failed to improve outcomes compared with standard treatment.

One early example of a biology-informed trial was based on the observation that overexpression of the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR/HER1) was associated with worse prognosis [14]. Hence, erlotinib, an EGFR-targeted tyrosine kinase inhibitor, was investigated in combination with gemcitabine [15]. The PA.3 trial met its primary end point and led to the approval of erlotinib in combination with gemcitabine in advanced pancreatic cancer. However, the modest survival benefit limited its clinical impact as reflected by an European Society for Medical Oncology—Magnitude of Clinical Benefit Scale (ESMO-MCBS) score of 1 [16], and the combination was never widely adopted.

The first clinically impactful molecularly guided trial involved a subgroup of patients in which PDAC develops against the background of pathogenic germline variants in genes of the homologous recombination repair (HRR) pathway, particularly BRCA1/BRCA2. Pathogenic variants in the HRR pathway can result in the emergence of homologous recombination repair deficiency (HRD) which confers sensitivity against platinum-based chemotherapy and PARP-inhibitors. As patients with BRCA1/2-mutated PDAC derive increased benefit from platinum-based chemotherapy [17], the PARP inhibitor olaparib was investigated and is now approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and European Medicines Agency (EMA) as maintenance therapy in patients with germline BRCA mutations and platinum-sensitive disease [5]. Compared with placebo, olaparib improved progression-free survival but failed to improve overall survival. While applicable only in a small subset (approximately 5%) of patients, the approval of olaparib introduced molecular stratification in the treatment of PDAC and contributed to the integration of routine genetic testing in clinical practice.

Advances in molecular profiling, together with the increasing availability of next-generation sequencing (NGS), and indeed whole-genome sequencing (WGS) [18], have enabled the identification of rare, therapeutically relevant subgroups in PDAC. These subgroups primarily feature activating mutations or gene fusions in receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs), or mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK)-associated downstream effector proteins. As they (partially) activate extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK), it is not surprising that these alterations are generally found in KRAS wild-type disease. The identification of patients with these molecular subgroups, together with the emergence of compounds targeting KRAS, has opened new avenues for biomarker-driven treatment strategies. In the following sections, we discuss these molecular subgroups and emerging targeted approaches in the context of their biological rationale, clinical relevance, and future perspectives.

Molecular Biology of PDAC

Genomic Landscape and KRAS Biology

Genomically, PDAC is a relatively homogeneous malignancy: activating mutations of the oncogene KRAS occur in approximately 90% of cases, while inactivating mutations are frequently detected in the tumor suppressor genes TP53 (approximately 70–80%), CDKN2A (approximately 30–80%), and SMAD4 (approximately 30–50%) [18–20].

With regards to oncogenesis, two complementary models currently exist to explain the acquisition of pathogenic mutations along the evolutionary trajectory of PDAC. The “traditional” model considers a stepwise process of premalignant to malignant progression, similar to the Vogelstein model of colorectal cancer tumorigenesis [21]. Specifically, PDAC is considered to regularly arise from histologically distinct preneoplastic lesions, such as pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia (PanIN), mucinous cystic neoplasms (MCN), or intraductal mucinous neoplasms (IPMN) among others, which gradually acquire pathogenic mutations [22]. This model aligns well with familial PDAC. By contrast, the more recent “catastrophic” model suggests that a subset of PDAC acquires complex genomic rearrangements during singular events, such as chromothripsis, which may result in polyploidization [23]. This model assumingly results in more aggressive clinical phenotypes.

At the molecular level, KRAS mutations in PDAC appear uniform at first glance, with around 90% occurring in codon 12. However, despite differing by only a single amino acid, the most common variants—G12D (approximately 40%), G12V (approximately 30%), and G12R (approximately 15%)—show distinct biological and clinical features [18, 24]. The G12R variant, for instance, was shown to be overrepresented in localized or locally advanced disease, and, if adjusted for stage, appears to be associated with a slightly more favorable outcome [24], possibly owing to higher immunogenicity of the G12R-variant tumor [25]. Notably, a potential mechanism to explain the distinct biological phenotype of G12R was also identified in the defective activation of the PI3Kα subunit [26], and different routes of macropinocytosis [27].

The KRAS variant-specific biology can be traced back to their respective functionality as a small GTPase. KRAS changes its conformation upon binding to guanosine triphosphate (GTP), thereby revealing binding domains for upstream regulators and downstream effector proteins. This defines the GTP-bound/ON state as active [28]. Conversely, hydrolysis of GTP to guanosine diphosphate (GDP) results in an inactive GDP-bound/OFF state. The hydrolysis occurs intrinsically by its enzymatic function as a GTPase; however, it can be influenced by GTPase activating proteins (GAP), such as NF1 [29], which promote the GDP-bound, i.e., inactive state. By contrast, guanine nucleotide exchange factors (GEF) such as SOS1 increase the rate of exchange of GDP to GTP, rendering KRAS to its GTP-bound, i.e., active state. Thus, KRAS signaling output is determined by the dynamics of cycling between GTP- and GDP-bound states, as well as intrinsic and extrinsic regulatory mechanisms and the affinity for upstream regulators and downstream effectors.

Mutations of codon 12 directly attenuate the intrinsic hydrolysis of GTP, but show a varying extent on the basis of the underlying amino acid exchange [30]. Mutant variants display a decreasing intrinsic hydrolysis rate from G12C, G12D, G12V to G12R. Of note, the extrinsic stimulus P120GAP/RASA1 results in similar hydrolysis rates among G12C, G12V, and G12R with a slightly increased rate for G12D, but overall strong decrease compared with wild-type KRAS.

Molecular Classification

A recently published study proposes a clinically meaningful, biology-informed classification of pancreatic adenocarcinoma that distinguishes three classes defined by NGS profiles: KRAS-mutant PDAC (95%), KRAS wild-type but other-MAPK-mutated PDAC (3%), and KRAS wild-type and MAPK wild-type PDAC (2%) [18]. Not only do these groups reflect the MAPK-driven nature of PDAC, but also separate by enrichment for targetable alterations, which is particularly useful in precision medicine. Recent data also suggest a broad spectrum of PDAC phenotypes reflecting distinct MAPK activation. For instance, BRAF V600E mutations can “phenocopy” KRAS mutations [31]. Of note, among the KRAS wild-type and MAPK wild-type tumors, mutations in GNAS or PIK3CA suggest a partial KRAS downstream signature, which may activate the transcription factors MYC or YAP. For instance, activation of PI3K signaling through PIK3CA mutations was shown to be sufficient to initiate pancreatic cancer and result in activation of ERK1/2 signaling [32]. The three classes may therefore reflect a spectrum of KRAS downstream transcriptional activation. In summary, despite revealing repetitive gene mutation patterns, pancreatic cancer is a highly heterogeneous disease on the genomic level.

Beyond the Genome—Transcriptional Programs and Plasticity

It has long been evident that genomic alterations alone are insufficient to explain interpatient and intrapatient heterogeneity. Biological advances in combination with increasing computing power eventually allowed widespread use of tools to identify previously latent patterns in transcription data [33]. Applied to PDAC, this approach revealed a degree of heterogeneity exceeding that expected from genomic alterations alone, and three defined molecular subtypes showed prognostic value [34]. Over the years, with repeating advances in biological techniques and bioinformatics, the subtypes were further refined [35–38]. Currently, the most robust model favors two molecular subtypes, “classical” and “basal-like,” and holds both prognostic as well as predictive value. Notably, predictive value of these molecular subtypes has recently been shown to be opposite for chemotherapy [39] and KRAS inhibition [40]. Owing to high costs and limited immediate clinical implication, these classifiers do not play a role in the management of pancreatic cancer outside of clinical trials, though. Attempts to build histopathological [41] or liquid biopsy-based classifiers [42] show potential, but are not broadly applicable yet.

Beyond these classifications, PDAC exhibits marked transcriptional plasticity. High-resolution spatial profiling of matched primary and multi-organ metastatic lesions has demonstrated that multiple lineage states, including classical, basal-like, and intermediate programs, coexist within the same tumor regions, often even within identical subclonal populations, and are spatially organized in distinct ecological niches [43]. Recent evidence based on large-scale single-cell atlases integrating hundreds of patients even reveal a far broader spectrum of malignant transcriptional programs beyond the classical/basal-like dichotomy, including EMT, hypoxic, proliferative, and invasive states, arranged along continuous trajectories rather than discrete categories, and are actively reshaped by therapy [44].

Targeted Treatment in Pancreatic Cancer

Past Therapeutic Strategies Targeting MAPK Signaling

The key role of KRAS in pancreatic tumorigenesis and progression has long been recognized. With no direct KRAS-targeting approaches available and KRAS long considered “undruggable,” repeated attempts were made to target alternative nodes along the MAPK signaling axis. Multi-kinase and selective inhibitors of EGFR/HER [45, 46], RAF [47–49], MEK [50, 51], and ERK [52] were investigated, but either showed limited efficacy or unacceptable toxicity. Accordingly, MAPK signaling in PDAC needs to be understood as a highly adaptive and dynamic effector network with KRAS as its central regulatory hub.

Using the presence of an activating KRAS mutation as a surrogate for KRAS-centered network signaling, the group of approximately 10% KRAS wild-type PDAC, and subgroups without defined oncogenic drivers, were shown to play special roles in pancreatic cancer precision medicine. Identification of these molecular subgroups and development of precision strategies resulted in the second conceptual epoch, biomarker-driven subgroup targeting.

Specific Molecular Subgroups

Deficient Mismatch Repair/Microsatellite Instability (dMMR/MSI)

Approximately 1–2% of pancreatic cancers display deficient mismatch repair (dMMR) which is often associated with microsatellite instability (MSI) [53]. Although rare, this subgroup represents the most clinically relevant biomarker-defined population for immunotherapy in pancreatic cancer.

The KEYNOTE-158 trial [54] demonstrated efficacy of the anti-PD-1 antibody, pembrolizumab, in dMMR/MSI-high tumors. A subsequent biomarker analysis led to the label expansion and tumor-agnostic FDA approval of pembrolizumab in unresectable or metastatic tumors with tumor mutational burden-high (≥ 10 mutations per megabase). In the PDAC subgroup of the trial, an objective response rate (ORR) of 18.2% was observed (n = 22). By contrast to the FDA, only cancer-specific approval was granted by the EMA, not including PDAC based on small numbers and relatively lower ORR.

Two recent publications focused on clinical outcomes in retrospective dMMR/MSI PDAC cohorts in the clinical setting. In a European multicenter retrospective analysis, treatment with ICI led to a clinically meaningful ORR of 48.4% (n = 15/31), and all responders with follow-up data (n = 14/15) experienced a durable response of greater than 12 months [55]. In a large US single-center analysis, 20 out of 55 patients with dMMR/MSI PDAC were treated with ICI and showed a substantial disease control rate of 80% [56].

Accordingly, patients with dMMR/MSI PDAC represent a clinically highly relevant subgroup that may benefit from treatment with immunotherapy. Routine testing for dMMR/MSI should, therefore, be considered in patients with pancreatic cancer.

Homologous Recombination Deficiency (HRD)

About 15% of pancreatic cancers harbor germline or somatic alterations of DNA damage repair genes, and 8% have alterations in the core homologous recombination repair genes (BRCA1, BRCA2, or PALB2), resulting in homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) phenotype characterized by impaired double strand DNA break repair [57]. Importantly, only pathogenic mutations in select core HRD and RAD51 paralog genes result in a true HRD phenotype and functional HRD phenotype. Genomic scar signatures such as COSMIC signature 3 or HRDetect are present in a subset of these tumors but remain infrequent, underscoring the biological heterogeneity and therapeutic implication within this subgroup [58, 59]. HRD is clinically relevant given its sensitivity to platinum-based chemotherapy [60–62], which remains the important backbone of the treatment. Accordingly, early genomic testing to identify HRD status, with consideration of zygosity when feasible, is recommended to guide platinum-based chemotherapy strategies and olaparib maintenance [63]. Rucaparib maintenance therapy also demonstrated efficacy in platinum-sensitive pancreatic cancer, and supports expansion of PARPi use to include rare pathogenic somatic variants of BRCA1/2 or PALB2 [64], particularly in the context of biallelic loss.

Beyond PARPi monotherapy, HRD has emerged as a promising biological context for combinatorial strategies incorporating immunotherapy. Preclinical murine models demonstrate that PARPi can enhance tumor immunogenicity and sensitize pancreatic cancer to ICI [65, 66] in part through innate immune sensing pathways, metabolic reprogramming, and increased neoantigen expression [67]. Although statistically significant, the effect of BRCA loss on TMB in human PDAC is relatively small and overall still categorizes as TMB-low, as for BRCA wild-type [68]. This disconnect suggests that quantitative neoantigen alone may be insufficient for effective immune responses, highlighting a potential role of neoantigen quality and tumor-intrinsic determinants of antigen processing and presentation, as well as microenvironmental constraints on immune engagement [69, 70].

Early clinical signals also suggest potential immunotherapy sensitivity in HRD pancreatic cancer, with responses and early immune correlates observed in small cohorts treated with dual checkpoint blockade [71]. This hypothesis is further supported by studies evaluating PARPi and ICI combinations, such as niraparib–ipilimumab, which demonstrated activity in platinum-sensitive pancreatic cancer despite the absence of HRD-based stratification [72]. Prospective studies, such as KEYLYNK-007 and POLAR [73], have further evaluated pembrolizumab and olaparib in biomarker-selected populations. More recently, the randomized phase II SWOG S2001 study demonstrated that pembrolizumab did not improve clinical outcomes when added to olaparib maintenance in patients with germline BRCA1/2-mutated metastatic pancreatic cancers [74], highlighting the biological heterogeneity that exists even within genotype-defined HRD subsets.

Together, this suggests that germline BRCA1/2 status alone is not sufficient to identify patients most likely to benefit from immunotherapy beyond anti-PD-1 therapy and support more refined biomarkers incorporating functional HRD status, genomic instability, neoantigen landscape, and immune contexture [69]. Further prospective, biomarker-driven studies are required to define the relationship between HRD and immune biology in pancreatic cancer.

Given the substantial clinical implications and heritable risk associated with HRD, structured genetic counselling and screening programs are recommended for affected individuals and direct families.

In summary, HRD is the largest molecularly defined subgroup in pancreatic cancer. Given established therapeutic implications, testing is now recommended in international guidelines [7, 75].

Alternative Drivers in KRAS Wild-Type Disease

The approximately 10% of KRAS wild-type PDAC are enriched in alternative MAPK pathway drivers, typically mutually exclusive with KRAS [76]. These alterations activate MAPK signaling to varying degrees, from upstream events such as activating mutations or fusions involving EGFR, FGFR, NRG1, ROS1, RET, and NTRK [77], to downstream alterations, most notably BRAF. Collectively, they confer phenotypes with varying similarity to KRAS-mutant tumors but generally more favorable survival [18].

Neuregulin1 (NRG1) is a ligand to ERBB3/ERBB4, which, upon binding, dimerizes with ERBB2/HER2. NRG1 fusions result in constitutive activation of downstream signaling. The HER2/3 bispecific antibody, zenocutuzumab, showed promising efficacy in pancreatic cancer harboring NRG1 fusions with an ORR of 42% (n = 15/36) [78] and received accelerated approval by the FDA for NRG1 positive lung and pancreatic cancers in 2024 [79].

Alterations in FGFR can be observed across tumor types and have been shown to be predictive of response to FGFR inhibitors. In the recent phase II RAGNAR trial, 10 out of 18 patients (56%) with PDAC harboring fusions in FGFR1 (n = 4) or FGFR2 (n =14) showed an objective response [80] to the oral FGFR1-4 inhibitor erdafitinib. Although FGFR fusions only occur in an estimated 0.8% of all patients with PDAC [81], approximately 90% are assumingly mutually exclusive with KRAS or PIK3CA mutations, rendering them enriched in KRAS wild-type.

Neurotrophic tyrosine receptor kinase (NTRK) gene fusions are rare in PDAC (< 1%); however, larotrectinib, entrectinib, and repotrectinib are approved in a tumor-agnostic fashion in NTRK fusion positive cancers by the FDA and EMA [82].

RET fusions can be targeted with the FDA- and EMA-approved RET-inhibitor selpercatinib, which demonstrated an ORR of 55% (n = 6/11) in pancreatic cancer [83].

Finally, mutations downstream to KRAS can occur in BRAF (approximately 2–3%), with V600E being the best-characterized across tumors. Accordingly, trametinib/dabrafenib was approved tumor-agnostically by the FDA in 2022 [84]. In a large, retrospective, multi-center cohort, BRAF alterations (mutations/fusions) in pancreatic cancer were categorized and their response toward target-matched inhibition evaluated. Despite relatively small sample size per group, the data indicate a decreasing response to BRAF/MEK inhibition from exon 15 alterations (primarily V600) over BRAF/RAF1 fusions to exon 11 alterations [85]. Notably, patients harboring additional driver mutations did not respond to BRAF or MEK inhibition. Taken together, the data suggest that class II BRAF mutations in PDAC are less sensitive to conventional BRAF/MEK inhibition and may require alternative strategies. By contrast to type I RAF inhibitors (e.g., dabrafenib, encorafenib, vemurafenib), type II RAF inhibitors such as tovorafenib [86] or exarafenib [87] can inhibit RAF dimers and may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for class II BRAF alterations. However, clinical data in pancreatic cancer remain limited.

Nimotuzumab, an EGFR-directed monoclonal antibody, further emphasizes the relevance of KRAS wild-type PDAC. A recent phase III trial evaluating nimotuzumab in combination with gemcitabine as first-line therapy in advanced PDAC, demonstrated improved PFS and OS in KRAS wild-type PDAC [88]; however, did not stratify for target-specificity.

Collectively, although rare, this subgroup defines a biologically distinct, target-enriched subset of PDAC. Over the past years, emerging new targeted therapies have been shown to potentially result in exceptional responses. Comprehensive molecular profiling to detect these non-KRAS drivers should be conducted early after diagnosis of advanced or metastatic PDAC.

Targeting RAS

The Paradigm of “Undruggability”

For decades, KRAS was considered undruggable, placing most patients with pancreatic cancer beyond the reach of effective targeted therapy. This paradigm shifted in 2013 with the discovery of small molecules selectively binding the GDP-bound state of KRASG12C at a cryptic switch II pocket [89]. Two G12C-selective inhibitors, sotorasib and adagrasib, have since gained approvals for non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and colorectal cancer (CRC). The proof of principle that RAS can be pharmacologically targeted represents a conceptual breakthrough. However, KRASG12C occurs in only 1–2% of pancreatic cancers compared with approximately 10% in NSCLC [90]. While clinically meaningful for these selected patients with ORRs of 20–40% [91, 92], it therefore has not fundamentally transformed the treatment landscape of pancreatic cancer.

Emerging (K)RAS Strategies

The momentum for pancreatic cancer changed with the discovery of the first-in-class KRASG12D-selective inhibitor, MRTX1133, in 2022 [93]. Evolving from structural modifications of adagrasib (MRTX849), MRTX1133 noncovalently binds to the switch II pocket and stabilizes the GDP-bound/OFF state of KRASG12D. While it showed promising activity in preclinical studies, clinical development was discontinued during phase I, reportedly owing to formulation challenges [94].

The bottleneck of new KRAS-targeting strategies finally opened with discovery of the pan-RAS inhibitor, RMC-6236, later named daraxonrasib [95]. This compound binds to GTP-bound RAS isoforms (HRAS, NRAS, KRAS) and the ubiquitous chaperone, cyclophilin A, thereby forming a ternary complex that inhibits downstream RAS signaling. Clinical investigation has advanced to ongoing phase III trials in the first-line (RASolute 303) and adjuvant (RASolute 304) settings, while the final results of the second-line RASolute 302 trial have recently been published [96].

The “outside-of-the-box” thinking that was necessary for development of RMC-6236, has now evolved into a diversifying landscape of (K)RAS-targeting strategies with clinical investigation ranging from phase I to III, and new strategies emerging regularly from preclinical studies. In the following sections of this review, we focus on the distinct mechanisms-of-action (MOAs) of current and upcoming (K)RAS-targeting strategies and their suggested implications for clinical translation in pancreatic cancer.

Small molecule inhibitors are currently the largest group of (K)RAS-targeting compounds under clinical investigation. While most are currently tested on efficacy and tolerability individually, these compounds feature sets of distinct properties that may eventually have major implications for treatment. Specifically, inhibitors can be categorized by (i) their ability to inhibit either or all of the three RAS isoforms, (ii) their ability to inhibit a certain variant or multiple variants, and (iii) their favorability toward GTP- or GDP-bound state.

The aforementioned daraxonrasib (RMC-6236) as well as ERAS-0015 [98] are disclosed by the companies as pan-RAS(ON) inhibitors, i.e., the compounds bind to all three RAS isoforms (HRAS, NRAS, and KRAS) if mutant or wild-type in the GTP-bound state, albeit to a compound-specific extent. Examples for currently investigated KRAS-targeting compounds are the pan-KRAS(OFF) inhibitors, LY4066434 (NCT06607185) or BI-3706674 (NCT06056024), and the pan-KRAS(ON/OFF) inhibitors, BBO-11818 (NCT06917079), or ERAS-4001 (NCT07021898), respectively. Based on clinical experience with daraxonrasib, this emerging drug class appears generally well-tolerated, with most adverse events consisting of clinically manageable gastrointestinal (e.g., nausea, vomiting, diarrhea) and dermatologic (rash, stomatitis, onycholysis) toxicities [99].

Mutant-selective G12D inhibitors are currently spearheaded by the KRASG12D(ON) inhibitor, zoldonrasib (RMC-9805), which has advanced to preparation of a phase III clinical investigation in PDAC (RASolute 305, RASolute 309), while KRASG12D(OFF) inhibitors, such as LY3962673 (NCT06586515), or KRASG12D(ON/OFF) inhibitors, such as VS-7375 (also known as GFH375) (NCT07020221), or HRS-4642 (NCT06520488), are equally under investigation, with reported promising preliminary results [100, 101].

Mutant-selective G12V inhibitors such as the so-called G12V-preferring multi-KRAS(ON/OFF) inhibitor QTX3544 [102] or the KRASG12V(ON) inhibitor RMC-5127 [103], will likely complement the therapeutic landscape soon.

Although, based on early-phase clinical data, these (K)RAS-targeting compounds show promising efficacy and a manageable safety profile. In the first-line setting, reported ORR ranges around 50% or up to 60% if combined with chemotherapy [100], while second-line ORR has been reported to around 30–40% [100]. In addition, a relevant proportion of disease stabilization could be achieved, resulting in a disease control rate of around 90%. In RASolute 302, daraxonrasib demonstrated a substantial improvement in median overall survival (mOS) compared with investigator’s choice treatment in the overall population (13.2 versus 6.7 months, hazard ratio (HR) 0.4). The median progression-free survival (mPFS) for daraxonrasib versus investigator’s choice of treatment in the overall population was 7.2 months and 3.6 months, respectively. While the FDA has permitted expanded access to daraxonrasib, regulatory approval remains pending. Although cross-trial comparisons should be interpreted with caution, this outcome approaches those reported for first-line chemotherapy regimens, such as the NAPOLI-3 trial (7.4 months).

The second class of KRAS-targeting MOAs involve targeted protein degradation (TPD). Here, the overarching aim is to deplete the tumor cell of (mutant) KRAS protein, primarily employing proteolysis targeting chimera (PROTACs) [104]. These compounds bispecifically bind to KRAS as well as an E3 ligase and, thereby, confer ubiquitination and eventually proteasomal degradation of KRAS protein. In a phase I trial, the KRASG12D-specific ASP3082 (setidegrasib) led to an ORR of 24% (n = 5/21) in second and mostly third-line treatment [105]. A phase III trial (NCT07409272) investigating the combination with mFOLRIFINOX/NALIRIFOX and setidegrasib is currently recruiting. Another KRASG12D-targeting PROTAC, ARV-806, is currently being investigated at phase I/II (NCT07023731). Taken together, targeted KRAS degradation complements the KRAS-targeting landscape with another MOA that potentially allows for an improved dose–toxicity balance.

Another group of emerging strategies involves KRAS-guided employment of the patient’s immune system. NW-301V, an autologous T-cell receptor therapy against KRAS G12V, was recently shown to be feasible with a reported ORR of 50% (n = 4/8) in metastatic pancreatic cancer (Bai et al., 2025 ESMO #1514O). Notably, G12V-specificity combined with engagement of HLA-A*11:01, which occurs at population frequency of 10–15% in the USA across ethnic groups [106], makes it a proof-of-concept rather than a therapeutic option outside of Asia, given an estimated 3% of eligible patients in pancreatic cancer. A cell therapeutic trial that investigates the autologous T-cell product NT-112, which targets KRAS G12D and is restricted to HLA-C*08:02 [107], is currently recruiting in the USA (NCT06218914).

Recent advances in vaccination strategies, vastly accelerated through developments during the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic, were recently introduced as emerging concepts in pancreatic cancer. “Off-the-shelf” mutant KRAS peptide-derived vaccination against two (ELI-002 2P) or seven (ELI-002 7P) KRAS variants are under clinical investigation, and preliminary results show expansion of KRAS-specific T cells [108, 109]. Early data on the combination of mutant KRAS peptide vaccination with a dual-checkpoint blockade shows KRAS-specific immune-engagement involving memory T cells, a requirement for potential durable antitumor immunity in resected patients [110]. If correlating with disease-free survival, these approaches may represent a future adjuvant treatment strategy for PDAC. The patient-agnostic formula makes it a feasible practical implementation with potential advantages over autogene cevumeran, a personalized mRNA vaccine [111]. In summary, multiple compounds with diverse MOAs for KRAS-targeting in pancreatic cancer are actively investigated in numerous clinical trials. Reported response rates are promising across disease stages and are accompanied by improvements in progression-free survival. However, preliminary clinical and early follow-up data suggest occurrence of both primary, i.e., tumor intrinsic, and acquired resistance [112].

Resistance Mechanisms

Although early-phase data demonstrate encouraging and previously unseen efficacy, limitations of monotherapy are already apparent. Identification of mechanisms of primary resistance is crucial for effective use of targeted therapy in clinical practice. On the genomic level for instance, primary resistance toward mutant-selective KRAS inhibition may arise from the (sub)clonal occurrence of another undetected oncogenic variant [113]. With regards to protein homeostasis, dysregulated KRAS wild-type degradation, resulting in accumulation of KRAS protein, was shown as a putative intrinsic resistance mechanism to RAS inhibition in lung cancer cell models [114]. Preclinical data also show that resistance to KRAS inhibition in PDAC can be conferred by cell state switching under evolutionary pressure [40, 115]. Notably, molecular subtypes cooccur and cell populations share the same genetic background [116]; therefore, KRAS signaling and KRAS dependency likely rewire upon cell state change as suggested by in vitro studies [40]. Past studies on PDAC highlight the importance of KRAS signaling in pancreatic cancer [117]. However, upon KRAS inhibition, tumors that are “addicted” to KRAS signaling, i.e., depletion results in cell cycle arrest or even cell death, and tumors that merely employ KRAS for outgrowth and environmental advantage but can compensate for its loss, may be exposed [118].

Resistance following initial response toward KRAS inhibition is frequently classified into “acquired” or “adaptive,” dependent on the underlying mechanisms being genetic or nongenetic. A common theme across mechanisms of resistance following response is the (partial) reactivation of ERK signaling. Under mutant selective inhibition, nongenetic mechanisms were shown to compensate ERK signaling through upregulation or overexpression of upstream RTKs, thereby activating wild-type KRAS and their paralogs [119]. Of note, adaptive resistance often involves rapid processes including transcriptional and signaling feedback loops and falls on a time scale prior to the first staging after 8–12 weeks in the clinical setting, hence may be observed as primary resistance.

On the genetic level, second-hit mutations in KRAS, upstream RTKs or downstream BRAF, or MEK1/2 equally result in ERK-reactivation, hence may confer acquired resistance to KRAS inhibition [40]. Notably, early evidence from the RAS(ON) inhibitor, daraxonrasib, also shows MAPK activating mutations, but as suggested by its MOA, no enrichment of activating on-target KRAS mutations [112]. However, a recent study reports RAS and RAF point mutations that may affect tricomplex formation or RAS–RAF interaction [120]. Amplification of RTKs, such as EGFR or MET, as well as KRAS, MYC, or YAP1 represent additional genetically acquired resistance mechanisms.

Taken together, KRAS-targeting strategies will likely soon redefine the treatment landscape in pancreatic cancer therapy. To overcome primary or secondary resistance, novel combination strategies, early detection, and potential reversal of resistance mechanism, also including emerging targets beyond KRAS, will be critical.

Opportunities to Transform PDAC Treatment—Combinations and Sequencing

The long-awaited emergence of KRAS-directed therapeutics now allow targeting of the core biology of PDAC and will help redefine the way we treat PDAC.

Real-world perturbation of KRAS biology will continue to refine our understanding of PDAC. Disease control rates approaching 90% validate the concept of KRAS dependency, yet the predominance of stable disease over complete remissions suggests that KRAS inhibition alone is unlikely to achieve durable responses. This is not a limitation but an opportunity: KRAS inhibition should be viewed not as a solution to PDAC but as the key to precision medicine in PDAC. Combination Strategies

From previous experience on KRASG12C inhibition in CRC, we know that compensational reactivation of ERK signaling can occur by amplified EGFR signaling. The combination of sotorasib and adagrasib with the anti-EGFR antibodies panitumumab or cetuximab, respectively, was shown to enhance response rates in CRC to a level comparable to NSCLC [121], and was hence approved for this indication. Similarly, in melanoma, resistance to BRAF inhibition was shown to be mediated by RTK upregulation [122]. The common theme of reactivation via rebound effects along the MAPK signaling pathway following KRAS inhibition results in the strategy of “vertical” RAS inhibition. In PDAC, inhibition of EGFR (NCT06770452), SOS1 (NCT06659341), or SHP2 (NCT04916236) are among the most prominent owing to their direct association with KRAS biology, and current body of preclinical data. SOS1 is a GEF that, downstream to RTK signaling, shifts KRAS toward its GTP-bound/ON state. SHP2 on the other hand, was shown to play an important role in linking RTK signaling to KRAS [123]. Preclinical data in lung cancer emphasizes synergy effects for combination of KRASG12C inhibition and SOS1 or SHP2, respectively, likely through SHOC2 and MRAS [124]. In PDAC, however, data are scarce and, given the strongly decreased GEF-dependence in G12D/V/R variants [30], efficacy is unknown.

As PDAC remains capable of evading pathway suppression, even after blockade of multiple MAPK nodes, a recent preclinical study elaborated on an opportunity arising from the underlying “orthogonal” or bypass signaling: inhibition of both SHP2 and MEK led to mitochondrial dysfunction that could be exploited by inhibition of glutathione peroxidase 4, eventually resulting in ferroptosis [125]. Challenging KRAS signaling together with immediate adaptation (“vertical” inhibition) and stress response (“orthogonal” inhibition) may currently be one of the most promising combination strategies. However, identification of the best partners is a highly active field, and updates on clinical trials will provide real-life input.

Valuable insights are likely gained by current trials that investigate the combination of chemotherapy with KRAS inhibition. While these combinations, in many cases, are primarily used as a head-to-head comparison rather than for synergy-finding, recent data suggest a mechanistic rationale for combination of KRAS inhibitors with chemotherapy. Specifically, the classical molecular subtype shows an increased resistance toward KRAS inhibition, whereas basal-like PDAC tends to be more sensitive [40, 115]. Preclinical data further suggest that classical cell states may serve as a reservoir for relapse [115]. Differences in KRAS dependency and downstream pathway wiring may contribute to subtype-specific responsiveness to KRAS inhibition; however, definitive mechanisms remain incompletely understood. Integrating these results with an improved response of the classical subtype toward chemotherapy, and particularly FOLFIRINOX [39], provides a rationale for expected synergy effects. Preclinical data even suggest a partial reversion of therapy-induced senescence by KRAS inhibition [126]. Of note, while emerging mechanistic insights into molecular subtype-specific response help to better understand resistance, current evidence does not justify patient selection.

Finally, removing the KRAS influence in nonaddicted tumors potentially reveals dependence on secondary drivers. Although speculative given the lack of much resistance data under KRAS inhibition, one of these may originate from the relatively common RNF43-mutation. This E3 ligase, among others, targets the frizzled receptor from canonical WNT signaling and relays it to proteasomal degradation. In the presence of inactivating mutations, it was shown to confer WNT dependence [127]. Although hypothetical, WNT signaling by itself is a potent cancer driver [128] and may keep PDAC cells active in the absence of KRAS signaling. With porcupine inhibitors under investigation, secondary WNT dependency may eventually be targetable [129].

Taken together, recent data suggest a large variety of resistance mechanisms following KRAS inhibition. To detect these as early, and counter them where targeted therapies exist, is a major part for upcoming precision strategies in pancreatic cancer.

The preclinical and clinical investigation of combinatorial strategies is rapidly evolving. While we discussed representative principles and emerging concepts, a comprehensive overview of all ongoing preclinical and clinical studies is beyond the scope of this narrative review.

Patients with Wild-Type RAS

With the rapid emergence of KRAS-targeted therapies, a clinically relevant question is the expected response in patients with PDAC that do not harbor a KRAS alteration. This subgroup, comprising approximately 10% of patients, is enriched for alternative targetable genomic alterations, but is not eligible for mutant-selective KRAS-targeted strategies. A preliminary answer can be inferred from the results of the RASolute 302 trial, which also enrolled patients with RAS wild-type tumors. However, consistent with the low prevalence of RAS wild-type PDAC, only nine patients were enrolled in the experimental arm, and seven patients in the chemotherapy arm [96]. The overall population reached its primary end point; therefore, regulatory approval may not be restricted according to RAS status. One might argue, though, that the overall effect was influenced by the predominance of KRAS G12D- and G12V-mutated PDAC (86.4%). The exploratory subgroup analysis, notably grouping wild-type RAS together with RAS G13 and Q61 alterations, suggested a hazard ratio of 0.37 (95% CI 0.15−0.93) for daraxonrasib (n = 20), compared with investigator’s choice treatment (n = 21). Based on the heterogeneous aggregation and small sample size, these findings should be interpreted with caution.

The observed activity of daraxonrasib in RAS wild-type PDAC is plausible though, as several non-KRAS driver alterations converge on MAPK pathway signaling, in which RAS functions as a central signaling node. Until prospective trials directly compare RAS-directed approaches across RAS status, these findings and current data support unselected RAS inhibition rather than selection on the basis of molecular subgroups.

Sequencing Strategies

The optimal sequencing of KRAS-targeting strategies within existing therapy algorithms remains to be defined. As an oncogene-directed strategy, a future first-line positioning appears mandatory. Key questions remain which MOAs should be combined to achieve maximal tumor response and minimal on-target, off-tumor toxicity. Both synergistic and sequential combinations may improve response; however, alternating algorithms may ease toxicity. Even in the case of resistance through ERK-reactivation, off phases may result in MAPK hyperactivation and senescence [130].

Immunotherapy Combinations

Beyond its molecular driver functions in tumor cell biology, emerging evidence highlights the role of oncogenic KRAS as a regulator of the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) that is characteristic of PDAC [131]. The immuno-cold environment that is associated with unresponsiveness to conventional ICI is increasingly linked to KRAS signaling [132]. Consistent with this observation, preclinical studies demonstrate immune infiltration and antitumor response following inhibition of KRAS [133]. This may be further amplified through immunotherapeutic combinations [134]. As durable tumor remission will likely require immune engagement, combination of KRAS inhibition with immunotherapy potentially represents one of the most impactful strategies.

New Approaches Beyond Targeting RAS

Methylthioadenosine Phosphorylase (MTAP) Loss

A decade ago, it was shown that deficiency of the enzyme methylthioadenosine phosphorylase (MTAP), which occurs in around 20–25% of pancreatic cancers, results in increased dependency on the arginine-methylating and transcription regulatory enzyme PRMT5 [135]. Phase I clinical trial data on AMG 193, a PRMT5 inhibitor, showed an ORR of 20% across tumor types [136]. It is currently being assessed in a phase II/III trial in combination with gemcitabine/nab-paclitaxel (NCT07076121). Another compound, BMS-986504, showed a similar cross-entity ORR of 23%, including 41 patients with PDAC [137]. In summary, targeting the synthetic vulnerability arising from MTAP deficiency shows promising cross-entity activity, while in PDAC, MTAP targeting may be most effective in combination with KRAS inhibition rather than as a standalone strategy [138]. This strategy is currently being investigated in early phase clinical trials such as NCT06922591 (active, recruiting) and NCT06360354 (active, nonrecruiting).

Antibody–Drug Conjugates

Antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs) have shown activity across a broad spectrum of cancers, while their clinical activity in PDAC seems limited to date [139, 140]. The results of the phase I/II IMMU-132-01 basket trial investigating the TROP2-directed ADC sacituzumab govitecan (SG), suggest a potential role of the payload. SG’s payload is SN-38, the active metabolite of the topoisomerase I inhibitor, irinotecan. Notably, in the pancreatic cancer (n = 16) and colorectal cancer (n = 31) subgroups, cancer types that are especially exposed to irinotecan in first- or second-line, the objective response rates were 0% and 3.2%, respectively. As these patients were enrolled after progression on previous, presumably irinotecan-based therapies, preexisting resistance to the payload may, at least in part, explain the limited efficacy observed.

Promising early data have emerged for novel ADCs. EBC-129, targeting glycosylated CEACAM5/6, achieved an ORR of 24% (5/21) in PDAC in a phase I trial (Lentz et al., 2025; NCT05701527) [141]. The c-Met-directed ADC ABBV-400 showed an ORR of 15% (4/26) with FOLFIRINOX and 40% (6/15) with gemcitabine/nab-paclitaxel in the second-line setting [142].

In summary, few promising approaches beyond targeting KRAS have emerged in pancreatic cancer. Among these, synthetic vulnerability arising from MTAP loss and antibody–drug-conjugates, show most promising activity, if not as monotherapy, potentially in combination with other therapeutic modalities. Of note, the advent of KRAS inhibitors might allow for reintroduction of therapeutics that have previously failed in PDAC.

Outlook and Conclusions

Therapeutic strategies for pancreatic cancer have evolved substantially over the past two decades. Although numerous innovative concepts have ultimately failed to replace systemic chemotherapy, these efforts have generated valuable biological insights. Pancreatic cancer is a molecularly heterogeneous and biologically highly plastic disease, and it has become increasingly evident that the ceiling for unstratified strategies was reached. Until recently, only a rare subgroup—comprising approximately 5% of cases harboring non-KRAS MAPK alterations—was eligible for targeted therapies. The recent emergence of (K)RAS-directed therapies holds the potential to substantially expand this population. Realizing this opportunity requires the integration of pancreatic cancer care into structured precision medicine programs. As preclinical and clinical efforts increasingly focus on overcoming adaptive resistance and optimizing therapeutic sequencing, pancreatic cancer care stands at the threshold of a new era—one that is complex, precision-guided, and potentially transformative.

Precision medicine in PDAC requires baseline molecular characterization (dMMR/MSI, germline BRCA1/2, KRAS; expanded NGS; and RNA panels if KRAS-wild-type) and repeated profiling at progression under targeted therapy to detect mechanisms of resistance. As the need for repeated testing expands, liquid biopsy might become essential for longitudinal monitoring and detection of emerging markers of resistance.

In summary, we have arrived at the doorstep of a new era in the treatment of pancreatic cancer. Our substantial knowledge of the molecular biology of pancreatic cancer, as well as experiences from other malignancies, in which the advent of targeted therapies redefined the diagnostic and clinical management of patients, will help us to confidently move ahead to deliver more effective treatment strategies to our patients and hopefully transform the management of yet another hard-to-treat malignancy.
