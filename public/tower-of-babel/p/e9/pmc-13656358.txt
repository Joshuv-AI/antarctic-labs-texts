Contributions of dorsal medial prefrontal cortex GABAergic mu opioid receptors on acute restraint stress-induced anxiety-like behavior
Authors: Yinan Du, Fenghua Xu, Junmin Zhang, Yukui Zhao, Shuyi Liu, Yuan Chang, Yiwei Liu, Yingchun Yang, Yingying Li, Hongyu Qin, Chunling Wei, Qiaohua Zheng, Yanning Qiao, Yihui Liu, Wei Ren, Jing Han, Aozhuo Zhang, Zhiqiang Liu, Fei Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The body’s defensive emotional reaction to stress presents as anxiety. Nevertheless, long-term anxious stimuli can cause pathological anxiety disorders together with endocrine system, neural system, and immune system dysfunctions. Thus, researchers should understand the neural processes that govern anxiety during stress to create preventive and care plans. By combining behavioral tests, chemogenetic strategies, c-fos stainings, and whole-cell patch clamp recordings, this study looked into the function of dorsal medial prefrontal cortex (dmPFC) mu opioid receptors (MORs), specifically GABAergic MORs (MORGABA), in anxiety-like behavior brought on by acute restraint stress (ARS). The dmPFC MORGABA is triggered during ARS. This activation reduced the inhibitory synaptic input onto projection neurons that target the basolateral amygdala (BLA) by suppressing the activity of neighboring dmPFC GABAergic interneurons. In turn, mice that showed anxiety-like behavior also had higher neural activity of dmPFC-BLA projection neurons. When combined, these results describe a local, MORGABA-mediated dmPFC pathway that governs acute stress-induced anxiety reactions. These findings could offer a possible logical foundation for dmPFC-focused medication screening and anxiety treatment.

Introduction

Anxiety is a combative mental response that arises in reaction to stressful situations, namely injury, severe workloads, or threat. Acute anxiety reactions can help people adjust to abrupt fluctuations in their environment [1]. However, when activated repeatedly or over an extended period of time, these reactions progress to pathological anxiety disorders. Such conditions are related to severe dysfunctions of endocrine, neurological, and even immune systems [2, 3]. Thus, developing preventative and curative measures requires an understanding the neural mechanisms governing anxiety reactions during stress.

In the central nervous system (CNS), the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) involves the modulation of anxiety-related behavior in response to stress [4]. Per morphological and biochemical research, mice exposed to stressors show raised expression of neuronal activity markers, including c-fos (an immediate early gene), and promote glutamate and dopamine (excitatory neurotransmitters) release in the mPFC; therefore, the mPFC can participate in stress reaction control [5, 6]. Anatomically, the mPFC, specifically the dorsal mPFC (dmPFC) projects widely to the basolateral amygdala (BLA), lateral septum, and hypothalamus, all emotion-regulation areas [7–9]. Thus, stress-related impulses can be transmitted quickly and effectively. Researchers have highlighted the key role of mPFC in controlling emotions by showing that dmPFC-BLA projections are crucial for stress-related anxiety-like behavior [7].

Despite the well-established role of the dmPFC-BLA projection in stress-induced anxiety, the local neuromodulatory mechanisms, particularly those involving the endogenous opioid system (EOS) that govern this pathway remain poorly understood. Given that stress potently engages the EOS, elucidating the underlying cellular mechanisms is critical for understanding how the dmPFC-BLA pathway functions under stressful conditions [10–12]. These local molecular mechanisms may control anxiety-like behavior that is dependent on the dmPFC.

The EOS is a stress-triggered neuromodulatory system in the CNS [13]. It primarily consists of kappa (KORs), delta (DORs), and mu opioid receptors (MORs) [14]. Among them, MORs appear to be the most sensitive to stress [15, 16]. MORs are expressed not only in glutamatergic neurons (MORGlut) but also in GABAergic neurons (MORGABA) [17]. Through their properties as inhibitory G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), these receptors regulate synaptic transmission and are behaviorally involved in the modulation of stress responses [15–17]. Recent study clarified that MORs in the dmPFC govern the activity of neural networks during acute stress [17]. Thus, they can modify behavior attributed to stress-driven anxiety. To test this hypothesis, three mouse models that were selectively knocked down for MORGlut, MORGABA, and dmPFC whole MORs were created. Applying chemogenetic, morphological, and electrophysiological methods in conjunction with anxiety-like behavioral tests, dmPFC MOR helps in the regulation of anxiety-like actions brought on by acute restraint stress (ARS). Specifically, MORGABA, rather than MORGlut, controls the mentioned processes. Furthermore, ARS substantially triggers dmPFC-BLA projections, and this activation largely depends on MORGABA. Mechanistically, MORGABA blocked dmPFC-BLA projections that induce anxiety-like behavior during restraint stress by suppressing the activity of dmPFC inhibitory interneurons. Taken together, these studies highlight the physiological role of dmPFC MORGABA in controlling anxiety. This mechanistic understanding could theoretically support new diagnostic indicators and treatments for anxiety disorders.

Materials and methods

Animals

The present study involved 79 male C57BL/6J mice and 40 male Oprm1flox/flox mice (both 8 to 14 weeks) following the Animal Protection Committee of Xi’an Medical University licenses, the Animal Protection Committee of Shaanxi Normal University licenses and the Chinese Animal Protection Commission guidelines. Female mice were not included in the experimental design to avoid estrous cycle-related variability in opioid responses. The Experimental Animal Center of Shaanxi Normal University MOE Key Laboratory of Modern Teaching Technology supplied all animals. Each separately vented cage contained five to six animals, with a 12-h light and dark cycle. The humidity was maintained at 60 ± 10%, and the temperature was 21 ± 1 ℃. From 8:00 to 12:00 A.M., all behavioral tests were conducted. To minimize individual variances, all animals had the opportunity to adjust to the surroundings for 5 min each day for 3 days.

ARS protocol

The ARS procedure has been explained previously [18]. Animals were kept in vented cylindrical plastic tubes (inner diameter: 30 mm; length of the straight part: 80 mm) under gentle restraint. Then, each restrained mouse spent 2 h in a separate ventilated enclosure to create stress. For each mouse, the ARS procedure was uniformly initiated at approximately 9:00 a.m. in a quiet, consistent environment with 22 ℃ and 20 lx. Animals in the unstressed (US) group, i.e., controls, were not physically restrained; instead, they spent 2 h in the same vented enclosures.

Emotional behavioral tests

Open-field tests (OFTs) and elevated plus maze (EPM) tests were conducted per previous research [19, 20]. The equipment for OFTs included four opaque walls (30 cm high) enclosing a 40 cm × 40 cm arena. For 10 min, each animal was left to roam freely in a corner that faced the walls. A video camera system was used to continually record the amount of time spent in the central region (20 × 20 cm) and the overall distance covered. Noldus Information Technology’s EthoVision software 1.9 (USA) was used for analysis. The EPM apparatus was 50 cm above the ground and had four arms: two open and two closed. These arms measured 30 cm in length and 7 cm in width. The open arms were left uncovered, whereas the closed arms were encased by opaque walls that were 16 cm high. All arms were connected by a central deck that measured 7 cm × 7 cm. For 5 min, each animal was left to roam freely after being positioned in the middle, facing an open arm. A video camera setup was used to capture the behavior, and Noldus Information Technology’s EthoVision software 1.9 (USA) helped analyze the recordings. The number of open-arm entries, time spent in the open arms, and overall distance covered were computed. The OFTs or EPM test equipment was carefully cleaned and disinfected with 75% ethyl alcohol after each emotional test.

Fluorescence in situ hybridization

Using our previously published techniques [17], MOR deletion in the dmPFC of MORGlut cKD, MOR cKD, and MORGABA cKD mice was detected via fluorescence in situ hybridization [17, 21]. Pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg body weight) was injected intraperitoneally to induce profound anesthesia, and the animals were perfused for 5 min with 0.9% saline. At -22 ℃, Sakura Finetek’s optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound (catalog no: Tissue-Tek 4583; Torrance, CA, USA) helped quickly harvest and implant the brains. Leica Biosystems’ CM1950 cryostat (Nussloch, Germany) was used to prepare mPFC in frozen slices (16 μm). At room temperature, these slices were dehydrated using a graded ethanol sequence (50%, 75%, and 100%), with each stage extending for 5 min, after being fixed at 4 °C in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15 min. Then, these slices underwent 15 min of hydrogen peroxide treatment and 20 min of protease IV treatment. ACD’s Advanced Cell Diagnostics (Newark, CA, USA) provided the probes that target Vgat (catalog no: 424548-C3), Oprm1 (catalog no: 544731-C2), and Vglut1 (catalog no: 416631-C1). These probes were conjugated to Atto 650, Atto 570, and Atto 520, respectively. ACD’s HybEZ™ oven helped with in situ hybridization per the manufacturer’s directions for the RNAscope Multiplex Fluorescent Reagent Kit (catalog no: 323100; ACD). Finally, an anti-fade mounting medium containing DAPI was used to mount these slices; Zeiss’ fluorescence microscope (ObercKOchen, Germany) helped capture pictures. Image count and calculation were analyzed using ImageJ.

Immunofluorescence for c-fos staining

In MORGABA CKD and MORGABA WT mice, the c-fos immunofluorescence test was used to assess the activating impact of dmPFC-BLA projections (before and 1 h after restraint stress). The experiment followed the techniques stated before [17]. Briefly, sodium pentobarbital was used to induce profound anesthesia in all animals; then, they were transcardially perfused with 0.9% saline as well as 4% paraformaldehyde (added to 0.1 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS)) in order. After being extracted and post-fixed for 24 h, the brains were cryoprotected by immersing them in 30% sucrose (added to 0.1 M PBS) for 48 h at 4 ℃. Next, 20 μm frozen slices comprising the mPFC were created after embedding the brain slices in OCT compound. These slices were initially allowed to adjust to room temperature before being blocked in PBS containing 0.5% Triton X-100 and 10% non-immune donkey serum for 60 min to minimize non-specific binding for immunofluorescence labeling. They were cultivated with Cell Signaling Technology’s rabbit anti-c-fos primary antibody (1:500; #2250; Danvers, MA, USA) for the entire night at 4 °C. The following day, these slices were incubated with Abcam’s Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated donkey anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:1000; ab150076; Boston, MA, USA) for 2 h in a dark-proof environment after being rinsed with 0.1 M PBS thrice. Finally, after applying an anti-fade mounting medium to these sections, a fluorescence microscope helped capture fluorescence images. Image J was used to analyze the image’s count and calculation.

Electrophysiological recording

Alterations in synaptic transmission of dmPFC-BLA projections were investigated through electrophysiological recordings. The procedures were conducted as published earlier [22]. Mice were quickly decapitated after being sedated with 50 mg/kg pentobarbital sodium. Brains were extracted immediately after and placed at 4 ℃ in oxygenated adjusted artificial cerebrospinal fluid (sucrose-substituted ACSF). ACSF composition (in mM) was 2.5 KCl, 250 sucrose, 1.2 NaH₂PO₄, 1.2 MgCl₂, 26 NaHCO₃, 2.4 CaCl₂, and 11 glucose. Then, the brains were immersed in 4 °C ACSF after being placed on Vibratome Company’s vibrating slicer (1000 Plus; St. Louis, MO, USA). Next, 300-µm-thick coronal sections comprising the dmPFC were cut and cultivated at room temperature in oxygenated standard ACSF (pH 7.4) for 30 min. ACSF consisted of the following concentrations (in mM): 2.5 KCl, 125 NaCl, 2 MgCl₂, 1.25 NaH₂PO₄, 25 glucose, 2 CaCl₂, and 25 NaHCO₃. These slices were moved to an immersion recording container after incubation, where they were superfused with 2 mL/min oxygenated standard ACSF. Molecular Devices’ Multiclamp 700B amplifier (Sunnyvale, CA, USA) was used for whole-cell readings. EGFP-positive dmPFC-BLA projections were the focus of these readings. A DM LFSA upright microscope (Leica, Germany) fitted with a 488-nm fluorescent module and infrared differential interference contrast optics helped visualize these neurons. When loaded with the internal solution containing 60 CsCl, 10 HEPES, 80 CsCH₃SO₃, 2 MgCl₂, 2 QX-314, 4 MgATP, 0.2 EGTA, 0.3 Na₂GTP, and 10 Na₂-phosphocreatine (pH adjusted to 7.2 using CsOH) (in mM), the recording pipettes exhibited a resistance of 4 to 8 MΩ. To record mIPSCs, whole-cell patch-clamp experiments were conducted at a holding potential of -70 mV. The recording solution contained TTX (1 µM, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) to isolate miniature synaptic events, as well as AP5 (100 µM, Sigma, USA) and DNQX (100 µM, Sigma, USA) to block NMDA and AMPA receptors, thereby eliminating excitatory currents. Clampfit 10.5 was used for mIPSC analysis.

Statistical analysis

SPSS version 22.0 was used to analyze the data, which are displayed as mean ± standard error of the mean (S.E.M.), or as mean ± S.E.M. % (in some cases). The following tests were conducted: one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), repeated ANOVA, two-way ANOVA, paired Student’s t test, and unpaired Student’s t test. After each ANOVA, Sidak’s multiple-comparisons tests were conducted. In every experiment, the significance level was chosen as p < 0.05. Figure legends elucidate the statistical data.

Results

ARS induces dmPFC MOR-dependent anxiety-like behavior

MORs modulate emotional responses [11, 23], but the mechanisms underlying dmPFC MORs remain poorly understood. To this end, the ARS protocol, a common paradigm of acute anxiety-like behavior [18], was used in both MOR WT and MOR cKD mice for emotional behavioral tests. First, the results affirmed that the ARS protocol successfully elicited anxiety-like behavior (Fig. 1A). As anticipated, animals that received ARS showed markedly stronger anxiety-like behavior, compared with the US group. Specifically, animals exposed to ARS spent a shorter span in the center zone of the OFTs (Figs. 1B, C). Moreover, in EPM tests, they spent a shorter period in the open arms and demonstrated fewer open arm entries (Figs. 1D, E). Notably, neither the OFT (Fig. 1C) nor the EPM tests (Fig. 1E) showed any significant difference in the overall distance covered between the US and ARS groups, suggesting that behavioral changes were attributed to anxiety rather than motor abnormalities. Next, the role of dmPFC MORs on modifying anxiety-like behavior was investigated. dmPFC MOR cKD and MOR WT mice were created as described earlier (Figs. 2A, D). The knockdown efficiency was validated by fluorescence in situ hybridization and subsequent cell counting tests, which confirmed significantly reduced MOR expression in the dmPFC of MOR cKO mice (Figs. 2B, C). By spending a shorter period in the center zone during OFTs (Fig. 2E) and a shorter span in the open arms, along with fewer open arm entries during EPM tests (Fig. 2F), MOR WT mice exposed to ARS demonstrated significant anxiety-like responses in behavioral tests. However, MOR cKD mice did not exhibit these ARS-mediated alterations (Figs. 2E, F). Furthermore, MOR cKD mice in both US and ARS groups showed comparably low levels of anxiety-like responses (Figs. 2E, F). Thus, MOR knockdown inhibited the expression of behavioral abnormalities produced by ARS. Additionally, neither the OFTs nor the EPM test results demonstrated any variations in the total distance covered between the groups (Figs. 2E, F), confirming that the observed behavioral effects were not attributable to altered locomotion. These results suggest the crucial function of dmPFC MORs in regulating ARS-mediated anxiety-like behavior.

dmPFC MORGABA, but not MORGlut, modulates anxiety-like behavior

The mentioned results demonstrated that dmPFC MORs have a functional role in regulating ARS-induced acute anxiety-like reactions. However, MORs are expressed in both excitatory glutamatergic neurons and inhibitory GABAergic neurons in the dmPFC [24]. Clarifying the mechanism by which dmPFC MORs contribute to regulating anxiety-like behavior is therefore warranted (Figs. 3D and 4D). To this end, MORGABA cKD and dmPFC MORGlut cKD mice, along with their corresponding wild-type controls (MORGABA WT and MORGlut WT mice, respectively) were created (Figs. 3A and 4A). Fluorescence in situ hybridization results validated the successful knockdown of dmPFC MORGlut in MORGlut cKD mice (Figs. 3B, C) and of dmPFC MORGABA in MORGABA cKD mice (Figs. 4B, C). Next, these models helped confirm the role of MORGlut and MORGABA in regulating anxiety-like behavior.

Both MORGlut cKD mice and their WT controls showed markedly increased anxiety-like responses after being exposed to ARS behavioral tests. Moreover, they spent significantly shorter periods in the central area during OFTs (Fig. 3E). Additionally, MORGlut cKD and MORGlut WT mice spent a shorter span in the open arms during EPM tests, besides exhibiting fewer open arm entries (Fig. 3F), compared with the corresponding US group. These results suggest a non-essential role of MORGlut in regulating ARS-dependent anxiety-like behavior. This is because MOR knockdown in glutamatergic neurons did not change ARS-induced anxiety tendencies.

For MORGABA mice, however, the results were notably different. Compared with their US controls, only MORGABA WT mice that received ARS exhibited significant anxiety-like behavior, as confirmed by the shorter span spent in the OFTs center zone (Fig. 4E), along with less time spent in the EPM open arms and fewer open arm entries (Fig. 4F). Interestingly, MORGABA cKD US mice and MORGABA cKD mice that received ARS showed comparable emotional responses in both OTFs and EPM tests (Figs. 4E, F). Thus, dmPFC MORGABA is central to modulating ARS-triggered anxiety-like behavior. Locomotor activity did not differ among groups in the OFT or EPM test (Figs. 3E and F and 4E and F), confirming behavioral assessment reliability.

dmPFC MORGABA modulates activity of dmPFC-BLA projections during anxiety-like behavior

Anxiety-like behavior is predominantly regulated by dmPFC MORGABA under ARS exposure. The dmPFC sends numerous excitatory projections to the BLA, which govern the production and maintenance of anxiety-like behavior in response to acute stress [7, 10]. It is well proofed that optogenetic stimulation of the dmPFC-BLA pathway promoted enhanced anxiety-related behaviors in rodents [7]. Thus, it was hypothesized that MORGABA-mediated regulation of dmPFC-BLA projection activity is vital for ARS-induced anxiety. Behavioral tests were conducted in conjunction with c-Fos staining and chemogenetic techniques to affirm these phenomena.

First, whether ARS-induced anxiety-like behavior necessitates increased activity of dmPFC-BLA projections was evaluated (Figs. 5A-C). As described earlier [25, 26], an inhibitory designer receptors exclusively activated by designer drugs (DREADDs) approach under ARS was used to specifically inhibit these projections (Figs. 5A, B). Animals received intracranial cannula injections of either saline as a control (hM4Di + SAL group) or clozapine-N-oxide (CNO) to suppress dmPFC–BLA projections after 28 days of viral expression as our previous study described [17].

There was an insignificant difference in emotional performances between the hM4Di + CNO US and hM4Di + SAL US groups in both OFTs and EPM tests (Figs. 5D, E). MORGABA modulates dmPFC-BLA projection activity by decreasing presynaptic GABAergic neurotransmission

Through specific “disinhibition” mechanisms, MORGABA increases the activity of dmPFC pyramidal projections [17]. Thus, it was hypothesized that dmPFC MORGABA may regulate anxiety-like behavior after ARS exposure through comparable pathways. To test this hypothesis, whole-cell patch-clamp recordings were used to identify GABAergic inputs onto dmPFC-BLA projections (Figs. 7A, B). The frequency of mIPSCs decreased after ARS exposure, but not their amplitude, which is in line with earlier observations. Upon comparing MORGABA WT ARS mice to MORGABA WT US mice, mIPSC events demonstrated a decrease in the normalized mIPSC frequency (Fig. 7C, D), along with a shift to the right in the cumulative frequency distribution (Figs. 7C, D). Therefore, ARS exposure reduces presynaptic GABAergic inputs onto dmPFC-BLA projections. However, MORGABA cKD mice did not exhibit these alterations. Neither the normalized mIPSC frequency (Figs. 7C, D) nor the cumulative frequency distribution of mIPSC events (Figs. 7C, D) demonstrated any significant difference between MORGABA cKD ARS mice and MORGABA cKD US mice. We also found no significant difference in mIPSC frequencies between the MORGABA WT US group and the MORGABA cKD US group, indicating that MORGABA lacks tonic inhibition at baseline but is selectively engaged under stress. Furthermore, the mIPSC frequency and amplitude did not differ between the MORGABA WT US and MORGABA cKD US groups (Figs. 7C-F), suggesting that MORGABA did not affect basal GABAergic transmission.

Discussion

The results demonstrated that dmPFC MORGABA substantially regulates anxiety-like behavior after ARS exposure. MORGABA enhances the functional activity of dmPFC-BLA projections by decreasing inhibitory inputs, which causes elevated anxiety-like reactions in behavioral tests conducted under ARS. These results provide a specific dmPFC MORGABA-mediated circuit mechanism underscoring anxiety brought on by acute stress.

Pain, composure, addiction, and fear memory are some behavioral traits largely modulated by MORs as a prototypical inhibitory GPCR [23, 27, 28]. Researchers have demonstrated the mechanism by which opioid receptors control emotional reactions [21, 29, 30]. For example, GABAergic plasticity induced by MOR activation in the striatum promotes negative affective states [29]. Likewise, MORs in the habenula drive unpleasant effects in a project-specific manner [30]. However, the precise function of cortical MORs in controlling emotional responses remains elusive. It is well established that the mPFC is critically involved in the regulation of emotional responses [7, 19]. Notably, the dmPFC, which encompassing most of the prelimbic (PrL) area and a dorsal part of the infralimbic (IL) area, sends extensive excitatory projections to the BLA [7, 19, 31]. These projections are positively activated under stress stimulation and contribute to the induction of anxiety-like behaviors [7, 19]. Morphological studies have revealed abundant MOR expression in the dmPFC, which is strongly implicated in the regulation of emotional responses in response to stress stimuli [17]. Our experiments have provided evidence supporting this hypothesis. By integrating conditional knockdown techniques with behavioral tests, this study confirms that dmPFC MORs are central to ARS-induced anxiety-like behavior. In MOR cKD mice, ARS-induced decrease in central exploration during OFT, as well as the time spent and entries in the open arms during EPM, were reversed. These results emphasize dmPFC MORs for inducing and sustaining elevated anxiety-like reactions under acute stress. Notably, dmPFC MOR systems react strongly to acute stress [17]. During acute stress, MORs in the CNS can quickly enhance the release of EOS, such as beta-endorphin and endomorphin [32]. Thus, acute stress-induced analgesia (ASIA) and conditional fear memory are two stress-related mechanisms that have been implicated in this activation [33, 34]. This study builds on existing evidence by elucidating the role of dmPFC MORs in ARS-induced anxiety-like behavior. Together, these findings expand on the knowledge of MOR-mediated control in the dmPFC and could theoretically explain physiological as well as pathological behavioral processes. The stress response is an instinctive reaction that promotes rapid escape from noxious stimuli. It triggers protective effects such as analgesia and heightened vigilance, while also inducing transient anxiety-like emotional disturbances [35–37]. This suggests that stress both defends and, to some extent, drives avoidance of the stressor. Our findings highlight the involvement of dmPFC MOR in this process, providing a theoretical framework for better understanding the complex regulatory logic of adaptive responses to environmental challenges.

Interestingly, dmPFC MORs are expressed in both glutamatergic and GABAergic neurons in the CNS [17]. MORs frequently confer opposing physiological effects on these two subtypes [22]. Thus, the present study investigated how MORGABA and MORGlut contributed diversely to ARS-induced anxiety-like behavior. MORGlut did not regulate these processes, whereas MORGABA did. In behavioral tests, both MORGlut WT mice and MORGlut cKD mice showed consistently significant anxiety-like reactions after ARS exposure. In contrast, MORGABA cKD mice did not demonstrate strong anxiety-like reactions observed in MORGABA WT mice under ARS. These results support the predominant role of dmPFC MORGABA in ARS-induced anxiety-like behavior. This finding is corroborated by our previous and present morphological results, which showed abundant MOR expression in dmPFC GABAergic interneurons [17]. In contrast, only 30% of dmPFC glutamatergic neurons expressed MORs [17]. MORGABA likely serves as the primary target of dmPFC MOR activation, wherein MOR engagement suppresses GABAergic interneuron activity, consistent with our earlier findings [17]. Thus, this disinhibition can subsequently activate excitatory pyramidal neurons originating from the dmPFC [17, 38]. The function of dmPFC MORs in ARS-induced anxiety-like reactions appears to depend on this mechanism, which was further supported by c-fos staining, and whole-cell patch clamp observations. These results show promise for clinical translation. Specifically targeting MORGABA within the dmPFC microcircuit, as opposed to systemic opioid receptors, could provide a more refined therapeutic approach for anxiety with a reduced side effects. In addition to emotional regulation, the dmPFC is involved in numerous higher-order cortical processes, such as pain modulation, decision making and language processing. Previous work has shown that targeted knockdown of MOR in cortical vGlut1-positive neurons produces antinociceptive-like responses in mice under moderate stress conditions [24]. Thus, MORGlut may contribute to these sensory or cognitive functions, despite its limited role in ARS-induced anxiety-like reactions.

The present study further demonstrated the contribution of dmPFC-BLA projections in ARS-induced anxiety-like behavior and emphasized how dmPFC MORGABA regulates these projections. The activity of dmPFC-BLA projections is strongly correlated with emotional disorder responses. Optogenetic activation of dmPFC-BLA projections specifically induce anxiety-like behaviors in the OFT. In contrast, inhibition of these projections markedly attenuate anxiety in rodents [7]. By using an inhibitory chemogenetic approach, suppressing dmPFC-BLA projection activity significantly reversed ARS-induced anxiety-like behavior. This finding is consistent with other research showing that the dmPFC-BLA pathway is a vital top-down modulator of behavior resembling anxiety and/or depression [7]. Notably, increased anxiety after exposure to several stressors has been associated with enhanced activity of these projections [7]. Furthermore, optogenetic activation of dmPFC-BLA projections intensifies anxiety-like reactions in both OFT and forced swim tests [7]. Our findings are in line with previous research, emphasizing the implications of dmPFC-BLA projections in anxiety-related behavior. Specifically, increased activity of dmPFC-BLA projections was strongly correlated with greater anxiety-like reactions during ARS. Moreover, dmPFC MORGABA modulates the activity of dmPFC-BLA projections under ARS, as evidenced by the use of c-fos staining. MORGABA WT mice demonstrated a higher frequency of spontaneous calcium signals after ARS, whereas MORGABA cKD mice showed a reverse effect. Likewise, MORGABA cKD mice did not exhibit the increased c-fos expression in dmPFC-BLA projections under ARS, which was observed in MORGABA WT mice. These results offer a molecular rationale for the increased dmPFC-BLA projection activity observed during acute stress and are in line with this MORGABA-controlled “disinhibition” hypothesis [39, 40].

The present study further looked into the electrophysiological mechanism by which dmPFC MORGABA controls dmPFC-BLA projections using whole-cell patch clamp readings. MORGABA lowered the GABAergic inputs to dmPFC-BLA projections, as indicated by decreased mIPSC frequencies in MORGABA WT mice but unaltered frequencies in MORGABA cKD mice after ARS, compared with US controls. Interestingly, MORs are common inhibitory GPCRs that effectively stop the inflow of sodium and calcium into neurons [41]. Consequently, after ARS, a significant decrease in GABAergic inputs to dmPFC-BLA projections was observed. In summary, the present study shows that dmPFC MORGABA is central to anxiety-like behavior caused by ARS exposure by integrating c-fos staining, and whole-cell patch clamp readings. Specifically, stress activated dmPFC MORGABA, which in turn reduced GABAergic inputs to dmPFC-BLA projections. This increased the neural activity of these projections and induced anxiety-like responses during behavioral tests.

Despite its strengths, this study has several limitations. First, notably, the findings are currently limited to male mice. This is because, in comparison to male mice, female mice exhibit non-negligible differences in the MOR-dependent modulation of emotional responses [42, 43]. Therefore, to obtain a deeper awareness of the modulatory role of dmPFC MORGABA in anxiety-like responses after ARS, future research involving female mice is needed. Second, this study only explored the modulation of MORGABA in dmPFC-BLA projections. However, the dmPFC also sends pyramidal projections to other areas of the brain, including the ventral tegmental area, laterodorsal tegmental nucleus, and median raphe nuclei, which are involved in emotional control [44, 45]. More research is necessary to determine how these projections contribute to anxiety-like behavior during ARS and how dmPFC MORGABA regulates them.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our present work demonstrated the physiological function of dmPFC MORGABA in controlling ARS induced anxiety-like responses by modulating of the activity of dmPFC-BLA projections. To decrease inhibitory inputs to dmPFC-BLA projections, the ARS efficiently enhanced the dmPFC MORGABA. Eventually, these projections became more active. Additionally, the active dmPFC-BLA pathway triggered by this disinhibition resulted in the expression of anxiety-like behavior. Through presynaptic disinhibition of dmPFC-BLA projections, our research demonstrated a projection-specific mechanism that correlates dmPFC MORGABA to ARS-induced anxiety reactions. By identifying a specific biochemical pathway by which dmPFC MORs influence anxiety-related behavior in acute stress, these results will provide a possible conceptual basis for diagnostic and therapeutic approaches targeted at anxiety disorders.
