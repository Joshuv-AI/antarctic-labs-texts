Zagreb indices of hyper carbon nanotube graphs
Authors: Ayesha Shabbir, Muhammad Faisal Nadeem, Yasir Bashir, Mohamad Nazri Husin, Yuslenita Muda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Carbon nanotubes are cylindrical structures composed entirely of carbon atoms, renowned for their exceptional mechanical strength, electrical conductivity, and thermal stability. These unique properties have made them a cornerstone of modern nanoscience. Hyper carbon nanotube structures take this innovation further by creating hierarchical architectures, where atoms and bonds in a base scaffold are systematically replaced with larger nanotube-derived building blocks. This approach results in intricate, multi-scale carbon frameworks with enhanced functionality and wide-ranging potential applications in nanoelectronics, energy-storage devices, sensing, composite reinforcement, and targeted nanomaterials designing. Topological indices are graph invariants that encode structural information of molecular graphs and are widely used in quantitative structure property and quantitative structure activity studies to correlate chemical structure with physico-chemical behavior. In this paper, we propose and formalize a graph-theoretic construction of hyper armchair carbon nanotubes and compute closed-form expressions for Zagreb-type degree-based topological indices in both additive and multiplicative settings for this constructed family of graphs. Moreover, we present numerical comparisons along with graphical illustrations for representative parameter choices. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first paper on topological indices for this family of graphs.

Introduction and preliminaries

Carbon nanotubes (CNTs) are prototypical one-dimensional \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$sp^{2}$$\end{document}-bonded carbon-based nanomaterials. They can be thought of as a sheet of graphene rolled into a nanometer-scale diameter cylinder of specific micrometer-scale length [1]. Ever since their first observation and successful scaled-up synthesis, CNTs have been at the very heart of nanomaterials research, characterized by their unique coupling of ultimate stiffness and strength values combined with extremely high electrical and thermal conductivity, low mass density, and a highly adaptable surface chemistry [2]. CNTs exist mostly as individual single-wall carbon nanotubes (SWCNTs), a single sheet of graphene rolled into a nano-cylinder, and multi-wall CNTs (MWCNTs), a structure composed of several nanocylinders [3]. The chiral vector (n, m) of a SWCNT defines both diameter and helicity of a SWCNT and, ultimately, defines whether a SWCNT is metallic or semiconducting depending on its chirality [4, 5]. Armchair CNTs, specifically, have (n, n) chiral symmetry and are of a special interest to nanomaterials physics because of their inherent metallic character (for a defectless structure, of course), very high symmetry, and very frequent usage as a prototype nanobuilding block [6].

The synergy of their high aspect ratio and robust covalent bonding properties makes carbon nanotubes (CNTs) excellent candidates for use as reinforcement filler materials for polymer, ceramic, and metal-matrix composite materials. CNT arrays or networks can potentially increase tensile strength, fracture toughness, and electric and thermal conductance at relatively low filler concentrations [7]. CNTs have been explored for their use in nanoelectronics, specifically for semiconducting SWCNTs for use as the transporting channels or components of field-effect transistors, flexible connectors, and electrically conductive films due to their near-ballistic transport and mechanical ductility [8]. CNTs have been widely explored for their use in chemical and biological sensing due to their conductance and surface potentials for sensitivity to adsorbates and local electrostatic environments [9]. CNTs have been recognized for their use as conductive frameworks for use in the reversible electrodes of lithium-ion batteries and supercapacitor electrodes, and their use within fuel cells for their catalytic support properties. CNTs have been also been explored for their use by encapsulating sulfur or silicon within their robust and porous structures to overcome their degradation due to changes in molecular volumes. Other diverse applications of CNTs are for use within cold cathode field source devices, electromagnetic interference protection, and thermal interface materials, and nanofibrous liquid membranes for filtration and desalination [10]. However, many of their diverse applications have focused on their use within macro-scale films, paper, or random networks of CNTs. Most of their applications have focused on their use within macro-scale films, paper, or random networks of CNTs [11, 12].

A natural route beyond disordered assemblies is to engineer designed CNT networks in which junction topology, porosity, and load paths are prescribed by construction. This motivation has led to the development of hierarchical CNT-based frameworks often referred to as super carbon nanotubes (SCNTs) or, in related mathematical-chemical literature, hyper carbon nanotubes (HCNTs) [13]. The central idea is conceptually simple but powerful, a large-scale parent lattice (commonly hexagonal, triangular, or other periodic nets) is chosen, and each edge of that lattice is replaced by a CNT segment, while each vertex is realized physically by a CNT junction such as a Y-, X-, or higher-order node created through topological defects (typically pentagon-heptagon pairs) or controlled growth processes [14, 15]. In this way, a CNT is elevated from a single one-dimensional object to a higher-level building block, yielding a network-of-nanotubes with tunable hierarchy, the nanoscale tube diameter sets the primary scale, while the parent lattice parameters set mesoscale cell size and macroscopic porosity.

However, within this very general family of hypernetworks, hyper armchair carbon nanotubes form a particularly natural and application-driven subfamily, given that hyper-network struts (or hyper-network edges) are chosen to be segments of armchair carbon nanotubes [16, 17]. The armchair specialization of hyper armchair carbon nanotubes is very appealing for at least three independent reasons. First of all, regarding the metallic conduction properties of purely (n, n) carbon nanotubes and their very high symmetry, this could favor electron conduction along a particular strut if purely hyper-network-based electrodes or a purely hyper-network-based electromagnetic shielding structure is envisioned [18]. Secondly, within a more fundamental perspective, and independently of any technological application of carbon nanotube architectures, purely armchair tubes happen to be a very privilegated subset of carbon nanotubes from a purely theoretical modeling point of view, there is a broad amount of knowledge about their band structure and about how these tubes behave when damaged, so that effects of carbon nanotube networks at a microscopical level could be more reliably separated from hyper-network effects. Choosing a particular chirality doesn’t increase the overall complexity of the hyper-network framework because carbon nanotube struts would be very homogeneous, this is a very important issue if a topological analysis of hyper-networks is planned to be performed by using a direct description of carbon nanotube networks in terms of very simple graphs.

Hyper(or super) CNTs are of high interest for their usage as ultralightweight load-carrying structures [19]. The truss structure of CNT struts would be suitable for high specific stiffness and strength properties, and hierarchical porosity would be beneficial for energy absorption and recovery in compression, hinting at their application in downhill shock protection, aircraft designs, and mechanical metamaterial designs. Importantly, their elastic properties and failure mechanisms or Poisson behavior could be controlled and engineered through the topological properties of the underpinning networks and geometric parameters of struts and nodes. As a corollary to high thermal conduction within CNTs, ordered CNT networks would provide a template for developing multifunctional materials combining high mechanical and high thermal functionality [20].

One of the main difficulties in this area is finding a way to deduce predictive structure–property relationships whereby a predicted network topology is associated with a corresponding measurable physicochemical property performance without necessarily relying on computationally extensive atomistic simulations to assess a large number of candidate networks. For this particular application, another complementary methodology is supplied by chemical graph theory because, the HCNT/SCNT system presents itself naturally to be modeled from a molecular graph perspective whereby vertices and edges respectively relate to nodes or junctions/units and to segments of a carbon nanotube. As such, topological features reflected in a variety of topological indices such as degree-based and/or distance-based invariant quantities such as Zagreb-type indices [21], Randić indices [22], Wiener-type values [23] and other polynomial expansions have been utilized in a successful manner across a broad spectrum of nanocarbon species to develop correlations with respect to stability, strain energy trends, boiling points, and a host of other properties. For hyper armchair carbon nanotubes, this particular methodology is very natural because of a clear-cut construction whereby a parent net is used to derive a vertex/edge partition and a structured substitution of each particular parent edge by a corresponding carbon nanotube strut results in a derived sequence of related graphs whereby corresponding topological indices can be formally derived analytically [14].

Topology-based indices are a family of graph theoretical quantities which furnish a numerical abstraction of the structure-related properties of a molecule [24]. Recent studies indicate that Zagreb-type descriptors continue to develop well beyond the classical Zagreb indices and remain an active topic in chemical graph theory and molecular-property modeling. In particular, Zagreb rho indices have been applied to the modeling of physicochemical characteristics of benzenes [25], while Zagreb eta indices have also been introduced and used for physicochemical analysis of benzene compounds [26]. Similarly, Zagreb upsilon indices have been employed for modeling benzene physicochemical properties [27]. These contributions show that degree-based Zagreb variants continue to play an important role in the mathematical characterization of molecular structures, and they provide a broader contemporary context for the present study on hyper carbon nanotube graphs. This sensitivity makes the indices highly successful in describing the physicochemical properties of compounds [28, 29]. They were first introduced in order to study the physicochemical properties of compounds. Taking advantage of their sensitivity to the structure of compounds, the Zagreb indices were applied to physicochemical property-quantitative structure-activity relationship analysis [30, 31]. In nano-structured carbon systems such as graph models of hyper carbon nanotubes, Zagreb indices form an effective method of tracing the extent to which the superposition of cylindrical units affects the connectivity.

Chemical graph theory is a powerful tool that has recently been widely used for predicting the physicochemical properties of nanomaterials without the expense of laboratory testing. Graph indices, particularly degree descriptors like the Zagreb indices, have been useful in the QSPR analysis.

The last five to six years have seen intense efforts in the computational analysis of carbon nanotubes and their derivatives. Nadeem et al. performed a comparison between topological indices for capped and uncapped carbon nanotubes and explained how capping affects the topological indices [32]. Shanmukha et al. designed new degree-related topological indices for characterizing carbon nanotubes [33]. Kwun et al. applied the existing computational techniques for Boron nanotubes and recounted the potential for degree-related indices for characterizing nanomaterials in general [34]. Ullah et al. brought new attention to irregular topological indices and obtained an analytical solution for 2D nanotubes [35]. One of the areas of intense focus has been the study of Y-junctions of Carbon Nanotubes. Shabbir and Nadeem performed calculation of entropies for the Y-junctions of CNTs using topological indices [36]. Lal et al. analyzed both topological indices and entropies of graphs of the Y-junctions of CNTs [37]. This approach has also been applied to other materials; for instance, entropy values have recently been determined for silicon nanotubes using degree-based topological indices by Fraz at al. [38]. A wide range of carbon nanotubes variants and corresponding weighted edge entropies have been nicely described by Ahmad at al. [39]. Shabbir discussed the bond energy of symmetrical single-walled armchair carbon nanotube Y-junctions [40].

Considered Zagreb indices with details

The Zagreb indices find their relevance in QSPR/QSAR studies wherein the structural properties of a molecule are correlated with its physicochemical/biological properties using graph-theoretic descriptors. In the study of carbon nanostructures, the indices based on degree can provide concise numerical values for comparing hierarchical structures and might be used for predicting various properties of materials, including stability, extent of branching, connectivity influence, and other structure-related phenomena. The current paper is more of a theoretical approach, the obtained closed formulas could serve as meaningful descriptors in future research studies on hyper carbon nanotubes.

Aim and methodology

The main purpose of the present work is to identify the specific expressions for a set of degree-associated Zagreb-type topological invariants for the Hyper Carbon Nanotube Structures (theoretical). Specifically, the work centers on the family of the Hyper Armchair Carbon Nanotube graphs derived by considering the armchair single-walled nanotube graphs as building units along with the Y-junction graphs units. Indeed, the computed invariants include the classical Zagreb indices as well as the corresponding redefined Zagreb indices. Furthermore, the work considers the computation of the hyper Zagreb index, the augmented Zagreb index, as well as the corresponding multiplicative Zagreb indices. These invariants allow the quantitative description of the degree-interactions along the carbon-carbon bonds in the structures. They also allow the quantitative comparison among the various structures derived by changing the parameters in the building units.

Our approach is based on a combinatorial argument and a so-called partition-based evaluation of degree-driven edge contributions. To be more concrete, we first construct a hyper carbon nanotube graph based on the selected armchair nanotube and Y-junction graphs as building blocks. Then, we classify the vertices of the molecular graph according to their respective degrees. More importantly, we classify all edges of the graph according to the degrees of their endpoints. This is a complete list of how frequently each such pair of edge endpoints occurs within the whole graph. Once these frequencies are determined, each Zagreb-type index is calculated through a sum or a product of corresponding edge-based values depending on the specific Zagreb-type index under investigation. Finally, we verify our derived expressions through numerical examples and make some comparisons to illustrate the behavior of these Zagreb-type indices with respect to variations in hyper nanotube parameters and their ability to discriminate between these type of hierarchical nanostructures made of carbon. As far as we are aware, this is the first study dedicated to exploring Zagreb-type topological indices for this particular family of graphs.

Construction of hyper carbon nanotubes

In this section, we present the graph-theoretic construction of the hyper armchair carbon nanotube family studied in this paper. The construction is hierarchical and uses two fixed molecular building blocks derived from armchair single-walled carbon nanotubes. Throughout the paper, all molecular graphs are assumed to be hydrogen-suppressed; that is, vertices represent carbon atoms and edges represent carbon–carbon bonds.

The construction is based on a replacement procedure. Informally, one first chooses an ordinary armchair nanotube skeleton. Then each vertex of this skeleton is replaced by a symmetric armchair Y-junction, and each edge is replaced by an armchair nanotube segment [14]. The two building blocks used in this replacement are defined as follows.

Concluding remarks

The comparative results in Tables 2-5 and the corresponding plots in Figs. 4, 5, 6 and 7 confirm that the derived Zagreb-type expressions capture the expected growth of structural descriptors as the hyper armchair carbon nanotube is expanded through the repetition parameters (p, q). Additive indices such as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_{1}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_{2}$$\end{document}, and the redefined Zagreb family increase monotonically and rapidly, reflecting the increasing prevalence of degree-3 vertices and (3, 3) edges created by repeated hyper-hexagonal units. The third Zagreb index \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_{3}$$\end{document} behaves distinctly, exhibiting comparatively mild growth because only mixed-degree edges contribute. The higher-order indices HM and AZI further amplify the impact of degree-3 incidence and therefore provide strong separation among hyper-structures of different sizes. Finally, the multiplicative indices \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$PM_{1}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$PM_{2}$$\end{document} demonstrate pronounced sensitivity to parameter growth, making them useful candidates for distinguishing large-scale hyper configurations.

Overall, the presented closed forms, together with the numerical comparisons, provide a consistent and computationally efficient toolkit for the topological characterization of hyper carbon nanotube structures derived from armchair single-walled carbon nanotube junctions and armchair tube segments. The construction illustrated in Figs. 1 and 2 enables systematic generation of hierarchical families, and the resulting degree-based partitions support direct evaluation of a broad class of Zagreb-type descriptors. Future work may extend the same methodology to alternative junction types, different chiralities, or additional degree-based indices beyond those listed in Table 1, and may further explore correlations between these descriptors and experimentally relevant physico-chemical properties.
