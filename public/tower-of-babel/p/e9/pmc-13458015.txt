The Erosion of Trainee Engagement in Critical Care: A Systems Problem or a Generational Failure?
Authors: Tanmoy Ghatak, Debmoy Ghatak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCritical care training traditionally depends upon immense bedside learning, responsible watchfulness, and physiological reasoning. However, teachers’ concern regarding declining trainee engagement, reduced procedural confidence, and diminished intellectual curiosity are increasingly reported. While these observations are often attributed to generational differences, emerging evidence suggests that systemic factors play a more substantial role. This viewpoint examines key contributors to perceived disengagement, including post-pandemic educational disruption, burnout, duty-hour reforms, increasing protocolization of care, digital-era attention fragmentation, and psychologically unsafe learning environments. These factors collectively influence trainee independence, professional behaviour, and bedside ownership. Rather than viewing disengagement as a failure of the current generation, we argue that it reflects evolving pressures within healthcare and medical education. Rebuilding engagement requires intentional redesign of training systems through protected bedside teaching, reduction of administrative burden, promotion of psychological safety and encouragement, and proactive strategies to address burnout. Sustaining the future of critical care will depend not only on technological advances but also on creating learning environments that foster curiosity, responsibility, resilience, and meaningful mentorship.Keywords: Burnout, Critical care education, Post-coronavirus disease training, Trainee engagement

Introduction

Critical care medicine has traditionally relied on an apprenticeship model built on bedside immersion, physiological reasoning, and moral ownership of the critically ill. Yet across many institutions, teachers are expressing concern regarding a perceived decline in trainee engagement, procedural confidence, independent thinking, and sustained curiosity within critical care and acute care residency programs.

This phenomenon is often simplistically framed as a generational issue. Yet doing so we often overlook the far more important question: What has changed in the environment in which trainees are learning? A closer examination suggests that there is no deterioration in talent or commitment in newer generation, rather there are profound changes in healthcare, medical education, and society itself.

Intra- and Postpandemic Training Disruption

The coronavirus disease-19 (COVID-19) pandemic fundamentally altered training environments by abrupt deployment, reduced elective exposure, heavy rotations, and limited procedural exposure among residents.1 Many critical care trainees completed their formative training by remote learning, reduced bedside teaching, and exposure.

Studies evaluating health care workers during COVID-19 documented high rates of psychological distress, anxiety, and burnout.2,3 Such stressors during early training years may have lasting effects on confidence and professional identity formation. Even intensivists working in non-COVID intensive care units (ICUs) reported significant disruption to workflow and social life, underscoring the pervasive impact of the pandemic across systems.4

Reduced experiential learning during a crisis inevitably affects skill acquisition. Recovery requires deliberate educational reconstruction, not assumption of automatic rebound.

Burnout and Cognitive Fatigue

Many educators opine that newer trainees as less curious or less invested than before. Yet what may often be burnout or exhaustion specially after COVID-19. Systematic reviews demonstrate high rates of emotional exhaustion and depersonalization among medical trainees.5 Burnout is associated with decreased empathy, impaired cognitive flexibility, and reduced engagement in learning.4–6

Critical care demands sustained vigilance, rapid integration of evolving data, and emotional resilience. When trainees operate in environments of chronic fatigue, staffing shortages, and administrative overload, cognitive bandwidth narrows. What may appear as reduced curiosity may in fact represent adaptive energy conservation.

Targeting engagement must address the burnout issues instead of through reasonable staffing, protected academic time, and psychological support, not through exhortation alone.

Work-hour Regulations and the Fragmentation of Ownership

Fixed weekly duty-hour regulations for residents were introduced for protecting trainee well-being along with improving patient safety. However, the study showed neither improvements in patient care nor resident well-being.7 Rather fragmented continuity of care, reduced longitudinal patient follow-up, and diminished ownership of complex ICU cases were reported.7,8

Critical care learning is closely tied to continuity of bedside care. Understanding how a patient responds to a ventilator adjustment, a fluid challenge, or a change in vasopressor support often requires observing that patient over hours and days. Clinical judgment develops when trainees witness the consequences and significances of their actions.

Balancing safety with meaningful continuous effort remains a central challenge in training design.

Protocolization and the Erosion of Physiological Reasoning

Modern ICUs operate with protocols, bundles, and algorithms that standardize care and improve outcomes. The threat arises when trainees are blindly following algorithms without fully understanding the physiology of the patient. Critical care remains a specialty where you interpret the uncertainty, and adapt with precision. Every patient is different and no protocol can fulfil every patient or every clinical scenario with same ease.

Educational research suggests that over-structured environ-ments can reduce deliberate practice and reflective thinking when not paired with guided debriefing.9 Trainees may execute tasks competently yet struggle with integrative decision-making when faced with atypical presentations.

The teacher must inculcate the thought in their mind that guidelines are the starting points for discussion rather than endpoints for thought. Protocols should not replace thinking.

The Digital Era and Attention Economy

Today's trainees have unlimited and immediate access to medical information than previous generations. This is a bewildering benefit and boon for the trainees now-a-days. They even participate in focused discussion, online courses and conferences etc., through which their reasoning, and multidimension thinking power can flourish better.

But the same digital burst can also fragment attention. Evidence from cognitive psychology demonstrates that multitasking and digital interruption impair working memory consolidation and critical reasoning.10 In a specialty that demands integrative thinking, sustained cognitive engagement is indispensable.

The Hidden Curriculum: Fear, Litigation, and Defensive Practice

When people feel safe enough to ask questions, accept and describe own mistakes, their curiosity thrives. But where criticism is punitive, or fear of administrative action dominates, trainees may become reluctant to explore their uncertainty. Studies suggest that psychologically unsafe environments inhibit question-asking and intellectual risk-taking.11

Educational culture, not generational attitude, often determines willingness to learn.

Reframing the Narrative

It is easy to think that less engagement is because of different generations, but the facts do not show any global decline in intellectual capacity, capability, or potential. Instead, new doctors are starting out in a world that is more complicated than ever, with too much information and feeling really stressed.

Conclusion

The perceived erosion of trainee engagement in critical care is not a generational failure but a reflection of evolving systemic pressures. The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated existing vulnerabilities in medical education, such as burnout, fragmentation, digital distraction, and protocol overdependence.

If critical care is to remain intellectually vibrant and morally grounded, training must prioritize ownership, curiosity, resilience, and protected mentorship. The next generation is capable, but systems must be worthy of their commitment.

The future of intensive care will depend not only on technology and infrastructure, but also on whether we create environments where young physicians feel intellectually challenged, emotionally supported, and meaningfully responsible.
