Identifying intra-hospital Norovirus GII transmission using whole-genome sequencing
Authors: Logan Grimes, Jeffrey Fink, Michael Elkan, Jessica Llewellyn, Rose C. Patrick, Ana Luiza Araújo Jernigan, Benjamin M. Liu, Kenneth P. Smith, Rebecca M. Harris, Ericka Hayes, Robert Fredrick Potter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNorovirus genogroup II (GII) is a common gastrointestinal pathogen that can cause spatially and temporally linked outbreaks due to its resistance to disinfectants and desiccation. Despite this threat, routine whole-genome sequencing has rarely been systematically applied to Norovirus.MethodsWe performed whole-genome amplification of Norovirus GII from 665 clinical stool specimens collected between 2019 and 2025 at Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia (CHOP), and 47 samples from Children’s National Hospital, using a modified PCR protocol and Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) sequencing. Viral genomes were assembled using long-read sequencing and paired with clinical metadata from the electronic medical record to identify suspected transmission events.ResultsOur assay improves on previous methods and achieved an 84% success rate in assembling CHOP samples into near-complete genomes. Genotyping analysis showed dominance of GII.4 Sydney [P16] from 2019 to 2024, with recent emergence of GII.17 [P17]. We identified multiple cliques (n = 44) of genetically indistinguishable or near-identical (≤ 5 SNP) strains. 32% (14/44) of these cliques were from patients on the same units and are considered “Likely” Norovirus GII transmission events. 20% (9/44) of the cliques are from different units or inpatient and emergency department and are “Possible” transmission events. All “Likely” Norovirus GII transmission events have a confirmed epidemiological link or occurred entirely within unit and 56% (5/9) of the “Possible” transmission events have an epidemiological link - either a shared clinician or shared off unit location or both.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates that whole-genome sequencing of Norovirus GII is a powerful tool for high-resolution viral surveillance. Prospective genomic monitoring combined with informatics enables detection of nosocomial outbreaks and reveals hidden transmission chains. Routine implementation of this platform could facilitate earlier implementation of containment measures that may ultimately reduce healthcare-associated Norovirus transmission.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13073-026-01621-1.

Background

Infectious gastroenteritis exerts a significant morbidity on patient populations in the United States and is, globally, the 8th leading cause of death in children under five [1]. Norovirus is the most common cause of viral gastroenteritis and is noteworthy for its proclivity to cause infections in confined locations, such as cruise ships and hospitals. This is partly a function of Norovirus’s ability to remain infectious for weeks on an exposed surface [2–4]. Additionally, Norovirus can spread via aerosols produced following vomiting [5]. Norovirus is composed of 10 genogroups, further subdivided into dual typed genotypes based on RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) (n = 60) and VP1 sequences (n = 40) [6, 7]. Of the genogroups, Norovirus genogroup II (GII) is the most common cause of human disease in the United States. Within the United States, the CaliciNet surveillance network operated by the Center for Disease Control (CDC) is instrumental in tracking national trends in epidemiology by amplicon analysis of several hundred base pair regions of the RdRp and/or VP1 gene [8]. Most of the infections in the United States are caused by the GII.4 genotype, particularly the GII.4 Sydney strain [9]. While the GII.4 Sydney strain has primarily caused infections in the United States and abroad, the GII.17 [P17] genotype that initially emerged in Asia in 2014 has replaced the GII.4 Sydney strain as the primary genotype in in China and South Korea, surpassing the prevalence of GII.4 Sydney strains around 2016 [10–12]. Additionally, other genotypes such as GII.3 [P12] can be associated with sporadic outbreaks [13]. While amplicon-based sequencing of the RdRp or VP1 gene (typically ~ 300–500 bp) is sufficient for genotyping and broad surveillance, it lacks the resolution to distinguish highly related outbreak strains [14, 15]. In contrast, whole-genome sequencing of norovirus GII (~ 7.5 kb) provides markedly improved resolution, enabling more precise identification of closely related strains and improved outbreak tracking [16, 17].

Genome spanning primers have been used to successfully assemble complete ~ 7.5kbp-sized Norovirus GII genomes in the United States with Illumina sequencing [16]. The improved resolution of whole-genome compared to previous RdRp or VP1 capsid sequencing enabled insights into the potential biological consequences of evolutionary pressures on the different Norovirus GII genotypes. This whole-genome amplification also worked using Oxford Nanopore Technology (ONT) and Illumina hybrid assembly method on samples from Canada [17]. Notably, ONT sequencing offered improved resolution on the 5’ and 3’ UTR portions of the Norovirus GII genome compared to Illumina sequencing [17]. In bacterial genomics, the replacement of multi-locus sequencing typing, analogous to RdRp/VP1 amplicon sequencing, with whole-genome sequencing has resulted in the ability to achieve increased resolution on phylogenetic analysis to identify related strains [18, 19]. Intra-hospital whole-genome sequencing to support surveillance has occurred at the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia (CHOP) in response to outbreaks caused by Adenovirus and Rotavirus [20, 21]. Prospective intra-hospital sequencing offers cost-savings and improved patient outcomes compared to reactive outbreak investigation but has not yet been systematically applied to Norovirus GII [22]. Given the burden of Norovirus GII on our patient population we sought to pair whole-genome PCR amplification and ONT sequencing with clinical informatic patient data to identify past transmission events and enable routine, prospective sequencing for infection prevention.

To address this gap in knowledge on the transmission dynamics of Norovirus GII, we performed whole-genome PCR amplification and ONT sequencing on 665 samples from CHOP and 47 samples from Children’s National Hospital by adapting an existing Norovirus GII whole-genome sequencing workflow [17]. We demonstrate reliable assay performance in assembling complete Norovirus GII genomes. We identify changes in Norovirus GII genotype seasonality at CHOP from 2019 to 2024. Finally, we identify cliques of highly related (≤ 5 SNP) genomes from inpatients. These findings demonstrate cryptic transmission of Norovirus GII within the hospital from shared clinicians and shared off unit locations. This work may advance viral strain tracking in hospitalized patients to uncover interruptible transmission patterns and inform best practices for preventing Norovirus GII spread.

Methods

Study site and patient cohort description

The Infectious Disease Diagnostics Laboratory (IDDL) at the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia (CHOP) tests for Norovirus GII on stool samples using a multiplex laboratory developed test (LDT) with TaqMan probe technology [23]. This clinical assay is performed on all stool specimens submitted to the IDDL for the GI virus PCR panel (CHOP Test Catalogue #786025). RNA is extracted from stool using the EZ2 Connect Platform (Qiagen, Germantown, MD, USA) and quantified via qPCR on a QuantStudio Dx Real-Time PCR Systems (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) with primers from Trujillo et al. [23]. PCR reaction mixture uses PerfeCTa qPCR ToughMix QuantaBio (Quanta Bio, Beverly, Massachusetts, USA) and the Ct value is recorded. Testing platform did not change over the course of the study. CHOP consists of a ~ 600 bed academic pediatric hospital in Philadelphia, Pennsylvania, USA, and a ~ 50- bed nearby community hospital in King of Prussia, Pennsylvania, as well as several local outpatient clinics. The patient population therefore represents a mixture of highly specialized and complex patient cohorts as well as community patients from urban, suburban, and rural environments. Both hospitals are further subdivided into specialized, designated areas called units. Inpatient samples are further analyzed via unit location. All available specimens that tested positive for Norovirus GII by the IDDL LDT during the study period were retrieved from archived clinical material and included in this study without additional inclusion or exclusion criteria. All samples were assigned a unique alphanumeric study identification string that was unrelated to any protected health information (Additional File 1: Table S1). The three cohorts include our test cohort of protocol optimization (T01-T048), our retrospective cohort (R001-R528) of samples from March 2019-November 2024 and our prospective cohort (P001-P092) from samples that were sequenced approximately twice a week for Infection Prevention & Control purposes from December 2024-March 2025. We additionally received 47 samples of completely de-identified Norovirus GII samples from Children’s National Hospital (collected from January 2024 to November 2024) as an outgroup comparison.

Viral RNA extraction

Positive clinical specimens (n = 665) from the clinical LDT from March 2019 through February 2025 had whole-genome amplification and ONT Sequencing using a modification of a previous protocol for Norovirus GII whole genome sequencing with the Tx30SXN and GII1-35 genome-spanning primer sets [16, 17]. For the test cohort, retrospective cohort and Children’s National samples, ~ 1 mL of reconstituted stool in nuclease free water was briefly pulse vortexed then centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 5 min in 2 mL screw-cap tubes. The entire supernatant was transferred to a new tube and again centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 5 min. 200 µl of processed supernatant was extracted into 50 µl of viral RNA using the EZ2 Connect Platform (Qiagen) with an EZ1&2 Virus Mini Kit v2.0 (Qiagen).

Whole-genome amplification and next-generation sequencing

Primers were synthesized by Integrated DNA Technologies (Coralville, Iowa, USA) using standard desalting. QuantaBio qScript Ultra Flex Kit was used to perform first strand cDNA synthesis with 4 µL of 5X qScript Ultra Reaction Mix, 2 µL 10X GSP Enhancer, 2 µL Tx30SXN (1µM), 7 µl nuclease free water, and 5 µL of viral RNA per reaction. The samples were processed in a Verity 96-well thermal cycler (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) at 55 °C for 10 min and then 85 °C for 5 min and infinite hold at 4 °C. repliQa HiFi ToughMix (QuantaBio) was used for whole-genome amplification with 25 µL repliQa HiFi ToughMix, 1.5 µL Tx30SXN 10µM primer, 1.5 µL GII1-35 10 μm primer, 17 µL NF Water, and 5 µL of template DNA. The PCR conditions for the repliQa HiFi ToughMix reaction were 98 °C for 30 s and then 40 cycles of 98 °C for 10 s and 68 °C for 40 s then an infinite hold at 4 °C. A second round of processing occurred for all samples that failed assembly via first round with the modification of increasing viral RNA to 10 µL (reducing water to 2 µL) for the qScript Ultra Flex Kit and, similarly, increasing the template to 10 µL (reducing water to 12 µL) for the repliQa HiFi ToughMix protocol. 20 µl of the PCR product was sent to Plasmidsaurus (South San Francisco, California, USA) for Oxford Nanopore Sequencing under “Standard with Cleanup” order for their proprietary Linear/PCR sequencing service [24]. This uses amplification-free long-read sequencing libraires made with ONT v14 chemistry from linear input DNA. Libraries are then sequenced on high-accuracy R10.4.1 flow cells. The raw reads (in fastq format) and the high-accuracy linear consensus assemblies were downloaded from the Plasmidsaurus webserver. All genomes are included for integration into other scientists’ investigations (Additional File 2). Assembly size was determined to ensure it fit expectation (Additional File 3: Fig. S1) (Table 1).

Clinical informatic review

A unique laboratory identification number corresponding to the individual samples tested was used to pull medical record number (MRN), Date of Birth, Collection Date & Time, Collection Location, and Cycle Threshold (Ct) Value. Inpatient samples are further analyzed for unit location.139 were queried using SOFTECH and 516 were queried using EPIC Beaker. Two samples from prior to SOFTECH implementation were processed but we were unable to retrieve Ct values or clinical metadata.

Processing and genotyping norovirus GII genomes

All assemblies from Plasmidsaurus were uploaded to the Center for Disease Controls Human Calicivirus typing tool and Norovirus Typing Tool Version 2.0 for genotyping (Additional File 1: Table S2) [6, 25]. CDC designation is used throughout the manuscript. All assemblies denoted to be in the minus orientation were then converted into reverse complement using in-house Python scripts (Additional File 4) so that all downstream genomic analysis used positive sense genomes. Assemblies were concatenated into a multifasta and aligned using MAFFT [26]. The alignment had all pairwise single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) quantified using SNP-dists [27]. The MAFFT whole genome alignment for all CHOP Norovirus GII genomes was also used as input for RAxML [28]. The newick file was visualized with the interactive tree of life (iTOL) webserver [29].

SNP distance analysis

In order to define a SNP threshold for inferring potential transmission events, we evaluated different cutoffs. We define cliques as reciprocal groups of Norovirus GII genomes in which every pair of genomes differs by ≤ X where X is a given SNP distance. Thresholds ranging from 0 to 25 SNPs were tested, and the resulting clustering behavior was analyzed to determine the number of genomes contained within cliques and the total number of cliques formed at each cutoff (Additional File 3: Fig. S2). To further assess threshold stability, we calculated the Normalized Mutual Information (NMI) across thresholds 0–25. NMI quantifies the similarity between consecutive clustering assignments, where a value closer to 1 indicates greater stability in clique structure. The NMI was calculated between each pair of successive thresholds (SNPₙ and SNPₙ₊₁) to quantify changes in clustering stability across the range of cutoffs. The optimal threshold of 5 SNPs was selected based on the point at which clique stability (NMI) peaked before subsequent instability at greater SNP cutoffs (Additional File 3: Fig. S3).

Pairwise SNP distances ≤ 5 were analyzed as a source-target-edge formatted network diagram with Cytoscape v3.10.3 [30]. The resulting cliques of highly related genomes were then analyzed with temporal data using in-house Python scripts (Additional File 4). Python scripts were created with the use of generative Large Language Model systems (ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude). To assess the likelihood of transmission among non-self cliques, we applied a rules-based classification using spatiotemporal criteria. Cliques were categorized as “Likely,” “Possible,” or “Not Likely” transmission events. A clique was classified as “Likely” if it contained more than two genetically similar genomes identified on the same hospital floor, within two weeks. Cliques were deemed “Possible” if cases occurred within a two-week window but in distinct hospital locations (e.g., emergency department and inpatient unit or different inpatient floors). Cliques were considered “Not Likely” if cases occurred in separate locations over a span exceeding two weeks or included outpatient samples, suggesting independent acquisition events. We plotted the “Likely” and “Possible” transmission events by setting the time axis on the same scale where day 0 is the first patient within each clique.

Epidemiologic analysis of genomic cliques

To further assess the likelihood of intra-hospital transmission among genomically defined cliques, we conducted a retrospective epidemiologic investigation using electronic medical record (EMR) metadata (developed by Epic Systems, Madison WI). For each identified clique, we used the EMR’s “bed trace” functionality to extract patient movement data and identify potential spatial or temporal overlap. At our Institution we define a cluster as three or more patients with positive norovirus GII test within the same unit over a 48-hour period. A cluster is reclassified as an outbreak when four or more patients are affected, or if transmission persists for more than three weeks.

We defined the exposure window for potential transmission as the period spanning from 48 h prior to the first Norovirus GII-positive stool collection date to 48 h following the last positive collection within a clique. This window was selected to encompass one full incubation period prior to symptom onset and the potential infectious period post-diagnosis.

Within this window, we searched for epidemiologic links in two key domains: (1) shared offsite clinical locations—including perioperative spaces (e.g., pre-operative areas, operating rooms, post-anesthesia care units) and radiology suites—and (2) shared exposure to “roving” clinical personnel, such as respiratory therapists, vascular access nurses, occupational therapists, or child life specialists. Presence of shared clinicians was inferred by EMR access log data, identifying instances in which the same clinician electronically signed into the charts of multiple patients within a clique during the defined exposure period. Due to system limitations, retrospective identification of precise temporal overlap within shared offsite clinical areas was not feasible; therefore, presence in the same offsite location during the exposure window was considered supportive but not definitive of transmission linkage. All EMR queries and access log reviews were conducted under institutional IRB approval.

Results

Norovirus cohort

Six hundred sixty-five samples from CHOP were included in this study (Table 2). Toddlers were the largest age group, with 32.5% (216/665) of samples, followed by infants with 19.7% (131/665). There were similar numbers of preschoolers (15.3% (102/665)) and school-age children (16.2% (108/665)). The smallest demographics were adolescents (12–17 years) at 9.2% (61/665) and adults (18+) at 5.3% (35/665). Age was not available for 1.8% (12/665). Our cohort displays the expected seasonality of Norovirus, with the winter months of February and January containing 23.3% (155/665) and 15.0% (100/665) of samples, with a waning of cases from April to November. Most of the samples, 68.7% (457/665), were from inpatients while 20% (133/665) were from the Emergency Department (ED), and 9.5% (63/665) were from outpatients. 90.5% (542/665) of samples were from patients tested only once. 5.5% (33/665) were from patients tested twice, 1% (6/665) were from patients tested three times and 0.5% (3/665) were from patients tested four or five times.

Assay performance

The Ct value can be used as an approximation of the viral load within a particular sample, with a lower Ct value indicating a higher burden of virus. Ct value is not an absolute quantification and cannot be compared across assays but all CHOP samples were tested clinically using the same LDT. We were able to recover a clinical Ct value for 99.7% (663/665) of samples from the clinical informatic query. Two samples did not have Ct values obtained but were able to produce assemblies. 83.7% (555/663) of samples with a Ct value successfully assembled into a ≥ 7 kb Norovirus single contig (Fig. 1). 16.3% (108/663) of samples with a Ct value failed sequencing and did not produce a Norovirus genome. There was a statistically significant difference (unpaired, two-tailed t test p < 0.0001) between the distribution of Ct values in the assembled versus failed categories (Fig. 1). The mean Ct value of an assembled sample was 21.02 while the mean Ct value of a failed sample was 29.77. The lowest Ct value that failed sequencing was 15.42. The highest value Ct value that produced a genome was 38.61. 87.5% (21/24) of samples with a Ct value ≥ 35 failed to produce assemblies. A below average 46.8% (22/47) of the Children’s National samples assembled into genomes. Norovirus GII has an expected genome size of ~ 7.5 kbp, however, as a positive sense RNA virus with a polyA tail, it is expected to show variability in length. 98.5% (549/557) of genomes were in the expected size range for Norovirus GII (Additional File 3: Fig. S1). Two samples had higher than expected genome sizes and 6 samples had lower than expected genome sizes.

Genotyping

Norovirus Typing Tool Version 2.0 (NoroNet) and Human calicivirus Typing tool (CDC) were used to classify Norovirus genomes into genotypes. 85.7% (475/554) annotations between the two tools are exactly congruent. The only discrepant classification sample (P079) was called GII.3 by NoroNet without a polymerase classification whereas the CDC typing tool called it GII.3[P4untypeable]. The remaining 78 incongruent samples different in their specific strain classification between the two tools despite identical polymerase and capsid alleles(e.g. R306 was called GII.4 New Orleans[P4] by the NoroNet typing tool while the CDC typing tool called it GII.4 Wichita[P4]. Similarly in some samples such as R506 the NoroNet classification tool called it GII.4 [P31] whereas CDC typing tool called it GII.4 Allegany[P31]. CDC designation is used throughout the manuscript. 32 Norovirus genotypes were identified within the CHOP cohort. Interestingly, 3 of the samples were not Norovirus GII but a different genogroup. One sample was GI.3[P13], one was GI.3[P3], and one was GIX.1[GII.P15]. Of the 554 confirmed GII samples, GII.4 Sydney [P16], GII.3[P12], and GII.17[P17] were the most prevalent genotypes at CHOP at 38.4% (213/554), 12.9% (72/554), and 11.7% (65/554), respectively (Additional File 1: Table S1). We next compared the distribution of Norovirus genotypes at CHOP for each season. Overall, we observed that the GII.4 Sydney [P16] genotype was consistently prevalent from 2019 to 2024 but the other genotypes exhibited seasonal variability in burden (Fig. 2). Notably, only 21 samples were from 2020, consistent with decreased laboratory testing for Norovirus during the COVID-19 pandemic. Interestingly, we observed that the GII.4 Sydney [P16] strain was relatively stable in prevalence at CHOP, ranging from 25.4%. to 53.8% and was the most prevalent genotype until 2024. The biggest shift in seasonality pattern occurred with GII.17[P17]. It was at low prevalence in 2019 (2.2% (2/93)) but rose to 15.9% (18/113) in 2023, then expanding to 36.6% (34/93) in 2024. Accordingly, GII.17[P17] was 2.2% units higher than GII.4 Sydney [P16] in 2024.

Clique SNP-distance analysis

We determined the relationship between SNP cutoffs and clustering patterns to identify a starting threshold for identification of highly related reciprocal groups (cliques). As the SNP threshold increased, the number of genomes contained within clusters rose continuously, whereas the total number of clusters increased linearly up to approximately 10 SNPs before plateauing, consistent with merging of existing clusters at higher thresholds (Additional File 3: Fig. S2). NMI analysis revealed a rapid increase in clique stability from 0.671 at 1 SNP to 0.965 at 5 SNPs, the first value exceeding 0.95 (Additional File 3: Fig. S3). Beyond 5 SNPs, NMI values fluctuated (0.951 at 6 SNPs, 0.940 at 7 SNPs, 0.967 at 8 SNPs), indicating instability in clique structure. Based on these results, 5 SNPs was selected as the optimal cutoff, representing the inflection point of maximal clique stability and strongest concordance with epidemiologically supported transmission events. To quantify assay error and confirm it does not overlap with our SNP cutoff, we selected 10 samples from the retrospective and prospective cohorts for three additional independent sequencing runs. Analysis of the resulting 40 genomes through our SNP quantification pipeline established an error range of 0–2 SNPs(Additional File 1: Table S3). Accordingly, we set a SNP-distance cutoff of ≤ 5 SNPs to identify reciprocal groups of highly related cliques which determined that 26.2% (146/557) of strains were contained in 50 cliques (Fig. 3). Of these, 44 were true cliques, as our analysis demonstrated that 6 of the cliques were multiple positive results originating from the same patient within the same clique (Additional File 1: Table S4). Interestingly, 6 patients had the opposite scenario – multiple positive results originating from the same patient but in different cliques (Additional File 1: Table S4).

Spatial-temporal genomic clique analysis

We compared floor locations to determine if each true clique represented a likely, possible, or not likely, transmission event. 31.8% (14/44) of the cliques were determined to be likely transmission events because they had at least ≥ 2 genomes occurring on the same floor within 2 weeks. 20.5% (9/44) of the cliques were determined to be possible transmission events because they occurred within a timespan of 2 weeks but across separate locations (either ED and inpatient or different inpatient floors). 47.7% (21/44) of the cliques were determined to be not likely because they occurred in separate locations > 2 weeks apart or contained an outpatient sample.

To further characterize the population structure of the CHOP Norovirus GII cohort we constructed a whole-genome maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree with the 554 CHOP genomes. We superimposed genotype, clique transmission likelihood, and patient location (Fig. 4). Predictably, the topology of the tree reflects the genotype assignment. Patient location for ordering (inpatient, outpatient, ED) and cliques occurs in all of the genotypes. There is an interspersing of patient ordering location and clique transmission likelihood within the major genotypes. For instance, the 19 non-self cliques that are GII.4 Sydney[P16] demonstrate 8 likely, 3 possible, and 8 not likely transmission events.

Epidemiologic links within genomic cliques

To further evaluate potential transmission pathways, we performed epidemiologic tracing of all 14 likely and 9 possible genomic cliques using EMR-based patient location data and clinician access logs (Fig. 6). Although not all met the CHOP traditional case-count threshold (≥ 3 patients), their combination of genetic identity and spatial–temporal proximity suggests that genomic data may warrant reevaluation of IPC cluster definitions. The most common transmission pattern, 64.3% (9/14) among likely cliques was complete confinement within a single unit. The remaining five likely cliques spanned multiple hospital units and had identifiable epidemiologic links. These links include a physical off-unit clinical location (e.g., perioperative or radiology suite) and a shared clinician—defined as personnel whose clinical roles routinely cross units, such as respiratory therapists, vascular access nurses, or child life specialists. In clique_15, patients shared both a clinician and off-unit location while clique_27 had a shared off-unit location. Clique_7, clique_8, and clique_25 all had shared clinicians as epidemiologic linkage within a clique. These findings underscore the potential for transmission mediated by hospital-wide personnel whose contact patterns span clinical environments.

Discussion

The GII.4 Sydney capsid strain first emerged in 2012 associated with the P31 polymerase; however, the recombinant GII.4 Sydney [P16] variant emerged around 2015 and has since become the dominant genotype of Norovirus GII in the US (28). This predominance may be influenced by mutations in the RdRp, which are suspected to enhance transmission compared to other P-types [31]. Supporting this, analyses of Norovirus GII strains from the NIH Hospital have shown that 80% (90/113) of Norovirus GII strains do not establish an infection enteric-derived organoid infections. Of the remaining 20%, the majority of strains are GII.4 backgrounds, indicating they might replicate more rapidly than other genotypes [32]. Further, the authors found that Sydney strains had higher fold genome copies compared to other GII.4 variants (Den Haag and New Orleans) [32]. While these findings suggest a biological basis for the increased fitness of GII.4 Sydney, it is important to note that the study utilized a chronic infection cohort, which may exhibit different viral dynamics than acute infections more typical in the general population. As the GII.4 strains in that study were associated with a variety of polymerase types (P4, P31, and P16), the observed replication advantages cannot be attributed solely to a single P-type. Therefore, further research is required to determine how these specific polymerase-capsid combinations contribute to the distinct transmission advantages seen in global acute outbreaks.

Additional surveillance data from the United States, England, Germany, Austria, Ireland, the Netherlands and France determined that Norovirus GII.4 strains were the most prevalent genotype in all countries prior to 2023/2024 [33]. Consistent with this, GII.4 Sydney [P16] genotype was the most common genotype identified from 2019 to 2023 in our study. These authors are the first to report a 4x increase in GII.17 and a decrease in GII.4 strains in 2024 from these six European countries and the United States [33]. Notably, the authors conclude that the difference was less pronounced in the United States [33]. However, a comprehensive analysis of the CaliciNet surveillance data, representing a national consortium of public health laboratories in the US, revealed that GII.17 caused 75% of all reported Norovirus GII outbreaks during the 2024–25 season [34].Our results support this global trend and demonstrate an increase from 2.2% in 2019 (2/93) to 15.9% (18/113) in 2023, then increasing to 36.6% (34/93) in 2024. At CHOP, GII.17 [P17] was higher than GII. 4 Sydney [P16] for the 2024 year. The Children’s National samples were overwhelmingly GII. 17 [P17] and collected in the second half of 2024. The GII. 17 genotype caused a significant increase in infections in Hong Kong, China, and Japan in 2014–2015 over the GII.4 Sydney [P31] strain [35]. Additionally, it caused an outbreak in Romania in 2021, but, until recently, did not stably establish itself as a predominant genotype [36]. Importantly, knowing the genotype of a Norovirus GII may impact clinical care as GII.4 infections required IV fluids more often [37]. Future work can focus on understanding clinical impact of GII.4 vs. GII.17 infections in pediatric patients and explore comparative mechanistic virology of the newly emerged GII.17 [P17] strains. Recent analysis of a global cohort of 1,471 GII.17 [P17] genomes identified recent back-and-forth evolutionary dynamics until stable strains reduced genetic diversity in 2024, consistent with the observed global epidemiology shift [38]. Machine-learning analysis of antigenic data from this GII.17 [P17] virus cohort revealed that a discrete set of residues concentrated in the P-domain loops of the VP1 capsid drive the antigenic shifts that differentiate the historical isolates from the recently emerged variants [38]. In CaliciNet surveillance data from the CDC, Norovirus outbreaks had a median 10-week duration [39]. The CDC data come from long-term care facilities, which likely have more long-term patients than our quaternary care pediatric hospital, which could explain this marked difference [39]. Further work is warranted to understand how strain replacement of GII.4 Sydney [P16] genotypes with GII.17 [P17] impacts the frequency and duration of Norovirus GII outbreaks.

Next-generation sequencing has significantly improved the ability to identify transmission networks, yet most of this focus has been on bacteria [22, 40]. In 2013, amplicon sequencing of 22 primer pairs on five patients from a Bone Marrow Transplant ward was used to assemble 13 whole-genomes and found one instance of intra hospital transmission between patients [41]. In 2019, 182 Norovirus GII isolates from the Great Ormond Street Hospital underwent whole-genome sequencing and 77 isolates were suspected to fall into closely related clusters [42]. These authors defined clusters as a monophyletic group with absent or short branch lengths within their phylogenetic tree [42]. In this setting whole-genome sequencing was able to identify nosocomial transmission greater than traditional IPC methods. Our analysis comparing the SNP cutoff with monophyletic clade showed strong concordance since only 3/44 cliques contained genomes monophyletic with a clique but not within that clique and 2/3 of these instances involved a patient who was positive twice (once within clique and once out of the clique). This supports additional observations that immunocompromised hosts can have prolonged Norovirus shedding such as 20/1,140 pediatric immunocompromised patients at St. Jude Children’s Research Hospital being positive for Norovirus for extended periods (> 31 days) [43]. Norovirus GII whole-genome sequencing showed additional utility in identifying outbreak strains linked to oyster consumption by similar isolates being linked to the same outbreak from Canada and United States [44]. Ion Torrent sequencing of 61 Norovirus GII.4 samples from Sweden yielded an ~5 kb segment of the capsid gene for genomic analysis [45]. This determined that two different genotypes (GII.4 Sydney and GII.4 New Orleans) each had two clusters of related genomes. This improved upon pure genotyping analysis by identifying two separate cliques [45]. The authors observed that the cliques were primarily associated with specific wards. Similarly, we compared spatial, temporal, and genomic data to identify 31.8% (14/44) likely Norovirus GII transmission events because the cliques contained at least ≥ 2 genomes occurring on the same floor within 2 weeks.

Our findings also demonstrate the value of integrating genomic surveillance with EMR-derived epidemiologic metadata used during traditional IPC investigations. Notably, many likely transmission events occurred within single units, reinforcing traditional IPC definitions of clusters. However, the detection of genomic cliques spanning multiple units, connected by shared clinical spaces or shared clinicians who work across units, the limitations of spatial criteria alone. By leveraging both high-resolution genomic data and dynamic EMR-based tools such as bed tracing and clinician access logs, small genomic cliques with defined epidemiologic overlap can be rapidly identified. This combined approach enhances the granularity and timeliness of transmission detection, enabling earlier, more targeted infection control responses. As such, this platform offers a scalable model for identifying intrahospital Norovirus GII transmission. This work contributes to existing studies by providing support for two models of Norovirus GII transmission occurs within the hospital – shared roving clinicians and shared off unit location. This identifies two specific targets for actionable intervention that can decrease transmission. Prospective sequencing of bacterial pathogens has been instrumental in identifying transmission via similar routes in across a variety of species [46]. This includes an outbreak of Enterococcus faecium in interventional radiology was attributed to differences in provider practices – reflecting how protocols at shared perioperative spaces can be revised to decrease intra-hospital transmission [47]. A limitation of the study is that we did not assemble all of the Norovirus GII samples attempted. It is likely that if RNA is degraded, the reverse polymerase used in first strand cDNA synthesis does not yield a full-length template that the two genome-spanning primers can successfully bind. This could explain why samples with a low Ct value (indicating high viral burden) failed to produce genomes in our assay. Another limitation of the study is that, although healthcare workers could serve as vectors for fomite transmission of Norovirus GII between patients, we did not have institutional review board approval to obtain data on provider coverage or EPIC access logs. When integrated with environmental source tracking, Norovirus whole-genome sequencing can pinpoint outbreak origins. For example, during a Norovirus GI outbreak at a school in Shandong Province, China, 17 of 21 students tested positive for Norovirus GI, and a nearby private well was also positive [48]. The clinical and environmental strains shared > 99% genomic identity, implicating the contaminated well as the probable source of the outbreak [48]. A final limitation of our manuscript is that we do not have access to broader community samples that could be used to further contextualize the genetic similarity of the intra-hospital cliques.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we performed whole-genome amplification and ONT sequencing on a cohort of Norovirus GII samples from two pediatric quaternary care hospitals. Our study builds upon previous work to use whole-genome sequencing to study Norovirus GII genomes. We used the same primers but had different viral RNA extraction methods, first strand synthesis kit/PCR conditions, and whole-genome amplification kit/PCR conditions [17]. We had a higher proportion (557/665 vs. 39/57) of genomes that assembled into a single ≥ 7 kb contig. We identified strong seasonal dominance of GII.4 Sydney [P16] from 2019 to 2023 but a recent decline relative to GII.17 [P17]. We found that 26.2% (146/557) strains initially fell into 50 cliques, though our analysis demonstrated that 5 cliques represented duplicate cases and were thereby excluded. Of the remaining 44 cliques, 14 were determined to be likely transmission events due to spatial and temporal closeness. This work demonstrates that intra-hospital Norovirus GII sequencing can be used to complement traditional Infection Prevention and Control efforts by facilitating early identification of transmission events. Timely detection may enable more rapid implementation of containment measures, potentially mitigating the development of cliques or outbreaks, thereby minimizing patient harm, operational disruptions, and associated healthcare costs. Future work can quantify the impact of prospective sequencing on intra-hospital transmission and expand into a multi-center approach that will capture the genetic diversity of annual circulating strains similar to work conducted on Salmonella spp. and Shigella spp.
