Best Evidence Summary for Postoperative Pain Management in Adult ICU Patients
Authors: Jie An, Xiang An, Bei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo identify and summarize the best available evidence for postoperative pain management in adult intensive care unit (ICU) patients to inform clinical practice.MethodsUsing evidence-based nursing methodology, an evidence-based question on postoperative pain management in adult ICUs was formulated. A systematic literature search was conducted. The quality of included literature was appraised, and evidence was graded and extracted according to the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) evidence-based healthcare center’s literature quality appraisal criteria and evidence grading system.ResultsEighteen publications were included, comprising 2 clinical decision tools, 4 guidelines, 5 evidence summaries, 4 systematic reviews, and 3 expert consensus documents. Through professional judgment, 35 best evidence recommendations were ultimately synthesized, covering six aspects: pain assessment tools, non-pharmacological pain management measures, pharmacological interventions, dynamic assessment, and quality improvement measures for pain management.ConclusionThis study summarizes the current best evidence for postoperative pain management in adult ICU patients. It can serve as an evidence-based reference for standardizing pain management practices among ICU healthcare professionals, helping to reduce the incidence of patient pain and related complications, improve patient outcomes, and enhance the quality of nursing care.Keywords: adult, critically ill patients, postoperative, pain management, evidence-based nursing

Introduction

Pain is internationally recognized as the fifth vital sign. Postoperative pain refers to a series of acute physiological, psychological, and behavioral responses triggered by surgical stimulation in patients, requiring clinical intervention.1 Severe postoperative pain can cause significant harm to patients’ physical and mental health.2,3 Studies indicate that pain manifestations in adult ICU patients are often unique due to critical illness, frequent impaired consciousness, or mechanical ventilation. However, commonly used pain assessment tools often fail to adequately consider the feasibility of assessment and patient response characteristics within the ICU context.4,5 Therefore, systematically reviewing evidence related to postoperative pain management in adult ICU patients is crucial for guiding clinical practice. Globally, approximately 300 million patients undergo surgery annually, with over 60% experiencing moderate to severe pain postoperatively.6 Adult ICU patients are particularly susceptible to severe pain due to their primary disease, invasive procedures, and postoperative immobilization.3 However, these patients often underreport pain because of sedation, communication barriers, or insufficient understanding of pain, posing challenges for healthcare providers in assessment and management. Severe pain can not only cause patient anxiety, sleep disturbances, and limited mobility but may also increase the risk of complications such as deep vein thrombosis, myocardial ischemia, and pressure injuries.7 Prioritizing ICU patients’ pain experience and implementing standardized management play a key role in improving healthcare quality, promoting recovery, and enhancing outcomes. Currently, high-quality research specifically on postoperative pain management in adult ICUs remains scarce, with existing literature often focusing on specific specialties or chronic pain management,6 lacking systematic evidence summaries for postoperative pain assessment and intervention within the unique ICU environment. Consequently, this study aims to identify, extract, and summarize the best evidence for postoperative pain management in adult ICU patients by systematically searching relevant domestic and international literature using evidence-based methods, to provide a reference for standardized clinical practice.

Data and Methods

Formulation of the Question

This study utilized the JBI PIPOST framework to construct the evidence-based question:7 P (Population): Adult postoperative patients in the ICU; I (Intervention): Evidence-based pain management strategies for ICU patients, including routine pain assessment, cryotherapy, music therapy, analgesic medications, etc; P (Professional): Clinical healthcare professionals (doctors and nurses); O (Outcome): Incidence of patient pain, healthcare professionals’ knowledge level regarding ICU pain management, incidence of complications (eg, unplanned extubation, prolonged mechanical ventilation, prolonged hospital stay); S (Setting): Intensive Care Unit (ICU); T (Type of Evidence): Guidelines, clinical decisions, recommended practices, evidence summaries, systematic reviews, expert consensus.

Search Strategy

English Search Terms: “pain/ache” AND “Intensive Care Units (ICU)/Cardiology Intensive Care Unit (CCU)/Neurosurgery intensive care unit (NICU)/Respiratory intensive care unit (RICU)/Emergency care unit (EICU)” NOT “child/infant/baby/pediatric”. Databases Searched: Following the 6S model of evidence retrieval,8 the following databases were searched from their inception until October 1, 2025: UpToDate, BMJ Best Practice, Cochrane Library, JBI Evidence-Based Healthcare Center Database, Embase, American Association of Nurse Anesthetists (AANA) website, American Academy of Emergency Medicine (AAEM) website, European Society for Emergency Medicine (EuSEM) website, Guidelines International Network (GIN), New Zealand Guidelines Group (NZGG), Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL), China Biology Medicine disc (CBMdisc), China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Wanfang Data. Search Date Range: From database inception to October 1, 2025.

Literature Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Inclusion Criteria

Study population: Adult postoperative ICU patients with pain.

Content

Involves clear recommendations regarding the prevention, assessment, intervention, physical therapy, or pharmacological treatment of pain in ICU patients.

Publication Type

Guidelines, clinical decisions, evidence summaries, systematic reviews, expert consensus. For multiple publications on the same topic and type, only the most recent version was included. Language: Chinese and English. Exclusion Criteria: Publication type: Protocols, abstracts, drafts, etc. Full text unavailable. Studies rated as low quality during the appraisal process.

Literature Quality Appraisal Criteria

Two researchers trained in evidence-based nursing independently appraised the quality of the included literature. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion within the evidence-based nursing team to reach consensus. Guidelines: The Appraisal of Guidelines for Research and Evaluation II (AGREE II) instrument9 was used. It includes 23 key items across 6 domains and 2 global rating items. Each item is rated on a 7-point scale. Domain scores are calculated as the sum of item scores and standardized as a percentage of the maximum possible score for that domain. Higher scores indicate higher quality. Guidelines were ultimately recommended based on a comprehensive judgment of the scores. Systematic Reviews, Expert Consensus, etc.: The corresponding JBI Critical Appraisal Tools (2016)10 were used. Items were rated as “Yes,” “No,” “Unclear,” or “Not Applicable”. Evidence Summaries: The quality was appraised by tracing the original source evidence cited in the summary and applying the appropriate critical appraisal tool based on the source publication type. Two researchers with backgrounds in evidence-based medicine independently performed the quality appraisals. Conflicts were resolved by inviting a third appraiser. When evidence from different sources conflicted, the principles for inclusion prioritized evidence-based sources, higher-quality evidence, and the most recently published authoritative literature.

Evidence Grading and Recommendation Levels

Two researchers independently analyzed, summarized, and extracted pain management evidence items based on the JBI FAME structure (Feasibility, Appropriateness, Meaningfulness, Effectiveness)11 and clinical nursing standards. Indicators cited more than once were extracted. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or adjudication by the research team. This study used the “JBI 2014 Evidence Pre-grading and Recommendation Level System”12 for preliminary evidence grading. Evidence levels were initially assigned from Level 1 (highest) to Level 5 (lowest) based on study design. These were then finalized considering factors for upgrading or downgrading according to the GRADE system. Recommendation levels were assigned based on feasibility, appropriateness, clinical significance, and effectiveness: Grade A (Strong Recommendation) and Grade B (Weak Recommendation).

Results

Literature Inclusion Flow

A total of 4011 records were identified. After removing 277 duplicates, 3734 records remained. Of these, 3678 were excluded based on title and abstract screening, leaving 56 full-text articles assessed for eligibility. Fifty-six full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Following full-text review and quality appraisal, 38 articles were excluded for not meeting inclusion criteria or low quality, resulting in 18 studies being included. Systematic Reviews

Four systematic reviews15–18 were included. The review by Admassie18 was rated “Unclear” for item 9 (“Were the methods used to combine studies appropriate?”) and “Yes” for all other items. The reviews by Durán-Crane15 and Zhao17 were rated “Unclear” for item 4 (“Was the status of publication used as an inclusion criterion?”) and “Yes” for all others. The review by Richard-Lalonde16 was rated “Yes” for all items. The overall quality of the systematic reviews was high, and all were included.

Expert Consensus and Clinical Decisions

Three expert consensus documents28–30 and two clinical decisions13,14 were included. All items for these five publications were rated “Yes,” indicating high overall quality, and all were included.

Evidence Summaries

Five evidence summaries23–27 from the JBI database, an internationally authoritative source, were considered high-quality evidence and included directly.

After rigorous selection and organization, the evidence was aggregated into 35 best practice recommendations across four themes: Pain Assessment Tools, Non-Pharmacological Interventions, Pharmacological Interventions, and Dynamic Assessment and Monitoring. Discussion

Establishing a Foundation of Pain Management Centered on Dynamic Assessment

Evidence statements 1–9 summarize the core components of pain assessment tools for perioperative ICU patients. The evidence strongly recommends routine pain assessment for all ICU patients,13,14,20,21,26 with the fundamental goals of achieving patient comfort and preventing adverse consequences such as physiological metabolic disturbances, chronic pain, and delirium.13,14 Research indicates that individualized, multidimensional assessment forms the basis of effective analgesia. For patients capable of self-report, subjective tools like the Visual Analog Scale or Numeric Rating Scale are recommended (Grade B recommendation), supplemented by open-ended questions to obtain comprehensive information.20,21 For non-communicative patients with observable behaviors, validated objective tools such as the Critical-Care Pain Observation Tool or Behavioral Pain Scale should be employed.13,14,20,21,26 Importantly, pain assessment should extend beyond intensity to incorporate patient history, physical examination, and psychological status for a holistic evaluation.13,14,20,21,26 Furthermore, the evidence underscores the importance of multidisciplinary collaboration and individualized planning, particularly for older patients with persistent chronic pain, where co-management with pain specialists is advised.20,21,28 Clinical departments should establish structured pain assessment protocols and checklists, integrating assessment quality into nursing performance metrics to promote consistent and standardized execution. Concurrently, specialized training for nurses, especially junior staff, on the use of pain assessment tools, communication skills, and behavioral observation is essential.

Implementing Multimodal Analgesia Strategies Led by Non-Pharmacological Interventions

Evidence statements 10–20 systematically outline non-pharmacological intervention strategies for perioperative pain in adult ICU patients. The evidence recommends multimodal analgesia as the core strategy, integrating various non-pharmacological and pharmacological interventions to create individualized plans.20,21 Pre-emptive use of pharmacological or non-pharmacological methods is advised before potentially painful procedures to mitigate procedure-related pain.14,18,21,24,27 Studies show that non-pharmacological interventions constitute the first line of defense in analgesia. Specific measures include cryotherapy, massage, distraction, aromatherapy, and music therapy.14–16 Additionally, encouraging patient participation in self-management and using resources like books, internet, or mobile apps to guide functional exercises can enhance their ability to cope with pain.14,18,21,24,27 The evidence further suggests that underlying causes of pain, anxiety, and agitation in the ICU should be addressed whenever possible, and pain should be prioritized and treated before initiating sedatives to reduce the incidence of delirium.13,19 Successful implementation of non-pharmacological interventions relies on teamwork and resource support. Departments are advised to create manuals and standard operating procedures for these interventions and provide necessary equipment. Nurses should act as advocates and primary executors of non-pharmacological interventions, explicitly incorporating these measures into nursing care plans.

Standardizing Evidence-Based, Individualized Pharmacological Analgesia Regimens

Evidence statements 21–28 focus on pharmacological intervention strategies. The evidence suggests adhering to the WHO analgesic ladder principles to develop individualized pharmacological plans. For moderate-to-severe non-neuropathic pain, intravenous opioids are recommended as first-line therapy due to their rapid onset and ease of titration.17,23–26 To reduce opioid consumption and associated adverse effects, the evidence strongly recommends the concomitant use of non-opioid analgesics.17,23–26 Drug selection requires particular caution in special populations: fentanyl or remifentanil are recommended for hemodynamically unstable patients; fentanyl or hydromorphone are preferred for those with renal or hepatic impairment; and opioids with minimal histamine release are favored for patients with acute bronchospasm.17,24–29 Furthermore, for complex cases like severe burns, a multimodal approach including adjuvants and non-pharmacological strategies is recommended, whereas continuous intravenous lidocaine is not advised.27–30 Collaboration between the ICU and Pharmacy Department is recommended to develop standardized drug formularies and titration protocols for postoperative pain management. Particular emphasis should be placed on alerts and reminders for medication use in special populations, along with close monitoring for opioid-related adverse effects to enable proactive risk management.

Achieving Continuous Optimization of Pain Management Through Multidisciplinary Collaboration

Evidence statements 29–35 emphasize the importance of dynamic assessment and monitoring. The evidence indicates that following analgesic administration, ongoing routine pain assessment is mandatory, including documentation of pain intensity, analgesic efficacy, and adverse effects, with subsequent dynamic adjustment of the treatment plan.13,16,22,28 For special populations, such as those with hepatic or renal impairment, intravenous fentanyl or hydromorphone are recommended with dose adjustments as needed.11 Appropriate sedation and analgesia can improve outcomes in cardiac critically ill patients.23 Additionally, ketamine can be combined with midazolam and/or propofol in trauma patients undergoing short-term procedures to enhance analgesia and reduce opioid dependence.17–23 Dynamic assessment should focus not only on pain intensity but also integrate the patient’s physiological response and medication tolerance to ensure the analgesic regimen is both safe and effective. It is recommended that hospital leadership promote the establishment of a rapid-response multidisciplinary team mechanism for ICU pain management and conduct regular joint rounds or case conferences. Utilizing information technology to create an electronic pain management platform can facilitate real-time sharing of assessment data, interventions, and outcome feedback among team members, thereby enhancing collaboration efficiency.

Conclusion

In summary, this study systematically summarizes the best evidence for postoperative pain management in adult ICU patients across four key areas: assessment tools, non-pharmacological interventions, pharmacological interventions, and dynamic assessment/monitoring. The evidence demonstrates that patient-centered individualized assessment, multimodal analgesia strategies, evidence-based pharmacological intervention, and continuous dynamic adjustment are central to improving the quality of pain management. In clinical practice, this evidence should be applied flexibly considering the specific patient context, available resources, and cultural background, reinforced by robust multidisciplinary collaboration and continuous enhancement of ICU nurses’ pain management competencies, ultimately aiming to improve patient outcomes and the quality of care.
