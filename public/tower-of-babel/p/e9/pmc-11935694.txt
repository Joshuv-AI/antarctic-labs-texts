Trends in Transcatheter Versus Surgical Aortic Valve Replacement Outcomes in Patients With Low‐Surgical Risk: A Systematic Review and Meta‐Analysis of Randomized Trials
Authors: Sina Kazemian, Parisa Fallahtafti, Mohammad Sharifi, Negin Sadat Hosseini Mohammadi, Hamidreza Soleimani, Arman Soltani Moghadam, Elaheh Karimi, Yaser Sattar, Yaser Jenab, Mehdi Mehrani, Alimohammad Hajizeinali, Mina Iskander, Mehrshad Fathian Sabet, Negar Salehi, Karim Al‐Azizi, Diaa Hakim, Mahboob Alam, Kaveh Hosseini
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLimited data exist on long‐term outcomes after transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) and surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR). This meta‐analysis aims to elucidate outcome trends following TAVR versus SAVR in patients with severe aortic stenosis and low‐surgical risk.Methods and ResultsA systematic search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and the Cochrane Library databases from inception until May 2024, to identify studies comparing TAVR versus SAVR in patients with low‐surgical risk (Society of Thoracic Surgeons predicted risk of mortality score <4%). The primary outcome was all‐cause mortality. Secondary outcomes included cardiovascular mortality, stroke, disabling stroke, rehospitalization, myocardial infarction, aortic valve reintervention, permanent pacemaker implantation, and new‐onset atrial fibrillation. Binary random‐effects models were used to compare the risk of each outcome across various follow‐up intervals and the risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane Collaboration's Risk of Bias‐2 tool. The meta‐analysis included 6 randomized trials including 4682 patients. TAVR was associated with a lower risk of all‐cause mortality than SAVR in the 30‐day (hazard ratio [HR: 0.45] [95% CI: 0.26–0.77], I 2: 0%) and 30‐day to 1‐year (HR: 0.55 [95% CI: 0.37–0.81], I 2: 16%) follow‐ups. However, the risk of all‐cause mortality was similar during >1‐year follow‐ups. TAVR was associated with a significantly lower risk of cardiovascular mortality, disabling stroke, rehospitalization, new‐onset atrial fibrillation, and a higher risk of permanent pacemaker implantation compared with SAVR during the 30‐day follow‐up.ConclusionsTAVR was associated with a lower risk of all‐cause mortality within the first year of post‐procedural follow‐up compared with SAVR. However, the risk of all‐cause mortality was similar in >1‐year follow‐ups.

Aortic stenosis (AS) is a common valvular heart disease, especially prevalent in the elderly population, and symptomatic severe AS is associated with a poor prognosis. 1 Conventionally, surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR) has been the gold standard for treating severe AS. 2 However, transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) has emerged as a less invasive alternative, initially indicated for patients with high surgical risk. 3 , 4 Over time, TAVR has demonstrated promising short‐ to intermediate‐term outcomes in both efficacy and safety, prompting consideration of its use in a broader patient population, including those at lower surgical risk, defined by a Society of Thoracic Surgeons predicted risk of mortality (STS‐PROM) score of <4%. 4 , 5 , 6

The annual number of TAVR interventions surpassed SAVR in the United States in 2019 and rapidly expanded worldwide. 7 , 8 This trend is likely to continue because of the aging population and the expanding role of the procedure to include intermediate‐ and low‐risk populations. 4 , 5 , 6 Nevertheless, the use of TAVR in patients at low surgical risk has raised concerns because of their relatively younger age, greater life expectancy, and potential TAVR‐specific complications. 9 A thorough understanding of temporal trends in complications following TAVR and SAVR is critical to guide individual treatment decisions for patients with severe AS. Currently, 7 clinical trials focusing on low‐surgical risk patients often used varied primary composite end points, 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 with only 3 trials—Nordic Aortic Valve Intervention (NOTION), Evolut low risk, and Placement of Aortic Transcatheter Valves 3 (PARTNER 3)—having follow‐up durations of >1 year. 12 , 13 , 14 Therefore, we conducted a comprehensive systematic review and meta‐analysis, integrating recent evidence from clinical trials targeting low surgical‐risk patients with severe AS to evaluate the trends in outcomes after TAVR compared with SAVR.

METHODS

The data supporting this study's findings are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

The current study is in accordance with the preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta‐analyses protocols (PRISMA). 17 Our systematic review and meta‐analysis protocol has been registered at PROSPERO (CRD42024498338). Ethical approval was not required since only summary data from previously published studies were used.

Search Strategy and Eligibility Criteria

We systematically searched PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and the Cochrane Library from inception to May 19, 2024. The search used the following key terms: (“TAVR” OR “TAVI”) AND “low risk”, along with their equivalent terms, without restriction on language or study type. Details about the search strategy for each database are provided in Data S1. Furthermore, we carefully reviewed the reference lists of review articles, meta‐analyses, and original studies identified through the search to identify potentially relevant studies.

Following the exclusion of duplicates, 4 independent investigators (N.H.M., A.S., E.K., M.S.) reviewed the titles and abstracts of the search results and discrepancies were resolved through mutual consensus. Studies were included if they met the following criteria: (1) randomized clinical trial (RCT), (2) reported at least 1 desired outcome comparing TAVR and SAVR, and (3) included patients with severe AS at low surgical risk, as indicated by an STS‐PROM score <4%. Observational studies, case reports, conference abstracts, reviews, and studies without clear outcome measures were excluded. The United Kingdom transcatheter aortic valve implantation (UK TAVI) trial was designed to study patients with moderately increased operative risks attributable to old age or comorbidities and did not use the STS‐PROM score as an inclusion criterion or randomization variable. 15 However, the results of the trial showed a median STS‐PROM score of 2.6% [interquartile range: 2.0%–3.4%], and the UK TAVI trial population could potentially be categorized as low‐risk based on these findings. Accordingly, we have included this trial only in the sensitivity analysis.

We incorporated 1 year follow‐up results of the NOTION‐2 trial, 10 DEDICATE trial, 11 UK TAVI trial, 15 SURTAVI low‐risk trial, 16 10 years results of the NOTION trial, 12 3 and 4 years data of the Evolut Low‐Risk trial, 13 , 18 and 5 years results of the PARTNER 3 trial. 14 Three independent authors (S.K., N.H.M., P.F.) extracted data on study characteristics, participants' characteristics, and clinical outcomes from the eligible studies onto a dedicated electronic spreadsheet. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion and mutual consensus.

Outcomes

The primary end point was all‐cause mortality. Secondary end points included cardiovascular mortality, stroke, rehospitalization attributable to heart failure or valve dysfunction, disabling stroke, myocardial infarction (MI), aortic valve reintervention, permanent pacemaker implantation (PPI), and new‐onset atrial fibrillation following TAVR or SAVR interventions.

Quality Assessment

The quality of the included studies was assessed using the Cochrane Collaboration's Risk of Bias‐2 (RoB‐2) tool. 19 Bias arising from the randomization process, deviations from the intended intervention, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and selection of the reported result constitute the key domains of RoB‐2. Two authors (E.K. and A.S.) independently assessed each study's quality based on predefined criteria. Any discrepancies in the quality assessment were resolved through discussion with a third author (S.K.).

Statistical Analysis

We synthesized data by calculating effect sizes using hazard ratio (HR) for all‐cause mortality and odds ratio (OR) for other outcomes to analyze binary outcomes. To address potential bias attributable to small sample sizes and zero cell counts, we used Firth's penalized likelihood correction in logistic regression models to estimate odds ratios for outcomes with small sample sizes (fewer than 10 events per group) or zero cell counts. We then used binary random‐effects models applying the DerSimonian and Laird method to pool the log odds ratios. Heterogeneity was assessed using Higgins and Thompson's I 2 statistic and the between‐study variance (τ 2) in our random‐effects models, using the Sidik‐Jonkman estimator for τ 2 determination. 20 The method introduced by Liu et al was used to obtain HRs and associated statistics for landmark analysis of the risk of all‐cause mortality from published studies. This method allows for the reconstruction of the individual patient data from the published Kaplan–Meier curves considering the number of patients at risk for each intervention at different time points. 21 Details about the individual patient data reconstruction method are presented in Data S1. To assess the risk of all‐cause mortality over different periods, we pooled HRs using a random‐effects model across 3 distinct time intervals: “first 30 days,” “30 days to 1 year,” and “1 year to the latest follow‐up available in each trial.” Similarly, the OR of secondary outcomes between TAVR and SAVR were calculated by separate meta‐analysis during “first 30 days,” “30 days to 1 year,” and “1 year to the latest follow‐up available in each trial” follow‐ups using the metabin function in R. Sensitivity analysis using leave‐one‐out method was conducted by sequentially excluding individual studies to assess the impact of each study on the pooled estimates. Additionally, we have compared random‐effects and fixed‐effects models across all outcomes to validate the results under different statistical approaches. Furthermore, in another sensitivity analysis to include reported outcomes from the UK TAVI trial, 15 we included data from 6 outcomes—all‐cause mortality, cardiovascular mortality, stroke, MI, aortic valve reintervention, and PPI—in the “30‐day” and “30‐day to 1‐year” meta‐analyses.

Meta‐regression analysis was utilized to investigate the effect of post‐procedural time on the OR pattern of each outcome when comparing TAVR versus SAVR, applying a mixed‐effects model. The results of the meta‐regression were visualized using bubble plots. We used visual inspection of symmetry in comparison‐adjusted funnel plots to evaluate potential publication bias in 30‐day and 1‐year findings. All analyses were conducted using the IPDfromKM, logistf, meta, metafor, and ggplot2 packages in R version 4.2.1 (The R Foundation, Vienna, Austria) with a significance threshold set at a 2‐sided P‐value of <0.05.

RESULTS

The systematic search yielded 2425 studies. After excluding duplicates, 1105 studies were screened based on title/abstract, and 13 full‐texts were assessed for eligibility. Eventually, 6 RCTs were included in the main meta‐analysis, and the UK TAVI trial was only included in the sensitivity analysis (Figure 1). These 6 RCTs comprised 4682 patients (2390 undergoing TAVR and 2292 undergoing SAVR). The mean age of participants was 74.1±5.2 years, and 62.3% were male. The STS‐PROM score ranged from a median of 1.1% in the NOTION‐2 trial 10 to a mean of 3.0% in the NOTION trial. 12 All included studies were multi‐centric, with follow‐up durations ranging from 1 year in the NOTION‐2, DEDICATE, UK TAVI, and SURTAVI trials 10 , 11 , 15 , 16 to 10 years in the NOTION trial. 12 Regarding TAVR valve types, self‐expandable valves were used in 5 RCTs (1373 patients, 57.4%), while balloon‐expandable valves were used in 3 RCTs (974 patients, 40.7%). All‐Cause Mortality

TAVR was associated with a decreased risk of all‐cause mortality compared with SAVR after both 30‐day (HR: 0.45 [95% CI: 0.26–0.77], P: 0.003, I 2: 0%; Figure 2A) and 30‐day to 1‐year (HR: 0.55 [95% CI: 0.37–0.81], P: 0.003, I 2: 16.1%; Figure 2B) follow‐ups. 10 , 11 , 12 , 14 , 16 , 18 The risk of all‐cause mortality was similar between TAVR and SAVR during the >1‐year follow‐up (HR: 0.95 [95% CI: 0.74–1.21], P: 0.68, I 2: 24.2%; Figure 2C).

SAVR indicates surgical aortic valve replacement; and TAVR, transcatheter aortic valve replacement.

Cardiovascular Mortality

TAVR was associated with a decreased risk of cardiovascular mortality compared with SAVR during the 30‐day follow‐up (OR: 0.50 [95% CI: 0.27–0.91], P: 0.023, I 2: 0%; Figure 3A). 10 , 11 , 12 , 14 , 18 There was a statistically non‐significant trend for lower risk of 30‐day to 1‐year cardiovascular mortality after TAVR compared with SAVR (OR: 0.62 [95% CI: 0.38–1.01], P: 0.057, I 2: 0%; Figure 3B). The risk of >1‐year cardiovascular mortality was similar between TAVR and SAVR interventions (OR: 1.13 [95% CI: 0.81–1.58], P: 0.47, I 2: 0%; Figure 3C).

SAVR indicates surgical aortic valve replacement; and TAVR, transcatheter aortic valve replacement.

Stroke

The risk of stroke after TAVR and SAVR interventions was similar during 30‐day (OR: 0.75 [95% CI: 0.47–1.21], P: 0.25, I 2: 22.5%; Figure 4A), 30‐day to 1‐year (OR: 0.80 [95% CI: 0.45–1.42], P: 0.44, I 2: 0%; Figure 4B), and >1‐year (OR: 1.13 [95% CI: 0.62–2.06], P: 0.70, I 2: 52.7%; Figure 4C) follow‐ups. 10 , 11 , 12 , 14 , 18

Forest plots representing the risk of stroke after transcatheter vs surgical aortic valve replacement in (A) 30‐day follow‐up, (B) 30‐day to 1‐year follow‐up, and (C) over 1‐year follow‐up.

SAVR indicates surgical aortic valve replacement; and TAVR, transcatheter aortic valve replacement.

However, TAVR was associated with a decreased risk of disabling stroke compared with SAVR at 30‐day follow‐up (OR: 0.37 [95% CI: 0.18–0.75], P: 0.005, I 2: 0%). The risk of disabling stroke was similar between TAVR and SAVR interventions in 30‐day to 1‐year (OR: 0.61 [95% CI: 0.28–1.32], P: 0.208, I 2: 0%) and >1‐year (OR: 1.58 [95% CI: 0.85–2.94], P: 0.152, I 2: 0%) follow‐up intervals (Figure S2).

Rehospitalization

TAVR was associated with a decreased risk of rehospitalization attributable to heart failure or valve dysfunction compared with SAVR during 30‐day follow‐up (OR: 0.66, [95% CI: 0.46–0.97], P: 0.032, I 2: 0%; Figure 5A). 10 , 11 , 13 , 14 We found that the risk of rehospitalization was similar between the TAVR and SAVR interventions during the 30‐day to 1‐year (OR: 0.82 [95% CI: 0.55–1.21], P: 0.316, I 2: 35.1%; Figure 5B) and during >1‐year (OR: 1.29 [95% CI: 0.90–1.83], P: 0.161, I 2: 0%; Figure 5C) follow‐ups.

SAVR indicates surgical aortic valve replacement; and TAVR, transcatheter aortic valve replacement.

MI and Aortic Valve Reintervention

The TAVR was associated with a non‐significant trend for lower risk of MI compared with SAVR during 30‐day follow‐up (OR: 0.61 [95% CI: 0.34–1.07], P: 0.086, I 2: 0%). The risk of MI and aortic valve reintervention were similar over other follow‐up intervals after TAVR versus SAVR interventions in patients with low‐surgical risk (Figures S3 and S4).

Permanent Pacemaker Implantation

TAVR was associated with a significantly increased risk of PPI compared with SAVR in 30‐day follow‐up (OR: 3.10 [95% CI: 1.81–5.32], P: <0.001, I 2: 75.2%). In sensitivity analysis, excluding the NOTION trial 12 resulted in a reduced heterogeneity (I 2: 34.1%) with no significant change in the pooled 30‐day risk of PPI (Table S3). We observed similar risk of PPI after TAVR and SAVR interventions during 30‐day to 1‐year and >1‐year follow‐up intervals (Figure S5).

New‐Onset Atrial Fibrillation

Furthermore, TAVR was associated with a significantly lower risk of new‐onset atrial fibrillation than SAVR in 30‐day follow‐up (OR: 0.14 [95% CI: 0.08–0.25], P: <0.001, I 2: 87.9%). The risk of new‐onset atrial fibrillation was similar between TAVR and SAVR interventions during the 30‐day to 1‐year follow‐up interval. However, we found that TAVR was associated with a significantly higher risk of new‐onset atrial fibrillation compared with SAVR during >1‐year interval (OR: 2.56 [95% CI: 1.68–3.90], P < 0.001, I 2: 6.9%; Figure S6).

Sensitivity Analysis

In a sensitivity analysis including the UK TAVI trial, 15 we observed no change in the pooled risk of all‐cause mortality between TAVR and SAVR interventions. Considering the UK TAVI trial, TAVR was associated with a lower risk of all‐cause mortality in 30‐day (HR: 0.50 [95% CI: 0.29–0.84], P: 0.009, I 2: 0%; Figure S7A) and 30‐day to 1‐year (HR: 0.58 [95% CI: 0.42–0.79], P: 0.001, I 2: 8%; Figure S7B) follow‐ups. In addition, after including the UK TAVI trial, 15 the risk of 30‐day cardiovascular mortality was similar between the 2 interventions (OR: 0.64 [95% CI: 0.37–1.11], P: 0.11, I 2: 0%; Figure S8A) and the risk of 30‐day to 1‐year cardiovascular mortality remained unchanged and TAVR was associated with a significantly decreased risk of 30‐day to 1‐year cardiovascular mortality compared with SAVR (Figure S8B). Furthermore, there was no change in the risk of stroke, MI, aortic valve reintervention, and PPI pooled estimates after including the UK TAVI trial (Figures S9 through S12). The accuracy tests for reconstructing individual patient data indicated that the extracted data points were captured with high precision across all trials. In another sensitivity analysis comparing fixed‐effects versus random‐effects models, the results were similar between the 2 models for all outcomes except for 30‐day to 1‐year new‐onset atrial fibrillation. The fixed‐effects model demonstrated a statistically significant lower risk of 30‐day to 1‐year new‐onset atrial fibrillation for TAVR versus SAVR, while the random‐effects models showed similar risks between the 2 interventions (Table S4).

Meta‐Regression

Meta‐regression analyses demonstrated that post‐procedural time had a significant modifying effect on the decreased risk of cardiovascular mortality (estimated regression coefficient (β^), representing the change in the log odds ratio of the outcomes per calendar month of post‐procedural time: 0.006, P: 0.043, I 2: 6.3%), disabling stroke (β^: 0.019, P: 0.009, I 2: 0%), and new‐onset AF (β^: 0.007, P: 0.048, I 2: 79.7%) after TAVR versus SAVR interventions. The analysis showed that the modifying effect of post‐procedural time on the risk of other outcomes was small and of uncertain magnitude. The corresponding bubble plots for each outcome are illustrated in Figures S13 through S20.

Publication Bias

No significant publication bias was noted upon reviewing the funnel plots across outcomes at 30‐day and 1‐year follow‐ups. Corresponding funnel plots are presented in Figures S21 through S29.

DISCUSSION

This study represents the most up‐to‐date meta‐analysis of 6 randomized trials, including 4682 patients with severe AS at low‐surgical risk. Our study demonstrated that TAVR was associated with a significantly lower risk of all‐cause mortality during the first year of post‐procedural follow‐up compared with SAVR. However, the risk of all‐cause mortality during >1‐year follow‐ups was similar between the TAVR and SAVR interventions. Regarding secondary outcomes, TAVR was associated with a lower risk of cardiovascular mortality, rehospitalization, disabling stroke, new‐onset AF, and a higher risk of PPI compared with SAVR in 30‐day follow‐up intervals. The risk of all secondary outcomes was similar between TAVR and SAVR interventions during “30 days to 1 year,” and “>1‐year” follow‐ups except for a higher >1‐year risk of new‐onset atrial fibrillation in TAVR compared with SAVR. Meta‐regression analysis suggested that the decreased risks of cardiovascular mortality, disabling stroke, and new‐onset atrial fibrillation with TAVR versus SAVR interventions were attenuated over post‐procedural time.

Over the past decade, TAVR has gained widespread acceptance as a treatment option for severe AS. According to the latest American and European guidelines for the management of valvular heart disease, 22 , 23 TAVR is favored over SAVR in patients with severe AS at high or prohibitive risk for SAVR, while SAVR is recommended for younger, low‐risk patients because pf limited data on the durability of prosthetic TAVR valves. 22

The landmark analysis indicated that TAVR was associated with a 55% reduction in the risk of all‐cause mortality and a 50% reduction in the risk of cardiovascular mortality during 30‐day post‐procedure follow‐up, compared with SAVR. This finding aligns with previous results in high 24 and intermediate‐risk 25 patients with AS undergoing transcatheter interventions, indicating a significant reduction in the risk of death at 30 days comparing TAVR versus SAVR interventions. Several factors may contribute to the lower mortality observed with TAVR, including the less invasive nature of TAVR, which allows for quicker mobilization and recovery compared with SAVR. Additionally, TAVR is associated with lower rates of complications such as new or worsening atrial fibrillation, life‐threatening bleeding, and acute kidney injury which are known to be linked with worse outcomes. 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 During the >1‐year follow‐ups, the risk of all‐cause mortality and cardiovascular was similar between TAVR and SAVR. In sensitivity analysis, after the inclusion of the UK‐TAVI trial, the risk of 30‐day cardiovascular mortality was similar between TAVR and SAVR interventions. The UK‐TAVI trial included older patients (median: 81 [interquartile range: 78–84] years) with a higher surgical risk (median STS‐PROM: 2.6% [interquartile range: 2.0%–3.4%]) than most other trials, which could explain the observed higher 30‐day all‐cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality event rate compared with most other trials.

Our findings indicated that the risk of stroke was similar between TAVR and SAVR interventions during all follow‐up intervals. However, TAVR was associated with a 63% reduced risk of 30‐day disabling stroke compared with SAVR with no between‐study heterogeneity, and the risks were similar during >30 days follow‐ups. In this study, TAVR was associated with an 86% lower risk of new‐onset atrial fibrillation during a 30‐day follow‐up interval compared with SAVR, a result partly driven by the minimally invasive nature of the TAVR approach. On the other hand, TAVR was associated with 2.56 times higher odds of new‐onset atrial fibrillation in >1‐year follow‐ups compared with SAVR. Previous studies have suggested that advanced age, severe mitral regurgitation, pulmonary hypertension, and peripheral vascular disease as important risks for new‐onset atrial fibrillation after TAVR. 30 However, we were not able to investigate these variables in our analysis, and further research is needed to better understand this important complication after aortic valve interventions. The risk of both stroke and disabling stroke exhibited similar results between the 2 interventions. Meta‐regression highlighted a substantial decrease in absolute risk reduction of disabling stroke during long‐term follow‐ups. The long‐term results raised doubt on the favorable outcomes of TAVR versus SAVR concerning disabling stroke, which is recognized as one of the most serious complications of aortic valve replacement.

Extensive investigations into TAVR have consistently demonstrated its non‐inferiority, and even superiority compared with SAVR across all surgical risk groups, particularly concerning the risk of short‐term and mid‐term disabling stroke. 1 , 2 , 31 , 32 Nevertheless, the PARTNER‐1A study, reflecting an early experience with a first‐generation device in high‐risk patients, raised concerns because of the higher risk of stroke in the TAVR in comparison with SAVR at early follow‐up in high‐risk patients. 6 In the acute phase following the procedure, ischemic stroke often occurs because of cerebral embolism resulting from debris dislodged during catheter manipulations and balloon dilation. 33 Cerebral embolic protection devices are designed to capture this dislodged debris, potentially reducing the risk of stroke 34 ; however, a recent large trial has shown mixed results, questioning the utility of this measure in patients undergoing TAVR. 35 It is noteworthy that among the included RCTs, the use of cerebral embolic protection devices was reported in the NOTION‐2 (15.5%) and DEDICATE (5.1%) trials. 10 , 11 Additionally, variations in post‐procedural antithrombotic regimens may exist across studies, which we were unable to account for in our analysis.

TAVR was associated with a significant increase in the risk of PPI during 30‐day follow‐up compared with SAVR. However, the risk of PPI was similar in follow‐ups beyond 30 days. Rhythm disturbances, especially common in early follow‐ups after TAVR, can be influenced by several factors. 36 Recognized predictors of the need for PPI include pre‐existing conduction abnormalities and anatomical factors, which pose challenges since they are beyond the control of the TAVR operator. 37 , 38 These concerns are exacerbated by potential conduction system injuries during TAVR, 39 prompting the need for specific recommendations tailored to each prosthesis type. Accounting for these factors may help reduce the risk of PPI. Furthermore, previous studies showed that the need for PPI after TAVR is higher with self‐expandable valves versus balloon‐expandable valves. 40 , 41

Improvement in the short‐term outcome of TAVR over the past several years can be attributed to new‐generation devices, characterized by smaller delivery profiles and improved safety. 42 Self‐expandable valves and balloon‐expandable valves are currently used in TAVR interventions. However, a key determinant of procedural success lies in the anatomical suitability of the aortic annulus and technical factors. 41 , 43 The outcomes of the 5 most commonly used second‐ and third‐generation transcatheter aortic valves in the Italian multicenter OBSERVANT II study revealed that the SAPIEN 3 valve demonstrated superior performance and lower rate of paravalvular regurgitation compared with other valves. 44 Additionally, in a meta‐analysis comparing new‐generation valves to early‐generation valves, a relative advantage was observed for the SAPIEN 3 valve in reducing all‐cause mortality compared with other valves. 45 The SAPIEN 3 balloon‐expandable valve and second‐ and third‐generation Evolut R and PRO self‐expandable valves reduced the incidence of PPI by more than one‐third. 46 Included RCTs in this study have used various valve designs from different generations, potentially leading to heterogeneity across the studies.

We should emphasize that the >1‐year outcome results need to be interpreted with extra caution, as only 3 trials with various follow‐up durations were pooled. These include the 3‐ to 4‐year results of the Evolut Low Risk trial, 13 , 18 the 5‐year results of PARTNER 3, 14 and 10 years in the NOTION trial. 12 In addition, the NOTION trial included 50 (17.8%) patients with an STS‐PROM score ≥4%, consisting of 24 patients in the TAVR arm and 26 in the SAVR arm. However, the NOTION trial reported similar risks of composite outcomes—all‐cause mortality, stroke, and MI—in patients with STS‐PROM scores ≥4% and <4% at the 5‐year follow‐up. 47

Our study differs from the latest meta‐analyses in several key aspects. 48 , 49 Firstly, we have incorporated the latest clinical trial data, encompassing the extensive 10‐year follow‐up data from the NOTION trial 12 and previously unpublished findings from the NOTION‐2 and DEDICATE trials. 10 , 11 Secondly, our analysis spans various time intervals, including the critical “30‐day” and “30‐day to 1‐year” follow‐up periods, providing a comprehensive understanding of outcomes. We used landmark analysis to assess all‐cause mortality HR. Furthermore, we utilized meta‐regression analysis to explore the impact of post‐procedural time on the OR pattern for each outcome when comparing TAVR and SAVR interventions. Our findings align with the latest meta‐analysis by Ahmad et al., showing that TAVR was associated with a decreased risk of all‐cause mortality, disabling stroke, rehospitalization, new‐onset atrial fibrillation, and higher risk of PPI compared with SAVR at 1‐year follow‐up. 48 However, our landmark analysis showed that this difference is only attributed to the first 30 days post‐procedural and that the risk of these outcomes becomes similar during the 30‐day to 1‐year follow‐up period.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, while overall heterogeneity was minimal, methodological differences such as variations in follow‐up duration, entry criteria, and primary end points existed among the included trials. Second, while the modified‐iKM algorithm is effective in reconstructing data from published Kaplan–Meier curves and accurately estimating survival probabilities, it operates under a uniform censoring assumption. This assumption may not be applicable in all trials, potentially affecting the reliability of our estimates. 21 Third, the absence of long‐term follow‐up data in NOTION‐2, DEDICATE, UK TAVI, and SURTAVI low‐risk trials restricted our comprehensive understanding of long‐term outcomes. Fourth, we were not able to conduct subgroup analyses based on age categories, sex, or investigate the durability of surgical and transcatheter valves, as these are crucial factors in deciding between interventions. Fifth, the analysis faced challenges in comparing different generations of TAVR systems because of their distinct technical, and procedural variations and limited reported data in the included studies. Additionally, despite the application of Firth's correction to reduce bias, zero cell counts in some trials may have led to wide confidence intervals, indicating inherent uncertainty in the estimates. Lastly, focusing exclusively on randomized trials, while minimizing bias, restricts the generalizability of our results to broader patient populations excluded from these studies because of complex clinical profiles such as pre‐existing prosthetic valves or significant concurrent valvular disease. These limitations necessitate a cautious interpretation of the results and underscore the need for further research.

CONCLUSIONS

In this meta‐analysis of 6 RCTs, TAVR was associated with a lower risk of all‐cause mortality during the first‐year post‐procedural follow‐up compared with SAVR. However, the risks of all‐cause mortality were similar between TAVR and SAVR interventions in >1‐year follow‐ups. Additionally, TAVR was associated with decreased risks of cardiovascular mortality, disabling stroke, rehospitalization, and new‐onset atrial fibrillation, as well as an increased risk of PPI during the 30‐day follow‐up compared with SAVR. The risks of all secondary outcomes were similar between TAVR and SAVR interventions during “30 days to 1 year,” and “>1 year” follow‐ups, except for a higher >1‐year risk of new‐onset atrial fibrillation after TAVR. Future research with extended follow‐up durations is warranted to understand better the long‐term risks associated with aortic valve interventions in patients with low surgical risk.
