Fever of Unknown Origin and Oncocytic Thyroid Cancer: An Unforeseen Association
Authors: George S Zacharia, Marcos Rosa Santana, Adrija Mehta, Shazia Khan, Misbahuddin Khaja
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fever of unknown origin (FUO) poses significant challenges for clinicians due to the myriad of possible etiologies, exhaustive evaluation requirements, diagnostic delays, patient and family frustration, and prolonged hospital stays. Defined as a fever lasting more than three weeks without a diagnosis despite one week of inpatient evaluation, this clinical phenomenon could result from infectious, autoimmune, neoplastic, or miscellaneous etiologies. We report the case of an elderly woman with FUO who was diagnosed with oncocytic carcinoma of thyroid origin on cervical lymph node biopsy. Interestingly, the biopsy was taken from a cervical node that was detected incidentally on neuroimaging. Oncocytic thyroid cancers, also known as Hürthle cell carcinomas, are differentiated but aggressive thyroid neoplasia. These rare tumors often present as thyroid nodules, detected incidentally during imaging or as palpable thyroid swellings. This case report highlights the clinical and diagnostic challenges in patients with FUO and reports an exceedingly rare presentation of oncocytic thyroid cancer as FUO. Additionally, it emphasizes the diagnostic utility of imaging and the need to follow even subtle diagnostic clues when evaluating patients with FUO.

Introduction

Petersdorf and Beeson defined fever of unknown origin (FUO) as a temperature of 38.3°C or higher for at least three weeks without a diagnosis, despite one week of inpatient investigations [1]. FUO is not a biologically uniform phenomenon but rather a manifestation of multiple, disparate disease processes. The most common etiologies include infections, neoplasia, autoimmune conditions, and miscellaneous causes; their incidence and prevalence vary worldwide. Malignancies account for 15%-20% of FUO cases, with lymphomas being the most frequent [2]. The diagnosis is often challenging and may take multiple weeks, requiring multidisciplinary interventions and care. FUO is not a biologically uniform phenomenon but rather a manifestation of multiple disease processes. The diagnostic evaluation extends from a thorough history, physical examination, and routine laboratory assays to extensive screening for infectious diseases, autoimmune disorders, and neoplasia, as well as radiology and invasive interventions. In the current case report, we describe a 71-year-old woman with FUO, ultimately diagnosed with oncocytic thyroid cancer on cervical lymph node biopsy. Oncocytic cancers are rare thyroid neoplasms, constituting around 3%-5% of all thyroid cancers [3]. These tumors often present as thyroid nodules on clinical examination or are incidentally detected on imaging; to the best of our knowledge, presentation as FUO has not been reported in the published medical literature. The tumor, synonymously known as a Hürthle cell tumor, is diagnosed with biopsy and is best treated with surgery [3]. It is typically not amenable to radioiodine therapy.

Case presentation

A 71-year-old female patient with no significant past medical history presented with fever, myalgia, and fatigue of seven days' duration, associated with transient altered mental status on the day of hospitalization. She denied having any respiratory, abdominal, or genitourinary symptoms. There was no history of recent travel or past incarceration. At presentation, she was febrile to 103°F and hemodynamically stable and had an otherwise normal physical examination. Neuroexamination revealed normal mentation, with no focal deficits or signs of meningeal irritation. The initial laboratory workup revealed mild anemia and leukocytosis (Table 1). Blood and urine cultures were collected and later reported to contain no isolates. Neuroimaging, stroke protocol with computed tomography (CT), revealed normal brain parenchyma but incidentally detected right level III cervical lymph nodes without adjacent inflammatory changes (Figure 1). The chest X-ray was within normal limits (Figure 2). As she continued to have high fevers without an identifiable focus of infection and taking into consideration the altered mentation, a lumbar puncture was attempted, but it failed due to extensive osteophytes. She was therefore initiated on broad-spectrum antimicrobials with coverage for meningitis, including ceftriaxone, ampicillin, vancomycin, and acyclovir, in accordance with the infectious diseases opinion.

Despite antimicrobials and supportive measures, she continued to have episodes of fever without other symptoms or clinical signs. She had 1-3 fever spikes daily, with no definite periodicity. Other than the right cervical nodes, no other clinically obvious lymphadenopathy was noted. Bacterial, fungal, and mycobacterial blood cultures returned negative for pathogens. She tested negative for the human immunodeficiency virus, hepatitis viruses, and the interferon gamma release assay. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed mild aortic stenosis, otherwise normal, and no evidence of infective endocarditis. CT scan of the chest identified prominent mediastinal and hilar lymph nodes, but normal lung parenchyma (Figure 2). Abdominal imaging, ultrasound, and CT revealed cirrhosis (Figure 2), without any evidence of hepatocellular carcinoma, otherwise normal abdominal viscera and no lymphadenopathy. Peripheral blood flow cytometry was negative for markers of leukemia and lymphoma. A cervical lymph node biopsy was performed to ascertain the etiology of lymphadenopathy on the seventh day of hospitalization. She continued to have fever spikes, and laboratory results revealed a rising trend in creatinine levels, suggesting acute kidney injury. Urinalysis was bland, and fractional excretion of sodium was estimated at 1.9%, while urinary imaging excluded obstruction, nephromegaly, or perinephric changes. Intrinsic renal failure related to drugs and/or contrast was considered likely, and the medications were dose-adjusted for renal function. The autoimmune workup revealed low-titer antinuclear antibodies (1:40), negative for specific autoantibodies, and normal complement levels.

The antimicrobials were discontinued after 14 days, but the patient remained febrile. The renal dysfunction also continued to worsen, and hemodialysis was initiated. Meanwhile, the cervical lymph node biopsy was reported as metastatic oncocytic carcinoma of thyroid origin (Figure 3). A thyroid ultrasound identified a one-centimeter nodule in the right lobe of the thyroid. The thyroid panel was normal.

A renal biopsy, performed to ascertain the etiology of renal failure, revealed dense interstitial inflammation composed predominantly of mononuclear leukocytes involving both atrophic and non-atrophic tubules, as well as diffuse interstitial edema, consistent with acute interstitial nephritis. The patient's renal dysfunction improved with hemodialysis over the next week. However, the patient continued to have episodes of fever. Oncology and otorhinolaryngology consultations were obtained. The patient is planned for positron emission tomography (PET) to exclude distant metastasis and possibly thyroidectomy, pending PET results. She was discharged on oral acetaminophen for fever spikes after scheduling the PET, with advice to follow up in the oncology clinic. However, the patient never followed up in the outpatient clinic or at the oncology clinic.

Discussion

FUO is defined as a temperature >38.3°C (≥101°F) for at least three weeks without a diagnosis, despite one week of inpatient investigations [1,4]. Based on the clinical profile, it is subclassified as classic FUO, nosocomial FUO, neutropenic FUO, and HIV-related FUO [5]. The etiology of FUO varies worldwide; infectious causes are common in the developing world, while noninfectious causes predominate in developed countries [5]. Malignancies, especially lymphomas, are frequent causes of FUO, primarily mediated by the pyrogenic cytokines interleukin-1 (IL-1) and IL-6, as well as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) [6]. Interestingly, in up to 75% of patients, FUO resolved before diagnostic work was completed or a diagnosis was made [7]. Factitious fever must be excluded before initiating a diagnostic workup, as it has been reported in up to 9% of patients with FUO [7]. A comprehensive history and physical examination could provide diagnostic clues in these patients. The initial evaluation typically includes a hemogram, a comprehensive metabolic panel, urinalysis, a peripheral smear, cultures, chest roentgenography, abdominopelvic sonography, serology for common viral infections, including human immunodeficiency virus, and exclusion of venous thrombosis and infective endocarditis. Additional modalities include computed tomography for occult infections or neoplasia, flow cytometry for hematological neoplasia, targeted tissue biopsies of suspicious lesions, tagged white-cell scans, and PET [4,7]. Treatment targets the underlying etiology, while supportive and symptomatic measures would benefit all patients.

Oncocytic carcinoma, or Hürthle cell carcinoma, is a rare type of thyroid cancer, constituting 3%-5% of all thyroid cancers [3,8]. It originates from oncocytic cells, a specialized type of follicular thyroid cell characterized by eosinophilic, granular cytoplasm and a high mitochondrial content [9,10]. Until 2022, oncocytic carcinoma was considered a variant of follicular thyroid carcinoma, but was subsequently reclassified by the World Health Organization as a separate subtype [6]. Compared with other forms of thyroid cancer, its aggressive nature and tendency to metastasize contribute to its clinical significance. Oncocytic carcinoma often occurs in middle-aged women as a thyroid nodule [10]. Asymptomatic oncocytic thyroid carcinomas may present as a palpable nodule or as an incidental finding on head and neck imaging. When symptomatic, it may present with neck swelling, dysphagia, voice changes, or signs of recurrent laryngeal nerve injury, such as hoarseness. Diagnosis is typically made through thyroid ultrasonography and fine-needle aspiration or core needle biopsy of the nodule. For staging oncocytic carcinoma, imaging studies are required due to the high tendency for metastatic disease. Primary treatment for oncocytic thyroid carcinoma is total thyroidectomy, with postoperative monitoring of thyroglobulin, a marker of recurrence [10]. When surgery is not possible, radiation therapy can be considered [11]. Prognosis can exceed 90% when detected early and managed effectively. Tumor size >4 cm, extrathyroidal extension, lymph node invasion, and age >55 years are associated with a worse prognosis [12].

Our patient meets the criteria for a diagnosis of FUO. The altered mental status at presentation could have been related to hepatic encephalopathy, which was precipitated by the febrile illness. Neuroimaging excluded structural pathologies in our case; however, meningitis could not be ruled out with absolute certainty, as attempts to perform a lumbar puncture failed even under imaging guidance. She was hence treated empirically with an antimicrobial regimen to cover bacterial and viral meningitis. The cervical lymphadenopathy detected during the stroke protocol imaging provided the initial diagnostic clue; a biopsy from the same ultimately clinched the diagnosis of oncocytic cancer in our case. The acute renal failure that the patient developed could be related to the medications or contrast that the patient received as part of diagnostic or therapeutic interventions, a therapeutic misadventure. The patient was managed with hemodialysis for the acute kidney injury, with subsequent improvement. Following consultation with medical oncology and otorhinolaryngology, it was decided to proceed with PET to exclude distant metastases, especially given the indeterminate lymph nodes on the chest CT. This case illustrates a rare diagnosis of oncocytic thyroid cancer presenting as FUO, a presentation, to the best of our knowledge, not reported in the published medical literature to date. The most frequent thyroid disease associated with FUO is subacute thyroiditis [13]. Published literature also reports cases of papillary thyroid cancer diagnosed in a patient with FUO [14].

The lack of availability of cerebrospinal fluid analysis is a limitation of this case of FUO, given the history of altered mental status. However, the lack of response to the meningitic antimicrobial regimen argues against intracranial infection. The transient nature of altered mentation is compatible with hepatic encephalopathy in a patient with liver cirrhosis. The lack of follow-up details limits a definitive cause-and-effect association between the thyroid neoplasm and FUO, even though the exclusion of alternative causes through comprehensive evaluation is highly suggestive of this association.

Conclusions

Oncocytic cancers are rare thyroid neoplasms, often manifesting as a thyroid nodule, detected during clinical examination or incidentally during head and neck imaging. Compared to other thyroid neoplasia, they are more aggressive, posing a higher risk of metastasis. This case report describes a patient with fever of unknown origin, in whom, after extensive evaluation, an oncocytic cancer of the thyroid was diagnosed, an exceedingly rare association. This case highlights the need for a comprehensive, multidisciplinary evaluation of patients with unexplained fever to reveal underlying occult pathologies, including neoplasia.
