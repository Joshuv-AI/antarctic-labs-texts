Pen Needle Reuse in People with Diabetes Who Inject Insulin: A Narrative Review
Authors: Diana Isaacs, Davida Kruger, Susan Cornell, Sanjay Kalra, Sandro Gentile
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionGlobal rates of insulin pen needle (PN) reuse in people with diabetes (PWD) vary across regions. However, it is estimated that more than 50% of PWD will reuse their PNs at some point over the course of their disease management. This highlights the need for better education to avoid this practice, which can lead to complications such as lipohypertrophy, pain, and poor glycemic management. This review aims to educate healthcare professionals (HCPs) on the issues surrounding PN reuse by PWD; discuss the scientific and clinical rationale for and against PN reuse; provide guidance on best practice for PN use by PWD who use insulin when reuse continues or is unavoidable; and provide actionable insights for clinical education and person-centered support interventions.Methods To compile the data on PN reuse, a PubMed search was conducted in November 2025, with “diabetes”, “insulin”, and “needle reuse” as the search terms. Over 100 articles were found and assessed for inclusion based on scientific vigor, leaving approximately 40 articles for inclusion.ResultsThe main reasons for PN reuse were stated to be convenience and cost in many regions around the world. ConclusionsThe findings of this review suggest that reducing PN reuse rates requires continued, ongoing education about injection technique and potential complications of PN reuse. Ideally, this education should be provided by HCPs and diabetes educators, as they are the most trusted source of information for PWD. It is vital to determine the reasons for reuse without judgment and discuss suitable solutions to mitigate the problem.

Key Summary Points

Insulin pen needle (PN) reuse practices in people with diabetes (PWD) who frequently inject insulin vary across the globe, but it is estimated to be carried out by more than 50% of PWD at some point in the course of their disease management, mostly because of convenience and cost.Reusing PNs can lead to complications, mainly lipohypertrophy and pain.Healthcare professionals (HCPs) should explore the attitudes, behaviors, and choices of PWD in a nonjudgmental and open manner.HCPs need to provide consistent and repeated education to PWD about correct injection techniques, highlighting the need to avoid PN reuse and potential repercussions of engaging in this practice.HCPs should support PWD who report frequent PN reuse to initiate a behavioral shift away from this practice by determining their reasons for reuse and discussing suitable solutions to mitigate the problem.

Introduction

According to the 2024 estimates from the International Diabetes Federation, diabetes affects 589 million adults (aged 20–79) worldwide, which represents 11% of the adult population [1]. By 2050, the global prevalence is projected to reach 853 million [1].

Insulin therapy is a fundamental component of diabetes management, and its effectiveness depends on correct injection technique and proper use of delivery devices, including insulin injection pens [2]. However, monitoring a person’s injection sites and ensuring that their insulin injection technique (IIT) is correct is often overlooked by the healthcare professionals (HCPs) involved [3]. Only approximately 28% of patients report having injection sites inspected routinely at every clinic visit and approximately 39% report that their injection sites were never checked [3].

Approximately 37% of patients report receiving IIT instruction within the past 6 months, while 10% report never receiving any instructions [3]. An area of continued concern for IIT is pen needle (PN) reuse, which remains common [2] despite clear recommendations against this practice by international diabetes care organizations [4–6]. Therefore, there is an unmet need for better education and guidance on reuse practices for HCPs and people with diabetes (PWD).

Various reviews have been published on IIT as a whole, but none are recent or focus specifically on PN reuse [7–10]. To our knowledge, this is the first review to examine all issues surrounding PN reuse, including prevalence, reasons for reuse, and potential complications associated with reuse, such as lipohypertrophy, bleeding, bruising, and pain. The findings from the literature search are presented alongside practical recommendations (from diabetes experts) on how to improve education on PN reuse and better assess reuse in clinical practice.

This review aims to educate HCPs on the issues surrounding PN reuse by PWD; discuss the scientific and clinical rationale for and against PN reuse; provide guidance on best practice for PN use by PWD who use insulin when reuse continues or is unavoidable; and provide actionable insights for clinical education and person-centered support interventions.

Methods

A PubMed search was carried out in November 2025, using the following terms: “diabetes” and “needle reuse”, “insulin” and “needle reuse”, “insulin[title]” and “needle reuse”. The search results were merged and duplicates removed, resulting in a total of 104 articles (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Flowchart of review process for inclusion. Articles were removed if they were case studies, were published more than 25 years ago, written in a non-English language, without an abstract, or were not diabetes related. The remaining articles (65) were assessed for inclusion based on scientific vigor (e.g., good experimental design, number of participants in study, number of participating centers).

In total, 31 articles from the literature search were included in the review. Most were based on injection technique survey questions that were posed to a set of PWD from specific regions. A few clinical studies/randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were identified, which focused on improving IIT education. Recommendations/guidelines were included from three sources; if specific organizations had multiple publications, only the most recent publication was included. An additional six references that were known to be of relevance by the authors were assessed against the inclusion criteria and later included (bringing the total to 37).

Ethical Approval

This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Results

PN Reuse Practices Differ Across Regions

Rates of PN reuse are generally based on injection technique questionnaire (ITQ) results, where needle reuse is just one factor investigated alongside injection site rotation, needle size, skin lifting, lipohypertrophy, and glucose management. One of the largest ITQ surveys ever performed was published in 2016 and included data from 13,289 PWD across 42 countries [2]. The results indicated that approximately 56% of PWD reuse their PNs at some point. Of those who reported their frequency of reuse (n = 3985), most (39.7%) did so three to five times; however, approximately 30% reused PNs more than six times [2].

The worldwide ITQ became the basis for subsequent surveys comparing injection technique in specific regions with the rest of the world [11–14]. For region-specific PN reuse rates, see Table 1. Higher PN reuse rates (> 70%) have been shown in low-income countries (e.g., India, China, Brazil, and the Latin America region), compared with the rest of the world [11, 13, 14]. Among available published data at the time of this literature search, rates of PN reuse in the USA were estimated to be high (47–52%), based on PN prescription fill rates across two insured cohorts [22]. This was confirmed by a recently published online survey, which demonstrated a 50% PN reuse rate, where most people who reused did so two to five times (70%) [23].

Taken together, PN reuse rates vary across the world but average out to over 50% of PWD. Analysis of all surveys captured by the literature search suggest that frequency of PN reuse correlates with the findings of the worldwide ITQ; many surveys show that most PWD who reuse PNs do so three to five times [11–23]. However, it is important to note that higher rates of reuse, i.e., more than 10 times, were reported more often in low-income countries than high-income countries (see Table 1 for a detailed breakdown per region) [2, 11–23].

Reasons Why PWD Might Reuse PNs

Reasons for PN reuse include convenience, cost, waste/environmental concerns, another PN not being available, reusing PNs not causing discomfort, not knowing PNs should be changed, forgetfulness, and habit (Table 1) [2, 11–14, 18, 20, 21, 23].

Convenience and cost were noted as the main reasons for reuse across many surveys, including the worldwide ITQ, which found that approximately 41% of PWD reuse PNs because of cost, and approximately 23% do so for convenience [2, 11–14, 18, 21].

A study in China looking at insurance coverage found that PWD without reimbursement had a greater median PN reuse than those with insurance (12 vs 7 times per PN, respectively) [24].

In the worldwide ITQ, only 6.8% of PWD said they reused PNs because of environmental concerns [2]. This concern was notably higher in the Bangladesh and US studies (14.7% and 40.8%, respectively) [20, 23].

Complications Associated with PN Reuse

Lipohypertrophy, the lumpy accumulation of fat tissue under the skin at the site of injection, is a common complication associated with repeated insulin injections [25]. It can lead to poor insulin absorption and affect glycemic management [25].

Data from the worldwide ITQ suggest that frequent PN reuse is a significant contributor to lipohypertrophy, but is a weaker factor than incorrect rotation, smaller injection areas, and longer time taking insulin [2, 3]. Numerous other publications captured by the literature search also support the association of lipohypertrophy with needle reuse across different populations [16–18, 20, 21, 26, 27]. Moreover, a recent meta-analysis of 51 studies found that needle reuse is the second greatest contributor to lipohypertrophy after incorrect injection site rotation, with a predicted odds ratio of 3.2 [8].

Pain is also thought to be associated with needle reuse. The literature search found two publications that support this association [3, 20]. The worldwide ITQ showed that approximately 50% of PWD who reported needle reuse also reported pain [3]. Moreover, pain seemed to increase as a function of the number of needle reuses; up to 62.9% of PWD who reported pain reused needles more than 10 times [3]. The increase in reported pain with frequency of reuse was also supported by the Bangladesh ITQ findings [20].

There was no robust literature to support the association of needle reuse with infection, bleeding, or bruising.

Data Supporting PN Reuse

Overall, the literature search results did not show any robust support for the reuse of PNs. One prospective study, published in 2005, assessed PN reuse by 15 PWD and four volunteers without diabetes, alongside blinded assessment of 123 PNs that had been reused a variable number of times (up to four or five times) by electron microscopy [28]. The authors concluded that using PNs four-to-five times did not lead to progressive needle tip deformity and did not increase pain intensity or unpleasantness [28].

A recently published preclinical study in an in vivo porcine model found evidence that PNs are now being designed that may be able to withstand repeated reuse without hooking or increasing skin trauma. However, more clinical research is required to investigate infection risk and residual insulin/air bubbles in the needles before these PNs can be documented as safe to reuse [29].

One meta-analysis conducted in 2016 aimed to investigate the safety of reusing needles; however, it stated that all the studies included had a high risk of bias [10]. Five studies found no association between infection at the site of injection and reuse of needles. Overall, the meta-analysis findings were not conclusive; as a result, the authors propose that currently there is no evidence for or against reusing needles [10].

Need for Education

Analysis of the worldwide ITQ suggests there is a need for education on IIT. When PWD were asked when they were last given injection instructions, 10% reported never being given instructions and 19% reported that they could not remember ever being trained not to reuse needles [3]. Furthermore, an Italian survey found that only 50% of PWD remembered being taught injection technique by a HCP, whereas 15% stated this was learned from other PWD [17]. When the authors analyzed differences in education and injection behavior between subjects classified as lipohypertrophy-positive or lipohypertrophy-negative, 90% of patients with lipohypertrophy reported learning injection techniques from a peer or did not remember who taught them, whereas 75% of patients without lipohypertrophy reported learning injection techniques from a HCP. This suggests that when people tend to learn from their peers, it is unlikely all the correct information and advice will be given, and thus more direct education from HCPs is needed [17].

Another Italian survey found 37% of patients with type 1 diabetes did not receive educational training on diabetes when first diagnosed, while 44% could not remember. This study reported that 25% of patients were entirely satisfied with their periodic diabetes visits, and nurses were considered more helpful than doctors (25% vs 18%, respectively). The most common reasons for dissatisfaction were a detached and directive approach from doctors (25% of PWD) or doctors being too technical (16% of PWD), while only 5% thought the same of nurses [30]. This distinction may reflect the differing roles of doctors and nurses. Doctors often need to adopt a more technical and information-focused approach, as they are required to convey essential clinical details within the limited time available for a consultation. In contrast, nurses may have greater opportunity to engage in a more person-centered approach, allowing for enhanced communication, reinforcement of education, and tailored support.

Findings from a number of other prospective trials indicate that educating PWD on proper IIT, including not to reuse PNs, is beneficial to reduce reuse rates [31–36]. Although the patient education methods differ between these studies (including an online platform consisting of four modules, intensive nurse-to-patient education with repeated education at follow-up visits, colored PNs packaged with education materials and reminder sound chips, structured training, and intensive education) [31–36], most studies demonstrated a reduction in reuse rates of approximately 75–100% at 6 months post-education [32, 33, 35, 36]. There is also some evidence that education reduces the number of times PWD reuse a single PN [31, 32].

However, the ISTERP-2 study showed that even well-trained and high-performing patients tend to revert to poor injection habits within 3–6 months when education reinforcement procedures are missing. Thus, there is a need for repeated education at least every 3 months to maintain good IIT habits and support glycemic management [37].

Educating patients about not reusing PNs as part of improving IIT has also been estimated to reduce the overall healthcare expenditure for PWD [34, 38]. In a RCT comparing 18-month continuous intensive education with repeated, structured, multimodal education on correct IIT (intervention group) vs a single educational session (control group), the estimated costs of health interventions for severe and symptomatic hypoglycemic events (which were in the order of €70,000 and €9300, respectively, at baseline) decreased by 5.9 times and 13.7 times, respectively, at the end of follow-up in the intervention group, whereas the reduction was only approximately half in the control group [34]. Additionally, a model was developed to compute the impact of behavioral shifts in PN reuse on clinical and economic outcomes of insulin-treated diabetes populations in Belgium. It was estimated that reducing PN reuse by 55% could result in healthcare savings of €52.6 million over 5 years [38].

A recent US survey on PN reuse showed education alone is not sufficient to prevent reuse and that the most effective educational messages for changing reuse behavior relate to hemoglobin A1c improvement, lipohypertrophy prevention, and prevention of air bubble build up that can lead to inaccurate dosing [23]. This study also showed the most influential source of information for potential behavior change is from endocrinologists, primary care physicians, and diabetes educators [23].

Recommendations Regarding PN Reuse

Most guidelines from diabetes organizations advise against needle reuse as part of good IIT [4–6]. However, they recognize it cannot be avoided in certain situations [4–6], such as when a new needle is not available, in which case PN reuse may be preferable to missing an injection [4].

The most recent global IIT best practice recommendations published in 2025 by the Forum for Injection Technique and Therapy Expert Recommendations (FITTER) are known as the “FITTER Forward” recommendations [4]. They were created by 16 diabetes specialists from 13 countries (across Europe, the USA, Pacific-Asia countries [PAC], and South America) and recommend:i.PWD should avoid reusing needles because they are no longer sterile and may become blunted or deformed over time, which can increase injection force/pain and contribute to elevated risk of lipohypertrophy.ii.HCPs should educate PWD on the possible consequences of needle reuse and recommend carrying extra injection supplies to mitigate the need to reuse needles.iii.Needles should never be shared/reused between individuals to prevent transmission of blood-borne infectious diseases.

Practical Pearls for Healthcare Professionals Involved in All Aspects of Diabetes Care

For Clinicians, Nurses, and Diabetes Educators

Practical pearls for clinicians, nurses, and diabetes educators are summarized in Fig. 2.

Practical pearls for clinicians, nurses, and diabetes educators. HR human resources, OTC over-the-counter, PN pen needle

Discussion

The data compiled in this review highlight PN reuse as a global concern, with over 50% of PWD self-reporting reuse at some point in their lives. This figure may underestimate the issue as PWD may be reluctant to report non-recommended practices. Moreover, PN reuse remains an under-researched topic and is often reported only as a subcomponent of improper IIT. Therefore, there is a need for more research into the nature of PN reuse, the factors driving it, and, more importantly, how PN reuse directly affects glucose variability. Gathering additional data that examine PN reuse in greater detail would help identify how often PWD reuse PNs and why, which would support the provision of tailored education to reduce reuse rates.

The definition of PN reuse is also important to consider. It is likely that many patients will report reuse once or twice at some point in their lives, but this practice is of less concern than instances in which it is more commonly practiced. The perception of reuse may also need to be factored in, for example, some patients with separate pens for basal and prandial insulin injections may reuse their prandial insulin injection PN throughout the day but not perceive it as reuse, because they view each pen as a separate device. Raising awareness and providing education about the possible risks associated with PN reuse should therefore focus on PWD who more routinely reuse PNs.

Across surveys, convenience and cost were cited as the most common reasons for reusing PNs. The term “convenience” itself varies; it may be convenient to reuse PNs at work because PWD do not want to carry numerous needles, or they cannot find a suitable place to sterilize their hands and change their PN. To minimize patient burden, companies should consider the usability of their pen devices, the difficulty and time needed to change needles, the size of the packaging, and the design of the needle cover. Addressing cost will depend on location, as reimbursement, insurance, and pen device availability vary across regions. However, pharmacy staff may be well placed to advise on the cost benefits at the patient level, such as cheaper alternatives available in the pharmacy, pre-payment certificates, and reimbursement options.

Reasons for PN reuse are subject to change as government policies, patient awareness, and global issues evolve over time. For example, environmental concern may have been a minor consideration when the worldwide ITQ was conducted (2014–2015), but was cited as a significant factor in a recent US survey. Consequently, companies supplying PNs should consider this, while HCPs can emphasize that patients should prioritize their health over environmental concerns.

Several complications and risks are often linked with PN reuse, including lipohypertrophy, pain, infection, bleeding, bruising, needle stick injury, and inaccurate insulin dose administration caused by air buildup in the cartridge when a PN is left on a pen injector. The literature search findings support the association of PN reuse with lipohypertrophy, with limited support for an association with pain, but no robust evidence for an association with infection, bleeding, or bruising. This may be due to greater interest in lipohypertrophy, as it is deemed a more serious complication (because of its associated effect on glycemic control), while there is less interest in investigating any association with pain, bleeding, and bruising, as they may be considered more routine side effects of any injection. However, the literature search did not find any robust data supporting the reuse of PNs. The few studies that were identified were either preclinical or had small patient numbers and are therefore unlikely to be reflective of the general population. Moreover, these studies were limited to reuse of up to five times, and so the findings cannot be applied to greater reuse frequencies.

PNs are being developed that are more robust and able to withstand repeated reuse; however, these studies used large-gauge needles (approx. 28–30G). A recent narrative review highlighted ultrathin needles (33–34G) to be available across America, Europe, Africa, and the PAC region, but there is currently no evidence to support their robustness [39]. Moreover, there are myriad needle types produced by different companies, with differing structural properties, and so the safest advice to give would be not to reuse needles.

Patients may not be fully aware of the associated risks, such as lipohypertrophy, which can adversely affect glycemic management. Targeted education can help improve awareness about the potential negative consequences of reuse, as well as the need for routine needle changes. This highlights the need for improved patient education by diabetes educators, HCPs, and pharmacists, who are well positioned to address the challenges associated with PN reuse and overall IIT. Engaging proactively with patients enables HCPs to discuss and recommend strategies to address behavioral factors such as forgetfulness and other established habits. Education needs to be regular, continued, and repeated every few months to ensure PWD can retain the information and correct potential errors as they occur. The role of HCPs and diabetes educators is vital for providing this patient education, as PWD deem them to be the most influential sources of information regarding IIT, and therefore they have the biggest potential to influence behavior change.

With the increasing availability of user-friendly PNs compared with traditional syringes, HCPs may inadvertently provide less comprehensive instruction on proper IIT. There may be a need for HCP-targeted IIT education to fill knowledge gaps, as demonstrated by two large nurse-orientated questionnaires in China, which showed that approximately 33% of nurses involved in the care of PWD had poor IIT knowledge [40, 41].

However, education alone against PN reuse may not be as effective in some circumstances. Some PWD will continue to reuse PNs despite knowing the risks, especially those who cannot afford the costs associated with repeat prescriptions. This suggests the need for greater advocacy for governmental reimbursement policies in some regions, especially since reducing reuse rates can lower the overall economic burden of diabetes. As such, HCPs, diabetes educators, PWD, and the public may want to consider lobbying for PN cost reimbursement.

The EADSG and ADA provide some guidance for instances where PWD continue to reuse PNs, but it is outdated and may not be applicable to current clinical practice. As such, there is a need for newer recommendations on injection practices that highlight ways to reduce reuse rates where possible, while also offering harm reduction advice where reuse does occur, such as in regions where some PWD have no option but to reuse PNs as a result of limited PN availability.

In this review, PN-specific data were separated where possible; however, this was not feasible for all outcomes, as obtaining accurate PN reuse rates across the world is challenging. Most available data come from ITQ surveys, in which only a subset of respondents answer questions on reuse; as a result, this data may not be fully representative of the entire PWD population. These surveys often use mixed populations of PN, syringe, and other device users when reporting “needle reuse,” and questions and information between surveys may differ. Furthermore, some ITQ surveys are 8–10 years old and may not reflect current practices. To capture more accurate data, newer surveys should clearly define the term “reuse” and include open-ended questions on the reasons for PN reuse. Nonetheless, most respondents were pen users, supporting the relevance of the conclusions to PN reuse.

Claims databases and prescription fill rates can carry further limitations: uninsured individuals are excluded, some insured PWD may not claim PNs if they can obtain them more cheaply elsewhere, PWD may fill prescriptions at different pharmacies or obtain needles from donations or nonprofit organizations, and reuse cannot be distinguished from a missed insulin injection. Reasons for PN reuse also differ between regions, influenced by factors such as culture and upbringing, personal income, governmental reimbursement policies, local availability of PNs (access or supply issues), and educational policies.

Conclusion

Overall, global PN reuse is common, with over 50% of PWD participating in the practice at some point in the course of their disease management. Although PN reuse rates and frequency of reuse may vary across regions, many PWD do so because of convenience and cost. The key to reducing PN reuse rates is continued, ongoing education against reuse and explanations of potential complications (that should be provided by HCPs and diabetes educators), as well as determining an individual’s reasons for reuse without judgment and discussing suitable solutions to mitigate the problem.
