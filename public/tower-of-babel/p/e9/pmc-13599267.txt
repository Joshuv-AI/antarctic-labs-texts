Non-pharmacological interventions to reduce postoperative anxiety in total hip arthroplasty: a systematic review
Authors: Elisabet Ripoll-Romero, Zaida Agüera, Montserrat Puig-Llobet, Andrea Vigueras, Jordi Galimany-Masclans
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionTotal hip arthroplasty is the surgical treatment of choice to improve the quality of life of patients with advanced osteoarthritis. This treatment aims to increase functional capacity and reduce pain. However, anxiety is one of the most frequently described uncomfortable experiences associated with this surgery. Although there has been extensive study of techniques to treat preoperative anxiety, little is known about interventions targeted at reducing postoperative anxiety associated with the fear and uncertainty of adjusting to life changes after surgery.AimTo conduct a systematic review of the literature to identify the non-pharmacological interventions that have been performed to reduce postoperative anxiety in patients undergoing hip arthroplasty and their effectiveness.MethodsA bibliographic search was conducted in Pubmed, Scopus, and Web of Science until April 2026. Search was based on the following selection criteria: randomized clinical trials, quasi-experimental pre-post studies with or without control groups. Study selection and data analysis were conducted according to the predefined eligibility criteria and analytical approach, and the reporting was conducted according to the PRISMA 2020 guidelines. Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (RoB 2) for randomized controlled trials and ROBINS-I for non-randomized studies.ResultsIncluded studies assessed technology-assisted interventions (immersive virtual reality, telemedicine, and video-based health education), nursing-led educational or behavioral programs (including self-efficacy–enhancing and continuous nursing care models), multimodal rehabilitation-based approaches, and complementary therapies such as aromatherapy. Overall, the most consistent reductions in anxiety were observed in studies evaluating immersive virtual reality and self-efficacy–enhancing interventions. Nursing-led and continuous care models also reported improvements, although findings were more variable. Educational and eHealth-based approaches showed benefits primarily in the short term. Telemedicine and aromatherapy demonstrated limited or inconsistent effects on anxiety. Substantial heterogeneity in interventions, outcome measures, and follow-up periods precluded quantitative synthesis; therefore, a structured narrative synthesis was performed.ConclusionMulticomponent nursing-led and technology-assisted interventions, particularly immersive virtual reality and self-efficacy–based programs, show the most consistent evidence for reducing postoperative anxiety following THA. Educational and eHealth-based interventions demonstrate short-term benefits, whereas telemedicine and complementary approaches such as aromatherapy show more limited or inconsistent effects. However, the overall evidence base remains heterogeneous and methodologically variable. Future research should prioritise adequately powered, multicentre trials with standardised outcome measures and longer follow-up periods.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12891-026-10146-y.

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a major cause of pain and disability in older adults and a leading indication for total hip arthroplasty (THA) [1, 2]. Although THA is generally associated with substantial improvements in pain and physical function, psychological outcomes, particularly postoperative anxiety, are increasingly recognized as important determinants of recovery quality [3]. Anxiety after THA may influence pain perception, sleep, engagement in rehabilitation, length of hospital stay, and overall satisfaction with surgical outcomes [3–5].

Postoperative anxiety following THA is multifactorial in origin. Reported risk factors in patients undergoing THA include female sex, younger age, pre-existing anxiety or depressive symptoms, high levels of preoperative anxiety, fear of surgical complications, previous negative healthcare experiences, limited social support, and maladaptive coping strategies [4, 5]. Importantly, preoperative anxiety has been consistently associated with higher levels of postoperative distress, suggesting a vulnerability profile that may persist beyond surgery. Early identification of at-risk individuals may therefore be clinically relevant for targeted intervention [6, 7].

Anxiety in THA patients is most commonly assessed using validated self-report instruments such as the State Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) and the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) [8, 9].These scales allow standardized assessment of symptom severity; however, they rely on subjective reporting and may be influenced by pain, medication effects, or postoperative fatigue [10–14]. Some studies have examined physiological markers of anxiety in patients undergoing THA, such as heart rate, blood pressure, and cortisol, but these are not routinely used and lack standardized thresholds [15, 16].

Management strategies for anxiety after THA include pharmacological treatment and non-pharmacological interventions. The latter encompass relaxation techniques, structured psychoeducation, cognitive-behavioral approaches, and therapeutic education programs often delivered by nurses or physiotherapists as part of perioperative care [7, 17, 18]. However, the current evidence base remains heterogeneous, and reported effects of these interventions appear variable and difficult to compare across studies. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis of preoperative educational interventions for patients undergoing elective hip or knee arthroplasty found only small reductions in anxiety and highlighted substantial clinical heterogeneity among trials in terms of intervention type and outcome measures [19].

Importantly, no systematic review has specifically synthesized the evidence on interventions targeting postoperative anxiety exclusively in patients undergoing total hip arthroplasty. Much of the existing literature combines total hip and total knee arthroplasty populations, which limits procedure-specific interpretation and represents a key limitation of the current evidence base [20].

Consequently, although perioperative interventions may be beneficial, inconsistency in timing, content, and measurement limits firm conclusions, particularly regarding anxiety persisting or emerging after surgery.

Despite the growing literature specifically in THA populations, there is currently no recent systematic review that has comprehensively synthesized this evidence, which remains inconsistent and fragmented across studies, limiting the ability to draw definitive conclusions regarding the effectiveness of interventions targeting postoperative anxiety.

Therefore, this review aims to systematically evaluate and synthesize the existing literature on interventions designed to reduce postoperative anxiety following THA and to assess their reported effectiveness.

Methods

Review design

Systematic review study was conducted and reported in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) reporting guidelines [21]. This systematic review was registered in PROSPERO on 19 June 2023 (CRD42023399001).

Search strategy

A bibliographic search was initially conducted between February and May 2023 in the following databases: PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science. The search strategy was subsequently updated in April 2026 to include the most recent evidence using the same search strategy and databases.

The search terms used were: ((hip arthroplast*) OR (arthroplasty hip) OR (hip replacement) OR (hip prothesis)) AND ((anxiet*) OR (nervousness) OR (fear) OR (panic) OR (distress) OR (emotional stress) OR (psychological stress) OR (anxious) OR (feel) OR (apprehens*) OR (feel*of dread) OR (feel*of worry) OR (feel* of worried)) AND ((surgery) OR (nursing care) OR (operative care) OR (surgical procedures) OR (operative) OR (operative care) OR (surgical) OR (postop*)). Grey literature was searched using Google Scholar. However, no studies meeting the inclusion criteria were identified. Therefore, no grey literature was included in the final analysis.

Study selection and inclusion and exclusion criteria

Study selection was performed independently in duplicate by E.R and A.V. After screening titles and abstracts, the selected studies underwent a full-text review. Disagreements between researchers were addressed through discussion.

The following inclusion criteria were used: (a) study design: experimental studies such as all randomized clinical trials (RCTs), and non-randomized intervention studies, including quasi-experimental pre-post studies, controlled before–after studies, and non-randomized controlled trials.; (b) population: adult patients over 18 years of age undergoing THA for degenerative conditions; patients undergoing hip arthroplasty for traumatic indications (e.g., hip fracture) were excluded; (c) language: articles published in English or Spanish; (d) studies evaluating postoperative anxiety after hip arthroplasty; and (e) studies assessing any healthcare-delivered intervention aimed to reduce postoperative anxiety following hip arthroplasty.

On the other hand, the exclusion criteria were as follows: (a) non-original studies, such as narrative reviews, systematic reviews, and meta-analyses, as well as congress abstracts, dissertations, unpublished reports, and clinical cases; and (b) studies that examined arthroplasty in general but did not present results differentiated from those of hip arthroplasty in particular.

Screening and data extraction

Study selection and data extraction were performed independently by E.R. and A.V. Each included study was assessed independently in duplicate by E.R and A.V. A third reviewer (Z.A.) participated in the process to resolve discrepancies when necessary.

The data extraction form included information on general study characteristics, study design, participant characteristics, intervention methods and delivery, outcomes, and main findings. This procedure ensured the transparency and reproducibility of the data extraction process.

Quality assessment

The risk of bias was assessed according to study design. The Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (RoB 2) was applied to randomized controlled trials [22], while ROBINS-I was used for non-randomized studies of interventions [23]. This approach aligns with current best practice recommendations for systematic reviews including both randomised and non-randomised studies.

Each study was independently assessed in duplicate by E.R.R. and A.V. Any discrepancies between the reviewers were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer (Z.A.).

For RoB 2, the following domains were assessed: randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported result. For ROBINS-I, the following domains were assessed: confounding, selection of participants, classification of interventions, deviations from intended interventions, missing data, measurement of outcomes, and selection of the reported result.

Dada analysis

Due to the heterogeneity across included studies in terms of design, population, and outcome measures, a quantitative meta-analysis was not feasible. Therefore, a narrative synthesis was conducted. Studies were grouped based on type of intervention (pharmacological, psychological, educational), and key findings were systematically compared to identify patterns, similarities, and differences across the literature.

Results

Selected studies

The database search fielded 3,163 records across three electronic sources. After removing 1,242 duplicates, a total of 1,921 titles and abstracts were screened. During the initial screening phase, 1,799 studies were excluded for not meeting the eligibility criteria, leaving 139 full-text articles for detailed review. From the first full-text assessment, 4 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final synthesis. Subsequently, a second search identified an additional 980 records. After removing 780 duplicates, 200 records were screened, of which 195 were excluded, leaving 5 full-text articles assessed for eligibility. From this second round, 5studies met the inclusion criteria. Overall, 9 studies were included in the final synthesis. Overall, most randomized controlled trials were judged to have either low risk of bias or some concerns across the evaluated domains. Several studies demonstrated low risk of bias in domains D1and D4, suggesting adequate methodological rigor in these areas.

However, some concerns were frequently identified, particularly in domains D2 and selection of the reported results D5. A smaller number of studies were judged to have high risk of bias in specific domains D3 and D2.

These methodological limitations may have influenced the estimated effects of the interventions. Lack of blinding and deviations from intended interventions may introduce performance bias, potentially leading to overestimation of treatment effects. Additionally, selective reporting of outcomes may introduce reporting bias, limiting the reliability and reproducibility of the findings. Therefore, although the overall methodological quality of the randomized studies can be considered moderate, the results should be interpreted with caution.

The non-randomized studies showed a higher overall risk of bias compared to randomized trials. Both included studies were judged to have serious risk of bias in multiple domains, particularly in relation to confounding and selection bias.

While some domains, such as measurement of outcomes, were rated as low risk, moderate to serious risk of bias was observed across several methodological domains. These limitations may substantially affect the internal validity of the studies and lead to biased estimates of intervention effectiveness.

Consequently, findings from non-randomized studies should be interpreted with considerable caution. No study was rated as having an overall low risk of bias.

Intervention delivery and key components

MDT multidisciplinary team, VR virtual reality

The included studies encompassed a broad range of intervention types, including immersive virtual reality (VR) [16, 29], educational and eHealth-based interventions [26, 30], nursing-led care models [24, 25, 27], telemedicine support [30], and complementary approaches such as aromatherapy [31]. These interventions targeted different aspects of perioperative care, including psychological preparation [16, 26], pain and stress management [28, 31], functional recovery [27, 29, 32], and postoperative support [24, 25].Overall, interventions could be grouped according to their primary mechanism of action into four categories: technology-assisted interventions (e.g., immersive virtual reality and telemedicine-based support), nursing-led educational and behavioural interventions, multimodal rehabilitation-based programmes, and complementary approaches such as aromatherapy. Across these categories, technology-assisted and nursing-led interventions appeared to show more consistent improvements in postoperative anxiety outcomes, whereas complementary interventions demonstrated more variable effects.

Interventions were delivered through multiple modalities. Digital approaches included immersive VR systems [28, 29] and video-based educational tools [26], while traditional approaches involved face-to-face education [25], rehabilitation protocols [32], and structured nursing care [24]. Several studies implemented hybrid models combining in-person delivery with remote follow-up, such as telephone-based monitoring or telemedicine platforms [24, 25, 27, 30].

The professionals involved in delivering the interventions varied across studies and included multidisciplinary teams [24, 27, 30], nurses [25, 26], physiotherapists [29], and perioperative clinical staff [28, 31]. Nursing-led and multidisciplinary approaches were frequently used in interventions involving continuous care [24], self-efficacy enhancement [25], and rehabilitation support [27, 29].

The timing and duration of interventions differed considerably. Some interventions were administered exclusively in the preoperative phase or immediate postoperative phase, such as educational video programs delivered prior to discharge [26] or VR used during spinal anesthesia [28]. Others were delivered postoperately and extended into follow-up phases lasting up to six months maintaining telemedicine support for up to twelve months [30]. The intensity and frequency of intervention delivery also varied considerably, ranging from single session intervention such as the aromatherapy patch applied for two days [31] or the educational video shown one priori to discharge [26], to repeated or continuous care models involving structured exercise progression, weekly telephone follow-up, and home visits [24, 25, 27].

Anxiety outcomes

Anxiety outcomes were assessed using a range of validated instruments, including the PROMIS-Anxiety [31], HADS [25, 26, 29, 32], SAS [24, 27], STAI-A [28] and VAS [30]. These measures were applied at varying perioperative timepoints across studies, limiting direct comparison. The most consistent reductions in anxiety were reported in association with immersive VR interventions, particularly during the preoperative and immediate perioperative periods. Self-efficacy enhancing programs also demonstrated sustained reductions in anxiety scores, with effects maintained up to six months postoperatively [25]. Nursing-led care models, including continuous nursing care and FMEA proactive nursing combined with individualized exercise, reported significant improvements in anxiety compared with routine care, although findings were more variable [27, 33]. Educational and video interventions were associated with improvements in psychological preparedness and short-term anxiety outcomes, though between group differences were not consistently significant after statistical adjustment [26, 30]. Complementary approaches such as aromatherapy showed limited and inconsistent evidence specifically related to anxiety reduction when compared with placebo [31], and rehabilitation protocol modifications showed no significant between group differences in anxiety at any timepoint [32]. Substantial heterogeneity was identified across studies in terms of intervention type, outcome measures, comparators, and assessment timing, which precluded formal meta-analysis. Nevertheless, the magnitude of anxiety reduction varied considerably across studies. The largest improvements were reported in virtual reality interventions, with reductions ranging from 22.7% in STAI scores during the perioperative period to 58.4% in HADS-A scores following a four-week rehabilitation programme. Overall, five of the nine included studies reported statistically significant between-group improvements in anxiety outcomes, whereas four reported no significant differences. The strongest effects were observed during the preoperative and immediate postoperative phases, while evidence for sustained long-term reduction was limited and primarily reported in studies incorporating extended follow-up and self-efficacy strategies [24, 25] .

Discussion

The findings of this review suggest that nursing-led interventions may contribute to the reduction of postoperative anxiety in patients undergoing THA. However, the strength and consistency of this evidence remain limited, and results should be interpreted with caution due to heterogeneity in interventions, outcome measures, and study quality.

Although several interventions demonstrated reductions in anxiety, the most consistent effects were observed in studies targeting the perioperative period, particularly before or immediately after surgery [27–29]. In contrast, evidence specifically addressing sustained postoperative anxiety was more variable. Interventions focused on self-efficacy and emotional preparation showed the most consistent benefits beyond the immediate postoperative phase, with some studies reporting reductions in anxiety up to six months after surgery [24, 25]. These findings suggest that interventions promoting coping strategies and patient engagement may be more relevant for long term psychological outcomes.

Immersive virtual reality interventions were associated with reductions in anxiety, particularly in the immediate perioperative setting [28, 29]. However, their impact on postoperative anxiety remains less clearly established, as most studies focused on short term outcomes [28, 29]. Similarly, educational and eHealth interventions appeared to improve psychological preparedness and reduce early postoperative anxiety [26, 30], although variability in intervention design and timing limits definitive conclusions.

Nursing-led and continuous care models demonstrated inconsistent effects on postoperative anxiety. While some studies reported improvements, others showed modest or non-significant differences compared to standard care. This variability may be related to differences in intervention fidelity, patient populations, and the extent to which psychological components were explicitly addressed within the intervention.

The timing of interventions appears to play an important role. Anxiety levels are typically highest in the preoperative and immediate postoperative phases [26, 34], which may explain why interventions delivered during these periods show stronger effects [29, 34] In contrast, postoperative anxiety at later stages may be influenced by additional factors, including pain, functional recovery, and complications, which are not consistently addressed by the interventions evaluated [31, 32].

The relationship between anxiety and functional recovery is likely multifactorial. While reduced anxiety may facilitate engagement in rehabilitation and adherence to postoperative recommendations, this association was not consistently examined across studies, and causal relationships cannot be established. Some evidence suggests a positive correlation between psychological improvement and functional recovery [29], and studies combining psychological and physical components reported improvements in both domains [25, 27], though the direction of this relationship remains unclear.

Overall, the current evidence suggests that nursing-led interventions may have a role in reducing postoperative anxiety, particularly when they include components targeting self-efficacy and psychological support [25, 27, 33]. However, the variability in findings and the presence of methodological limitations highlight the need for well-designed studies focusing specifically on postoperative anxiety outcomes, with standardized measurement timepoints and clearly defined intervention protocols.

Limitations and strengths

This review has several limitations. Considerable heterogeneity was observed across included studies in terms of populations, interventions, outcome measures, and study designs, which precluded quantitative synthesis. In addition, some studies included mixed arthroplasty populations or did not clearly distinguish hip specific outcomes, potentially affecting the applicability of findings to total hip arthroplasty.

Sample sizes were generally small, which may limit the statistical power and generalizability of results. Language restrictions may have led to the exclusion of relevant studies. Variability in intervention fidelity, including differences in intensity, duration, and adherence, may also have influenced the observed effects. Furthermore, the lack of standardized timing for outcome assessment limits comparability across studies, particularly for postoperative anxiety outcomes measured at different perioperative stages.

Methodological limitations were also identified in the assessment of risk of bias. Although validated tools were applied according to study design, challenges in evaluating non-randomized studies and differences between assessment tools may affect comparability across studies. In addition, allocation concealment and blinding were often unclear or at high risk, which may have introduced bias and influenced the estimated effects. Despite these limitations, this review has several strengths. It provides a focused synthesis of nursing-led and multidisciplinary interventions targeting anxiety in patients undergoing total hip arthroplasty, with particular attention to the postoperative phase. To our knowledge, this is one of the first reviews to specifically examine postoperative anxiety in this population. The inclusion of multiple intervention types and outcome measures allows for a comprehensive overview of current approaches and identifies gaps for future research.

Overall, the certainty of the evidence is limited by risk of bias across included studies, particularly due to lack of blinding, incomplete reporting of allocation procedures, and methodological heterogeneity.

Conclusions

Multicomponent interventions led by nurses, supported by technology and focused on self-efficacy, seem to be the most promising approach to reducing postoperative anxiety and psychological distress after THA, especially when continued after discharge. However, the current evidence is still limited due to differences in study designs, outcome measures, and follow-up periods, as well as the fact that anxiety tends to decrease naturally after surgery. More robust and standardized studies are still needed to draw firm clinical conclusions.
