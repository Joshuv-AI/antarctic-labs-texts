Does stereoelectroencephalographic depth electrode insertion alter seizure frequency? A baseline versus endpoint analysis
Authors: Shreyashish Roy‐Chowdhury, Jorge G. Burneo, Juan Toro‐Perez, David A. Steven, Michelle Lee‐Jones, Keith MacDougall, Jonathan Lau, Richard S. McLachlan, Ana Suller Marti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe implantation effect refers to a transient reduction in seizure frequency following intracranial electrode insertion in patients with epilepsy, independent of therapeutic intervention. This retrospective study examined potential clinical and demographic predictors of the implantation effect in 51 patients who underwent stereoelectroencephalographic investigation between December 2018 and February 2020. Follow‐up data were collected at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months postimplantation. A ≥50% reduction in seizure frequency defined responder status. Responder rates were 71.4% at 1 month, 35.0% at 3 months, 33.3% at 6 months, and 50.0% at 12 months. Complete seizure freedom was observed in 42.9% of patients at 1 month and 20.0% at 3 months. No significant associations were found between seizure reduction and age, sex, imaging findings, cortical stimulation, electrode count, or preimplantation antiseizure medication type or load. The lack of predictive variables, alongside seizure reduction in patients with previously uncontrolled epilepsy, supports the hypothesis that electrode‐induced microlesions may transiently disrupt epileptogenic networks. The quantity of electrodes did not influence outcomes, suggesting that implantation location may be more important. The effects of antiseizure medications remain unclear due to limited tracking of dosage or regimen changes. Further prospective research is needed to clarify the mechanisms underlying the implantation effect.

INTRODUCTION

Stereoelectroencephalography (SEEG) provides precise seizure localization during presurgical evaluation for drug‐resistant epilepsy (DRE), a complex brain network disease. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 However, prior studies document a transient “implantation effect” defined as an acute reduction in clinical seizure frequency lasting up to 5–6 months following the placement of intracranial electrodes or neuromodulatory devices without active stimulation or resective surgery. 6 , 7 , 8 , 9

Electrode‐induced neurological and behavioral changes observed across various conditions are hypothesized to stem from acute inflammation, gliosis, and neural plasticity. 10 Although the exact mechanisms remain unclear, the implantation effect can critically confound invasive monitoring by suppressing seizures and impairing accurate seizure onset localization. Given the inherent risks of SEEG, including infection, hemorrhage, and neurological deficits, characterizing this phenomenon is vital. 11 Therefore, our study explored potential demographic and clinical predictors of the implantation effect in patients who underwent SEEG investigation with depth electrodes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This retrospective analysis evaluated patients who underwent SEEG monitoring with depth electrodes between December 2018 and February 2020. Eligible participants were admitted to the Epilepsy Monitoring Unit (EMU) and met the following criteria: (1) provided informed consent; (2) completed follow‐ups at 1, 3, 6, or 12 months postdischarge; (3) received no subsequent therapeutic interventions (e.g., resective surgery, neuromodulation, or thermocoagulation); and (4) possessed documented postdischarge seizure frequency data.

Demographic and clinical data were extracted from paper and electronic medical records. Documented variables included age, epilepsy history, baseline seizure frequency, cortical stimulation, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and positron emission tomography findings, preoperative antiseizure medications (ASMs), the number of implanted electrodes, bilateral distributions of electrodes, total contacts per patient, and seizure frequency after SEEG investigation.

Standard protocol approvals

This study was approved by the Western University Health Sciences Research Ethics Board and followed the Declaration of Helsinki's code of ethics.

Statistical analysis

Patients were stratified into time‐specific cohorts based on the timing of their most recent seizure frequency record. To determine the seizure reduction rate for each patient, the most recent recorded seizure frequency was subtracted from the preimplantation baseline frequency and divided by the baseline value. The seizure reduction rate was categorized using a dichotomous parameter; patients achieving less than a 50% reduction were classified as nonresponders, whereas those achieving a reduction rate of 50% or more were categorized as responders. A secondary analysis comparing patients achieving complete seizure freedom and those who did not was also completed. Statistical analyses were performed using R (v4.2.2). Continuous variables were summarized as median (interquartile range [IQR]) and compared between cohorts using a t‐test or Mann–Whitney U‐test following normality verification via the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies (%) and evaluated using chi‐squared or Fisher exact tests, applying the latter if >20% of expected cell counts were <5. 12 To control the type I error rate, a Bonferroni correction was applied, establishing an adjusted statistical significance threshold of α = .0012.

RESULTS

Fifty‐one of the 110 patients who underwent SEEG met the study's inclusion criteria. Seizure frequency data were clearly identified and extracted from follow‐up records for seven of the 51 patients at 1 month, 20 at 3 months, 12 at 6 months, and 12 at 12 months. The median age of the participants was 34.0 years (IQR = 25.0–46.0), with 57.4% of the participants being women (n = 27). All 51 patients were diagnosed with DRE. The median duration of EMU stay was 14 days (IQR = 10.5–17.0).

Partial seizure control

From the total included patients, data available for the 1‐month follow‐up group consisted of seven patients, of whom five patients (71.4% of the 1‐month follow‐up cases) were reported as responders. Responders at this juncture experienced a median seizure rate of .0 seizures per month (IQR = .0–6.0). At the 3‐month follow‐up, 20 patients were included, yielding a responder rate of 35.0% (seven responders). These responders demonstrated a median seizure frequency of .0 seizures per month (IQR = .0–6.0). At the 6‐month follow‐up, the responder rate was 33.3% (4 of 12 patients followed up with), with a median of .7 seizures per month (IQR = .3–2.0). The 12‐month follow‐up group comprised 12 patients, with six individuals (50.0% of those followed up with at 12 months) as responders. These responders displayed a median seizure rate of .7 seizures per month (IQR = .0–5.4; Figure 1, Table S1). No statistically significant differences were observed in demographic or clinical characteristics between responder and nonresponder groups at each follow‐up period, including cortical stimulation, MRI abnormalities, number of implanted electrodes and contacts, or number and type of ASMs (Table 1).

Seizure freedom

Our investigation identified three patients who experienced complete seizure freedom 1 month following depth electrode insertion (3/7, 42.9% of patients; Table S1). At the 3‐month follow‐up period, four patients (4/20, 20.0% of patients) reported seizure freedom. Comparative analysis revealed no statistically significant predictive demographic or clinical characteristics for seizure freedom (Table S2).

DISCUSSION

This study evaluates the clinical and demographic determinants of the implantation effect, providing a more comprehensive analysis in a larger patient cohort than existing literature. Our investigation revealed a 71.4% responder rate at 1 month, 35.0% at 3 months, 33.3% at 6 months, and 50.0% at 12 months postimplantation without subsequent therapeutic intervention. Additionally, complete seizure freedom was observed in 42.9% and 20.0% of patients at 1 and 3 months, respectively. These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting short‐term seizure reductions following intracranial electrode implantation. For instance, Percy et al. noted ≥50% seizure reduction in 36% of patients at 1 year, with a smaller proportion achieving seizure freedom. In our cohort, no specific clinical or demographic variables were identified as potential predictors of reduced seizure frequency postimplantation. 6

Several patients in our cohort who were classified as responders had documented uncontrolled seizures prior to SEEG admission. Similar observations have been made in previous studies and extend to those investigating deep brain stimulation and responsive neurostimulation where seizure frequency was noted after lead placement without stimulation (Table S3). 9 , 13 , 14 , 15 This reduction in seizure frequency is unlikely to be a spontaneous event. Surgical factors, particularly anesthetic use, have been implicated, with desflurane speculated to prolong seizure relief after electrode implantation. 8 However, sustained long‐term improvements suggest the implantation effect is unlikely to be attributable to anesthetics. 6

The role of ASMs in the implantation effect remains unclear. In our study, neither the type nor the overall load of preimplantation ASMs differed significantly between responders and nonresponders. However, dosage, adherence, and changes in ASM regimens were not tracked. This limitation is shared by previous studies, which often assessed only inpatient medication load or overlooked medication adjustments altogether. 6 , 8 , 9 Future research should account for specific dosages and regimen changes as potential factors influencing seizure reduction following depth electrode implantation.

Given that clinical and demographic factors lack statistical significance here and in prior literature, 6 , 8 the precise timing of symptom reduction implicates electrode insertion as the primary driver of the implantation effect. We suspect this phenomenon is likely driven by electrode‐induced microlesions that disrupt seizure propagation networks within the epileptogenic zone. 7 , 16 , 17 At the molecular level, this disruption may be mediated by an acute inflammatory and neurophysiological cascade, including reactive astrogliosis, altered astrocyte–neuron signaling, gliotransmitter release, and localized neuroplastic axonal remodeling. 10 Crucially, because seizure reduction was independent of the total number of implanted electrodes, therapeutic modulation likely depends on the precise anatomical intersection of electrodes with critical network nodes rather than cumulative tissue trauma. Speculatively, this mechanism may also explain the lack of change in seizure frequency in some patients and increased seizure frequency in nonresponders. The former could indicate that electrodes missed critical epileptogenic networks, whereas the latter might arise from trauma‐induced reactive astrocytes releasing glutamate to elevate perielectrode neuronal excitability. 18 Ultimately, further research into these localized tissue–electrode interactions is required to elucidate the dual nature of this postimplantation network modulation.

This study is primarily limited by its retrospective design, which resulted in small, variable follow‐up cohorts due to inconsistent medical record documentation. Because patients were captured at differing intervals, these fluctuating sample sizes limit the reliability of longitudinal seizure dynamics. Furthermore, long‐term findings must be interpreted with caution, as intermediary seizure activity was not captured. Finally, postdischarge medication adherence, dosages, and adjustments were not systematically tracked. These limitations underscore the necessity for prospective studies utilizing standardized protocols to rigorously quantify seizure frequency at fixed intervals and comprehensively monitor longitudinal medication compliance.

CONCLUSIONS

Our study provides valuable insights into the implantation effect following depth electrode insertion for SEEG monitoring, with responder rates ranging from approximately 71.4% at 1 month to 50% at 12 months postimplantation. Despite multiple patients reporting decreases in seizure frequency, no statistically significant clinical predictors of a ≥50% reduction in seizure frequency were identified. However, because responders exhibited uncontrolled seizures immediately prior to admission, spontaneous reduction is unlikely. Although our findings support the hypothesis that electrode‐induced microlesions disrupt epileptic networks, and that anatomical location is a key determinant of efficacy, these mechanisms remain speculative. Larger prospective studies integrating systematic electrode localization data are required to comprehensively elucidate the variables driving the implantation effect.
