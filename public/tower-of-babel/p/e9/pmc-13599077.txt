Association between intermittent pneumatic compression use and deep vein thrombosis in hospitalized adult medical patients: a retrospective single-center case–cohort study in Japan
Authors: Daichi Arakaki, Mitsunaga Iwata, Teruhiko Terasawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundClinical practice guidelines of venous thromboembolism prophylaxis for acutely ill hospitalized medical patients recommend intermittent pneumatic compression (IPC) devices as a mechanical thromboprophylaxis option for those at high risk for bleeding. However, supporting evidence focusing on this patient population is limited.MethodsThis case–cohort study used a university hospital–based retrospective cohort to assess the association between IPC use and lower-extremity deep vein thrombosis (DVT) development within 30 days of admission among inpatients in Japan. The parental cohort included 3876 consecutive acutely ill patients aged 15 years and older admitted to a general internal medicine department between July 2016 and July 2021. Weighted multivariable survival models and analyses that additionally accounted for time-varying covariate effects and competing risks were employed.ResultsIn total, 52 patients who developed lower-extremity DVT within 30 days (9/52 [17%] with proximal lower-extremity DVT) and 144 who did not were finally included as cases and non-cases, respectively. Patients highly at risk for developing DVT, as classified by the proposed risk prediction scores, used IPC devices more frequently (p for trend range = 0.004–0.015). However, the association between pharmacologic prophylaxis frequency and the risk group demonstrated no difference. In the multivariable weighted Cox model, the adjusted point estimate indicated a lower risk of developing VTE associated with IPC use, although this result was not significant (hazard ratio [HR] = 0.23; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.03–1.56; p = 0.22). Regarding sensitivity, multivariable models accounted for both nonproportional hazards and deaths from non-DVT causes as competing risks showed that IPC use was significantly associated with reduced VTE development risk (HR = 0.16; 95% CI: 0.03–0.94; p = 0.04); however, the CI was wide.ConclusionsEvidence on the association between IPC use versus nonuse and reduced VTE risk in general medical inpatients remains inconclusive. Given that conducting randomized clinical trials requires substantial costs and resources, further research should focus on multicenter, practice-based, prospective studies with larger sample sizes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12959-026-00894-1.

Background

Venous thromboembolism (VTE), comprising deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism, is potentially fatal [1], with up to 30% of fatal cases [2]. VTE commonly develops in surgical patients and in high-risk hospitalized medical patients without preventive treatment. Symptomatic VTE incidence is up to 3% in acutely ill, hospitalized medical patients in North America and Europe [3, 4] and up to 1% in Asia [5].

VTE risk may be reduced through several prophylactic interventions [6], including pharmacologic thromboprophylaxis (unfractionated heparin [UFH], low-molecular-weight heparins, and other anticoagulants) and mechanical thromboprophylaxis (graduated compression stockings and intermittent pneumatic compression [IPC] devices). The individual’s risk of developing VTE, the benefit of prescribing a specific intervention, and the risk of bleeding are weighed to clinically determine the intervention’s indication. Tools to aid in predicting and weighing VTE [3, 7] and bleeding [8] risks are also proposed for acutely ill, hospitalized medical patients.

Recent evidence-based guidelines for hospitalized medical populations consistently recommend pharmacologic thromboprophylaxis for patients with acceptable bleeding risk and mechanical thromboprophylaxis for those with unacceptable bleeding risk [6, 9, 10]. However, evidence supporting IPC device use specifically for this population is limited. Therefore, current guideline recommendations rely largely on indirect or partially applicable evidence from other patient groups [6, 9, 10]. For instance, randomized comparative studies between IPC device use and no thromboprophylaxis mostly included surgical patient populations. In a recent meta-analysis of seven randomized clinical trials (RCTs) involving surgically treated patients, IPC device use was associated with reduced risks of developing lower-extremity DVT and bleeding by 60% and over 80%, respectively, compared with no preventive measure [11]. In an RCT targeting individuals with acute stroke, IPC use was associated with reduced lower-extremity DVT risk by 35% compared with no prevention [12]. However, evidence from real-world, observational settings was inconsistent. A propensity score–based observational study of critically ill patients requiring intensive care unit (ICU) admissions confirmed the benefit of IPC; similarly, results showed that IPC use was related to reduced lower-extremity DVT risk by 55% in comparison with no preventive measure [13]. Conversely, another U.S. single-center, propensity score–based study involving hospitalized medical patients found no difference in VTE incidence between IPC use and no thromboprophylaxis [14].

In Japan, the recently revised Joint Society Group Guideline on the Management of Pulmonary Thromboembolism, Deep Venous Thrombosis, and Pulmonary Hypertension is merely based on the recommendations of the aforementioned Western countries’ guidelines because of limited empirical data on VTE risk predictions and prevention interventions in Japanese populations, and their implementation relies on the discretion of the treating physicians [15]. Recently, in a university hospital setting in Japan, our group conducted a retrospective cohort study of acutely ill, hospitalized general medical patients and validated two VTE prediction scores (Padua Score and International Medical Prevention Registry on Venous Thromboembolism [IMPROVE]-VTE Risk Score [3, 7]) and a bleeding risk prediction score (IMPROVE Bleeding Risk Score [8]) [16, 17]. Given the lack of data on IPC effectiveness in Japanese acutely ill, hospitalized medical patients, examining its real-world effectiveness could contribute to the existing body of knowledge. Using an observational study design based on the same cohort, this study aimed to evaluate the preventive effectiveness of IPC in general internal medicine patients.

Methods

Study design and setting

This case–cohort study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and used the data of the Fujita Health University General Internal Medicine (FHUGIM) cohort obtained from a retrospective observational cohort study conducted at the Department of Emergency Medicine and General Internal Medicine in the abovementioned tertiary-care academic hospital in Japan [16]. The Fujita Health University Ethics Committee approved the protocols for both the original cohort study and the case–cohort study (approval numbers HM21-576 and HM24-149, respectively). The FHUGIM cohort included 3876 acutely ill, general medicine patients admitted to the ICU or general wards in our department between July 5, 2016, and July 5, 2021. Owing to the retrospective nature of the study, the written informed consent requirement was waived.

Participants and sample-size estimation

The FHUGIM cohort included 59 patients who developed DVT in the lower leg, thigh, or pelvis within 30 days of admission (case candidates) and 3817 patients who did not (non-case candidates). Retrospectively obtaining complete data on preventive interventions for VTE on over 3800 patients for the entire 30-day hospitalization period required significant effort and resources. Therefore, we decided to use a case–cohort design, which only analyzes a randomly selected subcohort and all cases [18].

Currently, no RCTs have examined the preventive effect of IPC specifically among acutely ill, hospitalized medical patients. Thus, we used data obtained by a U.S. single-center, propensity score–based study of a similar, acute medical setting (adjusted odds ratio range for IPC use vs. no prophylaxis, 0.90–0.99; DVT incidence, 0.22%) [14]. Assuming a 1% incidence rate for 30-day lower-extremity DVT and a 5% reduction in IPC’s preventive effect on DVT incidence (relative ratio [RR] = 0.95), we calculated 154 (4.0%) as the subcohort size according to the method proposed by Kubota [19], corresponding to the median subcohort sampling fraction of 4.1% (interquartile range [IQR]: 3.7%–9.1%) in previously reported studies with case–cohort designs [20]. Considering the possibility of exclusion because of missing data or ineligibility, or multiple inclusions of the same patients, the total number of randomly selected patients for the subcohort was ultimately increased to 160.

We used the same inclusion and exclusion criteria as applied in the original cohort [16]. Case and randomly selected subcohort patients had to be ≥15 years old and hospitalized for ≥3 days. Conversely, patients who had trauma, underwent surgery, were pregnant, received anticoagulation therapy for any reason at admission, or were hospitalized for treating VTE or bleeding were excluded.

Variables and data sources

One of the investigators (DA) extracted data on registered diagnosis procedure combination codes (equivalent to hospital admission codes) and individually described clinical information at admission from the FHUGIM database [16]. Similarly, data on baseline variables such as pharmacologic thromboprophylaxis, age, sex, BMI, active cancer occurrence, immobilization, heart or respiratory failure presence, acute infection or rheumatologic disorder presence, ICU admission, IMPROVE-VTE score, and Padua score were extracted [16].

The same investigator extracted data on VTE prophylaxis, including pharmacologic thromboprophylaxis that started <30 days of admission and IPC that started <2 days of admission, from the medical records. For patients admitted to the ICU, pharmacologic thromboprophylaxis with UFH (10,000 units per day) was routinely recommended unless contraindicated. In cases where heparin was contraindicated, mechanical thromboprophylaxis with IPC was employed. For patients admitted to the general ward, neither pharmacological nor mechanical thromboprophylaxis was routinely used and initiation was left to the discretion of the treating physicians. Graduated compression stockings were not routinely applied. IPC was standardized using either the Kendall SCD Express or the 700 Sequential Compression System with knee-length sleeves (Cardinal Health, Inc., Dublin, OH, USA). Devices were operated 24 hours per day under factory-default settings, delivering automated, non-adjustable sequential gradient compression. Compression cycles were dynamically adjusted by the device’s vascular refill detection system. Given that we typically started IPC on the day of or the day after admission in our clinical practice, only data obtained during the first 48 h after admission were extracted. We could not obtain data on IPC termination because our recording practice was nonstandardized. Therefore, this study assumed that the IPC therapy of a patient continued until discharge. All data were extracted by the same investigator (DA), who was blinded to the DVT event data.

Outcomes

The outcome of interest was the cumulative incidence of DVT in the lower extremities, including the lower leg, thigh, and pelvis, 30 days after admission. DVT that developed in the popliteal, femoral, or iliac veins was classified as proximal DVT. To confirm all DVT events, an investigator (DA) examined the imaging reports of contrast-enhanced computed tomography (interpreted by at least one board-certified radiologist) or lower-extremity venous ultrasonography (performed by a certified sonographer). These tests were performed as part of routine clinical practice in patients who developed symptoms suggestive of DVT.

Statistical analysis

We present continuous variables as means and standard deviations (SDs) or medians and IQRs as appropriate, and categorical variables as numbers and percentages. Group differences were assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and Fisher’s exact test for frequencies. The event rate across the ordinal risk groups was assessed using the Cochran–Armitage test for trend.

Initially, hazard ratios (HRs) and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between IPC use and DVT development were estimated using the Borgan II estimator for weighted Cox regression [21]. A weight of 1 was assigned to cases; for non-cases, weights were based on the inverse sampling fraction of the original cohort. We used the admission day as the timescale, with the follow-up beginning on day 1 of admission. Patients were censored on the date of DVT diagnosis, discharge without DVT, including non-VTE death, or 30 days, whichever occurred first. The two overlapping cases, which were randomly sampled as subcohort members, were analyzed as cases only, and the assigned weight was 1. Standard errors were estimated through robust variance. To assess the proportional hazards assumption, we visually evaluated covariate-adjusted log-log survival plots, along with quantitative analyses based on the Schoenfeld residuals.

Next, to address nonproportional hazards, we used weighted flexible parametric survival models to allow the effect of baseline variables to vary over time [22]. In this type of model, only variables possibly showing strong evidence of proportional hazards violation (a Schoenfeld residual test p < 0.0045 based on the Bonferroni correction for conservative control) were modeled with time-varying effects in the main analysis. Both the number of events and simultaneously assessed variables (n = 11) were small; hence, variables with nonsignificant and borderline (but not strong) evidence of violation (p ≥ 0.0045) were retained as baseline, time-fixed effects to maintain model stability and interpretability. The maximum number of degrees of freedom for specifying the restricted cubic splines to model the baseline (log cumulative) hazard was selected according to model convergence and interpretability [23]. Additionally, we specified a setting of four degrees of freedom (df = 4; i.e., 4 splines divided by 3 knots [specified at the 25-, 50-, and 75-centile positions of the data]) and three degrees of freedom (df = 3; i.e., 3 splines divided by 2 knots [specified at the 33- and 67-centile positions]), both for the restricted cubic spline function used for the baseline function and the time-varying effect, respectively. In sensitivity analysis, we relaxed this assumption and modeled all variables with any degree of evidence of proportional hazards violation (defined as p < 0.05) as time-varying effects.

To address non-VTE deaths (events preventing DVT development) as competing risks, we used the standard, Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard model, and the flexible parametric model accounting for the time-varying effect to estimate the cause-specific HRs [23]. In the flexible parametric model, we employed lower degrees of freedom than the main analysis to achieve model convergence, particularly df = 3 for the baseline function and df = 2 (i.e., 2 splines divided by 1 knot [specified at the 50-centile position]) for the time-varying effect.

According to the available baseline variables from the FHUGIM cohort to score the IMPROVE-VTE and Padua model, we adjusted for the use of pharmacologic thromboprophylaxis, age, sex, BMI, active cancer presence, prolonged pre-admission immobilization (as documented in medical records), heart or respiratory failure presence, acute infection or rheumatologic disorder presence, and ICU admission. Other binary variables were excluded because of collinearity. Given the small sample size, we did not conduct subgroup analyses. No missing data were noted. All statistical data were analyzed using StataNow SE 19.5 (Stata Corp, College Station, TX, USA). Two-tailed p-values below 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

In-hospital DVT development

During the 30-day admission period, the mean and median follow-up durations (days) were 14.5 (SD = 8.6) and 12 (IQR, 7–22 days), respectively. Overall, 7/196 (4%) patients died without DVT development within 30 days. In the weighted Kaplan–Meier plot, DVT developed in 3.5% of patients from the IPC group and 2.1% from the no-IPC group (log-rank p = 0.22; Fig. 2). In unadjusted analysis by the weighted Cox model, although the point estimate suggested increased DVT risk, the corresponding 95% CI was wide and not statistically significant (HR = 1.77; 95% CI: 0.71–4.42; p = 0.22) (Table 3). In the multivariable weighted Cox model, the adjusted point estimate indicated a lower risk for DVT associated with IPC use, although this result was not significant (HR = 0.23; 95% CI: 0.03–1.56; p = 0.13) (Table 3). Moreover, visual assessment of the covariate-adjusted log-log survival plots suggested a proportional hazards violation (Appendix Figure 1). Likewise, quantitative assessment suggested a proportional hazards violation (p = 0.007 for Global test), with only pharmacological prophylaxis use showing strong evidence of proportional hazards violation (p < 0.001) and active cancer presence exhibiting some evidence of proportional hazards violation (p = 0.01). Regarding the sensitivity analysis accounting for non-VTE deaths as competing risks by weighted Fine–Gray model, the result was nonsignificant, similar to the main analysis by the weighted Cox model (Table 3).

In the multivariable weighted regression analysis using the Royston–Lambert flexible parametric model, we specified the effect of pharmacological prophylaxis as time-varying in the main analysis. Although this adjusted analysis model further attenuated the adjusted HR for IPC use compared with the effect estimates obtained from the weighted Cox model, the association remained nonsignificant (HR = 0.18; 95% CI: 0.03–1.32; p = 0.09) (Table 3; Fig. 3). In the sensitivity analysis that additionally modeled non-VTE deaths as competing risks, the CI was wide, but IPC use was significantly associated with reduced DVT risk (HR = 0.16; 95% CI: 0.03–0.94; p = 0.04) (Table 3; Fig. 4). Discussion

In this retrospective, single-center, case–cohort study conducted at a Japanese university hospital, evidence of the association between IPC use versus nonuse and reduced risk of lower-extremity DVT was inconclusive, despite that the point estimate suggested an approximately 80% risk reduction in the adjusted analyses. The sensitivity analysis conducted in this study addressed both nonproportional hazards, likely caused by time-varying effects of pharmacologic thromboprophylaxis, and competing risks caused by early deaths from other causes that may have prevented DVT development. Results revealed that the adjusted effect of IPC use versus nonuse was significantly associated with DVT risk reduction. However, the estimated significant adjusted effect is not robust because the CI of the adjusted RRs was wide and the overlapping confidence regions of the cumulative DVT incidences was large in both groups.

In contrast to our inconclusive results, another similar observational study focusing on hospitalized medical patients, particularly a U.S. single-center, retrospective, propensity score–based cohort study, reported no difference in VTE incidence between the IPC group and the control (no-IPC group) (adjusted odds ratio [OR], 0.99: 95% CI: 0.57–1.74; and propensity score-matched OR, 0.90; 95% CI: 0.48–1.70) [14]. However, aside from various study designs and statistical analyses, clinically important differences were noted in the case-mixed group; our cohort included more patients at high VTE risk than that of the U.S. study (Appendix Table 2) [14]. Furthermore, our cohort included older and critically ill patients, including those with aspiration pneumonia, urinary tract infections, and sepsis, with additional severe heart failure in some patients. These patients were typically bedridden and were nursing home residents with a stroke history.

Our study has several strengths. First, this study is the first in East Asia to evaluate the effectiveness of using IPC in hospitalized medical patients. Although the study design is retrospective and the data were derived from clinical practice, our case–cohort study provides valuable insights. IPC may be effective for some general medical inpatients with higher risk. Second, we conducted rigorous medical record review to ensure that all IPC uses started within two days of admission for all selected individuals. This form of intervention is similar to that used in previous RCTs [24]. Our meticulous verification was achievable because we used a case–cohort design, which allowed us to obtain the pertinent data with limited resources by focusing on a smaller sample selected from the original, parental cohort. Third, unlike previous studies, we addressed both nonproportional hazards and competing risks.

Conversely, this study has several limitations. First, it was a retrospective analysis based on a single-center experience. Retrospective studies are susceptible to methodological limitations affecting data reliability. Our analysis relied on data from the FHUGIM cohort, a retrospective cohort involving nonstandardized, practice-based diagnoses of baseline comorbidities. Incorrectly assigned disease codes could also be possible. These limitations may have caused incorrect imputation of missing data on predictors. Second, although the original cohort had a total of 3,876 participants, the sample size may have still been small, with only 53 events, leading to inconclusive results. Moreover, the incidence of symptomatic and clinically diagnosed DVT in our study (1.4%) was comparable to that reported in Western studies (1–3%) [3, 4]. However, our analysis was based on outcomes observed at 30 days only; hence, events observed thereafter were censored, as in those studies. Therefore, even though late-onset, post-discharge VTE events are common, our results should not be applied to post-discharge, long-term VTE prevention [3]. Third, despite the use of a case–cohort design, the IPC therapy duration could not be extracted because of nonstandardized recording practices. Evaluation of the causal effect of sustained interventional strategies, such as IPC therapies, requires statistical adjustment for time-varying prognostic factors, using advanced analytical methods, including G-methods [25]. However, our analysis assumed that IPC use, as well as any pharmacologic thromboprophylaxis, began at baseline and continued until day 30 or discharge. In our flexible parametric survival models, the time-varying effect of pharmacologic thromboprophylaxis was modeled, but the time-varying status of the interventions or changes in other prognostic factors was not. In our clinical practice, starting any form of thromboprophylaxis after three days of admission was rare; hence, we conducted multiple regression analysis using only baseline variables as the time-fixed effects. Although we addressed all proposed prognostic factors except those that caused collinearity, we failed to assess how the number of days or additional post-admission initiation of pharmacologic prophylaxis, IPC, or both can affect the results. Depending on how prophylactic interventions were (additionally) initiated and/or terminated after the admission, the effect estimates based on our analytical framework may have been overestimated or underestimated.

In conclusion, evidence on the effectiveness of IPC in reducing VTE in hospitalized general medical patients remains inconclusive. Given that conducting an RCT requires substantial costs and resources, further research should conduct multicenter, practice-based, prospective studies with larger sample sizes instead. Aside from increasing the number of events, collecting reliable information on how IPC is applied, as well as pertinent prognostic factors at baseline and during hospitalization, is key to evaluating the causal effect more accurately. Therefore, developing a research-focused data recording system that functions effectively in real-world clinical settings is critical to generate reliable data. Furthermore, the data collected should include other patient-relevant outcomes such as reduction of edema and improvement of symptoms associated with immobility, including heaviness and fatigue in the limbs.
