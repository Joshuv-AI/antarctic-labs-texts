Proanthocyanidin‐Rich Cranberry Extract Lowers Glycemia in Established Obesity by Delaying Glucose Absorption
Authors: Sarra Beji, Mathilde Mouchiroud, Laura Tribouillard, Bernie Efole, Lia Perazza, Léa Favereaux, Audrey Poirier, Wandy Idris, Yves Gélinas, Thibault V. Varin, Carole Rovère, Fernando Forato Anhê, Frédéric Picard, Mathieu Laplante, André Marette, Andréanne Michaud, Alexandre Caron
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDietary polyphenols, including proanthocyanidins, have emerged as potential modulators of metabolic health. Evidence supports benefits on glucose and hepatic metabolism in diet‐induced obesity. However, reported effects vary widely across polyphenol sources and experimental design, and the key physiological mediators of benefit in established obesity remain incompletely defined. Moreover, ambient temperature, a key determinant of metabolic phenotype that may influence therapeutic responses, is rarely considered. Here we aim to determine the metabolic effects and mechanisms of action of a proanthocyanidin‐rich cranberry extract (PRCE) in established diet‐induced obesity under cold (10°C) and thermoneutral (30°C) housing conditions. Male mice with established obesity were supplemented with PRCE or vehicle while housed at 10°C or 30°C. Metabolic phenotyping included body composition, glucose homeostasis, intestinal carbohydrate digestion and glucose absorption, circadian profiling of peripheral and central clocks, and gut microbiota analysis. PRCE supplementation significantly improved glycemia and glucose tolerance independently of temperature, without altering body weight, adiposity, thermogenic gene expression, or circadian expression of clock genes centrally and peripherally. Mechanistically, PRCE inhibited α‐amylase activity and delayed early intestinal glucose absorption. These effects were accompanied by selective remodeling of the gut microbiota, including increased abundance of Akkermansia muciniphila . We conclude that PRCE improves glucose homeostasis in established obesity through intestinal mechanisms involving reduced carbohydrate digestion, delayed glucose absorption, and selective remodeling of the gut microbiota.

Introduction

Obesity and type 2 diabetes (T2D) continue to rise worldwide despite major advances in pharmacological and behavioral interventions [1, 2]. In 2022, more than 650 million adults were living with obesity, a prevalence that has nearly tripled over the past four decades [2], and which remains strongly associated with insulin resistance and cardiometabolic disease [3, 4]. Current therapies, including intestinal carbohydrate digestion inhibitors such as acarbose [5, 6], and more recent incretin‐based agents [7, 8], have substantially improved glycemic control and body weight management. However, important limitations remain, including gastrointestinal intolerance such as diarrhea [9], loss of lean mass [10], and modest or variable effects on energy expenditure (EE) [11]. Together, these limitations highlight the need to explore complementary strategies that can improve metabolic control or engage pathways not fully addressed by current treatments.

Dietary bioactive compounds have emerged as potential modulators of metabolic diseases [12]. Among these, polyphenols, abundant in fruits and vegetables, have been reported to influence glucose homeostasis, lipid metabolism, and energy balance in preclinical models [13, 14]. Proposed mechanisms include improved insulin sensitivity [15, 16], modulation of hepatic metabolism [17], reduced intestinal glucose absorption [18, 19], changes in thermogenic programs [20], chronobiotic potential [21, 22], and remodeling of gut microbiota [15, 16, 20]. However, the relative contribution of these mechanisms and how they interact in the setting of established metabolic disease remain incompletely defined.

Proanthocyanidins, a subclass of flavan‐3‐ol oligomers and polymers, have attracted particular interest due to their potent biological activity [23], and limited systemic bioavailability [24], suggesting that their metabolic effects are mediated predominantly through actions within the gastrointestinal tract [24]. Proanthocyanidin‐rich extracts derived from berries and grapes have been shown to improve glucose tolerance, attenuate diet‐induced insulin resistance, and modulate lipid metabolism in rodents [15, 16, 25, 26]. In addition to these metabolic effects, emerging evidence suggests that proanthocyanidins may influence circadian regulation, a key determinant of metabolic health [21, 22]. Circadian rhythms coordinate daily oscillations in glucose metabolism, insulin sensitivity, EE, and feeding behavior through molecular clocks in central and peripheral tissues [27]. Disruption of these rhythms is strongly associated with metabolic dysfunction and obesity [28, 29, 30]. However, whether modulation of circadian clock function contributes meaningfully to the metabolic effects of proanthocyanidins in established obesity remains unclear.

A limitation of the existing literature is that many studies investigating polyphenols and proanthocyanidin‐rich extracts rely on preventive protocols, in which treatment is initiated simultaneously with high‐fat feeding or prior to the development of obesity and insulin resistance [16, 20, 31, 32, 33]. Nevertheless, therapeutic studies have demonstrated that polyphenols can improve metabolic complications even after obesity is established. For example, administration of a proanthocyanidin‐rich cranberry extract to mice with established obesity reversed insulin resistance and hepatic steatosis independently of body weight loss [26]. Despite these advances, the relative contribution of distinct mechanisms, including intestinal glucose handling, circadian rhythms, and systemic energy balance, and the influence of environmental factors such as ambient temperature remains incompletely defined in the context of established metabolic disease.

Environmental temperature represents an additional, and often overlooked, determinant of metabolic outcomes in rodent models. Mice housed at standard temperature (~22°C) experience chronic mild cold stress that elevates sympathetic tone, activates brown adipose tissue (BAT) thermogenesis, and increases basal EE [34, 35]. In contrast, thermoneutral housing (~30°C) reduces cold‐induced thermogenesis and has been shown to exacerbate diet‐induced obesity and related metabolic dysfunction in mice, while also reducing inter‐individual variability in metabolic and inflammatory readouts [36, 37]. These considerations are particularly relevant when studying interventions that may engage intestinal, hepatic, and thermogenic pathways. Notably, housing temperature can influence the apparent metabolic efficacy of polyphenols [38, 39]. For example, the improvement in glucose tolerance reported with (−)‐epicatechin was more pronounced under thermoneutral conditions compared to standard housing [38]. Together, these observations support the rationale for evaluating proanthocyanidin‐rich interventions across environmental temperatures.

Here, we investigated the metabolic effects of a proanthocyanidin‐rich cranberry extract (PRCE) in a model of established diet‐induced obesity in mice housed under either cold exposure (10°C) or thermoneutral conditions (30°C). We examined whether PRCE supplementation improves metabolic health in obese mice and assessed the contribution of multiple candidate pathways, including intestinal glucose handling, hepatic metabolism, and potential roles for thermogenic capacity, circadian regulation, and gut microbiota composition.

Material and Methods

Animals and Experimental Design

All experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Université Laval (CPAUL) and conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Canadian Council on Animal Care. All experiments complied with ARRIVE guidelines.

Seven‐week‐old male C3H/HeJ mice (#000659, The Jackson Laboratory) were housed under a 12:12‐h light/dark cycle (lights on at 06:00, Zeitgeber time 0 [ZT0]) with ad libitum access to water and standard chow for 1 week prior to experimental manipulations. Body weight and food intake were measured daily from the start of the treatment period until the end of the study.

Cohort 1 (Diet‐Induced Obesity and Temperature Challenge)

Mice were fed a high‐fat diet (HFD; D12451, 45% kcal from fat; Research Diets) for 8 weeks to induce obesity. Animals were then individually housed at either thermoneutrality (30°C) or cold exposure (10°C) for an additional 4 weeks while continuing the HFD. During this period, mice received a daily oral gavage of vehicle (water) or cranberry extract rich in proanthocyanidins (PRCE; 0.2 g/kg body weight), administered at ZT2 (8:00). Each experimental group included eight mice. At the end of the protocol, mice were euthanized, and tissues, including brown adipose tissue (BAT), inguinal white adipose tissue (iWAT), epididymal white adipose tissue (eWAT), and liver were rapidly dissected and weighed. All tissues were then rapidly snap‐frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C for subsequent analyses.

Cohort 2 (Lean Controls and Temperature Challenge)

Mice were maintained on a low‐fat diet (LFD; D12450H, 10% kcal from fat; Research Diets) for 4 weeks and subsequently housed at either 30°C or 10°C for an additional 4 weeks while continuing the LFD. Animals received the same daily gavage protocol as described for Cohort 1. Tissue collection, weighing, and storage were performed as described above.

Cohort 3 (Circadian Analyses)

For circadian analyses, mice were initially maintained on LFD for 4 weeks, after which they were transferred to thermoneutral housing (30°C) for an additional 4 weeks while receiving daily oral gavage of either vehicle or PRCE. In parallel, a separate group of mice was fed a HFD for 8 weeks followed by 4 weeks at 30°C under the same gavage regimen. At the end of the protocol, mice were euthanized at six Zeitgeber time points (ZT4, ZT8, ZT12, ZT16, ZT20, and ZT24) to capture circadian variation. For dark‐phase collections, mice were transferred from the housing room in a light‐impermeable transport box to a dedicated procedure room maintained in complete darkness. Animals remained in the dark until induction of isoflurane anesthesia and euthanasia to minimize acute light exposure and photic entrainment. Room lights were turned on only after euthanasia for tissue collection. Tissues were rapidly harvested, snap‐frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C until further analysis.

Body Composition Analysis

Body composition was assessed by nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy with a Bruker Minispec LF90 analyzer.

Glucose Homeostasis

Oral Glucose Tolerance Test (OGTT)

For OGTTs, mice were fasted for 12 h (20:00–08:00) and orally administered D‐glucose (1 g/kg body weight; Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO), either alone or in combination with PRCE. Blood glucose levels were measured from the tail vein using a glucometer (Accu‐Chek Performa; Roche) at specified time points.

Insulin Tolerance Test (ITT)

For ITTs, mice were fasted for 6 h (07:00–13:00) and injected intraperitoneally with insulin (0.75 U/kg; Humulin, Lilly, Canada). Blood glucose was measured from the tail vein using a glucometer (Accu‐Chek Performa; Roche) at indicated time points.

Intestinal Glucose Absorption

Intestinal glucose absorption was evaluated using the non‐metabolizable glucose analog 3‐O‐methyl‐D‐glucopyranose (3‐OMG; Sigma, M4879), as previously described by Anhê et al. [40]. Briefly, following oral gavage with 3‐OMG (4 mg/mouse), blood samples were collected over 90 min, and tracer appearance in plasma was determined by high‐performance liquid chromatography coupled to tandem mass spectrometry (HPLC‐MS/MS) at the Centre for Microbial Chemical Biology (McMaster University). Plasma 3‐OMG appearance kinetics were analyzed to characterize the dynamics of intestinal glucose absorption. The early phase (0–20 min) represents the initial rate of tracer appearance in the circulation and primarily reflects proximal intestinal glucose transport across the enterocyte epithelium [41]. The early slope (0–20 min) was therefore calculated as an index of the initial rate of intestinal glucose uptake. The late phase (20–40 min) reflects the balance between continued intestinal absorption and systemic distribution or clearance of the tracer. Accordingly, the late slope (20–40 min) was used to assess the persistence of intestinal glucose absorption after the initial uptake phase. Positive slope values indicate ongoing intestinal absorption, whereas decreasing or negative slopes indicate that systemic clearance exceeds intestinal entry. To further quantify early glucose uptake, fractional absorption of 3‐OMG during the early phase was calculated. This parameter represents the proportion of the administered tracer that appears in the circulation during the initial absorption window and therefore provides an integrated measure of early intestinal glucose absorption efficiency. Lower fractional absorption values were interpreted as reduced or delayed intestinal glucose uptake.

Plasma Metabolites and Hormones

Blood glucose was measured from the tail vein samples using a glucometer. Terminal blood samples were collected by cardiac puncture into EDTA‐conditioned syringes. Plasma was isolated by centrifugation (4000 g × 10 min at 4°C) and stored at −80°C.

Plasma metabolites and hormones were measured according to the manufacturers' instructions using the following kits: insulin (Crystal Chem, 90 080), GLP‐1 (Novus biologicals, NBP3‐18782), C‐peptide (ALPCO, 80‐CPTMS‐E01), cholesterol (Randox laboratories, CH200), triglycerides (Randox laboratories, TR210), and non‐esterified fatty acids (NEFAs; NC9587, 999–34 691, 995–34 791, 991–34 891, 993–35 191; Fujifilm).

Hepatic Triglycerides and Glycogen Content

Hepatic triglycerides were extracted using the Folch method [42], and quantified using a commercial colorimetric assay (RANDOX, TR210). Hepatic glycogen concentration was measured as previously described [43]. Briefly, glycogen was extracted in 30% KOH saturated with Na2SO4, precipitated in 95% ethanol, resuspended in distilled water, and quantified using the phenol‐sulfuric acid method. Absorbance was measured at 490 nm in triplicate, and glycogen concentration was calculated using a standard curve (Millipore Sigma, 10 901 393 001).

Quantitative Real‐Time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from brown adipose tissue (BAT), liver, mediobasal hypothalamus (MBH) and duodenum using the Monarch Total RNA Miniprep Kit (Bio‐Rad, T2010S), including on‐column DNase I digestion. RNA concentration and purity were assessed by spectrophotometry (BioDrop Touch Duo, Cambridge, UK). For each sample, 700 ng of total RNA was reverse‐transcribed using qScript cDNA SuperMix (Quantabio, 95 047–100). cDNA was diluted (1:15) in nuclease‐free water prior to amplification. Quantitative PCR was performed in duplicate using PerfeCTa SYBR Green FastMix (Quantabio, 95 072–012) on a Bio‐Rad CFX384 real‐time system. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S1. Amplification specificity was confirmed by melt curve analysis, agarose gel electrophoresis, and sequencing. Gene expression was normalized to a housekeeping gene validated as stable across experimental conditions and expressed relative to vehicle‐treated mice housed at 30°C (or LFD vehicle controls for circadian studies).

α‐Amylase Inhibitory Activity

α‐Amylase inhibition was assessed using human salivary α‐amylase (A1031, Sigma) and starch substrate following the 3,5‐dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS) method [44]. Briefly, α‐amylase (MilliporeSigma) was prepared in sodium phosphate buffer (pH 6.9) containing NaCl, and starch (1% w/v) was prepared in the same buffer. Different concentrations of PRCE and acarbose (positive control) were pre‐incubated with the starch substrate, and the reaction was initiated by addition of α‐amylase. After incubation for 3 min at room temperature, the reaction was stopped by adding DNS reagent. Samples were boiled for 15 min, cooled to room temperature, diluted, and absorbance was measured at 540 nm in triplicate. Parallel control reactions using heat‐inactivated α‐amylase were performed to confirm that the signal was dependent on enzymatic activity. Percent inhibition was calculated relative to enzyme‐free and control reactions. Percent inhibition was calculated as follows: %inhibition=100×1−A_enzyme−A_blank/A_control−A_blank

Western Blotting

Liver tissues were homogenized in lysis buffer containing 50 mM HEPES, pH 7.4, 2 mM EDTA, 10 mM sodium pyrophosphate, 10 mM sodium glycerophosphate, 40 mM NaCl, 50 mM NaF, 2 mM sodium orthovanadate, 1% Triton‐× 100, 0.1% sodium lauryl sulfate, 1% sodium deoxycholate and one tablet of EDTA‐free protease inhibitors Roche per 25 mL. Protein concentrations were measured by Bradford assay. Equal amounts of total protein per sample were denatured (95°C, 10 min), separated on SDS‐PAGE, and transferred to PVDF membranes. Blots were probed with primary antibodies against AKT (CST, 4691S), phospho‐Ser473‐AKT (CST, 4060), and CEACAM1 (D1P4T, CST, 14771), at a dilution of 1:1000 according to the manufacturer's recommendations, followed by HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies. Signal was detected using Amersham ECL reagent and band intensities were quantified using Image Lab software (Bio‐Rad). Protein levels were normalized either to total AKT or to total protein per lane using corresponding strain‐free blot image. Band intensities were quantified using Image Lab software (Bio‐Rad).

16S rRNA Gene‐Based Gut Microbial Analysis

The microbiota composition of cecal contents was determined by 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing. Cecal DNA was extracted from fresh cecal content with the NucleoSpin 96 Soil kit (Macherey‐Nagel, Duren, Germany) and then sent to the sequencing platform at Génome Québec for PCR amplification of the V3‐V4 region using the primers 341F and 805R, and followed by sequencing on an Illumina NextSeq sequencing platform (Illumina, CA, USA), as detailed previously [45]. Forward and reverse primers were removed from raw paired‐end reads using Cutadapt (v4.1). Sequences were processed using the DADA2 package (v1.30) [46], in the R environment (http://www.R‐project.org). Reads with an expected error threshold > 2 and > 4 for the forward and reverse reads respectively, with ambiguous bases, and with quality score less than or equal to 2 were discarded. Dereplication and denoising of filtered sequences were carried out using DADA2 default parameters. The resulting amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were assigned to bacterial taxa using the RDP classifier algorithm (v2.14) [47], trained against the Silva database 138.1 (5). ASVs present in fewer than 3 samples in the whole dataset were discarded.

Statistical Analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± SEM. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 unless otherwise specified. Depending on the experimental design, two‐ or three‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, followed by Tukey's multiple‐comparisons test when appropriate. For circadian datasets, amplitude was calculated as (maximum—minimum)/2 across the measured Zeitgeber time points. Normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed prior to parametric testing. A p ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Limited Effects of PRCE on Body Weight in Obese and Lean Mice

To assess the interaction between ambient temperature and PRCE supplementation in established obesity, mice were first fed a high‐fat diet (HFD) for 8 weeks and then housed at either thermoneutrality (30°C) or cold exposure (10°C) for an additional 4 weeks while continuing HFD feeding (Figure 1A). During this period, mice received daily oral gavage of vehicle or PRCE (0.2 g/kg) administered at ZT2.

Ambient temperature, but not PRCE supplementation, alters body weight and body composition in obese mice. (A) Experimental design. (B, C) Body weight during the 4‐week treatment period. (D, E) Daily and cumulative food intake. (F) Feed efficiency. (G‐I) Fat, lean, and fluid mass. (J) Liver weight. (K) Brown adipose tissue (BAT) weight. (L) Inguinal white adipose tissue (iWAT) weight. (M) Epididymal white adipose tissue (eWAT) weight. Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical analyses were performed using two‐way ANOVA. *p ≤ 0.05, **p ≤ 0.01, and ***p ≤ 0.0001; n = 8/group.

As expected, cold exposure induced a significant reduction in body weight and body weight change compared to thermoneutrality (p = 0.0004, and p < 0.0001, Figure 1B,C). PRCE supplementation showed only a tendency to further reduce body weight (p = 0.0548, and p = 0.1065, Figure 1B,C). Daily and cumulative food intake were significantly increased under cold exposure, reflecting increased energetic demand (p < 0.0001, Figure 1D,E), with no effect of PRCE treatment. Accordingly, feed efficiency was significantly reduced under cold exposure (Figure 1F). Cold exposure significantly reduced both fat mass and lean mass (p < 0.0001, and p = 0.0040, respectively, Figure 1G,H), whereas PRCE supplementation did not alter body composition. Fluid mass was unchanged across all conditions (Figure 1I), Consistent with these observations, cold exposure reduced liver weight (p = 0.0139, Figure 1J), and increased brown adipose tissue (BAT) mass (p = 0.0216, Figure 1K). Inguinal and epididymal white adipose tissue (iWAT and eWAT) masses were significantly reduced by cold exposure (p < 0.0001, Figure 1L,M), with no effect of PRCE. To further determine whether PRCE influences thermogenic capacity in BAT, we examined the expression of key genes involved in thermogenesis and lipid metabolism. Neither Ucp1 nor Dio2 expression was altered by PRCE treatment (Figure S1A). Ppargc1a expression was increased (p = 0.0153), while genes involved in lipid oxidation and synthesis (Acox1, Fasn and Dgat1) were unchanged (Figure S1B).

To examine whether PRCE affects body weight regulation in lean conditions, mice fed a low‐fat diet (LFD) were subjected to the same thermal challenge (Figure S2A). In contrast to obese mice, cold exposure increased body weight gain without affecting body weight over time (p = 0.0434, Figure S2B,C) and cumulative food intake (p < 0.0001, Figure S2D,E), without affecting feed efficiency (Figure S2F). Fat mass was reduced (p = 0.0167, Figure S2G), whereas lean and fluid mass were unchanged (Figure S2H,I). Cold exposure increased both liver and BAT weights (p = 0.0090 and p < 0.0001, respectively; Figure S2J,K), with no differences observed in iWAT or eWAT mass (Figure S2L,M). PRCE supplementation had no measurable metabolic effects in lean mice.

Together, these results confirm that ambient temperature modulates energy balance and body composition in both obese and lean mice. In contrast, PRCE supplementation did not significantly affect body weight, food intake, adiposity, or BAT thermogenic gene expression under either thermal condition.

PRCE Improves Glycemic Control in Obese Mice

We next examined whether PRCE supplementation affects glucose homeostasis in obese mice. Basal glycemia was significantly reduced in PRCE‐treated mice compared with vehicle controls (main effect of PRCE: p = 0.0256, Figure 2A). Cold exposure did not modulate glycemia (main effect of temperature: p = 0.6559, Figure 2A), and no interaction between PRCE treatment and ambient temperature was detected (p = 0.3103), indicating that the glucose‐lowering effect of PRCE occurred independently of thermal conditions. Cold exposure significantly reduced glycemic excursion during glucose challenge (main effect of temperature: p < 0.0001, Figure 2B). PRCE supplementation further improved glucose tolerance (main effect of treatment: p = 0.0211), with lower glycemic values particularly evident during the early phase of the glucose tolerance test. Similarly, no interaction between PRCE treatment and ambient temperature was detected. Consistent with these observations, the area under the curve (AUC) was reduced by cold exposure (main effect of temperature: p < 0.0001), while PRCE treatment showed a trend toward reduced AUC (main effect of treatment: p = 0.0590), with no significant interaction between temperature and treatment (Figure 2C).

(B) Glycemic excursion during an oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT). (C) Area under the curve (AUC) of the oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT). (D) Plasma insulin levels. (E) HOMA‐IR. (F) Glycemic response during the insulin tolerance test (ITT). (G) AUC of the ITT. (H) Plasma C‐peptide levels. (I) Plasma GLP‐1 concentrations. (J) Plasma cholesterol levels. (K) Plasma triglyceride levels. (L) Plasma non‐esterified fatty acids (NEFA). (M‐O) Protein expression and quantification in the liver. Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical analyses were performed using two‐way or three‐way ANOVA. *p ≤ 0.05, **p ≤ 0.01, and ***p ≤ 0.0001; n = 8/group.

Cold exposure significantly decreased hyperinsulinemia in obese mice (main effect of temperature: p = 0.0153, Figure 2D), whereas PRCE supplementation did not significantly affect circulating insulin levels (p = 0.1989). Consistent with this finding, HOMA‐IR was significantly reduced under cold exposure (p = 0.0235, Figure 2E), suggesting improved insulin sensitivity. This interpretation was further supported by insulin tolerance tests, which demonstrated improved insulin sensitivity in cold‐exposed mice (Figure 2F,G). Circulating C‐peptide levels were significantly reduced by cold exposure (main effect of temperature: p = 0.0002), with a tendency toward lower levels in PRCE‐treated mice (p = 0.0652, Figure 2H). Plasma GLP‐1 concentrations were significantly increased by cold exposure (main effect of temperature: p < 0.0001), with no significant effect of PRCE treatment (p = 0.1078, Figure 2I). Similarly, plasma cholesterol, triglycerides, and non‐esterified fatty acids (NEFA) were all significantly reduced under cold exposure (Figure 2J–L), whereas PRCE supplementation had no detectable effect on circulating lipid levels.

To further explore whether PRCE influenced hepatic pathways involved in glucose homeostasis, we examined markers of insulin signaling, insulin clearance, and expression of genes involved in glucose metabolism in the liver. Basal hepatic AKT phosphorylation (Ser473), a downstream effector of insulin signaling, was significantly reduced by both cold exposure and PRCE treatment (main effect of temperature: p < 0.0001; main effect of PRCE: p = 0.0270; Figure 2M,N). In contrast, hepatic levels of CEACAM1, a protein involved in receptor‐mediated insulin clearance, were increased by cold exposure, with no significant effect of PRCE treatment (Figure 2M,O). CEACAM1 protein levels were quantified by normalization to total protein using the corresponding stain‐free image (Figure S3A–C). PRCE supplementation significantly increased the expression of genes involved in glycolysis and glycogen metabolism, including Hif1a, Pklr, and Pygl, independently of ambient temperature (p = 0.0128, p = 0.0354, and p = 0.0287, respectively, Figure S4A), while Ugp2 expression showed a trend toward increase (p = 0.0539, Figure S4A). Hepatic glycogen content was significantly reduced by cold exposure (p = 0.0316, Figure S3B), but was not affected by PRCE supplementation. PRCE also significantly reduced hepatic Pdk4 expression (p = 0.0247) and increased Pcx expression (p = 0.0437), whereas Pck1 expression was regulated primarily by temperature (Figure S4C). We observed a modest increase in Acox1 expression following PRCE supplementation (p = 0.0483), while Fasn expression and total hepatic triglyceride content were largely modulated by ambient temperature (Figure S4D,E).

Together, these data show that ambient temperature is a dominant determinant of systemic metabolic status, with cold exposure lowering fasting insulin and HOMA‐IR, improving insulin responsiveness, and reducing circulating lipid levels. In contrast, PRCE exerted a modest but consistent glucose‐lowering effect, reducing basal glycemia and attenuating early glycemic responses during glucose challenge.

PRCE Modulates Intestinal Glucose Absorption in Obese Mice

Given that PRCE improves glycemia without significant changes in fasting insulin levels, GLP‐1 concentrations, circulating lipid profiles, or hepatic markers of insulin signaling and clearance, we next investigated whether intestinal mechanisms could contribute to its glucose‐lowering effects. Notably, the reduction in glycemia was most apparent during the early phase of the glucose tolerance test, a period strongly influenced by intestinal glucose absorption. Polyphenols and proanthocyanidins have previously been shown to influence carbohydrate digestion and intestinal glucose handling [48, 49]. We therefore first evaluated the ability of PRCE to inhibit the carbohydrate‐digesting enzyme α‐amylase using a spectrophotometric assay measuring maltose release from starch. In this assay, inhibition is expressed as the percentage reduction in enzymatic activity relative to control. PRCE dose‐dependently inhibited α‐amylase, exhibiting partial inhibition compared with reference inhibitor acarbose (Figure 3A). To confirm that the measured signal reflected enzymatic activity rather than nonspecific optical interference from the extract, we performed control assays using heat‐inactivated α‐amylase. Under these conditions, no maltose production was detected and PRCE produced no measurable effect, confirming that the inhibitory signal observed in the active enzyme assay depended on functional α‐amylase activity (Figure 3B,C).

(B, C) Enzyme activity with active (+A) versus heat‐inactivated (−A) α‐amylase. (D) Plasma 3‐OMG levels. (E, F) Early (0–20 min) and late (20–40 min) slopes of 3‐OMG appearance. (G) Early fractional 3‐OMG absorption. Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical analyses were performed using two‐way ANOVA. *p ≤ 0.05, **p ≤ 0.01, and ***p ≤ 0.0001; n = 6–8/group and n = 3/group (for in vitro data presented in A–C).

We next examined whether PRCE alters intestinal glucose absorption in vivo using 3‐O‐methyl‐D‐glucose (3‐OMG), a non‐metabolizable glucose analog (Figure 3D). Kinetic analysis of plasma 3‐OMG appearance showed that PRCE did not alter the initial rise in tracer levels, as the 0–20 min slope was unchanged across temperatures (Figure 3E). This indicates that PRCE did not measurably affect the very early rate of tracer entry into the circulation immediately after gavage. In contrast, PRCE significantly modified the 20–40 min slope (p = 0.0026, Figure 3F), consistent with a slower and/or more prolonged absorption phase rather than a faster early influx. In line with this interpretation, early fractional absorption (F20) was significantly reduced by PRCE (p = 0.0016, Figure 3G), demonstrating that a smaller proportion of the administered tracer reached the systemic circulation within the first 20 min, independent of ambient temperature. We also examined the duodenal expression of key intestinal glucose transporters, including Glut1, Glut2, and Sglt1. Neither cold exposure nor PRCE treatment significantly altered the expression of these genes (Figure S5A,B).

Collectively, these data indicate that PRCE does not alter the very early rate of glucose entry into the circulation but could reduce early systemic glucose appearance and shift absorption toward a later time window. These findings suggest a delay in intestinal glucose availability, potentially arising from combined effects on carbohydrate digestion and intestinal glucose absorption.

Limited Impact of PRCE on Obesity‐Induced Circadian Disruption

Polyphenols have been proposed to exert chronobiotic effects by modulating circadian clock function and metabolic rhythmicity [21, 22]. In addition, environmental cues such as photoperiod, cold exposure, and feeding timing can strongly influence peripheral clock activity, metabolic rhythms, and feeding behavior [50, 51]. To determine whether PRCE supplementation influences circadian metabolic regulation, we assessed metabolic parameters and clock gene expression across multiple ZT in LFD and HFD‐fed mice housed at thermoneutrality and treated with vehicle or PRCE (Figure 4A). PRCE supplementation did not affect glycemia across the circadian cycle in lean mice. In obese mice, however, PRCE significantly lowered glycemia across the sampling window (main effect of treatment: p = 0.0310, Figure 4B). Importantly, this effect reflected a shift in overall glycemia rather than a modification of the circadian waveform. Circadian oscillations of insulin, triglycerides, and cholesterol were not significantly affected by PRCE supplementation, whereas GLP‐1 levels were increased by PRCE under LFD conditions (Figure S6A–D).

(C‐H) Hepatic mRNA expression of core clock genes (Bmal1, Clock, Cry1, Per2, Nr1d1, and Nr1d2). (I‐N) Amplitude of hepatic clock gene expression. Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical analyses were performed using two‐way ANOVA. *p ≤ 0.05, **p ≤ 0.01, and ***p ≤ 0.0001; n = 47 (LFD), n = 48 (HFD) (n = 3–4/ZT).

We next evaluated whether PRCE altered molecular clock outputs. In the liver, expression of core clock genes (Bmal1, Clock, Cry1, Per2, Nr1d1, and Nr1d2) was not directly modulated by PRCE supplementation (Figure 4C–H). In contrast, HFD significantly disrupted the rhythmic amplitude of Bmal1 and Clock (p = 0.0002, Figure 4I,J), whereas Cry1 and Per2 amplitudes were unaffected (Figure 4K,L). Amplitudes of Nr1d1 and Nr1d2 were also significantly altered by HFD (p = 0.0026 and p = 0.0004, respectively; Figure 4M,N), indicating that diet‐induced obesity was the dominant driver of clock disruption at the transcriptional level.

Similarly, PRCE did not modulate circadian gene expression in BAT (Figure S7A–D) or affect Ucp1 and Fasn expression (Figure S7E,F). BAT clock gene amplitudes were largely unaffected by PRCE (Figure S7G–I). In contrast, HFD altered the amplitude of Per2, Ucp1, and Fasn (Figure S7J–L). In the mediobasal hypothalamus (MBH), PRCE supplementation did not affect clock gene or neuropeptide expression, while HFD significantly disrupted rhythmic amplitude across multiple transcripts (Figure S8A–L).

Collectively, these findings indicate that diet‐induced obesity is the dominant driver of circadian disruption in peripheral and central tissues, whereas PRCE supplementation does not exert a chronobiotic effect on molecular clock programs in diet‐induced obesity.

PRCE Modulates the Gut Microbiota Composition Without Circadian Restructuring

Previous studies have shown that cranberry extracts can strongly modulate gut microbiota composition, contributing to their metabolic benefits [26]. However, whether these microbiota changes involve alterations in the circadian organization of the microbial community has not been investigated. To address this question, gut microbiota composition was analyzed in obese mice housed at thermoneutrality, as ambient temperature can strongly influence circadian and metabolic rhythms, as well as food intake, thereby potentially confounding the interpretation of circadian microbiota analysis. 16S rRNA sequencing was performed on cecal samples collected across multiple ZT under HFD. Relative abundance analysis revealed selective differences in the abundance of specific bacterial genera between vehicle‐ and PRCE‐treated groups (Figure 5A). PRCE supplementation significantly increased Akkermansia abundance independently of time (p = 0.0026, Figure 5B), and reduced Intestinimonas abundance (p = 0.0026, Figure 5C). Similarly, Lachnoclostridium and Acetatifactor abundances were also significantly reduced by PRCE (p = 0.0413 and p = 0.0375, respectively, Figure 5D,E). Overall, these data confirm previous reports that cranberry‐derived extracts modulate gut microbiota composition [26, 52], and further extend these findings by demonstrating that PRCE‐induced microbial shifts occur consistently across circadian time. These results suggest that PRCE could selectively alter the abundance of specific taxa without restructuring the temporal organization of the microbial community.

Discussion

Dietary polyphenols have been extensively investigated as modulators of obesity and T2D due to their ability to improve glucose homeostasis, regulate energy balance, and reduce adiposity in preclinical models [12, 13, 53, 54]. Among these compounds, proanthocyanidin‐rich extracts have attracted particular attention. Previous studies demonstrated that a cranberry extract could reverse metabolic impairments in obese mice, improving glucose tolerance, insulin sensitivity, and systemic metabolic health [26, 55]. These effects have been largely attributed to alterations in the gut microbiota and improvements in host metabolic signaling [26]. Importantly, the limited bioavailability of polyphenols [24, 54] suggests that the intestine may represent a major site through which these compounds exert their metabolic effects [14, 15, 20, 53].

Despite growing interest in these compounds, the mechanisms linking polyphenols to improved glycemic control remain incompletely defined, particularly in the context of established obesity and environmental factors such as housing temperature, a key determinant of metabolic phenotypes in rodents [38, 39]. Standard housing temperatures (~22°C) impose chronic cold stress on mice, increasing basal EE and sympathetic tone, which can significantly influence metabolic outcomes and the apparent efficacy of metabolic interventions [56]. In contrast, thermoneutral housing (~30°C) more closely approximates human thermal physiology by minimizing basal thermogenic demand [57]. Furthermore, recent work highlights that genetic background and environmental temperature interact to shape thermogenic responses and metabolic phenotypes [58]. These considerations highlight the importance of evaluating metabolic interventions across physiologically relevant environmental contexts.

Here, we examined the effects of PRCE in mice with established diet‐induced obesity housed at thermoneutral (30°C) or cold (10°C) temperatures. A key mechanistic finding is that PRCE can improve glycemic control, at least in part, by altering intestinal glucose handling. PRCE inhibited α‐amylase activity, consistent with prior reports demonstrating that polyphenols can directly interfere with carbohydrate‐digesting enzymes [19, 59, 60]. PRCE also delayed the early appearance of glucose in the circulation following administration of a non‐metabolizable glucose analog, suggesting reduced early intestinal glucose absorption. Importantly, this mechanism may parallel previously described microbiota‐dependent effects of cranberry polyphenols on intestinal inflammation and barrier function [26, 52], suggesting multiple intestinal pathways through which PRCE may influence glucose homeostasis. Importantly, our data do not allow us to distinguish whether the reduced early glucose appearance in circulation reflects impaired intestinal glucose absorption, delayed gastric emptying, or a combination of both. Consistent with this, PRCE treatment did not alter the duodenal expression of key glucose transporter genes.

Cold exposure produced the expected reduction in body weight and fat mass, with increased food intake, reflecting elevated energetic demand [61]. In contrast, PRCE did not significantly alter body weight, body composition, or adipose depot mass at either temperature. Preventive studies have reported weight‐lowering effects of polyphenol‐rich extracts when administered concurrently with high‐fat feeding [20, 62, 63], whereas therapeutic interventions initiated after obesity is established generally produce more modest effects on body weight [26, 55, 64]. Consistent with this, our result indicates that PRCE can improve glycemic control without major changes in body weight or adiposity. Although the obese phenotype observed after 12 weeks of HFD feeding was relatively modest, prolonged HFD exposure is well established to induce metabolic dysfunction in mice, including impaired glucose homeostasis and insulin resistance, even when the extent of weight gain varies depending on strain, diet composition, age, and environmental conditions [65].

Polyphenols have also been proposed to increase EE by stimulating BAT thermogenesis [20, 66, 67]. However, thermogenic responses are strongly influenced by ambient temperature. At thermoneutrality, basal BAT activity is minimal, whereas cold exposure already maximally recruits thermogenic pathways, potentially limiting the ability of additional interventions to further increase thermogenic gene expression. In this context, PRCE did not increase BAT thermogenic gene expression under either thermoneutral or cold conditions in our model, suggesting that enhanced thermogenesis is unlikely to represent the dominant mechanism underlying the glycemic improvements observed here. This interpretation should nevertheless be made cautiously, as BAT thermogenic activity was not directly assessed using gold‐standard approaches such as PET‐CT imaging [61]. Importantly, however, PRCE did not alter food intake, body weight, adiposity, or feed efficiency, collectively supporting the interpretation that PRCE had limited effects on whole‐body thermogenic activity under our experimental conditions.

PRCE also modulated hepatic metabolic gene expression, including increased Hif1a, Pklr, and Pygl, and reduced Pdk4 expression. Previous studies have reported that proanthocyanidin‐rich extracts can modulate hepatic gene expression and metabolic pathways in the liver [68]. We also observed reduced basal hepatic AKT phosphorylation at Ser473 following PRCE treatment and cold exposure. However, these measurements reflect basal signaling conditions, as liver samples were not collected following an acute insulin challenge. Therefore, the present study does not allow conclusions regarding insulin‐stimulated hepatic AKT responsiveness or hepatic insulin sensitivity per se, which should be addressed in future studies using dynamic insulin signaling approaches.

Consistent with prior literature, HFD disrupted clock gene amplitude in peripheral tissues and the mediobasal hypothalamus [69, 70]. Despite emerging reports suggesting that proanthocyanidins can modulate circadian gene expression in peripheral tissues [21, 22]. PRCE did not restore rhythmicity under obese conditions. Notably, HFD feeding did not disrupt the circadian pattern of blood glucose in our study. This observation is consistent with previous work demonstrating that environmental cues and feeding timing can strongly shape metabolic rhythmicity, independently of diet composition [51, 71]. Together, these findings suggest that systemic glucose rhythms can remain relatively robust despite obesity, even when molecular clock oscillations in metabolic tissues are altered.

PRCE induced selective changes in gut microbial composition, most prominently increasing Akkermansia muciniphila , a genus consistently associated with improved metabolic health [15, 72, 73]. Reductions in Intestinimonas and Acetatifactor, genera linked to obesity and metabolic dysfunction [74, 75], support a shift in gut microbial composition consistent with improved metabolic health. These findings are consistent with previous work showing that polyphenol‐ and proanthocyanidin‐rich interventions can remodel the microbiota and contribute to metabolic benefits, including in reversal paradigms where body weight changes are modest [26]. Importantly, the present microbiota analysis was performed exclusively in obese mice maintained at thermoneutrality (30°C) to specifically assess circadian modulation of the microbiota by PRCE while minimizing confounding effects of ambient temperature on thermogenesis, food intake, and circadian metabolic rhythms. This distinction is relevant because cold exposure itself is now recognized as a major regulator of gut microbial ecology and host metabolism. Indeed, Worthmann et al. demonstrated that cold exposure remodels bile acid metabolism, alters gut microbial composition, and promotes adaptive thermogenesis through increased hepatic cholesterol‐to‐bile acid conversion [76]. Consistent with these findings, we recently reported that cold exposure induces marked strain‐ and tissue‐specific remodeling of bile acid metabolism in mice, further supporting a strong interaction between ambient temperature, bile acid signaling, and gut‐associated metabolic pathways [77].

How these microbial shifts contribute to the altered intestinal glucose handling observed here remains unresolved, and the relationship between microbiota remodeling and glycemic improvement remains associative rather than causal. As suggested before [26, 73], enrichment of Akkermansia could indirectly influence postprandial glycemia by improving the mucus layer and epithelial barrier integrity, modulating low‐grade inflammation, and/or altering luminal metabolite signaling that affects carbohydrate digestion and absorption. Consistent with this, Akkermansia muciniphila has also been reported to limit nutrient uptake by constraining intestinal absorptive surface expansion and downregulating genes involved in glucose transport, suggesting an additional mechanism by which microbial remodeling could directly reduce carbohydrate absorption [78]. Further studies will be required to establish the causal contribution of these microbiota changes to the metabolic effects of PRCE.

Overall, the metabolic effects of PRCE are likely influenced by multiple experimental variables, including intervention timing (preventive vs. therapeutic), extract composition, polyphenol source, dosing regimen, circadian timing, and environmental conditions such as housing temperature [24]. Our results show that PRCE improves glycemic control in mice with established diet‐induced obesity independently of ambient temperature, without affecting body weight, BAT thermogenic gene expression, or circadian rhythmicity. Instead, these effects are likely mediated through intestinal mechanisms involving modulation of carbohydrate digestion, early glucose absorption, and gut microbiota composition. However, beyond this expected context dependency, our findings consistently suggest the intestine as a primary site of action, whereby PRCE improves glycemic control through coordinated modulation of carbohydrate digestion, early glucose absorption kinetics, and gut microbial composition.

Conclusion

PRCE supplementation improves glycemic control in mice with established diet‐induced obesity without affecting body weight, thermogenesis, or circadian clock rhythmicity. These benefits appear to be driven by inhibition of intestinal carbohydrate digestion, delayed early glucose appearance, and selective modulation of the gut microbiota. Together, these findings identify the intestine as a key site of action of PRCE and support the potential of proanthocyanidin‐rich interventions as therapeutic adjuncts for improving glycemic control in obesity independently of weight loss.
