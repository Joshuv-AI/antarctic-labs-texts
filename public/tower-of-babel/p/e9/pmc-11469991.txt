RBMS1 interference inhibits malignant progression of glioblastoma cells and promotes ferroptosis
Authors: Xiaosong Liang, Gang Wang, Chunxiao Xue, Yifu Zhou
Published: 2024

Introduction

Glioblastoma (GBM), which is a prevalent brain malignancy in adults, is responsible for 80% of all malignancies in the central nervous system [1]. Due to its high malignancy and aggressiveness, GBM is especially drug-resistant and has a high recurrence rate, which significantly lowers the life quality of GBM patients [2]. Despite the great progresses in surgical intervention, radiotherapy and temozolomide (TMZ) chemotherapy, the median survival rate of patients with GBM is only about 1 year [3]. Hence, it is imperative to find novel and reliable biomarkers and seek for more specific targeted therapies to improve the survival rate of GBM patients.

Ferroptosis, a unique form of regulated cell death, significantly differs from other types of cell death, such as apoptosis [4]. As reported, ferroptosis has close relation with some metabolic pathways involved with iron, ROS, and lipid metabolism [5, 6]. In addition, ferroptosis has been implicated in many pathologies, including ischemia brain injuries and cancers [7, 8]. Interestingly, GBM cells are particularly sensitive to intracellular iron levels because of the changes in iron metabolism-associated proteins [9]. With this regard, emerging researches have revealed the indispensable role of ferroptosis in GBM [10, 11].

Among the signaling entities essential for proper execution of radial migration for the neurons, RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) are supposed to be functionally pivotal for neurogenesis and have crucial regulatory roles in cell proliferation and migration [12]. RNA binding motif single strand interacting protein 1 (RBMS1) has been revealed to be expressed in many human tissues [13]. A previous study has also discovered that RBMS1 can also be found in the brain of adult mice (https://www.proteinatlas.org/ENSG00000153250-RBMS1/brain). Besides, RBMS1 has been implicated to play a critical role in many human cancers. For example, RBMS1 expression is upregulated in gastric cancer cells and RBMS1 promotes the migration and invasion of gastric cancer cells [14]. According to GEO database (https://ww.ncbinlm.nih.gov/geo/) and GEPIA database (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/), RBMS1 expression is upregulated in the tissues of GBM patients compared with normal tissues. Nevertheless, the role of RBMS1 in GBM remains obscure.

To conclude, this study intended to explore the role of RBMS1 in the proliferation, migration, invasion, apoptosis, ferroptosis and EMT process in GBM as well as to discuss its reaction mechanism associated with ferroptosis, which might shed novel insights into the therapeutic treatment of RBMS1 in GBM.

Material and methods

Cell culture and treatment

The normal glial cell line HEB (cat. no. HUM-iCell-n009) and human glioblastoma cell lines including A172 (cat. no. iCell-h002), LN229 (cat. no. iCell-h124) and T98G (cat. no. iCell-h210) were supplied from iCell Bioscience Inc (Shanghai, China). All cells were incubated in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Gibco) containing 10% foetal bovine serum (Gibco) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Sigma-Aldrich) in a humid incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. To explore the mechanism of RBMS1 associated with ferroptosis in regulating GBM cells, ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 (1 µM) was used to treat T98G cells for 1 h [15].

Cell transfection

To reduce RBMS1 expression, short hairpin RNAs targeting RBMS1 (sh-RBMS1-1/2) and the corresponding scrambled sequence as a negative control (sh-NC) were constructed by GenePharma (Shanghai, China). These recombinants were transfected into T98G cells using Lipofectamine 2000 reagent at 37 ℃ for 48 h according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After 48 h, T98G cells were harvested for follow-up experiments.

Reverse transcription-quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

The total RNA that extracted from sample T98G cells with TRizol reagent (Biosharp) was reverse transcribed into cDNA using a commercial RevertAid™ cDNA Synthesis kit (Bio-Rad) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Afterwards, the amplification of templates was conducted with SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Takara, Toyobo, Japan) on the 7500 Fast Real-time PCR system according to the manufacturer’s instructions. 2−ΔΔCt method was used for the calculation of relative gene expression [16]. The following were the primer sequences: RBMS1 forward (F), 5ʹ-TGGCATAGAGAAGGAGAGGCT-3ʹ and reverse (R), 5ʹ-TGAGGGAGTTAACACGGCAC-3ʹ or GAPDH F, 5ʹ-TGTGGGCATCAATGGATTTGG-3ʹ and reverse R, 5ʹ-ACACCATGTATTCCGGGTCAAT-3ʹ.

Western blot

The concentration of total proteins that extracted from sample T98G cells with RIPA lysis buffer (Solarbio) was quantified using bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kits (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The proteins that separated by 8% SDS-PAGE were transferred to PVDF membranes which were sealed by 5% BSA for 2 h at room temperature. Subsequently, the membranes were successively immunoblotted with primary antibodies targeting RBMS1 (ab150353; 1:1000; Abcam), Ki67 (ab92742; 1:5000; Abcam), PCNA (ab92552; 1:1000; Abcam), Bcl-2 (ab182858; 1:2000; Abcam), Bax (ab182733; 1:2000; Abcam), cleaved-caspase3 (ab32042; 1:500; Abcam), caspase3 (ab32351; 1:5000; Abcam), E-Cadherin (ab40772; 1:1000; Abcam), N-Cadherin (ab76011; 1:5000; Abcam), Vimentin (ab92547; 1:1000; Abcam), Snail (ab216347; 1:1000; Abcam), SLC7A11 (ab307601; 1:1000; Abcam), GPX4 (ab125066; 1:1000; Abcam), ACSL4 (ab155282; 1:10,000; Abcam), MMP2 (ab92536; 1:1000; Abcam), MMP9 (ab76003; 1:1000; Abcam) or GAPDH (ab9485; 1:2500; Abcam) overnight at 4 ℃. On the next day, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (ab6721; 1:2000; Abcam) at room temperature for 1 h. Finally, the protein bands were visualized with enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection reagent (Shanghai Yeasen Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) and ImageJ software (version 1.49, National Institutes of Health) was applied for the analysis of protein density.

Counting Kit-8 assay (CCK-8) assay

The effects of RBMS1 knockdown on the proliferation of T98G cells were investigated through the conduction of CCK-8 assay. Briefly, T98G cells were inoculated into 96-well plates at a density of 3 × 104 cells/well and then incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. After that, each well was added with 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology) to incubate cells for additional 2 h. Finally, the absorbance at 450 nm was detected with a microplate reader.

Colony formation assay

T98G cells were initially inoculated into 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 103 cells/well and then incubated for 14 days. After that, the colonies were fixed with 100% methanol for 10 min at room temperature and stained by 0.5% crystal violet solution for 30 min. With the application of a microscope, the colonies were observed.

Flow cytometry

The collected T98G cells were washed with pre-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and then got re-suspended in 100 μL binding buffer. After that, T98G cells were incubated with 2 μL Annexin‐V‐FITC (20 μg/mL, Sigma) on ice in the dark for 15 min and subsequently stained by propidium iodide (PI, BD Biosciences). Finally, the apoptotic cells were analyzed utilizing a FACS Calibur Flow cytometer (BD Bioscience) in accordance with the recommend specifications.

Wound healing

T98G cells were initially inoculated into 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells/well and then incubated until cell fusion has reached 70–80%. The wounds in the monolayer were made using a 200-µL pipette tip. Following, the PBS-rinsed cells were maintained in serum-free medium for 24 h at 37 °C. Finally, the images of wounds were observed at 0 and 24 h with an inverted microscope (Olympus Corp).

Transwell assay

T98G cells were initially inoculated into serum-free medium (200 μL) in the upper chambers pre-coated with Matrigel, at 37 °C for 1 h and the medium containing 10% FBS was injected on the lower chambers. Following 24 h of incubation at 37 °C, the invading cells on the lower surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained by 0.1% crystal violet for 30 min. Finally, the images of cells were captured with an inverted microscope.

Thiobarbituric acid reactive substance (TBARS) assay

For the detection of lipid peroxidation in T98G cells, TBARS Assay Kits (cat. no. E-BC-K298-M; Elabscience) were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, T98G cells were incubated with 0.5 mL 15% trichloroacetic acid and 10 μL of 500 mM butylated hydroxyanisole (BHA) and then centrifuged at 10,000×g for 10 min at 4 °C [17]. Subsequently, the collected supernatant was exposed to 0.5 mL 0.375% thiobarbituric acid and then boiled for 10 min. Finally, a microplate reader was employed to detect TBARS at 532 nm.

Lipid ROS detection

For the detection of lipid ROS, a BODIPY 581/591 C11 kit (cat. no. D3861, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was employed. Briefly, T98G cells were inoculated into 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well for 24 h and then exposed to 2 μM C11-BODIPY (581/591) probe according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Following, the cells were visualized with a laser scanning confocal microscope (Olympus) and analyzed using Image J software.

Measurement of iron level

For the measurement of total iron level, Iron Assay Kit (cat. no. E-BC-K772-M; Elabscience) was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, T98G cells were homogenized with iron assay buffer on ice and then centrifuged at 16,000×g for 15 min at 4 °C to obtain lysis supernatant. Subsequently, the collected lysis sample (100 μL) were incubated with 5 μL iron reducer for 30 min at 37 °C, following which was the incubation with 100 μL iron probe. Then, the mixture was incubated for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark and a microscope reader was employed for the detection of absorbance at 593 nm.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were replicated for three times. The collected experimental data were analyzed with GraphPad Prism 8.0 software (GraphPad software, Inc.) and then presented as mean ± standard deviation. For the demonstration of comparisons among multiple groups, one-way analysis of variance with Tukey’s post-hoc test was used. P < 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Results

RBMS1 expression was upregulated in GBM

According to GEPIA database, RBMS1 expression was upregulated in GBM (Fig. 1A). To explore the expression of RBMS1 in GBM cells, RT-qPCR and western blot were performed. Compared with the HEB group, the mRNA and protein expressions of RBMS1 were significantly increased in GBM cell lines A172, LN229 and T98G. It is noted that RBMS1 had the highest expression in T98G cells (Fig. 1B). Hence, T98G cells were selected for subsequent studies.

RBMS1 expression was upregulated in GBM. A According to GEPIA database, RBMS1 expression was upregulated in GBM. *P < 0.05. B The mRNA and protein expressions of RBMS1 were detected using RT-qPCR and western blot. ***P < 0.001 vs. HBE

RBMS1 silence inhibited the proliferation and promoted the apoptosis of T98G cells

To explore the role of RBMS1 in GBM cell proliferation and apoptosis, sh-RBMS1 was initially transfected into T98G cells to reduce RBMS1 expression and the transfection efficiency was examined with RT-qPCR and western blot. Compared with the sh-NC group, the expression of RBMS1 was significantly reduced following the transfection of sh-RBMS1. It was noted that RBMS1 had lower expression in sh-RBMS1-1 group, therefore, sh-RBMS1-1 was selected for follow-up experiments (Fig. 2A). Through CCK-8 assay and colony formation assay, the effects of RBMS1 silence on GBM cell proliferation and colony forming abilities were investigated. As Fig. 2B, C depicted, RBMS1 interference significantly reduced the proliferation and colony forming abilities of T98G cells compared with the sh-NC group. Results obtained from flow cytometry presented that the apoptosis of T98G cells was significantly promoted after depleting RBMS1 expression (Fig. 2D). The expressions of proliferation- and apoptosis-related proteins were detected with western blot and the results demonstrated that RBMS1 deficiency reduced the expressions of Ki67, PCNA and Bcl-2 whereas it increased the expressions of Bax and cleaved-caspase3 compared with those in sh-NC group (Fig. 2E).

RBMS1 silence inhibited the proliferation and promoted the apoptosis of T98G cells. A The transfection efficiency of sh-RBMS1 was examined with RT-qPCR and western blot. B The cell proliferation was detected using CCK-8 assay. C The colony forming ability was detected using colony formation assay. D The cell apoptosis was detected using flow cytometry. E The expression of proliferation- and apoptosis-related proteins were detected using western blot. **P < 0.01 and ***P < 0.001 vs. sh-NC

RBMS1 silence inhibited the migration, invasion and EMT process in T98G cells

The effects of RBMS1 silence on GBM cell migration and invasion were investigated through wound healing and transwell assays. Compared with the sh-NC group, the migration and invasion of T98G cells were significantly decreased following the transfection of sh-RBMS1 (Fig. 3A, B). In addition, the activities of MMP2 and MMP9 were explored with western blot and the results showed that RBMS1 interference significantly reduced the activities of MMP2 and MMP9 in T98G cells (Fig. 3C). The expressions of EMT-related proteins were assessed with western blot and it was discovered that RBMS1 depletion increased E-cadherin expression whereas it decreased the expressions of N-cadherin, Vimentin and Snail compared with the sh-NC group (Fig. 3D).

RBMS1 silence inhibited the migration, invasion and EMT process in T98G cells. A, B The cell migration and invasion were detected using wound healing and transwell assay. C The activities of MMP2 and MMP9 were detected using western blot. D The expressions of EMT-related proteins were detected using western blot. **P < 0.01 and ***P < 0.001 vs. sh-NC

RBMS1 silence promoted ferroptosis in T98G cells

As Fig. 4A demonstrated, RBMS1 depletion greatly increased the production of TBARS compared with the sh-NC group. Results obtained from BODIPY 581/591 C11 assay kits revealed that the relative fluorescence intensity in T98G cells was increased by sh-RBMS1 compared with the sh-NC group (Fig. 4B). Additionally, the total iron level was assessed with corresponding assay kits and it was discovered that the iron level in T98G cells was obviously increased after interfering RBMS1 expression (Fig. 4C). The expressions of proteins associated with ferroptosis were appraised with western blot and compared with sh-NC group, RBMS1 interference decreased the expressions of SLC7A11 and GPX4 whereas it increased ACSL4 expression (Fig. 4D).

RBMS1 silence promoted ferroptosis in T98G cells. A The lipid peroxidation was detected using TBARS Assay Kit. B The lipid ROS was detected using a BODIPY 581/591 C11 kit. C The total iron level was detected using corresponding assay. (D) The expressions of ferroptosis-related proteins were detected using western blot. ***P < 0.001 vs. sh-NC

RBMS1 silence inhibited the proliferation and promoted the apoptosis of T98G cells possibly through ferroptosis

To explore the mechanism of RBMS1 associated with ferroptosis in GBM, ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 was used to treat T98G cells and above functional experiments were conducted again. Compared with the sh-NC group, RBMS1 depletion decreased the proliferation and the colony forming ability of T98G cells, which were then partially revived by Fer-1 treatment (Fig. 5A, B). Results obtained from flow cytometry showed that the increased cell apoptosis in sh-RBMS1 group was obviously inhibited following the treatment of Fer-1 (Fig. 5C). Besides, RBMS1 deficiency decreased the expressions of Ki67, PCNA and Bcl-2 whereas it increased the expressions of Bax and cleaved-caspase3 in T98G cells compared with the sh-NC group, which were all reversed by Fer-1 (Fig. 5D).

RBMS1 silence inhibited the proliferation and promoted the apoptosis of T98G cells by promoting ferroptosis. A The cell proliferation was detected using CCK-8 assay. B The colony forming ability was detected using colony formation assay. C The cell apoptosis was detected using flow cytometry. D The expression of proliferation- and apoptosis-related proteins were detected using western blot. **P < 0.01 and ***P < 0.001 vs. sh-NC, ##P < 0.01 and ###P < 0.001 vs. sh-RBMS1

RBMS1 silence inhibited the migration, invasion and EMT process in T98G cells possibly through ferroptosis

Compared with the sh-NC group, RBMS1 depletion evidently inhibited the migration and invasion of T98G cells, while Fer-1 treatment exhibited opposite effects, evidenced by increased cell migration and invasion in Fer-1+sh-RBMS1 group (Fig. 6A, B). Besides, the decreased activities of MMP2 and MMP9 in sh-RBMS1 group were partially increased following the treatment of Fer-1 (Fig. 6C). Moreover, RBMS1 depletion increased the protein expressions of E-cadherin whereas it decreased the expressions of N-cadherin, Vimentin and Snail in T98G cells compared with the sh-NC group, which were all reversed by Fer-1 treatment (Fig. 6D).

Discussion

GBM is a major cause of cancer-related deaths in adults. In recent years, most GBM patients die within 2 years even though tremendous progresses have been made in treatment modalities [3]. In this study, RBMS1 expression was discovered to be increased in GBM cells. RBMS1 interference inhibited the proliferation, migration, invasion and EMT process whereas it induced cell apoptosis and ferroptosis in GBM. Mechanistically, this study revealed that the protective effects of RBMS1 interference on GBM were partially abolished by ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1. This study preliminarily revealed the potential therapeutic role of RBMS1 in GBM.

As we all known, RBPs have been implicated to regulate various cancer-related biological processes, such as apoptosis, EMT and autophagy [18, 19]. RBP RBMS1 has been initially deemed as a c-Myc gene single-stranded binding protein that can inhibit the transcription of target genes [20]. Previous study has elucidated that RBMS1 expression is downregulated in prostate carcinoma and RBMS1 overexpression suppresses the proliferation and colony forming ability of DU145 and LNCaP cells [21]. Yu et al. have evidenced that RBMS1 depletion facilitates the advancement of colorectal cancer in cell line and PDX models as well as clinical samples [22]. However, according to GEO database (GSE12657) and GEPIA database, RBMS1 expression was upregulated in the tissues of GBM patients. Results of the present study revealed that RBMS1 expression was conspicuously increased in GBM cell lines.

The imbalance between proliferation and apoptosis is a predominant contributor to tumor formation. The facilitation of cell apoptosis and the suppression of cell proliferation are supposed to be effective methods to impede tumor progression [23]. Zhai et al. have well-documented that RBMS1 overexpression suppresses the proliferation in hepatocellular carcinoma [24]. In our study, RBMS1 silence was found to inhibit cell proliferation and colony forming ability whereas it induced cell apoptosis in GBM. Bcl‐2 family and caspase family have close relation with tumor apoptosis. Bcl‐2 suppresses apoptosis via blocking an early link of programmed cell death while Bax protein facilitates apoptosis through inactivating Bcl-2 [25]. Caspase‐3 is the executor of apoptosis and the activated cleaved‐caspase‐3 can induce apoptosis [26]. Previous studies have demonstrated that PCNA is closely related with abnormal proliferation and Ki-67 antigen is a commonly used marker to detect cell proliferation [27, 28]. Herein, RBMS1 interference was attested to reduce the protein expressions of Ki67, PCNA and Bcl-2 whereas it increased the expressions of Bax and cleaved-caspase3 in GBM cells.

The migration and invasion of cancer cells into surrounding tissue and vasculature is a critical initial step in cancer metastasis [29]. Besides, EMT has been deemed as a crucial regulator of metastasis in several cancers through conferring an invasive phenotype [30]. Evidently, the intervention of migration, invasion and EMT in cancer cells is an effective way to slow down cancer progression. A recent study has illuminated that RBMS1 deficiency can inhibit the migration and invasion in lung cancer cells in vitro and suppress cell metastasis in vivo [31]. N-cadherin, Snail, and matrix metalloproteases including MMP2 and MMP9 are important EMT markers which have been implicated to be highly expressed to enable cancer cells to become more motile and invasive [32]. Besides, Habib et al. have evidenced that RBMS1 affects radial migration via the binding and stabilization of Efr3a [12]. Results of the present study showed that RBMS1 knockdown inhibited cell migration, invasion and EMT process, accompanied by decreased activities of MMP2 and MMP9, reduced protein expressions of N-cadherin, Vimentin and Snail as well as increased E-cadherin in GBM cells.

As reported, the induction of ferroptosis might be a reliable strategy to eradicate cancers when compared with the traditional anti-cancer therapies [33]. Iron metabolic imbalance, along with ferroptosis, has close association with the malignant advancement of multiple tumors. For instance, the inhibition of ferroptosis by N-acetyltransferase 10 can facilitate the advancement of colon cancer [34]. Intriguingly, GBM cells are particularly sensitive to intracellular iron levels because of the changes in a variety of iron-dependent enzyme activities as well as iron metabolism-related proteins [9]. It has been clarified that the suppression of RBMS1 improve the sensitivity of colorectal cancer cells to oxaliplatin through facilitating ferroptosis [35]. Additionally, Zhang et al. have presented that RBMS1 is involved in the advancement of lung cancer by mediating ferroptosis evasion [36]. Nevertheless, the regulatory role of RBMS1 in ferroptosis in GBM hasn’t been investigated so far. Results of the present study testified that RBMS1 interference elevated TBARS production, increased relative fluorescence intensity and total iron level in GBM cells, indicating the promotive effects of RBMS1 silence on ferroptosis in GBM.

In order to further elucidate the mechanism of RBMS1 associated with ferroptosis in regulating the phenotypes of GBM cells, ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 was used to treat T98G cells and above functional experiments were conducted again. The experiments results showed that the inhibitory effects of RBMS1 silence on GBM cell proliferation, migration, invasion and EMT as well as the promotive effects on cell apoptosis were all counteracted by Fer-1, implying that RBMS1 interference played its protective role in GBM possibly through ferroptosis.

Conclusion

In summary, this study discovered that RBMS1 knockdown suppressed the proliferation, migration, invasion and ETM process whereas facilitated the apoptosis in GBM possibly via the mediation of ferroptosis, which for the first time revealed the mechanism by which RBMS1 silence impeded the progression of GBM. However, there are also some limitations. For example, the findings of this study haven’t been explored in in vivo experiments; second, the detailed mechanism of RBMS1 on ferroptosis regulation in GBM hasn’t been investigated, both of which will be the research focuses of future studies.
