Epidemiological and clinical differences in Meniere disease across European and East Asian populations
Authors: Jose A Lopez-Escamez, Mingwei Xu, Qing Zhang, Benjamin K-T Tsang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis narrative review focuses on the epidemiologic, clinical, and molecular aspects of Meniere disease, comparing studies from European and East Asian populations. Meniere disease is a complex, heterogeneous, chronic disorder of the human inner ear characterized by recurrent episodes of spontaneous vertigo, fluctuating sensorineural hearing loss, tinnitus, and aural fullness.The overall prevalence of Meniere disease is generally between 0.05% and 0.2% among adults worldwide. In Europe and North America, the reported point prevalence is generally higher than in East Asia, at ∼50–200 per 100,000 adults, with a female-to-male ratio of around 1.5–2:1. This contrasts with a slightly higher Meniere disease female-to-male ratio in East Asian populations, based on Korean data (∼2.17:1). Epidemiological data and genetic studies from China and other East Asian countries remain limited, with a reportedly lower incidence of Meniere disease in Japan (∼17 per 100,000/year based on national hospital surveys). In terms of clinical aspects of Meniere disease, the core symptom spectrum does not differ substantially between European and East Asian patients. Interestingly, comorbidity patterns appear to differ, whereby European studies in Meniere disease report an association with migraine and autoimmunity, whereas in East Asian data, this association is less frequently observed. Conversely, allergies and systemic inflammatory responses mediated by Th2 or autoinflammation have been reported in both European and East Asian studies. Familial Meniere disease is more commonly observed in European than East Asian populations, suggesting that the genetic structure may contribute to its susceptibility. We summarize the commonalities and differences with respect to the clinical manifestations and treatment of Meniere disease. We propose that multinational research on the molecular diagnosis and treatment of Meniere disease will facilitate a better understanding of the condition and provide impetus for future research.Keywords: Vertigo, sensorineural hearing loss: tinnitus, vestibular disorders, Meniere's disease, inflammation, genetics of vestibular disorders, diagnosis, treatment

Introduction

Meniere disease (MD) is an “umbrella term” used to define a chronic disorder of the inner ear characterized by recurrent episodes of spontaneous vertigo, fluctuating sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL), tinnitus, and aural fullness, usually affecting one ear at onset.1–3 The condition has a significant genetic component and has been associated with several comorbidities, including migraine and autoimmune disorders. 4 The condition is defined according to the clinical criteria established in 2015 by the Bárány Society International Classification Committee for Vestibular Disorders 1 (Table 1).

The disease is typically slowly progressive. Early stages often feature prominent episodic vertigo and fluctuating hearing, whereas later stages are dominated by chronic imbalance and permanent hearing loss as the vestibular organs become globally dysfunctional. 5 Nevertheless, the syndrome shows a large heterogeneity in its initial clinical presentation, running from SNHL or tinnitus without vertigo attacks to the complete acute audiovestibular syndrome, usually preceded by changes in aural fullness and/or tinnitus. 6

The aim of this narrative review is to provide a broad perspective on the epidemiology, pathophysiology, and clinical management of MD in European and East Asian populations, comparing current clinical practice and examining the impact of molecular diagnosis on the diagnosis and treatment of MD.

Methods

This review was guided by the Scale for the Assessment of Narrative Review Articles (SANRA). 7 We performed a bibliographic search of the PubMed database on 15 January 2026 using the following keyword: “Meniere Disease.” Search limits included publications from the last 10 years, location of the search terms in title, abstract, or keywords, and articles published in English. We also examined selected references from the retrieved articles to identify additional relevant publications.

All records were evaluated based on their abstracts to identify articles focusing on different aspects of the disease, particularly epidemiology, clinical phenotype, and pathophysiology. Articles unrelated to MD were excluded. The remaining articles were organized and classified according to the study topic: epidemiology, clinical aspects comparing European and East Asian phenotypes, molecular aspects, diagnosis, and treatment of MD.

Epidemiology of Meniere disease

MD is uncommon worldwide, with prevalence generally ranging from ∼0.05% to 0.2% among adults. However, reported rates and patterns differ between European and East Asian populations, partly because of diagnostic criteria and health-system factors.4,8,9

Europe: Incidence, prevalence, and demographics

Contemporary reviews, largely based on European and North American data, report an incidence of ∼50–200 per 100,000 person-years and a point prevalence of ∼50–200 per 100,000 adults, with most cases presenting between 30 and 60 years of age. 10 In Europe, the incidence of MD is ∼50–200 per 100,000, with historical data from the UK, Spain 11 and Sweden falling within this range (e.g. 46 per 100,000/year in Sweden using strict triad criteria). 12 More recent reviews, including data from Europe and US, report prevalence estimates ranging from 3.5 to 513 per 100,000, reflecting very heterogeneous methods. 13 Notably, MD is reported more frequently among older, White, female patients, with prevalence increasing markedly with age. 4 European-led studies generally report a female predominance, with female-to-male ratios of ∼1.5–2:1 and a peak age at onset between 40 and 60 years. 14

East Asia (Japan, Korea, and China): Incidence and trends

A Korean nationwide claims database study (2013–2017) showed that the incidence of MD increased rapidly over time. Both prevalence and incidence increased with age up to the 70s, and the female-to-male ratio was 2.17:1, slightly higher than that reported in many European studies. 15 In that Korean cohort, seasonal analysis suggested a higher incidence of MD during summer and autumn (higher humidity and lower atmospheric pressure), although seasonal differences did not reach strong statistical significance, highlighting a possible climatic effect in East Asia. 9 Earlier comparative studies, summarized in a major review, reported a lower incidence in Japan (∼17 per 100,000/year based on national hospital surveys) than in European countries such as Sweden and the UK.16,17 Reviews focusing on global epidemiology emphasize that robust contemporary incidence/prevalence data from China and other East Asian countries remain limited. Much of the available evidence comes from Japan and Korea, and the apparent increase in Korea has been attributed to population aging, improved access to care, and lifestyle changes rather than to a true increase in underlying disease risk.

Comparative patterns: Europe vs. East Asia

Overall prevalence. Europe and North America generally report higher or comparable prevalence (up to ∼0.2%) than older Japanese estimates (∼17 per 100,000/year). This difference likely reflects diagnostic criteria, ascertainment, and health service organization. However, ancestry differences associated with the genetic variation and environmental factors, including seasonal variation, may also contribute to these differences between European and East Asian populations.

Clinical aspects of Meniere disease

The core clinical manifestations of MD are broadly similar in European and East Asian patients. Both populations are characterized primarily by episodic vertigo and fluctuating SNHL, and hearing tends to deteriorate progressively with increasing disease duration.2, 20 More meaningful comparisons lie in the distribution and relative importance of several key clinical characteristics across populations (Table 3).

European phenotype

European studies provide a relatively systematic characterization of the clinical heterogeneity of MD. A classification scheme proposed from a large Spanish cohort divides the disease into five phenotypic subgroups: classical, delayed, familial, migraine-associated, and autoimmune forms. 21 These clinical subgroups have also been reported among patients with MD in the United States, 22 Chile, 23 and the Netherlands. 24 This framework indicates that variation within European populations is not limited to symptom severity but also involves the sequence of symptom onset, comorbidities, and familial background.

European studies have also attempted to relate pathological findings to clinical variation. Based on histopathologic and imaging features of the endolymphatic sac, investigators proposed that MD comprises two pathologically distinct forms with different etiologic backgrounds: endolymphatic sac degeneration (MD-dg) and endolymphatic sac hypoplasia (MD-hp). 25 A Swiss cohort study showed that MD-hp was more common in men and was associated with higher rates of bilateral involvement, a positive family history, and concomitant semicircular canal dehiscence, whereas MD-dg more commonly presented with frequent vertigo attacks and more pronounced vestibular dysfunction. 25 Subsequent multicenter data further suggested that patients with MD-hp have a higher risk of progression to severe hearing loss requiring cochlear implantation. 26 Thus, the clinical heterogeneity observed in European populations may also reflect relatively stable differences in the underlying pathology.

Bilateral involvement is another issue that has been extensively examined in European studies. Reported rates vary widely, ranging from ∼5%–47%, largely depending on the inclusion criteria and duration of follow-up. A Spanish study found that most bilateral cases showed metachronous onset, with a median interval of 7 years between unilateral and bilateral disease. Patients with synchronous onset had more severe average hearing loss, and both a history of migraine and a greater number of symptoms at initial presentation were associated with progression to bilateral disease. 27

Regarding comorbidities, European studies have highlighted migraine and immune-related disorders. A Danish nationwide registry study including 7106 patients showed increased risks of migraine, depression, and anxiety among patients with MD, together with higher rates of disability pension. 14 Patients with the migraine-associated phenotype also tend to have poorer vertigo control. 28 In addition, a systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrated the strongest associations with allergic airway disease and autoimmune thyroid disease. 29 In European populations, therefore, MD is often regarded as a syndromic disorder extending beyond a purely otologic condition and closely linked to migraine or immune-mediated mechanisms.29,30

SNHL is a key feature of MD, and hearing profiles have been investigated in both cross-sectional and longitudinal studies in European individuals.31,32 Different profiles can be observed at the disease onset: (1) a flat line involving all frequencies; (2) a rising slope “peaked” at 2 kHz; and (3) a trough or “U” shaped configuration. 31 However, these profiles may evolve to a flat line in ∼60–70 dB HL in most patients over several years, whereas ∼25% will remain with the rising slope affecting only the low- and mid-frequency ranges. 33

Progressive hearing loss is observed in most patients with MD. The MD-hp phenotype is most commonly associated with early onset and bilateral SNHL and demonstrates more rapid hearing loss progression than MD-dg. 26 A study comparing hearing loss progression between Spanish and Chinese patients identified two clusters characterized by low and high hearing thresholds among individuals with a disease duration of less than 10 years. 33

East Asian phenotype

The overall symptom spectrum in East Asian populations is similar to that reported in Europe, but current evidence suggests that the major differences lie in comorbidity patterns, forms of familial aggregation, and research emphasis. Japan was among the earliest East Asian countries to study MD systematically. Nationwide survey data indicate that the pattern of initial symptoms among patients with MD in Japan is broadly similar to that reported in Europe, with vertigo remaining the most common presenting complaint. 16 However, Japanese follow-up studies found that approximately one-third of patients developed benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV)-like attacks during the disease course and that a substantial proportion of vertigo episodes were not typical Meniere attacks but more closely resembled positional vertigo. This observation suggests that, in East Asia, particularly in Japan, repeated reassessment of the nature of vertigo episodes and dynamic differential diagnosis may be especially important. Moreover, migraine is not commonly reported among patients with MD in Japanese series, suggesting epidemiologic differences.

Data from Korea further suggest differences from Europe in population distribution and comorbidity profiles. A nationwide study showed a stronger female predominance among patients with MD in Korea and suggested that disease occurrence may be associated with higher humidity and lower atmospheric pressure. 9 Regarding familial disease, Korean studies reported a prevalence of 9.8% for familial Meniere-like syndrome and 6.3% for definite familial MD, with autosomal dominant inheritance with incomplete penetrance being the most common pattern. Familial cases also had an earlier disease onset and a higher prevalence of migraine comorbidity. 19 In contrast to European cohorts, however, the frequency of systemic autoimmune disease was markedly lower among Korean patients with bilateral MD. 34

Respiratory allergies (allergic rhinitis and bronchial asthma) and autoimmune skin diseases, including atopic dermatitis and vitiligo,35,36 have been associated with MD in population-based studies from South Korea. 15 These findings suggest that the immune-related comorbidity patterns observed in European cohorts may not be fully generalizable to East Asian cohort, indicating that environmental factors may shape distinct subclinical immune responses.

Overall, there is no fundamental difference between European and East Asian populations in the core symptom spectrum of MD, but the prevalence of comorbidities that have a significant impact on the clinical course differs. European literature places greater emphasis on phenotypic classification, endolymphatic sac histopathologic categories, the risk of progression to bilateral disease, and migraine- or autoimmune-related comorbidities. East Asian studies more often emphasize dynamic changes during vertigo episodes, regional differences in familial aggregation, the relatively lower frequency of immune-related comorbidities, the strong association with allergies, 15 and the identification and management of functional and severe phenotypes.29,34,37,38 Thus, the clinical differences in MD between European and East Asian populations appear to reflect differences in genetic ancestry and environmental factors, such as diet, allergens, and immune responses that influence the clinical phenotype.

Molecular aspects in Meniere disease

Immune response

Although it is generally accepted that the adaptive immune system mediates autoimmunity and the innate immune system mediates autoinflammation, both immune responses are closely interconnected through multiple cytokine-related pathways. 39 Therefore, the immunophenotypes observed in MD are shaped by adaptive and innate responses to a different extent, influenced by each individual genetic background and environmental factors. Studies conducted in Southern European40–42 and East Asians 43 patients with MD have independently confirmed that ∼60% of patients with MD exhibit an abnormal systemic response44,45. Furthermore, cytokine and single-cell transcriptomic profile can be used to distinguish MD from vestibular migraine46,47

MD exhibits at least three distinct immune phenotypes: (1) a T-helper 2 (Th2) cytokine-mediated subtype characterized by allergy-like inflammation; (2) an autoinflammatory subtype characterized by elevated levels of interleukin (IL)-1β; and (3) a subtype associated with autoimmune and/or autoinflammatory comorbidities involving other organs beyond the inner ear (Table 4).48 These alterations may be secondary to other primary genetic or environmental triggers, but they demonstrate a persistent inflammatory response that may explain the relapsing/remitting course of MD. Notably, IL-1β mediates glutamate excitotoxicity, linking autoinflammatory responses with cochlear synaptopathy and neural damage. 49

Genetic structure

Differences in the prevalence of MD across different ancestries and the strong familial aggregation observed in European populations suggest a substantial genetic contribution. 18 Exome sequencing has been used systematically to identify rare variants and causal genes in multiple families with MD, particularly in Spain,44,50,51 supporting the role of several hearing loss genes in the molecular pathophysiology of MD. 52

Familial MD. Several British families were described in the UK and Finland during the 20th century,53,54 although genetic studies were limited by the technologies available Conversely, during the past decade, ∼100 families with MD have been identified, most of them in Spain, demonstrating autosomal dominant, autosomal recessive, and digenic multiallelic inheritance. 50 The first gene identified in familial MD was TIMCC (previously known as FAM136A). The affected individuals carried a loss-of-function mutation segregating in three women across consecutive generations. 55 TIMCC encodes an intermembrane mitochondrial protein that is essential for proteostasis, mitochondrial membrane potential, and ATP production. 56 To date, 23 genes have been reported in familial MD, most of them identified in only a single family. However, rare variants in OTOG, MYO7A, TECTA, and GJD3 have been identified in several unrelated Spanish families (Table 5; Figure 1).57–61 Familial MD is scarce in East Asian populations, and few genomic studies have been conducted in Asian population in familial MD. Although several families with mutations in COCH have been reported, 62 the genetic architecture in East Asian populations remains largely unknown.

Sporadic MD. Genomic studies have also been conducted in sporadic (nonfamilial) MD,63,64 supporting a partially shared genetic architecture between familial and sporadic MD in European populations. 64 Rare variants in several SNHL-related genes have been identified in patients with sporadic MD of European ancestry, including GJB2, USH1G, SLC26A4, ESRRB, CLDN14, MPHOSPH8, MYO18A, TRIOBP, OTOGL, TNC, and MYO6.63,65 Notably, ADGRV1, PCDH15, and MYO7A are of particular interest because their encoded proteins are localized to the stereocilia of hair cells at the tip links and ankle links. However, the gene most frequently reported in both familial and sporadic MD is GJD3. 61 A rare haplotype (TGAGT), composed of two missense variants, two synonymous variants, and one downstream variant, was identified in five individuals with FMD from three unrelated families (10 individuals) and in an additional eight individuals with sporadic MD. GJD3 encodes gap junction protein delta 3, also known as human connexin 31.9 (Cx31.9). The H175Y missense variant would modify the interaction between connexins and the connexon assembly, thereby affecting the gap junction between cells. 61

Two studies in South Korean populations have been performed using gene panels 37 and exome sequencing. 66 Rare missense variants in MYO7A and OTOP2 genes were identified, supporting MYO7A-mediated digenic inheritance in MD and highlighting MYO7A as an important causal gene across both European and East Asian ancestries. 66

Common and rare variants in inflammation and innate immune responses may also play important roles in the clinical course and progression of sporadic MD.67,68 Several studies in European patients with MD have identified noncoding regulatory variants in NFKB1 67 and in several genes within the TWEAK/Fn14 pathway. 69 Moreover, ultrarare variants in the KIF1B gene were reported in three unrelated patients with MD and an autoinflammatory immunophenotype. 70

Diagnosis of Meniere disease

Diagnostic criteria

In 2015, an international consensus on the diagnosis of MD was established by the Bárány Society. 1 The criteria classify the disease into two diagnostic categories: definite and probable. Definite MD requires at least two spontaneous episodes of vertigo lasting 20 min to 12 h, audiometrically documented low- to mid-frequency SNHL in the affected ear, fluctuating aural symptoms in the affected ear (hearing, tinnitus, and/or aural fullness), and exclusion of other vestibular disorders. These criteria have become the most widely used diagnostic framework worldwide and form the basis of clinical practice in both Europe and East Asia.

In 2020, the AAO-HNS released an updated clinical practice guideline for MD. 71 The guideline revised diagnostic and therapeutic pathways, emphasized the central role of detailed history taking and pure-tone audiometry (PTA), and recommended vestibular testing and imaging when the diagnosis is uncertain or when other disorders need to be excluded. In China, the national guideline for the diagnosis and treatment of MD, originally issued in 2017, similarly aligns with the International Bárány Society diagnostic criteria while introducing a PTA-based staging system using the three frequencies of 0.5, 1, and 2 kHz, thereby providing a quantitative basis for treatment stratification. 72

Hearing assessment

PTA remains the cornerstone of MD diagnosis. The typical audiometric pattern is low- to mid-frequency SNHL, which often fluctuates during the early phase and may progress to a flat or pan-frequency configuration later in the disease course. However, some patients with MD may exhibit involvement of all frequencies from disease onset, and the presence of high-frequency hearing loss has been associated with an increased risk of developing hearing loss in the second ear. 73

Electrocochleography (ECochG) is an important electrophysiologic technique for assessing cochlear hydrops. An increased SP/AP ratio suggests the presence of cochlear endolymphatic hydrops. More recently, the development of intracochlear ECochG during cochlear implantation has enabled more precise localization of dysfunction in different cochlear disorders, including MD, noise-induced hearing loss, and sudden SNHL. 74

Vestibular function tests

Vestibular testing can support the diagnosis and assess disease progression, although these tests are not included in the diagnostic criteria because of the considerable heterogeneity in MD. Caloric testing primarily evaluates horizontal semicircular canal and superior vestibular nerve function. The affected side often demonstrates canal paresis, although findings may be normal or even hyperactive during the early stages of disease. A Chinese cohort study suggested that vestibular test findings correlate with the degree and distribution of endolymphatic hydrops, although this relationship is not straightforward. 75

Vestibular evoked myogenic potentials (VEMPs) include cervical VEMP (cVEMP), which reflects saccular and inferior vestibular nerve function, and ocular VEMP (oVEMP), which reflects utricular and superior vestibular nerve function. In patients with MD, VEMP amplitudes may be increased during the early hydrops stage and become absent after vestibular function is lost. 76 Studies using multifrequency VEMP testing have demonstrated differences in VEMP tuning properties between MD and vestibular migraine, suggesting a potential role in differential diagnosis. 77 In addition, machine learning-based analysis of subjective visual vertical and oVEMP data has been applied to assess utricular function. 78

MRI visualization of endolymphatic hydrops

Imaging visualization of endolymphatic hydrops has become an important advance in the adjunctive diagnosis of MD. Between 2007 and 2008, investigators in Japan first reported the use of 3D-FLAIR MRI following intratympanic gadolinium administration to visualize endolymphatic hydrops. 79 This was followed by delayed MRI after intravenous gadolinium administration, which was also shown to demonstrate endolymphatic hydrops. 80 Compared with intratympanic administration, the intravenous approach is noninvasive and permits bilateral inner-ear assessment. Consequently, it is now more widely used in Europe and China, whereas intratympanic administration remains relatively common in Japan. 81

MRI is also increasingly valuable in the differential diagnosis of vestibular disorders. Delayed contrast-enhanced MRI helps distinguish MD from conditions such as vestibular migraine because patients with MD typically demonstrate more pronounced endolymphatic hydrops. 82 Nevertheless, MRI findings should always be interpreted together with clinical symptoms, audiologic findings, and vestibular function test results. The 2015 Bárány diagnostic criteria do not require MRI 1 ; however, both the 2020 AAO-HNS guideline and the Chinese guideline recognize its diagnostic value, although MRI cannot replace the clinical and audiometric criteria.71,72

Comparison of diagnostic practice between Europe and East Asia

Overall, the 2015 Bárány criteria have been widely adopted in both Europe and East Asia, although their implementation differs in practice. A minimum set of clinical variables should be included in clinical research on MD, including age at disease onset, disease duration, hearing thresholds across the standard frequencies of 0.25–8 kHz, and major comorbidities, such as migraine and immune-related disorders, including autoimmune and allergic conditions. In addition, lifetime occupational and recreational noise exposure and a family history of MD or hearing loss should be documented during the evaluation of all patients with probable MD.

The vestibular testing modalities available are broadly similar in both regions, although Japan has a longer tradition of ECochG research, whereas China has accumulated greater experience with the combined use of VEMP testing and MRI.83,84 Imaging of endolymphatic hydrops using MRI originated in Japan and was adopted relatively early throughout East Asia. It is now routinely used in multiple European centers, although its availability and technical protocols continue to vary among countries. Regarding molecular diagnosis, European research has advanced further in identifying genes associated with familial MD,50,85 whereas studies from China have focused more extensively on immune-related biomarkers.43,86,87

Treatment of Meniere disease

Lifestyle modification and conservative treatment

A low-salt diet with a daily sodium intake of less than 1.5–2 g is one of the most commonly recommended lifestyle interventions for the initial management of MD. Although high-quality evidence remains limited, this approach is widely adopted in both Europe and East Asia. Avoidance of caffeine, alcohol, and tobacco, together with regular sleep habits and reduction of psychological stress, is also commonly recommended. 2

Pharmacological treatment

Betahistine is one of the most widely used oral medications for MD in Europe and East Asia. Theoretically, it may improve inner-ear microcirculation through H1 receptor agonism and H3 receptor antagonism. However, a large multicenter, double-blind, randomized controlled trial published in 2016 failed to demonstrate that betahistine, administered at either a low dose (2 × 24 mg/d) or a high dose (3 × 48 mg/d), was superior to placebo in reducing vertigo attacks. 88 In China and Japan, betahistine remains a first-line oral therapy, and the Chinese guideline explicitly recommends it as a basic pharmacologic treatment. 89 In Japanese practice, isosorbide is also used as an oral dehydrating agent for MD, whereas it is rarely used in Western countries. In addition, Goreisan has been used in the conservative management of MD because of its diuretic properties, 89 reflecting the distinctive role of traditional medicine in East Asian practice.

During acute vertigo attacks, symptomatic treatment may include vestibular suppressants, such as antihistamines, benzodiazepines, and calcium channel blockers. These medications are used in both Europe and East Asia, although specific prescribing preferences vary according to local practice and drug availability.

Intratympanic drug delivery

When conservative treatment fails to adequately control vertigo attacks, intratympanic drug delivery is generally considered the next therapeutic step. In 2022, a Chinese–European consensus document specifically reviewed and recommended approaches to intratympanic drug delivery for MD. 90

Intratympanic corticosteroid therapy, primarily using dexamethasone (ITD), is a nondestructive treatment aimed mainly at controlling vertigo attacks. A recent systematic review of randomized controlled trials concluded that intratympanic corticosteroids may help control vertigo, although the overall quality of evidence remains limited. 91 Clinical reports indicate that ∼50% of patients with MD achieve complete vertigo control after a single course of ITD, and repeated treatment as needed may increase the control rate to 91%, with an average duration of symptom control of ∼14 months.92,93 Intratympanic corticosteroids are widely used in both Europe and East Asia, and the Chinese guidelines recommend their use after failure of standard pharmacologic therapy. 89

Intratympanic gentamicin (ITG) selectively suppresses vestibular function and can be regarded as a form of chemical vestibular ablation. Although ITG provides relatively high rates of vertigo control, it carries a risk of additional hearing deterioration. European studies suggest that an early reduction in vestibulo-ocular reflex gain after low-dose ITG may predict long-term vertigo control. 94 Korean investigators have also evaluated management strategies for patients with persistent, intractable vertigo following ITG treatment. 95 It is important to note that previous exposure to ITG may adversely affect hearing outcomes if cochlear implantation is subsequently required, 96 indicating that long-term auditory rehabilitation should be considered during treatment planning. A recent meta-analysis further compared intratympanic therapy with surgery for refractory MD, 97 whereas another meta-analysis led by Chinese investigators systematically evaluated the comparative efficacy of different pharmacologic regimens. 98 Although ITG has been used for many years in the treatment of MD, its use has progressively declined in favor of intratympanic corticosteroids because of the risk of hearing loss.

Surgical treatment

For patients with refractory vertigo that cannot be controlled with conservative treatment or intratympanic therapy, surgical intervention may be considered. Surgical options can be broadly classified as nondestructive or destructive procedures.

Endolymphatic sac surgery is the most commonly performed nondestructive procedure and includes endolymphatic sac decompression and endolymphatic sac shunting, both intended to relieve symptoms by reducing endolymphatic pressure. This approach has a long history in Japan, where Kitahara and colleagues have continued to report technical refinements and clinical outcomes. 99 In China, endolymphatic sac surgery is also widely performed. Chinese investigators have compared long-term vestibular and hearing outcomes among three surgical approaches: endolymphatic duct blockage, endolymphatic sac drainage, and endolymphatic sac decompression. 100 Reported efficacy varies across centers, and some studies suggest that heterogeneity in the underlying pathophysiology of MD may partly explain inconsistent surgical outcomes. 101

Vestibular neurectomy abolishes vertiginous input by selectively sectioning the vestibular nerve. Vertigo control rates exceed 90% in many studies, while cochlear nerve function can theoretically be preserved. However, the procedure requires a craniotomy and is associated with risks including cerebrospinal fluid leakage and facial nerve injury. 102 With the increasing use of intratympanic gentamicin, vestibular neurectomy has become less common in both Europe and the United States. 103

Labyrinthectomy eliminates vestibular function by surgically destroying the labyrinth. Although highly effective for vertigo control, it results in complete hearing loss in the operated ear and is therefore generally reserved for patients with nonserviceable hearing. Transmastoid labyrinthectomy can be combined with simultaneous cochlear implantation, and this strategy is now routinely performed in specialized centers throughout Europe and North America. Studies from the Gruppo Otologico in Italy, 104 as well as centers in the United States 105 and Germany, 106 have all reported favorable auditory and vestibular outcomes following combined labyrinthectomy and cochlear implantation. German investigators have also proposed a less destructive alternative consisting of lateral semicircular canal occlusion combined with endolymphatic sac surgery and cochlear implantation, with the aim of controlling vertigo while preserving residual vestibular function. 107

Triple semicircular canal occlusion (TSCO) is another alternative surgical strategy that controls vertigo by occluding all three semicircular canals without destroying cochlear function. Chinese investigators have accumulated substantial experience with this technique, including studies evaluating its long-term efficacy, 108 its combination with endolymphatic sac decompression, 109 and the causes of recurrence and revision surgery. 110 A recent randomized controlled trial directly compared TSCO with intratympanic gentamicin, providing head-to-head comparative evidence for this procedure. 110 TSCO has also been used in patients with MD and Tumarkin attacks. In one series of seven patients, drop attacks resolved completely after surgery, and 85.7% achieved Class A vertigo control, suggesting that TSCO may represent an alternative before proceeding to labyrinthectomy or vestibular neurectomy.111,112 TSCO remains uncommon in Europe, where it has primarily been discussed as a potential option for refractory disease rather than being widely adopted in practice. 113

Cochlear implantation

For patients with severe-to-profound SNHL caused by MD, cochlear implantation (CI) is the primary strategy for auditory rehabilitation. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses indicate that CI outcomes in patients with MD are generally favorable, and CI may also provide additional improvements in vertigo and tinnitus. 114 Long-term follow-up studies have demonstrated significant benefits in hearing, quality of life, and vestibular symptoms. 106 The use of CI for patients with MD is increasing in both Europe and East Asia, and simultaneous labyrinthectomy with CI is now performed in specialized centers in both regions. 115

More recently, combined CI with endolymphatic sac decompression has also been reported, with preliminary results suggesting concurrent improvements in hearing and vestibular function. 116 For patients with both refractory vertigo and severe hearing loss, simultaneous TSCO and CI has likewise been described, with early reports indicating that this approach may provide both vertigo control and hearing rehabilitation. 117 Looking ahead, the development of the vestibulocochlear implant (VCI) offers the prospect of simultaneous auditory and vestibular rehabilitation. Initial results from second-generation vestibular implants in humans have already been published, suggesting that this technology is progressing from proof of concept toward clinical translation. 117

Conclusions and future perspectives

Meniere disease is not a single condition, and clinical subgroups and immunophenotypes have now been defined as a first step toward guiding more effective treatments.

Beyond the clinical criteria, diagnosis should incorporate objective biomarkers, such as cytokine profiles and granulocyte and monocyte counts. Genetic testing is expected to increase our understanding of the molecular basis of MD in the coming years. Transgenic humanized mouse models carrying OTOG, MYO7A, and TECTA variants have been developed to model MD in mice and support the development of gene therapy.

In addition, a standardized set of clinical variables should be recommended for clinical research on MD, including age at disease onset, disease duration, hearing thresholds (0.25–8 kHz), and major comorbidities, such as migraine and immune-related disorders, including autoimmune and allergic conditions. This would provide a common framework to facilitate comparisons of MD research across different populations. 17
