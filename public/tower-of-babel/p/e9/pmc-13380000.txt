Renoprotective effects of gut NHE3 inhibitor tenapanor in a rat model of unilateral renal ischemia–reperfusion injury with contralateral nephrectomy
Authors: Mitsuhiro Yamaguchi, Yukihiro Hitaka, Shunya Tsuji, Masashi Ogawa, Kenji Takemoto, Akihiko Sakamoto, Yusuke Yamada, Naohito Isoyama, Koji Shiraishi, Masataka Asagiri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractKidney ischemia–reperfusion (I/R) injury is an unavoidable complication of kidney transplantation that contributes to acute kidney injury (AKI), chronic kidney disease (CKD), and limited long-term graft survival. Elevated serum phosphate has been implicated not only as a consequence of renal dysfunction but also as an active mediator of kidney injury. Tenapanor, an intestinal sodium/hydrogen exchanger 3 inhibitor approved for the treatment of hyperphosphatemia in dialysis patients, may therefore have renoprotective potential; however, its effects on renal I/R injury remain unclear. Here, we show that oral administration of tenapanor suppresses renal calcium phosphate deposition after I/R, mitigates AKI progression, and reduces subsequent renal fibrosis in rats. These findings suggest that tenapanor mitigates renal I/R injury and may represent a potential pre-transplant strategy to improve long-term graft outcomes.Keywords: Tenapanor, Ischemia–reperfusion injury, Acute kidney injury, NHE3 inhibitor, Phosphate, Rat model

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) presents a growing global health burden, with its rising prevalence continually contributing to a steady increase in the number of patients progressing to kidney failure [1,2]. Kidney transplantation is the optimal renal replacement therapy for patients with kidney failure [[3], [4], [5], [6]]. However, long-term graft survival remains limited [3,7], partly due to ischemia–reperfusion (I/R) injury during transplantation [8].

I/R injury induces cell death in renal tubular epithelial cells through ATP depletion and increased oxidative stress [[9], [10], [11]]. This subsequently triggers the recruitment of inflammatory macrophages, which secrete proinflammatory cytokines, such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF, leading to acute kidney injury (AKI) [[9], [10], [11]]. Following AKI, the upregulation of transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) promotes fibrosis in the transplanted kidney, leading to CKD and negatively affecting long-term graft outcomes [11]. Accordingly, mitigating I/R injury is considered a key strategy for improving long-term graft survival [7].

The kidneys play a central role in systemic phosphate excretion and serum phosphate homeostasis by filtering phosphate at the glomerulus and regulating its reabsorption, primarily in the proximal tubules [12]. Increased serum phosphate levels or decreased renal function enhance the phosphate burden delivered to the renal tubules, thereby creating a locally phosphate-rich environment that can promote the formation of calcium phosphate crystals in the tubular lumen [13]. Calcium phosphate deposition within the renal tubules induces tubular epithelial cell death, triggering inflammatory responses [13]. Because renal I/R injury acutely impairs kidney function, thereby potentially increasing tubular phosphate burden, we hypothesized that reducing phosphate burden before I/R could suppress renal calcium phosphate deposition and mitigate AKI progression following renal I/R injury.

Tenapanor, a clinically approved agent for the treatment of hyperphosphatemia in patients undergoing dialysis, binds to and blocks the intestinal sodium/hydrogen exchanger isoform 3 (NHE3), thereby reducing phosphate and sodium absorption in the gastrointestinal tract [14]. Previous studies have shown that tenapanor administration in a rat model of CKD significantly reduced serum phosphate levels and preserved renal function, demonstrating its renoprotective effects in the setting of phosphate overload [15]. In this study, we investigated whether pre-transplant tenapanor–mediated phosphate control could mitigate I/R injury in a rat model of kidney transplantation.

Materials and methods

Experimental animals and renal I/R model

All animal procedures were ethically approved by the Animal Experiment Committee of Yamaguchi University School of Medicine (39-004) and performed in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Animal studies are reported in compliance with the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines. Male Wistar rats (8–9 weeks old, 250–280 g; Kyudo Co.) were housed under controlled environmental conditions (25 ± 1 °C, 50–60% humidity, 12-h light/dark cycle) with ad libitum access to standard chow and water.

Renal I/R injury was induced under isoflurane anesthesia by clamping the left renal pedicle for 45 min, followed by reperfusion, with simultaneous right nephrectomy. Rats were randomly assigned to three groups: sham, I/R + vehicle, and I/R + tenapanor. Tenapanor (Kyowa Kirin Pharma) was administered in drinking water at 30 mg/L from 14 days before surgery. For the chronic-phase study, administration was continued until 14 days after I/R injury. Drinking water was refreshed every 2 days, at which time water consumption was monitored per cage (two rats per cage). The group-specific water consumption and the estimated 2-day tenapanor intake per rat are summarized in Supplementary Table S1 and S2. The 14-day duration of tenapanor administration was selected based on clinical studies showing that its therapeutic efficacy reaches a steady state [16].

Serum biochemical and electrolyte assessment

At the designated time points, blood samples were collected via cardiac puncture under anesthesia. Serum creatinine, phosphate, sodium, potassium, and chloride levels were measured using an automated biochemical analyzer (Model 7180, Hitachi).

Fecal phosphate measurement

Fecal samples were collected directly from the rectum of individual rats at euthanasia. Samples collected 24 h after I/R injury were lyophilized, resuspended in ultrapure water, and centrifuged. The supernatants were used for phosphate quantification with a PiBlue Phosphate Assay Kit (BioAssay Systems, POPB-500).

Histological and immunohistochemical analyses

Kidney tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned at 3 μm. Tubular injury was evaluated by hematoxylin and eosin staining and scored blindly by two independent observers using a 0–5 semiquantitative scale based on epithelial necrosis, tubular dilatation, brush border loss, and cast formation, where 0 = normal and 5 = ≥76% of tubules affected. Interstitial fibrosis and calcium phosphate deposition were assessed by Sirius Red and von Kossa staining, respectively. For immunohistochemistry, sections were subjected to antigen retrieval, incubated with anti-F4/80 antibody (1:4000; Proteintech, #28463-1-AP), followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibody and DAB visualization. TUNEL staining was performed using a commercial kit (Wako, #293-71501). F4/80-positive, TUNEL-positive, Sirius Red–positive, and von Kossa–positive areas were quantified in 10 non-overlapping fields per section using BZ-X800 Analyzer software (Keyence).

Quantitative PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from the kidney cortical tissue using TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen) and the RNeasy Plus Micro Kit (Qiagen), following the manufacturers’ protocols. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 1 μg of total RNA using qScript cDNA SuperMix (QuantaBio). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Takara) on a QuantStudio 1 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Gene expression levels were normalized to 18S rRNA and calculated using the comparative Ct (ΔΔCt) method. The primer sequences for the target genes were as follows:

18S rRNA: 5′-CTCAACACGGGAAACCTCAC-3’ (forward) and 5′-CGCTCCACCAACTAAGAACG-3′(reverse)

F4/80 (Adgre1): 5′-CTTTGGCTATGGGCTCCCAGTC-3’ (forward) and 5′-CACGATCAACTCTGCCCTCCTTGC-3’ (reverse)

Tnf: 5′-CTGAACTTCGGGGTGATCGG-3’ (forward) and 5′-GGCTTGTCACTCGAATTTTGAGA-3′(reverse).

Il-1β: 5′-GAAATGCCACCTTTTGACAGTG-3′(forward) and 5′-TGGATGCTCTCATCAGGACAG-3′(reverse).

Il-6: 5′-CTGCAAGAGACTTCCATCCAG-3′(forward) and 5′-AGTGGTATAGACAGGTCTGTTGG-3′(reverse).

Cell culture

MDCK-II cells (RIKEN BRC, RCB5148) were cultured in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 100 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin. Human renal proximal tubule epithelial cells (RPTECs; Lonza, CC-2553) were cultured in Renal Epithelial Cell Growth Medium (REGM™ BulletKit, Lonza) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Phosphate-induced cytotoxicity was assessed with slight modifications to a previously reported method [13]. Briefly, calcium and phosphate were added to the culture medium to achieve a final calcium concentration of 3 mM and the indicated phosphate concentrations in the presence of 0.1% FBS. Cell viability was evaluated 24 h after treatment using Cell Counting Kit-8 (DOJINDO, CK04), and cytotoxicity was assessed using Cytotoxicity LDH Assay Kit-WST (DOJINDO, CK12). For the in vitro I/R-like stress model, MDCK-II cells were subjected to 14 h of hypoxic and nutrient-deprived conditions, with different combinations of oxygen, serum, and glucose deprivation, followed by 8 h of reoxygenation under normoxic and nutrient-replete conditions. Tenapanor was present throughout both the deprivation and reoxygenation phases. Cytotoxicity was evaluated using Cytotoxicity LDH Assay Kit-WST (DOJINDO, CK12).

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's multiple comparison test (Fig. 1, Fig. 2, Fig. 3, Fig. 4, Supplementary Fig. S1, Supplementary Table S3), Student's t-test (Supplementary Fig. S5), unpaired t-test with Welch's correction (Supplementary Table S4), two-way ANOVA with Tukey's multiple-comparison test (Supplementary Table S1), or two-way ANOVA with Dunnett's test (Supplementary Fig. S3–S4). All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA); p < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Tenapanor ameliorates renal dysfunction in a rat model of I/R injury

To investigate the effects of tenapanor on the development of AKI following I/R, rats were administered tenapanor for two weeks prior to surgery. At 24 h after I/R injury, blood and tissue samples were collected. Serum creatinine, a pivotal indicator of glomerular filtration function, was markedly elevated by I/R injury and attenuated by tenapanor treatment (Fig. 1A). Additionally, serum phosphate levels, which were also elevated by I/R injury, were reduced following tenapanor administration (Fig. 1B). Consistent with this, fecal phosphate content was increased by tenapanor treatment (Supplementary Fig. S1). Histological evaluation with hematoxylin-eosin staining revealed that I/R injury induced severe tubular injury, characterized by epithelial necrosis, loss of brush border, and tubular dilation, while these features were alleviated by tenapanor treatment (Fig. 1C and D, and Supplementary Fig. S2). Taken together, these results indicate that tenapanor effectively mitigates I/R-induced AKI.

Tenapanor attenuates macrophage infiltration and inflammatory responses after I/R injury

Macrophage infiltration and subsequent inflammatory responses play critical roles in the development of I/R-induced AKI [9,11,17]. We assessed the gene expression of the macrophage marker F4/80 (Adgre1) and inflammatory cytokines, including Tnf, Il-1β, and Il-6, in the kidneys 24 h post-I/R injury. All genes were upregulated after I/R injury, but their expression was reduced by tenapanor treatment (Fig. 2A). In addition, immunohistochemistry for F4/80 showed extensive macrophage infiltration from the renal cortex to the outer medulla after I/R injury, which was attenuated by tenapanor (Fig. 2B and C). These findings suggest that tenapanor suppresses macrophage infiltration and inflammatory responses after I/R injury.

Tenapanor reduces tubular cell death and calcium phosphate deposition after I/R injury

Cell death following I/R injury is a well-known driver of macrophage infiltration [9,17]. TUNEL staining revealed that I/R injury induced cell death, which was alleviated by tenapanor treatment (Fig. 3A and B). However, tenapanor did not suppress I/R-like stress–induced kidney cell damage in vitro (Supplementary Fig. S3), suggesting that its inhibitory effect on tubular cell death in vivo may involve indirect mechanisms. A recent study has shown that calcium phosphate crystals trigger tubular epithelial cell death [13]. In line with this, tenapanor administration markedly reduced the increased calcium phosphate accumulation after I/R injury, as confirmed by von Kossa staining (Fig. 3C and D). These findings suggest that tenapanor suppresses calcium phosphate accumulation and cell death after I/R injury.

Tenapanor ameliorates renal fibrosis and may confer long-term renoprotective effects

I/R injury-induced AKI contributes to subsequent renal fibrosis and progression to CKD [18]. Tenapanor attenuated the increases in serum creatinine and serum phosphate levels on day 14 after I/R injury (Fig. 4A and B). Histological assessment revealed that tubular damage was mitigated by tenapanor administration (Fig. 4C and D). Renal fibrosis has been implicated in the progression of CKD and graft loss [19]. Sirius Red staining showed that tenapanor reduced renal collagen accumulation 14 days after I/R injury (Fig. 4E and F). These data suggest that tenapanor reduces collagen accumulation in the chronic phase after I/R injury and may improve long-term renal outcomes.

Discussion

In this study, we showed that tenapanor attenuates both acute and chronic kidney injury after renal I/R in rats. Tenapanor reduced calcium phosphate accumulation in the proximal tubules, which may exacerbate tubular cell death, and was accompanied by decreased macrophage infiltration and inflammatory responses. These effects may contribute to suppression of AKI progression and subsequent renal fibrosis, suggesting the potential of tenapanor to improve long-term renal outcomes after kidney transplantation.

A recent study demonstrated that calcium phosphate crystals directly induce tubular cell injury and inflammatory responses in renal tubular epithelial cells [13]. In line with this, we observed dose-dependent calcium phosphate-induced cytotoxicity in primary human renal proximal tubule epithelial cells (RPTECs) and a canine kidney epithelial cell line (MDCK-II cells) in vitro (Supplementary Fig. S4A–C). These findings support the notion that calcium phosphate crystal deposition after renal I/R actively contributes to tubular cell death and inflammation in our model. In addition, tenapanor did not suppress calcium phosphate-induced cell death under these in vitro conditions (Supplementary Fig. S4A–C). Given that tenapanor acts locally in the intestine with minimal systemic absorption [20], its renoprotective effects in this model are likely mediated, at least in part, by reduced intestinal phosphate absorption and the resulting decrease in renal calcium phosphate crystal deposition. Further mechanistic studies may help to clarify the pathogenic role of calcium phosphate crystal deposition in post-I/R injury.

Gut microbiota alterations have been deeply implicated in the pathophysiology of AKI [[21], [22], [23], [24]]. Given that tenapanor can induce mild diarrhea [20], its renoprotective effect might be partially modulated by shifts in the gut microbial composition. Supporting this possibility, NHE3-deficient mice, which mimic pharmacological NHE3 inhibition, exhibit altered gut microbiota profiles, including AKI-associated taxa [25,26]. Conversely, our recent study demonstrated that linaclotide, another locally acting intestinal agent, exerted potent renoprotective effects in rats without inducing apparent AKI-associated microbiota changes [27]. This discrepancy suggests that the therapeutic benefits of gut-targeted agents may not be solely mediated by microbiota alterations. Consequently, the precise relationship between the gut microbiota and the renoprotective effects of tenapanor remains unclear. Further comprehensive analyses are therefore required to address this limitation.

Tenapanor has shown favorable tolerability in healthy volunteers [28,29], patients with IBS-C, and dialysis patients [30,31], with diarrhea reported as the most common adverse event. In our study, tenapanor treatment also induced mild diarrhea; however, no significant body weight loss was observed during the experimental period (data not shown), indicating that the gastrointestinal symptoms did not adversely affect the general condition of the animals. Because diarrhea may affect systemic electrolyte balance, we examined serum sodium, potassium, and chloride levels. Tenapanor did not significantly affect these electrolyte levels in sham-operated rats, whereas I/R-induced electrolyte fluctuations, which were consistent with those reported in previous rat renal I/R models [32,33], showed a tendency toward attenuation in the tenapanor-treated group (Supplementary Table S3 and S4). These findings suggest that tenapanor was generally well tolerated under the present experimental conditions and did not exacerbate systemic electrolyte disturbances following renal I/R injury.

Regarding phosphate homeostasis, serum phosphate levels are tightly regulated through coordinated interactions among the kidney, intestine, and bone [12]. In our model, tenapanor did not significantly alter serum phosphate levels in normal rats (Supplementary Fig. S5), and no overt toxicity was observed. Nevertheless, because tenapanor chronically suppresses intestinal phosphate absorption, its possible effects on bone metabolism should be carefully considered. In the present study, bone mineral density, histomorphometric parameters, and serum markers of bone turnover, such as P1NP and CTX, were not assessed, which should be recognized as a limitation. Notably, in hemodialysis patients, 8 weeks of tenapanor treatment was reported to have no substantial effects on bone turnover markers [34,35]. These findings suggest that tenapanor treatment may have minimal adverse effects on bone homeostasis in the short term. However, further studies are required to evaluate the long-term skeletal safety of tenapanor.

A limitation of the present study is that the precise molecular pathways underlying the renoprotective effects of tenapanor remain to be fully defined. Because renal I/R-induced injury is spatially heterogeneous and predominantly affects specific tubular regions, future approaches focusing on specific tubular microenvironments, such as spatially resolved proteomics or single-cell analyses, may help clarify the precise local molecular landscapes involved.

Finally, this study demonstrates that tenapanor reduces serum phosphate levels following I/R injury, is associated with attenuation of tubular epithelial cell death, and exerts renoprotective effects. Because tenapanor is already clinically approved for patients with hyperphosphatemia and impaired renal function, its safety profile has also been evaluated in individuals with reduced renal function, such as kidney transplant recipients. From a drug repositioning perspective, the findings of this study may offer a readily available therapeutic strategy to improve the long-term prognosis of transplanted kidneys by targeting a previously underappreciated pathway of I/R injury.
