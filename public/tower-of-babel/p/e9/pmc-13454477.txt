Development and validation of a noninvasive machine learning model using urinary extracellular vesicle physical parameters for prostate cancer diagnosis
Authors: Kangxian Jiang, Dongwei Pan, Jiayin Yu, Wenbing Cai, Bingfeng Zhang, Zuheng Wang, Yuqing Lu, Dianyu Wang, Xiao Li, Deyong Zheng, Mingda Wang, Fubo Wang, Junyi Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Urinary extracellular vesicles (uEVs) are promising biomarkers for prostate cancer (PCa). Although novel uEV-based biomarkers have advanced early diagnosis, their detection processes remain cumbersome and cost-prohibitive. The physical parameters of uEVs offer untapped predictive potential for PCa, providing a more convenient, rapid, and cost-effective diagnostic approach. This study aims to construct a predictive model integrating uEV physical parameters with machine learning (ML) algorithms to enhance the early diagnosis of PCa. Urine samples were collected from 222 eligible participants. uEVs were isolated using a commercial kit, and their concentration and size parameters were quantified via nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA). Utilizing these physical parameters, five ML algorithms were trained and evaluated to identify the optimal diagnostic model for PCa. Model performance was systematically assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), learning curves, calibration curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA). Additionally, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) were employed to visualize the contributions of key predictors. Analysis of uEV physical parameters revealed that, compared to benign controls, PCa patients exhibited a significantly higher proportion of 30–150 nm uEVs and a smaller overall particle size (p < 0.001). Among the evaluated algorithms, the eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) model demonstrated superior performance. For discriminating PCa from benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH), the XGBoost model achieved AUC values of 0.934 and 0.864 in the training and testing cohorts, respectively. DCA demonstrated that the XGBoost model yielded a higher clinical net benefit across a threshold probability range of 0–60%. Overall, the diagnostic efficacy and clinical utility of the XGBoost model significantly outperformed routine clinical parameters, including prostate-specific antigen (PSA) and prostate-specific antigen density (PSAD). This study successfully developed and validated a noninvasive ML-based diagnostic model utilizing the physical parameters of uEVs. This approach serves as a preliminary adjunctive tool to assist clinicians in accurately identifying prostate cancer, thereby potentially reducing the incidence of unnecessary prostate biopsies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54920-3.

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) is one of the most prevalent malignant tumors severely threatening men’s health. Globally, PCa accounts for 7.3% of all incident cancers and ranks second in incidence among all male malignancies1. Early-stage PCa is typically asymptomatic, posing significant challenges for clinical diagnosis. Although blood prostate-specific antigen (PSA) testing is widely applied in clinical practice, its specificity and sensitivity remain sub-optimal2. Currently, the core clinical pathway for PCa diagnosis relies on risk stratification, image-guided evaluation, and targeted biopsy. According to the 2024 European Association of Urology (EAU) guidelines3, multiparametric magnetic resonance imaging (mpMRI) is recommended for high-risk populations presenting with a PSA level > 3 ng/mL or an abnormal digital rectal examination (DRE), followed by risk stratification based on Prostate Imaging Reporting and Data System (PI-RADS) scores. This paradigm allows approximately 28% of patients to avoid unnecessary biopsies while improving the detection rate of clinically significant prostate cancer (csPCa)4. However, mpMRI is constrained by several limitations, including high costs, heavy reliance on specialized equipment, and significant inter-observer variability. Furthermore, the indeterminate nature of certain PI-RADS 3 lesions complicates clinical characterization. Therefore, exploring noninvasive, cost-effective, and highly accessible supplementary diagnostic modalities to optimize biopsy decision-making and mitigate over-diagnosis and over-treatment remains an urgent clinical imperative.

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are nanoscale membranous vesicles actively secreted by cells. As vital mediators of intercellular communication, they play pivotal roles in tumor microenvironment remodeling, immune evasion, and pre-metastatic niche formation. Characterized by high stability and biological specificity, EVs have garnered widespread attention as an emerging frontier in the field of liquid biopsy5,6. van Niel et al. systematically elucidated the biogenesis, heterogeneity, and multidimensional functions of EVs in tumor progression7. Kalluri further emphasized that EVs are ideal targets for liquid biopsy, enabling the early detection and dynamic monitoring of tumors through the profiling of their cargo8. In PCa, tumor-derived EVs can promote the formation of a bone pre-metastatic niche, and the specific RNA and protein biomarkers they harbor have demonstrated substantial diagnostic potential.

Historically, research has predominantly focused on detecting specific RNAs, proteins, lipids, and other molecular biomarkers within EVs for cancer diagnosis. However, the physical parameters of EVs possess equally significant biological relevance9. Studies have indicated that tumor volumetric burden is positively correlated with the levels of EVs in body fluids10. More importantly, during the early stages of cancer, EV concentrations may elevate even before any lesions become radiographically detectable11,12. This phenomenon underscores the theoretical superiority of utilizing EVs for early cancer prediction.

In the field of urology, Logozzi et al.13 reported that, compared to patients with benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) and healthy controls (CTR), the levels of plasma exosomes co-expressing CD81 and PSA were significantly elevated in PCa patients. This specific profile achieved 100% specificity and sensitivity in discriminating PCa patients from healthy individuals. Additionally, a prospective study demonstrated that a discriminant formula—constructed via principal component analysis (PCA) and canonical discriminant analysis (CDA) based on the concentration and size parameters of plasma exosomes—could effectively differentiate PCa from CTR, yielding an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.8614. These findings strongly substantiate the potential clinical value of EV physical parameters in oncological diagnosis.

In the present study, standardized commercial kits were utilized to isolate urinary extracellular vesicles (uEVs), and nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) was employed to objectively and efficiently quantify uEV concentration and particle size distribution (PSD)15. We primarily evaluated the discrepancies in the physical parameters of uEVs between patients with PCa and BPH. Ultimately, this study aims to integrate these physical parameters with five distinct machine learning (ML) algorithms to screen, train, and validate a highly optimized diagnostic model for PCa. This approach is designed to enhance the diagnostic accuracy of PCa and provide preliminary evidence for a noninvasive adjunctive biomarker that may complement existing clinical risk assessment in biopsy-indicated patients.

Materials and methods

Study population and sample collection

Between January 2018 and April 2020, eligible participants were prospectively recruited from three medical centers (Shanghai Changhai Hospital, Zhongda Hospital, and Taizhou People’s Hospital). The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Changhai Hospital (Approval No. CHEC2013-115). As a multicenter collaborative study, this ethical approval was mutually recognized and authorized by all participating centers, covering all 222 enrolled patients. All centers strictly adhered to standardized operating procedures (SOP) for participant recruitment, sample processing, and prostate biopsies. All indicated patients underwent standard 12-core ultrasound-guided systematic prostate biopsies; mpMRI-guided targeted biopsies were not utilized. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to sample collection.

Following a standardized DRE, 20 mL of first-catch urine was collected. To minimize pre-analytical variables,such as vesicle degradation caused by temperature fluctuations or processing delays,and to control for potential batch effects, samples were immediately placed on ice. Initial processing, including centrifugation and cryopreservation, was strictly completed within two hours. Patients were enrolled based on the following inclusion criteria: (1)Pathologically confirmed diagnosis of either PCa or BPH. (2)Provision of an adequate urine volume meeting the quality control requirements for uEV isolation and analysis. (3)Complete general clinical and examination data. (4)Presence of nodules upon DRE or imaging abnormalities indicating a PI-RADS score > 3. (5)Age ≥ 18 years.The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1)History of prostate-related surgery, radiotherapy, or hormonal therapy within three months prior to sample collection. (2)Presence of concurrent malignancies.

Isolation of uEVs

uEVs were isolated using the Wayen Urine Exosome Isolation Kit (Cat. No. EIQ-03001; Wayen Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Reagents A and B within the kit utilize a proprietary polymer precipitation formulation. The procedure strictly followed the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, urine samples were thawed in a 37 °C water bath. A 20 mL aliquot was centrifuged at 3000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected and mixed with 7.5 mL of Reagent A and 670 μL of Reagent B. After thorough mixing, the solution was incubated overnight at 4 °C. The following day, the mixture was centrifuged at 3000 × g for 60 min at 4 °C. One milliliter of the supernatant was retained, and the remainder was discarded. The pellet was resuspended in the retained 1 mL of supernatant and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in 200 μL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane. A final centrifugation was performed at 10,000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant, representing the purified uEVs, was collected and stored at − 80 °C for downstream analysis.

Western blotting analysis

uEV aliquots were thawed on ice and mixed with radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer (at a 17.5:1 ratio) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (100:1 ratio). The mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected, and protein concentration was quantified using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) Protein Assay Kit. A 5 × loading buffer was added, and the samples were denatured in a metal block heater at 100 °C for 15 min. Proteins were separated using a PAGE GEL kit alongside a pre-stained protein marker via electrophoresis at 140 V for 1 h and 45 min. Polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes were pre-soaked in methanol for 10 s, assembled with the gel, and transferred at 100 V for 1 h and 30 min. The membranes were then cut according to target molecular weights, blocked with 5% bovine Serum Albumin (BSA) for 2 h, and washed. Membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against CD9, CD63, CD81, TSG101, Cytochrome C, Calnexin, and ACTC. The next day, the antibodies were recovered, and membranes were washed five times with 1 × tris-Buffered Saline with Tween 20 (TBST). Secondary antibodies were applied for a 2-h incubation, followed by another 1 × TBST wash cycle. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate, exposed, and digitally recorded.

Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA)

The concentration and particle size distribution (PSD) of uEVs were analyzed utilizing a NanoSight NS300 instrument (Malvern Panalytical, UK). Evaluated PSD metrics included Mean, Mode, Standard Deviation (SD), and D10, D50, and D90 (representing the mean size, most abundant size, size standard deviation, and the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles of the cumulative size distribution, respectively).

Samples were diluted in filtered PBS. The detection module was flushed with double-distilled water (ddH2O) prior to analysis. Diluted samples were introduced via a syringe pump. Focus, camera exposure, and field of view were optimized to record a 30-s video for particle counting. Particle diameters were calculated using the Stokes–Einstein equation. To ensure analytical stability and high reproducibility, each sample was measured in three consecutive replicates under identical settings, with the automated software calculating the final average size and concentration.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

uEV samples were diluted 1:10 in filtered PBS. A droplet of the dilution was applied to a copper grid until the surface was fully covered. After a 20-s incubation, excess liquid was wicked away using filter paper. Negative staining was performed by applying 20 μL of phosphotungstic acid solution for 5 s. Excess stain was removed, and the grid was loaded onto the TEM sample holder for imaging (JEOL Ltd., Akishima, Tokyo, Japan).

Study design and machine learning modeling

Supplementary Fig. 1 illustrates the study workflow and provides an overview of the patient cohort. Given the potential instability of feature selection after partitioning a moderate-sized clinical cohort and to prioritize the clinical rationale and biological robustness of candidate variables, initial univariate screening was performed using the entire cohort. Subsequently, 222 patients recruited from Shanghai Changhai Hospital, Zhongda Hospital and Taizhou People’s Hospital were randomly divided into a training set (n = 155, 70%) and a test set (n = 67, 30%). The test set was strictly separated during subsequent model training and hyperparameter tuning and reserved solely for evaluating model generalizability in the final analysis. Using the training dataset, five prostate cancer diagnostic models, including logistic regression (LR), support vector machine (SVM), extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), random forest (RF), and K Nearest Neighbors (KNN), were constructed and compared to assess their diagnostic performance.

Optimal parameters for the five diagnostic models were derived from the training set. Following internal cross-validation and comprehensive performance assessment, the best-performing classifier was selected to construct the final PCa diagnostic model. Model performance metrics, including accuracy (ACC), negative predictive value (NPV), and positive predictive value (PPV), were calculated (Supplementary Table 1). The diagnostic capacity for PCa was further evaluated using ROC curves, calibration curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA). Finally, the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) method was applied to rank the importance of the predictive features.

Statistical analysis

Data analyses were conducted using R (version 3.6.3), Python (version 3.7), and Prism V9.2 (GraphPad Software). Age and BMI differences were assessed using Student’s t-test. Differences in free PSA (fPSA), total PSA (tPSA), %fPSA, prostate volume, PSAD, and uEV physical parameters were evaluated via the Mann–Whitney U test. uEV parameters (including total uEV concentration,small uEV concentration, small uEV fraction, and PSD metrics) underwent z-score standardization. Key variables were initially screened utilizing LR. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Data were randomly partitioned into training and testing sets at a 7:3 ratio. Given the PCa positive rate of 29.3% in this cohort, representing a moderate class imbalance, the `scale_pos_weight` parameter was enabled during XGBoost training to dynamically increase the misclassification penalty for the minority class (PCa), preventing the model from biasing towards the majority class (BPH). During the model development phase, tenfold cross-validation was performed within the training set. A grid search approach was utilized to systematically tune key hyperparameters, such as learning rate and tree depth, optimizing model performance. The objective function aimed to minimize log-loss while maximizing the AUC to mitigate overfitting (Supplementary Table 2).

Descriptive statistics demonstrated an overall biopsy positive rate of 29.3% (65/222). In the training cohort, the mean age was 64.374 years (SD: 7.712), and the mean BMI was 24.674 kg/m2 (SD: 3.120). In the testing cohort, the mean age was 65.373 years (SD: 7.096), with a mean BMI of 24.624 kg/m2 (SD: 2.764). For clinical parameters in the training cohort, the median tPSA was 8.859 ng/mL (IQR: 6.800–12.580 ng/mL), median %fPSA was 0.128 (IQR: 0.092–0.218), median PSAD was 0.167 (IQR: 0.116–0.286), and median prostate volume was 52.229 mL (IQR: 37.871–77.333 mL). Correspondingly, in the testing cohort, the median tPSA was 9.762 ng/mL (IQR: 7.417–13.000 ng/mL), median %fPSA was 0.13 (IQR: 0.09–0.24), median PSAD was 0.206 (IQR: 0.114–0.290), and median prostate volume was 47.385 mL (IQR: 33.878–74.522 mL).

Within the training cohort, statistically significant differences were observed between the PCa and BPH groups regarding age, prostate volume, PSAD, and %fPSA. Specifically, the PCa group presented with a smaller median prostate volume, older mean age, and higher median PSAD and %fPSA. These clinical parameters showed no significant differences in the testing cohort. Notably, although the isolation and characterization standards for uEVs in this study were adapted from our team’s previously published work16, our prior research primarily focused on the complex molecular cargo encapsulated within the vesicles. In contrast, the current study achieved a methodological paradigm shift by relying solely on the macroscopic biophysical parameters (concentration and PSD) acquired via NTA. This strategy circumvented the cumbersome and costly RNA extraction and sequencing steps, substantially reducing diagnostic costs and improving the turnaround time for clinical testing.

Screening of key variables

Based on the preliminary exploration of diagnostic value and the results of univariate logistic regression analysis, we identified that the small uEV fraction and size parameters (Mean, Mode, D10, D50, and D90 in nm) served as independent predictors for PCa in the overall population (Table 2). Interestingly, small uEV concentration did not emerge as an independent predictor in this study. However, within machine learning methodologies—particularly tree-based ensemble algorithms like XGBoost—a single variable lacking independent predictive power in univariate analysis can still provide highly valuable synergistic classification information when engaging in non-linear interactions with other features (e.g., vesicle size metrics) in a high-dimensional space. Furthermore, previous studies have corroborated that elevated small uEV concentration in various bodily fluids of cancer patients play a crucial role in tumor diagnosis and prognosis17. Model development and evaluation

Following the determination of seven physical parameters for uEVs, including exosome proportion, concentration, Mean, Mode, D10, D50, and D90, PCa diagnostic models were developed utilizing five distinct machine learning algorithms (Fig. 2B). Although the random forest model yielded the highest AUC of 0.998 in the training set, this almost perfect performance indicated substantial overfitting. Conversely, the XGBoost model achieved an AUC of 0.934 and demonstrated superior generalization capability due to its inherent regularization mechanisms. Consequently, it was identified as the optimal model. Further evaluation of the XGBoost model revealed a mean AUC of 0.908 (95% CI: 0.862–0.955) in the training set (Fig. 3A), 0.795 (95% CI: 0.577–0.984) in the internal validation set (Fig. 3B), and 0.865 (95% CI: 0.766–0.964) in the test set (Fig. 3C). Because the AUC of the internal validation set did not exceed that of the test set, the model was deemed successfully fitted without severe overfitting.Fig. 3ROC curves of XGboost model. Performance validation of the XGBoost model

Further comparative analysis revealed that the XGBoost model significantly outperformed PSA and PSAD in the training cohort (Fig. 5A). In the testing cohort, the model also demonstrated substantial superiority over PSA alone (AUC: 0.865 vs. 0.567, DeLong’s test: p = 0.003) and PSAD alone (AUC: 0.865 vs. 0.627, DeLong’s test: p = 0.021) (Fig. 5B). Despite the relatively small sample size of the testing cohort, the XGBoost model maintained statistically superior diagnostic efficacy. To further elucidate its clinical value, DCA was conducted. The results indicated that the application of the XGBoost model yielded a higher clinical net benefit for patients compared to traditional PSA and PSAD metrics (Fig. 6A-B). Notably, within the highly controversial clinical biopsy decision threshold range of 10–30%, the XGBoost model maintained a consistently high net benefit. Model interpretability analysis

Figure 7A presents the SHAP summary plot for the XGBoost model. The plot illustrates the seven feature variables arranged along the y-axis based on their magnitude of impact on PCa prediction, with feature importance driven by the mean absolute Shapley values. A higher vertical position indicates a greater contribution to the model’s predictive output. The x-axis represents the actual SHAP values for individual patients, signifying the specific directionality and impact of each feature on the model’s final decision. Patients with higher SHAP values possess an elevated risk of PCa compared to those with lower values. Discussion

Despite current diagnostic methodologies improving the detection rate of PCa, significant challenges persist in clinical practice. The low specificity of PSA, particularly within the "diagnostic grey zone" of 4.0–10.0 ng/mL, compromises its diagnostic accuracy, thereby limiting its clinical utility in early PCa identification and biopsy guidance18. DRE relies heavily on the physician’s physical examination, rendering it highly subjective and prone to missed diagnoses. While mpMRI is an excellent imaging modality, its high costs and current accuracy limitations preclude it from entirely replacing the pathological gold standard of prostate biopsy. Conversely, completely foregoing biopsies would result in approximately 7–10% of men missing a diagnosis of clinically significant prostate cancer (csPCa)19,20. Consequently, exploring novel methodologies with superior diagnostic accuracy to better assist in clinical biopsy decision-making remains a shared objective among researchers.

Urine is considered an ideal source for discovering novel PCa biomarkers because its generation and storage occur entirely within the urinary tract. Compared to other biofluids, urinary EVs share a more unified cellular origin and are enriched with specific biological information highly relevant to prostatic diseases21,22. In this study, we utilized standardized commercial kits to isolate uEVs from post-DRE urine samples and objectively quantified their concentration and particle size using NTA23. NTA is currently recognized as a mainstream method for exosome characterization. First applied to EV measurement in biological samples in 2011, this technology boasts significant advantages—including short detection times, the avoidance of specialized chemical reagents, and operational simplicity—rendering it highly translatable to routine clinical laboratories24.

Previous studies investigating urine or uEVs as PCa biomarkers have predominantly focused on detecting specific internal molecular cargo, such as RNAs, proteins, and lipids25–30, often overlooking the diagnostic value of the overall expression levels and physical properties of exosomes. A review by Cappello et al. indicated that the absolute levels of exosomes released into body fluids could serve as predictive indicators for tumors. For instance, plasma exosome levels can act as biomarkers predicting the progression of malignant melanoma31, with concentrations significantly decreasing following primary tumor resection32. Furthermore, an acidic tumor microenvironment can stimulate the release of smaller EVs33 and induce the expression and secretion of specific exosome subpopulations34. A single-center prospective study constructed a discriminant function based on plasma small uEV concentration and size parameters, successfully discriminating PCa from healthy controls with an AUC of 0.86. Similarly, EV concentration in bile can differentiate malignant from non-malignant biliary strictures with 100% accuracy. These studies strongly underscore the potential application of EV physical parameters in early cancer warning systems.

This study uniquely reveals and emphasizes that small uEV fraction—rather than small uEV concentration or total EV levels—holds central significance in the early identification of PCa. It was previously widely accepted that higher exosome concentrations in body fluids typically indicated a heightened risk of malignancy. This discrepancy is likely attributable to variations in the biofluids selected or the specific cancer types investigated. Most prior studies focused on circulating blood exosomes; however, as a systemic biofluid, blood vesicle parameters are highly susceptible to interference from other physiological systems. In contrast, the small uEV fraction, calculated by dividing the absolute Small uEV concentration by the total uEV concentration, more accurately reflects the proportional status of exosomes as the core dominant subpopulation within total uEVs. This observation aligns with previous literature demonstrating a significant upward trend in the abundance of PSA-expressing exosome subpopulations in the plasma and urine of PCa patients13,28,34.

Regarding the size characteristics of uEVs, the overall particle size of uEVs derived from PCa patients trended smaller compared to BPH patients. Statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups across PSD parameters (Mean, Mode, D10, D50, and D90), a finding highly consistent with multiple other studies33,34. Within the overall study population, these PSD parameters also played a crucial role in predicting PCa, all yielding AUCs exceeding 0.7, a threshold indicative of robust clinical discriminatory efficacy.

Methodologically, this study introduces an innovative exploration by deeply integrating uEV physical parameters with machine learning technologies for the first time. This approach leverages the complex non-linear relationships among multi-dimensional parameters to construct a PCa-specific diagnostic model. As a core branch of artificial intelligence, supervised machine learning algorithms possess distinct advantages in processing non-linear high-dimensional data and capturing interactive predictive information among feature variables. This enhances model robustness and stability, thereby playing a pivotal role in elevating cancer detection rates and improving prediction accuracy35–37. By horizontally comparing five distinct supervised algorithms, we successfully developed and selected the optimal model architecture, providing a novel clinical scheme for the noninvasive diagnosis of PCa. Notably, recent reviews indicate that while urine-based multi-omics testing platforms show potential in PCa risk stratification, recent validation results for tools like SelectMDx have been sub-optimal, and all such tests require further prospective validation prior to clinical implementation38. Compared to these urinary molecular tests, the physical parameter panel proposed in this study is more direct and cost-effective. Moving forward, we plan to integrate this XGBoost model with existing clinical variables (e.g., age, prostate volume) and mpMRI risk calculators to further optimize current clinical diagnostic pathways.

Although this study presents a promising novel approach for the noninvasive diagnosis of PCa, several inherent limitations must be objectively acknowledged. First, the predictive model was developed based on a moderate-sized cohort (n = 222). To circumvent the profound instability of feature selection that often occurs when splitting moderate clinical samples, and to prioritize the clinical rationale and biological robustness of the incorporated variables, a preliminary univariate screening was performed on the entire cohort prior to dataset splitting. Consequently, it must be conceded that the reported testing set is not a strictly isolated independent validation set, and the study currently lacks validation from an independent external cohort. Furthermore, the diagnostic reference relied on the historical protocol of standard 12-core systematic biopsy without the incorporation of mpMRI, and the study endpoint was defined as the detection of any PCa. comparisons limited exclusively to PSA and PSAD are insufficient to reflect contemporary diagnostic benchmarks. Therefore, the proposed model should currently be regarded merely as a preliminary, auxiliary risk assessment tool. Further validation through multicenter, large-scale, prospective cohorts incorporating targeted biopsy is urgently warranted to corroborate and consolidate these findings.Second, NTA-based detection is potentially sensitive to pre-analytical variables; variations in urine centrifugation protocols, storage temperatures, and batch effects may interfere with vesicle concentration and PSD39. Concurrently, our dataset may not have comprehensively captured occult urinary tract inflammation or other non-neoplastic urological conditions. Biologically, inflammatory states can stimulate the secretion of variably sized vesicles, theoretically acting as significant confounders40. Such unaccounted pathophysiological states represent vital sources of variation that must be rigorously controlled in future designs. Third, regarding the target population and clinical scope, this cohort consisted of a biopsy-indicated population rather than a general screening population. Furthermore, the model has not yet been sub-group optimized specifically for identifying csPCa based on Gleason scores, which is crucial for contemporary precision diagnostics. Direct comparisons with mpMRI or other novel urinary biomarkers are also lacking.

Nevertheless, it is essential to emphasize that the primary design intent of this predictive model is not to entirely replace the existing gold standard for PCa diagnosis. Rather, its core positioning is to serve as a noninvasive, cost-effective early warning and risk stratification supplementary tool for patients within the PSA grey zone, thereby optimizing biopsy decision-making and providing clinicians with a valuable supplementary reference.

Conclusions

In summary, this study successfully developed and validated a novel noninvasive diagnostic model for PCa utilizing uEV physical parameters integrated with the XGBoost algorithm. This internally validated model demonstrated commendable preliminary diagnostic efficacy and strong translational prospects. Regarding clinical utility, the model offers patients a higher net benefit compared to routine clinical parameters. Consequently, it has the potential to significantly assist clinicians in avoiding unnecessary prostate biopsies, thereby alleviating patient suffering and reducing healthcare burdens.
