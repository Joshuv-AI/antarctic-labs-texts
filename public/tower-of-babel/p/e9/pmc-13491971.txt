PD-ViCo: an explainable AI-based contrastive captioner vision transformer with patch dropout for multi-class brinjal disease classification
Authors: Abu Kowshir Bitto, Md Hasan Imam Bijoy, Md Zahid Hasan, Mohammad Sarwar Hossain Mollah, Mohammad Shamsul Arefin, Pranab Kumar Dhar, Ohidujjaman, Tetsuya Shimamura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBrinjal (eggplant) is a critical crop in South Asia, especially in Bangladesh, but its production is drastically affected by numerous diseases that inhibit yield and quality. Manual diagnosis of disease is time-consuming, subjective, and prone to errors, necessitating automated, scalable technology. To address these issues, this paper proposes PD-ViCo, a lightweight, efficient transformer-based model for brinjal fruit disease classification using Simple Vision Transformer (ViT) with Patch Dropout and Contrastive Captioner (CoCa) methods. One new dataset of 1,823 field-harvested brinjal images encompassing five disease classes including Phomopsis Blight, Fruit and Shoot Borer, Fruit Cracking, Wet Rot, and Healthy samples were prepared through real-world agricultural data collection from Bangladesh. The approach includes extensive preprocessing, class balancing (under-sampling/oversampling), and resilient augmentation methods. The PD-ViCo model significantly improves classification performance under data imbalance with patch dropout regularization and CoCa-style aggregation, resulting in better generalization and robustness. On a range of imbalanced, under-sampled, and oversampled datasets, PD-ViCo achieved a classification accuracy of 99.12% and F1-score of 97.76%, outperforming both ViT and Swin Transformer across all key evaluation metrics. Explainability was also applied using Grad-CAM and Grad-CAM + + , generating visual explanations of model decisions and maintaining conformity to disease-affected regions in the images. These visualizations ensure the credibility of the model and its usability for real agricultural conditions. This study demonstrates that PD-ViCo is a highly accurate, interpretable, and lightweight model for multi-class brinjal disease diagnosis. Not only does it advance state-of-the-art in agricultural AI, but it also provides a valuable dataset and an understandable decision-making protocol that can be applied directly by farmers, agronomists, and agricultural technologists.Keywords: Brinjal disease, Eggplant disease, Transformer, PD-Vico model, Explainable AI, Agriculture, Bangladesh

Introduction

Eggplant (Solanum melongena), or brinjal, as it is more popularly known in South Asia, is a staple crop cultivated across the globe for its culinary purposes and high nutritional content [1]. With its richness in antioxidants, including vitamins A and C, as well as polyphenolic compounds that regulate blood sugar, cardiovascular health, bone health, brinjal is a key component of most diets [2]. It is of particular importance in Bangladesh as the third most significant vegetable crop, occupying over 50,000 hectares of land and making a substantial contribution to the national economy [3]. Agriculture remains a key sector in the national economy, contributing about 11.38% of GDP and 45.33% of employment during FY 2022–2023 [4]. Despite its nutritional and economic value, brinjal cultivation is exposed to severe threats from various diseases that drastically limit yield and crop quality. They include Phomopsis blight (Phomopsis vexans), Brinjal Fruit and Shoot Borer (BFSB), Growth Cracking, Rhizopus rot, and Colletotrichum wet rot [5]. Each disease occurs under specific environmental conditions, such as high temperatures and humidity or improper handling and storage, and causes premature fruit drop, rot, or deformation. Traditional disease detection relies heavily on manual observation and visual symptoms, which are generally time-consuming, subjective, and prone to error, especially when applied to large agricultural areas [6].

In recent years, deep learning (DL) technologies have demonstrated tremendous potential in the field of plant pathology, particularly in their robust image classification and disease detection capabilities. Computer vision, a revolutionary technology in agriculture, enables automated, scalable, and data-driven solutions for crop monitoring, disease diagnosis, yield estimation, and precision agriculture [7]. By analyzing visual data captured using smartphones, UAVs (drones), or field cameras, computer vision platforms can detect early symptoms of plant stress, nutrient deficiency, and pest and pathogen infections, sometimes even before they are visible to the naked eye [8]. This early detection significantly reduces crop loss, thereby reassuring farmers of the technology's effectiveness. In disease diagnosis, deep learning-based computer vision models can learn to recognize complex visual patterns across different stages of infection, leaf texture, color changes, and morphological variations. These technologies not only improve diagnostic accuracy but also permit on-field, real-time application, providing farmers with actionable intelligence. As agriculture is increasingly dependent on digital innovations, computer vision is one of the fundamental pillars of sustainable and intelligent farming [9].

To overcome the limitations of manual brinjal disease diagnosis, there is a critical need to integrate automatic, accurate, and scalable solutions into brinjal disease management systems. In this study, we explore the effectiveness of three advanced deep learning architectures including Vision Transformer (ViT) [10], Swin Transformer [11], and the proposed PD-ViCo (Patch-Dropped Vision CoCa) model for the classification and detection of brinjal fruit diseases. These models are adept at capturing fine-grained visual cues and structural variations between healthy and infected tissues, making them highly suitable for plant disease identification tasks. The classification task involves seven distinct brinjal health classes commonly observed in Bangladesh: Phomopsis Blight, Fruit and Shoot Borer, Fruit Cracking, Rhizopus Rot, Growth Cracking, Colletotrichum Wet Rot, and Healthy samples. To address the challenges posed by class imbalance, we evaluate model performance using various data balancing strategies, including standard (original), under-sampled, and over-sampled datasets. Additionally, we incorporate eXplainable AI (XAI) techniques, such as Grad-CAM [12] and Grad-CAM + + [13], to generate heatmap-based visualizations that highlight the critical image regions influencing each model’s predictions. This improves model transparency and facilitates trust among agricultural practitioners. Through comparative analysis, we demonstrate that PD-ViCo significantly outperforms both ViT and Swin Transformer, particularly under imbalanced data conditions, offering a lightweight yet highly accurate solution for practical deployment in the field. Related works

Recent advances in deep learning and computer vision have led to a surge in the recognition of plant diseases across various crops. Mohanty et al. [14] were the pioneers in using transfer learning through AlexNet and Google LeNet for plant disease diagnosis from smartphone images, marking a shift towards low-cost and scalable diagnosis. Chen et al. [15] trained LeafNet, a deep CNN, to recognize ten diseases of tea plants with high accuracy. Liu et al. [16] proposed a sensitive deep learning-based approach for rice leaf disease detection, while Ma et al. [17] employed CNNs for cucumber disease identification, reducing the necessity for manual inspection based on subjective and time-consuming examination. Ferentinos [18] trained CNNs on a big dataset for multi-crop disease detection with good performance across crops. Liang et al. [19] proposed a CNN-based rice blast disease detection system that outperforms conventional feature-based approaches.

In the case of Brinjal, Aravind et al. [20] used AlexNet and VGG-16 on smartphone images with reasonable accuracy in eggplant disease classification. Xie et al. [21] used hyperspectral imaging in combination with KNN and AdaBoost for early blight detection in eggplant. In contrast, Sabrol and Kumar [22] used a neuro-fuzzy classifier for tomato and eggplant diseases, achieving promising results that instill hope for the future of disease detection. Anand et al. [23] used k-means clustering for brinjal leaf disease segmentation, whereas Wu et al. [24] used spectral analysis for Botrytis cinerea infection detection on eggplant leaves. Mia et al. [25] explored the identification of jackfruit disease using clustering and segmentation with acceptable results. Other studies, such as those by Parul et al. [26], Brahimi et al. [27], and Gandhi et al. [28], have employed CNNs and GANs to enhance image quality in datasets and improve model accuracy in plant disease identification.

Additionally, Marcos et al. [29] demonstrated the feasibility of CNNs and transfer learning for disease recognition and nutritional deficiency, respectively. Maria et al. [30] implemented these concepts for cauliflower disease by combining transfer learning with conventional machine learning. At the same time, Revathi et al. suggested a deep neural network system for cotton leaf spot identification. Ahmed et al. [31] tackled the classification of rice disease through a two-phase intelligent segmentation and augmentation approach to overcome data scarcity. Koklu et al. [32] dealt with the classification of grapevine leaves using MobileNetV2 and feature selection methods, demonstrating greater accuracy with fewer features. Jafari et al. [33] introduced thermal imaging for pre-symptomatic detection of rose disease, a new direction in the techniques for early detection. Similarly, Bitto et al. [34] work with Potato Leaf disease detection using transfer learning. Liu and Wang proposed EggplantDet, a real-time eggplant disease detection model in complex environments based on the YOLOv8 lightweight model [35]. It integrates FasterNet for speedy feature extraction, TAM attention modules, a small-object detection head, and an adaptive WIoU loss function, achieving a high mAP of 92.61% with a detection speed of 88.39 FPS. Wang et al. [36] also added disease detection capabilities by introducing a multimodal data fusion method with inherent attention mechanisms. Their suggested model, which combined image and sensor data, demonstrated excellent performance with precision of 0.94 and mAP@75 of 0.91, indicating high robustness for real-world applications.

Some other studies emphasize the effectiveness of combining Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) with Transformer-based architecture. For instance, Si et al. [37] [A] introduced a novel dual-branch model that integrates both concepts, achieving an accuracy of over 92.2% on various plant disease datasets. This highlights the synergistic potential of CNNs and transformers in improving classification efficiency. Furthermore, Zhou et al. [38] critically assessed the applicability of transformers in plant disease identification, suggesting that transformer-based models excel in tasks involving complex spatial information and dependencies, which contrasts with the effectiveness of long-dominant CNN approaches. More recently, texture-aware and multi-stage deep learning frameworks have been explored to address fine-grained disease discrimination. JP Ortiz et al. [39] proposed an integrated methodology combining CNN-based leaf segmentation, GLCM texture features, and Vision Transformers to jointly capture local textural abnormalities and global contextual information for fungal disease classification in mango leaves. Their results demonstrated notable improvements in precision, recall, and F1-score over conventional classifiers, emphasizing the importance of feature fusion in challenging real-world scenarios. Similarly, transformer-centric hybrid architectures have shown strong potential in large-scale crop disease prediction. Gunasekaranet al. [40] [D] introduced Swin-HViT, a hybrid model that unifies Vision Transformer and Swin Transformer to leverage both global representations and fine-grained local details. Evaluated on benchmark datasets, the model achieved superior performance compared to recent methods, further validating that combining complementary transformer designs can significantly enhance robustness and accuracy in agricultural disease identification.

While traditional CNN-based models, such as VGG-16, InceptionV3, and ResNet-50, have been widely employed in plant and leaf disease classification, this study introduces and evaluates three state-of-the-art vision transformer models: Vision Transformer (ViT), Swin Transformer, and the proposed PD-ViCo (Patch-Dropped Vision CoCa) model. These models, with their novel architectural advantages, demonstrate robust performance across various image recognition tasks. Through comparison with conventional CNN approaches and utilization of explainable AI (XAI) techniques, we aim to push the state-of-the-art into brinjal disease detection. This work instills confidence in the future of disease detection and further understanding of model interpretability, scalability, and real-world applicability.

Dataset collection

We gave utmost importance to collecting correct and diverse data to ensure that our disease detection model is efficient. We conducted intense fieldwork in two of Bangladesh’s finest agricultural regions, Bogura and Dhaka, taking actual-world images of brinjal plants from nearby farms directly. Initially we gathered a total of 2273 raw images manually using a smartphone camera in natural light and under varied field conditions. It assisted us in generating a dataset that reflects well the diverse and intricate nature of brinjal diseases in real-world agricultural settings.

Our dataset contains 5 unique classes: Brinjal Fruit Cracking, Phomopsis Blight, Fruit and Shoot Borer, Brinjal Wet Rot, and Healthy plants. These types of classes are illustrated in Fig. 2, which provides you with a wide range of symptoms and plant appearances included. By harvesting and collecting this dataset in the field, we ensured that it captures the actual variability and severity of disease there, which is critical to building a robust and generalizable deep learning model. This experiential learning reinforces the validity of our research and contributes quality resources towards the development of precision agriculture technology for the management of brinjal diseases.

Image preprocessing

Duplicate image removal

To eliminate redundancy and preserve dataset integrity, we employed perceptual hashing (pHash) for image deduplication, as outlined in Algorithm 1 [41]. Each image was first converted to grayscale, resized to 32 × 32 pixels, and transformed using the discrete cosine transform (DCT). Low-frequency components were then extracted to generate a 64-bit hash representing the image’s visual content. A hash table was used to track unique entries: if the computed hash did not exist in the table, the image was added to both the hash table and the final dataset; if a matching hash was found, the image was identified as a duplicate and excluded. By removing exact duplicates (Hamming distance = 0), this approach ensures a high-quality, consistent dataset, improving its reliability for training and evaluating machine learning models. The final dataset consists of 1,823 distinct raw images.

Data preprocessing

In our study, robust data pre-processing was a crucial component to enable the deep learning architectures to be effective and generalized. The input images were all normalized and resized to dimension of 128 × 128 and 224 × 224 pixels in accordance with the model architecture demands. Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE) [42] with a clip limit of 2.0 and tile grid size of 8 × 8 was performed on every image prior to any transformation. This approach increases local contrast and improves visibility in non-uniform lighting situations common in real-world agri-environmental applications. Before and after applying CLAHE image shows in Fig. 3 though there are no major changes. To incorporate additional variability and avoid overfitting, we also used data augmentation techniques using PyTorch’s transforms library [43]. These included random horizontal and vertical flips, random rotation by up to ± 30 degrees, random zooms, and translations by 10 to 20%, and Color Jitter with controlled adjustments in brightness (± 0.2), contrast (± 0.2), saturation (± 0.2), and hue (± 0.1). Finally, all the images were converted to tensors and normalized by mean and standard deviation of [0.5, 0.5, 0.5]. The augmented data retained their class labels and were organized into a class-wise directory hierarchy, which facilitated efficient sampling and label tracking. The preprocessing pipeline significantly enhanced the robustness of the trained models to negative effects resulting from changes in lighting, orientation, and field noise observed in real-world scenarios.

Data balancing

We have 1,823 brinjal fruit images in our dataset, with five disease-related classes of varying sample sizes. The largest class, Shoot and Fruit Borer, consists of 725 images, followed by Healthy with 514 images, Wet Rot with 223, Brinjal Fruit Creaking with 200, and the smallest, Phomopsis Blight, with 161 images. This class imbalance is a critical threat of bias during model training, as models tend to overfit majority classes and perform poorly on minority classes. Figure 4 depicts the baseline class-wise distribution and serves comparative comparison.

Class-wise distribution of the original (imbalanced) brinjal fruit dataset

Alternatively, we attempted random under-sampling, where we reduced all the classes to match the sample size of the smallest class, Phomopsis Blight (161 images). We did this by random sampling without replacement (np.random.choice()), achieving a balanced set of 805 images (161 × 5 classes). While this approach handles class imbalance, it minimizes data size significantly and could potentially lose significant variation, particularly from majority classes like Shoot and Fruit Borer and Healthy. The distribution after under-sampling is depicted in Fig. 5, with each class being represented equally but on a smaller scale.

Balanced class distribution after random under-sampling

Random oversampling was used to address the imbalance without data removal. Using resample () with replacement, every minority class was oversampled to have the size of the majority class (725 images). This included duplicating available images within each minority class instead of creating new images through augmentation. The result was an oversampled final dataset consisting of 3,625 images (725 × 5 classes) with balanced representation across every class. The balanced class distribution obtained is shown in Fig. 6, which depicts the strength of oversampling in bias reduction.

Proposed Model—Patch-Dropped Vision CoCa (PD-ViCo)

We introduce PD-ViCo, a lightweight yet robust image classification model tailored for brinjal fruit disease detection (as seen in Fig. 7). This architecture synergizes the global contextual learning power of the Simple Vision Transformer (SimpleViT) [10] with an efficient, single-layer classification head inspired by CoCa (Contrastive Captioners) [44]. The primary goal of PD-ViCo is to achieve high accuracy and interpretability while remaining scalable and computationally efficient qualities essential for real-world agricultural settings, where datasets often exhibit class imbalance and subtle inter-class visual variations. PD-ViCo operates in three main stages: patch-based image embedding [45], transformer-based global representation learning with patch dropout regularization [46], and CoCa-style aggregation for classification [47].

Experiments and results

Experiment setup

This study investigates the performance of three transformer-based models including ViT, Swin Transformer, and the proposed PD-ViCo (SimpleViT with Patch Dropout and CoCa) for multi-class classification on a field-collected Brinjal Dataset with five disease-related classes. The dataset was split 80% for training and 20% for validation, with images resized to 224 × 224 (ViT, Swin) and 128 × 128 (PD-ViCo). Strong augmentations (flip, rotation, color jitter) were applied, mirroring real-world scenarios. All models were trained for 20 epochs, with a batch size of 16, using the Adam optimizer (learning rate = 0.0001) and the CrossEntropyLoss function, under a consistent setup. Experiments were conducted on an Intel i7 CPU, 16 GB of RAM, and an NVIDIA P100 GPU, which are commonly available resources. Performance was evaluated via accuracy, loss curves, classification reports, and confusion matrices. Architectural details are summarized in Table 1.

Experiments results

In this study, five classes were highly imbalanced in distribution, with the “Shoot and Fruit Borer” class dominating (725 images), while the “Phomopsis Blight” class was significantly underrepresented (161 images). Given the class imbalance, we evaluated model performance under three distinct conditions: the original imbalanced dataset, a balanced dataset created through under-sampling, and a balanced dataset created through over-sampling. Each condition allowed us to assess the robustness and generalizability of our models in realistic agricultural scenarios, where class imbalance is often unavoidable.

Performance on imbalance dataset

On the imbalanced dataset, all three models demonstrated reasonably high overall accuracy, yet their ability to accurately identify minority class samples varied significantly. The confusion matrix of three models on an imbalance dataset depicts in Fig. 8. As seen in Table 3, ViT achieved accuracy as high as 96.44%, but with poor True Positive Rates (TPR) in some classes, as low as 51.43%, and a fluctuating F1 Score (as low as 52.17), indicating difficulties in recognizing minority class instances. Swin Transformer performed even less reliably under this condition, with accuracy dropping to 74.25% in some classes, and F1 scores dipping to 36.36%. This poor performance is attributed to Swin Transformer’s hierarchical structure, which, while powerful for local features, appears less robust to skewed class distributions. In contrast, the proposed model (Simple ViT + Patch Dropout + CoCa) consistently achieved a balanced performance. While its overall accuracy ranged from 92.88% to 95.89%, its TPR, F1 score, and error rate remained consistently stable across all classes. For example, it attained a TPR of 95.19% and an F1 score of 90.00% in some classes, with very low False Positive Rates (as low as 1.78%). These results indicate that the proposed model effectively addresses class imbalance by learning more discriminative feature representations, leading to robust performance across classes. In addition, PD-ViCo requires only ~ 7.6 GFLOPs, which is substantially lower than ViT (~ 19.6 GFLOPs) and Swin Transformer (~ 46.5 GFLOPs), confirming its lightweight and computationally efficient design. Furthermore, in terms of inference efficiency, PD-ViCo achieves the lowest latency of 5.2 ± 0.2 ms per image, compared to 12.2 ± 0.3 ms for ViT and 28.9 ± 0.4 ms for Swin Transformer, highlighting its suitability for real-time applications.

Performance on under-sampling dataset (balanced)

When applied to the under-sampled version of the dataset, where the majority classes were reduced to match the minority ones, ViT’s performance dropped noticeably. The confusion matrix of three models on the under-sampling dataset (balanced) is depicted in Fig. 9. Its accuracy fell to 80.12% in the worst case, with an F1 score declining to 36.00%, revealing its dependency on sufficient training data per class as seen in Table 4. Swin Transformer, on the other hand, responded better to this balanced scenario, with accuracy reaching up to 94.41% and F1 scores improving to 86.57%. This suggests that Swin’s architecture benefits from class distribution parity, though some performance inconsistency remained across disease types. However, the proposed model continued to outperform both, maintaining an impressive high accuracy (up to 95.89%), excellent TPR (as high as 95.19%), and strong F1 scores (up to 90.00%). Even in the most challenging cases, it exhibited a low error rate (as low as 4.11%), indicating that the integration of Patch Dropout and CoCa mechanisms facilitated the learning of more robust feature representations even with limited samples per class. In addition, PD-ViCo requires ~ 7.2 GFLOPs, which is substantially lower than ViT (~ 21.3 GFLOPs) and Swin (~ 49.4 GFLOPs), while also achieving the lowest inference time of 5.6 ± 0.2 ms per image, compared to 12.8 ± 0.2 ms for ViT and 29.7 ± 0.4 ms for Swin Transformer, confirming its lightweight, efficient, and fast inference design.

The baseline ViCo model achieved an accuracy of 96.27% with an F1 score of 91.18%, serving as a reference point. Introducing Patch Dropout alone led to noticeable gains, increasing accuracy to 97.93% and reducing the error rate to 2.07%, indicating improved spatial robustness. Similarly, enabling only the Contrastive Captioner further enhanced representation learning, achieving 98.07% accuracy and an F1 score of 95.76%. A stronger improvement was observed when Patch Dropout was combined with a frozen captioner, yielding 98.48% accuracy and a reduced error rate of 1.52%, highlighting the complementary effect of spatial regularization and semantic guidance even without contrastive alignment. The full PD-ViCo model, incorporating both Patch Dropout and an actively trained Contrastive Captioner, delivered the best overall performance, achieving 99.12% accuracy, 97.76% F1 score, and the lowest error rate of 0.88%. Overall, these results indicate that PD-ViCo maintains consistently strong and balanced performance compared to ViT and Swin Transformer across the evaluated settings. In addition, the proposed PD-ViCo model requires approximately 6.8 GFLOPs per forward pass for a 128 × 128 input, which is substantially lower than ViT and Swin Transformer operating at 224 × 224 resolution. This reduction is primarily due to the smaller token count and compact transformer depth. Importantly, Patch Dropout does not introduce additional FLOPs, and the Contrastive Captioner adds only marginal overhead. Moreover, PD-ViCo achieves the lowest inference time of 5.1 ± 0.2 ms per image, compared to 11.4 ± 0.3 ms for ViT and 27.6 ± 0.4 ms for Swin Transformer, confirming its efficient and fast inference capability. These results demonstrate that PD-ViCo achieves strong performance with reduced computational complexity, supporting its lightweight and real-time efficient design.

Overall performance

The performance evaluation of three deep learning models including ViT, Swin Transformer, and the proposed PD-ViCo (Simple ViT + Patch Dropout + CoCa) across three data distribution strategies (Imbalanced, Under-sampling, and Oversampling) reveals a consistent trend: PD-ViCo outperforms the others in nearly all evaluation metrics (see Table 7). In the imbalanced scenario, PD-ViCo achieves an accuracy of 93.10%, which is higher than both ViT (92.55%) and Swin (85.21%). It also demonstrates superior F1 score (77.55%) and precision (77.57%), suggesting its robustness in handling class imbalance and subtle visual variations in disease patterns. The patch dropout mechanism, combined with a lightweight CoCa-style classification head, likely contributes to its ability to generalize better without overfitting dominant classes. When tested on the under-sampled (balanced) dataset, PD-ViCo continues to lead with 94.04% accuracy, a true positive rate (TPR) of 86.03%, and a precision of 85.70%, outperforming ViT and Swin by a significant margin. This indicates that even with reduced training data, PD-ViCo effectively captures discriminative features for brinjal disease classification. Notably, its F1 score of 85.19% under this setting reflects its balanced precision-recall tradeoff, which is crucial for medical and agricultural diagnostics. The most outstanding results are observed under the oversampling scenario, where PD-ViCo achieves the highest accuracy of 99.12%, a TPR of 97.77%, and an F1 score of 97.76%. These metrics are substantially higher than both ViT and Swin, confirming the model's ability to take full advantage of class-balanced data distributions. Additionally, it records the lowest false positive rate (0.55%) and false negative rate (2.23%), highlighting its strong capability in correctly identifying both diseased and healthy samples with minimal errors.

Explainable AI

To evaluate the interpretability of our proposed PD-ViCo model, we applied two well-known explainable AI (XAI) techniques including Grad-CAM and Grad-CAM + + to represent test examples of all five classes from the brinjal disease classification dataset (see Table 8). Our goal was to determine whether model decisions stemmed from disease-related visual regions and to identify differences in localization accuracy between the two XAI. These visual explanations were responsible for conveying how PD-ViCo discriminates and learning about fine-grained symptom patterns among different disease classes and normal samples. XAI techniques serve as an essential go-between deep learning predictions and human understanding, particularly in domains such as agriculture, where end-users have model interpretability requirements. To this end, Grad-CAM and Grad-CAM + + generate class-specific activation maps by backpropagating gradients of the output classification layer, helping to reveal the discriminative regions the model employed when making a prediction [49–51].

Table 8 presents side-by-side visualizations of raw images, Grad-CAM maps, and Grad-CAM + + maps for the five classes: Brinjal Fruit Cracking, Phomopsis Blight, Shoot and Fruit Borer, Wet Rot, and Healthy Brinjal for two random sets. As Table 8 would testify, Grad-CAM is able to pick out larger regions of interest for disease lesions. For Brinjal Fruit Cracking, the model's activation is concentrated around the center necrotic patch, thus PD-ViCo accurately zooms in on the characteristics of tissue rupture and rot. Similarly, for Phomopsis Blight, the model is activated around the darkened region of the lesion, reflecting high sensitivity towards discoloration and textural abnormalities. Shoot and Fruit Borer samples indicate heat localization at the points of larval entry and internal damage, demonstrating the model’s focus on localized structural anomalies. The results confirm the model's ability to highlight contextually significant areas irrespective of complex backgrounds.

Grad-CAM + + provides even more accurate and specific attention maps from the same image, especially in the case of those diseases characterized by infinitesimal, discrete lesions such as Wet Rot and Shoot and Fruit Borer. Compared to Grad-CAM, Grad-CAM + + produces sharper edges and less diffusion in non-relevant regions. For instance, in Wet Rot, Grad-CAM + + demarcates the infected basal region more sharply, which suggests that it serves better to represent low-frequency soft tissue degeneration. Further, in Healthy Brinjal class, both methods prevent activating any disease-like regions appropriately, which justifies the discriminative capability of the model for non-pathological samples. Together, these visualizations show that PD-ViCo not only achieves high classification accuracy but also exhibits high alignment between visual symptoms and decision-making trajectories, which is of paramount significance for trust and deployment in real-world agricultural applications [51]. Grad-CAM helps in the localization of the overall region of concern, whereas Grad-CAM + + enables more accurate understanding of feature localization [52]. This multi-layered explainability further facilitates model validation, error analysis, and semi-automated disease annotation tool development. The mutual consistency between both approaches across various disease classes only strengthens the arguments for PD-ViCo’s strong and explainable performance.

Discussions

The PD-ViCo model (Patch-Dropped Vision CoCa) introduced, the light-weight hybrid vision transformer model, outperforms across various test conditions for brinjal disease classification. With Simple Vision Transformer as its backbone, supported by Patch Dropout and aggregation with CoCa (Contrastive Captioner)-based aggregation, PD-ViCo is extremely resilient in real-world scenarios of data imbalance, noisy background, and limited samples. The model was evaluated on three scenarios including imbalanced, under-sampled and over-sampled datasets and performed better than the baseline ViT and Swin Transformer models on accuracy, F1 score, and error rate in all scenarios (see in Fig. 11). On the oversampled balanced dataset, PD-ViCo achieved best results: accuracy of 99.59%, F1 score of 98.96%, false negative rate of 1.38%, and an extremely low error rate of 0.41%. Even under the challenging imbalanced data scenario, the model showed strong class-wise discrimination with consistent true positive rates on minority classes. In addition, integration of explainable AI techniques such as Grad-CAM and Grad-CAM + + contributed to the validity of the model by showing that the model’s decision was based on visually and clinically significant areas of the brinjal images. These results together confirm PD-ViCo’s usability for real-world application in precision agriculture scenarios.

This highlights a significant gap in the literature, especially considering that fruit diseases have a direct impact on market value and crop yield. In the context of brinjal disease classification, Aravind et al. [20] utilized VGG-16 and achieved an accuracy of 96.70%. Recent approaches have attempted to enhance performance through more complex and hybrid architectures. For instance, Liu and Wang [35] proposed a YOLOv8-based pipeline augmented with FasterNet, TAM attention modules, and WIoU, achieving 92.61% mean average precision (mAP) in eggplant disease detection. While fast and detection-oriented, it was not optimized for fine-grained disease classification. Wang et al. [36] employed a multimodal framework combining image and sensor data through attention mechanisms, reaching 92.00% accuracy. Although innovative, the method requires costly sensor integration and complex data fusion, limiting its feasibility in low-resource environments. In contrast, the PD-ViCo model presented in this study surpasses all prior methods by achieving a classification accuracy of 99.12% on brinjal disease datasets. Beyond its high accuracy, PD-ViCo also delivers a strong F1 score and maintains minimal error rates across all evaluation settings, including imbalanced and balanced data scenarios. Notably, the model is lightweight, incorporates patch dropout regularization, and employs a CoCa-style classification head for computational efficiency. Furthermore, it is equipped with integrated explainability tools (Grad-CAM and Grad-CAM + +) to enhance transparency and trust in predictions nan area where many prior models fall short.

Despite its demonstrated strengths, PD-ViCo has certain limitations that warrant consideration. The model comprises approximately 23.2 million parameters, which, although lighter than Swin Transformer (~ 28.3 million), may still pose challenges for deployment on ultra-low-power edge devices in rural settings. Training and inference impose moderate computational loads, and real-time field deployment may require further optimization. Additionally, PD-ViCo relies on preprocessing techniques such as CLAHE-based contrast enhancement, data augmentation, and strict normalization; deviations from these guidelines during image acquisition could impact classification accuracy. The dataset, while collected under real field conditions, consists of only 1,823 images across five disease classes, which constrains the scope for deep model training without overfitting, even with augmentation. Furthermore, unlike multimodal approaches that incorporate external inputs such as temperature or humidity, PD-ViCo is limited to RGB visual data, which may reduce predictive power in complex cases with visually similar or overlapping disease symptoms. While PD-ViCo demonstrates strong overall performance, a few misclassifications were observed. False positives occurred when visually similar disease symptoms such as early-stage Phomopsis Blight and Wet Rot appeared on the same leaf, leading to confusion in local patch representations. False negatives tended to arise under challenging conditions such as low-light images, occluded leaves, or overlapping fruits, which obscured key disease features. These observations highlight the importance of high-quality image acquisition and suggest avenues for future improvement, including the incorporation of multi-view imaging, temporal monitoring, or multimodal data, to further enhance robustness and reduce misclassification in real-world field deployments.

The PD-ViCo model has immense practical applicability in real-world agricultural scenarios, enabling early, accurate, and automatic diagnosis of critical brinjal diseases such as Phomopsis Blight, Shoot and Fruit Borer, and Wet Rot. While training the model requires moderate computational resources, inference is lightweight and efficient, allowing farmers, agronomists, and extension agents to utilize smartphone cameras or edge devices to capture images of leaves or fruits and receive near-instantaneous diagnoses of potential infections [53, 54]. This capability reduces reliance on physical inspections and expert availability, accelerates response times, and mitigates crop losses. Additionally, the explainability features generate visual heatmaps that highlight the locations of disease symptoms, enabling targeted pesticide or fungicide application and thereby reducing chemical usage and associated costs [55]. The model’s lightweight design further facilitates deployment on AI-powered drones or handheld devices, supporting scalable field scouting solutions. Moreover, PD-ViCo demonstrates robustness to noisy and imbalanced data, making it suitable for uncontrolled field conditions a critical requirement for effective use in rural agrarian communities.

For the realization of the full potential of such models as PD-ViCo, some policy-level interventions are called for. First, governments and agricultural extension services should initiate pilot projects that involve AI-based disease detection models in national agricultural development plans, especially for high-value crops like brinjal. Sub-national programs in brinjal-growing districts (e.g., Bogura, Rangpur) can incorporate PD-ViCo as part of digital farming toolkits provided to lead farmers. Investment in agricultural data infrastructure is also necessary, including public repositories of labeled high-quality images of crop diseases to be built and hosted by agricultural research institutions, universities, and AI labs to drive innovation and reduce redundancy in data harvesting. Third, low-cost AI-based diagnostic kits or PD-ViCo mobile apps must be co-designed with local agritech start-ups and subsidized for smallholder farmers through cooperatives or input dealers. Fourth, agricultural officers and field assistants must be trained in AI literacy as well as how to read explainable AI outputs so that human-in-the-loop deployment where needed is assured. Finally, mobile apps for each of the many languages, with Bengali interfaces and local language dialects, must be developed so that non-technical farmers can also easily interact with disease prediction outputs through voice or visual commands. These interventions would not only increase the coverage of PD-ViCo’s impact but also make farming a more inclusive, digitally empowered community.

Conclusion

In this paper, we tackled the pressing problem of automating brinjal disease detection in South Asia by introducing PD-ViCo, an explainable and lightweight vision transformer model. By combining the key strengths of SimpleViT, patch dropout regularization, and CoCa-style aggregation, we formulated a model that strikes a wise balance between accurate classification performance and computation efficiency, a key necessity for realistic agricultural scenarios. Trained on a data set of 1,823 field-captured brinjal images gathered from Bogura and Dhaka, PD-ViCo is highly resilient. The model has surpassed two of the most well-known transformer models including ViT and Swin Transformer in all test cases, be it class imbalance or limited data settings. The model PD-ViCo offers a maximum accuracy of 99.12% and an F1-score of 97.76%. Not just is the model accurate, but it is also resilient and scalable. Why PD-ViCo is usable for farm applications is that it can be explained. By using Grad-CAM and Grad-CAM + +, we could see where the model was “focusing” to make its decisions on infected areas in sick samples and not on irrelevant areas in well samples. This adds an additional layer of trust and explainability that will be critical to farmers’ and field professionals’ adoption. Through the release of an open-source dataset, a novel transformer architecture, and an end-to-end open diagnostic pipeline, this work provides the foundation for real-world integration into precision agriculture pipelines. PD-ViCo can in the future be deployed onto mobile phones or drones (UAVs) for cheap and large-scale disease surveillance, especially in resource-constrained farming communities. Given its accuracy, speed, and explainability, PD-ViCo is poised to support more intelligent, quicker, and more trustworthy crop disease management.
