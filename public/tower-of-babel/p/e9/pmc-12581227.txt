Time to surgery in patients with colorectal liver metastases
Authors: Lucas Alexander Knøfler, Jeanett Klubien, Diana Elena Renteria Ramirez, Sophie Kollbeck, Peter Nørgaard Larsen, Paul Suno Krohn, Ida Ravnsbaek Johannsen, Claus Wilki Fristrup, Mogens Tornby Stender, Susanne Dam Nielsen, Hans-Christian Pommergaard

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSurgical resection and locoregional therapies are crucial for the curative treatment of colorectal liver metastases (CRLM). We also sought to identify clinical predictors of time to surgery.MethodsThis nationwide study included patients with CRLM between March 2013 and December 2023 in Denmark. Background

Metastatic liver disease is common in patients with colorectal cancer and is associated with a detrimental effect on prognosis [1]. Surgical resection and locoregional therapies are central in the curative treatment of colorectal liver metastases (CRLM). The impact of clinical factors on outcomes following surgery has been extensively studied [2, 3], and risk stratification models have been developed [4]. However, the prognostic impact of waiting time to surgery when metastases are in situ is unclear.

Previous studies on hepatocellular carcinoma, cholangiocarcinoma, and pancreatic cancer suggest that the waiting time before surgery does not adversely affect long-term survival. Instead, they emphasize the benefits of comprehensive preoperative evaluation and optimization of the patient [5–9]. Research specific to CRLM is limited. To date, only one study has explored the impact of time to surgery on survival in this population. However, that study measured time to surgery in months, potentially confounding the effect of time to surgery with the natural progression of disease [10]. This justifies investigations into how shorter time scales might influence clinical outcomes. Our study sought to address this aspect of patient management with insights drawn from a large, national, multicenter registry.

Fast-track cancer pathways have been widely adopted in many countries to reduce delays from initial cancer suspicion to definitive treatment. In Denmark, the “Kræftpakkeforløb” model mandates that diagnostic workup begins within 14 calendar days of referral, and that treatment be initiated within 28 days of the referral for treatment [11].

This study aimed to investigate how the time between referral for surgical treatment and surgical intervention influenced survival and mortality risk in patients with CRLM. In addition, we investigated associations between clinical preoperative information and the time elapsed between referral for surgical treatment and surgical intervention.

Methods

Study design and settings

Between March 2013 and December 2023, patients undergoing surgery for CRLM were identified from the Danish Liver Cancer Group (DLGCD) registry. Patients included in the registry were referred and treated at one of Denmark’s four hepatobiliary centers: Aalborg University Hospital, Aarhus University Hospital, Copenhagen University Hospital, and Odense University Hospital. This comprehensive nationwide registry, maintained by the Danish Clinical Quality Program – National Clinical Registries, includes patients referred for multidisciplinary team (MDT) conferences for liver malignancies, including metastases.

At the MDT conferences, attended by surgeons, radiologists, interventional radiologists, oncologists, and hepatologists, the treatment options are discussed based on the patient history, diagnostic imaging, histology, and blood tests. Following the conference, patients are seen by a surgeon for an operability assessment.

The study adhered to the STrengthening the Reporting of OBservational studies in Epidemiology guidelines [12]. Approval to conduct the study was granted by the Danish Data Protection Agency (P-2022-628), and according to Danish legislation, retrieval of informed consent was not required for this study.

Participants

A total of 4485 MDT decisions recommending liver-directed surgery for CRLM were identified in the DLGCD registry. Of these, 1486 decisions related to repeat liver surgeries were excluded, leaving 2999 patients with a planned first liver-directed surgery. Further exclusions included ethanol ablation (n = 2), two-stage resections (n = 79), exploratory laparotomy (n = 179), and other unspecified procedures (n = 7). The final study population comprised 2732 patients who underwent resection and/or ablation as their first liver-directed surgery for CRLM. The derivation of the study population is illustrated in Additional file 1.

Patients were eligible for inclusion if they were aged 18 years or older and underwent either hepatic resection, ablation, or a combination of resection and ablation for as the first liver-directed surgery for CRLM. Patients receiving two-stage hepatectomy were excluded, as the scheduled addition of time to surgery between stages rendered them incomparable to the rest of the study population. Patients receiving ethanol ablation or exploratory laparotomy was excluded. The final study population includes patients with both colon and rectal primary tumors, as well as synchronous and metachronous liver metastases. However, the present registry query does not allow for stratification by these features.

Surgical approach and systemic therapy

Hepatic resections and combination therapies were performed using open, laparoscopic, or robotic approaches, whereas ablation therapies were delivered percutaneously, laparoscopically, or via open surgery using either radiofrequency or microwave modalities. In Denmark during the study period (2013–2023), patients with upfront resectable CRLM were typically managed with primary liver-directed treatment (resection and/or ablation), with systemic therapy tailored to relapse risk.

Upfront surgery was generally favored for low-risk cases, while preoperative oxaliplatin-based doublets (FOLFOX or CAPOX) were considered for higher-risk presentations, and postoperative fluoropyrimidine was offered if no neoadjuvant therapy had been administered. For potentially resectable disease, systemic therapy was selected to maximize tumor shrinkage and was guided by primary tumor sidedness and mutational profile. Standard regimens included FOLFOX or FOLFIRI combined with anti-EGFR or FOLFOX/CAPOX or FOLFIRI (± anti-VEGF), with FOLFOXIRI reserved for fit patients [13].

Definitions

Mortality was defined as death from any cause. Survival time was defined as the time between surgery and time of death or last follow-up in December 2023, whichever came first. Time to surgery was defined as the number of calendar days between the referral for the MDT leading to surgical treatment and the surgery date. The World Health Organization (WHO) performance status (PS) was a preoperative evaluation categorized as PS0, PS1, or ≥ PS2. The size of the largest tumor was described as the diameter of the largest hepatic metastasis measured on its longest axis using preoperative imaging and categorized as below 3 cm–3 cm and above. The number of tumors was defined as the total number of detectable hepatic metastases on preoperative imaging and categorized as one, two, or three or more. Preoperative chemotherapy was defined as any history of referral for chemotherapy before the surgery date, recorded as a dichotomous variable (yes/no). Finally, preoperative MDTs were defined as the number of registered conferences held concerning the present metastatic disease. These were categorized as either a single MDT conference or more. If surgery was postponed, time to surgery includes the waiting time until the surgery subsequently occurred.

Statistical methods

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.0 and RStudio. Statistical significance was defined as a p-value < 0.05. Descriptive statistics are presented as medians and interquartile ranges for continuous variables, and frequencies with percentages for categorical variables.

Missing data were handled with Multivariate Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE). The predictive mean matching method was applied for 25 iterations and 45 imputations. The MICE model included the hospital where the surgery was performed and all available patient and disease characteristics as predictors. Imputation was performed for the following variables: WHO performance status, size of largest tumor, number of tumors, and number of preoperative MDTs. Pooled estimates for the linear regression and Cox proportional hazards models, both before and after propensity score matching, were derived using the MICE model.

Propensity scores were estimated for all patients using a multivariable logistic regression model, with median time to surgery (28 days) as the dichotomous dependent variable. Patients were matched in a 1:1 ratio using the nearest neighbor algorithm with a caliper width of 0.05. A Love plot was generated to assess covariate balance. Propensity scores and all regression estimates from the propensity-matched cohort were based on the pooling of the MICE model.

A linear regression model was developed to estimate the regression coefficients (β) of time to surgery, with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI). Clinically relevant independent factors were assessed in univariable models and included in the adjusted multivariable model if statistical significance was detected. The linear relationship between predictors and time to surgery was assessed by plotting residuals against fitted values. No violation of the linearity assumption was detected. Multicollinearity was evaluated using the variance inflation factor. All predictors included in the adjusted model had inflation factors less than 1.1.

A Cox proportional hazards model was developed to estimate the mortality risk, reported as hazard ratios (HR) with corresponding 95% CI. Clinically relevant independent factors were assessed in univariable analyses and included in the adjusted multivariable model if statistical significance was detected. Preoperative chemotherapy was included in the adjusted model despite not reaching statistical significance due to its clinical relevance and narrow confidence interval in unadjusted analysis. The proportionality assumption governing the relative hazards was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals. Violations of the proportional hazards were detected for age and sex, and they were thus excluded from the model.

The Kaplan-Meier estimator was used to report cumulative survival probabilities and corresponding 95% CIs, stratified by time to surgery and the number of preoperative MDTs. A log-rank test was performed to compare observed events between groups.

Results

Study population

In total, 2999 eligible patients were identified with a referral for first liver-directed surgery in the DLGCD registry. Among them, 2732 patients underwent surgical resection, ablation, or a combination of both and were thus included in the analyses. Most patients were male (63%), the median age was 69 years, and 32% had a WHO performance status of 0. The majority were evaluated at one MDT conference before the surgery (74%), and 84% had not been referred for preoperative chemotherapy. Among the included patients, 1458 (53%) underwent single-stage hepatic resection, 747 (24%) received ablation therapy, and 527 (19%) received a combination of resection and ablation. The median time between referral for surgical treatment and surgery was 28 days, while the median time between the final MDT and surgery was 22 days. Survival analyses

At the end of the study period, 1494 patients (55%) were deceased. In the univariable Cox regression analysis, time to surgery was associated with a 3% increase in mortality risk per week (HR 1.03, 95% CI 1.01–1.05; P = 0.015). However, when adjusting for baseline patient, tumor, and treatment characteristics in the multivariable model, the confidence interval lacked certainty about the true effect size (HR 1.02, 95% CI 0.99–1.05; P = 0.256). The lack of association persisted after propensity-score matching the adjusted analysis (HR 1.02, 95% CI 0.98–1.07; P = 0.342). No difference in the cumulative survival probability was observed for patients with a time to surgery of less than 28 days, compared to those with a time to surgery of 28 days or more (Fig. 2a). Five years after surgery, survival probabilities were 45% (95% CI 42–48%) for those with less than 28 days of waiting time and 42% (95% CI 39–45%) for those with 28 days or more, respectively.

Kaplan-Meier survival estimators. The figures illustrate the 7-year cumulative survival probability following surgical treatment for CRLM, stratified by time to surgery less than or above 28 days (a), and number of multidisciplinary team conferences (b)

Univariable analysis showed that the need for more than one preoperative MDT conference was associated with a 16% increase in mortality risk (HR 1.16, 95% CI 1.04–1.30; P = 0.010). However, the adjusted analysis (HR 1.14, 95% CI 0.99–1.31; P = 0.071) and analysis after propensity-score matching (HR 1.16, 95% CI 0.93–1.43; P = 0.187) showed no significant effects. Conversely, a difference in cumulative survival was observed between patients evaluated at one MDT conference, and those evaluated at more than one (Fig. 2b). Five years after surgery, survival probabilities were 45% (95% CI 42–47%) for the former and 38% (95% CI 35–43%) for the latter, respectively.

Preoperative chemotherapy was not associated with mortality in either the univariable (HR 1.03, 95% CI 0.89–1.18; P = 0.710), the multivariable model (HR 0.96, 95% CI 0.81–1.13; P = 0.595), or the propensity-matched adjusted model (HR 0.94, 95% CI 0.77–1.15; P = 0.538). Predictors of time to surgery

Both univariable and multivariable linear regression analyses indicated that ablation treatment was associated with a shorter time to surgery than hepatic resection. Specifically, ablation treatment was associated with a 3.6-day reduction in time to surgery in the multivariable model (β −3.60, 95% CI −4.86, −2.34; P < 0.001). Similarly, preoperative chemotherapy was associated with a 3.5-day reduction in the time to surgery in the univariable analysis (β −3.50, 95% CI −5.19, −1.81; P < 0.001). However, this association was not significant in the multivariable analysis (β −1.23, 95% CI −2.73, 0.27; P = 0.108). Conversely, >1 MDT conferences was associated with 18-day longer time to surgery, adjusted for treatment type and preoperative chemotherapy (β 18.2, 95% CI 16.77, 19.54; P < 0.001). Discussion

In this nationwide multicenter study, we investigated the relationship between time to surgery and long-term survival outcomes for patients with CRLM. Our analyses revealed no significant association between time to surgery and mortality risk per week of waiting. Although unadjusted analyses suggested a slight increase in mortality risk with a longer time to surgery, this association disappeared after adjusting for patient, tumor, and treatment characteristics, as well as in propensity-matched models. Additionally, no difference in long-term survival was observed between patients undergoing surgery within 28 days and those waiting longer. A similar relationship was noted for patients assessed at multiple preoperative MDT conferences. While unadjusted analysis indicated a 16% increase in mortality risk for this group, adjusted analyses found no significant association.

The median time from referral to surgery was 28 days, reflecting the nationwide implementation of fast-track cancer pathways mandating initiation of treatment within this timeframe [11]. Delays beyond 28 days likely represent circumstances where fast-track protocols could not be satisfied, though the reasons for these delays could not be explored in this study. As a result, a concentration of waiting time observations is seen at 28 days. Ideally, this research question is answered in a population with a broader distribution of waiting times.

Time to surgery does not appear to correlate with adverse survival outcomes in patients with primary gastrointestinal cancers, including hepatocellular carcinoma, cholangiocarcinoma, and pancreatic cancer. However, its prognostic impact on CRLM is underexplored [5–9]. To our knowledge, only one study has investigated this relationship in CRLM, suggesting that a surgical delay longer than six months is associated with increased mortality, whereas a 3–6-month delay is not. Although we cannot rule out an association, their findings align with our observations, as we found no contradictory evidence. Still, direct comparison is challenging because no patients in our cohort waited beyond 3.3 months, and survival was measured from the time of surgery [10].

The observed five-year survival rates align with existing literature on the study population [14]. We found cumulative survival rates of 45% for patients undergoing surgery within 28 days and 42% for those waiting longer. Across the 7-year follow-up, no significant difference was identified between these groups. While these crude estimates do not account for confounding factors or propensity scores, they reinforce the notion that time to surgery beyond 28 days does not adversely affect survival.

Factors influencing time to surgery are underexplored, but prior research suggests that patient and disease characteristics may contribute to variations in waiting times [15]. In our adjusted analyses, attending more than one MDT conference was associated with 18 days longer time to surgery. This likely reflects additional diagnostic imaging, estimation of the future liver remnant, or preoperative optimization of comorbidities. The reduced long-term survival observed in these patients may be attributed to more advanced disease or higher comorbidity burdens. Conversely, prior referral for chemotherapy and ablation therapy was associated with a shorter time to surgery, potentially due to less extensive diagnostic requirements. In our cohort, patients undergoing percutaneous ablation were managed by interventional radiologists, whose schedules are less affected by surgeries being postponed, thereby reducing the likelihood of cancellations [16, 17].

This study’s strengths include the use of a large, nationwide, multicenter registry, minimizing selection bias and ensuring representation of a heterogeneous patient population. Comprehensive follow-up data over a decade allowed us to capture temporal trends in mortality. While a randomized controlled trial would provide the most robust evidence, ethical concerns make such designs unrealistic.

However, the study had limitations. The registry lacked detailed data on preoperative chemotherapy, providing only binary indicators of prior referral. Consequently, this limitation assumes efficient MDT decision-making but may overlook nuances in treatment administration. Additionally, data on primary colorectal tumor characteristics, such as sidedness and temporal classification (synchronous versus metachronous), were unavailable despite their prognostic significance [18]. Further, we were unable to characterize the patient’s metastatic disease in detail. As such anatomical location of liver lesions could not be specified. Nevertheless, key patient and disease characteristics, including WHO performance status, tumor size, and number of tumors, were included. To address missing data, we employed multiple imputation by chained equations, a method shown to provide accurate and unbiased estimates compared to simpler methods, even with missing data rates as high as 50% [19].

Conclusions

In conclusion, this nationwide multicenter study found no evidence that time to surgery adversely affects long-term survival or mortality risk. Prolonged time to surgery associated with multiple MDT conferences likely reflects preoperative patient optimization. Future research should investigate the impact of surgical timing in populations not governed by fast-track legislations to better understand these relationships.
