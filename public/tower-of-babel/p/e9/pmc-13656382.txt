Heterogeneity characterization and therapeutic marker discovery of endometrial carcinoma using GeoMXT™ digital spatial profiling
Authors: Shanliang Zhong, Jianyao Liu, Hanzi Xu, Juan Lv, Huixin Li, Huanhuan Hu, Shuyue Xiao, Xinyi Xie, Yue Li, Yinan Wu, Wangfei Wu, Zhen Gong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to explore the intra- and inter-tumoral heterogeneity, gene expression profiles, tumor microenvironment (TME) alterations and the potential of therapeutic marker discovery in endometrial cancer (EC).MethodsDigital spatial profiling (DSP) technology was used to quantify transcript abundance in distinct spatial regions of EC tissues from 4 patients. The differences among the regions were explored using bioinformatics analysis, and the mechanisms contributing to these differences were preliminarily explored using in vitro experiments.ResultsWe focused on 23 regions of interest (ROI), including 8 from normal cells, 10 from tumor cells (TC), 1 from stroma adjacent to normal cells and 4 from regions that encompassed the former three types of cells. Gene set variation analysis with 8 gene sets associated with EC revealed significant heterogeneity both within and between tumors. We estimated the abundance of immune cells in each region and found that significant disparities were observed in both the abundance and proportional representation of immune cells across all 23 ROIs. ATF5, identified as a key transcription factor, and its functionally linked target genes STMN1, CENPF, and TROAP were significantly overexpressed in TC, which was confirmed by RT-qPCR and external database analyses. Correlation analysis combined with functional assays demonstrated that ATF5 directly activates STMN1 transcription by binding to its promoter. ATF5 or STMN1 knockdown induced G2/M arrest and suppressed proliferation. Importantly, STMN1 re-expression rescued the proliferative and clonogenic defects caused by ATF5 knockdown.ConclusionOur exploratory study provides preliminary evidence of significant intra- and inter-tumoral heterogeneity in the TME and signaling pathways in EC using DSP technology. Genes such as ATF5, STMN1, CENPF, and TROAP, particularly the ATF5-STMN1 axis as a potential oncogenic pathway, may contribute to this heterogeneity and warrant further investigation as potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets in larger validation cohorts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12967-026-08606-z.

Introduction

Endometrial cancer (EC) ranks as the sixth most common malignancy among women globally, with 420,242 new cases and 97,704 deaths reported in 2022, posing a significant threat to women’s health [1]. The primary treatment for EC is surgery, followed by additional therapies tailored to the patient’s clinical and histopathological characteristics. While early-stage EC patients generally have a favorable prognosis following standard treatment, the 5-year survival rate for advanced-stage patients remains below 20% [2]. Consequently, identifying potential molecular targets for EC diagnosis and treatment is critically clinical importance.

EC is classified (World Health Organization system) histologically into specific subgroups, including endometrioid, plasmacytoid, clear cell, uterine carcinosarcoma, and other rare types. The Bokhman classification divides EC further into type I and type II [3]. Type I, which is estrogen-dependent, is predominantly represented by endometrioid endometrial carcinoma (EEC). Type II, typically nonestrogen-dependent and more aggressive, includes serous carcinoma and clear cell carcinoma as its main subtypes [4]. However, traditional staging and histological classification systems suffer from low reproducibility and microscopic overlap between types, leading to varying prognoses even among patients with the same subtype [5].

In 2013, the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) proposed a molecular classification of EC based on multi-omics analysis, identifying four molecular subtypes: POLE hypermutations, microsatellite instability hypermutations, low copy number, and high copy number [6]. These subtypes exhibit varying tumor mutational burdens (TMB), which influence their responsiveness to immunotherapy. The significant heterogeneity observed in EC, both histopathologically and molecularly, complicates diagnosis, treatment decisions, and prognosis, contributing to therapeutic failure. Therefore, investigating EC heterogeneity based on clinical features and gene expression profiles holds substantial clinical value. A comprehensive understanding of inner and intra-tumor heterogeneity is essential for accurately assessing clinical efficacy and developing novel targets for immunotherapy.

In this study, we employed digital spatial profiling (DSP) to explore gene expression alterations within and between EC tumors. Using DSP, we analyzed formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissues from four EC patients, focusing on regions of interest (ROI) to identify region-specific differences among normal cells (NC), tumor cells (TC), and stroma adjacent to normal cells (NS). This approach aims to provide insights into the spatial heterogeneity of EC and its implications for diagnosis and treatment.

Methods and materials

Tissue preparation

All tissue samples utilized in the present study (A total of 46 tissue samples, comprising 26 from EC patients and 20 from healthy controls) were collected from Women’s Hospital and/or the Affiliated Cancer Hospital of Nanjing Medical University in 2020–2022. The EC tissues were obtained from EC patients who underwent hysterectomy, while the corresponding healthy controls (HC) tissues were collected from patients who underwent hysteroscopy or hysterectomy for benign lesions. All participants enrolled in this study were treatment-naïve at diagnosis, and having received no prior radiation therapy, chemotherapy, or other anticancer treatments before surgical intervention. All pathological diagnoses were independently confirmed by two board-certified experienced pathologists. FFPE slide-mounted tissue was then subjected to a standard antigen retrieval protocol, followed by incubation with fluorescent imaging reagents (specific antibodies or biological stains and dyes) and a cocktail of RNA probe conjugated to PC-oligos.

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the ethics committee of Women’s Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (2020NFKSL-079) and all patients were informed before surgery and signed informed consent.

DSP design

Four specimens were randomly selected from the 26 EC tissues for DSP (Supplementary Table 1). DSP scans a × 20 image of up to four-channel fluorescence. Fluorescent reagents differentiate tissue structures, with antibodies for PanCK and CD45 identifying epithelium and immune cells. Regions of Interest (ROI) are marked based on fluorescence for profiling. Oligonucleotides from these ROI are released upon localized exposure to UV light. Photocleaved oligonucleotides are collected through a microcapillary tube, and deposited into a microtiter plate for subsequent quantitation. Repeat localized UV exposure and PC-oligo collection are repeated for each ROI, with extensive washing between each cycle. Spatially resolved oligonucleotides are then pooled, purified, and quantified by NGS. Reads are processed into digital counts and mapped back to each ROI to generate a transcript activity map within the tissue architecture.

Regions of interest selection strategy

Pathologists selected ROI based on H&E morphology and immunofluorescence markers (PanCK, α-SMA and CD45). The strategy sampled morphological variation within individual tissue sections. Selected areas included tumor cell regions, normal cell regions, stroma adjacent to normal cells, and mixed regions. The specific number of Regions of Interest per patient corresponded to the available tissue area meeting these morphological criteria.

Data quality control and gene expression quantification

The GeoMx NGS Pipeline & convert software converted FASTQ files to DCC files, performed alignment and quantified counts. Technical signal QC assesses the quality of sequencing of each ROI. The raw read threshold set the minimum raw reads allowed. The percent aligned reads specified the minimum percentage of raw reads aligned to target sequences. Sequencing saturation set the minimum allowed saturation percentage, calculated as 100% minus (deduplicated reads divided by aligned reads). A 100% sequencing saturation indicated a representative sample, while values below 50% might require resequencing. The Negative probe count geomean determined the technical noise level and flagged ROIs with signals below it, calculated separately for each probe kit. Probes were filtered using the Grubbs outlier test for each target in every ROI. If a probe was an outlier in ≥ 20% of segments, it was removed globally. ROIs were also filtered based on data and background quality control. Normalization balanced results across segments using specific probe counts. Q3 normalization, recommended for NGS data above the LOQ, used the top 25% of expressers for normalization across ROI, ensuring robustness to individual gene expression changes and facilitating ROI comparisons.

Quantify the expression of morphologically marker antibody-related genes

Fluorescently labeled antibodies can distinguish between different tissue structures in sections. Since spatial proteomic data analysis was not performed here, a gene panel was ensembled to estimate the level of tagged protein in defined regions: tumor epithelial PanCK (KRT3, KRT10, KRT16, KRT5, KRT8, KRT19, KRT2, KRT14, KRT1, KRT6A, KRT6B) [7], immune cells CD45 (PTPRC) [8] and normal cells SMA (ACTA2) [9], in order to confirm that DSP technology can reliably distinguish tumor epithelial cells, immune cells and normal cells, and to quantify the expression of relevant gene sets in each of the different characteristic ROIs. Box plots were generated using ggpubr package, and the Wilcoxon rank sum test was applied to analyze differences in gene expression between groups (P < 0.05 was statistically significant).

Signaling pathway activity and gene expression heatmap

GSVA is an unsupervised gene set enrichment analysis method that translates gene-level changes into pathway-level changes to determine the biological function of a gene set [10]. The gene sets used in this analysis were summarized from the previous study [11], mainly including Estrogen signaling, Transcription Regulation, Chromatin Remodeling, DNA Damage Response, RAS/RAF Pathway, PI3K Pathway, WNT Signaling and CA125, and the specific gene list is shown in Supplementary Table 2. All pathway activity scores of the above gene sets were calculated using the GSVA algorithm to evaluate potential biological changes among different patients and different ROI. According to the grouping information, the clustering heatmap is displayed for all the groups, which can reflect the similarity of expression between ROI samples. The data were normalized by Z-score. By default, the minimum variance method ward. D2 clustering method is used to construct the distance matrix.

Estimation of cell type by RNA transcripts

SpatialDecon is an algorithmic approach for the quantification of cell populations delineated by a spatial gene expression study region [12]. It incorporates a number of innovative techniques for the deconvolution of gene expression. SpatialDecon package was used to characterize the immune cell type composition of each ROI based on the gene transcript levels. The expression data obtained from DSP were reverse deconvoluted using the SpatialDecon package in order to estimate the abundance and proportions of 18 cell types in 23 ROI. Heatmaps as well as stacked plots were used to show cell abundance in different ROI.

Pathway enrichment & transcription factor analysis

The ROIs were divided into 3 groups regardless of CD45 labeling: normal cell regions (NC), tumor cell regions (TC), and normal cell-adjacent stromal regions (NS). To compare the pathway activities among the 3 groups, GSVA were performed with Hallmark50pathways gene set [13], which was downloaded from the MSigDB database [14]. Transcription factor analysis was conducted using the SCENIC [15] package to identify the potential transcription factors contributing to the difference of the 3 groups.

Differentially expressed genes

Differential expression analysis was performed using a linear mixed-effects model framework. Q3-normalized transcript counts were log2-transformed. The duplicateCorrelation function within the limma package was utilized to account for the nested data structure. Patient ID was incorporated as a random effect to adjust for patient-level clustering and pseudoreplication across multiple regions of interest. Variance distribution and sample clustering by patient and tissue compartment were assessed using a Multidimensional Scaling plot (Supplementary Fig. 1). Volcano plots were generated to visualize differentially expressed genes using the ggplot2 package. A heatmap was drawn to display the differentially expressed genes across different ROI groups. RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) files of EC patients (TCGA-UCEC) were downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database [16] obtaining expression profiling files for tumors and normal endometrial tissues. The DESeq2 package was used to identify differentially expressed genes from the TCGA datasets based on the criteria of |fold change| >2 and adjusted P-value < 0.05 (BH method). (tumor tissue vs. normal tissue).

Internal cohort validation through real-time quantitative PCR

To elucidate the profiles for the above candidate differentially expressed genes (DEGs), 22 EC samples and 20 age matched HC samples were used for internal cohort validation. The expression levels of the candidate genes were detected as reported previously [17], using the primers listed in Supplementary Table 3.

External database validation

Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis version 2 (GEPIA2) [18, 19], the UALCAN database [20, 21] and the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database [22, 23] were used to further assess the expression levels of candidate genes as well as their associations with EC survival.

Correlation analysis

Using RNA-seq data from the uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma (UCEC) cohort of TCGA, we evaluated the co-expression of ATF5 with STMN1, CENPF, and TROAP. Transcript-per-million (TPM) counts were log2-transformed after adding a pseudo-count of 1 [log2(TPM + 1)] to approximate a normal distribution and minimize technical noise. Pearson correlation coefficients were then computed to quantify the linear relationships between ATF5 and each of the three genes. Genes with a mean log2(TPM + 1) expression below 1.0 were excluded to avoid artifacts from low-abundance transcripts. Results were visualized with scatter plots.

Cell culture

The human EC cell line Ishikawa, HEC-1 A and RL95-2 was purchased from the Cell Culture Collection of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). HEC-1 A cells were cultured in 5 A medium. Ishikawa and RL95-2 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM, high glucose; KeyGEN BioTECH) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 80 U/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin. The cells were cultured in a cell incubator with 5% CO2 and 37℃ saturated humidity. The cell lines were authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling.

Cell transfection

The siRNAs targeting ATF5, STMN1, CENPF, and TROAP, as well as the negative controls (NC) were procured from Invitrogen (Shanghai, China). The cells were transfected with the siRNAs or NC using a Nepa21 pulse generator (Nepa Gene, Chiba, Japan) as described previously [24]. The sequences of the siRNAs are listed in Supplementary Table 4.

Colony formation assay

Cells were cultured for one to two weeks until macroscopic clones formed. The medium was aspirated, and each well was washed with PBS. Cells were then fixed in the plate using paraformaldehyde solution, washed again with PBS and stained with 0.1% crystal violet. After drying at room temperature, images were captured for cell counting and analysis.

Wound healing assay

The transfected monolayer was scratched with a pipette tip and the floating cells were removed with PBS. The cells were cultured in serum-free medium, and images were captured at 12-hour intervals. Cell migration was evaluated by analyzing the cells migrated into the wounded region.

Western blotting

Transfected EC cells were lysed on ice in RIPA buffer, proteins separated by 4–20% SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes, blocked with 5% skim milk, and incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-ATF5, -STMN1, and -GAPDH (Proteintech, Wuhan, China). Membranes were probed with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody 2 h and visualized by ECL chemiluminescent regents. Densitometric analysis was performed using ImageJ 2.16.0.

Cell counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

Cell viability was assessed with the CCK-8 assay. 24 h after transfection, cells were plated in 96-well plates at 3 × 10³ cells/well. CCK-8 reagent (10 µL) was added at 24, 48, 72 and 96 h, the plates were incubated for 2 h at 37 °C, and absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader.

Cell cycle analysis by flow cytometry

Cells were harvested 48 h post-transfection, fixed in 70% ethanol, and treated with RNase A. Cellular DNA was stained with propidium iodide (PI). The cell cycle distribution (G0/G1, S, and G2/M phases) was analyzed using a flow cytometer, and the data were processed with ModfitLT 5.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

The wild-type STMN1 promoter sequence was cloned into the pGL3-Basic vector, and a mutant reporter gene vectors with point mutations at the predicted binding sites was generated. Cells were co-transfected with either the wild-type or mutant reporter plasmid, along with an ATF5 overexpression plasmid or empty vector control, and the pRL-TK Renilla luciferase vector for normalization. Luciferase activity was measured 48 h after transfection using a dual-luciferase assay system.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using R software (version 4.2.2). All in vitro functional tests were performed at least 3 times independently. Data are shown as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The Mann-Whitney test was applied to assess real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) data and Student’s t-test was applied to evaluate other data. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The workflow of this study is shown in Fig. 1. The workflow consisted of three main phases: spatial transcriptomic profiling, computational analysis, and mechanistic validation. First, digital spatial profiling (DSP) was applied to 23 distinct regions of interest across four endometrial cancer patients to acquire localized transcript abundance. Second, bioinformatics approaches, including gene set variation analysis and immune cell deconvolution, were utilized to characterize spatial heterogeneity and identify candidate regulators, specifically the transcription factor ATF5 and its targets (STMN1, CENPF, and TROAP). Finally, the identified ATF5-STMN1 axis was validated through external database analysis, RT-qPCR, and in vitro functional experiments comprising gene knockdown, cell cycle analysis, promoter binding assays, and transcriptional rescue experiments.

DSP identifies inter-individual differences in EC

Patients with EC exhibit a number of shared characteristics, including Estrogen signaling, Transcription Regulation, Chromatin Remodeling, DNA Damage Response, RAS/RAF Pathway, PI3K Pathway, WNT Signaling and CA125 [25]. But for individual patients, even with the same type of tumor, they actually may exhibit distinct signaling pathway activities, which in turn can influence both treatment selection and prognosis assessment through potential biomarkers. By calculating the activity scores of these pathways as previously described [10], we observed significant heterogeneity in the activity of the same signaling pathway among different patients. For instance, estrogen pathway activity scores varied markedly among four patients, with patient 4 showing significantly lower activity compared to the others (Fig. 3A-B). Additionally, among patients with the same histological type, transcriptome gene expression also differed (Fig. 3C-D). The boxplot of the proportion of relevant DEGs to total analyzed genes is shown in Supplementary Fig. 2A-B. Collectively, substantial inter-individual differences were observed among EC patients.

DSP identifies intra-tumoral heterogeneity in EC

A distinctive feature of the DSP technology lies in its capacity to precisely quantify transcripts within defined regions of a tumor, encompassing selected foci in tumor-rich areas, normal cells, and stromal components. To evaluate intra-tumoral heterogeneity, we conducted a comparative analysis of transcript measurements across all ROIs obtained from each patient. Our findings revealed significant inconsistencies in signaling pathway activity among different ROIs within the same patient. Furthermore, the expression levels of genes associated with these pathways also demonstrated notable heterogeneity across different ROIs in the same individual. Specifically, the analysis of ROIs in Patient 1 indicated a lack of consistency in both pathway activity scores and gene expression levels (Fig. 4A). Analysis of signature scores from tumor ROIs using boxplots identified significant outliers in tumor ROI scores for the same patient (Fig. 4B), further underscoring the variability among different ROIs within the same tumor. Differential expression analysis of ROI transcripts from the same patient revealed that two TC ROIs (Fig. 4C) as well as ROI transcripts from CD45 + TCs and TCs (Fig. 4D) exhibited distinct expression patterns at the individual gene level. Key immune-related genes, such as IL1B, are highlighted in the figure for reference. The boxplot of the proportion of relevant DEGs to total analyzed genes is shown in Supplementary Fig. 2C-D.

Therapeutic/predictive marker discovery

To investigate pathway enrichment across distinct cellular compartments, we conducted GSVA enrichment analysis on four categories: NC, NS, TC, and Mixed (Fig. 6A). Additionally, a separate GSVA analysis was performed specifically on TC and NC populations (Fig. 6B). The analysis revealed distinct pathway enrichment patterns between tumor and normal cells. Tumor cells exhibited significant enrichment in pathways related to oxidative phosphorylation, protein secretion, MYC targets v1, P53 pathway, mTORC1 signaling, DNA repair, Adipogenesis, Glycolysis, Peroxisome function, Cholesterol homeostasis, and G2M checkpoint. In contrast, normal cells demonstrated enrichment in pathways such as KRAS signaling, spermatogenesis, and Bile acid metabolism.

To further elucidate transcriptional regulation, we performed SCENIC analysis as a preliminary exploratory screening to identify transcription factors regulating gene expression in four categories (Fig. 6C). RNA expression profiles from 10 tumor cell ROIs and 7 normal cell ROIs were acquired using the GeoMx DSP system. Differential expression analysis of these profiles, intersected with genes from the 11 tumor-enriched pathways, initially identified 77 upregulated genes.

To validate these findings, we analyzed 545 EC tissues and 35 HC tissues from the TCGA database, identifying 4593 differentially expressed genes, including 2659 upregulated genes. These upregulated genes were intersected with the 77 upregulated genes identified from the above GeoMx DSP analysis. The resulting gene set was further refined by intersecting with genes regulated by the upregulated transcription factors identified through SCENIC analysis. The final candidate therapeutic & predictive markers are presented in Supplementary Table 5.

Among the identified candidates, ATF5 emerged as a transcription factor exhibiting upregulated expression in tumor cells. Interestingly, three of its downstream target genes—STMN1, CENPF, and TROAP—were also significantly upregulated in tumor cells. Furthermore, all four genes were functionally linked to the “G2M checkpoint” pathway, which plays a critical role in regulating cell proliferation. Given their consistent involvement in this pathway and their potential relevance to tumorigenesis, we selected these four genes for further investigation through in vitro functional experiments.

Marker confirmation through internal cohort

To confirm the above differentially expressed targets, we included an internal cohort of samples (22 EC vs. 20 HC) collected during the same period and from the same region as those used in the GeoMx DSP system for preliminary validation. RT-qPCR analysis demonstrated that ATF5, STMN1, TROAP, and CENPF were significantly upregulated in human EC tissues (Fig. 7), aligning with the findings from our GeoMx DSP analysis. These results further corroborate the reliability of our initial observations.

Marker confirmation through external databases

Given the inherent heterogeneity of EC and the limited sample size (only 4 EC patients included) in this GeoMx DSP study, we further validated the identified candidates using external databases to ensure the robustness of our findings. Three publicly available databases: GEPIA2, UALCAN, and HPA database were utilized for independent validation randomly.

In GEPIA2, the expression levels of ATF5, STMN1, TROAP, and CENPF were significantly upregulated in the EC cohort compared to normal tissues (Supplementary Fig. 3A). Similarly, analysis in the UALCAN database revealed that patients with high ATF5 expression exhibited a significantly poorer prognosis compared to those with low expression (p = 0.0019) (Supplementary Fig. 3B). Consistent with these findings, the HPA database also confirmed that high expression of ATF5, TROAP and CENPF was associated with worse prognosis (Supplementary Fig. 3C). These external validation results aligned closely with both our GeoMx DSP analysis and the internal cohort validation, underscoring the reliability of our findings again. Collectively, these results demonstrate the feasibility of identifying potential biomarkers using the GeoMx DSP system, even with a very limited sample size, and highlight the clinical relevance of ATF5, STMN1, TROAP, and CENPF in EC.

Primary functional validation of four markers through cell experimental

To investigate the functional roles and further validate the contributions of ATF5, STMN1, CENPF, and TROAP in EC biological processes, we individually knocked down these four genes in Ishikawa cells. Silencing these genes significantly reduced EC cell proliferation. Knockdown of these targets also impaired EC cell migration (Fig. 8A and B). These genes promoted EC cell proliferation and migration, highlighting their roles in EC pathogenesis and therapeutic potential.

TCGA-UCEC dataset analysis revealed co-expression among ATF5, STMN1, CENPF, and TROAP. Significant correlations were observed between ATF5 and STMN1 (r = 0.676, P = 2.97 × 10⁻⁷⁵), CENPF (r = 0.231, P = 3.94 × 10⁻⁸), and TROAP (r = 0.613, P = 2.29 × 10⁻⁵⁸) (Supplementary Fig. 4). ATF5 and STMN1, exhibiting the strongest correlation, were selected for subsequent functional validation.

ATF5 drives G2/M checkpoint and proliferation by directly transactivating STMN1

To investigate the ATF5-STMN1 regulatory relationship, we knocked down ATF5 expression in three EC cell lines including Ishikawa, HEC-1 and RL95-2. Consistent with bioinformatics predictions, ATF5 knockdown significantly reduced STMN1 protein and mRNA levels (Fig. 9A-C). However, STMN1 knockdown did not reciprocally affect ATF5 protein levels (Supplementary Fig. 5A). Despite efficient overexpression, ATF5 did not upregulate STMN1 protein levels (Supplementary Fig. 5B), subsequent validation revealed that ATF5 overexpression successfully upregulated STMN1 at the mRNA level (Fig. 9D). Dual-luciferase assays confirmed ATF5 binding to the STMN1 promoter (The binding site of ATF5 and STMN1 was shown in the Supplementary Table 6), as ATF5 overexpression significantly increased wild-type promoter activity compared to empty vector control. In contrast, this trans-activation was completely abolished when the predicted ATF5 binding sites in the promoter were mutated (Fig. 9E). These results demonstrate that ATF5 is a positive upstream regulator of STMN1 that directly activates its promoter.

To investigate the functional role of STMN1 in cell cycle progression, we analyzed the cell cycle distribution following STMN1 knockdown. Flow cytometric analysis revealed a significant accumulation of cells in the G2/M phase upon STMN1 silencing compared to the control group. Concurrently, a marked decrease in the proportion of cells in the G1 phase was observed (Fig. 9F and G). This distinct G2/M phase arrest indicates that the loss of STMN1 impedes mitotic progression, underscoring its critical role in the successful completion of cell division. In addition to the expected G2/M arrest, we observed a concomitant increase in the S-phase population upon ATF5/STMN1 depletion, suggesting a potential slowdown in S-phase progression.

Consistent with this finding, the CCK-8 assay showed that knocking down ATF5 and STMN1 markedly inhibited cell proliferation over 96 h (Fig. 9H). These data indicate that the two genes are essential for cell cycle progression and proliferation.

Overexpression of STMN1 rescues the phenotypes induced by ATF5 knockdown

To confirm the specific involvement of STMN1 downregulation in the observed phenotypes, we conducted a rescue experiment by overexpressing STMN1 in ATF5-knockdown cells. This overexpression largely restored the suppressed proliferation and impaired clonogenicity caused by ATF5 deficiency (Fig. 10A and B). These results strongly suggest that STMN1 is a key downstream effector of ATF5 in regulating these cellular processes.

Discussion

Tumor heterogeneity represents one of the major challenges in contemporary cancer therapy, contributing to treatment resistance and refractoriness, and is a key factor underlying treatment failure in malignancies [26]. The traditional histomorphometry classification of EC lacks sufficient accuracy for diagnosis and treatment. The current molecular classification of EC, based on multi-omics, suggests that EC is a heterogeneous group of tumors with diverse phenotypes [6]. Meanwhile, numerous studies have elucidated the associations among the TME, tumorigenesis, and progression, as well as their correlations with drug resistance [27–29]. These findings highlight the importance of accurately identifying inter-individual differences and intra-tumoral heterogeneity in EC patients.

However, conventional sequencing methods often fail to definitively determine whether the tested tissue consists entirely of tumor cells, potentially overlooking the spatial heterogeneity of tumors. This limitation can lead to inaccurate results, especially when working with limited sample sizes. In contrast, emerging technologies such as single-cell sequencing and spatial transcriptomics offer new perspectives by detecting both inter- and intra-tumor heterogeneity. These advancements not only facilitate the exploration of novel biological markers but also provide valuable guidance for tumor therapy.

In this study, Digital Spatial Profiling (DSP) was used to quantify transcript abundance in formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissues. This technology allows pathologists to select specific Regions of Interest based on histomorphology. This avoids the tissue dissociation process required in single-cell RNA sequencing and preserves the original spatial location of tumor and adjacent normal cells. However, DSP has limitations in this study. The technology provides bulk transcriptomic data from selected Regions of Interest that contain multiple cells. It lacks true single-cell resolution and cannot confirm individual cell identities directly. The analysis depends on computational deconvolution algorithms. Additionally, the targeted gene panel provides lower whole-transcriptome coverage compared to spatial platforms like 10x Visium. Therefore, the spatial data, including the identified ATF5-STMN1 axis and immune cell estimates, require future validation using single-cell technologies.

Since EC remains a clinically heterogeneous disease with varying molecular profiles, treatment responses, and patient outcomes, here we employed DSP in this study in 23 ROIs of four patients to dissect the spatial heterogeneity of EC at the transcriptomic level, identifying novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets. Our findings underscore the utility of DSP in unraveling TME complexity, revealing distinct gene expression patterns, immune infiltration dynamics, and pathway activities across intra- and inter-tumor regions.

Decoding spatial heterogeneity in EC: transcriptomic gradients and potential celluar crosstalk

Our study revealed significant molecular heterogeneity among EC patients with identical histomorphology, manifested through divergent signaling pathway activation and gene expression profiles. We specifically focused on two clinically relevant molecular subtypes: the p53abn subtype (associated with the poorest prognosis) and the NSMP subtype (most prevalent in Chinese populations [30] yet lacking targeted therapies), aiming to identify novel therapeutic targets for these high-need patient groups. Notably, even within the p53abn classification (TP53-mutated subgroup), treatment-naïve patients exhibited substantial interpatient variability in pathway activation and transcriptional signatures, highlighting the intrinsic biological diversity within this high-risk molecular category. Furthermore, we observed spatial heterogeneity in gene expression and pathway activity across different tumor regions, suggesting that both inter- and intra-tumor heterogeneity may contribute to treatment resistance in EC. The ability of DSP technology to resolve this spatial heterogeneity provides a valuable foundation for developing personalized therapeutic strategies.

The tumor ecosystem extends beyond malignant cells to include diverse stromal components - endothelial cells, fibroblasts, and immune populations - that collectively shape the tumor microenvironment (TME). Our cell deconvolution analysis of regionally distinct ROIs revealed fibroblasts as the predominant stromal component in both tumor core (TC) and adjacent normal (NC) regions, with consistently higher abundance in NC areas, aligning with previous reports [31]. This spatial distribution pattern suggests fibroblasts may play dual roles in EC pathogenesis, maintaining microenvironmental homeostasis while potentially facilitating tumor progression through multiple mechanisms including inflammation promotion, immune evasion, and angiogenesis [32–34]. Current evidence supports fibroblast functional heterogeneity, with distinct subpopulations exerting either tumor-promoting or suppressive effects during different stages of carcinogenesis [35–39]. Interestingly, Gao et al. [40] demonstrated tissue-specific fibroblast distribution patterns, with certain progenitor subpopulations preferentially enriched in non-cancerous adjacent tissues. While our findings confirm regional differences in fibroblast abundance, the lack of single-cell resolution prevents definitive subpopulation characterization. We hypothesize that specific fibroblast subsets in NC regions may acquire pro-tumorigenic properties during early carcinogenesis, though this requires validation through future single-cell studies with temporal resolution.

Novel therapeutic markers: from bench to bedside

The current study leveraged DSP technology to identify four candidate biomarker genes-ATF5, STMN1, CENPF, and TROAP in EC using a limited but clinically relevant cohort of four patient samples, including two molecular subtypes associated with suboptimal treatment responses. These findings provide novel insights into prognostic stratification and potential therapeutic targeting, particularly for high-risk EC subsets with unmet clinical needs. Specifically, we confirmed the overexpression of these genes in EC samples via RT-qPCR and demonstrated their prognostic significance through survival analyses in public databases. Functional in vitro assays in Ishikawa cells initially revealed that knockdown of ATF5, STMN1, CENPF, or TROAP significantly impaired proliferation and migration, supporting their roles as potential EC biomarkers and therapeutic targets.

The down-regulation of ATF5, STMN1, CENPF, and TROAP in EC tumors suggests their oncogenic roles, consistent with prior reports in other cancers. Notably, ATF5 appears to function as a transcriptional regulator of the other three genes and is associated with the G2/M checkpoint pathway. While ATF5 has been implicated in EC prognosis in single-cell studies [41], its mechanistic role remains underexplored. Intriguingly, ATF5 exhibits tissue-specific effects: it acts as an oncogene in bladder, breast, and ovarian cancers [42–44] but as a tumor suppressor in hepatocellular carcinoma [45].

STMN1, a microtubule-destabilizing protein linked to cell cycle progression [46, 47], has been associated with aggressive phenotypes in multiple malignancies [48–52]. Our findings align with previous EC studies showing that STMN1 knockdown suppresses proliferation and migration [53].

CENPF (a kinetochore protein involved in mitosis) has been linked to poor prognosis in various cancers [54–56], including EC in bioinformatics analyses [57–59]. Its role in gastric cancer metastasis via m6A modification and FAK/MAPK signaling [60] suggests a possible parallel mechanism in EC, though this remains speculative without experimental validation. And TROAP, a spindle-associated protein, has been shown to promote EC progression [61], consistent with our functional data demonstrating that its knockdown inhibits proliferation and migration. The collective evidence positions TROAP as a promising therapeutic target.

While our initial bioinformatic identification of ATF5, STMN1, CENPF, and TROAP as dysregulated genes in endometrial carcinoma (EC) was substantiated by functional in vitro assays—where their knockdown impaired Ishikawa cell proliferation and migration—this evidence alone remained descriptive. It failed to elucidate the hierarchical relationships or direct mechanistic interactions between these potential oncogenes. To move beyond a list of candidate genes and define a causative molecular pathway, we pursued a deeper investigation.

A critical finding from our following study is the identification of a direct regulatory axis between ATF5 and STMN1. Correlation analysis within the UCEC cohort revealed a strong positive co-expression between ATF5 and STMN1, prompting mechanistic investigation. We demonstrated that ATF5 acts as a direct transcriptional activator of STMN1. This relationship functionally underpins a key oncogenic circuit. Knockdown of ATF5 or its downstream target STMN1 induced a pronounced G2/M phase arrest, aligning with their established roles in cell cycle progression. The concurrent increase in the S-phase population upon depletion suggests a potential secondary disruption in S-phase dynamics, implicating this axis in coordinating multiple cell cycle checkpoints. Crucially, the overexpression of STMN1 effectively reversed the anti-proliferative effects caused by ATF5 knockdown, establishing STMN1 as a key functional mediator of ATF5’s oncogenic activity. We acknowledge that the rescue was partial in certain cell lines, suggesting the existence of additional ATF5 downstream effectors beyond STMN1. Transcriptomic analysis identified other potential targets such as CENPF and TROAP. These molecules likely function in parallel with STMN1 to drive proliferation. Re-expression of the downstream target STMN1 did not alter upstream ATF5 protein levels. Although ATF5 knockdown reduced STMN1 protein, ATF5 overexpression did not increase STMN1 protein despite promoter activation. RT-qPCR data demonstrate that ATF5 overexpression significantly upregulates mRNA level of STMN1, confirming that ATF5 acts as a positive transcriptional activator of STMN1. This suggests that STMN1 protein may be subject to post‑translational regulation or feedback inhibition once a certain threshold is reached. Loss‑of‑function data, being more physiologically representative, remains the key evidence for the ATF5‑STMN1 axis.

While our initial functional screen indicated that CENPF and TROAP also contribute to EC cell proliferation and migration, the ATF5-STMN1 axis emerges as a central, transcriptionally defined pathway. This hierarchy suggests that therapeutic strategies targeting the ATF5-STMN1 interface may be particularly effective. Our findings transform a bioinformatic gene list into a mechanistically prioritized model, where ATF5-driven transcriptional upregulation of STMN1 is a pivotal event promoting cell cycle progression and proliferation in EC. This established axis warrants further exploration as a potential target for disrupting EC tumorigenesis.

While our study demonstrates that ATF5 transcriptionally regulates STMN1, the upstream drivers of ATF5 upregulation in EC tumor cells remain to be fully elucidated. Our analysis identified fibroblasts as the most abundant cellular component within the TME. Tumor-associated fibroblasts are known to remodel the extracellular matrix and secrete various paracrine factors including cytokines and growth factors that induce metabolic and inflammatory stress in adjacent neoplastic cells [62]. Given that ATF5 is a stress-responsive transcription factor, we hypothesize that the fibroblast-rich microenvironment provides the necessary paracrine signals or stress stimuli to drive ATF5 upregulation in EC tumor cells. Additionally, a recent study indicates that extracellular matrix stiffness can activate ATF5 through the JAK-MYC pathway to promote cancer cell proliferation, suggesting another potential mechanism linking the tumor microenvironment to ATF5 expression [63]. This potential spatial-mechanistic link warrants further investigation, such as co-culture experiments, to determine whether crosstalk between stromal fibroblasts and tumor cells contributes to ATF5 expression.

In summary, we propose a model in which the oncogenic driver ATF5 accelerates EC progression by directly trans-activating STMN1, a master regulator of microtubule dynamics, thereby disrupting the G2/M checkpoint. This core axis is embedded within a broader mitotic failure network that additionally involves CENPF and TROAP. The functional interdependence of these proteins underscores their combined potential as biomarkers and therapeutic targets for a multi-pronged attack on aggressive EC.

Study limitations

There are still some limitations of this study. While DSP provides high-resolution data, the sample size limits generalizability. Only four samples of EC from two pathologic (EEC and SEC) & molecular (P53 abn and NSMP) types were used in this study, due to the small sample size spanning distinct molecular subtypes (NSMP and P53 abn), our study was underpowered to perform robust inter-subtype statistical comparisons. Consequently, the observed inter-tumoral variations represent descriptive trends that require validation in substantially larger, subtype-specific cohorts. A methodological limitation of our study is the application of the SCENIC algorithm to a relatively small number of spatial ROIs (n = 23). Since regulatory network inference relies on co-expression patterns, the statistical power is constrained by our pilot sample size. Therefore, the identified regulons, including ATF5, should be interpreted with caution. Meanwhile, our study was not analyzed by proteomics for more in-depth study. The estimation of protein levels through the utilization of relevant gene expression levels may not be as precise as initially presumed. Finally, in vivo models are initially needed to confirm the therapeutic efficacy of targeting these identified genes. Although our initial identification of these markers was based on a limited sample size, their established roles in related oncogenic processes, as documented in prior studies, lend credence to our findings. Nonetheless, validation in larger, independent cohorts is essential to firmly establish their clinical utility for endometrial carcinoma.

In conclusion, this exploratory study suggests that DSP can map EC heterogeneity and identify spatially informed biomarkers. Functional validation supports the oncogenic roles of ATF5, STMN1, CENPF, and TROAP, with mechanistic findings indicating STMN1 as a direct transcriptional target of ATF5. This interaction represents a potential key node within the DSP-derived network. Together, our findings offer candidate therapeutic targets and provide a functional rationale for their further investigation. Future efforts integrating multi-omics approaches may help advance precise and personalized treatment strategies for EC patients.
