Ethanol disrupts cerebellar synaptic modulation by enhancing EAAT4-mediated glutamate uptake
Authors: Shin’Ichiro Satake
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ataxia is a common consequence of alcohol consumption. Although ethanol enhances GABAergic neurotransmission in the cerebellum, its effects on glutamatergic synaptic signaling remain unclear. Electrophysiological studies in rat cerebellar slices have shown that ethanol, at pharmacologically relevant concentrations (25–100 mM), restricts the diffusion of excitatory neurotransmitters from synaptic clefts into the extracellular space. This restriction suppresses intersynaptic modulation between glutamatergic climbing fibers and GABAergic molecular layer interneurons that converge on the same Purkinje cell. This effect is mediated by increased glutamate uptake via the excitatory amino acid transporter EAAT4 in Purkinje cells, operating in concert with the sodium pump (Na,K-ATPase, NKA). Enhanced EAAT4 activity may represent a central mechanism underlying alcohol-induced cerebellar dysfunction. These findings indicate that neuronal EAATs function both as clearance systems and dynamic modulators of synaptic signaling. Ethanol-induced upregulation of glutamate uptake may inform therapeutic strategies targeting EAAT and NKA to prevent excitotoxic neuropathies.

Introduction

The cerebellum plays a critical role in coordinating sensory, motor, and cognitive functions, which are acutely and chronically disrupted by alcohol consumption1,2. Previous studies have shown that ethanol (EtOH) enhances GABAergic neurotransmission in specific brain regions, including the cerebellum, as well as cortical areas such as the amygdala and hippocampus3. These findings indicate that EtOH can modulate inhibitory signals across the central nervous system. However, it remains unclear whether EtOH also affects excitatory signaling in a manner that alters neuronal coding, synaptic processing, and plasticity at central synapses.

Neurotransmitters act on postsynaptic receptors for point-to-point signaling at homosynaptic connections. They also diffuse beyond the synaptic cleft to activate distant receptors on neighboring neurons, thereby modulating neurotransmission at nearby synapses. The excitatory neurotransmitter l-glutamate induces diverse forms of presynaptic modulation by activating autoreceptors at homosynaptic connections or heteroreceptors on adjacent neurons4,5. Ionotropic glutamate receptors, such as AMPA, NMDA, and kainate receptor subtypes, are involved in the presynaptic regulation of neurotransmission6,7.

In the cerebellum, the excitatory transmitter, presumably glutamate, released during repeated activation of olivocerebellar climbing fibers (CFs) serves a dual role: it excites postsynaptic Purkinje cells (PCs) and inhibits GABA release from the axon terminals of molecular layer interneurons (MLIs), primarily basket cells, that innervate the same PC8. The mechanisms underlying CF-mediated presynaptic inhibition at MLI-PC synapses include (i) extrasynaptic diffusion of the CF transmitter from CF-PC synapses, (ii) activation of GluA2-containing AMPA receptors (AMPARs) on neighboring MLI axon terminals, and (iii) inhibition of Cav2.1 channel-mediated GABA release at MLI-PC synapses via a Gi/o protein-coupled signaling pathway9–11. Collectively, these processes reinforce the cerebellar cortical output through PCs.

Extrasynaptic diffusion of CF transmitters is regulated by excitatory amino acid transporter (EAAT)-mediated glutamate uptake12. Different EAAT subtypes occur in neuronal dendrites and glial processes13,14. Of the five structurally related EAAT proteins (GLAST/EAAT1, GLT1/EAAT2, EAAC1/EAAT3, EAAT4, and EAAT5), the EAAT4 subtype is specifically and predominantly expressed in the extrasynaptic membranes of dendritic spines in cerebellar PCs15. The strength of CF-mediated presynaptic inhibition at the MLI-PC synapse is inversely correlated with EAAT4 protein expression levels in individual PC populations12.

To determine whether EtOH interferes with glutamate-mediated modulation, we examined its effects on heterosynaptic inhibitory crosstalk between CFs and MLIs converging on the same PCs in rat cerebellar slices. Pharmacologically relevant concentrations of EtOH (up to 100 mM) suppressed CF-mediated inhibition of GABAergic transmission. This EtOH-induced suppression was prevented by an EAAT4-selective blocker. Moreover, EtOH prolonged the decay of EAAT currents generated in PCs by glutamate photo-uncaging, indicating that EtOH enhances EAAT4-mediated glutamate uptake into PCs. Consequently, EtOH reduced the intersynaptic diffusion of CF transmitters from CF-PC synapses, thereby attenuating AMPAR-mediated presynaptic inhibition of MLI-PC GABAergic transmission. Thus, neuronal EAATs play a critical role in regulating the spatiotemporal dynamics of extracellular glutamate and contribute to alcohol-induced impairment of cerebellar function.

Results

Using paired-pulse stimulation with an interstimulus interval (ISI) of 80 ms through a glass microelectrode (S1) placed in the deeper quarter of the molecular layer (Fig. 1a), we recorded MLI-mediated inhibitory postsynaptic currents (IPSCs) from PC somata under whole-cell voltage-clamp conditions (VH = − 30 mV unless otherwise noted; Fig. 1b). After a conditioning stimulus delivered to the CFs at 5 Hz for 1 s through a second microelectrode (S2) positioned in the granular layer, the test IPSCs at MLI-PC synapses exhibited a pronounced amplitude reduction (Fig. 1a–c) that fluctuated in an all-or-none manner as the intensity of the CF conditioning stimulus was gradually increased8. The paired-pulse ratio (PPR), defined as the amplitude ratio of the second IPSC to the first, significantly increased from 0.90 ± 0.05 before to 0.99 ± 0.06 after CF conditioning (n = 13, P < 0.009). Because acute and chronic exposures to EtOH impair cerebellar function1,2, we investigated its effects on CF-induced inhibition of MLI-PC IPSCs. EtOH reduced the magnitude of CF stimulation (5 Hz for 1 s)-induced inhibition in a concentration-dependent manner (25–100 mM, r = −0.59; Fig. 1b–d). This effect occurred within 15 min of EtOH exposure and remained evident at physiological temperatures (33 °C–37 °C, Fig. 1e, f; see also ref. 12). EtOH intoxication increases in a concentration-dependent manner, progressing from mild behavioral effects at low blood alcohol concentration (BAC) to overt neurological dysfunction at higher levels. Experimental EtOH concentrations are typically compared with BACs from in vivo intoxication. Although low concentrations (approximately 18 mM) cause modest motor and cognitive impairment, higher concentrations (approximately 50 mM) reliably induce pronounced locomotor disruption, cognitive deficits, and sedation17. Accordingly, for subsequent experiments, we focused on 50 mM EtOH, a concentration associated with pronounced behavioral impairment.

Treatment with 50 mM EtOH did not significantly alter the amount of excitatory transmitters released from the CF terminals (Supplementary Fig. 1a–c), the activity of “inhibitory AMPARs” on MLI axon terminals (Supplementary Fig. 1d), or the intrinsic properties of GABAergic neurotransmission at the MLI-PC synapses (Supplementary Fig. 2). Although 100 mM EtOH increased the osmolarity of the external solution from 315 to 415 mOsm/L, a comparative osmotic increase induced by adding 100 mM of sucrose or urea to the perfusate (i.e., artificial cerebrospinal fluid [ACSF]) had no significant effect on CF-induced inhibition of IPSCs (20.8 ± 2.1% in control ACSF and 20.7 ± 3.9% with sucrose, n = 8, P = 0.98; 22.6 ± 3.8% in control and 22.7 ± 4.8% with urea, n = 8, P = 0.99). These results rule out changes in osmolarity as a cause of the inhibitory effect of EtOH on heterosynaptic inhibition.

Involvement of EAAT4 in EtOH-induced suppression of heterosynaptic inhibition

A plausible explanation for the EtOH-induced suppression of CF-mediated inhibition of IPSCs is that EtOH attenuates intersynaptic diffusion of the CF-released transmitter, thereby reducing AMPAR activation on neighboring MLI axon terminals. To test this hypothesis, we determined whether the broad-spectrum EAAT inhibitor threo-β-benzyloxyaspartic acid (TBOA)18 interferes with the effect of EtOH. The addition of 30 µM TBOA alone significantly increased the magnitude of CF stimulation-induced inhibition (Fig. 1g, h), consistent with the notion that EAATs limit CF transmitter diffusion12. Notably, in the presence of TBOA, 50 mM EtOH failed to produce a further significant change in CF-induced inhibition (P = 0.93 versus pre-EtOH; Fig. 1g, h). This finding supports the hypothesis that EtOH exerts its effect by enhancing EAAT-mediated glutamate uptake, thereby preventing CF transmitter diffusion.

At the CF-PC synapse, glial and neuronal EAATs may play distinct roles in clearing CF-released glutamate19. Glial glutamate transporters, specifically the GLAST/EAAT1 and GLT1/EAAT2 subtypes, are expressed in the radial processes of Bergmann glia (BG) that surround CF-PC synaptic clefts20,21. In contrast, the neuronal transporter EAAT4 is specifically and abundantly localized to the extrasynaptic membranes of PC dendritic spines15. GLAST prevents glutamate spillover from the synaptic cleft during the early phase of CF-evoked excitatory postsynaptic currents (EPSCs), whereas EAAT4 clears residual glutamate that escapes GLAST-mediated uptake during the late decay phase of the EPSC19. In contrast, GLT1 in BG has a minimal role in CF transmitter clearance22.

We previously demonstrated that both glial GLAST and neuronal EAAT4 control the intersynaptic diffusion of the CF transmitter from the CF-PC to MLI-PC synapses12. To determine whether EAAT subtypes differentially contribute to the EtOH-induced suppression of CF-mediated inhibition, we examined the effects of subtype-selective EAAT blockers23. The EAAT4- and GLT1-selective inhibitor threo-3-methylglutamate (T3MG, 30 µM)24 significantly enhanced CF-induced inhibition of IPSCs (Fig. 1i). Notably, T3MG prevented the suppressive effect of EtOH on CF-induced inhibition (P > 0.99 versus pre-EtOH; Fig. 1i), which was similar to the effect observed with the broad-spectrum EAAT blocker TBOA. These results suggest that EAAT4 acts as a key inhibitory regulator of the intersynaptic diffusion of CF-derived glutamate. EtOH likely enhances EAAT4-mediated glutamate uptake into PCs, thereby limiting CF transmitter diffusion to neighboring MLI axon terminals.

The GLAST- and GLT1-selective blocker (2S,3S)-3-[3-(4-methoxybenzoylamino)benzyloxy]aspartic acid (PMB-TBOA, 0.1 µM)25 enhanced CF-mediated inhibition of IPSCs (P = 0.014; Fig. 1j). However, 50 mM EtOH still suppressed heterosynaptic inhibition in the presence of PMB-TBOA (P = 0.013 versus pre-EtOH; Fig. 1j). In contrast, the GLT1-selective blocker dihydrokainic acid (DHK, 300 µM)26 did not promote CF-induced inhibition (Fig. 1k, P = 0.96), and EtOH suppressed the inhibitory effect of CF conditioning on IPSCs following DHK treatment (P = 0.010; Fig. 1k). Although the neuronal transporter EAAT4 and glial transporter GLAST contribute to CF transmitter clearance12,19, we found that EtOH impairs CF stimulation-induced inhibition at MLI-PC synapses by selectively enhancing EAAT4-mediated uptake of CF transmitter escaping from CF-PC synapses.

EtOH increases the turnover rate of EAAT-mediated glutamate uptake in PCs

In neurons and glial cells, glutamate uptake is mediated by the cotransport of three Na+, one H+, and one glutamate−, along with the countertransport of one K+ across the plasma membrane. This results in a net inward movement of two charges per transport cycle14, rendering the process electrogenic. To directly assess glutamate uptake activity, we recorded EAAT-mediated currents from PCs in response to glutamate photo-uncaging, termed photo-uncaging-evoked transporter currents (PTCs). This recording provides a direct measure of glutamate transporter activity while bypassing potential confounding factors associated with synaptic current recordings.

In the presence of 30 µM RuBi-glutamate (applied for at least 20 min in a bath) and a mixture of glutamate and GABA receptor antagonists in the ACSF, photostimulation for 5 ms reliably elicited PTCs in PCs (VH = − 60 mV; Fig. 2a). The peak amplitude remained stable for at least 1 h of recording. TBOA (200 µM) almost completely abolished PTCs (Fig. 2a), confirming that EAAT activation mediated these currents. Consistent with the regional variation in EAAT4 expression, which was more abundant in lobule X PCs than in lobule III PCs27, the amplitude of CF stimulation-induced synaptic transporter currents (STCs) was markedly greater in lobule X than in lobule III PCs27. Similarly, the amplitude of PTCs was significantly greater in lobule X PCs than in lobule III PCs (Fig. 2a, b). These consistent results indicate that PTC amplitude is a reliable proxy for EAAT4 expression and, by extension, the glutamate uptake capacity of PCs. Furthermore, the PTC amplitude was markedly greater at 60 µM RuBi-glutamate than at 30 µM (3.07 ± 0.59-fold, n = 4), suggesting that lower concentrations help prevent transporter saturation and more accurately reflect uptake activity. The application of EtOH (50 mM for 10 min) did not significantly affect the amplitude of PTCs in the PCs (103.5 ± 6.8% of control, n = 10, P = 0.60; Fig. 2c, d). However, it prolonged their decay time (117.2 ± 6.9% of control τdecay, n = 10, P = 0.048; Fig. 2c, e). This effect was reversible, and the decay time returned to baseline following EtOH washout (Fig. 2e). EtOH marginally decreased the 20%–80% rise time of PTCs (6.35 ± 0.20 ms in control ACSF versus 6.03 ± 0.15 ms with EtOH, n = 10), although this change was not significant (P = 0.078). The decay kinetics of the PTCs were better described by a double-exponential function. EtOH treatment did not significantly alter the decay time constants of the fast (τfast) and slow (τslow) components (Fig. 2f, g). However, it significantly increased the contribution of the τslow component (i.e., the amplitude ratio of the slow part to the total PTC current, %slow) to 173 ± 22% of control (n = 10, P = 0.018; Fig. 2h). As the total charge transfer during a PTC reflects the extent of glutamate uptake by a single cell14, EtOH-induced prolongation of τdecay (Fig. 2c, e) suggests that EtOH enhances PC-mediated glutamate uptake.

EAATs clear extracellular glutamate via the elevator-like movement of their transport domain across the cell membrane28,29. This involves switching between two distinct conformations of the protein: an outward-facing state that exposes the substrate-binding site for glutamate binding and an inward-facing state that enables the release of the substrate into the cytoplasm. The time required to complete one transport cycle (i.e., the EAAT turnover rate) is considered an important indicator of transporter activity14. Bergles et al. showed that the continuous presence of glutamate enables a more precise analysis of transport kinetics with improved temporal resolution30. Based on this principle, we examined the glutamate uptake kinetics using a prolonged photo-uncaging pulse of RuBi-glutamate (30 µM in the bath). In response to extended stimulation (500 ms), the PC exhibited a biphasic PTC consisting of a transient inward current followed by a steady-state (Fig. 2i). The transient current (PTCtransient) reflects the initial phase of glutamate transport, whereas the time course of the steady-state current (PTCsteady) represents the turnover rate14.

After the PTCtransient peak, PTCsteady gradually increased in amplitude until reaching a plateau, showing a 26 ± 7.6% increase (n = 8) during the 300 ms period of the steady-state current phase between 200 and 500 ms. However, this change did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.065). This biphasic current profile is consistent with the kinetics of the EAAT4 subtype, which operates at a relatively low turnover rate31,32. EtOH (50 mM) did not alter the peak amplitude of the PTCtransient (100.4 ± 9.9% of control, n = 8, P = 0.58). However, it significantly increased the amplitude of the PTCsteady measured just before the end of the 500-ms light stimulation (135.3 ± 11.5% of control, n = 8, P = 0.047; Fig. 2i, j). These results suggest that EtOH enhances the turnover rate of EAAT4-mediated glutamate uptake in PCs, likely through an ionic mechanism coupled to the transporter (see Discussion).

The PTCs recorded from BG comprised a transient peak current with a rapid decay and a sustained current with an extremely slow decay (Fig. 3a). The τfast of BG-PTCs (13.1 ± 1.7 ms, n = 9) was comparable to that of PC-PTCs (15.5 ± 1.2 ms, n = 10, P = 0.27), whereas both τslow (228.5 ± 50.9 ms, n = 9) and %slow (51.0 ± 5.1%, n = 9) were significantly greater in BG-PTCs than in PC-PTCs (96.1 ± 20.8 ms, n = 10, P = 0.035; 9.2 ± 2.1%, n = 10, P < 0.001, respectively). These findings are consistent with faster glutamate clearance mediated by GLAST in BG than that mediated by EAAT4 in PCs31. EtOH (50 mM, 10 min) did not significantly alter the amplitude (94.3 ± 6.3%, n = 9, P = 0.44; Fig. 3b), single-exponential decay time (105.0 ± 3.2%, n = 9, P = 0.15; Fig. 3c), or any parameters derived from double-exponential fitting, including τfast (P = 0.72), τslow (P = 0.37), and %slow (P = 0.19), of BG-PTCs (Fig. 3d–f). Therefore, EtOH is unlikely to enhance EAAT activity in BG. Effects of EtOH on EAAT-mediated anion currents in PCs

The EtOH-induced prolongation of PTCs (Fig. 2c) may involve mechanisms unrelated to glutamate uptake, including (i) enhanced efficacy of glutamate uncaging, (ii) disrupted glutamate diffusion, or (iii) increased EAAT-mediated anion flux. Although EAAT-associated anion flux is not kinetically coupled to the glutamate transport cycle32, its amplitude is modulated by extracellular glutamate concentration33,34. These anion currents were abolished when methanesulfonate was used as the primary intracellular anion35. To examine these alternative explanations, we recorded PTCs from PCs (VH = − 60 mV) using electrodes filled with a NO3-based internal solution in combination with an external ACSF containing a mixture of glutamate and GABA receptor antagonists27,36.

A single photo-uncaging pulse (5 ms) of RuBi-glutamate (30 µM in the bath) resulted in an inward current mediated by the anion NO3−. This current was abolished by adding the EAAT blocker TBOA (200 µM; Fig. 4a), which confirmed that NO3-mediated PTCs are dependent on EAAT activation in PCs. Notably, the decay time of NO3-PTCs (129 ± 16.6 ms, n = 7) was significantly longer (P < 0.001) than that of typical glutamate-uptake-coupled PTCs recorded under control conditions using a methanesulfonate-based internal solution (27.7 ± 2.8 ms, n = 10). Exposure to EtOH (50 mM) did not cause significant changes in the amplitude or decay time of NO3-mediated PTCs (amplitude: 104.2 ± 3.7% of control, n = 7, P = 0.28, Fig. 4a, b; τdecay: 137 ± 21.5 ms, n = 7, P = 0.19 versus before EtOH, Fig. 4a, c), which was in sharp contrast to the effects of EtOH observed with PTCs recorded using a methanesulfonate-containing internal solution (Fig. 2c). Increased glutamate uptake in PCs modulates extrasynaptic diffusion of CF transmitters

To determine the physiological consequences of the EtOH-induced prolongation of PTCs generated by the uncaging of exogenous glutamate (Fig. 2c), we examined the effect of EtOH on EAAT currents mediated by the synaptic release of endogenous glutamate at CF-PC junctions (i.e., CF-PC STCs). EtOH (50 mM) reversibly reduced the amplitude of CF-PC STCs to 73.7 ± 4.5% of the control (n = 9, P = 0.036; Fig. 5a, b). The PPR of STCs induced at 200-ms intervals was significantly higher in the presence of EtOH than in control ACSF (P = 0.0054; Fig. 5a, c). This finding is consistent with the notion that EtOH enhances the turnover rate of EAAT4-mediated glutamate uptake in PCs, potentially by facilitating the efficient clearance during repetitive CF stimulation. EtOH did not significantly affect STC decay (P = 0.063, Fig. 5a, d). To further investigate this possibility, we examined the effects of dextran, an inert macromolecule, on the CF-PC STC. The addition of dextran to the perfusate increases the viscosity of the extracellular milieu, thereby impeding neurotransmitter diffusion within the perisynaptic sites38,39. Superfusion with dextran (40 kDa, 5% weight/volume in ACSF) increased the amplitude of CF-PC STCs (124.6 ± 7.0% of control, n = 9, P = 0.044) without causing significant changes in their decay or PPR values (P = 0.22 and P = 0.29, respectively; Fig. 5e–g). Collectively, these results support the hypothesis that EtOH limits the escape of CF-released glutamate to extrasynaptic regions by enhancing its uptake into PCs.

Next, we recorded CF stimulation-evoked NO3-STCs in PCs and BG (both at VH = − 60 mV) to compare the effects of EtOH treatment across distinct cellular sites, each characterized by distinct glutamate environments. The amplitude of CF-PC NO3-STCs remained stable over 30 min (104.2 ± 8.6%, n = 8). However, the application of EtOH (50 mM) decreased their amplitude to 80.2 ± 8.4% of the control (n = 11, P = 0.047; Fig. 5h, i) without significantly altering decay kinetics (47.5 ± 13.4 and 52.1 ± 15.1 ms, respectively, n = 9, P = 0.17), which is similar to the effects observed in glutamate transport-coupled STCs (Fig. 5a, b). In contrast, CF-induced NO3-STCs recorded in BG were largely unaffected by EtOH treatment, even at a concentration of 100 mM (92.6 ± 6.0% of control, n = 6, P = 0.63; Fig. 5j, k). Considering the differential distribution of EAAT4 proteins on PC dendrites15 and GLASTs on BG processes20,21, these results suggest that EtOH selectively decreases glutamate levels in the extrasynaptic region around CF-PC synapses by enhancing EAAT4 activity.

In summary, EtOH-induced increase in EAAT4 activity in PCs may suppress CF transmitter-mediated presynaptic inhibition of GABAergic transmission at MLI-PC synapses by (i) restricting the diffusion of CF transmitters from CF-PC contact sites into the extrasynaptic space, and (ii) concomitantly decreasing the amount of CF-derived glutamate that reaches AMPARs on adjacent MLI terminals.

Discussion

This study examined the effects of EtOH on heterosynaptic crosstalk, a phenomenon previously characterized in the cerebellar cortex8. Our results revealed that EtOH impairs CF transmitter-induced presynaptic inhibition of GABAergic transmission at MLI-PC synapses. This modulation is attributable to EtOH-induced enhancement of neuronal glutamate uptake by the EAAT4 transporter in PCs. Furthermore, EtOH attenuated the intersynaptic diffusion of glutamate released at CF-PC clefts, thereby limiting its access to AMPARs on neighboring MLI terminals. This ultimately suppressed the GABA release inhibition at MLI-PC synapses. The mechanism underlying this EtOH-induced increase in the turnover rate of EAAT4-mediated glutamate uptake in cerebellar PCs differs from the well-documented mechanism of EtOH-induced facilitation of GABAergic neurotransmission in certain brain regions3. Thus, the present study provides evidence that neuronal EAATs function as dynamic regulators of information processing by modulating uptake activity and shaping the spatiotemporal profile of extracellular glutamate. Proper brain function depends on a finely tuned balance between synaptic excitation and inhibition. The EtOH-induced enhancement of EAAT4 activity may disrupt communication between adjacent excitatory and inhibitory synapses, thereby contributing to alcohol-related cerebellar dysfunction.

The results of all three experiments support the hypothesis that EtOH suppresses CF-mediated inhibition of MLI-PC GABAergic transmission by enhancing EAAT4 activity in PCs. First, the EAAT4-selective blocker T3MG enhanced CF-mediated inhibition of IPSCs and eliminated the EtOH-induced suppression of this inhibition (Fig. 1). Another neuronal glutamate transporter subtype, EAAC1/EAAT3, is expressed in the soma and dendrites of PCs as well as in GABAergic MLIs42. EAAC1 exhibits a higher turnover rate of glutamate uptake than EAAT431. In a Xenopus oocyte expression system, EtOH increased EAAC1 activity43. However, considering the lack of selective EAAC1 inhibitors, we were unable to determine whether EAAC1 contributes to the observed EtOH-induced modulatory effects in our system. Although these findings suggest that EAAT4 is the primary mediator, the potential contribution of EAAC1 remains unclear and warrants further investigation. Further research is necessary to clarify the mechanism by which the observed changes in synaptic modulation affect the intrinsic excitability of PCs and ultimately contribute to motor dysfunction. The GLAST-selective blocker PMB-TBOA enhanced CF-mediated heterosynaptic inhibition without interfering with the EtOH-induced suppression of this inhibition (Fig. 1). These results indicate that although glial EAATs such as GLAST may restrict the extrasynaptic diffusion of CF-derived glutamate12,19, they do not mediate the modulatory effects of EtOH.

Second, EtOH increased the amplitude of PTCsteady generated in response to glutamate uncaging on PCs (Fig. 2). This result is consistent with that of a previous study showing that EtOH increased the Vmax of EAAT4-mediated currents in Xenopus oocytes44. The magnitude of EtOH-induced increase in PTCsteady amplitude in PCs was approximately 35% (Fig. 2i), which was smaller than the suppression of CF-mediated inhibition of IPSCs observed with EtOH (60%, Fig. 1d). Moreover, the EAAT4 blocker T3MG increased CF-induced inhibition by 50% (Fig. 1i) but decreased EAAT4-mediated currents in Xenopus oocytes by only approximately 20% (ref. 23). Thus, even relatively modest alterations in EAAT function may yield disproportionately amplified effects at the circuit level.

Third, EtOH reduced the amplitude of NO3-STCs evoked in PCs by CF stimulation (Fig. 5h). Because the magnitude of this anion-mediated STC reflects the extracellular concentration of synaptically released glutamate33,34, the EtOH-induced decrease in STC amplitude likely results from reduced glutamate levels in the perisynaptic regions surrounding CF-PC contact sites. This interpretation is consistent with those of a previous study12 and the known regional distribution of EAAT4 in PCs45,46. Collectively, EAATs in PCs may regulate the spatiotemporal dynamics of CF transmitters that escape the synaptic cleft and reach perisynaptic sites, thereby modulating crosstalk between CF and MLI terminals converging on adjacent regions of the same PC.

The PTCsteady amplitude represents the turnover rate of EAAT-mediated glutamate uptake14. Treatment with the NKA inhibitor ouabain decreased PTCsteady amplitude (Fig. 6d, e), indicating that NKA plays a crucial role in regulating the turnover rate. Furthermore, all EtOH-induced effects were essentially abolished upon treatment with 0.2 µM ouabain (Figs. 7 and 8). This lidocaine derivative inhibits NKA activity in a concentration-dependent and isoform-specific manner, with α3 and α2 isoforms (Ki in the low µM range) being markedly more sensitive than the α1 isoform (Ki in the low mM range)47. Because α3-subunit immunoreactivity occurs on the PC soma surface48, EtOH may enhance glutamate uptake into PCs by stimulating α3-containing NKAs. This stimulation promotes Na+ efflux and K+ influx across the plasma membrane, enabling EAATs to cycle between inward- and outward-facing conformations and sustain glutamate transport13,14. These experiments were conducted in cerebellar slices prepared from juvenile rats, therefore, the findings may not fully reflect the effects of EtOH on adult animals. Future research in adult models, potentially incorporating imaging or behavioral approaches, is necessary to address this issue.

EAAT molecules exhibit high mobility on the membrane surface of glial processes. The EPSC waveform has been considered to be shaped by EAAT diffusion within the membrane at synaptic and extrasynaptic sites49. EtOH increases the fluidity of the phospholipid bilayer and alters membrane protein composition50. Therefore, these changes may influence the lateral mobility and distribution of EAAT4 proteins in the plasma membrane of PCs, thereby affecting the glutamate uptake capacity at CF-PC synapses.

Afferent inputs from various brain regions project to subregions of the inferior olivary nucleus. Each subregion, in turn, gives rise to CF projections targeting topographically distinct PC populations that exhibit longitudinal distribution patterns and molecular diversity, characterized by differential expression of specific protein or gene markers (e.g., zebrin II/aldolase C)51. CFs release excitatory neurotransmitters from both release sites within CF-PC synapses and ectopic sites adjacent to BG processes and MLIs (e.g., CF transmitters diffuse into the extracellular space to activate dendritic glutamate receptors in MLIs)52. A burst of spikes from a single CF elicits synchronized activation of multiple MLIs through CF transmitter diffusion. This results in the simultaneous activation of a subpopulation of MLIs within the parasagittal band corresponding to the CF innervation territory53. By promoting EAAT4-mediated glutamate uptake into PCs, EtOH may affect the crosstalk between CFs and MLI dendrites52, as well as that between CFs and MLI axon terminals8,12.

Metabotropic glutamate receptors (mGluRs) are located in the postsynaptic membrane at the periphery of the parallel fiber (PF)-PC contacts54–56. EtOH-induced upregulation of glutamate uptake may also influence mGluR activation on PC dendrites. Abundant neuronal EAATs in the PC attenuate mGluR1 activation26, thereby modulating long-term depression (LTD, postsynaptic origin) of excitatory transmission at the PF-PC synapse57. Another potential target of EtOH-induced action is the PF transmitter acting on mGluR1 under conditions of EAAT-regulated perisynaptic diffusion. Consistent with this, EtOH disrupts LTD at the PF-PC synapse58,59 and suppresses mGluR1-mediated slow inward currents in PCs60.

Chronic exposure to EtOH (up to 100 mM for 24–96 h) is reported to reduce the activity of EAAT4 expressed in Xenopus oocytes61. Furthermore, in the rat nucleus accumbens, a critical brain region involved in alcohol-related reward responses, repeated in vivo EtOH exposure decreases glutamate uptake capacity and increases extracellular glutamate levels62. Depending on the exposure level, EtOH may differentially affect the spatiotemporal dynamics of glutamate released within synapses and diffusing to perisynaptic sites across brain regions. Thus, alcohol-induced modulation of synaptic signaling through extracellular glutamate-dependent mechanisms may be diverse, leading to distinct pathophysiological consequences in brain function.

Cerebellar PCs are highly susceptible to ischemic insults. PCs have been reported to degenerate after global brain ischemia, particularly in zones where EAAT4 and zebrin II/aldolase C are less abundantly expressed46,63. A major trigger of ischemia-induced PC death is cardiac arrest, which results in the accumulation of pathologically synchronous, high-frequency activity in CFs46. Because EAAT is the only mechanism for removing excess glutamate from the extracellular space, it plays a crucial role in protecting PCs from excitotoxic damage64,65. Moreover, mutations and reduced expression of the EAAT4 gene SLC1A6 and the NKA α3-subunit gene ATP1A3 have been identified in patients with neurological disorders, including epilepsy, schizophrenia, and mood disorders66,67. The upregulation of neuronal EAAT activity observed here may inform therapeutic strategies aimed at enhancing EAAT and NKA function, thereby counteracting excitotoxic neuropathies associated with excessive glutamatergic activity.

Methods

All experiments were conducted in accordance with the institutional guidelines for animal experimentation and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the National Institutes of Natural Sciences (NINS, Japan). Wistar rats (Slc:Wistar, Japan SLC Inc., Shizuoka, Japan) were housed in standard cages under a 12 h light/dark cycle in a temperature-controlled environment (21 °C ± 2 °C), with ad libitum access to food and water.

Preparation of rat cerebellar slices

Juvenile Wistar rats (12–18 days old, both sexes), in which cerebellar circuits are largely functional68 yet remain accessible for precise electrophysiological recordings, were decapitated under isoflurane anesthesia. The cerebellar vermis was dissected and parasagittally sectioned using a vibratome (VT1000S, Leica, Nussloch, Germany) in ice-cold Na+-deficient saline containing the following in mM: sucrose 300, KCl 3.4, CaCl2 0.3, MgCl2 3.0, HEPES 10.0, NaH2PO4 0.6, and glucose 10.0 (equilibrated with 100% O2, pH 7.4). The 250 µm-thick slices were stored at room temperature (23 °C ± 2 °C) for at least 1 h in a chamber containing ACSF equilibrated with 95% O2/5% CO2. The ACSF composition was as follows (in mM): NaCl 138.6, KCl 3.35, CaCl2 2.5, MgCl2 1.0, NaHCO3 21.0, NaH2PO4 0.6, and glucose 10.0 (equilibrated with O2/CO2, pH 7.4).

The slices were transferred to a recording chamber mounted on a microscope stage (BX50WI, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) and continuously superfused with fresh ACSF at a flow rate of 1.0–1.5 mL/min. Neurons and glial cells were identified visually using infrared differential interference contrast (IR-DIC) optics with a water-immersion objective (60×, NA 0.90, Olympus) and a digital charge-coupled device (CCD) camera (ORCA-R2, Hamamatsu Photonics K. K., Hamamatsu, Japan).

Electrophysiological examination in cerebellar slices

Synaptic responses were recorded from PCs using the whole-cell voltage-clamp technique. Patch-clamp electrodes were prepared from thin-walled glass tubing (GD-1.5, Narishige, Tokyo, Japan) with a pipette puller (P-97/IVF, Sutter Instrument, Novato CA, USA). The electrodes were filled with an internal solution containing (in mM): Cs-methanesulfonate 150.0, KCl 5.0, EGTA 0.1, HEPES 5.0, Mg-ATP 3.0, and Na3-GTP 0.4. The pH was adjusted to 7.4, and the resistance of the electrodes was 3–6 MΩ. For most experiments, the membrane potentials were controlled by a voltage-clamp amplifier (EPC-10, HEKA Elektronik, Lambrecht, Germany) and “PatchMaster” software (HEKA Elektronik). The current signals were filtered at 3 kHz and digitized at 20 kHz. No corrections for liquid junction potential and series resistance (typically <15 MΩ) were applied, as these values were consistent across recordings and did not affect relative comparisons8. Leakage currents were monitored continuously, and if this parameter changed by more than 200 pA, the recordings were not used for the analysis.

CF-mediated EPSCs and MLI-mediated IPSCs were induced by focal stimulation (single pulse, 5 V, 200 µs) in the granular and molecular layers, respectively, with ACSF-filled glass electrodes (resistance, 3–8 MΩ). CF-EPSCs were identified as described previously8. Miniature IPSCs (mIPSCs) were recorded from PCs in an ACSF containing 1 µM tetrodotoxin (TTX) and analyzed based on the detection threshold8. Because the extent of CF conditioning-induced inhibition of MLI-PC IPSC does not differ significantly between room temperature and higher temperature (35 °C–37 °C)12, all experiments were conducted at room temperature unless otherwise stated. The temperature was monitored, as cellular glutamate uptake can be enhanced at high temperatures69.

Glutamate- or GABA-evoked responses were elicited in PCs by uncaging 30 µM RuBi-glutamate or 5 µM RuBi-GABA (both from Tocris Bioscience, Bristol, UK), respectively, in the presence of 1 µM TTX. Transient illumination (430 nm for 5 ms) was delivered through the epifluorescence pathway using a mercury arc lamp (Olympus)70–73. Following RuBi-glutamate cleavage, the released glutamate in the slices was not rapidly decreased by simple diffusion, but could be removed by EAATs, depending on their uptake kinetics. Therefore, PTCs recorded in PCs using a Cs-methanesulfonate-based pipette solution reflect the total amount of glutamate taken up by a single PC74.

Glutamate transport-coupled EAAT currents were recorded in PCs (VH = − 60 mV) using a Cs-methanesulfonate-based patch-clamp internal solution. Recordings were performed under conditions in which glutamate- and GABAA-receptor-mediated currents were blocked by 50 µM NBQX, 50 µM d-AP5, 100 µM (RS)-1-aminoindan-1,5-dicarboxylic acid (AIDA), and 10 µM bicuculline in the external ACSF12,27,36. In the presence of the glutamate/GABA receptor blocker mixture, CF stimulation elicited current responses in PCs (i.e., STCs). However, their amplitude was small and often overlapped with stimulation artifacts. These issues were minimized by using a recording patch electrode filled with an internal solution containing QX314 (5 mM), a lidocaine derivative that blocks voltage-gated Na+ channels. Current amplitudes were measured from the peak to the baseline level immediately before electrical or light stimulation.

NO3-mediated (glutamate transport-uncoupled) transporter currents were recorded from PCs or BG held predominantly at −60 mV with the patch electrode containing an internal solution (in mM): CsNO3 130.0, EGTA 10.0, HEPES 10.0, MgCl2 0.5, Mg-ATP 3.0, and Na-GTP 0.4 (pH 7.4 with CsOH), in the presence of blocker mixtures for the glutamate/GABAA receptors12,27,36. In some experiments, the morphology of PC and BG was assessed by intracellular staining with biocytin (5 mg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), which was added to the cells during whole-cell recording10.

Drug application

(2S,3S)-3-[3-(4-methoxybenzoylamino)benzyloxy]aspartic acid (PMB-TBOA) was provided by K. Shimamoto (Suntory Institute for Bioorganic Research, Osaka, Japan). All other reagents were purchased from Tocris Bioscience, Sigma-Aldrich, and Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Corporation (Osaka, Japan). Dextran (40 kDa) was diluted to 5% (weight/volume) in ACSF, and the solution was made iso-osmotic with distilled water39.

Stock solutions of PMB-TBOA and threo-β-benzyloxyaspartic acid (TBOA) were prepared in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). The final DMSO concentration did not exceed 0.1% (volume/volume) in any experiment. Unless otherwise stated, drugs were applied to the cerebellar slices by perfusion, and their effects were evaluated at least 15 min after application.

Calculation of PPR and decay time constant

The PPR of EPSC, IPSC, and PTC amplitudes was calculated as the ratio of the second to the first response75. The decay time constant (τdecay) of EAAT-mediated currents was determined using single- or double-exponential fitting procedures implemented in FitMaster (HEKA Elektronik) and Igor Pro (WaveMetrics Inc., Portland, OR, USA)76.

Statistical analysis

“KyPlot” software (KyensLab Inc., Tokyo, Japan) was used for statistical analyses. The results are expressed as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical data were obtained by analyzing different slices independently. Although the sample sizes were comparable to those used in previous studies, no formal power calculation was employed to determine them. Statistical significance of differences was determined by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by a post hoc t-test, Tukey-Kramer, or Dunnett multiple comparison test, as appropriate. P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.
