Achievable directive antenna gain measurements and modeling for 60 GHz indoor links
Authors: Sebastián Bruna, Mauricio Rodríguez, Rodolfo Feick, Francisco Pizarro, Reinaldo Valenzuela, Jinfeng Du, Dmitry Chizhik, Jakub Sapis, Sergio Chapana
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highly directive antennas may be a key component in millimeter wave links to offset their inherently high path losses. In cluttered scenarios such as Non-Line-Of-Sight (NLOS) indoor settings, antenna gain is degraded by the interaction of the channel angular spread with the antenna pattern. We conducted extensive measurements at 60 GHz indoors to evaluate the effect of angular spread on antenna gain degradation and its relation to small-scale fades. Our empirical results, obtained in corridors using five antennas with azimuthal Half-Power Beam Widths (HPBW) ranging from 6° to 60°, characterize effective gain of antennas in both Line-Of-Sight (LOS) and NLOS conditions. We find that narrow beam antennas can experience substantial gain degradation in NLOS scenarios, up to 4.4 dB at the 90th percentile for a 6° HPBW antenna. Our results confirmed this effect is less severe for wider beam antennas, which will capture a larger proportion of incident energy. We found that a Rician model can explain gain degradation to within a fraction of 1 dB for LOS links. In contrast, for NLOS links, a diffuse model with Gaussian-shaped channel angular spectrum and no dominant components yields better results, with less than 1 dB gap from 10th to 90th percentile between the empirical and simulated CDFs of azimuthal gains. Taking into account the phase profile of the antenna is particularly important when using this model, in line with the hypothesis of constructive/destructive interference of multiple wavefronts in NLOS links.

Introduction

Millimeter wave (mmWave) links have the potential of providing the bandwidth increases needed for the next-generation of wireless systems. One of the drivers for this demand is the proliferation of IoT and mobile devices that require bandwidth-intensive applications. The global IoT base is projected to grow from 18.2 billion to 29 billion IoT devices over the next decade1–3. A substantial fraction of these devices use Wi-Fi (e.g., ≈ 31% of IoT connections in 2023)3,4, and growth directly increases the number of Wi-Fi-attached devices. Currently Wi-Fi operates in crowded microwave frequency bands1,3, where available spectrum is scarce. As a result, even significant advances in spectral efficiency cannot accommodate such explosive growth. In response to this challenge, the next generation of Wi-Fi is shifting to much higher frequencies. Most notably, to the 60 GHz millimeter wave (mmWave) band that offers several gigahertz of unlicensed spectrum. Reflecting this trend, two IEEE 60 GHz standards 802.11ad5 and 802.15.3c6 have recently been ratified for local and personal area networking. In a more general context, the use of mmWave links offering data rates of several Gbps will enable wireless applications such as uncompressed video streaming, cordless computing, and even wireless data centers7.

Wireless links using mmWave bands require directive antennas to overcome the attenuation losses inherent to such frequencies2,5,8–10. Nominal or free-space antenna gains may be degraded in multipath-rich indoor environments. Therefore, understanding and characterizing the interaction between the angular spread of incoming wavefronts and the antenna reception pattern is crucial for link budget prediction and system level performance evaluations. The parameter frequently used for this purpose is “azimuth gain” (AG), defined as the ratio of maximum to average power across all azimuth angles. AG can be characterized as a random variable, dependent on scenario and position8,11–13. Increasing free-space (nominal) antenna gain will not increase received power in the same proportion when the angular spread of incoming wavefronts is comparable or wider than the antenna beamwidth. This results in signal power falling outside the main lobe, and thus less of it being captured by a single lobe directive antenna11,14,15. The effect that this produces on link-budget for indoor mmWave WLANs, characterized by strong specular reflections and clustered multipath from walls, ceilings, and furnishings16–18 must be quantified. The above is illustrated in Fig. 1, which shows an angular power spectrum measured by our equipment, as will be described in detail in this paper. Superimposed are two antenna beam patterns. The 6° half power beamwidth (HPBW) antenna has a gain pattern that is narrower than the spread of the dominant wavefronts and will miss power seen by the 24° HPBW antenna. The increased nominal gain of the narrower-beam antenna will therefore not result in a proportional increase of received power, as the failure to capture all available power must be taken into account in the link budget. Generally accepted small-scale fade characterizations for sub-6 GHz bands, such as Rice/Rayleigh models may not apply to mmWave channels, often reported as being sparse19. Thus, gain degradation based on such assumptions may not hold, as in fact confirmed in this study. For example, two dominant wavefronts with similar amplitude within the antenna beamwidth will interfere constructively or destructively depending on their phase relation, resulting in large spatial-dependent fades. If instead the antenna beam is narrow enough to resolve the wavefronts as separate, it can only extract power from one of them, eliminating the position-dependent interference of the other, but losing its power contribution. This illustrates interaction between antenna patterns and multipath channels.

In contrast to deterministic antenna patterns and resulting gains observed in an anechoic chamber, the angular power spectra in multipath-rich indoor settings is best modeled as an angle-dependent random process, that also varies with position and environment. Thus, the power gain achieved by a directive antenna should be characterized as a position-dependent random variable. Moreover, this characterization will vary with the antenna beamwidth, since narrow-beam antennas are more likely to show the effect of power lost due channel angular spectrum exceeding antenna beamwidth. This may lead to a progressive departure of gain increases being inversely proportional to HPBW, i.e. a condition where antenna gain begins to saturate. Characterizing such gain saturation is one of the main objectives of this work.

To the best of our knowledge there is no published study that systematically quantifies effect of multipath on a collection of directive antennas of progressively narrower beamwidths as we here report. The parameter chosen to characterize this is the AG8,11, Departure of the AG from the value obtained in an anechoic chamber is our indicator of gain degradation and its increase as antenna HPBW narrows will indicate gain saturation. Given the large ensemble of our measurements, we are able to describe these effects statistically. This description, treated in detail in the last three sections, also leads to channel models that allow link budget predictions for users distributed within the type of indoor scenarios here considered.

We describe below relevant related studies, and their differences with respect to our work.

Related work

There are relatively few reported comparisons14,15 of the antenna gain degradation as a function of beamwidth for indoor scenarios. Yet, most mmWave channel studies assume the use of directive antennas8,16,20–24. For example, the study by Bechta et al8. at 28 GHz introduced the notion of an “effective antenna pattern,” i.e., the realized pattern produced by the interaction of the nominal radiation pattern with the channel’s power–angular spectrum. Across factories, offices, suburban fixed wireless areas, and Manhattan street canyons, they showed that angular spread can substantially reduce realized (effective) gain relative to the nominal value. However, the empirical surveys they summarize11,12,25,26predominantly employed a single 10° HPBW horn, limiting insight into how degradation scales with beamwidth. In contrast, we here quantify antenna-gain degradation and show that the achievable gain saturates as the beamwidth narrows due to antenna main lobe becoming comparable or smaller than the indoor-observed angular spread at 60 GHz. Measurements using rotatable narrow-beam antennas (HPBWs 10°, 7°, and 8°) at 28, 73, and 140 GHz for the characterization of directional path loss in building interiors were reported by Xing et al16. Their path-loss analysis de-embeds the antenna contribution by subtracting the horn gains. However, beamwidth-dependent gain degradation as we here present is not included in this work.

Work at 60 GHz by Manabe et al27. showed that antenna directivity and polarization choices markedly affect multipath statistics, particularly delay spread, but antenna gain degradation as a function of beamwidth for link budget calculations is not included. Shrivastava and Rao28 characterize link budgets and path loss at 60 GHz using hallway measurements with linear tapered slot antennas, but achievable gains across various antenna beamwidths is not treated.

The use of a diverse beamwidth antennas (≈ 3.4°, 22.5°, 45–90°, and 180°) for link-level indoor measurements at 60 GHz was reported by Sur et al29.. They discuss coverage, directional path loss, penetration losses, and how achievable rate and range scale when transmissions are limited by the United States Federal Communications Commission’s maximum radiated-power rules for 57–71 GHz devices. Antenna gain degradation due to multipath as a function of HPBW is not presented as a specific result of this work.

Indoor campaigns at 60 GHz by Geng et al10. used a broadband channel sounder with a rotatable narrow-beam horn (9.1° azimuth HPBW) and an omnidirectional biconical antenna to report directional and omnidirectional path-loss models, RMS delay-spread statistics, and direction-of-arrival profiles in rooms and corridors (LOS/NLOS). Antenna gain is treated as a fixed, known quantity (i.e., de-embedded from path-loss estimation) limited to the specific antennas used.

Gain degradation has been measured and modeled30 and15 at sub-6 GHz frequencies, for outdoor-to-indoor scenarios. In30gain degradation for wideband channels was characterized defining as parameter the “gain reduction factor” (GRF), but this did not include different-gain antennas as done in our work. Instead, Feick et al14. measured outdoor-to-indoor links using 3 directive antennas spanning azimuth HPBWs of 60°, 25°, and 15°. They found that the mean GRF (in dB) increases as the lobe narrows: in LOS, from 0.74 dB (60°) to 0.81 dB (25°) to 1.06 dB (15°); in NLOS, from 1.60 dB (60°) to 2.09 dB (25°) to 2.30 dB (15°). This trend indicates a modest rise in gain degradation with narrowing beamwidth, providing some evidence of saturation. The work we here present extends this study to the 60 GHz band using 5 antennas to illustrate the interaction with angular spread in indoor settings.

All above studies at least implicitly include gain degradation resulting from interaction between the angular spread and antenna pattern, but without a detailed study on how this effect varies as a function of antenna beamwidth. Moreover, the channel’s angular spread can be the result of very different conditions. For example, the channel may contain two dominant wavefronts separated in angle, as modeled by the two-wave with diffuse power (TWDP) model, introduced by31 and shown to provide a more accurate characterization of mmWave propagation than the Ricean model32. On the other hand, the channel may contain a bundle of many similar-in-power wavefronts. Our work addresses both issues through measurements with 5 antennas ranging from 60° to 6° HPBW and also evaluating different channel models to determine which shows the same interaction with antenna gain patterns as observed in our measurements.

Our contribution

We report on indoor narrowband measurements conducted with five directive antennas with HPBWs of 6°, 10°, 24°, 40°, and 60°, sweeping full angular scans in steps of 1°. All antennas have the same vertical beamwidth of 30 degrees to assure a fair comparison of the effect of azimuth angular spread. The measurements were done indoors in two buildings, resulting in a total of 961,560 power measurements as detailed in the next sections. The novel aspects of our work are summarized as follows:

Measurement equipment, scenario and procedure

Measurement equipment

Our channel sounder uses a continuous wave transmitter at 60 GHz described in13composed of a 15 GHz synthesizer and an active frequency multiplier as shown in Fig. 2a. The output power of this system is 20.5 dBm with a frequency stability such that drift does not exceed 1.2 kHz. The transmit (Tx) antenna is a 23.1 dB gain horn. The receiver (Rx) is based (Fig. 2b) on a rotating platform housing a portable spectrum analyzer (Anritsu-MS2760A33 with a 15 dB gain low noise pre-amplifier (LNA), cable-connected to a portable computer. The computer controls angular position through a stepper motor and a slotted disk allowing 1° angular resolution. At each slot the spectrum analyzer performs a 1,000-sample frequency scan over a 5 kHz span with a resolution bandwidth of 2 kHz. Each scan takes 250 milliseconds, and thus the total 360° measurement has a duration of 90 s. The measured receiver (Rx) noise floor under these conditions is −101 dBm. The choice of Rx span allows capturing signal power reliably making up for the drifts of the observed peak inherent to free-running oscillators.

Fig. 2Channel sounder system, (a) transmitter that consists of a continuous wave signal generator plus a frequency multiplier, (b) receiver based on a handheld spectrum analyzer with LNA and interchangeable horn antennas.

Channel sounder system, (a) transmitter that consists of a continuous wave signal generator plus a frequency multiplier, (b) receiver based on a handheld spectrum analyzer with LNA and interchangeable horn antennas.

The receive antennas were designed and constructed using the simulation software ANSYS HFSS34 and Computer Numerical Control (CNC) machined in 7020 Aluminum. The normalized power gain patterns obtained in an anechoic chamber are presented in Fig. 3. Equipment limitations restrict results to magnitude measurements. These closely match the full-wave simulations: The HPBW was found to differ from the design values by less than 10% and 2%, for the 6° and 60° antennas, respectively. The peak-gain deviation was less than 0.4 dB, and the RMS error of the main lobe within the − 10 dB contour less than 0.2 dB for the 60° antenna and less than 0.5 dB for the 6° horn.

Before initiating measurements at each environment, two calibration procedures were performed. First, the transmitter and the receiver system were connected directly with a short wave guide section and a calibrated variable attenuator, bypassing the antennas, to verify the expected received power. Next, the transmitter and receiver antennas were connected to the system and a free-space calibration at 2 m separation between antenna phase centers was performed in an anechoic chamber. Additionally, before connecting our custom made antennas we used a calibrated horn antenna at the Rx to verify that the path gain measured by the system in an anechoic chamber matched the value obtained from system specifications to within +/- 1dB. These procedures confirmed proper operation and allowed validating the dynamic range for reliable measurements. In all field measurements the received power peak was at least 20 dB above the noise floor. The operating parameters of the system are summarized in Table 1.

The measurement campaign was conducted in two buildings, each with one corridor including a 90° corner. Care was taken to assure no movement of objects or persons, resulting in static environments. In total, we measured 2,795 links using the five directive antennas, obtaining 360 power samples per rotation per link. This resulted in a total of 1,006,200 individual power measurements. The first corridor is 2 m wide and 102 m long, with a corner at 27 m. The interior walls and floor are concrete and ceramic. The second corridor is 4 m wide and 55 m long with a corner at 15 m, the site has plywood walls and wooden doors separating different classrooms. As described schematically in Fig. 4(a), the transmitter was placed at a fixed position in the center of the corridor with an antenna aimed along its central direction. The receiver with rotating antenna was moved in steps of 1 m along the corridor. To obtain statistics on the effect of small spatial displacements, for every LOS and NLOS position the receiver was displaced in steps of 10 cm from his central position Fig. 4(b), resulting in 5 measurements in a horizontal plane and 3 different heights.

Channel models

We here present physically plausible models capable of explaining the observed results for the LOS and the NLOS cases. We tested several classical options described below and based on the fit to data draw conclusion regarding likely the propagation mechanism for the respective type of scenario.

Diffuse channel model

We model the channel angular power response as containing only diffuse components with a Gaussian power spectrum, i.e. as described by (4).

All models are assumed to be time-invariant, i.e. the phases and amplitudes are location-dependent random variables but remain fixed during the antenna rotation.

Comparison of empirical results and simulation

This section presents how the model parameters were obtained, the simulation results, and a comparison between the empirical results with the simulations using Rician, TWDP and Diffuse models for each antenna The best fit models described in the tables below were obtained by minimizing the RMS error between the empirical CDF of AG and that resulting from the simulation models. We also plot the corresponding CDFs, which illustrates the gap between them at various percentile values.

Empirical LOS results and simulation

Using Matlab38, we generated 10,000 channel angular responses, for each model, with an azimuthal resolution of 3,600 components, which we convolved with the complex antenna pattern. The latter was obtained using ANSYS HFSS34, rotating the antenna on its phase center. The very close match between measured and simulated magnitude gain patterns validates the use of the simulation. From this we computed average, and peak received power to obtain effective gain using (1). We also confirmed that when using the measured gain pattern combined with the simulated phase, the resulting CDFs would not vary by more than 0.5 dB.

The CDF for the empirical results and for the Rician models (3) with gaussian-shaped multipath components are presented in Fig. 7. Nominal gains for each antenna measured in an anechoic chamber are indicated by vertical lines. For the models the parameters and the RMS errors for the best fit are listed in Tables 4 and 5, respectively. The parameters were found minimizing the RMSE between the empirical CDF and simulated CDF. An RMS angular spread of 10.2º, aligns most closely with our measurements for both LOS and NLOS propagation links. For the Rician case (3), the best fit K-factor, modeled as log-normally distributed, had a mean (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\upmu\:}$$\end{document}) of 4.3 dB and a standard deviation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\upsigma\:}$$\end{document}) of 1.2 dB. This model accurately explains our empirical results for all antennas with differences of less than 0.1 dB at the 10th and 50th percentiles and RMS errors less than 0.6 dB. The largest error at the 90th percentile occurs for the 60° antenna, but this is still less than 1 dB. The 10.2° RMS angular spread of diffuse components also matches our observed angular spectra, which we omit for compactness. A simple Rice model with isotropic diffuse components, while physically not justifiable, nevertheless resulted in RMS errors only 0.2 dB worse for all antennas. In contrast, the anisotropic diffuse condition is required to achieve a good match in the NLOS case, as will be seen in the next subsection. For consistency we thus consider anisotropic diffuse models in all cases.

From the Table 5 we see that the improvement in fit achieved by the TWDP (3) is negligible, less than 0.1 dB and that the diffuse model (4) does not offer a good fit for the 3 wide beamwidth antennas. Consistent with the assumption of a Rice channel we found that using only the magnitude antenna gain (i.e. constant phase) caused variations of less than 0.16 dB in the RMS error for the Rician and TWDP models. Instead, the antenna phase response had a much larger effect for the diffuse model due to interaction of multiple comparable power components within the main lobe, particularly for the 60° antenna which exhibits significant phase variation. This aspect is further discussed in the next section. We thus conclude that for the LOS indoor corridor, the simple Rician channel model combined with the scalar free-space antenna gain pattern adequately characterizes achievable antenna gains.

Table 6 shows the best-fit model parameters and Table 7 the RMS errors of fit. As seen the average values for the K-factor are now lower so that the RMS error will only decrease on average by 0.2 dB when ignoring dominant components. For simplicity, in what follows we only use the purely diffuse model (4). The CDF-based comparison of empirical results and simulation with the diffuse model (4) are shown in Fig. 8 for the five directive antennas. Anechoic chamber azimuth gains were included as before as vertical dotted lines. As show in Fig. 8, its CDF differs from the observed one by only 0.1 dB at the 50th percentile and 0.4 dB at the 90th percentile. At the 10th percentile, the differences do not exceed 1 dB. In contrast to the LOS case, assuming isotropic diffuse components results in a poor match to the observed CDF with RMS errors far greater than those listed in Table 7. For example, the best-fitting TWDP model with isotropic scattering was found to produce RMS errors of up to 2.3 dB for the 3 antennas with the larger beamwidths, the other models performing even worse.

Our simulations reproduce the empirical observation that the AG can in some instances exceed the value measured in an anechoic chamber. As shown Fig. 8, this occurs in 40% and 70% of realizations for the 40° and 60° antenna, respectively, while in only 5%, for the 6° and 10°. This higher effective gain stems from constructive/destructive interference that occurs when the channel includes multiple wavefronts of comparable amplitude, as opposed to the Rician case with a single strong component.

Thus, as the 60° antenna is scanned over the incoming wavefronts of the diffuse model, the phase variation introduced by the antenna will affect the vector sum of the wavefronts. In contrast, if the phase were constant, the result of the vector sum would only be scaled in amplitude by the modulus of the gain pattern. The effect of including phase is expected to be particularly noticeable off the HPBW, where its variation is largest. This was in fact confirmed by our simulation, a case of which is shown in Fig. 12. As seen, the angular fades of received power can result in a narrowing of the azimuth pattern and thus in AGs exceeding the nominal free-space value.

In our setup, accuracy limitations in the mounting of the antennas may result in offsets of the rotation center from the antenna phase center by no more than +/- 3 mm. Using ANSYS HFSS34 we found that this possible inaccuracy had no measurable effect on the variation of the phase pattern and thus would not affect the AG statistics we here presented.

Empirical average power deviation

The local average power obtained by rotating a directive antenna has been argued15 to be equivalent to spatial averaging. However, this may not hold for sparse channels as have been reported for mmWave19. For example, for the two-ray ground-reflection model, a deep power fade will remain so regardless of directive antenna’s aim, while a spatially displaced antenna can average out such fades, but only if displacements are larger than the coherence distance.

Additionally, it is also well-known that when using narrow-beam antennas, as will be the case at mmWave frequencies, the channel correlation distance is much greater than predicted by the Clarke-Jakes model, often being tens of wavelengths40. In our study focused on the effect of multipath we used a 10 cm (50 wavelengths) displacement of the receiver as a reasonable compromise between exceeding the shadow-fade correlation distance41 and a spacing too close to obtain uncorrelated multipath fades42. We observed that the average power obtained under such displacements of our rotating antenna resulted in power variations exceeding 3 dB in 50% of cases. It follows that the local average power must be considered a position-dependent random variable. Such random variations in average power are part of channel models being the deviations from a “slope and intercept” regression11. It is thus important to determine if these variations are correlated with the (random) AG or can be treated as independent when generating model results. Conclusion

Based on an extensive measurement campaign in indoor corridors at 60 GHz, several statistically reliable conclusions can be drawn. Gain degradation of directive antennas depends on two main factors, LOS vs. NLOS and antenna beamwidth. In the LOS case this degradation is less than 2 dB at the 95th percentile, for all antennas measured, the largest values occurring for the 6° antenna. In contrast, for the NLOS links the degradation can exceed 5 dB, being as expected highest for the narrowest beamwidths. We also find that while for LOS links average gain matches the free-space values to within 0.5 dB, this is no longer so for the NLOS case where gain saturation becomes evident. In fact, for such links average gain remains within 1 dB of nominal value for antennas with a beamwidth up to 24°, but starts to lag nominal values for narrower antennas, the difference reaching 2.4 dB for the 6° antenna. This is explained by the empirically based observation that in our setting the channel has a 10.2° RMS angular spread, which results in significant power outside the main lobe for the narrower beam antennas. The above findings are well explained by two types of models. For the LOS cases a Ricean channel with a normal distribution of K-factors results in less than 0.5 dB RMS error between the CDFs of measured and model-based antenna gains. Instead for the NLOS case, a diffuse channel model, based on many similar-power wavefronts provides an equally good fit for all antennas tested. Consistent with this, we also find that for the LOS cases it is enough to use the modulus of antenna gain, while for NLOS, including the antenna phase and the resulting constructive/destructive interference of multiple wavefronts is essential to achieve a good fit to observations.

It is worth noting that our observed RMS angular spread of approximately 10° differs substantially from the 50° RMS angular spread recommended by the widely used 3GPP TR 38.901 standard, which assumes a Gaussian azimuth spectrum. This difference underscores the importance of scenario-specific measurements for accurate channel modeling at mmWave frequencies, highlighting that general standards may not always reflect the particularities encountered in all indoor propagation environments.
