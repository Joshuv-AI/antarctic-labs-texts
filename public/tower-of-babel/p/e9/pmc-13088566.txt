Relationship between scapular dyskinesis test and lateral scapular slide test in office workers with chronic neck pain: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Seong Eun Moon, Young Kyun Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundScapular dyskinesis is associated with shoulder dysfunction and chronic neck pain, particularly in computer-based office workers. Although scapular dyskinesis and lateral scapular slide tests are commonly used in clinical settings, each has limitations when used independently. In addition, only a few studies have examined the clinical relationship between these two testing methods, particularly in individuals with chronic neck pain in whom scapular dysfunction is highly prevalent. This study aimed to examine the relationship between scapular dyskinesis and lateral scapular slide tests and explore their combined use for a comprehensive assessment of scapular dysfunction in individuals with chronic neck pain.MethodsThis was a cross-sectional study. Eighty-three office workers (49 males and 34 females) with chronic neck pain were recruited from three information technology companies in Seoul, Korea. The severity of scapular dyskinesis was assessed using a three-category classification scapular dyskinesis test (normal, subtle, and obvious), and the scapulothoracic distance was measured at three arm positions using the lateral scapular slide test. Neck disability index and visual analogue scale scores were also assessed. One-way ANOVA and correlation analyses (Kendall’s and Pearson’s) were used to examine the relationships between the variables.ResultsSignificant differences were found in the lateral scapular slide test position 2 distance between the scapular dyskinesis test grades in both the dominant (p = 0.0021) and non-dominant arms (p = 0.0195). In both the dominant and non-dominant arms, individuals with subtle or obvious scapular dyskinesis exhibited significantly greater scapulothoracic distances than those with normal scapular motion. A weak positive correlation was observed between scapular dyskinesis test severity and lateral scapular slide test position 2 in both arms (dominant, r = 0.22, p = 0.040; non-dominant, r = 0.21, p = 0.0492). Participants with scapular dyskinesis also had higher neck disability index scores on the dominant side (p = 0.0214), but no significant associations were found with the visual analogue scale.ConclusionsThe grade of dynamic scapular dyskinesis positively correlated with static scapulothoracic distance and neck disability. The combined use of the scapular dyskinesis test and lateral scapular slide test identifies both dynamic control deficits and static positional in office workers with chronic neck pain.Keywords: Scapular dyskinesis, Chronic neck pain, Computer-based office workers, Scapular dyskinesis test, Lateral scapular slide test

Background

Scapular dyskinesis (SD) is the loss of normal scapular control [1]. While SD is often associated with muscular imbalances, it can also stem from bony or articular origins, such as thoracic kyphosis or acromioclavicular joint injuries [2]. However, SD is not an injury and can be found in an asymptomatic population [3, 4]. The glenohumeral (GH) joint is the most mobile joint, and its stability is mostly dependent on soft tissues [5]. Positioned on the posterior thorax, the scapula connects the neck and shoulder to the trunk, providing both mobility and stability [6]. Therefore, the optimal position and function of the scapula are important for serving as an efficient bridge [3]. Abnormal scapular kinematics can increase mechanical strain on cervical structures. Altered scapular muscle function can cause mechanical dysfunction in the cervical region and neck pain [7]. SD is related to shoulder dysfunction [3]; however, recent studies have reported that high rates of SD are associated with CNP [8, 9]. Prolonged computer use can lead to poor posture, including forward head posture and protraction. Scapular protraction is defined as the anterior displacement of the scapula away from the vertebral column, often creating an imbalance in periscapular muscle activity [10–12]. Disturbed scapular muscles can cause mechanical dysfunction of the cervical spine, leading to neck pain [2]. Computer-based office workers with CNP showed a 91.3% prevalence of SD [9]. Additionally, a quasi-experimental study reported that scapular stabilization exercises were effective in decreasing pain in patients with CNP [13]. Karaağaç et al. (2023) recommended evaluating scapular muscle function for CNP due to high prevalence of SD in this population [14].

The scapular dyskinesis test (SDT) and lateral scapular slide test (LSST) are recommended to measure SD using scapular movement and position [15–17]. Although the tests were reported to be reliable, there are limitations in measuring SD [18]. The limitation of the SDT lies in its dependence on a clinician’s subjective visual assessment [15]. The SDT is a clinical assessment used to test SD by observing scapular movement during weighted shoulder flexion and extension to detect altered scapular movement [1]. The reliability of the SDT showed moderate to substantial agreement (κ = 0.48–0.61) [19]; however, the SDT is a qualitative method that depends on the examiner’s clinical experience [20]. Kibler et al. [21] recommended a four-pattern classification for the SDT; however, the severity of SD cannot be graded using this method [17].

The LSST is a quantitative method for assessing SD by measuring the distance between the spinous process of the thoracic spine and the inferior angle of the scapula in three shoulder positions [16]. The LSST provides objective results using a caliper measure of distance [22]. However, it only provides a static assessment of scapular position [16, 19]. Moreover, the LSST assumes that the contralateral scapula is normal [23]; therefore, it is limited for assessing SD [8]. In addition, while often reported as reliable, its consistency under different conditions has been questioned [24]. Paraskevopoulos et al. (2019) [19] suggested that both qualitative and quantitative measurements are necessary to assess and identify improvements in SD. Therefore, to build upon these complementary methods, a direct comparison is necessary, providing a stronger rationale for an integrated clinical approach [25]. Furthermore, the asymmetric functional demands of office work, particularly repetitive mouse use, may impose different neuromuscular control requirements on the dominant and non-dominant sides.

Both the SDT and LSST are widely used owing to their simplicity, noninvasiveness, and accessibility in clinical settings [15, 26]. The SDT enables the dynamic evaluation of scapular movement patterns, whereas the LSST provides a quantitative assessment of scapular position [1, 21]. However, few studies have examined the clinical relationship between these two methods, particularly in individuals with chronic neck pain (CNP), in whom scapular dysfunction is highly prevalent. An integrated approach combining dynamic and static assessments may offer a more comprehensive understanding of SD in this population. This distinction is critical to guide more specific treatment planning. Therefore, assessing SD in individuals with chronic neck pain (CNP) is important for understanding a potential contributor to their symptoms [2]. Given that computer-based office workers typically exhibit scapular protraction [10–12], this study aimed to investigate the relationship between the SD test and the LSST to assess SD using both qualitative and quantitative approaches in individuals with CNP. Our hypothesis was that increasing SD severity would be associated with a greater distance between the thoracic spine and the scapula in individuals with CNP.

Methods

Study population

This cross-sectional study was designed and reported in accordance with the STROBE guidelines [27]. Using convenience sampling, participants were recruited via email from three information technology companies in Seoul, Korea, between April and September 2023. The study included office workers aged 20–50 years who had experienced CNP for at least 3 months [28], regardless of whether the pain was unilateral or bilateral, and with a visual analogue scale (VAS) score of 3.5–8. This range was chosen to include participants with clinically relevant pain (mild to moderate) [28], while excluding those with severe pain (> 8) that might interfere with the physical performance tests. Those with a history of neck or shoulder fracture or surgery, scoliosis, upper body nerve injuries, current shoulder pain that required medical treatment, or a recent acute exacerbation of their chronic pain that hindered their ability to perform the tests were excluded. or participation in structured upper body resistance training more than twice per week within the last 6 weeks were excluded [29]. The presence of CNP was initially determined by participant self-report, in accordance with the inclusion criteria, and eligibility was subsequently confirmed through a screening interview conducted by a musculoskeletal rehabilitation trainer with 15 years of clinical experience. Participants who were unable to undergo the SDT or LSST were also excluded. To ensure sufficient statistical power and minimize Type II errors, G*Power software 3.1 was used to calculate sample size. The expected proportion (p) was set at 0.42 based on a previous study reporting a 42% prevalence of SD among office workers [30]. The precision (d) was set at 0.1. Considering a dropout rate of 5%, 83 participants were recruited. Informed consent was obtained prior to measurements. The Institutional Review Board of CHA University approved the study (1044308-202303-HR-070-02). The study protocol was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Protocol

Demographic data, including age, sex, height, and weight, were collected. The three-grade SDT [15] was used to assess SD, and the LSST was used to measure the distance between the thoracic spine and scapula [16]. The SDT was performed by a certified athletic trainer with 25 years of experience, and the LSST was performed by a musculoskeletal rehabilitation trainer with 15 years of experience. To ensure consistency, intra-rater reliability for each examiner was established on 10 participants prior to the main data collection, demonstrating substantial agreement for both the SDT (k = 0.78) and the LSST (k = 0.76) [9]. Evaluators were kept blinded to the measurement results obtained by other evaluators throughout all assessment procedures (Fig. 1).

Flow chart of measurement. SDT: Scapular Dyskinesis Test; LSST: Lateral Scapular Slide Test; NDI: Neck Disability Index; VAS: Visual Analogue Scale

SDT

Participants were asked to grasp dumbbells corresponding to their body weight (1.4 kg for those weighing < 68.1 kg and 2.3 kg for those weighing ≥ 68.1 kg), in accordance with the original standardized protocol for the SDT [15]. The participants were then asked to flex their shoulders with the elbow straight to 180° in the thumbs-up position for 3 s, then extend the shoulder to return to the neutral position for 3 s. The test was repeated five times [15] (Fig. 2). The clinician palpated the scapular movement bilaterally to determine the SD grade. No evidence of abnormality in scapular movement was rated as normal, mild abnormality as subtle, and apparent abnormal movement as obvious [15]. The reliability of the SDT with palpation has been reported to be moderate to substantial (k = 0.49–0.64) [19].

Scapular Dyskinesis Test. (a) Start position; (b) mid phase; (c) end position

LSST

The participants were asked to stand up, and three different shoulder positions were used to measure the distance between the thoracic spine and inferior angle of the scapula. The first position was the neutral position; the second was with the hands on the hips with the palm facing inferiorly; and the third was with the shoulder at 90° abduction and internal rotation of the thumb to point inferiorly [16]. A caliper (CAS, Yangju, Korea) was used to measure the distance from the T7 spinous process to the inferior angle of the scapula at all three positions [16, 26] (Fig. 3). All measurements were taken twice, and the mean was calculated for data analysis. A difference of ≥ 1.5 cm in any of the three positions was considered positive for LSST [31]; however, we used only quantitative distance data for analysis. The reliability of the LSST is high (intraclass correlation coefficient [ICC] > 0.87–0.92) [26].

Lateral Scapular Slide Test. (a) Position 1; (b) Position 2; (c) Position 3

Neck Disability Index (NDI)

Participants were asked to complete the NDI questionnaire. The NDI is widely used for neck pain and disability [32]. The Korean version of the NDI questionnaire was used, and its reliability was excellent (ICC > 0.93) [33]. The NDI is scored on a scale of 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating greater neck disability due to neck pain [34].

VAS for neck pain

Participants were asked to determine their eligibility for the study by recording the worst neck pain they had experienced over the past week on a 100-mm horizontal line. They were instructed to rate their pain on a scale from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst pain imaginable) [35].

Statistical analysis

All the statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess normality, and the quantitative variables were found to be normally distributed (p>0.05). Quantitative data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and qualitative data are presented as frequencies and percentages (%). The dependent variable was the SDT grade (normal, subtle, and obvious), and the distances of LSST positions 1, 2, and 3 were independent variables. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare LSST distance, age, sex, body weight, neck VAS, and NDI across SDT grades. Fisher’s Least Significant Difference (LSD) post-hoc test was used. The SDT results were ranked data, and the LSST results were scaled data. Therefore, the Kendall rank correlation was used to analyze the relationship between the SDT and LSST. Pearson’s correlation was used to analyze the results of the LSST, neck VAS, and NDI. Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen’s d to assess the magnitude of differences, with 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 representing small, medium, and large effect sizes, respectively. Dominant and non-dominant arms were analyzed separately. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Discussion

Our sample consisted of office workers with CNP, a population known to exhibit high rates of SD due to prolonged sedentary postures and repetitive tasks [9–12]. In this representative group, the primary finding was a significant positive correlation between the dynamic qualitative SDT grades and static quantitative LSST Position 2 distance in both the dominant (p = 0.040) and non-dominant (p = 0.049) shoulders.

Subtle and obvious SD demonstrated significantly greater scapulothoracic distances in the LSST position 2. These findings are consistent with previous research [9]. Kibler (1998) reported that LSST positions 1 and 2 were performed with the shoulder relaxed, inducing minimal muscle activation, whereas position 3 was performed with the scapular stabilizers activated and the hands unsupported [16], increasing the likelihood of scapular control. The LSST assesses the bilateral distance between the thoracic spine and scapular inferior angle in three different shoulder positions, and a side-to-side difference > 1.5 cm is used to identify SD [31]. This approach assumed that the opposite shoulder was normal [23]. However, a previous study found that 72.48% of computer programmers exhibit bilateral SD [9]. Moreover, the SD rates were significantly higher with the SDT (89.9%) than with the LSST (23%) in the same population [9]. The LSST is a static posture measurement; therefore, scapular motion cannot be evaluated [19, 36]. SD should be evaluated based on dynamic movement patterns [37]. Although computed tomography has been proposed as a highly accurate tool for assessing scapular position [38, 39], limitations in cost and accessibility may hinder its practical application, and, like the LSST, it is also a static posture measurement [40]. Nevertheless, the LSST demonstrates high inter-rater reliability (ICC > 0.87) [26] and serves as a simple yet reliable method for quantitative assessment using basic tools such as a tape measure or caliper [21]. Therefore, combining the SDT (qualitative method) and LSST (quantitative method) offers a practical, clinically feasible approach for SD evaluation in CNP with SD [25].

A positive correlation was observed between the SDT grades and LSST position 2 distance in both the dominant (p = 0.040) and non-dominant (p = 0.049) shoulders. This suggests that an underlying neuromuscular deficit manifests across both static and dynamic conditions, providing a key clinical application. The quantitative LSST can serve as an objective marker to monitor progress for a qualitative SDT classification. These findings suggest that severe SD grades in office workers with CNP are associated with increased scapulothoracic distance in LSST position 2. This may reflect a loss of control over scapular positioning under both static and dynamic conditions, leading to scapular protraction [41]. Yildiz et al. (2019) reported a significant reduction in middle trapezius activity in SD compared with that in normal scapular movement [8]. Scapular protraction with SD increases the distance between the scapula and thoracic spine [38, 39]. This may be related to the weakness and lengthening of the middle trapezius and rhomboid muscles. SD involves an excessively protracted scapula at rest or during arm movement [3]. The protracted position is characterized by anterior displacement of the scapula [42], which is often associated with imbalances in the serratus anterior and pectoralis minor muscles [43]. Scapular stabilization relies on the coupled forces of the trapezius, serratus anterior, and rhomboid muscles [44]. Although muscle activity was not directly measured, the increased scapulothoracic distance observed in our study aligns with previous findings linking SD to such muscular deficits [45, 46]. This suggests that the static positional faults measured by the LSST may reflect underlying issues with muscle performance. The rhomboid muscle particularly contributes to the mediolateral control of scapular motion [3]. Chaudhary et al. (2024) suggested that an effective shoulder SD rehabilitation emphasized rhomboid-focused training [47]. Based on the present findings, office workers with CNP and more severe SD showed a greater distance between the scapula and thoracic spine. Further research is warranted to evaluate the effects of rhomboid and middle trapezius-centered interventions for treating SD in office workers with CNP.

A significant finding of this study was the association between the SDT on the dominant arm and higher NDI scores (p = 0.021). Office workers with subtle or obvious SD in our study reported greater neck disability (mean NDI > 10) compared to a previous LSST-based study in the same population (mean NDI 4.78) [30], suggesting a potentially more impaired cohort. This association on the dominant side may be explained by the specific functional demands of office workers, as prolonged mouse use involves sustained low-level contraction of the upper trapezius and levator scapulae, which directly connect the scapula to the cervical spine [29, 48]. In addition, repetitive dominant-arm use has been shown to alter resting scapular alignment, resulting in greater internal rotation and anterior tilt on the dominant side compared to the non-dominant side [23], potentially increasing mechanical loading on the cervical region during arm elevation. This can lead to altered neuromuscular timing, a dynamic deficit captured by the SDT that manifests as functional disability (NDI) [15, 34]. Notably, the static LSST distances on this arm were not correlated with NDI, suggesting that dynamic motor control is a more critical factor for function on the dominant side. In contrast, a different biomechanical pattern emerged on the non-dominant side, with a significant negative correlation between LSST Position 3 and neck VAS scores. This finding may be related to posture-related pain [49] and may indicate that the non-dominant arm, which may have less refined neuromuscular efficiency than the dominant arm, is more susceptible to fatigue during scapular stabilization under a demanding static load, thereby contributing to neck pain [50]. Unlike in athletes, where SD is often associated with repetitive high-load activity [51, 52], for office workers, these deficits may develop from prolonged sitting and concomitant muscular imbalances [1, 53]. The SDT measures dynamic stability, requiring coordinated activation of muscles such as the serratus anterior and rhomboid [37]. while the LSST measures static positional differences [22, 26]. In aggregate, our distinct bilateral findings underscore the value of a comprehensive evaluation that assesses both dynamic control and static stability to fully understand the clinical presentation of CNP in this population.

A significant association was observed between SDT severity in the dominant arm and height (p = 0.038), consistent with previous studies suggesting that anthropometric factors may influence upper-extremity kinematics [54]. By contrast, no such association was found on the non-dominant side. While this discrepancy might reflect the generally lower functional demand placed on the non-dominant limb, the precise biomechanical explanation remains unclear. Therefore, this isolated finding should be interpreted with caution until it can be confirmed by further research. No significant differences were found in age, sex, body weight, or neck VAS scores among the normal, subtle, and obvious SD groups. Although female office workers are known to experience neck pain more frequently than males [55], our findings did not reveal significant sex differences in the neck VAS scores among individuals with SD. In athletes, repetitive overhead motions and muscle fatigue increase the prevalence of SD [56, 57]. By contrast, computer-based office workers are more likely to adopt prolonged postures involving a forward head position and rounded shoulders, which can alter scapular kinematics and muscle activation, thereby contributing to the development of SD [58]. Unlike athletes, office workers do not typically perform overhead activities, suggesting that reliance on the SDT alone may be insufficient for identifying SD in this population. Therefore, combining the static quantitative assessment of LSST position 2 with dynamic qualitative SDT may provide a more comprehensive evaluation of SD by accounting for both resting posture and movement characteristics in office workers with CNP. Further research is needed to explore the underlying mechanisms of SD in computer-based office workers and develop effective interventions that address both static and dynamic contributors.

This study has several limitations. First, the absence of an asymptomatic control group prevented a direct comparison of scapular kinematics between office workers with and without CNP. Second, because the participants were limited to office workers with CNP, the current findings may not apply to other populations, such as athletes or individuals with shoulder pathologies. Additionally, the SDT has been used to classify SD, although there is currently no established gold standard for its diagnosis. The three-grade classification system (normal, subtle, and obvious) used in this study demonstrated good reliability (ICC = 0.86) [59]; however, its interpretation still depends on the evaluator. Furthermore, individual anthropometric differences were not controlled for by normalization by height, and personal factors such as occupational tasks, prior sports participation, and sex-specific differences were not accounted for. Moreover, we analyzed data by shoulder dominance rather than by the side of reported neck pain. Given that CNP can be diffuse or bilateral, this approach was chosen for consistency, but a future study analyzing by symptomatic side could yield more specific insights. Finally, all participants were recruited from Seoul, South Korea, which may limit the external validity of the findings to other regions or populations. Future research should build on these findings by including asymptomatic control groups for comparison, employing longitudinal designs to investigate causal relationships, and exploring the effectiveness of interventions targeting both static posture and dynamic scapular control in this population.

Conclusion

This study identified a significant correlation between the dynamic SDT grades and static LSST Position 2 distances in office workers with CNP. These findings empirically support the combined use of the SDT and LSST for a more comprehensive clinical assessment of SD in office workers with CNP. The significant correlation between the qualitative, dynamic SDT and the quantitative, static LSST suggests that clinicians can use both tests synergistically. The SDT is used to classify the functional movement pattern, and the LSST serves as an objective marker for monitoring therapeutic progress. Clinically, these results suggest that rehabilitation programs for this population may benefit from including exercises that aim to improve dynamic scapular control, as dynamic deficits appeared to be linked to functional disability in our observations. These findings highlight the importance of evaluating both static postural alignment and dynamic scapular control in clinical assessments. Future research should build on these findings by including asymptomatic control groups for comparison, employing longitudinal designs to investigate causal relationships, and exploring the effectiveness of interventions targeting both static posture and dynamic scapular control in this population. As the findings of this study are limited to the specific population of office workers, further research on diverse populations is warranted [60].
