Caregivers’ perceptions, beliefs and behavior influence dental caries experience in children with autism spectrum disorder: A qualitative study
Authors: Ninuk Hariyani, Oktarina, Lily Azura Shoaib, Maryani Mohamed Rohani, Kamal Maher Batra Hanna, Hyewon Lee
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesWhile many children with autism spectrum disorders (ASD) experience dental caries, some of them are caries-free. This study aimed to understand the caregivers’ oral health perceptions, beliefs, and behavior that could influence dental caries experience in children with ASD.MethodsThe qualitative study was part of a study employing a mixed-methods design conducted to explore the severity and risk factors for caries experience. Individual interviews and two focus group discussions (FGD) involving caregivers of children with ASD aged 5–18 years were conducted. The caregivers’ perception of caries risks, oral care beliefs and behavior were explored. Interviews and FGDs recordings were transcribed and then analyzed thematically by two independent coders.ResultsTwenty-two caregivers provided their thoughts. The study found that caries experience was influenced by fatalistic belief, low self-efficacy and control. Caregivers of children with dental caries thought that caries is not dangerous. Caregivers experienced social pressure to provide their ASD children with what they want. Conversely, Caregivers of caries-free ASD children thought that strict commitment from an early age was needed to prevent caries.ConclusionsOur findings support that caregivers’ perceptions, beliefs and behavior influence dental caries experience in children with ASD which might need the clinicians’ consideration.Keywords: Caries, Autism spectrum disorder, A qualitative study, Child oral health, Caregivers

Introduction

Autism spectrum disorders (ASD) refers to disorders with varying severity, marked by impairments in language, social interaction, and a limited repertoire of activities and interests. The etiology of autism is unknown but is likely to have multiple etiologies including genetic factors. The number of children diagnosed with ASD has increased. It is estimated that 1 in every 68 children in the United States has been identified with ASD (Centre for Disease Control and Prevention, 2016). As a systematic review indicated, the ASD prevalence in Asia is 0.36 % (Qiu et al., 2020). Children with ASD might have a higher caries risk considering their sensitivity issues for touch, tooth brushing or tooth paste, lower motor function and ability to perform quality tooth brushing, sugar-rich and soft food preference, frequent use of food as rewards or habitual food pouching (Junnarkar et al., 2023).

Many studies have demonstrated that children with ASD show poor oral health, indicated by the prevalence and severity of caries experience, ranging from 21 % to 79 % (DeMattei et al., 2007, Hariyani et al., 2019, Jaber, 2011, Kopycka-Kedzierawski and Auinger, 2008, Marshall et al., 2010). Conversely, some other studies do not agree with these results considering they found similar caries experience in children with ASD compared with neurotypical children (Fahlvik-Planefeldt and Herrström, 2001, Loo et al., 2008, Morinushi et al., 2001). These findings suggest that caries experiences in children with ASD vary and are worth exploring further to understand the factors behind such contradictory observations and might suggest potential caries prevention interventions for children with ASD.

Research has shown that caries is a multifactorial disease. Research among general children has revealed that caregivers’ role is a significant factor influencing children’s health behaviors, which further influences children’s health. Caregivers, as primary caretakers, significantly influence their children's oral health through their beliefs, behaviors, and preventive practices (Saied-Moallemi et al., 2008, Olak et al., 2018). Their low self-efficacy, dental knowledge, and higher parenting stress are linked to increased caries rates in preschool children. However, the influence of parental knowledge and attitudes on the oral and dental health status of ASD children appear to be contradictory (Salmeh et al., 2023), in which the majority of cross-sectional studies showed no association, while the experimental studies reported significant oral health improvements following educational interventions; suggesting a strong correlation. Furthermore, research on the influence of caregivers’ oral health perceptions, beliefs and behavior on ASD children’s oral health is scarce.

A qualitative research design can offer a more profound understanding of the perceptions, beliefs, and behaviors of caregivers of children with ASD, as well as explore their experiences in caring for these children. This qualitative study is part of a study employing multi-methods design aimed to explore caregivers’ oral health perceptions, beliefs and behavior that could influence dental caries experience in children with ASD.

Methods

Study design and ethical considerations

This qualitative study included in-depth interviews and a less than two-hour focus group discussion among caregivers of children with ASD aged 5–18 years in order to explore caregivers’ oral health perceptions, beliefs and behavior of dental caries risks in greater depth. The study protocol received approval from the Ethics Committee of Dental Medicine Faculty, Universitas Airlangga, adhering to the Declaration of Helsinki (World Health Organization, 2001). Caregivers were guaranteed data confidentiality for research purposes. Written informed consent was obtained from all caregivers of children with ASD after explaining the study and providing an information sheet. This study report conforms to the recommendations provided by the COREQ 32 guidelines (Tong et al., 2007).

Participants and sampling

Caregivers were recruited from samples of the quantitative study aimed to investigate factors influencing dental caries severity among Indonesian children with ASD, conducted in Surabaya Indonesia (Hariyani et al., 2019). The recruitment of participants was strongly supported by several autism organizations and autism rehabilitation centers in Surabaya, East Java, Indonesia.

The sampling frame consisted of child-caregiver dyads registered at Surabaya's autism rehabilitation centers in East Java Province, Indonesia. Using cluster sampling based on autism rehabilitation centers, a quarter of the caregivers were invited, with 22 voluntarily taking part in in-depth interviews or focus group discussions. Participants of this study were parents of children with autism.

Data collection

Caregivers of free caries (DMFT+dmft and PUFA+pufa = 0) and caries group (DMFT+dmft and PUFA+pufa > 0) of children with ASD were the study participants. Six caregivers each for both free caries and caries group of children with ASD followed an individual in-depth interview with the researchers. Each interview lasted from 30 to 60 min. Ten other caregivers from both groups of free caries and caries children who could not make a time for an individual interview and indicating a willingness to participate in a focus group discussion (FGD), were invited to participate in the FGD. Two different FGD sessions were conducted, while each FGD was conducted in 1.5 and 2 h. The focus group was kept small to ensure active participation from all members. Following the individual in-depth interviews and FGD sessions and analysis of data, the data saturation has been reached and thus, no further participants were required. Data saturation occurs when all relevant concepts have been identified, explored, and no new insights emerge from the data (Bryant and Charmaz, 2010).

For this stage of the research, a guide for semi-structured interviews was developed to enable participants to discuss issues within a specific topic. The guide addressed caregivers oral health perceptions, beliefs and behavior concerning dental caries risks.

Focus groups were selected to enable parents to share common experiences, fostering a more comprehensive discussion than individual interviews. Semi-structured FGDs, guided by the study's focus, promoted interactive discussions among participants and were led by experienced researchers. The interview questions aimed to uncover caregivers' views on dental caries risks in ASD children. Questions were developed by the researchers, combining dental public health and psychological perspectives. The FGD moderators started the session by outlining its aims and initiating discussions with the first semi-structured question. The question list acted as a guide, allowing for exploration of additional experiences based on participants' responses. Interviews and FGDs were audio-recorded and transcribed whereas transcripts were checked for consistency in preparation for data analysis.

Data analysis

The demographic characteristics of the caregivers of the ASD children with autism (such as age, gender, race/ethnicity and socioeconomic status) were not provided because it was intended to capture a broaden phenomenon that come from caregivers' voice of their oral health perceptions, beliefs and behaviors. The only differentiation used in the analysis was the children’s caries experience (caries vs non-caries group).

Data were analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis (Braun and Clarke, 2019) with an inductive approach to enable the free emergence of new themes without being bounded by existing concepts and this approach believed to achieve the aims of our study. Thematic analysis was conducted in six phases (Braun and Clarke, 2006) and the thematic analysis was facilitated by using NVivo 12 software (QSR International Pty Ltd., 2017). Transcripts from the individual in-depth interviews and the FGDs were analyzed by two independent research teams. Each research team familiarized themselves with the transcripts, independently generated codes, and then collaborated to refine the code list. Using a combined set of codes, team members individually recoded the transcripts before discussing and resolving any coding discrepancies. This process led to a comprehensive understanding of caregivers’ beliefs and behaviors regarding children’s dental caries. Themes were developed to capture the majority of responses, with sub-themes highlighting differing participant perspectives. Direct disagreements were noted in the findings. Caregivers’ perceptions were analyzed separately for ASD children with and without caries. The analysis culminated in a comprehensive report of the findings.

To ensure rigor in the qualitative research, some strategies to ensure the credibility and trustworthiness of the findings (Sago, 2023) were implemented. Research rigor was ensured through triangulation, gathering data from various sources such as interviews and focus group discussions to validate the consistent and reliable findings. Additionally, reflexivity was promoted by transparently discussing codes and themes to minimize bias and achieve consensus. Data saturation guaranteed a comprehensive exploration of identified themes.

Results

A total of 22 caregivers provided data from both an individual interview and focus group discussions were included in our analysis. Different themes emerged from caregivers’ of caries-free and carious ASD children during the in-depth interview and focus group discussion. Fig. 1 showed the diagram of the study’s finding. The thematic analysis revealed one overarching theme for each of the themes; ‘Caries risk – a combination of fatalistic beliefs, low self-efficacy, and control’ was the overarching theme revealed among caregivers of carious ASD children while ‘Caries prevention – strict for the future’ was the overarching theme revealed among caregivers of caries-free ASD children’.

A diagram of the study’s finding.

Caries – a combination of fatalistic believe and low self-efficacy and control

The theme ‘caregivers’ oral health perceptions, beliefs and behavior in carious ASD children’ consists of four sub-themes: (1) Bad teeth are not problems as long as they function. (2) caries is a common condition. (3) no brushing or sweet was needed to muffle a bigger problem and (4) social pressure.

Bad-teeth are not problems as long as they function

Some parents noted that dental caries is not concerning unless it causes chewing difficulties or pain. They only view it as problematic when eating or drinking becomes painful due to food getting stuck in cavities. Toothache prompts dental care, but generally, caries is not a significant worry in daily life. Respondent mother of two years old carious girl with ASD said:

“As long as it was not hurt, I am not bother with her tooth decay. When it hurt her, I will bring her to the dentist. It is common to see kids with such conditions here. Kids still can chew, so there is no problem at all.”

Caries is a common condition

Some parents thought that caries is a common condition. They also thought that caries is not dangerous. One mother said that:

“Many of children have caries. I think it is a normal condition, and I never heard that there are any problems come out of it. All of my nieces also have caries.”

As they thought that it is not serious for kids to have caries, they felt sorry to prevent it by reducing the kid’s sweet consumption. One caregiver revealed her feeling about limiting her kid’s sugary food as follow:

“I feel pity of her, and sometimes feel guilty. I cannot take away something that makes her happy (sugary snacks). I think all people will do the same. Moreover, no bad thing will happen (due to this behavior).”

No toothbrushing or giving sweets to muffle a bigger problem

Some parents explained that they struggled in handling their kids to brush their teeth. Some kids with ASD refused to brush their teeth for many reasons. Some of them do not want to open their mouth, just playing with water for so long, uncontrollable or just getting angry. Finally, the parents surrendered and allowed them not to brush their teeth. A mother of two kids (5 years old child with ASD and 5 months old younger child) explains:

“I am quite busy with the little one, if he (the older kid with ASD) started to make a problem due to teeth brushing, I cannot handle both. I just want peace at home.”

“It is getting hectic sometimes. Many times, he just got angry if I ask him to open his mouth (to brush his teeth). He started to cry and make a problem in the bathroom, and it will be dangerous for him as bathroom usually slippery… In that case I usually allowed him not to brush his teeth.”

Some parents also reported difficulties in preventing eating sugary snacks in children with ASD. The parents felt unable to manage their child's cravings for sugary snacks, prompting them to ultimately give in to their children's desires.

“She always cries, and she will be angry if I restrict her from sugary snacks. I don’t have any choice to make her stop crying except giving her what she wants. If I don’t do that, she will keep crying all day.”

Social pressure

Furthermore, some caregivers feel that there was a kind of family or social pressure to allow the kids with ASD to get what they want. A mother said:

“It was difficult to be strict to this (ASD) child. I do not want to be that cruel to her. We always be a point of attention of others, whether among family members or society. If I am too strict, what will people think of me. Being so cruel to the kid like her…”

Caries prevention – Strict for the future

The overarching theme for ‘caregivers’ oral health perceptions, beliefs and behavior in caries-free ASD children’ consists of four themes: (1) prevent tooth ache from earlier age. (2) reduced sweet and tooth brush as daily habit. (3) once you allowed–kids learn and (4) parental commitment is needed.

Prevent tooth ache from earlier age

Caregivers of caries-free ASD children have perceived important believe that tooth ache could be prevented from the earlier aged by good oral health behavior such as tooth brushing habit. This understanding keeps them motivated to develop a good behavior in the ASD children despite all the difficulties faced. One caregiver said that she spends much of her time helping her kid to do the brushing.

“I always help her brushing her teeth. If she did it by her own, she will not brush them properly. It does take much of my time. I hope later she will be able to do it (by her own).”

“Dentist said that I have too much problems with my teeth. The dentist taught me how to brush my teeth correctly. So I applied it to my child.”

“As he already common with brushing activity before bed, he often reminds me of it. Sometimes when I feel lazy to brush his teeth, he will complain. As it was kept in his mind that not brushing teeth was gross and let his mouth smelly.”

Reduced sweet and tooth brush as daily habit

Caregivers of caries-free children with ASD revealed that they had set daily tooth brushing and reduced sugary snacks as daily habit for their children.

“We set rules that no sugary snacks at night as only dinner was allowed. He can eat snack but not the sweet one.”

However, many of the caregivers who set rules of reducing sweet for the ASD children did not do it for reason of caries prevention. Some of them who did it said that their child has a problem such as allergy to sugary food.

“I restricted the amount of sugary snacks to my kid, as she was sensitive with sweet. She was easily got pharyngitis, soon afterwards.”

Once you allowed –kids learn

Many caregivers stated that they need to strict to the rules they set up for their ASD children. They noticed that kids are good learner who learn fast from their experiences. One mother said:

“I let her cry (as long as she keeps brush her teeth)… I have set the rule not allow her to eat sugary food at night since she was young. If it is done during the day, she still can brush her teeth afterwards, but if it is at night, it will be difficult to ask her to brush her teeth again.”

A working mother of three children (the youngest child with ASD) said that she can replicate revealed:

“I give extra care for my youngest one. In the beginning, I need to push her to do tooth brushing activity, especially at night. However, as we trained her since the early age, now she has memorized it. This strategy works with my previous children, and it work for her too. It just needs a longer process.”

Some caregivers revealed that they need to be strict in keeping the rules for their ASD children, as children are good learners.

“If I let her not to brush her teeth, she must think that it is allowed. And she must ask it again later. It will ruin all the exercise we have. We need to train her again from the beginning.”

“Once we give it (the sugary snacks), she will think that it is allowed.”

“Even he cried, I need to be strict. Sometime it is hard as well as people will think that I am a strict and cruel mother, but if I allowed him (not to brush his teeth), he will keep do it again and again.”

Parental commitment needed

Rearing a kid needs strong commitment.

“All of the family members are unified, especially his father. At night after brushing the teeth, no more candy or sugary food allowed.”

“At the beginning, I had a problem when my mother in law came. She often allowed everything that my children’s want. I am lucky that my husband has the same commitment with me. I let him talk to her mum, and now all has set up.”

The commitment sometime comes from their own experiences with caries, as stated by a mother:

“I have very bad teeth. Since I was in the kinder garden, my teeth already have caries. I really look after my children’s teeth, as I know that the pain from caries is very hurt. I do not want my children feel the same pain.”

Discussion

This qualitative study aimed to explore how caregivers’ oral health perceptions, beliefs, and behaviors impact dental caries in children with autism spectrum disorder (ASD). Through in-depth interviews and focus group discussions with caregivers of children with and without caries, thematic analysis revealed differences in their attitudes and behaviors regarding caries. Caregivers of children with caries exhibited fatalistic beliefs and low self-efficacy, while those with caries-free children focused on strict preventive measures for the future. These findings can inform tailored educational programs and support for caregivers of children with ASD. The qualitative approach adopted enabled us to explore in-depth the parenting perception, believe and behaviour in relation to dental caries occurrence in two different groups of ASD children: those with carious teeth and those who were caries-free. Our results showed that caries occurrence is a combined results of fatalistic believe and low self-efficacy and control by the ASD children’s caregivers. Parents of kids in the caries group thought that caries is not dangerous and will not be a problem since children still able to chew their food. This finding was consistent with previous research (Schroth et al., 2007). Parents with these believes tend to neglect the caries and unhealthy behaviors leading to it. Children with ASD often encounter challenges such as social communication difficulties, repetitive behaviors, sensory sensitivities, cognitive and learning problem, behavioral issues, anxiety and depression, as well as challenges in daily living, which might lead their caregivers to pay less attention to oral health, considering their believe that it is less important.

During child rearing, food is one of the “rewards” most often used to modify child’s behavior. Thus, parents thought of it as a useful strategy to muffle a bigger problem in the ASD children with caries experience. Candy, gum, carbonated beverages or sweetened juices have traditionally been used as reinforcement in the behavior modification programs for children with behavioral disorders or mental impairments due to their low cost and high appeal (Entwistle and Casamassimo, 1981). Children with ASD having a strong affinity for sweets (Kakodkar et al., 2012, O'Brien and Whitehouse, 1990) despite it is known to cause poor health outcomes. Considering this, the caregivers should take responsibility on restricting the use of food as a modifying behavior tool for their kids and they should look for an alternative to it. It was surprising that caregiver of children with ASD expressed that caries is not a serious condition as long as not causing a problem with daily functions. The believe might lead to disease progression and hospitalization (Azimi et al., 2022). Such caregivers seemed not received adequate oral health education and being aware that ASD children are different and thus, could not communicate that they have a problem. These findings might be used to develop oral health educational programs centered to the needs of caregivers of ASD children.

Parents of ASD children without dental caries emphasized the necessity of early commitments to prevent caries, stressing the importance of strict oral hygiene and diet control. They attributed their children's cavity-free status to proper oral health education and early behavioral training. Caregivers believed in the effectiveness of teaching children from a young age, underscoring the importance of initiating oral hygiene practices early for optimal oral health.

While parenting style of caregiver was known to influence disease outcomes, this study revealed that permissive type of parenting among ASD children leads to caries development whereas the authoritative and authoritarian parents seem to be more control in caries prevention. The finding was in accordance with the findings of previous study among Indian children (Viswanath et al., 2020). However, this permissive type of parenting among parents of ASD children seems to be influenced by social support and pressure. Thus, it should be bear in mind that providing good support for parent with ASD children might help in preventing caries among ASD children.

Even though qualitative research has a limitation in terms of generalizability, it is hoped that the broad themes found and discussed in this research may offer better picture in understanding the risk of developing dental caries among children with autism spectrum disorder in relation to their parents’ perception, believe and behaviour. The findings from this study could also provide the foundation for developing a questionnaire for a quantitative study aimed at generalizing the results. Another limitation might be not stratifying the findings according to ASD severity considering it is outside the scope of this study given our broad aim.

On the other hand, the current study has several strengths. For instance, the reliability and validity in qualitative research considering its importance (Silverman, 2013) has been established. For instance, inter-rater reliability (Mason, 2018) was assessed by involving a second researcher in reading through transcripts and discussing codes and themes. To increase the validity of the findings, a form of respondent validity (Silverman, 2013) was employed, where later interviewees were asked more direct questions relating to ideas and themes that had been developed from earlier analysis—an approach known as members’ check. Besides, triangulation was used by combining data from two sources: in-depth interviews and FGDs within two different groups of caregivers as well as using reflexivity by transparently discussing the codes and themes in meetings to reduce bias and reaching consensus. Moreover, data saturation has been reached which ensured no gaps in the identified themes (Sago, 2023). Additionally, the voices of the carers of ASD children in Indonesia which is limited in the available literature could be reported. The findings addressed the gap in knowledge on how the caregivers’ parenting perception, believe and behavior that could influence dental caries experience among children with ASD by comparing them across caregivers of ASD children with caries and those who were caries-free. Furthermore, the findings might be used to design oral health education and parental support programs to be provided to caregivers of ASD children.

Conclusion

Caregivers’ perceptions, beliefs and behavior influence dental caries experience in children with ASD. Caregivers of children with ASD who had dental caries exhibited a combination of fatalistic beliefs and low self-efficacy and control. They false believed that caries was not a serious health issue, and there was no compliance with toothbrushing or sweet restrictions in response to social pressure. Conversely, caregivers of caries-free children with ASD adopted early and strict caries-prevention strategies for better oral health outcomes. They were committed to preventing dental caries from an earlier age, reducing sugar intake, and enabling their children to develop daily toothbrushing routines. Our findings might help clinicians design and implement educational programs that are centered on the needs of caregivers of ASD children as well as provide the needed support.
