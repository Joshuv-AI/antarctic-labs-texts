Ultrasound assessment of pleural effusion volume in clinical practice: a narrative review
Authors: Lucía Ferreiro, Carlos Zamarrón, María E Toubes, Nuria Rodríguez-Núñez, Luis Valdés
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivePleural effusion (PLE) is a common clinical condition with a broad spectrum of underlying etiologies. Accurate estimation of pleural fluid (PF) volume can support clinical decision-making, particularly in critically ill patients, by guiding invasive procedures, monitoring treatment response, and informing prognosis. This review aims to provide an overview of the main ultrasound (US) techniques for estimating PLE volume and to discuss their limitations in routine practice.MethodsA narrative review of the literature was conducted, synthesizing the most relevant evidence identified through structured manual searches and consultation of authoritative sourcesKey Content and FindingsComputed tomography (CT) is considered the reference standard for volumetric assessment, but it is costly, exposes patients to ionizing radiation, and often requires transfer, which may carry additional risks. Multiple sonographic approaches are available for estimating PLE volume, including linear methods based on interpleural distances and more complex multiplanar models. Introduction

Pleural effusion (PLE) is a common clinical finding, with an estimated prevalence of 400 cases per 100,000 inhabitants and more than 60 possible etiologies, including pleural, pulmonary, and systemic diseases, multiorgan failure, and adverse drug reactions (1,2). The diagnostic approach should be integrated, considering clinical history, pleural fluid (PF) characteristics, microbiological and cytohistological studies of the fluid and pleural tissue, as well as imaging findings (2).

In specific clinical scenarios, quantifying PLE volume can provide relevant information. Computed tomography (CT) is regarded as the most accurate method for estimating PF volume (3,4); however, it is costly, involves exposure to ionizing radiation, and often requires patient transfer, which may carry additional risks (5). In recent years, ultrasonography has emerged as a reliable alternative. In a meta-analysis of 12 studies including 1,554 patients (42% with PLE), the sensitivity and specificity of ultrasound (US) for the detection of PLE were 94% and 98%, respectively, values that were significantly higher than those of chest radiography (51% and 91%, respectively) (6). Nevertheless, these results should be interpreted in the context of selected populations and experienced operators and may not reflect performance across all clinical settings. Thoracic US has been incorporated as an extension of the bedside clinical examination, allowing systematic evaluation of the pleural space in diverse clinical contexts (7). US can detect fluid collections as small as 5 mL, compared with approximately 150 mL required for radiographic detection (8). Furthermore, it shows high correlation with CT, is non-invasive, rapid, and can be performed at the bedside (9). However, while evidence supporting US for PLE detection is robust, volume estimation requires additional technical considerations and has specific limitations that must be recognized.

The aim of this manuscript is to provide a concise summary of practical principles, main US techniques, and their limitations for estimating PLE volume, with a clinical focus on routine practice, rather than an exhaustive literature review. We present this article in accordance with the Narrative Review reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1-0375/rc).

Methods

This review was conducted to determine the role and limitations of US in estimating PLE volume. A PubMed search and a complementary manual search were performed based on the recommendations of the Spanish and British scientific societies of pulmonology (1,2). The search strategy also combined MeSH terms and free-text keywords, including: “thoracic ultrasound”, “pleural effusion”, “pleural fluid”, and “pleural effusion volume”.

The literature search focused on articles published within the last 25 years (January 1, 2000, to December 31, 2024). However, additional studies published before this period were also included if they were considered relevant or frequently cited in key publications or consensus documents. Only studies involving adult human participants and published in English or Spanish were considered. Eligible articles included original research, randomized controlled trials, systematic reviews, narrative reviews, and expert consensus guidelines. A formal risk of bias assessment and meta-analysis were not performed (Table 1).

Results

Why quantify PLE volume?

In routine clinical practice, qualitative assessment of PLE [small (limited to a single intercostal space), moderate (between 2 and 4 contiguous intercostal spaces), or large (occupies half of the hemithorax)] (10) is usually sufficient to determine the feasibility and safety of thoracentesis (11). However, quantitative assessment of PF volume adds value in multiple clinical scenarios. It has been associated with etiology (12), severity (3), and prognosis of PLE (13); can guide decisions regarding drainage in mechanically ventilated patients (14); allows monitoring of treatment response in various conditions (neoplastic, infectious, cardiac, or systemic) (15-20); helps differentiate isolated PLE from opacities associated with pulmonary consolidation or atelectasis; and contributes to determining the optimal timing for chest drain removal (21). Thus, volume quantification should not be viewed as an end in itself but as a tool to reduce clinical uncertainty in specific scenarios. Its usefulness is maximized when integrated with the clinical context, respiratory mechanics, and temporal evolution of the effusion.

Despite these potential advantages, US-based PLE volume estimation is not fully standardized, and published results show considerable inter-study variability (22). Clinical practice has benefited from international recommendations for standardizing thoracic US technique, although specific guidelines for volume quantification are still required (23).

Imaging techniques for volume estimation

Validation of US methods has primarily relied on comparisons with two reference standards: thoracentesis (24-29) and CT (30). The correlation between US measurements and drained volume is generally good, with coefficients of determination (R2) ranging from 0.68 to 0.90 (14,21,24,29). Multiplanar US models have demonstrated higher accuracy, with R2 values up to 0.98 compared to CT (9). Compared with thoracentesis, US has the advantage of detecting minimal pleural separations, which is especially useful in small effusions. In comparison with CT, it avoids diagnostic delays, patient transfers, and radiation exposure, making it especially attractive in critically ill patients. However, it should be noted that the volume obtained by thoracentesis does not always reflect the actual effusion volume, as it can be influenced by technical and clinical factors such as incomplete drainage or PF compartmentalization.

Technical considerations

Multiple factors influence the accuracy of US volume estimation. First, spontaneous breathing patients should be differentiated from those on positive-pressure mechanical ventilation, as intrapleural pressure variations affect fluid distribution and dynamics. In mechanically ventilated patients, positive pressure can alter pleural space morphology and modify the relationship between linear US measurements and actual fluid volume. Second, patient positioning is critical: in the supine position, especially in ventilated patients, fluid tends to accumulate posteriorly, whereas in seated patients it predominantly accumulates in the basal regions. Even in supine patients, torso elevation can significantly modify effusion distribution (24,30). Consequently, scanning sites vary according to the clinical scenario. In supine patients, the probe is usually placed along the posterior axillary line, exploring multiple intercostal spaces (24,25,30). In seated patients, measurements are typically obtained at the mid-scapular line (28).

Associated lung lesions, such as consolidations or atelectasis, can displace underlying fluid and result in underestimation of true volume. Alterations in diaphragm position, such as phrenic paralysis, may also affect anatomical landmarks. Some studies have reported lower correlations in the left hemithorax compared to the right (R2 0.51 vs. 0.78) (26), possibly due to smaller left lung capacity and cardiac interference, although this has not been consistently confirmed (31).

Low-frequency curvilinear probes (3.5–5 MHz) provide adequate visualization of intrathoracic structures and PF, while high-frequency linear transducers (5–10 MHz) are useful for detailed pleural examination (32). Most studies employ a transverse probe orientation (24-26), although longitudinal approaches have also been described (28). Operator experience remains a key determinant of measurement reliability.

Practically, simple one-dimensional formulas, such as that proposed by Balik et al. (24), stand out for their speed and bedside applicability in critically ill patients, whereas multiplanar models offer higher accuracy at the cost of greater technical complexity, limiting routine use. Method selection should be adapted to clinical context and operator experience.

Limitations and sources of variability

Despite promising results, US quantification of PLE has notable limitations. Reproducibility depends heavily on operator experience and the standardization of measurement points. Interobserver variability, although acceptable in trained hands, may limit applicability in settings with limited US experience, emphasizing the need for simplified protocols and targeted training. A methodological limitation is that many validation studies use drained volume as a reference standard, which may underestimate true effusion volume in the presence of loculations, incomplete drainage, or early procedure termination. This introduces potential bias when interpreting formula accuracy. International consensus recommendations exist for standardized thoracic US, however they do not specifically address precise PLE volume quantification (23). Most formulas have been validated in specific clinical scenarios and should not be indiscriminately extrapolated.

Conclusions

Thoracic US is a practical and generally reliable tool for estimating PLE volume in routine clinical practice. Rather than providing an exact measurement, US quantification should be interpreted as a semi-quantitative estimate complementing clinical judgment. Future research should focus on standardizing measurement protocols, improving interoperator reproducibility, and validating simplified and multiplanar models in diverse clinical settings. The goal is not to replace other imaging modalities but to provide clinicians with a reasonable, reproducible estimate that facilitates bedside decision-making.
