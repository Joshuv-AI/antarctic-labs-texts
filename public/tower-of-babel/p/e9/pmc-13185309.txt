Effect of airborne particle abrasion on the surface roughness of high-translucent 5Y-PSZ following simulated long-term oral aging: a non-contact optical profilometric in-vitro study
Authors: Reem Mohsen Almutairi, Angel Mary Joseph, Abdullah Alhussein, Saad H Almujel, Raghdah M Alshaibani, Yahya Theeban
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the effects of airborne particle abrasion (APA) using 50 μm Al₂O₃ and 100 μm glass microbeads on the surface roughness of high-translucent 5Y-PSZ and to assess the stability of these surface modifications following simulated long-term oral aging. Sixty zirconia discs (Shofu ZR Lucent) were randomly allocated into three groups (n = 20): the control, Al₂O₃, and glass microbead groups. Long-term oral aging was simulated by 25,000 thermocycles (5–55 °C) and 30 days of immersion in cold brew coffee. The surface roughness was analyzed by 2D non-contact optical profilometry at baseline, post-thermocycling, and post-coffee immersion.ResultsCompared with 100 μm glass microbeads (Ra = 1.93 ± 0.11 μm) and the control group (Ra = 0.14 ± 0.14 μm), the APA with 50 μm Al₂O₃ particles (Ra = 2.28 ± 0.06 μm; p < 0.001) produced the highest surface roughness, which was corroborated by profilometry line scans. The surface modifications remained stable following aging, although the Rq and Rp parameters demonstrated greater sensitivity to subtle clinical changes than Ra (p < 0.05). APA using 50 μm Al₂O₃ produced the highest surface roughness and most stable modification, highlighting the clinical need to balance micromechanical retention with the preservation of structural integrity for the long-term success of 5Y-PSZ restorations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13104-026-07816-4.Keywords: Zirconia, Polycrystalline ceramics, CAD/CAM, Airborne particle abrasion, Surface roughness, Mechanical properties

Background

Zirconia is a widely used biomaterial in restorative dentistry and prosthodontics because of its mechanical properties, biocompatibility, and optical characteristics [1]. The clinical shift toward metal-free and monolithic restorations has led to the transition from conventional 3 mol% yttria-tetragonal zirconia polycrystal (3Y-TZP) to high-translucent 5 mol% yttria-partially stabilized zirconia (5Y-PSZ) [2]. Though this generation of ceramics has the necessary cubic phase for anterior esthetic restoration, it is done at the expense of reduced transformation toughening and fracture resistance [3]. Thus, 5Y-PSZ needs optimal processing techniques, where the surface needs to be sufficiently roughened for bonding without causing defects that lead to early failure [4].

Several mechanical and chemical surface treatments have been utilized to obtain surface roughening, but none of these methods could ensure optimal bonding [5, 6]. The mechanical techniques include laser treatment, selective infiltration etching, and plasma or coating application [6–8]. Nevertheless, airborne particle abrasion (APA) is now considered the “gold standard” for establishing micromechanical bonds and enhancing chemical adhesion [8, 9]. Beyond its technical efficacy, the prevalence of APA in clinical settings makes it directly relevant to everyday restorative workflows and common chairside procedures [8, 9]. The compromised mechanical properties of 5Y-PSZ have led to the exploration of less aggressive APA media, such as 50 μm aluminum oxide/alumina (Al₂O₃) or 100 μm glass microbeads, to provide the required surface roughness with minimal iatrogenic damage [7, 10, 11]. Studies have indicated that controlled roughening using 50 μm Al₂O₃ is preferable for bonding [10–12]. On the other hand, larger particles (larger than 110 μm) are very effective in creating surface roughness but tend to cause microcracking and surface defects that impair the durability of the restoration [13, 14]. Therefore, identifying an APA protocol with these particle sizes and materials that could optimize bonding and maintain structural integrity is of paramount importance for the success of 5Y-PSZ restorations.

Beyond the initial bonding, the long-term stability of these surface modifications is important, as the oral environment subjects these restorations to constant thermal stress and dietary chromogens. Thermocycling and coffee immersion are established protocols for simulating these oral conditions, replicating the thermal fatigue and acidic chromogens that can degrade the resin‒zirconia bonding interface over time [9, 15, 16].

To the best of our knowledge, peer-reviewed data focusing on the impact of less aggressive APA protocols on the surface roughness of specific 5Y-PSZ materials, such as Shofu ZR Lucent, is not available. This study aimed to address these gaps by evaluating the effects of APA using 50 μm Al₂O₃ and 100 μm glass microbeads on the surface roughness of high-translucent 5Y-PSZ and to assess the stability of these surface modifications following simulated long-term oral aging. The null hypotheses (H₀) stated that neither the 50 μm Al₂O₃ nor the 100 μm glass microbeads would result in significant differences in surface roughness following aging, whereas the alternative hypothesis (Ha) predicted significant roughness alterations.

Sample size calculation

To ensure adequate statistical power for comparing surface roughness endpoints among the experimental groups, the sample size was calculated on the basis of the data reported in the key reference article [10]. Using the two-tailed alpha level of 0.05 and a power of 80% and using the effect size reported in the reference study [10], a minimum of n = 15 samples per subgroup is required to detect significant differences between APA protocols. Therefore, a sample size of n = 20 per subgroup was selected not only to satisfy this statistical requirement but also to account for potential sample loss during handling and thermal aging, ensuring the integrity of the data analysis.

Test material (manufacturer data) [17]

Shofu ZR Lucent is a polychromic, multilayered 5Y-PSZ disk marketed to synchronize strength and esthetics. According to the manufacturer, it is produced from high-quality Tosoh zirconia powder and has a five-layer graded design (with an approximate enamel/ dentin/ cervical distribution reported as 30%/ 35%/ 35%, respectively), with a uniform flexural strength of approximately 1019 MPa across the gradient and translucency values ranging from approximately 31–37% (comparable to lithium disilicate), with improved masking of discolored substrates. The manufacturer data reported a Vickers hardness of 45 HV (unsintered), a coefficient of thermal expansion of 10.2 × 10⁻⁶ K⁻¹ and a sintering temperature of 1450 °C. This material is intended for anterior and posterior crowns, short-span bridges, veneers, inlays and onlays with options for staining/ micro-layering.

Sample fabrication

Sixty disc-shaped samples (15 × 1.2 mm) were fabricated from pre-sintered zirconia blocks using subtractive CAD-CAM technology (Imes iCore 350i, Germany). The samples were sintered in a furnace (S-600; Add-in Co., Ltd., Goyang, Korea) at 1450 °C for 120 min with a heating and cooling rate of 5–10 °C/minute following the manufacturer’s instructions. The surfaces were sequentially finished to minimize surface defects under wet conditions with 600-, 1000- and 1200-grit silicon carbide paper (15 s per grit). The samples were polished using a polishing system from medium to superfine (SofLEX, 3 M) and then ultrasonicated (GT Ultrasonic Co. Ltd., China) in distilled water for 10 min and air dried.

Grouping of samples

The samples were randomly allocated into three groups using electronic randomization software (Stata, StataCorp, USA) according to the APA protocol, with n = 20 per group.

Group I (Control): Untreated (No APA).

Group II: APA using 50 μm Al₂O₃ was performed at 2-bar pressure in a sandblaster (BEGO Easy Blast sandblaster, Germany) for 20 s, which was equipped with a 0.8 mm internal-diameter pencil nozzle positioned 90° to the center of the sample at a 10 mm distance.

Group III: APA using 100 μm glass microbeads under identical conditions with a 1.2 mm internal-diameter pencil nozzle.

All surface treatments were performed on both sides of each disc by a skilled dental laboratory technician with 15 years of experience. All the samples were ultrasonically cleaned with 99% isopropanol for 180 s and air-dried before aging.

Simulated long-term oral aging protocol

The samples underwent simulated long-term oral aging through thermocycling and coffee immersion. Thermocycling was performed by 25,000 thermocycles in a distilled water bath (5–55 °C), using a thermocycler (Haake Circulating Bath DC-15 with Haake DC 10 Thermocontroller, Thermo Haake, USA). Each 30 s cycle consisted of 10 s in a 5 °C bath, 5 s of transfer, 10 s in a 55 °C bath, and 5 s of return to the 5 °C bath. This thermocycling protocol simulates approximately 2.5 years of intraoral use on the basis of the established correlation that 10,000 cycles represent one year of clinical service [21]. This protocol exposes the restoration to thermal fatigue at the zirconia‒resin bonding interface in the oral environment.

The samples were fully immersed for 30 days in either distilled water for the control group or Turkish coffee for the experimental group. The cold brew was prepared by steeping one part of finely ground coffee in four parts of cold water for 12 h at 4 °C and then straining. A cold brew coffee was selected to provide a high level of chlorogenic acid and suspended particles, which are the main contributors to dental material discoloration [22]. The coffee immersion protocol replicates 2.5 years of intraoral exposure based on the established in-vitro/in-vivo correlation, whereby 24 h of coffee immersion corresponds to one month of coffee consumption [16]. Since the quality of the zirconia‒resin bond is fundamentally dependent on the achieved surface roughness, this protocol allows staining agents to penetrate the bonding interface to simulate marginal percolation and assess the long-term integrity of the treated surfaces [16]. The solutions were changed daily, and the samples were rinsed for 10 s and gently dried before the measurements were taken. All immersions were conducted in a 37 °C incubator (Bionics Scientific Technologies, India).

Surface roughness measurements

Surface roughness was analyzed by a 2D non-contact optical profilometer (Talysurf CLI 1000, Taylor Hobson, UK), which provides high-resolution roughness data while preventing surface damage. The samples were ultrasonically cleaned with distilled water for 5 min, then air dried, and positioned centrally on the profilometer stage. Line profile measurements were performed over one mm scan length at 0.5 μm spacing, with a scanning speed of 50 μm/s and a sampling rate of 100 Hz. The measurements were processed via commercial software provided within the system (TalyMap, Taylor Hobson). The surface roughness parameters, such as arithmetic mean roughness (Ra), root mean square roughness (Rq), and maximum profile peak height (Rp), were evaluated. Three different lines were scanned on both sides of each sample to obtain average values. The surface roughness was analyzed at baseline after sample preparation. The measurements were repeated post-thermocycling, and post-coffee immersion. Profilometry line scans were conducted to provide a detailed quantitative visualization of the topography, supplementing the standard roughness parameters. All measurements were conducted by a trained operator blinded to group allocation and group concealment ensured through coded sample IDs. All the measurements were conducted at room temperature under standard laboratory conditions.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using the IBM SPSS statistics 30 version program (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The Shapiro‒Wilk test was applied to check the normality of the data (p > 0.05). Equal variances were checked with Levene’s test. Surface roughness was analyzed by two-way ANOVA to determine the effects of APA and aging. Following ANOVA, a post-hoc Tukey test was subsequently conducted for multiple comparisons. The results are presented as the means ± SDs, and statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05. Descriptive 95% confidence intervals were calculated for each subgroup as Mean ± t (0.025, df = 19) × (SD/√n), where n = 20.

Results

Descriptive analysis revealed that, compared with the control, the APA groups resulted in a substantial increase in surface roughness. The 50 μm Al₂O₃ group presented the highest initial roughness (Ra = 2.28 ± 0.06 μm), followed by the 100 μm glass microbead group (Ra = 1.93 ± 0.11 μm), whereas the control group remained smooth (Ra = 0.14 ± 0.14 μm). Following aging, the surface roughness remained stable across all the groups. The Ra values of the 50 μm Al₂O₃ and 100 μm glass microbead groups after coffee immersion were 2.00 ± 0.49 μm and 1.74 ± 0.02 μm, respectively. The descriptive data for Rq and Rp followed a similar trend to Ra, with both APA groups showing significantly higher Rq and Rp than the control at all experimental stages (Table 2).

Discussion

The H₀ for APA was rejected, confirming that the use of both 50 μm Al₂O₃ and 100 μm glass microbeads can result in statistically significant surface roughening compared with that of the controls. In contrast, the H₀ for aging and the interaction effect were accepted, indicating that aging did not significantly alter the surface roughness of pre-abraded samples.

This APA protocol was used to balance micromechanical retention and structural stability of high-translucent 5Y-PSZ. We compared 50 μm Al₂O₃ and 100 μm glass microbeads to evaluate two distinct surface interactions: the aggressive, angular cutting action of Al₂O₃ versus the gentler “peening” effect of spherical glass beads [10–14]. The aging protocol was deliberately rigorous to assess the structural stability of the modified surfaces under long-term clinical wear [16, 21, 22]. The surface roughness of the samples was characterized using the Ra, Rq, and Rp parameters. The average peak-to-valley height (Rz) parameter was excluded in favor of Rq and Rp to reflect more critical changes in surface morphology during simulated oral exposure in the current experimental design [23].

The results show that, in comparison with the control, both APA groups significantly increased surface roughness on all measured parameters. Compared with the 100 μm glass microbead and control groups, the 50 μm Al₂O₃ group showed the highest initial values of surface roughness, which is in accordance with well-established abrasive mechanical principles [11, 14, 24, 25]. These results confirm the role of abrasive kinetic energy in the modification of 5Y-PSZ and are in agreement with the study of Abdou et al. [26] and Mehari et al. [11], who found that Al₂O₃ blasting is significantly more effective than glass bead treatment in creating micromechanical retentive surfaces. The control group at baseline reported a standard deviation (Ra = 0.14 ± 0.14 μm) that represents the minute roughness changes of highly polished zirconia at the nanometer level rather than procedural inconsistency. As both APA protocols caused a tenfold increase in Ra, Rq, and Rp values, this variability did not interfere with the detection of differences in surface modifications.

Profilometric analysis confirmed these results, showing different roughness patterns depending on the abrasive material used. These variations are caused mechanistically by the particle shape and abrasive hardness. Al₂O₃ has a relatively high Mohs hardness and an angular morphology that produces sharp micromechanical retentive features. In contrast, the rounded glass microbead particles presented shallower peaks and valleys, as they tended to deform the surface rather than being aggressively cut into the zirconia matrix [10, 25, 27].

A notable observation in our study was that the surface modifications remained relatively stable following aging, as reflected by the significant Rq and Rp values and borderline significant Ra value. While Vasiliu et al. [28] and Patil et al. [29] reported surface changes in other ceramic systems, the polycrystalline structure of the 5Y-PSZ used here appears to be more resilient to the aging protocol employed in this study.

Our results have direct implications for the optimization of resin‒zirconia bonding through the specific APA protocols evaluated in this study. Indeed, Al₂O₃ APA combined with 10-methacryloyloxydecyl dihydrogen phosphate (10-MDP)-containing primers is currently the most widely recommended protocol for achieving maximum bond strength [11, 26, 30, 31]. While our results confirm that Al₂O₃ APA successfully creates the necessary surface area for micromechanical bonding, the aggressive nature of abrasion is a double-edged sword in clinical translation. This is because aggressive abrasion can theoretically introduce surface flaws or increase the degree of antagonist tooth wear. Because we focused on quantitative surface roughness data rather than destructive testing, we cannot confirm whether these specific values compromise the flexural strength or cause microcracking. Therefore, these findings must be considered in the context of a balance between maximizing the potential for bond durability and recognizing that the structural constraints of 5Y-PSZ under these conditions necessitate further mechanical verification [22, 32]. Considering these structural issues, there has been a shift towards less invasive surface modifications. Our findings regarding glass microbeads indicate that they may be considered a viable alternative to more invasive Al₂O₃ surface treatment, which provides sufficient surface roughness for bonding while maintaining structural integrity [11, 12, 26, 33].

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the results, which include-

Single zirconia brand (Shofu ZR Lucent) was evaluated in this study. The variability among zirconia may restrict the generalizability of the results to other 5Y-PSZ or graded zirconia systems [10, 13, 34–37].

Single zirconia brand (Shofu ZR Lucent) was evaluated in this study. The variability among zirconia may restrict the generalizability of the results to other 5Y-PSZ or graded zirconia systems [10, 13, 34–37].

Aging did not include simulations of combined aging protocols, such as brushing simulations, cyclic loading, or biofilm formation, which may better reflect complex clinical oral environments.

Methodologically, the surface roughness evaluation was limited to 2D line profile analysis and did not capture the full 3D areal topography by estimating arithmetic mean height (Sa) or qualitative microstructural topography.

Conclusions

Within the limitations of this study, the highest surface roughness was recorded for APA with 50 μm Al₂O₃ on high-translucent 5Y-PSZ, followed by 100 μm glass and the control group. The surface modifications were stable after aging. Clinically, although these surface modifications are favorable for micromechanical bonding, the high surface roughness emphasizes the need for balancing micromechanical retention with the preservation of structural integrity to ensure the long-term success of 5Y-PSZ restorations. These findings are preliminary, and further research mitigating the limitations is needed before definitive clinical recommendations can be established.
