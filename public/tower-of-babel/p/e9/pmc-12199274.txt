Ozone sensitivity to high energy demand day electricity and onroad emissions during LISTOS
Authors: Alexandra Karambelas, Paul J Miller, Jeffrey Underhill, Jonathan Pleim, Eric Zalewsky, Joseph Jakuta
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractUsing a high-resolution, 1.33 km by 1.33 km coupled Weather Research and Forecasting-Community Multi-scale Air Quality Model (WRF-CMAQ), we quantify the impact of emissions of nitrogen oxides (NOx) from high energy demand day (HEDD) electricity generating units (EGU) and onroad vehicles on ambient ozone air quality in the Long Island Sound Tropospheric Ozone Study (LISTOS) region covering New York City (NYC); Long Island, NY; coastal Connecticut; and neighboring areas. We test sensitivity scenarios to quantify HEDD EGU NOx contributions to ozone: (1) zero out HEDD EGU emissions, (2) dispatch HEDD EGUs starting with the lowest NOx emitting units first, (3) reduce onroad emissions by 90%, (4) combine zero out HEDD EGU emissions and reducing onroad emissions by 90%, and (5) dispatch HEDD EGUs starting with the lowest emitting units coupled with a reduction in onroad emissions by 90%. Results determine that HEDD EGUs lead to highly localized impacts on ambient concentrations of ozone while onroad emission reductions lead to large-scale regional concentration impacts. Further, reducing onroad emissions by 90% leads to spatially smaller VOC-limited regions and spatially larger transitional and NOX-limited regions around NYC. Despite the limited scale at which the EGU emission reductions occur, modifying HEDD EGU NOX emissions still provides substantial benefits in reducing ozone concentrations in the region, particularly at elevated ozone concentrations above 70 ppb.Implications:High-resolution coupled meteorology-chemistry modeling was used to quantify the impacts of high energy demand day (HEDD) electricity generating units (EGUs) and onroad transportation emissions changes on ozone air quality in the LISTOS region. Despite being highly localized and variable, HEDD EGUs NOX emissions sensitivity tests led to quantifiable changes in ozone. Further, reducing onroad emissions by 90% produced large decreases in ozone concentrations and led to a more NOX-sensitive ozone photochemical regime. With a transition to greater NOX-sensitivity, urban NOX-titration weakens and ozone is more likely to decline with the removal of additional NOX from sources like HEDD EGUs.

Introduction

New York City O3: average, trends over time, seasonality

Tropospheric (ground-level) ozone pollution is largely driven by reactions between its primary precursors nitrogen oxides (NOX) and volatile organic compounds (VOCs) during favorable meteorological conditions on sunny days (Seinfeld and Pandis 2016). Urban and regional ozone air quality across the U.S. has seen improvement since the adoption of the 1990 Clean Air Act Amendments (EPA 2023), which initiated a shift in ozone reduction strategies by placing greater emphasis on controlling NOX emissions in addition to the historical focus on VOC measures (National Research Council 1991). However, in recent years, ozone trends in the New York City (NYC) area are no longer downward (Chen et al. 2023; Civerolo et al. 2017; Singh and Kavouras 2022; Squizzato et al. 2018) and remain in excess of the current ozone national ambient air quality standard (NAAQS) of 70 ppb (annual 4th maximum 8-hr concentration averaged over 3 consecutive years). Additional pollution reduction measures are needed to reduce the health risk of millions of people living in the NYC region that continue to be exposed to elevated ozone levels.

Over the years, a number of studies have investigated the ozone formation dynamics of the NYC urban plume (Bernier et al. 2022; Cao et al. 2023; Coggon et al. 2021; Fehsenfeld et al. 2006; Han et al. 2022; Kleinman et al. 2000; Lee et al. 2011; Ring et al. 2023; Sebol et al. 2024; Singh and Kavouras 2022; Tao et al. 2022; Tran, Kumar, and Knipping 2023; White et al. 2007; Wolff et al. 1977; Wu et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2022, 2024; Zhao et al. 2019, 2023). These studies demonstrated an evolving atmospheric chemical regime within the NYC region over time as NOX emission control strategies were increasingly implemented. To further probe the current plume dynamics in light of these trends, the Northeast States for Coordinated Air Use Management (NESCAUM) launched the Long Island Sound Tropospheric Ozone Study (LISTOS) in 2018. LISTOS was a multi-institutional field campaign involving academic and government researchers having the purpose of better characterizing persistent elevated ozone concentrations in the New York City-Long Island Sound (LIS) region. A key goal was to gain insight into ozone transport and chemistry in the region that would help inform improved ozone control policies implemented by air quality planners (Karambelas 2020). LISTOS research thus far has helped improve understanding of local and regional ozone air pollution through identifying the emergence of low-level jets producing complex vertical ozone structures transported over the sound (Couillard et al. 2021), evaluating differences in land–sea interactions along the north and south shores of Long Island (Zhang et al. 2022), and leveraging existing observational tools to evaluate new remote sensing methods (Judd et al. 2020).

Within the LISTOS region, elevated ozone concentrations are coincident with prime meteorological conditions for ozone production, including warm temperatures and abundant sunlight. While stagnant winds are often also associated with high ozone, high ozone along the coastal Long Island Sound is more typically associated with wind speeds of about +1 m/s from the southwest, which brings upwind pollution, including the NYC urban plume, over the Sound and into downwind regions (Fiore et al. 2003; Kleinman et al. 2000; Tao et al. 2022; EPA 1984). High ozone conditions typically lead to an increased demand for cooling, which in turn requires increased electricity generation to meet the higher cooling demand. On warm sunny days, or when electricity demand otherwise exceeds baseload, seldom-used high energy demand day (HEDD) electricity generating units (EGUs) may be brought on-line for this purpose. New York City is a transmission-constrained urban area; therefore, many of these HEDD EGUs are located within NYC and nearby areas, thus can be emitting NOX within the urban pollution plume on the worst days for ozone formation. Because HEDD EGUs may be infrequently used, they may also be equipped with less efficient air pollution controls. During a multi-month “ozone season,” HEDD EGUs can comply with restrictions on NOX by obtaining allowances under “cap and trade” emission programs. Because of their relatively infrequent use, the amount of allowances needed over the course of a multi-month ozone season can be relatively small. On days they run, however, their NOX emissions can be significant.

An additional layer of complexity in ozone air quality planning is the shifting atmospheric chemical regime over time and space within the LISTOS region. The core of urban New York City (e.g., Manhattan) is a dense localized area of high NOX emissions, largely from mobile sources. Ozone formation in areas of high NOX emissions relative to VOCs is typically more sensitive to VOC reductions than NOX due to the non-linear relationship of ozone formation to its precursors. During the 2018 LISTOS campaign, measurements and modeling of VOCs associated with consumer products indicated a VOC-sensitive region within the NYC metropolitan area (Coggon et al. 2021). Tao et al. (2022) derived formaldehyde to nitrogen dioxide (NO2) ratios from aircraft and satellite data collected during the 2018 LISTOS campaign and found ozone formation over the NYC urban core can be more sensitive to VOCs than NOX. Similarly, modeling analysis by Tran, Kumar, and Knipping (2023) found that reducing anthropogenic NOX and VOC emissions in the region by 50% enhanced VOC-sensitivity in the urban core. However, farther downwind over Long Island Sound and coastal Connecticut, where ozone concentrations are often the highest and affected both by regional transport and local production (Zhao et al. 2023), ozone formation becomes more sensitive to NOX. The spatial size of the VOC-sensitive region changes from day to day, with its size decreasing on days most conducive to ozone production. In addition, as precursor emissions decrease over time, the overall New York City area appears to be trending since 2005 from NOX-saturated (VOC-limited) to more regionally NOX-limited (Jin et al. 2017).

The VOC/NOX sensitivity spatial extent in the LISTOS region is poised to change even more in the future as the states pursue aggressive transportation electrification policies. For example, New York State has set a goal in legislation that all new passenger cars and trucks sold in the State will be zero-emissions by 2035 and has adopted regulations consistent with the legislative goal (New York State Assembly Bill A4302 2021; New York State Department of Environmental Conservation 2023). New Jersey has also adopted a regulation to require all new light-duty vehicles to be zero emissions by 2035 (New Jersey Register 2023). Additionally, in 2024 the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) set new, stricter emission standards for cars and light- and medium-duty vehicles that it estimates will reduce NOX and VOCs by 25% and 46% nationwide as a result of fleet turnover and transition to electric vehicles by 2055 compared to business as usual (EPA 2024). This level of electric vehicle fleet penetration would significantly reduce NOX emissions from onroad transportation within the LISTOS region and beyond, and shift ozone formation further toward NOX-sensitive conditions. In light of this potential shift, this modeling study explores how a discreet ozone control strategy, specifically NOX reductions from local power plants on high-electric demand days, undertaken now within the current mix of NOX emission sources may differ in effectiveness in the future when evaluated under an atmospheric chemical regime where the onroad transportation fleet is dominated by electric vehicles.

Materials and methods

CMAQ model and inputs

We used the coupled Weather Research and Forecasting v4.1.1-Community Multi-scale Air Quality model v5.3.1 (WRF-CMAQ) (Wong et al. 2012) at a horizontal grid resolution of 1.33 km by 1.33 km with 36 vertical layers through 50 hPa following Torres-Vazquez et al. (2022). The model was run from May 2 through September 1, 2018, with all of May discarded as spin up. Additional sensitivity scenario simulations were conducted for July 1 through September 1, 2018. The chemical initial and boundary conditions for the 1.33 km horizontal grid resolution domain were nested from a 4 km horizontal grid resolution WRF-CMAQ run that was initialized with output from a previously described 12 km horizontal grid resolution continental U.S. off-line simulation using WRF v4.0.2 and CMAQ v5.3 (Torres-Vazquez et al. 2022). The 12 km horizontal grid resolution simulation was initialized with output from a 108 km horizontal grid resolution hemispheric CMAQ v5.0 simulation (Mathur et al. 2017). Meteorology input data are from the North American Mesoscale (NAM) 12 km reanalysis data product (https://rda.ucar.edu/datasets/ds609.0/).

Model configurations and additional details are provided in Torres-Vazquez et al. (2022). Briefly, WRF physics options included the Pleim-Xiu land surface model (Pleim and Xiu 1995; Xiu and Pleim 2001) and the Asymmetric Convective Model version 2 (ACM2; Pleim 2007a, 2007b) planetary boundary layer (PBL) scheme. CMAQ configuration included the Carbon-Bond 6 (CB6) chemical mechanism version 3 (Luecken, Yarwood, and Hutzell 2019) and AERO7 aerosol module (Pye et al. 2019). Four-dimensional data assimilation (FDDA) was used to nudge wind, temperature, and water vapor mixing ratios above the PBL (Gilliam, Godowitch, and Rao 2012) every 3 hrs and sea surface temperatures (SSTs, Appel et al. 2014) with 1 km SST data from the Advanced Very-High Resolution Radiometer satellite (https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/data/oceans/ghrsst/L4/GLOB/JPL_OUROCEAN/). Ground-level temperature and humidity optimization is applied using the UnRestricted Mesoscale Analysis data set at 2.5 km grid resolution in 3-hr increments and the PX LSM indirect soil moisture and temperature nudging scheme (Pleim and Xiu 2003; Pleim and Gilliam 2009; https://www.nco.ncep.noaa.gov/pmb/products/rtma/).

Emissions were developed from the EPA’s 2016 modeling platform version 7.2 with additional updates for onroad mobile sources, fires, and EGUs. The 2016 modeling platform was based upon the 2014 National Emissions Inventory (NEI) and updated through a collaboration of multi-state regional planning organizations (EPA 2019). The MOtor Vehicle Emissions Simulator (MOVES) version 2014b was used to model onroad mobile sources for 2018 at county-level for 21 counties in and near New York City, including several counties in New Jersey and Connecticut. For all other counties, the Sparse Matrix Operator Kernel Emissions-MOVES (SMOKE-MOVES) tool was used to process and grid 2018 emissions from 2016 using an adjustment factor. Fires were updated to year 2018. We replace EPA EGU emissions with point and non-point EGU emissions from Eastern Regional Technical Advisory Committee (ERTAC) emissions modeling software prepared by the Mid Atlantic Regional Air Management Association (MARAMA). Similar to the EPA methodology, ERTAC EGU software inputs annual EGU activity and emission data from the EPA Clean Air Markets Program Data (CAMPD, https://campd.epa.gov/data) to produce inputs for postprocessing through SMOKE in preparation for photochemical modeling. More information about EGU emissions for sensitivity scenarios is in the HEDD EGU Emissions section. Biogenic emissions were calculated in-line using the BEIS v3.61 model (Bash, Baker, and Beaver 2016). Additional updates were included for airports and commercial marine vessels from the 2017 NEI, and solvents were included as a separate non-point sector following Seltzer et al. (2021).

Five emission sensitivity scenarios were prepared for this study to compare the air quality impacts of HEDD units under the current ozone photochemical environment in the Ozone Transport Region (OTR) and one in which drastic emission cuts to the onroad sector were introduced. A description of emission scenarios modeled with WRF-CMAQ is provided in Table 1.

HEDD EGU emissions

The Base case simulation uses 2018 EGU emissions as described above. Sensitivity simulations were conducted using variations of the ERTAC EGU emission inventory for 2018/19. HEDD units are contained in the point source inventory files that list individual emitting units and relevant stack information for all EGUs. HEDD EGUs were determined based on unit operation in 2018 and 2019 as identified from EPA CAMD Part 75 CEMS reporting units in the OTR. HEDD units operate infrequently when electricity demand is high, which often coincides with high ozone concentrations. To be classified as a HEDD EGU, a unit needed to operate less than 20% of hours in each of 2018 and 2019 ozone season, the average total heat input during these ozone seasons was 10% or less the unit’s maximum heat capacity, has started operating prior to 1996, and has no retirement scheduled prior to the 2023 ozone season and to not have an operation exception in 2018 and 2019. Units that were not classified as Part 75 units (non-Part 75) were also included and were determined from the emission inventory based on information from states and EPA regarding which units typically respond on peak days. Simulations using these HEDD emissions were structured to identify: (1) contributions from HEDD EGUs through a brute-force simulation zeroing out HEDD EGU emissions and (2) impacts when lowest NOX-emitting HEDD EGUs were dispatched first. Many HEDD units are older and produce high rates of NOX per MWh of electricity production, thus we focus on NOX reallocation for this analysis.

For the Low HEDD scenario, HEDD EGUs are regionally grouped loosely based on urban and non-urban areas (Supplemental Figure 1). In each regional grouping, HEDD EGUs are ranked from lowest to highest average hourly NOX emission rate that meet actual daily generation during the operational time-period. High demand electricity load is used to determine HEDD EGU dispatch and corresponding emissions from the ranking based on emission rates.

The OTR consists of states in New England and the upper Mid-Atlantic regions including parts of Maine, New Hampshire, Vermont, Massachusetts, Rhode Island, Connecticut, New York, Pennsylvania, New Jersey, Delaware, Maryland, Washington, DC, and parts of Virginia. States and regions with HEDD EGUs operational in 2018 and 2019 that fall within the modeling domain include all but DC.

Evaluating localized source impacts under different ozone chemistry regimes

We further investigated modeled ozone chemistry in the region by applying a 90% across-the-board reduction to emissions from the onroad transportation sector with the Base case ERTAC EGU, Zero HEDD, and Low HEDD simulations. While this is a steep decrease in mobile source emissions, it is consistent with announced state climate policies and adopted vehicle rules around the Northeast such as the federal Control of Air Pollution From New Motor Vehicles: Heavy-Duty Engine and Vehicle Standards (Federal Register 2023), California Air Resources Board (CARB) Heavy-Duty Engine and Vehicle Omnibus Regulation (CARB 2021a), and CARB Advanced Clean Trucks regulation (CARB 2021b) initiative for heavy-duty vehicles, and CARB Advanced Clean Cars II regulations (CARB 2022) for light-duty vehicles. Onroad emission reductions include NOX, VOCs, primary PM, and more, in contrast to only NOX modifications in the HEDD simulations. Onroad emissions contributed 21,096 tons of NOX and 10,707 tons of VOCs during July and August 2018 or 19% and 9% percent of total anthropogenic emissions in the domain. Comparatively, HEDD EGUs contribute 1552 tons of NOX (1.3%) and 72 tons (<0.1%) of VOCs during July and August 2018. By combining onroad and HEDD sensitivities, we are able to evaluate impacts from highly localized HEDD sources in two very different atmospheric ozone chemistry regimes in and around NYC.

Model evaluation

The WRF-CMAQ output on this domain and with the baseline emission inputs has been extensively evaluated and tested in Torres-Vazquez et al. (2022). Here, we focus on model evaluation of the Base case simulation that uses ERTAC EGU emissions. Replacing EPA EGU emissions with those from ERTAC produces near identical model biases of maximum daily 8-hr ozone (MDA8) across the model domain (Figure 1). Minimal changes occur, including reduced model biases at two sites near New York City and several more across New Jersey, Delaware, Massachusetts, and Pennsylvania. Notably, there are no changes to model performance at coastal Connecticut monitoring locations as a result of changes in the EGU emission inventory. The similarities between the use of EPA EGUs and ERTAC are unsurprising as daily total NOX emissions across the domain average 97 tons/day for EPA EGUs with ERTAC slightly higher at 134 tons/day, and average diurnal emission rates are similar for both inventories (Supplemental Figure 2). The normalized mean bias (NMB) is identical for both simulations here (NMB = 5.7%), as is the correlation coefficient (r = 0.74), which compares well to an r = 0.76 in Torres-Vazquez et al. (2022). Our ozone performance also compares well against that in Tran, Kumar, and Knipping (2023), which uses WRF coupled with the Comprehensive Air Quality Model with Extensions (CAMx) and produces monthly MDA8 ozone correlations between 0.77 and 0.80, NMB from 6.7% to 27.1%, and normalized mean error (NME) from 14.8% to 31.9%. Regardless of which EGU inventory is chosen, WRF-CMAQ exhibits a large low bias in NO2 concentrations across the domain (NMBEPA = −38%, NMBERTAC = −35%), though our correlation of r = 0.65 compares well with the r = 0.59 in Torres-Vazquez et al. (2022). The combination of a low model bias in predicting NO2 and a high model bias predicting MDA8 ozone could mean there is an overproduction of ozone via NOX-chemistry pathways, which we consider as we alter emission inputs.

Concentration impacts from sensitivity scenarios

For HEDD scenarios, only changes to NOX emissions from HEDD EGUs were applied. In contrast, onroad emissions were reduced by 90% for all pollutants. The resulting daytime NO2 and MDA8 ozone modeled concentration changes are shown to be different from the ERTAC Base case scenario in Figure 3. Average NOX concentrations for July and August 2018 show dense regions of elevated concentrations well above 15 ppb around Philadelphia, Pennsylvania; NYC; and Boston, Massachusetts, connected by elevated NO2 concentrations along highways (Figure 3a). Eliminating HEDD EGUs (Zero HEDD) leads to decreases in NO2 concentrations (Figure 3b) that are large (up to −7.3 ppb) but are isolated and present at locations in NYC, on Long Island, in the lower Hudson River Valley, New York, and scattered throughout Massachusetts and New Jersey. Dispatching the lowest emitting HEDD EGUs first as in Low HEDD produces a similar distribution to Zero HEDD with a notable increase in NO2 concentrations near Philadelphia, Pennsylvania, and Wilmington, Delaware, as lower emitting HEDD EGUs are dispatched more to meet demand (Figure 3c). Onroad 90% scenario impacts on NO2 are more widespread throughout the domain, tracing populous urban centers and highways in between (Figure 3d). Concentrations are largely reduced as a result of 90% emission reductions from onroad sources, declining on average by 19.5% (Supplemental Figure 3) but reaching a maximum change of −27.1 ppb.

Average episodic MDA8 ozone concentrations for the Base case simulation show peak concentrations at urban areas including Baltimore, MD; Philadelphia, PA; and, most notably, NYC (Figure 3e). Summertime average concentrations in NYC and downwind on the north shore of Long Island and coastal Connecticut approach 60 ppb. Removing HEDD EGU emissions without a reduction in onroad emissions leads to increases in MDA8 ozone concentrations of up to +9.8 ppb (Figure 3f) due to a reduction in NOX titration. Increases are localized to NYC and Long Island, New York, with some additional sporadic localized increases, including in eastern Pennsylvania; New Jersey; Hudson Valley, New York; and Massachusetts. Regionally, there are some small decreases to MDA8 of a few tenths of a ppb. Differences in ozone concentrations from Low HEDD are similar to that of Zero HEDD, with more widespread increases in MDA8 ozone concentrations (up to +5.3 ppb) and decreases (up to −6.3 ppb) (Figure 3g). Conversely, reducing onroad sources by 90% causes broad reductions in MDA8 ozone by up to −4.1 ppb (Figure 3h). Increased MDA8 ozone of 1−4 ppb as a result of reducing onroad emissions occurs in a few highly localized areas – namely Danbury, CT; New York City, NY; and near West Hartford, CT – distributed across the domain. The HEDD scenarios reduce MDA8 ozone by an average of 0.1% while the Onroad 90% scenario leads to a decline in MDA8 ozone by 4.4% (Supplemental Figure 3).

Changed ozone environment due to highly reduced onroad emissions

Reducing onroad emissions by 90% leads to a dramatically different chemical environment with respect to ozone formation. We compare ozone formation using columnar formaldehyde to NO2 ratios (FNR) based on Jin et al. (2020) during ozone non-exceedance and exceedance days as described in Tao et al. (2022). Exceedance days occur when any monitor in the domain experiences a MDA8 ozone exceedance of 70 ppb or more, and non-exceedance days occur when there are no MDA8 ozone exceedances at any monitor in the domain. There are 19 days each for the exceedance and non-exceedance categories, and they are partitioned by day of the week so as to minimize weekday/weekend effects. On non-exceedance days in the Base case scenario, the NYC region is characterized by strong NOX-saturated and VOC-limited conditions stretching from northern New Jersey across to Long Island (Figure 4a). A transitional ring of FNR values between 2.9 and 3.8 surrounds the region of highest NOX saturation, and the domain beyond is subject to NOX-limited conditions. Reducing onroad emissions by 90% leads to a large shrinking of both the NOX-saturated and transitional zones in the NYC region (Figure 4b), and a shift to more NOX-limited conditions overall as evidenced by the average FNR increasing from 9.3 in the baseline to 11.3 in the onroad reduction scenario.

FNR values for selected exceedance days are also shown for both the Base case (Figure 4c) and the Onroad 90% scenario (Figure 4d). In both cases, there is a tightening of the gradient between NOX-saturated and NOX-limited conditions with a slight expansion of relatively stronger NOX-saturated conditions compared to the non-exceedance conditions. The overall reduction in spatial scale of NOX-saturated/VOC-sensitive regions on exceedance days follows the findings in New York City and other urban areas in Jin et al. (2017). Interestingly, Newark Liberty and John F. Kennedy International Airports are predominantly NOX-saturated in all cases.

Impact of HEDD sensitivity scenarios in different ozone regimes

Shifting ozone regimes from NOX-saturated to transitional or NOX-limited environments plays a significant role in ozone chemistry and influences the impact of emission controls on ozone formation, particularly in regions where ozone concentrations continue to exceed the NAAQS. Figure 5 includes all instances of days when WRF-CMAQ predicts MDA8 ozone concentrations to exceed 70 ppb in the Base case at each grid cell. In the Base case scenario, modeled summertime MDA8 ozone concentrations greater than 70 ppb are common in NYC and surrounding areas, including New Jersey, upstate and Long Island New York, and Connecticut (Figure 5a). The greatest average modeled concentrations in excess of 85 ppb are widespread and border Long Island Sound. Using the same days that exceeded an MDA8 ozone concentration of 70 ppb as in the Base case scenario (Supplemental Figure 4), we find that MDA8 concentrations after a 90% emission reduction in the onroad scenario lead to a considerable decline in ozone concentrations (Figure 5b). In the Onroad 90% scenario, ozone concentrations fall across the domain, with peak instances averaging in the high 70 ppb to low 80 ppb ranges across NYC and Long Island. Ozone concentrations decreased along coastal Connecticut by 10 ppb on average between the Base case and the Onroad 90% scenarios. Similar distributions of MDA8 ozone concentrations occur on average, with the greatest July and August average ozone concentrations occurring in New York City, Long Island, and coastal Connecticut in both the Base case and Onroad 90% case (Supplemental Figure 5).

We compare introducing two HEDD scenarios, zeroing out all HEDD EGUs (Zero HEDD), and prioritizing dispatching the cleanest HEDD units (Low HEDD) at elevated ozone concentrations in both the Base case and the Onroad 90% scenarios. Doing so indicates how strategies with highly localized and relatively lower emission reductions may still influence ozone concentrations in the domain, particularly during instances of potential ozone NAAQS exceedances. Under the Base case ozone conditions, the Zero HEDD scenario leads to instances of both increased ozone concentrations by up to 1.5 ppb and decreased ozone concentrations of up to −7.5 ppb (Figure 5c). Increases are predominant where ozone exceedances greater than or equal to 70 ppb regularly occur in the domain, such as NYC, across New York including Long Island, and northern New Jersey. However, under the conditions resulting from the Onroad 90% scenario, ozone reductions due to removing HEDD EGUs (Onroad_Zero HEDD) are much more widespread, reaching −7.9 ppb, with increases of up to 1.6 ppb much more localized to NYC and nearby airports (Figure 5d). Applying the Low HEDD scenario in the Base Case produces nearly the same result as Zero HEDD (Figure 5e). However, some differences occur in Onroad_Low HEDD under Onroad 90% conditions, namely some additional instances of isolated increased MDA8 ozone for example, near the New York–New Jersey border (Figure 5f). The two HEDD scenarios have similar influences in July and August average MDA8 ozone concentrations under Base Case and Onroad 90%, leading to 2+ ppb increases in ozone in the New York City center (Supplemental Figure 5).

Limitations

The analysis presented here comparing modeled ozone outcomes from modifying onroad and HEDD EGU emissions is limited in scope. Namely, two limitations stand out in this study. First, we only modified NOX emissions from HEDD EGUs compared to all pollutant emissions from onroad sources. The modified HEDD EGU scenarios were the result of modeling and analysis led by the Ozone Transport Commission who prioritized quantifying NOX emissions from potential contributing sectors. Although HEDD EGUs emit VOCs, emissions relative to those from other sectors are very small and are dwarfed in comparison to biogenic sources. In the future, additional pollutants may be investigated in a similar analysis. Second, we did not account for added electricity generation due to onroad vehicle emission reductions akin to vehicle electrification. Due to this, our study represents a lower bound exercise in ozone contributions under the assumption of renewable electricity generation adoption across the region (e.g., off-shore wind and solar).

Discussion

We use a recently established high-resolution coupled WRF-CMAQ modeling platform to investigate the impacts of HEDD peaking EGUs on air quality in the LISTOS region of NYC, Long Island, coastal Connecticut, and surrounding areas on average and under conditions where onroad emissions are reduced by 90%. We design scenarios to test the contributions of HEDD units on average and when the lowest emitting units are dispatched first to meet demand. Our goal is to compare these varying ozone conditions to understand if relatively limited and more localized NOX emission reduction strategies are more effective when regional emissions of all pollutants from onroad sources are strongly reduced.

Introducing domain-wide onroad transportation emission reductions of 90% leads to an average reduction in summertime MDA8 ozone of about −1 ppb with reductions widespread across the domain. Interestingly, even after a shift in the ozone regime as a result of reducing domain-wide anthropogenic NOX by 44% and onroad formaldehyde by 26%, emissions from HEDD EGUs still have sizable contributions to MDA8 ozone concentrations greater than 70 ppb. Under current baseline emission conditions, removing HEDD EGUs leads to NOX reductions but isolated MDA8 ozone increases of up to 9.8 ppb in urban cores and at large EGU facilities due to the reduction in NOX titration. Similar modeling by Tran, Kumar, and Knipping (2023) found that reducing onroad emissions by 50% led to a 17% decrease in onroad contribution to ozone exceedances, but a slight increase in relative importance of EGU contributions at the Queens College, NYC monitoring site, which is in-line with our analysis. Shifting priority dispatch to lower emitting HEDD units in the modeling yields a similar behavior. In a future scenario with large-scale transportation electrification, the extent of localized ozone increases from HEDD NOX reductions diminishes, and reductions in the highest MDA8 ozone levels increase.

Even assuming a relatively cleaner mix of HEDD units (e.g., Low HEDD), the modeling indicates that these sources can continue to have meaningful contributions to ozone formation on days when the MDA8 ozone levels exceed 70 ppb. Eliminating HEDD EGU NOX emissions results in MDA8 ozone concentration reductions of up to −7.9 ppb. Modeled FNRs indicate NOX-saturated conditions in NYC as observed by both airborne and remote sensing observations in Johnson et al. (2023) and Tao et al. (2022), and we are able to detect a subtle transition to greater NOX sensitivity on ozone exceedance days, similar to Tao et al. (2023). With a strong reduction in anthropogenic emissions under the Onroad 90% scenario, there is a demonstrable transition to greater NOX sensitivity outside of the city center. Further, as NOX and VOC emissions decline and there is a transition to a NOX-sensitive ozone regime, the remaining NOX is expected to produce ozone more efficiently, as demonstrated with an increase in modeled ozone production efficiency with reduced anthropogenic emissions in Tran, Kumar, and Knipping (2023). In the current study, we find that Low HEDD becomes more beneficial to reducing ozone under the Onroad 90% scenario at monitors representative of three ozone production regimes – NOX-saturated, transitional, and NOX-limited – as the LISTOS region transitions to greater NOX sensitivity. Therefore, the displacement of these HEDD EGUs with non-emitting sources can still have large ozone benefits locally around the densely populated NYC non-attainment region when coupled with transportation electrification.

We note that large-scale onroad transportation electrification can increase EGU emissions due to vehicle recharging where the increased electric demand is served by fossil-fueled generation, particularly if coal-fired EGUs increase generation. For the purposes of this study, our HEDD scenarios were designed to investigate localized source impacts of NOX from HEDD sources in the NYC region. On a larger scale, the northeastern U.S. (considered here to be New Jersey, New York, and New England) has minimal coal-fired generation, and there are no coal-fired EGUs within Connecticut, New Jersey, and New York. Schnell et al. (2019) found that with the less carbon-intensive electricity generation in the northeastern U.S., overall ozone-forming emissions from light-duty electric vehicles when accounting for recharging with the current electric grid mix decrease relative to the displaced tailpipe emissions from conventional vehicles with internal combustion engines. Furthermore, if large-scale transportation electrification occurs at the scale assumed for these HEDD scenarios to address climate change, it is reasonable to expect that the electric grid will also decarbonize, as one without the other would not meet climate targets. Bistline et al. (2022) noted that air quality modeling that does not account for structural changes in electricity systems over time and instead uses short-run marginal emissions estimates fails to capture the full extent of future emission reductions from transportation electrification. Knobloch et al. (2020) indicate that with the current technology trajectories occurring in many parts of the world, the power sector’s emission intensity is gradually decreasing. They project that this trend in combination with technology efficiency improvements would result in mean emission intensities of electric vehicles being 20% lower in 2030 and 30% lower in 2050 (weighted by transport demand in 2015). In the U.S., new federal fundings for renewables in the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act (2021) and the Inflation Reduction Act (2022), along with announced northeastern state climate plans for significant expansion of off-shore wind and other non-emitting power generation, are consistent with this expectation. For example, New York State’s Climate Leadership and Community Protection Act (2019) sets goals of getting 70% of the state’s electricity from renewables in 2030 and 100% by 2040.

Finally, from a policy perspective, the Clean Air Act requires states to achieve national ambient air quality standards “as expeditiously as practicable” (1990 Clean Air Act Amendments, 42 U.S. Code Section 7502). Achieving localized HEDD NOX reductions is responsive to this statutory requirement because it “brings forward” in time the attainment of ozone air quality standards as deeper NOX reductions occur over a more extended time period during which the onroad vehicle fleet turns over to cleaner cars and trucks.

In summary, as broad-scale vehicle emissions reduction strategies are implemented in urban areas such as NYC, smaller contributing sources will have increasingly larger relative impacts as the urban area becomes more NOX-sensitive. Further NOX emission reductions from local sources such as HEDD units while NOX emissions from mobile sources are concurrently decreasing will have additional ozone reduction benefits and will assist in meeting the public health ozone air quality standards on a more expedited basis.
