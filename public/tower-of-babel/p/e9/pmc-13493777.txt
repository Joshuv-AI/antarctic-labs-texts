Refractory inclusions in Bennu samples indicative of outer Solar System accretion
Authors: Noriyuki Kawasaki, Toru Matsumoto, Sota Arakawa, Naoya Sakamoto, Ken-ichi Bajo, Sara S. Russell, Jessica J. Barnes, Ann N. Nguyen, Timothy J. McCoy, Pierre Haenecour, Hisayoshi Yurimoto, Harold C. Connolly, Dante S. Lauretta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
NASA’s OSIRIS-REx mission returned material from the carbonaceous asteroid Bennu, which is dominated by aqueous-alteration products but preserves rare high-temperature components. Here we present an integrated petrographic, oxygen-isotopic and 26Al–26Mg chronological characterization of refractory inclusions in Bennu samples. All identified inclusions are small (<~100 µm) and commonly preserve mineralogical evidence of in situ aqueous alteration, indicating incorporation into Bennu’s parent body prior to hydration. Their mineralogical characteristics and oxygen-isotopic and 26Al–26Mg systematics demonstrate formation during the earliest stage of Solar System evolution and genetic affinity with refractory inclusions in asteroid Ryugu samples and chondritic meteorites. The shared refractory-inclusion signatures across diverse chondritic materials are consistent with widespread outward transport and mixing of early-formed inner-disk solids. In Bennu samples analyzed thus far, large (>submillimeter) refractory inclusions are absent, in contrast to most carbonaceous chondrites. This size distribution implies that Bennu’s constituents accreted in the outer Solar System beyond the pressure bump associated with proto-Jupiter, which effectively filtered distinct dust populations.

Introduction

Refractory inclusions, namely calcium-aluminum–rich inclusions (CAIs) and ameboid olivine aggregates (AOAs), formed in high-temperature regions of the solar protoplanetary disk during its earliest stage and were subsequently transported to the accretion regions of chondritic planetesimals1–9. The genetic relationships among refractory inclusions across asteroidal samples and chondritic meteorites provide constraints on the environments in which their parent planetesimals accreted, as variations in inclusion size and abundance reflect dust transport, retention, and mixing in the solar protoplanetary disk10,11. While most carbonaceous chondrites commonly contain large (>submillimeter) refractory inclusions, samples from Ryugu collected by JAXA’s Hayabusa2 spacecraft12 and CI chondrites have only rare, much smaller inclusions—typically tens of micrometers or smaller13–15, suggesting a distinct accretion environment for their parent planetesimal(s) compared with other chondrite groups11,14.

Samples from Bennu collected by NASA’s OSIRIS-REx spacecraft show close chemical and petrographic affinities with Ryugu samples and CI chondrites16–18. They are all predominantly composed of secondary phases formed through interaction with aqueous fluids, indicating extensive aqueous alteration on their parent planetesimal(s)18–24. Bennu samples contain rare, relict high-temperature minerals such as olivine, low-Ca pyroxene, spinel, and hibonite, typically occurring as small (<~100 µm), isolated mineral fragments embedded in a phyllosilicate-rich matrix17,18,25, similar to the occurrences in Ryugu samples and CI chondrites13,15,20,26,27. Based on their chemical and isotopic characteristics, these anhydrous monomineralic grains have been inferred to be fragments of refractory inclusions and possibly chondrules, or grains formed in similar environments in the solar protoplanetary disk. However, the size distribution and genetic affinities of refractory inclusions in Bennu—key diagnostics of selective filtering, retention, and mixing of early-formed solids—have not yet been established. Here, we characterize refractory inclusions and their fragments in Bennu samples through integrated petrography, oxygen isotopes, and 26Al–26Mg chronology, and compare them with inclusions in Ryugu samples and chondritic meteorites to constrain the accretion environment of Bennu’s parent planetesimal.

Results

Mineralogy and petrology

Polished sections of 12 Bennu particles were investigated in this study (Supplementary Fig. 1), with eight belonging to the angular lithology and four to the hummocky lithology16,28. Anhydrous primary minerals were identified in six particles (two angular, four hummocky), occurring predominantly as isolated monomineralic grains of olivine, low-Ca pyroxene, and spinel. In addition, six AOAs and three CAI-like inclusions were recognized in the same particles, whereas chondrules or their pseudomorphs were absent throughout all particles investigated. Although anhydrous primary minerals are generally rare, they were especially abundant in OREX-803114-0 particle #1 (hummocky, ~1.5 mm in length) and in Fe,S-rich, Mg,Si-poor clasts (~100–600 µm) within hummocky-stone section OREX-800088-8 (Fig. 1). In OREX-803114-0 particle #1, calcite, magnesite enclosing fine calcite grains, and Mg,Na-phosphate are common, whereas dolomite is exceedingly rare. The AOAs have irregular shapes and range from ~30 to 70 µm in size (Fig. 3a, b; Supplementary Fig. 2). They are mainly composed of forsteritic olivine (Mg# > ~99; Supplementary Table 1), with minor amounts of submicrometer diopside, anorthite, and spinel with irregular shapes. An electron-transparent section was extracted from the AOA HKBA02, which is dominated by angular olivine grains ~1 µm in size and exhibits a highly porous texture (Fig. 3c). Most olivine grains in this inclusion are unaltered, although some show aqueous alteration to poorly crystalline phyllosilicates. One olivine grain appears pseudomorphed by phyllosilicates (Fig. 3d, e), and another is partially replaced (Fig. 3f, g). Measurements of ~0.7 nm lattice fringes suggest that these phyllosilicates are composed mainly of serpentine (Supplementary Fig. 4f). The phyllosilicates replacing olivine are more Al-rich and Fe-poor than the phyllosilicates surrounding HKBA02 and typical Bennu matrix phyllosilicates (Supplementary Fig. 3). Isotopic compositions and chronology

We obtained O-isotope compositions of minerals in refractory inclusions by secondary ion mass spectrometry (SIMS) (“Methods”, Supplementary Table 2). The isolated spinel grain, the hibonite in HKBC01, spinel and olivine in HKBC03, and olivine grains in the AOAs are all 16O-rich relative to the terrestrial O-isotope composition (Fig. 4a–c). These minerals exhibit Δ17O values, defined as the deviation from the terrestrial mass-dependent fractionation law (δ17O – 0.52 × δ18O), of –24.3 ± 1.3‰ (2 standard deviations (2 SD); n = 7) for CAI-like inclusions and –23.3 ± 1.6‰ (n = 10) for AOAs. We also used SIMS to measure the Al–Mg isotope compositions of the hibonite and olivine in HKBC01 and HKBA03, respectively (Supplementary Table 3). The hibonite shows excess radiogenic 26Mg from the decay of 26Al, as indicated by δ26Mg* values (deviation from a mass-dependent fractionation line; “Methods”) that exceed analytical uncertainties (Fig. 4d). To determine the initial 26Al/27Al ratio [(26Al/27Al)0], we used the HKBA03 olivine as a proxy for the initial δ26Mg* composition of the HKBC01 formation region, given that its δ26Mg* value (0.03 ± 0.09‰; Supplementary Table 3, Fig. 4) is indistinguishable from the Solar System’s initial δ26Mg* of –0.040 ± 0.029‰ (ref. 2) or –0.0159 ± 0.0014‰ (ref. 3) inferred from the bulk Al–Mg isochrons of CAIs and AOAs in Vigarano-type (CV) chondrites. Following the same approach as in previous work14, we constructed the Al–Mg model isochron for HKBC01 based on the Al–Mg isotopic compositions of the hibonite and the HKBA03 olivine. The model isochron gives (26Al/27Al)0 of (5.4 ± 0.4) × 10−5.

Discussion

Aqueous alteration of refractory inclusions

Our observations of phyllosilicates replacing olivine and interstitial phyllosilicates between olivine grains within AOAs (Fig. 3; Supplementary Fig. 2o), together with the magnetite in HKBC03 (Fig. 2e), collectively indicate that these refractory inclusions experienced aqueous alteration on Bennu’s parent planetesimal. Therefore, they were incorporated into the parent planetesimal prior to the onset of aqueous alteration, during which phyllosilicates and magnetite formed. Additionally, the phyllosilicates replacing AOA olivine grains and the coarser phyllosilicates adjacent to HKBC02 (Fig. 2c) are chemically distinct from matrix phyllosilicates surrounding these inclusions or typical Bennu matrix phyllosilicates (Supplementary Fig. 3). This evidence indicates that aqueous alteration in these cases was controlled by local fluid–mineral interactions—whereas the occurrence of extensive evaporite sequences, including carbonates, sulfates, halides, and Na-bearing phosphates, indicates that Bennu’s parent body also experienced prolonged, system-wide brine evolution and fluid circulation24. Furthermore, the higher abundance of primary minerals and refractory inclusions in calcite-bearing Bennu particles or clasts (Fig. 1), compared with other portions of the samples, suggests that these components survived because they experienced locally low degrees of alteration, perhaps reflecting low water-to-rock ratios20. The coexistence of clasts and lithologies with different alteration histories, not only among separate Bennu particles but also within individual particles, especially those of the hummocky lithology28,31, highlights the complex interplay between localized fluid–mineral reactions and more extended brine-driven alteration recorded in the evaporite phases24.

Comparable evidence of aqueous alteration experienced by refractory inclusions has been observed in Ryugu samples, including a CAI composed of hibonite and spinel enclosing magnetite plaquettes14 and a CAI with interstitial phyllosilicates20. In the CI chondrite Ivuna, similar features are observed, such as an AOA-like inclusion with interstitial phyllosilicates and an olivine grain partially replaced at its surface by phyllosilicates13. These findings collectively demonstrate that refractory inclusions and high-temperature minerals were among the building blocks of the parent planetesimal(s) of Bennu, Ryugu, and CI chondrites prior to aqueous alteration. In contrast, a melilite-rich CAI and an anorthite-diopside-bearing AOA have been identified in Ivuna that show little to no signs of aqueous alteration13,32, despite melilite and anorthite being highly susceptible to alteration33. These inclusions may have been incorporated into the parent planetesimal after most secondary minerals had already formed.

Common origins of refractory inclusions in Bennu and Ryugu samples and chondrites

Beyond their alteration features, the mineralogical and isotopic properties of the refractory inclusions provide insights into their origin and formation environment. The mineral chemistry (e.g., Mg# and CaO contents) (Supplementary Fig. 5), textural associations, and 16O-rich compositions (Δ17O ~ –23‰) of olivine grains in the Bennu AOAs are consistent with those of AOAs in carbonaceous chondrites, including CI6,13,34–36. These olivine grains are also likely genetically related to 16O-rich, isolated olivine grains in Bennu, Ryugu, and CI chondrites13,14,17,25,27. Similarly, the mineral chemistry and 16O-rich compositions (Δ17O ~ –24‰) of the Bennu CAIs (HKBC01 and HKBC03) and the isolated spinel grain are also consistent with those of CAIs in carbonaceous chondrites, including CI6,7,14,37, the unequilibrated ordinary chondrite Semarkona38, and Ryugu samples13–15. Thus, refractory inclusions in the Bennu samples were likely formed in a high-temperature (>~1300 K) (ref. 1), 16O-rich nebular gas environment similar to those in which CAIs in other chondrites originated.

In contrast to inclusions with 16O-rich signatures, an Al-Ti diopside grain in HKBC02 exhibits a 16O-poor composition (Δ17O ~ –4‰) and variable Al2O3 (~11.6 to 22.0 wt%) and TiO2 (~0.5 to 9.7 wt%) contents. The isotopic and chemical compositions of the diopside, together with its association with Mg-Al spinel, closely resemble those of coarse-grained Type B (pyroxene-rich) and Type C (anorthite-rich) CAIs found in CV chondrites37,39,40. We therefore interpret HKBC02 as a fragment of a coarse-grained CAI. Its 16O-poor composition likely reflects melting in a 16O-poor nebular gas followed by O-isotope exchange with the ambient gas.

The inferred (26Al/27Al)0 of (5.4 ± 0.4) × 10−5 for the CAI HKBC01 is consistent with the canonical CAI value representing the Solar System’s initial ratio (~5.2 × 10−5; refs. 2,3), and corresponding to the formation age of –0.02 (+0.08/–0.07) Ma relative to the canonical CAIs, assuming homogeneous distribution of 26Al in the CAI-forming region. The (26Al/27Al)0 value is also consistent with values reported for CAIs in carbonaceous and ordinary chondrites5,6,8,41 and for a CAI in a Ryugu sample14. The HKBC01 formed during the earliest stage of the solar protoplanetary disk, within the first ~0.1 Ma of the Solar System evolution.

These mineralogical, isotopic, and chronological similarities among refractory inclusions in Bennu samples, Ryugu samples, and CI chondrites provide evidence that their parent planetesimal(s) accreted a common suite, and likely similar relative abundances, of high-temperature refractory inclusions and their fragments. This suggests a close affinity in the primary building blocks of their parent planetesimal(s). These inclusions are also mineralogically, isotopically, and chronologically similar to those in ordinary and other carbonaceous chondrites, demonstrating the ubiquitous presence of refractory inclusions across chondritic astromaterials more broadly. Bulk isotopic systematics further indicate that CI-like dust was widespread throughout the region of the solar protoplanetary disk from which carbonaceous chondrites accreted42–44. Thus, parent planetesimals of carbonaceous chondrites shared at least some building blocks in common, though in different relative proportions, probably reflecting dust migration and filtering in the disk.

Constraints on the accretion environment of Bennu’s parent planetesimal

Refractory inclusions (CAIs and AOAs) formed in the high-temperature regions of the solar protoplanetary disk during its earliest stage and were subsequently transported across the disk to the accretion regions of various parent planetesimals9. In Bennu samples analyzed thus far, as in those from Ryugu and CI chondrites14,15,20, large (>submillimeter) refractory inclusions are absent. In contrast, inclusions larger than ~100 µm, including submillimeter- to centimeter-sized objects, as well as numerous smaller inclusions, are common in non-CI carbonaceous chondrite groups6,34,36,45–47. CAIs in CM chondrites are mostly small (<~100 µm) (ref. 47), whereas large (>~100 µm) AOAs are commonly observed34,48. Considering the drift timescale49, small (<~100 µm) refractory inclusions could have avoided rapid inward drift toward the Sun and remained in the protoplanetary disk for several million years after its formation. Their presence in Bennu and Ryugu samples and CI chondrites can be explained in this framework, and the same mechanism could also account for the occurrence of similarly small inclusions in ordinary chondrites38,41,47. By contrast, larger (>submillimeter) inclusions, common in other carbonaceous chondrite groups, would have been lost to rapid inward drift toward the proto-Sun unless retained by additional mechanisms. One plausible mechanism is the formation of gas pressure bumps, where large dust particles are locally trapped and prevented from drifting inward50. Although planets are not the only possible source of such pressure bumps, proto-Jupiter provides one plausible example in the early Solar System and could have concentrated submillimeter- to centimeter-sized refractory inclusions, accounting for their occurrence in carbonaceous chondrites other than CIs11. Based on this size-dependent migration model, Kawasaki et al.14 inferred that the parent planetesimal(s) of Ryugu and CI chondrites formed at a greater heliocentric distance, beyond the influence of the proto-Jupiter–induced pressure bump. If the parent planetesimal(s) of Ryugu and CI chondrites accreted later than those of other carbonaceous chondrite groups containing large refractory inclusions, the refractory inclusions remaining in the disk would have been predominantly small, and such later accretion could in principle account for the absence of large refractory inclusions in Ryugu and CI chondrites. However, their parent planetesimal(s) are inferred to have accreted earlier than 2.0 Ma after the formation of the canonical CAIs51,52, earlier than the inferred accretion ages of other carbonaceous chondrite parent bodies53–55. These chronological relationships support formation beyond the dust-trapping region associated with proto-Jupiter.

Bennu shares the same size distribution of refractory inclusions as Ryugu and CI chondrites, suggesting a similar accretion setting. Within this outer-disk environment beyond the dust-trapping region associated with proto-Jupiter, streaming instability may have played a role in assembling 100-km–scale parent bodies56, sweeping up fine dust particles that dominated the local solid reservoir, whereas larger inclusions had already been removed by rapid inward drift before the onset of streaming instability and/or had been concentrated near the proto-Jupiter pressure bump. This possibility is also compatible with scenarios in which streaming instability is triggered by photoevaporation-driven enhancement of the dust-to-gas ratio57,58.

The formation of Bennu’s parent planetesimal beyond the dust-trapping region of the pressure bump, together with the paucity of large refractory inclusions, is consistent with Bennu’s chemically primitive nature (i.e., a near-solar bulk composition)16,17, because even minor addition of CAI-like refractory material would shift Bennu’s bulk composition away from solar values32. This strengthens the interpretation, based on bulk isotopic compositions17, that Bennu, Ryugu, and CI chondrites likely formed in a common outer Solar System reservoir. Furthermore, the apparent rarity of chondrules in Bennu samples18, as well as in Ryugu samples and CI chondrites, suggests that their parent planetesimal(s) formed at a different time and/or in a different location from other carbonaceous chondrite groups containing abundant chondrules. This view is further supported by chronological evidence of the accretion age of Ryugu’s parent planetesimal51,52 and distinct nucleosynthetic isotope signatures for Ryugu samples and CI chondrites58,59.

We conclude that Bennu’s parent planetesimal, like those of Ryugu and CI chondrites, accreted small refractory inclusions that persisted in the outer Solar System beyond the influence of the proto-Jupiter–induced pressure bump. Taken together, these findings highlight the need to integrate Bennu samples into comparative studies of early Solar System solids to better constrain the diversity and possible genetic links among planetesimal formation pathways.

Methods

Sample preparation and electron microscopy

We prepared polished sections of 12 Bennu particles (Supplementary Figs. 1, 2). The Bennu samples OREX-803111-0, OREX-803112-0, and OREX-803113-0 were individually embedded in 1-inch epoxy disks (Buhler EpoxiCure 2), while seven particles (OREX-803114-0 particle #1 to OREX-803114-0 particle #7) were embedded in a single 1-inch epoxy disk. These particles were derived from aggregate samples (unsorted bulk Bennu material with particle sizes <0.5 cm). They were polished with an automatic polishing machine (Musashino Denshi MA-200e). The sample polishing procedures are similar to those described by Kawasaki et al. (13). Diamond slurries with polycrystalline diamond particles of ~3 µm and ~1 µm dissolved in ethylene glycol and polishing plates composed of copper and tin-antimony alloy were used for polishing. Only >99.5% ethanol was used for cleaning during and after the polishing. NASA’s Johnson Space Center allocated the polished sections OREX-800088-8 and OREX-800055-15. For these sections, dry polishing was performed, first with 600-grit silicon carbide film, followed by sequential diamond powders of 6, 3, and 1 µm. These two samples were prepared from centimeter-sized stones of interest.

The polished sections of Bennu were coated with a thin ( ~ 20 nm) carbon film for electron microscopy. Backscattered electron (BSE) images were obtained using a field emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM; JEOL JSM-7000F) at Hokkaido University. X-ray elemental mapping and quantitative analysis of chemical compositions were conducted using an energy dispersive X-ray spectrometer (EDS; Oxford X-Max 150) installed on the FE-SEM. A 15 keV electron beam probe with currents of ~2 nA and ~1 nA was used for the mapping and quantitative analysis, respectively. Quantitative calculations were conducted using Oxford AZtec software. BSE images and pseudo-color images made by X-ray elemental mapping were used to determine petrological and mineralogical characteristics of the samples. After electron microscopy was completed, the sections were recoated with an additional thin ( ~ 70 nm) gold film using a high-resolution sputter coater (Cressington 208HR) for SIMS measurements.

For the transmission electron microscopy (TEM), electron-transparent sections (OREX-803114-100) were extracted from the region of interest on the HKBA02 AOA in OREX-803114-0 particle #1 using a focused ion beam (FIB) system (Helios NanoLab G3 CX at Kyoto University). The sample was coated with an electron-beam-deposited Pt layer (at 2 kV) followed by a Ga ion-beam-deposited Pt layer (at 30 kV). The sections of a few tens of micrometers in size were extracted and thinned to 100–150 nm using a 16–30 keV Ga+ beam, and finally cleaned using a 2 keV Ga+ beam at 77 pA. The FIB sections were analyzed using an FE-TEM (JEOL JEM-2100F) equipped with an EDS (JEOL JED-2300T) at Kyoto University. Bright-field TEM images, selected-area electron-diffraction (SAED) patterns, and scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) images at 200 kV were obtained using a CCD or CMOS camera (Gatan Orius200D, Rio9). EDS analysis was performed in STEM mode. Quantitative elemental abundances were calculated using the ζ-factor method60.

Oxygen isotope analysis

O-isotope compositions in diopside, hibonite, spinel, and olivine from the Bennu samples were measured with the Cameca ims-1280HR SIMS instrument at Hokkaido University. The analytical conditions follow those described in Kawasaki et al. (13). A 133Cs+ primary beam accelerated to 20 keV was employed in the experiment. A normal-incidence electron flood gun was used for electrostatic charge compensation of the analyzing areas during the measurements. Negative secondary ions (16O−, 17O−, and 18O−) were measured simultaneously in the multicollection mode. The mass resolution of M/ΔM for 17O− was set at > 6000 to resolve 17O– from 16OH–, while that for 16O− and 18O− was ~2000. The automatic centering program (DTFA and magnetic field) was applied before data collection. Takashima augite (δ18O = 6.3‰), Russian spinel (δ18O = 8.5‰), and San Carlos olivine (Mg# = 89; δ18O = 5.2‰) were used as standards to correct the instrumental mass fractionation for diopside, hibonite and spinel, and olivine, respectively.

For the measurements of hibonite in HKBC01 and spinel in HKBC03, a ~3-pA primary beam with an elliptical shape of 0.5 × 1.2 µm (~0.8 × 1.9 µm including beam halo) was used. 16O−, 17O−, and 18O− were measured using a multicollector FC (1011 Ω, designated as L1), an axial EM, and a multicollector EM (defined as H2), respectively. The secondary ion intensities of 16O− were ~2.0 × 106 cps. The data were collected for 200 cycles with a 4-s integration time per cycle. The 16OH− count rate was measured immediately after the measurements, but we did not make a tail correction on 17O− because its contribution to 17O− was calculated as typically ~0.04‰, comparable to that for standards. The external reproducibility of the standard measurements (2 SD), which was larger than the internal error (2 standard error, 2SE of the cycle data) of the samples, was reported as the analytical uncertainties.

For the measurements of other minerals, a ~ 30-pA primary beam with an elliptical shape of 1.7 × 2.7 μm (~2.3 × 3.6 µm including beam halo) was used, and the measurements were conducted during the same sessions as those by Barnes et al. (17). Detector settings are the same as those for the ~3-pA condition above. The data were collected for 60 cycles with a 4-s integration time per cycle. The 16OH− count rate was measured immediately after the measurements, but we did not make a tail correction on 17O− because its contribution to 17O− was calculated as typically ~0.02‰, comparable to that for standards. The reported uncertainties were the larger of the external reproducibility of standard measurements (2 SD) or the internal error (2SE of cycle data) of samples.

Al–Mg isotope analysis

Mg-isotope compositions and 27Al/24Mg ratios for the hibonite and olivine from the Bennu samples were measured using the same SIMS instrument used for the O-isotope analysis. The analytical conditions follow those described in Kawasaki et al (14, 61). For the measurement of hibonite in HKBC01, an ~16-pA, 16O− primary beam accelerated to 23 keV with an elliptical shape of 1.0 × 2.0 μm (~1.8 × 3.4 µm including beam halo) was used. Mg-isotopes (24Mg+, 25Mg+, and 26Mg+) were measured using an axial electron multiplier (EM), while 27Al+ was measured simultaneously with 25Mg+ using a multicollector Faraday cup (FC) (1011 Ω, designed for H2*) in the peak-jumping mode. A waiting time of 2 s was set before counting 27Al+ intensities to compensate for the time constants of the FC. The mass resolution of M/ΔM was set at ~4000, which is sufficient to resolve ion interferences (e.g., 48Ca2+, 24MgH+, 25MgH+, and 52Cr2+). The secondary ion intensity of 24Mg+ was ~1.2 × 104 cps. The measurement was conducted for 100 cycles, with a counting sequence of 24Mg+ for 2 s, 25Mg+ for 6 s, 27Al+ for 4 s (simultaneously with 25Mg+), and 26Mg+ for 6 s. The obtained count rates were corrected for the FC background and EM deadtime. A synthetic hibonite standard (Hib100) with 27Al/24Mg = 100.71 and δ25MgDSM3 = –1.69 ± 0.13‰ (ref. 61) was used as a standard to obtain the relative sensitivity factor (RSF) of 27Al/24Mg and correct the instrumental mass fractionation. The mass-dependent fractionation of Mg-isotopes is shown as the DSM-3 scale62. The external reproducibility of standard measurements (2 SD), internal error (2SE of cycle data), and the uncertainties of reference δ25MgDSM3 and 27Al/24Mg were propagated to obtain the reported uncertainty of δ25MgDSM3 and 27Al/24Mg, because the internal error is much smaller than the external reproducibility. The δ26Mg* values were calculated using an exponential fractionation law with coefficient αnatural = 0.5128 and as δ26Mg* = δ26Mgsample – [(1 + δ25Mgsample/1000)1/α – 1] × 1000 (ref. 63). The reported uncertainty for the δ26Mg* was assigned as internal error (2SE).

For the measurement of olivine in HKBA03, an ~0.5-nA, 16O2− primary beam with an elliptical shape of 4 × 6 μm (~5 × 8 µm including beam halo) was used, and the measurements were conducted during the same sessions as those for olivine grains in Ivuna AOAs by Kawasaki et al. (14). Mg-isotopes (24Mg+, 25Mg+, and 26Mg+) and 27Al+ were measured simultaneously in the multicollection mode with four Faraday cups: 24Mg+ for L2* (1011 Ω), 25Mg+ for L1 (1012 Ω), 26Mg+ for H1 (1012 Ω), and 27Al+ for H2* (1010 Ω). The mass resolution of M/ΔM was set at ~2000. The contributions of ion interferences (e.g., 48Ca2+, 24MgH+, 25MgH+, and 52Cr2+) were negligible under these conditions. The secondary ion intensity of 24Mg+ was ~1.4 × 108 cps. The measurement was conducted with 20 cycles of counting the secondary ions for 10 s. The obtained count rates were corrected for FC background and the relative yield of each detector. The RSF of 27Al/24Mg, defined for (27Al/24MgSIMS)/(27Al/24Mgreal), for olivine was assumed to be 1. The δ25MgDSM3 values for the unknown olivines were not determined because the mass-dependent fractionation for Mg-rich olivine (Mg# > ~80) strongly depends on chemical compositions64, and the use of a single standard is not appropriate. The δ26Mg* values were calculated with the same equation as the hibonite and corrected using the San Carlos olivine standard. The external reproducibility of standard measurements (2 SD) and internal error (2SE of cycle data) were propagated to obtain the reported uncertainty in δ26Mg*.

Areas for the O- and Al–Mg isotope analyses were precisely determined according to scanning ion image of 16O− or 24Mg+ collected by electron multipliers (C for 16O− and axial for 24Mg+) with a procedure similar to that described in Kawasaki et al. (13, 14). Before measurements, we made a few sputtered craters near measurement targets using a ~30-pA, 133Cs+ primary beam by the SIMS and then electron images were obtained by the FE-SEM to obtain distances from the sputtered craters to the measurement targets. The craters were visible in 16O− or 24Mg+ scanning images and used to locate the target minerals. Measurement spots were observed by the FE-SEM after SIMS measurements.
