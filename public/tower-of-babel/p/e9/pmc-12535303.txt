Antifungal Resistance and Synergistic Therapy in Candida Spp. Isolated From Bottlenose Dolphins Under Human Care: Implications for One Health Management
Authors: Barbara Biancani, Federica Mauri, Francesco Di Nezio, Diana Binanti, Stefano Furlati, Samuele Roman, Giorgia Matteucci
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionRoutine antifungal susceptibility testing in animals is uncommon, yet increasingly relevant as antifungal resistance rises among marine mammals under human care. Despite the critical health risks posed by fungal infections, little research addresses multidrug‐resistant Candida spp. in marine mammals under human care. This study aims to investigate the prevalence and antifungal susceptibility profiles of Candida species isolated from faecal and blowhole samples of bottlenose dolphins (Tursiops truncatus) under human care and to evaluate the in vitro efficacy of antifungal combination therapies within a One Health framework that considers environmental reservoirs and potential zoonotic risks.Methods Candida species were isolated from 14 faecal and 9 blowhole exhalate samples of 7 bottlenose dolphins. Minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) were determined through broth microdilution, revealing resistance to commonly used azoles (itraconazole, posaconazole, voriconazole and fluconazole). Synergy tests of antifungal combinations indicated that certain drug pairings could enhance treatment efficacy against resistant strains. Environmental monitoring of pool water samples identified Candida spp. using fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH) a more sensitive diagnostic tool compared to traditional culture‐based methods.ResultsIn this study, Candida species (C. albicans, C. tropicalis, C. parapsilosis and N. glabratus) were isolated from faecal and blowhole exhalate samples of seven bottlenose dolphins (T. truncatus) to evaluate susceptibility profiles. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to report multidrug‐resistant Candida spp. in dolphin faeces and blowholes and to assess the in vitro efficacy of synergistic antifungal treatments.ConclusionThis study underscores the importance of routine antifungal susceptibility testing and environmental surveillance in managed‐care marine mammals to mitigate infection risks within a One Health framework.Summary Increases of antifungal resistance underscore the need for One Health approach.The in vitro synergy tests to identify appropriate treatment for candidiasis in bottlenose dolphins.FISH, complementary tool to detect Candida spp. in the water environment.Control the spread of drug‐resistant Candida to enhance both animal and human health safety.

Introduction

Routine antifungal diseases are increasingly recognized as a significant concern for both human and animal health, emphasizing the need for a ‘One Health’ approach that integrates human, animals and environment. Since the mid‐20th century, global fungal infections have risen sharply, now impacting approximately 1 billion people annually and resulting in over 3 million deaths (Carpouron et al. 2022; Denning 2024). Emerging fungal infections have been linked with modern human activities, increased human–animal interactions and significant environmental changes. Although infections in human and domestic animals have been extensively studied, the last decades have seen growing attention to mycotic infections in wildlife and wild animals under human care. Various fungal species, including Aspergillus spp., Blastomyces spp., Cryptococcus spp., Candida spp. and Lacazia loboi, have been reported as causes of disease in marine mammals (Reidarson et al. 2018; Reif et al. 2008; Morris et al. 2011; Waltzek et al. 2012), whether in research, management or medical settings (Hunt et al. 2008).

Marine mammals, including cetaceans (19 species, 9.8%) and carnivores (11 species, 5.7%), represent the second largest fraction of fungal pathogen hosts among aquatic organisms (Pang et al. 2021). Both wild and managed‐care marine mammals are known to be susceptible to infections from a range of fungal agents, with the respiratory system of cetaceans being particularly vulnerable due to the absence of nasal turbinates. Symptoms in animals under human care often involve respiratory, skin and gastrointestinal infections (Delaney et al. 2012; Haulena et al. 2010, Pang et al. 2021).

Candida spp. have been isolated from the upper respiratory tract in wild bottlenose dolphins (Tursiops spp.), suggesting they may be part of the normal flora of these animals (Morris et al. 2011). However, in managed‐care cetaceans, Candida spp. can cause disease, secondary to stress, immunocompromise situations, concurrent infectious diseases, unbalanced water disinfection with chlorines and indiscriminate or prolonged antimicrobial therapies (Field 2024; Reidarson et al. 2018). In aquaria, fungi have been detected in the water of breeding facilities and among care staff (Kohata et al. 2013), highlighting close contact as a potential pathway for the spread of resistant microbes (Radhouani et al. 2014). Candida spp. typically respond to azole‐based treatments, including itraconazole (IZ) and fluconazole (FZ), as well as to terbinafine (TER) and nystatin (NYS), which are commonly used as first‐line treatments for dolphin infections (Reidarson et al. 2018). Nevertheless, recent studies showed that the use of antifungal agents in agriculture resulted in an increase of antifungal resistance. Additionally, among the numerous Candida species, C. albicans, C. tropicalis, N. glabratus, C. parapsilosis and Pichia kudriavzevii, which was formerly and is still commonly known as Candida krusei, are responsible for over 90% of invasive infections (Pappas et al. 2018), and several reports remarked on an increase in antimicrobial resistance (AMR) among Candida spp. in both humans (Farmakiotis et al. 2014; Pristov and Ghannoum 2019; Sakamoto et al. 2021) and dolphins (Shirakata et al. 2022; Takahashi et al. 2010).

Recently, Garcia‐Bustos et al. (2023) reported that Candida auris, a global threatening pathogen due to its multidrug resistance and its ability to form biofilms and persist on surfaces, might have originated zoonotically from the marine reservoir, where marine mammals act as potential intermediate hosts in association with climate change.

The valuable insights obtained from detecting fungal pathogens in marine mammals illustrate an intricate web of ecological and health interdependencies that also affects human populations. The shifting patterns of fungal disease emergence, along with the rising resistance to antifungal therapies, underscore the need for a comprehensive ‘One Health’ approach not only for the welfare of the animals but also for trainers, veterinarians, biologists and researchers working daily with these animals (Garcia‐Bustos, Acosta‐Hernández, et al. 2024).

Garcia‐Bustos, Rosario Medina, et al. (2024) reported as Candida spp. isolated from cetaceans presented a much higher prevalence of antifungal resistance compared to human Candida spp. isolated in clinical settings, highlighting the concern of pharmaceutical pollution in sea water and public health consequences.

The increasing spread of AMR is a critical concern in human and animal medicine, with implications for global health, agriculture and wildlife conservation. Therefore, the appropriate use of antimicrobials is essential to mitigate the frequency of infections caused by resistant microorganisms, which aligns with the ‘One Health’ framework—a multidisciplinary approach recognizing the interconnected health of humans, animals and the environment (Mackenzie and Jeggo 2019).

This study explores the emergence of antifungal resistance in Candida spp. within bottlenose dolphins under human care, contributing to a ‘One Health’ perspective by addressing the potential transfer of resistant organisms across species and environment. Previous research reported the presence of the same pathogens in dolphins and trainers sharing the same environment (Takahashi et al. 2010) underlying not only the potential zoonotic risk but also the increasing risk of antimicrobic resistance passing from animals to human and vice versa through the aquatic environment. The overall goal of this study is to determine the prevalence and the susceptibility profile of Candida species in faecal and blowhole samples from captive bottlenose dolphins, considering the physio/pathological status of the animals. Given that animals of the present study were housed in an outdoor, closed‐system pool, cultural and molecular methods were employed to detect the presence of Candida species in water samples collected from their tanks. This approach underscores the environmental aspect of the ‘One Health’ framework in identifying potential sources of infection.

With the emergence of AMR, limited therapeutic options are available for multidrug‐resistant microorganisms, and managing these infections often requires combination therapy. Synergy testing method was employed to assess the in vitro interaction of antimicrobial combinations and determine whether they act (i) additively, when the combined activity is the sum of the two antimicrobials acting together, (ii) synergistically, when the combined activity is greater than the sum of the two antimicrobials used individually or (iii) antagonistically, when the combined activity is less than the activity of the two antimicrobials used individually.

Materials and Methods

In the present study, a total of seven bottlenose dolphins (Tursiops truncatus) were observed in outdoor, closed‐system pools with depths ranging from 2 to 6 m. The pools contained artificial saltwater with a salinity of 25.6 ± 0.5 g/L and a pH of 7.69 ± 0.03. Temperatures were maintained between 18.3°C and 25.9°C, and chlorine was used as the primary disinfectant at a concentration of 0.8 ± 0.2 mg/L (mean ± SD for total chlorine). In previous years, none of the animals had been showing signs of infection due to Candida spp., and none of the animals had received antimycotic drugs before the present case. One animal suddenly presented clinical signs of gastrointestinal discomfort, anorexia and abnormal faeces that tested positive to the presence of yeast cells at the cytology examination.

Sample Collection

Samples were collected as part of the standard clinical examination under voluntary behaviour following routine positive reinforcement techniques, consistent with EAAM standards for facilities housing bottlenose dolphins (http://www.eaam.org/housing_standards) and the EU directive for zoo animals (http://eur‐lex.europa.eu).

Faecal Samples

Each dolphin was presented in a ‘belly‐up’ position to dry the anal area, allowing direct rectal sampling using a sterile 4 mm Levin's tube (Pennine Healthcare, Derby, UK), inserted 10–20 cm into the rectum. The faecal material was collected on a sterile swab and placed in Amies transport medium.

Blowhole Exhalates Samples

Blowhole samples were collected using sterile plastic specimen cups. After drying the external blowhole with a clean gauze, each dolphin was prompted to breathe once, followed by repeated exhalations into the cup. Sterile swabs were then collected from the cup and stored in Amies transport medium at room temperature and delivered to the laboratory within 24 h.

Laboratory Analysis

Culture and Identification

Stool and exhalate samples were cultured on Sabouraud Dextrose Agar (Liofilchem, Italy) and ChromAgar Candida (Liofilchem, Italy) at 27°C for 5 days. CHROMagar colonies consistent with Candida spp. were identified through biochemical tests according to de Hoog et al. (2020).

Antifungal Susceptibility Testing

The minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of amphotericin B (AMB), 5‐flucytosine (FC), IZ, FZ, miconazole (MCZ), posaconazole (PZ), voriconazole (VOR), NYS, TER, caspofungin (CAS), anidulafungin (AND) and micafungin (MF) were assessed using the broth microdilution method according to Clinical Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) M27 guidelines. Tests were performed in duplicate, with isolates classified as susceptible (S), intermediate (I) or resistant (R) following CLSI M60 clinical breakpoints.

Synergy Testing

To evaluate potential synergistic effects of FZ and TER, VOR and TER and VOR and NYS, the fractional inhibitory concentration (FIC) index was determined by broth microdilution checkerboard method. The checkerboard method assesses the activities of antifungal combinations tested in serial 2‐fold broth dilutions following the CLSI M27 guidelines. The FIC is calculated by comparing the value of the MIC of each agent alone with the combination‐derived MIC. Antimicrobial combinations with an FIC index <0.5 were considered synergistic, whereas values between 0.5 and 4 were additive, and values >4 were antagonistic (Hall et al. 1983).

Environmental Sampling

A total of 20 water samples were collected two times per month in September and October from the suction points of five different interconnected pools sharing the same Life Support System (collection points identified from A to E). Samples were filtered through 0.22 µm polycarbonate filters (Merck Millipore). Half of each filter was used for cultural tests, whereas the other half was fixed with paraformaldehyde and stored at −20°C in ethanol and PBS (phosphate buffer saline) for fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH) analyses.

For Candida spp. detection with cultural method, filters were placed on Sabouraud Dextrose Agar (Liofilchem, Italy) agar and incubated at 27°C for 5 days.

For FISH analysis, filters were vortexed to detach microorganisms. Samples of 5 µL were mixed with 5 µL of PBS and Triton 1%, samples of 5 µL were deposited on a microscope slide and dried for 20 min at 46°C, and samples were dehydrated by immersing the slide in different % of ethanol (3 min 50%, 3 min 70%, 3 min 96%) then permeabilized with methanol and dried at 46°C. Hybridization was performed for each sample using 9 µL of buffer with 20% formamide (with C. tropicalis’s probe, 10% of formamide was used) and 1 µL of probe. We used five probes specific to C. albicans, C. parapsilosis complex, P. kudriavzevii, N. glabratus (Kempf et al. 2000) and C. tropicalis (Lischewski et al. 1996). After overnight incubation at 46°C, samples were washed, stained with 0.0001% DAPI (4′,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole) solution and observed under a fluorescence microscope (Axiolab, Zeiss).

No comparative statistical analysis between culture and FISH results has been done due to low number of samples.

Results

A total of 14 samples of faeces and 9 sputum samples from blowhole were analysed.

All the 14 samples from faeces were positive for Candida spp., identified as C. albicans (n.9), C. tropicalis (n.2), N. glabratus (n.2) and C. parapsilosis (n.1).

All the isolates from faeces showed resistance to azoles (100%). Overall, 50% of the isolates showed resistance to FC and 21.4% to AMB. No isolate showed resistance to echinocandins (CAS, AND and MF). For NYS and TER, there are no breakpoints available, so it is not possible to categorize the results as S, I or R. MIC values ranged from 16 to 128 µg/mL for TER (arithmetic mean 107.4 µg/mL) and from 8 to 128 µg/mL (arithmetic mean 52 µg/mL) for NYS (Table S1).

The nine samples from the blowhole tested positive for Candida spp., specifically C. albicans (n = 6), C. tropicalis (n = 1), N. glabratus (n = 1) and C. parapsilosis (n = 1). All isolates demonstrated complete resistance to azoles (100%). Resistance to FC was present in 22.2% of isolates, and 11.1% exhibited resistance to AMB. No resistance was detected against echinocandins (CAS, AND and MF). The MIC values for NYS ranged from 8 to 128 µg/mL (arithmetic mean 63.1 µg/mL), whereas those for TER ranged from 16 to 128 µg/mL (arithmetic mean 115.5 µg/mL) (Table S2).

Due to MIC results, none of the tested antifungals were considered eligible for the treatment on their own, and combination therapy was evaluated.

Synergy tests were performed to identify possible synergic activities. For the Candida spp. isolated from the faecal samples, results of in vitro synergy test by checkerboard method for FZ and TER showed a synergistic effect on 71.4% and an additive effect on 28.6% of the tested isolates. For VOR and TER, an additive effect on 71.4% and a synergistic effect on 28.6% of the isolates were detected. For VOR and NYS, an antagonistic effect on 71.4% and an additive effect on 28.6% of the isolates were detected (Table S1). The in vitro synergy testing using the checkerboard method for Candida spp. isolated from blowhole samples revealed a synergistic effect between FZ and TER in 55.6% of the isolates, whereas an additive effect was observed in 44.4% of the isolates. In contrast, the combination of VOR and TER showed an additive effect in 77.8% of the isolates, and a synergistic effect was detected in 22.2% of the isolates. When examining the interaction between VOR and NYS, an antagonistic effect was observed in 66.7% of the isolates, with an additive effect in 33.3% (Table S2).

According to results, combination therapy with FLU and TER was started for the animal presenting clinical signs. FLU and TER for the combined therapy were selected on the basis of the results of in vitro synergy tests, the safety profiles of these antifungals for use in dolphins, and the legal constraints on the administration of such drugs within the country of application. Symptoms resolved completely, confirmed by two bi‐weekly negative faecal cultures.

Of the 20 water samples tested with cultural methods, only site ‘C’ was positive for Candida spp., yielding C. tropicalis.

FISH analyses revealed the presence of Candida species in water samples (Figure 1). C. albicans was present in all sampling points except point B that was negative for all Candida species tested. Sample collected from point C was positive also for C. parapsilosis and C. tropicalis, confirming the result obtained with cultural method. All samples were negative for N. glabratus and P. kudriavzevii.

Discussion

The relevance of Candida spp. isolated from bottlenose dolphin remains uncertain as Candida spp. colonization appears to be a typical component of cetacean tissue microbiota, analogous to that observed in humans. These yeasts have been identified in both healthy and diseased animals, as well as in various sample types, including blowhole, gastric and faecal samples (Fothergill and Jogessar 1986; Matteucci et al. 2022). Nevertheless, a high load of Candida spp. may manifest clinically. Already, Buck (1980) reported the presence of human‐associated yeasts in the faeces and pool waters of captive bottlenose dolphins, underlying an important overlap between human and cetacean microbiota. It also has to be considered that Candida spp. are common pathogens present in faeces of birds (Medina et al. 2017) which frequently fly above outdoors pools. The most frequently isolated species in cetaceans under human care are C. albicans, C. tropicalis, N. glabratus and C. parapsilosis, which is consistent with our results. These species have been found in both healthy individuals and dolphins exhibiting clinical signs, including respiratory infections and skin lesions (Van Bressem et al. 2009; Lee et al. 2019; Ohno et al. 2019; Garcia‐Bustos, Rosario Medina, et al. 2024).

Antifungal resistance is a significant concern in cetaceans, with high rates of resistance against azoles observed among Candida spp. isolates (Ohno et al. 2019; Garcia‐Bustos, Rosario Medina, et al. 2024). Previous studies have reported FLZ‐ or ITZ‐resistant C. albicans and C. tropicalis from the respiratory tracts of bottlenose dolphins (Matteucci et al. 2022; Shirakata et al. 2022; Takahashi et al. 2010).

The reduced susceptibility of clinical isolates to azoles in this study may result from the emergence of acquired resistance mechanisms. In humans, Candida spp. can develop azole resistance through prolonged antifungal therapy or inter‐individual transmission. Furthermore, as azoles exert only fungistatic effects on Candida species, this can lead to the emergence of azole‐resistant isolates (Pristov and Ghannoum 2019). Similar processes are likely to occur in marine mammals, both in the wild and in aquaria.

Azoles are the most used antifungals in dolphins, which may contribute to the increasing azole resistance observed. However, dolphins involved in this study have not received antimycotic therapies recently. Indeed, strains of C. albicans, N. glabratus, C. tropicalis and C. parapsilosis that lacked susceptibility to azoles have also been isolated from dolphins with no recent history of azole treatment (Cordeiro et al. 2015). Further studies are needed to explore possible mechanisms of environmental acquisition of resistance in dolphins without prior azole exposure.

The results from the synergy test between FZ and TER show synergistic effects of this combination against Candida spp. isolates resistant to azoles, providing a promising alternative therapeutic strategy for azole‐resistant infections. These findings support previous evidence (Canton et al. 2005) and suggest that combination therapy might offer effective treatment options in cases where azole resistance limits single‐agent efficacy. Future studies should aim to validate these in vitro results in clinical settings, examining the long‐term efficacy of combination therapies in cetacean care.

Molecular analyses (FISH) of water samples confirmed the presence of C. albicans, C. parapsilosis and C. tropicalis in the surrounding aquatic environment. This finding suggests that the environment may act as a reservoir for resistance. Notably, point C was identified as the most contaminated sampling site, highlighting the potential role of specific critical areas within the tanks in facilitating the spread of Candida spp.

The direct isolation of Candida spp. from water samples using culture methods is inherently challenging; therefore, we employed FISH as an alternative. However, a significant limitation of molecular techniques like FISH, compared to culture‐based approaches, is their inability to assess antifungal susceptibility. As a result, although FISH successfully detected the presence of Candida species in the pool water, it could not determine their susceptibility profiles or confirm whether these strains exhibit antifungal resistance traits.

During a previous culture‐based screening on water samples collected from various points in the tanks, the sampling site ‘C’ tested positive for C. tropicalis. At that time, a MIC test was performed on the environmental isolate. The antifungal susceptibility profile was found to be consistent with that of the C. tropicalis strain isolated from dolphins, supporting the hypothesis of the environmental dissemination of Candida spp.

The potential for environmental shedding of Candida spp. among dolphins suggests the possibility of yeast transmission within captive settings. The prevalence of antifungal resistance in such environments may be influenced by factors such as seawater composition and pharmaceutical pollution (Garcia‐Bustos, Rosario Medina, et al. 2024).

Takahashi et al. (2010) reported that azole‐resistant Candida spp. are likely to spread from one dolphin to another or from contaminated pool water. It can be reasonably inferred that azole‐resistant Candida spp. may disseminate between dolphins occupying the same environment. The higher incidences of resistance to azole antifungal agents in the isolates from dolphins might be also related to the sodium chloride in seawater, given the postulated correlations between chemical compound resistance, pathogenicity and sodium chloride (Schmid et al. 1995).

This study has several limitations that should be taken into account when interpreting the results. First, the sample size was small—only seven animals and a limited number of isolates—constraining the generalizability of the findings. Second, the cross‐sectional design prevents assessment of colonization persistence, temporal infection dynamics or the effects of interventions over time. Third, although environmental monitoring detected Candida spp. in pool water, positive culture isolation occurred at only a single site and timepoint. More extensive molecular typing would be necessary to definitively link environmental and clinical isolates. Finally, although FISH analysis effectively confirmed the presence of Candida species in the aquatic environment, it cannot assess antifungal susceptibility, and its correlation with culture‐based results remains limited. These limitations underscore the need for larger, longitudinal studies that integrate molecular and culture‐based methods to clarify the epidemiology and resistance patterns of Candida spp. in dolphins under human care.

Preventive Measures and Implications for One Health

Given the potential zoonotic and environmental impact of resistant Candida spp., implementing preventive measures to reduce transmission between individuals and their surroundings is essential. In addition, incorporating antifungal susceptibility screening for asymptomatic dolphins could enhance early detection of resistant strains, facilitating timely intervention. By situating these practices within a ‘One Health’ approach, facilities can address AMR in environmental, animal and human health dimensions, thus promoting sustainable health practices for marine mammals under human care.

Based on our findings and previous reports (Garcia‐Bustos, Rosario Medina, et al. 2024; Ohno et al. 2019), we hypothesize that the prevalence of azole‐resistant pathogenic Candida spp. is increasing in dolphins and their aquarium environments.

Azole‐resistant Candida species might directly or indirectly spread to humans, even if they are not thought to be pathogenic in healthy humans. Nonetheless, the control of drug‐resistant Candida spp. is necessary for both dolphins and human health.

Although susceptibility testing is an important tool for selecting appropriate therapy, it is not yet a common practice everywhere. It is important to conduct culture and susceptibility testing before starting any empirical therapeutic regimen. Azoles are the most used antifungal drugs in dolphins, but in the case of azole‐resistant Candida spp., appropriate antifungal agents alone or in combination other than azoles should be considered. Susceptibility screening of Candida spp. isolated from dolphins with no clinical signs is also advisable as a routine test to monitor azole resistance. If azole‐resistant Candida spp. are confirmed in an aquarium, the use of antifungal agents other than azole should be considered an empiric first choice in the case of candidiasis.

The results obtained by the checkerboard method corroborate the findings of previous report, indicating that the combination of FZ with TER is synergistic for C. albicans, N. glabratus (strain‐dependent) and C. tropicalis (Canton et al. 2005).

There is a paucity of outcome data to support the use of synergy testing to predict which antimicrobial combinations are most effective. In the present study, we report a favourable outcome of combination therapy with FZ and TER in a dolphin that exhibited clinical signs and demonstrated complete resolution following the initiation of therapy.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrates that FZ exhibits in vitro synergistic antifungal activity with TER against azole‐resistant strains of C. albicans, C. tropicalis, C. parapsilosis and N. glabratus isolated from captive bottlenose dolphins. Further studies are needed to determine reliability of these methods and the correlation of in vitro and in vivo results.

As azoles are the most used antifungals in dolphin care, our findings highlight an alarming trend of azole resistance in these marine mammals, likely influenced by environmental and host factors. As the tested dolphins had no recent history of azole treatment, resistance may also be environmentally derived. These findings underscore the need for monitoring of environmental reservoirs as potential sources of antifungal resistance within aquarium systems.

Thus, further studies and screening of multidrug‐resistant Candida spp. in animals and their environment are needed to better understand resistance transmission in a ‘One Health’ approach. In this context, antifungal susceptibility screening should become routine in aquaria, including screening of asymptomatic animals, to identify resistant strains early and allow for targeted interventions. This approach, aligned with ‘One Health’ principles, can enhance both animal and human health safety by controlling the spread of drug‐resistant Candida. The results of this study provide actionable insights for treatment, supporting the use of combination therapies for managing Candida spp. infections in cases where single‐agent therapy is ineffective.
