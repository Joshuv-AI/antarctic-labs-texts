Commentary on “The Safety and Effectiveness of a Self‐Made Negative Pressure Suction Device in the Treatment of Chronic Wounds”
Authors: Mostafa Javanian, Mohammad Barary, Soheil Ebrahimpour
Published: 2024

Dear Editor,

We read with great interest the recent publication by Wang et al. [1], titled “The Safety and Effectiveness of a Self‐made Negative Pressure Suction Device in the Treatment of Chronic Wounds.” The study provides valuable insights into the potential of this device to improve chronic wound healing in elderly patients. However, addressing several methodological and analytical limitations could strengthen its conclusions and enhance future research.

Firstly, while the study highlights promising findings, the lack of a randomized controlled trial (RCT) weakens the evidence for the efficacy and safety of the self‐made device. RCTs represent the highest standard for evaluating new medical interventions and are essential for minimizing bias [2]. Incorporating an RCT design would significantly bolster the validity of the results, particularly in the context of chronic wound management.

Moreover, the study's short‐term follow‐up period limits the ability to assess the long‐term effectiveness of the self‐made device. Chronic wounds require extended observation to fully evaluate outcomes, notably as healing rates can fluctuate over time due to various factors, including patient comorbidities. A follow‐up extending beyond 6 months would provide more conclusive evidence regarding the sustainability of the treatment benefits.

Another primary concern is the absence of a comparative analysis between the self‐made device and established commercial alternatives, such as vacuum sealing drainage (VSD). Without this comparison, it is not easy to evaluate the relative effectiveness of the self‐made device. Comparative studies are critical for determining whether the innovation provides tangible benefits over widely available alternatives.

Additionally, the small sample size undermines the statistical power of the study. While the findings are intriguing, a more considerable, more diverse patient population would improve the generalizability of the results [3]. Future studies should aim for a larger cohort to ensure the findings are robust and reflect broader clinical scenarios.

The omission of vascular assessments in patients with lower extremity wounds is another limitation that could lead to confounding results. Proper vascular testing is critical in assessing wound healing potential, especially in elderly patients, who are more likely to have underlying conditions such as peripheral arterial disease [4]. Implementing vascular assessments would provide a more comprehensive evaluation of the patient population and help tailor treatment strategies.

Finally, the selection of biomarkers in the study appears limited. Biomarkers such as the neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (PLR), systemic inflammation response index (SIRI), and systemic immune‐inflammation index (SII) have been shown to correlate with outcomes in chronic wound healing [5]. Including these biomarkers would provide a more nuanced understanding of the inflammatory processes involved and could help predict which patients are at higher risk for poor outcomes.

In conclusion, while the study by Wang et al. contributes significant findings, addressing these methodological limitations would strengthen the validity of the results. We hope our suggestions offer valuable perspectives for future research and further advancement in chronic wound management.
