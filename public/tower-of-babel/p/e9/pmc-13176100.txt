Optimization and verification of high‐fat diet formulation for establishing a rat model of obesity‐related precocious puberty
Authors: Jiayi Gong, Wei Qian, Deji Song, Kang Li, Li Zhang, Li Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChildhood obesity is closely linked to the rising incidence of precocious puberty (PP), yet valid preclinical models of obesity‐related PP remain lacking. High‐fat diets (HFD) are widely used for obesity models, but the suitability of different HFD formulations for inducing PP is unknown. Female Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats were randomized to control, 45% HFD, or 60% HFD groups at postnatal day (PND) 21. Body weight and daily caloric intake were monitored; vaginal opening (VO) was recorded from PND 35. At study termination, serum levels of estradiol (E2), luteinizing hormone (LH), follicle‐stimulating hormone (FSH), gonadotropin‐releasing hormone (GnRH), leptin, and adiponectin were measured. Ovarian histology and vaginal cytology were assessed. In vitro, GT1‐7 cells were treated with recombinant mouse leptin for 24 h, and GnRH secretion was quantified. Compared to controls and the 60% HFD group, rats fed 45% HFD exhibited significantly increased body weight, earlier VO onset, and elevated weights of organs. The 45% HFD group also had higher serum E2, LH, FSH, GnRH, and leptin levels, alongside reduced adiponectin; ovarian histology showed advanced follicular development, and vaginal smears displayed estrus‐stage cytology. In contrast, the 60% HFD group had no significant changes in body weight or hormonal parameters, and limited ovarian development. In vitro, leptin treatment significantly upregulated GnRH secretion in GT1‐7 cells. The 45% HFD formulation is optimal for constructing a juvenile rat model of obesity‐related PP, as it recapitulates key phenotypic, histological, and endocrine features of the disease.Keywords: diet‐induced obesity, gonadotropin‐releasing hormone, high‐fat diet, leptin, precocious puberty

INTRODUCTION

Puberty is a pivotal developmental milestone in the life course of mammals, marking the transition from childhood to adolescence through coordinated physiological, endocrine, and reproductive changes. While pubertal timing is primarily governed by genetic factors, accumulating evidence has highlighted its susceptibility to a spectrum of environmental factors, among which dietary patterns are increasingly recognized as key regulators. 1 This interplay between nutrition and reproductive development has heightened attention worldwide.

Over the past decades, the prevalence of childhood obesity has surged globally, paralleled by a significant rise in the incidence of obesity‐related precocious puberty (PP). 2 PP is defined as the onset of secondary sexual characteristics before 8 years of age in girls and 9 years of age in boys. 3 PP is particularly prominent in girls, and imposes profound health consequences. Affected children may experience psychological distress due to physical maturation disparity, while long‐term risks include an increased likelihood of adult obesity, metabolic syndrome, cardiovascular diseases, and even reproductive disorders such as polycystic ovary syndrome. 4 , 5

The clinical management of PP is currently dominated by gonadotropin‐releasing hormone (GnRH) analogs, which suppress the hypothalamic–pituitary‐gonadal (HPG) axis to halt premature pubertal progression. 6 However, this therapy is not universally applicable, some patients exhibit poor responsiveness, while others face potential adverse effects such as bone density loss or metabolic disturbances. 7 , 8 Thus, there is an urgent need to develop novel therapeutic strategies for obesity‐related PP, which depends on a comprehensive understanding of its underlying mechanisms and the availability of valid preclinical models.

The initiation of puberty is tightly regulated by the activation of the HPG axis. Hypothalamic GnRH secretion triggers the release of luteinizing hormone (LH) and follicle‐stimulating hormone (FSH) from the pituitary gland, which in turn stimulate gonadal development and sex hormone (e.g., estradiol, E2) production. 9 Obesity is hypothesized to disrupt this axis via adipose tissue‐derived factors, with leptin, a key adipokine involved in energy balance and reproductive regulation, proposed as a critical mediator linking excess adiposity to premature HPG axis activation. 10 To dissect this mechanism and accelerate drug development, preclinical models that recapitulate the pathophysiological features of obesity‐related PP in children are indispensable.

High‐fat diets (HFD) are widely used to construct diet‐induced obesity (DIO) models in rodents, with two predominant formulations: 45% and 60% of calories derived from fat. 11 , 12 These models have been instrumental in studying obesity‐related metabolic disorders, but their suitability for inducing obesity‐associated PP in juvenile rodents, whose developmental stage mirrors that of children, remains unvalidated. Moreover, a well‐characterized juvenile model that mimics the clinical features of childhood obesity‐related PP is currently lacking, limiting translational research progress.

In the present study, we aimed to address this gap by comparing the efficacy of 45% and 60% HFD formulations in inducing obesity and PP in juvenile rats. We systematically evaluated phenotypic, histological, and endocrine alterations to validate the model. Additionally, we explored the role of leptin in regulating GnRH secretion using a hypothalamic GnRH‐secreting cell line (GT1‐7) to provide mechanistic insights. The successful optimization and validation of this model will offer a robust preclinical tool for investigating the pathogenesis of obesity‐related PP and screening potential therapeutic agents.

METHODS

Animal experiment

Six pregnant Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats (10–12 weeks old) were purchased from Shanghai Bikai Keyi Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All experimental procedures involving animals were reviewed and approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the Shanghai University of Traditional Chinese Medicine (Approval No.: PZSHUTCM2306150009), and strictly complied with the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Rats were housed in a specific pathogen‐free (SPF) animal facility under controlled environmental conditions. All animals had ad libitum access to standard drinking water throughout the experiment.

Female offspring were separated from their dams on postnatal day (PND) 20 and randomly assigned to three experimental groups (n = 6 rats per group) on PND 21: Control group: fed a standard chow diet, 12% of calories from fat (C22010702, Research Diets, USA); HFD45 group: fed a HFD formulation containing 45% of calories from fat (D12451, Research Diets, USA); HFD60: fed a HFD formulation containing 60% of calories from fat (D12492, Research Diets, USA).

Food intake and body weight was measured every three days starting from PND 29 to monitor energy consumption and obesity development. From PND 35 onwards, the vaginal status of each rat was examined daily between 09:00 and 10:00 a.m.; the date of vaginal opening (VO) was documented immediately upon observation. For cytological confirmation of pubertal stage, vaginal lavages were collected from rats with VO using 0.9% sterile saline, smeared onto glass slides, air‐dried, and stained with crystal violet (G1059, Solarbio, China) for microscopic analysis.

Under physiological conditions, the onset of VO in rats typically occurs between postnatal day (PND) 43 and 45. The experiment was terminated on PND 43, as over 80% of rats in the HFD45 group had exhibited VO by PND 42. Prior to sacrifice, rats were anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium (40 mg/kg body weight, intraperitoneal injection). Blood samples were collected from the abdominal aorta, and serum was separated by centrifugation (3000 g, 15 min, 4°C) and stored at −80°C for subsequent hormonal assays. Ovaries, uteri, periuterine fat pads, and livers were immediately dissected, rinsed with ice‐cold phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS), blotted dry, and weighed using an analytical balance. Ovarian tissues were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin (Sigma‐Aldrich, USA) for histological examination; all other tissues were snap‐frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C for further analysis.

Ovarian histological analysis

Ovarian tissues fixed in formalin were processed for histological examination following standard protocols. Serial 5 μm‐thick coronal sections were cut from paraffin blocks. Sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated through a reverse ethanol gradient ethanol, and rinsed in distilled water. Subsequently, sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin solution (Yixin Biotech, China), respectively. After staining, sections were dehydrated again and mounted with permanent mounting medium. Stained ovarian sections were examined under a light microscope.

Hormonal assays

Serum concentrations of E2, FSH, LH, GnRH, leptin, and adiponectin were quantified using enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits to assess endocrine alterations associated with PP. Specifically:E2 (E2‐ml002871M), leptin (ml002969M), and adiponectin (ml002882) were measured using commercial ELISA kits (mlbio, Shanghai, China); GnRH (ml003038‐2), LH (ml002860M) and FSH (ml002872M) were detected via super‐sensitive sandwich ELISA kits (mlbio, Shanghai, China), used in accordance with the manufacturer's protocols. The analysis was performed with a BioTek Epoch 2 Microplate Spectrophotometer (BioTek, USA), with optical density readings taken at a wavelength of 450 nm.

Transcardial perfusion and fixation of brain tissue

To assess pathological changes in the hypothalamus, rats were anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (40 mg/kg, intraperitoneally). The heart was exposed, and an injection needle was gently inserted into the left ventricle immediately adjacent to the cardiac apex at an angle roughly parallel to the left–right midline of the heart. A small incision was made in the right atrial appendage to allow efflux of perfusate. Subsequently, a peristaltic pump was activated to infuse 0.9% normal saline at a constant rate of 10 mL/min. Saline perfusion was stopped once the effluent fluid appeared clear (indicating complete exsanguination of the cerebral vasculature). Immediately after saline perfusion, 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS was perfused at the same rate to fix the brain tissue in situ. The whole brain was then carefully dissected, post‐fixed in 10% formalin at 4°C overnight, and sequentially transferred into 15%, 20%, and 30% sucrose solutions (in PBS) for gradient dehydration at 4°C until the brains sank to the bottom of the solution. Dehydrated brains were stored at −80°C until further processing.

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

Following transcardial perfusion and fixation, brain tissues were sectioned into 20 μm‐thick coronal sections. The sections were post‐fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, rinsed three times with PBS (5 min each wash), and then blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. The sections were incubated with a rabbit anti‐GnRH primary antibody (ab281844, Abcam, USA) at 4°C overnight, followed by incubation with a fluorescently conjugated anti‐rabbit secondary antibody (A11012, Invitrogen, USA) for 30 min in the dark. For nuclear counterstaining, 4′,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole (DAPI) reagent (D9542, Sigma‐Aldrich, USA) was applied to the sections for 10 min. After thorough washing and air‐drying, the sections were visualized under a fluorescence microscope.

Cell culture and treatment

The mouse hypothalamic GnRH‐secreting neuronal cell line GT1‐7 was purchased from QuiCell Biotech (Shanghai, China, No.: G612). Cells were maintained in high‐glucose Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) supplemented with 8% heat‐inactivated fetal bovine serum, 8% horse serum, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin under standard cell culture conditions: 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Culture medium was refreshed every 2 days.

GT1‐7 cells were seeded into 6‐well plates (1 × 105 cells/well) and allowed to adhere overnight. Cells were treated with recombinant mouse leptin (100 ng/mL, HY‐P7233, MedChemExpress, USA) dissolved in sterile PBS; control cells were treated with an equal volume of PBS. All treatments were performed for 24 h at 37°C with 5% CO2, and each experimental group was set up in triplicate to ensure technical reproducibility. After 24 h of treatment, cell culture supernatants were collected and centrifuged at 3000 g for 10 min at 4°C to remove cellular debris. Concentrations of GnRH in the supernatants were quantified using an ELISA kit (mlbio, Shanghai, China; Catalog No.: ml7018552M). For IF staining of GnRH in GT1‐7 cells, treated cells were processed using hypothalamic tissue protocols with minor adaptations for cells.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R 4.1.0 (Version 26; IBM Corp., USA), the graphs were produced via GraphPad Prism 8.0. Data were presented as mean ± standard error ( mean ± SE). If the data were normally distributed and had equal variance, one way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for multiple comparisons, and an LSD t‐test was used for two‐group comparisons. Otherwise, a Kruskal–Wallis test was applied. p < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant.

RESULTS

Comparison of different HFD formulations in inducing obesity and early VO in juvenile rats

Female juvenile rats were randomly assigned to three groups: control group, HFD45 group, and HFD60 group. The dietary intervention was initiated on PND 21, and the timing of VO, a well‐recognized phenotypic marker of puberty onset in female rodents, was monitored daily starting from PND 35 (Figure 1A). Daily energy intake was recorded throughout the experimental period to evaluate dietary consumption patterns. ANOVA analysis revealed statistically significant differences among groups (p < 0.001). As shown in Figure 1B, rats in both HFD groups exhibited significantly higher caloric intake compared to the chow‐fed control group (p < 0.001). Notably, the HFD60 group showed a relatively lower caloric intake than the HFD45 group (p < 0.01), suggesting that extremely high fat content may induce sensory‐specific satiety and reduce overall food consumption.

Body weight changes, a core indicator of obesity development, were measured regularly. As presented in Figure 1C, rats fed the 45% HFD displayed a remarkable and statistically significant increase in body weight relative to the control group (PND40, p < 0.05; PDN43, p < 0.01). In contrast, the HFD60 group maintained body weights comparable to that of control rats, which is consistent with the lower caloric intake observed. In addition, the HFD60 group rats exhibited fecal changes, characterized by high moisture content and high viscosity, suggesting potential digestive and absorptive disorders in juvenile rats given this diet.

Consistent with the body weight results, the HFD45 group rats showed a significant advancement in puberty onset, as the percentage of rats with VO was significantly higher starting from PND 39 (Figure 1D). Since over 80% of rats in the HFD45 group had exhibited VO by PND 42, all animals were sacrificed on PND 43 to collect reproductive and metabolic tissues.

Post‐mortem analyses revealed that ovarian (p < 0.05) and uterine (p < 0.05) weights were significantly increased in the HFD45 group compared to both the control (Figure 1E,F). Additionally, periuterine fat mass (p < 0.05), a key indicator of metabolic disturbance associated with DIO, was also elevated in the HFD45 group (Figure 1G). Liver weight did not differ statistically among the three groups (Figure 1H), and rats in the HFD60 group exhibited comparable organ weights to those of the control group for all measured organs (Figure 1E–H).

Collectively, these data demonstrate that the 45% HFD formulation effectively induces both obesity and PP in juvenile rats, whereas the 60% HFD fails to elicit these phenotypic changes. Therefore, the 45% HFD is an optimal choice for constructing a obesity‐related PP animal model.

45% HFD more effectively induces pathological features of PP

To confirm the PP phenotype, we collected vaginal lavages and subjected the smears to crystal violet staining. As shown in Figure 2A, vaginal smears from the control group contained a large number of nucleated epithelial cells and a small number of keratinized cells, which is a typical cytological feature of the proestrus stage. In contrast, smears from the HFD45 group showed a large number of desquamated keratinized epithelial cells with absent nuclei and irregular shapes, consistent with the estrus stage that indicates advanced reproductive development. The HFD60 group showed an intermediate pattern, with smears containing a mixture of nucleated epithelial cells and keratinized cells, suggesting a less pronounced effect on reproductive cycle advancement.

Ovarian histological staining further confirmed the differential effects of HFD with different fat contents on reproductive development (Figure 2B). In the HFD45 group, follicles at all developmental stages were observed in the ovarian cortex, with secondary follicles being the most predominant. Additionally, a small number of mature follicles, atretic follicles, and corpora lutea were detected, the pathological features indicative of premature ovarian activation. In contrast, the control group showed only primordial and primary follicles in the ovaries, with no mature follicles or corpora lutea. The HFD60 group exhibited limited ovarian development, with only a small number of secondary follicles and no mature follicles or corpora lutea. Collectively, these cytological and histological alterations demonstrate that the 45% HFD more effectively induces the pathological characteristics of PP than the 60% HFD.

45% HFD upregulates PP‐related hormones

To elucidate the endocrine mechanism underlying 45% HFD‐induced PP, we first detected the levels of key peripheral hormones associated with pubertal initiation in juvenile rats from the control and HFD45 groups. To quantify the endocrine alterations, we measured serum concentrations of E2, LH, and FSH, as shown in Figure 3A–C, the HFD45 group exhibited significantly increased serum levels of E2 (p < 0.01), LH (p < 0.001), and FSH (p < 0.01) compared to the control group, which was in agreement with the earlier cytological (vaginal lavage smear) and histological (ovarian follicle) manifestations of advanced pubertal development.

To fully assess the activation status of the HPG axis, we further examined the expression and secretion of GnRH, the upstream regulatory hormone of the HPG axis that initiates the cascade of pubertal hormone secretion. We simultaneously detected the peripheral serum level of GnRH and its expression in the hypothalamus. Serum GnRH (p < 0.05) concentration was significantly increased in the HFD45 group compared to the control group (Figure 3D). Consistent with this, IF staining results demonstrated a marked upregulation of GnRH expression in the hypothalamic tissues of 45% HFD‐fed rats relative to controls (Figure 3E). Collectively, the coordinated elevation of E2, LH, FSH, and GnRH (both peripherally and centrally) not only confirms the successful induction of PP by 45% HFD feeding but also clearly indicates that activation of the entire HPG axis is a key mechanism underlying this pathological process.

Serum leptin increase in 45% HFD rats and its role on GnRH secretion

Clinical and preclinical evidence has established a close link between childhood obesity and the onset of PP, where adipose tissue‐derived adipokines are hypothesized to act as critical mediators. Given that adipokine secretion is correlated with adipose tissue mass, we first detected the serum levels of two representative adipokines, leptin and adiponectin, in our rat model. Consistent with the obese and PP phenotypes observed in the HFD45 group, these rats exhibited significantly elevated serum leptin levels (p < 0.01) and decreased serum adiponectin levels (p < 0.05) compared to the control group (Figure 4A,B).

Leptin, a key adipokine involved in energy metabolism and reproductive regulation, has been previously reported to modulate HPG axis activation, prompting us to explore its direct effect on GnRH secretion. To verify whether leptin directly regulates GnRH secretion, we utilized GT1‐7 cells, a well‐established GnRH‐secreting cell line derived from hypothalamic neurons. Cells were treated with recombinant mouse leptin (100 ng/mL) for 24 h, and GnRH levels in the culture supernatant were measured by ELISA. As shown in Figure 4C, leptin treatment significantly increased extracellular GnRH concentrations (p < 0.05) compared to the vehicle control group (Figure 4C), indicating that leptin actively promotes GnRH secretion in these cells. This finding was further corroborated by IF staining, which revealed enhanced GnRH signal intensity in leptin‐treated GT1‐7 cells, consistent with increased secretory activity (Figure 4D). Collectively, these results suggest that adipose‐derived leptin, whose levels are elevated by 45% HFD feeding, acts as a key mediator to promote GnRH secretion, thereby contributing to HPG axis activation and the development of PP.

DISCUSSION

The present study systematically compared the efficacy of two widely used HFD formulations (45% and 60% calorie from fat) in inducing obesity‐related PP in juvenile rats, and identified the 45% HFD as the optimal choice for constructing this preclinical model. This finding fills a critical gap in current animal model research, as classic HFD‐based DIO models are predominantly established in adult rodents (≥8 weeks old) and prioritize metabolic endpoints such as obesity or fatty liver. 13 , 14 The 60% HFD is reported to be more potent in inducing metabolic disturbances in adult animals, 15 but our data demonstrate that this formulation fails to elicit either obesity or PP phenotype in juvenile rats, highlighting the importance of tailoring diet formulations to the developmental stage of experimental animals.

Two landmark studies have established diet‐induced obesity‐related PP models in juvenile rodents, providing critical context for our findings. Consistent with our study, Huang et al. 16 found that a 45% fat‐derived calorie HFD accelerated pubertal onset, increased body weight, and elevated HPG axis hormones (E2, LH, FSH), confirming the reproducibility of HFD‐induced obesity‐PP in juvenile SD rats. Separately, another study utilized a high‐fat/high‐glucose diet to investigate pubertal development in juvenile C57BL/6J mice, revealing that dietary metabolic cues disrupt pubertal timing. Their observation of elevated GnRH and gonadotropin levels also aligns with our endocrine data, reinforcing the conserved role of HPG axis activation in obesity‐related PP across rodent species. 17

The ineffectiveness of the 60% HFD in juvenile rats can be attributed to the unique physiological characteristics of juveniles, which differ substantially from those of adult rodents. First, our results showed that the 60% HFD group exhibited lower caloric intake compared to the 45% HFD group, a phenomenon likely driven by the immature gastrointestinal (GI) tract of juvenile rats. 18 Unlike adult rats, which have a well‐developed GI system capable of handling high‐lipid loads, juvenile rodents have reduced secretion of digestive enzymes and bile acids, key factors for lipid emulsification and absorption. 19 Excess dietary fat in the 60% HFD may overwhelm the juvenile GI tract, leading to discomfort or malabsorption, and consequently reducing food intake via sensory‐specific satiety or GI feedback mechanisms. Second, the gut microbiota composition of juvenile rats is distinct from that of adults, with lower diversity and reduced abundance of lipid‐metabolizing bacterial taxa. 20 Gut microbiota play a pivotal role in lipid absorption and energy harvest, and the immature microbial community in juvenile rats may further impair their ability to utilize the high‐energy 60% HFD, contributing to the lack of obesity induction. Collectively, these data emphasize that HFD formulations optimized for adult rodents cannot be directly extrapolated to juvenile models, and highlight the necessity of validating diet suitability based on the developmental stages of animals, especially when modeling childhood‐related diseases.

A central mechanistic insight from our study is the confirmation of the leptin‐GnRH axis as a key mediator linking obesity to PP. Consistent with clinical and preclinical observations, 21 , 22 we found that 45% HFD‐fed rats exhibited significantly elevated serum leptin levels (a hallmark of obesity) alongside increased hypothalamic GnRH expression, serum GnRH concentrations, and downstream HPG axis hormones (LH, FSH, E2). Our in vitro experiments further validated that leptin directly promotes GnRH secretion in GT1‐7 cells, providing evidence for the regulatory role of leptin in pubertal initiation. Leptin is known to cross the blood–brain barrier (BBB) and bind to leptin receptors in hypothalamic nuclei (e.g., arcuate nucleus), where it modulates the expression of key neuropeptides involved in GnRH release. 23 , 24 Our data align with this established pathway and extend it to the context of juvenile obesity‐related PP, reinforcing the notion that excess adiposity‐driven leptin elevation is a critical trigger for premature HPG axis activation. This mechanistic validation not only strengthens the biological relevance of our model but also provides a targetable pathway for future therapeutic development.

The optimized 45% HFD‐induced PP model offers several key advantages for preclinical research. The model recapitulates the core pathophysiological features of childhood obesity‐related PP, including accelerated pubertal onset (vaginal opening), obesity‐related metabolic alterations (increased body weight, periuterine fat, and liver weight), reproductive tissue abnormalities (enlarged ovaries and uterus), and consistent endocrine and histological changes (advanced follicular development, estrus‐stage vaginal cytology, and elevated HPG axis hormones). In addition, the model is highly feasible: the dietary intervention is simple to implement, the experimental period is short (≈3 weeks from weaning to PP manifestation), and the endpoints (vaginal opening, hormone levels, tissue histology) are easily quantifiable, facilitating high‐throughput drug screening. Moreover, the model focus on juvenile rats (PND 21) avoids chemical stimulation and mimics the developmental window of childhood, enhancing its translational relevance to clinical settings where PP typically occurs in prepubescent children.

Beyond post‐weaning dietary exposure, growing evidence highlights that maternal consumption of unbalanced diets during gestation and lactation represents another critical window for inducing PP in rodents. Takumi et al. 25 demonstrated that maternal HFD exposure exclusively during lactation accelerated puberty onset and increased body weight in female rat offspring weaning. Notably, this effect was associated with elevated serum leptin. A recent study extended this to the maternal western diet (WD), showing that gestational and lactational WD exposure induced early pubertal traits in mouse offspring by PND26. Importantly, this study identified the liver as an emerging mediator of puberty timing. 26 Consistent with these findings, Shi et al. 27 confirmed that combined lactational and post‐weaning HFD exposure induced prepubertal obesity in female mice, with neuroendocrine changes including upregulated hypothalamic Kiss1, GPR54, and GnRH. Collectively, these studies align with our findings that the HPG axis is a conserved target of dietary metabolic cues but expand the scope to developmental programming.

While our study provides a validated model for obesity‐related PP, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the study was limited to female rats, and future research should confirm whether the 45% HFD formulation similarly induces PP in male juvenile rats, as the pathophysiology of PP may differ by sex. Second, we focused on leptin as a key adipokine, but other adipose‐derived factors (e.g., adiponectin, resistin) or metabolic intermediates (e.g., free fatty acids) may also contribute to HPG axis activation, and their roles merit further investigation. Third, the long‐term consequences of 45% HFD‐induced PP (e.g., adult reproductive function, metabolic syndrome risk) were not evaluated in the current study. Extending the observation period would provide valuable insights into the predictive validity of the model for long‐term health outcomes.

In summary, we successfully optimized and validated a 45% HFD‐based juvenile rat model of obesity‐related PP, which exhibits consistent phenotypic, histological, and endocrine features of the disease. The model is further supported by mechanistic evidence confirming the leptin‐GnRH axis as a key mediator of HPG axis activation. This model addresses the unmet need for a clinically relevant preclinical tool to study the pathogenesis of obesity‐related PP and screen novel therapeutic agents. Given its simplicity, reproducibility, and translational potential, we anticipate this model will serve as a valuable resource for researchers in the fields of pediatric endocrinology, metabolic disease, and reproductive biology.
