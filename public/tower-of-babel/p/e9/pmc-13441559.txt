Real-World Performance of Non-Allergist-Led Penicillin Allergy Delabeling: Prospective Head-to-Head Comparison of APAAACI vs. PENFAST
Authors: Freya KL Chung, Weihong Shi, Man Yui Chong, Elaine Lee, Jackie SH Yim, Valerie Chiang, Philip H Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeAlthough around 90% of penicillin allergies are mislabelled, delabeling initiatives have been hindered by limited access to drug allergy services. Asia Pacific Association of Allergy, Asthma, and Clinical Immunology (APAAACI) and PENFAST risk-stratification tools can facilitate non-allergist delabeling, but their comparative real-world performance remains unknown. The purpose of this study was to prospectively evaluate APAAACI and PENFAST against allergist assessment and drug provocation testing (DPT) outcomes.MethodsIn a blinded, head-to-head study, 103 adults with penicillin allergy labels underwent independent risk stratification using both APAAACI and PENFAST tools, followed by allergist evaluation and allergy testing (skin tests, in vitro tests, and DPT). We assessed diagnostic performance (area under the curve [AUC]-receiver operating characteristic, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative predictive values [NPVs]) and agreement (Cohen’s κ) of these tools.ResultsOverall, allergist assessment demonstrated the highest AUC of 0.82 (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.69–0.95), outperforming APAAACI (AUC, 0.79; 95% CI, 0.66–0.92; P = 0.012) and PENFAST (AUC, 0.75; 95% CI, 0.62–0.89; P = 0.010). Both APAAACI and PENFAST demonstrated high sensitivity (0.88) and NPV (APAAACI, 0.99; PENFAST, 0.98), missing only one non-severe DPT-positive case. APAAACI showed excellent agreement with allergist assessment (κ = 0.96, P < 0.001) versus moderate agreement for PENFAST (κ = 0.43). Allergist evaluation had superior specificity (0.77 vs. 0.71 [APAAACI] and 0.63 [PENFAST]) and overall accuracy (0.78 vs. 0.72 and 0.65).ConclusionsAPAAACI and PENFAST can both effectively identify low-risk patients labelled with penicillin allergy when used by non-allergists, with comparable sensitivity to allergist assessment. APAAACI’s stronger agreement with allergist assessment may suggest particular utility in Asian populations. These tools can significantly improve access to delabeling in settings lacking specialist allergy services.Keywords: Allergy; APAAACI, delabeling; non-allergist; penicillin; PENFAST; risk; stratification; triage

INTRODUCTION

Penicillins, the most prescribed β-lactam antibiotic, continue to serve as first-line therapy for treating most bacterial infections and use in surgical prophylaxis. However, their use is hindered by the high and increasing global burden of drug allergy labels.1,2,3 For example, approximately 2% of Hong Kong’s population are labelled as allergic to penicillins and other β-lactam antibiotics with more than 8,000 penicillin allergy labels created in the city every year.2 Yet, studies indicate that up to 90% of such “allergy” labels are found to be incorrect upon evaluation by negative drug provocation testing (DPT).4,5,6 The impact of these mislabelled allergies has profound clinical and public health consequences, including prolonged hospital stays, use of alternative antibiotics, development of antimicrobial resistance as well as impaired health-related quality-of-life.7,8,9,10

To overcome the burden of mislabelled antibiotic allergies, penicillin allergy delabeling (removal of incorrect allergy labels following formal allergy assessment) has emerged as a cornerstone of antimicrobial stewardship. In recent years, global efforts have focused on improving the efficiency and accessibility of delabeling processes, particularly for non-allergy specialists. Examples include the use of drug allergy education courses as well as the use of various risk stratification scores to help triage patients into “low-risk (LR)” of genuine allergy.11,12,13,14,15 However, it is important to recognize that these tools were developed in different regions, reflecting local epidemiology and expert consensus, with notable regional differences in allergy patterns, especially in Asia.12,13,16,17 Another critical barrier to widespread delabeling is the shortage of allergists and specialized testing services, particularly in resource-limited settings.18,19,20 Although widely adopted across diverse healthcare settings, few studies have directly compared the performance of these scores across populations, and their accuracy may vary by the demographic and clinical characteristics of the target group, particularly in regions such as Asia, where prescribing practices and healthcare systems differ from Western countries.20,21,22,23,24,25

Recommendations by the Asia Pacific Association of Allergy, Asthma, and Clinical Immunology (APAAACI) have advocated for direct DPT (without need for prior skin testing) for LR patients assessed by trained non-allergists, supported by risk stratification protocols to differentiate between LR and non-low-risk (NLR) patients.13 Several successful initiatives led by non-allergist physicians, nurses, and pharmacists have demonstrated that penicillin allergy evaluation and delabeling can be performed with comparable efficacy and safety to traditional allergist-led services.26,27,28 In many Asian countries, the APAAACI Clinical Pathway and PENFAST scores are widely used risk stratification tools in clinical practice.11,13 However, there remains a lack of direct comparative studies evaluating their performance (particularly when applied by non-allergists) highlighting the need for real-world validation to guide evidence-based clinical decision-making.

This study aims to address this knowledge gap by conducting a prospective, head-to-head comparison of the APAAACI and PENFAST risk stratification tools when applied by non-allergists. We evaluate their performance against a reference standard of blinded allergist assessment and real-world allergy testing (including DPT) outcomes, to guide optimal implementation in non-allergist delabeling programs.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study population

All adult patients (≥ 18 years) with a documented penicillin allergy label who attended and completed full allergy evaluation at the Penicillin Allergy Clinic of the Hong Kong West Cluster (HKWC) between July 2024 and December 2024 were recruited. HKWC remains the only public referral centre in Hong Kong with specialists in Immunology and Allergy and receives adult referrals with suspected or confirmed penicillin allergy from across the entire territory. As such, our study population represents a specialist referral cohort rather than unselected primary-care patients. Informed consent was obtained from all patients, and the study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Hong Kong/HKWC (IRB-2026-255).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria included all patients who had completed penicillin allergy evaluation via either positive in vitro or skin test results, or a positive or negative DPT. In vitro tests included basophil activation tests for immediate-type reactions, and lymphocyte transformation tests for non-immediate-type reactions. The decision to perform in vitro or skin testing prior to DPT was at the discretion of the consulting allergist. Skin tests included skin prick tests (SPTs) and, if negative, intradermal tests (IDTs) for immediate-type reactions and delayed IDT for non-immediate-type reactions. SPT and IDT were performed in a dedicated allergy day unit by trained nurses under allergist supervision, with resuscitation facilities available, using non-irritant concentrations and standard positive/negative controls in line with international recommendations and our published protocol.6,15 SPT used benzylpenicillin, amoxicillin and/or the index penicillin (when available), with major/minor penicillin determinants included where accessible. Immediate SPT positivity was defined as a wheal ≥ 3 mm larger than the negative control at 15–20 minutes. If SPT was negative, IDT was performed on the volar forearm (typically 0.02–0.04 mL) to raise a 4–6 mm bleb, with stepwise dilution-to-target escalation in higher-risk patients. Readings were taken at 15–20 minutes (immediate) and at 24–48 hours (up to 72 hours if indicated) for delayed reactions; immediate IDT positivity required a > 3 mm wheal increase over the initial bleb with flare. DPT with the index penicillin (most commonly amoxicillin or amoxicillin–clavulanate) was conducted in patients with negative skin tests and no contraindications using a one- or two-step graded oral challenge to a full therapeutic dose at 30–60-minute intervals, followed by ≥ 2 hours of observation. For suspected non-immediate reactions, patients were discharged after the challenge for home observation for 3–5 days, with written instructions and rescue medication for delayed reactions.6,29 Patients were excluded if they declined allergy testing or had an incomplete workup (e.g., no positive in vitro or skin tests, or incomplete DPT).

Risk stratification protocol

All patients underwent independent and parallel risk stratification using both the APAAACI and PENFAST tools during their clinic visit. As per protocol, each patient was first interviewed independently by a trained allergy nurse, who obtained a comprehensive drug allergy history. Following the interview, the nurse applied both risk stratification tools to classify the patient's allergy risk. Patients were categorized as “PENFAST-LR” (LR) if they scored less than 3 points on the PENFAST score, and as “APAAACI-LR” if they exhibited only LR features and no high-risk or “other” features, as defined by the APAAACI Clinical Pathway (Supplementary Table S1).11,13 The nurse’s risk stratification using both tools was blinded to the patient and other healthcare professionals and did not influence subsequent allergy workup.

Subsequently, each patient was independently assessed by a consulting allergist, who was blinded to the nurse’s risk classifications. Based on a comprehensive allergy history and clinical judgment, the allergist assigned a final risk category: Allergist-LR or Allergist-NLR. Allergist-LR patients proceeded to direct DPT, whereas Allergist-NLR patients underwent in vitro and/or skin testing as deemed appropriate by the allergist; those with negative results subsequently underwent DPT.

Throughout the entire process, an independent research assistant supervised all interviews, ensuring adherence to the protocol, accurate documentation of results, and maintenance of blinding between the nurse and the allergist.

Data collection and analysis

Data on demographic and clinical characteristics, including age, sex, type and duration of the index reaction, and details and outcomes of all allergy testing, were collected anonymously and analysed. The primary objective was to compare the performance of the APAAACI and PENFAST tools against the reference standard of allergist-assessed risk classification. The diagnostic performance of each tool (including sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value [PPV] and negative predictive values [NPV], and overall accuracy) was calculated using the allergist’s risk assessment as the reference standard. Agreement between tool-derived classifications and allergist judgment was assessed using Cohen’s κ coefficient, with values interpreted as follows: < 0.20 (poor), 0.21–0.40 (fair), 0.41–0.60 (moderate), 0.61–0.80 (good), and > 0.80 (excellent agreement).

To compare the area under the curve (AUC) receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves for APAAACI and PENFAST, DeLong’s test for correlated ROC curves was used. Differences in sensitivity and specificity between the two tools were evaluated using a two-sample proportion test, while overall classification accuracy was compared using McNemar’s test on paired classifications, given that both tools were applied to the same patient cohort. SPSS Statistics 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used for all analyses. Categorical variables are presented as proportions (percentages), and continuous variables are expressed as either mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range [IQR]) when appropriate. All tests were two-tailed, with P < 0.05 indicating statistical significance.

RESULTS

A total of 103 patients with a documented penicillin allergy were recruited during the study period. The median age was 61 (IQR, 44–71 years), with over one-third (37.9%) being aged ≥ 65 years. The female-to-male ratio was 3:1 and all patients were Hong Kong Chinese. The median duration of penicillin allergy labelling was 17 years (IQR, 5–20). Nearly half of the cohort (46.6%, 48/103) reported a history of isolated cutaneous reactions, with no associated systemic features. Seven patients (6.8%) reported a history suggestive of anaphylaxis, while severe cutaneous adverse reactions (SCAR) were documented in 2 patients (1.9%). All assessments achieved near-perfect NPV (allergist, 0.99; APAAACI, 0.99; PENFAST, 0.98). However, allergist showed superior specificity (0.77; 95% CI, 0.67–0.85) compared to APAAACI (0.71; 95% CI, 0.60–0.79; difference, 0.06; P = 0.041) and PENFAST (0.63; 95% CI, 0.53–0.73; difference, 0.14; P = 0.021), a pattern mirrored in accuracy (allergist, 0.78; APAAACI, 0.72; PENFAST, 0.65) and PPV (allergist, 0.24; APAAACI, 0.20; PENFAST, 0.17).

As shown in Table 3, subgroup analyses by reaction type yielded patterns consistent with the overall cohort. In immediate reactions (n = 45, 5 positives), allergist assessment, APAAACI and PENFAST performed identically (sensitivity 1.00, specificity 0.63 for all). In delayed reactions (n = 25, 2 positives), all three approaches again achieved sensitivity 1.00, but specificity was 0.87 for allergist assessment, 0.74 for APAAACI and 0.43 for PENFAST, indicating more false-positive NLR classifications with PENFAST. In reactions of unknown timing (n = 33, 1 positive), all three approaches missed the single positive case (sensitivity 0), while specificity remained high (0.88, 0.78 and 0.78, respectively); these subgroup findings are exploratory given the small number of positive tests.

DISCUSSION

This is the first prospective, head-to-head comparison of the risk stratification tools (APAAACI and PENFAST) for penicillin allergy performed by non-allergists, directly evaluated against both allergist assessment and real-world allergy testing outcomes, including DPT. This enhances the clinical relevance of our findings and supports the pragmatic implementation of risk stratification tools’ use by non-allergists, further validating their efficacy and safety in real-world clinical settings. Importantly, it also highlights the practical applicability and ease of use of these tools by trained healthcare professionals in routine clinical practice. These tools may be particularly valuable in regions with limited access to allergists or structured drug allergy services, such as many parts of the Asia-Pacific, where initiatives led by non-allergists and multidisciplinary teams have shown promise in improving access to allergy care.26,27,28,30,31,32

In this study, although allergist assessment outperformed both APAAACI and PENFAST in other performance metrics, they demonstrated comparable high sensitivity, with each missing only one patient who had a positive DPT and was stratified as LR. This is particularly reassuring as high sensitivity is arguably the most important performance metric when screening out genuine (or “high risk”) penicillin allergy and ensures that very few patients with true allergy are classified as LR, which supports the safety of using these tools especially in non-allergist settings. This illustrative case, with a vague and remote history of a suspected penicillin rash, underscores that even LR patients may very rarely have true allergy (false negative), highlighting the need for clear counselling and appropriate monitoring during DPT, especially in non-allergist-led programmes. This is also in line with prior studies which also demonstrate that PENFAST may misclassify a significant proportion of patients as LR.16. This also aligns with the intended purpose of using risk stratification tools, which are not designed to definitively confirm diagnosis, but to triage LR patients unlikely to have a true allergy, thereby facilitating safe and efficient delabeling through streamlined assessments such as direct DPT.

Although both tools demonstrated generally similar performance, APAAACI showed numerically higher values across all metrics and exhibited excellent agreement (κ = 0.96, P < 0.001) with allergist assessment, in contrast to PENFAST, which showed only moderate agreement (κ = 0.43, P < 0.001). This difference may be attributed to the development of the APAAACI Clinical Pathway, which was based on the clinical experience and consensus of allergy experts in Asia.12,13 As a result, it may be more closely aligned with the characteristics of the present study population. APAAACI may have performed better in our cohort as it explicitly classifies remote or vague cutaneous-only or gastrointestinal-only histories as LR while reserving high-risk status for objective systemic features such as anaphylaxis and severe cutaneous adverse reactions, which closely mirrors our allergists’ usual practice. Nevertheless, further validation in more diverse populations will be necessary to determine whether this observed difference is specific to the current study population or reflects a broader trend in the relative performance of these tools. Notably, in the original PENFAST study, PPV and NPV varied substantially across external cohorts, underscoring that the performance of penicillin allergy decision rules is strongly influenced by the underlying patient population and healthcare setting.11

These subgroup data support our main findings: allergist assessment generally achieved the highest specificity and accuracy, while APAAACI and PENFAST maintained high sensitivity. In delayed reactions, PENFAST preserved sensitivity 1.00 but had clearly lower specificity than allergist assessment and APAAACI, misclassifying more truly tolerant patients as non–LR. This is consistent with previous studies which reported that PENFAST performs less well in delayed hypersensitivity reactions.33 Overall, PENFAST remains a useful screening tool but caution is needed, especially among patients presented with delayed phenotypes.

This study has several limitations. First, as a single-center study conducted at the only public referral center in Hong Kong with specialists in Immunology & Allergy, our findings may lack external validity. Second, although the tools are user-friendly, the nurses administering them had prior experience and training in drug allergy assessment, which may limit generalizability to settings without similarly trained personnel. Third, many patients had remote index reactions and long-standing allergy labels, so risk stratification relied on patient recall or historical documentation. Recall bias or incomplete records may therefore have affected the accuracy of both the history-based tools and the allergist assessment. Lastly, despite overall good performance, variability in clinical judgment may affect consistency across different users and populations. Lastly, the region-specific and relatively small sample size also limits broader applicability.

Our results support the use of both APAAACI and PENFAST as safe and effective tools for penicillin allergy risk stratification by trained non-allergists. While they cannot replace formal allergy evaluation (especially for NLR patients) or drug allergy training, they serve as valuable decision-support tools, especially for new or less experienced staff. Evidence-based and structured education courses, such as the Advances in Drug Allergy & Penicillin Testing course, remain essential for ensuring competency in drug allergy evaluation.14 However, the use of standardized risk stratification tools can enhance confidence, promote consistency, and support clinical decision-making across varying levels of experience.

Future efforts should focus on simplifying implementation through development of user-friendly digital tools to further streamline risk stratification. While patient-directed tools (such as SELF-FAST) show promise, implementation of such tools requires translation and population-specific validation.34 An artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted app using ChatGPT-4o is being piloted to generate APAAACI and PENFAST scores from patient-reported allergy histories in their native language, aiming to improve global accessibility.35 Ongoing trials in Chinese-speaking populations support the potential of AI-assisted tools to enhance the scalability and accessibility of standardized allergy risk assessments worldwide.

In conclusion, both APAAACI and PENFAST are useful tools for penicillin allergy risk stratification, demonstrating comparable sensitivity to allergist assessment and serving as effective triage instruments to identify LR patients. We advocate for broader implementation of these tools to further promote access to non-allergist-led delabeling initiatives, particularly in regions with limited allergy specialist resources.
