CGF induces ROS-mediated metabolic reprogramming and mitochondrial dysfunction to suppress colorectal cancer progression
Authors: Jiebang Jiang, Xinxin Zhang, Yipeng Sun, Jing Chen, Bo Yuan, Xincheng Li, Xi Chen, Xin Chen, Rongpeng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryColorectal cancer (CRC) remains a leading cause of cancer mortality, necessitating effective therapeutic strategies. This study investigates the antitumor properties and mechanism of Cya-Gly-Fer (CGF), a natural anthocyanin derivative. Using CRC cell lines, organoids, and xenograft models, we show that CGF significantly inhibits tumor growth and metastasis. Integrated transcriptomics and metabolomics indicate that CGF disrupts central carbon metabolism and oxidative phosphorylation. Mechanistically, CGF downregulation of ABC transporters causes intracellular ATP accumulation, triggering mitochondrial dysfunction and excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation. This metabolic stress suppresses the MAPK/ERK1/2/c-MYC signaling axis, leading to cell-cycle arrest and apoptosis. These findings identify CGF as a potent agent that targets mitochondrial homeostasis to halt CRC progression.Subject areas: Molecular biology, Cell biology, Omics

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) ranks among the most common malignant tumors affecting the digestive system globally. Data from the International Agency for Research on Cancer indicates that CRC is the third most common cancer worldwide and the second leading cause of cancer deaths.1 With the aging global population and the increasing adoption of unhealthy lifestyles, the incidence of CRC continues to rise annually, placing a significant medical and economic burden.2,3 Currently, curative surgery combined with postoperative adjuvant therapies—such as chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and targeted treatment—constitutes the primary approach for CRC management. However, about 20%–30% of patients undergoing curative treatment experience recurrence and metastasis after surgery.4,5 Moreover, around half of the patients receive their diagnosis at advanced stages, missing the opportunity for surgery and urgently needing more effective treatment options.

Natural products have garnered widespread research attention in oncology due to their broad bioactivity profiles and promising therapeutic potential.6 For instance, polyphenols such as resveratrol and quercetin exert anti-tumor effects through multidimensional molecular mechanisms, including modulation of p53, nuclear factor-κB, and PI3K/AKT pathways; disrupting the tumor cell metabolism; and induction of cell cycle and apoptosis.7,8,9 Clinically applied compounds such as paclitaxel and camptothecin inhibit cell division by targeting microtubule polymerization or DNA topoisomerase activity and have been widely applied as first-line drugs in clinical cancer treatment.10,11,12 Importantly, natural products possess unique chemical structures and mechanisms of action, making them not only direct therapeutic benefits but also offer valuable scaffolds for the development of next-generation anticancer agents.13 Therefore, drug discovery based on natural products holds immense promise for future breakthroughs in cancer therapy.

Natural bioactive compounds such as anthocyanins, polyphenols, and polysaccharides are abundant in purple sweet potatoes. It offers several advantages, including abundant resources, low development costs, and excellent biocompatibility.14,15,16 Previous studies have confirmed that its known active components can inhibit tumor progression via multiple pathways, including inducing autophagy and apoptosis, cell cycle blockade, and inhibiting epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT).17,18,19 Nevertheless, there are still many bioactive components in purple sweet potato that remain unexplored. A systematic approach to identifying and understanding the mechanisms of these bioactives could reveal new therapeutic strategies for cancer treatment.

In this study, we extracted the natural product Cya-Gly-Fer (CGF) from purple sweet potato tubers. For the first time, its anti-tumor activity was investigated in CRC models, alongside an in-depth exploration of its molecular mechanisms. Our results demonstrate that CGF inhibits the ABC transporter pathway, leading to intracellular ATP accumulation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation. These events converge on ROS-dependent signaling cascades to ultimately inhibits CRC progression. This discovery provides new insights into CRC metabolic regulation and proposes CGF as a promising candidate for natural product-based drug development.

Results

Pharmacological impact of CGF on CRC in living organisms

To assess the pharmacological impact of CGF on the progression of CRC in a living organism, we used subcutaneous tumor xenograft and liver metastasis models were established. To determine the optimal dosing regimen, we monitored body weight changes in mice following continuous administration of CGF at varying concentrations and selected 20 mg/kg as the working concentration (Figure S1A). In the subcutaneous model, tumor formation derived from HCT116 and HT29 cells was significantly suppressed following CGF treatment (Figure 1A). Consistent with this, immunohistochemical (IHC) staining for Ki-67 revealed reduced proliferative activity, while TUNEL-based immunofluorescence analysis showed increased apoptosis, as evidenced by enhanced TUNEL-positive signals (Figures S1B and S1C). Similarly, the tumor sizes and weights were significantly decreased in mice treated with CGF compared to the control group treated with PBS (Figures 1B and 1C). Furthermore, in the liver metastasis model involving the injection of HCT116 and HT29 cells into the spleens of nude mice, CGF treatment led to a notable decrease in liver metastatic nodules compared to the group treated with PBS (Figure 1D). Consistent with this, CK20 IHC revealed lower CK20 positivity in livers from the CGF group versus the PBS group (Figure S1D). H&E staining further confirmed that CGF-treated mice exhibited fewer and smaller metastatic lesions in the liver relative to controls (Figure 1E). Collectively, these findings indicate that CGF successfully suppresses CRC tumor growth and spread in living organisms.

Biocompatibility and pharmacokinetic profiles of CGF

To assess the biocompatibility of CGF in the treatment of CRC, the body weights of mice bearing subcutaneous tumors were continuously monitored throughout the study. No significant body weight fluctuations occurred between the PBS-treated and CGF-treated mice (Figure 2A). After the treatment, we collected mouse serum and organs, including the intestines, liver, spleen, and kidneys. H&E staining revealed no notable structural or pathological abnormalities in the organs of CGF-treated mice compared to PBS controls (Figure 2B). Additionally, biochemical analyses assessing liver function (ALT and AST) and kidney function (UA, UREA, and CREA) showed that all parameters remained within normal physiological ranges (Figures 2C–2G). Pharmacokinetic studies further demonstrated that oral administration of CGF (20 mg/kg) resulted in a high maximum plasma concentration and area under the concentration-time curve (AUC), accompanied by low clearance, suggesting favorable systemic exposure and potential long-acting advantages (Figure 2H). Additionally, we assessed CGF distribution in various organs and tumors post-administration, finding that CGF was detectable in liver tissue, spleen, kidney, small intestine, and tumor (Figure S2).

CGF inhibits CRC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion

The impact of CGF on the proliferation, migration, and invasion of CRC cells was assessed using human-derived HCT116 and HT29 cells. Cell viability assays showed that CGF significantly inhibited the survival of both HCT116 and HT29 cells in a time- and concentration-dependent manner (Figures 3A and 3B). 5-ethynyl-2'-deoxyuridine (EdU) incorporation and colony formation tests showed that groups treated with CGF significantly decreased the percentage of EdU-positive cells and colony formation compared to the controls, particularly at elevated concentrations (Figures 3C and 3D). In patient-derived organoids (PDOs), CGF treatment caused a reduction in both the number and size of organoids in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 3E). Transwell migration and invasion tests demonstrated that CGF notably reduced the migration and invasion capabilities of HCT116 and HT29 cells, particularly at elevated concentrations (Figure 3F). Moreover, CGF treatment significantly reduced the protein expression of EMT markers (vimentin, N-cadherin, SlUG, and SNAIL), further supporting the results from the Transwell assays (Figures 3G and S3A). Notably, CGF showed similar effects to oxaliplatin in inhibiting cell proliferation, migration, and invasion (Figures S3B and S3C). In summary, these findings indicate that CGF greatly reduces the proliferation, migration, and invasion of CRC cells.

Multi-omics analysis reveals broad regulatory effects of CGF in CRC cells

To explore the regulatory impact of CGF on CRC, transcriptomic sequencing and untargeted metabolomics analyses were conducted using HCT116 cells. Analysis of the overall gene expression profile identified 1,943 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between CGF-treated (Treat) and control (Control) groups, comprising 896 downregulated and 1,087 upregulated genes (Figure 4A). The top 20 enriched Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways indicated that the DEGs were significantly enriched in key tumor biological processes such as metabolism, cell proliferation, and signal regulation (Figure 4B). Untargeted metabolomic profiling further demonstrated distinct metabolic alterations: principal-component analysis (PCA) revealed clear separation between the Treat and Control groups, with tight clustering within each group, indicating high data stability and reliability (Figure 4C). Differential metabolite analysis showed that 168 significantly changed metabolites with 59 metabolites increased and 109 decreased following CGF treatment (Figure 4D). Analysis of KEGG pathway enrichment showed that these differential metabolites were significantly associated with critical metabolic pathways, notably central carbon metabolism in cancer and oxidative phosphorylation (Figures 4E, S4A, and S4B), and profoundly influenced metabolic reprogramming in CRC cells. Overall, these multi-omics results demonstrate that CGF has extensive regulatory effects on gene expression and metabolic networks, intervening in core biological processes essential for CRC progression and providing mechanistic insights into its therapeutic potential.

CGF induces cell-cycle arrest and apoptosis

A thorough transcriptomic analysis showed that DEGs were mainly concentrated in processes related to the cell cycle. Flow cytometry analysis showed that low-concentration CGF impeded cell cycle progression from G1 to S phase, while higher concentration of CGF induced arrest at the G2 to M phase, suggesting that the cell-cycle arrest shifts from G1-S to G2-M with increasing CGF concentration (Figures 5A and S5A). Consistently, western blot analysis further showed that decreased cyclin B1 protein expression and elevated levels of p-CDK1 (Tyr15) aligned with the increased proportion of cells in the G2/M phase (Figures 5B and S5B). Considering that many anticancer treatments impact the cell cycle and promote apoptosis, we conducted a deeper analysis of apoptosis-related gene expression identified through transcriptomic profiling. Quantitative PCR and western blot analyses revealed that CGF treatment significantly upregulated the expression of PUMA(BBC3) and NOXA(PMAIP1), along with alterations in other apoptosis-associated proteins in both HCT116 and HT29 cells, indicating activation of the caspase-dependent apoptotic pathway (Figures 5C, 5D, and S5C). Flow cytometry findings further validated that CGF treatment triggered apoptosis in a dose-dependent way, reinforcing the pro-apoptotic impact of CGF on CRC cells (Figure 5E).

CGF inhibits the ABC transporter pathway, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction and ROS accumulation in CRC cells

ROS plays an important role in cancer therapy. Flow cytometry results indicated that CGF significantly increased intracellular ROS levels in CRC cells in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 6A). As ROS generation is often associated with mitochondrial dysfunction, we further investigated mitochondrial morphology using transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Compared to the control group, CGF-treated cells exhibited notable mitochondrial matrix swelling, formation of vacuoles, and loss of cristae structure, indicating substantial mitochondria damage (Figure 6B). JC-1 staining indicated that CGF treatment resulted in an increase in green fluorescence, indicating a reduction in mitochondrial membrane potential (Figure 6C). Enzymatic assays showed that CGF significantly inhibited the activities of key antioxidant enzymes, including CAT (Figure 6D) and SOD (Figure 6E). To explore the mechanism behind ROS accumulation, transcriptomic and untargeted metabolomics data indicated that the ABC transporter signaling pathway was significantly enriched and downregulated following CGF treatment, with a corresponding reduction in related metabolite levels (Figures 4E, S6A, and S6B). To validate the transcriptomic findings, quantitative reverse-transcription PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis was performed to detect expression changes in four ABC transporter-related DEGs—ABCA1, ABCC2, ABCB5, and CFTR—that were enriched in transcriptomic sequencing. The results aligned closely with the transcriptomic data (Figures 6F and 6G). Additionally, we extended our analysis to two ABC transporter family members linked to drug resistance—ABCG2 and ABCB1—and found no significant alterations in their mRNA levels (Figures 6F, 6G, and S6C). Moreover, ATP detection assays revealed that intracellular ATP levels progressively increased with rising concentrations of CGF (Figure 6H), while co-treatment with the CFTR activator ivacaftor partially reversed the ATP levels (Figure S6D). To determine whether ATP accumulation contributed to ROS production, exogenous ATP was administered, leading to a significant enhancement of intracellular ROS levels (Figure 6I). These results suggest that CGF may interfere with the ABC transporter pathway, resulting in intracellular ATP accumulation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and excessive ROS accumulation in CRC cells.

CGF-induced ROS inhibits the MAPK/ERK 1/2/c-MYC signaling pathway

Western blot analysis showed that CGF treatment significantly reduced the phosphorylation levels of MEK and ERK 1/2 compared to the control group. Additionally, the expression of the downstream effector molecule c-MYC, which is phosphorylated by ERK 1/2, was also significantly inhibited by CGF (Figures 7A and S4A). Immunofluorescence staining further demonstrated that CGF inhibited the nuclear translocation of phosphorylated ERK 1/2 (Figure 7B). Importantly, co-treatment with the ROS scavenger NAC (N-acetylcysteine) partially reversed the phosphorylation levels of MEK and ERK 1/2, as well as the expression of c-MYC, suggesting that ROS accumulation mediates the inhibition of the MAPK/ERK1/2/c-MYC axis (Figures 7C and S4B). Given the central role of c-MYC as an oncogene transcription factor in metabolic reprogramming, untargeted metabolomics analysis was performed and revealed that CGF treatment impeded the synthesis of key glycolytic metabolites, including Glc-6-P, DHAP, and lactate (Figure 7D). RT-qPCR showed that CGF significantly downregulated the expression of glycolysis-related metabolic genes such as PKM2, SLC2A1, LDHA, and HK2, compared to the control group (Figures 7G and 7H). ELISA experiments confirmed that CGF inhibited lactate secretion in the CGF-treated cell culture supernatant (Figure 7F). Notably, overexpression of c-MYC significantly upregulated the expression of glycolytic metabolic genes in the control group and partially recovered the downregulation of these genes in the CGF group, accompanied by an increase in lactate secretion (Figures 7E–7H). Furthermore, western blot demonstrated that c-MYC overexpression reversed the CGF-induced effects on apoptosis-related proteins, such as C-caspase 9, C-caspase 3, and EMT markers vimentin and N-cadherin, in both HCT116 and HT29 cells (Figures 7I, 7J, S4C, and S4D). Overall, these findings suggest that ROS mediates CGF-induced inhibition of the MAPK/ERK 1/2/c-MYC signaling pathway, contributing to impaired glycolytic metabolism and enhanced apoptosis in CRC cells.

Discussion

The newly identified anthocyanin natural product, CGF, extracted from purple sweet potato tubers, exhibits potent anti-tumor efficacy with minimal toxicity in both in vitro and in vivo CRC models, offering a new option for the development of dietary-derived anti-cancer natural products. Specifically, CGF inhibited the proliferation, organoid growth, migration, and invasion of HCT116 and HT29 cells; induced cell-cycle arrest; and promoted apoptosis in vitro. In vivo, CGF significantly suppressed subcutaneous tumor growth and liver metastasis in mouse models. Furthermore, CGF displayed favorable biocompatibility and pharmacokinetic characteristics. These findings highlight the potential application of CGF as a naturally derived anti-cancer agent.

CGF, an anthocyanin natural product isolated from purple sweet potato tubers, demonstrated significant biological activity against CRC. In three-dimensional (3D) organoid models, CGF markedly reduced both organoid size and number. In a nude mouse model, CGF effectively inhibited tumor volume and growth in subcutaneous CRC tumors. Its therapeutic concentration range was comparable to those of other natural compounds like curcumin and resveratrol, demonstrating its high therapeutic potential.20,21 Unlike traditional anthocyanins like P40 and ACN, which have primarily been validated at the cellular level for anti-CRC pharmacological activity,17,22 our study extended to in vivo metastatic model, finding that CGF significantly inhibited liver metastasis—the most common metastatic type in clinical CRC cases. Additionally, mechanistic investigations provided a more comprehensive understanding of CGF’s anti-tumor biological mechanisms.

Mechanistically, CGF induces mitochondrial dysfunction, structural damage, and ROS accumulation in CRC cells. Given that tumor cells meet their rapid proliferation energy needs through mitochondrial metabolic reprogramming (such as enhanced oxidative phosphorylation or glycolysis-dominated Warburg effect),23,24 mitochondria thus play a crucial role in tumorigenesis and progression through energy metabolism, signaling, and cell fate determination.25,26,27 By impairing mitochondrial function, CGF may reverse CRC metabolic reprogramming, establishing mitochondrial dysfunction as one of the key mechanisms underlying its anticancer effect. Consistent with this, CGF significantly increased ROS levels in CRC cells. As mitochondrial dysfunction often leads to impaired oxidative phosphorylation efficiency and excessive ROS production,28 In cancer therapy, ROS is seen as a double-edged sword: physiological ROS levels promote tumor progression, while excessive ROS accumulation inhibits tumor development.29 Our findings align with most studies, indicating that natural products, such as ginsenosides and quercetin, inhibit CRC progression via ROS generation.30,31 Notably, excessive ROS may induce lipid peroxidation of mitochondrial membrane, increasing permeability and triggering irreversible oxidative damage cascade, which may explain the mitochondrial structural damage observed following CGF treatment.32

Multi-omics KEGG pathway analysis reveals that enrichment of ABC transporter pathways is a critical target of CGF, which may be closely related to ROS accumulation in CRC. Combined transcriptomic and metabolomic KEGG pathway analyses showed significant downregulation of ABC transporter components, such as ABCA1 and ABCC2, accompanied by a global decrease in related metabolites. ABC transporters are key regulators of membrane proteins for transmembrane transport and drive substance movement across membranes through ATP hydrolysis.33 Disruption of the ABC transport pathway hinders ATP utilization within cells,34 potentially leading to ATP accumulation and mitochondrial dysregulation. Our results confirmed that CGF treatment significantly increased ATP levels and exogenous ATP addition promoted ROS accumulation. Based on these findings, we speculate that CGF may cause ATP utilization impairment by inhibiting the ABC transporter pathway, contributing to mitochondrial dysfunction and ROS overproduction. However, our results also indicate that CGF does not broadly inhibit all members of the ABC transporter family. It had no significant effect on the transcriptional levels of ABCB1 and ABCG2, two classical drug efflux transporters. These results suggest that ATP metabolic dysregulation is associated with ROS imbalance and mitochondrial damage, potentially serving as a critical link in CGF’s anticancer mechanism. However, a limitation of our current data is that they do not fully clarify how these elements interconnect. Further studies are needed to explore in greater depth the association between ABC transporter dysregulation and mitochondrial function.

Furthermore, CGF-mediated ROS accumulation led to inhibition of the MEK/ERK 1/2/c-MYC signaling pathway, a key oncogenic axis in CRC progression. Studies have shown that ROS can activate the MEK/ERK pathway to promote tumor progression,35 while other studies suggest that compounds like miltirone inhibit the MEK/ERK 1/2 pathway through ROS accumulation, thereby promoting cell death.36 Further, suppression of ROS levels with the ROS inhibitor NAC partially restored the activity of the MEK/ERK 1/2/c-MYC pathway, confirming the role of ROS as a key upstream regulatory factor. Additionally, CGF treatment reduced glycolytic activity, increased apoptosis levels, and inhibited the EMT process in CRC cells. Based on these findings, we speculate that CGF may exert a synergistic regulatory effect on CRC cell energy metabolism, apoptosis, and invasion/metastasis via the ROS/MEK/ERK 1/2/c-MYC signaling pathway. c-MYC, a key transcription factor in cancer development, regulates multiple biological processes such as metabolism, apoptosis, and migration.37,38,39 We observed that c-MYC overexpression reversed the changes in glycolysis capacity, apoptosis proteins, and EMT markers induced by CGF in CRC cells. Overall, CGF-induced excessive ROS acts as a “molecular switch,” negatively regulating the MEK/ERK 1/2/c-MYC signaling pathway and triggering multiple anti-cancer effects dependent on c-MYC. Collectively, our results demonstrate that CGF likely modulates ATP transport pathways, causing mitochondrial dysfunction. Consequently, this suppresses the MEK/ERK signaling pathway—a key “central dispatcher” that orchestrates multiple CRC phenotypes. To summarize, CGF intervenes in CRC progression via a synergistic “oxidative stress-signal transduction-metabolic regulation” network (Figure 8).

CGF treatment downregulates the expression of multiple ABC transporters (ABCA1, ABCB5, ABCC2, and CFTR). Limitations of the study

Finally, CGF demonstrates excellent biocompatibility and favorable pharmacokinetic properties. No significant toxicity was observed in vital organs such as the liver and kidneys in nude mice and CGF exhibited high plasma exposure with low clearance rates, supporting its potential for therapeutic development. Additionally, CGF was detectable in tumors and key organs. Regrettably, our current exploration of deeper mechanisms via multi-omics approaches still has gaps, particularly for metabolic pathways closely linked to tumorigenesis (e.g., steroid biosynthesis), which urgently require further investigation in subsequent studies. Nevertheless, further optimization of dosage regimens and administration methods is necessary to maximize its clinical utility. Moreover, while our research focused on the CRC models, future investigations should explore CGF’s efficacy across different tumor types to evaluate its broader anticancer applicability.

Experimental model and study participant details

Human subjects

Human colorectal cancer tissue samples were obtained from the Department of General Surgery at Yancheng Third People’s Hospital. The tissue collection and subsequent establishment of patient-derived organoids (PDOs) were conducted in strict accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and were approved by the Ethics Committee of Yancheng Third People’s Hospital (Approval No. 2024-96). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to sample collection. PDOs were derived from colorectal cancer in male patients aged 55–65. Authors have omitted details that could reveal the identity of patients to protect confidentiality. The generated PDOs were randomly allocated to different experimental groups for in vitro treatments. Due to the exclusive use of male patient samples, the influence of sex on the findings related to patient-derived organoids cannot be fully assessed, which represents a limitation of this study.

Animal studies

Male BALB/c nude mice (4-5 weeks old) were purchased from GemPharmatech (Jiangsu, China). The mice were housed in specific pathogen-free (SPF) facilities under controlled conditions with a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle and free access to food and water. All animal experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of Jiangsu Normal University (Approval No. JSNU-IACUC-2024047) and were conducted in strict accordance with the relevant regulatory standards.

For the in vivo tumor xenograft and metastasis models, mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups (n = 5 per group for the xenograft model; n = 5 per group for the metastasis model) to ensure balanced distribution of variables. As only male mice were used in this study, the direct influence of sex on the in vivo experimental results cannot be determined, and this represents a limitation of the study in assessing sex-specific responses.

Cells lines

The human colorectal cancer cell lines HCT116 and HT29 were obtained from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC:HCT116 CCL-247 and HT29 HTB-38). Cells were verified by STR profiling and tested negative for mycoplasma contamination. The cells were grown in DMEM medium with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37°C in an incubator with 5% CO2. Cell lines were purchased, tested routinely for negative contamination of mycoplasma, as certified by the manufacturers, and were not further authenticated before use.

Method details

Isolation and characterization of CGF

Cya-Gly-Fer (CGF) was isolated from the fresh tubers of purple sweet potato (Ipomoea batatas L.). Briefly, the fresh tubers were diced and ground into a fine homogenate using a commercial blender (Philips HR2100). The homogenate was then extracted with 70% ethanol (v/v) at a ratio of 10 mL ethanol per 10 g tubers for 2 h at 50°C with continuous stirring. The extract was filtered, and the filtrate was concentrated using a rotary evaporator (Buchi R-300]) at 40°C under reduced pressure 150 mbar to remove ethanol. The crude extract was further purified via silica gel column chromatography. The column (2.5 cm × 30 cm) was packed with silica gel 60 (0.040–0.063 mm, Merck Millipore). The anthocyanin-rich fractions were collected and freeze-dried. The chemical structure of the isolated compound was elucidated using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy. For LC-MS analysis, LC-MS was performed using an Agilent 1290 Infinity II Ultra-High Performance Liquid Chromatography system coupled with an AB SCIEX 4600 Q-TOF LC-MS/MS system (AB SCIEX). Chromatographic separation was achieved on an Agilent ZORBAX Eclipse Plus C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 μm). The mobile phase consisted of water with 0.1% formic acid (A) and acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid (B), utilizing a gradient elution program at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. Data were acquired in both positive and negative ion modes. NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker AVANCE III HD 400 MHz spectrometer (Bruker BioSpin) using DMSO-d6 as the solvent (Graphical abstract).

Chemicals and treatments

For mechanistic studies, the following commercial compounds were used: Oxaliplatin (20 μM), Ivacaftor (200 nM), Adenosine 5′-triphosphate (ATP) disodium salt (5 mM), and N-acetylcysteine (NAC, 500 μM). All compounds were dissolved in DMSO or sterile water, following the manufacturers’ instructions. In all cell culture experiments involving these drugs, the final concentration of DMSO was maintained below 0.1% (v/v) to avoid vehicle toxicity. Specific treatment times and concentrations are detailed in the Figure Legends. Catalog numbers and supplier details for all chemicals are listed in the key resources table.

Ex vivo organ culture

CRC patient-derived organoids (PDOs) were dissociated into single cells using TrypLE Express Enzyme. The cells were collected by centrifugation and resuspended in ice-cold Matrigel (Corning) to create a single-cell suspension. The mixture was seeded as domes (approx. 10 μL/well,1 × 106 cells/mL) in pre-warmed 96-well plates. The plates were inverted and incubated at 37°C for 15 min to allow the Matrigel to polymerize and solidify, ensuring the cells were embedded in the 3D matrix. Subsequently, Human iPSC-Derived Intestinal Organoid Maintenance Kit(STEMCELL Technologies) was added, and cultures were maintained at 37°C in a 5% CO2 humidified incubator.

For drug treatment, organoids were exposed to the indicated concentrations of CGF for 3 days. The medium was refreshed, and the cultures were maintained for an additional 4 days. Morphological changes were monitored, and images were captured using an inverted microscope to assess organoid size and integrity.

In vivo tumor xenograft and metastasis models

For the subcutaneous xenograft model, HCT116 or HT29 cells (1×107 cells per mouse) were resuspended in PBS and injected subcutaneously into the right axilla of the mice. Tumor dimensions (length and width) and body weights were monitored every three days. Tumor volume was calculated using the formula: Volume = 0.5×Length×Width2. When the tumors reached a palpable size (Day 6 post-injection), mice were randomly assigned to groups and administered CGF (20 mg/kg, dissolved in PBS) or vehicle control via intragastric gavage every 2 days. After 21 days of treatment, mice were euthanized. Tumor tissues, as well as the intestine, liver, spleen, and kidneys, were harvested. Serum was collected for biochemical analysis.

For the liver metastasis model, mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 1.2% tribromoethanol (0.2 mL/10 g body weight). A small incision was made in the upper left abdomen to carefully exteriorize the spleen. A suspension of HCT116 or HT29 cells (in 1×107 cells 50 μL PBS) was slowly injected into the splenic capsule using an insulin syringe. The injection site was compressed for 1 min to prevent leakage and hemostasis. The spleen was then repositioned into the abdominal cavity, and the abdominal wall was sutured in layers. Starting 6 days post-surgery, mice were treated with CGF (20 mg/kg) via gavage every 2 days. At the experimental endpoint, livers were excised and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. Metastatic nodules on the liver surface were macroscopically identified and counted.40

Plasmid construction and transfection

c-MYC expression plasmids and the empty vector (pcDNA3.1) were purchased from Generay. Cells were seeded into 6-well plates and cultured overnight to reach 70%–90% confluence at the time of transfection. Transfection was performed using the Lipofectamine 3000 Transfection Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) strictly following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, for each well of a 6-well plate, 2.5 μg of plasmid DNA and 5 μL of P3000 Reagent were diluted in 125 μL of Opti-MEM Medium. Separately, 3.75 μL of Lipofectamine 3000 Reagent was diluted in 125 μL of Opti-MEM Medium. The two solutions were mixed (1:1 ratio) and incubated at room temperature for 10–15 min to form DNA-lipid complexes. The complexes (250 μL) were then added dropwise to the cells maintained in fresh culture medium. Transfection efficiency was evaluated by Western blotting after 24 h of incubation.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Cells were harvested by centrifugation and washed twice with PBS. The cell pellets were immediately fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) for 2 h at room temperature and then stored at 4°C. Post-fixation was performed using 1% osmium tetroxide in 0.1 M phosphate buffer for 2 h. After washing, the samples were dehydrated through a graded series of ethanol (50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, 95%, and 100%) and acetone, followed by infiltration and embedding in epoxy resin. Ultrathin sections (60–80 nm) were cut using an ultramicrotome, stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and examined using a transmission electron microscope(FEI) at an accelerating voltage of 80 kV.

ATP quantification assay

The cellular ATP levels were assessed using an advanced ATP detection kit, following the instructions provided by the manufacturer.

Briefly, cells were washed with PBS and lysed using the specific lysis buffer provided in the kit (200 μL per well for 6-well plates). The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 5 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was collected. For the detection, the ATP detection working solution was prepared by diluting the ATP detection reagent with the diluent. Crucially, 100 μL of the working solution was first added to each well of a opaque 96-well plate and incubated at room temperature for 3–5 min to consume any background ATP. Subsequently, 20 μL of the sample supernatant or standard was added to the wells and mixed quickly. The luminescence (RLU) was measured immediately using a multimode microplate reader. ATP concentrations were calculated based on the standard curve and normalized to the protein concentration of each sample (determined by BCA assay).

ELISA assay

Using a lactate ELISA kit from Kselida, lactate concentrations in the supernatant were assessed as per the manufacturer’s guidelines.

Briefly, 50 μL of standards or cell culture supernatants were added to the microwells pre-coated with anti-lactate antibodies. Subsequently, 50 μL of HRP-conjugated detection antibody was immediately added to each well (except for the blank control). The plate was gently mixed and incubated at 37°C for 30 min.

After incubation, the wells were washed 5 times with the provided washing buffer. Then, 50 μL of Chromogen Solution A and 50 μL of Chromogen Solution B were added to each well, mixed, and incubated at 37°C for 15 min in the dark. The reaction was terminated by adding 50 μL of Stop Solution. The optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm within 15 min using a microplate reader. Lactate concentrations were calculated based on the standard curve.

Assays for catalase (CAT) and superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity

Cells were harvested and lysed using the specific extraction buffers provided in the kits. The cell suspensions were sonicated on ice and then centrifuged at 8,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatants were collected and kept on ice for subsequent assays. Protein concentration was determined to normalize the enzymatic activity. Intracellular CAT and SOD activities were measured using specific CAT and SOD detection kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Flow cytometry detection

Flow cytometric assays were performed to assess apoptosis, ROS levels, and cell cycle distribution(BD FACSCalibur).

Intracellular ROS levels were determined using the DCFH-DA fluorescent probe. Cells were treated with indicated concentrations of CGF for 24 h. After treatment, DCFH-DA was diluted in serum-free medium to a final concentration of 10 μM. The diluted probe solution was added directly to the adherent cells and incubated at 37°C for 30 min in the dark. Following incubation, cells were washed three times with serum-free medium to remove the unloaded probe. The cells were then trypsinized, harvested by centrifugation, and resuspended in PBS. ROS levels were analyzed by flow cytometry at an excitation wavelength of 488 nm and an emission wavelength of 525 nm.

For cell cycle analysis, approximately 5 × 105 cells cells were collected and washed with cold PBS. The cells were fixed by adding 1 mL of pre-cooled 70% ethanol and incubated at 4°C for at least 30 min. After fixation, the cells were centrifuged to remove the ethanol and washed with PBS. The cell pellets were resuspended in 0.5 mL of Staining Buffer containing Propidium Iodide (PI) and RNase A. The mixture was incubated at 37°C for 30 min in the dark. DNA content was analyzed by detecting red fluorescence (PI) upon excitation at 488 nm. The percentage of cells in G0/G1, S, and G2/M phases was calculated.

Cell apoptosis was detected using an Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit. Harvested cells were resuspended in binding buffer and stained with Annexin V-FITC and Propidium Iodide (PI) for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. The cells were analyzed by flow cytometry within 1 h of staining. Cells positive for Annexin V only (early apoptosis) and double-positive cells (late apoptosis) were quantified.

RNA extraction and quantitative Real-Time PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from the treated cells using TRIzol reagent. Subsequently, RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using HiScript II Q RT SuperMix following the standard synthesis protocol. The qPCR amplification was conducted with Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCR Premix on an Applied Biosystems 7500 Real-Time PCR System. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2-ΔΔCT method, and the specific primer sequences are listed in Table S1.

Protein preparation and western blotting

Following cell treatment, total proteins were extracted with RIPA buffer that included protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein levels were determined with the enhanced BCA protein assay kit. Briefly, equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 45 min, incubated with specific primary antibodies overnight at 4°C, and then treated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h. All primary antibodies were used at a dilution of 1:1000, and appropriate HRP-labeled secondary antibodies were used at a concentration of 1:10000. Protein bands were visualized using an ECL chemiluminescence kit and analyzed with ImageJ software.41

Immunofluorescence

Cells were plated in 24-well plates with culture inserts. After treatments, they were fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 15 min, and blocked with 5% donkey serum for 30 min. The cells were incubated overnight at 4°C with a primary antibody targeting P-ERK1/2 at a 1:50 dilution, then treated with a CoraLite488-conjugated Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG(H + L) secondary antibody and stained with DAPI. A Zeiss fluorescence microscope was used to capture fluorescence images.

JC-1 staining assay

Cells were placed in 24-well plates and treated according to the instructions. Following incubation, they were exposed to JC-1(10 μg/mL) staining solution at 37°C for 25 min. Subsequently, the cells were rinsed twice with JC-1 staining buffer and promptly examined using a fluorescence microscope.

Histological analysis (H&E staining)

Once tissue samples were gathered, they were preserved in 4% paraformaldehyde for a day and then underwent hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining (Servicebio, China). The tissue samples, which were stained, were examined under a microscope to evaluate morphological alterations.

Liver and kidney function testing

Mouse serum was collected, and serum levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), uric acid (UA), urea (UREA), and creatinine (CREA) were measured using an automatic biochemical analyzer(Mindray, China).

Cell proliferation, colony formation, and transwell migration and invasion assay

Cells in the logarithmic growth phase were collected by trypsinization and seeded for subsequent experiments. For cell proliferation assays, HCT116 and HT29 cells were treated with CGF at indicated concentrations and incubated with CCK-8 reagent for 2.5 h at 37°C. The absorbance at 450 nm was then measured using a microplate reader. For colony formation assays, 500 cells per well were seeded in 6-well plates. After 48 h, cells were treated with CGF for 24 h, followed by replacement with fresh medium. After 14 days, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 15 min, rinsed with distilled water, and imaged for colony counting.

Transwell migration and invasion assays were performed using 8.0-μm pore size chambers. For migration assays, cells were seeded in the upper chamber without Matrigel coating. For invasion assays, the upper chamber was pre-coated with Matrigel. The lower chamber was filled with complete medium containing 20% FBS. CGF or oxaliplatin was added to the medium in the upper chamber. After incubation, non-migrated/invaded cells on the upper membrane surface were gently removed with a cotton swab. Cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and rinsed with water. For quantification, stained cells were dissolved in 70% ethanol, transferred to a 96-well plate, and the optical density (OD) was measured at 570 nm.

EdU assay

Cells were seeded at an appropriate density in 24-well plates with cell-culture inserts and incubated overnight. Following treatment, cells were incubated with EdU reagent for 2 h according to the manufacturer’s protocol. After fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, cells were permeabilized at room temperature for 15 min. EdU reaction cocktail was then added, and cells were incubated at room temperature in the dark for 30 min. After washing, nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33342. Fluorescent images were captured using a fluorescence microscope.

Transcriptome sequencing and untargeted Metabolomics Analysis

Total RNA was extracted from CGF-treated and control HCT116 cells and subjected to RNA sequencing using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (APTbio, China). Data analysis was performed using DESeq2 software, with genes showing a p-value <0.05 and log2 fold change >1 or < −1 defined as differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were performed with these DEGs.

Pre-treated HCT116 cells and corresponding control cells were collected for metabolite extraction and LC-MS analysis (APTbio, China). Metabolites were separated using an ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide column and analyzed by Sciex TripleTOF 6600 mass spectrometer. Data were processed using XCMS software. including baseline correction, peak detection, and area integration. Metabolites were annotated using the KEGG database, and metabolic pathway enrichment analysis was analyzed using Fisher’s exact test, with p-values <0.05 considered statistically significant.

Pharmacokinetics (PK) study

Plasma sample collection and processing: nude mice were fasted overnight and then orally gavaged with CGF (20 mg/kg). Blood samples were collected at 5, 15, 30 min, 1, 2, 4, 8,12, 24 and 48 h post-administration into heparinized tubes. Tissue sample processing: after the final administration, organs and tumors were harvested and ground using liquid nitrogen. The samples were centrifuged (5000 rpm, 10 min), and the supernatant was mixed with an equal volume of methanol, centrifuged again, and analyzed using an ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography system (UPLC). The chromatographic column used was OL Column GS-5-120 C18-EX 4.6 × 250 mm, with mobile phase A as water (0.1% formic acid) and phase B as acetonitrile (0.1% formic acid). The flow rate was set at 0.6 mL/min, and the UV detection wavelength was set to 280 nm over a 40-min run time.

IHC and tissues IF

After immersion in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, the collected tumor tissues and liver tissues were transferred to Wuhan Baiqiandu Biotechnology Co., Ltd for key processing steps, including sectioning, antigen retrieval, blocking of non-specific binding, and primary antibody incubation (targeting Ki67, TUNEL, and CK20). Brightfield and fluorescence imaging were subsequently performed using a Zeiss microscope.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.0 (GraphPad Software). Differences between groups were assessed by one-way or two-way ANOVA as appropriate. Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All experiments reported in the manuscript were conducted with three independent biological replicates, unless otherwise specified in the figure legends. Where applicable, specific statistical details (test type, exact n, what n represents, center, dispersion, and precision measures) are reported in the corresponding figure legends.
