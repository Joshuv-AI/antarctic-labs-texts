Unlocking microbial interactions: Multi-Plant-based-substrate fermentation with water kefir starters for functional beverage innovation
Authors: Adineh Tajmousavilangerudi, Chiara Viretto, Nicolò Anzelini, Alessandro Stringari, Anna Prati, Michele Larcher, Marco Gobbetti, Raffaella Di Cagno, Ali Zein Alabiden Tlais
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The specificity of ingredients and fermenting microorganisms is critical in designing fermented plant-based beverages and maximizing their protective properties. We aimed to replicate the kefir microbial ecosystem in a novel multi-plant formulation, investigating synergistic interactions between water kefir-derived starters (Leuconostoc mesenteroides, Lacticaseibacillus paracasei, Lactiplantibacillus plantarum, and Pichia bruneiensis) and plant substrates. Starter screening using growth kinetics modelling and acidification identified 16-h fermentation at 30 °C as optimal. Various fermentation methods were employed, including the use of single starters, a ternary lactic acid bacteria (LAB) mixture, and a final combination of all LAB strains with the yeast starter. Subsequently, a comprehensive analysis was conducted, including: microbial growth and acidification; HPLC for microbial metabolites; proteolysis (OPA method, amino acid analyzer); phenolic profiling (LC-MS) and antioxidant activity (DPPH/ABTS); antinutrient quantification (Megazyme kits); texture and syneresis analysis; and VOC profiling (GC-MS). Compared to the beverage without microbial inoculum and incubation (Raw-MPBB), fermentation with Lc. paracasei alone or the ternary LAB culture showed superior microbial growth, strong acidification through the lactic acid production, and enhanced proteolysis as indicated by peptide and free amino acid concentrations. Although phenolic compounds metabolism was starter composition dependent, the same beverages promoted a significant release of phenolics (chlorogenic acid, epicatechin, catechin, and rutin). Other phenolics with enhanced antioxidant potential (such as caffeic, gallic, and protocatechuic acids) were released mainly by the beverage fermented with Leuc. mesenteroides. Only the ternary LAB culture achieved phytic acid degradation, while the beverage fermented with Lc. paracasei not only exhibited the lowest fructan and raffinose contents among all beverages but, together with the beverage fermented with P. bruneiensis, developed more complex volatile organic compound profiles. Beverage stability was improved especially by P. bruneiensis, which effectively minimized syneresis after fermentation. All beverages showed higher firmness than dairy yogurt, regardless of the fermentation effect. Overall, our findings highlight the potential of kefir microbial starters combined with plant-based formulations to create nutritious, stable, and sustainable functional dairy alternatives.

Introduction

Although fermentation likely originated as a method for preserving food substrates, fermented foods have sustained their role in human diets for centuries due to their functional benefits. Through the consumption of fermented foods, positive modulation of the gut microbiota and improvement in overall health can be achieved (Guzel-Seydim et al., 2021). These benefits arise either from microbial metabolism, which bio-transforms substrates into bioactive compounds capable of modulating microbiota balance, strengthening the intestinal barrier, and regulating immune responses (Dimidi et al., 2019; Mukherjee et al., 2024; Padhi et al., 2025), or from live microorganisms that transiently colonize the gut, outcompete pathogens, and stimulate anti-inflammatory and immune-enhancing effects upon ingestion (Zhou et al., 2024). Hence, the specificity of ingredients and fermenting microorganisms is critical in designing fermented foods and maximizing their protective properties.

Generally, yogurt and fermented milk are perceived by consumers as valuable sources of probiotic microorganisms and bioactive metabolites (Panahi and Tremblay, 2016). Nevertheless, shifting dietary preferences, driven by ethical and health concerns, have increased demand for non-dairy fermented alternatives, particularly among plant-based consumers (Gamba et al., 2020; Egea et al., 2022; Guzel-Seydim et al., 2021). The safety and efficacy of plant-based fermented beverages are well-documented (Montemurro et al., 2021; Hidalgo-Fuentes et al., 2024), and concerns over the environmental impact of dairy production, including greenhouse gas emissions (González et al., 2011; Rossi et al., 2024), further support the transition toward sustainable alternatives. Globally, this trend reflects a growing market for fermented plant-based products, which is projected to reach $422.26 million by 2026 at a compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of 5.0 % (Research and Markets, 2021). Despite extensive research efforts (Wang et al., 2018; Ziarno and Cichońska, 2021; Demarinis et al., 2024; Hurtado-Murillo et al., 2025), this sector still offers considerable potential for innovation, driven by the diverse range of rich bioactive ingredients.

For instance, tritordeum, a sustainable food ingredient combining the nutritional benefits of durum wheat and barley, is rich in antioxidants like carotenoids and phenolic acids, as well as β-glucans (Arora et al., 2022). Faba bean flour combines high protein content, dietary fiber, and essential amino acids with functional emulsifying properties and prebiotic oligosaccharides (Multari et al., 2015; Thomsen et al., 2025). Pumpkin seeds are valuable sources of not only protein but also polyunsaturated fatty acids, essential minerals, and bioactive compounds (polyphenols, phytosterols, and squalene). Daily consumption of pumpkin seeds can offer neuroprotective benefits (Singh and Kumar, 2024). Quince, traditionally recognized for its medicinal value, has gained recognition as a functional beverage component owing to its rich nutritional profile, comprising abundant organic acids and polyphenols, which also contribute to its strong astringent and sour flavor (Hernández-García and Carbonell-Barrachina, 2020). Therefore, if these ingredients are collectively fermented, they might synergistically contribute to more functional and sustainable formulations.

Given that water kefir harbors a symbiotic community of yeasts and bacteria with documented health-promoting potential, including probiotic effects, antioxidant and immunomodulatory properties, and the production of bioactive metabolites (Pendón et al., 2022), our focus was directed toward leveraging these microbial drivers in novel plant-based formulations. Numerous studies have validated their efficacy in developing functional beverages from various fruits and vegetables juices, characterized by mild acidity, refreshing flavor, slight effervescence, and low ethanol and acetic acid levels (Randazzo et al., 2016; Corona et al., 2016; Güzel‐Seydim et al., 2023). Mediterranean fruit fermentations, particularly those involving apple and grape, resulted in high-value products that were highly appreciated by consumers (Corona et al., 2016). Likewise, vegetable juices such as carrot, fennel, melon, onion, tomato, and strawberry successfully supported kefir fermentation through backslopping, with strawberry, onion, and tomato beverages retaining notable antioxidant activity (Güzel‐Seydim et al., 2023). However, to the best of our knowledge, research remains limited regarding the application of multi-ingredient matrices incorporating cereals, legumes, oilseeds, and fruits in simulated water kefir fermentation.

Consequently, this study aimed to investigate the fermentative potential of lactic acid bacteria (LAB) and yeast starters, previously isolated from water kefir, with the objective of assessing their effects, both individually or in combination, on the development of plant-based, multi-ingredient beverage formulations. Specifically, we investigated four fermentation conditions: (i) individual LAB starters, (ii) a single yeast starter, (iii) a ternary consortium of LAB starters, and (iv) the ternary LAB consortium combined with the yeast strain. Through comprehensive analysis of nutritional (including protein, peptide, free amino acid, and anti-nutrient contents), functional (such as phenolic compound levels and antioxidant activities), and sensory properties (including volatile organic compounds and textural characteristics), we demonstrated the potential of kefir microorganisms to develop novel plant-based, multi-ingredient beverage formulations, offering sustainable and nutritionally enriched dietary options.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

All chemicals and reagents were of analytical grade and used without further purification. Butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT), chloramphenicol, cycloheximide, glycerol, o-Phthalaldehyde (OPA), potassium phosphate buffer, sodium chloride (NaCl), sodium hydroxide (NaOH), 1,1-diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH), 2,2′-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS), and Trolox were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), unless otherwise specified. Culture media were obtained from Oxoid (Ltd, Basingstoke, Hampshire, England). Mass spectrometry-grade solvents (methanol, ethanol, and acetonitrile), as well as sulfuric acid (H2SO4), perchloric acid, and formic acid, were from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany).

Plant materials and kefir source

Tritordeum, faba bean flour, and pumpkin seeds were purchased from local supermarket. Botanical extract derived from rowan berries (Sorbus aucuparia L.) and supplemented with tapioca starch was provided from Millbo s.r.l. (Trecate, Italy). Quince concentrate (65 °Brix) was supplied by Iprona Com (Bolzano, Italy). Powder samples were stored in dry conditions at room temperature, whereas the juice was stored at −20 °C. Homemade water kefir was collected and immediately stored at 4 °C.

Kefir microbiota characterization

Ten grams of kefir sample were homogenized with 90 mL of sterile 0.9 % (w/v) NaCl solution for 2 min at room temperature. Following serial dilutions, samples were plated on different agar media. Mesophilic LAB were enumerated and isolated on de Man, Rogosa and Sharpe (MRS) agar, supplemented with 0.1 % of cycloheximide (Sigma Chemical Co., Milan, Italy), incubated at 30 °C for 48 and 72 h under anaerobic conditions. Yeast cultures were grown on Malt Extract Agar (MEA), containing 0.1 % chloramphenicol, and incubated at 30 °C for 72 h. Isolation of LAB and yeasts was performed as described by Lhomme et al. (2015). LAB and yeast isolates underwent identification by partial sequencing of the 16S rRNA and 26S rRNA genes, respectively (Lhomme et al., 2015).

Microbial starters and culture conditions

To replicate the microbial starter ecosystem of water kefir and given the limited diversity of LAB and yeasts identified in homemade kefir, we selected additional LAB starters (Lacticaseibacillus paracasei KFAL5 and Lactiplantibacillus plantarum KFI5) from the Culture Collection of the Micro4Food laboratory of the Faculty of Agricultural, Environmental and Food Sciences, Free University of Bolzano (Bolzano, Italy). These strains were previously isolated from a different water kefir sample. The cultures were preserved in 20 % glycerol stock solutions at −20 °C and routinely propagated at 30 °C for 24 h in their optimal growth medium (MRS broth) to maintain viability and functionality.

Multi-plant-based beverage (MPBB) preparation

Ingredient proportions were determined through preliminary trials aimed at establishing a baseline product with acceptable textural and sensory attributes. The final recipe comprised tritordeum flour (5 %, w/w), faba bean flour (5 %, w/w), pumpkin seeds (5 %, w/w), quince concentrate (4 %, v/w), sugar (1 %, w/w), botanical extract from tapioca (0.5 %, w/w) (natural stabilizer) (Pachekrepapol et al., 2021), and water (79.5 %, v/w). Initially, pumpkin seeds were peeled, deseeded, and blended using a Classic Blender 400 (PBI International). All ingredients were then properly mixed under continuous stirring for 5 min, followed by homogenization with an Ultra-Turrax homogenizer at 20,000 rpm for 1 min. Afterwards, the pH was adjusted to 6.80 pH units using 2 M NaOH, and the mixture was pasteurized at 90 °C for 15 min before cooling for further bioprocessing.

Starters screening

A beverage-like media (BLM) was used as a growth model system to assess the pro-technological performance of bacterial strains. The BLM, composed of equal parts of pre-prepared beverage and sterilized distilled water, was incubated overnight under shaking conditions (200 rpm), followed by centrifugation (12,500 rpm, 20 min, at 4 °C, Thermo Scientific SL16R centrifuge). The supernatant was recovered and sterilized by filtration through 0.22-μm membrane filters (Millipore Corporation, Bedford, MA, USA). LAB and yeast cells were harvested after 24 h by centrifugation (10,000 rpm, 10 min, 4 °C, Thermo Scientific), washed twice with 50 mM sterile potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.00), and separately inoculated into BLM. LAB were standardized to ca. 7.00 Log CFU/mL, while two yeast inoculum sizes were studied: 5.00 Log CFU/mL (to assess propagation efficiency from a low initial population) and 6.00 Log CFU/mL (to determine if a higher initial cell density accelerates metabolic activity). A non-inoculated BLM but incubated under the same conditions was used as the control (Control-BLM). Afterwards, samples were incubated at 30 °C, and growth kinetics were determined over 20 h by measuring optical density (OD) at 620 nm. Data were modelled using four different equations, namely, Richards, Gompertz, Logistic and Grompertz exponential (Zwietering et al., 1990). The main parameters taken into consideration included: A, which represents the difference between the OD at the time of inoculation and that in the stationary phase; μmax (OD unit h−1), which is the maximum growth rate; λ (h), which is the length of the lag phase. Growth capacity was also determined by integrating the area under the curve (AUC). The acidification capacity of all LAB strains was evaluated at intervals of 0, 8, 13, 16, and 20 h. The pH was measured by a Foodtrode electrode (Hamilton, Bonaduz, Switzerland).

Fermentation of MPBB beverage

Based on starter screening, a 16-h incubation period was chosen as optimal for fermenting plant-based beverages. Subsequently, various fermentation methods were employed. One method involved the inoculum of single LAB starters (ca. 7.00 Log CFU/mL), while another used single yeast starter (ca. 5.50 Log CFU/mL). Additionally, a third method included a mixture of ternary LAB culture, and the last combined all three LAB starters with the yeast (Fig. 1). All samples were incubated at 30 °C. Raw multi-plant-based beverage (Raw-MPBB), without microbial inoculum and incubation and MPBB without inoculum but incubated at 30 °C for 16 h (Unstarted-MPBB) were used as the controls. Sugars, organic acids, and ethanol quantification

Sugars consumption and organic acids and ethanol production were quantified from water-soluble extracts obtained from fresh beverages. For extract preparation, 2 mL of sample was mixed with 8 mL of water/perchloric acid (95:5, v:v), stirred at room temperature for 1 h, incubated overnight at 4 °C, then centrifuged (Thermo Scientific) at 10,000 rpm for 10 min and filtered through 0.22-μm Millex-HA filters (Millipore Co.). Quantitative analysis of glucose, fructose, mannitol, lactic acid, acetic acid, and ethanol was performed using Ultimate 3000 high-performance liquid chromatography (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, MA, USA) analysis equipped with an Aminex HPX-87H column (ion exclusion, Biorad Los Angeles, California), a PerkinElmer 200a refractive index (RI) detector, and a UV detector operating at 210 nm (Tlais et al., 2022). Elution was performed at 70 °C with a flow rate of 0.6 mL/min, using 5 mM H2SO4 as the mobile phase. The injection volume was 20 μL. Glucose, fructose, mannitol, and ethanol were detected using RI detector, while lactic and acetic acids were quantified by UV detection. Calibration curves were generated with pure external standards (0.5–15 g L−1), showing strong linearity between peak areas and analyte concentrations, with regression coefficients exceeding 0.99. Standards were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (Steinheim, Germany).

Total proteins and peptides quantification, and free amino acids profiling

Total nitrogen content was determined using the NDA 702 Dumas Nitrogen Analyzer (VELP Scientifica, 2014). One gram of freeze-dried sample was weighed and carefully wrapped in tin foil before the analysis. Based on the nature of the sample, oxygen dosage was fixed at a rate of 400 mL/min to achieve the best combustion. Total nitrogen results were obtained using VELP DUMASoftTM 6.1.0 and converted to total protein content by multiplying with the standard conversion factor of 6.25 (UNIT B1.2, 2001).

Water-soluble extracts (PWSE) from freeze-dried samples were used to quantify peptides and profile of free amino acids (FAAs). PWSE was prepared by extracting 1 g of powder with 10 mL of water/perchloric acid solution (95:5, v/v), stirred for 1 h at room temperature, incubated overnight at 4 °C, then centrifuged (10,000 rpm, 10 min, at 4 °C, Thermo Scientific) and filtered (0.22-μm). Total peptide concentration was estimated using OPA method as originally described by Church et al. (1983). Whereas total and individual FAAs were determined by a Biochrom 30 Amino acid Analyzer equipped with a Li-cation exchange column (4.6 mm × 200 mm internal diameter) as described by De Pasquale et al. (De Pasquale et al., 2021).

Free phenolic compounds profiling

Free phenolic compounds in methanol/water-soluble extracts (MWSE), prepared following Tlais et al. (2021), were quantified using a liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) platform composed of an Ultimate 3000 RSLCnano system, coupled to a QExactive Plus Hybrid Quadrupole-Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Chromatographic separation of phenolic compounds was performed through a Waters Acquity HSS T3 column (1.8 μm, 100 mm × 2.1 mm) (Milford, USA) by setting the binary gradient program as follows: 0 min, 2 % B; from 0 to 3 min, linear gradient to 20 % B; from 3 to 4.3 min, isocratic 20 % B; from 4.3 to 9 min, linear gradient to 45 % B; from 9 to 11 min, linear gradient to 100 % B; from 11 to 13 min, wash at 100 % B; from 13.01 to 15 min, and back to the initial conditions of 5 % B (solvent A = water + 0.1 % formic acid; solvent B = acetonitrile + 0.1 % formic acid). The flow rate was 300 μL/min and the temperature of the column was 40 °C. The LC-MS system was equipped with a Higher Collisional energy Dissociation cell (HCD) and a Heated Electro Spray Ionization (HESI) interface was adopted for LC-HRMS coupling. MS detection occurred in a Targeted-SIM/dd-MS2 mode with a negative polarity, according to the following parameters: spray voltage, 2.80 kV; sheath gas flow rate, at 35 arbitrary units; auxiliary gas flow rate, at 10 arbitrary units; capillary temperature, at 300 °C; S lens RF level, at 50 arbitrary units; capillary gas heater temperature, at 280 °C. The Q-Exactive plus™ mass spectrometer settings were the following: mass scan range, 80–1200 m/z; resolution, 70.000 (FWHM); Automatic Gain Control (AGC) Target, 5∗106 ions; maximum injection time (IT), 100 ms. Compounds were identified based on their reference standard, retention time, and [M− H]− ions. Peak areas, obtained from eXtracted Ion Current (XIC) chromatographic traces (i.e., chromatograms created by extracting the ion current from HRMS spectra in a m/z interval, including the monoisotopic peak) were used as a measurement of MS response and employed for calibration curve preparation based on commercial standards acids and for the quantification of phenolic compounds in MWSE. Data processing was carried out through the Xcalibur™ software (v. 3.1., Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Antioxidant activity

The in-vitro antioxidant activity of PWSE and MWSE prepared from raw and fermented beverages was evaluated through their radical scavenging activities against DPPH and ABTS. The DPPH radical scavenging activity was estimated using stable DPPH radical, following the method described by Yu et al. (2003), with BHT employed as the positive control. Appropriate sample dilutions were performed to ensure that the measurements were within the linear range of the assay. The analyses were performed at UV wavelengths of 517 nm. ABTS radical scavenging activity was measured by using the Antioxidant Assay kit (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The ABTS assay relies on the formation of a ferryl myoglobin radical from metmyoglobin and hydrogen peroxide, that oxidizes the ABTS to produce a radical cation, ABTS+, a soluble chromogen that is green in color and that can be determined spectrophotometrically at 405 nm. Trolox, a water-soluble vitamin E analog, was used as the antioxidant reference. The data were expressed as Trolox Equivalent Antioxidant Capacity (mM Trolox eq. g−1).

Antinutrients profiling

Residual phytic acid and raffinose contents were assessed using the Phytic Acid (Phytate)/Total Phosphorous (K-PHYT 05/19) Megazyme kit (Megazyme International, Ireland), which is based on the AOAC method 986.11, and the Raffinose/D-Galactose (K-RAFGA) Megazyme kit (Megazyme, Ireland), respectively, following the manufacturer's instructions. The fructans content was determined using the Fructo-oligosaccharides (K-FRUC 11/22) Megazyme kit (based on AOAC 999.03 and AACC method 32.32.01), following the standard assay procedure.

Color, syneresis, and texture analysis

The color of the raw and fermented beverages was assessed using a CR-400 Chroma Meter, employing the L∗, a∗, b∗ color space analysis method. L∗ corresponds to lightness (ranging from black to white), while a∗ and b∗ represent the chromaticity coordinates (red-green and yellow-blue, respectively).

The extent of syneresis (the separation of liquid from the matrix) in raw and fermented beverages was determined by centrifugation method, as described by Purwandari, Shah, & Vasiljevic (Purwandari et al., 2007). Samples were centrifuged (Thermo Scientific) at 700×g for 10 min at 8 °C, and the volume of expelled liquid was recorded. Syneresis was expressed as the percentage of separated liquid relative to the initial sample weight.

To evaluate the rheological properties of the raw and fermented beverages, a traditional setup was employed, consisting of a 2 cm-wide, 50 cm-long channel with an adjustable slope (0°–45°). Commercial diary yogurt purchased from a local supermarket (Bolzano, Italy) was used as reference control. All samples, each weighing 100 g and stored overnight at 4 °C, were poured at the top of the channel and allowed to flow autonomously until they stopped. Tests were conducted at a fixed 45° slope, and the channel was cleaned and dried between trials to ensure consistent friction. The movement of the samples was recorded using a high-speed color camera (10–150 fps), and images were then analyzed in MATLAB using Image-processing-based tracking methods to determine (1) the stopping time (s), reflecting firmness, and (2) the flow depth (mm), indicating consistency.

Volatile organic compounds (VOCs) profiling

For the analysis of VOCs, each sample was prepared by weighing 0.2 g NaCl and mixing with 2 g of beverage inside 10-mL glass vial sealed with a magnetic screw cap with polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE)/silicone septa. 3 μL of internal standard (4-methyl-2-pentanol in ethanol, 1000 mg/L) were added to the samples before sealing. A gas chromatography system (GC) equipped with mass detector (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, United States) was used. The VOCs were separated on a DB-WAX column (30 m × 0.25 mm ID × 0.5 μm) (Agilent technologies, USA). The temperature ramp of the main GC oven was 40 °C for 6 min (at injection), then ramped from 40 °C to 230 °C at 3 °C/min. The mass spectrometer operated in EI mode at 70 eV. The mass range was 34–360 m/z at 1 spectrum/s; the temperature of the ion source and quadrupole were 230 °C and 150 °C, respectively. Helium was used as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 1 mL/min in splitless mode. The ionizing electron energy was 70 eV, and the mass range scanned was 33–300 amu in full-scan acquisition mode. Compounds were identified by comparison based on NIST 23 (Full Mass Spectral Library plus Agilent Format) database. Analyses were performed in duplicates.

Shelf life

The shelf life of fermented beverages during refrigerated storage (4 °C) was assessed by monitoring pH and microbial cell densities at 0 (after 16 h of fermentation), 7, 14, and 21 days, as described by Pontonio et al. (2020a). The values of pH and presumptive LAB and yeast cell number were determined as reported elsewhere. Molds were plated on Potato Dextrose Agar (PDA) medium at 25 °C for 48 h. Total Enterobacteriaceae were enumerated on Violet Red Bile Glucose Agar (VRBGA) medium at 37 °C for 24 h. Staphylococcaceae were counted on Baird-Parker Agar (BPA) medium, supplemented with egg yolk/tellurite emulsion (5 %, v/v), at 37 °C for 24 h.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed in triplicate across two biological replicates. Data from the BLM were analyzed using two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), while the data from fermented beverages were evaluated with one-way ANOVA within the framework of the Generalized Linear Model (GLM). The data were tested for normality and homogeneity of variance assumptions using the Shapiro-Wilk test on residuals and Levene's Test, respectively. In cases where the assumption of homoscedasticity was violated, the reliability of standard ANOVA was limited. Consequently, GLM was employed to determine statistical differences among group means, offering a more robust alternative due to its flexibility in incorporating interaction effects and managing variance heterogeneity. Pairwise comparison of treatment means was achieved by Tukey-adjusted comparison procedure with p-value <0.05. Hierarchical clustering analysis, using the default method available in R and based on the Euclidean distance and the McQuitty linkage, was performed on data referring to VOCs.

Results and discussion

Kefir microbiota characterization

Based on selective agar media, the cell density of presumptive LAB and yeasts in water kefir were 8.16 ± 0.14 and 7.20 ± 0.20 Log CFU/mL, respectively. Partial rRNA gene sequencing indicated low microbial diversity, with all LAB isolates identified as Leuconostoc mesenteroides and all yeast isolates as Pichia bruneiensis. Although water kefir may initially harbor a broad diversity of microorganisms, competitive dynamics driven by carbon and energy source availability likely favor the dominance of specific taxa over time (Hsieh et al., 2012). Additional factors, such as geographical origin and fermentation conditions, may also influence species prevalence and dominance (Rosa et al., 2017; Lynch et al., 2021). To increase the diversity of microbial species to be investigated for the further investigation, Lc. paracasei (KFAL5) and Lp. plantarum (KFI5), previously isolated from a different water kefir sample, were also included in this study.

Staters performance in BLM

The final formulation of the beverage was optimized through preliminary trials. Ingredients were blended and homogenized, pH-adjusted, and pasteurized prior to cooling for bioprocessing. Given that microbial behavior in complex food substrates often differs from that in standard laboratory media, LAB and yeasts were evaluated for their growth kinetics and acidification capacity in BLM, a model medium specifically designed to closely mimic MPBB (Koskenniemi et al., 2009). The initial inoculum density was ca. 7.00 Log CFU/mL for LAB and 5.00 or 6.00 Log CFU/mL for yeast, respectively. The initial pH value was 6.40 ± 0.00 (Fig. S1). All strains showed robust growth with distinct kinetic patterns, achieving an increase of the cell densities (A) in the range of 1.12 ± 0.03 (KY6-BLM-6Log) to 1.91 ± 0.00 (KL3-BLM) OD620, maximum growth rates (μmax) of 0.23 ± 0.00 (KY6-BLM-6Log) to 1.09 ± 0.01 (KL3-BLM) OD h−1, and lag phase (λ) of 1.13 ± 0.00 (KL3-BLM) to 8.83 ± 0.54 (KY6-BLM-5Log) h. Among LAB, Leuc. mesenteroides was the most-performing strain, whereas for P. bruneiensis, the higher inoculum (approx. 6.00 Log CFU/mL) reduced the lag phase by approximately 6 h compared to the lower inoculum (approx. 5.00 Log CFU/mL) (2.61 ± 0.11 (KY6-BLM-6Log) h compared to 8.83 ± 0.54 (KY6-BLM-5Log) h) while maintaining a similar maximum growth rate. In terms of acidification, all LAB fermented-BLM showed a continuous decline in pH (in a range from 5.07 ± 0.01 (KFI5-BLM) to 4.16 ± 0.00 (KFAL5-BLM) pH units), at different extent, until 16 h. After this, comparable pH values were generally observed, except for Lp. plantarum KFI5, which underwent a further significant (p < 0.05) reduction at 20 h. Considering 16 and 20 h, the lowest pH values were consistently found for Lc. paracasei KFAL5 and Leuc. mesenteroides KL3.

Fermentation set-up

Based on starter performance in BLM, where optimal yeast growth was observed and most LAB strains achieved the desired pH of 4.30 pH units (a value favorable for safety and promoting protein gelation) (Montemurro et al., 2021; Huang et al., 2022; Hurtado-Murillo et al., 2024) within 16 h, MPBB fermentation conditions were standardized at 30 °C for 16 h. For P. bruneiensis KY6, an initial inoculum of ca. 5.50 Log CFU/mL was proposed to balance the lag phase and growth rate, as it offers a compromise between a shorter lag phase at higher inoculum sizes (Ding et al., 2009). LAB and yeast were inoculated individually, while co-culture fermentations included LAB alone or with P. bruneiensis KY6 (Fig. 1). Despite undergoing thermal treatment, Raw-MPBB showed presence of presumptive LAB (3.51 ± 0.47 Log CFU/mL), but no detectable yeasts (Table 1). However, the catalase-positive reaction of the bacterial colonies suggested their non-LAB origin, and their potential association with spoilage-related endogenous microorganisms (Mefleh et al., 2024; Alinovi et al., 2025). After 16 h of incubation, this count in Unstarted-MPBB increased (p < 0.05) to 6.36 ± 0.08 Log CFU/mL, reaching levels comparable to KY6-MPBB (6.24 ± 0.21 Log CFU/mL) (Table 1). The continued catalase-positive response suggests the persistence of non-LAB populations, potentially explaining the absence of significant acidification. In contrast, samples inoculated with LAB showed a substantial increase in viable counts, from ca. 7.00 Log CFU/mL to values ranging between 9.10 ± 0.02 (C-Y-LAB-MPBB) and 9.56 ± 0.14 (KFAL5-MPBB) Log CFU/mL. This growth correlated with notable acidification, with the greatest pH reduction observed in KFAL5-MPBB (4.23 ± 0.07) and the least in KFI5-MPBB (5.05 ± 0.03). In samples inoculated with P. bruneiensis alone, yeast counts increased significantly (p < 0.05) from 5.50 Log CFU/mL to 6.82 ± 0.07 (KY6-MPBB) (Table 1). Sugars, organic acids, and ethanol quantification

The sugar profile of Raw-MPBB was dominated by fructose, followed by maltose and glucose, with levels comparable to Unstarted-MPBB (Table 1). Similarly, KFI5-MPBB showed no significant (p > 0.05) differences for fructose and maltose, while glucose remained unchanged in KY6-MPBB. The highest reductions in fructose and glucose occurred in KL3-MPBB, reflecting distinct carbohydrate consumption patterns among the starters (Canon et al., 2020), as well as in co-cultured samples, suggesting synergistic sugar metabolism. Due to Leuc. mesenteroides metabolism using fructose as an electron acceptor via mannitol-2-dehydrogenase (Behare et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2020), mannitol release was the significantly (p < 0.05) highest in KL3-MPBB and C-LAB-MPBB. In contrast, yeast inclusion promoted the mannitol hydrolysis in KY6-MPBB but not in C-Y-LAB-MPBB. Although several yeast species are known to convert mannitol into fructose-6-phosphate or ethanol (Quain and Boulton, 1987; Rocher et al., 2025), the ability of P. bruneiensis KY6 to metabolize mannitol contradicts earlier findings (Sipiczki, 2012), suggesting strain-specific metabolic variability. The sugar depletion was accompanied by increased production of organic acids, primarily lactic and acetic acids. Among single LAB starters, KFAL5-MPBB showed the highest significant (p < 0.05) lactic acid accumulation. Ternary inoculum of LAB strains reduced this level, with a further decrease observed upon yeast addition in C-Y-LAB-MPBB. Acetic acid was found at significantly (p < 0.05) higher levels where Leuc. mesenteroides was inoculated (Baek et al., 2024) mainly in KL3-MPBB, and to a lesser extent in C-LAB-MPBB and C-Y-LAB-MPBB. P. bruneiensis is generally characterized as a low ethanol-producing yeast, contrasting with conventional fermentative species like Saccharomyces cerevisiae (Ivit et al., 2020). Our results confirmed this metabolic trait, with P. bruneiensis KY6 demonstrating minimal ethanol production in both KY6-MPBB and C-Y-LAB-MPBB. This aligns with its preferential respiratory metabolism, where energy generation occurs primarily through the TCA cycle under aerobic conditions rather than alcoholic fermentation (van Rossum et al., 2016; Bertels et al., 2021). Additionally, Leuc. mesenteroides KL3-inoculated samples (KL3-MPBB and C-LAB-MPBB) produced detectable ethanol, albeit in traces (Baek et al., 2024; Jung et al., 2012).

Total protein, peptides, and individual free amino acids quantifications

Research and industry prioritize protein fortification to meet health-conscious consumer demands, as evidenced by the incorporation of protein-rich ingredients such as faba bean flour, tritordeum, and pumpkin seeds in the formulation (Sethi et al., 2016; Qamar et al., 2020), which achieved high initial protein concentration (19.44 ± 0.21 g/100 g dry weight (DW)) (Table 1). While fermentation affected protein metabolism, the precise mechanisms underlying protein fraction fluctuations remain complex to elucidate. Initial analysis revealed minor, non-significant (p > 0.05) protein increases across most samples, except for C-Y-LAB-MPBB (23.04 ± 0.00 g/100 g DW), which showed maximal (p < 0.05) protein enrichment. This may suggest that co-culturing (LAB-yeast) preferentially stimulates single-cell protein synthesis (Murali Sankar et al., 2023), although differential proteolytic activity among microbial consortia may concurrently enhance peptide liberation following protein synthesis. The proteolysis hypothesis is partially supported by peptide estimation, where only C-LAB-MPBB showed significantly (p < 0.05) elevated peptide levels relative to Raw-MPBB (3.24 ± 0.44 vs. 2.08 ± 0.27 g/100 g DW) (Table 1).

To further highlight other proteolytic activities (Gobbetti et al., 2014), complementary free and individual amino acid profiling was required. Fermentation markedly modified free amino acid composition, with TFAA levels peaking in KFAL5-MPBB and declining mostly in KL3-MPBB compared to Raw-MPBB (Table 2). These changes demonstrated proteolytic enzyme-mediated liberation of FAAs from proteins/peptides (Islam et al., 2022) and microbial utilization of FAAs for energy generation, redox balance, and biosynthetic pathways (Takagi, 2019), reflecting the dual metabolic activity of starter cultures. Asn, the most abundant in Raw-MPBB, underwent a significant (p < 0.05) reduction, along with Val and Ile, in most fermented beverages. For Glu, His, and Arg, KFAL5-MPBB demonstrated the highest release compared to Raw-MPBB, followed by C-Y-LAB-MPBB and C-LAB-MPBB. Functionally, Glu contributes to the reduction of the risk of developing type-2 diabetes (Vangipurapu et al., 2019), while Arg and His have several advantages, within which they may support immune function and alleviate oxidative stress (Niu et al., 2012). In contrast, KL3-MPBB showed the most significant (p < 0.05) reduction in Glu and, along with KFI5-MPBB, in His, while Unstarted-MPBB exhibited the lowest Arg levels. Fermentation significantly (p < 0.05) enhanced Gly levels among all started beverages, particularly in KFAL5-MPBB, while Gln increased only in KFAL5-MPBB, KFI5-MPBB, KY6-MPBB, and C-Y-LAB-MPBB. Among these, the functional roles of Gly include enhancing antioxidant capacity, boosting immune response, supporting wound healing and maintaining gut integrity (Wang et al., 2013) while Gln exerts potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects, and may promote cardiovascular health (Durante, 2019). Lys, which supports growth, bone health, and brain function (Gunarathne et al., 2025), showed significant increases in KFAL5-MPBB, KFI5-MPBB, and KY6-MPBB, undetectable levels in KL3-MPBB, and unchanged levels in the remaining beverages. Ala, which is involved in key pathways that support human cells growth, metabolism, and immunity (Ling et al., 2023), varied significantly relative to Raw-MPBB, peaking in KFAL5-MPBB and dropping in KL3-MPBB. Free phenolic compounds profiling

Phenolic compounds represent another class of key bioactive phytochemicals frequently targeted in plant-based beverages to amplify functional properties (Marafon et al., 2025). While diversifying raw materials in formulations increases the intrinsic variety of phenolic compounds (van Breda and de Kok, 2018), combining this approach with microbial biotransformation proves more effective for maximizing nutritional value while maintaining consumer acceptability (Septembre-Malaterre et al., 2018; Vieira et al., 2021). Generally, fourteen compounds, belonging to various phenolic classes, were identified and quantified in MWSE by LC-MS analysis (Table 3). Sinapic acid was the most abundant in Raw-MPBB, followed by epigallocatechin, and chlorogenic acid. Fermentation induced a profound modulation in the phenolic compound compositions of the beverages. A significant (p < 0.05) decline in ferulic acid levels was observed among all fermented samples, likely converted to bioactive derivatives (vinyl/reduced forms) with enhanced antioxidant potential and characteristic fermented flavors (Rodríguez et al., 2008). In contrast, reductions in 4-hydroxybenzaldehyde were limited to KL3-MPBB, KY6-MPBB, C-LAB-MPBB, and C-Y-LAB-MPBB, and in p-coumaric acid to KFI5-MPBB, C-LAB-MPBB, and C-Y-LAB-MPBB, indicating strain-dependent phenolic metabolism (Cueva et al., 2010; Filannino et al., 2015). Compared to Raw-MPBB, only C-Y-LAB-MPBB showed complete epigallocatechin hydrolysis and a pronounced increase in sinapic acid, a multifunctional bioactive compound with antioxidant, antimicrobial, and anti-stress properties (Nićiforović and Abramovič, 2014). Chlorogenic acid, catechin, and epicatechin displayed contrasting trends based on microbial starters employed. Collectively, they contribute to antioxidant and protective functions via free radical scavenging and pathway modulation (Naveed et al., 2018; Isemura, 2019; Qu et al., 2021). KL3-MPBB, KFAL5-MPBB, and C-LAB-MPBB yielded the highest chlorogenic acid and epicatechin levels, whereas fermentation with P. bruneiensis KY6, alone or with LAB, resulted in their complete metabolism. Catechin levels significantly (p < 0.05) increased in KFAL5-MPBB and C-LAB-MPBB but decreased in KL3-MPBB and C-Y-LAB-MPBB. Other phenolics were comparable to or exceeded Raw-MPBB levels. Rutin significantly (p < 0.05) increased in KFAL5-MPBB and C-LAB-MPBB. Gallic and protocatechuic acids showed substantial increases, particularly in KL3-MPBB, followed by KFAL5-MPBB and Unstarted-MPBB, with C-LAB-MPBB also contributing to the rise in gallic acid. These compounds are associated with significant health benefits (Kakkar and Bais, 2014; Ganeshpurkar and Saluja, 2017; Hadidi et al., 2024). Fermentation with Lp. plantarum KFI5 and P. bruneiensis KY6 led to the formation of catechol, which has antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, preservative, and flavor enhancing properties (Kakkar and Bais, 2014), while caffeic acid was exclusively released in KL3-MPBB. Antioxidant activity

To elucidate health-promoting potential of the fermented beverage, antioxidant capacity was assessed. Their ability to scavenge ABTS and DPPH radicals might be an indicator to mitigate oxidative damage in biological systems, thereby supporting cellular redox homeostasis and enhancing overall health resilience (Kotha et al., 2022; Le Gal et al., 2021). Notably, ABTS results showed that all PWSEs from fermented samples exhibited significantly (p < 0.05) higher antioxidant activity than the control, except for C-Y-LAB-MPBB (Fig. 2). For MWSEs, only KL3-MPBB showed a significant increase, emphasizing the contribution of antioxidants as gallic acid, protocatechuic acid, and caffeic acid (Kakkar and Bais, 2014; Hadidi et al., 2024; Gülçin, 2006; Purushothaman et al., 2022), where they are present at higher concentrations, while other samples remained comparable to the control. Conversely, DPPH assay showed no significant (p > 0.05) changes in radical scavenging activity across samples, regardless of the extract type, despite minor fluctuations. Antinutritional profiling

Phytic acid is known to chelate minerals, thereby reducing their bioavailability (Pontonio et al., 2020b). Due to their naturally high phytic acid content, tritordeum, faba bean flour, botanical extract from tapioca, and especially pumpkin seeds (Devi et al., 2018) contributed substantially to the phytic acid concentration in Raw-MPBB (3.91 ± 0.08 g/100 g DW). Fermentation yielded comparable phytic acid levels in most beverages relative to Raw-MPBB, with two notable exceptions: a significant (p < 0.05) decrease in C-LAB-MPBB (1.19 ± 0.02 g/100 g DW), likely due to the synergistic action of microbial phytases from the LAB consortium (Joudaki et al., 2023), and a marked increase (p < 0.05) in KY6-MPBB (4.93 ± 0.25 g/100 g DW), presumably resulting from yeast-induced release of bound phytates during fermentation (Arjmand et al., 2023) (Table 4). Fructans, which are poorly absorbed in the small intestine and may exacerbate irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) symptoms (Yan et al., 2018) significantly decreased only in KFAL5-MPBB (ca. 95 % decrease). This reduction is likely attributed to strain-specific differences in enzymatic activities involved in fructans degradation and in transport systems facilitating their uptake and internalization (Borowska et al., 2025). Color, syneresis, and texture analysis

Visual appeal, primarily governed by color, serves as the first indicator of beverage acceptability (Jeske et al., 2017). Fermentation can modulate color through microbial metabolism and biochemical transformations, leading to pigment degradation, browning reactions, or the release of soluble phenolics, which may alter the original chromatic profile of the matrix (Uruc et al., 2022). Our findings demonstrated that fermentation minimally affected L∗ values (Table S1). In contrast, significant (p < 0.05) increases in a∗ values were observed for Unstarted-MPBB, KFAL5-MPBB, and KFI5-MPBB compared to the control, while only Unstarted-MPBB and KFAL5-MPBB significantly reduced b∗ values. Notably, KFAL5-MPBB consistently exhibited the strongest deviation in both metrics. This resulted in an overall darker appearance, which some consumers might perceive as a sign of oxidation or spoilage and thus less appealing (Giacalone et al., 2022), while others may associate darker tones with a higher content of natural ingredients and minimal processing (Ghosh et al., 2024). Syneresis and texture can also affect visual appeal, consequently influencing consumer perception and acceptance (Arab et al., 2023). Fermentation significantly reduced syneresis in most samples compared to the control (p < 0.05) (Table S1), except for KFI5-MPBB, which exhibited increased phase separation. Notably, KY6-inoculated samples showed the lowest syneresis levels (p < 0.05), indicating enhanced matrix stability. Reduced syneresis is generally desirable, as it reflects a well-structured network with high water-holding capacity (Arab et al., 2023). For texture analysis, commercial dairy yogurt was used as a reference control (Fig. 4). When the samples were allowed to slide down the inclined stand, all took more time to stop autonomously compared to the commercial yogurt, suggesting higher firmness. This effect can be attributed to the presence of polysaccharides and plant proteins, which form more rigid gel networks (Tang et al., 2024). However, no significant differences were observed between the fermented beverages and Raw-MPBB, although the KFAL5-MPBB beverage displayed the highest firmness. This effect may be attributed to a slight increase in plant protein aggregation (Tan et al., 2023) and/or alterations in the starch-to-protein ratio (Boukid et al., 2024) during fermentation. In terms of consistency, only Unstarted-MPBB showed a flow depth comparable to dairy yogurt, while all other beverages demonstrated significantly lower values.

VOCs profiling

The biochemical transformation mediated by kefir microorganisms during fermentation, through lipolysis, glycolysis, and proteolysis, critically defines the organoleptic properties of the final product, primarily by generating characteristic aroma-active compounds and flavor nuances that govern hedonic perception and consumer preference (Walsh et al., 2016; Farag et al., 2020). As a key driver of innovation in functional beverages, our microbial starters shaped the volatile profile of fermented plant-based matrices. GC-MS analysis identified 24 VOCs spanning five chemical classes: alcohols (7), aldehydes (9), ketones (1), carboxylic acids (3), and furans (3). Notably, only 9 of these VOCs were detected in the Raw-MPBB, highlighting fermentation-dependent synthesis (Fig. 3S). Distinct VOC signatures emerged in KFAL5-MPBB (5 unique compounds) and KY6-MPBB (3 unique compounds), which correlated with their segregation into individual clusters during heat map analysis (Fig. 3). This multivariate approach further resolved two additional clusters: one comprising KL3-MPBB and C-Y-LAB-MPBB, and another containing Raw-MPBB, Unstarted-MPBB, KFAL5-MPBB, and C-LAB-MPBB samples. In addition to five VOCs exclusively detected in KFAL5-MPBB (1-octen-3-ol, octanal, (E)-2-nonenal, hexanoic acid, and 2-propyl furan), this sample showed enhanced abundance of others, including nonanal, (E)-2-octenal, (E,E)-2,4-decadienal, 1-octen-3-one, benzaldehyde, 1-pentanol, hexanal, 2-pentyl furan, and (E)-2-heptenal (Fig. 3S). These VOCs may contribute to a broad aroma profile, ranging from pleasant fermented, herbal, floral, and fruity notes (e.g., 1-pentanol, 1-octen-3-one, (E,E)-2,4-decadienal) to green, grassy tones (e.g., 2-octenal, 2-pentyl furan), and potential off-flavors such as rancid or oxidized notes from nonanal, benzaldehyde, and hexanal (Park et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2022; Pétel et al., 2017). The three VOCs produced uniquely in KY6-MPBB might be strongly associated with fruity and floral notes (2,4-decadien-1-ol, heptanol and benzeneacetaldehyde) (Murat et al., 2013; Ferreira et al., 2022; Xiang et al., 2023), and mild scented, woody, aromatic, and spicy aromas (heptanol) (Menezes et al., 2018). Leuc. mesenteroides KL3 promoted 2-hepten-1-ol (E), a compound known for its pungent and fatty odor (Wang et al., 2014) character, only in the KL3-MPBB formulation, but increased acetic acid in all formulations where it is present, contributing to possible mild acidity and flavor enhancement (Ferreira et al., 2022). While combining LAB strains had limited impact on the overall VOC profile (van Mastrigt et al., 2019), co-fermentation with yeast significantly diversified the aroma profile (Ferreira et al., 2022; Freire et al., 2015), including the production of 1-butanol, 3-methyl, an alcohol linked to malty, whiskey-like, and fruity notes that can improve beverage sensory quality (Chung et al., 2015; Aregbe et al., 2024). Shelf life

To ensure product quality, safety, and consumer acceptance during storage (Mishra et al., 2024), the shelf life was assessed under refrigerated conditions (Fig. S2). After 21 days, no significant post-acidification in any of the fermented samples was observed, expect in KFI5-MPBB. Similarly, presumptive LAB populations remained stable across all samples, while catalase-positive colonies were detected only in Unstarted-MPBB and KY6-MPBB, suggesting their non-LAB origin and possible association with undesirable endogenous microbiota, as previously reported (Mefleh et al., 2024; Monica et al., 2025). However, samples inoculated with P. bruneiensis KY6 maintained stable yeast viability. In contrast, no growth of molds, Enterobacteriaceae, or Staphylococcaceae was detected, supporting the microbiological safety of the fermented products during cold storage (Demarinis et al., 2024). Nevertheless, future studies should address sensory, textural, and bioactive compounds stability to validate consumer acceptability and health benefits over time (Farid et al., 2024).

Conclusions

Our study effectively optimized novel multi-plant-based beverages using water kefir-derived starters through tailored fermentation approaches. Both monoculture fermentation with Lc. paracasei and the ternary LAB cultures showed performing proteolytic and acidifying activities while facilitating the release of key phenolic compounds. Fermentation with Leuc. mesenteroides specifically enhanced antioxidant phenolic production. From antinutritional standpoint, the ternary LAB culture uniquely mediated phytic acid degradation, whereas Lc. paracasei fermentation yielded the lowest fructan and raffinose content. Fermentation with only Lc. paracasei or P. bruneiensis generated more complex volatile organic compound profiles. Notably, P. bruneiensis significantly improved beverage physical stability by minimizing syneresis but consistently showed non-LAB microbial growth that can compromise shelf life, unlike LAB starters which maintained better microbiological stability. Collectively, these findings demonstrate how kefir-derived microbial starters can be leveraged to develop nutrient-rich and environmentally friendly plant-based dairy alternatives.
