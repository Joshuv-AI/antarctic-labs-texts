Estimation of daily energy requirements using a hybrid artificial intelligence model
Authors: Zeliha Uçar, Betül Çiçek
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurately estimating energy requirements is critical for individuals to maintain a healthy life. Traditional methods may be time-consuming, complex, low in accuracy, and costly, thus creating a need for new approaches. This study explores the applicability of hybrid artificial intelligence models for calculating daily energy requirements based on individuals’ anthropometric measurements and demographic data. The study’s primary goal is to develop a model that offers a reliable and practical solution with higher accuracy than existing methods while ensuring ease of use in field settings. This study used data collected from volunteer individuals at Sakarya University between September 2023 and February 2024. Anthropometric measurements were performed by a bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) device, and demographic data were obtained through face-to-face surveys. Eighty-seven features were analyzed using the Spearman feature selection algorithm, and these were utilized to estimate energy requirements. Based on collaborative hybridization, the hybrid artificial intelligence model integrates three distinct Gaussian Process Regression (GPR) models using squared exponential, rational quadratic, and Matern52 kernels. These models were structured based on gender, and performance evaluation was carried out using criteria such as MAPE, MAD, MSE, R, and R². The best model performance in males was achieved at level 10 with 100% R², while the highest accuracy in females was observed at level 15. To increase model simplicity, the PCA method was applied; however, a decrease in performance was detected (R = 0.48, R² = 0.23). The accuracy of the artificial intelligence models proposed in this study was significantly higher than that of traditional formulas commonly preferred in the current literature. Hybrid artificial intelligence models offer practicality, accuracy, and flexibility in estimating energy requirements. Gender-based modeling has enhanced prediction performance while providing more reliable results by accounting for individual differences. Introduction

Energy is the capacity to perform work1,2. For both humans and animals, energy expenditure is one of the fundamental characteristics3. The energy required is obtained from nutrients, which are classified as carbohydrates, proteins, and fats. Humans receive these nutrients from plant- and animal-based foods in their diets1,2. Factors such as gender, age, level of physical activity, body weight, and height determine the energy necessary for sustaining life. The energy needed to support optimal growth and development, as well as to maintain life based on these factors, is referred to as “energy requirement”2,4.

Individuals can maintain a healthy life by balancing energy requirements and energy expenditure. Thus, energy expenditure plays a crucial role in calculating energy requirements. Total energy expenditure over 24 h consists of three main components: basal metabolic rate, activity-induced thermogenesis, and the thermic effect of foods. Basal metabolic rate is the minimum energy expended to sustain life, accounting for approximately 65% of the total daily energy expenditure in sedentary individuals2,5. It is measured while the individual rests in a thermoneutral environment following an overnight fast3. Activity-induced thermogenesis, also known as the thermic effect of physical activity, refers to the energy required for daily tasks and bodily movement, constituting about 25% of the total daily energy expenditure in sedentary individuals. Lastly, the thermic effect of foods—also referred to as diet-induced thermogenesis—covers the energy required for food consumption, digestion, absorption, and metabolism. In sedentary individuals, this component represents around 10% of total daily energy expenditure2,5. All these components collectively influence an individual’s energy requirement.

Numerous equations are used to estimate energy requirements6. In addition to various influencing factors, these equations consider the energy required for new tissue synthesis during growth and development periods, lactation, illness, and injury, as well as the individual’s total energy expenditure over 24 h7. Furthermore, body weight reflects the balance between energy intake and expenditure. Any imbalance in energy affects body weight2,4. To maintain this balance, it is crucial to determine energy requirements accurately1,8. This is especially important in the context of malnutrition, obesity, and diabetes, which have become global public health concerns9.

The unit used when measuring energy is the calorie. However, since the energy released during nutrient metabolism is significant, kilocalorie (kcal) is used as the standard unit. There are many different methods, both direct and indirect, for estimating energy requirements. These methods include direct calorimetry, indirect calorimetry, respiratory quotient, and the double-labelled water method2,10. Indirect calorimetry is accepted as the gold standard2,11. This method involves measuring the individual’s oxygen consumption and carbon dioxide production over a specific period. The measurement uses a mask or a ventilated hood that covers the mouth and nose and collects the exhaled carbon dioxide. Various software tools are available to analyze these measurements, though they are often expensive. Their accuracy and reliability are very high2,11,12. This method is suitable for both field and laboratory applications. Indirect calorimetry is the most commonly used method to assess energy efficiency by measuring oxygen consumption. Calculations are made using an energy equivalent of 5 kcal per liter of oxygen consumed by the body. It can measure oxygen consumption and calculate energy expenditure under different conditions, such as resting, running, or sleeping9. Moreover, it can be conducted to examine the energy requirements of individuals under different metabolic conditions or to evaluate the effects of various dietary models13. However, specific protocols should be followed for these measurements to be accurate. These include avoiding caffeine for at least 4 h before measurement and refraining from smoking and alcohol consumption for at least 2 h beforehand12.

The first calorimeter used for measuring energy requirements was the direct calorimeter9. These are costly devices that require specialized equipment. Their operation necessitates the presence of full-time technicians. Compared to indirect calorimeters, direct calorimeters provide less data. They measure the heat lost within a chamber10. Due to the requirement of conducting the measurement in an enclosed space, their accuracy in energy measurement is lower than that of indirect calorimeters9.

In the double-labelled water method, individuals are given an oral dose, followed by the measurement of the labelled water to estimate energy requirements. This method is suitable for use in vulnerable groups such as premature infants, newborns, adolescents, pregnant and lactating women. It is a non-invasive method ideal for community use, with high accuracy9,14. While the double-labelled water method can calculate total energy requirements, it does not provide detailed information regarding physical activity type, intensity, or duration. In this method, laboratory equipment is required to analyze biological samples, and the sample analysis process is complex and costly (R = 0.85)9. In a study conducted by Wong and colleagues in 2014, the double labelled water method is repeatable for measuring energy expenditure, can be used to estimate daily energy intake, and is suitable for monitoring changes in body composition over a 2.5–4.4-year period14. In addition to all these methods, various other approaches have recently been developed, such as accelerometers, heart rate monitors, sensewear armbands, and smart devices for monitoring energy expenditure and physical activity. These are also referred to as wearable devices. They are lightweight, portable technological tools. Being non-invasive, these devices have recently become increasingly popular. Although their accuracy is assumed to rely on heart rate monitoring and is considered lower than the devices above, their affordability and suitability for community applications refer to them as a preferred option15.

Due to the high cost, complexity, unsuitability for community use, and the burden placed on participants by precise measurement devices under free-living conditions, accelerometers have been developed as an alternative method. Accelerometers are produced in various types, such as single- or tri-axial models, and may be worn on the hip or waist. However, these measurements may vary due to factors like age (R²=0.04–0.08)16. In a study conducted in 2022 on British Army officers, energy expenditure of the individuals was measured by a wrist-worn accelerometer (R = 0.8)17.

There are many different formulas used to estimate energy expenditure6. However, their reliability is lower than the measurement methods mentioned above. Nevertheless, their use is more common in the field due to their affordability and practicality. These formulas first calculate basal metabolic rate, adding a physical activity factor. In this way, total energy expenditure is obtained. Standard formulas include Harris-Benedict, Mifflin-St. Jeor and World Health Organization (WHO) equations, among others5,18,19. Harris and Benedict published the first of these, including parameters such as age, body weight, height, and gender5,19. Their study involved 239 adults aged between 16 and 63, including 136 men and 103 women (ER = 0.8, KR = 0.6)19. New cross-validated equations were developed after researchers identified deviations in this formula3. Owen and colleagues published two studies evaluating the requirements of men and women aged 18–65. The studies involved 60 men and 44 women. Resting metabolic rate and the thermic effect of food were determined by indirect calorimetry, and separate regression equations were developed for men and women20,21. In 1990, Mifflin and colleagues developed a new formula through multiple regression analysis with a sample of 498 healthy individuals (247 women, 251 men) using indirect calorimetry (R = 0.71)18. Although many equations exist, new ones continue to be developed3. One such example is the formula developed by Gerrior and colleagues in 2006, which includes age, height, and body weight based on gender. A formula was created by combining basal metabolic rate with the metabolic equivalent to calculate the physical activity factor. Then, grouping was made according to this formula to estimate the effect of physical activity on energy expenditure, and energy requirement was calculated by multiplying the two values7. In a 2022 study, the accuracy of equations used to estimate energy expenditure in individuals with Down syndrome (DS) was compared, as these formulas had not been validated for this population. After a 12-hour overnight fast and 48 h without aerobic exercise, energy expenditure was measured via a 90-minute indirect calorimetry session. The accuracy of seven different equations was compared, and the one developed by the Institute of Medicine provided the most accurate prediction for adolescents with DS22. Another example involves individuals with phenylketonuria (PKU), where calculating resting energy expenditure is crucial. A study was conducted to develop a formula specifically for children and adolescents with PKU. Indirect calorimetry, bioelectrical impedance analysis, and anthropometric measurements were used—most existing equations, not specific to these individuals, overestimated resting energy expenditure. Therefore, a new formula was proposed (R²=0.942)23. In a study conducted on overweight and obese individuals aged 20–60 years, a new equation to estimate resting energy expenditure was developed, involving 410 participants. The new formula showed less deviation than existing equations, such as Mifflin-St Jeor, Owen, and Carrasco (R = 0.72)24. At the Pennington Biomedical Research Center, 119 individuals were included in a study using the double-labeled water method to develop tiered models for estimating energy requirements. The first model included age, height, weight, and gender (R²=0.65), while the second model also incorporated body composition and resting metabolic rate (R²=0.74). Models including body composition and resting metabolic rate based on gender provided higher correlations25. In another study, researchers aimed to estimate energy requirements in patients with chronic kidney disease, including those in stages 3–5 and those receiving renal replacement therapy. Indirect calorimetry was used for validation, and BIA measurements and handgrip strength were also included. Different models were developed depending on the variables involved (R²=0.44–0.46)26. Finally, in a study on elderly individuals using the double-labeled water method, new predictive equations were developed to estimate energy requirements (R²=0.43–0.74)27.

Another method used to estimate energy requirements is the calculation based on energy intake. This approach relies on the individual’s dietary intake records. However, since it is based on recall and self-reporting, it often leads to inaccuracies in estimation2. In a study utilizing dietary intake records, Thai workers aged between 20 and 50 years were included. Using the 24-hour recall method, nutritional data were collected over two days. The average energy intake was 1485 kcal/day for men and 1428 kcal/day for women, respectively28. In another similar study conducted in China, the energy intake of children aged 6-to-17 years was calculated using the 24-hour recall method. A three-day record was taken, and the average intake was calculated29. As mentioned above, although numerous methods are available in the literature, there remains a need for reliable, AI-based systems that are practical, highly accurate, and suitable for use in field settings with the integration of newer technologies.

Recent studies have increasingly applied machine learning (ML) and artificial intelligence (AI) methods to estimate human energy requirements and metabolic rate. For example, probabilistic graphical models such as bootstrap particle filters have been used to combine continuous heart rate and core body temperature data, significantly improving the accuracy of metabolic rate prediction (RMSE = 133 ± 26 W)30. Smartphone sensors, including heart rate monitors and inertial sensors, have also been evaluated for estimating energy expenditure during daily activities and sports, showing performance comparable to or better than commercial devices such as the SenseWear armband31. Neural network-based approaches have likewise been explored; for instance, models developed with General Regression Neural Networks (GRNN) and Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLP) using body composition data outperformed traditional equations like Harris-Benedict and Mifflin-St Jeor, achieving higher correlation (R = 0.85) and lower error (RMSE = 134.91 kcal/day)32. Other studies have integrated AI with sensor systems such as Kinect cameras to estimate metabolic rate across different physical activities, offering simplified alternatives to conventional measurement techniques33. Furthermore, regression-based models have been embedded into web applications, enabling the prediction of total daily energy expenditure from anthropometric and body composition variables without relying on physical activity level inputs34. Beyond direct estimation of energy expenditure, wearable devices employing ML algorithms have also been investigated for related applications such as non-invasive blood glucose forecasting in diabetes management35. These studies highlight the growing role of ML and AI in improving the accuracy, accessibility, and personalization of metabolic rate and energy requirement estimation.

Recent studies have increasingly employed machine learning (ML) and artificial intelligence (AI) methods to estimate human energy requirements and metabolic rate. For instance, particle filter-based probabilistic graphical models have been applied using heart rate and core body temperature data, achieving high accuracy in metabolic rate estimation30. Similarly, Kinect camera–based AI approaches have enabled metabolic rate estimation using simple postural and activity observations33. Other studies have applied machine learning models such as Random Forest, trained on motion sensor data, to predict activity-specific metabolic rate with accuracies exceeding 90%36. In addition, cloud-based boosted decision tree regression has been explored for energy expenditure prediction from physiological sensor data, highlighting the scalability of AI applications in health monitoring37. More recently, neural network models such as General Regression Neural Networks (GRNN) have been developed to predict resting metabolic rate, demonstrating superior accuracy over traditional predictive equations (R = 0.85)32. In addition to model development, effective feature selection is crucial in improving prediction accuracy and computational efficiency in AI-based health applications. Recent work, such as the Binary Arithmetic Optimization Algorithm (BAOA) with a K-Nearest Neighbor classifier, has shown strong potential to identify the most relevant variables and reduce dimensionality, which could also be valuable when modeling energy requirements38. Collectively, these studies illustrate the growing role of AI and ML in developing accurate, scalable, and accessible tools for energy requirement estimation.

However, unlike traditional equations that rely on fixed parameters and linear assumptions, AI-based approaches can capture complex, non-linear interactions between demographic and anthropometric variables, making them more adaptable and practically valuable for diverse real-world settings.

Although numerous methods exist for estimating energy requirements, many remain limited in accuracy, practicality, or adaptability for field applications. Therefore, the development of artificial intelligence–based systems has the potential to address these shortcomings by providing faster, more reliable, and more applicable solutions.

This study uses artificial intelligence based on anthropometric measurements and demographic data to calculate individuals’ daily energy requirements. The proposed method provides reliable, field-applicable, technician-free, fast, and highly accurate estimations. Therefore, demographic information and results from bioelectrical impedance measurements—which are easy to obtain—were utilized. A feature selection algorithm was applied during the analysis phase to enhance model performance. Additionally, gender-specific models were developed to improve accuracy further. Since body composition differs between men and women, gender-based models have the advantage of yielding better performance39. Performance evaluation criteria for the developed models were calculated and compared with existing studies in the literature.

Materials and methods

The study was conducted in accordance with the flowchart presented in Fig. 1. In the first step, data acquisition included anthropometric measurements, demographic information, and outputs obtained from bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA). Then, data preprocessing was performed, which involved handling missing values, normalization, and removal of outliers. The preprocessed data were directed into two dimensionality reduction/feature selection pathways: Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Spearman correlation-based feature selection. The resulting feature subsets were subsequently used as inputs for three different Gaussian Process Regression (GPR) models: GPR-S (Squared Exponential kernel), GPR-R (Rational Quadratic kernel), GPR-M (Matern52 kernel). Finally, the outputs of these models were combined under a hybrid machine learning algorithm using an ensemble averaging strategy to obtain the final prediction. Since males and females differ significantly in terms of body composition40, gender-specific models were also developed in this study to improve predictive accuracy and account for physiological variation.

The questionnaires included data on participants’ demographic characteristics and physical activity status. Parameters obtained via BIA, such as body weight (kg), lean mass weight (kg), lean mass percentage (%), fat mass (kg), fat percentage (%), body fluid weight (kg), and fluid percentage (%), were used in the analyses (Table 2).

Bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) was conducted using the Tanita BC-601 Segmental Body Composition Monitor (Tanita Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). This device employs an 8-electrode segmental measurement system providing whole-body and regional assessments (arms, legs, trunk). It has a maximum weight capacity of 150 kg, with a measurement precision of 100 g for body weight and 0.1% for body fat percentage. The device operates at a frequency of 50 kHz, 500 µA, a low-level current that complies with international safety standards for biological measurements. The BC-601 also allows storage and transfer of results via SD card and proprietary software. Calibration was performed automatically per the manufacturer’s guidelines before each measurement session. The validity and reliability of this model have been confirmed in previous clinical studies, and it is widely used in research settings for body composition analysis.

Energy requirement calculation

Energy requirements were calculated based on age, height, and gender using the formula established by the World Health Organization (WHO) (Eqs. 1 and 2). In this formula, the constant value is accepted as 1 for males (Eqs. 1) and 0.95 for females (Eq. 2)41. The authors do not select these constants, but are predefined in the WHO guidelines to account for metabolic differences between males and females. Physical activity level (PAL) is a fixed coefficient that ranges between 1 and 2.5 depending on the daily activity level of the individual, and is incorporated into the Eqs7,41. PAL values of 1–1.39 are used for sedentary activity levels, 1.4–1.59 for light activity, 1.6–1.89 for moderate activity, and 1.9–2.5 for high physical activity levels, respectively41.

The selected features are grouped progressively as 5%, 10%, 15%, and so on, up to 100%. Each level represents a specific proportion of the total features selected, and these selections are made to reduce model complexity and optimize performance. The table provides a comparative overview of the number of features selected for males (M), females (F) and total (T). The findings indicate that the number of selected features at each level is similar, mainly between males and females. This suggests that similar features may be equally crucial for model performance in both genders. Notably, at higher levels (80–100%), a more significant number of features were selected for all individuals, indicating that the model operates at its highest level of complexity and utilizes all available features. These results demonstrate that the AI-based model may produce more accurate and reliable estimates of energy requirements by accounting for gender-specific differences. In this study, the term level refers explicitly to the feature subsets generated by the Spearman method. Each level corresponds to a different combination of the original input variables, starting from the most strongly correlated feature (Level 1) to the complete feature set (Level 20). Levels do not introduce new models, weights, or data; they define which subset of features is used for model training.

Machine learning

This study’s most successful machine learning models included Gaussian process regression (GPR) with squared-exponential, rational-quadratic, and Matern52 kernels and a hybrid algorithm.

GPR model

Gaussian process regression (GPR) models are flexible, robust, and non-parametric. They are preferred in high-dimensional, small-sample, or non-linear datasets42. GPR works by learning non-linear mappings through kernel functions, constructing a covariance matrix between input features and real-valued outputs. In this way, it provides Gaussian uncertainty estimates for predictions, which enables integration into predictive control, adaptive control, and Bayesian filtering techniques43.

GPR is simpler compared to neural networks and support vector machines. It possesses non-parametric flexibility and may self-adjust its hyperparameters43. However, some recent approaches—such as the variance-adjusted gradient boosting algorithm—have enhanced the performance of GPR, making it comparable to or even superior to other regression methods like random forests and gradient boosting machines42.

GPR models are commonly used to fit data when the regression function is unknown44. For instance, they are widely utilized in system modeling, forecasting, control techniques, and industrial processes43,45,46. Additionally, GPR has been applied in diverse areas as estimating unknown functions, modeling and forecasting human mortality rates, and predicting the safety margin of a nuclear power plant46.

GPR models may not be effectively used with large datasets due to their high computational complexity. They scale cubically with the number of observations, restricting their use to small- and medium-sized datasets. Consequently, GPR models are powerful tools for probabilistic regression however, their applicability to large-scale data remains limited47.

GPR squared exponential (GPRS), GPR rational quadratic (GPRR), and GPR matern52 (GPRM) are different kernel functions used within the GPR framework. Each function has distinct characteristics that influence the behavior and performance of the GPR model.

To enhance model performance, hyperparameters of the Gaussian Process Regression (GPR) models (e.g., kernel length-scale and variance) were optimized using automated search strategies. These strategies included Bayesian optimization and randomized/grid search techniques, which iteratively adjust parameter values to minimize prediction error based on log-marginal likelihood. This process ensured that the selected kernel configurations provided the best trade-off between model accuracy and generalizability.

GPR squared exponential

GPR squared exponential (GPRS), also known as the Radial Basis Function (RBF), operates assuming the modeled function is smooth and continuous. Its flexibility and smoothness contribute to its frequent use. It is an effective method for capturing complex data relationships48.

GPR rational quadratic

It is a mixture of GPRS functions with different length scales. Compared to GPRS, it is more flexible and capable of producing models with varying degrees of smoothness3. This kernel is particularly used when the data exhibit different levels of variability45.

GPR matern52

It is smoother than the GPRS kernel. However, it behaves more flexibly when constructing rougher models. It provides a good balance between flexibility and smoothness. It is frequently preferred in cases where the underlying function is less smooth47.

Hybrid machine learning algorithm

It is a combination of different algorithms aimed at solving complex problems. By integrating the advantages of multiple algorithms, it seeks to create a more powerful solution method49,50. There are two distinct types of hybridization: collaborative and integrative. Collaborative hybridization approaches the problem by applying the algorithms sequentially or in parallel, allowing for information exchange. Integrative hybridization, on the other hand, focuses on different aspects of the problem-solving process for each algorithm51.

Hybrid algorithms enable more efficient and effective outcomes by combining various techniques. They improve exploration strategies and enhance solution quality52. They exhibit better performance than conventional algorithms, particularly in solving non-deterministic polynomial-time challenging optimization problems, thereby generating a synergistic effect53. These algorithms have been successfully applied to real-world problems52,53. In summary, hybrid algorithms combine the strengths of different approaches to offer more balanced and practical solutions, leading to successful implementations.

In the present study, the hybrid approach integrates three Gaussian Process Regression (GPR) models with different kernels (squared exponential, rational quadratic, and Matern52). Each model was trained independently, and their prediction outputs were combined by taking the simple arithmetic mean (unweighted average) to obtain the final estimate. No specific weights were applied to individual models. This averaging strategy corresponds to a collaborative hybridization, as parallel models contribute to a consensus result. The rationale for this design is that each kernel captures different structural characteristics of the data, and combining them reduces variance, balances individual weaknesses, and improves the overall robustness of the predictions.

Performance evaluation criterion

In machine learning models, performance evaluation criteria (PEC) are essential for selecting the appropriate model for a given task. Depending on the characteristics of the model and user expectations, various experiments may be designed to assess performance. Techniques such as cross-validation and bootstrapping may be used for performance evaluation in supervised and unsupervised models54. In addition, metrics like accuracy, precision, recall, F1 score, and area under the ROC curve are key indicators for assessing model performance55. In supervised learning, models are trained with input data and target variables. In unsupervised learning, only input data is used, and the algorithm attempts to identify patterns within the data56. In the current study, to evaluate the results, the following metrics were employed: Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE), Mean Absolute Deviation (MAD), Standard Error (SE), Mean Squared Error (MSE), Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), R, and R² (Eqs. 1–5).

MAPE provides a mapping procedure based on measurable criteria and allows for a visual presentation of the results57. It is used in research and development organizations to evaluate performance against specified targets58,59. MAPE is calculated by averaging the absolute percentage errors between predicted and actual values. It is a simple and easily interpretable method; however, it is sensitive to outliers and unsuitable for cases where exact values are zero or near zero60.

MAD is a method that estimates dispersion for continuous and finite-valued random variables without relying on absolute values61. It is especially appropriate when data are skewed and is easy to apply and interpret62.

MSE measures the average of the squared errors or deviations and provides detailed insights into model performance63–65. SE measures the accuracy of predictions and indicates the precision of the estimates66.

R² shows the proportion of variance in the dependent variable that the independent variables can explain. However, when used alone as a PEC, it may be insufficient, as it does not indicate which aspects of model performance are strong or weak67–69. Among the PECs used in this study, values of MAPE, MAD, SE, MSE, and RMSE are expected to be close to 0, while R and R² are expected to be close to 1.

In addition to the classical error-based metrics (MAPE, MAD, SE, MSE, RMSE, R, and R²), computational efficiency indicators were also considered: Prediction Speed (PS) expressed as observations predicted per second, Training Time (TT) measured in seconds required for model training, and Model Size (MS) measured in kilobytes. These parameters provide practical insights into the computational cost and feasibility of the models, which are critical for clinical and field applications.

For testing model performance, the dataset was split into 80% for training and 20% for testing. The training set was used to enable the models to learn, while the test set was reserved to measure the generalizability of performance. During the modeling process, different algorithms were applied, and gender-based models were utilized to enhance performance. Feature selection techniques were employed to achieve higher accuracy with fewer but more effective inputs.

k-fold cross-validation

To ensure the robustness and generalizability of the developed models, k-fold cross-validation was employed. This approach randomly partitioned the dataset into k equally sized folds. At each iteration, one fold was used as the validation set, while the remaining k-1 folds were used for training. This process was repeated k times so that each fold was used once for validation. The final performance metrics were obtained by averaging the results across all folds. This procedure reduces the risk of overfitting and provides a more reliable estimate of model performance, particularly when working with relatively small datasets. This study set k to 10, a commonly used choice in the literature for balancing computational efficiency and statistical reliability.

Principal component analysis (PCA)

Strong correlations among high-dimensional datasets may increase model complexity and reduce predictive accuracy. For this reason, dimensionality reduction methods are frequently employed in AI-based modeling studies to maintain model simplicity while enhancing generalizability. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is a statistical technique that represents correlated variables with fewer independent components, thereby eliminating redundancies in the data while preserving information content70,71.

This study applied PCA directly to the dataset and subsequently integrated into the AI-based modeling process. PCA was used independently as an alternative dimensionality reduction method, rather than in conjunction with Spearman-based feature selection.

Computational platform

All computational analyses were conducted using MATLAB R2024b (MathWorks Inc., Natick, MA, USA). Standard toolboxes such as the Statistics and Machine Learning Toolbox were utilized where available, while all remaining procedures—including feature selection implementation, hybrid model construction, and performance evaluation—were coded manually by the authors. This ensured flexibility in model development and allowed adaptation of the algorithms to the specific requirements of the study.

Discussion

The calculation of daily energy requirement is one of the fundamental assessments used in the field of nutrition and dietetics. Separate calculations are performed for individuals with special needs, such as athletes, laborers, and newborns. Therefore, various methods have been developed to ensure accurate estimations for each individual. Various direct and indirect methods are used to calculate daily energy requirements. However, these methods are generally expensive, time-consuming, and unsuitable for field use2,10. Therefore, various methods have been developed for field use, including practical formulas, accelerometers, heart rate monitors, Sensewear armbands, and smart devices designed to track energy expenditure and physical activity. However, although these methods and formulas are more convenient for field application, they tend to be less reliable6,15. Therefore, there is a growing need for new methods that are practical, highly reliable, and suitable for field use.

In the results section of this study, an artificial intelligence-based prediction model for estimating energy requirements is proposed for all individuals and by gender. In this section, the proposed models will be discussed about existing studies in the literature. In this study, an artificial intelligence-based model was developed to estimate individuals’ daily energy requirements based on anthropometric measurements and demographic data. The results indicate that the developed model successfully predicts energy requirements. The models developed specifically for males demonstrated exceptionally high performance (R = 1 and R²=1). Similarly, the models developed for females also exhibited high performance (R = 1 and R²=1). These results demonstrate that the developed model is a reliable and accurate tool for predicting energy requirements. Furthermore, these results are consistent with previous studies in literature. For example, Mifflin et al. (1990) developed an equation to estimate healthy individuals’ resting energy expenditure (REE). The equation developed by Mifflin et al. (1990) demonstrates a similarly high level of accuracy as the model proposed in this study18. However, in the study by Mifflin et al. (1990), the accuracy of the equation was tested only in healthy individuals. In contrast, the model developed in this study was validated in both healthy and obese individuals. The high accuracy observed in both groups suggests that the model may be applied to a broader population. In the literature, numerous studies have evaluated the accuracy of equations used to estimate energy requirements. For instance, Owen et al. (1987) re-assessed the energy needs of males and developed a new weight-based prediction equation21. This equation has a lower accuracy level than the equation developed by Mifflin et al. (1990). To provide another example, while the study by Batista et al. (2021) focused on evaluating the accuracy of existing equations, our study proposes a new artificial intelligence-based method27. These studies have demonstrated that the proposed formulas or methods effectively estimate Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR). When the results of the reviewed articles are examined in terms of the correlation coefficient (R value), the artificial intelligence-based models proposed in this study exhibit higher R values compared to the models presented in studies that suggest traditional equations. This result indicates that artificial intelligence-based models may offer a more successful approach in predicting energy expenditure. Beyond the numeric superiority, the conceptual advantages of AI-based models compared to traditional equations should also be emphasized. Conventional formulas, such as those of Mifflin or Harris-Benedict, rely on fixed parameters and assume linear relationships, which may not fully capture the complexity of human energy metabolism. In contrast, AI models can identify non-linear interactions between demographic and anthropometric variables, adapt to gender-specific differences, and dynamically select the most relevant features through feature selection methods. Moreover, they can be updated with new data, providing flexibility and adaptability that traditional equations cannot offer. Such flexibility has also been highlighted in other domains, such as smart city traffic prediction, where recurrent neural networks with GRU-based architectures enhanced performance in capturing complex spatiotemporal patterns72. Similarly, other biomedical applications have evidenced the effectiveness of AI-based approaches. For example, Khafaga et al. (2022) developed a deep convolutional neural network optimized with the Al-Biruni Earth radius algorithm for automated monkeypox disease classification, achieving superior diagnostic accuracy compared with conventional methods73. Such results highlight the broader potential of AI models in healthcare and reinforce our conclusion that AI-driven prediction models can outperform traditional approaches in reliability and applicability. These properties explain why AI-based models achieve higher performance metrics and conceptually outperform classical approaches. During the model development process, other advanced machine learning methods such as support vector machines and random forests were also tested; however, they did not achieve competitive results on the current dataset. For this reason, the study focused on the hybrid Gaussian Process Regression approach, which demonstrated consistently superior predictive performance. Furthermore, an analysis of feature importance highlighted that feature 30 (fluid mass min, kg), feature 31 (fluid mass max, kg), and feature 13 (maximum lean mass, kg) were consistently among the strongest predictors of daily energy requirements. These findings are physiologically meaningful, as fluid mass reflects the body’s hydration status and lean mass is a key determinant of basal metabolic rate. These features’ prominence reinforces the proposed models’ biological plausibility and provides additional confidence in their predictive validity. For clarity, the correspondence between feature numbers and variable names is provided in Table 2.

Recent studies have also explored large-scale deep learning models, such as graph neural networks, transformers, and attention-based architectures, for biomedical prediction tasks74,75. These models reported high accuracies and low error rates but at the cost of significant computational requirements, reliance on specialized hardware, and long training times, which can limit their feasibility in clinical or field applications. By contrast, the hybrid GPR-based approach presented in this study combines feature selection and gender-specific tailoring to deliver high predictive performance using substantially fewer computational resources. This efficiency makes the proposed model more accessible and practical, particularly in low-resource or field settings, while retaining strong accuracy.

On the other hand, Gerrior et al. (2006) present a more straightforward approach for estimating individuals’ daily energy requirements. This approach utilizes a spreadsheet template to calculate daily energy requirements based on the physical activity levels of individuals. The article explains using this template and outlines the key considerations when estimating energy requirements. By enabling the estimation of individual energy requirements, this approach makes achieving energy balance a more attainable goal7. Although a more straightforward method was proposed, the artificial intelligence-based approach in this study exhibited a higher correlation in terms of R values. Accordingly, AI models offer higher accuracy in predicting energy expenditure. The survey by Orozco-Ruiz et al. (2017) aimed to develop a new equation for estimating energy requirements in adults. In the study, the accuracy of the newly developed equation was compared with equations previously formulated for different populations. According to the results, the new equation had a higher accuracy level than the others24. The R value of the newly developed equation was 0.72. This value is lower than the R values achieved by the artificial intelligence-based models presented in this study. Artificial intelligence-based models exhibit higher R values than those in the survey by Orozco-Ruiz et al. (2017), suggesting that AI-based models may offer a more successful approach for estimating energy expenditure. Although the newly developed equation for patients with chronic kidney disease also demonstrates a high level of accuracy, the artificial intelligence-based models in this study yield more accurate results when compared to the R value26.

A perfect performance (R² = 1.00) was observed in several of the developed models. This outcome should be interpreted cautiously and can be attributed to several factors. First, the relatively homogeneous dataset, derived from a specific and limited population group, may have facilitated the models in capturing strong relationships between input features and energy requirements. Second, the selected features at the optimal levels (10 for males, 15 for females, and 11 for the combined group) were highly correlated with basal metabolic rate, enabling the models to identify nearly perfect associations. Finally, owing to its non-parametric and kernel-based structure, the Gaussian Process Regression (GPR) algorithm is highly flexible in learning linear and non-linear interactions. This may explain its ability to achieve an exact fit under such conditions. While these results indicate strong internal consistency, further external studies with larger and more heterogeneous datasets are recommended.

In this study, 10-fold cross-validation (k = 10) was also applied to strengthen the internal validity of the models. The cross-validation results demonstrated that the models consistently achieved outstanding performance at feature levels 10 for males, 15 for females, and 11 for the combined dataset (R = 1, R² = 1). In addition, the computational costs were evaluated, revealing that prediction speeds reached thousands of observations per second, training times remained within seconds to a few minutes, and model sizes were relatively small. These findings indicate that the proposed models are highly accurate, computationally efficient, and feasible for practical clinical and field applications.

In addition to predictive accuracy, the analysis of computational costs (Tables 6 and 7) provides further insights into the practicality of the proposed models. Specifically, prediction speeds reached hundreds of thousands of observations per second, training times remained within fractions of a second to a few minutes depending on model type, and model sizes were manageable, typically ranging from a few hundred kilobytes to a few megabytes. These characteristics confirm that the developed AI-based models are statistically robust, computationally efficient, and scalable for real-world applications.

In addition, a critical limitation of this study is the lack of an external validation dataset. Although 10-fold cross-validation was performed to enhance the robustness of the results and reduce the risk of overfitting, the absence of independent testing restricts the generalizability of the models. Therefore, future studies should incorporate external and more heterogeneous cohorts to confirm and extend the applicability of the proposed models. Furthermore, the computational efficiency of the proposed models is an essential consideration for real-world applications. In this study, the GPR-based models demonstrated sufficient efficiency to produce predictions within seconds for small- to medium-sized datasets, supporting their applicability in clinical and field settings. However, due to the cubic computational complexity of GPR, scalability challenges may arise when working with much larger and more heterogeneous datasets. Future work should therefore explore optimized or approximation-based approaches—such as sparse GPR to reduce kernel matrix complexity, lightweight neural approximations to retain non-linear mapping at lower cost, or model compression/distillation techniques to balance accuracy and efficiency—to reduce computational costs in large-scale applications.

In addition, the practical integration of AI-based models extends beyond technical performance. Ethical aspects, including protecting personal health data, algorithmic transparency, and the risk of bias across different populations, must also be carefully addressed. Beyond these issues, effective data governance frameworks are needed to ensure secure storage, controlled access, and accountability in handling sensitive health information. Moreover, the explainability of AI models is critical, as a lack of transparency may hinder clinical adoption and trust among practitioners. Finally, practical challenges such as software integration into existing healthcare systems and compliance with regulatory approval processes must be considered to support safe and reliable real-world deployment. Therefore, ethical and trustworthy implementation strategies will be essential for successfully integrating such models into mobile health applications and clinical decision support systems.

In this study, applying Principal Component Analysis (PCA) resulted in a noticeable decline in prediction performance (R = 0.48, R² = 0.23). The main reason for this reduction is that PCA is a linear transformation method that projects the original features into orthogonal components. While this approach successfully reduces dimensionality and multicollinearity, it may simultaneously eliminate or weaken non-linear interactions among the features, which are crucial for accurate energy requirement prediction. In particular, the initial principal components are dominated by features that explain the largest proportion of variance but not necessarily those with the strongest predictive relationship with the target variable. As a result, certain anthropometric and bioelectrical impedance variables that carried complex non-linear associations with basal metabolic rate might have been underrepresented or lost in the transformed feature space. This explains why the models trained with PCA-transformed features underperformed compared to those trained with the original selected features.

Recent evidence also suggests that metaheuristic optimization approaches can overcome such limitations of linear dimensionality reduction. For instance, El-Kenawy et al. (2022) introduced a hybrid optimization framework combining Grey Wolf Optimizer and Sine Cosine Algorithm to optimize feature selection and ensemble classifiers, achieving classification accuracies above 97% in high-dimensional image datasets. These findings highlight that optimization-driven feature selection strategies can enhance model robustness and predictive performance, particularly in complex biomedical applications76.

Similar conclusions have been reported in recent studies on optimization-based feature selection. For instance, Abdelhamid et al. proposed a hybrid Binary Sine Cosine Weighted Dipper Throated Optimization (bSCWDTO) algorithm that effectively balances exploration and exploitation to identify the most relevant subset of features, thereby enhancing classification accuracy and stability across high-dimensional datasets77. These findings support our observation that careful feature selection is crucial in achieving reliable predictive performance, particularly in health-related applications where data complexity and redundancy are common.

In addition to all these formulas, wearable devices are also used to estimate energy requirements. These are lightweight, portable, and non-invasive technological tools15. In contrast to these methods, the artificial intelligence-based model proposed in this study demonstrates a high level of accuracy, is also suitable for field use, and is cost-effective. For example, the Polar Grit X Pro is reliable in estimating energy expenditure. These findings are significant as they indicate the potential of wearable technologies to predict energy expenditure15. However, the use of wearable technologies has certain limitations. Wearable devices may be expensive and may not be accessible to everybody. Secondly, the accuracy of wearable devices may vary depending on the type of device and how it is used. Lastly, wearable devices may not be capable of fully measuring all components of energy expenditure (e.g., basal metabolic rate, physical activity, thermic effect of foods). The artificial intelligence-based model developed in this study offers certain advantages over wearable technologies. Firstly, the model is more cost-effective and more easily accessible. Secondly, its accuracy is higher compared to that of wearable devices. Finally, the model provides a more comprehensive estimation by considering all components of energy expenditure. In a similar study, Dougherty et al. (2022) demonstrated that accelerometers may be used to estimate energy expenditure; however, they emphasized that the accelerometer’s placement and participants’ demographic characteristics should be considered. Wrist-worn accelerometers demonstrated a stronger association with energy expenditure than chest-worn devices. These findings provide valuable insights into using accelerometers to estimate energy expenditure. Moreover, the study highlights the importance of considering accelerometer placement and participant demographic characteristics16. Artificial intelligence-based models have been demonstrated to possess a higher accuracy level than the approach presented by Dougherty et al.

Indirect calorimetry is the gold standard for estimating energy requirements2,11. However, measurement with indirect calorimetry is labor-intensive and requires trained technical staff. The calculation is performed by measuring the carbon dioxide exhaled and the oxygen consumed using a mask that covers the mouth and nose. This process requires the use of specialized, high-cost software2,11,12. However, specific protocols should be followed to conduct these measurements accurately. For instance, individuals should refrain from consuming caffeine for at least 4 h and avoid drinking alcohol and smoking for at least 2 h before the measurement12. The model proposed in this study may estimate energy requirements using simpler parameters than the gold standard. Therefore, the proposed model represents a more easily applicable method. In addition, artificial intelligence software enables energy requirement estimations to be performed at a lower cost. Moreover, the models proposed in this study do not require any food/beverage consumption-related restrictions. This feature allows for the convenience of performing measurements at any time of the day.

In addition to the gold standard of indirect calorimetry commonly used in the literature, several other methods, such as the double-labeled water technique and direct calorimetry, are also available2,10 Although the results obtained from all these methods have a high degree of accuracy, they also have certain disadvantages. For instance, direct calorimetry measures the heat lost within a controlled room environment10Therefore, this method is unsuitable for use in field settings. In contrast, the model used in this study may estimate energy requirements without requiring a specialized environment. On the other hand, the double-labeled water method estimates energy requirements through oral administration of isotopes. It is a complex and costly method9. Unlike the double-labeled water method, this study presents a more straightforward and cost-effective artificial intelligence model. In addition, there is no need for technical staff. Considering the features of the proposed model, it may also be suitable for clinical use. In the literature, numerous studies estimate energy requirements using the abovementioned methods. In the current study, artificial intelligence-based models have higher R values78. In the study conducted by Helsel et al. (2023) on individuals with Down syndrome, the model equations used have high R values. The IOM equation provides the most accurate estimation in adolescents with Down syndrome22. However, the correlation coefficients are observed to be higher in this study. In the study conducted by Plucker et al. (2018), a new model was developed using the double-labeled water method. Estimating energy requirements does not require knowledge of the PAL (Physical Activity Level) value, and therefore may lead to more accurate predictions. Plucker used the adjusted R² value in models predicting free-living energy requirements. The R² values were 0.65 for the formula including age, height, body weight, and gender, while models incorporating body composition and resting metabolic rate reached values as high as 0.7425. When R² values are compared, the artificial intelligence-based model may provide more accurate predictions, while the method developed using the double-labeled water technique may be more practical and applicable.

It is not device-based; energy requirements may be estimated using Food Consumption Records2,6. However, collecting food consumption records is labor-intensive, time-consuming, and based on self-reported recall, reducing reliability. Therefore, it is not considered as a preferable method2. As mentioned above, there are numerous different formulas in the literature to estimate energy requirements5,18,19. Due to the high variation among these formulas, studies proposing new formulas are still being conducted. As demonstrated in this study, the developed artificial intelligence-based model shows a notable improvement in the prediction errors of energy requirements compared to the formulas in the current literature. In other words, it demonstrates a higher level of accuracy in estimating energy requirements compared to other methods in the current literature. This is due to the high capability of artificial intelligence models to capture complex and non-linear relationships. Therefore, these models exhibit higher R values. Nevertheless, the performance of both the models used in this study and those found in the literature is acceptable in predicting energy expenditure.

As shown in the Results (Table 7), the AI-based models consistently outperformed the traditional literature-based formulas such as WHO, Harris-Benedict, and Mifflin, achieving R² values close to 1. This superior performance can be attributed to the ability of AI models to capture complex, non-linear relationships between demographic and anthropometric variables, unlike traditional equations that rely on fixed parameters and linear assumptions. However, a significant limitation is that both AI-based and literature-based models were evaluated on the same dataset, which may have inflated the observed differences in performance. Future studies should therefore include independent external datasets to provide a more robust and generalizable comparison.

In addition to methodological contributions, the proposed model demonstrates promising potential for practical applications. It can be integrated into clinical decision support systems or mobile health applications to provide rapid, cost-effective, and non-invasive estimations of daily energy requirements. Such an approach may assist dietitians in tailoring nutritional interventions and support public health monitoring by enabling large-scale assessment of energy balance. However, the generalizability of the current model should be interpreted with caution. Since the dataset was limited to a relatively homogeneous group, the model may not fully represent populations with different ethnic, clinical, lifestyle, geographic, or environmental characteristics. Therefore, further validation across larger and more diverse cohorts will be essential before widespread application.

Although there are many different methods/formulas in the literature, the advantages of this model are that it is non-invasive, practical, highly reliable, suitable for field use, and cost-effective. In conclusion, the artificial intelligence-based model developed in this study has a higher accuracy in estimating energy requirements than other methods in the literature, providing specific advantages. These advantages may be attributed to the ability of artificial intelligence models to analyze the variables used in estimating energy expenditure more effectively. Artificial intelligence models may process large amounts of data and identify complex relationships between variables. Therefore, AI models may provide more accurate results in predicting energy expenditure. This study, along with all the published articles in the current literature, provides valuable insights for developing and improving methods for future estimations of energy requirements. In future studies, the performance of these artificial intelligence models may be evaluated in more detail and tested across different populations and conditions worldwide. Furthermore, developing an artificial intelligence model using data collected through indirect calorimetry—the gold standard for estimating energy requirements—will enhance the model’s performance.

Conclusion

There are several factors underlying the success of the model we developed. First, the anthropometric measurements and demographic data used in the model are key factors influencing energy requirements. The accurate and comprehensive inclusion of these variables in the model has enhanced its performance. Secondly, the artificial intelligence algorithms used in the model effectively analyze the data and accurately estimate energy requirements. Finally, during the model’s development process, its performance was tested and optimized using different datasets. Thus, the model may be successfully applied across various populations and conditions.

Estimating energy requirements is used in many fields, particularly in dietary planning and counseling, healthcare, and for athletes. In this study, energy requirement estimation was performed using artificial intelligence based on BIA measurements and demographic data. The proposed models have several advantages over existing devices and models in the literature. These include: (1) energy requirements may be calculated by BIA measurements and demographic data, which are easy to obtain; (2) a highly accurate artificial intelligence-based system has been developed; (3) it is easy to use and does not require technical expertise/staff; (4) it does not require additional installation costs; and (5) the system saves time. Considering all these features, the proposed model is evaluated as more advanced, practical than the literature, and suitable for daily use.

In future research, it will be essential to validate the proposed models on larger and more diverse datasets, including individuals from different ethnic backgrounds, age groups, and clinical conditions. Such external validation will help assess the generalizability of the models and ensure their applicability in broader real-world healthcare and nutritional contexts. Moreover, integrating additional physiological or lifestyle-related variables could further enhance predictive accuracy and practical utility.

An additional strength of the present study is demonstrating that the proposed models achieve superior predictive accuracy compared to traditional equations and high computational efficiency. The models showed very short training times, rapid prediction speeds, and compact model sizes, indicating their suitability for integration into mobile health applications, clinical decision support systems, and large-scale population studies.
