Ice Nucleation Activity by Fusarium tricinctum Can Enhance Cold Stress: A Potential Contributor to Cold Injury in Apple Trees
Authors: Jin-Sil Choi, Aung Htay Naing, Diane Avalos-Ruiz, Jova Riza Campol, Seong-Keun Lim, In-Kyu Kang, Chang-Kil Kim, Seung-Yeol Lee, Hee-Young Jung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCold stress is a major abiotic factor that adversely affects apple trees and associated dwarf rootstocks, increasing the susceptibility of apple trees and dwarf rootstocks to secondary biotic infections. Among these biotic factors, certain Fusarium species exhibit ice nucleation activity (INA), which can exacerbate cold injury. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the effects of INA-positive Fusarium tricinctum KNUF-21-F29 and INA-negative F. solani KNUF-21-F27 on the growth of in vitro apple dwarf rootstocks (cultivars Malling 9 and Malling 26). Apple shoots treated with KNUF-21-F29 culture filtrates and exposed to low temperatures showed significantly reduced growth compared with those treated with KNUF-21-F27, suggesting that the INA of F. tricinctum contributes to growth inhibition. Gene expression analysis revealed that KNUF-21-F29 filtrates upregulated genes associated with ethylene biosynthesis, antioxidant activity, and cold response. Furthermore, Malling 9 rootstocks treated with KNUF-21-F29 filtrates for 3 months had lower survival rates than both potato dextrose broth (PDB)-treated and untreated controls. These findings demonstrate that fungal INA can negatively affect apple growth and represent a critical biotic factor in the development of cold injury.Keywords: Apple, cold stress, Fusarium tricinctum, ice nucleation activity, cold injury

Introduction

Apple (Malus domestica) is a widely cultivated crop of significant economic importance worldwide [1]. Apple trees are exposed to various abiotic and biotic that can severely affect growth and productivity [2]. Abiotic factors such as drought, heat, and cold stress can cause physiological disorders, including leaf chlorosis, necrosis, spotting, and overall tree decline [3]. Among these, cold stress is particularly critical because this factor directly affects tree survival, productivity, and vigor [4]. Specifically, exposure to freezing temperatures disrupts cellular membranes and organelles, potentially leading to plant death [3]. To survive winter conditions, apple trees enter dormancy, a physiological state that reduces metabolic activity and minimizes cellular damage [5]. As temperatures rise in spring, dormancy is released, and active growth resumes [6]. Subsequently, trees become vulnerable to low temperatures, which can cause lethal injury to the leaves and flowers [7]. Furthermore, recent climate change has intensified weather variability, with unexpected low temperatures occurring more frequently in apple orchards during spring [8,9]. Such temperature fluctuations can lead to excess production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in chloroplasts and disrupt coordination between the photosynthetic light reactions and the Calvin–Benson cycle [10].

Dwarfing rootstocks have been widely adopted in the apple industry owing to the ability of these rootstocks to support high-density planting systems and enhance apple productivity [11]. A diverse range of apple rootstocks has been used worldwide, including the Malling, Malling–Merton, Geneva, and Cornell–Geneva series [12]. In domestic apple orchards, Malling 9 (M.9) and Malling 26 (M.26) are the predominant dwarfing rootstocks [13,14]. However, Moran et al. [15] reported that M.9 and M.26 apple rootstocks are susceptible to cold damage, with M.9 particularly vulnerable at low temperatures. Apple trees subjected to cold stress are weakened, thereby increasing the susceptibility of these trees to secondary damage from biotic factors [16]. Biotic factors, including fungi, bacteria, and viruses, also directly affect the growth and quality of apple trees [17]. In Korea, apples are affected by a wide range of pathogens, including two viroids (such as apple scar skin viroid and apple hammerhead viroid), 11 viruses (such as apple mosaic virus and apple latent virus), four bacterial species (such as Pseudomonas syringae pv. syringae and Erwinia amylovora), two nematode species (such as Longidorus sylphus and Xiphinema radicicola), and 47 fungal species belonging to genera including Fusarium and Botryosphaeria [18]. Among these, multiple studies have implicated Fusarium species in apple decline [19–21]. Moreover, the genus Fusarium was the first fungal genus reported to exhibit ice nucleation activity (INA) [22], the capacity of external particles to induce ice formation at temperatures above the typical freezing point of ultra-pure water (approximately −38 °C) [23]. Ice-nucleating particles (INPs) are prevalent in the atmosphere and include various substances, such as minerals, dust, pollen, lichens, bacteria, viruses, and fungi [24,25]. Biological INPs, particularly bacteria and fungi, are highly effective at inducing ice formation at temperatures between −15 °C and 0 °C [22,26,27]. Furthermore, previous studies have demonstrated that biological INPs can induce frost injury in frost-sensitive plants at temperatures only a few degrees below freezing [28,29]. In Korea, INA has been reported in Fusarium species isolated from declining apple trees [30,31]. However, studies specifically investigating the role of Fusarium INA in apple tree decline remain limited. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the effects of the INA in Fusarium species isolated from declining apple trees on the growth of apple shoots under in vitro conditions, as well as the associated gene expression. Additionally, this study aimed to examine the potential influence of Fusarium INA on the growth of apple rootstocks under greenhouse conditions.

Materials and methods

Sample selection and preparation

In a previous study, the fungal strains Fusarium tricinctum KNUF-21-F29 and F. solani KNUF-21-F27, isolated from declining apple trees, were identified as INA-positive and INA-negative, respectively [31]. These strains were reassessed using a modified protocol based on the previous method, which included 1 mL of culture filtrate, a treatment temperature of −5 °C, and a treatment duration of 20 min [32]. The tube freezing assay results were consistent with those reported by Avalos-Ruiz et al. [31], supporting the selection of both strains for further testing. The two fungal strains were cultured in potato dextrose broth (PDB; Difco, Detroit, MI, USA) at 25 °C for 7 days in a shaking incubator at 150 rpm. To remove mycelial fragments, the liquid cultures were first filtered through sterile gauze, then through AVANTEC® 0.45 µm disposable membrane filters (Advantec, Tokyo, Japan). All filtration procedures were conducted on a clean bench to maintain sterile conditions. From each strain, 1 mL of filtrate was aliquoted into 1.7 mL tubes (Axygen Scientific Inc., Union City, CA, USA).

Evaluation of ice nucleation activity on the growth of apple shoots in vitro

Shoots from M.9 and M.26 apple rootstock cultivars were defoliated and pruned to lengths of 1.0–1.3 cm, each retaining a single terminal bud. Subsequently, these shoots were cultured on Linsmaier and Skoog (LS) medium (including vitamins) supplemented with sucrose, 6-benzyladenine (BA), and indole-3-butyric acid (IBA) (Duchefa Biochemie, Haarlem, Netherlands). The shoots were subcultured twice on the same medium at 4-week intervals. Following this, uniform, healthy shoots were selected to assess the effects of the fungal strains KNUF-21-F29 and KNUF-21-F27 on shoot growth. Uniform shoots were individually placed in 1.7 mL tubes containing filtrates of either KNUF-21-F29 or KNUF-21-F27. Half of the tubes were maintained at room temperature for 20, 40, or 60 min, while the other half were placed in a refrigerated circulator bath (JEIO Tech Co., Daejeon, Korea) at −5 °C for the same durations. Each treatment consisted of five tubes (one shoot per tube), with three replicates per treatment. At each treatment time point, the shoots were removed from the tubes, dried on sterile filter paper, and transferred to the same culture medium as previously described. All cultures were maintained at room temperature. Shoots that were not treated with either fungal filtrate served as control samples. Cultures were photographed on days 7, 14, 21, and 28 to monitor shoot growth. After 4 weeks, the percentages of green leaves, callus formation rates, and survival rates were determined for each treatment, as shown in the examples (Figure S1).

RNA extraction and real-time qPCR analysis

To assess the expression profiles of apple genes associated with cold-stress responses, target genes representing ethylene biosynthesis, antioxidant activity, and cold response were selected based on previous reports [33–35]. Leaf samples were collected immediately after shoots treated with either KNUF-21-F29 or KNUF-21-F27 were exposed to −5 °C for 30 min, and total RNA was extracted from the collected leaves. RNA extraction was performed using a Qiagen kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA) ­following the manufacturer’s instructions. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized by reverse transcription of RNA using an oligo(dT)20 primer and the Applied Biosystems High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), following the protocol described by Naing et al. [36]. Subsequently, the expression levels of 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylate synthase 1 (ACS1), 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylate oxidase 1 (ACO1), peroxidase (POD), superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and C-repeat binding factor 1 (CBF1) were measured using specific primers and PCR conditions (Table S1). Beta-actin 2 was used as the reference gene, and relative gene expression was calculated using the quantitative comparative cycle threshold method as described by Livak and Schmittgen [37]. Three independent biological replicates were used for each analysis, and each real-time qPCR reaction was performed in technical triplicate.

Growth assessment of apple rootstock in a greenhouse

A total of 12 apple plants with M.9 rootstock were each sprayed with 2 mL of KNUF-21-F29 filtrate. To determine whether PDB alone affects apple rootstock growth, an additional group received 2 mL of PDB, and a control group remained untreated. The treated rootstocks were placed in cold storage under low-temperature conditions. Cold treatment dates, times, and temperatures were established based on temperature data collected from 2020 to 2022 in the five principal apple-producing regions of Korea. From January to March, the treatment temperature was consistently maintained at −5 °C. Treatment duration and daily exposure time varied by month: in January, plants were treated for 20 days at 8 h per day; in February, for 14 days at 7 h per day; in March, for 3 days at 3 h per day. After the final cold treatment, plants were stored under greenhouse conditions at Kyungpook National University. After 6 weeks, plants were photographed, and survival rates were recorded.

Statistical analysis

Data normality was assessed using the D’Agostino–Pearson normality test in GraphPad Prism software. Subsequently, an ANOVA test was conducted to evaluate the significance of differences among treatments. The threshold for statistical significance was set at p = 0.05.

Results

Effect of ice nucleation activity on apple shoots

In vitro apple shoots were treated with culture filtrates from strains KNUF-21-F27 and KNUF-21-F29 at either room temperature or −5 °C for 20, 40, or 60 min, then transferred to the culture room. Untreated shoots were designated as the control group. After 4 weeks of culture, the percentages of green leaves, callus formation, and survival were evaluated. At room temperature, no significant differences were observed between KNUF-21-F27 and KNUF-21-F29 for any of the three parameters (Figure 1). Likewise, treatment duration had no significant effect, and the results for both strains were similar to those of the control. In contrast, significant differences were observed in apple shoots treated at −5 °C. For KNUF-21-F29, all three parameters were lower than those of both the control and KNUF-21-F27. For M.9 and M.26 treated at −5 °C for 20, 40, and 60 min, the percentages of green leaves were 80.1%, 67.1%, and 49.2%, and 84.0%, 38.2%, and 35.3%, respectively (Figure 2). Callus formation was not observed in either M.9 or M.26 cultivars when the treatment duration exceeded 40 min (Table 1). The survival rates of M.9 and M.26 under −5 °C treatments for 20, 40, and 60 min were 100%, 83.3%, and 45.5%, and 83.3%, 8.3%, and 0.0%, respectively. These results indicate that apple shoot growth was more adversely affected by KNUF-21-F29 treatment at −5 °C than by KNUF-21-F27 treatment.

Gene expression analysis of apple shoots exposed to Fusarium ice nucleation activity at low temperature

At −5 °C, apple shoots treated with KNUF-21-F29 exhibited significantly lower growth than those treated with KNUF-21-F27 (Figure 1). Therefore, gene expression levels related to plant growth were evaluated under freezing conditions in apple shoots treated with strains KNUF-21-F29 and KNUF-21-F27. For the ethylene biosynthesis genes (ACS1 and ACO1), ACS1 expression was higher in KNUF-21-F29-treated samples than in KNUF-21-F27-treated samples; however, this difference was not statistically significant (Figure 3(A)). Similarly, no ­significant difference was observed in ACO1 expression between the two treatments (Figure 3(B)). Expression levels of ROS-scavenging genes, including POD and SOD, also did not differ between treatments (Figure 3(C,D)). In contrast, CAT expression was significantly higher in KNUF-21-F29-treated samples than in those treated with KNUF-21-F27 (Figure 3(E)). Furthermore, expression of the cold-responsive gene CBF1 was markedly higher in KNUF-21-F29-treated samples than in KNUF-21-F27-treated samples, indicating a more pronounced cold stress response induced by the INA-positive strain (Figure 3(F)).

Effect of ice nucleation activity on apple rootstock growth

Six weeks after the final treatment with KNUF-21-F29 filtrate and PDB, the treated apple rootstocks were evaluated alongside a control group that received only low-temperature treatment (Figure 4). Among the 12 rootstocks in both the control and PDB-treated groups, only two died. However, in the KNUF-21-F29-treated group, 5 of the 12 rootstocks died. These findings indicate that, consistent with the results observed in apple tissue culture, neither KNUF-21-F29 filtrate treatment nor low temperature exposure has a beneficial effect on apple rootstock growth.

Discussion

Cold stress is one of the most important abiotic stresses, inducing a wide range of symptoms, such as seedling stunting, leaf curling and wilting, chlorosis, tissue damage, and necrosis [4]. In apple cultivation, cold stress poses a significant threat to tree survival and productivity, underscoring the importance of rootstocks for enhancing cold resistance [38]. In Korea, the M.9 and M.26 apple rootstocks are widely used, with M.9 being the most prevalent [39,40]. Malling rootstocks are generally characterized by limited cold hardiness or tolerance, and M.9 is particularly susceptible to cold stress compared with M.26 [41,42]. Additionally, a recent study suggested that certain Fusarium species possess INA, which may increase cold injury in plants [31]. Therefore, this study investigated the effects of cold stress exacerbated by fungal INA on M.9 and M.26 apple rootstocks under low-temperature conditions. Consequently, no significant differences were observed at room temperature; however, apple shoots treated with KNUF-21-F29, which exhibited INA, showed reduced growth at low temperatures. This reduction in growth may be attributed to ice-nucleation-active fungi increasing cold stress in apple shoots at low temperatures. Moreover, the M.9 rootstock exhibited significantly greater susceptibility to damage by ice-nucleation-active fungi than M.26, consistent with a previous study reporting that M.9 is more vulnerable to cold stress than M.26 [42], thereby indicating increased risk for apple orchards. Similarly, apple rootstocks treated with KNUF-21-F29 exhibited lower growth than those treated with PDB or left untreated. Extensive research has been conducted on cold stress damage in various plants, including citrus, tomato, and wheat [43–45]. To our knowledge, this study provides the first experimental evidence that ice-nucleation-active Fusarium can intensify cold stress and contribute to damage in apple shoots and rootstocks under controlled conditions. These findings may provide a basis for further research on the role and characteristics of such fungi under cold stress conditions in apple trees. The presence of ice-nucleation-active fungi may elevate freezing temperatures, potentially causing adverse effects on apple growth at temperatures above those typically associated with cold damage. Most current research on ice-nucleation-active fungi has focused on the associated climatic and ecological roles [27,46–48]. In contrast, this study evaluated the associated physiological effects on apple shoots and rootstocks under low-temperature conditions.

Cold stress can lead to increased production of stress-induced ethylene [49]. This occurs through ethylene biosynthesis, which is regulated by the enzymes 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylic acid synthase (ACS) and 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylic acid oxidase (ACO) [50]. Under low-temperature conditions, ACS expression is upregulated, leading to the accumulation of 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylic acid (ACC), which is subsequently converted into ethylene by ACO, thereby completing the biosynthetic pathway activated in response to cold stress [50]. A recent study demonstrated that the expression of ethylene signaling pathway-related genes, such as MdACS1 and MdACO1, is elevated in apple seedlings under cold stress conditions [33]. Similarly, cold stress has been shown to increase ACC content, an indicator of ACS activity, and ACO activity in grapevines [51]. In this study, the expression of ACS1 and ACO1 was also upregulated in response to cold treatment.

We investigated the expression of ROS-scavenging enzymes, including POD, SOD, and CAT, to evaluate the effects of ice-nucleation-active fungi on apple shoots under low-temperature conditions. Low temperature is known to induce the accumulation of ROS, including hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and superoxide (O2−), which can cause oxidative damage to lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids, thereby compromising cell membrane integrity [52,53]. Excessive ROS production under low-temperature conditions activates antioxidant defense systems, particularly ROS-scavenging enzymes [54]. Niu et al. [53] reported increased ROS levels and elevated activities of SOD, POD, and CAT in bitter gourd seedlings under cold stress conditions. Similarly, Xu et al. [35] observed increased levels of ROS, including O2− and H2O2, in apples exposed to low temperatures. In this study, the expression levels of POD, SOD, and CAT also increased in response to low-temperature treatment, consistent with previous findings. In addition to the associated damaging effects, ROS function as signaling molecules in cold stress responses [55]. Under low-temperature conditions, ROS and abscisic acid accumulate in plant tissues, where they facilitate stomatal closure to minimize water loss and induce the expression of cold-responsive genes [56,57]. Cold perception begins at the plant cell membrane, where cold receptors detect temperature drops and trigger a calcium (Ca2+) influx [58]. This activates a cascade of protein kinase-mediated phosphorylation events, ultimately inducing the ICE1–CBF–COR signaling pathway, which is recognized as one of the most crucial cold stress response pathways in plants [58]. A study demonstrates that CBF genes are significantly induced by low temperatures [59]. Similarly, our data indicate that the expression of the cold-responsive gene CBF1 is elevated upon exposure to low temperatures. Indeed, this study revealed that the expression levels of CAT and CBF1 were significantly higher in apple shoots treated with KNUF-21-F29 than in those treated with KNUF-21-F27. This indicates that apple shoots treated with KNUF-21-F29 may have experienced greater cold stress than those treated with KNUF-21-F27. These results support the hypothesis that ice-nucleation-active fungi exacerbate cold-induced physiological stress and adversely affect apple growth under low-temperature conditions. These gene expression results further support the physiological observations by showing that exposure to the INA-positive F. tricinctum strain was associated with stronger activation of cold-stress-related transcriptional responses in apple shoots. However, this study was limited to the comparison of one INA-positive F. tricinctum strain and one INA-negative F. solani strain and did not evaluate practical control strategies for reducing INA-mediated cold injury. Therefore, future studies should evaluate INA-mediated effects across a broader range of Fusarium species and strains, assess cultivar-specific and whole-plant responses in apple, and develop integrated management strategies to mitigate cold injury associated with ice-nucleation-active fungi.

Recently, a global phenomenon known as rapid apple decline (RAD) has been observed, causing significant economic losses in apple orchards [16]. RAD is characterized by the rapid or sudden collapse of apple trees, resulting in tree death shortly after symptom onset [60]. This phenomenon primarily affects young apple trees aged 2–8 years, particularly those grafted onto M.9 dwarf rootstocks [60]. Various studies suggest that both biotic and abiotic factors, including fungi, viruses, insects, and weather-related stressors such as drought and extreme cold, contribute to RAD; however, the underlying causes remain unclear [61,62]. Previous studies indicate a correlation between RAD and fungal pathogens, particularly Fusarium species, which have been isolated from RAD-affected trees in Korea [31]. F. tricinctum has been identified as the causal agent of a recently reported apple disease in China [63], and ongoing research continues to demonstrate the pathogenic effects of Fusarium on apple crops. Several Fusarium species isolated from RAD trees exhibit INA, and one specific strain with INA was shown to affect Chrysanthemum morifolium cv. Shinma under low-temperature conditions [31]. However, the relationship between Fusarium INA and the associated effects on apple crops has not been extensively investigated. Therefore, this study offers foundational insights that may guide future research on the role of fungal INA in cold injury to apple trees.

Climate change has become a significant concern, characterized by rising mean temperatures and persistent episodes of extreme cold [45]. In Korea, winter temperatures are increasing, along with a higher frequency of extreme weather events such as cold waves [64]. Apple trees withstand adverse winter conditions by entering dormancy; however, climate change is disrupting the release of dormancy, resulting in earlier flowering [65,66]. In Korea, apple tree dormancy typically breaks between January and early February [67]. After dormancy, apple trees become susceptible to spring frosts, which can damage or even kill flower buds at temperatures just below freezing [68]. Spring frosts often occur in Korea in March and April, and the severity of associated damage is projected to increase over time [69]. If apple trees break dormancy early due to elevated temperatures and subsequently experience a cold wave, significant damage may occur. Furthermore, the presence of ice-nucleation-active fungi during these periods can induce cold injury at higher temperatures, potentially contributing to apple decline. A previous study suggested that fungal pathogens such as Botryosphaeria spp., Diaporthe eres, and Cytospora mali may be associated with RAD [16]. Apple trees weakened by cold injury may be more susceptible to infection by these fungi, leading to secondary damage.

As climate change progresses, the combined effects of cold stress and disease pressure on plants are becoming increasingly evident. This study confirms that ice-nucleation-active fungi can exacerbate cold stress, highlighting the need for further research into practical strategies to mitigate these effects. Future work should examine whether cultural, chemical, or biological approaches can reduce the impact of INA-positive fungi under low-temperature conditions. Additionally, further investigations are required to determine whether cold-injured apple trees become more susceptible to secondary pathogens and clarify the association between fungal INA and RAD.
