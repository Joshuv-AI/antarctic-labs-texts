Effect of sugammadex titration versus manufacturer’s recommendation for reversal of rocuronium-induced neuromuscular block: a prospective, randomized, controlled trial
Authors: Lingqi Gao, Bing Li, Jingjing Shen, Yuran Zhao, Jiazheng Qi, Tingting Gu, Wenxiang Bao, Yue Hu, Zhoujing Yang, Bo Xu, Zhao Zhang, Bingyan Zhang, Mengqiang Luo, Qiong Yu, Yingwei Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBoth under-dosing and over-dosing of sugammadex for reversing neuromuscular block pose risks to patients, yet the optimal dosage for different degrees of neuromuscular block remains undefined. This study compared titration with single bolus of sugammadex recommended by manufacturer to determine the optimal dose for achieving a train-of-four ratio (TOFR) of 0.9 or greater while minimizing residual paralysis.MethodsPatients were randomly assigned to titrated group or manufacturer-recommended group. In titrated group, sugammadex was given in 50-mg increments every 5 min until a TOFR of 0.9 or greater was reached. In recommended group, a single dose (2 mg/kg or 4 mg/kg) was given based on train-of-four count (TOFC). Neuromuscular block was monitored continuously, and residual paralysis was assessed in the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU).ResultsOf 210 enrolled patients, 205 were evaluated. The median dose of sugammadex for reversing different depths of neuromuscular block induced by rocuronium was ascertained via the titration method in this study. Titrated group required significantly less sugammadex to achieve a TOFR of at least 0.9 compared to recommended group (P < 0.001). Residual paralysis incidence was similar between groups (5.4% in titrated group vs. 4.9% in recommended group; P = 0.564). Although recovery time was longer in titrated group, extubation time and length of stay in the PACU were comparable.ConclusionsTitrated sugammadex administration significantly reduces required dosage without increasing residual paralysis risk. Continuous neuromuscular monitoring postoperatively is strongly recommended due to individual variability in dosage needs.Trial registrationThe study was registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry on March 4, 2024(ChiCTR2400081540).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-025-03398-z.

Introduction

Sugammadex, a modified γ-cyclodextrin compound, has proven to be a highly effective reversal agent for neuromuscular block at different depths. It encapsulates amino-steroidal agents, such as rocuronium and vecuronium, in a 1:1 ratio, and its use has been associated with a reduced incidence of postoperative pulmonary complications [1–3]. The manufacturer’s guidelines recommend administering a single intravenous bolus of sugammadex, with the dose determined based on the train-of-four count (TOFC). Specifically, a dose of 2 mg/kg is recommended when TOFC is 2 or higher, and 4 mg/kg when TOFC is less than 2, provided there is at least one post-tetanic count (PTC) (https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2015/022225lbl.pdf). However, increasing evidence suggests that the labelled dose of sugammadex may not be necessary for all patients [4, 5]. For instance, while TOFC values of 2 and 4 both suggest a sugammadex dose of 2 mg/kg, research has demonstrated that TOFC 4 can be rapidly and effectively reversed to a train-of-four ratio (TOFR) of 0.9 with a reduced dose of just 1 mg/kg [6]. Furthermore, Olivier et al.‘s study indicated that despite the recommended doses of 2 mg/kg or 4 mg/kg for TOFC 2 or 1, respectively, there was no significant difference in the time required for full recovery with the same dosage [7]. These observations raise questions about the precision of the manufacturer’s dosing recommendations.

Previous studies have explored the dose-dependent relationships of sugammadex concerning recovery times to a TOFR of 0.9. Many findings suggest that although a lower dose may still lead to full recovery, it often results in a longer recovery time [8, 9]. Insufficient sugammadex dosing can cause adverse respiratory events due to residual neuromuscular block. Conversely, excessive dosing not only escalates costs but may also be related to the following side effects, including coagulation events, laryngospasm, and severe bradycardia [10–13]. Moreover, significant inter-individual variability exists regarding patient sensitivity to sugammadex [14]. All the above indicate that a single-dose injection may not provide a comprehensive solution for ensuring complete and reliable neuromuscular reversal.

Recent observational research has shown that titrated sugammadex administration, paired with quantitative neuromuscular monitoring, effectively reduces the total amount of sugammadex used without increasing the incidence of residual paralysis [15]. In this study, 97 patients were given small incremental doses of sugammadex (50 mg), repeated every 5 min until a TOFR of 0.9 or higher was achieved. Subsequently, postoperative monitoring of the TOFR was carried out using electromyography until sedation was discontinued before extubation or for a maximum of 7 h in the intensive care unit. However, the study primarily focused on patients undergoing cardiac surgery, who often remain sedated and intubated for extended periods postoperatively, making the results less applicable to non-cardiac surgeries, where extubation generally occurs within an hour after surgery.

In this prospective, randomized controlled trial, we aimed to further evaluate the efficacy of sugammadex titration, combined with quantitative neuromuscular monitoring, in achieving rapid and complete neuromuscular block reversal in patients undergoing non-cardiac surgeries.

Materials and methods

This randomized, placebo-controlled, safety assessor-blinded, parallel group study was designed to evaluate the effects of sugammadex titration compared with the manufacturer’s recommended single bolus administration during the reversal of rocuronium-induced neuromuscular block. The study received approval from the institutional ethics committee (No.2023822, Huashan Hospital, Fudan University, Shanghai, China) and was registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry on March 4, 2024(ChiCTR2400081540). All participants provided written informed consent after receiving a full explanation of the study’s purpose and potential risks. Patient recruitment commenced on March 11, 2024, and was completed by June 30, 2024. The study included patients aged 18 to 65 with a body mass index (BMI) below 30 kg/m², and classified as American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I–II, undergoing general anesthesia for surgery. Exclusion criteria included refusal to sign the consent form, known allergies to any drugs used in the protocol, medications that could interact with muscle relaxants, kidney or liver dysfunction, pregnancy, or breastfeeding. Additionally, patients who had participated in another clinical trial within the last three months were excluded. Each patient received a unique identification number based on their enrolment sequence. Randomization was performed using a random number table generated with Microsoft Excel 2010 (Microsoft, USA). The work has been reported in line with the CONSORT criteria [16].

Anesthesia protocol

Both groups followed standardized general anesthesia protocols. In the operating room, an intravenous cannula was inserted into a forearm vein to administer lactated Ringer’s solution and other necessary drugs. Continuous monitoring of vital signs, including electrocardiography, non-invasive arterial pressure, and pulse oximetry, was maintained throughout the procedure. General anesthesia was induced with intravenous propofol (2 mg/kg) and fentanyl (4 µg/kg) while patients received 100% oxygen via an anesthesia facemask. Neuromuscular monitoring began before the administration of rocuronium. A stable baseline of the train-of-four (TOF) response was recorded for at least 2 min prior to rocuronium administration. Patients with a TOFR ≥ 0.9 were included in the study. Rocuronium (0.9 mg/kg) was given intravenously following the loss of consciousness, with intubation performed when TOFC reached 0. This dose was selected as some included participants underwent gastrointestinal surgeries (carrying high risk of reflux aspiration), and to standardize induction agents, thereby minimizing confounding factors. Anesthesia was maintained with 2–3% sevoflurane, while the depth of anesthesia was monitored using the Bispectral Index (BIS, target range: 40–60). Rocuronium (0.2–0.3 mg/kg) and fentanyl (50–100 µg) were given hourly or as needed, based on TOF stimulation showing > 2 twitches, to maintain a target of 1–2 twitches for adequate surgical relaxation. Oxygen saturation was kept at or above 98%, and normocapnia was maintained by adjusting ventilator settings as necessary. Fluid infusion and vasopressor administration were employed to maintain hemodynamic stability.

Neuromuscular monitoring

Neuromuscular monitoring was performed using an acceleromyograph (TOF-Watch SX™; Organon Ltd, Ireland) [17, 18]. Prior to anesthesia induction, an acceleration sensor was attached to the thumb to assess adductor pollicis muscle contractions in response to supramaximal electrical stimulation of the ulnar nerve using a hand adapter. Data were continuously collected and monitored via computer throughout the study. A TOFR measurement obtained after the patient lost consciousness was considered the baseline. Prior to each measurement, the TOF-Watch SX was calibrated per its operation manual: when the patient was fully anesthetized, the calibration button was pressed to activate the device’s automatic calibration function, with the value set to 100% of the control twitch height. TOF stimulation was repeated at 15-second intervals throughout the procedure, except when the PTC mode was used to assess deep neuromuscular block.

Study protocol

Following surgery, the TOF response was recorded, and patients received sugammadex according to one of two protocols. In the titration group, a 50 mg bolus of sugammadex was given, followed by repeated doses of 50 mg every 5 min until a TOFR of at least 0.9 was achieved. In the recommended group, sugammadex was given in a bolus dose based on body weight: 4 mg/kg for TOFC < 2 with PTC ≥ 1, and 2 mg/kg for TOFC ≥ 2, additional dose of 50 mg was given if TOFR was less than 0.9 after 5 min. Bradycardia was defined as a heart rate of less than 50 beats per minute according to previous study [19]. Postoperative target TOFR values were normalized to baseline if the initial TOFR was greater than 100% (i.e., if baseline TOFR = 105%, then postoperative target TOFR = 0.9 × 105%) [20]. Inhalation anesthesia was maintained until the patient left the operating room.

When the TOFR reached ≥ 0.9, the patients were transferred to the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) while the endotracheal tube still in situ. Quantitative monitoring was continued in the PACU every 5 min before tracheal extubation. Extubation decisions were made by an anesthesiologist who was blinded to the group allocations. Patients remained in the PACU for at least 60 min under close observation for signs of muscle weakness or critical respiratory or circulatory events. If extubation criteria were not met within 3 h post-surgery, the endotracheal tube was left in place, and the patient was transferred to the intensive care unit.

Definitions of primary and secondary variables

The primary endpoint was the dose of sugammadex required to achieve a TOFR of 0.9 or greater. The secondary outcome was the incidence of residual paralysis in the PACU. In addition to a TOFR < 0.9 for three consecutive TOF measurements before extubation, symptoms and signs of muscle weakness (Supplementary data) were also used to define residual paralysis [21]. Other secondary endpoints included time interval between sugammadex administration and achieving a TOFR of 0.9 or greater, time to extubation after admission to PACU, and the length of stay in PACU. All data were extracted from the anesthesia information management system (Do Care 5.0, Medical system, China) by an investigator who was blinded to the group assignments.

Sample size calculation

The sample size calculation was based on previous studies examining sugammadex titration [15]. A two-sided type I error of 0.05 and a power of 80% were assumed, indicating that 84 patients per group were required to detect a significant difference, defined as a 20% reduction in sugammadex dose in titrated group compared to recommended group. To account for an anticipated 20% dropout rate, 105 patients were enrolled in each group, resulting in a total of 210 participants.

Statistical analyses

Data were summarized as mean (standard deviation, SD), median (interquartile range), or number of patients (percentage), as appropriate. The normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. The Fisher exact test or Pearson χ² test was employed for categorical outcomes, and the Student’s t-test was used for continuous variables. For non-parametric data, the Mann–Whitney rank-sum test was applied for between-group comparisons, while the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used for within-group variable changes. Time-to-event analysis was performed using the log-rank test and Kaplan–Meier curves. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (IBM Corporation, Chicago, USA), and a p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Subject characteristics

A total of 240 patients were screened for eligibility, and 210 met the inclusion criteria and were enrolled in the study. Five patients were excluded after allocation. Four experienced spontaneous recovery, achieving a TOFR of at least 0.9 by the end of surgery, and one patient received an additional dose of sugammadex after reversing to a TOFR of 0.9 or greater. Ultimately, 205 patients (102 in recommended group and 103 in titrated group) completed the study and were included in the primary analysis. Discussion

In this study, we determined the median dose of sugammadex for reversal of different depth of neuromuscular block induced by rocuronium through titration method. Our findings highlight the economic and safety benefits of sugammadex titration that it significantly reduced the required dosage without increasing the risk of residual paralysis compared to the manufacturer-recommended single-dose administration.

Sugammadex is still expensive in many countries, although its patent has expired. The higher cost of sugammadex compared to neostigmine has prompted clinicians to explore ways to reduce sugammadex dosage [22–24]. However, most dose-finding studies for sugammadex in neuromuscular block reversal have used fixed dosages (e.g., 0.5, 1, or 2 mg/kg) [25, 26]. These studies, however, were unable to establish the optimal sugammadex dose for different depths of neuromuscular block. Our study demonstrates that titrated sugammadex administration can result in a reduced dosage compared to the recommended amount for both moderate and deep neuromuscular block. This observation aligns with findings by Bowdle et al. [15]. More importantly, this titration method enables a more precise determination of the appropriate sugammadex dose within a relatively short period.

There have been increasing reports of neuromuscular block recurrence following sugammadex administration. This phenomenon is primarily attributed to the redistribution of unbound muscle relaxants from peripheral to central compartments, and effect-site compartments [10, 27]. Sugammadex’s encapsulation capacity is relatively short-lived due to pharmacokinetic imbalance with muscle relaxants (e.g., rocuronium): Sugammadex is rapidly eliminated via kidneys, lowering its circulating levels quickly. In contrast, rocuronium undergoes two-step elimination—first dissociating from the neuromuscular junction, then hepatic metabolism—leading to its prolonged persistence in the bloodstream. Though sugammadex binds rocuronium tightly to form stable complexes, the declining free sugammadex can no longer bind newly available, persistently circulating rocuronium. This inability to form additional stable complexes ultimately reduces sugammadex’s encapsulation capacity rapidly [28, 29]. Consequently, using a single-dose of sugammadex carries the risk of under-dosing, which may lead to the reappearance of neuromuscular block after an initial recovery. In our study, eight patients in recommended group required an additional dose 5 min after the initial administration. Furthermore, seven patients in titrated group required a higher dose than the manufacturer’s recommended amount. This highlights the patient safety issue where in the required dose for an individual cannot be accurately determined based solely on the TOF response immediately before reversal. These findings further emphasize the importance of continuous quantitative neuromuscular monitoring following sugammadex administration [30–32]. It is important to note that some patients exhibit obvious muscle weakness even when their TOFR exceeds 0.9 after tracheal extubation, and such cases are therefore classified as residual paralysis. Plausible explanations for this phenomenon include calibration errors, technical limitations of acceleromyography, and inter-individual variability in neuromuscular recovery [18].

Recovery time in titrated group was longer than in recommended group, which is expected given that sugammadex was titrated in 50 mg increments every 5 min. Administering smaller doses over multiple intervals takes longer than administering a single bolus dose. However, there was no difference in extubation time and length of stay in the PACU between the two groups, indicating that the titration protocol did not affect the overall operation time or PACU turnover. In clinical practice, sugammadex has facilitated a more rapid recovery of muscle function compared to the restoration of consciousness. Additionally, we must remain vigilant about the potential for the reappearance of muscle activity during the reversal of rocuronium-induced neuromuscular block to artifactually elevate BIS values, this is particularly relevant when BIS monitoring is relied upon to assess the recovery from propofol/remifentanil anesthesia [33]. Consequently, it would be inappropriate to regard the recovery time of muscle function alone as the primary endpoint in clinical practice, especially when consciousness has not yet been fully recovered [34].

In theory, a shallower neuromuscular block would require a lower sugammadex dose. While shallow neuromuscular block (TOFC = 4) is relatively common, specific dosage recommendations for this scenario remain unclear [35, 36]. Our study revealed considerable variability in patient response to sugammadex, with doses required to achieve a TOFR of at least 0.9 ranging from 0.5 mg/kg to 4.6 mg/kg. The cause of variability in patient sensitivity to sugammadex, similar to nondepolarizing blockers, remains unclear and is believed to be associated with the individual’s neuromuscular junction density [37].

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted at a single center, and some practices of muscle relaxant management specific to this department may have influenced the results. Second, the use of a variable dosing regimen (0.2–0.3 mg/kg, hourly or as needed) for rocuronium to maintain neuromuscular block (instead of a fixed-dose protocol), which may have introduced modest heterogeneity into the results. Third, the 5-minute interval between sugammadex doses may not reflect real-world clinical practice, where anesthesiologists may be unwilling to wait this long before administering additional sugammadex if reversal is not achieved promptly. Finally, it should be noted that the exclusion of elderly patients and those with renal or hepatic impairment may limit the generalizability of our findings.

In conclusion, our study suggests that sugammadex may be given in smaller, repeated doses when combined with quantitative monitoring to ensure appropriate sugammadex dosing while minimizing the total dose required. With current availability of generic sugammadex that has significantly alleviated cost pressures, We acknowledge that the need for cost reduction through the strategy of reducing drug dosage has become less urgent. Titration may be more practical in low-throughput or specialized settings (e.g., patients at high risk of drug-related adverse events such as elderly individuals or those with renal/hepatic impairment). To validate these findings, future studies should be conducted across multiple centers and include diverse participant populations.
