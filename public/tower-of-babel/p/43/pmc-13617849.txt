Serum carotenoids modify the association between porphyromonas gingivalis IgG and mortality: a prospective cohort study with 25.8 years of follow-up
Authors: Weihong Qiu, Lijun Zheng, Yingying Yu, Zhiquan Zhang, Zhenzhen Li, Zihan Zhang, Heng He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPorphyromonas gingivalis (P. gingivalis) is a vital pathogen that can increase mortality. However, whether serum carotenoids, commonly known antioxidants, modify the association of serum P. gingivalis immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibody levels with mortality remains uncertain. We aimed to explore the modification of this association by serum carotenoids.MethodsThe associations of serum P. gingivalis IgG levels with mortality among 7,043 participants from the Third U.S. National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey were assessed. The modifications of serum carotenoids on the associations were also examined. We used the recommended sampling weights to account for the complex survey design. Surveycox proportional hazards regression models were used to calculate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for all-cause and specific-cause mortality.ResultsWe identified 2,646 deaths during a median follow-up period of 25.8 years. Compared with participants with low serum P. gingivalis IgG levels (< 69 EU), those with high levels (≥ 69 EU) were associated with an HR (95% CI) of 1.10 (1.04–1.16) for all-cause mortality. Similar results were found in mortality analyses for cardiovascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, and diabetes mellitus (all P < 0.05). Notably, significant interactions were detected between the serum α-carotene, β-carotene, or lutein/zeaxanthin concentrations and the serum P. gingivalis IgG levles on all-cause mortality (all Pint < 0.05). Compared with high P. gingivalis IgG participants with low carotenoids, low P. gingivalis IgG participants with high carotenoids had the lowest risk of all-cause mortality, with HR of 0.74 (0.68–0.80) for α-carotene, 0.78 (0.72–0.86) for β-carotene, 0.70 (0.64–0.76) for β-cryptoxanthin, 0.79 (0.73–0.85) for lutein/zeaxanthin and 0.64 (0.59–0.69) for lycopene, respectively.ConclusionSerum carotenoids mitigate the associations between serum P. gingivalis IgG levels and mortality.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40795-026-01420-2.

Introduction

Diverse and abundant microbial communities are present in the human oral cavity, and play a pivotal role in maintaining the delicate balance of the local oral environment. When the homeostasis of these microbial communities is disrupted, a wide array of pathological reactions can be triggered, ranging from periodontal diseases to cardiovascular and metabolic ailments, neurologic disorders, gastrointestinal issues, and even cancers [1]. Among the numerous microbiota, Porphyromonas gingivalis (P. gingivalis) stands out as a particularly troublesome and widely recognized gram-negative microbe [2]. This microorganism is one of the most severe pathogens associated with periodontal infections, posing a significant threat to oral health [3].

In most individuals afflicted with oral diseases, there is a marked elevation in serum immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies directed against P. gingivalis (P. gingivalis IgG) [4]. Serum P. gingivalis IgG titers persist at elevated levels for up to 15 years [5], which is indicative of both past and ongoing periodontal infections. Consequently, P. gingivalis IgG serves as a cumulative measure of periodontal infection over time [6]. P. gingivalis IgG has been linked to various health outcomes, including all-cause mortality [7], cardiovascular diseases (CVD) [8–10], rheumatoid arthritis [11], chronic kidney disease [12], cognitive impairment [13, 14], and various types of cancers [15, 16]. In previous research conducted by Damian et al., a nonlinear correlation was identified between serum P. gingivalis IgG and mortality via the data from the Third US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES III), which followed up to 2006 [7]. We further utilized NHANES III data followed up to 2019 to explore the associations between P. gingivalis and all-cause and specific-cause mortality.

Carotenoids are a widely recognized class of antioxidants and immunomodulatory compounds, with more than 700 unique varieties identified to date. Among them, α-carotene, β-carotene, β-cryptoxanthin, lycopene, lutein, and zeaxanthin account for more than 95% of the total carotenoids [17, 18]. Researches have shown that carotenoids can effectively prevent diseases such as periodontitis, indicating their potential to regulate the oral microbiota, including inflammation caused by P. gingivalis [19, 20]. Given the strong correlation between periodontitis and mortality, coupled with the antioxidant and anti-inflammatory functions of carotenoids, we hypothesize that serum carotenoids may modify the association between serum antibodies against P. gingivalis and all-cause and specific-cause mortality.

Therefore, in this study, we utilized data from NHANES III to further evaluate the associations between serum P. gingivalis IgG and mortality, with follow-up extending until 2019. Additionally, we examined the modified associations of carotenoids on these associations, providing valuable insights and evidence for the prevention of death linked to the oral microbiota.

Methods

Study population

The study participants were from NHANES III, which was described previously [21]. In short, NHANES III was conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) between 1988 and 1994 in two phases in which the civilian noninstitutionalized U.S. population was sampled to provide a multidimensional overview of physical health status and aspects of functional health status. After excluding participants with missing information on serum carotenoids, serum P. gingivalis IgG, mortality status, and those who were pregnant or already had cancer at the baseline, a total of 7,043 participants ≥ 20 years of age from NHANES III were included in this study (Figure S1). Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

Covariate collection

Each participant’s baseline demographic characteristics, disease and medication history, and lifestyle were collected via face-to-face questionnaires, including sex (male or female), age groups (20–39,40–59, ≥ 60 years), race-ethnicity (non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, Mexican American, other), education (less than junior high school, junior high school, high school, college or above), annual total income of the family (< $20,000, ≥ $20,000), smoking status (current smokers, former smokers, never smokers), drinking status (≥ 12 drinks in the past 12 months, < 12 drinks in the past 12 months), exercise status (more active, less active, about the same), pregnancy status (yes or no), and self-reported cancer status (yes or no). Body mass index (BMI) was calculated by dividing weight in kilograms (kg) by height in meters squared (m2) and was divided into four groups (< 18.5, 18.5–24.9, 25–29.9, or > 30 kg/m2).

Assessment of the serum IgG antibody against P. gingivalis

Serum P. gingivalis IgG levels at baseline was chosen for this study to reflect evidence of systemic exposure to this bacterium, as it is a validated periodontal pathogen [22] and has well-described periodontal pathogenicity [23]. The levels of the serum P. gingivalis IgG were quantified via enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, and the antibody measurements were reported in units of IgG (EU), which has been reported in detail in the documentation of periodontal pathogens of NHANES III [24]. Based on a previous study [15], we divided the P. gingivalis IgG levels into three categories: low (< 69 EU), intermediate (69–119.0 EU), and high (> 119.0 EU).

Assessment of serum carotenoids

In this study, we included six serum carotenoids represented by five serum measures including α-carotene, β-carotene, β-cryptoxanthin, lutein/zeaxanthin, and lycopene at baseline which are available from NHANES III. Serum concentrations of these carotenoids were measured via high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) in NHANES III. The laboratory procedures and quality control methods used for the serum carotenoids measurements were described in detail previously [25, 26]. Serum carotenoids concentrations were divided into low or high groups based on the median.

Ascertainment of mortality

Data on all-cause and specific-cause of death were obtained from the public-use data files available from the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS). Vital status was determined by the National Death Index (NDI), and the cause of death was determined by the basic cause of death listed on the death certificate. Vital status and the cause of death were linked to data from NHANES III for further analysis. The International Classification of Diseases (ICD), 9th Revision (ICD-9) was used for coding all causes of death occurring before 1999, while the ICD-10 was used for coding deaths after 1998. The NCHS created a UCOD_113 variable to recode all deaths into comparable underlying causes of death groups, to assist researchers in analyzing specific death periods throughout the entire survey. We selected all causes of death and several major causes of death, including CVD, cancer, cerebrovascular diseases (CeVD), and diabetes mellitus (DM) as outcomes. For each study participant, follow-up extended from baseline to the date of death or end of follow-up (31 December, 2019), whichever occurred first.

Statistical analysis

Baseline demographic characteristics were described according to serum P. gingivalis IgG levels (low [< 69 EU], intermediate [69–119.0 EU], and high [> 119.0 EU]). The demographic characteristics of the different P. gingivalis IgG groups were compared via the chi-square test (categorical variables) or one-way analysis of variance (continuous variables). Person-years were calculated from baseline to the date of death or the end of follow-up (31 December, 2019), whichever occurred first.

We used the recommended sampling weights to account for the NHANES' complex survey design. Specifically, we applied the MEC- and home-examined sample final weight (WTPFHX6), with variance estimated using the pseudo-stratum (SDPSTRA6) and pseudo-PSU (SDPPSU6) variables as recommended by NCHS. Two variable-adjusted Surveycox proportional hazards regression models were conducted to calculate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for all-cause and specific-cause mortality in relation to serum P. gingivalis IgG levels. Model 1 was adjusted for sex and age groups. Model 2 was additionally adjusted for potential confounders: race-ethnicity, education, income, smoking status, drinking status, exercise, and BMI groups. The proportional hazards assumption was examined by creating a product term of follow-up time and exposure, and we found no significant deviation from the assumption.

We further conducted stratified analyses by groups of serum carotenoids (low or high) to investigate associations of the serum P. gingivalis IgG levels with all-cause and specific-cause mortality. To assess multiplicative interaction, we included in the Cox model the main terms for serum P. gingivalis IgG (dichotomized as low [< 69 EU] vs. high [≥ 69 EU]) and serum carotenoids (low vs. high), along with their product term. Both dichotomous variables were coded as 1 and 2, and the product term was calculated as the simple multiplication of the two. The Wald test for the coefficient of the product term was used to evaluate interaction on the multiplicative scale, and the corresponding hazard ratio (HR) with its 95% confidence interval (CI) is reported as the measure of interaction. To analyze the joint associations of P. gingivalis IgG concentration and carotenoids concentration on mortality, we divided the participants into four groups according to P. gingivalis IgG (< 69 EU vs. ≥ 69 EU) and the serum carotenoids (low vs. high) and estimated HRs (95% CIs) of mortality in different groups compared with those with serum P. gingivalis IgG ≥ 69 EU and low serum carotenes.

We also performed sensitivity analyses using tertiles of the serum P. gingivalis IgG distribution. In addition, the high-exposure group was redefined as the top two tertiles combined, and the stratified analyses, interaction analysis as well as the joint association models were repeated using the alternative cutoff.

Taking into account the complex survey sampling of NHANES, the survival probabilities for all-cause mortality and specific cause mortality were calculated and plotted via the R packages “survey” and “jskm”, respectively. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) curves for sampling weights were depicted to show the nonlinear relationships between serum P. gingivalis IgG levels and mortality. All analyses were performed via SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) and R 4.0.2 (R Core Team, Vienna, Austria), and a two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Associations of serum P. gingivalis IgG levels with mortality

The survival probability of participants with all-cause mortality was highest among those with low serum P. gingivalis IgG levels (< 69 EU), followed by those with intermediate (69–119 EU) and high P. gingivalis IgG levels (> 119 EU) (P < 0.001; Fig. 1A). Similar results were found for CVD, cancer, and CeVD mortality (Figure S2). Discussion

In the national population-based prospective cohort study conducted in NHANES III, we observed that serum P. gingivalis IgG levels were positively associated with all-cause mortality. We also found that the association was significantly modified by serum α-carotene, β-carotene, and lutein/zeaxanthin, and the associations between serum P. gingivalis IgG levels and all-cause mortality were weaker among those with higher carotenoid levels. The lowest risks of all-cause mortality were observed in those with low P. gingivalis IgG levels and high carotenoids levels. In addition, we observed that serum P. gingivalis IgG levels were positively associated with CVD, cancer, CeVD, and DM mortality. Significant interactions were found between serum carotenoid and serum P. gingivalis IgG levels with respect to CeVD and DM mortality.

Our findings revealed a positive association between serum P. gingivalis IgG levels and all-cause mortality. Dose–response association analysis revealed a linear relationship between the serum P. gingivalis IgG concentration and the risk of all-cause mortality. However, a prospective cohort study conducted by Damian et al. in 2015 reported a nonlinear association of serum P. gingivalis IgG levels with mortality [7]. This difference in findings may suggest that the relationship between P. gingivalis IgG levels and mortality is more complex than previously understood. Although the precise mechanisms underlying the observed positive association between the serum P. gingivalis IgG and the all-cause mortality remain unclear, several potential mechanisms may explain this relationship. The pathogenicity of P. gingivalis is primarily attributed to its ability to interfere with the host immune system, through virulence factors and cellular immune responses, which are mediated mainly by T cells. These immune modulations may influence the etiology of various systemic diseases, such as atherosclerosis, hypertension, adverse pregnancy outcomes, inflammatory bowel disease, DM, nonalcoholic fatty liver disease, rheumatoid arthritis, and Alzheimer’s disease [27]. These pathological effects may ultimately contribute to increase risks in all-cause mortality.

In addition, our study revealed that serum P. gingivalis IgG levels were positively associated with CVD, cancer, CeVD, and DM mortality. A previous study revealed that the P. gingivalis antibody titer in the serum is significantly positively correlated with the aortic valve area and Doppler velocity index [10]. Additionally, P. gingivalis has been shown to regulate atherosclerosis through immune pathways [28], further indicating a substantial relationship between this oral bacterium and the pathogenesis of CVD. Possible oncogenic mechanisms of P. gingivalis include the production of virulence factors or active metabolites, the activation of oncogenic signaling pathways, the promotion of aberrant immune responses, and the inhibition of host cell apoptosis, all of which align with our findings related to cancer mortality [29]. This study also revealed a significant link between serum P. gingivalis IgG levels and DM mortality, possibly because P. gingivalis can trigger a systemic inflammatory response, disrupt insulin signaling, and induce β-cell hypofunction and decrease [30]. These findings suggest that P. gingivalis infection may play a significant role in the pathogenesis and prognosis of these life-threatening conditions.

For the first time, we found that serum carotenoids significantly modified the relationship between serum P. gingivalis IgG levels and all-cause mortality. This discovery suggests that serum carotenoids may reduce the risk of death associated with P. gingivalis. Serum carotenoids are well-established composite biomarkers of fruit and vegetable intake and systemic antioxidant status [31, 32]. Their modifying associations likely reflects a direct counteraction of P. gingivalis-induced oxidative stress and inflammation through multiple mechanisms. Carotenoids are potent lipophilic antioxidants that may scavenge reactive oxygen species (ROS) generated during P. gingivalis infection and other metabolic processes [33]. At the molecular level, carotenoids activate the transcription factor nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2), which in turn upregulates the expression of a battery of antioxidant and cytoprotective enzymes, such as heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) [34]. Concurrently, carotenoids are known to inhibit the activation of nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) [34], a key pro-inflammatory transcription factor, and suppress the production of major pro-inflammatory cytokines including tumor necrosis factor (TNF), interleukin (IL)−1β, and IL-6 [35]. This finding explains the specific positive impact that carotenoids exert on the body, highlighting their potential health benefits. Similarly, vitamin D has been reported to reduce P. gingivalis infection by promoting autophagy, enhancing innate immunity in gingival epithelial cells, and modulating periodontal inflammation [36–39]. These findings suggest that, like carotenoids, vitamin D may also exert a protective effect against P. gingivalis-related mortality, although this hypothesis warrants further investigation.

The interactions between serum carotenoid and serum P. gingivalis IgG levels on DM are obvious both in primary analyses and sensitivity analyses. Individuals with high carotenoid levels have a reduced risk of DM mortality associated with serum P. gingivalis IgG levels. These results indicated that carotenoids may effectively alleviate the association of P. gingivalis IgG levels on DM mortality. Under high glucose conditions, β-carotene significantly inhibited the production of TNF, IL-1 and monocyte chemotactic protein-1 (MCP-1) induced by P. gingivalis lipopolysaccharide (LPS) in human acute monocytic leukemia cells (THP)−1 monocytes [40]. This study suggested that β-carotene may be a potential anti-inflammatory nutrient for circulating P. gingivalis LPS-mediated cytokine production in diabetic patients with periodontitis [40]. β-carotene may help interfere with diabetic complications by targeting intracellular NF-kB. Diabetic patients may be able to prevent or delay the development of several complications mediated by periodontitis by using appropriate dietary treatment with β-carotene [20]. For cancer mortality, the modifying associations of serum carotenoids was less consistent. No significant interaction was observed in the primary analysis, but a significant interaction emerged in the sensitivity analysis. This discrepancy may reflect differences in cut-point selection and statistical power, as well as the complexity of the tumor microenvironment and cancer heterogeneity. Further studies are needed to validate these findings.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first prospective study to assess the interaction between serum carotenoid and serum P. gingivalis IgG levels on mortality. The main purpose of this study was to explore the associations between serum P. gingivalis IgG levels and all-cause mortality and specific-cause mortality in a national prospective cohort survey population with a median follow-up period of 25.8 years and to evaluate the modifying associations of serum carotenoids on this association. The findings revealed that carotenoids may have a protective effect on mortality associated with serum P. gingivalis IgG levels. Our study also has several limitations. First, while P. gingivalis is a well-characterized oral bacterium, serum antibody assessment is an indirect measure of oral gastrointestinal bacterial exposure on the basis of individual variable vascular exposure and the immune response. Second, serum carotenoids and P. gingivalis IgG were measured only at baseline, and no repeated dietary data were collected during follow-up. This single-measurement approach cannot account for temporal changes in biomarkers or diet, potentially leading to regression dilution [41] and additional exposure misclassification. Third, although baseline age was adjusted for in the Cox modles, age-related mortality risk over the long-term follow-up may still influence the survival curves. The irregularities observed in later follow-up, such as rapid declines or jumps, likely reflect statistical instability due to the small number of participants still at risk. Fourth, we acknowledge that multiple comparisons were made across several carotenoids measures and mortality outcomes, which may increase the risk of type I error. Finally, as an observational cohort study, although we adjusted for multiple covariates, residual confounding from factors such as changes in dietary habits, smoking, oral hygiene practices, or socioeconomic variables during follow-up cannot be completely ruled out; therefore, causal inference in this study is limited.

Conclusion

Higher serum levels of selected carotenoids were associated with a weaker relationship between serum P. gingivalis IgG levels and all-cause, CeVD, and DM mortality. These findings suggest that carotenoids-related nutritional status may play a modifying role in the association between periodontal pathogen exposure and long-term mortality.
