Impact of Overweight and Obesity in Severe Asthma Patients and Its Influence on Achieving Clinical Remission
Authors: Mona Al-Ahmad, Asmaa Ali, Wafaa Talat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesObesity exacerbates asthma, resulting in more severe symptoms, poor disease control, and a reduced quality of life. With the increasing use of biologic therapies, achieving clinical remission (CR) has become an important treatment goal. This study evaluated the prevalence and impact of obesity on biologic response and CR in severe asthma patients.MethodsThis prospective observational study included 525 patients enrolled in the Kuwait Severe Asthma Registry (KSAR). This prospective observational study included 525 patients enrolled in the Kuwait Severe Asthma Registry (KSAR). Patients were categorized according to body mass index (BMI) into normal weight, overweight, mild-to-moderate obesity, and morbid obesity groups. Demographic, clinical, and biomarker data, including blood eosinophil count (BEC), total immunoglobulin E (IgE), fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO), and IL4 and IL6 gene polymorphisms, were collected. Patients treated with biologics for at least 12 months were followed. CR was defined as absence of exacerbations, no oral corticosteroid (OCS) use, Asthma Control Test (ACT) score ≥20, Asthma Control Questionnaire (ACQ-6) score ≤0.75, and predicted forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1) ≥80%.ResultsOverweight and obesity were highly prevalent, with only 15.4% of patients having normal weight. Morbidly obese patients were older, had longer disease duration, worse asthma control, lower lung function, and higher rates of diabetes and allergic rhinitis. Biologic therapy significantly improved ACT, ACQ-6, exacerbation frequency, OCS use, and lung function across all BMI groups (p<0.001). However, morbidly obese patients continued to demonstrate poorer asthma control, lower FEV1 and FVC, and higher exacerbation and OCS rates. No significant changes were observed in BEC, total IgE, or FeNO. CR was achieved in 17.3% of patients overall, with higher rates in normal-weight individuals (25%) and lower rates in morbid obesity (11%). Younger age and the IL4 CC genotype were associated with greater likelihood of CR.ConclusionObesity was highly prevalent among severe asthma patients and was associated with poorer disease outcomes and lower CR rates despite biologic therapy. These findings highlight the importance of weight management and personalized treatment strategies in severe asthma.Keywords: severe asthma, overweight and obesity, biologic therapy, IL4 and IL6 gene polymorphism, clinical remission

Introduction

Asthma, a chronic airway disease affecting 262 million globally, causes wheezing, breathlessness, and coughing.1 While most cases respond to inhalers, 5–10% have severe asthma, characterized by persistent symptoms, frequent attacks, and heavy reliance on oral steroids.2,3 Severe asthma drives over 50% of asthma healthcare costs due to hospitalizations and complex treatments.2,4 It also significantly impacts quality of life and lung function.5 Managing severe asthma is difficult due to varied underlying causes and poor response to standard therapies.6

The prevalence of obesity and its associated health conditions, including asthma, is both significant and increasing worldwide. Globally, the World Health Organization estimates that approximately 2 billion people are either obese or overweight.7 The impact of obesity extends to chronic diseases, as highlighted by a longitudinal Australian study (2009–2017), which found that obese adults with body mass index (BMI ≥30) had significantly higher risks of developing asthma (1.97 times) compared to their healthy-weight counterparts.8 A study of 32,644 Portuguese adults found that obesity is strongly associated with increased asthma prevalence, incidence, and severity in a dose-dependent manner. Obesity more than quadrupled the odds of developing asthma and significantly increased the likelihood of persistent and severe asthma phenotypes.9

In Gulf region, overweight and obesity are common among asthma patients, with prevalence rates of 31.1% and 46.0%, respectively.10 Obese asthmatic patients were more likely to have comorbidities than non-obese patients.11 A retrospective study of 684 adults with asthma found that obesity significantly impairs lung function.12 Obesity is also highly prevalent among patients with severe asthma, particularly those with type 2 high inflammation.13 Approximately 50–60% of severe asthma patients exhibit type 2 high inflammation, and within this group,14 50–63% are obese.15 Another study reported that 60–70% of severe asthma patients have type 2 high inflammation, with obesity present in about 50% of these individuals.16 Additionally, the prevalence of obesity in patients with severe eosinophilic asthma, a subtype of type 2 high asthma, has been reported to range from 45–55%.17

Obesity is a major comorbidity in severe asthma, associated with worse treatment outcomes, increased severity, reduced ICS response, and a higher risk of exacerbations.14 It also exacerbates airway inflammation and alters asthma phenotypes, making symptom control more challenging.18 Obese individuals with severe asthma may respond differently to biologic therapies.19 While biologics like anti-IL-5 and anti-IgE therapies are effective for managing T2-high asthma, their efficacy in obese patients remains limited.19,20 However, some biologics have shown potential treatment response in obese patients with severe eosinophilic asthma.21

Emerging evidence suggests that obesity is associated with altered inflammatory patterns in asthma and may modify conventional type 2 (T2) biomarker expression independently of treatment effects. Previous studies demonstrated that obese asthmatic patients exhibit lower fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO) levels and reduced predictive performance of blood eosinophil counts (BEC), total IgE, and FeNO for identifying eosinophilic airway inflammation compared with non-obese asthmatics.22,23 In contrast, obesity has also been associated with increased systemic inflammation, neutrophilic activity, and elevated inflammatory mediators such as IL-6, suggesting the presence of distinct obesity-related inflammatory phenotypes in severe asthma.22

Recent evidence has also identified an obesity-associated IL-6-high asthma phenotype characterized by systemic inflammation and metabolic dysfunction. The data demonstrated that patients with elevated circulating IL-6 levels were more likely to be obese, exhibit worse lung function, and experience more frequent asthma exacerbations compared with IL-6-low patients.24 Importantly, the IL-6-high phenotype reflects elevated systemic IL-6 inflammatory activity rather than IL6 gene polymorphisms, highlighting the complex interaction between obesity, inflammation, and severe asthma heterogeneity.24 In addition to environmental and metabolic influences, host genetic variation contributes substantially to asthma heterogeneity and may affect disease severity, inflammatory profiles, treatment response, and long-term outcomes.24 Polymorphisms in cytokine-related genes, particularly IL4 and IL6, have been implicated in immune regulation and asthma susceptibility.18 However, the potential interaction between obesity, genetic susceptibility, and treatment outcomes in severe asthma remains incompletely understood, particularly in Middle Eastern populations.

Recent real-world and registry-based studies have increasingly recognized clinical remission as an achievable treatment target in severe asthma following biologic therapy. Large international and national registry analyses demonstrated that remission can be achieved in a subset of patients treated with biologics, although attainment rates vary according to the remission definition used and baseline disease characteristics. The International Severe Asthma Registry (ISAR) analysis reported that approximately one-fifth of severe asthma patients achieved stringent four-domain remission after biological initiation, with shorter asthma duration, better baseline lung function, lower oral corticosteroid burden, and less severe disease associated with higher remission rates.25 Similarly, data from the Danish Severe Asthma Register demonstrated that lower BMI and shorter disease duration were among the strongest predictors of clinical remission following biologic therapy.26 Moreover, a recent systematic review and meta-analysis confirmed that obesity, maintenance oral corticosteroid use, longer asthma duration, impaired lung function, and increased disease severity are important barriers to achieving remission in severe asthma.27 These findings highlight the importance of patient-related factors and comorbidities, particularly obesity, in influencing remission outcomes and treatment response in severe asthma. However, despite the growing evidence linking obesity with poorer biologic outcomes and reduced likelihood of clinical remission, limited data are available regarding the prevalence and clinical impact of overweight and obesity among severe asthma populations in the Middle East. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the prevalence of overweight and obesity in Kuwaiti patients with severe asthma and identify factors associated with increased body mass index. A secondary objective was to assess the impact of obesity on biologic treatment response and clinical remission rates.

Methods

Study Design and Target Populations

This longitudinal study included all patients with severe asthma enrolled in the Kuwait Severe Asthma Registry (KSAR). Severe asthma was defined according to the American Thoracic Society/European Respiratory Society (ATS/ERS) criteria and was characterized by persistent symptoms, frequent exacerbations, regular or intermittent use of oral corticosteroids (OCS), and significant impairment in lung function.28–30 Patients were stratified into four groups based on their BMI: normal weight (BMI < 25), overweight (BMI 25–29.9), mild to moderate obesity (BMI ≥ 30 and < 40), and morbid obesity (BMI ≥ 40).31

Adult patients (aged ≥18 years) with severe asthma who had received continuous biologic therapy for at least one year were included in the follow-up. Patients who received biologic therapy for less than one-year, discontinued treatment, or had incomplete follow-up data were excluded from the study.

Ethics Approval and Consent to Participate

The study received approval from the Kuwait Ministry of Health Ethical Committee (approval number 2256/2023) and was conducted in accordance with local guidelines and the Helsinki Declaration. All participants provided written informed consent, acknowledging their understanding of the study’s purpose and procedures. Their participation was entirely voluntary, ensuring ethical compliance and adherence to global research standards.

Follow Up Data

After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, genotype data for IL4 and IL6 gene polymorphisms were retrieved from our database. These data had been generated previously as part of larger genetic studies investigating cytokine gene polymorphisms (IL4, IL6, IL13, and TNF-α) in asthma patients.35–37 The severe asthma-patients included in the present study constituted a subset of these previously genotyped cohorts. Genotype data were linked to the current clinical dataset using unique registry identifiers. All patients included in the biologic follow-up analysis had available IL4 and IL6 genotype data and were therefore included in the genetic analyses. Clinical and functional parameters collected at the end of the one-year follow-up were compared with baseline values to evaluate the impact of biologic therapy, including the rate of clinical remission (CR). Follow-up variables included ACT and ACQ-6 scores, annual number of exacerbations, number of OCS courses, spirometry results, and biomarkers (BEC, total IgE, and FeNO). For patients included in the follow-up cohort, baseline and follow-up spirometric percent-predicted values, including post-bronchodilator FEV1 (% predicted), FVC (% predicted), and FEV1/FVC ratio (% predicted), were retrieved from the original clinical records and used for longitudinal assessment of treatment response.

CR was defined by meeting all of the following criteria: no asthma exacerbations requiring emergency department visits or hospital admissions in the past year, no OCS use within the past year, an ACT score ≥ 20, an ACQ-6 score ≤ 0.75, and a post-bronchodilator FEV1% predicted ≥ 80%.38

Statistical Analysis

The data were organized in an Excel spreadsheet and statistically analyzed using Minitab 17.1.0.0 for Windows (Minitab Inc., 2013, Pennsylvania, USA). Numerical data were presented as mean and standard deviation, while categorical data were expressed as numbers and percentages. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess data normality. For group comparisons, one-way ANOVA or the Kruskal–Wallis’s test, followed by post hoc Tukey methods for multiple comparisons, was applied to analyze mean or median values. The Chi-square test was used to compare categorical variables when expected cell frequencies satisfied test assumptions. For contingency tables containing small, expected counts or zero-frequency cells, Fisher’s exact test or the Fisher-Freeman-Halton exact test was applied as appropriate. To evaluate changes in continuous variables before and after biologic treatment, a paired t-test or Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used according to data distribution. Logistic regression analysis with a backward elimination technique was conducted to identify independent predictors of successful remission. All tests were two-sided, with statistical significance set at ≤0.05.

Impact of Overweight and Obesity on Biologic Treatment Response

Figure 2 illustrates the patient selection process. More than half of the severe asthma patients (52.0%) had received biologic therapy. Of these, 193 patients (36.8% of the overall cohort) remained ongoing biologic treatment. Among patients receiving ongoing biologic therapy, 60 were excluded from the follow-up analysis due to transfer of care to another center, travel outside the country, discontinuation of biologic therapy, or incomplete follow-up data. Consequently, follow-up data were available for 133 patients, representing 25.3% of the overall severe asthma cohort and 68.9% of those receiving ongoing biologic therapy. These follow-up results demonstrated significant symptomatic improvement across all groups as evidenced by improvements in both ACT and ACQ-6 scores (p < 0.001). However, among the obesity subgroups, patients with morbid obesity demonstrated significantly lower ACT scores and higher ACQ-6 scores, with p-values of 0.003 and 0.001, respectively. Additionally, the number of exacerbations and the requirement for OCS courses significantly decreased across all groups during the follow-up period (p < 0.001). Nevertheless, patients with morbid obesity continued to experience a higher frequency of exacerbations and greater need for OCS courses, with p-values of 0.001 and <0.001, respectively (Figure 3).

Discussion

In 2014, a cross-sectional study of 3,915 Kuwaiti adults found that nearly 80% were either overweight or obese, with higher prevalence among women and positive associations with older age, diabetes, and marital status.39 A decade later, our findings remain consistent with this baseline39 and more recent data,40 confirming a high prevalence of overweight and obesity among Kuwaiti severe asthma patients; only a small fraction maintained a normal weight. Nearly half of our cohort had mild-to-moderate obesity, and while morbid obesity was less common, it still affected a significant proportion. Compared to normal-weight counterparts, overweight and obese severe asthma patients were significantly older, had longer disease duration, a lower proportion of males, and an increased occurrence of diabetes mellitus particularly in cases of morbid obesity. While allergic rhinitis was more common in morbidly obese patients, chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps did not differ significantly across weight groups. Crucially, asthma control worsened with increasing obesity, and lung function (FVC) was notably reduced in morbid obesity, underscoring the impact of weight on asthma severity.

Obesity intensifies asthma severity and complicates management through a complex interplay of chronic inflammation, structural alterations, and immune dysregulation. Airway inflammation in these patients is driven by adipose tissue-released pro-inflammatory adipokines, such as leptin, IL-6, and TNF-α, which perpetuate systemic and airway inflammation. Specifically, leptin enhances innate lymphoid cell 2 (ILC2) survival, while visceral fat macrophage infiltration drives Th1-polarized inflammation.41–43 Mechanically, excess abdominal and chest wall fat restricts diaphragmatic movement, reducing lung function as evidenced by decreased FVC and FEV1.44,45 Furthermore, obesity shifts the immune response toward Th1 polarization, reducing corticosteroid efficacy and fostering mixed neutrophilic/eosinophilic inflammation. This is coupled with macrophage dysfunction, oxidative stress from reduced nitric oxide bioavailability, and dysanapsis (disproportionate lung growth relative to airway size).41,43 Additionally, elevated IL-6 and leptin levels contribute to airway hyperreactivity.43 Metabolic dysregulation including insulin resistance and dyslipidemia amplifies systemic inflammation, while gut dysbiosis diminishes anti-inflammatory pathways, further exacerbating Th2 inflammation and airway hyperresponsiveness.42,43

In this study, biologic use particularly omalizumab was significantly higher among obese patients, aligning with evidence that allergic pathways remain relevant in this population.46,47 Although obesity is often linked to non-type 2 (non-T2) inflammation, many obese asthmatics exhibit T2-driven components like IgE-mediated allergies, supporting the rationale for anti-IgE therapy.14 However, obesity can suppress key T2 biomarkers like FeNO and blood eosinophils, potentially masking underlying T2 inflammation. This may introduce a selection bias in clinical practice, leading to a preference for omalizumab despite a complex inflammatory profile.14 Additionally, the frequent use of omalizumab may reflect its longer availability in Kuwait compared to other T2-high biologics.

Despite symptomatic improvements across all groups, morbidly obese patients experienced worse overall asthma control. This was driven by mechanical limitations (excess visceral and chest fat reducing lung compliance and limiting FEV1/FVC improvements),46 alongside adipokine- and ILC-driven non-T2 pathways that promote steroid-resistant neutrophilic/Th1 inflammation untargeted by omalizumab.48,49 Metabolic comorbidities further compounded airway hyperreactivity.49 While biologics improved lung function across all groups by alleviating T2-driven bronchoconstriction and remodeling, structural constraints from morbid obesity capped functional gains.47

In contrast to symptomatic and functional improvements, T2 inflammatory markers (FeNO, BEC) showed no significant changes during follow-up across obesity subgroups. This lack of change may stem from obesity-induced biomarker suppression, as higher BMI correlates with reduced FeNO and eosinophils independent of steroid use.48,49 Alternatively, biologics may optimize already attenuated T2 pathways in obese patients, yielding minimal measurable biomarker shifts.14,49

A novel finding of this study is that severe asthmatic patients with morbid obesity were significantly more likely to carry the TT genotype of the IL4 gene and the recessive genetic pattern (CT+TT), whereas normal-weight patients were associated with the CC genotype and the dominant pattern (CC+CT). Additionally, IL6 gene polymorphisms in overweight and obese patients exhibited a significant association with the GG genotype and the recessive pattern (CG+GG). These associations highlight a complex interaction between genetic predisposition and obesity-driven inflammation. The IL4 polymorphism enhances IL-4 production in T-allele carriers. In morbidly obese patients with the TT genotype, this amplifies IL-4 signaling;32,50 an effect intensified by leptin resistance (which upregulates IL-4Rα expression) and adipose-derived IL-6 (which promotes IL-4-driven M2 macrophage polarization, worsening airway remodeling). Conversely, obesity introduces non-T2 pathways including IL-17/IL-23-mediated neutrophilic and IL-6/TNF-α-driven metabolic inflammation that creating a mixed phenotype resistant to single-target biologics.41 While the TT genotype drives high IL-4 expression, IgE synthesis, and eosinophilic inflammation, the CC genotype in normal-weight individuals tracks with lower IL-4 activity and a pure Th2 phenotype that responds well to biologics.32,41,50 Furthermore, the absence of metabolic inflammation in normal-weight individuals preserves corticosteroid efficacy.32 However, despite elevated local IL-4 in TT carriers, systemic biomarkers like serum IgE may fail to reflect airway inflammation due to obesity-related T2 marker suppression.32,41

Similarly, the IL6 gene polymorphism plays a critical role in overweight and obese patients.51 The GG genotype is associated with elevated IL-6 production, contributing to systemic inflammation and metabolic dysfunction.52 Obesity-induced low-grade inflammation, driven by leptin and TNF-α, amplifies IL-6 activity, increasing adipose tissue macrophage recruitment and exacerbating airway remodeling.50,53 Higher IL-6 levels correlate with insulin resistance and dyslipidemia,54 compounding asthma severity.51,55 In the airways, IL-6 overexpression shifts immune responses toward Th17/neutrophilic pathways, reducing corticosteroid and biologic efficacy while increasing bronchial hyperreactivity and impairing lung function.55,56 The predominance of the GG genotype in overweight and obese patients likely reflects gene-environment interactions where obesity enhances IL-6 expression to worsen outcomes.51 GG carriers may thus possess an inflammatory predisposition toward obesity-associated asthma rather than classical T2-driven asthma.55–58

Our findings align with previous registries and meta-analyses demonstrate lower clinical remission rates among obese patients.25–27,59,60 The observed gradient in remission rates across our BMI categories (25%, 19%, 16%, and 11%) closely mirrors data from the ISAR analysis,25 the Danish Severe Asthma Register,26 the UK Severe Asthma Registry,59,60 and a recent systematic review,27 all identifying obesity as a powerful negative predictor of remission.

Overall, 17.29% of our severe asthma patients achieved clinical remission, with a stark contrast between normal-weight individuals (25%) and the morbidly obese group (11%). Biologics like omalizumab, mepolizumab, and benralizumab show significant potential in driving remission, with literature rates ranging from 20.3% to 72.97% depending on criteria.61–63 Early biologic initiation enhances these rates, particularly in patients with shorter disease duration and less baseline impairment, and sustained remission is achievable with prolonged use.25,61 For example, continued omalizumab over five years increased remission to 47.4%, while benralizumab demonstrated sustained lung function gains and reduced exacerbations over two years, yielding a 42.1% remission rate.58,62

However, obesity remains a major barrier to remission61 due to its distinct mixed eosinophilic/neutrophilic phenotype, compromised lung function, and heightened corticosteroid resistance, which limit biologic efficacy.61,62 Obese patients experience poorer control and higher exacerbation rates despite biologic therapy, underscoring the need for adjunctive interventions.62 Notably, weight loss significantly improves asthma control, reduces medication dependence, and restores lung function with symptomatic remission rates ranging from 48% to 100%.61 While biologics markedly improve clinical outcomes, addressing obesity through targeted weight loss strategies remains crucial to optimizing long-term asthma remission and control.63

Strength and Limitations

This study provided a comprehensive overview of severe asthma in the context of obesity, offering novel insights into the relationship between overweight and obesity, biologic treatment response, clinical remission, and the potential role of IL4 and IL6 polymorphisms. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. The reliance on a single national database may restrict the generalizability of the findings to other populations, and the availability of follow-up data for only a quarter of the patients introduces potential bias in assessing long-term treatment outcomes. Additionally, the absence of mechanistic exploration into how obesity influences genetic and inflammatory pathways limits causal inferences. Furthermore, the study did not account for lifestyle factors such as diet and physical activity, which could impact obesity-related asthma outcomes. The genetic analyses were exploratory in nature and involved relatively small BMI-stratified subgroups, which may have limited statistical power to detect modest genotype–phenotype associations. Therefore, the observed associations involving IL4 and IL6 polymorphisms should be interpreted with caution and warrant validation in larger independent cohorts. In addition, the number of patients achieving clinical remission was relatively small compared with the number of variables included in the multivariable logistic regression model. Consequently, the regression analysis should be considered exploratory, as the limited number of remission events may have reduced the precision and stability of the estimated odds ratios. Therefore, the identified factors associated with clinical remission require confirmation in larger studies with a greater number of outcome events. Finally, clinical remission was assessed after a minimum of 12 months of biologic therapy. Although this duration is consistent with current real-world studies and registry-based remission definitions, longer follow-up periods may identify additional patients achieving remission and better capture delayed treatment responses, particularly among individuals with obesity.

Conclusion

This study highlights the high prevalence of overweight and obesity among patients with severe asthma in Kuwait and demonstrates that increasing BMI is associated with poorer asthma control, greater comorbidity burden, reduced lung function, and lower rates of clinical remission. Although biologic therapy significantly improved asthma control, exacerbation frequency, oral corticosteroid use, and lung function across all BMI categories, patients with morbid obesity continued to experience poorer absolute clinical outcomes than patients with lower BMI.

The observed inverse relationship between obesity and clinical remission is consistent with previous registry studies and meta-analyses identifying obesity as an important negative predictor of remission in severe asthma. Exploratory analyses also identified associations between obesity status and IL4 and IL6 gene polymorphisms, as well as between the IL4 CC genotype and remission attainment. However, given the relatively small genetic subgroups and observational study design, these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and require confirmation in larger independent cohorts.

Taken together, our findings support the importance of addressing obesity as part of the comprehensive management of severe asthma. In addition to biologic therapy, weight-management strategies may help optimize clinical outcomes and improve the likelihood of achieving remission in this population.
