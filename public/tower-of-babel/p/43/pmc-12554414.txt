Molecular Layer Atrophy Mediates the Association of Cumulative Blood Pressure Exposure With Cognitive Decline
Authors: Xiaoshuai Li, Ying Hui, Huijing Shi, Jiacheng Fan, Ling Yang, Mengmeng Bai, Shun Zhang, Xiaoliang Liang, Xianquan Shi, Pengfei Zhao, Shuohua Chen, Yuntao Wu, Shouling Wu, Zhenchang Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHigh blood pressure (BP) is a known risk factor for cognitive decline and dementia, but the underlying mechanisms are largely unknown. We investigated the associations of cumulative BP exposure with hippocampal subfield volume and cognitive function and determined whether hippocampal subfield atrophy mediates the association between cumulative BP exposure and cognitive decline.MethodsBetween December 2020 and March 2023, participants were prospectively included from the Kailuan study. The cumulative BP was calculated as the area under the curve (mm Hg×years) using measurements from 2006 to 2023. A brain magnetic resonance imaging and the Montreal Cognitive Assessment was conducted beginning in 2020. The hippocampus was automatically segmented into 12 subfields via FreeSurfer 7.4.1 software (http://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/).ResultsA total of 1035 participants were included in this study. Increased cumulative systolic BP was associated with decreased volumes in the parasubiculum, presubiculum, subiculum, CA4, dentate gyrus, and molecular layer, and higher cumulative diastolic BP was associated with a lower volume in the presubiculum. Higher cumulative systolic BP and diastolic BP were associated with lower Montreal Cognitive Assessment scores. Decreased volumes in the subiculum; cornu ammonis (CA) 1, 2‐3, and 4; dentate gyrus; molecular layer; fimbria; and hippocampal tail were associated with lower Montreal Cognitive Assessment scores. The molecular layer volume mediated negative associations of cumulative systolic BP and diastolic BP exposure with Montreal Cognitive Assessment scores, with mediating effect proportions of 5.61% and 8.68%, respectively.ConclusionsIncreased cumulative BP was associated with hippocampal subfield atrophy and cognitive decline, and the molecular layer volume mediated the negative association between cumulative BP and cognitive decline.

With an aging population, the prevalence of cognitive decline and dementia has increased. The number of patients with dementia worldwide is expected to increase from the current population of 50 million to 150 million by 2050. 1 Owing to the lack of effective prevention and treatment, dementia has become the seventh leading cause of death worldwide. Therefore, identifying modifiable risk factors for cognitive decline and dementia is important for delaying or reducing the risk of onset. Hypertension is a risk factor for cognitive decline and dementia 2 , 3 and is associated with structural and functional changes in the brain 4 , 5 , 6 including hippocampus. Hippocampal structural changes also occur in Alzheimer disease, the most common neurodegenerative cause of dementia. 7 , 8

However, most previous studies on blood pressure (BP), cognitive function, and brain structure have used a single BP measurement. 5 , 6 A single BP measurement does not accurately reflect the true BP of an individual over a certain period, which may also explain the inconsistent results of previous studies regarding the association between BP and hippocampal structural changes. 6 , 9 , 10 The hippocampus is composed of anatomically and functionally specific subfields that combine to form specific functional units. 11 , 12 Different pathological changes in certain diseases are associated with specific hippocampal subfields including cognitive decline. 13 , 14 , 15 Previous studies examining the effects of BP on the hippocampus have evaluated the hippocampus as a whole, 6 , 16 , 17 which masks changes in specific hippocampal subfields. A previous study revealed that systolic BP (SBP) variability was negatively associated with hippocampal volume. 17 Glodzik et al reported that SBP was negatively correlated with hippocampal cerebral blood flow, whereas diastolic BP (DBP) was not significantly associated with hippocampal cerebral blood flow. 16 Another study revealed that DBP was negatively associated with hippocampal cerebral blood flow, while SBP was negatively associated with hippocampal volume. 6 In addition, it remains unclear whether structural alterations in hippocampal subfields mediate hypertension‐induced cognitive decline. To address the shortcomings of these previous studies, we used multimodal brain imaging data extracted from the Kailuan study to systematically investigate the associations among long‐term cumulative BP exposure, hippocampal subfield volumes, and cognitive function to further explore whether hippocampal subfield volume mediates the association between cumulative BP exposure and cognitive decline.

Methods

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the Kailuan community, but restrictions apply to the availability of these data, which were used under license for the current study and are not publicly available. Data are, however, available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request and with permission of corresponding author.

Participants

The Kailuan study was a prospective cohort study conducted in Tangshan, China. From July 2006 to October 2007, a total of 101 510 participants from 11 hospitals in the Kailuan community underwent their first health examinations and completed questionnaire assessments, clinical examinations, and laboratory tests. All participants received a follow‐up every 2 years after 2006. The seventh follow‐up began in December 2020, and some participants who were followed up at Kailuan General Hospital voluntarily underwent both a head magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and cognitive function assessment on the same day. The inclusion criteria for the present study were as follows: (1) attended the seventh follow‐up and underwent a head MRI and cognitive assessment beginning in December 2020; (2) participated in the first physical examination between 2006 and 2007; and (3) attended at least 1 follow‐up between 2008 and 2018. Participants with a history of stroke, tumors, neuropsychiatric diseases, head trauma, or incomplete imaging data were excluded (Figure 1).

Standard Protocol Approvals, Registrations, and Patient Consent

This study protocol was approved by the ethics committee of the local institution, and all participants provided informed consent.

Cumulative BP Exposure

SBP and DBP (unit: mm Hg) were measured by a professional using a digital BP monitor (Omron HBP‐1100U). After the participants sat in a quiet room for 5 minutes, BP was measured on the right brachial artery 3 times at 1‐ to 2‐minute intervals. The average BP value of the 3 measurements was recorded.

Cumulative SBP, DBP, and pulse pressure (PP) (unit: mm Hg×years) were calculated for each participant as the sum of average BP measurements between consecutive visits multiplied by the time interval between visits (unit: year) 18 , 19 : cumulative BP exposure=[BP2006+BP2008]/2×[Visit2008–Visit2006]+[BP2008+BP2010]/2×[Visit2010–Visit2008]+…+[BP2018+BP2020]/2×[Visit2020–Visit2018] (an example for participants with all visits).

MRI Data Acquisition

Participants were scanned via a 3.0 T MRI scanner (750 W; General Electric, Milwaukee, WI). Brain structural images were acquired via a 3‐dimensional T1‐BRAVO sequence. The detailed parameters are as follows: echo time/repetition time=2.6/6.7 milliseconds; slice thickness=1 mm; flip angle=15°; field of view=256 mm×256 mm; gap=1 mm; and 170 slices.

MRI Data Processing

FreeSurfer 7.4.1 software (http://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/) was used to automatically segment the bilateral hippocampus on the 3‐dimensional high‐resolution T1‐weighted images into 12 subfields. 20 Computational mapping of the hippocampal structure is a combination of manual labeling of in vivo and in vitro data using Bayesian inference. This algorithm is based on 15 ex vivo MRI scans of the hippocampal formation that were obtained at ultra‐high resolution. 21 Iglesias et al 21 validated the segmentation capability of the model using high‐resolution MRI data and reported a high degree of agreement with manual segmentation. Several studies further supported the reliability of this automated segmentation method on normal subjects and patient data. 22 , 23 Furthermore, Brown et al 24 confirmed the high test–retest reliability of the FreeSurfer automated hippocampal subfield segmentation within and across scanners. The above results indicate that this automatic segmentation method provides a robust method for measuring the hippocampal subfields. Therefore, an increasing number of studies have adopted this automatic segmentation method to investigate changes of hippocampal subfield volume under physiological and pathological conditions. 25 , 26 , 27 Two experts visually assessed the coronal, sagittal, and axial slices of the hippocampal subfield. These subfields included the subiculum, hippocampal fissure, hippocampal tail, presubiculum, parasubiculum, molecular layer (ML), dentate gyrus (DG), cornu ammonis (CA) 1, CA2‐3, CA4, fimbria, and hippocampal amygdalar transition area (Figure 2). The left and right regions were combined, 26 and total intracranial volume was obtained as covariate in the statistical analysis below.

Cognitive Function Assessment

The cognitive function of each participant was assessed via the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) before a head MRI. The scale assesses 7 cognitive domains (visuospatial/executive functions, naming, language, attention, abstraction, recall, and orientation) and has been widely used to screen for mild cognitive impairment. For participants with ≤12 years of education, the total MoCA score was increased by 1 point. The total MoCA score ranges from 0 to 30, with lower scores indicating poorer cognitive function.

Covariate Measurement

Information on demographic characteristics, lifestyle factors (including smoking status, drinking status, and physical activity), medical history, and household medication use was collected via a standardized questionnaire. Physical examinations (weight, height, and body mass index [BMI]) were performed by experienced professionals during each survey. Laboratory tests such as fasting blood glucose (FBG), triglyceride, total cholesterol, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C), and high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol levels were measured with automated analyzers (Hitachi 747; Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan).

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed via SAS 9.4 software (SAS Institute, Cary, NC). Demographic and clinical characteristics were calculated according to tertiles of cumulative SBP exposure and are expressed as count (percentage) for categorical variables, mean±SD for continuous variables if normally distributed, and median (interquartile range) if skewed. The baseline characteristics were compared via 1‐way ANOVA for continuous variables with a normal distribution, Wilcoxon rank‐sum test for continuous variables with a skewed distribution, and χ2 test for categorical variables. Regression coefficients β and 95% CIs were calculated via generalized linear regression models to explore the associations of cumulative BP exposure with hippocampal subfield volumes and cognitive function. Cumulative BP exposure was assessed on both continuous and tertile‐based categorical scales. The cumulative BP values were ranked from smallest to largest and then divided into 3 equal groups (1/3, 2/3, and 3/3) to obtain the tertiles of cumulative BP. Two models were established in this study. Model 1 was adjusted for sex, baseline age, smoking status, drinking status, physical activity, BMI, FBG, LDL‐C, use of antihypertensive medications, use of hypoglycemic medications, use of hypolipidemic medications, and total intracranial volume. To account for possible changes in BMI, FBG, LDL‐C, and medication use during follow‐up, model 2 was adjusted for the mean BMI, mean FBG, mean LDL‐C, history of medication use during follow‐up rather than the baseline BMI, FBG, LDL‐C, or medication use as in model 1. The longitudinal data in model 2 take into account changes in variables over the follow‐up period, which may be more robust and meaningful than a single measurement in model 1. Therefore, the result obtained from Model 2 is reported as the primary result. The missing values of the covariate were compensated for using multiple imputation by chained equations. 28 Logistic and linear regression imputed missing values for categorical and continuous variables, respectively.

To examine whether the hippocampal subfield volume affects the association between cumulative BP exposure and cognitive function, a mediation analysis was performed via the SAS CAUSALMED procedure, which is based on the asymptotic theory for maximum likelihood estimates and uses bootstrap methods that are based on resampling (Monte Carlo simulation times=1000) to calculate CIs. This includes adjustment for the covariates in model 2 as follows: sex, baseline age (grouped at 10‐year intervals), smoking status, drinking status, physical activity, mean BMI, mean FBG, mean LDL‐C, use of antihypertensive medications, use of hypoglycemic medications, and use of hypolipidemic medications during follow‐up. In all these tests, P<0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Results

Participant Characteristics

A total of 1035 participants were included in this study. The mean age of participants at the time of the MRI was 57.8±10.0 years (ranging from 36.6 to 83.9 years), and 522 (50.4%) were men. There was an average of 6 BP measurements across the study (range, 3–8), and the number of measurements per participant ranged as follows: 3 measurements (n=75), 4 measurements (n=88), 5 measurements (n=116), 6 measurements (n=190), 7 measurements (n=265), and 8 measurements (n=301). The median time from the first physical examination to brain MRI was 14.7 years (range, 13.4–18.8). No participants were excluded from this study due to segmentation errors. Table 1 details the demographic, clinical, and radiographic characteristics of all participants at the seventh follow‐up according to tertiles of cumulative SBP exposure. Participants in the highest tertile of cumulative SBP were older males; had a higher BMI, FBG, and mean cumulative SBP, DBP, and PP; were more likely to smoke, drink, exercise, and receive antihypertensive, hypoglycemic, and lipid‐lowering treatments; and had lower MoCA scores (all P<0.05). In addition, participants in the highest tertile of cumulative SBP had a lower volume in the subiculum, CA2‐3, ML, and fimbria and a greater volume in the hippocampal fissure (all P<0.05).

Cumulative BP Exposure and Hippocampal Subfield Volumes

Associations between cumulative BP exposure and hippocampal subfield volumes are shown in Figure 3, Table 2, and Table S1. Compared with those in the lowest tertiles, participants in the highest tertiles of cumulative SBP had lower volumes in the parasubiculum (β, −7.78 [95% CI, −12.95 to −2.6]; P=0.003), presubiculum (β, −26.4 [95% CI, −39.63 to −13.17]; P<0.001), subiculum (β, −34.48 [95% CI, −51.5 to −17.47]; P<0.001), CA4 (β, −8.82 [95% CI, −17.24 to −0.41]; P=0.04), DG (β, −13.62 [95% CI, −23.51 to −3.72]; P=0.007), and ML (β, −28.07 [95% CI, −46.02 to −10.12]; P=0.002), and participants in the highest tertiles of cumulative DBP had lower volumes in the presubiculum (β, −17.22 [95% CI, −30.26 to −4.19]; P=0.010). Participants in the highest tertiles of cumulative PP had lower volumes in the parasubiculum (β, −5.18 [95% CI, −10.05 to −0.31]; P=0.037), presubiculum (β, −19.65 [95% CI, −32.11 to −7.19]; P=0.002), subiculum (β, −30.73 [95% CI, −46.72 to −14.74]; P<0.001), and ML (β, −20.17 [95% CI, −37.06 to −3.28]; P=0.019). When cumulative BP exposure was a continuous variable, for every SD increase in cumulative SBP, the volumes of the parasubiculum, presubiculum, subiculum, DG, and ML decreased by 2.81 mm3, 12.35 mm3, 14.46 mm3, 5.59 mm3, and 12.38 mm3, respectively. With each increase in the SD of the cumulative DBP, the volumes of the presubiculum, subiculum, and ML decreased by 8.56 mm3, 7.24 mm3, and 7.86 mm3, respectively. For every SD increase in cumulative PP, the volumes of the parasubiculum, presubiculum, subiculum, CA4, DG, ML, and fimbria decreased by 2.90 mm3, 10.43 mm3, 14.76 mm3, 3.54 mm3, 4.88 mm3, 11.13 mm3, and 2.62 mm3, respectively. The scatter plots of cumulative SBP and key hippocampal subfield volumes were shown in Figure S1. In addition, the effect of age on hippocampal subfield volumes was stronger in the highest tertiles of cumulative SBP (Figure S2).

Cumulative BP Exposure and Cognitive Function

Figure 4 and Table S2 summarize the association between cumulative BP exposure and cognitive function. Compared with those in the lowest tertile, participants in the highest tertiles of cumulative SBP, DBP, and PP had lower MoCA scores (cumulative SBP: β, −1.03 [95% CI, −1.66 to −0.41]; P=0.001; cumulative DBP: β, −0.99 [95% CI, −1.61 to −0.37]; P=0.002; cumulative PP: β, −0.99 [95% CI, −1.58 to −0.40]; P=0.001). When cumulative BP was considered a continuous exposure variable, MoCA scores decreased by 0.52, 0.42, and 0.39 for each 1‐SD increase in cumulative SBP, DBP, and PP, respectively.

Mediation Analysis

As shown in Figure 6, mediation analysis showed that the ML volume mediated the negative associations of cumulative SBP and DBP exposure with MoCA scores (cumulative SBP – indirect effect: β, −0.03 [95% CI, −0.07, −0.0004]; direct effect: β, −0.58 [95% CI, −0.85 to −0.31]; and proportion: 5.61%; cumulative DBP – indirect effect: β, −0.04 [95% CI, −0.07 to −0.001]; direct effect: β, −0.37 [95% CI, −0.64 to −0.11]; proportion: 8.68%). Other hippocampal subfield volumes did not mediate effects of cumulative BP exposure on cognitive decline.

Discussion

In the Kailuan study population, we found that high cumulative BP exposure was not only negatively associated with hippocampal subfield volume but also a risk factor for cognitive decline. Moreover, decreased hippocampal subfield volumes were positively associated with cognitive decline. A reduced ML volume mediated the negative association between high cumulative BP exposure and cognitive decline (cumulative SBP, proportion: 5.61%; cumulative DBP, proportion: 8.68%). These associations were independent not only of traditional risk factors but also of baseline SBP and DBP. In addition, the effect of high cumulative BP exposure on hippocampal subfield volumes is different.

Our key finding was that high cumulative BP exposure was negatively associated with hippocampal subfield volume, with consistent trends in both cumulative SBP and DBP. However, the cumulative SBP was more negatively associated with the hippocampal subfield volume than the cumulative DBP. Notably, the average cumulative SBP and DBP values in the intermediate tertile were only 125.93±6.44 mm Hg and 80.63±5.80 mm Hg, respectively, which did not meet the European and World Health Organization diagnostic criteria for hypertension and the US criteria for stage 1 hypertension. 29 , 30 , 31 However, the volume of the hippocampal subfield decreased compared with that in the lowest tertile. Moreover, each SD increase in cumulative SBP and DBP was negatively associated with decreased hippocampal subfield volume. These findings suggest that even in nonhypertensive individuals, higher cumulative SBP and DBP may lead to decreased hippocampal subfield volume, which also suggests the importance of long‐term maintenance of ideal blood pressure levels for brain health.

We found that high cumulative BP exposure had different effects on the hippocampal subfield, and the cumulative SBP was negatively associated with the volume of the parasubiculum, presubiculum, subiculum, DG, and ML. For each SD increase in the cumulative SBP, the volume of the hippocampal subfield decreased by 2.81, 12.35, 14.46, 5.59, and 12.38, respectively. Although the association between cumulative BP exposure and the hippocampal subfield has not been investigated in previous studies, elevated SBP has been shown to be negatively associated with total hippocampal volume, with a decrease of 0.07 per SD increase in SBP. 6 Compared with previous studies, we used cumulative BP as an exposure metric, which is more reliable than a single BP measurement. Our results suggest that (1) there may be heterogeneity in the effects of hypertension on the hippocampal subfield structure and that (2) the susceptibility of the hippocampal subfield to hypertensive damage may differ.

This study confirmed that high cumulative BP exposure is a risk factor for cognitive decline, and different cumulative BP parameters have different effects on cognitive decline. We found that after adjusting for potential confounders, MoCA scores decreased by 0.52 and 0.42 for every SD increase in cumulative SBP and DBP, respectively. Previous studies have explored association between cumulative BP exposure and cognitive function. In the CARDIA (Coronary Artery Risk Development in Young Adults) cohort, Mahinrad et al 18 reported that cumulative BP exposure in youths was negatively associated with cognitive function in middle age and that for every 1‐SD increase in cumulative SBP and DBP exposure, cognitive function in the memory domain was reduced by 0.16. Another cohort study with a 25‐year follow‐up period in adults also confirmed negative associations of cumulative SBP and DBP exposure with cognitive function. 32 The above findings are consistent with our results. However, in an older population, Li et al 33 reported that cumulative SBP is negatively associated with cognitive function, whereas cumulative DBP is positively associated with cognitive function. The reasons for the inconsistent results remain unclear, and further studies are warranted to validate these findings.

In addition, this study confirmed that a reduced hippocampal subfield volume was significantly associated with cognitive decline, which is consistent with findings of previous studies. 26 , 34 , 35 Furthermore, through mediation analysis, we found that the ML volume mediated the negative associations of cumulative SBP and DBP exposure with MoCA scores and accounted for 5.61% and 8.68% of the total effect, respectively. The ML is one of the hub regions in patients with Alzheimer disease. 36 Cai et al 37 explored the relationships among hypertension, hippocampal subfields, and cognitive function in patients with subjective cognitive decline and reported that, compared with those without hypertension, those with hypertension presented a lower volume of the ML. Chu et al 36 also reported, that compared with healthy controls, patients with Alzheimer disease exhibited a significantly lower ML volume, which was correlated with cognitive performance. These studies suggest that the ML is closely associated with hypertension and cognitive decline. The ML consists of interneuron synaptic connections and plays a key role in regulating activity in the hippocampus. 37 , 38 The associated interneurons connect the subiculum with other CA regions. 20 Additionally, ML neurons can control Purkinje cell activity through inhibitory synaptic transmission, which plays an important role in cerebellar information processing and enable precise motor coordination and cognition. 38 However, ML atrophy can lead to reduced interneuron connectivity between hippocampal subfields. 37 Therefore, cumulative BP exposure may lead to a decrease in ML volume, which reduces neuronal connectivity between hippocampal subfields and ultimately leads to cognitive decline.

This study has several limitations:

1. All participants in this study were Chinese, which limits the generalizability of our findings to other races.

2. In this study, the hippocampus was automatically segmented. Although automated segmentation guarantees highly standardized and objective results, the technique may still have drawbacks in terms of regional boundaries compared with expert manual segmentation, 39 especially in smaller substructures. The previous FreeSurfer 5.1 was criticized because parts of the large CA1 region were redistributed to adjacent CA2/3 regions, resulting in a smaller CA1 region than in anatomic studies. 40 This situation is addressed in FreeSurfer 6.0, which has a high level of test reliability, with intraclass correlation coefficients >0.9 for 20 regions including CA1. 22

3. The accuracy of cumulative BP calculation increases with the number of physical examinations attended by participants. In this study, cumulative BP was derived from participants who attended at least 3 physical examinations. Given that the exposure duration for cumulative BP was consistent across all participants—each attended both the first physical examination and the seventh follow‐up—it may not have a meaningful impact on the results.

4. MoCA was used to assess global cognitive function and was not particularly sensitive to hippocampal function, which may have potentially affected the results.

5. This study measured cumulative BP exposure over an average of 14.7 years; thus, results may not be generalizable to BP exposures over shorter periods (such as days).

In summary, the present study revealed that higher cumulative BP exposure was associated with hippocampal subfield atrophy and cognitive decline in the general adult population, independent of other vascular risk factors. Decreased hippocampal subfield volumes were associated with cognitive decline. Furthermore, the ML volume mediated the associations between cumulative SBP and DBP exposure and MoCA scores. These findings highlight the impact of BP on brain structure and function and underscore the need for early prevention of hypertension.
