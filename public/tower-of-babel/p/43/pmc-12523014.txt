Identification of intraoperative hypotension endotypes and revolution with a temporal deep learning algorithm
Authors: Yihao Zhu, Xuechao Hao, Peiyi Li, Yaqiang Wang, Fei Wang, Tao Zhu, Huadong Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIdentifying specific causes of intraoperative hypotension (IOH) is a challenge in clinical practice. Improving the causal treatment of hypotension requires a more detailed understanding of the underlying hemodynamic alterations during hypotension. This study aims to identify distinct hemodynamic endotypes of IOH by applying a deep learning model to high-resolution intraoperative hemodynamic data.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis for surgical patients who had undergone continuous intraoperative monitoring of systemic vascular resistance index (SVRI), stroke volume index (SVI), stroke volume variation (SVV), cardiac index (CI), and heart rate (HR). IOH was defined as a mean arterial pressure (MAP) < 65 mmHg sustained for at least 1 min. A long short-term memory (LSTM)-based autoencoder was developed to compress multivariate time-series data into a two-dimensional latent space. Unsupervised clustering, k-means, was performed on the two-dimensional latent representations, and the optimal number of clusters was determined by Calinski–Harabasz (CH) and Davies–Bouldin (DB) index.ResultsA total of 184 patients experienced at least one episode of IOH, and 1304 hypotensive episodes with 253,380 data points were included for analysis. K-means identified five distinct IOH endotypes. Based on the characteristic hemodynamic profiles of each cluster, we labeled the five endotypes: (1) severe vasodilation with high CI, (2) hypovolemia, (3) myocardial depression, (4) bradycardia, and (5) mild vasodilation with preserved CI. Mild vasodilation with preserved CI occurred mainly in the first and second quartiles of the procedure. Bradycardia occurred throughout the procedure, but more frequently in the fourth quartile. Myocardial depression occurred primarily in the second quartile, and hypovolemia occurred frequently throughout the procedure. It was also observed that intra-event transitions between endotypes within the same hypotensive episode occurred in five events.ConclusionsFive endotypes of IOH were identified, and this may support the development of causal treatment strategies of intraoperative hypotension, pending future validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13741-025-00598-6.Keywords: Hypotension, Anesthesia, Unsupervised learning, Blood pressure management, Hemodynamic monitoring, Deep learning model, Autoencoder

Introduction

Perioperative hypotension is a frequent and clinically significant complication associated with adverse outcomes, including myocardial injury, acute kidney injury (AKI), and increased postoperative mortality [1, 2]. Current hypotension management often relies on empirical fluid resuscitation and vasopressor administration without precise insight into the underlying cause [3–5]. Improving the causal treatment of hypotension requires a more nuanced understanding of the hemodynamic alterations during these events.

Advanced hemodynamic monitoring now enables real-time acquisition of high-resolution physiological data, including systemic vascular resistance index (SVRI), stroke volume index (SVI), stroke volume variation (SVV), heart rate (HR), and cardiac index (CI). Recent studies have attempted to identify the endotype of intraoperative hypotension, which proposed four and six hypotensive types [4, 6], respectively, based on advanced hemodynamic variables such as SVRI, SVI, and SVV. These efforts have demonstrated the feasibility of data-driven classification of hypotension and offered a promising approach to guide precise intervention. Nonetheless, due to the limited sample size and inconsistent methodology in previous research, further study is necessary to validate the reliability of endotypes classification and disclose its clinical significance.

Additionally, time-related etiology evolution underlying hypotension development has been demonstrated, for instance, in various types of shock. However, the critical role of temporal characteristics of key parameters (e.g., SVRI, SVV) in real-time, rather than static, detecting hypotension endotype has not yet been addressed. Clinically, failure to account for this temporal complexity risks oversimplified, one-size-fits-all treatment, which may be ineffective or even harmful. In order to optimize the validity and medical rationality of intraoperative hypotension endotypes and reveal their evolution profiles, we applied the temporal deep learning approach to cluster the hypotension features at the individual time-point level in this present study.

Recent advances in deep learning, particularly recurrent neural networks (RNNs), have enabled new opportunities for modeling complex time-series data in healthcare. Autoencoders built upon RNN architectures are well-suited for extracting low-dimensional representations of sequential physiological signals, preserving temporal dependencies while reducing redundancy (Dipietro and Hager 7. Compared to the methods used in previous studies [4, 6], RNN could enhance the feature capture and model the intrinsic temporal evolution of hemodynamic parameters, allowing the hypotensive event to be treated as a dynamic, non-independent event(Wang et al. 8.

In this study, we aim to develop an unsupervised learning algorithm based on RNN to identify distinct endotypes of intraoperative hypotension and their evolution, with the goal of informing etiology-driven therapeutic strategies for hypotension during surgery.

Methods

We conducted a retrospective study using the vitalDB database (https://vitaldb.net/), a publicly available repository containing high-fidelity intraoperative physiological data [9]. Surgical patients were screened for the availability of advanced hemodynamic monitoring at the same time, including SVRI, SVR, SVI, SVV, SV, CI, CO, and MAP, with invasive pulse wave analysis. In this study, all these hemodynamic data were monitored using the EV1000 device, a cardiac output monitor (EV1000, Edwards Lifesciences; Irvine, CA, USA). HR was monitored by Solar8000M (Solar 8000 M, GE Medical Systems, UK).

Patients were included to identify endotypes of hypotension if they experienced at least one episode of intraoperative hypotension, defined as a mean arterial pressure (MAP) < 65 mmHg sustained for at least 1 min. Episodes with incomplete or poor-quality data and MAP that does not fall below 65 mmHg for the entire monitoring period or for a duration of less than 1 min were excluded.

Hemodynamic monitoring data

For each identified hypotensive event, hemodynamic variables were extracted at 2-s intervals. The EV1000 has a total of nine monitoring indicators, specifically SVRI, SVR, SVI, SV, SVV, CI, CO, MAP, and CVP. The primary candidate variables were mainly selected based on correlation with the hypotension mechanism and clinical availability. For SVRI and SVR, which reflect systemic vascular resistance, SVRI was considered instead of SVR, as it adjusts for body surface area (BSA) and provides a more individualized assessment of vascular tone. For myocardial contractility, SVI and CI were chosen over SV and CO for the same reason. For volume responsiveness, SVV was chosen primarily because it is more sensitive to fluid responsiveness and accessible than CVP in clinical practice (Chin et al. [10, 11], which facilitates clinical applications. Finally, SVRI, SVV, SVI, HR, and CI were considered as the input feature variables.

Data preprocessing and autoencoder model architecture

Firstly, we normalized all SVRI, SVV, SVI, HR, and CI values to a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1 to give each variable the same potential weight on the results [4]. Then, we developed an RNN autoencoder using LSTM units for both the encoder and decoder components. In this study, temporal dynamic information was incorporated during the autoencoder stage, where LSTM models processed the data using a sliding window (length 10, step 5).

The autoencoder encodes normalised data into a lower, two-dimensional latent space representation. A two-dimensional latent space was chosen for the autoencoder primarily to facilitate visualization and to be applied with clustering algorithms such as k-means, reducing computational complexity. Learning curves of the autoencoder model were performed to show the reconstruction loss. If both curves are converging, it will indicate effective learning with minimal overfitting, suggesting that the model captures meaningful features from the multivariate original data.

The decoder takes as input the low-dimensional representations generated by the encoder, specifically, the two-dimensional latent vectors used for clustering, and reconstructs the original normalised data. This decoding process enables the mapping of each identified hypotension endotype back to its corresponding hemodynamic patterns, thereby facilitating clinical interpretation of the clustering results. The architecture of the autoencoder model is shown in Supplementary Fig. S1. The learning curve of the autoencoder model is shown in Supplementary Fig. S2, demonstrating effective convergence and generalization.

Identify the endotype of hypotension using the cluster method

After dimensionality reduction with the autoencoder, k-means clustering, an unsupervised clustering method, was applied to identify hemodynamic endotypes within the two-dimensional latent space. It aims to minimize the sum of squares between observations and their assigned cluster centers. K-means was selected for its computational efficiency, scalability, and compatibility with Euclidean latent spaces [12], which are typical outputs of a neural network encoder.

Every 10 consecutive data points (corresponding to 20 s) were grouped into one segment, and each segment was treated as an analytical unit for dimensionality reduction and clustering. A range of cluster numbers (2 to 8) was explored. The optimal number of clusters was determined by evaluating both the Calinski–Harabasz (CH) index, which favors well-separated and compact clusters, and the Davies–Bouldin (DB) index, which penalizes high intra-cluster similarity. In a word, a higher CH index and a lower DB index indicate superior clustering performance. The silhouette plot was also employed as a metric to evaluate the clustering quality across different numbers of clusters. The coefficient range of the silhouette scale is from − 1 to 1. A value close to 1 indicates that samples are well-clustered, whereas a value close to 0 suggests that samples lie on the boundary of clusters, and negative values indicate potential misclassification into incorrect clusters.

For sensitivity analysis, alternative clustering methods, hierarchical clustering, and the Gaussian mixture model (GMM) were also considered to detect the robustness of this study in the optimal number of clusters, and it was also determined by evaluating both the CH index and DB index. In addition to k-means clustering, we performed a sensitivity analysis using GMM to test the robustness of the identified endotypes.

Statistical analysis

For continuous variables, this study employs the median and quartiles to summarize the data. For categorical variables, counts and frequencies are used to perform statistical analysis. Box plots and radar charts are used for visualization. Boxplots were computed to illustrate non-normalized (raw data) values of the five hemodynamic variables, SVRI, SVV, SVI, HR, and CI values among different hypotension endotypes. Furthermore, radar charts were employed to provide a comprehensive display of hemodynamic parameters across different hypotension endotypes identified by the k-means clustering method. A heatmap is performed to show the relation of endotype and duration time of hypotension with the surgery procedure. Missing values in SVRI, CI, SVI, HR, and SVV were linearly interpolated over time. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.3; Core Team, Vienna, Austria, 2020), and the RNN model was performed by using the keras library of R software. In this study, we employed unsupervised learning techniques, specifically k-means clustering and an autoencoder, to identify distinct endotypes of hypotension; however, the sample size calculation was not applicable.

Among these, 184 patients experienced at least one episode of intraoperative hypotension (Fig. 1). A total of 1304 hypotensive episodes were recorded, with durations ranging from a minimum of 1 min to a maximum of 38 min. Endotypes of intraoperative hypotension

A total of 253,380 data points recorded during all hypotensive episodes were included for analysis. An autoencoder model was applied to encode the high-dimensional hemodynamic data into a latent two-dimensional space. Based on the CH index and DB index, the optimal number of clusters determined by k-means clustering was five (Fig. 2), indicating five distinct hemodynamic endotypes of intraoperative hypotension. The average silhouette width also indicated that five was the optimal number of intraoperative hypotension endotypes (Supplementary Fig. S5).

Endotypes of intraoperative hypotension visualized in the autoencoderlatent space. A A two-dimensional latent space representation of hypotensive episodes, generated by the autoencoder, with data points clustered into five distinct hypotension endotypes using k-means clustering. Each color represents a different endotype. B Optimal number of endotypes determined using Calinski–Harabasz and Davies–Bouldin index across different cluster numbers. The Calinski–Harabasz index (red) peaked, and the Davies–Bouldin index (blue) reached its minimum at five clusters, indicating that five is the optimal number of hypotension endotypes

These endotypes were characterized and labeled with clinically interpretable names based on their dominant hemodynamic features as follows: (1) severe vasodilation with high CI, (2) hypovolemia, (3) myocardial depression, (4) bradycardia, and (5) mild vasodilation with preserved CI (Fig. 3A). The most common endotype was Hypovolemia, accounting for 447 hypotensive episodes (34%), followed by Bradycardia, with 428 episodes (33%). Mild vasodilation with preserved CI accounted for 258 episodes (20%), and myocardial depression for 137 episodes (11%). The least frequent endotype was severe vasodilation with high CI, comprising 40 episodes (3%) (Fig. 3C). A similar distribution was observed in the number of patients experiencing each endotype of intraoperative hypotension (Fig. 3D).

A retrospective analysis of the distribution of clustering results, endotypes of IOH, during the surgical procedure, showed that Hypovolemia was mainly distributed in the first quartile, but not in the third and fourth. The distribution of bradycardia was similar in the second to fourth quartiles (Fig. 4A, 4). The proportion of mild vasodilatation with preserved CI gradually increased with increasing duration. The heatmap showed that mild vasodilation with preserved CI occurred mainly in the first and second quartiles of the procedure. Bradycardia occurred throughout the procedure, but more frequently in the fourth quartile. Myocardial depression occurred primarily in the second quartile, and hypovolemia occurred frequently throughout the procedure (Fig. 4C). No clear pattern was observed in the intraoperative distribution of hypotensive events with varying duration time (Fig. 4D). The sankey diagram illustrated the intraoperative transitions among different hypotension endotypes (Fig. 4E), demonstrating the dynamic evolution of hemodynamic states during surgery. The evolution of hypotensive endotypes was identified in five independent hypotensive episodes (Supplementary Table S1), one of which had the evolution of three hypotensive endotypes (Supplementary Figure S6).

To visualize the distinct hemodynamic profiles of the five identified hypotension endotypes, radar plots were constructed using five key variables: SVRI, SVV, SVI, HR, and CI (Fig. 3B). The severe vasodilation with high CI endotype was characterized by markedly reduced SVRI and elevated CI, indicating profound vasoplegia with preserved or increased cardiac output. The hypovolemia endotype exhibited increased SVV and reduced SVI, consistent with volume depletion and decreased preload. The myocardial depression endotype showed a reduction in both CI and SVI, reflecting impaired myocardial contractility. The bradycardia endotype was distinguished by significantly lower HR with relatively preserved SVI. The mild vasodilation with preserved CI endotype presented with moderately reduced SVRI but maintained CI and other parameters within near-normal ranges (Table 3).

To evaluate the robustness of the clustering results, hierarchical clustering and GMM were performed as a sensitivity analysis using the same two-dimensional latent space representations generated by the autoencoder. The optimal number of clusters was again determined to be five, consistent with the k-means clustering results. Cluster validity was assessed using both the CH index and DB index, which supported the selection of five clusters as the optimal solution (Supplementary Figs. S3 and S4). Sensitivity analysis with GMM identified endotypes that were similar to those derived from k-means clustering, both in terms of cluster composition and clinical interpretation. This supports the stability and robustness of results (Supplementary Fig. S7 and Table S3).

Discussion

In this study, we identified five endotypes of intraoperative hypotension: (1) severe vasodilation with high CI; (2) hypovolemia; (3) myocardial depression; (4) bradycardia; and (5) mild vasodilation with preserved CI, using an RNN-based autoencoder followed by k-means clustering, an unsupervised machine learning method.

Our analysis identified five clinically interpretable endotypes. Our findings are similar to other work that categorized hypotension into four and six endotypes [4, 6]. The study by Kouz et al. used a traditional unsupervised learning method, hierarchical clustering, to identify six hypotensive endotypes including a heterogeneous “mixed type” with no clearly dominant features in a single-center dataset containing 100 patients undergoing abdominal surgery [4]. Another study used a GMM to identify four hypotensive endotypes [6]. That study failed to differentiate between vasodilatory hypotension with preserved versus elevated CI, two physiologically and clinically distinct states, which are similar to two different types of shock, high output with low resistance phase, and low output with low resistance. Our findings provide a finer resolution of hypotension endotypes, particularly in distinguishing vasodilation severity and cardiac output status. For mild vasodilation with preserved CI, it can be treated by either anesthetic depth adjustment or vasopressor administration or both [13], and small doses of positive inotropes and chronotropes may be compounded [14]. By contrast, potent vasopressor or higher doses are required for severe vasodilation with high CI, whereas positive inotropes and chronotropes may not be necessary [13, 14].

In this study, the optimal number of clusters was determined by evaluating both the CH index and the DB index. In the GMM, the CH index reached its maximum when the number of clusters was four, whereas the DB index was lowest when the number of clusters was five. This suggests that determining the optimal cluster number between four and five may be less straightforward in this method. By contrast, other clustering approaches, k-means and hierarchical clustering, consistently favored five clusters. These discrepancies highlight the influence of different unsupervised algorithms on cluster validation metrics and suggest the need for cautious interpretation when determining the optimal number of endotypes.

In this study, the most prevalent hypotension endotype was hypovolemia (34%), which differs from the findings of Kouz et al. (8%) [4] but aligns with the results reported by Jian et al. (32.1%) [6]. This predominance may be attributed to the prolonged preoperative fasting required for patients undergoing general anesthesia [15, 16], which can result in intravascular volume depletion. Additionally, a moderate proportion (22%) of patients in our cohort underwent hepatic resection, during which restrictive fluid management is commonly practiced [17, 18]. These factors may have contributed to the high incidence of hypovolemia. On the contrary, this study further demonstrated that hypovolaemic hypotension is usually easy to diagnose, more responsive to treatment, and its duration is shorter [3].

The second most common endotype identified was bradycardia (33%), which is consistent with the findings reported by Kouz [4]. Intraoperative bradycardia may result from anesthetic-induced suppression of cardiovascular reflexes [19, 20], administration of vasoactive drugs [21, 22], or surgical stimulation [23]. Even in the absence of reduced stroke volume, bradycardia can lower cardiac output and contribute to hypotension [24–26]. Treatment for intraoperative bradycardia is typically reserved for cases where it is severe or accompanied by hypotension or a significantly reduced CI. Although perioperative bradycardia is relatively common [27–29], it is rarely recognized as a primary cause of intraoperative hypotension. Current clinical management strategies still primarily rely on vasopressors and fluid therapy [3]. Our findings suggest that bradycardia may be an underappreciated but important contributor to hypotension during surgery, especially in the late phase of surgery, this endotype is more frequent. Importantly, it also suggested the need for greater clinical attention and the potential role of positive chronotropic agents in the management of bradycardia-associated hypotension.

Previous studies have often treated hypotensive events as temporally independent, either by sampling data at fixed intervals (e.g., every 15 min) [4]. Such approaches may overlook the dynamic and heterogeneous nature of hypotension. Interestingly, our study identified dynamic transitions between distinct hypotension endotypes that were associated with different hemodynamic features, responding to different pathophysiological mechanisms, despite all presenting as hypotension (MAP < 65 mmHg), throughout the whole surgery. More importantly, we observed intra-event transitions between endotypes within the same hypotensive episode, although it was only noted in five events. It may be attributed to the limited sample and the relatively short duration of most hypotensive episodes. This finding highlights the heterogeneous and evolving nature of intraoperative hypotension, with potential implications for individualized treatment strategies. Moreover, this evolution mainly occurred in long-duration hypotension episodes, suggesting that clinical refractory hypotension should take into account multiple etiological as well as pathophysiological mechanisms. Honestly, our findings also suggested that the main cause of hypotension is not hypovolemia but vascular resistance, heart rate, and myocardial contractility in hypotensive events with a longer duration (> 3 min). This may indicate that vasodilatation, e.g., cryptogenic sepsis, and allergy, should be considered for prolonged hypotension that does not respond well to fluid resuscitation therapy, followed by consideration of bradycardia and underlying myocardial depression.

Etiology-directed therapy represents the most accurate and effective approach to managing perioperative hypotension [4, 6]. The primary goal of treatment is not merely to raise MAP, but to ensure adequate organ perfusion and preserve organ function [13]. Elevating MAP without addressing the underlying hemodynamic disturbance may not improve tissue perfusion [30]. For example, in patients with septic shock, studies have shown that increasing MAP from 65 to 85 mmHg using vasopressin does not enhance sublingual microcirculatory flow or the proportion of perfused capillaries [31–33]. The hypotension endotypes identified in this study may provide clinicians with a more physiologically informed framework for guiding targeted, etiology-specific interventions (Supplementary Table S2). In our study, IOH was defined as MAP < 65 mmHg sustained for at least 1 min. Because our model identifies endotypes based on 20-s segments, an alert can be generated as soon as hypotension persists for 20 s. This implies that our model may provide approximately 40 s f earlier warning for endotype-specific recognition, thereby offering a potential window for timely clinical intervention. However, it is important to acknowledge that the clinical utility of these endotypes still requires further validation in prospective studies. In future research, it would be beneficial to focus on distinguishing between different hypotension endotypes through analysis of traditional invasive arterial waveforms. Additionally, investigating the relationship between various hypotension endotypes and the incidence of AKI could yield valuable insights.

From a physiological perspective, the intraoperative hypotension endotypes identified in this study are clinically interpretable. Although experienced clinicians may differentiate these hemodynamic patterns by interpreting advanced monitoring parameters, artificial intelligence (AI) offers a distinct advantage by enabling real-time, automated recognition of hypotension endotypes without increasing cognitive burden [34, 35]. This is particularly valuable for supporting less experienced clinicians and enhancing consistency in the diagnosis and management of hypotension. Notably, AI has increasingly been applied in medicine and profoundly changed clinical practice, including perioperative care and critical care medicine [36]. Our findings lay the groundwork for AI-assisted hemodynamic guidance during surgery. The proposed model could be integrated into existing hemodynamic monitoring systems to provide early warnings and support clinical decision-making. Moreover, continuous monitoring of advanced variables such as CI, SVV, SVI, and SVRI is expensive, and recent developments have shown that such variables can be estimated from standard arterial pressure waveforms using specific algorithms [37]. When combined with our model, that approach could enhance the utility of conventional arterial monitoring and reduce healthcare fees without compromising clinical insight.

In this study, we included only 184 patients with IOH out of 242 monitored, representing a modest cohort compared with the size of the VitalDB dataset. We did not perform external validation using an independent dataset. Notably, nearly half of our patients had pre-existing hypertension, which may alter blood pressure autoregulation and potentially affect both the definition of IOH and the identification of endotypes. Collectively, these factors may limit the generalizability of our findings and highlight the need for future studies with larger sample sizes, external validation, and stratification by baseline comorbidities to confirm and refine the proposed endotypes.

In our study, although the hypovolemia endotype showed the highest SVV values, the median (11%) was only modestly elevated. This may reflect intraoperative fluid and vasopressor management that prevented more extreme hypovolemia. Likewise, the median HR of 64 bpm in the bradycardia endotype appears relatively high, but even modest reductions in HR during anesthesia may be clinically relevant. These findings emphasize that intraoperative physiology often presents in attenuated forms due to ongoing interventions.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, it was a retrospective analysis based on data derived from Seoul National University Hospital, and the sample size is limited. Secondly, although the autoencoder effectively reduced the dimensionality of time-series hemodynamic data, the interpretability of latent features remains limited, and the model’s behavior may vary under different input preprocessing schemes. Thirdly, SVI was used as a surrogate marker of myocardial contractility, as direct measurements such as the maximum rate of pressure rise in the ventricle (dP/dt max) were not available in our dataset. SVI has been widely accepted in clinical practice as a reasonable proxy of myocardial contractility [38]. Furthermore, clustering was performed retrospectively on 20-s segments, which is a limitation. From another perspective, this short interval also allows for near-real-time recognition of IOH endotypes. In clinical practice, this provides timely insights that could guide early interventions. Future work should focus on optimizing the model for a fully real-time application.

In addition, each IOH episode was analyzed as an independent event. However, we acknowledge that multiple episodes within the same patient may be correlated, which could influence clustering and subsequent interpretation. Future research should incorporate modeling approaches that account for within-patient dependence to improve robustness and clinical applicability.

Moreover, a previous study reported that using dP/dt max instead of SVI to represent myocardial contractility did not alter the classification outcomes and ultimately excluded dP/dt max from their analysis as well [6]. Lastly, future studies are needed to validate the clinical relevance of the identified intraoperative hypotension endotypes, especially the association with postoperative outcomes, and to determine whether endotype-guided therapeutic strategies can reduce the incidence of postoperative complications.

Conclusion

This study identified five endotypes of intraoperative hypotension: severe vasodilation with high CI, hypovolemia, myocardial depression, bradycardia, and mild vasodilation with preserved CI. These endotypes provide a foundation for etiology-directed therapy of intraoperative hypotension if it is validated.

Additional file 1: Fig. S1. Architecture of the recurrent autoencoder used for dimensionality reduction. The model was designed to process multivariate time-series data of intraoperative hypotension. Raw hemodynamic signals were first preprocessed using normalization. The normalized sequences were then passed through an input layer followed by a masking layer to handle padded time steps. The encoder consisted of two long short-term memory (LSTM) layer that captured temporal dependencies, followed by a dense bottleneck layer that projected the input into a two-dimensional latent space. The decoder mirrored the encoder structure in reverse, reconstructing the original sequence from the latent representation. This architecture allowed the model to learn compact, denoised representations of hypotensive episodes suitable for unsupervised clustering. Fig. S2. Training and validation loss during autoencoder learning. Learning curves of the autoencoder model during training, showing the reconstruction loss on the training set (blue) and validation set (red) across epochs. The convergence of both curves indicates effective learning with minimal overfitting, suggesting that the model captures meaningful temporal representations from multivariate hemodynamic time-series data. Fig. S3. Optimal number of intraoperative hypotension endotypes determined by unsupervised clustering. The Calinski–Harabasz index (red) and Davies–Bouldin index (blue) were used to evaluate the optimal number of clusters (hypotension endotypes) based on the autoencoder-derived latent space. Hierarchical clustering was applied. The plot demonstrates that five clusters yielded the highest Calinski–Harabasz index and the lowest Davies–Bouldin index, indicating that five was the optimal number of distinct hypotension endotypes in this dataset. Fig. S4. Optimal number of intraoperative hypotension endotypes determined by unsupervised clustering. The Calinski–Harabasz index (red) and Davies–Bouldin index (blue) were used to evaluate the optimal number of clusters (hypotension endotypes) based on the autoencoder-derived latent space. Gaussian mixture model was applied. The plot demonstrates that five clusters yielded the second highest Calinski–Harabasz index and the lowest Davies–Bouldin index, indicating that five was the one of the optimal number of distinct hypotension endotypes in this dataset.Fig. S5. Optimal number of hypotension endotypes using silhouette plot. Silhouette coefficients were calculated for different numbers of clusters (k) using k-means clustering in the latent space derived from the autoencoder. The silhouette score peaked at k = 5, indicating that five clusters yielded the most coherent and well-separated hypotension endotypes. Fig. S6. llustrative example of intraoperative hypotension endotype evolution within a single episode. This schematic shows a representative hypotensive event in which the hemodynamic endotype evolves sequentially from myocardial depression to hypovolemia, and then to bradycardia over time. Each endotype reflects a distinct hemodynamic profile, highlighting the dynamic nature and potential pathophysiological progression of hypotension during surgery. MAP: mean artery pressure; BP: blood pressure. Fig. S7. Box plots showing the distribution (median and interquartile range) of five original (non-normalized) hemodynamic variables, across the five hypotension endotypes identified by GMM.
