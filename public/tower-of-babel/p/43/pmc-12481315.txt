Association between household food insecurity and underweight status among women in flood-prone regions of Bangladesh: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Sharif Uddin Lotus, Shahriar Manik Akash, Nishat Salsabil, Md Takrib Hossain, Showrov Sarker, Zubayer Ibn Zaman, Rajiul Kawnine, Kazi Sazzadul Haque, M Z E M Naser Uddin Ahmed, Ahmed Hossain
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesBangladesh is highly prone to recurrent flooding that disrupts all four pillars of food security. This study aimed to explore the effect of household food insecurity on the underweight status of women in flood-affected areas of Bangladesh, which remains underexplored.DesignThis is a cross-sectional analysis.SettingThis study was conducted in eight sub-districts (upazilas) across eight districts in Bangladesh that experience severe to moderate river flooding, flash floods and substantial tidal surges.ParticipantsA total of 532 women participated in the study. The inclusion criteria for participation were as follows: (1) being at least 18 years of age, (2) residing in the household for at least 1 year and (3) having experienced limited food access in the 4 weeks before data collection due to flood-related constraints.Primary outcome measuresHousehold food insecurity was measured using the U.S. Agency for International Development Household Food Insecurity Access Scale questionnaire. An underweight status was evaluated through anthropometric measurements of women. Adjusted prevalence ratios (aPRs) were estimated using robust log-linear models.ResultsModerate food insecurity was the most common (58.3%) among the participants. The prevalence of underweight was the highest (52.1%) in the severely food-insecure group and decreased significantly with improved food security. Severe household food insecurity was strongly associated with a higher prevalence of underweight individuals (aPR = 4.12; 95% CI, 1.60 to 10.60). An underweight status was also prevalent in women from moderately food-insecure households (aPR = 1.75; 95% CI, 0.68 to 4.55).ConclusionThis study reveals a significant association between household food insecurity and underweight status, highlighting the major challenges faced by women living in flood-prone areas of Bangladesh. These findings emphasise the urgent need to address household food insecurity to improve nutritional outcomes for women in vulnerable communities.

Introduction

Bangladesh is the most densely populated and disaster-prone country in the world, frequently experiencing recurrent flooding.13 Most of Bangladesh lies in the delta of three major rivers, the Ganges, Brahmaputra and Meghna (GBM basins), characterised by low, flat topography. Flooding results from a complex mix of factors: massive upstream water inflows combined with heavy monsoon rains, low-lying floodplains, congested drainage, river convergence, sudden intense rainfall, coastal tidal surges and polders that worsen flooding outside protected areas.4 Bangladesh experiences four main flood types: flash, monsoon, river and coastal floods, each varying by cause and timing. Flash floods occur from April to May in the northeast due to heavy rainfall runoff from upland areas. Monsoon floods, triggered by intense rain, affect the southwest between April and August. River floods, caused by Himalayan snowmelt and monsoon rains, affect the GBM basins from April to September. Coastal floods occur from June to September in the southwestern coast due to storm surges and cyclone-related tidal flooding.4

Although flooding can have some beneficial effects,5 6 it is one of the most devastating natural disasters, causing substantial harm to lives, economies and ecosystems.7 8 A study showed that approximately 60% of Bangladesh’s population faces increased flood risk, with 45% exposed to fluvial flooding – the highest proportion globally.9 From 1954 to 2021, frequent severe floods have devastated infrastructure, livelihoods, housing, health and education, especially food security.10 11 For instance, the 1954 flood affected 55% of the country, whereas the 1974 flood caused a famine resulting in over 30 000 deaths.12 13 Floods in 1987 and 1988 submerged over half the country, displaced millions and caused over US$1 billion in damages each.14 The catastrophic 1998 flood inundated nearly 100 000 sq. km, killed 1100 people and left 30 million homeless.12 Floods in 2004, 2007 and 2008 destroyed hundreds of thousands of homes, affected millions and caused billions in losses.1517 More recently, the 2017 flood affected 8 million people, killed 145 and destroyed crops, homes and schools.18 The 2019 flood affected 7.6 million people and damaged over 6600 km of roads and 1275 bridges.12 In 2020, over 5.4 million people were affected, with 1.27 million homes damaged and >220 deaths reported.19 20 Smaller-scale floods in 2021 still caused major disruption, damaging thousands of hectares of cropland and displacing large populations.21 22

In Bangladesh, flood-prone families rely heavily on agriculture. Seasonal floods extensively damage crops and stored food, sharply reducing food availability at both household and community levels. Despite improvements in national food production, these climatic shocks continue to severely disrupt food availability.2326 Flooding not only results in direct economic losses but also disrupts transportation and market systems, limiting both physical and financial access to food.17 25 26 Additionally, inflation, income loss and damaged infrastructure further reduce households’ purchasing power and access to adequate nutrition.27 28 Floods often contaminate water sources and damage sanitation, increasing waterborne disease risk. These health issues impair nutrient absorption, raising underweight rates, especially among vulnerable groups like women and children.29 Frequent climatic shocks negatively affect long-term food security by reducing household resilience.30 31 In areas prone to flooding, continuous flooding results in chronic food insecurity and persistent nutritional deficiencies.32 Recently, Integrated Food Security Phase Classification (IPC) reports on Bangladesh clearly highlight how flooding and related climatic events affect all four pillars of food security – availability, access, utilisation and stability.32 These disruptions force households, especially women, to adopt negative coping mechanisms, such as reducing meal frequency and dietary diversity, which in turn increases the risk of underweight.33

Globally, women are disproportionately affected by food insecurity compared with men,34 as they are more likely to skip meals or eat smaller portions.35 Women in rural areas of low-income and middle-income countries, including Bangladesh, face a heightened risk of adverse effects from household food insecurity (HFI) due to sex-based disparities in food distribution.36 Research shows that HFI is associated with maternal underweight among reproductive-aged and breastfeeding women.37 38 Motbainor et al reported a significant correlation between food insecurity and low mid-upper arm circumference among non-pregnant women of reproductive age.39 In this context, understanding HFI is vital, as it plays a central role in shaping nutritional outcomes and overall health, especially for women living in flood-prone and resource-constrained areas like Bangladesh. Food insecurity is defined as having insufficient or uncertain access to adequate food for an active, healthy life.40 HFI is a serious public health concern owing to its significant effect on health and nutrition outcomes.41

Several studies in the Bangladeshi context have investigated the effects of climate-related shocks, especially flooding, on agricultural yields,14244 maternal health4548 and food insecurity.30 49 Despite the well-established links between flooding and food security, as well as between food insecurity and nutritional outcomes, there is a notable gap in research specifically exploring the relationship between HFI and an underweight status among women residing in flood-prone areas. This study aimed to explore this relationship among women in flood-affected areas of Bangladesh.

Methods and materials

Study site

In August 2020, a cross-sectional survey was carried out shortly after the usual monsoon floods in this geographical region. The survey employed quantitative methods to assess the relationship between HFI and an underweight status among women (aged ≥18 years) living in flood-prone areas. This study was conducted in eight flood-prone districts of Bangladesh: Dinajpur, Kurigram, Kushtia, Madaripur, Noakhali, Pabna, Shariatpur and Manikganj. These districts were purposefully selected using the flood-prone area map developed by the Bangladesh Agricultural Research Council (BARC). The selection was based on their classification as areas experiencing severe to moderate river flooding, flash floods and significant tidal surges.50

Sample size estimation

The sample size was calculated for women aged ≥18 years living in selected flood-prone areas. Using the formula n = z²*[pq/d²], where n is the desired sample size, z is the CI (1.96 for 95%), p is the estimated population proportion (0.5), q is (1−p=0.5) and d is the margin of error (0.05), the sample size was determined. Based on the 2020 monsoon flood, rapid sex analysis report from ReliefWeb,51 the estimated population proportion was 50%. Applying this, the estimated sample size was n = 1.962* 0.5* (0.5) / (0.05)², yielding n=384.16. After adjusting for a 10% non-response rate, the estimated sample size was 423. Ultimately, 532 women from flood-prone areas were identified and surveyed.

Sampling and data collection

Data collection was conducted using a multistage sampling strategy with purposive selection at the first stage.52 Initially, eight districts were purposefully selected based on the flood-prone area map developed by BARC. Following district selection, one sub-district (upazila) was randomly chosen from each of the eight districts. Subsequently, a minimum of 67 households per selected upazila were systematically sampled, targeting a total of 532 women for the study. Household selection began with a randomly selected household near the geographical centre of the upazila. Data collectors then proceeded to neighbouring households sequentially until the target of 67 households per upazila was reached. Within each household, one eligible woman was surveyed using a semi-structured questionnaire. Eligibility criteria included residing in the household for at least 1 year and having experienced limited food access in the 4 weeks before data collection due to flood-related constraints. Women who were pregnant (n=20), had comorbidities (n=10) or did not comply with data collection procedures (n=56) were excluded. Before the data collection began, the data collectors contacted local government officials and community leaders to explain the purpose and procedures of the study. The questionnaire was originally developed in English and subsequently translated into Bangla. Trained data collectors verbally administered the survey to eligible participants.

Several strategies were employed to mitigate potential biases throughout the data collection period. This included the random selection of sub-districts and the systematic household sampling, following predefined sampling procedures and using trained data collectors to ensure consistency. Multiple visits were conducted to maximise participant engagement. Experienced senior researchers closely monitored all procedures to reduce the risk of potential selection and response biases.

Training and pilot testing

A skilled team of four data collectors received comprehensive training in anthropometric measurement techniques, accurate field data recording and maintaining neutrality. They were also educated about ethical issues, privacy concerns and cultural sensitivity. The training and evaluation were conducted by a senior public health researcher with extensive experience in community-based nutritional assessments and quantitative data collection. To evaluate the effectiveness of the tools and techniques and address potential challenges during the survey, a pilot study was conducted. The aim was to assess the ability of data collectors to understand the relevant techniques and handle challenging situations during the survey. Necessary adjustments were made based on observations from the piloting. Subsequently, the data collectors were divided into two groups and then proceeded to their assigned upazilas to begin data collection.

Household Food Insecurity Access Scale

The Household Food Insecurity Access Scale (HFIAS) is a standardised tool adapted to measure the prevalence of food insecurity within households and track changes in food access over time.53 It comprises two types of related questions. The first set includes nine occurrence questions, which ask whether specific food insecurity-related conditions were experienced in the past 4 weeks (30 days). Each of these is followed by a frequency-of-occurrence question to determine how often the condition occurred during that period. The nine-item questionnaire addresses three key dimensions of food insecurity related to access. One item assesses anxiety or uncertainty about household food supply. Three items focus on the inadequacy of food quality, including limited variety and unmet preferences. The remaining five items capture insufficient food intake and the physical consequences of food deprivation. The HFIAS uses a 4-week recall period to provide a comprehensive understanding of food insecurity at the household level.

Households are categorised into four levels of food insecurity based on their access to food: food secure, mildly food insecure, moderately food insecure and severely food insecure.53 A food-secure household does not experience any food insecurity and rarely worries about food availability. Mildly food-insecure households may sometimes or often worry about having enough food.53 They may be unable to eat their preferred foods, have a monotonous diet or occasionally consume less desirable foods. However, they do not reduce the overall quantity of food they eat or experience the most severe levels of food deprivation. Households that are moderately food insecure frequently compromise on food quality.53 They often eat monotonous or undesirable foods and may occasionally reduce meal sizes or skip meals; however, they do not face the most extreme forms of deprivation. Severely food-insecure households regularly reduce meal sizes and skip meals.53 They may also experience the most severe conditions, such as running out of food, going to bed hungry or going an entire day and night without eating. A household is classified as severely food insecure if it encounters one or more of these severe conditions at least once in the past 30 days.

Outcome measurement

In this study, underweight was the primary outcome, assessed through anthropometric measurements of women. Data on key anthropometric indicators were collected using questionnaires adapted from Bangladesh Demographic and Health Surveys (eg, BDHS 2017) and similar studies, which were carefully translated into Bangla and culturally tailored with modified wording and examples to ensure clarity and relevance. Weight and height were measured using calibrated digital weighing scales (Omron HN-289) and portable stadiometers (SECA 213) to calculate the body mass index (BMI), which is defined as weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in metres (kg/m²). BMI is a widely accepted indicator of nutritional status (WHO, 2004). Based on BMI classification criteria from the American Cancer Society, participants were categorised as being underweight (BMI < 18.5), having a normal weight (BMI 18.5–24.9) or being overweight/obese (BMI ≥ 25).

Statistical analysis

The study’s technical team managed data entry and verification, ensuring accuracy through careful review, processing and analysis using STATA version 15.0 SE. Data were double-entered and cross-checked to maintain consistency and reliability. Univariate and multivariable analyses were performed. Categorical variables are reported as frequencies and percentages, whereas continuous variables are summarised using means and SD. HFI was measured using the HFIAS. The HFIAS score, ranging from 0 to 27, was derived by adding responses to nine standard questions, with higher scores indicating greater levels of food insecurity (figure 1).53 Based on the Household Food Insecurity Access Prevalence (HFIAP) indicator, households were categorised into four levels: food secure, mildly food insecure, moderately food insecure and severely food insecure.53 Households were considered food insecure if they reported more severe conditions and higher frequencies of food insecurity experiences. To assess the relationship between underweight and HFI, prevalence ratios (PRs) and adjusted prevalence ratios (aPRs) were estimated using a robust log-linear model. For adjusted PRs, each variable in the model has been adjusted for all other variables. After conducting surveys with 20 women, we began the analysis to enhance reliability and validity.

Prevalence based on the nine-item Household Food Insecurity Access Prevalence (HFIAP) scale.

Quality control and assurance

Quality control and assurance were conducted at several stages throughout the study. First, data collectors reviewed completed forms on the same day of data collection to detect any discrepancies or issues, revisiting households if necessary to correct errors. A second quality check took place later that day, during which inconsistencies were identified and resolved. During data entry, range and consistency checks were performed before the cleaned data were transferred to STATA for analysis. Ethical considerations included providing participants with a clear explanation of the study’s objectives, potential risks and benefits.

Ethical permission

The North South University Institutional Review Board granted ethical approval for this study (Ref: 2020/OR-NSU/1204). Informed consent was obtained from all participants, ensuring that their participation was voluntary and free from undue influence and that they had the option to withdraw at any stage of the study. Prior to giving consent, all participants were thoroughly informed about the study’s objectives, methods, procedures and the implications of their participation. Written consent was obtained through signatures from participants with formal education and thumbprints from those without formal education. Only participants who provided consent were included in the study. All collected data have been protected, and strict measures were implemented to ensure participant confidentiality and anonymity throughout the processes of data collection, analysis and interpretation.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

The characteristics of participants by the four categories of HFI are given in table 1. The prevalence of moderate food insecurity was higher (58.3%) than that of severe and mild food insecurity in flood-prone areas of Bangladesh. Severe food insecurity was more common among individuals aged 25–34 years (44.4%). Within this group, educational attainment varied, with 36.1% having completed secondary education or higher and 34.0% having no formal education. A large proportion of individuals from severely food-insecure households were either married (62.5%) or widowed/divorced/separated (31.3%). This group also tended to come from poorer households (61.8%) and had higher chronic disease prevalence (56.3%). An underweight status was most prevalent among the severely food-insecure households (52.1%), decreasing progressively with improved food security; none of the individuals from food-secure households were underweight. The mean age (SD) of the 532 participants was 32.35 (7.9 years), and their household size was 4.40 (1.9 persons). Significant differences (p<0.05) were found in food security levels based on participants’ characteristics. Factors such as educational level, marital status, number of living children, number of income-generating individuals, source of income, wealth index, chronic disease status and BMI demonstrated strong associations with food security levels (p<0.001). The age group showed marginal significance (p=0.052), whereas household size was not significantly linked to food security levels (p=0.849).

The chart presents the prevalence of various food insecurity experiences based on the HFIAP tool (figure 1). A striking majority of households reported concerns about food availability, with 86.09% worrying about not having enough to eat and 85.90% unable to access their preferred foods. In contrast, more severe forms of food insecurity were relatively rare, with only 2.44% going an entire day and night without eating, 1.32% going to bed hungry and 3.20% having no food at all.

The PRs for underweight, both unadjusted and adjusted, are detailed in table 2. In the adjusted analysis, women aged 25–34 years had a significantly lower prevalence of underweight, showing a 34% reduction (aPR=0.66; 95% CI, 0.47 to 0.92). Similarly, women aged ≥45 years had a 41% lower prevalence (aPR=0.59; 95% CI, 0.36 to 0.97) than women aged 18–24 years. Marital status was strongly associated with an underweight status. Compared with unmarried women, married ones had a 41% lower prevalence (aPR=0.59; 95% CI, 0.41 to 0.87), whereas widowed, divorced or separated women had a 50% lower prevalence (aPR=0.50; 95% CI, 0.30 to 0.81). Wealth status did not show a significant association with an underweight status. Women in the middle wealth tertile had a prevalence similar to those in the poorest or poorer groups (aPR=1.02; 95% CI, 0.70 to 1.49), whereas those in the richer or richest group had a slightly higher, but non-significant, prevalence (aPR=1.23; 95% CI, 0.87 to 1.75). Women with a chronic disease were 27% less likely to be underweight than those without a chronic disease (aPR=0.73; 95% CI, 0.56 to 0.97). Food insecurity was significantly associated with an underweight status; compared with women with mild food insecurity, those from severely food-insecure households had four times the prevalence of being underweight (aPR=4.12; 95% CI, 1.60 to 10.60). Women from moderately food-insecure households also had a higher prevalence of underweight (aPR=1.75; 95% CI, 0.68 to 4.55), although this result was not significant. Factors such as education, number of household members, number of living children and number of income-generating individuals did not show a significant association with an underweight status.

Discussion

Our findings revealed a significant association between severe HFI and an increased prevalence of underweight women living in flood-prone areas. Consistent with previous studies, HFI strongly correlates with underweight status among women in vulnerable settings. In Ethiopia’s Afar region, which often faces droughts and flooding, nearly three-fourths of households had food insecurity, and nearly half of the women were underweight, showing a significant association.37 Similarly, in flood-affected communities in Nepal, about one in four women from severely food-insecure households were underweight, whereas about one in eight women from less food-secure households were underweight.54 On Malaysia’s Tuba Island, which faces regular environmental challenges, including flooding, women experiencing food insecurity were found to have insufficient consumption of essential nutrients, linking food insecurity to poor nutritional status.55 These findings suggest that food insecurity affects both the quantity and quality of food intake, greatly increasing the risk of underweight among women. In the present study, severe HFI was strongly associated with an underweight status among women in Bangladesh’s flood-prone regions. This strong association may be attributed to the disruption of regular sources of income due to recurrent flooding, which reduces households’ ability to purchase adequate and nutritious food.30 Moreover, flood-affected households often alter their dietary habits, consuming less diverse and less nutrient-rich foods, which exacerbates the risk of becoming underweight.49 Hence, our results highlight the urgent need for interventions addressing both economic stability and dietary improvements to mitigate underweight associated with food insecurity in these regions.

Another significant factor influencing an underweight status among women in flood-prone areas was age. Women aged 25–34 years were less likely to be underweight compared with those aged 18–24 years, consistent with the findings of other studies.5659 Unawareness about their own health and nutritional status among adolescent women may contribute to the higher prevalence of underweight.60 Limited access to healthcare and sanitation during floods increases susceptibility to infections, which further impairs nutrient absorption and contributes to underweight conditions.61 Furthermore, many adolescent girls come from socioeconomically disadvantaged households, which are disproportionately affected by floods, thereby limiting their ability to access adequate nutrition.62 Women who had never been married were more likely to be underweight than married women. Other studies have reported similar results.6365 In flood-prone areas, married women often benefit from more stable pooled resources and social support, helping in easing food shortages caused by flooding. In contrast, unmarried women are more vulnerable to food insecurity-induced underweight because of limited economic and social buffers, as shown by demographic surveys in Bangladesh and other flood-affected regions.6668 This vulnerability stems from cultural norms and limited decision-making autonomy, with many women lacking access to higher education and well-paying jobs, making them dependent on others for adequate nutrition.69 70

In this study, a strong association was found between the source of income and HFI, particularly for households whose primary income sources were affected by floods. This finding is consistent with earlier reports from Bangladesh and similar regions, demonstrating that agricultural dependence significantly increases households’ vulnerability to seasonal floods that devastate crops and food supplies.2326 Study findings showed that households that are primarily dependent on agriculture and day labour were more likely to experience food insecurity, with >88% and 94% of respondents in these groups falling into either the severely or moderately food-insecure categories. In contrast, families supported by government or private jobs exhibited higher food security, with nearly 39% categorised as food-secured and only 8% facing severe food insecurity. These results suggest that reliance on irregular, low-paying or seasonal income sources such as agriculture and daily labour substantially increases vulnerability to food insecurity, whereas employment with stable salary offers a protective effect. This observation aligns with findings from the IPC in Bangladesh.32 While the source of income had a significant effect on HFI, it did not show a significant association with the underweight status of women in our adjusted model. This may be due to the complex interplay of environmental, health and social factors in flood-prone areas that influence how income affects nutritional outcomes.29 30 33 49 Additionally, factors such as intra-household food distribution, quality of income and burden of chronic diseases are likely to complicate this relationship.29 35 37 38

Similarly, a large proportion of women suffering from severe food insecurity came from low-wealth families, highlighting their intense financial struggles.55 71 Our findings suggest that household wealth index significantly affects food insecurity but does not show a statistically significant association with women’s underweight status, which is consistent with existing evidence. While wealth reliably predicts food insecurity risk in low-income settings, its independent effect on women’s underweight status is not universally significant across contexts, especially after adjusting for other sociodemographic variables such as age, education and region. Additionally, wealth may influence underweight indirectly through food insecurity, which was included as a separate covariate in the model, potentially absorbing its explanatory power. However, the connection between severe food insecurity and higher rates of underweight clearly indicates that financial hardship often leads to inadequate dietary intake. This pattern aligns with global studies, which consistently identify poverty as a key driver of food insecurity, particularly in ecologically vulnerable regions.5 72 73

HFI showed a clear association with education, suggesting that lower educational attainment limits access to resources and knowledge related to food security. Similar findings have been reported in studies where education influences nutrition primarily through socioeconomic status and food security rather than having an independent direct effect on the underweight status.1 74 However, in the adjusted model, an underweight status among women was not significantly associated with education. This discrepancy may be attributed to several inter-related factors. Although education contributes to reducing food insecurity, its direct effect on underweight may be overshadowed by more immediate determinants such as household food access and economic factors.75

Although having a chronic disease usually increases health risks, this study found that women with chronic diseases were less likely to be underweight. Although a severe underweight status increases the risk of adverse health outcomes such as stroke, myocardial infarction and death,76 this inverse association in our data may reflect complex interactions involving healthcare access, disease management, nutritional interventions or survival bias.

Several study limitations should be acknowledged. As this study employed a cross-sectional design, it cannot conclusively claim whether HFI leads to underweight or if the reverse is true. The analysis cannot determine women-specific effects, as the survey did not include male participants for comparison. In addition, the relatively small sample size necessitates caution when interpreting some results, which may limit the applicability of the findings to a broader population. Furthermore, the survey was conducted shortly after a typical monsoon flood, which may have influenced respondents to emphasise recent hardships, potentially skewing perceptions of food insecurity. This timing may have introduced response bias; therefore, findings should be interpreted with caution regarding temporality and generalisability.

Nevertheless, this study provides valuable insights into how environmental challenges affect women’s health, clearly highlighting the connection between HFI and an underweight status in flood-prone areas. By concentrating on a particularly vulnerable and often under-represented group of women in Bangladesh, this study tackles a timely and socially significant issue, which remains a key strength of our work.

Conclusion

The study reveals a significant association between HFI and an underweight status, highlighting the major challenges faced by women living in flood-prone areas of Bangladesh. The results show that food insecurity seriously affects nutritional status, indicating that women who are underweight are more likely to experience high levels of food insecurity. These findings emphasise the urgent need to directly address HFI to improve nutritional outcomes for women in vulnerable communities. By providing targeted nutritional and food security interventions, women in disadvantaged flood-affected communities can strengthen their resilience against ongoing environmental and food access challenges.
