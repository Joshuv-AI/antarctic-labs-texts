Dilemmas and strategies in the working experience of resident training nurses rotated in the intensive care unit: a qualitative study
Authors: Ruike Zhang, Daxing Shao, Haifeng Huang, Yichun Zhang, Jing Lin, Wenping Wu, Haiyan Liang, Rongli Lian, Song Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn high-pressure environments such as intensive care units (ICUs), newly graduated nurses often experience heightened transition shock during role adaptation, which increases the risk of burnout and turnover. Consequently, facilitating a smooth transition for nurses during their ICU rotations is a persistent challenge for nursing managers and educators.AimThe purpose is to explore the dilemmas and strategies perceived by resident training nurses during their ICU rotation work.MethodsA qualitative study was conducted. Nineteen resident training nurses who rotated in the ICU were recruited. Face-to-face interviews, semi-structured interviews, and content analysis were utilized to analyze the data.ResultsFour key themes were identified: (1) Experiencing Overwhelming Work Tasks; (2) Experiencing a Fast-Paced Working Pattern; (3) Experiencing a Strained Working Atmosphere; and (4) Experiencing a Generalized Teaching Program. The subthemes of each theme reflect the unique nature of ICU work as perceived by the rotating nurses, corresponding to their perceptions of specific work dilemmas and the strategies they have derived.ConclusionsThe resident training nurses experienced a complex journey of disorientation and adaptation during their ICU rotation. This can be attributed to overwhelming work tasks, fast-paced work patterns, strained work atmospheres, and generalized teaching programs. Nursing managers and educators need to optimize the teaching system for resident training nurses rotated in the ICU and enhance the humanistic care mechanisms for nurses at the organizational level.Keywords: Transition shock, Resident training nurses, Intensive care unit, Qualitative study, Clinical adaptation, Nursing education

Background

Transition Shock is a concept that encapsulates the negative experiences of individuals who fail to shift their roles when faced with various situations and cultures [1]. For graduates, there is a gap between nursing students and professional nurses, as their work environments and roles are significantly different [2]. Research has shown that the first one to three years of a working career are a critical period during which newly graduated nurses’ psychosomatic health is particularly susceptible to the stresses of transition [3, 4]. Poor transition makes it difficult for graduates to perform nursing work as formal nurses and causes them to lose faith in their career progression [5]. Reality shock significantly endangers the professional identity of newly graduated nurses and also increases their turnover and willingness to resign from the nursing profession [1].

In China, as the cornerstone of residency training, the rotation system has been implemented to reduce transition shock among new nurses. In addition to passing relevant theoretical and practical examinations, new nurses must also pass regular rotational assessments to qualify as formal nursing staff [6]. However, the frequent changes in work specialties, situations, shifts, and contents during rotations may induce reality shock, leading them to experience exhaustion and a sense of dislocation [7].

As the highest level of life support, the Intensive Care Unit (ICU) is the optimal environment for novice nurses to learn to observe critically ill patients, perform resuscitation, and utilize various instruments and equipment [8]. Meanwhile, the complex ICU environment, with its multitude of instruments and devices, and frequent resuscitations, means that the benefits and risks of rotating staff also coexist. Results from a meta-analysis indicate that depression or depressive symptoms are more prevalent among ICU nurses than among general ward nurses, with an overall prevalence rate of approximately 24.99% [9]. As clinical novices, rotating nurses are at a higher risk for psychological distress due to the highly specialized and complex nature of the ICU [4].

Ineffective coping with the high intensity of ICU work is considered the primary cause of transition shock among residency training nurses rotating in the ICU [10]. To facilitate the better adaptation of these ICU novices to clinical work, the dilemmas and needs they perceive during their ICU rotation should be identified and targeted for refinement of the current nurse residency training system [11]. From the perspectives of nursing education and management, this study utilized a qualitative approach to investigate the real dilemmas encountered by resident nurses during their ICU rotations and to uncover the coping strategies they utilize or consider effective. The purpose of this study is to provide valuable insights for nursing managers and educators that could incorporate the subjective needs of rotating nurses into future educational reforms. The ultimate goal of these findings is to facilitate a seamless transition for resident nurses from nursing students to nursing professionals, thereby sustaining their enthusiasm for clinical work.

Methods

Design

This study employed a descriptive qualitative approach to investigate the work experiences of residency training nurses during their ICU rotation. Semi-structured interviews are frequently utilized in this design to deeply capture individuals’ feelings, thoughts, and inner emotions, thereby offering a comprehensive and descriptive summary of the research phenomenon of interest [12]. Thus, the design is suitable for this study to extract and analyze, in depth, the dilemmas and strategies encountered by residency training nurses during their ICU rotations.

Recruitment and sample

This study was conducted in the surgical ICU of a university-affiliated, tertiary, first-class general hospital in Guangdong, China. The unit consists of 19 open beds and is staffed by a team of 48 nurses and 19 doctors. This specialized unit is dedicated to providing comprehensive care to postoperative or severely traumatized ICU patients. In this hospital, the residency training and unit rotation of new nurses are conducted in accordance with the “Training Guide for Newly Admitted Nurses (for Trial Implementation)” developed by the General Office of the China National Health and Family Planning Commission [6]. The duration of nurse residency training spans two years. When a nurse rotates through the ICU, the first month is spent under the one-to-one supervision of a mentor. Nurses must complete specialty training and pass related exams before they can be assigned patients to perform primary nursing care independently.

Purposive sampling was used to select nurses with maximum differentiation who were rotating in the Surgical ICU of this hospital from September 2023 to February 2024. Inclusion criteria: nurses undergoing the residency training period; informed consent to participate in this study; and more than 3 months of working experience in the ICU. Our researchers conducted a purposive selection, taking into account the residency training nurses’ gender, age, education, title, and work experience to ensure a diverse background among the study participants. Unlike convenience sampling, purposive sampling allows for a more heterogeneous group, which enhances the representativeness, objectivity, and comprehensiveness of the study results. This diversity in demographic characteristics enables a deeper exploration of the ICU rotating experiences [13]. The saturation of the sample size is determined by the fact that no new themes emerged from the content of the interviews.

A total of 19 residency training nurses were ultimately interviewed, numbered N1 through N19. The mean age of the participants was 24.42 ± 1.54 years. They were predominantly female (73.68%), held a bachelor’s degree (89.47%), held the title of Nurse (57.89%), had experience two rotations in different units (57.89%); the majority were from other provinces (73.68%). Their work duration ranged from 4 to 22 months (mean 14.28 ± 7.26 months), and their ICU work duration ranged from 4 to 8 months (mean 5.29 ± 1.16 months). Data collection

Face-to-face semi-structured interviews for residency training nurses on rotation in the intensive care unit were conducted by one of the researchers. The recorded transcriptions were reviewed and confirmed for data saturation within 24 h after the interviews concluded. After the transcriptions of all audio recordings were completed, they were double-checked for consistency by the other two researchers. For any discrepancies, the recordings were re-listened to correct the transcriptions, or the corresponding interviewees were contacted for verification.

The interview syllabus was developed by the research team through literature review and expert consultation. The interview process and syllabus details were further checked and refined through a pre-survey. The final version of the interview syllabus is shown in Table 2.

Ethical considerations

This study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. Approval was granted by the Ethics Committee of the Fifth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (Approval Date: 9/11/2023, Reference Number: K194-1). All interviewees clearly understood the aim, process, and privacy protection methods of this interview. Informed consent was obtained from each participant to volunteer for this study. All participants were explicitly informed of their right to refuse participation or withdraw from the study at any time, without the need to provide a reason, and their decision would not affect their working relationship in any way. Interviews were conducted in a quiet, private room. Critical information that could identify the interviewees has been omitted from all recordings and texts. The interviewer adhered to the principles of being supportive and nonjudgmental throughout the interviews and interactions to minimize the secondary psychological damage caused by the exposure of negative experiences. In-depth interviews were conducted in an open and honest atmosphere to uncover authentic experiences.

Data analysis

Qualitative content analysis was employed to extract and analyze the experiences of participants from the interview recordings. Content analysis is a quasi-statistical method that incorporates both qualitative interpretation and basic quantitative elements. This method was particularly suitable for analyzing the subjective experiences of residency training nurses during the ICU rotation, focusing on dilemmas and coping strategies.

The research team utilized Nvivo 14.0 for data analysis. The analytical process followed a structured approach. Firstly, researchers classified the “words” or “sentences” of these texts and extracted semantic units according to the purpose of the study. Initially, the research team immersed themselves in the data through repeated reading of transcripts to gain familiarity. Two researchers then independently performed inductive coding on the same transcripts to identify initial semantic units. Through constant comparison and discussion, a preliminary codebook was developed and refined. These codes were subsequently grouped into categories and broader themes based on conceptual similarities. Regular team meetings were held to resolve discrepancies and ensure consensus in thematic interpretation. Finally, illustrative quotes representative of each theme were selected to support the findings [14].

To enhance the rigor of the analysis, two additional researchers independently verified the results against the interview content. In cases of disagreement, the three researchers reconvened to discuss the discrepancies or consulted the interviewee for clarification, thus ensuring a consensus was reached. Moreover, to ensure the accuracy of the results presented in English, the analysis outcomes and illustrative quotes were first translated by one researcher. Another researcher, blinded to the original Chinese text, then back-translated the material into Chinese. Finally, both researchers collaboratively compared the back-translated version with the original texts to resolve any discrepancies and ensure conceptual and semantic consistency.

Results

Through analysis, four themes have emerged that represent the work experiences of residency training nurses during their ICU rotation. These themes are: (1) Experiencing Overwhelming Work Tasks; (2) Experiencing a Fast-Paced Working Pattern; (3) Experiencing a Strained Working Atmosphere; and (4) Experiencing a Generalized Teaching Program. The subthemes of each theme reflect the unique nature of ICU work as perceived by the rotating nurses, corresponding to their perceptions of specific work dilemmas and the strategies they have derived. Dilemmas refer to the barriers and difficulties encountered by residency training nurses during their ICU rotation. Strategies encompass the perceived coping methods and suggestions of residency training nurses facing ICU work dilemmas. Experiencing overwhelming work tasks

ICU patients’ conditions are often critical and unstable. Nurses must provide each patient with their undivided attention, even while working continuously and intensely. This theme summarizes the subjective experiences of residency training nurses in the ICU, who are overwhelmed by the working tasks, including dealing with fluctuating conditions, performing specialized procedures, and working night shifts.

Change of condition

Dilemma: irregular and serious patient condition

Compared to non-ICU wards, the lives of ICU patients are more fragile and their conditions change more rapidly. Without sufficient working experience, residency training nurses cannot properly monitor the subtle changes in complex conditions, making it difficult for them to effectively manage those ICU patients.

N3: A relatively mild patient suddenly developed ventricular fibrillation. Well, the team leader rushed to assist me in calling the doctor and retrieving the defibrillator. I was in a panic and didn’t know what to do.

N15: Unlike the general ward, the patients here are very sick. A patient may appear normal one moment, but require urgent assistance the next.

Strategy: subtle patient observation

Life crises can arise at any moment, and only through careful observation can we detect subtle signs of illness to provide timely treatment. Residency training nurses must remain highly vigilant at all times to ensure the safety of critical patients.

N14: Observing the patient’s condition requires meticulous attention. It is crucial for an ICU nurse to be cognizant of the patient’s current state in real time. This can assist in identifying potential risks to the patient.

Professional manipulation

Dilemma: unfamiliar nursing manipulation

The primary goal in rescues for emergency and critical incidents involving ICU patients is to keep them alive. Nurses must be proficient in all advanced life-support techniques and the use of relevant equipment, particularly in setting parameters for monitoring equipment, operating ventilators, and managing multiple tubes. Residency training nurses frequently feel overwhelmed by their unfamiliarity with the procedures involved in performing resuscitations or accompanying patients outside the ICU for consultations.

N3: There are fewer intubated patients in non-ICU wards. However, in the ICU, the nurse must assist the physician with intubation, such as helping with fiberoptic bronchoscopy, performing PICC line insertions, or maintaining other medical equipment.

N13: I had to use a transport ventilator to take a patient with an endotracheal tube out of the ICU for a CT scan. Before embarking on this journey, I was apprehensive about the instrument alarm, as I was unfamiliar with how to manage it. The physician accompanying me, who was only a resident in training, was equally unfamiliar with the emergency management of the transport ventilator. Consequently, we were in a state of panic throughout the process.

Strategy: real-world training and exercise

Unlike basic care, specialty operations in the ICU are more complex and require precision. Each new nurse requires specialized training and must pass related tests before they can practice independently. Real-scenario practice and real-time examination are considered by residency training nurses rotating in the ICU as the most appropriate training tools to help them firmly grasp the essentials of each operation.

N9: How to handle unpredictable emergencies is what we most desire to learn more about. I pondered how I could best resolve these issues and how I could effectively collaborate with my coworkers should I encounter them.

N15: Testing procedures can be more flexible; they do not have to occur at a fixed time. Most tests should be conducted in real-life scenarios that mimic those encountered in the clinic. Any mentor can administer this test during their free time, and they only need to sign off on the results.

Night shift

Dilemma: frequently working the night shift

Due to the critical condition of ICU patients, the ward management system mandates a nurse-to-patient ratio of at least 2.5. Furthermore, the number of nurses on night shifts must match that of day shifts. Consequently, ICU nurses end up working more night shifts than their counterparts in general wards. Typically, residency training nurses are expected to take on more night shifts than senior nurses, as a result of their lower seniority and younger age. This situation often prevents them from getting sufficient rest, leading to ongoing physical and mental exhaustion.

N13: Night shifts are too frequent. I tend to get a little dizzy on the subsequent day shift if I only have one day off to adjust my biological clock after a night shift.

Strategy: no strategy

There is an inherent conflict between the shortage of ICU staff and the high workload, necessitating a rotation of nurses between day and night shifts to accommodate the ICU wards’ workload. Nurses frequently opt to accept these scheduling arrangements to ensure the ward’s normal functioning. However, it is challenging for them to devise an effective strategy to address this dilemma.

Experiencing a fast-pace working pattern

Time is life for ICU patients. The only way to protect more patients’ lives in the ICU is to enhance the working efficiency of physicians and nurses in a fast-paced environment. This theme categorizes the subjective experiences of residency training nurses regarding time management and emergency resuscitation, based on the ICU’s fast-paced working pattern.

Work timeframe

Dilemma: narrow work timeframe

Work schedules in the ICU are very tight. Nurses must complete their tasks within a limited timeframe. Residency training nurses are not only inexperienced in performing operations but also lack organization in their work planning. Failure to complete tasks on time may sometimes delay the progress of others’ work or even patients’ follow-up treatments.

N12: Every task comes with a time limit, necessitating the completion of assigned work within a specific timeframe. For me, this is the primary source of stress. I fear not finishing my work on time, potentially delaying patient treatment or affecting my coworkers’ work. Consequently, I often find myself rushing during these periods. When I am ensnared in anxious moods, I am prone to making mistakes.

N18: I once failed to draw a patient’s blood during my morning shift, as the physician urgently needed the results of the arterial blood gas analysis. However, my colleagues were too preoccupied with their own tasks to assist me. At the time, I felt I had not managed my time effectively, having overestimated my work speed.

Strategy: efficient time planning

To keep pace with their colleagues, residency training nurses need to gradually master the ICU working patterns and patient care methods. This is an extremely lengthy process of adaptation and learning. Proper time management is an effective tool for coping with fast-paced work. Furthermore, novice nurses can anticipate their workload by understanding the patient’s condition beforehand. Strategic time planning will effectively enhance nurses’ work efficiency and reduce working hours.

N12: The ICU rotation is a process of adaptation. As my work experience increased, I gradually became familiar with my patients’ conditions. Now, I can more quickly predict what patients may experience and which machines may alarm. I can also organize my work in advance without becoming overwhelmed.

Emergency and rescue

Dilemma: unforeseen emergencies and rescue

ICU patients are unstable and prone to a variety of critical changes, which residency training nurses may not be able to quickly respond to. When multiple emergency or rescue incidents occur simultaneously, colleagues often cannot provide effective mutual support in a timely manner. Consequently, residency training nurses may find themselves isolated and unable to administer effective treatments to their patients.

N4: When I was suddenly faced with a patient requiring resuscitation, I was at a loss for what to do. My operating speed is slow, yet I must make time to send my patients for urgent checkups. At that moment, I was feeling rushed and truly panicking!

Strategy: seamless and tacit cooperation

Regarding the sudden deterioration of the patient’s condition, residency training nurses should first manage their emotions and calmly assist physicians in performing the resuscitation in an orderly manner. If they encounter difficulties during resuscitation, residency training nurses should not stay silent; instead, they should seek timely and courageous assistance from colleagues and superiors to avoid any delay in the patient’s treatment.

N3: As a nurse, the ability to remain calm in any situation during resuscitation should be a fundamental skill. Only when we know what to do can we better cooperate in the resuscitation without interfering with our teammates.

Experiencing a strained working atmosphere

A clear division of labor and efficient teamwork define the work atmosphere in the ICU. There is little tolerance for errors made by residency training nurses in this environment. This theme categorizes the subjective experiences of residency training nurses regarding the ICU work atmosphere, as perceived through teamwork and relationships with their colleagues.

Teamwork

Dilemma: urgent teamwork

ICU staff may often find themselves performing their own duties without sufficient time to focus on others. Given the fast-paced nature of their work, teamwork among ICU staff must also be tight and efficient. Residency training nurses frequently face persistent urging and even blame from their colleagues if they fail to keep pace with the team’s working speed, which can lead to feelings of anxiety, anger, and frustration.

N4: The scene that often occurs during resuscitation is that my superiors are busy, the physicians are anxious, and I work slowly. I am overwhelmed by their urgency.

Strategy: seek peer support and effective mutual communication

Nurses with similar experience and hierarchical status are more likely to have fewer barriers to mutual understanding and communication. Mutual support among junior nurses is an effective strategy for these novices to quickly adapt to the ICU working environment. However, the complexity of the work still relies on specialized guidance from superiors. The good utilization of communication skills is conducive to building friendly relationships among colleagues, thereby promoting efficient teamwork.

N9: I sometimes seek advice from my senior team members. Our peer nurses also engage in discussions with each other. In this manner, my facing problems could be slowly resolved.

Colleague relationship

Dilemma: unclosely colleague relationship

Residency training nurses are considered transient staff, not permanently assigned to the ICU. Some ICU colleagues do not value the establishment and maintenance of rapport with these rotating nurses. Because of their junior status, it is challenging for residency training nurses to gain full recognition and respect from others. Their mistakes may elicit excessive doubt and blame from some senior nurses. In the ICU, rotating nurses experience pressure at work and a lack of belonging, which diminishes their motivation to work.

N5: I had a poor sense of belonging in the ICU. It feels like no one cares about the feelings of the rotating nurses. In any case, I have a poor relationship with those senior colleagues. I only feel comfortable working with my peers.

N11: It feels as though we have never truly been respected. Whenever a mistake occurs, we are the first to be blamed. Despite our low seniority, we have tried our utmost to work diligently. We dare not resist, so we work each day in fear of making errors and facing blame.

Strategy: mutual adoption and respect

As a novice in the ICU, it is understandable that residency training nurses will encounter more challenges due to unfamiliar tasks. Instead of merely criticizing and correcting, senior colleagues should also focus on fostering the mental resilience of junior nurses. They should be treated with greater tolerance and humanistic care, rather than having their working confidence unnecessarily undermined.

N10: If you respect me, I will respect you. If everyone trusted each other, work wouldn’t be so stressful.

N11: Seniors should create opportunities for us to receive more care, whether verbal or physical. Increased incentives lead to better work performance.

Experiencing a generalized teaching program

The solid foundation of knowledge and skills is crucial for the ICU nurse’s role. The generalized training of residency nurses does not align with the actual needs of ICU work. Consequently, nurses are seeking more specialized education and training in the ICU. This theme categorizes the dilemmas experienced and strategies perceived by residency training nurses regarding the style, content, and timing of ICU mentors’ teaching.

Teaching style

Dilemma: mismatched teaching style

The intensity and urgency of ICU work can lead some mentors to unconsciously adopt a strict approach with residency training nurses. However, during busy periods, they might overlook the importance of coaching their nurses, prioritizing their own clinical tasks instead. As a result, obtaining valid and timely feedback from their mentors becomes challenging for these ICU rotating nurses.

N2: With a more responsible clinical mentor, you will be patiently taught by hand. However, if the clinical mentor is not responsible, he or she will guide you perfunctorily, leaving you to figure out most problems on your own.

Strategy: regulate teachers’ qualifications and enhance teachers’ incentives

The teaching styles of clinical mentors reflect their personality traits and professional literacy. It is essential to further standardize teaching methods and patterns among clinical nurse mentors. The training and evaluation system for ICU teaching staff should account for the unique characteristics and needs of rotating nurses. This will ensure that residency training in the ICU is more standardized and homogeneous.

N4: In my opinion, …They should also undergo regular recertification. Mentors should experience appraisal to ensure that the knowledge and skills they impart are more rigorous. The teaching process becomes more authentic as teachers impart their expertise to students.

N10: I believe that each clinical mentor has their own unique training style. Systematic training for ICU mentors could have narrowed the skills gap among the new nurses they taught.

Teaching content

Dilemma: superficial teaching content

The role of residency training nurses is that of both nurses and students. The purpose of residency training rotations is not merely to fill workforce gaps. They should primarily be used as an opportunity for residency training nurses to gain more clinical experience. However, ICU colleagues often overlook the student aspect of residency training nurses. Consequently, there is a lack of awareness regarding the guidance for these ICU rotating nurses. The ICU is frequently busy, and as a result, residency training nurses are often treated as teacher’s assistants rather than receiving proper teaching.

N9: My teacher is often too busy to teach me, so I have to work with them on my own. I don’t think I can learn much from these busy days.

Strategy: restructuring teaching syllabus

ICU work experience is crucial for new nurses to enhance their clinical skills and professional literacy. The residency training syllabus for ICU rotating nurses should be further refined to emphasize the specialization, practicality, and standardization of its training content. In this manner, residency training nurses can effectively apply what they have learned in their rotations to become qualified for formal nursing positions.

N5: Each position entails various working forms, including roles such as secretary and team leader, among others. Each shift also has its unique characteristics. We should undertake more clinical shifts to learn the skills of patient management. Additionally, rotating nurses require more detailed training content.

Teaching timing

Dilemma: inappropriate teaching framework

Due to their hectic schedules, some ICU mentors may rush the development of residency training nurses to manage patients independently at the earliest opportunity. The early push for independent practice, combined with delayed training, extends the growth period for residency training nurses. Inadequate timing of instruction results in new nurses being unfamiliar with the equipment and operations. Consequently, they are unable to manage patients’ condition fluctuations, even after taking on an independent shift.

N5: I had thought I would become independent sooner. However, when I actually achieved independence, I was still terrified because I didn’t know how to solve these problems.

N10: It’s too late for training. We often received specialty training after we had already begun working independently.

Strategy: plan time in advance

Both external support and the nurse’s personal drive for growth are essential for a successful transition from a residency training nurse to a fully qualified nursing staff member. Strict criteria for authorizing independent shifts during ICU rotations should be established by clinical mentors, ensuring that nurses are fully proficient in basic ICU operations and equipment use before assuming solo responsibilities. Concurrently, nurses should proactively plan their learning, actively seek out training opportunities, and accelerate their competency development through self-motivated engagement.

N1: As new ICU nurses, we need to have a plan for our own learning. Do not neglect work quality just because you’re not yet practicing independently. Otherwise, you may just panic when you get to your independent shift.

N11: I still believe that a specific test is necessary before we undertake independent work. This way, new nurses can better define what should be mastered in each time period. They can become more proficient in independent shifts after passing a series of tests.

Discussion

This study conducted a detailed exploration of the work experience of residency training nurses during their ICU rotation. Through this analysis, the study identified four key themes: “Experiencing Overwhelming Work Tasks,” " Experiencing a Fast-Paced Working Pattern,” " Experiencing a Strained Working Atmosphere,” and " Experiencing a Generalized Teaching Program.” Each theme encompasses several dilemmas and derived coping strategies, as perceived by residency training nurses during their ICU rotation, representing the new nurses’ overall perception of ICU residency training.

The “Experiencing Overwhelming Work Tasks” dilemmas and coping strategies experienced by resident training nurses during ICU rotations. Specialized operations, emergency resuscitation, and work errors are significant stressors for new nurses as they adjust to clinical work. However, these challenges are also difficult to confront intuitively through school learning and clinical practice [8]. The real dilemma was visualized by the shock of critical and changing conditions faced by residency training nurses during ICU rotations [15]. This study indicates that the primary challenge faced by ICU rotating nurses is an overwhelming workload. These nurses continue to experience symptoms such as dizziness, insomnia, and burnout due to the disrupted biorhythms from frequent shifts, which aligns with previous research findings [16–18]. For critical patients, communication can be challenging, so any subtle changes in monitoring equipment, facial expressions, body movements, and so on, are indicators of condition changes that ICU nurses must closely monitor. Training and examination in a variety of specialized skills within clinical scenarios will aid in enhancing the spiritual response of residency training nurses amidst the complex changes of the ICU work environment [2]. An inherent paradox exists between the inadequate human resources and the large workloads in ICUs. As highlighted by our findings on ‘overwhelming working tasks,’ managers could enhance the logic and continuity of shift scheduling to alleviate the burden of shifts, rather than expecting nurses to cope individually. Strategies, such as ensuring at least two consecutive days off following night shifts and standardizing patient assignments, may help regulate biorhythms. This approach is supported by previous research indicating that predictable rest periods and consistent schedules are associated with reduced physical and mental strain among nurses [19, 20].

The " Experiencing a Fast-Paced Working Pattern” dilemmas and coping strategies faced by resident training nurses during ICU rotations. The work of nursing in the ICU is characterized by its overload and irregularity. Rescue operations, particularly in emergencies, demand a high level of timeliness and collaboration and frequently consume a significant amount of the ICU nurse’s time and energy [21]. Clinical nurses have had to work at a fast pace to cover limited time frames, ensuring there is time for emergencies whenever they occur. Consistent with the findings of Kisanuki et al. [21], the need for a supportive team structure to mitigate its challenges bring by the ‘fast-paced work pattern’. To bolster productivity and time management under pressure, we suggest that nursing managers prioritize a robust hierarchical management system in clinical nursing team. Ensuring proper workload-resource matching within teams is critical. Furthermore, providing residency training nurses with strong support from clinical mentors, including tolerance and assistance with unfinished tasks and unachieved cooperation. Residency training nurses should receive stronger support and assistance from the nursing team, including clinical mentors who offer appropriate tolerance and help for their unfinished work. In the environment, new nurses may potentially reduce their psychological stress, which could better help them adapt to the ICU’s intensity. This aligns with the understanding that supportive supervision and collaborative teamwork significantly alleviate workplace stress and enhance adaptive capacity among critical care nurses [22].

The " Experiencing a Strained Working Atmosphere” dilemmas and coping strategies faced by resident training nurses during ICU rotations. ICU staff are chronically overstressed due to the high intensity of their work. Compared to the normal group, ICU nurses are at a higher risk of paranoia and interpersonal sensitivity. However, residency training nurses are more exposed to questioning and criticism because they are not yet competent enough to match the working rhythm of the team [8, 15]. Besides, the rotation system prevents residency training nurses from confirming their unit affiliation. The frequent shocks of unit transitions exacerbate the integration difficulty of rotating nurses with the original nursing team of the ICU [7, 23]. Addressing the ‘strained working atmosphere’ experienced by our participants necessitates proactive leadership. Our research findings indicate that organizational support and acceptance can counteract avoidant thinking and boost motivation. Consequently, we suggest that ICU senior nurses adopt a more friendly and open demeanor, explicitly acknowledging the professional value of rotating nurses. Furthermore, ICU managers should actively guide both physician and nurse teams in cultivating an inclusive environment. Importantly, managers must oversee and mediate unhealthy colleague relationships to establish a conducive atmosphere for professional growth. This is a recognized factor in alleviating the pressures of transition, as supported by studies that link transformational leadership and structural empowerment to improved team efficacy and psychological adaptation in ICU settings [24].

The " Experiencing a Generalized Teaching Program” dilemmas and coping strategies faced by resident training nurses during ICU rotations. Study results reveal that some residency training nurses had a unfavorable experience with the current ICU teaching model, which is consistent with previous study [8]. The demanding clinical work in ICUs complicates the ability of clinical mentors to address the individual needs of each nursing student in their teaching approach, content, and scheduling [25]. The generalized teaching program fails to offer timely and effective assistance for the professional development of residency training nurses, instead increasing their time expenditure and psychological stress during role transition [26]. In response to the dilemmas caused by a ‘generalized teaching program,’ our findings suggest the need for a more flexible and responsive approach. We recommend tailoring clinical teaching to match the individual learning and adaptation states of rotating nurses [7]. The implementation of such personalized programs heavily depends on the mentors’ capabilities. Previous research has demonstrated that the teaching skills of ICU nurses who undergo formal training are significantly superior to those who do not [27]. Therefore, nursing managers should reinforce scrutiny of mentor qualifications and provide formal training opportunities. Additionally, offering adequate support, such as adjusted shift schedules, and establishing an education-related reward system could enhance mentorship quality and ensure the standardized implementation of clinical teaching programs [28].

Limitations and future research

This study details the dilemmas and coping strategies encountered by residency training nurses, through an in-depth review and analysis of their work experiences during rotations in the ICU. However, the study focused exclusively on a pilot hospital in Guangdong Province, China, resulting in homogeneity in the background characteristics of the study population. Hospitals worldwide that offer residency programs for new nurses lack standardized education and training protocols for ICU rotations. There is considerable disparity in the allocation of workloads and salary structures across ICUs. Globally, hospitals providing residency training for new nurses do not adhere to a uniform standard for ICU rotation training, as the work and salary systems differ significantly [2]. Given the diversity in residency training systems and ICU management across hospitals globally, the findings do not entirely capture the subjective perceptions of residency training nurses regarding their ICU rotation experiences. Additionally, the sampling for this study was not restricted by the gender, education, or native place of the participants included. Demographic characteristics continue to exhibit a limited range, predominantly female (73.68%), with bachelor’s degrees (89.47%), and from out-of-province (73.68%). The credibility of the study’s results may be compromised by cognitive biases in the residency training nurses’ perceptions of ICU work experience. Moreover, as the research team comprised individuals with diverse clinical and academic nursing backgrounds, implicit biases related to nursing training and ICU workflows may have unconsciously influenced data interpretation. Although strategies such as team discussions, independent coding, and translation-back-translation procedures were implemented to mitigate these potential biases, some may persist. Therefore, subsequent studies should aim to enhance sample diversity by employing multi-center recruitment to include a broader range of demographic and geographic backgrounds. This would improve the generalizability of the findings and strengthen the validity of the results.

Conclusions and recommendations

As a novice in the ICU, the disparity between the high-intensity clinical environment and generalized teaching methodologies may not fully support the development of residency training nurses as intended. Many felt unprepared in core acute and critical care competencies, leading to a complex process of confusion and adaptation amid transition shocks such as overwhelming workloads, fast-paced work patterns, and strained working atmospheres. This study utilized the high-pressure ICU context as a lens to examine the gaps between educational goals and clinical reality in residency training, as well as strategies to navigate these challenges.

This study emphasizes the importance of providing comprehensive support and care at the organizational level of the ICU for residency training nurses during their ICU rotation learning. The initial step involves implementing regular peer review and systematic oversight of clinical mentors to maintain teaching quality and accountability, as well as to better prepare their educational qualifications and instructional abilities, thereby helping to reduce the theory-practice gap. Secondly, standardized mentorship programs with validated competency checks should be implemented before granting clinical autonomy to rotating nurses, ensuring that their theoretical knowledge and clinical skills are consistent and aligned with the ICU’s working demands. Moreover, awareness of humanistic care for rotating nurses should be elevated. Managers need to foster a collaborative and supportive working atmosphere by assigning rotating nurses to consistent team partners, adjusting assignments based on learning progress, and recognizing their contributions publicly. Additionally, the introduction of structured recovery periods following night shifts is critical for mitigating physical and mental exhaustion and protecting well-being. These concrete local actions aim to create a more supportive and effective learning environment, easing the transition for residency training nurses and enhancing both their competency development and psychological adaptability in the ICU.
