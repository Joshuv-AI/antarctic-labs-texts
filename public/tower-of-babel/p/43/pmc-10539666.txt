The relationship between disrupted anhedonia-related circuitry and suicidal ideation in major depressive disorder: A network-based analysis
Authors: Xiaoqin Wang, Yi Xia, Rui Yan, Huan Wang, Hao Sun, Yinghong Huang, Lingling Hua, Hao Tang, Zhijian Yao, Qing Lu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Highlights•The severity of anhedonia in MDD is associated with suicidal ideation.•MDD patients with anhedonia symptoms had a disrupted functional in frontal-limbic circuit.•The anhedonia symptoms could mediate the relationship between the anhedonia-related network metrics and suicide ideation in MDD.Keywords: Major depressive disorder, Anhedonia, Suicidal ideation, Network-based statistic, Graph-theory, Frontal-limbic

Introduction

Anhedonia, profoundly decreased interest or pleasure, is a defining symptom of major depressive disorder (MDD) (Treadway and Zald, 2011). Self-reported anhedonia is a reliable predictor of a worse long-term course of MDD across several prospective studies (Morris et al., 2009). Notably, some studies suggested that state anhedonia in MDD represents a substantial predictor for suicide ideation (SI) (Hein et al., 2022, Hawes et al., 2018, Loas et al., 2019). Anhedonia, as the psychological pain, is a fundamental factor associated with suicide risk concerning short-term fluctuations in SI (Yang et al., 2020, Liu et al., 2021).

The underlying neural correlates of anhedonia and reward process disturbance in MDD have significantly progressed over the past ten years. The neural bases of the anhedonia reflect a deficit in the reward process, including hedonic capacity, reward valuation, decision-making, anticipation and motivation (Höflich et al., 2019, Der-Avakian and Markou, 2012). According to the reward dual process, two separate neural networks in the reward process of the human brain: the brain regions in the reward network for primary rewards (immediate rewards) and the cognitive control network for secondary rewards (delayed rewards) (McClure et al., 2004). Decisions involving immediately accessible rewards preferentially activate parts of the limbic system related to the midbrain dopamine system, whereas delayed rewards engage prefrontal cortex sections (McClure et al., 2004). Therefore, the neural circuits involving anhedonia include the prefrontal-limbic regions and afferent and efferent projections (Treadway and Zald, 2011, Enneking et al., 2019).

Reward process disturbance and dysfunction in regions within the reward-related network were also shown in MDD patients with SI symptoms (Misaki et al., 2016, Quevedo et al., 2017, Xie et al., 2014). For example, MDD patients with SI failed to show any feedback-dependent caudate regulation in the monetary incentive delay task (Xie et al., 2014). Similarly, the delayed reward discount rates are significantly increased in patients with suicide (Bryan and Bryan, 2021). Additionally, evidence of reward-related abnormalities has emerged not only in reward tasks but also during resting (Chin Fatt et al., 2021). Indeed, in the triadic motivation model (Ernst et al., 2006); the SI might be explained by the unbalance of the reward or defensive system (limbic) and the supervisory system (prefrontal) ineffective work (Clark and Watson, 1991, Watson et al., 1995, Brown et al., 1998). Therefore, abnormalities in the prefrontal-limbic network are the critical neural basis of SI.

Furthermore, some studies have reported that dysfunctional limbic systems and prefrontal cortex connections during the rewarding task impact anhedonia and SI together (Pizzagalli et al., 2009, Misaki et al., 2016, Quevedo et al., 2017, Xie et al., 2014, Bryan and Bryan, 2021). Therefore, considering the associations among reward-related network abnormalities, anhedonia and SI, anhedonia may link dysfunctional brain networks and SI. Therefore, this study examined whether anhedonia mediated the association between dysfunctional brain networks and SI (mediation model).

Within the human connectome, the brain creates a large-scale complex network of linked areas, providing the anatomical basis for neural communication, functional processing, and information integration (van den Heuvel and Hulshoff Pol, 2010). Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) to assess resting-state functional brain networks has dramatically expanded the research opportunities for mental illnesses. The inherent variations in Blood Oxygen Level Dependent (BOLD) signals have allowed researchers to pinpoint which brain areas are synchronized (Vijayalaxmi et al., 2015). Although it is commonly assumed that traditional seed-based analysis reflects the underlying altered connectivity, the network-based statistic (NBS) analysis is a developed tool for identifying network components that differ between groups (Zalesky et al., 2010); which can reveal functional connectivity in brain networks. Additionally, graph theory has become a powerful tool for quantifying efficient information segregation and integrating complex brain networks (Bullmore and Sporns, 2009). This topological organization can be dramatically altered in neurological and psychiatric disorders (Liao et al., 2017). Therefore, the network-based analysis and graph theory revealed the intrinsic network-level dysfunction in anhedonia.

This study explores abnormal functional connectivity networks in MDD patients with anhedonia, their internal topological properties, and their relationship with SI. First, we used an impartial data-driven paradigm to establish aberrant functional connectivity in the brain network. Next, we utilized an NBS analysis to assess the functional connectivity pattern in MDD patients with HA and a graph theory technique to assess the topological aspects of intrinsic functional brain networks (global and regional attributes). Second, we evaluated SI levels in patients with MDD and verified that the above abnormal reward network metrics in patients with anhedonia were associated with SI. Finally, mediation analysis explored the relationship among anhedonia symptoms, abnormal brain network and SI.

Materials and methods

Participants

Between June 2021 and March 2023, 278 patients with MDD in a major depressive episode were recruited from the Affiliated Brain Hospital of Nanjing Medical University. At least two attending psychiatrists diagnosed all MDD patients according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 5th Edition (DSM-V) criteria. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age between 18 and 50 years; (2) Chinese Han; (3) no comorbidity with other mental disorders that meet the criteria of DSM-V axis-I disorders; (4) right-handed; (5) the 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD17) (Hamilton, 1960) scores ≥ 17; (6) no psychotropic medication use in the past six weeks. (7) no physical therapy or systematic psychotherapy in the past six months; For healthy controls (HC), 151 participants matched in age, sex, and education were recruited in the community, and the inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) screened to have no MDD using the DSM-V nonpatient version of the Structured Clinical Interview; (2) right-handedness; (3) aged between 18 and 50 years old; (4) junior school or higher education level; (5) Chinese Han; (6) a HAMD17 score < 7; (7) no neurological illness or mental illness; (8) no family history of neurological or psychiatric illness. Besides, participants were excluded if they had the following conditions: (1) any other pertinent medical or mental problem other than MDD psychopathology; (2) a history of systemic illness; (3) current pregnancy or breastfeeding; (4) substance abuse or dependence; (5) any physical therapy such as repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) within the previous six months before the scan; (6) any contraindications to MRI scanning.

MRI scanning was performed on all MDD depression patients before the therapy, within two days of the clinical assessment. Due to significant head movements and low picture quality, 5 MDD and 1 HC subjects were removed. The final analysis comprised 273 MDD and 150 HC.

Although we did not conduct a pilot study to calculate statistical power, a review showed that the neuroimageing study requires 34 participants to surpass 80 % power to detect an effect size of D = 0.5 at α = 0.05. If α level is decreased to 0.001, D = 0.5 effect size with 80 % power would require 74 participants (Szucs and Ioannidis, 2020). In the current study, our sample size met the requirements.

Ethical statement

The Research Ethics Review Board of the Affiliated Nanjing Brain Hospital of Nanjing Medical University authorized this experiment. After thoroughly explaining all the research details to all the participating individuals, formal informed consent was obtained.

Clinical assessments

All subjects’ gender, age, and educational background were recorded. Except for the total duration of the illness, the number of episodes and the duration of each acute episode are essential measures of the course of the disease. The total duration of each episode was recorded to ensure comparability between the two groups of patients with MDD (high anhedonia, low anhedonia). Considering the HAMD17 item 7 (work and interests decline) is highly correlated with anhedonia symptoms, we used the HAMD17 excluding item 7 representing depression severity to eliminate the influence of anhedonia symptoms. Furthermore, the anhedonia symptoms were evaluated with the Snaith Hamilton Pleasure Scale (SHAPS) (Snaith et al., 1995). Suicide ideation was evaluated using the Chinese version of the Beck Scale for Suicide Ideation (BSSI) (Beck et al., 1979, Li et al., 2021). Subsequently, MDD patients were divided into two subgroups: 144 patients with SHAPS scores ≥ 34 (HA group) and 129 patients with SHAPS scores ≤ 33 (LA group).

Neuroimaging data acquisitions and preprocessing

MRI data acquisition

At the Affiliated Brain Hospital of Nanjing Medical University's Department of Medical Imaging, fMRI data from each participant was collected using a 3.0 T Siemens MRI system (Siemens Medical Solutions, Germany). The participants were told to keep their eyes closed, avoid head movement, stay alert, and not think about anything when recording the resting-state functional data. When questioned immediately following the scanning, no individuals admitted to dozing off. The maximum head movement was 2.5 mm in translation and/or 2.5 degrees in rotation.

The structural images were acquired by T1-weighted magnetisation-prepared. The parameters for the T1 anatomic axial imaging were as follows: repetition time (TR) = 7900 ms, echo time (TE) = 3.06 ms, flip angle (FA) = 8°, number of slices = 196, slice thickness = 1 mm, in-plane voxel resolution = 1 mm × 1 mm, and field of view (FOV) = 240 × 240 mm2. Resting-state fMRI data were obtained using an echo-planar imaging (EPI) sequence. The parameters were as follows: TE = 25 ms, TR = 2,000 ms, FA = 90°, slice thickness = 4 mm, slice gap = 0 mm, number of slices = 40, FOV = 240 × 240 mm2, matrix size = 64 × 64, in plane voxel resolution = 3.8 mm × 3.8 mm, and volume number = 240.

Resting-state functional connectivity pre-processing

The functional imaging data were analyzed using the Data Processing Assistant for Resting-State fMRI (DPARSF; https://rfmri.org/DPARSF) (Chao-Gan and Yu-Feng, 2010). The first six volumes were excluded to allow signal equilibrium. Then, the remaining volumes were correlated for slice timing, realigned for head motion corrections, and spatially normalized to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space. The images were then resampled to 3 × 3 × 3 mm3, smoothed with a 6 mm kernel, and band-pass filtered at 0.01–0.08 Hz to decrease low-frequency drift effects and high-frequency noise. Linear regression was used to eliminate the sources of false variances (for example, the Friston 24 parameter derived by head motion correction, signals from the entire brain, white matter, and cerebrospinal fluid). Participants were barred from participating if their head movements exceeded 2.5 mm translationally or 2° in any direction.

Statistical analysis

Demographic statistics

The demographic and clinical characteristics statistics were conducted using Statistical Product and Service Solutions (SPSS, version 25.0). For statistics between three groups, Chi-square tests were used to compare gender and family history. Age and education were analyzed using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). When comparing HA and LA groups, two-sample t-tests were used to compare the illness duration, number of episodes, HAMD17, HAMD17 excluding item 7 (work and interests decline), SHAPS and BSSI. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Functional connectivity network construction

We defined each region of interest from the automated anatomical labelling 3 (AAL3) atlas consisting of 170 regions as a network node (Rolls et al., 2020). AAL3 adds some previously undefined brain regions, including many reward-related regions, such as nucleus accumbens (NAc), substantia nigra, ventral tegmental area, red nucleus, locus coeruleus, raphe nuclei. Using inverse transformations, the AAL3 atlas from the MNI space was registered to the individual’s native space. Next, the averaged fMRI time series were extracted from each AAL atlas with 170 regions to obtain the FC network. Because finer parcellations of these regions are provided in AAL3, the original numbering in AAL2 for the bilateral anterior cingulate cortex, and thalamus is left empty in AAL3. Therefore, AAL3 has 166 parcellations, with a maximum label number of 170 (Rolls et al., 2020). Then, Pearson correlations were computed on the averaged time series between all paired regions, resulting in a 166 × 166 FC matrix. To improve the normality, Fisher-Z transformation was applied to the correlation coefficients.

Network-based statistic (NBS) analysis

NBS (Zalesky et al., 2010) is a method for controlling the family-wise error (FEW) rate when mass-univariate testing is conducted at each link in the graph based on the concepts of classical cluster-based thresholding of statistical parametric maps. A one-way ANOVA was performed on each connectivity value when comparing the functional network differences between HA, LA, and HC. To build the topological clusters, connections with F values more significant than the main criterion were chosen. The observed cluster score was defined as the number of connections inside each cluster. The number of connections inside each cluster was used to calculate the observed cluster score.

It should be noted that the researchers chose the first cluster-forming threshold (primary threshold) arbitrarily, and there is no universally recognized criterion. We followed the current NBS research and set the main criterion at p = 0.001 to ensure strict control of Type I error (Jiang et al., 2020). The data from each group was then randomized 5000 times to generate the reference cluster distribution (Zalesky et al., 2010). To create the reference distribution, we utilized the maximum number of connections across all clusters for each randomization. The observed cluster scores above the 95th percentile were then judged significant (p < 0.05). The method was the same for the post hoc paired comparisons (HA versus LA). The distinctions were characterized as follows. We masked the network connectivity matrix with identified significant connections between three groups before doing an independent two-sample t-test on the connectivity value.

Graph-theory network analysis

Although recent graph-theoretic studies found that MDD patients with anhedonia have abnormal reward-related network topological properties, from the perspective of the whole brain (Ely et al., 2021, Kim et al., 2021), the intrinsic topological properties of anhedonia-related network are still unclear.

To explore the alterations of topological properties within the network associated with anhedonia symptoms, we explored the topological network/nodal properties of an anhedonia-related network (a 19 × 19 matrix) calculated in NBS at both the global and regional levels in patients with HA compared to LA.

The clustering coefficients (Cp, a measure of the extent of clustered connectivity around individual nodes) and characteristic path length (Lp, a measure of the average distance for all possible pairs of nodes in the network) were two critical parameters of the brain networks in terms of small-world network properties. The small world (sigma = gamma/lambda, quantitative parameter) and the normalized clustering coefficient (gamma, the ratio of Cp between natural and random networks) were defined. Furthermore, the selection was performed with the following parameters: gamma ≫ 1 and lambda ≈ 1 or sigma = gamma/lambda > 1. The brain network demonstrated the small world attribute. In terms of network efficiency (Rubinov and Sporns, 2010), the global efficiency (Eglob) represents the global efficiency of the parallel information transfer in the network. The local efficiency (Eloc) of the network measured its fault tolerance. The nodal network measures of nodal degree, nodal betweenness centrality, nodal efficiency, and nodal local efficiency were used to evaluate nodal characteristics (Rubinov and Sporns, 2010). For each network parameter over the whole sparsity range, the area under the curves (AUC) was determined, which was sensitive to the topological modifications of the brain disorders (Rubinov and Sporns, 2010, Zhang et al., 2011).

Notably, we extracted the values of the anhedonia-related network that were calculated in NBS and Gretna software and used the general linear model to calculate the specific differences between the two groups (HA and LA) with sex, age, and the HAMD17 excluding item 7 as covariates.

We applied thirty-five sparsity thresholds (0.16 – 0.5, with an interval of 0.01) to create the binary matrix. The minimum is calculated in v2.0 Gretna software (https://www.nitrc.org/projects/gretna/) using the code “gretna_get_rmax(rand(N, N))”, with N = 19 here, denoting the number of nodes. The maximum was selected when the small-world scalar sigma of the threshold networks was larger than 1.1 for all participants (Zhang et al., 2011). The generated threshold range of 0.16–0.5 determined by this procedure guaranteed that the threshold networks were estimable for small-world (Watts and Strogatz, 1998) and had sparse properties with as few spurious edges as possible (Achard et al., 2007).

Correlation analysis

This study used correlation analysis to explore the relationship between the scores of SHAPS and SI. Additionally, the SHAPS and SI scores were correlated with different brain network metrics of anhedonia-related network respectively, including the mean FC values, global metrics (e.g. the AUC values of gamma, sigma and lambda, Cp, Lp, Eglob, Eloc), and local metrics (e.g. nodal degree, nodal betweenness centrality, nodal efficiency, and nodal local efficiency).

Mediation analysis

According to our key hypothesis, the mediation model in the patients with MDD was tested using the SPSS macro PROCESS (model 4) to examine whether brain network metrics directly affected SI or indirectly affected SI by anhedonia symptoms. The model was tested after controlling for age, sex, and disease severity. We used bootstrapping (95 % bias-corrected bootstrap confidence intervals [CIs] for the indirect effects based on 5,000 bootstrap resamples) for analysis. If the 95 % bias-corrected CI did not contain zero, the indirect effect was regarded as significant.

Disrupted functional connectivity in anhedonia

We investigated the FC network differences between HA, LA, and HC. This network included 38 regions and 73 connections, predominantly containing bilateral posterior orbital frontal cortex (OFC), superior frontal gyrus (SFG), middle frontal gyrus (MFG), inferior frontal gyrus (IFG) pars orbitalis, lingual gyrus, temporal gyrus and cerebellum (Fig. 2A). Subsequently, the post hoc analysis between HA and LA showed a significantly increased frontal-limbic FC network (Fig. 2B). This altered FC network compromised 19 regions and 26 connections, predominantly involving bilateral SFG, left IFG pars orbitalis, left MFG pars orbitalis, bilateral striatum (including bilateral putamen, bilateral globus pallidus and left NAc), bilateral anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) and right amygdala. In this single altered network (a 19 × 19 matrix), the mean functional connectivity values in patients with HA were significantly higher than LA (p < 0.001).

Disrupted global and nodal topological properties in anhedonia

For the whole anhedonia-related network, both HA and LA exhibited many typical features of a small-world organization (Fig. 3). Nevertheless, compared with LA (Fig. 3B), the HA (Fig. 3A) had significantly lower AUC values of lambda (F (2 6 8) = 15.661, p < 0.001), gamma (F (2 6 8) = 12.275, p < 0.001), sigma (F (2 6 8) = 4.081, p = 0.044, uncorrected), local efficiency (F (2 6 8) = 4.292, p = 0.039, uncorrected) in the anhedonia-related network (Fig. 3C), which suggests a relatively randomized global topology in HA. Regional topological analysis showed that the nodal local efficiency in NAc was lower in HA than in LA (F (2 6 8) = 11.047, p = 0.001). These results indicate a disorganization of the global and regional topological properties in anhedonia-related network.

Relationship between connectivity/topological metrics and clinical symptoms

We found modest evidence for relationships between anhedonia symptoms and SI (r (2 7 1) = 0.323, p < 0.001).

The SHAPS scores were positively associated with the FC indices (r (2 7 1) = 0.527, p < 0.001). In contrast, they were negatively associated with the AUC values of gamma (r (2 7 1) = −0.162, p = 0.008) and lambda (r (2 7 1) = −0.186, p = 0.002), and nodal local efficiency in NAc (r (2 7 1) = −80.215, p < 0.001).

The BSSI scores were positively associated with the FC indices (r (2 7 1) = 0.294, p < 0.001). In contrast, they were negatively associated with the AUC values of gamma (r (2 7 1) = −0.229, p < 0.001) and lambda (r (2 7 1) = −0.280, p < 0.001), and nodal local efficiency in NAc (r (2 7 1) = −0.140, p = 0.021).

Mediation analysis analysis in anhedonia, SI, and network metrics

We tested the conceptual model shown in Fig. 4, assessing the SHAPS scores mediated the mean values of FC, the AUC values of gamma, and nodal local efficiency (NAc) on BSSI scores. Among the patients with MDD, we found a significant indirect effect on the relationship between these network metrics (the mean values of FC, the AUC values of gamma and NAc efficiency) and BSSI scores separately via the SHAPS scores, above and beyond the effects of age, sex and the disease severity (HAMD17 excluding item 7) (Table 2). Specifically, increased mean values of FC and decreased gamma and NAc efficiency in the reward-related network predicted the severity of the anhedonia symptoms seriously, leading increased SI level.

Discussion

We discovered a widespread network-level pathophysiological signature in anhedonia regarding connectivity patterns and topological metrics using network-based and graph-theory analysis. These were also involved in the underlying neuropathological mechanisms of MDD patients with SI.

Our result demonstrated an enhanced FC of frontal-limbic in HA. MDD is a stress and emotion dysregulation syndrome involving compromised structural integrity of frontal-limbic networks (Shen et al., 2021). The current results were consistent with previous findings, which reported that patients with anhedonia showed enhanced functional patterns in the frontal-limbic (Olson et al., 2018, Bracht et al., 2022). This system promotes reward-seeking behavior by activating limbic, striatal, and frontal networks, enhancing an individual's capacity to transfer positive motives into adaptive actions (Wahlstrom et al., 2010). In particular, the dopaminergic mesocorticolimbic pathway originating from the ventral tegmental area projects to the dorsal (caudate, putamen) and ventral (NAc) striatum and then runs to the OFC, more dorsal aspects of the prefrontal cortex (PFC), and various subregions of the ACC (Pizzagalli, 2014, Pizzagalli, 2022). This dopaminergic pathway overlaps with our results of the anhedonia-related network in NBS.

The increasing connectedness pattern, from a network standpoint, is an increased coupling and integrated communication within linked regions. It is a sign of a compensatory mechanism, or excess connectivity can be directly pathological and may intimately tie to alterations in the internal topology of the network (Yang et al., 2019). Specifically, the human brain is a complex and interconnected network characterized by an efficient small-world network with high local clustering and short path length (Achard et al., 2006). The regular network has a high local clustering and a long path length, whereas the random network has a low local clustering and a short path length (Suo et al., 2018). This study found a lower normalized Cp (gamma) and Lp and normalized Lp (lambda) in the anhedonia-related network. Cp indicates the extent of clustered connectivity around the frontal-limbic network, and Lp of the network quantifies the mean distance or routing efficiency between any pair of nodes (Liao et al., 2017). These results of small-world metrics indicated that the small-world network organization was transferred towards a relatively randomized global topology in the reward-related network.

At the regional level, we found a lower nodal local efficiency in NAc. NAc, part of the ventral striatum, as a hedonic hot spot, is implicated in the hedonic experience and hence in the reward processing per se (Pizzagalli et al., 2009). The dorsal striatum contains the putamen and caudate. Aside from motor control and psychomotricity, it primarily engages in reward learning, anticipation and action (Treadway and Zald, 2011, Haruno et al., 2004). From a neuroendocrine perspective, opioid and endocannabinoid receptors in the NAc and ventral pallidum mediate the hedonic perception of rewards (Der-Avakian and Markou, 2012, Kelley et al., 2002, Berridge and Kringelbach, 2008). On the one hand, numerous investigations have demonstrated weakened striatum function in MDD patients with anhedonia (Pizzagalli et al., 2009, Keedwell et al., 2005, Heller et al., 2013), which may relate to the lower NAc efficiency. On the other hand, enhanced prefrontal-striatal functional connectivity implies over-recruitment of blood oxygen and decreased efficiency of critical nodes in anhedonia patients. Both conditions perturb the brain function in patients with anhedonia. Anyway, the physiological relationship between functional connectivity and nodal efficiency is worthy of further investigation, especially in MDD patients with anhedonia symptoms.

Our mediation analysis revealed that anhedonia symptoms mediated the relationship between the dysfunction frontal-limbic network and SI in MDD patients. For the direct effect, patients with abnormal frontal-limbic network tend to have SI. The unique role of the prefrontal-limbic network in suicide has been widely reported. Our previous work revealed that the Cp, Eglob and Eloc metrics of limbic decreased in patients with suicide (Wang et al., 2020). Another work demonstrated that a significantly increased FC strength between the right ACC and the regions within the subcortical network was related to suicide (Wang et al., 2022).

For the indirect effect, patients with abnormal frontal-limbic network tend to have severe anhedonia symptoms, leading to higher levels of SI. According to the reward dual process, two separate neural networks in the reward process of the human brain: the brain regions in the reward network for primary rewards (immediate rewards) and the cognitive control network for secondary rewards (delayed rewards) (McClure et al., 2004). Decisions involving immediately accessible rewards preferentially activate parts of the limbic system related to the midbrain dopamine system, whereas delayed rewards engage prefrontal cortex sections (McClure et al., 2004). SI reflects a pattern of decision-making that favors the selection of courses of action with uncertain and possibly harmful consequences. The frontal-limbic disruption impacts the confusion of reward, withdrawal and supervisory system, leading to incorrect decision-making, such as SI (Ernst et al., 2006, Clark and Watson, 1991, Watson et al., 1995, Brown et al., 1998). Our model suggested that a disruption in the reward-related network is a potential neural basis for higher SI in MDD patients with anhedonia. This supports our initial hypothesis that disruption of the reward network in MDD leads to SI through anhedonia symptoms. Therefore, we should focus more on patients with anhedonia symptoms in clinical suicide prevention.

Limitation

Unfortunately, this study still has some shortcomings that need to be considered. Firstly, although no psychotropic drugs were taken in the six weeks preceding the neuroimaging scanning, it may take longer for people to stabilize after altering or discontinuing the prescription. Secondly, the functional pattern was only evaluated for anatomical areas, but FC might also be caused via indirect channels. Thirdly, this study artificially creating groups based on a single cut-off value (SHAPS scores ≥ 34 or SHAPS scores ≤ 33) will real costs in increases in Type II errors through loss of power (McClelland et al., 2015). Due to slight differences in participants near the critical point, the quartile-based grouping may be a better method to avoid the influence of the boundary near the point. For consistency with subsequent mediation analyses and to retain as many samples as possible, we had to cut out some statistical power. After using the cut-off value to assess the brain network disruption in patients with high anhedonia, correlation analysis was used to test whether SHAPS scores were linearly correlated with the disruption. Last, because it was a cross-sectional study, the findings cannot be linked to future suicide risk, and longitudinal research may give further insight into this issue.

Conclusion

The current study identified the group difference in brain networks and its internal topology between individuals with HA and LA, providing a further neuroimaging mechanistic understanding of the relationship between anhedonia and SI in MDD. It seems primed to play a significant clinical role in guiding diagnostic, treatment and suicide prevention planning decisions in MDD patients with anhedonia. Additionally, whether there is a link between the function pattern and topological metrics in a physiological sense needs further exploration. Future research will be required to determine the potential relevance of our results as biomarkers for clinical use.
