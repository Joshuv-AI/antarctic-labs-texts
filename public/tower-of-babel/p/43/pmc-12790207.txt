Authors: Aabha Divya, Niti Dalal, Michel Pompeu Sá
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

Postmyocardial infarction ventricular septal defect (PMI-VSD) remains one of the most catastrophic mechanical complications of acute myocardial infarction with mortality approaching 100% without intervention.1 Contemporary guidelines recommend urgent repair within days of diagnosis, citing the high risk of rapid hemodynamic deterioration.2,3 Yet, despite decades of surgical and percutaneous experience, the optimal timing of intervention remains uncertain.

In this issue, Al Kindi et al4 describe the case of a 69-year-old man with anterior ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction complicated by a 1.2-cm apical VSD, managed conservatively with intra-aortic balloon pump support and medical therapy for nearly 4 weeks before undergoing combined coronary artery bypass graft and VSD patch repair. The patient's remarkable stability throughout this period permitted infarct maturation and fibrotic remodeling, leading to successful closure and durable 1-year outcomes without residual shunt.4

The guidelines recommend the need for urgent surgical repair in PMI-VSD; however, they differ regarding the timing of repair in stable patients.1.The 2013 American College of Cardiology Foundation/American Heart Association ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction guideline recommends urgent surgical repair of (PMI-VSD) in all patients (Class I).22.The 2021 ACC Expert Consensus advocates for early repair while recognizing the necessity of individualized decisions depending on hemodynamic stability.5

However, early repair has been associated with strikingly high mortality due to the friability of necrotic myocardium. There is accumulating evidence1,5, 6, 7 and real-world experience suggesting that delayed repair is not only feasible but may also confer a survival advantage to patients who remain stable without end-organ dysfunction.1.The CAUTION international registry reported an early mortality of 40.4% after surgical repair of PMI-VSD and markedly worse outcomes in those undergoing surgery within 7 days of infarction.12.Analysis of 2,876 patients in the STS National Database demonstrated 42.9% operative mortality overall, with significantly worse outcomes when repair occurred within 7 days compared with after 7 days (54% vs 18%).63.A systematic review and meta-analysis by Matteucci et al7 reported an overall mortality of 38.2% with worse outcomes in those undergoing emergent repair, presenting in shock, or with posterior PMI-VSD.4.The French Intensive Care Unit registry, presented in 2025, reported mortality of 45% for repairs within 7 days vs 27% for later repair, although many patients deteriorated before reaching delayed surgery.8

In an expert review by Tirone David9, he emphasizes that operative outcomes are driven by the biological substrate of the myocardium at the time of repair. He reports an operative mortality of approximately 10% in hemodynamically stable patients, compared with mortality exceeding 50% in those presenting in shock or requiring urgent surgery.9 The strategy described by Al Kindi et al embodies this paradigm. Their patient was stable enough to undergo several weeks of observation and optimization and underwent successful repair with an excellent long-term outcome.

In addition, mechanical circulatory support devices help bridge the gap in stabilizing these patients and providing the needed hemodynamic support.1.Intra-aortic balloon pump has been the gold standard (Class IIa) and provides hemodynamic stability by reducing afterload and shunting.22.The Cleveland Clinic series describes the use of Impella in PMI-VSD patients, reporting hemodynamic stabilization and 100% survival to discharge in patients bridged to delayed surgery.103.In a case series by Jalli et al11, PMI-VSD supported with Impella as a bridge-to-closure reported VSD closure at ∼10.5 days of support; nonetheless, 30-day mortality was 46%, highlighting the severity of illness and survivor bias associated with delayed strategies.

The conflict between early and delayed intervention underscores the heterogeneity of PMI-VSD. While patients in shock require urgent surgical repair or percutaneous management, those with preserved perfusion, minimal inotropic needs, and stable end-organ function may benefit from a tailored strategy of initial stabilization and planned delayed closure. Mechanical support may provide a bridge to safer surgery.

The management of PMI-VSD remains one of cardiac surgery's most daunting challenges (Figure 1).
