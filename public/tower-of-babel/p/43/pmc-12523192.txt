The severity of Tramadol misuse among youth in urban informal settlements in Ghana: patterns, co-use, and sociodemographic factors
Authors: Solomon Osei-Tutu, Obed Jones Owusu-Sarpong, Felix Asante, Williams Agyemang-Duah, Lawrencia Pokuah Siaw, Isaac Obeng Darkwa, Razak M Gyasi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTramadol misuse poses a significant public health and safety risk globally. However, research on this topic is limited in informal settlements, particularly among youth in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA). We assessed the trends, frequency, and social factors associated with the severity of Tramadol misuse among the youth residing in urban informal settlements in Ghana.MethodsCross-sectional data from 200 individuals who use Tramadol aged 15–35 years from selected communities in the Asokore Mampong Municipality were analyzed. The severity of Tramadol misuse was assessed with the item “Have you ever used Tramadol for any reason other than medical? If yes, how often do you take Tramadol?” with a 5-point response scale. Multivariable ordinal logistic regression models evaluated the associations between sociodemographic factors and the severity of Tramadol misuse.ResultsTramadol was used daily (44.5%), multiple times (68.5%), and in combination with other substances, mainly energy drinks (60%) or alcohol (12.5%). Our regression models showed that being younger (aOR = 0.87; 95%CI = 0.42–0.98); male (aOR = 1.87; 95%CI = 1.16–2.29); unemployed individuals (OR = 1.02; 95%CI = 1.50–2.86), and having low income (GH¢300–699[US$19.3–45]: aOR = 2.16; 95%CI = 1.01–4.65); < GH¢300[US$ 19.3]: aOR = 2.59; 95%CI = 1.13–5.95) were associated with higher odds for severity of Tramadol misuse. Having higher education (high school: aOR = 0.64; 95%CI = 0.25–0.96); tertiary (aOR = 0.58; 95%CI = 0.23–0.89), lowers the odds for the severity of Tramadol misuse.ConclusionChallenged social well-being indicators relate to the severity of Tramadol misuse among youth in urban informal settlements in Ghana. Interventions of regular drug surveillance, drug use educational programs, and the creation of gainful employment may help reduce Tramadol and other substance misuse among youth living in informal settlements.Keywords: Tramadol, Opioids, Non-medical use, Informal settlements, Youth, Ghana

Background

Opioid misuse remains a critical public health concern worldwide. It is the second most used drug (60 million users) after cannabis (288 million users) globally, but it is responsible for the most drug-related harm, including fatal overdoses [1]. Pharmaceutical opioids including morphine, heroin, methadone, buprenorphine, codeine, Tramadol, oxycodone, and fentanyl accounted for 69% of deaths attributed to drug use disorders, and 71% (12.9 out of the 18 million) of the healthy years of life lost due to premature death and disability in 2019 [2].

While the advanced regions including North America and Europe grapple with the opioid crisis, particularly fentanyl and its analogous, low-and-middle income countries’ (LMICs) version- the nonmedical use of Tramadol (mainly, in Africa and the Middle East) [1, 2] has been termed a double tragedy due to the additional burden it incurs on the affected countries who are already constrained with fragile healthcare systems [3]. Report from the Pan African epidemiological network on drug use (PAENDU) indicates that about 267,534 kg of Tramadol were seized between 2016 and 2021 in Africa [4]. The West Africa Drug Report shows that opioid misuse, including Tramadol, accounted for 12.7% of all treatment admissions in 2018 and 2019 [5]. In state-owned treatment centers in Egypt, about 70% of patients who were receiving treatment had harmfully used Tramadol [6].

Originally, Tramadol was chemically formulated for use under prescription to manage moderate-to-severe pain [7]. However, the analgesic has been widely reported for different off-label purposes, including enhancing sexual activities, physical performance, euphoria, and reducing fatigability [8–11]. The painkiller is readily available and accessible in chemical shops, pharmacies, open markets, and street drug dealers in many African countries, including Togo, Egypt, Benin, Ghana, and Cameroon [6]. The relative ease with which the painkiller can be accessed has been associated with its widespread nonmedical use [12–14]. Hitherto, Tramadol was reported to possess a low risk of misuse and dependence [15, 16], but recent studies suggest that its adverse effects are consistent with other opioids [17–19], including the risks of physical dependence, especially when it is used daily for a few weeks [7]. The health risks of Tramadol use include nausea, collapse, constipation, lethargy, dizziness, vomiting, seizures, tachycardia, respiratory depression, and coma [7, 20]. Its long-term and frequent use, especially in high doses, may exacerbate these adverse effects [21]. Tramadol misuse is also associated with other social issues, including job loss, risky sexual behaviors, petty theft, violence, and conviction [22–24].

Like its neighboring countries, Ghana is not spared the Tramadol ‘crisis’; its market was inundated with Tramadol with dosage strengths of 120 mg, 225 mg, 250 mg, 400 mg, and 450 mg between 2017 and 2018. However, 50 mg (tablets), 100 mg (capsules), and 50 mg/mg-2 ml for injection are what is recommended for medical treatment [20]. Meanwhile, higher dosage intake of Tramadol has been shown to exacerbate its adverse health effects, including fatal intoxication or seizure [21]. These dosage strengths are mostly co-used with other psychoactive substances such as alcohol, energy drinks, cough syrups, marijuana, methamphetamine, and benzodiazepines [19, 21, 25, 26]. This further worsens its toxicity in the body [27]. Although substance use permeates all sociodemographic groups, literature shows that it is prevalent among informal settlement dwellers [28, 29]. The harsh socio-environmental conditions of such dwellings [30, 31], with their attendant chronic psychological stressors, may predispose their inhabitants to drug use [32]. Research also indicates that the risk of substance use is higher among the youth [33]. Opioid misuse, for instance, has been found to increase during adolescence and peak between 18 and 21 years [33].

Literature on Tramadol misuse in Ghana continues to accumulate. For example, a cross-sectional survey by Elliason et al. [26] found that the youth in the Wassa Amenfi District in the Western Region of Ghana mostly used higher doses of Tramadol as an energy booster and to enhance sexual activities. In a community-based analytic study in the Jirapa Municipality of the Upper West Region, Saapiire et al. [34], reported a Tramadol misuse rate of 77.6%. They showed that formal sector employment shielded against Tramadol misuse. In the Accra Metropolis, Danso and Anto [12] revealed a Tramadol misuse prevalence of 24.9% among commercial drivers and their assistants. They found that socio-environmental factors, including tension or fighting among family members, drug use by a family member, and having friends who use drugs, were associated with Tramadol misuse. A recent institutionally-based cross-sectional study among university students in the Northern Region of Ghana reported the desire to improve academic performance, curiosity, and pleasure as the main reasons for participants’ initiation of Tramadol nonmedical use [35]. Peprah et al. [11] in a qualitative study, discovered that commercial vehicle drivers and their assistants in Kumasi used the painkiller for sexual, physical, and psychological reasons. Another qualitative finding showed that the vulnerable youth in Ghana used the painkiller in an attempt to cope with harsh economic conditions [36]. The study indicated that Tramadol’s use bolstered respondents’ energy levels, which enabled them to work harder and for longer hours to meet daily sustenance from the poor daily earnings of their jobs.

Despite this knowledge, studies on the trend and social risk factors for the severity of Tramadol misuse among the youth living in informal urban settlements in Ghana are limited. This hinders the international community’s declaration to “reach the furthest behind first” in achieving global development goals, particularly Goal 3 (Good health and wellbeing) [37]. The current study aims to fill this gap.

Methods

Study design and population

The study employed a cross-sectional design and a quantitative research approach to analyze Tramadol misuse trends and risk factors in the Asawase and Aboabo communities of the Asokore Mampong Municipality, Ghana. The cross-sectional design was employed due to its suitability for gathering data on multiple variables among a population at a snapshot and its relative cheapness [38]. The quantitative approach was also adopted to enhance the generalizability of findings due to its inherent objectivity, rigor, reliability, and validity [39].

The Asokore Mampong municipality (Fig. 1), has a total landmass of 23.91km2 and 191,402 inhabitants [40]. This makes it one of the most densely populated administrative areas in the Ashanti Region. About 59, 622 representing 31.1% of the municipality’s population are youth (15–35 years) compared to the national average of 38.2% [40]. The selection of these communities is justified because they meet most of the criteria for classifying settlements as informal [30, 31, 41]. These settlements are also perceived to be the hub for illegal drug use and trade [42]. Figure 1 shows the study area map in the national and regional context.

Map showing the study communities in the Asokore Mampong Municipality, Ghana Source: Map room, Department of Geography and Rural Development, Kwame Nkrumah University of Science and Technology, Kumasi, Ghana (2021)

The target population was the youth (15 to 35 years) as defined by the African Youth Charter [43] and Ghana’s Ministry of Youth and Sports [44] who use Tramadol for nonmedical purposes in the research sites. The study involved this age cohort because evidence from the literature suggests widespread nonmedical use of Tramadol among them [6, 10, 26, 34, 45]. A snowball sampling procedure was employed due to the study’s sensitivity. This procedure is suitable for recruiting hidden and hard-to-find populations, such as drug users, because they feel vulnerable, threatened, victimized, stigmatized, or socially excluded [46, 47]. With this, 200 participants, comprising 116 and 84 from Asawase and Aboabo, respectively, were reached through contact tracing. The process commenced when an informant introduced the researchers to a de facto leader of some Tramadol users in the Asawase community. The purpose of the study was explained to him, and trust and rapport were established after a lengthy conversation. The leader then agreed to serve as the first participant in the study. After completing his responses, the researchers requested that he recommend other prospective participants to participate in the study. A second participant was thus obtained through the referral from the first. The referral continued until it generated a network of 116 respondents for data collection in Asawase. The same protocols were followed to recruit 84 participants at Aboabo.

Primary data were obtained via a structured questionnaire. However, for the accuracy and consistency of data, the interviewer approach was employed due to the inability of some respondents to read and write. Due to its popularity as a spoken vernacular among respondents, the Twi dialect was used as the medium for interaction. The anonymity of respondents was strictly protected. The identity cards indicating the institutions the researchers were affiliated with were shown to the respondents to allay possible fears and suspicions they may harbor. Before data collection, participants signed a written informed consent form. Again, participants joined the study out of their own volition, and they had the opportunity to exit whenever they wished.

Measures

The severity of Tramadol misuse was the dependent variable for the study. This was defined as the frequency respondents used Tramadol without a medical prescription. This was assessed with the question, “Have you ever used Tramadol for any reason other than medical? If yes, how often do you take Tramadol? The responses were initially recorded on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 = daily, 2 = every three days, 3 = weekly, 4 = monthly, and 5 = more than monthly. This metric has been confirmed to help gauge the severity of substance use [48]. For analytical purposes, these responses were reverse-coded so that increasing scores reflected the severity of Tramadol misuse.

The exposure variables included age categorized into 1 = 15–24 years and 2 = 25–35 years), sex (1 = female, 2 = male), nativity status (1 = indigene, 2 = migrant), length of stay (1 = ≤ 4 years, 2 = 5–14 years, and 3 = ≥ 15 years), and marital status (1 = not partnered/single, 2 = partnered). Religious affiliation was categorized into 1 = Islam, 2 = Christianity, and 3 = Traditional religion. Living arrangement was grouped into 1 = living with others and 2 = living alone, and household size (1 = person, 2 = 2–3 persons, and 3 = ≥ 4persons). Other socioeconomic variables were educational level (1 = primary, 2 = secondary school level, 3 = tertiary), employment status (1 = employed, 2 = unemployed) and income level (GH¢) (1 = ≥ 700, 2 = 300–699, 3 = < 300).

Data analysis

Descriptive and inferential statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows application (version 21; Chicago, IL, USA) with p ˂ 0.05 as the significance level (two-tailed). Descriptive statistics were generated as frequencies and percentages to describe and compare the background characteristics of respondents. Subsequently, ordinal logistic regression models were fitted to evaluate the association between Sociodemographic factors and the severity of Tramadol misuse as an outcome measure. First, the unadjusted odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated by regressing the outcome variable on each sociodemographic variable. Finally, a multivariable regression model was built, constituting the independent variables that met the statistical significance threshold at p-value < 0.05 during the bivariate phase of analyses. We estimated the adjusted OR and 95% CI by evaluating the independent factors associated with the severity of Tramadol misuse in the overall sample. Before regression modelling, the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) and Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests were used to verify the normality of the data. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov normality test revealed that every continuous variable was normally distributed and met the assumptions. The VIF values indicated that there was no significant multicollinearity among the variables.

Results

Sample characteristics

The study recruited 200 individuals as the analytic sample. Most of them were males (90%), in the age brackets of 20–24 years (41%), and belonged to the Islamic faith (54.5%). Over half had never married (51.5%), and 39.5% each lived alone or in a single-person household. Indigenes constituted 51.7% of the overall sample, and 31% had stayed in the study communities for 15–19 years. Senior high school graduates formed 44.5%, and the majority (55.5%) earned GH¢100–299 [US$ 6.5–19.3] as monthly income. However, 21.5% of them were unemployed (Table 1).

Trend and intensity of Tramadol misuse

As shown in Fig. 2, about 36.5% of the respondents used 225 mg Tramadol dosage strength or higher. Only 11.5% of the sample used the medically recommended dose of 50 mg even though it was without prescription and also constituted misuse. Moreover, 44.5% used the painkiller daily, while 33.5% used it every three (3) days (Table 2). Out of those who used the painkiller on daily basis, 68.5% used it twice or more times (Table 2). Meanwhile, a substantial proportion (75%) always combined and used Tramadol with other substances (Table 2), mainly energy drinks (60%) or alcohol (12.5%) (Fig. 3).

The results of the associations between Sociodemographic factors and the severity of Tramadol misuse are reported in Table 3. The crude estimates at the bivariate analysis stage (Model 1) found that age, sex, length of stay in the community, educational level, employment status, and income significantly influence the severity of Tramadol misuse (p-value < 0.05). Discussion

Male participants dominated our study. A study by Ibrahim et al. [49], which involved 93% male participants, is consistent with the present study’s outcome. One possible explanation for the male dominance could be the generally higher rate of substance use among males than females [50]. Also, the added shame and stigma associated with female substance use in the Ghanaian socio-cultural context may lead them to conceal their substance use habits [45].

Our findings showed that most respondents used a ≥ 225 mg dosage of Tramadol. This aligns with previous studies. For instance, Chikezie and Ebuenyi [51] in a case study report in the Niger Delta State, Nigeria, observed that subjects used Tramadol dosages 800 mg -1400 mg which is 2–4 times higher than the recommended maximum dose per day [20, 52]. Similarly, Saapiire et al. [34] found that the youth in Jirapa Municipality of the Upper West Region of Ghana mostly used Tramadol dosages ≥ 100 mg.

We further found that the painkiller was mainly used daily. Additionally, over two-thirds of those who used it daily did so multiple times. The outcome of a study by Ibrahim et al. [49] among patients receiving treatment for harmful Tramadol use in North-Eastern Nigeria, reflects our current results. A study in Sweden revealed that participants kept increasing doses of Tramadol until they exceeded the medically acceptable daily limit [53]. The craving for and use of higher doses of Tramadol at shorter intervals may be due to the urge to increase the perceived benefits of the painkiller. Perhaps it is also a sign of drug tolerance.

Another noteworthy finding of our study was that three-quarters (75%) of the respondents engaged in Tramadol co-use with other psychoactive substances, predominantly energy drinks, akpeteshie (a locally distilled alcohol), or marijuana. The poly-substance use of Tramadol with other substances mirrors previously published literature [35]. For example, cough syrups, marijuana, methamphetamine, and benzodiazepines are some of the substances that participants co-used with Tramadol in Egypt [25], Sweden [24], Iran [21], and China [19]. In the Western Region of Ghana, Elliason et al. [26] identified alcohol and energy drinks as the primary substances participants used together with Tramadol. Tramadol effects are short-lived- 4–6 hours [54], and its use with other substances may complement and extend these effects. Nonetheless, studies suggest that poly-substance use [55] results in severe risky behaviors and adverse health outcomes [56]. For example, Boostani and Derakshan [57] in a three-year study among patients receiving treatment for Tramadol misuse-related disorders in Imam Khomeini and Shahid Motahari Hospital in Iran, observed that subjects who used more than 400 mg of the painkiller with either alcohol, other illicit drugs, anti-depressants or psychotics experienced seizure within 24 hours.

Our regression models show that the severity of Tramadol misuse decreased with age. Respondents aged 15–24 years had higher odds (13%) for severe use of Tramadol compared to their older (25–35 years) counterparts. Nonmedical prescription opioid use has been found to increase at adolescence, peaking at ages 18–21 years and decreasing through ages 30–34 years [33]. The outcome by Muchiri and Dos Santos [58] in Pretoria, South Africa, which showed that the odds for substance use, particularly central nervous depressants, decreased by 1.4 times as the adolescent ages, also validates our finding. The outcome was expected as most respondents were 15–24 years old and were likely to show signs of adolescence, including risk-taking [59]. Duru et al. [60], however, found that age was not a significant risk factor for substance use among tertiary students in Imo State, Nigeria. This disparity may strongly relate to population and contextual traits.

Our models further showed that respondents who had resided in the study communities for five or more years had a 64% lower urge to intensify the misuse of Tramadol than those who had lived there for less than five years. A study in the Accra Metropolis of Ghana reported that commercial drivers who had worked for more than 21 years were less likely to misuse Tramadol [12]. Perhaps those who had lived in the study communities for a significantly longer period had ‘adapted’ to the conditions in their immediate environment and could better cope with the socio-environmental challenges than those who had lived there for a relatively short period.

Those with secondary and tertiary education were also 36% and 42% less likely to severely misuse Tramadol compared to those with basic or no formal education. This is in line with what Agberotimi et al. [61] observed that educational attainment significantly relates to Tramadol and codeine misuse among skilled workers in Osogbo, Nigeria. Conceivably, those with higher education may be better enlightened and guided by the potentially harmful consequences of substance misuse, including Tramadol misuse. Relatedly, unemployed individuals and low-income earners were found to intensify Tramadol misuse. Ibrahim et al. [49] revealed that the educational status of respondents determined the kind of job they did. Those with less than 12 years of education were either unskilled workers or completely unemployed and were more likely to misuse Tramadol severely. Findings from the US revealed that those in lower-income groups were 1.36 times more likely to report substance use and related problems than those in higher-income groups [62]. Possibly, unemployed individuals engaged in severe use of Tramadol to overcome the emotional and psychological distress tied to it [63]. Alhassan [36], for instance, revealed that the youth in Ghana use Tramadol as a coping mechanism for the harsh economic and social difficulties, including joblessness, soaring prices, and power instability (popularly called dumsor in Ghana). Contrary to our findings, El Wasify et al. [64] found that people who are employed and students severely misused the painkiller in Egypt. They explained that the harsh economic conditions in Egypt at the time forced most people, including students, to work manually in exhausting jobs for long hours. They needed to improve their work power to cope with the situation. Their finding reinforces Tramadol’s off-label efficacy for enhancing physical performance in randomized controlled trials in the UK and Spain [65, 66].

Finally, males showed increased odds of severe misuse of Tramadol compared to females. This is in alignment with previous studies, including Saapiire et al. [34] in Ghana, Baptiste-Roberts and Hossain [62] in the US, and Bassiony et al. [25] in Egypt. For instance, in a survey among university students in the Northern Region of Ghana, Lasong et al. [35] found that males were more than 2.6 times more likely to misuse Tramadol than females. However, this outcome must be interpreted cautiously due to the disproportionately low female participation in this study.

Implications of the study

At the policy level, we recommend the creation of decent and gainful job avenues to help ameliorate the living conditions of youth living in informal settlements. For instance, through its Ministry of Trade and Industry, the government may establish at least one factory in each study community as part of its flagship policy, “One District, One Factory”. This will contribute significantly to controlling Tramadol and other drug use and related behaviors. We also propose that the Asokore Mampong Municipal Assembly, together with the Municipal Education Directorate, put in place actionable strategies to ensure that all children and young people of school-going age in the study communities take advantage of the central government’s free basic and secondary level education policy and enroll. Finally, the National Scholarship Secretariat, in collaboration with the Municipal Assembly, may also initiate scholarship schemes and award needy but brilliant students in the study areas to enable them to further their education at the tertiary level. This can be a medium-to-long-term measure to reduce drug use, including Tramadol.

In terms of service delivery, our study proposes the organization of regular educational programs to sensitize the youth on the health, social, economic, and security implications of substance use, including Tramadol misuse. This should be the responsibility of the Asokore Mampong Municipal Assembly in collaboration with the Food and Drugs Authority (FDA) and the Municipal Directorate of the Ministry of Health. It is, again, recommended that the FDA conducts random inspections in the hide-out spaces within the study sites to search for and seize unapproved and usually fake dosages of Tramadol and other illicit drugs. Government should also make a conscious effort to cover the funding for the treatment and recovery support for individuals with problematic substance use, including Tramadol use. For instance, the National Health Insurance Scheme (NHIS) could cover such therapies’ cost.

Study limitations

The study contributes to the limited knowledge of drug use, specifically regarding the trend and severity of Tramadol misuse in vulnerable populations within the SSA context. Despite the strength, some limitations should be acknowledged. First, our study adopted a cross-sectional design, which prevented drawing causal conclusions. Future research may benefit from longitudinal data and build on our findings. The sensitive nature of the subject under investigation and the self-report employed could lead to social desirability biases and overestimating the findings.

Conclusions

Although substance use, including Tramadol, permeates all socio-demographic groups, it is widely perceived to be pervasive among vulnerable populations such as informal urban settlement dwellers. Yet, there is a shortage of scientific studies on the problem in this geographically unique space. The study was therefore critical as it sought to fill this knowledge gap by assessing the trend, frequency, and social risk factors that affect the severity of Tramadol misuse among the youth in informal urban settlements in the Asokore Mampong Municipality, Ghana. This study found that Tramadol was mainly used daily and multiple times to exceed the medically recommended dose and in concomitance with other substances that have psychoactive properties. The study further showed that being younger, unemployed individuals, having a lower income, and being male significantly increased the odds of severe use of Tramadol. However, the severity of Tramadol misuse decreased with higher educational attainment and a longer period of stay in the study communities.
