JNK/c-Jun cascade activation enhances malignant proliferation of psoriatic keratinocytes by regulating ROS levels and mitochondrial membrane potential
Authors: Yu Zhang, Anqi Yin, Nannan Tong, Yadong Xue, Yuzhen Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Psoriasis is a chronic immune-mediated skin disorder characterized by excessive keratinocyte proliferation, inflammation, and oxidative stress. This study investigates the role of the JNK/c-Jun cascade in psoriasis pathogenesis, focusing on its impact on keratinocyte proliferation and inflammatory responses. An in vitro psoriasis model was established using M5-stimulated HaCaT keratinocytes, while an in vivo model was created with imiquimod-treated Wistar rats. The results indicated that M5 stimulation significantly enhanced keratinocyte viability and proliferation, as demonstrated by increased optical density, EdU incorporation, and Ki-67 expression. M5 treatment also elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, while inducing oxidative stress through increased ROS production, lipid peroxidation, and mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) disruption. Blocking the JNK/c-Jun cascade with the SP600125 inhibitor effectively reduced keratinocyte hyperproliferation, cytokine secretion, and oxidative stress, while restoring mitochondrial integrity. In addition, knockdown of Nrf2 suppressed M5-induced ROS generation, inflammatory signaling, and antioxidant enzyme activity. In psoriasis rats, JNK/c-Jun inhibition significantly alleviated skin tissue damage, reducing inflammatory cell infiltration, epidermal hyperkeratosis, and phosphorylation of key inflammatory markers. Correspondingly, serum pro-inflammatory cytokines were decreased, and oxidative stress indices improved. These findings suggest that the JNK/c-Jun cascade plays a central role in psoriasis pathogenesis by regulating keratinocyte proliferation, inflammation, and oxidative stress. Targeting this pathway presents a promising therapeutic strategy for psoriasis treatment. Introduction

Psoriasis is an immune-mediated, hereditary skin disease involving the interplay between innate and adaptive immune systems and presents numerous challenges due to its high prevalence, chronic course, potential for disfigurement and disability, and associated comorbidities [1, 2]. Clinically, psoriasis typically manifests as chronic, symmetrical erythematous lesions with scaly papules and plaques, and is marked by uncontrolled keratinocyte proliferation, impaired differentiation, and excessive immune cell infiltration in both the dermis and epidermis [3, 4]. Keratinocytes play a pivotal role in initiating and sustaining the inflammatory state characteristic of psoriasis. Various factors—including reactive oxygen species (ROS), cytokines, and cell signaling pathways—regulate keratinocyte function and thus impact disease progression [5]. In particular, heightened ROS levels stimulate immune cells and keratinocytes, activating multiple signaling pathways implicated in psoriasis pathogenesis [6]. The resultant overproduction and accumulation of ROS further exacerbate inflammatory responses in keratinocytes [7]. Interventions targeting these molecular and cellular factors represent promising strategies for the treatment of psoriasis.

Psoriasis pathogenesis involves multiple signaling cascades, among which the JNK pathway plays a crucial role [8]. As a subset of MAP kinases, JNK is primarily activated by cytokines and environmental stressors. Once activated, it promotes keratinocyte proliferation and drives the secretion of various chemokines and cytokines, thereby recruiting immune cells and further disrupting skin cell homeostasis, ultimately exacerbating disease severity [9]. In addition, the AP-1 transcription factor c-Jun, a downstream target of JNK, has been implicated in regulating cell proliferation in cutaneous lichen planus [10]. Although the involvement of the JNK/c-Jun cascade in psoriasis has been established, its precise mechanism remains controversial, likely due to cell-specific effects. For instance, blocking the JNK/c-Jun cascade mitigates psoriasis-like inflammation in dendritic cells [11], whereas activating the JNK/c-Jun cascade through erianin treatment inhibits keratinocyte proliferation and induces apoptosis [12].

Given the complex nature of psoriasis pathogenesis and the limitations of the current treatment approaches, this study aimed to address the abnormal proliferation of keratinocytes by targeting oxidative stress, inflammation, and mitochondrial dysfunction. Specifically, we investigated how modulating the JNK/c-Jun cascade might help in managing psoriasis.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

HaCaT keratinocytes (BeNa Culture Collection, Beijing, China) were maintained in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco) and 100 U/mL penicillin–100 μg/mL streptomycin (Sangon, Shanghai, China). Cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂.

Establishment of psoriasis model

When HaCaT keratinocytes reached 60–70% confluence, they were serum-starved in DMEM for 12 h and then stimulated with M5 (10 ng/mL; PeproTech) to establish an in vitro psoriasis model. Cells treated with PBS served as controls. To block the JNK/c-Jun cascade, the keratinocytes were subsequently exposed to 10 μM SP600125 (HY-12041, MedChemExpress) for 12 h following M5 stimulation.

CCK-8 experiment

Keratinocytes were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5000 cells per well. At the designated time points (24, 48, and 72 h), 10 µL of CCK-8 solution (Dojindo, Japan) was added to each well, and the plates were incubated in the dark for 2 h. Absorbance was then measured at 450 nm using a Thermo Fisher microplate reader (USA).

EdU proliferation experiment

Keratinocyte proliferation was assessed using an EdU assay kit (RiboBio). Briefly, 1 × 104 keratinocytes were seeded into each well of a 96-well plate and incubated with 50 μM EdU at 37 °C for 2 h. Cells were then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min and treated with 2 mg/mL glycine for 5 min. After permeabilization with 0.5% PBS–Triton X-100 for 10 min, cells were stained with the Apollo reaction solution for 30 min. Subsequently, multiple washes with 0.5% PBS–Triton X-100 were performed, followed by Hoechst staining in the dark for 30 min. Images were captured using a Nikon microscope, and the percentage of EdU-positive cells was determined by counting four randomly selected fields.

Western blot

Total proteins were extracted from skin tissues and keratinocytes at 4 °C using pre-chilled RIPA lysis buffer. Protein concentrations were measured with a BCA assay kit (20201ES76, Yeasen, Shanghai, China). Equal amounts of protein were separated by polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE), transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA), and blocked with 5% BSA at room temperature for 1 h. Membranes were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, followed by incubation with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (ab6721, Abcam) at room temperature for 1 h. Protein bands were visualized using an electrochemiluminescence system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and analyzed with ImageJ software (version 1.50b; NIH). The primary antibodies used included Ki-67 (ab15580, Abcam), JNK (#9252, Cell Signaling Technology [CST]), p-JNK (#9251, CST), c-Jun (#9165, CST), p-c-Jun (#sc-822, Santa Cruz Biotechnology), p-p65 (#3031, CST), p65 (#ab16502, Abcam), and Nrf2 (#12721, CST).

Inflammation and oxidation index detection

The concentrations of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 in both cells and serum were measured using an ELISA kit (eBioscience) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Meanwhile, the levels of superoxide dismutase (SOD), malondialdehyde (MDA), and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px) were determined using commercial kits from the Nanjing Jiancheng Institute of Bioengineering (China). All measurements were performed in accordance with the corresponding kit protocols.

ROS detection

Keratinocytes (6 × 105 cells per well) were seeded in 6-well plates, collected by centrifugation, resuspended in PBS, and then incubated with 20 μM DCFH-DA at 37 °C for 30 min. After labeling, the cells were washed twice with PBS to remove excess dye and analyzed by flow cytometry (Beckman Coulter, USA) with an excitation wavelength of 480 nm.

Mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) assessment

Keratinocytes (5 × 105 cells/well) were seeded into 6-well plates, collected by centrifugation, resuspended in PBS, and then stained with JC-1 at 37 °C for 20 min. After staining, cells were washed twice with 1 × JC-1 staining buffer and analyzed by flow cytometry.

Establishment of a psoriasis rat model

Animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the 2nd Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University. Fifteen adults male Wistar rats (250–300 g; Hunan SJA Company, Changsha, China) were housed under standard conditions (24 ± 2 °C, 50–60% humidity) with free access to food and water. Psoriasis was induced using imiquimod (IMQ). Briefly, the fur on the dorsal-lumbar area was removed, and 5% IMQ cream (Aldara; 3 M Pharmaceuticals) was applied topically once daily for six consecutive days. To inhibit the JNK/c-Jun cascade, SP600125 (30 mg/kg) was administered to the rats via the caudal vein before the first IMQ application. At the end of the treatment, rats were sacrificed by inhalation of excess CO₂, and blood was collected via the orbital vein for serum preparation. Skin samples were harvested, with one portion fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and the remainder stored at − 80 °C.

HE staining

Skin tissues were removed from 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4 μm thickness using a Leica SM2000R microtome (Germany). The sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and observed under a Nikon E200 optical microscope at 400 × magnification.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were carried out in GraphPad Prism 10.0. Data are presented as mean ± SD from at least three independent biological replicates (each measured in technical triplicate for cell‑based assays) or from individual animals. Normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk and Brown–Forsythe tests, respectively. When both assumptions were met, comparisons used one‑way or two‑way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple‑comparison test; otherwise, the Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn’s post hoc correction was applied. Two‑tailed P < 0.05 defined statistical significance (*P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001).

Results

Cytokine and mediator elevation by M5 and attenuation by NAC

Having mapped the trigger arm, we next examined whether the antioxidant transcription factor Nrf2 modulates this response. Neutralizing ROS with NAC restored MMP and normalized proliferation, cytokine output and oxidative indices (Fig. 2D–J), indicating that ROS lies upstream of the phenotypes observed in Section “Introduction”. M5 induced phosphorylation of NF-κB p65, c-Jun, and JNK were suppressed by NAC treatment (Fig. 2K). Consistent with a built‑in feedback mechanism, M5 caused rapid nuclear translocation of Nrf2 (Fig. 3A, B). Blocking ROS with NAC prevented this translocation (Fig. 3C, D), and shRNA‑mediated Nrf2 knock‑down exaggerated ROS accumulation, MMP loss, JNK/c‑Jun/NF‑κB phosphorylation and downstream proliferative and inflammatory read‑outs (Fig. 3E–M). Similarly, Nrf2 inhibitor ML385 inhibited M5-induced phosphorylation of JNK/c‑Jun/NF‑κB (Fig. 3N). Thus, Nrf2 functions as a brake on the ROS‑JNK loop identified above. Blocking the JNK/c-Jun cascade reduces ROS production and stabilizes MMP

To pinpoint JNK as the pivotal conduit between oxidative stress and downstream pathology, we pharmacologically silenced its activity. SP600125 treatment resulted in significant reductions in the phosphorylation levels of JNK, c-Jun and NF-κB p65 (Fig. 4A). Knockdown of Nrf2 plus SP600125 treatment further reduced in the phosphorylation levels of JNK, c-Jun and NF-κB p65 (Fig. 4B). Consequently, cell proliferation was suppressed (Fig. 4C–E). Treatment with SP600125 led to a significant reduction in the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 (Fig. 4F). Oxidative stress markers including lipid peroxidation, and ROS levels were inhibited following SP600125 treatment (Fig. 4G and H). Concomitantly, mitochondrial integrity was improved by SP600125 (Fig. 4I). Because JNK inhibition fed back to lower ROS and p‑p65, these data position JNK between the oxidative burst and the downstream NF‑κB/proliferative responses, validating its pivotal mediator role. Blocking the JNK/c-Jun cascade effectively ameliorates skin tissue damage in psoriatic rats

Finally, we translated the in‑vitro findings to an animal model to evaluate therapeutic relevance. In the IMQ rat model, phosphorylated JNK, c-Jun and NF-κB p65 were significantly increased in psoriasis rats, while administration of SP600125 inhibited phosphorylated JNK, c-Jun, and NF-κB p65 (Fig. 5A). H&E staining showed enhanced immune cell infiltration and epidermal hyperkeratosis in the skin of psoriatic rats, which was effectively alleviated by administration of SP600125 (Fig. 5B). In addition, serum TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and MDA were increased, while SOD and GSH-PX activities were decreased. SP600125 reversed the changes in psoriatic rats (Fig. 5C and D). IHC staining of Ki67, JNK, p-JNK, c-Jun, and p–c-Jun were consistently indicated that SP600125 can alleviate inflammation in psoriatic rats (Fig. 5E). Discussion

This study was designed to delineate how the psoriatic cytokine cocktail M5 drives keratinocyte dysfunction and to test whether interrupting the implicated signaling hub offers therapeutic benefit. Our data reveal a coherent pathway in which M5 provokes ROS that activates the JNK/c Jun kinase cascade. JNK, in turn, amplifies NF κB activity, establishing a self-reinforcing inflammatory loop, while the antioxidant transcription factor Nrf2 provides a negative feedback brake. Disrupting this signaling circuit with the selective inhibitor SP600125 restores mitochondrial membrane potential, curtails keratinocyte hyper proliferation and cytokine release in vitro, and markedly mitigates epidermal hyperkeratosis and inflammation in an imiquimod induced psoriasis model, highlighting JNK inhibition as a promising strategy for redox driven skin pathology.

JC 1 analysis revealed a rapid loss of ΔΨm in M5 treated keratinocytes, an effect fully reversed by the JNK inhibitor SP600125. Three converging mechanisms can account for this collapse. First, the ROS surge triggered by M5 oxidizes cardiolipin, loosening respiratory chain super complexes and sensitizing the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) to opening [13, 14]. Second, M5 provokes JNK phosphorylation, which in turn drives Bax/Bim translocation to the outer membrane; this pro apoptotic shift, documented in both psoriasis and cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma, enhances cytochrome c leakage and further dissipates ΔΨm [14, 15]. Third, TNF α within the M5 cocktail can signal through TNF R1 to activate caspase 8, producing an even faster “quick drop” in membrane potential that is relevant to psoriasis and atopic dermatitis [16]. Together, these routes rationalize the profound mitochondrial depolarization we observed and underscore ROS–JNK–TNF convergence as a central bioenergetic liability in psoriatic keratinocytes.

Activation of JNK phosphorylated Nrf2, promoting its nuclear accumulation, consistent with JNK dependent priming reported in psoriatic and atopic skin [17, 18]. Once in the nucleus, Nrf2 up regulated detoxifying enzymes, lowered ROS levels and blunted ASK1/JNK signaling, feedback seen when NAC blocked Nrf2 translocation, whereas shNrf2 prolonged p JNK and exacerbated oxidative damage [17–19]. Notably, excessive or chronic Nrf2 activation can itself aggravate cutaneous inflammation [20], underscoring the need for a balanced, dual phase control that we interpret as a self-limiting ROS sensor within the M5 response.

SP600125 collapsed p p65 both in vitro and in IMQ treated rat skin, confirming a pharmacological link between the NF-κB and JNk signaling [21, 22]. Because ROS amplifies both pathways [23, 24], the M5 milieu forms a feed forward loop in which oxidative stress maintains JNK, JNK maintains NF κB, and NF κB derived cytokines (TNF α, IL 1β) feed back to JNK. Clinically, this explains why JNK inhibitors—by hitting the loop at its enzymatic chokepoint—indirectly temper NF κB driven inflammation in psoriasis and cSCC [21, 25].

The efficacy of SP600125 in normalizing epidermal architecture and inflammatory indices in psoriatic rats provides proof of concept that interrupting the JNK node is therapeutically actionable. Among small molecules, tanzisertib and AS602801 have already reached early clinical testing in psoriasis, showing acceptable safety with attention to hepatotoxicity [26, 27]. Topical peptide inhibitors, such as D JNK 1 are emerging for UV photo damage, offering high local exposure with minimal systemic risk [28]. Extending JNK blockade to atopic dermatitis, UV induced damage and cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma is biologically plausible, given the shared ROS–JNK signature in these diseases [28, 29]. Our data therefore fit a growing translational landscape in which route of delivery and liver monitoring will determine how broadly JNK inhibitors can be deployed.

Our work has several limitations that should guide the next experimental steps. First, because we relied on pharmacological blockade, we cannot exclude off target effects of SP600125; confirmation in keratinocyte specific JNK1/2 knockout models will be essential to establish causality unequivocally. Second, the in vivo study is insufficient to evaluate long-term efficacy, cutaneous tolerability, or systemic safety. Extended dosing in chronic psoriasis models, coupled with serum chemistry and histopathology, is therefore warranted. Third, our data hint that Nrf2 provides an endogenous brake on the ROS–JNK loop; testing combinatorial regimens that pair a JNK inhibitor with an Nrf2 activator could reveal additive or synergistic benefits while allowing lower JNK inhibitor doses to mitigate safety concerns. Future studies should also explore topical delivery platforms to concentrate drug action in the epidermis and minimize systemic exposure.

In summary, we reported a pathogenic circuit in which M5 induced ROS activates the JNK/c Jun cascade, which reinforces NF κB signaling, while Nrf2 acts as a modulatory brake; pharmacological disruption of this loop restores mitochondrial integrity and alleviates psoriasis like skin pathology.
