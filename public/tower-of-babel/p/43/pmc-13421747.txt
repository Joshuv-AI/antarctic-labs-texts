Goal attainment scaling for patients with chronic low back pain receiving physical therapist care: a feasibility and reliability study
Authors: Douglas Haladay, Ava Rabbers, Zoe Morris, Rebecca Edgeworth Ditwiler, Jason Beckstead, Rebecca Miro, Aimee Klein
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLow back pain is a widespread health concern in the United States. Traditional measures used for low back pain may lack meaningfulness to patients and be unable to capture patient’s individual experience. This study aimed to examine the feasibility of a goal attainment scaling application for chronic low back pain patients (GAS-Back) and the nature of goals identified by patients with chronic low back pain (cLBP) receiving physical therapist (PT) care.MethodsThis feasibility study consists of a single-group, longitudinal, preliminary study with variation in baseline, intervention, and follow-up for each participant. It was conducted at the University of South Florida Health Physical Therapy Center (USF PT Center) in Tampa, FL. Convenience sampling was used to recruit potential participants. Thirty-three participants (n = 33) with cLBP, ranging from 21 to 64 years old, were enrolled in this study and completed GAS-Back in PT care. The primary outcomes of this study were GAS-Back training time (hours), GAS-Back implementation time (minutes and seconds), PT adherence (percent adherence to protocol), interrater reliability, and patient satisfaction and engagement. Secondary outcomes were the domain and subdomain for the nature of goals identified (percentages) according to the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF) categorization.ResultsGAS-Back clinician training time (7 h) and the average GAS-Back implementation time (9 min 9 s) were both considered to be efficient. Clinician adherence (89%) was considered feasible. High interrater reliability was observed for both GAS-Back goal comparison (95%) and GAS T-Score comparison (0.782). The majority of patient’s (93%) noted “Very Good” or “Excellent” satisfaction and PTs (63%) observed mostly “Very Good” or “Excellent” patient engagement with GAS-Back. Participants set an average of 3 goals to which majority of participants (88%) identified at least one goal pertaining to activity limitations.ConclusionThis study demonstrates the feasibility and fidelity of using GAS-Back for patients with cLBP receiving PT care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-026-10033-6.Keywords: Goals, Low back pain, Physical therapy, Rehabilitation, Feasibility studies

Background

Low back pain (LBP) is a common health condition with a lifetime prevalence of 65–80% in adults living in the United States [1, 2] Additionally, LBP is the primary cause of years lived with disability and workdays lost in the United States [1, 2]. Just over one-sixth of working Americans lose about 10.5 days of work per year due to LBP [3]. In addition to the personal burden, direct healthcare costs exceeding $134.5 billion and indirect costs exceeding $50 billion are spent on LBP annually [4]. While the prognosis for LBP is favorable, it is estimated that approximately one-third of patients with LBP report symptoms exceeding three months referred to as chronic low back pain (cLBP) [5]. The chronicity and variability in etiology of LBP create a challenge when determining effective treatment [6]. The standard for measuring the impact of physical therapist (PT) interventions for cLBP patients is the use of patient-reported outcomes (PROs) such as the Numerical Pain Rating Scale (NPRS) and the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) [7–9]. These measures are often inadequate and may lead to errors in clinical decision-making due to their standardized fixed-item questionnaires [10]. These measures often disregard the individualized needs, impairments, and restrictions of each patient. Therefore, the low to modest treatment effects reported for most cLBP intervention studies may be due to the inadequacy of standardized tools to measure outcomes based on cLBP patients’ individual experience [11–19].

Patient-centered care and GAS

Studies suggest that patient participation, or patient-centeredness, in decisions about care improves compliance [20, 21], patient confidence [22], patient autonomy [23, 24], and clinician rapport [23, 24]. The present healthcare environment emphasizes patient-centered outcomes. However, clinical outcomes used for patients with LBP often fail to incorporate patient-centeredness and may not fully reflect the scope of a patient’s individual needs [25].

Goal attainment scaling (GAS) is an internationally recognized patient-centered approach where patients identify and set individualized SMART (specific, measurable, attainable, realistic, and timely) treatment goals in collaboration with their healthcare provider [26–28]. The individuality of GAS provides a unique measurement for patient populations with complex presentations by incorporating biopsychosocial factors that affect the whole person as an appropriate tool for heterogeneous patient populations [29–32].

GAS can be used to define and incorporate a patient’s impairments, activity limitations, and participation restrictions from a biopsychosocial perspective represented by domains of the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF) [33–36]. The use of these domains from the ICF when setting and evaluating goals can provide consistency and structure.

Challenges to GAS

While GAS does not replace established standardized assessment tools, it provides supplementation to better reflect goals that are important for individual treatment success in patients with cLBP [37–39]. GAS T-scores are used in GAS to measure achievement level of a patient’s individualized goal(s) based on difficulty and importance [26]; this measure allows patients and PTs to have a more thorough understanding of an individual’s progress in PT care. Recent studies have shown that GAS has robust psychometric properties appropriate for clinical use; however, there is a need to standardize GAS practices and validate the feasibility and reliability of GAS processes in clinical settings [40–42]. Several concerns regarding the use of GAS have been identified, including time consuming implementation, methodological inconsistencies, scale variation, inconsistencies in selecting expected outcomes, and difficulty with identifying specific measurable outcomes [42–44].

Although prior research on GAS has been conducted in patients with cLBP, feasibility assessment of a structured GAS procedure for cLBP patients in a clinical setting has not been published [42, 45]. A feasibility study, which includes measurements such as protocol adherence, percent lost to follow-up, and the response rate of eligible participants [46], is warranted to determine GAS feasibility and reliability. Therefore, the primary purpose of this study was to establish the feasibility and reliability of GAS-Back, an application of GAS for cLBP patients, provided by PTs to patients with cLBP in clinical practice. Additionally, understanding the nature of goals that patients with cLBP set for themselves can improve patient-provider collaboration, effectiveness, and efficiency in planning, implementing, and assessing PT treatment [45]. Thus, the secondary aim of this study was to determine what domains of ICF patients with cLBP identified most when setting goals.

Methods

Participants

After approval from the University of South Florida’s Institutional Review Board (IRB) (Pro00035236), participants were recruited and consented by the University of South Florida Health Physical Therapy Center (USF PT Center) in Tampa, FL to participate in a single-group longitudinal preliminary study. Participants were recruited through convenience sampling. The demographics of Tampa, FL and the surrounding area are representative of national demographics. Participants meeting all of the following criteria were included in this study: 21–64 years of age, a presence of LBP (defined as pain located between the lower rib cage and gluteal fold) that has been present for > 12 weeks and not attributable to a specific pathology. In addition, eligible participants must have reported pain on at least 50% of the days in the past 6 months [47], and an average pain intensity > 2/10 per the NPRS. Participant exclusion criteria and sample size justification are described elsewhere [45]. There was no control group or randomization due to the nature of feasibility studies [46]. Potential participants were screened and provided informed consent before participation. Participants completed the ODI, NPRS, and National Institutes of Health (NIH) Minimal Dataset surveys at baseline as part of the usual standard of care and to describe the population of interest. The ODI was used to assess disability, the NPRS to assess pain intensity at different periods, and the NIH Minimal Dataset survey to record demographics, medical history, and LBP history. More information on the enrollment process can be found elsewhere [45].

GAS-back clinician training

PTs from the USF PT Center received training and follow-up assessments in GAS-Back based upon the principles of Williams and Stieg [48]. PTs were introduced and familiarized with the unique GAS-Back form developed from the first phase of the study. More information detailing the training received can be found elsewhere [45].

GAS-back implementation

In this study, GAS-Back was used to track and evaluate goals in a patient-centered collaborative format. The GAS-Back form was developed for this study and has been further described elsewhere [45]. This form can be found in the supplementary files. The feasibility of GAS-Back was measured by capturing the time of training and implementation, checklist adherence, interrater reliability, and patient satisfaction and engagement (Table 1). GAS-Back was considered feasible if training was completed within 10 h [49], implementation was completed within 15 min [50], checklist adherence was ≥ 80% [45, 51], interrater reliability was ≥ 0.75 [52, 53], and patient satisfaction and engagement surveys respectively had a completion rate of ≥ 80% [54]. In addition to feasibility, the nature of goals identified by participants were categorized by ICF domain.

GAS-Back feasibility measures

All participants enrolled in GAS-Back identified three to five SMART treatment goals in collaboration with their PT during their first visit at the USF PT Center [28]. Each goal was written on the GAS-Back form in the appropriate ICF domain category. Importance and difficulty were decided by the participant and PT. Patient satisfaction and engagement were captured at the end of this initial session. All initial GAS-Back visits were audio recorded.

Two independent PTs reviewed the forms from the GAS-Back sessions and listened to the audio recordings. The reviewers determined the time to implement GAS-Back, checklist adherence, and goal comparison from the recordings.

All participants received standard care for their cLBP based on the goals they and their PT set during their initial visit. Interventions were based on contemporary clinical practice guidelines [55, 56], but varied based on the nature of the participant’s goals, PT expertise, and status of cLBP. Warranted variation based on individual needs is expected and accepted across PT practice [57]. Following the intervention, the participant’s performance was evaluated using the GAS-Back form against the expected outcome for each goal set at the initial visit. The GAS-Back form was used to calculate a GAS T-score by two independent PT reviewers for each participant after intervention completion. GAS T-scores are the standard measure of goal achievement in GAS. A more detailed description of the GAS-Back clinical implementation can be found elsewhere [45]. After completing all GAS-Back sessions, PTs were interviewed by members of the research team. Interview transcripts were read by the research team to develop and understand the main themes presented, and thematic analysis of these interviews was completed using Copilot [58].

Statistical analysis

Means and standard deviations (SDs) were used to summarize continuous participant demographic characteristics and baseline NPRS and ODI scores. Percentages were used to summarize categorical characteristics.

Mean, SD, and range of initial GAS-Back sessions were used to summarize the time to implement GAS-Back. PT checklist adherence to the GAS-Back procedure was measured for each initial GAS-Back session as a percent; the average percent of these was then reported as the checklist adherence. Percent agreement of participant goals was used to determine one measure of interrater reliability. The intraclass correlation coefficient was used to determine the interrater reliability of GAS T-score calculation. Patient satisfaction and engagement levels were reported as percentages. Table 1 describes these measures and their preset cutoff values in further detail. The mean number of goals set per participant and percentage of goals in each domain and subdomain were reported as measures of the nature of goals identified (ICF). All analyses were performed using version 26 of the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

Results

Participant flow and retention is shown in Fig. 1. Of those assessed for eligibility (n = 50), 17 did not meet the inclusion criteria. Thirty-three participants were enrolled in the study. Twenty-seven of the 33 completed the final GAS-Back form with their clinician (82% retention rate). Participants were recruited between March and September 2021. Physical therapy sessions and follow-up time varied with each participant. Outcome data is not available for the six participants who were lost to follow-up or withdrew. The study was concluded when all enrolled participants completed their episode of care.

Participant characteristics

Five PTs (4 males and 1 female) with an average of 10.4 years of professional experience (range: 1–22 years) from the USF PT Center received GAS-Back training. Four research PTs were American Board of Physical Therapy Specialties Board Certified Orthopaedic Clinical Specialists, while one PT was a Fellow of the American Academy of Orthopedic Manual Therapy.

Feasibility measures

The total time for PTs to complete GAS-Back training was six hours. The post-intervention follow-up knowledge and fidelity assessments took approximately one hour to complete. The total GAS-Back clinician training time was seven hours, completed in one workday which met the preset feasibility cutoff (≤ 10 h). PTs who completed GAS-Back training received continued education units (CEUs) from USF.

Average time to administer GAS-Back during the participants (n = 33) initial visit was 9 min and 9 s (± 2 min and 30 s) which met the preset feasibility cutoff (< 15 min). The time of GAS-Back sessions ranged from 5 min to 30 s to 16 min and 13 s.

Average checklist adherence (n = 33) was 89%, exceeding the minimum feasibility requirements (≥ 80%). The average goal comparison score (n = 33) was 95% which exceeded the minimum feasibility requirements (≥ 80%). The intraclass correlation coefficient when comparing GAS T-score calculations (n = 27) was 0.782, which exceeded the minimum preset feasibility cutoff (≥ 0.75). This indicated “good” agreement between reviewers [52].

For patient satisfaction and patient engagement (n = 33), 94% of patients and 100% of PTs completed their patient satisfaction and engagement surveys which exceeded the feasibility requirements. Table 3 displayed that the majority of participants reported “Excellent” (67%) satisfaction while 27% reported “Very Good” satisfaction with GAS-Back and PTs reported “Very Good” (37%) or “Excellent” (33%) patient engagement during the GAS-Back process.

Patient satisfaction and engagement with GAS-Back

Following the study, the five trained PTs acknowledged that the GAS-Back training was sufficient, but additional roleplay would have enhanced their confidence. They noted that GAS-Back encouraged more holistic conversations and increased patient engagement which may have contributed to treatment effectiveness. Furthermore, they utilized GAS-Back outside the study. When time is a restricting factor, PT modification of GAS-Back during the initial session may facilitate feasibility.

Participants (n = 33) set an average of 3 SMART goals and approximately 88% of participants set at least one goal under activity limitations. Majority of the goals identified (Table 4) pertained to activity limitations (52%). Most (82%) activity limitation goals concerned sustained/prolonged postures/positions/activities. Participation restrictions (39%) and body function/stability (9%) goals were less prevalent than activity limitations.

Discussion

This study demonstrates the preliminary feasibility and reliability of a new standardized GAS procedure (GAS-Back) for patients with cLBP in a clinical setting by demonstrating timeliness, clinician adherence, interrater reliability, and patient engagement and satisfaction. This study also described the nature of goals set by participants with cLBP. We built upon the work of others in further developing and testing GAS procedures for patients with cLBP [38, 61]. This study differed from prior research due to the development of training materials, structured GAS-Back procedures, and evidence of its feasibility and reliability. Based on our findings, GAS-Back can be delivered by PTs in a reliable and efficient manner in a real-world clinical setting.

GAS training

Practical considerations for GAS should include formal, structured training to understand how to align patient goals with clinically meaningful goals [62]. In our study, we developed a standardized training program for PTs based on principles from Turner-Stokes with knowledge and fidelity assessments upon completion of training [28]. An early review of GAS by Cytrynbaum et al [43]. found that more than half of the studies they reviewed failed to describe the training and expertise of the goal setters, which is critical for the success and reliability of GAS. There are no studies to our knowledge that have developed a standardized GAS training program for cLBP with this rigor and fidelity.

Time and productivity constraints within PT clinics pose as potential challenges in training interested PTs in GAS-Back. Our structured GAS-Back training program with follow-up assessments was streamlined to approximately 7 h. Due to CEU requirements for physical therapy license renewal, clinician GAS training time does not appear to be an additional barrier or burden to clinician time.

GAS implementation

Another time-related barrier to GAS-Back is clinician/patient goal setting duration. Few studies have addressed the average time to administer GAS. This study found that the average time (9 min and 9 s) to administer GAS-Back was feasible. This is also within the time constraints of average physical therapy sessions which last between 30 and 90 min [63]. In a study observing patient-PT interactions [64], the mean duration of interaction time during the treatment sessions was 14 min 51 s. Although these findings support that GAS-Back can be implemented in an efficient time, only one PT clinic was observed. Therefore, more research at different clinics should be conducted to further generalize results.

The GAS-Back form developed from phase 1 of this study was standardized using ICF domains. Although prior research suggests that a less structured goal approach allows for more flexibility when setting goals with patients [65], participants with cLBP from phase 1 predominantly reported specific ICF domains related to impairment, activity, and participation limitations, which was reinforced by this study. Not only did the ICF framework provide a structured goal setting template, but its uniformity may also assist PTs in directing conversation and reducing time spent goal setting.

GAS methodology is not standardized and often varies between studies. A recent systematic review by Haladay et al [42]. found five different procedures within seven articles for administering GAS to patients with LBP. Our study created standardized GAS-Back training and implementation materials for other clinicians or studies to adequately set and measure patient progress. Part of the GAS-Back standardization included the use of ten-item checklists by PTs to maintain consistency in the GAS-Back process. PT checklist adherence was evaluated by two independent PT reviewers who listened to each initial GAS-Back recording. Results from this study showed excellent PT adherence (89%) to GAS-Back procedures. The use of two independent PT reviewers was also utilized to showcase the interrater reliability of GAS-Back goal setting and achievement. A recent study looking at the interrater reliability for the construction of GAS goals found good goal content agreement across two or more clinicians [66]. The percent agreement for goal comparison was high and the ICC showed good GAS T-score agreement between reviewers [52]. This demonstrates feasibility to administer GAS-Back consistently in a standardized way across patients with cLBP within a clinical setting.

Study limitations

The use of only one physical therapy clinic presents as one possible challenge to generalizing conclusions of feasibility. Although the USF PT Center represents a diverse and productive physical therapy clinic, only recruiting participants from one location may not be representative of our target population. Furthermore, the PTs who participated in this study were clinical specialists and/or fellows. More research is needed to determine the feasibility of GAS-Back by PTs with varying levels of specialization and experience across other clinics in multiple geographical locations. Given the pragmatic nature of this study, care received by each participant was individualized. We did not control for the treatment received by each patient. This is common in physical therapy practice, and we do not see this as a limitation as much as an unavoidable aspect of pragmatic research that is different from clinical trials.

Conclusion

GAS-Back provides a patient-centered approach to treating and measuring improvements in cLBP that is complementary to traditional, fixed-item, PRO measures (e.g., ODI and NPRS). While GAS is not new, its feasibility had never been studied in a real-world PT care setting for cLBP. This study suggests that GAS-Back is feasible and reliable in a clinical setting through its timeliness, clinician adherence, interrater reliability, and patient engagement and satisfaction. However, the smaller sample size and single clinic from which participants were recruited may act as potential limitations to generalizing the study results. Future research expanding the GAS-Back training and implementation to a wider range of clinics and patients may further support GAS-Back’s feasibility and reliability among patients with cLBP.
