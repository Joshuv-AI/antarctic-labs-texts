Regulating interface electric field to stabilize high-voltage KVPO4F positive electrode for sustainable potassium-ion batteries
Authors: Haijie Qi, Yichen Du, Jianfeng Ding, Lili Song, Jiaying Liao, Congying Yu, Zeyu Yuan, Shiang Chen, Xiaosi Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFluorophosphates have garnered widespread attention in potassium-ion batteries due to their robust three-dimensional frameworks and high operating voltage. However, their practical implementation is restricted by inherent limitations, including low conductivity and an unstable cathode electrolyte interface. Herein, we propose an interfacial electric field regulating strategy to stabilize the cathode electrolyte interface of the KVPO4F positive electrode material. Based on theoretical calculations and in situ characterization, we find that the enhanced interfacial electric field can simultaneously optimize the transport of electrons and K+, improve the stability of the crystal structure, and facilitate the formation of a thin (~2.7 nm), stable cathode electrolyte interface layer. The designed composite material of KVPO4F and nitrogen-doped carbon nanotubes achieves a high specific energy of 454.8 Wh kg−1 (based on the mass of the positive electrode) in the voltage window of 2.0–5.0 V at 0.5 C (1 C = 131 mA g−1). The relevant full cell also exhibits good cycling stability, with a capacity retention of 80.6% after 2000 cycles at 1 C. Furthermore, this interfacial electric field regulation strategy can also be extended to other polyanionic systems such as KFeSO4F and KTiPO4F, demonstrating practical application potential. This study elucidates an interfacial electric field regulation approach and provides alternative insights for the development of high-energy-density and long-cycle-life potassium-ion batteries.Subject terms: Batteries, Batteries, Batteries, Energy

Introduction

In recent years, potassium-ion batteries (PIBs) have emerged as highly promising candidates for grid-scale energy storage due to their cost-effectiveness, abundant potassium resources, and favorable standard electrode potential (−2.93 V versus standard hydrogen electrode)1–3. However, the large ionic radius of K+ (1.38 Å) often leads to structural collapse of electrode materials, eventually hindering the development of high-efficiency PIBs4–6. Among various positive electrode materials, polyanionic compounds stand out due to their robust three-dimensional (3D) frameworks and open ion diffusion channels7,8. In particular, KVPO4F (KVPF) has attracted considerable attention for its high operating voltage (greater than 4 V) and ideal theoretical capacity (131 mAh g−1)9. Nevertheless, KVPF still faces two major challenges: its inherently poor electronic conductivity, which limits the charge/ion transport kinetics10; and electrolyte-induced lattice corrosion at high voltages that triggers crystal structure degradation and the formation of a thick and unstable cathode electrode interphase (CEI), ultimately resulting in rapid performance decay11.

Although traditional carbon-coating strategies can moderately improve the conductivity of KVPF, their effectiveness in enhancing high-voltage stability of PIBs remains limited, especially in modulating the composition and structure of CEI12–14. By comparison, interfacial electric field (IEF) engineering offers an ideal solution to this challenge. Previous studies have shown that constructing IEF can effectively optimize electrode/electrolyte interface15–17. For instance, Zhang et al. incorporated potassium-nonafluoro-1-butanesulfonate into LiNi0.5Mn1.5O4, where the synergistic effect of Li+/K+ facilitated the formation of a surface electric field, effectively stabilizing the CEI layer and enhancing cycling stability at high voltages (up to 4.6 V)18. Similarly, Song et al. developed Sn3O4/Sn2S3 heterostructures to construct built-in electric fields, which not only enhanced the reactivity of the electrolyte but also promoted the generation of high-entropy solid electrolyte interphases, thereby successfully improving the battery lifespan19. These findings highlight the critical role of electric fields in interface stability. However, several fundamental questions remain to be addressed: (1) the intrinsic relationship between electric field intensity and CEI composition; (2) quantitative criteria for determining the optimal field strength; and (3) the underlying mechanisms of electric field optimization for enhancing the performance of polyanion positive electrodes.

In this work, we employ carbon nanotubes (CNTs) as composite partners for KVPF, due to their good tunability and electronic conductivity. Interfacial charge redistribution further establishes an electric field20. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations reveal that electrons transfer from CNTs with higher Fermi levels to KVPF, generating an IEF with an effective depth of approximately 3 Å. To systematically evaluate the effect of IEF intensity, we select three types of CNTs as composite matrices for KVPF through Bader charge analysis. Quantitative simulations indicate that the composite of nitrogen-doped CNTs (NCNTs) and KVPF (denoted as KVPF/NCNTs) possesses the strongest IEF (273.2 mV Å−1). As a positive electrode material for PIBs, the KVPF/NCNTs composite exhibits considerable specific energy (454.8 Wh kg−1, based on the mass of the positive electrode) and cycling stability (with a capacity retention of 82.9% after 2000 cycles at 10 C rate). On one hand, in situ X-ray diffraction and synchrotron radiation unveil the evolution of the lattice. On the other hand, multiscale characterization techniques, including cryogenic transmission electron microscopy (cryo-TEM), X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) depth profiling and time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (ToF-SIMS), collectively illustrate that the enhanced IEF promotes the formation of a thin (2.7 nm), KF-rich CEI layer, thereby improving interfacial stability. From an application perspective, full cells assembled with KVPF/NCNTs as the positive electrode and either graphite or soft carbon (SC) as the negative electrode both show advantages in terms of cycling stability and rate capability. In addition, this strategy is also applicable in other polyanionic systems (e.g., KFeSO4F, KTiPO4F). By elucidating the intrinsic relationship between IEF intensity and CEI composition, this study is expected to provide an avenue for developing low-cost and high-energy-density PIBs.

Results

Design and properties of the positive electrodes

This study focuses on the composite system of CNTs and KVPF, systematically investigating the regulatory effects of IEF intensity on the composition of the CEI and the electrochemical performance. As shown in Fig. 1a, KVPF is in contact with only a limited number of CNTs at the microscopic interface, and the influence of multi-layer carbon structures on KVPF performance is limited21. Therefore, a simplified KVPF/single-layer carbon model is used in theoretical calculations to facilitate mechanism analysis. Work function (Φ) analysis indicates that the Fermi level of the carbon layer is higher than that of the KVPF (001) plane, thereby forming an energy gradient that promotes the spontaneous transfer of electrons from the carbon layer to KVPF (Fig. 1b)22. This redistribution of charges results in the formation of a unidirectional IEF in KVPF/CNTs, as revealed by the 3D and two-dimensional (2D) differential charge density (DCD) analyses (Fig. 1c). In addition, one-dimensional (1D) charge distribution mapping shows that the penetration depth of the IEF is approximately 3 Å (Fig. 1d). The amount of charge transfer serves as an indicator for evaluating the intensity of the IEF23. Therefore, in this study, Bader charge analysis was adopted to quantitatively determine the electron transfer between different types of CNTs and KVPF (Fig. 1e). The results show that the KVPF/NCNTs composite exhibits the largest charge transfer, reaching 0.94 e, indicating that KVPF/NCNTs may possess the strongest IEF. According to the calculation results, we selected three types of CNTs with different charge transfer capabilities, including undoped CNTs, sulfur-doped CNTs (SCNTs), and NCNTs, to assess the correlation between IEF intensity and electrode properties.

Consistent with the Bader charge analysis, DCD analysis shows that the interfacial charge density distribution of the three composites follows the order: KVPF/NCNTs > KVPF/SCNTs > KVPF/CNTs, while their IEF penetration depths remain approximately 3 Å (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. 1). This further demonstrates that KVPF/NCNTs possesses the strongest IEF. Meanwhile, we observe that more electron clouds gather around the V–F bonds, indicating that the V–F bonds in KVPF/NCNTs are stronger. Binding energy calculations unveil the hierarchical stability of the interface, with the KVPF/NCNTs system exhibiting the highest binding energy (3.12 eV), compared to KVPF/CNTs (2.23 eV) and KVPF/SCNTs (2.48 eV), indicating a stronger interaction between NCNTs and KVPF (Supplementary Fig. 2). Meanwhile, KVPF/NCNTs, which encompasses the strongest IEF, displays the lowest K+ diffusion barrier (0.35 eV) (Fig. 1f and Supplementary Fig. 3), highlighting its excellent ionic transport properties. Crystal orbital Hamilton population (COHP) analysis further elucidates that the IEF strengthens the covalency of the V–F bond (Fig. 1g), effectively enhancing the lattice stability of KVPF24. Consequently, we designed and successfully synthesized composites of KVPF with various CNTs through self-assembly and solid-state synthesis methods (Supplementary Fig. 4). Comprehensive characterization confirms that all precursor materials exhibit the desired morphological and structural features (Supplementary Figs. 5–8), validating the effectiveness of our synthesis strategy. Experimental results demonstrate that the strong IEF in KVPF/NCNTs enhances electrode performance through a dual synergistic mechanism (Fig. 1a): (1) the IEF facilitates rapid ion/electron transport, suppressing the accumulation of ions and organic species on the electrode surface and thus forming a thin and KF-rich CEI25; (2) the surface negative charges can repel PF6− from the positive electrode, effectively mitigating its erosion of the crystal architecture (detailed evidence provided in subsequent sections)26,27. The synergy of these mechanisms endows KVPF/NCNTs with satisfactory cycling stability.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) were used to observe the microstructure of the KVPF samples. As shown in Fig. 2a–c, KVPF/NCNTs exhibits a spherical morphology with a particle size of approximately 1 μm, and the NCNTs are homogeneously distributed within the matrix. High-resolution TEM (HRTEM) displays clear lattice fringes with an interplanar spacing of 0.573 nm, which corresponds to the (110) facet of KVPF. In addition, NCNTs are also observed to be evenly interspersed within KVPF (Fig. 2d), further verifying the formation of a compact composite architecture. Other control samples also present similar morphological features (Supplementary Figs. 9–11), demonstrating the stability and reproducibility of the synthesis method. Element mapping analysis corroborates the uniform distribution of all target elements (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Figs. 12–14), suggesting the successful combination of various CNTs with KVPF. Compared with other samples, KVPF/NCNTs exhibits the most intense fluorine (F) signal, indicative of a higher F content. This phenomenon was quantitatively validated by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) (Supplementary Fig. 15 and Table 1), showing that the F content increased by 5.6% compared to pristine KVPF. We attribute this phenomenon to the strengthened V–F chemical bonds, which inhibit bond cleavage during high-temperature synthesis28. In order to comprehensively understand the carbon composition, thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and Raman spectroscopy were also implemented (Supplementary Fig. 16). The carbon contents of KVPF/CNTs, KVPF/SCNTs and KVPF/NCNTs are 2.94 wt%, 3.01 wt% and 3.11 wt%, respectively, confirming the consistent carbon incorporation among the composites. Intriguingly, KVPF/NCNTs manifests the lowest ID/IG ratio (1.04), reflecting a higher degree of graphitization and optimized electronic conductivity29.

In order to ascertain the precise structure of the KVPF samples, synchrotron radiation X-ray diffraction (SRXRD) measurements were conducted (Supplementary Tables 2–6). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 17, with the enhancement of the IEF, the diffraction peaks of the samples gradually shift toward lower angles, signifying an increase in lattice parameters. The SRXRD data were refined to obtain detailed lattice parameters and atomic coordinates (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 18). The results show that although KVPF/NCNTs exhibits the largest lattice parameters among all samples, its average V–O/F bond length is the shortest (Supplementary Tables 7–10), which seems contradictory. This can be explained by previous reports stating that the substitution of F− by O2− in phosphates forms a V = O covalent bond30. The process induces changes in the lattice structure and further affects the unit cell volume. As the IEF weakens, the V2–F bonds exhibit more pronounced heterogenization. Specifically, the V2–F2 bond in the KVPF sample shortens to 1.707 Å, suggesting a partial transformation into a V = O bond. This inference is further corroborated by the more marked V = O signal in the Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) spectrum of KVPF (Fig. 2g).

To delve deeper into the coordination environment and bonding state of vanadium, XANES measurements were carried out (Fig. 2h). As highlighted by Togonon et al.31, techniques such as X-ray emission spectroscopy can offer additional insights into the electronic configuration of vanadium, thereby complementing the information obtained from XANES. In this study, XANES reveals the unoccupied electronic states and local coordination environment. In the pre-edge region, two small pre-edge peaks at 5468.1 and 5469.8 eV were observed for KVPF/NCNTs, corresponding to the well-symmetrized VO4F2 octahedral structure32. In contrast, KVPF exhibits a strong pre-edge peak at 5469.4 eV, indicating apparent hybridization of V d-p orbitals, which leads to distortion of the V-centered octahedral architecture and the formation of an asymmetric [V = O]O4F coordination environment30. In the absorption edge region, all the sample spectra are located between those of V2O3 and VO2, and KVPF/NCNTs exhibits a red-shifted absorption edge, suggesting a lower vanadium valence state and higher redox activity33. Extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) analysis shows that with decreasing IEF intensity, the concerned peak position R-space shifts to lower values and the peak broadens (Supplementary Fig. 19). Among them, the peak near 1.55 Å is attributed to V–O/F single bonds and the one near 1.31 Å is ascribed to the V = O double bonds34. Furthermore, fitting results confirm that the V–O/F bond is the strongest in KVPF/NCNTs, while V = O bonds with different coordination numbers are detected in other samples (Supplementary Table 11). These findings demonstrate that the IEF enhances the V–F bond and inhibits the oxidation of KVPF.

In light of the above results, a schematic diagram was drawn to summarize the formation mechanism of the F-defective phase KVPO4+xF1-x and the origin of unit cell contraction. As depicted in Supplementary Fig. 20, under the influence of a strong IEF, the robust V–F bonds in KVPF/NCNTs effectively suppress oxidation during the synthesis process. In contrast, in KVPF, the partial substitution of the F2 sites by O2− leads to the formation of shorter V = O bonds. To maintain structural stability, the opposing V2–F1 bond is elongated, ultimately resulting in a distorted [V = O]O4F octahedral structure. Meanwhile, the V1–O = V2 bond angle sharply decreases from 126.4° to 116.6°, and the V1–V1 interatomic distance is shortened from 7.105 Å to 7.002 Å, collectively causing an overall contraction of the KVPF structure and a reduction in cell parameters. In summary, a strong IEF promotes electron-cloud accumulation around the V–F bonds, thereby enhancing their bonding strength. These robust V–F bonds can resist external oxidation and inhibit lattice distortion, enabling KVPF/NCNTs to maintain a larger unit cell volume. The resulting spacious ion diffusion channels further facilitate K+ migration. XPS analysis (Supplementary Figs. 21 and 22) confirms the presence of nitrogen and sulfur in KVPF/NCNTs and KVPF/SCNTs, respectively. Characteristic peaks can be observed in the survey spectra, and the corresponding signals are also detected in the high-resolution C 1 s spectra. Notably, the V 2p spectra show that the peaks of KVPF/NCNTs appear at the lowest binding energies, which once again indicates that the vanadium oxidation state is the lowest. This is related to its enhanced air stability32.

The surface potential distribution of the target sample was characterized using atomic force microscopy (AFM) equipped with scanning Kelvin probe microscopy (SKPM) (Fig. 2i and Supplementary Figs. 23 and 24). Among the tested materials, KVPF/NCNTs exhibits the highest surface potential (91.7 mV, Fig. 2k), illustrating that nitrogen doping obviously boosts the electric field strength of the composite material. Surface charge density was evaluated by zeta potential measurements. As evidenced in Fig. 2j, KVPF/NCNTs displays a high negative surface potential of −35.3 mV, demonstrating substantial charge accumulation. As a result, quantitative calculations unravel that the IEF intensity of KVPF/NCNTs reaches up to 273.2 mV Å −1, which is 1.53 times that of KVPF/SCNTs and 2.52 times that of KVPF/CNTs (Fig. 2k). This enhanced electric field strength facilitates the kinetics of charge transfer and K+ diffusion37.

Electrochemical performance of KVPF samples with different IEF

To comprehensively investigate the impact of IEF on electrochemical performance, the potassium storage properties of four KVPF-based positive electrode materials were tested within the voltage range of 2.0–5.0 V. For the half-cell measurements, the electrolyte used was 1 mol L−1 KPF6 dissolved in EC/PC (volume ratio 1:1), with the addition of 5 vol% FEC (denoted as 1 M KPF6 EC/PC + 5% FEC). Supplementary Fig. 25 shows that in the first cycle of linear sweep voltammetry, the current curve exhibits a distinct peak around 4.5 V, corresponding to the formation of CEI38. In the subsequent cycles, no discernible peak below 5.0 V was observed, indicating its good high-voltage stability and compatibility with operating voltages up to 5.0 V.

Cyclic voltammetry (CV) examinations were carried out at a scan rate of 0.1 mV s−1 (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 26), and all samples exhibit four distinct pairs of redox peaks, corresponding to the continuous extraction and intercalation of K+. In particular, the first three CV curves of KVPF/NCNTs overlap well, indicating favorable electrochemical reversibility. Galvanostatic charge/discharge tests were performed at a specific current of 0.5 C (Fig. 3b). It can be observed that with the decrease of IEF intensity in the material, the discharge plateau near 4.7 V gradually shortens, showing a typical voltage hysteresis phenomenon, which can be attributed to sluggish ion diffusion kinetics39. Benefiting from the IEF-enhanced ion and electron transport, KVPF/NCNTs delivers a satisfactory specific capacity of 107.7 mAh g−1, which is higher than those of KVPF (90.7 mAh g−1), KVPF/CNTs (98.5 mAh g−1) and KVPF/SCNTs (103.9 mAh g−1). Integrated with the high working voltage of 4.22 V for KVPF/NCNTs, we calculated that its specific energy is as high as 454.8 Wh kg−1 (based on the mass of the positive electrode material).

Cycling stability tests further confirm that KVPF/NCNTs retains 91.9% of its initial capacity after 100 cycles, which is superior to other comparison samples (Fig. 3c). Through in-depth analysis of the relevant charge/discharge profiles (Fig. 3d, e and Supplementary Figs. 27 and 28), KVPF/NCNTs exhibits enhanced stability in the high-voltage region, whereas the comparison samples represent obvious voltage decay and polarization. This phenomenon may stem from differences in the composition of the CEI: the strong IEF generated by NCNTs facilitates the formation of a more robust CEI layer on the surface of KVPF, which will be discussed in detail later. As shown in Fig. 3f, KVPF/NCNTs also exhibits good rate performance, delivering reversible capacities of 107.7, 105.6, 101.8, 96.9, 89.8 and 81.1 mAh g−1 at rates ranging from 0.5 C to 20 C, respectively. When the rate is returned to 0.5 C, a high discharge capacity of 105.1 mAh g−1 is still achieved, further confirming its improved charge transfer kinetics. In contrast, the other samples suffered from severe capacity loss and voltage polarization (Supplementary Fig. 29). To further validate the stability of KVPF/NCNTs, long-term cycling tests were carried out. After 2000 cycles at 10 C, KVPF/NCNTs still maintains a high capacity of 74.6 mAh g−1, with a capacity retention rate of 82.9% (Fig. 3g). These results strongly demonstrate that the IEF-optimized KVPF/NCNTs has considerable specific energy and stable potassium storage performance, which is competitive in PIBs (Fig. 3h and the source of the literature data shown in this figure can be found in Supplementary Table 12).

Potassium-ion storage mechanism and kinetic analysis

To elucidate the effects of IEF regulation on the structural evolution of the material and ion transport kinetics, we first employed in situ XRD to monitor the structural evolution of KVPF and KVPF/NCNTs during the first cycle (Supplementary Figs. 30 and 31). Previous studies have shown that KVPF undergoes four steps of K+ de-/intercalation during the charge and discharge processes: K1VPO4F–K0.75VPO4F–K0.625VPO4F–K0.5VPO4F–K0VPO4F, where K1 is preferentially removed from the crystal lattice compared with K2 (Supplementary Fig. 32)40,41. For in-depth analysis, we divided the charging process into four characteristic regions based on the charge/discharge profile, and tracked the structural evolution by variations in unit cell parameters. As depicted in Fig. 4a–d, the initial charging stage (region I) exhibits a typical two-phase reaction behavior: the (212) peak disappears, while the (011)/(201), (022)/(402) and (411)/(221) peaks overlap. This is due to the fact that, in the initial state, the lattice parameter a is almost twice that of b (Fig. 4d).

As K+ continues to be extracted (regions II and III), the change in the b-axis is greater than that of the a-axis, resulting in the separation of several overlapping diffraction peaks. This stage embodies the coexistence of solid-solution reactions and two-phase reactions. Notably, in region IV, the characteristic diffraction peaks at 16.5° and 33.5° suddenly vanish, exhibiting typical features of a two-phase transition, which are more evident in the 3D figure (Fig. 4c). During the discharge process, the diffraction peaks almost symmetrically return to their original positions, demonstrating the good structural reversibility of KVPF/NCNTs. Similar characteristics can also be noticed in the in situ XRD patterns of KVPF (Fig. 4e–h). Nonetheless, after cycling, the intensity of its diffraction peaks diminishes significantly, meaning its relatively poor structural stability. Analysis of the unit cell parameters shows that the unit cell volume change of KVPF/NCNT is merely 6.41%, which is lower than that of KVPF (6.71%), further verifying that a stronger IEF is conducive to improving lattice stability.

XANES spectroscopy was utilized to monitor the changes in the oxidation state of V and its local coordination environment in KVPF positive electrode materials during depotassiation/potassiation processes. As demonstrated in Fig. 4i, j, the pre-edge peaks of both KVPF and KVPF/NCNTs are located around 5470 eV, and shift to higher energies upon charging to 5.0 V, which corresponds to the lattice contraction process. After discharging to 2.0 V, the pre-edge peak of KVPF/NCNTs almost returns to its original position, while that of KVPF remains at a higher energy position, indicating the lattice units remain in an oxidized state and the structure exhibits poor reversibility during cycling42. The absorption edge region further reveals that V3+ in KVPF/NCNTs is oxidized to V4+ during charging, with the average valence of V increasing from +3.13 to +3.94, and nearly recovers to its initial state upon discharging. By contrast, the average valence of V in KVPF increases from +3.21 to +3.90 during charging and remains slightly elevated after discharging, reflecting its inferior redox activity and reversibility. This conclusion is further supported by ex situ XPS analysis (Supplementary Fig. 33). Ex situ Fourier-transformed EXAFS spectra provide additional insight into the dynamic changes of V-based bonds under electrochemical conditions (Supplementary Fig. 34). In the fully charged state, the bond lengths of V–O/F and V = O in KVPF/NCNTs and KVPF are shortened by approximately 0.037 Å and 0.026 Å, respectively. Upon discharging, the bond lengths in KVPF/NCNTs are fully restored, whereas those in KVPF are partially recovered.

Wavelet transform (WT) analysis further elucidates the coordination environment of V, with the position of its signal along the horizontal axis correlated with the relative atomic mass of the coordinating atoms (Fig. 4k, l)43. Specifically, the results show that the WT signal intensity of both KVPF and KVPF/NCNTs decreases during charging and increases upon discharging. However, after full discharge, the signal intensity of KVPF diminishes by more than 10%, while that of KVPF/NCNTs declines by only about 3%. Furthermore, the signal peak of the V–F bond in KVPF decreases sharply during cycling, manifesting that some chemical bonds are broken or replaced. These findings collectively indicate that KVPF exhibits poor electrochemical reversibility and structural stability during cycling, accompanied by considerable structural degradation and V–F bond breaking44. In contrast, KVPF/NCNTs demonstrates optimized reversibility and a stable structure.

To assess the final structural degradation state of the KVPF positive electrode materials after long-term cycling, XRD and FTIR tests were carried out on electrodes after 200 cycles (Supplementary Fig. 35). The results show that the diffraction peaks of KVPF/NCNTs and KVPF/SCNTs remain intact, while impurity peaks attributable to K0.33V2O4.67 appear in KVPF/CNTs and pristine KVPF. Additionally, the diffraction intensity of KVPF decreases significantly, indicating severe structural deterioration. FTIR spectra further reveal a marked weakening of PO43− characteristic peaks and an enhancement of V = O peaks in KVPF, which is attributed to the destruction of the phosphate framework and the formation of vanadyl double bonds45. XPS provides supplementary information on the elemental composition and chemical bonding states. The high-resolution F 1 s and P 2p spectra of KVPF both weaken simultaneously, indicating that side reactions during cycling lead to continuous dissolution and loss of P and F elements (Supplementary Fig. 36).

Based on the aforementioned results, this work proposes a structural degradation pathway of KVPF during cycling (Fig. 4m). Initially, the VO4F2 units in KVPF are oxidized to distorted [V = O]O4F units, which further weakens the V–F bonds. Subsequently, these vulnerable V–F bonds break and trigger structural transformation. In one scenario, F− is replaced by O2− again, forming [V = O]O5 octahedra, which gradually convert KVPO4F into KVOPO4. In another scenario, the breaking of V–F bonds induce hybrid orbital rearrangement, promoting the formation of V = O5 square pyramids and leading to the collapse of the phosphate framework, ultimately generating the electrochemically unstable K0.33V2O4.67 (specific reaction equations are provided later)46. In summary, the robust chemical bonds in KVPF/NCNTs, especially the V–F bonds, effectively maintain its structural stability and electrochemical activity, whereas the easily damaged V–F bonds in pristine KVPF accelerate lattice degradation, resulting in inferior electrochemical performance.

CV tests at different sweep rates and galvanostatic intermittent titration techniques (GITT) were adopted to clarify the discrepancies in K+ transport kinetics among several samples. The CV curves, coupled with Randles–Sevcik fitting, indicate that KVPF/NCNTs exhibits lower polarization and higher K+ diffusion coefficients (DK+) compared to KVPF, KVPF/CNTs and KVPF/SCNTs (Supplementary Figs. 37 and 38 and Table 13). GITT and the related calculations further corroborate that the DK+ values of KVPF/NCNTs, ranging from 10−11 to 10−7 cm2 s−1 (Supplementary Fig. 39), exceed those of other samples. Moreover, the electronic conductivity of the materials was assessed by density of states calculations (Supplementary Fig. 40). KVPF shows a wide band gap of 1.49 eV, while other composite materials exhibit no band gap near the Fermi level, displaying conductive properties. This demonstrates that CNTs can effectively enhance the mobility of electron carriers. This finding is also supported by electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), where KVPF/NCNTs exhibits a smaller charge transfer resistance (Rct), confirming its decent electronic conductivity (Supplementary Fig. 41 and Table 14). Overall, these results account for the favorable specific capacity and cycling stability of KVPF/NCNTs. The highly efficient charge compensation mechanism, synergized with the optimized IEF, jointly promotes rapid K+ transport and prominent electrochemical performance.

Interface investigation

Post-cycling characterization of the electrodes was conducted to explore the mechanism by which the IEF affects the CEI components and electrode stability. SEM observations reveal a progressive deterioration of electrode morphology as the IEF weakens. The KVPF electrode exhibits severe particle agglomeration, indicating its poor electrochemical stability (Supplementary Fig. 42). Given that the composition and thickness of the CEI play a critical role in electrode performance, cryo-TEM was used for nanoscale analysis. As depicted in Fig. 5a, b, an ultrathin ( ~ 2.7 nm) CEI layer is formed on KVPF/NCNTs, which is mainly composed of inorganic KF. In stark contrast, the CEI on the KVPF electrode is apparently thicker (7–16 nm) and chemically heterogeneous, containing KF, K2CO3 and K3P components. As expected, lattice fringes of K0.33V2O4.67 are also observed within the KVPF electrode, which is consistent with the XRD results and provide direct evidence of irreversible phase transition during cycling.

To gain a deeper comprehension of the chemical composition and structural properties of the CEI, we carried out a detailed analysis combining XPS depth profiling and ToF-SIMS. High-resolution C 1 s XPS spectra indicate that the surface of KVPF is rich in organic components (C–F, C–C and C–O) as well as detectable CO32− species (Fig. 5c). After 20 s of etching, a C–N signal appears in KVPF/NCNTs, suggesting that the bulk electrode material is exposed. Analysis of the F 1 s spectra shows that the CEI of KVPF is dominated by organic components (C–F), while the CEI of KVPF/NCNTs primarily consists of inorganic compounds (K–F) (Fig. 5d). Compared to KVPF (30 s of etching), the V–F signal emerges at just 20 s of etching in KVPF/NCNTs, further substantiating its thinner CEI layer. Moreover, in the P 2p spectra, PF6− and P–K species can be identified on the surface of KVPF, while only PO43− signals are detected in the interior of KVPF/NCNTs (Supplementary Fig. 43), which is in accordance with the above cryo-TEM observation. ToF-SIMS 3D images further validate the distribution of CEI components, in which C2HO−, KF2−, CO3K− and P− correspond to organic matter, KF, K2CO3 and K3P, respectively (Fig. 5e). The results indicate that KVPF/NCNTs sample contains fewer organic components and K2CO3 species, and no K3P is detected. Overall, these findings demonstrate that a stronger IEF can effectively stabilize the CEI layer by promoting the formation of inorganic KF and reducing interfacial side reactions.

In situ EIS and distribution of relaxation times (DRT) analyses were employed to uncover the conductivity characteristics of the CEI and electrodes. As illustrated in Fig. 5f, the EIS curves of KVPF exhibit a contraction of the impedance semicircle during the charging process and an expansion during discharging. Although KVPF/NCNTs also demonstrates a similar dynamic trend, its impedance values are significantly lower (Fig. 5h). According to literature reports, DRT analysis reveals two distinct characteristic peaks corresponding to time constants τ1 and τ2, which are separately assigned to the CEI resistance (RCEI) and Rct47,48. Comparative analysis indicates that the Rct and RCEI values of KVPF/NCNTs are considerably lower than those of KVPF (Fig. 5g, i and Supplementary Fig. 44 and Table 15), confirming its good interfacial charge transfer performance.

Comprehensive characterization indicates that the KVPF/NCNTs electrode maintains high structural integrity even after 200 cycles, forming a thin and homogeneous CEI layer on its surface, which is mainly composed of ion-conductive KF49. In sharp contrast, the KVPF electrode undergoes apparent structural degradation, accompanied by the formation of K0.33V2O4.67 impurity phases and the development of a thick CEI layer containing multiple side reaction products. Based on the above experimental data, we summarize the following mechanism: a strong IEF can simultaneously enhance charge transfer and inhibit electrolyte decomposition, thereby promoting the formation of KF. The negative surface charge (ζ = −35.3 mV) facilitates K+ migration while electrostatically repelling PF6−, which minimizes the formation of byproducts such as K3P and K0.33V2O4.67 (Supplementary Fig. 45). It should be noted that the significant gas expansion observed in pouch cell tests further confirms the release of gas during side reactions (Supplementary Fig. 46)50. On the other hand, the formation of a large amount of impurity phases leads to rapid deactivation of the KVPF electrode under high voltage, accompanied by additional electrolyte/interface reactions. Full battery testing and universality research

Full cells were assembled using commercial graphite and asphalt-derived soft carbon (SC) as the negative electrode materials to evaluate the practical application potential of KVPF/NCNTs (Fig. 6a and Supplementary Figs. 47 and 48). As shown in Fig. 6b,c, the Graphite | |KVPF/NCNTs and SC | | KVPF/NCNTs full cells deliver discharge capacities of 102.1 and 101.4 mAh g−1, respectively, within the voltage range of 1.0–5.0 V at a specific current of 0.5 C. Meanwhile, the Graphite | |KVPF/NCNTs system achieves a considerable specific energy of 283.1 Wh kg−1 (calculated based on the total mass of both electrodes) with an average working voltage of 3.88 V. When the specific current is adjusted to 1 C, 2 C, 5 C, 10 C and 20 C, the SC | | KVPF/NCNTs full cell delivers discharge capacities of 99.3, 96.5, 91.1, 84.0 and 73.3 mAh g−1, respectively, all of which are superior to those of the Graphite | |KVPF/NCNTs full cell (Fig. 6d–f). Furthermore, the Graphite | |KVPF/NCNTs full battery exhibits remarkable cycling stability, retaining a reversible capacity of 79.6 mAh g−1 after 2000 cycles at 1 C, corresponding to a capacity retention of 80.6% (Fig. 6g and Supplementary Fig. 49). Its practical feasibility is further corroborated by the successful operation of Graphite | |KVPF/NCNTs pouch cells in lighting LED lamps and powering a mobile phone (Supplementary Fig. 50 and Movie 1). In summary, the Graphite | |KVPF/NCNTs full cell exhibits ideal specific energy and cycling stability, while the SC | | KVPF/NCNTs full cell possesses favorable rate capability. These two types of full-cell configurations can be adapted to different application scenarios, and both surpass the majority of reported potassium-ion full cells in terms of overall performance (Fig. 6h and the source of the literature data shown in this figure can be found in Supplementary Table 16).

To validate the universality of the IEF regulation strategy for polyanion positive electrode materials, we further selected and conducted extended experiments on KFeSO4F and KTiPO4F (Fig. 7a). Work function calculations unravel that effective IEF can be generated between them and CNTs (Supplementary Fig. 51). Accordingly, four types of KFSF samples and four types of KVPF samples were synthesized, and their XRD patterns all match the standard cards (Supplementary Fig. 52). The XRD refinement results also confirm that, similar to the KVPF system, the unit cell parameters tend to increase with the strengthening of the IEF, which can be ascribed to the inhibitory effect of the IEF on the oxidation reaction of transition metals (Supplementary Figs. 53 and 54 and Tables 17–26). In addition, morphological characterization demonstrates that they maintain good microstructural integrity after combination with diverse types of CNTs (Supplementary Figs. 55 and 56).

The IEF intensity was investigated by SKPM and zeta potential measurements (Supplementary Figs. 57–59). According to the calculation based on Eq. (1), both KFSF/NCNTs and KTPF/NCNTs exhibit the strongest IEF (Fig. 7b and Supplementary Table 27). Subsequently, the electrochemical performance of the KFSF samples was evaluated. As illustrated in Fig. 7c, KFSF/NCNTs delivers a reversible capacity of 105.3 mAh g−1 at a rate of 0.5 C, with an average voltage of 3.60 V. As the specific current increased to 1 C, 2 C, 5 C, 10 C and 20 C, the discharge capacities remain at 101.7, 96.8, 91.4, 82.2 and 70.5 mAh g−1, respectively, indicating its enhanced reaction kinetics and rate capability compared with KFSF, KFSF/CNTs and KFSF/SCNTs (Fig. 7d). Moreover, KFSF/NCNTs exhibits respectable cycling stability, maintaining 79.7% of its initial capacity after 500 cycles (Supplementary Fig. 60a). In summary, benefiting from the optimized IEF, KFSF/NCNTs achieves the best electrochemical performance among all KFSF samples (Fig. 7e). Similarly, in the KTPF system, KTPF/NCNTs with the strongest IEF displays better electrochemical performance than that of KTPF, KTPF/CNTs and KTPF/SCNTs (Fig. 7f–h and Supplementary Fig. 60b). To further assess the practical potential of the high-performance IEF materials, full cells were assembled and tested. The Graphite | |KFSF/NCNTs and Graphite | |KTPF/NCNTs full cells deliver high reversible capacities of 105.4 and 86.6 mAh g−1 at 0.5 C, respectively (Fig. 7i), and also demonstrate favorable rate performance (67.4 mAh g−1 for Graphite | |KFSF/NCNTs and 49.3 mAh g−1 for Graphite | |KTPF/NCNTs at 20 C, Fig. 7j) and cycling stability (capacity retention of 86.2% and 80.1% after 500 cycles at 1 C, respectively, Fig. 7k). These results strongly affirm the broad applicability of the IEF regulation strategy in polyanionic positive electrode systems.

Discussion

In summary, we have demonstrated that the mechanism by which the IEF regulates the potassium storage performance of KVPF was systematically investigated through the combination of DFT predictions and experimental studies. Theoretical simulations show that an enhanced IEF can simultaneously improve electronic conductivity, reduce K+ diffusion barriers and reinforce structural stability. Cryo-TEM and XPS depth profiling provide direct evidence that a strong IEF effectively suppresses electrolyte decomposition and reduces side reactions on the electrode surface, thereby facilitating the formation of a thin (~2.7 nm) and stable KF-rich CEI layer. Benefiting from the strong IEF (273.2 mV Å −1), KVPF/NCNTs achieves an optimized rate performance of 81.1 mAh g−1 at 20 C and maintains a capacity retention of 82.9% after 2000 cycles at 10 C. More importantly, this interfacial modulation strategy has shown universal applicability, as similar performance improvements were observed in other polyanionic systems (KFSF and KTPF). Our results establish a distinct positive correlation between the strength of the IEF and the electrochemical performance metrics (specific energy, rate capability and cycling stability) in polyanion positive electrode systems. This work not only provides fundamental insights into the pivotal role of IEF in CEI formation and electrode stabilization, but also offers an alternative theoretical foundation and research pathway for sustainable PIBs.

Methods

Materials and chemicals

Vanadium pentoxide (V2O5), potassium fluoride (KF), isobutanol, polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF), phosphoric acid (85%), urea, sulfur powder, ferrous sulfate heptahydrate (FeSO4·7H2O), ethylene glycol, titanium powder (Ti), titanyl sulfate (TiOSO4) and potassium dihydrogen phosphate (KH2PO4) were purchased from Aladdin. Acetone was obtained from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. Potassium difluoride (KHF2) was purchased from Shanghai Lingfeng Chemical Reagents Co., Ltd. Carbon nanotubes (XFM22, 100270) were obtained from XFNANO. KPF6 (99.5%), EC (99%), PC (99%), FEC (99%) and dimethyl carbonate (DMC, 99%) was purchased from dodochem. All chemical reagents were used as received without further purification.

Preparation of nitrogen-doped carbon nanotubes (NCNTs)

Pristine carbon nanotubes (CNTs) were uniformly mixed with urea via mechanical grinding in a planetary ball mill at 300 rpm for 10 min, and then the mixture was thermally treated at 700 °C for 2 h with a heating rate of 5 °C min−1 under an argon atmosphere to obtain NCNTs.

Synthesis of VOPO4·2H2O precursor

V2O5 was dissolved in a mixed solution of phosphoric acid and deionized water (volume ratio 1:4), followed by refluxing at 110 °C for 6 h. The resulting product was thoroughly washed with deionized water and acetone, and subsequently dried overnight in a vacuum oven at 80 °C.

Fabrication of VOHPO4·0.5H2O/NCNTs composite

The as-prepared NCNTs and VOPO4·2H2O were uniformly dispersed in isobutanol under continuous stirring for 12 h at 25 ± 1 °C followed by refluxing at 110 °C for 8 h. The obtained solid was repeatedly washed with ethanol and dried overnight in a vacuum oven at 80 °C to yield the VOHPO4·0.5H2O/NCNTs intermediate.

Formation of VPO4/NCNTs

The VOHPO4·0.5H2O/NCNTs composite was annealed at 800 °C for 2 h with a heating rate of 5 °C min−1 under a flowing H2/Ar (5 vol% H2) atmosphere to produce the final VPO4/NCNTs precursor.

Synthesis of KVPF/NCNTs

The KVPF/NCNTs composite was synthesized via a solid-state reaction approach. Briefly, the VPO4/NCNTs precursor was ultrasonically mixed with stoichiometric KF (V:K molar ratio = 1:1, with 5% excess KF) in acetone solution for 30 min, followed by solvent evaporation. The resulting mixture was then calcined at 650 °C for 1 h with a heating rate of 5 °C min−1 under continuous Ar flow to obtain the final KVPF/NCNTs product. For comparison, control samples including KVPF (without CNTs), KVPF/CNTs (with conventional CNTs) and KVPF/SCNTs (with sulfur-doped CNTs) were also prepared under identical conditions. Note that the synthesis of SCNTs involves thoroughly grinding pristine CNTs with sulfur powder (mass ratio 1:5) for 10 min, followed by thermal treatment at 500 °C for 2 h with a heating rate of 5 °C min−1 under an Ar atmosphere.

Preparation of KFSF samples

KFSF samples was synthesized according to our previous report, using ethylene glycol as the solvent52. To prepare KFSF/NCNTs, 50 mg NCNTs were ultrasonically dispersed into 50 mL ethylene glycol for 30 min. Following this, 5 mmol FeSO4·7H2O and 5 mmol KF were introduced into the dispersion, with continuous stirring for 30 min to ensure homogeneity. Subsequently, the mixture was transferred to a 100 mL Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave, which was then sealed and maintained at 160 °C for 6 h in an oil bath under constant agitation. Upon cooling to 30 °C, the resulting precipitate was collected by centrifugation, rinsed three times with acetone, and vacuum-dried at 80 °C for 12 h. Finally, the product was annealed in a tube furnace at 350 °C for 2 h in a H2/Ar (5 vol% H2) atmosphere with a heating rate of 3 °C min−1. By adding CNTs and SCNTs, KFSF/CNTs and KFSF/SCNTs were also prepared, respectively. For KFSF, the initial ultrasonic dispersion step for CNTs was omitted.

Synthesis of KTPF samples

KTPF was synthesized according to Fedotov’s report53. Specifically, 0.12 g Ti, 0.4 g TiOSO4, 2.04 g KH2PO4 and 0.38 g KHF2 were added to 30 mL distilled H2O and stirred 0.5 h. The resultant suspension was then transferred into a 50 mL Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave, sealed and maintained at 200 °C for 3 h in an oil bath under constant stirring. After cooling to 30 °C, the resulting precipitate was collected by centrifugation, washed thoroughly with acetone three times, and dried under vacuum at 80 °C for 12 h to obtain KTPF material. The preparation methods for KTPF/CNTs, KTPF/SCNTs and KTPF/NCNTs are similar, except that different types of CNTs were ultrasonically dispersed in distilled water in advance.

Materials characterization

The morphological structures of the products were examined by SEM (JSM-7600 F) and TEM (FEI Talos F200X). Elemental mapping and EDS analyses were conducted using the FEI Talos F200X field-emission transmission electron microscope equipped with a high-brightness field emission gun and a Super-X G2 EDS detector. The crystal structures were analyzed by XRD (Rigaku, SmartLab). The Rietveld method, combined with the GSAS package and the EXPGUI interface, was used to refine the datasets. In situ XRD patterns were recorded using an X’Pert Pro MPD X-ray diffractometer (D8 Bruker Advance, Germany) equipped with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) at 40 kV and 0.2 C. XPS depth profiling was performed using Ar+ ion etching to analyze the depth distribution of the surface CEI layer on KVPF and KVPF/NCNTs (with a reference etching rate of 0.16 nm s−1 on SiO2).

TGA was carried out to using a NETZSCH STA 449 F3 thermogravimetric analyzer under a flowing air atmosphere at a heating rate of 10 °C min−1 to analyze the sample composition. FTIR was performed on a Thermo Nicolet Nexus 670 FTIR spectrometer to distinguish the chemical bonds in the samples. Raman spectra were obtained using a HORIBA LabRAM HR Evolution instrument equipped with a 532 nm laser as the excitation source. The zeta potentials of different samples were measured using the laser Doppler method (Zetasizer Ultra, Malvern Instruments, UK). To detect the negative secondary ion fragments, ToF-SIMS measurements was carried out using a ToF-SIMS 5-100 instrument (IONTOF GmbH) equipped with a Bi+ analysis ion beam and a Cs+ sputtering beam. Cryo-TEM images were acquired using a JEOL JEM F200 microscope at cryogenic temperatures ( ~ 100 K) and an accelerating voltage of 200 kV. The active materials were first scraped from the cycled KVPF/NCNTs or KVPF electrodes in an Ar-filled glovebox and then ultrasonically dispersed in DMC. AFM with SKPM measurements was performed using a Bruker Dimension Icon instrument. SRXRD data were collected at the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF) with an X-ray wavelength of 0.6887 Å. The SXRD signals were acquired using a Pilatus 2 M detector and integrated with Fit2D software. X-ray absorption spectroscopy was performed in transmission mode at the BL20U1 beamline of SSRF, and the XANES, EXAFS and WT data were processed using the Athena, Artemis and Hephaestus software packages, respectively.

Electrochemical characterization

CR2032-type coin cells were assembled in an argon-filled glovebox (H2O < 0.1 ppm, O2 < 0.1 ppm). The positive electrodes were prepared by mixing the active materials (KVPF, KFSF and KTPF samples), Super-P conductive carbon black (99%, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.) and PVDF (Mw = 534,000, Sigma-Aldrich) in N-methyl pyrrolidone binder (99%, Aladdin) at a weight ratio of 8:1:1. The resulting homogeneous slurry was single-side coated on aluminum foil (99%, 20 µm thick) using a wet film applicator and then dried in a vacuum oven at 80 °C for 12 h. Then, the electrode sheet was cut into circular disks with a diameter of 10 mm using a manual slicer, in which the loading mass of the active material was approximately 3.2 mg cm−2 and the thickness was about 15 µm. The diameter of the battery is about 20 mm, and the assembly sequence is as follows: stainless steel negative electrode shell, potassium metal (99.99%, Aladdin, potassium metal stored in the glovebox, the oxide layer was first removed, then approximately 5 mg of potassium was cut off and pressed into a pellet with a diameter of about 3 mm using a stainless steel rod), glass fiber separator (Whatman, diameter 16 mm, thickness 675 µm, porosity 90%, pore size 2.7 µm) and 100 µL electrolyte (1 M KPF6 EC/PC + 5% FEC), positive electrode, stainless steel gasket, stainless steel spring sheet and stainless steel positive electrode shell. CV, EIS and GITT measurements were conducted on a PARSTAT 4000 electrochemical workstation, in which the EIS measurements were performed with a signal amplitude of 30 mV over a frequency range of 105 to 0.01 Hz, applying a constant current of approximately 200 μA, with a data acquisition rate of 10 points per decade and no pretreatment was applied prior to the measurements. For ex situ measurements, the cells were first charged/discharged to various states at 0.5 C. They were then promptly transferred into an argon-filled glovebox and disassembled to harvest the electrode. The obtained electrodes were rinsed twice with DMC and dried. Subsequently, they were placed in a vacuum-sealed bag and stored until use. All electrochemical tests are conducted at 25 ± 1 °C.

To assemble potassium-ion full batteries, graphite (Guangdong Canrd New Energy Technology Co., Ltd.) and SC were chosen as negative electrode materials. The negative electrode was prepared by mixing graphite or SC with Super-P conductive carbon black and carboxymethyl cellulose binder in a weight ratio of 8:1:1. The resultant slurry was uniformly single-side coated on copper foil (99%, 10 µm thick) with a wet film applicator and then dried in a vacuum oven at 80 °C for 12 h. Then, the electrode sheet was cut into circular disks with a diameter of 10 mm using a manual slicer, in which the representative loading mass of active material was about 1.7 mg cm−2 and the thickness was approximately 10 µm. The electrolyte was 1 M KPF6 dissolved in EC/PC (1:1 by volume, 100 µL). Before assembling the full cell, the graphite and SC electrodes were pre-cycled in half-cells between 0.01–2.0 V and 0.01–3.0 V, respectively (Supplementary Figs. 47c and 48c), to eliminate the irreversible capacities in the initial cycles. The voltage window for the full cell was set as follows: 1.0–5.0 V for KVPF positive electrodes, 1.0–4.5 V for KFSF positive electrodes and 1.0–4.2 V for KTPF positive electrodes. The specific capacities of all materials were calculated based on the mass of the positive electrode material.

Theoretical calculations

DFT simulation calculations were performed on KVPF, KVPF/CNTs, KVPF/SCNTs and KVPF/NCNTs using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP)56 and the projector-augmented wave (PAW) method. The electron exchange-correlation interactions were described by the general gradient approximation (GGA) and the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) exchange-correlation functional. In order to optimize the model structures, the plane-wave basis set was set with a kinetic energy cutoff of 400 eV. The electronic energy convergence was set to less than 10−6 eV, and the force convergence criterion was 0.02 eV Å−1. During geometric structure optimization, all atomic positions were allowed to relax. Geometry optimization was carried out at the gamma point.
