Is fondaparinux safe for use during pregnancy? A state-of-the-art review of current evidence
Authors: Yuexiao Wu, Yurou Ji, Siqi Wang, Yayi Hu, Dan Shan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Fondaparinux, a synthetic pentasaccharide, represents the smallest heparin-based molecule and functions as a potent selective indirect inhibitor of activated factor Xa. Its high bioavailability, ease of dosing, and favorable tolerability profile render it an ideal antithrombotic agent for pregnant patients who are intolerant to unfractionated heparin (UFH) and low-molecular-weight heparins (LMWHs). Recent studies have highlighted the advancements in the use of fondaparinux in pregnant patients over the past decades. This research provides a comprehensive summary of the safety profiles of fondaparinux, noting that its application in pregnant women may elevate the risk of bleeding events during pregnancy and postpartum. Caution is advised when administering fondaparinux to patients with renal insufficiency. The advantages of fondaparinux include a lower risk of thrombocytopenia, liver injury, allergic reactions, and osteopenia compared with UFH and LMWHs. Although fondaparinux can cross the placental barrier and be detected in the umbilical-cord blood, real-world data have not established a direct association between fondaparinux and teratogenicity or adverse fetal outcomes. Overall, fondaparinux appears to be a viable option for certain groups of pregnant patients requiring anticoagulation. However, further research is warranted to provide deeper insights into the benefits and risks associated with fondaparinux use in this population. The long-term effects of intrauterine exposure to fondaparinux remain an important area for future investigation.

Introduction

Over the past few decades, the demand for anticoagulation prophylaxis and therapeutic use in pregnant women has been increasing. Low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) has become the anticoagulant of choice because of its ﻿efficacy and favorable safety profiles over unfractionated heparin (UFH). ﻿However, due to its metabolism and pharmacokinetic characteristics, LMWH can still lead to ﻿heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT), liver injury, and ﻿hypersensitivity reactions in occasional conditions [1, 2].

Fondaparinux sodium potently and selectively inhibits activated factor Xa via an indirect mechanism. It is distinguished from animal-extract heparins and heparinoids by being a synthetic pentasaccharide, the minimal heparin structure (molecular weight 1.728 kDa). Fondaparinux can bind to antithrombin and promote the deactivation of factor Xa without interference with factor II or platelets. During the early twentieth century, fondaparinux was approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration and European Medicines Agency for the postoperative prophylaxis of venous thromboembolism (VTE) [3, 4]. For pregnant patients intolerant to UFH or LMWHs, fondaparinux was an ideal choice due to its favorable pharmacokinetic profile, simple dosing, and good tolerability [5–7]. For the last decades, fondaparinux has been a mainstay anticoagulant for pregnant patients intolerant to heparins and other anticoagulants. The efficacy and safety profiles of fondaparinux have been testified in studies in pregnant women.

A comprehensive review examining the potential benefits and risks of fondaparinux administration during pregnancy was published in 2015, synthesizing available evidence from case reports and case series [6]. Since that time, a number of clinical trials with certain sample size have been published, and shed more light on the pros and cons of this agent in pregnant patients [8–14]. With the emerging studies elaborating the advances of fondaparinux in pregnant patients, the safety parameters of fondaparinux need to be revisited and updated. This article aims to review the present recent studies pertaining to the use of fondaparinux in pregnant women.

Fondaparinux: mechanisms, pharmacokinetics, and recommendations in guidelines

Fondaparinux has 100% absolute bioavailability and is instant onset of action via subcutaneous administration [15, 16]. The peak plasma concentration is reached within 2 h and the time to peak effect is about 2–3 h [16, 17]. It binds exclusively to antithrombin (AT) and significantly enhances the affinity of AT for factor Xa. After promoting the formation of a covalent complex of AT factor Xa, fondaparinux is discharged unchanged from the complex and can function repeatedly [18]. The half-life of fondaparinux varies from 17 to 21 h based on the demographic characteristics of the subject [16, 19]. Due to its high bioavailability and long elimination half-life, fondaparinux has a once-daily dosing schedule. The direct renal excretion of fondaparinux makes it a safe option in patients with impaired liver function. However, in patients with renal insufficiency, the use of fondaparinux should be undertaken with caution. No dose adjustment based on body weight is needed for the prophylactic use of fondaparinux. Monitoring of the platelet count is generally unnecessary in most cases, as fondaparinux exhibits very low intra- and inter-subject variability [7, 19]. Based on the literature, the prophylactic dosage of fondaparinux is 2.5 mg. In pregnant women, weight gain as pregnancy progresses can pose a challenge. However, as with other populations, the prophylactic dosage of fondaparinux has been used effectively across a wide range of body weights. The weight gain during pregnancy might be negligible because fondaparinux has a broad therapeutic range and safety profile. Further clinical trials are needed to confirm these findings.

﻿Major international guidelines consistently identify LMWH as the standard first-line agent for VTE prophylaxis and treatment during pregnancy, with fondaparinux considered a second-line option when heparins are not tolerated. Evidence supporting its use in pregnancy is regarded as limited, derived primarily from case reports or small case series. Fondaparinux is recommended by the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) and Society of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists of Canada (SOGC) in pregnant patients with heparin allergies or heparin-induced thrombocytopenia for VTE prophylaxis and treatment [20, 21]. Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists (RCOG) recommends that in pregnant women who are unable to tolerate UFH and other heparinoids, consideration should be given to the use of fondaparinux [22]. Queensland clinical guideline and American College of Chest Physicians (ACCP) recommend limiting the use of fondaparinux to women with severe allergic reactions to heparins and heparinoids who cannot receive danaparoid [23, 24]. The American Society of Hematology supports its use during lactation but notes that, due to placental transfer, additional pregnancy-specific safety data are needed [25]. The Chinese expert consensus recommends the use of fondaparinux in pregnant patients with acute or subacute HIT, despite limited evidence supporting its application [26].

The VTE prophylaxis dosage of fondaparinux is typically 2.5 mg once daily in patients after major orthopedic surgery, major abdominal surgery and for those with cancer [18, 19, 27, 28]. A dosage of 7.5 mg of fondaparinux is recommended as initial treatment for pulmonary embolism (PE) and deep vein thrombosis (DVT) [5, 7, 29]. In pregnant patients, it is also suggested that a 2.5 mg once-daily dosage of fondaparinux is used for prophylaxis, and the same standard dose is used for therapeutic purposes in DVT and PE treatment in pregnant patients. But this dosage should be adjusted in patients with extreme body weight and should be reduced in patients with Lower body weights. Clinical reports indicate that doses may reach up to 10 mg depending on individual circumstances [9]. Fondaparinux at the prophylactic dosage should be discontinued at least 24 h before delivery, and if possible, stopping it 72 h prior is even more appropriate. The re-administration of fondaparinux should start no less than 6 h after delivery, or 12 h after the removal of the anesthetic neuraxial catheter [6, 7, 10, 11]. In specific populations, such as patients with preeclampsia, renal function and coagulation parameters should be closely monitored throughout treatment.

For superficial thrombophlebitis, fondaparinux is recommended at a dose of 2.5 mg once daily for a duration of 45 days [30, 31]. In cases of recurrent thrombosis, available evidence remains limited; however, existing studies suggest that dosing should be individualized. Reports indicate that when recurrence cannot be controlled with increased doses of LMWH or UFH, fondaparinux at 7.5–10 mg once daily may be considered [32].

Studies on the safety profiles of fondaparinux in pregnancy have been published in recent years [9–14, 33–35]. We found that fondaparinux was mostly used in patients diagnosed with recurrent spontaneous abortion (RSA). Most studies used fondaparinux at prophylactic dosage of 2.5 mg once daily. The most frequently reported adverse events were bleeding.

Adverse events of fondaparinux in pregnancy

Bleeding

Bleeding events is the most reported complications related with fondaparinux. Minor vaginal bleeding during pregnancy and postpartum hemorrhage (PPH) at delivery were reported in the literature at a low incidence. Ecchymosis on the injection site were frequently reported. Hematoma were reported in two studies [9, 10].

Three patients with postpartum hemorrhage were reported by Knol. et al [35]. Dumpfle et al. reported seven bleeding events [9]. Muhamed. et al. reported a case complicated with preeclampsia and two previous cesarean section histories developed large Hematoma over the Pfannenstiel incision 3 days post-delivery [10]. Dumpfle et al. reported a case developed uterine hematoma [9]. There were no reports of major or life-threatening bleeding. Notably, Elsaigh et al. reported a unique case where surgery was delayed in a patient with reduced fetal movement due to concerns about maternal bleeding risks associated with fondaparinux [14]. It is not uncommon for pregnant women on UFH or LMWH use to experience hemorrhage events or hematoma. Ecchymosis and erythema at the injection site are commonly observed in patients using heparins. However, from the literature we reviewed, following an appropriate withdrawal period, no instances of life-threatening bleeding attributable to fondaparinux were observed. In emergencies, maternal bleeding risk and fetal safety require careful balancing. A planned labor is recommended, allowing a sufficient time interval from the most recent administration of anticoagulants. In emergent situations, careful monitoring and hemostasis suture procedures during cesarean section are crucial.

Considering the Long half-life of fondaparinux, RCOG recommends waiting 36–42 h from the last dose before regional anesthesia can be implemented [22]. The Queensland Clinical Guidelines Steering Committee recommends withholding fondaparinux for 5 days before birth [23]. According to the recommendations of the American Society of Interventional Pain Physicians (ASIPP) and the American Society of Regional Anesthesia and Pain Medicine (ASRA), it is suggested to discontinue fondaparinux at least 4 days before any moderate- or high-risk interventional techniques, and to restart treatment 8–12 h afterward [17]. However, in our reviewed literature, most researchers stopped fondaparinux 24 h before delivery [9–11]. And the earliest reintroduction of fondaparinux started at 6 h [10]. Bleeding events were reported, but hemorrhage rates associated with fondaparinux were either comparable to or notably Lower than those in LMWH-treated patients. Considering the above recommendations, regional anesthesia in pregnant women might be recommended after 3 days from the last prophylactic dose of fondaparinux. However, if the therapeutic dose is applied, more time may be required. In emergent situations, such as suspected fetal distress, cesarean sections should still be performed, with clear notification of the bleeding risks.

Of note, although the incidence of fondaparinux-induced severe bleeding events is low, there are still certain risks for serious hemorrhage. Previous case reports described cases with severe bleeding in elder patients with myocardial infarction and chronic renal impairment [36, 37]. Fondaparinux-induced massive leg Hematoma and compartment syndrome in a 77-year-old after total hip arthroplasty was reported by Hannon. Et al [38]. Bordes.et al. reported a life-threatening case with subdural Hematoma with transtentorial Herniation in a 76-yr-old man receiving fondaparinux 7.5 mg daily for prevention of stroke due to atrial fibrillation [39]. These life-threatening bleeding events occurred in elder patients with prolonged use and large dosage of fondaparinux. In pregnant patients with certain bleeding risks, the prescription of fondaparinux either for prophylactic or therapeutic use should still be with caution. In patients who receive therapeutic doses of fondaparinux, an appropriate withdrawal period and careful surgical technique are crucial. The reintroduction of heparins after delivery should be based on a thorough evaluation of bleeding risk. Strict blood pressure control in patients with preeclampsia after delivery have key significance in preventing hemorrhagic events after delivery.

There is no specific antidote for fondaparinux reversal [40]. In a randomized, placebo-controlled trial, recombinant factor VIIa (rFVIIa) has been shown to reverse the anticoagulant effects of fondaparinux in healthy volunteers [41]. Ex vivo studies showed that addition of rFVIIa can correct the delayed clot formation induced by fondaparinux. The acceleration of clot lysis time by fondaparinux was partially reversed by rFVIIa [42]. But evidence from case series of fondaparinux-induced severe bleeding demonstrate that the efficiency of rFVIIa is only 50% [43]. Fresh frozen plasma (FFP) or prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC) are rapid onset and can be used to reverse the anticoagulant action of fondaparinux. The novel recombinant-modified human factor Xa decoy protein, andexanet alfa, can bind to and reverse the effects of factor Xa inhibitors [40]. Andexanet alfa may be used to reverse the function of fondaparinux. But there were lack of evidence supporting the efficacy and safety of andexanet alfa in pregnant women.

Thrombocytopenia (HIT)

Thrombocytopenia, known as heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT), is a life-threatening condition. The negatively charged sulfate groups of Heparins can induce the formation of ultra-large CXC chemokine platelet factor 4 (PF4)/heparin complexes. Antibodies against this compound were then stimulated and resulted in the organization of antibody/PF4/heparin complexes. Cross link with the FcγRIIa receptors will then induce intensively enhanced activation and aggregation of platelets [44, 45]. Monocytes, neutrophils, and other immune regulatory cells responsible for this response are activated, and lead to the devastating HIT. HIT occurred in 0.1–5% of patients receiving therapeutic doses of Heparins. In patients undergo cardiac surgery, development of HIT can lead to a 50% increase in early mortality [46].

However, it is assumed that fondaparinux cannot cause HIT, because fondaparinux has shortest molecular sequence compared with UFH and LMWHs. It is undeniable that the severity of HIT differs in the heparin family according to the molecular weight. Because the binding affinity for PF4 varies. Assumptions regarding the lack of HIT induction are based on findings that fondaparinux-PF4 complexes have the lowest binding affinity. This could not induce the conformational change of PF4 [47, 48]. Antibodies cannot be elicited because there are no functional immune complexes formed between fondaparinux and PF4, hence platelets remain inactive [48].

Controversies exist on fondaparinux and HIT. Bhatt. Et al reviewed the literature and reported eight fondaparinux-related HIT cases [49]. They found fondaparinux-associated HIT occurred in patients of pro-inflammatory state. However, the majority of fondaparinux-related HIT cases were previously exposed to UFH or LMWH. The possibility of delayed onset HIT due to UFH or LMWH could not be ruled out. Most recent studies have shown a positive view on the advantages of fondaparinux for HIT. Evidence from clinical trials and case series supported the off-label use of fondaparinux in patients with HIT [47, 50]. According to the American Society of Hematology (ASH) guideline, once HIT is confirmed or strongly suspected, all forms of heparin should be discontinued immediately and replaced with a non-heparin anticoagulant, among which fondaparinux is an option [51]. Multiple case reports and clinical studies have documented its use as an important alternative anticoagulant in practice. It may be considered when conventional direct thrombin inhibitors (DTIs) are unavailable and anticoagulation is still required [52]. A retrospective, national, Multicenter study in Germany included 195 patients diagnosed with HIT (4 T score ≥ 4), 84 of the participants were treated with fondaparinux. They found that fondaparinux demonstrated convincing efficacy and safety compared with argatroban, lepirudin, and danaparoid. Fondaparinux did not cause further HIT-specific complications, including thromboembolic events, amputation, or skin necrosis. The all-cause in-hospital mortality was 0.0% in fondaparinux group. Bleeding events occurred in 4.8% of patients treated with fondaparinux. The main contributing factors for the bleeding were inappropriate dosing in patients with impaired renal function [47]. Mauermann. el al reported treatment with fondaparinux in a 29-year-old pregnant woman of congestive heart failure who developed HIT from UFH [53]. No intraoperative complications occurred and the estimated blood Loss in cesarean section was 400 ml. Hajj-Chahine et al. reported successful management with fondaparinux in a woman during the second trimester with HIT and a massive pulmonary embolism. Embolectomy under cardiopulmonary bypass was performed at 27th gestational week and the women delivered at 38th gestational week without severe complications [54]. Similar successful treatment of HIT cases with fondaparinux in pregnant women were reported in patients with acute PE in third trimester and patients of advanced age with DVT in the first trimester [55, 56]. In addition, some reports have described its use as a perioperative and postoperative bridging anticoagulant in HIT patients undergoing vascular surgery [57].

From the literatures we reviewed, we found no cases of HIT caused by fondaparinux. Given the increasing use of fondaparinux in treating HIT, it appears to be an option for managing pregnant patients with HIT. This is because evidence from previous studies indicates that fondaparinux is well-tolerated for HIT and has not been associated with further adverse events like bleeding and thrombosis.

The classic anticoagulants for HIT included argatroban, lepirudin, and danaparoid. However, there is a lack of evidence regarding the safety of these agents during pregnancy and breastfeeding [58–60]. But we should still bear in mind that fondaparinux is still in the “niche” position for treating HIT. Ex vivo studies by Chen. et al. indicated that at an appropriate molecular ratio (fondaparinux and PF4 at 3 ~ 4:1), large and stable PF4/fondaparinux complexes could still be formed [61]. This can further lead to activation of antibodies to PF4/fondaparinux and initiation of cross-linking with platelets. They found fondaparinux at the concentration of 4 mg/l could significantly facilitate the binding of antibodies to the complexes and enhance platelet aggregation and activation. Although, in general, reaching a concentration of 4 mg/L is typically not possible in patients with normal kidney function receiving the routine dosage of fondaparinux. Their findings still indicate that in pregnant patients with renal insufficiency and in patients who receive fondaparinux at extreme dosage, monitoring of the fondaparinux concentration is needed.

Potential in teratogenicity

Evidence from ex vivo models using human placenta indicates that fondaparinux does not cross the placenta. Evidence from ex vivo models using human placenta indicate that fondaparinux did not cross the placenta [62, 63]. However, evidence from in vivo studies found that even fondaparinux at the prophylaxis dosage from maternal use could result in measurable anti-factor Xa activity in umbilical-cord blood [64]. But none of the four pregnant women with measurable fondaparinux in umbilical-cord blood had adverse events in their newborns. This may be due to the fact that the concentration of fondaparinux in umbilical-cord blood is far below the effective anticoagulation concentration. Nevertheless, the possibility of a detrimental influence on the fetal coagulation system should be kept in mind in women undergoing fondaparinux treatment.

Birth defects were reported in only one patient treated with fondaparinux. Elsaigh et al. reported a case in which the patient terminated Her pregnancy at 23 gestational weeks due to severe fetal abnormalities (tetralogy of Fallot and Dandy–Walker syndrome) [14]. But evidence for a direct causal link between fondaparinux and fetal birth defects is lacking. The concentration of fondaparinux was not assessed in the cord blood in their research [14]. However, fondaparinux’s ability to cross the placenta necessitates strict fetal monitoring during therapy. Studies on the fetal safety profile in women using fondaparinux are required. The potential effects of fondaparinux treatment duration and dosage on teratogenesis and fetal bleeding risk also require investigation. Future research on the long-term influence of intrauterine exposure to fondaparinux is warranted.

Liver injury

Since fondaparinux is not metabolized hepatically, routine monitoring of liver function is typically not required during its use. However, previous studies have indicated a potential for fondaparinux-induced liver injury. Yang et al. included 463 patients with pulmonary embolism treated with LMWH or fondaparinux sodium. Compared to enoxaparin sodium or nadroparin calcium, fondaparinux sodium had the lowest incidence of drug-induced liver injury (DILI). They found that 7.4% (2 out of 27) of patients treated with fondaparinux were diagnosed with DILI [65]. Characterization of fondaparinux-induced DILI included mild elevations in ALT/AST levels and infrequent increases in bilirubin without clinical symptoms. Hosaka. et al. included 575 patients who underwent total knee arthroplasty, 8.3% (23 in 276) of the patients had elevated alanine aminotransferase level. No patient exhibited clinical symptoms of disrupted liver function [66]. The most serious case of fondaparinux-induced DILI was reported by Orostegui. et al [67]. They reported a case of a 6-year-old child with acute Hepatitis-like symptoms and a 15-fold increase in alanine and aspartate aminotransferases after switching from enoxaparin to fondaparinux. The symptoms resolved rapidly upon reintroduction of enoxaparin [67]. The authors concluded that the reason for this acute onset hepatitis symptoms was caused by fondaparinux with high possibility because the signs appeared soon after fondaparinux use and disappeared rapidly after the drug was discontinued.

There have been no reported severe cases of DILI caused by fondaparinux in pregnant women. Only Yang reported two patients with moderate elevated aminotransferases without clinical symptoms [34]. The underlying mechanisms of fondaparinux-induced DILI remain elusive. Since fondaparinux does not undergo hepatic metabolism and was excreted as unchanged compound from kidney, the potential role of immediate inflammatory mediators’ release could not be ruled out. The case reported by Orostegui et al. had a sudden onset [67]. In Yang’s large cohort study of 463 patients, liver dysfunction was observed within the first week of administration [65]. This rapid-onset injury may be attributed to the immediate enhancement of inflammatory reactions. The mechanism of DILI is multifaceted, dysfunctions in mitochondria, immune response, signal transduction and drug metabolism could all lead to the disrupted and magnified inflammatory responses [68, 69]. Furthermore, DILI is typically regarded as unpredictable due to the uncertainty of risk factors. The risk of DILI can occur in individuals with varying demographic characteristics and fluctuating health statuses. The drug’s chemistry, metabolism, and treatment regimen must also be taken into account. Evidence from previous studies indicated that fondaparinux seems to remain as the safest choice for liver injury compared with LMWHs and UFH, either in non-pregnant participants or in pregnant women. Although rare, the prescription of fondaparinux in pregnant women should still be based on the awareness of the potential for liver injury.

Allergy

Except for the bleeding complications, heparin-induced allergic reactions of skin lesions are very frequently reported adverse effects after subcutaneous injection [70]. Previous studies showed that fondaparinux had good tolerability among patients. In a Germany study, which included 231 patients using fondaparinux, no patient developed typical delayed-type IV hypersensitivity erythematous skin lesions. Only one woman experienced abdominal pruritus, which is also confirmed as a hypersensitivity reaction. The overall prevalence for fondaparinux-induced allergic skin reactions was 0.4% [70]. However, cases of positive allergic skin lesions related to fondaparinux have been reported in patients diagnosed with type IV hypersensitivity to UFH and LMWH [71, 72]. The cause of the allergic reaction to fondaparinux remains unknown—whether it is due to cross-reactivity with previously used heparin molecules or represents a new sensitivity response to a different epitope. But fondaparinux was showed to be tolerated well in pregnant women with allergies to LMWH and UFH. Wijesiriwardana et al. reported a woman with good tolerance of fondaparinux in the third trimester for prevention of DVT, following her allergy to enoxaparin and dalteparin [73]. Buonomo.et al. reported a patient with essential thrombocythemia and recurrent PE. She was allergic to UFH and several types of LMWHs but tolerated 2.5 mg of subcutaneous fondaparinux with no side effects. She continued her fondaparinux during pregnancy without major bleeding events or hypersensitivity reactions [74]. In our reviewed studies, allergic reactions were not reported in the pregnant patients. However, since most of the cases were in Asia, the tolerability of fondaparinux in pregnant women with different demographic characteristics should be explored in future studies.

Osteopenia

In general condition, pregnancy- and lactation-associated osteopenia and osteoporosis are rare. But in pregnant patients on medication which has deteriorating effects on calcium metabolism, long-term use of these agents may lead to pathophysiological changes in the skeletal structure [75]. UFH and LMWHs were considered to have possible influence for osteopenia in women during pregnancy and lactation [76, 77]. But in vitro studies using osteocytes cell cultures demonstrated that bone metabolism was not influenced by fondaparinux. No inhibition of osteoblast proliferation was observed when cells were incubated with fondaparinux within the therapeutic range [78]. Furthermore, the effects of fondaparinux on mitochondrial activity and protein synthesis in osteoblasts were minimal compared to UFH and LMWH [79]. Fondaparinux did not affect mesenchymal stem cell proliferation or their differentiation potential into chondrocytes and osteoblasts [80]. Based on the current limited evidence, it appears that fondaparinux is safe, has no adverse impact on bone metabolism, and can be used in pregnant women with an increased risk of osteopenia.

Fondaparinux in special situations

Renal insufficiency

﻿Fondaparinux is primarily eliminated via urinary excretion. The plasma clearance of fondaparinux is 5.1 to 7.9 mL/min and renal clearance is 4.0 to 7.9 mL/min in individuals with normal renal function [16, 81]. A previous study showed that fondaparinux at the dosage of 2.5 mg once daily could lead to a significant elevation anti-factor Xa activity [82]. But in patients with impaired kidney function, the renal clearance rate of fondaparinux was significantly decreased. Accumulation of fondaparinux could occur even more rapidly. Risk for bleeding was as well elevated. It is generally recommended that in patients with severe renal dysfunction (creatinine clearance [CrCl] < 30 ml/min), the benefits and risks of fondaparinux should be considered carefully.

Attempts to reduce the dosage of fondaparinux have been made in previous studies for patients with renal insufficiency. In patients with moderate renal impairment (CrCl 20–50 mL/min), available evidence supports the use of a reduced fondaparinux dose of 1.5 mg once daily [83]. In those with severe renal impairment (CrCl < 30 mL/min), fondaparinux should generally be used with caution. One study reported that an extended-interval dosing regimen of 2.5 mg every 48 h in patients with severe renal impairment achieved peak and trough anti-Xa levels comparable to those observed with the standard 2.5 mg once-daily regimen in non-critically ill patients with normal renal function [84]. In patients with renal dysfunction, measurement of anti-Xa activity may be used to guide dose adjustment. In the PROPICE trial, 442 patients with renal impairment underwent orthopedic surgeries. A daily dosage of 1.5 mg of fondaparinux resulted in major bleeding events for 4.5% of participants [85]. In the FONDAIR study, 206 patients with renal insufficiency (creatinine clearance between 20 and 50 ml/min) received 1.5 mg of fondaparinux once daily. The rate of major bleeding was 0.49%, and the rate of non-major bleeding was 3.88% [86]. From our literature review, no patients with impaired renal functions were included. But we should still bear in mind the potential bleeding risk due to fondaparinux accumulation in patients with renal insufficiency. Especially in patients with preeclampsia, there are growing demands for anticoagulation treatment in this population. The high VTE risk scores and the needs to modulate reproductive immunology contribute to the increase in LMWH and fondaparinux use in patients with preeclampsia. Renal function and anticoagulation time should be monitored, and the elevated risk for bleeding should be notified with these patients.

Of note, fondaparinux is primarily eliminated through the kidneys, making renal function a critical factor in its pharmacokinetics. By the end of the first trimester, CrCl can increase by approximately 50% and remains elevated throughout the pregnancy. This increase in CrCl can potentially enhance the clearance of renally excreted drugs like fondaparinux, reducing their plasma concentrations. However, to date, there is no evidence supporting the need for dose adjustment of fondaparinux as pregnancy progresses. The dosage is primarily determined based on the patient’s weight, and clinical trials involving pregnant women typically use a standardized dosage [20]. This suggests that despite the increased renal clearance rate during pregnancy, fondaparinux remains effective and safe within its therapeutic range. However, we also need to note that the pharmacokinetics of fondaparinux during pregnancy have not been extensively studied. Similar anticoagulants may experience increased volume of distribution and altered clearance due to physiological changes during pregnancy, potentially affecting the drug’s half-life. The Vd and t1/2 of LMWH were altered as pregnancy progressed [87]. This may contribute to the increased risk for bleeding events in the third trimester. But the pharmacokinetic parameters such as changes in Vd and t1/2 of fondaparinux were not reported in detail. Whether this may impact the risk for bleeding events of fondaparinux was not explored in current available studies. Further studies are necessary to clarify the pharmacokinetic changes of fondaparinux during the third trimester, particularly regarding changes in volume of distribution and half-life, to better guide clinical use.

Obesity

﻿In general, no dose adjustments of prophylactic indication were needed for fondaparinux use in obese patients, because the pharmacokinetics of fondaparinux included high bioavailability and negligible intra- and inter-subject variability. However, recent studies have suggested monitoring anti-factor Xa levels in extremely obese patients to ensure effective prophylactic anticoagulation efficacy [7]. For therapeutic use, a dosage of 10 mg of fondaparinux is recommended for individuals weighing over 100 kg, and 5 mg for those weighing under 50 kg [5, 88]. Treatment of fondaparinux at 10 mg was only reported in two studies. In Dempfle’s study, four cases utilized a 10 mg dose of fondaparinux [9]. Elsaigh. et al. reported a case of patient with a body weight of 99 kg [14]. A daily dosage of 7.5 mg of fondaparinux was deemed insufficient for anticoagulation, resulting in a second DVT event. No maternal or fetal bleeding events were reported for this patient at a daily fondaparinux dose of 10 mg. Body weight has been identified as an independent factor inversely associated with anti-factor Xa activity. Studies have shown that patients with low body weight exhibit higher anti-factor Xa activity after the second dose of fondaparinux, but this was not associated with an increased risk of bleeding [89]. Although some literature recommends adjusting the dose according to both body weight and renal function, the available studies are limited in number and quality, and no specific dosing regimen has been established for low-body-weight patients [90].

Monitoring of fondaparinux

﻿Monitoring of fondaparinux is typically unnecessary in most cases because of its high bioavailability and the more predictable way of clearance through urinary excretion. However, in specific patient groups, monitoring the efficacy of fondaparinux may provide additional benefits and improve the safety. In patients with renal impairment, particularly those with dialysis-dependent chronic kidney disease receiving renal replacement therapy, altered pharmacokinetics of fondaparinux may warrant monitoring [91, 92]. In patients with hepatic impairment accompanied by antithrombin deficiency, daily monitoring of antithrombin III-mediated anti-Xa levels can help ensure that drug concentrations remain within a safe range, thereby reducing the risk of thrombotic or bleeding events [93]. In critically ill patients, the anticoagulant effect of fondaparinux can be unpredictable, and anti-Xa monitoring alone may be insufficient; comprehensive assessment incorporating thrombin–antithrombin complex (TAT) levels and thromboelastography (TEG) has been suggested [94]. Although not mandatory, monitoring may also be useful in individuals with extreme body weights. In obese patients, it may help confirm adequate anticoagulation, whereas in those with low body weight—who may exhibit elevated anti-Xa activity—monitoring can assist in risk assessment. ﻿There is no consensus on the therapeutic range for fondaparinux. Among Healthy males receiving prophylactic doses of fondaparinux, the average steady-state concentration is 0.39–0.5 mg/L. In patients receiving therapeutic dosage for VTE, the mean steady-state concentration is 1.20–1.26 mg/l [95, 96].

Measurement of anti-Xa activity is an alternative method that can be utilized to assess the effectiveness of fondaparinux. However, monitoring anti-Xa levels do not ensure that fondaparinux has reached the target concentration, and the anticoagulation efficacy was not guaranteed [97]. TEG monitoring is a potential method for assessing hemostasis adequacy during fondaparinux therapy [96]. TEG involves five parameters, thereby providing a more complex assessment of an individual’s coagulation system. TEG might be useful to monitor the efficacy of fondaparinux by ensuring whether the individual is adequately anticoagulated. In addition, TEG can be used to assess bleeding risk and inform fondaparinux treatment decisions. Routine monitoring of anti-Xa levels and TEG is not performed in pregnant patients. But monitoring of TEG and anti-Xa might be used to guide fondaparinux treatment, especially in patients approaching the due date and those with certain bleeding risks.

Conclusion

Fondaparinux demonstrates favorable efficacy and safety, with markedly lower risks of HIT, liver injury, and osteopenia compared with UFH and LMWH, and with rare allergic reactions (Fig. 1). It is particularly recommended in pregnancy for women with HIT, LMWH hypersensitivity, or increased risk of bone loss, as current evidence—despite its ability to cross the placenta—shows no direct teratogenic effects or fetal hemorrhage. Caution is advised in patients with renal impairment due to its prolonged half-life, and in those nearing delivery, where precise discontinuation timing is necessary to reduce bleeding risk. Long-term fetal outcomes remain uncertain.
