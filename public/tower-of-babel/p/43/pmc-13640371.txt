Evaluation of prevalence and length of the anterior loop of mental nerve using cone beam computed tomography
Authors: Thekra Radwan, Can Pelin, Raghib Suradi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe anterior loop (AnL) of the mental nerve is anatomically defined as the segment where the neurovascular bundle courses anteriorly and inferiorly to the mental foramen (MF) before looping back to exit through it. This structure is a critical consideration in determining optimal implant placement in the mandibular premolar region. The MF itself is an essential anatomical landmark, particularly for effective local anesthesia and for minimizing the risk of iatrogenic injury during surgical procedures in the premolar area. Cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) enables enhanced visualization of anatomical landmarks, offering precise localization, delineation, and assessment of their spatial relationships. Accordingly, CBCT is recommended when detailed anatomical information is required, such as in the identification and evaluation of the AnL.AimThis study was designed to evaluate the vertical and horizontal positioning of the MF and to assess the visibility, classification, and length of the AnL using CBCT in dentate and edentulous patients across various age groups.Materials and methodsA retrospective analysis was performed on 260 mandibular CBCT scans to determine the location of the MF and the presence, type, and length of the AnL. Associations between these parameters and patient age, gender, mandibular side and dental status were statistically analyzed.ResultsThe overall frequencies of AnL types 1, 2, and 3 were 7.7%, 20.8%, and 71.5%, respectively. The mean AnL length was 2.75 mm, with a range of 0.6 mm to 6.7 mm. Statistically significant differences in AnL prevalence were identified across age and dental status groups, while AnL length varied significantly with respect to gender, age, and dental status. Additionally, the vertical position of the MF was significantly correlated with both AnL prevalence and length.ConclusionsThe findings indicate a high prevalence of the AnL, underscoring the necessity of evaluating its presence and length in preoperative planning to mitigate the risk of neurovascular injury during maxillofacial surgical procedures.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-09031-w.

Introduction

In classical anatomical literature, the mental nerve (MN) is described as the terminal branch of the inferior alveolar nerve (IAN), particularly upon its emergence from the mental foramen (MF), typically located deep to the depressor anguli oris muscle [1]. At this point, the MN commonly divides into three unnamed branches [2]. In some anatomical variations, the terminal segment of the IAN courses beneath the lower and mesial walls of the MF, emits a short incisive branch, and subsequently loops back to exit the foramen, continuing as the MN in the soft tissues—this configuration is referred to as the anterior loop (AnL) of the IAN (Fig. 1) [3]. Bavitz et al. characterized the AnL as the segment where the mental neurovascular bundle extends anterior to the MF before reversing direction and exiting through it [4]. The posterior migration of the MF from the deciduous canine region to an area near the deciduous molars, resulting from mandibular growth and remodeling, may explain the anatomical variability observed in this region [5].

The branching pattern of the MN can be systematically classified into three distinct morphological types : Type I: the anatomy is Y-shaped, the AnL is hardly perceptible, and there isn’t a loop to be found. The mental branch emerges from the IAN posterior to the entrance of the MF. Type II: the anatomy is T-shaped and the AnL is lacking. The mental branch enters the MF on a perpendicular pattern, while the incisive branch runs perpendicular to the main branch. Type III: the anatomy is Y-shaped and the AnL is apparent [6] (Fig. 2).

In the planning of implant-supported fixed prostheses, the anatomical localization of the MF and the AnL of the mental neurovascular bundle serves as a critical determinant for the placement of the most distal implants within the interforaminal region [7]. To increase the anterio-posterior spread and decrease distal cantilever, it is essential to position the implant closest to the MF while paying attention to the AnL [8]. Post-implant placement procedures may lead to neurosensory disturbances, including altered sensation of the lower lip and chin, due to surgical trauma involving the MN [9]. In the presence of an AnL, insufficient knowledge of its anatomical extension significantly increases the risk of iatrogenic injury [10]. Although the location of the MF can be identified by radiological imaging techniques, Kuzmanovic et al. came to the conclusion that panoramic radiographs (PR) are unreliable and frequently indicate the AnL in the wrong place [11].

Panorama x-rays are still a traditional approach that can be used to analyze and study jaw anatomy, such as the bifid mandibular canals [12]. Even though CBCT presents better observation of important anatomical structures including position, detailed distinction and interactions with adjacent structures [13]. Therefore, CBCT should be suggested when the clinician determines that more anatomical information is necessary such as AnL and MF [14].

This study aims to identify and classify the types of AnL of the MN, and to quantitatively assess its length by CBCT in relation to mandibular side (right/left), patient age, gender, MF position and dental status.

Materials and methods

This retrospective cross-sectional analytical study evaluated CBCT data from patients who sought general dental care. CBCT scans were obtained at Dentistomo Radiology Centre in Ankara, Turkey, between July 2020 and January 2022. Data analysis was conducted by two observers using anonymized CBCT images with permission from the imaging center.

As this study involved retrospective evaluation of previously acquired anonymized CBCT images, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the ethics committee of Başkent University (Approval No: K22/260). All procedures followed the principles of the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments.

CBCT images were acquired using a Dentri S unit (HDXWILL Corp., Seoul, South Korea) according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. The imaging parameters were: voxel size 0.2 mm; field of view (FOV) 16 × 8 cm; 90 kVp; 8 mAs; and exposure time 24 s. Image processing and analysis were performed using OnDemand 3D App software (Cybermed Inc., Seoul, South Korea).

The sample size was calculated using G*Power version 3.1 based on a previous CBCT study evaluating the MF and AnL of the mental nerve. Considering a Cohen’s d effect size of 0.349, a significance level of 0.05, and a statistical power of 80%, the minimum required sample size was determined to be 260 cases. Thus, a total of 260 mandibular CBCT (145 females and 115 males) scans were included in the study to ensure sufficient power for detecting statistically significant differences in MF position and AnL-related measurements. The mean age of participants was 44.77 years (± 13.5). Groups were categorized based on dentate/edentate status, gender, side (right/left) and age (40 years or older vs. under 40).

Vertical measurements of mental foramen position

A vertical straight measured line was drawn from the superior tangent (ST) of the most superior point of MF up to the upper alveolar cortex of the mandible (X1) and from the inferior tangent (IT) of the most inferior point of the MF down to the lower cortex of the mandible (X2) to measure the vertical position of the MF [see Additional file 1(Fig. 1)] .

Horizontal measurement of mental foramen position

Before the measurements, two reference points were drawn. The mandible’s left and right sides were separated by a straight line that was drawn from the nasal spine down to the mandibular symphysis. A straight horizontal line was drawn from the anterior border of the MF to the reference line in order to measure the distance between the mandibular symphysis and the MF (MF-Midline) (X3) [15] [see Additional file 1(Fig. 2)] .

Identification and measurement of length of AnL

Multiplanar reconstruction enables the generation of coronal, sagittal and oblique images from the original axial dataset. Initially, the sagittal reference line was adjusted on the axial plane to optimize visualization of the MF in the sagittal view (Fig. 3.a). Subsequently, the coronal reference line (purple) (Fig. 3.a) was refined within the axial plane to achieve an optimal coronal depiction of the MF (Fig. 3.b). Thereafter, the coronal plane was extended and an oblique section was generated, oriented to traverse the center of the MF, thereby enabling concurrent visualization of both the AnL and the MF (Fig. 4). Finally, the image was magnified to facilitate precise linear measurements with a resolution of up to 0.4 mm.

Fifth, a red reference line was drawn perpendicular to a yellow line oriented parallel to the buccal cortical plate, intersecting the most anterior extent of the AnL. This red line represents either the origin of the incisive canal or the anterior-most boundary of the AnL. The anterior loop length (AnLL) was measured as the linear distance from the most anterior margin of the MF to the reference demarcation line (red line) [10] (Fig. 5). In type 2 and type 3 mental branching, the MN ends at the MF (Fig. 6).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analysis were made using IBM SPSS Statistics software version 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Quantitative variables were expressed as mean, standard deviation, median, minimum, maximum, and qualitative variables were expressed as frequency and percentage. The chi-square test for qualitative variables and the independent samples t-test for quantitative variables were used to compare the differences between the two groups. The relationship between two numerical variables was analyzed using the Pearson correlation coefficient. Interobserver agreement was evaluated with the intraclass correlation coefficient. P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The study population consisted of 145 females and 115 males, with an age range of 20–71 years and a mean age of 44.77 ± 13.5 years. When the mandible was assessed bilaterally as two hemi-mandibles, 133 hemi-mandibles were classified as dentate and 127 as edentulous. The overall distribution of AnL types was 7.7% for Type 1, 20.8% for Type 2 and 71.5% for Type 3.

Distribution values for types 1, 2, and 3 were evaluated as follows: 7.7%, 21.9%, and 70.4% for the right side and 7.7%, 19.6%, and 72.7% for the left side respectively (Table 1).

Discussion

Radiographic screening is required before the dental implant procedure in the interforaminal region in order to prevent neurological trauma. One of the most severe side effects of lower jaw implant surgery is dysesthesia of the lower lip due to MN damage [3]. CBCT has recently taken on a more significant role in dentistry diagnosis thanks to its advantages of lower radiation exposure in comparison to CT with equivalent resolution and precision as well as its accessibility and affordability [13].

Solar et al. reported that in a study of 37 cadaveric mandibles, AnLs in 60% of the cases [6]. Neiva et al. showed an AnL in 88% of 22 cadavers [16]. Greenstein and Tarnow recommended using a probe to find the AnL. They came to the conclusion that it was impossible to tell whether the AnL or the incisive canal was being evaluated [17]. Moreover, preoperative assessment could not be done using this procedure. Eskenazi et al. found that the AnL was present in 48.8% of CBCT cases, compared to 36.6% in PR emphasizing the limitations of PR in accurately detecting the AnL before implant procedures, because of its severe size distortions and low resolution [3]. Kuzmanovic et al. recommended that clinicians should not depend on PR for identifying the AnL of the MN [11].

CBCT studies revealed an AnL prevalence of 48-85.2% [10, 18]. Apostolakis et al. in their study of 93 patients with CBCT images, AnLs were found to have a 48% frequency mostly bilaterally [18]. Nascimento et al. reported the presence of the AnL in 41.6% of cases, with the majority occurring bilaterally, followed by unilateral presentations on the left and right sides, respectively. In comparison, our study demonstrated a higher prevalence, with the AnL identified in 71.5% of cases, of which 81.5% were bilateral, and the remainder observed unilaterally more frequently on the left side than the right. These results coincide with those mentioned by Filo et al. (69.73%) [19]. There were found to be 7.7%, and 20.8%, total frequencies of type 1 and type 2, respectively. A higher prevalence of the AnL was observed by Parnia et al. in 96 CBCT scans, AnL was seen in 84.4% of the cases [20]. The 1 mm CBCT slice thickness used in this investigation may be the cause of this disparity. The less percentage of prevalence of AnL in our study may be because the reconstructed sections with a thickness of 2 mm were used for image evaluation. Similar prevalence statistics (85.2%, n = 366) were obtained in USA by Lu et al. [10]. The different properties of the machine that has been used and the larger sample size (366) could be the reason of the higher prevalence of AnL than that found in our study. Using vertical cross-sectional slices, Demir et al. found that CBCT identified AnL in over half of the cases (59.5%), with type 2 loops seen in 31.9% and type 1 in 8.6% [21]. Apostolakis and Brown noted the difficulty in recognizing the anterior portion of the mandibular nerve on cross-sectional views, especially with low bone density [18]. These findings were lower than those in our study, which utilized oblique slices for better visualization of the mandibular canal, AnL, incisive canal, MF [10].

In our study no significant differences in frequency of AnL between the right and left sides and between males and females were found. Similar to the results of the present study Filo et al. [19], Lu et al. [10] and Sahman and Sisman [22] concluded that neither side of mandible nor sex had an impact on frequency of AnL.

In the present study the frequency of AnL in 20–40 age group was 97.5% while in (41–70) age group was 55.0%. Older subjects may have trouble seeing the AnL since there is less calcification of the cortex as people get older. Bone goes through a variety of quantitative and qualitative changes, although the process of remodeling seems to slow down with age [23]. Cortical porosity and the fraction of Haversian canals resorb both obviously after the age of 50 [24]. This resorption causes the marrow space to widen and the presence of disorganized trabeculae frequently, which makes it difficult to recognize the AnL [25]. As a result of the bone canals becoming radiolucent, this may help to explain why a large majority of people older than 50 did not have the AnL visible.

Solar et al. reported on a study of 37 cadaveric mandibles, finding that the AnLs had a mean length of 1 mm, with lengths ranging from 0.5 to 5 mm [6]. Apostolakis et al. examined 93 patients using CBCT images and found the mean length of AnLs to be 0.89 mm, with a range of 0 to 5.7 mm [18]. In another study by Parnia et al., involving 96 CBCT scans, the average length of the AnL was reported as 3.54 mm [20]. A study conducted in the USA by Lu et al. yielded a different mean length of 1.46 mm [10].

In our analysis of 260 subjects, the mean of AnLL was 2.75 mm, with observed values ranging from 0.6 mm to 6.7 mm. No statistically significant difference in AnLL was detected between the right and left sides of the mandible. These findings align with those reported by Filo et al. [19], Lu et al. [10] and Sahman and Sisman [22], who also concluded that neither mandibular side nor sex significantly influenced AnL length. However, in contrast to those studies, our results demonstrated a statistically significant difference in AnLL based on sex, with males exhibiting a greater mean length (2.99 mm) compared to females (2.55 mm) (P < 0.001). This sex-based variation is in agreement with the findings of Sinha et al. [26], Wei et al. [27], Nascimento et al. [14], and Uchida et al. [28].

Our findings revealed that the AnLL was significantly greater in the 20–40 age group compared to individuals aged 41–70 years. This observation is consistent with the results reported by Lu et al. [10], Ngeow et al. [23], and Uchida et al. [28], who noted a decrease in the visibility and length of the AnL with advancing age. Additionally, the mean AnLL was significantly higher in dentate subjects (2.91 mm) than in edentulous individuals (2.43 mm) (P < 0.001). However, this difference was not supported by the findings of Rosa et al. [29] and Prados-Frutos et al. [30], who reported no statistically significant variation in AnLL between dentate and edentulous populations.

The position of the MF is subject to alteration throughout mandibular development [31]. Gershenson et al. [32] reported that with tooth loss and subsequent alveolar bone resorption, the MF tends to migrate superiorly toward the alveolar crest. As resorption progresses, the MN may exit the MF in closer proximity to, or at, the alveolar margin. Furthermore, following tooth extraction, the MF shifts anteriorly relative to the AnL, resulting in a reduction of the AnLL due to the resorptive changes in the surrounding bone structure [10, 32].

This study analyzed the distance from the MF to the superior border of the mandible X1, revealing significant associations with age, gender and dental status, but no side-specific differences. The mean X1 was 11.91 mm. Consistent with prior studies [33], females exhibited shorter distances (11.36 mm) than males (12.6 mm). A reduced X1 value in edentulous subjects was also observed, aligning with previous findings [34].

Specifically, the X1 value was 13.37 mm in dentate individuals and 10.39 mm in edentulous individuals. This suggests that mandibular edentulism may be linked to specific morphological changes in the mandible, as dental status can influence mandibular shape. Age also significantly affected the X1 distance, with older individuals (10.96 mm) showing shorter distances than younger individuals (13.41 mm). This decrease in X1 within edentate and older age groups is attributed to bone resorption associated with aging or tooth loss. A significant association was found between X1 and the frequency of the AnL. Positional changes of the MF are linked to alveolar bone remodeling, wherein tooth loss and subsequent resorption cause the alveolar crest to shift inferiorly toward the MF. In advanced resorption, the MF and adjacent mandibular canal may become exposed at the alveolar margin [35], potentially reducing both the occurrence and length of the AnL.

The distance from the MF to the inferior border of the mandible X2 averaged 13.56 mm, with no significant variation based on mandibular side or age. In the present study, the mean X2 value was greater in males (14.37 mm) compared to females (12.92 mm). It was related to the fact that mandibles of females were smaller than that of males [36]. We discovered a significant difference in the X2 distance with regard to dental status. Chrcanovic et al. [35] reported a reduction in X2 distance in edentate subjects. These authors explain that presence and absence of teeth has a higher impact on the mandibular morphology than does gender, and that dental loss causes greater alterations in mandibular height than mandibular width dimensions. While the position of the MF varied throughout primary dentition, it largely remained consistent during the eruption of the permanent and mixed dentitions [5]. Because our samples were older than 20 years old, the space between the MF and the inferior border of the mandible remained constant with age.

The distance between MF and mandibular symphysis was observed to be 23.18 mm and showed great values only in males. It was related to the fact that women’s mandibles were smaller than that of men [37]. It was no significant difference between X3 distance and side of the mandible, age and dental state. The distance from MF to midline was constant between different ages according to Guler et al. [38] and Lindh et al. [39]. The surface of the mental protuberance is still used as an area of bone deposition with the total loss of dentition and age (or at least the bone there does not actively resorb), according to Enlow et al. [40], so overall mandibular arch length is not lowered.

A statistically significant association was observed between X1 and both the prevalence and length of the AnL (p ≤ 0.001), as well as between X2 and AnL parameters (p = 0.011). Mandibular morphology is more profoundly affected by dental status, as tooth loss leads to marked reductions in mandibular height [30]. Alveolar bone resorption following edentulism causes the alveolar crest to migrate inferiorly and closer to the MF. In advanced resorption, the MF and adjacent mandibular canal may become exposed at the alveolar margin [35], potentially diminishing both the occurrence and length of the AnL.

Conclusion

The AnL of the MN serves as a critical surgical landmark during preoperative planning for implant placement in the interforaminal region. Given the considerable variability in AnLL and emergence patterns observed in our study, no fixed mesial or anterior distance from the MF can be reliably considered “safe” for avoiding injury to the MN and its associated neurovascular bundle. So, we suggest performing a tridimensional and individual investigation by CBCT in each patient. Using the technique outlined in this article to analyze CBCT images can help prevent complications from implant surgery. In situations where CBCT is not available, previously suggested safety margins (e.g., 7 mm anterior to the MF) should be interpreted with caution, as they may not account for anatomical variations and could increase the risk of iatrogenic injury. It is essential to examine the mandibular, mental and incisive nerves’ three-dimensional locations in addition to the AnL of the MN’s length.
