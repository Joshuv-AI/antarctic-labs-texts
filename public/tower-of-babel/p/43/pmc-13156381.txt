Clinical and Metabolic Characteristics of Spontaneous Remission in Type 2 Diabetes: A Real-World Study from South India
Authors: Saravanan Jebarani, Bhavadharini Balaji, Viswanathan Baskar, Raghavan Bhuvaneswari, Coimbatore Subramanian Shanthi Rani, AnandaPerumal Lavonya, Sadasivam Ganesan, Ananthakrishnan Subramanian Akshay, Manodip Acharyya, Phillips Routray, Ranjit Unnikrishnan, Viswanathan Mohan, Ranjit Mohan Anjana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThis study aimed to evaluate clinical characteristics and outcomes of individuals with type 2 diabetes (T2D) who achieved spontaneous remission without structured intervention.MethodsWe examined electronic medical records of 243,400 adults with T2D attending a tertiary care center in India between 1993 and 2018. Spontaneous remission was defined as achieving glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) < 6.5% for at least 3 months without glucose-lowering medication in those who earlier had HbA1c ≥ 6.5%. A matched group without remission was selected for comparison.ResultsAmong 243,400 individuals with T2D, 275 (0.11%) achieved spontaneous remission. Remission occurred in 0.34% of individuals with baseline HbA1c < 7.0%, 0.13% with HbA1c 7.0–7.9%, and 0.08% with HbA1c ≥ 8.0%. Diabetes recurred in 69%, 80%, and 87% of these respective groups, after a median remission duration of 2.1 years. Sustained remission was observed in 55 individuals (0.02%). Individuals who achieved remission were older, had lower body weight and glucose levels, shorter duration, higher C-peptide, and greater physical activity. Relapse was associated with higher body weight and postprandial glucose levels. Individuals who had longer remission had greater weight loss.ConclusionSpontaneous remission of T2D is rare and mostly transient. Higher baseline HbA1c predicts relapse, while sustained weight loss is strongly associated with long-term remission.Keywords: Spontaneous remission, Asian Indians, Weight loss, Diabetes relapse, Type 2 diabetes

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes (T2D) is a metabolic disorder that has reached epidemic proportions worldwide, affecting an estimated 537 million adults, with projections indicating this number could rise to 783 million by 2045 [1]. In India the Indian Council of Medical Research–India Diabetes (ICMR-INDIAB) study estimates that there are 101 million individuals with diabetes as of 2021 [2]. Traditionally, T2D is characterized by chronic hyperglycemia resulting from insulin resistance and beta cell dysfunction. Standard management has largely focused on pharmacological therapy for glycemic control aimed at delaying the progression of the disease and the onset of complications [3].

Growing evidence over the past decade has challenged the notion that T2D is irreversible. Several landmark trials, including the UK Diabetes Remission Clinical Trial (DiRECT) [4], the Diabetes Intervention Accentuating Diet and Enhancing Metabolism (DIADEM-I) [5, 6], and Look AHEAD (Action for Health in Diabetes) [7], have demonstrated that intensive lifestyle interventions primarily involving significant calorie restriction and weight loss can achieve remission in a substantial proportion of patients. In parallel, the American Diabetes Association (ADA) has defined remission as achieving an HbA1c < 6.5% for at least 3 months without the use of glucose-lowering medications [8]. While this has advanced the conceptual framework for remission, it remains distinct from cure; the underlying metabolic derangements persist, and the risk of relapse is high, particularly with weight regain or progressive beta cell failure [9, 10].

The mechanisms underlying remission appear to involve reductions in ectopic fat in the liver and pancreas, improved insulin sensitivity, and partial restoration of beta cell function [11, 12]. Although these mechanisms are best documented following structured interventions such as bariatric surgery [10] or very low-calorie diets (VLCDs), remission can also occur spontaneously in some individuals, without formal therapeutic intervention. Such spontaneous remission is rare and poorly understood, with limited evidence regarding its clinical profile, metabolic predictors, and durability. Large population-based studies have estimated spontaneous remission rates of 1.6% and 1.7% over 7 years in the USA [13] and England [14], respectively, with slightly higher rates observed in those with recent diagnosis and greater weight loss.

Understanding the characteristics of individuals who achieve spontaneous remission, including those who later relapse, is essential for informing prevention and management strategies. Such insights may guide the development of personalized interventions and help clinicians set realistic expectations around remission and its sustainability.

In this study, we used electronic records to retrospectively analyze the clinical and metabolic profiles of individuals with T2D who achieved spontaneous remission at a large tertiary care diabetes center in India. We aimed to assess the frequency, durability, and predictors of remission and relapse, contributing to the limited real-world evidence on this uncommon, but clinically relevant phenomenon.

Methods

Inclusion Criteria

We retrospectively reviewed electronic records of 243,400 individuals with T2D who presented between 1993 and 2018 at a tertiary diabetes care center in India. These individuals had all relevant clinical and biochemical parameters available at both baseline and follow-up. Mean duration of follow-up was 6.7 ± 5.6 years. From this group, individuals who received glucose-lowering medications on a continuous basis or had glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) levels ≥ 6.5% both at baseline and follow-up (n = 235,655), those with serum creatinine levels, > 1.2 mg/dL (n = 7465), and those who had undergone bariatric surgery (n = 5) were excluded. The remaining 275 individuals who initially had HbA1c ≥ 6.5% and were receiving glucose-lowering medications, but later achieved HbA1c < 6.5% despite being off all glucose-lowering medications for at least 3 months, were included in the study.

An equal number of controls (n = 275) were selected by matching for age, sex, and baseline HbA1c with the spontaneous remission group from the electronic records. Baseline and follow-up biochemical and clinical data were extracted for both groups. Ethics committee approval was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee of Madras Diabetes Research Foundation, reference number MDRF/NCT/07/05/2023. The study included only patients who had previously provided written consent allowing the use of their anonymized medical data for research purposes.

Definitions

A series of anthropometric measurements, including height, weight, and waist circumference, were collected using standardized methods [15]. Height (in centimeters) was measured using a stadiometer (SECA Model 214, Seca GmbH Co, Hamburg, Germany), and weight (in kilograms) was measured using an electronic weighing scale (SECA Model 807, Seca GmbH Co, Hamburg, Germany) placed on a flat horizontal surface. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (in kilograms) divided by height (in meters squared). Waist circumference was measured at the end of expiration at the narrowest horizontal point between the costal margin and the iliac crest. Blood pressure was measured in the right arm in the sitting position using a mercury sphygmomanometer, recorded to the nearest 1 mmHg (Diamond Deluxe, Pune, Maharashtra, India).

Biochemical Parameters

All biochemical analyses were conducted at a laboratory accredited by the College of American Pathologists (CAP) and the National Accreditation Board for Testing and Calibration Laboratories (NABL). Plasma glucose was measured by hexokinase method, serum cholesterol by cholesterol oxidase peroxidase amidopyrine method, serum triglyceride by glycerol phosphate oxidase peroxidase amidopyrine method, and high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol by direct immune inhibition method using Beckman Coulter AU680 (Fullerton, CA, USA) and Beckman kits. Low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol was calculated using the Friedewald formula [16]. HbA1c was determined using high-performance liquid chromatography. Insulin levels and serum glutamic-oxaloacetic transaminase (SGOT)/aspartate aminotransferase (AST), serum glutamic-pyruvic transaminase (SGPT)/alanine aminotransferase (ALT), alkaline phosphatase, and gamma-glutamyl transferase were measured using a Hitachi 912 autoanalyzer using kits provided by Roche Diagnostics GmbH according to standardized procedures.

Fasting and stimulated (post breakfast) C-peptide levels were estimated by the electrochemiluminescence method on an Elecsys 2010 machine (Hitachi). For this, a fasting blood sample was obtained after an overnight fast of at least 8 h and a postprandial sample was obtained after 90 min and ingestion of a standard breakfast of about 250 kcal [17].

Retinopathy

The retinal (fundus) examination was performed by a trained retinal specialist using both direct and indirect ophthalmoscopy. Fundus images were obtained using Carl Zeiss camera, model number FF 450 Plus. Diabetic retinopathy was diagnosed using the Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) grading system, as modified and standardized in prior population-based studies [18].

Nephropathy

Microalbuminuria was defined as an albumin excretion rate between 30 and 299 μg/mg, and macroalbuminuria as an excretion of ≥ 300 μg/mg. Chronic kidney disease (CKD) was defined as an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2, calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equation. Nephropathy was defined as the presence of CKD and/or albuminuria [19].

Neuropathy

Neuropathy was assessed using biothesiometer (Biomedical Instrument Co., Ohio, USA). Vibratory perception threshold (VPT) was measured at the great toes in a standardized manner by a single trained observer, and the bilateral readings were averaged. Neuropathy was defined as VPT ≥ 20 V [20].

Peripheral Vascular Disease (PVD)

PVD was assessed using ankle-brachial pressure index (ABPI) measured with a Doppler probe. An ABPI value < 0.9 was considered diagnostic of PVD [21].

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed with R (version 4.0.4). Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas categorical variables were presented as percentages and analyzed using the chi-square and McNemar test. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant and p value < 0.001 as highly significant. Cohen’s d value was calculated to complement hypothesis testing; d was defined as the mean change from baseline divided by the standard deviation of the paired differences. Effect sizes were interpreted using established thresholds, with values around 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 representing small, moderate, and large effects, respectively, and values greater than 1.2 indicating very large or clinically substantial changes. The 95% confidence intervals (CI) were estimated to characterize the precision of each effect size.

Results

Out of 243,400 individuals with T2D who had longitudinal follow-up data available for a minimum of 2 years, 275 individuals (0.11%) met the criteria for spontaneous remission. Among these, 55 individuals (20%) maintained remission throughout the follow-up period, while 220 (80%) experienced relapses after a mean remission duration of 2.1 years at which point diabetes treatment was resumed.

Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of individuals who experienced spontaneous remission versus those who did not. Those who achieved spontaneous remission were older and had a shorter diabetes duration compared to the no remission group (p < 0.05). Individuals in the spontaneous remission group were more likely to be employed, were less likely to have a family history of diabetes, or to smoke or consume alcohol (p < 0.05), and had a lower mean dietary calorie intake (p < 0.001), compared to the no remission group. Individuals in the spontaneous remission group had slightly higher BMI (p < 0.05) and overall body fat percentage (p < 0.001); however, they had smaller waist circumferences (p < 0.05). Glycemia at baseline was lower among those in spontaneous remission (both p < 0.001) and stimulated C-peptide levels were higher (p < 0.05). A greater proportion of individuals in the spontaneous remission group reported walking for physical activity (p < 0.05). Metformin (p < 0.001) and sulfonylureas (p < 0.05) were more frequently used as initial therapy in the spontaneous remission group (Table 1), while those in the no remission group were more likely to be on insulin and multiple oral glucose-lowering drugs.

Frequency of spontaneous remission varied by baseline HbA1c level. Among individuals with HbA1c < 7.0%, 83 of 24,450 (0.34%) achieved remission. In the 7.0–7.9% group, 66 of 51,114 (0.13%) achieved remission, and in the ≥ 8.0% group, 126 of 167,836 (0.08%) did so. Relapse of diabetes occurred in 69% of individuals with baseline HbA1c < 7.0%, 80% in the 7.0–7.9% group, and 87% in the ≥ 8.0% group. The average time from diabetes diagnosis to remission was 2.7 years, and the mean duration of remission prior to relapse was 2.1 years (Fig. 1).

Flowchart showing the frequency of spontaneous remission and relapsed at various HbA1c levels

Table 2 summarizes the longitudinal changes in clinical characteristics among individuals with sustained remission and those who experienced relapse. In the sustained remission group, there was significant reduction in weight (from 72 ± 14 to 61 ± 12 kg), BMI (from 26.9 ± 4.3 to 23.3 ± 3.7 kg/m2), and waist circumference (from 93 ± 11 to 87 ± 9 cm). Improvements were also observed in blood pressure, with systolic and diastolic BP decreasing by 5 mmHg and 4 mmHg, respectively. Significant reductions were noted in lipid parameters including total cholesterol (− 34 mg/dL), triglycerides (− 44 mg/dL), and LDL cholesterol (− 26 mg/dL), along with an increase in HDL cholesterol (+ 2.2 mg/dL). These changes were accompanied by an improvement in glycemic control, with HbA1c decreasing from 8.6% to 5.7% (70 to 39 mmol/mol), fasting plasma glucose from 140 to 98 mg/dL (7.8 to 5.4 mmol/L), and postprandial glucose from 215 to 128 mg/dL (11.9 to 7.1 mmol/L). The C-peptide levels showed an overall rise during remission compared to baseline, with fasting C-peptide rising by 0.45 pmol/mL and stimulated C-peptide by 0.30 pmol/mL. Caloric intake also declined significantly during remission (− 347 kcal/day).

In the relapse group, mean body weight decreased from 73 ± 15 to 66 ± 13 kg during the remission period, but rebounded to 71 ± 16 kg at relapse. Similar trends were observed for BMI (− 2.5 kg/m2 during remission; − 1.2 kg/m2 at relapse) and waist circumference (− 3 cm during remission; − 0.4 cm at relapse). Glycemic parameters worsened at relapse, with HbA1c rising from 5.9% during remission to 7.2% at relapse, fasting glucose increasing by 24 mg/dL, and postprandial glucose by 63 mg/dL. Likewise fasting C-peptide decreased from 1.21 pmol/mL during remission to 1.05 pmol/mL at relapse and stimulated C-peptide declined from 2.69 to 2.23 pmol/mL. While total and LDL cholesterol remained lower than baseline at relapse, HDL levels declined, and triglycerides increased. Notably, the difference in weight reduction between sustained remission and relapsed groups at follow-up was 9 kg, while the difference in BMI was 2.4 kg/m2 and that of waist circumference was 5.6 cm.

Among the 275 individuals who achieved remission, 42% (n = 116) remained in remission for less than 1 year, 43% (n = 119) for 1–6 years, and 15% (n = 40) for more than 6 years (Fig. 2).

Distribution of remission duration

Of the 275 individuals who achieved remission, 151 met the classification criteria established by Buse et al. [22]. Among these, 78% had partial remission, defined as hyperglycemia below diabetes diagnostic levels (HbA1c < 6.5% for ≥ 1 year), 8% had complete remission characterized by normal glycemic values maintained for at least 1 year without treatment (HbA1c < 5.7% for ≥ 1 year), and 14% had prolonged remission, defined as complete remission sustained for at least 5 years (HbA1c < 5.7% for ≥ 5 years) (Fig. 3).

Classification of remission types using criteria established by Buse et al. [22]

The prevalence of complications was highest in the no remission group, with retinopathy, nephropathy, neuropathy, and PVD observed in 31%, 14%, 25%, and 11% of individuals, respectively (Fig. 4). In contrast, the sustained remission group showed the lowest complication rates (7%, 5%, 5%, and 5%, respectively). The relapse group had intermediate values, with complication rates significantly lower than the no remission group but higher than those who sustained remission.

Diabetes complications across categories of remission

Temporal trends in HbA1c levels differed across the sustained remission, relapse, and no remission groups (Fig. 5a). In the sustained remission group, HbA1c decreased sharply following remission and remained consistently below 6.5% throughout follow-up. The relapse group showed a similar initial decline in HbA1c, but levels increased progressively after relapse, reaching values above 7.0% at final follow-up. In contrast, the no remission group maintained elevated HbA1c levels above 7.0% across all timepoints. Weight trajectories varied distinctly across the sustained remission, relapse, and no remission groups (Fig. 5b). The sustained remission group demonstrated the most pronounced and durable weight loss. In the relapse group, weight initially decreased (− 6.6 kg) during remission, but progressively increased over time. The no remission group showed minimal weight change.

Because statistically significant differences may not always reflect the magnitude or clinical importance of change, Cohen’s d was used to quantify the effect size of changes in key parameters across the sustained remission, no remission, and relapsed groups. Cohen’s d showed clinically meaningful changes in the sustained remission group, with very large effects for weight (1.68), BMI (1.71), HbA1c (1.41), caloric intake (1.40), and fasting C-peptide (2.68), and large effects for postprandial glucose (0.90). The relapse group demonstrated small-to-moderate improvements during remission consistent with partial weight regain and worsening glycemia over time. The no remission group showed uniformly small effects across parameters, indicating minimal metabolic change (Table 3).

Discussion

This large, real-world analysis of over 243,400 individuals with T2D offers important insights into the clinical profile, timing, and sustainability of spontaneous remission in Asian Indians. Remission was rare, occurring in only 0.11% of the cohort and typically within 2.7 years of diagnosis. Individuals who achieved remission were more likely to be older with lower baseline body weight and adiposity, lower glucose levels, shorter diabetes duration, higher C-peptide, have greater engagement in physical activity; they were also less likely to be on insulin therapy. Those who achieved remission even temporarily had lower risk of complications including retinopathy, nephropathy, neuropathy, and PVD compared to those who did not. However, remission was often short-lived, with 80% of individuals in spontaneous remission experiencing relapse, typically after a median duration of 2.1 years, with weight gain and increased body fat being key drivers of glycemic deterioration. These findings highlight both the rarity and instability of spontaneous remission and underscore the need for ongoing metabolic monitoring and lifestyle interventions. Although the remission group showed slightly higher BMI, their smaller waist circumference indicates lower central adiposity, a phenotype associated with better insulin sensitivity. This metabolically healthier fat distribution may help explain why these individuals were more likely to achieve remission.

While remission is increasingly recognized as an achievable goal in T2D management, our findings provide important real-world context compared to data from structured intervention trials. In contrast to our low remission rate, population-based studies such as the Scottish registry analysis [23] reported a remission prevalence of 4.8%, particularly among individuals with older age, lower HbA1c at diagnosis, no glucose-lowering medication use (OR 14.6), or a history of bariatric surgery (OR 11.9), suggesting that early diagnosis and lower disease severity may improve the likelihood of remission. Similarly, data from the National Diabetes Audit in England [14] reported a remission incidence of 9.7 per 1000 person-years in a cohort of 2.3 million people with T2D, with higher remission rates (44.9 per 1000 PY) among individuals diagnosed within the past year who achieved significant weight loss of ≥ 10% with minimal medication use. These findings suggest that spontaneous remission tends to occur in a small subset of individuals with T2D with favorable clinical profiles. Our study adds to this evidence by providing data from a South Asian population, which has distinct obesity phenotypes and metabolic risk profiles compared to white European populations. Prior work has shown that among Asian Indians, β-cell dysfunction is a central driver of early-onset diabetes, even more so than insulin resistance, highlighting intrinsic deficits in insulin secretory capacity that may limit the durability of remission [24].

Structured lifestyle interventions, particularly those involving intensive weight management, have demonstrated much higher remission rates than those observed in routine care [4, 25]. In the DiRECT trial [26], nearly half of participants (46%) achieved remission after 12 months, and more than one-third (36%) maintained remission at 2 years, with success strongly linked to the degree of weight loss, particularly among those who lost 10 kg or more. Real-world data from the NHS Type 2 Diabetes Path to Remission Programme [27] showed more modest remission rates of 27% to 32%, despite being delivered at national scale through primary care in England. Participants achieving remission lost an average of 14.8 kg (13.4% body weight), comparable to trial results, although overall programme completion and data capture were lower compared to controlled trial settings [27].

A meta-analysis by Jayedi et al. [28] confirmed that calorie restriction diets results in 38 more remissions per 100 individuals at 6 months and 13 more remissions per 100 individuals at 12 months when compared to usual diet or usual care. Each 500 kcal/day reduction in intake results in approximately 6 kg weight loss and a 0.8% reduction in HbA1c at 6 months [28]. Extending this evidence beyond calorie restriction alone, a recent systematic review and meta-analysis of nonsurgical randomized controlled trials by Sherifali et al. [29] demonstrated a significantly higher likelihood of achieving remission with structured interventions, particularly nonpharmacological and multimodal programs, which were also associated with improvements in HbA1c, weight, quality of life, and hypoglycemia risk. The twin cycle hypothesis [12] has been introduced to explain these effects, showing that reductions in liver and pancreatic fat improve hepatic insulin sensitivity and beta cell function. However, sustaining these outcomes remains a challenge. In one study [30] of Chinese adults with early T2D, nearly 39% of participants maintained remission for almost 8 years following a short-term VLCD intervention. However, weight regain is a key factor in relapse, with up to 25% of individuals relapsing within 2 years despite initial success [10, 31]. Therefore, while spontaneous remission is biologically achievable, the behavioral and clinical support available in trial settings significantly influences an individual’s ability to achieve and sustain the necessary weight loss—support that was notably absent in the real-world setting of our study.

Pharmacologic therapies have also shown increasing promise. Glucagon-like peptide 1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists and dual incretin agonists such as tirzepatide achieved normoglycemia in over 80% of clinical trial participants [32]. Combination therapies, including dapagliflozin and orally administered semaglutide, resulted in 55% of patients reaching near-normal HbA1c levels [33], and dapagliflozin paired with calorie restriction achieved 44% remission [34]. Short-term use of degludec/liraglutide [35] and glargine/lixisenatide combinations [36] has demonstrated reductions in relapse risk and short-term glycemic improvements, particularly when initiated early. Despite these promising results, the long-term durability of remission after discontinuing these therapies is still unknown.

Bariatric surgery remains the most reliable intervention for durable remission, with remission rates exceeding 70% in studies of Roux-en-Y gastric bypass and sleeve gastrectomy [37, 38]. Various surgical procedures, including one-anastomosis gastric bypass, appear to achieve comparable outcomes, although higher complication risks and limited access to surgical services remain concerns [39], including among older adults [40]. Sustained glycemic control has been observed for up to 6 years in individuals maintaining approximately 20% weight loss [41]. However, given its limited scalability, access and morbidity risks, surgery is not a population-wide solution, reinforcing the need for affordable, effective nonsurgical strategies in line with current clinical guidelines [42].

In our study, 80% of individuals who initially achieved spontaneous remission eventually relapsed, highlighting the difficulty of maintaining long-term normoglycemia without structured support. Other studies have similarly reported that relapse is common, even in the context of intensive lifestyle programmes or pharmacologic interventions, with remission rates in trials such as Look AHEAD trial [7] and those reported by Wu et al. [43] declining over time as weight is regained and glycemic control deteriorates. Together, these data emphasize that remission should be viewed as a dynamic state that requires continued monitoring and support to be sustained.

Remission does not eliminate cardiometabolic risk although it may reduce the risk due to the “good metabolic memory” achieved. This is particularly relevant in South Asia, where CVD is the leading cause of death and where hypertension, diabetes, abdominal obesity, and other modifiable factors together account for the majority of CVD and mortality risk [44]. Data from Denmark [45] suggest that individuals achieving remission without pharmacologic therapy may be at higher risk of cardiovascular events, potentially due to reduced use of cardioprotective medications such as statins or renin–angiotensin system inhibitors. In contrast, the Look AHEAD study [7] showed that participants who sustained remission for at least 4 years experienced significantly lower risks of microvascular complications and all-cause mortality, independent of medication use or baseline characteristics. A large Japanese cohort [46] also showed a 29% cardiovascular risk reduction among individuals achieving remission, independent of weight loss.

Despite growing recognition of remission as a clinical goal, healthcare professionals have expressed concerns about the risk of patient disappointment following relapse and the difficulty of sustaining behavior change without structured, ongoing support. Qualitative research from Scotland [47] highlighted concerns about patient disappointment following relapse and the challenges of sustaining behavior change without structured follow-up. Positive patient–provider relationships, characterized by empathetic communication and patient-centered care, have been associated with better remission outcomes, emphasizing the critical role of primary care in supporting lifestyle change [48].

Global research trends reflect growing interest in weight loss, bariatric surgery, and VLCDs as strategies for diabetes remission, but gaps remain in developing scalable, long-term solutions. However, as illustrated by the contrast between structured trials, national programmes, and the real-world findings of the present study, major gaps remain in translating these approaches into scalable, durable care models that can be effectively implemented in routine clinical practice. Addressing this gap remains a critical priority for healthcare systems seeking to make diabetes remission a realistic and sustainable goal.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several strengths. By leveraging data from over 243,400 individuals, it represents one of the largest real-world analyses of spontaneous remission of T2D to date. Unlike previous research that has primarily focused on remission achieved through structured interventions such as VLCDs, pharmacotherapy, or bariatric surgery, this study provides insights into remission occurring in routine clinical practice without formal lifestyle or surgical programs. This adds valuable context regarding the frequency and durability of spontaneous remission in a real-world setting. Additionally, the study was done on a large South Asian cohort which is known to have distinct metabolic characteristics and a higher risk of early-onset T2D compared to Western populations [49]. The ability to track both initial remission and subsequent relapse, observed in the majority of individuals who achieved remission, further strengthens the study by highlighting the challenges of sustaining long-term glycemic control outside of structured intervention programs.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. The retrospective design limits the ability to draw causal conclusions about the factors contributing to remission or relapse. The study lacks detailed information on behavioral and lifestyle factors, such as dietary habits, physical activity, and weight management, which are known to influence remission outcomes and often differ by level of urbanization in low- and middle-income settings [50]. Remission was defined based on HbA1c levels and medication discontinuation, without confirmation through fasting glucose or oral glucose tolerance testing, which may have led to misclassification in some cases. The study included only those with diabetes who were on glucose-lowering drugs at baseline. This could potentially exclude a group of individuals who may have higher chance of spontaneous remission. This study did not include sex-specific analyses as overall numbers with spontaneous remission were too small for subanalysis. Finally, as the data were derived from individuals managed in a specialized diabetes care center, the findings may not be fully generalizable to populations receiving care in primary or community-based settings.

Conclusion

In this large, real-world analysis of individuals with T2D from a South Asian population, spontaneous remission was found to be both rare and typically short-lived. Only 0.11% of individuals achieved remission without structured intervention, and the majority of these eventually relapsed over time. However, even temporary remission had advantageous effects on micro- and macrovascular complications. These findings provide important context to emphasize the need for sustainable strategies to achieve and maintain long-term remission of diabetes.
