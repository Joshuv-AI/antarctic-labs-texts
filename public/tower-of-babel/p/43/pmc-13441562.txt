Multi-Omics Analysis Reveals Abnormal Metabolism of Lysophosphatidylinositol in Nasal Polyp
Authors: Jieying Lie, Zhaofeng Xu, Hangtian Zhang, Xiang Liu, Hua Dang, Hua Zou, Peng Tian, Hua Zhong, Yun Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeChronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps (CRSwNP) is an inflammatory disease in which eosinophil (Eos) infiltration into polyp tissues is associated with disease prognosis. Our previous study found that linoleic acid is an important differential metabolite. However, lipid metabolism in nasal polyps has been insufficiently investigated. This study aimed to explore the characteristics of lipid metabolism in CRSwNP tissue.MethodsThis study employed a multi-omics approach by integrating lipidomics and single-cell transcriptomics. The lipidomics analysis was performed using nasal mucosal tissues obtained from 32 patients with nasal polyps and 20 healthy controls. The sequencing findings were validated by using immunofluorescence staining and Western blotting.ResultsPhosphatidylethanolamine and phosphatidylcholine were the most abundant lipids expressed in the sinus mucosa, and lysophosphatidylinositol (LPI) was significantly increased in the eosinophilic CRSwNP group compared to the noneosinophilic CRSwNP and the control groups. The LPI levels showed significant positive correlations with the Eos counts and percentages in nasal polyp tissue. The expressions of membrane-bound O-acyltransferase 7 and receptor G-protein coupled receptor 55 in the LPI metabolic pathway were significantly elevated in eosinophilic nasal polyps, and their expression was mainly localized to Eos in polyp tissues.ConclusionsThere are differences in the lipidomic patterns between healthy individuals and patients with CRSwNP. The LPI metabolic axis may be associated with Eos activation in polyp tissue. These findings could provide new insights into the pathogenesis and endotyping of CRSwNP.Keywords: Rhinosinusitis, eosinophils, lipidomics, nasal polyps, lysophosphatidylinositol

INTRODUCTION

Chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS) is a chronic inflammatory disease of the nasal and sinus mucosa characterized by symptoms such as rhinorrhea, nasal obstruction, headache, and olfactory dysfunction.1 Previous epidemiological studies have reported that CRS affects approximately 8% of the population in China,2 9.9% in Europe,3 and 6.7%–19.7% in other countries and regions worldwide. The prevalence of CRS has increased in recent decades, which has imposed an increasing burden on socioeconomic development and public health.

CRS can be divided into CRS without nasal polyps (NPs) and CRS with nasal polyps (CRSwNP). Furthermore, based on eosinophil (Eos) infiltration into NP tissue, CRSwNP can be subdivided into noneosinophilic CRSwNP (non-Eos NP) and eosinophilic CRSwNP (Eos NP), with these endotypes showing different clinical manifestations, treatment outcomes, and cellular and immunopathological characteristics.1 Eos NP is characterized by more refractory polyps, a higher incidence of comorbid asthma, more severe symptoms, a worse response to treatment, and a higher risk of recurrence compared to non-Eos NP.5 Therefore, preoperative differentiation between non-Eos NP and Eos NP is important for making optimal treatment decisions. Moreover, treatment selection and prognosis could be improved by identifying an objective biomarker or indicator to preoperatively distinguish the CRSwNP endotypes.

Lipids are important constituents of metabolites and play important roles in physiological processes including structural building blocks, energy storage, hormone precursors, and cell signaling molecules. Recently, studies have found that lipids are also involved in inflammatory and immune-mediated diseases, such as asthma.6 However, the role of lipid metabolism in CRS remains poorly understood.

Lipidomics facilitates the identification of differential lipid metabolites as phenotypic features in NP tissues, whereas single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) enables the precise characterization of the specific cell types and gene expression signatures that mediate these metabolic alterations. This integration provides a comprehensive view of the “metabolite-gene-cell” interaction network, which cannot be achieved by either method alone. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to explore abnormalities in lipid metabolism in NP tissues and their relation to polyp endotypes by integrating lipidomics and scRNA-seq multi-omics analyses.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patients and samples

This study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Sun Yat-Sen Memorial Hospital of Sun Yat-Sen University (SYSKY-2023-1304-01). Thirty-two patients diagnosed with CRSwNP and 20 healthy controls were recruited from the Department of Otorhinolaryngology at Sun Yat-Sen Memorial Hospital. Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

The clinical characteristics of all participants are shown in Table 1. CRSwNP was diagnosed and classified into non-Eos NP and Eos NP according to the European Position Paper on rhinosinusitis and NP 2020.1 The control group consisted of patients who underwent septoplasty and partial inferior turbinectomy. Exclusion criteria for the study were systemic diseases, acute upper respiratory tract infection, genetic defects, use of antibiotics within 2 weeks, or oral glucocorticoids within 4 weeks of enrollment. Inferior turbinate mucosa from control participants and NP tissues from CRSwNP patients were obtained during surgery. Visual analog scale scores of nasal symptoms, Lund-Kennedy endoscopic scores, and Lund-Mackay computed tomography (CT) scores were collected before the operation.

Untargeted lipidomics

Lipids were extracted from tissue samples and analyzed by ultra-high performance liquid chromatography, as described in the Supplementary Data S1.

scRNA-seq analysis

Our previously published scRNA-seq data and a scRNA-seq dataset (HRA000772) from the Genome Sequence Archive (GSA) database were analyzed.7 Details are provided in the Supplementary Data S1.

Histological staining

Histological staining was performed on paraffin-embedded tissue sections. Lipid deposition in tissues was observed by using Oil Red O staining. Immunostaining was performed on NPs, with details provided in the Supplementary Data S1.

Cell culture and transfection

Eol-1 cell lines were cultured in RPMI medium 1640. Transfection was performed in Eol-1 cell lines 24 hours before stimulation, and the transfection efficiency is shown in Supplementary Fig. S1. Details are provided in Supplementary Data S1.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were described as mean ± standard deviation and analyzed using Student’s t-test, one-way analysis of variance, or Wilcoxon rank-sum test, depending on whether the variables were normally distributed. Categorical data were presented as count and percentage, and compared using the χ2 test. Spearman’s correlation analysis was used to assess correlations between lipid classes and Eos levels. A value of P < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software for Mac (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA).

A total of 32 lipid classes and 1,095 lipid species were identified from all samples. Details of the quality control are shown in the Supplementary Data S1 (Supplementary Fig. S2). First, analysis was performed at the lipid class level. The numbers of lipid species in each lipid class are shown in Fig. 1B. The most abundant lipid classes were phosphatidylcholine (PC), phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), and ceramides (Cer). The differential expression of different classes of lipids in NP tissues and tissues from the control group were analyzed. Compared to the control group, NP tissues exhibited significantly decreased levels of sphingomyelin (SM), CerG3NAc1, triglyceride, phosphatidylinositol (PI) bisphosphate, PE, and PI phosphate, while CerG2 expression was significantly upregulated (Fig. 1C). The correlation heatmap of lipid class showed strong positive correlations between the expression levels of PE, phosphatidylserine, PC and PI (r > 0.95, P < 0.0001) (Fig. 1D).

Analysis at the lipid species level was then performed, initially using multivariate analyses. Based on the orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) score plot for lipid species, the NP and control groups were separated along the x-axis (Fig. 2A). The main parameters in this model were R2X = 0.653, R2Y = 0.95, and Q2 = 0.832, suggesting that the model had a good fit. The permutation test revealed no overfitting in the original model (Fig. 2B).

Lysophosphatidylinositol (LPI) and Eos in NPs

As previously mentioned, CRSwNP can be divided into different subtypes based on Eos infiltration in NPs; thus, next differences in lipidomics between NP subtypes were examined. LPI was the only lipid class that significantly distinguished the two NP subtypes, showing a significant increase in the Eos NP group compared to both non-Eos NP (P = 0.0088) and control groups (P = 0.0004) (Fig. 3A).

LPI has 3 different species: LPI (18:0), LPI (16:0), and LPI (18:1). Among them, LPI (18:0) (Eos NP vs. control, P = 0.0098; Eos NP vs. non-Eos NP, P = 0.0006) and LPI (16:0) (Eos NP vs. control, P = 0.0022; Eos NP vs. non-Eos NP, P = 0.0003) had significantly higher expression of Eos NP than control and non-Eos NP tissues (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Fig. S3). The correlation between LPI levels and Eos was then examined. The LPI class level exhibited a significant positive correlation with the Eos percentage (r = 0.6264, P = 0.0001) and count (r = 0.5143, P = 0.0026) in NP tissues (Fig. 3C and D). At the lipid species level, both LPI (18:0) and LPI (16:0) exhibited a significant positive correlation with the Eos percentage and count, but LPI (18:1) only showed a weak positive correlation with the Eos percentage (r = 0.3627, P = 0.0413) and no significant correlation with Eos count (r = 0.2759, P = 0.1263). However, there was no significant correlation of LPIs with Eos count and percentage in peripheral blood (Fig. 3E and F). Furthermore, LPI levels had no significant correlations with Sino-Nasal Outcome Test 22, total immunoglobulin E (IgE) level, Lund-Mackay CT score, and Lund-Kennedy endoscopy score (Fig. 3G-J).

LPI metabolic axis is activated in Eos of NPs

LPI is a bioactive lipid, which can be converted to PI by membrane-bound O-acyltransferase 7 (MBOAT7) after acylation with arachidonic acid (AA)-CoA by acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain 3 (ACSL3). LPI can also undergo transmembrane transport via ATP-binding cassette subfamily C member 1 (ABCC1), and then bind to its receptor, G-protein-coupled receptor 55 (GPR55). The LPI/GPR55 axis signals through Gαq and Gα12/13 proteins to activate the RhoA/ROCK and MAPK signaling pathways, respectively. It then acts through transcription factors such as activating transcription factor 2, nuclear factor of activated T cells 1 (NFATc1), cAMP responsive element binding protein, nuclear factor kappa B and ETS variant transcription factor 4 (ETV4) (Fig. 4A).

We re-analyzed our previously published scRNA-seq data and found that the expression levels of GPR55 and ACSL3 were extremely low in NP polyp tissues. MBOAT7 expression was mainly localized in mast cells and myeloid cells, and ABCC1 expression was mainly localized in mast cells (Supplementary Fig. S4A). Both MBOAT7 and ABCC1 expression at all cellular levels was significantly higher in NP tissues than in inferior turbinate tissues (Supplementary Fig. S4B); however, at the mast cell level, MBOAT7 was the only gene whose expression was significantly higher in NP tissues (Supplementary Fig. S4C). Considering that our database does not characterize NPs on the basis of Eos, we analyzed a publicly available single-cell sequencing database of NPs (GSA HRA000772). The major cell clusters in the nasal tissue from HRA000772 and the feature plot are showed in Fig. 4B and C. The analysis showed that MBOAT7 was predominantly expressed in mast cells and granulocytes, ABCC1 was highly expressed in mast cells, and ACSL3 was highly expressed in granulocytes (Fig. 4B-D). The expression levels of MBOAT7 in mast cells was significantly higher in the Eos NP group than in the healthy control group (P < 0.0001), CRSsNP group (P < 0.0001), and the non-Eos NP group (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 4E). The MBOAT7 gene showed low expression in granulocytes across all subgroups in the scRNA-seq data, with a median expression value of 0 in each subgroup (Fig. 4F). To understand the biological reality behind this, we performed the multi-color immunofluorescence validation (Supplementary Fig. S4D). These results revealed that MBOAT7 is primarily expressed in Eos, rather than neutrophils, in NPs.

We first verified the expression of LPI metabolism-related proteins in NPs by immunohistochemistry. We found that the expression levels of MBOAT7 and GPR55 proteins were significantly higher in Eos NP compared to non-Eos NP, and localized to inflammatory cells infiltrated in the polyp mesenchyme. ACSL3 protein expression was significantly increased in the epithelium of Eos NP, whereas ABCC1 protein expression was low in NPs and did not differ significantly between Eos NP and non-Eos NP (Fig. 5A and B). Since the predominant granulocytes in Eos NP tissue are Eos, we labeled Eos with Eos cationic protein and mast cells with mast cell tryptase. The analysis showed that MBOAT7 expression is higher in Eos NP that in non-Eos NP (Fig. 5C and D). Co-localization analysis showed that MBOAT7 was predominantly localized to Eos not neutrophils (Supplementary Fig. S4D) in Eos NP, and localized to both Eos and mast cells in non-Eos NP (Fig. 5E).

We further validated the role of the LPI/GPR55 axis and its downstream pathway in NPs. By immunostaining, we found that GPR55 protein was highly expressed in Eos NP and was mainly localized to Eos in NP tissues (Fig. 6A-C). In vitro experiments showed that the Eol-1 cell line exhibited up-regulation of p-ERK1/2 (mean difference [MD], 0.3363; 95% CI, 0.1052–0.5674; P = 0.0071) (Fig. 6D and E) and p-MEK1/2 (MD, 0.3026; 95% CI, 0.0015–0.6038; P = 0.0489), and feedback down-regulation of RhoA (MD, −0.3416; 95% CI, −0.5299, −0.1532; P = 0.0018) and ROCK (MD, −0.1971; 95% CI −0.3787, −0.0156; P = 0.0341) upon activation by interleukin 5 (IL-5; 10 ng/mL) and granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF; 10 ng/mL) for 48 hours (Fig. 6F and G). After transfection and GPR55 knockdown by si-RNA, this difference disappeared (Fig. 6D-G). This suggested that LPI/GPR55 may act through both the RhoA/ROCK and MAPK signaling pathways. Next, we screened the transcription factors downstream of the 2 signaling pathways by qPCR and found that IL-5 + GM-CSF stimulated the increase of mRNA expression of NFATc1 (MD, 0.2333; 95% CI, 0.0522–0.4145; P = 0.0232) and ETV4 (MD, 0.2833; 95% CI, 0.0280–0.5386; P = 0.0369) in Eol-1 cells (Fig. 6H). Western blotting verified that the protein levels of NFATc1 (MD, 0.4163; 95% CI, 0.0176–0.8150; P = 0.0432) and ETV4 (MD, 0.1698; 95% CI, 0.0058–0.3339; P = 0.0437) showed significant elevation after IL-5 + GM-CSF stimulation in Eol-1 cells (Fig. 6I and J).

DISCUSSION

CRSwNP is an upper airway inflammatory disease4 that can be classified as non-Eos NP and Eos NP depending on Eos infiltration in polyp tissue.1 Eos NP involves type 2 cytokines,8 and it is sensitive to systemic glucocorticoids.9 Furthermore, Eos NP is more likely to be associated with comorbid asthma, high postoperative recurrence rate, and refractory CRS.10 However, the pathogenesis of eosinophilic sinusitis remains unclear. Metabolomics can directly and accurately reflect the physiological state of organisms, and additionally it closely reflects endotype.11 In our previous study of untargeted metabolomics, we found that linoleic acid, a lipid metabolite, contributed the most to distinguishing between the healthy control, non-Eos NP, and Eos NP groups. This led us to further investigate lipid metabolism in NPs.

We identified differentially expressed 7 lipid classes and 89 lipid species between the control and NP groups, which suggests that the pattern of lipid metabolism is altered in NP tissues. In our previous study, we reported several differentially expressed lipid molecules using untargeted metabolomics, and this study provided a more in-depth analysis of lipid metabolism.

The most abundant lipids were members of the PE and PC. Moreover, PC (34:2) + H, PC (34:1) + Na, and PE (16:0e/22:6) − H showed the highest VIP values in the OPLS-DA model of the NP compared to control group. The glycerophospholipids PC and PE are widely present in the membrane structure of eukaryotic cells. They play a role in membrane-mediated signaling that regulates physiological processes such as cell differentiation, proliferation, and apoptosis. PC is also a major component of pulmonary surfactant. Elevated PC levels in airway surfactants in asthmatics are believed to be associated with limited lung capacity.12 Furthermore, oxidized PCs may contribute to asthma pathobiology by inducing a pro-inflammatory phenotype and promoting airway remodeling.13 In the airway epithelium, PE can be oxidized to 15-OOH-eicosatetraenoic acid-esterified PE, which can exacerbate asthma by triggering ferroptosis.14 The lipid with the greatest difference between normal sinus tissues and NP tissues was SM. Lower concentrations of SM are associated with increased airway resistance and an increased risk of developing asthma in children.15 Our findings with respect to PC and PE are similar to those of a previous CRS study in Canada,16 but that study did not report differences in SM, which may be due to differences in ethnicity or sample size.

LPI was the only lipid species differentiating Eos NP from non-Eos NP. This is quite different from the differential lipid species between the control and NP groups, suggesting that the lipids involved in polyp formation and those involved in eosinophilic inflammation are different. LPI is a bioactive lipid produced by the phospholipase A lipase family. It is thought to play a significant role in various physiological and pathological processes. Evidence suggests that a high LPI level in serum is involved in fat deposition.17 MBOAT7 encodes lysophosphatidylinositol acyltransferase (LPIAT) 1, and it is involved in Land’s cycle by remodeling the acyl-chain of phospholipids. MBOAT7 integrates AA and other polyunsaturated fatty acids into LPI and other lysophospholipids.18 Unlike previous studies, our study is the first to report altered metabolism of LPI in NPs without being limited to lipid metabolism of AA. GPR55 was thought to be the main receptor of LPI, and recent studies suggested a possible role of the LPI/GPR55 axis in the inflammatory system.19 Our findings further focused on the role of the LPI metabolic axis on NPs, and immunofluorescence co-localization showed that MBOAT7 and GPR55 were highly expressed in Eos NP and were mainly localized to Eos and some mast cells. However, we noticed that the expression level of LPI varies considerably among patients with Eos NP. We propose three potential reasons for this variability: First, as shown in Fig. 3C, LPI levels are positively correlated with the Eos count. It is possible that the specific case with lower LPI had a relatively lower Eos infiltration burden compared to the other four highly inflamed cases, even though it met the diagnostic criteria for Eos NP. Second, LPI levels are regulated not only by local inflammatory enzymes, such as Phospholipase A2 and LPIAT, but also by systemic lipid metabolism parameters, including body mass index and hyperlipidemia.20 Variations in the patients’ systemic metabolic status could contribute to the observed differences in local tissue LPI. Third, the secretion of LPI involves multiple cell types, including macrophages and mast cells. Individual differences in the immune microenvironment composition may lead to differential LPI production.21

Mast cells and Eos are essential components of allergic inflammation and express many of the same receptors and cytokines.22 However, they have different effector functions. Binding of IgE to mast cells triggers an immediate hypersensitivity reaction, while Eos are granulocytes and play essential roles in sustaining inflammation.23 Therefore, the roles played by mast cells and Eos in Eos NP and non-Eos NP may not be fully consistent, which may explain the differences in the expression of MBOAT7 in mast cells in Eos NP and non-Eos NP. However, both mast cells and Eos have been shown to be closely related to lipid metabolism. The immediate response stimulated by mast cells is determined by pre-formed mediators and rapidly synthesized lipid mediators. Meanwhile, Eos contain a lipid body and produce pro-inflammatory lipid mediators such as leukotrienes.24

Our study revealed that MBOAT7 and GPR55 are predominantly localized in Eos within Eos NP, whereas they are expressed in both Eos and mast cells in non-Eos NP. This observation suggests that mast cells may exert distinct roles in different subtypes of NPs. Mast cells have long been recognized to be involved in type 2 inflammation, but recent studies have demonstrated that a subepithelial population of CD38highCD117high mast cell, rather than all mast cell populations, can promote eosinophilic inflammation in NPs.25 Furthermore, bioactive lipid LPI can also exert regulatory effects on mast cells. LPI can induce significant effects on mast cell actin cytoskeleton remodeling, cytokine mRNA expression and cell migration, by differentially activating GPR55.26

The LPI/GPR55 axis can function through the RhoA/ROCK and MAPK pathways, and it has been reported that the Gα12/13-RhoA-ROCK pathway is involved in LPI-induced cell rounding, while the Gαq- MAPK pathways are largely involved in Ca2+ mobilization.27 We verified that Eos activation stimulates both RhoA/ROCK and MAPK signaling pathways, and further acts through transcription factors such as NFATc1 and ETV4.

This study also has certain limitations. Because of the technical constraints, we used Eol-1 cells rather than primary Eos isolated from NP tissues in the in vitro assays. The underlying mechanism governing mast cell function also remains to be elucidated. And in line with ethical requirements, we used inferior turbinate tissues as controls following the methodology reported previously,28 which may be associated with potential regional heterogeneity.

In conclusion, the lipidomic patterns of local tissues in the control group and the NP group are different. PE and PC are the most abundant lipids expressed in the sinus mucosa and contribute the most to distinguishing the 2 groups. The LPI metabolic axis may be associated with Eos infiltration in NP tissues. These findings provide new insights into the endotype and pathogenesis of CRSwNP.
