A two-stage cascaded purification framework for backdoored object detectors via backdoor feature suppression
Authors: Lihui Xia, Lu Zhao, Junjie Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObject detectors have been widely deployed in safety-critical applications such as surveillance, autonomous driving, and industrial vision. However, stealthy backdoor poisoning during training can induce systematic abnormal outputs under a trigger condition (e.g., object disappearance or spurious object generation) while preserving apparently normal performance on clean inputs, posing severe yet hard-to-detect security risks. Compared with image classification, object detection features multi-branch architectures and tightly coupled multi-term losses, which make backdoor representations more prone to diffuse across layers/channels and become stubbornly entangled with benign detection features. As a result, existing defenses often struggle to simultaneously achieve effective backdoor suppression and model utility preservation. We observe that detection backdoors typically rely on a small set of trigger-sensitive channels that form severable structural pathways; nevertheless, even after the dominant pathways are removed, the trigger–malicious-behavior association may persist as residual coupled features in the parameter space, leading to backdoor re-activation1. Motivated by these findings, we propose a two-stage cascaded purification framework for object detection, termed DAPS–PCMD. In Stage I, DAPS (Differential-Activation guided Path-Severing Pruning) localizes highly toxic channels via stability-enhanced differential activation statistics and iteratively prunes them to physically sever the primary trigger pathways, producing a channel-level structural prior. In Stage II, PCMD (Prior-Constrained Residual Feature Decoupling) performs targeted decoupling and effective suppression of residual trigger associations under the prior constraint, while suppressing utility degradation via clean detection anchors. Extensive experiments and ablations demonstrate that explicitly decomposing purification into a cascade of structural path severing and residual feature decoupling is key to achieving a superior security–utility trade-off across diverse attack types and strengths, stably suppressing the Attack Success Rate (ASR) to 0.008–0.038 while maintaining the clean mAP at 0.837–0.862. Compared to the baseline RNP, our method further achieves a relative ASR reduction of 78.9%–92.1%.Keywords: Backdoor defense, Object detection, Model purification, Channel pruning, Unlearning, Security–utility trade-offSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Object detection has become one of the most fundamental perceptual capabilities in modern computer vision systems, driven by the rapid progress of deep learning2,3. It has been widely deployed in safety-critical applications such as autonomous driving, intelligent surveillance, industrial inspection, and smart healthcare2,3. In these scenarios, a detector not only decides whether an object exists, but also determines where it is and whether downstream decisions should be triggered. Therefore, backdoor vulnerabilities in object detectors can directly compromise system reliability and narrow the operational safety margin4,5.

Unfortunately, the data-driven nature of deep neural networks also exposes object detectors to severe backdoor threats5. By injecting only a small fraction of trigger-embedded samples during training, an attacker can induce the detector to produce malicious behaviors once the trigger is present, such as systematic missed detections of critical objects (Object Disappearance) or spurious detections of attacker-specified categories (Object Generation)4,6–8. More stealthily, a backdoored detector often preserves near-normal mean Average Precision (mAP) on standard clean test sets, making conventional validation-based evaluation ineffective for risk exposure4,5. This “cleanly normal but triggeredly malicious” characteristic renders backdoor attacks particularly hazardous in safety-critical deployments4,5,8.

To mitigate such threats, a broad spectrum of backdoor defenses has been developed, mostly originating from image classification5. These defenses can be broadly categorized as: (i) input purification, which attempts to detect and weaken triggers at the preprocessing stage5,9,10; (ii) model reconstruction, e.g., Fine-Pruning, which removes neurons with low activation on clean inputs to suppress backdoor pathways11; and (iii) trigger inversion with unlearning, e.g., Neural Cleanse, which reverse-engineers potential trigger patterns and removes them via purification/unlearning9,12. Recently, a few studies have attempted to extend these ideas to object detection by designing branch-specific strategies for either the classification head or the bounding-box regression head13–15. However, directly transferring classification defenses to object detection is often unstable and suffers from several fundamental limitations4,14.

First, detection outputs are structured predictions over multiple objects and locations16. Triggers are typically local and condition-dependent, which makes defenses based on full-image purification or global statistics prone to missing stealthy backdoors4,6. Second, detection training couples multiple loss components under severe positive–negative imbalance16; isolation-and-unlearning mechanisms based on fixed thresholds or global loss statistics can easily misclassify hard clean samples as suspicious, leading to training instability and noticeable performance degradation12,14. Third, existing defenses tend to treat the structural and parametric characteristics of backdoors in a separated manner5. Pure structural pruning (e.g., Fine-Pruning) often fails to decouple the residual backdoor features dispersed in the remaining parameters, resulting in backdoor relapse4,11. Conversely, parameter-space unlearning without structural priors (e.g., global fine-tuning) is frequently insufficient to disrupt the dominant trigger pathways and can severely damage multi-scale localization features, causing substantial mAP drops12,17. Taken together, object detection backdoor defense calls for a fine-grained and controllable purification framework that can better balance model security and model utility5,15.

To this end, we propose a two-stage cascaded purification framework, termed DAPS–PCMD, establishing a defense paradigm of “sever structure first, decouple parameters next.” Our key insight is that detection backdoors often rely on a small set of trigger-sensitive feature units (channels) to form dominant propagation pathways4,17, which exhibit pronounced and stable differential activations between triggered/suspect inputs and clean inputs15. Meanwhile, due to multi-branch coupling in detector training, backdoor functionality may also persist as residual coupled features dispersed in the parameter space12,17. As a result, neither pruning alone nor global unlearning alone can consistently yield a stable security–utility trade-off11,12,17.

Specifically, in Stage I we introduce DAPS (Differential-Activation guided Path-Severing Pruning). DAPS jointly models differential activation scores, stability-enhanced moving statistics, and task-importance penalties to localize and iteratively prune the channels that support the backdoor, thereby physically severing the primary trigger pathways with minimal structural cost and producing a channel-level localization prior11,17. In Stage II we introduce PCMD (Prior-Constrained Residual Feature Decoupling). Under the transferred channel prior, PCMD performs detection-aware targeted unlearning via dynamic suspicious mining and stage-wise optimization, and constrains updates and suppression at the channel level to restrict unlearning perturbations to the sensitive subspace12,17. This design decouples and effectively suppresses residual backdoor features, reduces re-activation risks, and preserves detection utility under explicit constraints, achieving a more robust balance between model security and model utility12,17.

Related work

Deep learning-based object detection

Mainstream object detectors are classified into two categories. Two-stage detectors generally favor accuracy by decoupling proposal generation and final prediction. Faster R-CNN introduced by Ren et al.16 establishes a canonical two-stage pipeline by incorporating the Region Proposal Network (RPN). FPN proposed by Lin et al.18 further improves detection performance via multi-scale feature aggregation. He et al. extend this paradigm with Mask R-CNN19, demonstrating strong scalability of the architecture to multi-task settings. In contrast, one-stage detectors are designed for efficiency by predicting dense detections in a single pass. The YOLO series proposed by Redmon et al.20 formulates detection as direct regression, enabling real-time inference. SSD proposed by Liu et al.21 achieves a favorable balance between speed and accuracy through multi-scale predictions. Despite their outstanding performance, these detectors are not developed with explicit mechanisms against training-time poisoning, and can be vulnerable under backdoor attacks.

Backdoor-oriented security defense

Existing studies on backdoor defenses predominantly focus on image classification. They can be broadly categorized into three streams: trigger inversion and input purification, model reconstruction (e.g., pruning, fine-tuning, and resetting), and forgetting-based training (e.g., distillation and unlearning). In contrast, defenses tailored to object detection are comparatively limited, and many existing efforts emphasize backdoor detection rather than removal.

For backdoor defenses in image classification, a variety of post-training purification strategies have been proposed. Fine-Pruning attempts to suppress backdoors by pruning neurons that remain dormant on clean samples11. NAD uses knowledge distillation to encourage a student model to forget backdoor behaviors guided by a teacher22. Neural Cleanse reverse-engineers potential trigger patterns and mitigates backdoors via purification or adversarial training9. In addition, I-BAU and ABL aim to realize backdoor forgetting via implicit hypergradients or by isolating suspicious samples12,23. However, transferring these classification-oriented strategies to object detection is non-trivial. Modern detectors involve tightly coupled modules (e.g., RPN and detection heads) and multi-scale representations, which often yield dispersed and branch-dependent backdoor footprints. Consequently, coarse interventions commonly used in classification (e.g., layer-level pruning or global distillation) may either severely impair localization-sensitive features (leading to notable mAP drops) or leave backdoor functionality partially intact.

In the context of object detection backdoor defenses, early works mainly demonstrated the limitations of directly applying classification defenses. Studies by Chan et al.4 and Luo et al.7 show that for detection-specific attacks such as object disappearance, naive fine-tuning or pruning is often ineffective. To date, detector-specific defenses remain relatively scarce, and many methods are limited to the diagnosis/detection stage13,14. For example, ODSCAN13 and Django14 primarily focus on scanning and diagnosing backdoors rather than repairing backdoored detectors. More recently, Zhang et al.15 propose a removal approach based on module inconsistency; however, module-level resetting is still a coarse intervention and may incur non-negligible performance degradation on complex detectors.

Despite these advances, a central challenge remains: how to effectively suppress backdoors while preserving clean detection performance, i.e., achieving a favorable security–utility trade-off. This motivates us to develop a representation-level, fine-grained purification mechanism tailored for object detectors, which can reliably localize and remove trigger-sensitive components while remaining compatible with detector training dynamics.

Backdoor threat model

End-to-end training of object detectors heavily depends on the training data distribution and the correctness of supervision signals16. Once the training pipeline is poisoned, abnormal decision patterns may be encoded into model parameters and activated at deployment time5. Problem formulation

Under the detection setting in Sec. 3.1 and the backdoor threat model in Sec. 3.2, the defender aims to purify a suspicious detector without knowing the trigger or the attack configuration5,9. Compared with classification, backdoor effects in object detection are harder to remove in a localized manner without compromising utility, which often makes existing defenses unstable in detection scenarios4,14.

The core challenge can be summarized as a fundamental contradiction: the model security–utility trade-off5,12,17. Improving model security (reducing ASR) often comes at the cost of decreased utility (lower mAP), and vice versa4.

Stronger interventions can more effectively suppress abnormal behaviors under triggered inputs (lower ASR), but are also more likely to damage normal representations and localization capability, leading to significant mAP drops11,17. Conversely, milder interventions may preserve mAP, yet leave backdoor representations partially intact and still activatable under triggers4,12.

This trade-off is difficult to alleviate mainly because backdoor-related representations in detectors often exhibit sparse, dispersed, and non-interpretable distributions4,15. Without reliable priors, a defense strategy cannot precisely localize and suppress only the true “backdoor carriers”, and thus tends to degenerate into coarse and broad interventions with low efficiency and high performance cost5,11.

To satisfy both objectives, our design principle is to avoid global, coarse-grained uniform interventions11,12. Instead, we adopt a cascaded strategy of “structural path severing first, and parameter decoupling next” to perform targeted suppression of trigger-sensitive representations, thereby reducing ASR while minimizing adverse impact on normal detection functionality12,17.

The proposed method

Overall framework

As discussed in Section “Preliminaries”, the central challenge of backdoor defense for object detection lies in the model security–utility trade-off4,5. Existing defenses are often polarized into two categories. Aggressive interventions (e.g., global pruning or module resetting) aim to suppress backdoor functionality forcefully, yet they tend to damage multi-scale and localization features that are essential for detection, leading to substantial mAP degradation11,15. In contrast, milder strategies (e.g., global fine-tuning) are relatively utility-friendly, but they are frequently unable to dislodge the dominant trigger pathways, leaving non-negligible residual risks4,12.

We argue that neither “aggressive” nor “mild” strategies alone can satisfactorily balance security and utility, mainly because they do not disentangle the structural dependency of backdoors (i.e., physical carrier pathways) from the parametric coupling of backdoor behaviors (i.e., residual associations entangled in the parameter space)5,17. Following the principle of “severing physically first, decoupling logically next”, we propose a two-stage cascaded purification framework, termed DAPS–PCMD, as illustrated in Fig. 2, which provides an end-to-end data flow from coarse-grained structural disconnection to fine-grained parameter decoupling11,12,17.

The necessity of this cascaded architecture stems from the inherent limitations of single-stage methods: structural pruning alone may fail to eradicate residual toxicity coupled with benign features, whereas global unlearning devoid of reliable structural priors frequently compromises detection functionality11,12,17. Consequently, the proposed framework strategically aligns these components to ensure the two stages are mutually complementary17.

Stage I: Differential-activation guided path-severing pruning (DAPS)

As noted in Section “Preliminaries”, detection backdoors often exhibit channel-level sparsity—a small number of channels can dominate the abnormal behaviors under triggers4,5. However, due to multi-branch architectures and cross-level feature fusion in detectors, these channels may spread across layers and become entangled with benign detection representations15. Without interpretable localization signals, defenses tend to degenerate into coarse interventions, resulting in either insufficient removal (high ASR) or severe utility damage (mAP drop)4,14. Therefore, the goal of Stage I is to (i) localize and sever the dominant backdoor propagation pathways with minimal structural cost, and (ii) output an explicit channel-level prior that can be passed to Stage II to constrain where to decouple17.

We propose DAPS. The core idea is that a channel participating in trigger-induced abnormal behaviors should exhibit a significant and stable activation shift between triggered inputs and clean inputs4,5. In contrast, spurious spikes caused by training noise or mini-batch fluctuations are typically inconsistent across iterations. Accordingly, DAPS jointly models “differential activation metric + stability statistics + task-importance regularization” to enable fine-grained localization and iterative pruning, while producing as a structural prior11,17.

To avoid spurious peaks caused by mini-batch fluctuations, DAPS further stabilizes the signals using moving statistics (i.e., exponential moving averages over iterations). It then introduces a task-importance penalty to reduce the risk of pruning channels that are critical for clean detection11,17. Following the criterion of being differentially significant and stable, yet not essential for the clean task, DAPS ranks channels and iteratively removes the most suspicious ones. This process not only achieves precise structural severing of the primary backdoor carriers, but also provides Stage II with an explicit structural prior, i.e., a clear where-to-decouple guidance17.

Stage II: Prior-constrained residual feature decoupling (PCMD)

Although DAPS severs the primary trigger pathways structurally, residual backdoor associations may still be diffusely encoded in the remaining parameters and entangled with benign detection functionality, thereby re-activating under certain inputs or subsequent training12,17. Directly applying classification-style global unlearning (e.g., indiscriminate gradient ascent) to object detection typically causes two issues: (i) detection loss is multi-term with significantly different scales, making fixed-threshold isolation prone to misclassifying hard clean samples as suspicious, which destabilizes training; (ii) global updates perturb localization and multi-scale representations, causing utility degradation12,14.

We propose PCMD, whose goal is to decouple and effectively suppress residual trigger associations from the pruned model under the structural prior , while preventing perturbation spillover via clean performance anchoring12,17. PCMD is built around two control questions: when to decouple (sample-level selection) and where to decouple (parameter/channel-level constraint). The former reduces unnecessary disturbance to hard-but-benign samples; the latter restricts unlearning updates to a sensitive subspace near , avoiding global drift14,17.

As shown in Fig. 4, PCMD operates at two complementary levels. At the sample level, it performs dynamic suspicious mining to determine when to decouple by selecting the samples that are most likely to carry residual backdoor traces12,14. At the parameter level, it leverages the prior to constrain updates to the vicinity of sensitive pathways, specifying where to decouple and preventing global perturbation drift17. Under a unified optimization framework, PCMD preserves detection utility via a clean-performance anchor, suppresses residual backdoor behaviors through the decoupling objective, and further reduces re-activation risks using channel suppression12,17. A projection constraint is additionally provided as an optional stabilization strategy to improve training robustness when needed12.

Experiments

Experimental setup

Model Structure and Dataset We adopt the two-stage detector Faster R-CNN as the default architecture, with VGG16 as the backbone16,24. The detection head and training pipeline follow standard implementations16. This choice is made for two reasons: (i) it aligns well with commonly used backdoor attack settings for object detection4,6,7; and (ii) its canonical two-stage structure provides a clear feature propagation path from the backbone to the detection head16, which facilitates representation-level differential activation statistics, pruning, and targeted unlearning, thereby enabling a faithful evaluation of the proposed DAPS–PCMD framework.

All experiments are conducted on the CompCars dataset25. We use the provided bounding-box annotations for object detection training25. Following the official split, we build the training and test sets, and evaluate clean performance on the clean test set25.

Attack Setup We follow the training pipeline in6 to construct backdoored object detectors, and adopt a mask-based invisible trigger injection mechanism to enhance stealthiness6. Concretely, instead of using a fixed handcrafted trigger pattern, we employ a generative trigger: a generator network produces an instance-wise trigger perturbation conditioned on the input image6. By default, the generator is implemented as an AutoEncoder; in some comparative experiments we replace it with a UNet to analyze how the trigger-generation architecture affects attack behaviors26.

We consider two poisoning rates, , and inject triggers and manipulate supervision according to the specified proportion during training, resulting in backdoored detectors with different attack strengths4,6. To reduce perceptible perturbations on the background, the trigger is masked and only overlaid within the ground-truth bounding-box region of the target instance6. The perturbation magnitude is bounded by a coefficient λ = 0.056.

Attack Tasks We evaluate under two typical object-level backdoor attacks for detection: (1) Object Generation Attack (OGA), which induces the detector to produce spurious detections of a target class under the trigger; and (2) Object Disappearance Attack (ODA), which suppresses detections of a target class when the trigger is present4,6. By varying attack type and poisoning rate, we systematically analyze the effectiveness and stealthiness of generative triggers in object detection6.

Evaluation Metrics To characterize detector behaviors on both clean and triggered inputs, we report: (1) Clean detection utility: mAP@0.5 and mAP@[0.5:0.95] (IoU thresholds from 0.5 to 0.95 with step 0.05) on the clean test set16. (2) Attack success rate (ASR): evaluated on the triggered test set4,6. For ODA, ASR measures the fraction of target-class ground-truth instances that are successfully suppressed (missed) under triggers4. For OGA, ASR measures the fraction of cases where the model generates target-class predictions in the specified region under triggers4,6. We compute these metrics on the clean and triggered test sets respectively to quantify the backdoor impact4.

For fairness and reproducibility, all training-based defenses share the same trusted clean subset and the same repair training budget9,11,12,17.

ODA In the Object Disappearance Attack setting (Fig. 5(a–b)), different methods exhibit clear separation in the final residual ASR plateaus (Table 1)4,6. For p=0.3, Original yields ASR=0.950, indicating a consistently effective attack4,6. Among external baselines, RNP reduces ASR to 0.101 but still leaves noticeable residual risks; Neural Cleanse and I-BAU remain higher (0.280 and 0.220, respectively)9,12,17. In contrast, our full framework DAPS–PCMD achieves the lowest ASR (0.008), reducing ASR by 0.942 compared to Original and by 0.093 compared to RNP. This advantage stems from the two-stage decomposition: DAPS first severs the dominant trigger-sensitive pathways at the structural level, sharply reducing the dominant contribution of the backdoor; PCMD then performs prior-constrained decoupling to effectively suppress residual coupled features, further pushing the residual plateau down11,12,17. The dynamics show that DAPS–PCMD quickly drops to a low ASR region early and stays stable thereafter, indicating faster convergence and a lower final plateau. The ablations highlight complementarity: PCMD-only (0.058) and DAPS-only (0.025) are both effective, yet remain above the full framework (0.008), suggesting that the cascade of “path severing + targeted forgetting” further reduces the residual upper bound11,12,17.

When the poisoning rate increases to p=0.5, the gap under stronger attacks becomes more pronounced (Fig. 5(b) and Table 1)4,6. Original increases to ASR=0.980; I-BAU and Neural Cleanse rise to 0.321 and 0.341, respectively, reflecting aggravated residuals under stronger backdoor implantation; RNP reaches 0.1509,12,17. DAPS–PCMD still achieves the lowest ASR (0.015), remaining clearly better than PCMD (0.074) and DAPS (0.036), demonstrating stable suppression under higher poisoning rates11,12,17. Intuitively, higher poisoning strengthens the dominance of the primary trigger pathway; by cutting it first, PCMD can focus on residual features in a constrained subspace, avoiding broad global perturbations while maintaining strong suppression17.

OGA In the Object Generation Attack setting (Fig. 5(c–d)), the overall ranking is consistent with ODA, while residuals of baselines become more pronounced (Table 1)4,6. For p=0.3, Original yields ASR=0.930; RNP, I-BAU, and Neural Cleanse remain at 0.180, 0.292, and 0.302, respectively9,12,17. Our DAPS–PCMD achieves 0.017, improving over RNP by 0.163, while PCMD and DAPS alone are 0.109 and 0.054. This suggests that in OGA, backdoor effects are more prone to persist as dispersed residual coupling in the remaining parameters; thus, structural severing alone or parameter unlearning alone tends to encounter a higher residual plateau, whereas the two-stage cascade is more effective at suppressing late-stage residual plateau12,17.

For p=0.5, OGA further amplifies the differences: I-BAU increases to 0.368, Neural Cleanse to 0.380, and RNP remains 0.180; in contrast, DAPS–PCMD reaches 0.038, still the lowest, and improves over DAPS (0.084) by 0.0469,12,17. The dynamics indicate a slow-decaying decrease under OGA, consistent with gradual suppression of dispersed residual features: after the primary pathway is weakened, remaining abnormality is more weakly coupled and thus requires constrained decoupling to progressively dissolve, yielding a longer tail but a lower final plateau12,17.

Overall, DAPS–PCMD achieves the lowest ASR across all four settings (0.008/0.015/0.017/0.038) with smaller plateau drift as poisoning increases, while ablations show each stage is effective yet bounded. This confirms that the two-stage complementarity—reducing the structural contribution of dominant trigger pathways in Stage I and suppressing residual coupled features under a prior constraint in Stage II—is critical for stable and robust suppression11,12,17.

This is because OGA more readily forms strongly coupled features between abnormal generation and normal detection; without structural localization and constrained update scope, unlearning tends to perturb benign detection representations more aggressively, accelerating mAP degradation17. Neural Cleanse also decreases from 0.680 to 0.622 for similar reasons9,14, while RNP performs the worst (0.400/0.380), suggesting severe utility collapse under stronger structural backdoor effects when pruning lacks precise localization11,17.

The key is the two-stage constraint applied to both dominant pathways and residual features: DAPS reduces the contribution of the primary propagation routes supporting abnormal generation, avoiding the need for broad perturbations later; PCMD then decouples remaining coupling under the prior and limits spillover via the clean anchor, enabling utility to remain close to Original even under stronger attacks11,12,17.

The ablations exhibit a consistent ordering: PCMD-only yields 0.829/0.794 and DAPS-only yields 0.748/0.655, indicating that unlearning alone is more utility-friendly than pruning alone, while the full two-stage cascade significantly increases the attainable utility upper bound under strong security constraints11,12,17. This also explains why the improvement is not a trivial stacking: Stage I provides a clear where-to-decouple prior that makes Stage II more focused and less harmful, while Stage II compensates for residual parameter-level representations that may remain after Stage I, jointly pushing both security and utility toward a better operating point17.

Discussion

Comparing Table 1 and Table 2 reveals distinct failure modes of different strategies9,11,12,17. For unlearning-style baselines such as I-BAU, mAP is relatively better preserved (e.g., 0.824 in ODA with p=0.3), yet ASR residuals become significant under stronger attacks and higher poisoning rates (e.g., 0.321 for ODA with p=0.5 and 0.368 for OGA with p=0.5), suggesting that parameter-only adversarial forgetting can stagnate on persistent dominant trigger pathways4,12. For one-shot methods such as RNP and Neural Cleanse, the curves often exhibit fixed plateaus: while they provide static references, their residual ceilings are difficult to further improve9,17. In particular, RNP yields markedly low mAP across all settings (0.52/0.48/0.40/0.38), indicating that pruning-based defenses can easily harm benign detection representations17; Neural Cleanse also shows enlarged residuals at higher poisoning (e.g., 0.341 in ODA with p=0.5 and 0.380 in OGA with p=0.5)9,14.

In contrast, DAPS–PCMD simultaneously achieves lower ASR plateaus and higher mAP plateaus across all four settings, with smaller performance drift as poisoning increases: ASR only rises from (0.008, 0.017) to (0.015, 0.038), and mAP only decreases from (0.862, 0.850) to (0.850, 0.837). This trend indicates that the two-stage cascade is less sensitive to hyperparameter/attack-strength variations and thus more robust. The ablations further clarify the complementarity: DAPS is more aggressive in reducing ASR (e.g., 0.025 in ODA with p=0.3) but incurs larger utility loss (mAP=0.750), whereas PCMD is more utility-friendly (mAP=0.830/0.794) but leaves residual ASR under strong attacks (0.074/0.159). Therefore, the sequential design of “structural severing → residual decoupling” is crucial for achieving a stable security–utility trade-off by jointly improving both the suppression upper bound and the utility upper bound11,12,17.

Conclusion

This work addresses backdoor threats against object detectors that may be implanted during training, and proposes a two-stage purification framework, DAPS–PCMD4,5. The defense pipeline is explicitly decomposed into two consecutive steps: (i) path-severing pruning (Stage I: DAPS) and (ii) residual feature decoupling (Stage II: PCMD), aiming to achieve a better security–utility trade-off—suppressing backdoor behaviors under trigger conditions while maximally preserving clean detection performance11,12,17.

Our key observation is that detection backdoors often rely on a small set of trigger-sensitive channels, which exhibit more salient and stable activation shifts between triggered inputs and clean inputs4,5. Based on this, DAPS performs fine-grained ranking and iterative pruning using differential activation statistics and stability modeling, physically severing the primary trigger pathways and producing a channel-level prior11,17. Then, under this prior constraint, PCMD performs targeted decoupling and effective suppression of dispersed residual trigger associations in the pruned model, while suppressing perturbation spillover through clean detection anchoring and channel constraints12,17. This enables further reduction of re-activation risks while maintaining detection utility17.

Under typical detection backdoor settings with ODA/OGA and poisoning rates p=0.3/0.5, experimental results show that DAPS–PCMD substantially improves model security while retaining high utility4,6: across four settings, ASR is consistently reduced to 0.008–0.038 (no more than 3.8%), a pronounced drop from 0.930–0.980 without defense; meanwhile, clean-test mAP remains at 0.837–0.862, with only a small decrease of 0.008–0.026 compared to the original model (0.863–0.870). Compared with representative baselines, DAPS–PCMD further reduces ASR by 0.093–0.163 over the pruning-based defense RNP (ASR=0.101–0.180), corresponding to a relative reduction of about 78.9%–92.1%; additionally, it improves mAP by 0.038–0.103 over the unlearning baseline I-BAU (mAP=0.734–0.824), demonstrating a superior security–utility balance9,12,17.

Future work will focus on controllable unlearning under unknown backdoors1,5. The current framework relies on available backdoor cues to construct triggered/suspect signals and obtain structural priors. A promising direction is to develop prior-free residual feature decoupling under unknown triggers and unknown target abnormal behaviors, potentially via self-supervised statistics, distribution-shift characterization, or online anomaly evidence5,27. We also plan to systematically summarize commonalities of trigger distributions and propagation pathways across attacks to form more transferable defense priors, and further extend the two-stage purification paradigm to privacy-constrained scenarios (e.g., federated learning), making it closer to real-world deployment requirements5.
