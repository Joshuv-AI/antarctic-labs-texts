Integrated Structural Modulation Inducing Fast Charge Transfer in Aqueous Zinc‐Ion Batteries
Authors: Nibagani Naresh, Youngtae Park, Su Hwan Jeong, Sang Jun Lee, Dong Park Lee, So Hyun Lee, Gyeong Hee Ryu, Young Hwa Jung, Joo‐Hyung Kim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAqueous Zn‐ion batteries (AZIBs) are promising energy‐storage devices owing to their exceptional safety, long cycle life, simple production, and high storage capacity. Manganese oxides are considered potential cathode materials for AZIBs, primarily because of their safety, low cost, simple synthesis, and high storage capacity. However, MnO2‐based cathodes tend to deteriorate structurally during long‐term cycling, which reduces their reversible capacity. In this study, an advanced α‐MnO2@SnO2 nanocomposite via facile hydrothermal synthesis is developed. The synergistic effects of lattice disorder and increased electron conductivity in the α‐MnO2@SnO2 nanocomposite mitigate structural degradation and enhance the overall electrochemical performance. The nanocomposite exhibits a high reversible capacity of 347 mAh g−1 at a current density of 100 mA g−1 after 50 cycles. Furthermore, it exhibits excellent rate performance and stable capacity even after 1000 cycles, maintaining a capacity of 78 mAh g−1 at a high current density of 5 A g−1. This excellent electrochemical performance is attributed to the reversible Zn intercalation in α‐MnO2@SnO2 nanocomposites due to the increased structural stability and fast ion/electron exchange caused by the distortion of the tunnel structure, on the basis of various ex situ experiments, density functional theory calculations, and electrochemical characterizations.

Introduction

Advances in battery technology over the past few decades have had far‐reaching consequences for modern life, reshaping communication, labor, transportation, and the utilization of renewable energy sources to reduce carbon emissions.[ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 ] In addition to enabling advanced applications, such as the Internet of Things and smart cities, the ongoing improvements in energy‐storage performance, which include increased energy density and power density and extended lifecycles, have played a crucial role in the decarbonization of society.[ 5 , 6 ] Rechargeable Li‐ion batteries, which are renowned for their light weight and high energy densities, are widely used for electrical energy storage.[ 7 , 8 , 9 ] However, despite their impressive progress, Li‐ion batteries face challenges that limit their broader adoption—particularly in emerging technologies.[ 10 , 11 ] Safety concerns, material availability issues, and cost considerations limit their use—particularly in mini‐grid scale and off‐grid applications, such as the storage of solar and wind energy.[ 12 ] As an alternative, aqueous Zn‐ion batteries (AZIBs) have attracted attention owing to their excellent safety profile, long cycle life, ease of manufacturing, and acceptable storage capacity.[ 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 ] They have several significant advantages. i) AZIBs can be produced in an open environment without the need for an inert atmosphere, which reduces the cost and complexity compared with air‐sensitive batteries.[ 13 ] ii) The Zn anode in AZIBs exhibits high capacities (820 mAh g−1 and 5855 mAh cm−3) and can be operated with inexpensive aqueous electrolytes, such as ZnSO4 and Zn(CF3SO3)2.[ 13 ] iii) Zn is abundantly available (79 ppm) and relatively cost‐effective ($0.5–$1.5 $ per lb) compared with Li, making it an attractive option. These key features make AZIBs well‐suited for residential and commercial energy‐storage applications, meeting the necessary criteria for stationary mini‐grid and off‐grid storage systems.

The development of suitable cathodes is essential for the practical application of AZIBs. The most commonly used cathodes in AZIBs are manganese oxides, vanadium oxides, and Prussian blue analogues.[ 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 ] Among the aforementioned cathodes, Mn‐based cathodes are promising for AZIBs owing to their remarkable safety, ease of synthesis, low cost, and high capacity (theoretical capacity of 616 mAh g−1).[ 23 , 24 ] There are polymorphisms in the crystal structure of MnO2, such as α‐MnO2, β‐MnO2, δ‐MnO2, γ‐MnO2, and ε‐MnO2. MnO2 is composed of MnO6 octahedra, which are typically connected at their edges or corners and piled in a layered or tunnel‐like form.[ 25 , 26 ] MnO2‐based cathodes are viable cathodes for AZIBs because of their high voltage, high capacity, ease of synthesis, natural abundance, and environmental friendliness.[ 27 ]

One of the significant challenges with AZIBs is the structural degradation of electrode materials during repeated charge–discharge cycles.[ 19 , 28 ] The combination of two different metal oxides can lead to a more robust and stable crystal structure that can better accommodate the strain and volume changes associated with Zn‐ion intercalation and de‐intercalation. In addition, the doping of heteroelements into MnO6 octahedra within the bulk structure can modify the lattice structure, creating pathways that allow more efficient movement of Zn2+ ions, which is particularly important for fast charging and discharging capabilities in AZIBs. This structural stability is crucial for maintaining the integrity of the electrode over many cycles, which extends the battery's lifespan. Collectively, these improvements brought by heterointerfaces in metal oxides contribute to the overall efficiency and durability of AZIBs. Various strategies have been employed to prevent structural transformation for increasing ion conductivity and achieving long‐term stable cyclability, such as heteroatom doping and defect engineering.[ 29 , 30 ] Sun et al. reported that pre‐intercalated Ca in layered MnO2 had a reversible capacity of 298 mAh g−1 after 5000 cycles.[ 31 ] Wu et al. and Yadav et al. reported that Cu intercalated in a layered MnO2 cathode had an excellent capacity after 6000 cycles.[ 32 , 33 ] Zhang et al. reported an enhancement in the Zn‐ion storage performance of birnessite manganese oxide with La intercalation.[ 34 ] Peng et al. reported that Na+‐intercalated ultrathin δ‐MnO2 nanoflakes had an impressive reversible capacity of 335 mAh g−1 after 1000 cycles.[ 35 ] It is evident that heteroatom doping and hybrid nanocomposites of MnO2‐based cathodes can widen the tunnel area, cause lattice distortion, and enhance the electronic conductivity and structural stability during Zn2+‐ion intercalation and de‐intercalation, resulting in high‐performance AZIBs.

In this study, we prepared a novel highly stable α‐MnO2@SnO2 nanocomposite cathode with a heterointerface via a facile and cost‐effective hydrothermal method. This approach can easily incorporate SnO2 on the surface of α‐MnO2, while simultaneously doping the lattice structure of α‐MnO2 with Sn. It not only enhances electrochemical activity by introducing active sites for redox reactions essential for Zn2+‐ion intercalation and de‐intercalation but also plays an important role in promoting structural stability, which is critical for withstanding repeated charge–discharge cycles. Additionally, such interfaces accelerate ion diffusion, increasing the ionic conductivity for fast charging and discharging while also increasing the electronic conductivity for efficient electron transfer. The synergistic effects with structural modulation and interface chemistry of α‐MnO2@SnO2 nanocomposites were characterized using various methods. Furthermore, the effects of Sn doping on the lattice distortion and ionic conductivity were verified by density functional theory (DFT) calculations. Various experimental and theoretical characterization indicated that the α‐MnO2@SnO2 composites exhibited excellent electrochemical properties.

Results and Discussion

The synchrotron X‐ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of α‐MnO2 and α‐MnO2@SnO2 samples and their Le Bail refinements are shown in Figure 1a,b, respectively. For both materials, the Le Bail refinement based on two MnO2 with the hollandite phases minimized the chi‐squared values to 3.54 (α‐MnO2) and 2.90 (α‐MnO2@SnO2), and the fitted MnO2 phases were labeled major (pink) and minor (green) according to their fractions. Additionally, in Figure 1c, an enlarged view of the (110) plane reveals that the XRD peaks of the α‐MnO2@SnO2 sample split into doublet peaks, indicating Sn doping of MnO2 and associated lattice distortion. The lattice parameter of MnO2 (major) did not differ significantly between the α‐MnO2 and α‐MnO2@SnO2 samples; however, the XRD peak of the (110) plane of MnO2 (minor) was shifted to the left for the α‐MnO2@SnO2 sample, which implied an increase in the lattice parameter a. Figure 1d,e shows good agreement between the predictions of the a‐axis and the volumes, respectively, of the α‐MnO2 and α‐MnO2@SnO2 samples from the DFT calculations and Le Bail refinement. Sn doping causes deformation of the MnO6 octahedra, which can increase the lattice parameter a and unit volume, significantly affecting the tunnel structure. Field‐emission scanning electron microscopy (FE‐SEM) images of the α‐MnO2 nanorods and α‐MnO2@SnO2 nanocomposites are shown in Figure 1f. The SEM image of α‐MnO2 indicates a nanorod‐shaped morphology with an average diameter of 100 nm and a length of ≈1 µm. In contrast, the SEM image of α‐MnO2@SnO2 reveals spherical SnO2 nanoparticles distributed over the surface of the α‐MnO2 nanorods. The valence states and chemical elements of the α‐MnO2 and α‐MnO2@SnO2 samples were investigated by analyzing the Mn 2p XPS spectra, as shown in Figure 1g. The XPS peaks were deconvoluted into two peaks using XPSPEAK 4.1. Figure 1g shows the Mn 2p3/2 and Mn 2p1/2 XPS peaks with spin–orbit coupling of 641 and 652 eV, respectively. The Mn 2p3/2 XPS spectrum of α‐MnO2 is deconvoluted into two states: Mn3+ (640.05 eV) and Mn4+ (641.65 eV). The Mn 2p1/2 XPS spectrum is deconvoluted into the two states Mn3+ (651.80 eV) and Mn4+ (653.01 eV). Similarly, the Mn 2p3/2 XPS spectrum of α‐MnO2@SnO2 is deconvoluted into the two states Mn3+ (640.80 eV) and Mn4+ (642.43 eV), while the Mn 2p1/2 XPS spectrum is deconvoluted into the two states Mn3+ (652.5 eV) and Mn4+ (653.73 eV). The α‐MnO2@SnO2 nanocomposites exhibited a shift in the Mn 2p XPS spectra to a higher binding energy. Two Mn 2p peaks of α‐MnO2@SnO2 exhibited a positive shift of ≈0.7–0.8 eV, indicating that electrons were drained from the Mn4+ in the oxide matrix owing to the presence of Sn4+. This suggests that fast electron transport is possible at the α‐MnO2@SnO2 interface because of the imbalance in the electron distribution.[ 36 ] The XPS peaks of the α‐MnO2 and α‐MnO2@SnO2 samples are shown in Figure S1 (Supporting Information). Three separate states are observed in the O 1s XPS spectrum of α‐MnO2: Mn‐O‐Mn (529.29 eV), Mn‐O‐H (530.25 eV), and H‐O‐H (531.85 eV). The O 1s spectrum of α‐MnO2@SnO2 also exhibits three peaks: Mn‐O (528.25 eV), Mn‐O‐Mn (528.73 eV), and Mn‐O‐H (530.10 eV). The BET surface areas of α‐MnO2 and α‐MnO2@SnO2 were 18.03 m2 g−1 (Figure S2a, Supporting Information) and 38.07 m2 g−1 (Figure S2b, Supporting Information), respectively, and this large difference was due to the distribution of SnO2 nanoparticles on the surface of α‐MnO2 nanorods.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was performed to further investigate the differences in the microstructures and crystalline structures of the α‐MnO2 and α‐MnO2@SnO2 samples. The α‐MnO2 nanorods exhibited a rod‐shaped morphology with an average width of 70 nm, as shown in Figure 2a. A high‐resolution TEM (HR‐TEM) image and fast Fourier transform (FFT) pattern of α‐MnO2 nanorod are shown in Figure 2b. The α‐MnO2 nanorods exhibit highly ordered regular lattice planes with an interplanar spacing of 0.692 nm. Figure 2e shows the uniform distribution of Mn and O throughout the α‐MnO2 nanorods. In addition, Figure 2c shows spherical SnO2 nanoparticles distributed over the surface of the α‐MnO2 nanorods. Notably, the size of the α‐MnO2 nanorods in the α‐MnO2@SnO2 composite appears reduced compared with the pure α‐MnO2 nanorods, as shown in Figure 2c. The HR‐TEM image and FFT pattern of the α‐MnO2@SnO2 nanocomposite shown in Figure 2d depict the lattice distortion and the interconnected structure of SnO2 nanoparticles with α‐MnO2 nanorods. The observed interplanar spacing in the α‐MnO2@SnO2 nanocomposite is 0.719 nm, which exceeds that of pure α‐MnO2. This increase in interplanar spacing and the associated lattice distortion in the α‐MnO2@SnO2 nanorods suggest the incorporation of Sn‐doped MnO2 into the hybrid nanocomposite. This structural change can accelerate Zn2+‐ion transport during the intercalation/de‐intercalation process. Figure 2f displays the uniform distributions of Mn, O, and Sn throughout the α‐MnO2@SnO2 nanocomposite. Moreover, the distribution of SnO2 nanoparticles on the surface of α‐MnO2 nanorods increased the overall electronic conductivity in the α‐MnO2@SnO2 nanocomposite owing to the imbalance in the electron distribution.[ 37 ] The tunnel structures and Zn2+‐ion diffusion processes of α‐MnO2 and α‐MnO2@SnO2 are illustrated in Figure 2g,h, respectively. DFT calculations revealed lattice distortion and tunnel enlargement due to the Sn‐doped MnO2. The MnO2 structure consists of an MnO6 octahedral edge shared at the corner. The horizontal and vertical distances of the α‐MnO2 structure are 5.808 and 7.097 Å, respectively. The tunnel area of the α‐MnO2 structure is 95.67 Å2. In contrast, the Sn‐doped α‐MnO2 structure has horizontal and vertical distances of 7.253 and 6.108 Å, respectively, creating lattice distortion with an increased tunnel area of 97.49 Å2. Figure 2i shows that the tunnel area of α‐MnO2 was reduced after Zn intercalation, whereas that of Sn‐doped α‐MnO2 was increased slightly. In addition, Sn‐doped MnO2 structures have a lower diffusion energy (0.556 eV) than α‐MnO2, as shown in Figure 2j. This is due to the enlarged tunnel area and small angle changes, which are expected to relieve the strain applied to the material during Zn2+‐ion intercalation. The diffusion barrier energy of α‐MnO2@SnO2 was lower than that of α‐MnO2, indicating that it has reversible charge and discharge with little strain on the crystal structure.

The electrochemical mechanisms of α‐MnO2 and α‐MnO2@SnO2 were investigated through ex situ XRD and ex situ XPS in various states, i.e., pristine, 1st discharge, 1st charge, 2nd discharge, and 2nd charge. Ex situ experiments were performed by disassembling the coin cell after the charge–discharge test (Figures 3a and 4b) to a specific voltage point (0.8–1.9 V). The ex situ XRD patterns of α‐MnO2 and α‐MnO2@SnO2 at various voltages are shown in Figures 3c and 4d, respectively. As shown in Figure 3c, the XRD pattern of pristine α‐MnO2 well matched the standard XRD data. According to the XRD pattern of α‐MnO2 in the 1st discharge (0.8 V) state, the structure of α‐MnO2 transformed into an intermediate phase,[ 38 ] which consists of both the birnessite phase and the α‐MnO2 phase, with some additional peaks corresponding to Zn(CF3SO3)2 salts. During the 1st charge state (1.9 V) the structure reverts to the α‐MnO2 phase; however, the crystallinity is weakened. In addition, the XRD pattern of α‐MnO2 in the 2nd discharge indicates that the structure of the α‐MnO2 phase undergoes a complete transformation into the dominant birnessite phase.[ 43 ] Subsequently, the α‐MnO2 phase with a broad peak in the 2nd charge is observed at 12.6°. During the electrochemical process, the crystal structure of α‐MnO2 repeatedly changes to the dominant birnessite phase and ultimately results in structural collapse. Figure 3d shows the ex situ XRD patterns of the α‐MnO2@SnO2 nanocomposite at various voltage points. The pristine XRD pattern of α‐MnO2@SnO2 aligns well with the Le Bail refinement for α‐MnO2. In the 1st discharge state, the structure predominantly exhibits the α‐MnO2 phase. During the 1st charge state, the α‐MnO2 phase is retained, and there are a few peaks corresponding to Zn(CF3SO3)2. In the 2nd discharge state, the crystal structure is estimated to have similar lattice parameters to the α‐MnO2 phase in the 1st discharge state and does not exhibit significant changes. Similarly, in the 2nd charge stage, the structure is primarily composed of the dominant α‐MnO2 phase, with a higher peak intensity observed over the α‐MnO2 nanorods. There was no change in the crystal structure of the α‐MnO2 phase of the α‐MnO2@SnO2 nanocomposite, even though further peak shifts occurred during the repeated cycles, as confirmed by ex situ XRD. To confirm the peak shift of the 110 plane (2θ ≈ 12.52°) for both α‐MnO2 and α‐MnO2@SnO2, the corresponding regions of the XRD patterns were enlarged, as shown in Figure 3e,f, respectively. In Figure 3e, the XRD peak of the (110) plane of pristine α‐MnO2 is observed at 12.54°. The XRD peaks of the 110 plane shift toward larger 2θ values in the 1st discharge state, indicating a reduction in the interlayer gap due to the simultaneous intercalation of Zn2+ ions and de‐intercalation of H2O.[ 23 , 39 ] During the 1st charge state (1.9 V) the XRD peak of the (110) plane shifts to a smaller 2θ value (left side) owing to the intercalation of H2O and de‐intercalation of Zn2+ ions. Similarly, in the 2nd discharge state, the XRD peak of the (110) plane shifts to a larger 2θ value, with an increase in the peak intensity. This indicates the intercalation of Zn2+ ions and deintercalation of H2O. Most importantly, it indicates a structural transformation to the predominantly birnessite phase. Similarly, the enlarged XRD pattern of the (110) plane and peak shift for the α‐MnO2@SnO2 nanocomposite are depicted in Figure 3f. The ex situ XRD pattern of the (110) plane for α‐MnO2@SnO2 indicated an electrochemical ion‐transport mechanism similar to that described above. However, in the 1st and 2nd cycles, no structural transformation was observed for α‐MnO2@SnO2 in repeated charge–discharge cycles. In the 2nd discharge state, the (110) peak of α‐MnO2@SnO2 was shifted to the right compared with α‐MnO2 owing to the large amounts of intercalations of Zn2+ ions, which is attributed to the weaker repulsion between the oxide layers. The larger number of Zn2+ ions intercalated in α‐MnO2@SnO2 is inferred from the initial charge and discharge capacities of the two materials. The stability of the crystal structure of the α‐MnO2@SnO2 composite is attributed to the Sn doping of MnO2 and the synergistic effects of α‐MnO2 and SnO2 in the nanocomposite. The initial distortion due to Sn doping in MnO2 minimizes structural changes during the intercalation and de‐intercalation of Zn2+ ions and H2O. These results agree well with the DFT calculations, which predicted efficient Zn2+ intercalation due to the large tunnel area despite the large peak shift of α‐MnO2@SnO2 during the charge–discharge process.

The Zn2+ ions de‐intercalated in the 1st charge state, resulting in the strong association of the α‐MnO2/SnO2 interface, with MnO2 donating electrons to SnO2 and having a high oxidation number. The ex situ O 1s XPS spectra of α‐MnO2 and α‐MnO2@SnO2 in the pristine, 1st discharge (0.8 V), and 1st charge (1.9 V) states are presented in Figure S3 (Supporting Information). This indicates intercalation and de‐intercalation of H2O molecules and Zn2+ ions during the charge–discharge process. The excellent electrochemical performance of the AZIBs using the advanced α‐MnO2@SnO2 composite cathode was confirmed by DFT calculations, HR‐TEM images, ex situ XRD analysis, and ex situ XPS.

To further understand the electrochemical mechanism and Zn2+‐ion transport mechanism, we performed CV at different scan rates, GITT analysis, and EIS. The CV measurements at scan rates of 0.5–4 mV s−1 for α‐MnO2 and α‐MnO2@SnO2 are presented in Figure 4. In the CV curves of the α‐MnO2 cathode (Figure 4a), the cathodic peak shifts from 1.36 to 1.27 V (left side) as the scan rate increases. Additionally, as the voltage increases, the anodic peak moves 0.11 V to the right—from 1.61 to 1.72 V. Similarly, in the CV curves of the α‐MnO2@SnO2 cathode, the cathodic peak shifts by 0.08 V to a lower voltage (left side, from 1.35 to 1.27 V) and the anodic peak shifts by 0.10 V to a higher voltage (right side, from 1.59 to 1.69 V) as the scan rate increases from 0.5 to 4 mV, as shown in Figure 4d. In the CV measurements, α‐MnO2@SnO2 exhibited less voltage polarization than α‐MnO2. The enhanced electronic conductivity, lattice distortion, and synergistic impact of α‐MnO2@SnO2 contributed to the reduced voltage polarization. The Sn‐doped α‐MnO2 reduces the structural distortion during Zn2+‐ion transport and facilitates the movement of ions. This phenomenon is well supported by the DFT calculation revealing that the Sn‐doped MnO2 structure has an enlarged tunnel area and negligible distortion during Zn2+‐ion intercalation and de‐intercalation. We further investigated the CV curves for α‐MnO2 and α‐MnO2@SnO2 to acquire a deeper understanding of the capacitive‐ and diffusion‐controlled mechanisms, as illustrated in Figure 4b,e.[ 41 ] As given by the following equation, a power‐law relationship was used in this analysis to evaluate these mechanisms across a range of scan rates:[ 42 ] (1) i=aνb

or (2) logi=loga+blog(ν)

The b value is calculated from the slope of log(v) versus log(i), where a and b are two independent variables. Typically, b varies between 0 and 1. If it is close to 0.5, a diffusion‐controlled electrochemical process is likely to dominate, and if it is close to 1, a capacitive‐controlled electrochemical process is likely to dominate.[ 42 ] Figure 4b,e presents the b values for the cathode and anode peaks of α‐MnO2 and α‐MnO2@SnO2, respectively. The calculated b values for peaks 1 and 2 of α‐MnO2 are 1.161 and 1.060, respectively. In contrast, for α‐MnO2@SnO2, they are 0.758 and 0.768, respectively. These results indicate that the α‐MnO2 cathode has predominantly a capacitive‐controlled electrochemical process. However, α‐MnO2@SnO2 exhibits a combination of diffusion‐controlled and capacitive‐controlled electrochemical processes. The diffusion‐controlled process is more dominant in α‐MnO2@SnO2 than in α‐MnO2. The percentages of the diffusion‐ and capacitive‐controlled mechanisms for the α‐MnO2 and α‐MnO2@SnO2 electrodes at various scan rates are shown in Figure 4c,f, respectively. The capacitive contribution percentages for α‐MnO2 and α‐MnO2@SnO2 were 78.14% and 64.74%, respectively. This indicates that the diffusion‐controlled mechanism is more dominant in α‐MnO2@SnO2 owing to the stable crystal structure and enlarged tunnel area, which facilitate the diffusion of Zn2+ ions. GITT was used to investigate the diffusion coefficient of Zn2+ (DZn2+) ions for the α‐MnO2 and α‐MnO2@SnO2 cathodes.[ 43 , 44 , 45 ] The GITT curves of α‐MnO2 and α‐MnO2@SnO2 are presented in Figure 4g,h, respectively, and the corresponding diffusion‐coefficient curves are shown in Figure 4i. The AZIBs were subjected to a 10 min cycle of charging and discharging at a current density of 0.05 A g−1, followed by a 30 min rest period, to reach a steady voltage. The following formula was used to calculate the diffusion coefficient DZn2+: (3) DZn2+=4πτmVMS2ΔEsΔEτ2 where M represents the molecular weight (g mol−1) of the working substance, m represents the amount of active material on the electrode (g), τ represents the charging or discharging duration (s), V represents the molar volume (cm3 mole−1), ΔEs represents the difference between the initial and steady‐state voltages, ΔE represents the change in voltage during the titration, and S represents the electrode–electrolyte contact area. As shown in Figure 4i, the diffusion coefficient of Zn2+ (DZn2+) of α‐MnO2@SnO2 was higher than that of α‐MnO2 throughout the charge and discharge process. In the first cycle, the initial diffusion coefficients of α‐MnO2 and α‐MnO2@SnO2 were 56.12 × 10−6 and 98.96 × 10−6 cm2 s−1, respectively. Throughout the Zn deposition process, the diffusion coefficient of α‐MnO2@SnO2 was stable and higher than that of α‐MnO2. Similarly, at the beginning of the Zn stripping process, α‐MnO2@SnO2 had an exceptional diffusion coefficient of 119.69 × 10−6 cm2 s−1, which was far higher than that of α‐MnO2 (73.52 × 10−6 cm2 s−1). At the end of the Zn stripping process, α‐MnO2@SnO2 exhibited a significantly higher diffusion coefficient of 36.83 × 10−6 cm2 s−1, whereas α‐MnO2 had a diffusion coefficient of 18.75 × 10−6 cm2 s−1. The outstanding diffusion coefficient of α‐MnO2@SnO2 was due to the increased interlayer distance, reduced distortion during Zn2+ intercalation and de‐intercalation due to Sn‐doped MnO2, and synergistic effect of the α‐MnO2@SnO2 nanocomposite. The EIS curves of the AZIBs with the α‐MnO2 and α‐MnO2@SnO2 cathodes are presented in Figure S4 (Supporting Information). They are composed of a depressed semicircle and an inclined plane. The inclined spike indicates the Warburg impedance (W s) caused by the diffusion of Zn2+ ions toward the electrodes, and the semicircles in the high and mid frequency ranges are due to charge‐transfer resistance (R ct). The α‐MnO2@SnO2 cathode exhibits lower charge‐transfer resistance (R ct) than α‐MnO2. The calculated R ct values for the α‐MnO2 and α‐MnO2@SnO2 cathodes are 342 and 228 Ω, respectively. The EIS curves of a symmetric cell with α‐MnO2 and α‐MnO2@SnO2 are presented in Figure S4b, S4c, respectively. The lower R ct value of the α‐MnO2@SnO2 cathode is due to the faster Zn2+‐ion transport, lower polarization, and higher electronic conductivity.

The electrochemical performance of α‐MnO2 nanorods and the α‐MnO2@SnO2 composite as a cathode for Zn‐ion batteries was evaluated. First, elemental analysis was performed to determine the composition of SnO2 in the α‐MnO2@SnO2 nanocomposite via ICP analysis. The weight ratio of Mn:Sn in the α‐MnO2@SnO2 nanocomposite was found to be 90.5:9.5, indicating that the α‐MnO2:SnO2 molar ratio was ≈95.4:4.6. SnO2 alone—synthesized via the experimental procedure without a Mn precursor—was tested to determine whether it has electrochemical activity in an aqueous Zn electrolyte system. The α‐MnO2@SnO2 cathode had a higher capacity after 50 cycles than the α‐MnO2 cathode. The exceptional capability of the α‐MnO2@SnO2 cathode may have been due to the combined effects of α‐MnO2@SnO2, the increased electronic conductivity, and lattice distortion. The α‐MnO2@SnO2 nanocomposite exhibited a higher capacity even at the higher current density. Conclusion

The novel α‐MnO2@SnO2 nanocomposite as a high‐performance cathode for AZIBs are fabricated by using facile hydrothermal synthesis. The increased interlayer distance and lattice distortion in the α‐MnO2@SnO2 nanocomposite compared with pure α‐MnO2 are verified by using various analysis. The mechanism of structural transformation and stability of the structure during Zn‐ion intercalation and de‐intercalation were evaluated using ex situ characterization techniques. DFT calculations revealed that the initial distortion caused by Sn‐doped α‐MnO2 significantly reduces the change in tunnel size during Zn‐ion intercalation. The large tunnel area and electron deficiency in α‐MnO2 due to Sn doping enhanced Zn‐ion transport during the charge–discharge process. The α‐MnO2@SnO2 composite exhibited a high capacity of 347 mAh g−1 at a current density of 100 mA g−1 after 50 cycles. It exhibited excellent rate performance and an ultralong cycle life of up to 4000 cycles. Even after 1000 cycles, the α‐MnO2@SnO2 composite retained a capacity of 78 mA g−1 at a high current density of 5 A g−1, whereas pure α‐MnO2 retained a capacity of 36 mAh g−1. Cyclic voltammetry at various scan rates revealed a combination of diffusion‐ and capacitive‐controlled mechanisms. The synergistic effect, increased interlayer distance, lattice distortion, and increased electronic conductivity of the α‐MnO2@SnO2 nanocomposite were the key reasons for the outstanding electrochemical performance of the AZIBs. Therefore, the α‐MnO2@SnO2 cathode in this work can provide a feasible pathway to develop the large‐scale AZIBs.

Experimental Section

Synthesis of α‐MnO2 Nanowires

Facile hydrothermal synthesis was used to synthesize α‐MnO2 nanowires. First, 60 mL of distilled water and 4 mmol of KMnO4 (Sigma‐Aldrich) were thoroughly mixed at 25 °C for 1 h. The resulting homogenous solution was placed into an autoclave lined with Teflon for the hydrothermal reaction. The autoclave was kept at 180 °C for 24 h inside an oven until it was cooled to room temperature. The resultant product was centrifuged three times with deionized (DI) water and three times with ethanol. The precipitated product was subsequently dried in a vacuum oven at 80 °C for 12 h. Finally, the dried product was subjected to additional heat treatment at 600 °C for 4 h to produce α‐MnO2 nanowires.

Synthesis of α‐MnO2@SnO2 Nanocomposite

First, 4 mmol of KMnO4 (Sigma‐Aldrich) was mixed with 60 mL of distilled water. Then, 0.4 mmol of SnCl2 (Sigma‐Aldrich) was mixed with the resulting solution via continuous stirring at 25 °C for 1 h. The obtained solution was transferred into a Teflon‐lined autoclave for the hydrothermal reaction. The reaction temperature was kept at 180 °C for 24 h inside an oven. The final obtained product was centrifuged three times with DI water and three times with ethanol. The precipitated product was dried in a vacuum oven at 80 °C for 12 h. The obtained product was heated at 600 °C for 4 h under an N2 atmosphere.

Material Characterization

Synchrotron X‐ray diffraction (XRD) was employed to obtain the high‐resolution XRD patterns of the prepared samples at the Pohang Accelerator Laboratory (PAL), Pohang, South Korea, using the 9B HRPD beamline (λ = 0.15 297 nm). A six‐detector system was used to gather data at room temperature over an angular range of 10°–100° with a step width of 0.01°. Ex situ XRD measurements were performed using a BRUKER D8 Advance A25 X‐ray diffractometer powered by Cu K radiation with a 1.5418‐Å wavelength. A low‐accelerating voltage field‐emission scanning electron microscope (JEOL JSM‐7900F), an energy‐dispersive X‐ray spectrometer, and a 300‐kV field‐emission transmission electron microscope (TECNAI) were used to investigate the morphologies and compositions of the prepared samples. The samples were subjected to X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurement using a Thermo Fisher Scientific K‐Alpha+ instrument (X‐ray source, 200 m). To measure the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) surface areas and pore‐size distributions of the samples, a surface area and pore size analyzer (D14) was used. Inductively coupled plasma (ICP) optical emission spectroscopy (PerkinElmer Optima 8300 DV) was used to determine the atomic ratios of Mn and Sn in the cathode material.

Electrochemical Measurements of Full‐Cell AZIBs

Galvanostatic charge–discharge, cyclic voltammetry (CV), galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT), and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were performed on the fabricated full‐cell (CR2032 coin cell) AZIBs using Zn foil (Sigma‐Aldrich) as an anode and α‐MnO2 or α‐MnO2@SnO2 as a cathode. The α‐MnO2 or α‐MnO2@SnO2, Super P, and polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) were mixed at a weight ratio of 8:1:1 to form a uniform slurry in N‐methyl pyrrolidone (NMP) using a slurry mixer for 10 min. The doctor‐blade method was used to cast the homogenous as‐prepared slurry on carbon paper. The casted carbon‐paper electrode was then dried for 12 h in a vacuum oven at 80 °C. The Zn foil used had a thickness of 0.25 mm. The cathode and anode were punched into parts with sizes of 12 and 16 mm, respectively. The mass of the active materials on the cathode electrode was ≈1.0 mg cm−2. For the fabrication of AZIBs, an electrolyte consisting of 2 m zinc trifluoromethanesulfonate (Zn(CF3SO3)2) and 0.1 m manganese trifluoromethanesulfonate (Mn(CF3SO3)2) mixed with DI water was used. The separator was an 18‐mm‐diameter circular Whatman glass microfiber filter paper (GF/D).

Computational Details

The Vienna Ab initio Software Package (VASP) was used to perform DFT calculations via the projector augmented wave (PAW) method. The electron exchange‐correlation energies were calculated using the revised version for solids of the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBEsol) functional within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA).[ 47 ] The kinetic energy cutoff was set to 500 eV. The convergence criteria for the electronic structure and mean force in bulk calculations were set to 10−5 eV and 0.001 eV Å−1, respectively. The Brillouin zone was sampled with 2 × 2 × 7 k‐points. The Hubbard U‐parameter (DFT + U) was used to improve the description of the correlation effects and reduce the self‐interaction error.[ 48 , 49 , 50 ] The optimized effective interaction parameters Ueff (Ueff = U – J) of 4 eV was used for Mn in MnO2 and Sn‐doped MnO2.[ 51 ] The climbing image nudged elastic band (CI‐NEB) method was used to determine energy barriers associated with Zn‐ion diffusion in the cathodes, and a 1 × 1 × 2 supercell was employed for these calculations.
