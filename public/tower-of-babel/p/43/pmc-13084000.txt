Impact of over compressing gas diffusion electrodes in alkaline zero-gap CO2 electrolyzers
Authors: Aida Farsi, Vasant Batta, Alexandre Tugirumubano, Tess Seip, Aimy Bazylak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe electrochemical reduction of CO2 offers a promising route to valuable fuels; however, stable performance is hindered by gas–liquid transport limitations in the cathode gas diffusion electrode (GDE), which are directly affected by GDE compression. Here, we showed that operating an alkaline zero-gap CO2 electrolyzer at a compression ratio (CR) of 10% achieved higher CO faradaic efficiency compared to CR = 20% and 30%. The improved performance is attributed to the fewer obstructions to gas transport in the 10% compressed cathode GDE, as evidenced by lower quantities of flooded anolyte and salt precipitates observed via synchrotron X-ray radiography. Less salt precipitate formation in the cathode at CR = 10% is further demonstrated by an 82% smaller mass transport resistance compared to CR = 30%, a result attributed to larger pores in the less compressed GDE that mitigate anolyte entrapment. Additionally, lower amounts of anolyte are observed at the cathode flow field–GDE interface at CR = 10%, whereas at CR = 30%, excess liquid at this interface led to CO2 gas inlet blockage and cell voltage instabilities. As such, operating the CO2 electrolyzer at lower CRs is recommended to facilitate gas–liquid transport within the cathode GDE and thereby improve overall cell performance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42959-1.Keywords: CO2 electrolyzer, Mechanical compression, Faradaic efficiency, Precipitate degradation, FloodingSubject terms: Chemistry, Energy science and technology, Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

The growing awareness of the effects of anthropogenic CO2 emissions has driven the advancement of CO2 utilization technologies. One promising approach to utilize CO2 is via the electrocatalytic CO2 reduction reaction (CO2RR), which presents a low-carbon pathway for producing carbon-based products1. Specifically, by utilizing the CO2RR in an electrolyzer reactor, reactant CO2, water, and renewable electricity can be converted into valuable chemicals and fuels. Moreover, the CO2RR can be tuned for the production of specific C1/C2+ products such as carbon monoxide, ethylene, ethanol, and n-propanol, which are conventionally produced on a million-ton scale via thermochemical processes that are known for their high emissions2,3. The successful deployment of CO2RR at scale necessitates advances in electrolyzer component designs to enable efficient and selective operation under industrially relevant conditions.

The adoption of the membrane electrode assembly (MEA) cell architecture has enabled CO2 electrolysis to operate at industrially relevant current densities ( 200 mA cm−2) while maintaining selective conversion to desired products, such as C1 and C2+ products4,5. A crucial component of MEA cells is the gas diffusion electrode (GDE). The structure and morphology of the GDE plays an essential role in delivering reactant CO2 gas to the catalyst layer, transporting products to the flow field, and preventing the intrusion of liquid anolyte into the cathode electrode6,7. The role of GDEs in MEA CO2 electrolyzers becomes especially significant when operating at relatively high current densities (> 150 mA cm−2), since ensuring reactant supply via effective diffusion of CO2 to the catalyst layer is required. To boost CO2RR selectivity over the competing hydrogen evolution reaction (HER), alkaline MEA CO2 electrolyzers are typically utilized8,9. However, these alkaline MEA CO2 electrolyzers are plagued by the formation of hygroscopic carbonate-based precipitate salts in the cathode GDE, which inhibits both CO2RR and HER (Fig. 1). The salts reduce the hydrophobicity and induce flooding, diminish CO2 access to CO2RR reaction sites, and promote the undesired HER (Fig. 1). Hence, improving mass transport behaviour within the GDE is key to designing next generation of CO2 electrolyzers with high efficiencies at high current density conditions.

(a) Schematic of a zero-gap MEA alkaline CO2 electrolyzer, where humidified CO2 in the cathode and potassium bicarbonate (KHCO3 (aq)) in the anode facilitate the production of CO as the desired product at the silver cathode. (b) Schematic diagram illustrating species transport, electrochemical reactions, anolyte flooding, and salt precipitation in the cathode GDE. In the ideally wet GDE region, CO2 reduction reactions (CO2RR) occur, resulting in CO production, in the anolyte-flooded GDE region, the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) occurs, producing undesired H2, and in the salt-precipitation region, neither CO2RR nor HER takes place.

The role of the reaction microenvironment in MEA-type CO2 electrolyzers has been described in prior studies, showing that tailoring ionomer distribution in the catalyst layer10,11 or adjusting feed composition12,13 can influence local concentrations of CO2, H2O, and ionic species (e.g., hydroxide, bicarbonate), which in turn affects activity and selectivity14,15,16. Since these microenvironmental conditions depend on how reactants and ions distribute within the porous network, component design in the MEA, including the morphology of the gas diffusion electrode, contributes to shaping these effects. Consequently, alterations or deformation of the GDE structure influence local transport pathways and modify the microenvironmental parameters within the MEA.

To better understand GDE transport processes, previous studies have sought to link GDE design characteristics (e.g., microstructures and hydrophobicity) to desirable performance attributes7,17–22. For example, Samu et al.17 demonstrated that the absence of a microporous layer (MPL) significantly increased the undesired HER in an MEA CO2 electrolyzer. They also found that the presence of cracks in the MPL further enhanced electrochemical performance due to improved mass transport behavior within the cathode GDE. Dinh et al.22 introduced a novel cathode electrode configuration with enhanced hydrophobicity, where the catalyst layer was sandwiched between polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) and carbon nanoparticles, achieving a 70% CO2-to-ethylene faradaic efficiency (FE) over 150 h of operation. Furthermore, Baumgartner et al.18 investigated gas diffusion layer (GDL) substrates with various microstructures (carbon paper and carbon cloth) coated with a silver catalyst for CO production. They found that the bimodal pore structure of the carbon cloth GDEs exhibited better performance compared to carbon papers, resulting in an average CO faradaic efficiency of 57% at 180 mA cm− 2 over 150 h, even when flooded with electrolyte.

These studies explored various GDE designs to enhance the performance of MEA CO2 electrolyzers; however, even with an optimized GDE design, the performance of CO2 electrolysis may not improve if unoptimized GDE compression in the cell is present23. Variations in compression alter the thickness and porosity of the GDE, significantly impacting cell selectivity for desired product formation, making compression a key parameter for the optimization of large-scale MEA CO2 electrolyzer performance24. Inappropriate GDE compression can adversely affect the delivery of reactant and product gases and exacerbate liquid anolyte invasion into the cathode, thereby impairing the performance of CO2 electrolysis for efficient production of desired products17,24. 3D structural analysis of GDEs using X-ray computed tomography and focused ion beam scanning electron microscopy24,25 has been employed to elucidate the compression-induced morphological changes in the GDE. However, these techniques do not capture dynamic, time-resolved mass transport phenomena under operating conditions, limiting the ability to correlate alteration in GDE structure with corresponding changes in mass transport behaviour.

Furthermore, analytical techniques, such as electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), have proven useful for characterizing electrochemical losses caused by improper cell compression23; nonetheless, the impact of compression on gas and liquid transport within the GDE remains ambiguous. Therefore, in addition to the analytical methods, experimental validation, especially spatially resolved measurements, is essential for fully understanding and optimizing the effects of compression on liquid and gas transport within a MEA CO2 electrolyzer.

Real-time distributions of salt, gas, and liquid within the membranes and GDEs in CO2 electrolyzers has been uncovered in the literature through X-ray and neutron operando imaging, offering valuable insights into mass transport behaviour and operational behaviors26–32. For example, localized salt precipitation under cathode channels has been revealed using neutron imaging, which offers resolutions as fine as 6 μm27. Furthermore, dynamic oscillations between salt precipitation and dissolution have been observed through operando wide-angle X-ray scattering (WAXS), highlighting strategies to optimize anolyte composition and concentration28. Enhanced membrane hydration and significant reductions in ohmic resistance, achieved by increasing cell temperature or humidifying CO2 reactants, have been identified using X-ray synchrotron radiography29. These discoveries demonstrate the utility of advanced imaging techniques in addressing mass transport challenges and advancing CO2 electrolyzer performance. As such, using operando imaging techniques can reveal how solid salt, gaseous reactants and products, and liquid anolyte distribute in real time in the membranes and GDEs of CO2 electrolyzers. In particular, investigating the impact of GDE compression—a parameter that directly affects the mass transport behaviour responsible for anolyte flooding and salt precipitation—is a critical first step towards advancing CO2 electrolyzers.

In this work, we correlate the compression of the cathode GDE to the distribution of anolyte and reactant/product gases, as well as the resulting salt-induced flooding in the cathode of an alkaline MEA CO2 electrolyzer. Three compression ratios (10%, 20%, and 30%) were applied by varying the PTFE gasket thickness surrounding the cathode and anode GDEs. We characterize the temporal and spatial liquid/gas transport behavior within the compressed GDEs through both electrochemical analysis and synchrotron operando X-ray imaging.

Results and discussion

Lower GDE compression reduces anolyte stagnation and salt precipitation in the cathode

We first present the X-ray absorbance through-plane profiles of the cathode GDE (across the thickness of the cathode) for both land and channel areas at compression ratios of CR = 10%, 20%, and 30% (Fig. 2) to examine the impact of GDE compression on the distribution of the anolyte. In both the land and channel regions, we observed a higher average X-ray absorbance of the anolyte at higher cathode GDE CRs (Fig. 2). Due to the known occurrence of flooding and salt precipitation in the cathode GDE of an alkaline CO2 electrolyzer1,9,25 we attribute the observed increase in X-ray absorbance to one or both of the following factors (i) higher anolyte saturation and (ii) higher anolyte concentration (potentially leading to salt precipitation) within the cathode. Moreover, since humidified CO2 was supplied to the cathode GDE, the increase in X-ray absorbance can also indicate higher water saturation in the cathode GDE due to condensation. Notably, we were not able to determine the proportion of anolyte and condensed water in the cathode from Fig. 2, since the two likely mix with one another, altering the local anolyte concentration and consequently the X-ray attenuation coefficient. However, depending on the specific region (flow field-GDL interface, substrate, and MPL + CL) and the morphology of that region at different CRs, the relative contribution of each of these factors to the increase in X-ray absorbance may vary, as described below.

At CR = 30%, we observe a local X-ray absorbance maximum in the MPL + CL region. The magnitude of this local absorbance maximum increases over time, particularly in the land regions (Fig. 2a). Given that the CL was saturated with anolyte for ionomer conditioning, the pores of the CL were saturated with anolyte prior to the experiment. As such, we attribute the increase in X-ray absorbance in the MPL + CL region to an increase in anolyte concentration and the subsequent salt formation during cell operation. At high CRs (e.g., CR = 30%), a reduction in GDE thickness was observed (Fig. S1). Since the carbon fibers in the GDE can be considered incompressible33, we assume the cathode GDE became less porous and more tortuous. This decrease in porosity at high CRs has been extensively demonstrated in the compression of fuel cell GDLs34–37. At the highest compression ratio, CR = 30%, we assume that the cathode GDE became more tortuous and less porous (as has been extensively demonstrated in the compression of fuel cell GDLs34–37, especially under the land regions, which impede anolyte drainage from the CL to the GDL. We expect that at a high current density of 200 mA cm-2, water is rapidly consumed from the anolyte to sustain the CO2RR and HER. Due to the restricted water replenishment in the less porous GDE at CR = 30%, the local anolyte concentration is expected to increase and promote salt crystal formation at the cathode. Our rationale for more salt precipitation at higher GDE CRs is further confirmed by EIS results at each CR. As such, we attribute the increase in X-ray absorbance in the MPL + CL region to an increase in anolyte concentration and the subsequent salt formation during cell operation. Previous XRD and WAXS experiments28 have identified both K2CO3 and KHCO3 as possible precipitate phases when dilute KHCO3 electrolytes are used.

At CRs of 10% and 20%, a local maximum in X-ray absorbance is observed in the transition region, specifically within the channel area (Fig. 2b). We also attribute this local maximum to anolyte accumulation, which increases the local anolyte saturation at this region. Previous works have demonstrated that a local porosity minimum exists in the MPL (previously reported in the SGL 25 BC substrate by Banerjee et al.38); this porosity minimum leads to locally reduced capillary pressure and promotes liquid accumulation. The observed local X-ray absorbance maximum is more pronounced at CR = 10%, where the porosity gradient between the GDL substrate and MPL is larger compared to that at CR = 20%. A similar rationale applies to the channel area at the transition region, where lower compression compared to the land area leads to a larger local porosity gradient between the GDL and MPL, resulting in larger quantities of anolyte accumulation, and consequently higher X-ray absorbance in the channel. The elevated anolyte saturation at the transition region, specifically at lower CRs, suggests that designing GDEs with a more gradual through-plane porosity gradient could promote a uniform capillary pressure distribution, mitigating anolyte accumulation in the transition region.

The regions in white, light grey, grey, and dark grey indicate flow field (FF)-GDL interface, GDL substrate, MPL+ catalyst layer (CL) transition region, and MPL + CL, respectively. The region labeled as MPL + CL (dark grey) was based on the assumption that a portion of the MPL intruded into the CL. At CR = 10%, we observed lower X-ray absorbance of anolyte, specifically at the FF-GDL interface and MPL + CL, compared to CR = 20% and 30%, indicating more gas transport pathways in the cathode GDE.

It is interesting to note that the local X-ray absorbance peak in the GDL substrate shifts toward the flow field (FF) region and becomes more pronounced over time and with increasing CR, particularly in the land region (Fig. 2). We attribute this intensification to water condensation from the humidified CO2 inlet which accumulates in the less permeable substrate at higher compression ratios (CR = 20% and 30%). The local X-ray absorbance peak at the GDL–FF interface is more prominent in the land region than in the channel region, likely due to additional water condensation on the surface of the hydrophilic titanium flow field land (Fig. 2b).

We next present the cell voltage behavior at CRs of 10, 20, and 30% (Fig. 3) and observe an instability in cell voltage, which likely results from a temporal increase in liquid saturation at the cathode FF–GDL interface shown in Fig. 2. We hypothesize that this increase in the liquid saturation creates an unstable two-phase flow (humidified CO2 gas and condensed water and anolyte), which disrupts the CO2 gas flow to the CL and results in voltage fluctuations. During the 20–30 min interval, the voltage fluctuation amplitude peaked at 0.06 V for CR = 30%, significantly higher than the 0.007 V observed for both 10% and 20% CRs (Fig. 3). While voltage fluctuations may occur due to GDE collapse, irrecoverable GDE deformation or damage was not observed after ex-situ SEM analysis of the GDEs (see Fig. S2). As such, the greater voltage fluctuation at CR = 30% is primarily due to the higher liquid saturation at the GDL-FF interface compared to CR = 10% and 20% (Fig. 2), rather than from irrecoverable GDE deformation or damage. The voltage fluctuations cannot be attributed to oxygen bubble accumulation at the anode GDE since the X-ray absorbance measurements for all CRs revealed a homogeneous anolyte distribution in the anode CL, indicating that oxygen bubble accumulation did not occur (Fig. 4). Instead, the more significant accumulation of water at the FF-GDL transition region at CR = 30% compared to CR = 10% and CR = 20% establishes a barrier to the CO2 gas flow at the FF-GDL transition region, which leads to the voltage fluctuations observed at 30% CR (Fig. 3). As such, we reveal that operating the CO2 electrolyzer cell at lower CRs improves gas-liquid two-phase transport within the cathode GDE.

Lower GDE compression reduces CO2 electrolyzer losses

To correlate the changes in liquid/precipitate saturation in the cathode GDE with the electrochemical performance of the electrolyzer, we quantified the charge transfer, ohmic, and mass transfer resistances, as shown in Fig. 5. The charge transfer, ohmic, and mass transfer resistances in the cell are calculated by fitting a modified Randles circuit39 to the Nyquist plots obtained at the beginning (t = 0) and end (t = 90 min) of the galvanostatic operation for each compression ratio (10%, 20%, and 30%) and plotted in Fig. 5. The equivalent circuit model fitting and Kramers–Kronig test both show good agreement with the Nyquist plots (Fig. 5a–e), demonstrating the reliability of the EIS analysis conducted on the AEM CO2 electrolyzer. Since a two-electrode configuration is used for the EIS measurements, the charge transfer resistances associated with the CO2RR and HER (cathode) and OER (anode) were convoluted in the defined frequency range. Consequently, the total charge transfer resistance, including both anodic and cathodic reactions, is reported in Fig. 5g.

(d) Modified Randles model used to fit the Nyquist plots in (a-c). (e) Lin-KK test fit comparing impedances obtained at t = 90 min to the Kramers-Kronig relations at 10%, 20%, and 30% CRs. Right: Changes in (f) charge transfer resistance (RCT), (g) ohmic resistance (Rohm), and (h) mass transfer resistance (Rmt) at 10%, 20%, and 30% CR before and after galvanostatic step (t = 0 min and 90 min). The error bars represent standard deviation among three independent tests. The cell with GDE CR = 10% exhibited an improvement in losses.

EIS results shown in Fig. 5f reveal a larger mass transfer resistance (Rmt) at CR = 30% (1.38 Ω cm2) compared to that at 10% CR (0.19 Ω cm2) at the end of cell operation (t = 90 min) (Fig. 5f). The larger mass transfer resistance reflects the inhibited diffusion of both CO2 and water reactants to the cathode for CO2RR and HER, likely due to salt precipitation that blocks access to the active sites. The greater amount of precipitates at higher CRs arises from anolyte stagnation at higher CRs, which increases precipitate formation and accumulation within the cathode GDE (see Fig. 2). Although less precipitation occurs at the cathode with lower anolyte concentrations (e.g., 0.01–0.1 M KHCO312,13 compared to the 2 M KHCO3 used here), we expect that precipitation would still occur preferentially at higher CRs under these lower anolyte concentrations due to similar compression-induced transport effects in the GDE (i.e., anolyte stagnation at higher CRs). However, we propose that salt-induced degradation mechanisms at low anolyte concentrations would likely proceed more slowly over longer timescales and result in a more gradual divergence between CR = 10% and 30%. It is important to note that although anolyte flooding shifts the reaction selectivity from the CO2RR to the HER, the reactant water remains present at the cathode for the HER, indicating that mass transport resistances do not occur solely due to anolyte flooding.

We attribute the increase in mass transport resistance to cathode salt precipitates rather than oxygen accumulation in the anode. In the anode, we observe only minimal spatial variations in X-ray absorbance of the anolyte in both the land and channel regions for all CRs (0.014, -0.011, and 0.035 for CR = 10, 20, and 30%, respectively, Fig. 4), indicating negligible changes in anolyte distribution during operation compared to the reference state (reference state defined as the time when the anode was fully saturated with anolyte). The uniform anolyte distribution in the anode results from sufficient liquid anolyte flow towards the hydrophilic anode electrode, which facilitates the removal of oxygen gas bubbles generated from the OER, thereby mitigating mass transport losses in the anode. Thus, the mass transport loss in the cell is primarily attributed to the reactant transport losses on the cathode side, where salt precipitation inhibits both CO2 and water transport to the reaction sites.

While variations in the mass transport resistance reflects limitations in reactant transport to the reaction sites, changes in the charge transfer resistance (RCT) reveal how compression influences ionic accessibility at the catalyst–electrolyte interface. At CR = 10%, RCT was initially larger (2.46 Ω cm2, Fig. 5g) than that at 30% compression (1.31 Ω cm2). This higher resistance is attributed to the lower amount of anolyte observed in the MPL-CL of the 10% compressed GDE (i.e., lower X-ray absorbance at MPL-CL at CR = 10% compared to CR = 30% in Fig. 2), which reduced the availability of ions at the electrode–electrolyte interface that facilitate charge transfer. After 90 min of operation, we attribute the decrease of RCT at CRs of 10% and 20% to additional anolyte flooding in the CL. In contrast, RCT at 30% CR increases over the same period (Fig. 5g) which can be ascribed to the precipitate formation in the CL that disrupts ionic and electrical connections between the CL and reactants, reducing the electrochemically active surface area (ECSA).

It is interesting to note that the ohmic resistance did not change significantly when varying the compression ratio (Fig. 5h). This minimal variation indicates that the benefit of shorter ionic conductance pathways in the compressed membrane was suppressed by the reduced ionic conductivity of the membrane caused by lower water uptake at higher compression ratios and possible mechanical degradation from over-compression. Previous works have also linked reduced membrane ionic conductivity to mechanical degradation and reduced water content in CO2 and water electrolyzers29,40–42. Thus, EIS results demonstrate that lower CRs, which still provide sufficient interfacial contact between the membrane and the GDE, reduce losses in the AEM CO2 electrolyzer. Although operation of CO2 electrolyzers at lower CRs is recommended, extremely low CRs (CR < 10%) increase the risk of cell gas and liquid leakages and insufficient contact between the membrane and electrodes, as has also been reported in fuel cell applications43. Evaluation of ohmic resistance via EIS, as well as other approaches such as pressure-sensitive films, can qualitatively assess the adequacy of interfacial contact between the membrane and electrodes in a sealed electrolyzer cell44,45.

Lower GDE compression improves CO FE stability

We next determined the stability of the AEM CO2 electrolyzer via analysis of the CO and H2 faradic efficiencies over time as a function of GDE compression ratios, as shown in Fig. 6a,b. The GDE at the CR of 10% exhibits a higher average CO FE (52.8%) compared to CR = 20% and 30% (48.8% and 41.9%, respectively). This higher CO FE at CR = 10% is attributed to more gas transport pathways in the cathode GDE due to the less liquid and salt precipitate at the cathode GDE compared to CR = 20% and 30%, as seen in Fig. 2 (lower average X-ray absorbance at CR = 10% compared to CR = 20% and 30%).

Top: Variations in the (a) CO and (b) H2 faradaic efficiencies (FEs) during the operation of the cell at 10%, 20%, and 30% CRs. The error bars represent the standard deviation among the three independent tests performed at each CR. Connecting lines are shown for readability and do not represent actual data. The cell at CR = 10% exhibited smaller CO FE drop and higher average CO FE over 90 min of cell operation.

A higher initial CO FE is observed at CRs = 30% and 20% (78% and 72%, respectively) compared to CR = 10% (65%) (Fig. 6a), and we attribute this to a shorter diffusion length of the CO2 reactant at higher compressions of GDE. However, over time, the increased occupation of GDE pores by the anolyte and salt precipitate at CR = 30% (Fig. 2) hindered CO2 transport. As a result, CO FE decreased more significantly and rapidly at CR = 30% (from 78% to 17%) compared to CR = 10% (from 65% to 32%) and CR = 20% (from 72% to 23%) by the end of the experiment. We attribute these observations to the less permeable and more tortuous transport pathways in the cathode GDE at higher CRs, leading to the GDE retaining anolyte and promoting undesired competing reactions (i.e., HER). Ultimately, these conditions encouraged unfavorable salt precipitate formation in the cathode. While the H2 FE increases significantly during the first 4000 s (66 min) of operation at CR = 30%, the H2 FE remains relatively stable after 66 min (Fig. 6b). The stabilization of the H2 FE further indicates precipitate formation in the compressed GDE, where we expect that precipitates will restrict water access to the reaction sites and suppress the HER. We propose that during longer cell operation, more salt (which is hygroscopic) precipitates in the GDE at CR = 30% than at other CRs, leading to reduced GDE hydrophobicity and more severe anolyte flooding in the cathode. While previous fuel cell works suggest that a change in the contact angle on the GDL surface may occur with higher compression46,47, the extent of this change in the contact angle has not been measured in the interior regions of the GDL. Other GDL works48,49 note that since the chemical composition of the GDL remains unchanged under compression, the wettability of the GDL will also remain unchanged. In accordance with the works of Gostick et al.48 and Park49, we attribute the changes in mass transport behaviour over a range of compression ratios to changes in GDL pore morphology rather than fiber wettability.

This work examines the electrochemical performance of an AEM CO2 electrolyzer under accelerated degradation conditions (high current density and high anolyte concentration) for a single cathode GDE structure. While the effect of compression can be assessed across various GDE morphologies and reaction rates and environments over extended operating times, we anticipate similar trends (i.e., enhanced two-phase transport and improved stability and electrochemical performance at lower CRs) to occur in other cell configurations. The reduction in GDE thickness observed in X-ray radiography images, along with the insights from prior fuel cell studies on compression-induced morphological changes34,35,37,50 indicate that compression reduces porosity in the GDE. These changes in the morphology of the GDE consequently hinder reactant and ion transport and influence the local reaction environment in the cathode. Prior studies have also shown that under high current density operation, the morphology of the GDE, which is directly influenced by compression and the associated mass-transport behavior, predominately governs electrolyzer performance24, whereas at low current densities, performance is dictated more by microenvironmental factors such as local pH51,52. Nonetheless, evaluation of CO2 electrolyzer behavior across diverse operational and design scenarios over long stability tests can provide broader insight into the extent of compression effects at various GDE micro-/nanostructures and reaction environments, informing generalized guidelines for optimizing CO2 electrolyzer operation.

Conclusions

In this study, we demonstrated that lower compression ratios (CRs) facilitate preferential gas–liquid mass transport behaviour in the cathode gas diffusion electrode (GDE) of an alkaline zero-gap CO2 electrolyzer, which leads to improved overall electrochemical cell performance. Synchrotron X-ray radiography revealed less anolyte inside the cathode GDE and at the flow field–GDE interface at lower CRs. This anolyte distribution indicates more CO2 gas transport pathways leading to the reaction sites and less salt formation due to anolyte entrapment. Additionally, insignificant variations in X-ray absorbance in the anode at all CRs indicated that compression predominantly affects mass transport behavior in the cathode. The lower CR (i.e., CR = 10%) helped to mitigate the tendency for increasing mass and charge transport losses over time, and this CR was still sufficient for establishing good interfacial contact between the GDE and the membrane electrode assembly (MEA). Ultimately, this improved mass transport behaviour through more permeable and less tortuous transport pathways in the cathode GDE at the lower CRs led to higher average CO faradaic efficiency (CO FE) over the duration of cell operation.

This work offers insights into improving cell performance by controlling mass transport behaviour in the MEA through optimized compression, thereby extending device lifetimes and advancing CO2 electrolysis toward industrial applications.

Methods

In this section, we first describe the AEM CO2 electrolyzer hardware used for the GDE compression study. Then, we detail the methods used to evaluate the effect of GDE compression on the electrochemical performance of the cell through galvanostatic operation, gas chromatography (GC), and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS)). Finally, we explain the methods and procedures used in operando synchrotron X-ray radiography imaging to determine anolyte distributions within the cathode GDE and anode catalyst layer (CL)-gas diffusion layer (GDL) interface during cell operation.

AEM CO2 electrolyzer hardware

A custom AEM CO2 electrolyzer was designed for operando X-ray synchrotron radiography. The titanium cathode and anode flow field plates were electroplated with 2.5 μm-thick Pt and 0.5 μm-thick Au to enhance corrosion resistance during cell operation. Although the Pt coating may have introduced some catalytic activity, we assume that H2 production from the Pt coating would be negligible since similar anti-corrosion coatings have been successfully used in water electrolyzers53–55. The flow field plates had 16 parallel channels with a cross-sectional area of 500 μm x 500 μm separated by 500 μm-wide lands. Commercially available cathode and anode GDEs (Dioxide Materials) were used. The cathode GDE consisted of an SGL 25 BC substrate coated with a silver catalyst with an areal density of 1 mg cm⁻², and the anode GDE was comprised of a Toray GDL substrate coated with an iridium oxide catalyst with an areal density of 1 mg cm⁻². Additionally, a commercially sourced Sustainion membrane (Sustainion® X37-50 grade RT, Dioxide Materials) was used as the anion exchange membrane. To activate the ion transport properties of the ionomer within the membrane and catalyst layer (CL) of the GDEs before cell operation, the membrane and CL side of the GDEs were soaked in a 1 M potassium hydroxide (KOH) solution (ACS Reagent > 85% Pellets and 18 MΩ deionized water) for 24 h and 1 h, respectively. An active area of 0.8 cm2 (length of 1.6 cm and width of 0.5 cm) was employed in the cell to ensure sufficient X-ray transmission within the typical X-ray spot size.

To ensure sufficient contact between different components of the AEM CO2 electrolyzer, polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) gaskets were used to control the compression condition of the cell. The cell was tightened using a torque of 15 lb in. Electrochemical experiments

The electrochemical performance at various compression ratios was measured in conjunction with X-ray imaging to investigate the effect of non-uniform GDE deformation on the stability of the cell voltage, the selectivity of CO product over time, and the electrochemical losses in the cell. For the electrochemical testing, the anolyte (2 M potassium bicarbonate (KHCO3, ACS Reagent > 99% powder dissolved in 18 MΩ deionized water)) was supplied at a flow rate of 2 mL min− 1 to the anode compartment using a peristaltic pump (Masterflex L/S precision variable speed console drive, Cole Parmer). A pulse damper (Masterflex Pulse Dampener for L/S Tubing) was used to stabilize the anolyte flow before entering the cell. The cell was heated to an operating temperature of 40 °C using a Thermo Fisher water bath (Fisherbrand Isotemp 4100) and monitored using a thermocouple (OMEGA 5SRTC). Humidified CO2 (Linde U.S.P > 99% by volume) with a relative humidity (RH) of 90% was fed at a flow rate of 60 standard cubic centimeters per minute (sccm) into the cathode compartment through a mass flow controller and a gas humidifier in a Scribner 850E fuel cell test station.

Transient variations in the cell voltage in response to the applied current density at each compression ratio were measured under galvanostatic operation using a Gamry Reference 3000 Potentiostat. To observe the impact of salt-induced flooding on the AEM CO2 electrolyzer performance, salt precipitation and flooding at the cathode were intentionally accelerated by applying a relatively high current density of 200 mA cm− 2 (for 90 min) and using a high-molarity anolyte (2 M KHCO3 (typical molarity is 1 M (Sassenburg et al., 2022)) during a 90-minute operando imaging session at each compression ratio. The 90-minute operation duration for each compression ratio was chosen due to the limited imaging session time. This time window facilitated the completion of all experiments while still being sufficient to capture cell performance degradation under the accelerated conditions of high current density and elevated anolyte concentration. Moreover, the CO FE dropped below 25% for most experiments after 90 min of operation, indicating that the cell selectivity for CO2RR was no longer practically desirable.

Operando X-ray radiography for revealing anolyte distribution in cathode GDE

To investigate the relationship between the GDE deformation caused by compression and the distribution of anolyte within the cathode, the custom CO2 electrolyzer was visualized at the Biomedical Imaging and Therapy Facility Insertion Device Beamline (BMIT- ID, 05ID) at the Canadian Light Source (CLS) in Saskatoon, Canada65. The operando X-ray radiography imaging was conducted using the testing conditions previously defined. The X-ray beam penetrates the CO2 electrolyzer cell along the Z axis parallel to the flow fields as shown in Fig. 7a. The attenuated X-ray beam was converted into visible light using an AA40 scintillator (CRYTUR spol. S r. o.) and captured with a scientific complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (sCMOS) camera (ORCA-Flash4.0 C11440-22CU, Hamamatsu Photonics K.K.), resulting in a pixel resolution of 6.5 μm/pixel. The images were captured at an energy level of 30 keV with a 0.2 s exposure time, a beam current of 219 mA, a field delay of 0.02 s, and a frame rate of 4.45 frames per second. A raw and a processed X-ray radiograph of the compressed MEA are shown in Fig. 7b and c, respectively.

To visualize the distribution of anolyte in the cathode GDE, three sets of images were captured: (i) reference images captured when the cathode and anode GDEs were fully saturated with their respective reactants (humidified CO2 in cathode and anolyte in anode) under the reference state (zero current density), (ii) operational images acquired during electrochemical operation (constant current density of 200 mA cm− 2) after steady state was established (12 min after applying constant current density), and (iii) dark field images obtained in the absence of X-ray beam. The dark field images were then subtracted from the reference and operational images to remove the background noise caused by the camera. The reference and operational images were used to calculate X-ray absorbance (described in the next section).

Prior to calculating the X-ray absorbance, to correct the images for any unintended cell movement during the imaging, the Stackreg plugin in Fiji66 was used to align the operational and reference images. Further, to correct for changes in the incident beam intensity during the experiments, the intensity of each image was normalized to the initial image using a reference region unaffected by any reactant and product over time (such as the regions above the MEA). These image-processing steps and procedures were consistent with the past literature29,67–69.
