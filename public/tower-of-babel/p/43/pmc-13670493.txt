Constitutive overexpression of GRF21, a 14-3-3 family protein, delays flowering time in maize (Zea mays)
Authors: Zetian Deng, Nipapan Kanjana, Jinghui Dong, Chuanhong Wang, Feiyang Zhan, Yibing Zhao, Tengyue Wang, Xin Peng, Peijin Li, Zhen Tao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFlowering time represents a pivotal agronomic trait that determines regional adaptability and grain yield in maize (Zea mays). Members of the 14-3-3 protein family, also known as general regulatory factors (GRFs), function as central modulators of diverse developmental and physiological processes in plants. However, their specific roles in regulating flowering time in maize remain undefined.ResultsHere, we present a functional characterization of GRF21, a maize GRF family member, with particular emphasis on its role in floral transition. Through an integrative approach combining phylogenetic analysis, gene transformation, and genome-wide transcriptomic analysis, we show that constitutive overexpression of GRF21 delays flowering in maize, suggesting a potential role in flowering-time regulation. GRF21 localizes to both the nucleus and cytoplasm and accumulates predominantly in roots, stems, and reproductive tissues. Furthermore, transcriptomic analyses, supported by quantitative RT-PCR and dual-luciferase reporter assays, reveal that GRF21 represses the transcription of key flowering-promoting genes, including Zea mays CENTRORADIALIS 7 (ZCN7), ZCN8, and ZCN12, thereby suppressing floral transition.ConclusionsCollectively, these findings implicate GRF21 as a negative regulator of flowering time in maize, as evidenced by the delayed flowering phenotype associated with its overexpression. This work expands the current molecular framework underlying floral initiation and highlights GRF21 as a promising target for maize breeding.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09471-0.

Background

Maize (Zea mays) is one of the world’s most important cereal crops, serving as a major source of food, feed, and industrial raw materials. Consequently, stable grain yield is essential for global food security and agricultural sustainability [1]. Flowering time represents a critical agronomic trait that determines regional adaptability and yield potential in maize by coordinating the vegetative phase transition with subsequent reproductive development [2, 3]. This complex developmental process is tightly regulated by the integration of endogenous genetic programs and phytohormonal signals with external environmental cues [4]. Accumulating evidence indicates that maize flowering time is highly responsive to environmental signals, including photoperiod, drought stress, and temperature fluctuations [5]. Photoperiod-dependent regulation has been particularly well characterized. For instance, the expression level of ZmCCT, a key regulator of photoperiod sensitivity, influences flowering time and facilitated the historical adaptation of maize from tropical to high-latitude temperate regions, thereby providing an essential genetic basis for breeding programs targeting day-length adaptability [6]. Similarly, the circadian clock regulator GIGANTEA1 (GI1) represses the expression of CONZ1 and the FLOWERING LOCUS T (FT) ortholog Zea mays CENTRORADIALIS 8 (ZCN8) under long-day conditions, resulting in delayed flowering; under short-day conditions, this repression is alleviated, promoting floral transition [7, 8]. Notably, modifications of flowering-associated genes frequently exert pleiotropic effects on plant growth and developmental architecture [9]. Recent studies have further demonstrated that ZmFKF1b significantly contributes to both geographic adaptation and yield improvement through modulation of flowering time. In particular, the advantageous haplotype Hap_C7 accelerates flowering and has facilitated the widespread cultivation of maize across temperate and tropical regions [10].

The 14-3-3 protein family, also known as general regulatory factors (GRFs), comprises a group of highly conserved acidic dimeric proteins ubiquitous in eukaryotic cells [11–13]. These proteins function as canonical phosphopeptide-binding modules that recognize phosphorylated motifs in client proteins via a conserved dimer interface and central cargo-binding groove, thereby regulating diverse intracellular signaling pathways and biological processes [14, 15]. In plants, 14-3-3 proteins participate in many aspects of growth, development, and stress responses [12, 13]. For instance, the maize gene ZmGF14-3 is associated with plant height regulation; its overexpression in Arabidopsis results in dwarfism through modulation of gibberellin- and auxin-related signaling pathways [16]. In addition, 14-3-3 proteins positively regulate plant immunity by interacting with defense-related proteins such as RESISTANCE TO POWDERY MILDEW 8 (RPW8) and the N protein [17]. They are also implicated in responses to multiple abiotic stresses, including drought, salinity, low temperature, and heavy metal exposure [18]. Collectively, these findings establish 14-3-3 proteins as integrative signaling hubs linking environmental perception, hormone signaling, and developmental regulation [19, 20].

Recent advances have revealed that 14-3-3 proteins play pivotal roles in floral organ development and flowering time control [21]. Acting as core components of the florigen activation complex (FAC) [22, 23], 14-3-3 proteins mediate the interaction between florigen (FT-like proteins) and FLOWERING LOCUS D (FD) transcription factors, thereby converting systemic flowering signals into transcriptional outputs at the shoot apical meristem. Specifically, 14-3-3 proteins function as molecular “signal sorters” by selectively binding distinct FD proteins (e.g., OsFD1 and OsFD2) and directing them into the FAC [24]. In cotton, GhGRF proteins interact with GhFT and GhFD to assemble the FAC, with GhGRF3/6/9/15 acting as flowering repressors to ensure appropriate flowering timing and environmental adaptation [25]. Similarly, in rice, the florigen Hd3a forms a 14-3-3-dependent FAC to regulate flowering and additionally functions as a mobile signal promoting lateral branching [26]. More recently, 14-3-3 proteins have been shown to facilitate FAC assembly in floral primordia by modulating the liquid–liquid phase separation properties of FD transcription factors, enhancing their dimerization and DNA-binding capacity. These findings further underscore the central role of 14-3-3 proteins in integrating environmental signals with developmental programs during floral transition [27]. Despite these advances in other plant species, functional studies of 14-3-3 proteins in maize remain limited. In particular, whether specific maize GRF members regulate flowering time has not been systematically investigated. Addressing this knowledge gap is of considerable importance given the central role of flowering time in maize adaptation and breeding.

To elucidate the functional roles of 14-3-3 family members in maize flowering time regulation, GRFs were selected for molecular characterization. Transcriptome profiling data retrieved from the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database indicated that GRF21 (Zm00001d032231) is preferentially expressed in reproductive tissues, specifically tassels and ears, suggesting a functional role in floral transition. Subcellular localization analysis demonstrated that GRF21 is distributed across both the cytoplasm and nucleus. Overexpression of GRF21 in transgenic maize plants resulted in significantly delayed flowering, suggesting that GRF21 may act as a negative regulator of floral transition. Transcriptome profiling, quantitative RT‑PCR, and dual‑luciferase reporter assays further demonstrated that GRF21 is negatively correlated with the expression of key flowering‑promoting genes, including ZCN7 and ZCN8, providing a mechanistic basis for its inhibitory effect on flowering time. These results identify a previously uncharacterized function for a maize 14-3-3 protein in floral transition regulation and extend the molecular regulatory network governing floral transition in maize.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

To investigate the flowering phenotype associated with GRF21, a ubiquitin promoter–driven GRF21–MYC fusion construct (Ubi::GRF21–MYC) was generated, and GRF21-overexpressing (GRF21-OE) transgenic maize lines were produced through Agrobacterium-mediated transformation by Weimi Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Changzhou, China). The wild-type inbred line B104, obtained from the same company, was used as the control. To evaluate flowering performance under natural conditions, field trials were conducted in Sanya, Hainan, China (18°15′N, 109°30′E) [28]. A randomized planting method was adopted, with 10–15 replicates per genotype under standard agronomic management practices. For flowering time assessment, the number of days from seedling emergence to tasseling and to anthesis (pollen shedding) was recorded. At least 10 individual plants per line were included for phenotypic evaluation. In addition to field experiments, plants were grown under controlled greenhouse conditions in the greenhouse at Anhui Agricultural University. The growth conditions were maintained at 28 °C under a 16 h light / 8 h dark photoperiod and 22 °C during the dark period, with a light intensity of approximately 400 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹ [29].

Gene cloning and vector construction

Total RNA was extracted from the young leaves of the maize inbred line B104 using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. First-strand cDNA was synthesized using a reverse transcription kit (TaKaRa, China, cat: RR047A). The full-length coding sequence (CDS) of GRF21 was amplified by PCR using gene-specific primers (GRF21-F: 5′-ATGGCGAGGGAGGAGAATGTCTA-3′; GRF21-R: 5′-CTGTCCGTCACCAGCCTCACTT-3′), with B104 cDNA as the template. The PCR product was purified and ligated into the pEASY-T3 cloning vector (TransGen, China, cat: CT301-01), followed by transformation into Escherichia coli DH5α competent cells. Positive colonies were screened by colony PCR and subsequently confirmed by Sanger sequencing. For overexpression analysis, the verified GRF21 CDS was subcloned downstream of the maize ubiquitin (Ubi) promoter using the In-Fusion cloning system (Vazyme, China, cat: C112-02), generating the recombinant construct Ubi::GRF21–MYC. The construct was further confirmed by sequencing prior to maize transformation.

Molecular phylogenetic analysis

To examine the relationships of 14-3-3 family proteins between maize and Arabidopsis thaliana (A. thaliana), a phylogenetic tree was constructed using the Neighbor-Joining (NJ) method implemented in MEGA11 software [30]. Amino acid sequences of GRF proteins from maize and Arabidopsis were retrieved from the GenBank database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov). Accession numbers for all sequences used in the analysis are listed in Table 1. Multiple sequence alignment was performed prior to phylogenetic reconstruction using the default parameters in MEGA11. The reliability of tree topology was evaluated using bootstrap analysis with 1,000 replicates.

Subcellular localization analysis

To determine the subcellular localization of GRF21, the full-length CDS without the stop codon was amplified with PCR method and cloned into the multiple cloning site of a modified pCAMBIA1305-GFP vector under the control of the cauliflower mosaic virus (CaMV) 35 S promoter, generating the fusion construct 35 S::GRF21–GFP.

For transient gene expression analysis in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves, the recombinant construct was introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101. The transformed bacterial suspension was adjusted in infiltration buffer (10 mM MgCl₂, 10 mM MES, pH 5.6, and 200 µM acetosyringone) [31] to an optical density at 600 nm (OD₆₀₀) of 1.0. The suspension was then delivered into the abaxial surface of fully expanded leaves of 4 to 5-week-old N. benthamiana plants using a needleless syringe. Following infiltration, plants were maintained under long-day growth conditions (16 h light and 8 h dark) at 25 °C for 48 h prior to confocal imaging. Fluorescence signals were captured using a laser scanning confocal microscope (Zeiss LSM800, Jena, Germany).

To complement the heterologous system, homologous expression was assessed by delivering the recombinant plasmid into maize mesophyll protoplasts via a polyethylene glycol (PEG)-mediated transformation protocol [29]. A nuclear localization signal (NLS) derived from Saccharomyces cerevisiae fused to red fluorescent protein (RFP) was co-transformed to serve as a nuclear marker [29]. Transformed protoplasts were subsequently incubated in darkness at 26 °C for 24 h, after which fluorescence signals were imaged using the same confocal microscope system described above.

Quantitative Real-Time PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from leaves of wild-type B104 and GRF21-overexpression (GRF21-OE) plants collected at the V6 stage (6th leaf fully expanded) using the RNAiso Plus Kit (TaKaRa, Dalian, China, ALF1045A), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The V6 stage was selected because it marks the initiation of floral transition in maize and has been widely used for transcriptome and flowering‑related gene expression analyses [32]. Genomic DNA contamination was removed, and first-strand cDNA was synthesized using the PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Perfect Real Time; TaKaRa, Dalian, China, RR047A). Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed using FastStart SYBR Green Master Mix (Roche, Basel, Switzerland; 4 673 492 001) on a real-time PCR system LC480C. The maize GAPDH gene was used as an internal reference control [9]. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2^−ΔCt method [33]. Primer sequences used for RT-qPCR are listed in Supplementary Table S1. For GRF21 amplification, the primer pair was designed to span an exon-intron junction, with the forward primer annealing within exon 2 and the reverse primer within exon 3, thereby preventing co-amplification of genomic DNA and ensuring that amplification products are derived exclusively from cDNA templates.

For tissue-specific expression analysis of GRF21, total RNA was extracted from roots, stems, tassels, ears, leaf sheaths, leaves, and flag leaves collected at the mature stage. For developmental expression analysis, leaves were harvested at different vegetative stages (V6, V8, and V10–V16) to examine expression changes during the transition from vegetative to reproductive growth. Three independent biological replicates were performed for each stage [34], and each biological replicate included two technical replicates of qPCR.

Transcriptome analysis

Transcriptome sequencing was performed by LC-Bio Technology Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China). Total RNA was extracted using the RNAprep Pure Plant Kit (Tiangen, China; DP441) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA quality and integrity were evaluated using a NanoPhotometer® spectrophotometer (Implen, USA), a Qubit® 2.0 Fluorometer (Life Technologies, USA), and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA). High-quality RNA samples were used for library preparation. Briefly, mRNA was enriched and fragmented, followed by first-strand cDNA synthesis using random hexamer primers and reverse transcriptase. Second-strand cDNA synthesis was subsequently performed. After end repair, A-tailing, and adapter ligation, cDNA fragments of approximately 200 bp were size-selected using AMPure XP beads. The resulting libraries were amplified by PCR, purified, and subjected to paired-end sequencing on the Illumina NovaSeq 4000 platform (Illumina, USA) [35]. Raw reads were filtered to obtain clean reads by removing adaptor sequences, reads containing poly-N regions, and low-quality reads. Clean reads were aligned to the maize B73_v5 reference genome [36] using Hierarchical Indexing for Spliced Alignment of Transcripts 2 (HISAT2) tool. Gene expression levels were quantified as fragments per kilobase of exon per million mapped fragments (FPKM).

Statistical analysis of differential gene expression was performed using DESeq2. P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method to control the false discovery rate (FDR). Genes with |log₂ (fold changes) | > 1 and an adjusted P-value < 0.05 were defined as differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Functional enrichment analyses of DEGs were conducted using the Lianchuan OmicStudio cloud platform (https://www.omicstudio.cn/tool).

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

To assess the regulatory effect of GRF21 on ZCN8 promoter activity, the full-length CDS of GRF21 was cloned into a CaMV 35 S promoter-driven GFP expression vector to generate the effector construct (35 S::GRF21–GFP). The promoter region of ZCN8 (− 2,200 bp upstream of the transcription start site) was amplified with PCR, cloned into pEASY-T3, sequencing verified, and inserted into the pGreenII 0800-LUC [37] vector to generate the reporter construct (proZCN8::LUC). Both effector and reporter constructs were introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 and co-infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves. After infiltration, plants were incubated in the dark for 24 h and subsequently grown under long-day conditions (28 °C, 16 h light / 8 h dark) for an additional 48 h [38]. Leaves were sprayed with 1 mM D-luciferin containing 1% (v/v) Triton-x100, and luminescence signals were captured using a Tannon 5200 chemiluminescence imaging system (Shanghai, China).

For quantitative analysis, maize mesophyll protoplasts were co-transformed with 35 S::GRF21-GFP and proZCN8::LUC constructs using a polyethylene glycol (PEG)-mediated method [29]. Firefly luciferase (LUC) activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase (REN) activity to control for plasmid transformation efficiency. Relative promoter activity was determined using the Dual-Luciferase® Reporter Assay System (Yeasen Biotechnology, China). At least six independent biological replicates were performed for each experimental combination [9].

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) unless otherwise stated. Figure preparation and data organization were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0.1 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA), and all statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 18.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) [39]. For comparisons between two independent groups, a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test was employed. For multiple-group comparisons, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, followed by Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) post hoc test.

Result

Phylogenetic and protein sequence analysis of GRFS in maize and Arabidopsis

To define the evolutionary placement of GRF21 within the 14-3-3 protein family, a Neighbor‑Joining phylogenetic tree was constructed using 24 maize GRF proteins and 12 Arabidopsis homologs. Consistent with established classifications, GRF members segregated into two major clades, designated as the ε (epsilon) and non-ε subgroups (Fig. 1A). The ε and non-ε subgroups are distinguished by differences in exon‑intron structure and exon numbers [40], and these two clades appear to have undergone subfunctionalization following divergence from a common ancestral gene, reflecting distinct functional specialization within the 14-3-3 family [41].

In Arabidopsis, AtGRF1-8 clustered within the non-ε clade, whereas the remaining members grouped within the ε clade. Similarly, maize GRF proteins were distributed across these two subgroups [42], indicating structural conservation across monocots and dicots. Notably, GRF21 was positioned within the ε subgroup and shares conserved motifs with AtGRF3, AtGRF5, and AtGRF7; however, as these three AtGRFs belong to the non-ε subgroup, subgroup membership and motif conservation do not always correspond, and the phylogenetic relationships based on amino acid sequence structure are not simply defined by these two clades. The evolutionary relationship between maize and Arabidopsis 14-3-3 proteins is therefore more accurately reflected by the presence of shared conserved motifs (Fig. 1B) than by subgroup membership alone. Among the proteins examined across both species, the number of amino acid residues ranged from 135 residues (GRF28) to 500 residues (GRF3), and molecular weights ranged from 15.01 kDa (GRF28) to 53.42 kDa (GRF3).

To assess structural conservation, conserved motif analysis was performed using MEME. At least ten distinct motifs were identified across maize GRF proteins, among which motifs 1–6 were broadly conserved (Fig. 1B). The widespread preservation of these core motifs across both subgroups indicates strong sequence conservation within the maize GRF family. Collectively, these data establish the phylogenetic classification of GRF21 (Zm00001d032231) and demonstrate substantial structural conservation among maize and Arabidopsis 14-3-3 proteins.

Expression pattern of GRF21

To characterize the spatial and temporal expression pattern of GRF21, transcript abundance was quantified by RT–qPCR in different maize tissues and at multiple developmental stages. The results showed that GRF21 transcripts accumulated in all examined tissues, with preferential accumulation in reproductive and meristem-associated tissues. Relatively high expression levels were detected in tassels and ears, whereas transcript abundance was comparatively lower in roots, stems, and leaves (Fig. 2A). The expression dynamics of GRF21 during maize development showed that its transcript levels gradually increased during vegetative growth, reached a peak at the V14 stage, and subsequently declined during the transition to reproductive growth (Fig. 2B).

Overexpression of GRF21 delays flowering in maize

To determine the functional role of GRF21 in maize development, transgenic lines overexpressing GRF21 fused to a C-terminal MYC tag (Ubi::GRF21-MYC) were generated. RT–qPCR analysis demonstrated markedly higher GRF21 transcript levels in three independent lines (#2, #12, and #15) than in the wild-type control B104 (Fig. 3A). Consistently, immunoblot analysis confirmed robust accumulation of GRF21-MYC protein in these lines (Fig. 3B), validating successful overexpression at both the transcriptional and protein levels.

Phenotypic assessment revealed a pronounced delay in floral transition in GRF21-OE plants. Specifically, tasseling time occurred 9.2, 8.4, and 8.3 days later in lines 2, 12, and 15, respectively, than that in wild-type. Similarly, anthesis time was delayed by 7.4, 6.8, and 7.1 days in the corresponding lines (Fig. 3C-F). In addition to delayed flowering, GRF21-OE plants exhibited a significant reduction in plant height (Fig. S1A-B). Furthermore, ZMM4, a transcription factor with established roles in plant height regulation and floral transition, was downregulated in GRF21-OE plants (Fig. S1C), suggesting that GRF21 exerts pleiotropic effects on maize development beyond flowering time alone. Taken together, these findings demonstrate that elevated GRF21 expression delays the onset of floral transition and suppresses vegetative growth in maize.

GRF21 localizes in the cytoplasm and nucleus

To investigate the subcellular distribution of GRF21, a 35 S::GRF21–GFP fusion construct was generated and transiently expressed in N. benthamiana leaves via Agrobacterium-mediated infiltration. The results showed that GFP alone driven by the 35 S promoter exhibited ubiquitous fluorescence throughout the cell, including both the cytoplasm and nucleus. In contrast, the fluorescence signal of GRF21–GFP was relatively more abundant in the cytoplasm, although nuclear accumulation was also observed (Fig. 4A). For validate this localization pattern in the native system, GRF21–GFP was transiently expressed in maize protoplasts. Consistent with the observations in N. benthamiana, the fluorescence signal was more distributed in the cytoplasm, with a lower level of enrichment also detected in the nucleus (Fig. 4B).

GRF21 modulates the expression of flowering-related genes

To examine the transcriptional consequences of GRF21 overexpression, RNA sequencing was performed in leaves of wild-type (B104) and GRF21-OE plants collected at the V6 stage (6th leaf fully expanded), which represents the transition from vegetative to early reproductive development and is a critical stage for capturing flowering-related transcriptional changes. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed clear segregation between wild-type and overexpression samples, with tight clustering within each genotype, indicating high experimental reproducibility (Fig. 5A; Table S2). Differential expression analysis identified 359 genes exhibiting significant expression changes in GRF21-OE plants relative to wild type, including 145 up-regulated and 214 down-regulated genes (Fig. 5B; Table S3). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis demonstrated that these differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were significantly enriched in biological processes related to plant hormone signal transduction, regulation of flower development, and short-day photoperiodism, in addition to secondary metabolic and stress-associated pathways such as phenylpropanoid metabolism and coumarin biosynthesis (Fig. 5C, Table S4).

Discussion

GRF21 acts as a potential negative regulator of flowering time in maize

Flowering time is a pivotal determinant of maize regional adaptability and yield stability, and has undergone intense artificial and natural selection during the expansion of maize into diverse agroecological zones [45, 46]. Although numerous flowering regulators have been identified, the contribution of 14-3-3–mediated signaling networks to flowering control in maize remains largely unexplored [13, 19, 47]. Here, through integrated transgenic phenotyping, transcriptome profiling, and promoter activity assays, we demonstrate that constitutive overexpression of GRF21 significantly delays floral transition (Fig. 3B-F), establishing GRF21 as a negative regulator of flowering in maize. This result identifies a previously uncharacterized role for a 14-3-3 family member in maize flowering time regulation and extends the molecular framework governing this process in cereal crops. We acknowledge, however, that the present conclusions are based primarily on gain-of-function evidence, and that loss-of-function analysis and complementation studies will be required to fully establish the regulatory role of GRF21 in floral transition. Nevertheless, the convergent evidence from dual-luciferase assays, RT-qPCR, and RNA-seq analysis presented here provides a valuable foundation for future mechanistic dissection of 14-3-3-mediated flowering regulation in maize and related cereals.

Unlike classical transcription factors, 14-3-3 proteins function primarily as phospho-dependent adaptor proteins that modulate the stability, localization, and activity of client proteins [21, 48]. Therefore, the delayed-flowering phenotype observed in GRF21-OE plants likely reflects broader reprogramming of signaling networks. The presence of multiple highly conserved 14-3-3 homologs in maize further suggests potential functional diversification or redundancy, complicating reverse-genetic dissection but also indicating that flowering regulation may be embedded within a multilayered 14-3-3 interaction network.

Mechanistically, our data place GRF21 upstream of the florigen pathway [49]. RNA-seq and RT-qPCR analyses revealed significant downregulation of ZCN8, ZCN7, and ZCN12, maize orthologs of FT, in GRF21-OE lines (Fig. 6), providing a molecular basis for the observed delay in floral transition. Dual-luciferase assays further demonstrated that GRF21 represses ZCN8 promoter activity, consistent with a model in which GRF21 negatively modulates florigen transcription [50]. Given that 14-3-3 proteins typically lack conventional DNA-binding domains and exert regulatory effects primarily through protein-protein interactions, we propose that GRF21 may influence ZCN8 expression indirectly, likely through interaction with upstream transcriptional activators or signaling intermediates. Further yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) and co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) studies will be required in future to identify the interacting partners that mediate this regulatory effect.

In addition to its impact on the florigen pathway, GRF21 overexpression significantly downregulates ZMM4 expression (Fig. S1A-C), a MADS-box transcription factor known to promote floral transition and to function within the gibberellin (GA) pathway, thereby affecting plant height [32]. Previous studies have shown that ZMM4 overexpression accelerates flowering and partially rescues late-flowering phenotypes of id1 and dlf1 mutants, underscoring its important role in reproductive transition [51]. The concurrent repression of both ZCN genes and ZMM4 suggests that GRF21 may coordinate multiple flowering-promoting modules, including florigen and GA-associated pathways. Such multilayer repression would amplify the delay in floral transition and indicates that 14-3-3–mediated signaling operates at a convergence point of parallel flowering pathways.

Despite the consistent evidence from overexpression analyses, loss-of-function mutants are essential to unequivocally define the endogenous role of GRF21 in flowering time regulation. To address this issue, we plan to generate CRISPR/Cas9 knockout lines targeting GRF21 and its closely related homologs, which may exhibit functional redundancy (Fig. 1A). These complementary approaches will further confirm that GRF21 acts as a genuine negative regulator under the native expression conditions.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that constitutive overexpression of GRF21 delays flowering time in maize, revealing a role for this 14-3-3 protein in the regulation of floral transition. Through integrative genetic, molecular, and transcriptomic analyses, elevated GRF21 expression was shown to significantly delay floral transition, accompanied by coordinated repression of key flowering-promoting genes including ZCN7, ZCN8, ZCN12, and ZMM4. These results indicate that GRF21 overexpression acts upstream of the florigen pathway, and 14-3-3-mediated signaling represents a previously uncharacterized regulatory layer controlling maize reproductive timing. The present findings identify GRF21 as a candidate molecular target for precision modulation of flowering time in maize breeding programs.
