Advances in T-Lymphokine-activated Killer Cell-originated Protein Kinase Research in Cancer Over the Past Thirty Years
Authors: Mengyu Zhao, Ran Zhao, Zigang Dong, Mee-Hyun Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Nearly thirty years ago, T-lymphokine-activated killer (T-LAK) cell-originated protein kinase (TOPK), also known as PDZ-binding kinase, was first identified as a serine/threonine kinase with limited known functions. Over time, this molecule has gradually revealed a far more striking role in cancer biology. Initially detected mainly in proliferative tissues such as testes and activated lymphocytes, TOPK is now recognized as a protein that becomes aberrantly overexpressed in many human cancers, where it is consistently linked to aggressive tumor behavior and poor clinical outcomes. Research accumulated over the past three decades shows that TOPK governs a wide range of oncogenic processes, including proliferation, metastasis, cell cycle progression, DNA damage repair, resistance to apoptosis, autophagy regulation, inflammatory signaling, and immune modulation. Mechanistic studies reveal that TOPK communicates extensively with major signaling molecules such as extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK), β-catenin, the tyrosine-protein kinase Src/glycogen synthase kinase 3 beta/signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (Src/GSK3β/STAT3), phosphoinositide 3-kinase/phosphatase and tensin homolog/protein kinase B (PI3K/PTEN/AKT), TGF-β/small mother against decapentaplegic (SMAD), NF-κB/Snail, and HIF-1α. Positive feedback interactions with ERK2, Src and other oncogenic regulators further intensify its tumor-promoting activity. TOPK also contributes to resistance to anti-cancer agents such as doxorubicin, gefitinib, oxaliplatin, and sorafenib through its influence on activator protein-1, phosphatase and tensin homolog, sirtuin 1 (SIRT1), p53, and additional downstream effectors. In the tumor immune microenvironment, TOPK enhances programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression and reduces CD8+ T-cell infiltration, promoting immune evasion. Although numerous natural and synthetic inhibitors of TOPK have been identified, their clinical application remains at an early stage. Overall, current evidence presents TOPK as a promising biomarker and therapeutic target with broad relevance across diverse cancer types.

INTRODUCTION

Nearly three decades after its initial identification, PDZ-binding kinase/T-lymphokine-activated killer (T-LAK) cell-derived protein kinase (PBK/TOPK) has been recognized as a serine/threonine protein kinase of the MAPKK family which is essential for controlling diverse cellular events, including proliferation, growth, apoptosis, and inflammation [1,2]. Physiologically, TOPK is most abundantly expressed in testis and is also detectable in a limited number of normal tissues, including the placenta and T-LAK cells [3,4]. Accumulating evidence indicates that TOPK is aberrantly elevated in a variety of malignancies, such as breast cancer [5,6], colorectal cancer [7-9], esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) [10], gastric cancer [11], lymphoma [12,13], and oral cancer [14]. In contrast, data from the GEPIA 2.0 platform (http://gepia2021.cancer-pku.cn/) demonstrate that the expression of TOPK is reduced in acute myeloid leukemia and testicular germ cell tumors [15].

Elevated TOPK levels have been associated with enhanced metastatic potential and unfavorable clinical outcomes, including shortened overall survival and earlier recurrence [16]. Because of its restricted expression in proliferative normal tissues and its frequent upregulation in tumors, TOPK has attracted considerable interest as a potential biomarker and therapeutic target in cancer. It promotes tumor development through multiple mechanisms, such as influencing signal transduction, metabolic pathways, and immune-related processes [17]. In this review, we outline the discovery of TOPK and summarize its functional roles in cancer.

THE DISCOVERY AND CHARACTERIZATION OF TOPK

Since its initial identification nearly three decades ago, TOPK has undergone extensive functional and structural characterization (Fig. 1). In 2000, Gaudet et al. [18] identified TOPK, a 322-amino acid serine/threonine kinase, through two-hybrid screening. This kinase was shown to specifically interact with the PDZ2 domain of hDlg via the C-terminal T/SXV motif and to be phosphorylated during mitosis by the cell division cycle 2 (CDC2)/cyclin B complex [18]. In the same year, Abe et al. [19] isolated TOPK from a subtractive cDNA library derived from T-LAK cells. They likewise predicted TOPK to be a 322 amino acids protein and proposed it to be a novel MAPKK member related to MEK3/6, potentially participating in lymphocyte activation and supporting the testicular function. In non-neural cells, TOPK phosphorylates and activates p38 MAPK [19]. In neural progenitor cells, p38 MAPK targeted by TOPK is also regulated in a cell cyclin-dependent manner [20]. It was further observed that TOPK remains persistently phosphorylated at Thr198 in sperm germ cells of the testis, suggesting a role in spermatogenesis [3].

In 2012, the three-dimensional structure of TOPK was modeled using multiple structural templates. The protein was predicted to contain 11 α helices and 11 β-strands and its functional annotations included phosphotransferase activity, serine/threonine kinase activity, and broader transferase functions involving phosphorus-containing groups [21]. In 2016, the crystal structure of human TOPK was resolved, revealing a pH-dependent conformational transition between its dimeric and monomeric states [22]. In 2019, a novel link between TOPK and autophagy was uncovered. TOPK directly binds to and phosphorylates unc-51 like autophagy activating kinase 1 (ULK1), reducing its activity and stability, thereby suppressing autophagy initiation [23].

In 2021, TOPK was shown to correlate with tumor immune cell infiltration and contribute to immune evasion [24]. More recently, TOPK was implicated in chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR T-cell) therapy. Thus, inhibition of TOPK was found to enhance T-cell proliferation, memory formation, and immune checkpoint regulation, thereby improving CAR T-cell functions [25]. Together, these discoveries highlight the evolutionary expansion of TOPK research from its biochemical identification to its recognition as a key regulator in cancer biology.

THE FUNCTIONAL ROLES OF TOPK IN CANCER

The roles in proliferation and metastasis

TOPK plays a critical role in regulating cell proliferation, and accumulating evidence has further clarified its phosphorylation-dependent activation mechanisms (Fig. 2). TOPK expression is markedly increased in highly proliferative malignant cells as well as in normal fetal tissues, as revealed by Northern blot analysis of malignant cell lines, clinical samples and fetal specimens from patients with leukemia or lymphoma [12]. TOPK phosphorylates histone H3 at Ser10 both in vitro and in vivo, and exerts its growth-promoting function partly through histone modification [26]. Furthermore, TOPK positively regulates UVB-induced c-Jun-NH2-kinase 1 (JNK1) activity and is essential for H-RAS-driven, JNK1-mediated cellular transformation [27]. Src phosphorylates TOPK at Y74 and Y272 in vitro, and these phosphorylation events enhance TOPK stability and activity, thereby promoting colon tumor formation in vivo [28].

TOPK has also been identified as a downstream target of BCR/ABL. Inhibition of protein phosphatase 2A increases TOPK phosphorylation and subsequently promotes cell proliferation [29]. Additionally, phosphorylation of TOPK at Tyr74 by Janus kinase 2 enhances TOPK protein stability and drives Burkitt lymphoma growth in vivo [30]. Beyond its role in proliferation, TOPK can bind and phosphorylate p53-related protein kinase (PRPK) at Ser250, thereby facilitating colorectal cancer metastasis via PRPK-dependent mechanisms [31].

The roles in cell cycle regulation and DNA damage response

Beyond its functions in promoting proliferation and metastasis, TOPK also plays essential roles in regulating cell cycle progression and maintaining genomic integrity (Fig. 2). The intracellular localization of TOPK and its association with cyclin-dependent kinase 1 (CDK1)/cyclin B as well as microtubules suggest that TOPK contributes to the recruitment of CDK1/cyclin B to the mitotic spindle and may participate in spindle midzone formation and cytokinesis [32]. Inactivation of protein phosphatase 1 α (PP1α) by CDK1/cyclin B1 promotes autophosphorylation, enabling its activation early in mitosis [33]. Elevated TOPK levels may also facilitate tumor progression by suppressing p53 function, consequently reducing the expression of cell cycle regulators such as p21 [34].

Check point protein with FHA and RING domains (CHFR) is a pivotal mitotic checkpoint protein that delays mitosis entry under stress conditions [35]. TOPK and PTEN are central regulators of the CHFR-dependent mitotic transition. TOPK phosphorylates and inhibits PTEN, leading to protein kinase B/AKT activation which prompotes normal G2/M progression [36]. TOPK has been shown to enhance S-phase progression in cancer cell lines, whereas its depletion increases replication fork arrest and collapse under replication stress and DNA damage [37]. Among numerous C2H2-zinc finger targets, TOPK plays a crucial role in displacing CCCTC-binding factor and zinc finger proteins encoded by IKAROS family zinc finger (IKZF) 1, IKZF3, zinc finger protein 131 and zinc finger and BTB domain containing 11, thereby supporting chromosome condensation during mitosis [38].

Inhibition of TOPK reduces p38 MAPK activation, decreases γ–H2AX formation, increases DNA damage, and compromises cell survival [39]. TOPK interacts with histone H2AX, and TOPK knockdown in melanoma cells treated with As3+ diminishes H2AX phosphorylation and attenuates As3+-induced apoptosis [40]. Moreover, TOPK depletion enhances chromosomal abnormalities, multinucleation and apoptosis following irradiation, indicating that TOPK participates in radiation-induced DNA damage checkpoints [41].

The role in apoptosis

In addition to its functions in cell proliferation and cell cycle control, TOPK also influences apoptotic signaling pathways (Fig. 2). TOPK phosphorylates the Ser32 residue of peroxiredoxin 1 and regulates its peroxidase activity to prevent UVB-induced apoptosis in RPMI7951 melanoma cells by limiting intracellular H2O2 accumulation [42]. TOPK also maintains strict control of ROS levels through stabilization of nuclear factor (erythroid-derived 2)-like 2, promotes cell cycle progression, and inhibits apoptosis, thereby enhancing promyelocytes proliferation [43].

The role in autophagy

TOPK also participates in the regulation of autophagy, through multiple phosphorylation-dependent mechanisms (Fig. 2). TOPK directly binds to and phosphorylates ULK1 at Ser469, Ser495 and Ser533. These phosphorylation events decrease ULK1 activity and stability, ultimately suppressing autophagy initiation [23]. Additionally, TOPK can activate the NF-κB pathway by upregulating histone deacetylase 1 (HDAC1), thereby further promoting autophagy [44].

The role in chemotherapy resistance

Besides its involvement in proliferation, apoptosis, and autophagy, TOPK contributes significantly to chemotherapy resistance through its regulation of multiple signaling pathways (Fig. 2). Early mechanistic studies identified a classical TOPK-mediated chemoresistance mechanism in which TOPK directly phosphorylates IκBα at Ser32, thereby promoting p65 nuclear translocation and NF-κB activation in response to doxorubicin treatment [45]. In addition to downstream signaling events, resistance to TOPK-targeted therapy can also arise at the level of drug disposition. Clinically relevant TOPK inhibitors, such as OTS964, have been identified as substrates of the ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters including ABC sub-family G member 2 and ABC sub-family B member 1, resulting in reduced intracellular drug accumulation and acquired resistance [46,47].

TOPK further contributes to resistance against targeted therapies. In lung cancer, TOPK regulates cellular responsiveness to the epidermal growth factor receptor tyrosine kinase inhibitor gefitinib through phosphorylation of the AP-1 component c-Jun, leading to activation of AP-1 target genes such as cyclin D1 and CDC2. Accordingly, silencing TOPK restores sensitivity to gefitinib in resistant lung cancer cells [48]. Consistently, the cyclooxygenase-2/hepatocyte growth factor receptor/TOPK signaling axis has been shown to suppress apoptosis in gefitinib-resistant cells, suggesting that combinatorial targeting of this pathway may represent a cost-effective strategy to overcome resistance [49].

TOPK-mediated resistance has also been observed in platinum and multikinase inhibitor–based therapies. In hepatocellular carcinoma, silencing TOPK increases oxaliplatin sensitivity, whereas TOPK overexpression attenuates oxaliplatin-induced apoptosis and promotes migration and invasion by modulating of PTEN signaling [50]. Moreover, ERK2-dependent phosphorylation of TOPK at Ser32 has been identified as a critical determinant of sorafenib resistance in renal cell carcinoma, and combined inhibition of TOPK and sorafenib synergistically induces apoptosis in resistant cells [51]. Finally, post-transcriptional regulation of TOPK also influences chemotherapy responsiveness. MicroRNA-216b (miR-216b), a negative regulator of TOPK, downregulates TOPK expression in lung adenocarcinoma and consequently increases cellular sensitivity to oxaliplatin [52].

The role in inflammation

In addition to its roles in tumor growth and therapy resistance, TOPK also participates in the regulation of inflammatory responses through multiple signaling pathways (Fig. 2). TOPK negatively modulates p38α activation by phosphorylating the p38α-specific phosphatase mitogen-activated protein kinase phosphatase 1 (MKP1) and enhancing MKP1 stability, thereby preventing UV-induced inflammation [53]. TOPK also contributes to the lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced inflammatory response through NF-κB activation. As a positive regulator of inducible nitric oxide synthase expression in response to LPS, TOPK mediates the downstream cascade of the LPS/Toll-like receptor 4 pathway [54]. In clinical observations, acute solar-simulated light irradiation markedly increased epidermal thickness as well as the total protein levels and phosphorylation of TOPK and PRPK in human skin tissue. Inhibition of PRPK reduced the expression of proliferation and inflammation-related markers, including cyclooxygenase-2, cyclin D1, and matrix metallopeptidase 9 [55].

The role in immunity

Emerging evidence indicates that TOPK not only regulates intrinsic tumor cell signaling but also plays an important role in shaping the tumor immune microenvironment (Fig. 2). TOPK is associated with immune cell infiltration in multiple cancer types and shows positive correlations with tumor-associated macrophages, regulatory T cells, myeloid-derived suppressor cells and T-cell exhaustion signatures in kidney clear cell carcinoma, brain lower grade glioma and liver hepatocellular carcinoma [24]. TOPK is also linked to immune infiltration in breast cancer as evidenced by positive correlations with CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell infiltration [56]. In addition, TOPK inhibition enhances interferon-γ and TNF-α secretion in NK-92MI cells, a highly cytotoxic natural killer (NK) tumor cell line, thereby promoting NK cell mediated antitumor activity [57].

TOPK inhibition has further been shown to enhance CAR T-cell proliferation, memory formation, and regulation of immune checkpoint molecules, ultimately improving CAR T-cell antitumor efficacy [25]. Additional studies further revealed that TOPK contributes to immunotherapy resistance by driving PD-L1 expression and suppressing antitumor CD8+ T-cell immunity. Together, these findings extend the role of TOPK in immune evasion as well as therapy resistance of cancer cells, highlighting its broader impact on antitumor immunity [58].

TOPK-ASSOCIATED SIGNALING PATHWAYS IN CANCER

Beyond its fundamental roles in cell cycle control and stress responses, TOPK participates in a wide array of oncogenic signaling pathways, contributing to tumor progression through extensive pathway crosstalk and feedback regulation (Fig. 3, Table 1). A positive feedback loop exists between TOPK and ERK2. In HCT116 human colon cancer cells, knockdown of either TOPK or ERK2 reduces the phosphorylation of the other following EGF stimulation, indicating reciprocal regulation [59]. TOPK is transcriptionally controlled by forkhead box M1 and promotes cell proliferation and migration through activation of the β-catenin signaling pathway [60].

In gastric cancer, FYN, one of src family tyrosine kinases, directly binds to TOPK and phosphorylates it at Y272, thereby enhancing gastric cancer cell proliferation and metastasis. The FYN-TOPK axis further drives gastric cancer progression by phosphorylating heat shock protein family B [61]. TOPK also phosphorylates Y-box binding protein 1 (YB1) at Thr89 and Ser209, and phosphorylated YB1 subsequently binds to the promoter of eukaryotic translation elongation factor 1α1 (eEF1A1) to activate its transcription, thereby promoting esophageal cancer growth [62]. Additionally, the LIN28B-let-7-TOPK pathway has been shown to enhance the proliferation of Group 3 medulloblastoma [63]. A feedback loop between Src and TOPK regulates transcription factor RB1, altering the expression of growth-associated genes and promoting tumorigenesis [64].

TOPK also promotes phosphorylation of AKT at Ser473, reduces the levels of PTEN, and modulates the PI3K/PTEN/AKT signaling pathway to facilitate cellular migration [65]. TOPK increases cell invasiveness through β-catenin-TCF/LEF signaling [66]. In esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, TOPK contributes to metastasis and enhance cell migration via activation of Src/GSK3β/STAT3 and ERK pathways [67]. TOPK upregulates the T-box transcription factor TBX3 to potentiate TGF-β1-induced epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and invasion in MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells [68]. Under hypoxic conditions, TOPK positively regulates HIF-1α expression, which subsequently increases Snail expression and then promotes EMT and invasion of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) cells [69]. Moreover, TOPK mediates TGF-β1-deriven EMT and invasion in breast cancer cells via the NF-κB/Snail pathway [70].

TOPK inhibition suppresses the TNF-α-mediated transcriptional activation of SIRT1. Loss of SIRT1 activity leads to increased acetylation of p53, indicating that TOPK attenuates TNF-α-induced apoptosis by modulating the p53/SIRT1 axis [71,72]. Treatment with HI-TOPK-032 can induce a significant increase in ROS and activate the p38/JNK pathway to promote cell apoptosis [73]. Additionally, anaplasticlymphoma kinase (ALK) phosphorylates TOPK at Y74, and dual inhibition of ALK and TOPK synergistically induces apoptosis in ALK-positive NSCLC cells both in vitro and in vivo [74]. In renal cell carcinoma, TOPK activates ERK2 and TGF-β/SMAD signaling to enhance PD-L1 expression, thereby inhibiting CD8+ T cells infiltration and promoting immune evasion [58]. Together, these diverse signaling interactions underscore the importance of TOPK in cancer progression and support its continued development as a promising target for therapeutic intervention. To date, several naturally ocurring TOPK inhibitors have been identified, including gossypol [75], acetylshikonin [1,76], and 3-deoxysappanchalcone (3-DSC) [2,77]. Synthetic compounds, such as HI-TOPK-032 [78], ADA-07 [79], OST514 [80,81], OST964 [82], and SKLB-C05 [83], have also been developed, though their mechanisms of action require further investigation.

CONCLUSION

TOPK has emerged as a critical oncogenic kinase that regulates multiple hallmarks of cancer through its extensive involvement in proliferation, metastasis, cell cycle, DNA repair, oxidative stress response, autophagy, inflammation, and immune regulation. Its restricted expression in normal tissues but frequent upregulation in malignancies makes TOPK particularly attractive as a selective cancer target. Mechanistic studies have demonstrated that TOPK modulates several key signaling cascades, including ERK, β-catenin, AKT, STAT3, NF-κB, and TGF-β pathways, positioning it as a central node in tumor-promoting networks. Furthermore, TOPK contributes to therapeutic resistance through modulation of transcriptional programs and stress-response pathways, underscoring its relevance to treatment outcomes.

Collectively, accumulating evidence strongly supports the potential of TOPK as both a prognostic biomarker and a candidate for targeted therapeutic intervention. Despite significant advances in understanding TOPK biology, several important questions remain unresolved. First, although numerous TOPK inhibitors have been developed, their specificity, bioavailability, and safety profiles require further optimization before clinical application can be achieved. Rational drug design based on high-resolution structural information and allosteric regulatory mechanisms may accelerate inhibitor development. Second, given the extensive interplay between TOPK and oncogenic signaling pathways, identifying predictive biomarkers to stratify patients who may benefit from TOPK-targeted therapy is a critical next step. Third, the role of TOPK in shaping the tumor immune microenvironment, particularly in regulating PD-L1 expression, T-cell exhaustion, and CAR T-cell responsiveness merits further investigation, as it may open new avenues for combination immunotherapies. Moreover, the contribution of TOPK to chemotherapy resistance suggests that dual targeting strategies combining TOPK inhibitors with conventional or targeted agents may overcome treatment failure. Finally, expanding our understanding of TOPK functions in tumor heterogeneity, stemness, and metastasis will be essential to fully harness its potential as a therapeutic target. Continued mechanistic and translational studies will be pivotal in advancing TOPK-directed therapies toward clinical implementation.
