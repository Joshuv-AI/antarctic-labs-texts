Dual-atom Rh-Co catalysts for synergistically boosting nitrile hydrogenation
Authors: Jiawei Chen, Hongqiu Chen, Xiangbin Cai, Yue Wang, Mi Peng, Bo Sun, Jiangyong Diao, Geng Sun, Ding Ma, Hongyang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSingle-atom catalysts have found widespread application in selective hydrogenation reactions partially due to their well-defined active site structures, which ensure exceptional chemical selectivity. However, the limited binding sites on single-atom catalysts hinder their application in hydrogenating larger multidentate substrates (e.g., benzonitrile). In this work, we introduced a heteronuclear Rh-Co dual-atom catalyst stabilized on the defective graphene supports (Rh1Co1/ND@G), which resolves the activity-selectivity trade-off in nitrile hydrogenation reaction. The Rh1 site primarily activates H2, whereas the Co1 site synergistically optimizes the adsorption of benzonitrile. The cooperative interaction between Rh-Co dual sites enhances the activation of the C ≡ N bond, significantly reducing the apparent activation energy compared to Rh SACs. The Rh1Co1/ND@G catalyst achieves exceptional performance under mild reaction conditions, delivering a TOF of 4068 h-1 with >98% dibenzylamine selectivity, surpassing all previous reported heterogeneous catalysts. Remarkably, the Rh1Co1/ND@G catalyst still maintains robust catalytic performance even after 12 cycles. This work not only presents a breakthrough in dual-atom catalyst design for nitrile hydrogenation, but also opens an avenue for developing high-performance industrial hydrogenation catalysts.Subject terms: Catalyst synthesis, Heterogeneous catalysis, Catalytic mechanisms

Introduction

Due to the widespread usage of amines in pharmaceutical (over 80% of the top 200 bestselling small molecule drugs feature amines, 20241), agrochemical, and specialty chemical sectors2–4, amine synthesis constitutes an important part of modern chemical manufacturing, with its annual global production exceeding 6 million metric tons to meet soaring demands. While primary amines represent a crucial class, secondary amines are equally indispensable, serving as key structural motifs in numerous bioactive molecules, functional materials, and ligands. Heterogeneous nitrile hydrogenation represents a valuable route for amine synthesis5. Since the first discovery of nickel-catalyzed nitrile hydrogenation in 19056, tremendous efforts have been devoted to developing more efficient heterogeneous catalysts for this crucial reaction, whether targeting primary or secondary amines7–15. However, the synthesis of structurally defined amines through heterogeneous nitrile hydrogenation remains challenging. Conventional catalysts suffer from a fundamental activity-selectivity trade-off: (ⅰ) inherent difficulty in precise regulation of product distribution (primary, secondary, or tertiary) due to side reactions induced by multisite adsorption (typically yielding only 60-75% of the target amine)13,16,17, (ⅱ) low atom efficiency, and (iii) requirements for harsh conditions ( > 373 K, 3–10 MPa H2)18–20.

The single-atom catalysts (SACs) seemed to address some of the challenges, for example, previous studies showed SACs achieved 100% atom utilization with high amine selectivity ( > 90%)15,21,22. However, the nitrile hydrogenation catalytic activity of SACs reported to date remains limited. It is widely recognized that the success of SACs partially originates in their isolated and unique active site structure, which preserves the indispensable sites for key reactions while preventing undesired side reactions23–25. However, the merits turn into demerits when the reaction substrates are larger molecules requiring adjacent active sites for synergetic adsorption and reactions. This is the case in nitrile hydrogenation, in which the mechanism inherently benefits from adjacent metal sites, particularly for substrates containing sterically demanding aromatic rings that may also interact with the catalyst surface. Consequently, although SACs resolve the selectivity deficit of conventional catalysts, their intrinsically low activity prevents overcoming the broader activity-selectivity trade-off in industrial nitrile hydrogenation.

The emerging dual-atom catalysts (DACs), featuring paired adjacent metal sites, could offer a distinct strategy to overcome the aforementioned constraints in SACs. Herein, we fabricated a Rh1-Co1 dual-atom catalyst stabilized on defective graphene-coated nanodiamond supports (Rh1Co1/ND@G). The constructed Rh1Co1/ND@G catalyst delivers robust catalytic performance, achieving a turnover frequency (TOF) of 4068 h-1 (1.6-fold enhancement over Rh SACs) and >98% dibenzylamine selectivity under mild conditions, surpassing all previously reported heterogeneous catalysts. Combined experimental and density functional theory (DFT) calculations revealed that Rh1-Co1 dual-site cooperation lowers the apparent activation energy for nitrile hydrogenation compared to Rh SACs. The arrangement of Rh1-Co1 dual-atom favors a synergetic mechanism, in which the Rh1 site facilitates the activation of H2, whereas benzonitrile (BN) activation proceeds over the bridge site of Rh1-Co1 bond. Such a mechanism is not available on Rh SACs. This atomic-scale design and division of the Rh1-Co1 dual-atom catalyst successfully resolves the persistent activity-selectivity trade-off in nitriles selective hydrogenation, offering a pathway for developing efficient industrial hydrogenation catalysts.

Results

Structure characterization of catalysts

The Rh1Co1/ND@G catalyst was prepared by sequential depositing of Rh and Co onto a defective graphene-coated nanodiamond support (ND@G), as detailed in the Methods. The catalyst displayed a weight loading of 0.061 wt% for Rh and 0.088 wt% for Co, with a mole ratio of approximately 1:2.5 (Table S1). No additional peaks were observed in the X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern (Fig. S1) apart from the ND@G support. Moreover, N2 adsorption-desorption analysis results (Table S1) further confirmed no significant change in the structure of the as-prepared catalysts after the deposition of Rh and Co species.

Aberration-corrected high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) images displayed that the Rh1Co1/ND@G catalyst featured well-dispersed Rh and Co atoms. In addition to the isolated single-atom Rh or Co bright spots (marked as light yellow circles), a substantial number of paired bright spots (marked as purple rectangles), indicative of dual-atom sites, were observed on the ND@G support surface (Fig. 1a). Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) analysis in Fig. 1b demonstrated the coexistence of uniform Rh and Co species on the support. Due to the Z-contrast difference between the metal atoms and the carbon support, the three-dimensional (3D) atom-overlapping Gaussian-function fitting mapping (Fig. 1c) and the corresponding intensity profile (Fig. 1d) for the marked sites in Fig. 1c clearly indicate the presence of adjacent Rh-Co dual-atom pairs on the ND@G support. Furthermore, within the 100 randomly selected regions in Fig. 1c and Fig. S2, the distance of the dual-atom pairs identified by the intensity profile was approximately 2.55 Å, providing compelling evidence for the formation of the Rh-Co dual-atom sites in the Rh1Co1/ND@G catalyst (Fig. S3). Additional HAADF-STEM images of Co1/ND@G were presented in Fig. S4, while XRD confirmed the atomic dispersion of Co.

To investigate potential electronic interactions between Rh and Co species in Rh1Co1/ND@G, we conducted in-situ DRIFTS of CO chemisorption. No CO adsorption was detected on the Co1 site, as shown in Fig. 2a. On the other hand, the in-situ DRIFTS spectrum of Rh1/ND@G displays only two bands at 2065 and 1992 cm-1, assigned to the symmetric and asymmetric stretches of the gem-dicarbonyl Rh(CO)2 species26, thereby confirming the absence of Rh clusters or nanoparticles. The CO chemisorption results for Rh1Co1/ND@G resembled those of Rh1/ND@G, affirming the atomical dispersion of Rh species in the catalyst, consistent with HAADF-STEM and XRD results. However, a notable redshift in geminal peaks was observed in Rh1Co1/ND@G, shifting to 2063 and 1988 cm-1, respectively, which suggested an increase in the surface electron density of Rh1 atoms in Rh1Co1/ND@G, leading to a stronger bond between Rh (4 d orbitals) and CO (2π* orbitals) via enhanced π back-donation and a redshift in the CO vibrational frequency27. Clearly, the construction of Rh-Co dual-atom site changed the local electronic environment of Rh.

X-ray absorption fine-structure (XAFS) spectra were collected to further investigate the coordination environments of Rh and Co species in Rh1Co1/ND@G. The Rh K-edge X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) curves for Rh1Co1/ND@G and Rh1/ND@G were depicted in Fig. 2b, alongside reference samples of Rh foil and Rh2O3. The near-edge feature of Rh1Co1/ND@G was positioned between those of Rh foil and Rh2O3, indicating that Rh species in Rh1Co1/ND@G were in a positive valence state (0 <δ < +2). The absorption edge of Rh1Co1/ND@G shifted to a lower valence state compared to Rh1/ND@G due to the electron-rich Rh1 site in Rh1Co1/ND@G. Similarly, in the XANES curves of the Co K-edge, Rh1Co1/ND@G displayed a lower absorbed energy than Co1/ND@G (Fig. 2c) since the absorption edge of Rh1Co1/ND@G was located to the left of Co1/ND@G. Additionally, Fourier-transform extended X-ray absorption fine structure (FT-EXAFS) spectra were presented to distinctly identify the configurations of Rh and Co species in Rh1Co1/ND@G. In the Rh K-edge EXAFS spectra (Fig. 2d), both Rh1Co1/ND@G and Rh1/ND@G exhibited a major peak around 2.03 Å, attributed to the Rh-O/C path. A weak peak at ~2.56 Å in Rh1Co1/ND@G corresponds to the Rh···Co scattering path28,29. And a major peak around 1.96 Å in the Co K-edge EXAFS spectra was attributed to the Co-O/C scattering path (Fig. 2e). The absence of significant Rh-Rh or Co-Co scattering paths, in contrast to Rh and Co foil references, confirmed the atomic dispersion of Rh and Co species on the ND@G surface, consistent with the findings of HAADF-STEM and CO-DRIFTS. Least squares EXAFS fitting in both R space and k space (Fig. 2f, g and Fig. S5) was applied to Rh1Co1/ND@G, and the extracted structural parameters were listed in Table S2. The successful formation and stabilization of these Rh-Co dual-atom pairs, as unequivocally demonstrated by the above characterization techniques, is a direct outcome of the designed sequential synthesis strategy. The method leverages the defect-rich ND@G surface to direct heteronuclear pairing: Rh atoms are first anchored at stable vacancy sites under basic conditions, followed by the adsorption of Co via Co(NH3)42+ complexes, which favors coordination adjacent to pre-anchored Rh due to ligand-assisted orientation. This process, coupled with the final H2 reduction at 573 K, facilitates atomic migration and stable pair formation without aggregation.

X-ray photoemission spectroscopy (XPS) was used to further investigate the electronic structure of catalysts. As depicted in Fig. S6a, in comparison to Rh1/ND@G, the Rh 3 d binding energy in Rh1Co1/ND@G exhibited a slight shift towards lower energy, suggesting higher electron density and lower valence states for Rh species in Rh1Co1/ND@G. Simultaneously, the binding energy of Co 2p electrons in Rh1Co1/ND@G also displayed a slight shift towards lower energy relative to Co1/ND@G (Fig. S6b). These correlated results may be due to electronic coupling between adjacent Rh1 and Co1 atoms. This mutual electronic modulation, potentially facilitated by electron donation from the ND@G support, contrasts with traditional unidirectional charge transfer models and highlights the unique electronic synergy achievable in atomically dispersed bimetallic systems.

Hydrogenation Test of Nitrile

The hydrogenation of BN was chosen as the model reaction to assess the catalytic performance of Rh1Co1/ND@G at 333 K and 0.6 MPa H2. The optimization of reaction conditions is outlined in Tables S3-S4 and Figure. S7. Figure S8 illustrated the time course of BN conversion and dibenzylamine (DBA) selectivity. Rh1Co1/ND@G achieved complete BN conversion with over 98% selectivity for DBA after 3 h, whereas Rh1/ND@G required 5 h to achieve complete BN conversion. Conversely, the catalytic activity of BN hydrogenation was negligible for Co1/ND@G under the same conditions (Fig. 3b). These results collectively suggested a synergistic effect between Rh1 and Co1 sites in promoting BN hydrogenation to DBA. Comparatively, the physical mixture of Rh1/ND@G and Co1/ND@G exhibited slightly higher BN hydrogenation performance than Rh1/ND@G alone, but still inferior to Rh1Co1/ND@G. Rh1Co1/ND@G exhibited superior reactivity with a TOF value reaching 4068 h-1, 1.6 times higher than Rh1/ND@G under identical reaction conditions. Combined with its exceptional selectivity ( > 98%) for DBA, Rh1Co1/ND@G overcame the trade-off between activity and selectivity commonly observed in nitrile hydrogenation. Moreover, the performance of the Rh-Co dual-atom sites surpassed that of previous reported catalysts for BN hydrogenation (Table S5 and Fig. 3c).

The apparent activation energies (Ea) of Rh1/ND@G and Rh1Co1/ND@G catalysts were compared to elucidate the enhanced activity of the Rh1Co1/ND@G. As shown in Fig. 3d, the corresponding Arrhenius plot showed a good degree of linearity, and the Ea of BN hydrogenation over Rh1Co1/ND@G was 30.2 kJ/mol, significantly lower than that of Rh1/ND@G (41.1 kJ/mol), indicating that Rh1Co1/ND@G with Rh-Co dual-atom sites was more favorable for catalyzing BN hydrogenation. Remarkably, besides high activity and selectivity, Rh1Co1/ND@G also exhibited excellent recyclability. Figure 3e showed that the yield of DBA remained high ( > 85%) even after 12 cycles. Furthermore, the HAADF-STEM images and XAFS spectra of Rh1Co1/ND@G-cycled revealed that the Rh and Co species were still atomically dispersed on the support (Fig. S9), further confirming the excellent recyclability of Rh1Co1/ND@G.

The catalytic hydrogenation of various nitriles was explored over the Rh1Co1/ND@G catalyst, as displayed in Fig. 4. Benzonitrile derivatives containing electron-withdrawing groups (e.g., halogens and trifluoromethyl groups) or electron-donating groups (e.g., methyl and methoxy groups) were efficiently converted to corresponding secondary amines. Furthermore, the Rh1Co1/ND@G catalyst demonstrated suitability for certain heteroaromatic compounds, such as furan, yielding corresponding secondary amines at high yields without dearomatization. Notably, the reactivity of aliphatic nitriles is limited, while they exhibited a high yield of secondary amines in the hydrogenation of acetonitrile. Thus, the Rh1Co1/ND@G exhibits broad applicability across a wide range of nitriles for amine hydrogenation, highlighting its significant potential for industrial applications.

Study of reaction mechanisms

The reaction orders of BN and H2 are both positive, as depicted in Fig. 5a, b, implying that the adsorption of both H2 and BN is crucial to the overall kinetics. Interestingly, the reaction orders for BN and H2 over the Rh1Co1/ND@G were lower than those observed over the Rh1/ND@G. The higher reaction orders may originate from the competitive adsorption of BN and H2 on the single Rh sites, whereas the Rh-Co dual-atom site enables simultaneous adsorption and activation of both reactants. This mitigates the active-site competition typically encountered with SACs, accounting for the lower reaction orders and contributing to the enhanced catalytic performance of the Rh1Co1/ND@G catalyst.

As the H2 activation is crucial in the overall hydrogenation activity, we investigated the H2 activation capability of these catalysts by hydrogen-deuterium (H2-D2) exchange experiments (Fig. 5c and Fig. S10). HD generation was observed only at temperatures above 340 K on the Co1/ND@G catalyst, while the HD generation temperatures are lowered to approximately 312 K for both Rh1/ND@G and Rh1Co1/ND@G catalysts. These findings imply that the poor activity of Co1/ND@G catalyst in H2 activation partially holds accountable for its low performance in nitrile hydrogenation, while since the H2 activation capabilities of Rh1/ND@G and Rh1Co1/ND@G are similar, there should be other factors contributing to the different catalytic performances between those two catalysts. These results were corroborated by WO3 coloration experiments, where activated H* reacts with yellow WO3 to form blue-black HxWO30,31. As shown in Fig. S11, Co1/ND@G maintained its original gray color after H2 treatment, whereas both Rh1/ND@G and Rh1Co1/ND@G turned blue-black, which confirms that H2 activation is predominantly driven by Rh species in these catalysts.

As implied by the positive reaction orders, the adsorptions of the H2 and BN are crucial in understanding the reactivities. TPD experiments, depicted in Fig. 5d, were carried out to study the BN adsorption on different catalysts. A desorption peak below 500 K, observed for all samples, is attributed to the π-π adsorption between BN and the sp2 carbon on the ND@G support (Fig. S12). The signals that occurred at higher temperatures are BN desorption from Rh1/ND@G at 575K and from Co1/ND@G at 619K, indicating stronger BN interaction with the Co site than the Rh site. Remarkably, Rh1Co1/ND@G exhibited two distinct desorption peaks at 619 K and 651 K. The former peak at 619 K corresponds to BN adsorbed on a single Co1 species, and the latter peak at a higher temperature of 651 K reflects significantly stronger binding between BN and the Rh-Co dual-atom site. BN-TPD-FTIR results (Figs. S13–S15) agree on enhancing BN adsorption strength. This enhanced binding energy could be either a consequence of electronic structure modification, or of a more favorable binding geometry between the BN and the dual-atom catalysts. This question is further clarified in Fig. 5e. Pure BN displayed a characteristic C ≡ N stretching vibration at 2227.9 cm-1 and C = C bond vibrations on the benzene ring at 1598.3 cm-1, 1581.6 cm-1, 1490.1 cm-1, and 1447.1 cm-1. On the Rh1/ND@G and Co1/ND@G, the C ≡ N peak exhibited a slight red shift, while the C = C peaks remained unchanged, indicating that the interaction is primarily between the C ≡ N group and the catalyst. However, for Rh1Co1/ND@G, the C ≡ N peak exhibited a more significant red shift to 2224.5 cm-1. Furthermore, the C = C bond vibrations also shifted to 1595.8 cm-1, 1577.9 cm-1, 1489.6 cm-1, and 1446.2 cm-1. These combined shifts suggest substantial alteration of BN adsorption geometry on the Rh1Co1 dual-atom catalyst compared to that observed on SACs.

DFT calculations were further performed to elucidate previous discoveries, including the origin of the superior performance and the stabilization of the Rh-Co dual-atom catalyst. Structural models of the catalysts were established based on experimental characterization, particularly the coordination numbers (Table S2). The single-atom Rh and Co catalysts were modeled as metal centers anchored at double-vacancy graphene sites (denoted as Rh@DVG and Co@DVG, respectively, Fig. S16a, b), while the RhCo dual-atom catalyst adopted a neighboring Rh-Co configuration stabilized at a triple-vacancy graphene site (RhCo@TVG, Fig. S16c). These optimized models agree well with the experimental coordination numbers (Table S2). To fundamentally explain why Rh and Co atoms preferentially form adjacent heteronuclear pairs, we constructed computational models in which homonuclear Rh2, homonuclear Co2, and heteronuclear Rh1Co1 dimers were anchored on an ND@G support containing graphene surfaces with two carbon vacancies (Fig. S16). Specifically, the Bader charge of Rh in RhCo@TVG (0.21 e) is smaller than in Rh@DVG (0.47 e). Similarly, the Bader charge of Co in RhCo@TVG (0.46 e) is lower than in Co@DVG (0.66 e).

Mechanistic investigation of nitrile hydrogenation pathways revealed fundamental differences between mono- and dual-atom catalysts. BN adsorption over single-atom Rh site occurs via a single Rh-N bond with a moderate adsorption energy of −0.89 eV (Fig. 6a). However, on the dual-atom Rh-Co sites, BN interacts with the catalysts through multiple bonds: Rh interacts with the π orbital of the BN moiety while Co interacts one of the carbon atoms in the phenyl group, resulting in slightly enhanced adsorption energy (−1.13 eV, Fig. 6c), which align precisely with TPD and FTIR observations. During the BN hydrogenation over the single-atom Rh catalyst, the BN occupies the Rh site, and the following hydrogenation takes place through an Eley-Rideal mechanism, in which the formation of the N-H bond and the dissociation of the H-H bond occur simultaneously. This Eley-Rideal mechanism is challenging and exhibits a Gibbs free energy barrier of 0.81 eV. The subsequent hydrogenation step forming imine is rather efficient showing a small barrier of 0.26 eV (Fig. 6d). On the dual-sites Rh-Co catalysts, the first hydrogenation step also follows an Eley-Rideal mechanism, in which the dissociating H attacks the nitrogen. In the transition state of the first hydrogenation step on RhCo@TVG, the bond length of the dissociating H-H is 3.57 Å, indicating a preferable late transition state. The subsequent hydrogenation toward carbene is also much more efficient than the first hydrogenation. Given the experimentally observed high selectivity towards the DBA, the first hydrogenation is the rate-determining step (RDS). The significantly lower RDS barrier on RhCo@TVG (0.61 eV) than on Rh@DVG (0.81 eV), as shown in the energy diagram (Fig. 6d), provides a clear explanation for the superior performance of the RhCo dual-atom sites. Further analysis of the key intermediate (PhCNH in Fig. 6d), that is the product for the first hydrogenation step, showed that the remaining H atom preferentially adsorbs on the Rh site rather than the Co site (Fig. S18, being 0.63 eV lower in energy), establishing Rh as the primary active center for H2 dissociation.

Discussion

In summary, we report a heteronuclear Rh-Co dual-atom catalyst (Rh1Co1/ND@G) that effectively overcomes the activity-selectivity trade-off in nitrile hydrogenation due to the synergistic cooperation between adjacent Rh and Co sites. The Rh1 site activates H2 to generate reactive hydrogen species (H*), while the Co1 site adsorbs the benzonitrile substrate predominantly via the phenyl ring. This distinct adsorption configuration polarizes the C ≡ N bond, enabling efficient hydrogenation. Combined experimental results and DFT calculations confirm the dual-atom configuration and elucidate the nitrile hydrogenation mechanism over Rh1Co1/ND@G, revealing that the synergy between Rh and Co sites lowers the apparent activation energy. As a result, Rh1Co1/ND@G achieves exceptional performance under mild conditions, delivering a TOF of 4068 h-1 with >98% DBA selectivity, surpassing all known heterogeneous catalysts. The present work offers a broadly applicable approach for developing highly efficient and selective industrial hydrogenation catalysts.

Methods

Catalysts preparation

Synthesis of ND@G

Nanodiamond (ND) powders (Beijing Grish Hitech Co., China) were annealed at 1373 K for 4 h in Ar flow (100 mL/min), following established procedures as detailed in our previous studies. Subsequently, purification was carried out using hydrochloric acid22.

Synthesis of Rh1/ND@G

Rh1/ND@G was prepared according to our previous reports22. Generally, 200 mg purified ND@G was dispersed in 30 mL of DI water and then ultrasonically treated until a uniform suspension was achieved. The carbon support suspension was then adjusted using a 0.25 M Na2CO3 aqueous solution to reach a pH of approximately 10. Subsequently, 4 mL of as-prepared Rh(NO3)3 solution (0.075 mg/mL Rh; Rh(NO3)3 from Aladdin; CAS: 10139-58-9) was gradually added dropwise into the ND@G suspension, which was stirred in an oil bath at 373 K for 1 h. Once the mixture naturally cooled to room temperature, it was separated by filtration, washed several times with deionized water, and subsequently dried under vacuum at 333 K for 12 h. Finally, the sample underwent reduction with pure H2 (flow rate = 20 mL/min) at 573 K for 1 h.

Synthesis of Co1/ND@G

Typically, 25 μL of Co(NO3)2 solution (2 mg/mL Co; Co(NO3)2 from Innochem; CAS: 10026-22-9) was diluted into 4 mL of 5 M NH4OH to form a Co(NH3)42+ solution in a 10 mL long-neck bottle. Subsequently, 200 mg of ND@G was dispersed into the above solution and stirred at room temperature for 12 h. Following this, the mixture was collected by filtration and washed several times with deionized water until reaching a neutral pH. The washed product was then dried under vacuum at 333 K for 12 h. Finally, the sample was reduced with pure H2 (flow rate = 20 mL/min) at 573 K for 1 h.

Synthesis of Rh1Co1/ND@G

Generally, 200 mg purified ND@G was dispersed in 30 mL of DI water and then ultrasonically treated until a uniform suspension was achieved. The carbon support suspension was then adjusted using a 0.25 M Na2CO3 aqueous solution to reach a pH of approximately 10. Subsequently, 4 mL of as-prepared Rh(NO3)3 solution (0.075 mg/mL Rh; Rh(NO3)3 from Aladdin; CAS: 10139-58-9) was gradually added dropwise into the ND@G suspension, which was stirred in an oil bath at 373 K for 1 h. Once the mixture naturally cooled to room temperature, it was separated by filtration, washed several times with deionized water, and subsequently dried under vacuum at 333 K for 12 h. The resulting sample was then dispersed into a Co(NH3)42+ solution (prepared as described in the synthesis of Co1/ND@G) and stirred at room temperature for 12 h. Following this, the mixture was collected by filtration, washed multiple times with deionized water until reaching a neutral pH, and then dried under vacuum at 333 K for 12 h. Finally, the sample underwent reduction with pure H2 (flow rate = 20 mL/min) at 573 K for 1 h.

Characterizations

N2 physisorption measurements were conducted at 77 K using a Micrometrics ASAP-2020 instrument. The porosity of the samples was determined via Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) analysis, with the pore volume measured at p/p0 = 0.98. Additionally, the pore size distribution was analyzed using the Barrett-Joyner-Halenda (BJH) method from the desorption branch. The Rh and Co loadings of these samples were determined using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES 730). X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were collected using a Bruker Smart APEX II X-ray diffractometer with a Cu Kα source, scanning at a rate of 2°/min. The reduced catalyst samples were characterized using a JEOL JEM ARM 200CF aberration-corrected cold field-emission scanning transmission electron microscope (HAADF-STEM) operating at 200 kV. Energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) spectroscopy was also performed using the same equipment.

Diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy of CO chemisorption was carried out using a Thermo Scientific Nicolet IZ10 Fourier transform infrared spectrometer equipped with a high-temperature and high-pressure chamber and an MCT detector. For instance, the catalyst, diluted with KBr at a mass ratio of about 1:20, was placed in the in-situ pool and pretreated in pure hydrogen gas at 573 K for 1 h. Cooling to 293 K under He flow, adsorbent (10% CO/He) was introduced to the chamber until adsorption saturation. DRIFTS spectra were continuously recorded with 64 scans at a resolution of 4 cm-1 during the He flow purging.

X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) of the Rh K edge (23220 eV) was conducted at the BL14W1 beamline at the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF). The SSRF operates at an energy of 3.5 GeV with a maximum current of 250 mA. A Si (311) double-crystal monochromator was utilized, covering the photon energy range from 9 to 35 keV. XAS of the Co K-edge (7709 eV) was carried out at the 1W1B beamline at the Beijing Synchrotron Radiation Facility, which operates at 2.5 GeV with a current of 250 mA, employing a Si (111) double-crystal monochromator. The samples were measured in fluorescence mode, with data collection using a Lytle detector. After activation, the XAS samples were sealed in Kapton films with Ar protection, and the entire process was performed in a glovebox. All XAS spectra were analyzed using the Ifeffit package version 1.2.11.

1H NMR and 13C NMR were recorded on a JEOL ECS-400 spectrometer with CDCl3 as the solvent. Chemical shifts were reported in parts per million (ppm), and the residual solvent peak was used as an internal reference: proton (CDCl3: δ 7.26 ppm), carbon (CDCl3: δ 77.07 ppm), and tetramethylsilane (TMS δ 0.00). Multiplicity is indicated as follows: s (singlet), d (doublet), t (triplet), q (quartet), dd (doublet of doublet), m (multiplet). Coupling constants J are reported in Hz.

H2-D2 exchange experiments were conducted in a quartz-bed flow reactor equipped with an online mass spectrometer (MS, Pfeiffer OMNIstar™) to evaluate gas mixtures. Typically, a 100 mg sample was reduced with pure H2 in He at 573 K for 1 h (flow rate = 20 mL/min) and then cooled to room temperature in a He flow. Subsequently, the samples were further cooled in a He flow with an ice-water bath to 273 K. A mixture gas containing 1 vol.% H2 and 0.5 vol.% D2 in He was introduced into the reactor and maintained for 1.5 h to stabilize the MS baseline (flow rate = 10 mL/min). The sample was then heated with 1 vol.% H2 and 0.5 vol.% D2 in He flow (flow rate = 10 mL/min) from 273 K to room temperature, and then to 373 K for Rh1/ND@G and Rh1Co1/ND@G, and to 473 K for Co1/ND@G, respectively. The intensities for the m/z values equaling 2 and 3 were continuously recorded for H2 and HD, respectively.

H2 activation detection by WO3 was performed in a quartz-bed flow reactor. In a typical experiment, a mixture containing 1.5 g of WO3 and 0.03 g of catalyst was placed in a quartz reaction tube and secured with silica wool. The temperature was then adjusted to 333 K, and hydrogen was allowed to flow through the reaction tube at a rate of 30 mL/min. After 1 h, the hydrogen flow was halted, and the reaction tubes were removed from the oven. Any color changes in the powder samples were observed.

Adsorption of organic substrates on the samples was measured using a JASCO-770 UV-Vis-NIR spectrometer. Specifically, 30 mg of catalyst was added to 5 mL of methanol with BN at an initial concentration of 5000 ppm and stirred at room temperature. At predetermined time intervals, 250 μL of the suspension was diluted 40 times with water, and the supernatant was obtained via centrifugation.

Temperature-programmed desorption (TPD) of BN was conducted in helium. Initially, 100 mg of catalyst underwent pretreatment in flowing pure H2 (30 mL/min) at 573 K for 1 h. Subsequently, the system temperature was cooled to 303 K under helium. BN was then introduced into the tube by passing helium (50 mL/min) from the bubble reactor (maintained at 303K) and treated for 5 h. Following this, the system was swept with a flowing helium stream (50 mL/min) for 20 h to obtain a stable baseline. Once achieved, the temperature of the catalyst was increased from 303 to 773 K at a heating rate of 2 K/min, while the mass spectrometer (MS) signals of the reactant and product were recorded using an Omnistar mass spectrometer (model: GSD 301 O1).

The BN-TPD-FTIR experiments were conducted using a Thermo Scientific Nicolet IZ10 Fourier transform infrared spectrometer equipped with a high-temperature and high-pressure chamber and an MCT detector. For instance, the catalyst, diluted with KBr at a mass ratio of about 1:20, was placed in the in-situ pool and pretreated in pure hydrogen gas at 573 K for 1 h. After cooling to 303 K under helium flow, BN was introduced into the tube by passing helium (10 mL/min) from the bubble reactor (maintaining the temperature of the bubble reactor at 303 K) and treated for 1 h. Subsequently, the system was swept with a flowing helium stream (10 mL/min). Once a stable spectrum was obtained, the temperature of the catalyst was increased from 303 to 573 K at a heating rate of 3 K/min. FTIR spectra were continuously recorded with 64 scans at a resolution of 4 cm-1 during the helium flow purging.

In situ FTIR spectra of BN molecule experiments were conducted using a Thermo Scientific Nicolet IZ10 Fourier transform infrared spectrometer equipped with a high-temperature and high-pressure chamber and an MCT detector. For instance, the catalyst, diluted with KBr at a mass ratio of about 1:20, was placed in the in-situ pool and pretreated with pure hydrogen gas at 573K for 1 h. Then BN was introduced into the tube by passing helium (10 mL/min) from the bubble reactor at 443 K and treated for 1 h. Subsequently, the system was swept with a flowing helium stream (10 mL/min). FTIR spectra were continuously recorded with 64 scans at a resolution of 4 cm-1 during the helium flow purging.

Catalytic performance tests

Catalytic performance tests for the hydrogenation of benzonitrile were conducted in a stainless-steel autoclave reactor equipped with a magnetic stirrer and a digital temperature controller (see Fig. S21 for a detailed schematic of the experimental setup). In a standard procedure, 0.5 mmol of benzonitrile (from Alfa Aesar), 1 mmol m-xylene (from SCR) as internal standard, and 30 mg of catalyst were precisely weighed and added to 10 mL of anhydrous methanol (GC grade) in the reactor liner. The reactor was then sealed and purged seven times with high-purity hydrogen gas (99.999%) at 0.5 MPa to completely remove air and ensure an oxygen-free environment. The reaction was initiated by heating the system to 333 K under constant stirring at 800 rpm, and maintained for 3 h under a constant H₂ pressure of 0.6 MPa.

After reaction completion, the mixture was cooled to room temperature, and the products were quantitatively analyzed using gas chromatography (GC, Agilent 7890 A) equipped with a flame ionization detector (FID) and an HP-5 capillary column (30 m × 320 μm × 0.25 μm). Nitrogen was used as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 20 mL·min⁻¹. The injector and detector temperatures were set to 260 °C and 280 °C, respectively. The oven temperature program was as follows: initial hold at 60 °C for 8.5 min, ramp to 80 °C at 25 °C·min⁻¹ and hold for 4 min, then ramp to 280 °C at 20 °C·min⁻¹ and hold for 10 min. Quantification was achieved using m-xylene as an internal standard, with calibration curves established for all relevant compounds. Raw chromatographic data are provided in Supplementary Information.

Stability test

The hydrogenation reaction was maintained under the same conditions as described above, using the recycled catalyst. After each run, the catalyst was recycled through filtration and cleaned by washing with ethanol and deionized water several times to remove residual impurities. Finally, the recycled catalyst was dried under vacuum at 333 K for 3 h and then reused in the next run.

Computational details

All calculations were performed using the Vienna Ab Initio Simulation Package (VASP.6.3.2), and the Projector-Augmented Wave (PAW) pseudopotential is exploited to describe the effective potential of the nuclei and core electrons32,33. The Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) exchange-correlation functional was employed to evaluate the exchange-correlation interactions of electrons. Plane-wave basis sets with a cutoff energy of 400 eV are used34 and spin-polarized calculations were consistently applied throughout this study35. The Grimme DFT-D3 method, implemented in VASP, was employed to account for van der Waals (vdW) interactions. Additionally, an implicit solvent model was enabled using the VASPSOL + + module36. The dielectric constant was set to 1.77 to be consistent with the methanol solvent in experiments, and the molecular number density and the dipole moment of the solvent are set to 0.0265 and 0.5800, respectively. Structural optimization was carried out until the maximum force residue below 0.03 eV/Å. Transition states were identified using the NEB and subsequently refined by DIMER methods37–39.
