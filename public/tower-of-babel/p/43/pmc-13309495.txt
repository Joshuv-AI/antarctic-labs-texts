Prevalence, characteristics, and prognostic associations of sarcopenia in older adults with heart failure
Authors: Kakeru Hashimoto, Akihiro Hirashiki, Koki Kawamura, Ikue Ueda, Shigeharu Yamazaki, Shunya Tanioku, Takahiro Kamihara, Hitoshi Kagaya, Hidenori Arai, Atsuya Shimizu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key summary pointsAimTo examine the clinical characteristics of Japanese older adults with heart failure (HF) diagnosed with sarcopenia using the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia 2025 (AWGS 2025) criteria.FindingsSarcopenia, as defined by the AWGS 2025 criteria, was highly prevalent in Japanese older adults with HF and was significantly associated with an increased risk of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, regardless of the skeletal muscle mass adjustment method used.MessageOlder patients with HF have a high prevalence of sarcopenia, and sarcopenia, as defined by the AWGS 2025 criteria, is associated with an increased mortality risk.Keywords: Sarcopenia, Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia 2025 (AWGS 2025), Heart failure, Older adults

Introduction

The aging global population is accelerating, leading to an increase in the number of patients with heart failure (HF) [1]. In Japan, the number of older adults with newly diagnosed HF is expected to increase [2, 3]. Older adults with HF often have reduced exercise tolerance and comorbidities, such as cognitive impairment, depression [4], frailty [5], and sarcopenia [6]. Rapid population aging across Asia, including Japan, has increased the epidemiological impact of age-related conditions, including sarcopenia, on healthcare systems [7].

Sarcopenia, defined as an age-related decline in skeletal muscle mass and strength, is strongly associated with adverse outcomes, including an increased risk of falls, functional impairment, dementia, and all-cause mortality [8]. The prevalence of sarcopenia in community-dwelling adults aged ≥ 65 years was reported to be 11.5% in males and 16.7% in females [9]. Furthermore, the prevalence rate increases with age, reaching approximately 22% for both men and women aged 75–79, 32.4% for men aged ≥ 80, and 47.7% for women [9]. Patients with HF are known to have a higher rate of sarcopenia than older adults without HF. A study of Japanese patients with HF aged ≥ 65 years found that 57% had sarcopenia, which increased the risk of all-cause mortality [10]. Therefore, the need for sarcopenia management to reduce mortality in older adults with HF has been highlighted [11].

Each country has its own diagnostic criteria for sarcopenia. In Asia, including Japan, the criteria established by the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) are used to define sarcopenia. In 2014, the first diagnostic criteria in Asia were proposed [7], which were revised in 2019 as the AWGS 2019 criteria [12]. Subsequently, the importance of a unified global standard for sarcopenia was discussed, and the concept and definition of sarcopenia by the Global Leadership Initiative in Sarcopenia (GLIS) were reported [13]. In line with the GLIS concept, the AWGS updated its consensus in 2025 to reframe the management of sarcopenia through a life-course approach, promoting muscle health, as outlined in the AWGS 2025 criteria [14]. In the AWGS 2019 criteria, sarcopenia is diagnosed based on a decrease in skeletal muscle mass, as well as decreased muscle strength, and physical function. However, in the AWGS 2025 criteria, this was changed to a diagnosis of sarcopenia based on a decrease in skeletal muscle mass and muscle strength. Since the AWGS 2019 criteria report, there have been reports on the prevalence of sarcopenia in patients with HF and its association with mortality; however, no studies have examined the characteristics of patients with sarcopenia, such as the prevalence and association with mortality, in Japanese older adults with HF using the AWGS 2025 criteria.

Therefore, this study aimed to examine the impact of sarcopenia, defined by the AWGS 2025 criteria on mortality in Japanese older adults with HF.

Methods

Study population

This was a retrospective cohort study design. This study included participants admitted for worsening HF to the cardiology department of the National Center for Geriatrics and Gerontology Hospital (Obu, Japan) between August 2018 and September 2025. All participants were at least 65 years old and underwent laboratory measurements, echocardiography, physical function assessments, and the Mini Nutritional Assessment Short-Form (MNA-SF). The prevalence of sarcopenia was also assessed in stable conditions just before discharge. All participants were prospectively followed up for clinical adverse events after discharge.

The inclusion criteria were symptomatic HF due to non-ischemic cardiomyopathy, tachycardia, bradycardia, valvular hypertension, or other causes in patients aged ≥ 65 years. Non-ischemic cardiomyopathy was defined as ventricular myocardial abnormalities in the absence of coronary artery disease or valvular, pericardial, or congenital heart disease [15]. Tachycardia and bradycardia included atrial, supraventricular, and ventricular arrhythmias, as well as sick sinus syndrome and atrioventricular block in the absence of structural heart disease. Valvular heart disease was diagnosed based on hemodynamic or echocardiographic findings or a history of valvular or congenital cardiac surgery, according to the American College of Cardiology and American Heart Association Guidelines for the Management of Patients with Valvular Heart Disease [16]. Hypertension was defined as a systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg, or a history of hypertension treatment. Worsening HF was defined as a clinical syndrome comprising symptoms and signs of structural and functional cardiac abnormalities, elevated natriuretic peptide levels, and objective evidence of pulmonary or systemic congestion [17].

The exclusion criteria included severe respiratory disease (i.e., patients receiving long-term oxygen therapy due to respiratory disease), liver dysfunction (Child–Pugh class C), renal dysfunction (glomerular filtration rate stage G5), and malignant tumors with a prognosis of less than one year. Additionally, patients admitted for non-cardiac reasons or for planned cardiovascular diagnostic procedures were excluded.

Clinical characteristics

The background data of the participants included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), underlying diseases, HF phenotypes, medication status, echocardiographic findings, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), ratio of transmitral Doppler early filling velocity to tissue Doppler early diastolic mitral annular velocity, and blood tests, including plasma brain natriuretic peptide (BNP), hemoglobin, albumin, C-reactive protein (CRP), and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR).

Diagnosis of sarcopenia

Sarcopenia

Sarcopenia was assessed according to the AWGS 2025 [14] and AWGS 2019 [12] criteria. In the AWGS 2025 criteria, sarcopenia was diagnosed based on muscle weakness and loss of skeletal muscle mass. In this study, we calculated the prevalence of sarcopenia using each assessment method, adjusting for skeletal muscle mass by height−squared (height-adjusted) and adjusted for BMI (BMI-adjusted). Furthermore, we examined the differences in the prevalence of sarcopenia diagnosis due to differences in skeletal muscle mass adjustment methods in participants diagnosed with sarcopenia.

The AWGS 2019 criteria diagnose sarcopenia when there is a decrease in either or both physical function and muscle strength, along with a decrease in the skeletal muscle mass. In this study, physical function decline was determined using the Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB), and skeletal muscle mass decline was measured using bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA; InBody 770, Seoul, Korea). In addition, in AWGS 2019, the number of participants with sarcopenia and severe sarcopenia was calculated.

SPPB

The SPPB was utilized to evaluate lower limb function [18]. It comprised three components: balance test (closed leg, semi-tandem, and tandem standing), walking time, and standing ability from a seated position. The maximum score was 12 points, with higher scores indicating better physical function.

Handgrip strength

The grip strength was measured using a dynamometer (5030JI; JAMAR, Bolingbrook, IL, USA). The participants sat with their elbow joints flexed at 90° and performed the test, alternating between their right and left hands [12]. The maximum reading from the two trials using both hands was recorded in kg [19].

FIM

The FIM was used to measure daily living activities, comprising motor (FIM-motor) and cognitive items [20]. In this study, the FIM motor was evaluated. The FIM-motor consists of 13 items, and the degree of independence for each item was evaluated on a 7-point scale, ranging from total assistance to independence. The FIM motor scores ranged from 13 to 91, with higher scores indicating greater independence in the activities of daily living (ADL).

MNA-SF

The MNA-SF is a nutrition screening tool that is specifically designed for older adults [21, 22]. It consists of six items: changes in food intake, changes in weight, mobility, stress, psychiatric problems, and body mass index (BMI). The total score was used to determine whether the patients were undernourished, at risk of undernourishment, or in good nutritional condition.

Adverse clinical events

In this study, all-cause mortality was the primary endpoint, and cardiovascular mortality was the secondary outcome. Each event was defined as follows: all-cause mortality referred to death from any cause, and cardiovascular mortality encompassed sudden cardiac death, HF, and other cardiovascular deaths.

Mortality was assessed using electronic medical records, routine outpatient visits, and telephone contact. Participants without a death event were censored at the date of last contact.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD), ordinal variables as median [interquartile range], and categorical variables as percentages. Patients were divided into two groups based on the presence or absence of sarcopenia, and their backgrounds were compared using unpaired t tests, the Mann–Whitney U test, and the chi-square test. In addition, survival time analysis (Kaplan–Meier method) was performed to examine the association between sarcopenia and all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. Kaplan–Meier analysis was used to evaluate the log-rank test to compare event-free survival between the two groups. If a statistically significant difference was identified in the survival analysis, a Cox proportional hazards model was used for further analyses. In the Cox proportional hazards analysis, adverse clinical events were considered as the dependent variables. Age, sex, HF stage, LVEF, log-transformed BNP, hemoglobin, albumin, and eGFR, which are established prognostic indicators for HF [1], were used as the explanatory variables. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software version 27 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, USA), and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Sarcopenia diagnosed by either adjusted skeletal muscle mass

When comparing the participants’ backgrounds between non-sarcopenia and sarcopenia, statistically significant differences were observed in age, sex, BMI, SPPB, handgrip strength, skeletal muscle mass, FIM-motor score, and MNA-SF score.

Prevalence of sarcopenia

The prevalence of sarcopenia among all participants was 59.3% after either adjustment, 53.8% after adjustment for height, and 47.4% after adjustment for BMI. Furthermore, 57.5% of the patients were diagnosed with sarcopenia or severe sarcopenia according to the AWGS 2019 criteria.

Figure 1 shows the overlap between sarcopenia diagnoses based on height- and BMI-adjusted skeletal muscle mass. Of those diagnosed with sarcopenia using either adjustment method, 70.7% were diagnosed with sarcopenia using both adjustment methods.

Figure 2a–d shows the association between BMI and the prevalence of sarcopenia. Comparing the AWGS 2019 and AWGS 2025 (either-adjusted) criteria, the AWGS 2025 (either-adjusted) criteria had a higher detection rate of sarcopenia in those with a BMI of ≥ 25 kg/m2 (the AWGS 2019 criteria n = 9; the AWGS 2025 criteria (either-adjusted) n = 17). When comparing height and BMI adjustment in the AWGS 2025 criteria, height adjustment had a higher sarcopenia detection rate for BMI < 22 kg/m2 (height-adjusted n = 100; BMI-adjusted n = 71), while BMI adjustment had a higher sarcopenia detection rate for BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 (height-adjusted n = 8; BMI-adjusted n = 17).

Association of sarcopenia (AWGS 2025) with clinical adverse events

The median [Interquartile range] follow-up period after discharge in this study was 1059 [503–1684] days. Among all participants, 71 all-cause deaths (28.1%) and 50 cardiovascular deaths (19.8%) occurred during the follow-up period of this study. The all-cause mortality rates were 19 (18.4%) and 52 (34.7%) for non-sarcopenia and sarcopenia, respectively. The cardiovascular mortality rates were 13 (12.6%) and 37 (24.7%) for non-sarcopenia and sarcopenia, respectively.

Figure 3a–d presents the results of the Kaplan–Meier analysis comparing sarcopenic and non-sarcopenic patients, as defined by the AWGS 2019 or AWGS 2025 criteria. The log-rank test revealed statistically significant differences between the AWGS 2019 and AWGS 2025 criteria for both all-cause and cardiovascular mortality.

Kaplan–Meier for all-cause and cardiovascular mortality

Table 2a–d shows the results of the Cox proportional hazards analysis. Sarcopenia diagnosed using the AWGS 2025 criteria (either-adjusted) was significantly associated with all-cause mortality (hazard ratio [HR]: 2.361, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.203–4.637, p = 0.013) and cardiovascular mortality (HR: 2.751, 95% CI 1.170–6.740, p = 0.020). Furthermore, significant associations were observed for both all-cause and cardiovascular mortality when either height- or BMI-adjustment was used alone. Similarly, sarcopenia diagnosed using the AWGS 2019 criteria was significantly associated with only all-cause mortality (HR: 1.923, 95% CI 1.020–3.625, p = 0.043).

Discussion

The main results of this study were as follows: (1) the prevalence of sarcopenia diagnosed using the AWGS 2025 criteria was 59.3% (if satisfied with either adjustment), 53.8% (height-adjusted), and 47.4% (BMI-adjusted); (2) among patients diagnosed with sarcopenia using each adjustment method for skeletal muscle mass in the AWGS 2025 criteria, 70.7% were diagnosed with sarcopenia using both adjustment methods; and (3) a significant association for all-cause and cardiovascular mortality was found between non-sarcopenia and sarcopenia diagnosed by the AWGS 2025 criteria.

We discuss the clinical characteristics of patients with sarcopenia diagnosed in AWGS 2025. The prevalence of sarcopenia among all participants was 59.3% if satisfied with either adjustment, 53.8% after adjustment for height, and 47.4% after adjustment for BMI. The prevalence of sarcopenia diagnosed using the AWGS 2019 criteria was 57.5%, and when sarcopenia was diagnosed using either skeletal muscle conditioning method, the diagnostic rate was equivalent to that of the AWGS 2025 criteria.

Sarcopenia patients diagnosed by the AWGS 2025 criteria were older, and had more females, lower BMI, poorer muscle strength, ADL, skeletal muscle mass, and nutritional status than non-sarcopenia patients. These characteristics of sarcopenic patients were consistent with those of sarcopenic patients in previous studies using the AWGS 2019 criteria [10, 23]. The overlap of sarcopenia diagnoses based on height- and BMI-adjusted skeletal muscle mass was examined; approximately 30% of patients were diagnosed with sarcopenia using only one diagnostic method. In addition, examining the relationship between BMI and the prevalence of sarcopenia revealed that the prevalence of sarcopenia differed depending on the patient’s BMI and the method of skeletal muscle adjustment. Visser M et al. examined the relationship between BMI and the proportion of patients diagnosed with low skeletal muscle mass, adjusting for skeletal muscle mass using different methods [24]. The results of the verification revealed that, in Japanese patients, the detection rate of patients with low skeletal muscle mass was lower when height adjustment was used than, when BMI adjustment was performed, particularly in patients with a BMI of 22 or higher. This suggests that the method used to correct for skeletal muscle mass may affect the diagnosis of sarcopenia. We believe that the discrepancy in the diagnosis of sarcopenia in some patients, depending on the skeletal muscle adjustment method, reflects the characteristics of the previous study. In clinical practice, it is essential to note that the prevalence of sarcopenia detected using different skeletal muscle mass adjustments varies.

Next, we consider the association between sarcopenia and mortality. In this study, sarcopenia diagnosed according to the AWGS 2019 criteria was associated with all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, even after adjusting for typical prognostic factors such as age, sex, and heart failure; similarly, a significant association was observed with the AWGS 2025 criteria. According to the AWGS 2025 criteria, skeletal muscle mass was associated with the risk of mortality from heart failure, regardless of the adjustment method. Saito et al. investigated the association between sarcopenia and mortality risk using the AWGS 2019 criteria [23] and found that an increased mortality risk was observed only in patients classified as having severe sarcopenia—defined by low skeletal muscle mass, low muscle strength, and impaired physical performance—according to the AWGS 2019 criteria. In older patients with HF, previous studies have shown that although low skeletal muscle mass and low muscle strength are not associated with mortality risk, impaired physical performance is associated with an increased risk of mortality. These findings highlight the importance of assessing physical performance in older patients with HF. Katano et al. examined the association between sarcopenia diagnosed by the AWGS 2019 criteria and mortality risk [10]. Their study reported that sarcopenia and severe sarcopenia were associated with mortality risk. The AWGS 2025 criteria excluded physical function from the diagnostic criteria, which may have weakened its association with prognosis, but the results of this study contradicted this possibility. The participants in this study were older than those in previous studies, and the proportion of patients with HF reduced EF (HFrEF), as well as the method of measuring the skeletal muscle mass, differed. The prevalence and prognostic impact of sarcopenia in patients with HF have been reported to vary [11]. This study was unable to verify the influence of patient background or skeletal muscle measurement methods; therefore, further verification is needed in the future.

The AWGS 2025 criteria propose new cutoff values for adjusting the skeletal muscle mass by BMI. Therefore, we also applied the cutoff values of BMI-adjusted skeletal muscle mass for the analysis and found an association with mortality risk, even after adjusting for BMI. Katano et al. also examined the association between skeletal muscle mass and all-cause mortality using BMI adjustment in the AWGS 2019 criteria [10]. The results of the verification showed that sarcopenia diagnosed using the conventional height adjustment method was associated with all-cause mortality, but no association was observed when sarcopenia was diagnosed using the BMI adjustment method. Their study validated the cutoff values ​​for skeletal muscle mass measurement using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry in the AWGS 2019 criteria. In this study, skeletal muscle mass measurements were performed using the BIA method, and the AWGS 2025 criteria proposed a cutoff value for BMI adjustment in Asians. This study revealed that applying a cutoff for BMI adjustment suitable for Asians was associated with the risk of mortality in Japanese patients with HF.

While an association with the risk of mortality in patients with HF was observed using both height-adjusted and BMI-adjusted methods, the detection rate of sarcopenia varied depending on the patient’s BMI. Using only one skeletal muscle mass adjustment method may result in missing HF patients with a truly poor prognosis. This study revealed that sarcopenia, regardless of the adjustment method, is associated with mortality risk. In clinical practice, two types of skeletal muscle mass adjustment methods should be used to treat sarcopenia in patients with HF.

In addition, this study found that sarcopenia, HF stage, and BNP were each independently associated with mortality risk. HF is known to cause skeletal muscle loss due to the progression and severity of the disease, including anorexia, cachexia, oxidative stress, and inflammation [25, 26]. Furthermore, it has been reported that sarcopenia interacts with HF and may contribute to the severity of HF [6, 26, 27]. The results of this study may indicate a relationship between sarcopenia and mortality. However, this study was retrospective, making it difficult to verify the mechanism and causal relationships; therefore, further verification is required.

This study is the first to examine the clinical characteristics of older adult patients with HF and sarcopenia using the AWGS 2025 criteria in a Japanese population. The AWGS 2025 criteria proposed diagnostic criteria using two methods: adjusting the skeletal muscle mass for height or BMI. By using either adjustment method, it was revealed that there was an association with the risk of mortality in older adult patients with HF.

However, this study had some limitations. First, it was a single-center study with a small sample size. Just as the sarcopenia detection rate differed depending on the adjustment method for skeletal muscle mass and BMI, the optimal adjustment method may differ depending on the patient’s clinical characteristics (sex, HFrEF or HFpEF, lean or obese). However, the number of cases in this study was small, making it difficult to examine the subgroups. In the future, it will be necessary to verify which adjustment method is superior to clinical practice across various subgroups.

Second, because this was a retrospective cohort study, the screening tests for sarcopenia proposed in the AWGS 2025 criteria were not performed. Therefore, no verification has been conducted to compare the prognosis of patients with possible sarcopenia with that of patients with sarcopenia. We believe that future prospective studies are necessary to accumulate sarcopenia screening test data for patients with HF and to further verify the validity of the AWGS 2025 criteria.

Conclusion

In this study, we examined the clinical characteristics of older adult patients with HF diagnosed with sarcopenia using the AWGS 2025 criteria. Patients with sarcopenia, as determined by the AWGS 2025 criteria, were older, had poor physical function and nutritional status, and had a higher risk of mortality.
