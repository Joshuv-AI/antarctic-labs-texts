Supraglottic Lymphatic Malformation Managed With Transoral Microelectrode Surgery (TOMES)
Authors: Rosa Hernández, Altea Esteve, Carmen Fernández, Enrique Zapater
Published: 2024

Laryngeal lymphatic malformation (LM) is a rare vascular congenital pathology 1 which can grow or become infected, potentially leading to airway obstruction. 2 We present the case of a 4‐year‐old child with acute epiglottitis secondary to an infected supraglottic LM. Here we present the first case of LM in a child removed by transoral microelectrode surgery (TOMES).

Case Report

A 4‐year‐old female patient with a history of an asymptomatic vascular alteration on the lingual face of the epiglottis. She was taken to the emergency department with dyspnea, fever, and a cervical mass that had evolved over a few days. Fibrolaryngoscopy identified an oedema and epiglottic displacement with a partial supraglottic obstruction (Figure 1A). This episode required orotracheal intubation in the intensive care unit and was resolved with a parenteral antibiotic and high dose steroids. Ultrasound and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) confirmed the presence of a LM and we decided upon a surgical excision strategy, taking a transoral approach.

(A) An endoscopic view of a supraglottic macrocystic lymphatic malformation from the left side of the epiglottis and vallecula. Note the epiglottic deviation and difficulty in viewing the glottis. (B) Postoperative follow‐up of the patient 3 months after the intervention. Note the total resolution of the lymphatic malformation and adequate view of the larynx. The epiglottis was partially resected.

First, we inserted the pediatric laryngoscope and then used TOMES to remove the cyst, applying the coagulation mode and employing the 15 W setting, along with suction because of the smoke produced in the process. The resection was performed by following the healthy tissue surrounding the tumor. In this case, partial resection of the epiglottis and of the pharyngoepiglottic fold was required. The patient was successfully intubated and was immediately extubated in the postoperative period. She was then monitored for 2 days in the intensive care unit. She was administered a supraglottic diet and was finally discharged 5 days later.

The anatomopathological examination described a thin‐walled polypoid cystic lesion that was immunohistochemically positive for CD34 and D240. Given that D240 expression is limited to cells of lymphatic differentiation, a diagnosis of LM was reached. Three months later, fibrolaryngoscopy revealed total resolution of the LM (Figure 1B).

Discussion

Supraglottic LMs are a rare, congenital, low‐flow vascular malformation caused by lymphatic system development aberrations. 1 , 2 The clinical presentation of LM depends on its size and location, although typical head and neck LMs will present as a mass within the first few years of life, which will then slowly increase in size as the child gets older. However, LM exacerbations are also possible which may coincide with viral upper respiratory infections and can pose a risk of airway obstruction, 3 as was the case in our patient.

Ultrasound and MRI imaging studies allow the diagnosis of LM and help show the extent of the disease for planning treatment. The typical appearance of macrocystic LMs on MRI is hyperintense on T2 and variable on T1, and enhancement of the walls or septa of the cysts can usually be observed with contrast in the T1 sequences. In the case of complications, MRI signal alterations will indicate signs of inflammatory changes.

Pediatric LMs are a particular challenge because otolaryngologists must decide when and how to best treat them. This will depend on the disease stage, 1 as defined according to the staging system proposed by Berg. 2 The case presented here was a stage II LM (a macrocystic lesion involving the vallecular surface of the epiglottis with a free glottic plane). Surgical excision is the most efficient treatment for macrocystic lesions of this type. 2 The academic literature suggests several possible techniques for surgical approaches, including open surgical excision, cauterization, laser surgery, and radiofrequency ablation. 1 , 2 However, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first time the use of TOMES has been described for treating a pediatric laryngeal LM with a partial obstruction of the larynx.

Microelectrodes were designed in our Department in 2003 for transoral surgery of malignant tumors. We used a set of straight ang angulated needle microelectrode connected to a Valleylab Force 2 electrosurgical generator (Pfeizer) set to an output power of 15–20 W (Figure 2). The main advantages are excellent hemostasis and the view of a tidy and well‐defined surgical field, the speed and the low cost of the equipment when compared with CO2 laser. The tissue damage is similar. 4 , 5 The surgical technique using TOMES is based on conventional direct suspension laryngoscopy, employing microsurgery instruments. The TOMES device comprises a monopolar microelectrode inserted on a handle designed for a transoral approach, which is connected to an electrosurgical generator set to output high‐frequency electric current power at 15 W. 4 In this patient case, we were able to safely remove the LM in a short period of time while also ensuring adequate haemostasis. Nevertheless, adequate patient follow‐up is important to detect a possible recurrence.
