Glofitamab combined with tislelizumab in relapsed/refractory diffuse large B-cell lymphoma: a single-center pilot trial
Authors: De Zhou, Wenjuan Yu, Mixue Xie, Ehsan Ghoushi, Xuelian Hu, Xin Huang, Juying Wei, Xingnong Ye, Xiaolong Zheng, Jie Jin, Wanzhuo Xie, Hongyan Tong
Published: 2025

To the Editor,

The prognosis for patients with relapsed/refractory (R/R) diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) remains poor, and bispecific antibodies have emerged as a promising therapeutic approach. Glofitamab, a CD20×CD3 bispecific antibody, engages and redirects T cells to eliminate malignant B cells. In a single-arm phase 2 study, glofitamab monotherapy achieved a complete response (CR) rate of 39% [1]. Furthermore, improved overall survival (OS) was observed with Glofit-GemOx compared to R-GemOx in patients with R/R DLBCL [2].

Bispecific antibodies and chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cell therapies share similar mechanisms of action, both relying on the quantity and functional integrity of T cells or CAR-T cells. T-cell exhaustion has increasingly been recognized as a major contributor to CAR-T cell dysfunction [3]. We hypothesize that T cell exhaustion may likewise impair the efficacy of bispecific antibodies. Moreover, the baseline quantity and function of T cells may be even more critical for bispecific antibody therapy than for CAR-T cell therapy. Real-world studies have shown that glofitamab demonstrates relatively poor efficacy in R/R DLBCL patients previously treated with bendamustine [4], potentially due to T cell depletion or dysfunction in these individuals. These findings suggest that enhancing T cell function and mitigating T cell exhaustion could represent a novel strategy to optimize the efficacy of bispecific antibodies in R/R DLBCL.

Programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) is a critical negative regulator of CD8⁺ T-cell activation and is highly expressed on exhausted T cells in cancer [5]. Downregulation of PD-1 has been shown to alleviate T cell exhaustion [6, 7]. Although most studies combining CAR-T with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have yielded negative results in patients with R/R DLBCL [8], some studies have reported a potential prolongation of the duration of remission (DOR) [9, 10]. Moreover, PD-1-integrated CAR-T cells have shown promising efficacy in both xenograft models and clinical studies [11]. Considering that CAR-T therapy ensures the quantity and function of CAR-T cells through ex vivo expansion and modification, while patients receiving glofitamab may have insufficient T cell quantity and function; therefore, we speculate that the combination of PD-1 inhibitor and glofitamab may have more promising prospects than CAR-T therapy.

Based on these findings, we hypothesize that ICIs may enhance the efficacy of glofitamab by mitigating T cell exhaustion. Therefore, we conducted a pilot study to evaluate the efficacy and safety of glofitamab combined with tislelizumab in patients with R/R DLBCL.

Patients aged 18 years or older with a confirmed diagnosis of DLBCL were enrolled. All participants had relapsed or refractory disease following at least two prior lines of therapy. The study followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital Zhejiang University School of Medicine. The protocol is illustrated in Fig. 1A. Each cycle lasted 21 days. During the first cycle, patients received obinutuzumab (1000 mg) on Day 1, followed by glofitamab on Day 8 (2.5 mg) and Day 15 (10 mg). If no cytokine release syndrome (CRS) of grade ≥2 occurred during this period, subsequent cycles consisted of glofitamab (30 mg on Day 1) and tislelizumab (200 mg on Day 8). Disease response was evaluated every three cycles using whole-body ¹⁸F-FDG PET/CT or contrast-enhanced CT. For patients with central nervous system (CNS) relapse (e.g., Patient 9), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was used instead. Ultrasound and physical examinations were performed at each cycle. Patients who experienced progressive disease (PD) were withdrawn from the study and transitioned to salvage therapy. Those with stable disease (SD) or partial remission (PR) continued treatment for up to 12 cycles unless unacceptable toxicity was observed. The primary endpoint was safety, while secondary endpoints included CR rate, overall response rate (ORR), DOR, progression-free survival (PFS), and OS. Treatment response was assessed according to the 2014 Lugano response criteria. Adverse events (AEs) and CRS were graded using CTCAE version 5.0 and the ASTCT criteria, respectively.

Ten patients were enrolled between February 20, 2024, and June 11, 2024, at the First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine. The clinical characteristics of the enrolled patients are summarized in Table 1. The cohort included six female and four male patients, with a median age of 68.5 years (range, 25–80). The median International Prognostic Index (IPI) score was 3.5 (range, 2–5), and the median number of prior lines of therapy was 3.5 (range, 2–7). All patients had previously received rituximab; 90% had undergone treatment with an anthracycline-containing regimen, and 60% had been treated with polatuzumab vedotin. All patients were refractory to their most recent line of therapy. Patients were followed until June 11, 2025, with a median follow-up duration of 8.5 months (range, 2.4–15.9). No patients were lost to follow-up. All 10 patients were included in the safety analysis. Overall, the combination of glofitamab and tislelizumab was well tolerated, with treatment discontinuation observed in two patients. Patient 3 died of COVID-19 after two cycles, and Patient 1 developed intestinal perforation after three cycles. Notably, the site of perforation corresponded to the original lymphoma lesion, suggesting that the event may not have been treatment-related. In terms of CRS, only one patient developed grade 2 CRS, five patients experienced grade 1 CRS, and no immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) or grade 3–4 CRS was observed. Grade 3–4 hematological adverse events occurred in four patients, including one case of grade 4 neutropenia combined with grade 3 thrombocytopenia, two cases of grade 3 neutropenia with grade 3 thrombocytopenia, and one case of grade 3 neutropenia alone.

Patient 3 died of COVID-19 after two cycles of treatment and could not be evaluated for efficacy. The remaining nine patients were evaluated for efficacy. The ORR was 66.7% (6/9), with five patients (55.5%) achieving CR and three patients (33.3%) showing PD. Among the six responders, one patient (Patient 6) experienced disease relapse, while the remaining five patients maintained remission (Fig. 1B).

Patient 1 developed intestinal perforation after completing three cycles of treatment, necessitating surgical intervention. Postoperatively, the patient experienced a severe intra-abdominal infection. After successfully managing these complications, anti-lymphoma therapy was discontinued. A subsequent PET-CT scan revealed that the standard uptake value (SUV) in certain areas of the intestine was slightly higher than that of the liver blood pool. Although it remains unclear whether these findings were influenced by the surgery and intra-abdominal infection, the therapeutic response was classified as PR. The patient had previously received four lines of treatment, none of which were effective. However, after discontinuation of anti-lymphoma therapy, she has remained progression-free for over 12 months.

Patients who developed grade ≥2 CRS during the first cycle of glofitamab monotherapy were excluded from the study due to concerns about an increased risk of higher-grade CRS with the addition of tislelizumab. Additionally, such patients may have inherently robust T-cell function and may not derive additional benefit from combination therapy. In this study, an increase in CRS severity was observed in four patients following the introduction of tislelizumab. Patient 1 experienced an escalation from grade 1 to grade 2 CRS, while Patients 3, 6, and 9 progressed from grade 0 to grade 1. Efficacy data were unavailable for Patient 3 due to early death. Patient 1 achieved PR, and Patients 6 and 9 achieved CR. The observed increase in CRS severity in these patients may be associated with the PD-1 inhibitor alleviating T-cell exhaustion and potentially enhancing the response rate.

The patients included in this study represented a highly challenging population, with a median of 3.5 prior lines of therapy, and all patients were refractory to their most recent line of therapy. Although the sample size was small and the follow-up period was short, our findings suggest that the combination of glofitamab and tislelizumab does not increase the risk of CRS or ICANS and demonstrates acceptable efficacy. To our knowledge, this is the first report evaluating the safety and efficacy of glofitamab in combination with a PD-1 inhibitor. We aim to share these preliminary results to inform future studies and treatment strategies.

In summary, this study provides preliminary evidence that the combination of glofitamab and tislelizumab is safe and demonstrates acceptable efficacy in patients with R/R DLBCL. These findings suggest that combining bispecific antibodies with PD-1 inhibitors warrants further investigation.
