Step-specific learning curve of the TMINI robotic system for total knee arthroplasty: a single-surgeon CUSUM analysis
Authors: Andres Wong, Jonathan Brutti, Ivan Garces, Trevor Cotter, Alexander Sah, Charles Lawrie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRobotic total knee arthroplasty (R-TKA) adoption continues to expand, yet most learning curve analyses focus on global operative time rather than individual procedural steps. The TMINI system is a handheld, imageless robotic platform with limited published data. We evaluated step-specific learning curves in a single surgeon’s first 75 consecutive cases with complete intraoperative timing data. We retrospectively reviewed 75 primary TKAs performed with the TMINI system (January-August 2025). Four intraoperative timing metrics were recorded: bone registration time, intraoperative planning time, bone resection time, and time from incision to implants. Learning curves were assessed using cumulative sum (CUSUM) analysis; turning points were identified at peak CUSUM values. Early-versus-later comparisons used Mann-Whitney U tests. Sensitivity analysis excluded extreme outliers (> mean + 3 SD). CUSUM turning points varied considerably by procedural step. Time to implants stabilized earliest (case 8; 12.0% median reduction; P = 0.006). Intraoperative planning time turned at case 12 (32.1% reduction; P < 0.001). Bone resection time trended toward improvement but did not reach significance in the primary cohort (P = 0.131); significance was achieved after outlier exclusion (35.0% median reduction; P < 0.001). Bone registration was most gradual, turning at case 56 (10.1% reduction; P = 0.002). Sensitivity analyses were consistent. The TMINI system demonstrated measurable but nonuniform step-specific learning curves across 75 cases. Cognitive and software-driven steps improved early, while platform-specific tasks such as bone registration required more cases to stabilize. Introduction

Robotic total knee arthroplasty (R-TKA) has become one of the fastest-growing segments within the field of arthroplasty [1], reflecting its recognized advantages of improved precision and reproducibility [2, 3]. Several robotic platforms have been popularized in routine practice over the past decade [4]. As adoption continues to expand, understanding both the benefits and the practical challenges these systems introduce in the operating room is clinically relevant.

As with any new technology or technique adopted into practice, R-TKA has consistently been associated with longer operative times during the learning curve [4, 5]. This time penalty can present a barrier to adoption and has been linked to system complexity, planning demands, and workflow differences between platforms [4, 5]. It is important for surgeons to understand this requisite time investment prior to adopting a new system to ensure adequate time and resources are allotted to getting through the learning curve.

The number of cases required to achieve procedural time neutrality varies by robotic platform [4]. Procedural time neutrality here refers to within-platform consistency, when TMINI case times stabilize from one case to the next, rather than to TMINI cases matching conventional TKA times. Only a single study has been published on the learning curve for the THINK Surgical TMINI system for TKA, which evaluated overall case times [6, 7]. However, there is no published data on the learning curve for each procedural step using this robotic system. Granular insight into where efficiency may initially be lost can help surgeons anticipate challenges and train to lower the learning curve of this technology. To address this gap in the literature, the purpose of this study was therefore to evaluate the learning curve associated with the TMINI system using step-specific intraoperative efficiency metrics, including registration, intraoperative planning, bone resection, and time to implants. These metrics were chosen to clarify where efficiency gains occur and when meaningful procedural proficiency is achieved, rather than relying on global operative time (skin-to-skin) alone. Table 1

Materials and methods

Institutional review board approval was obtained from Baptist Health South Florida (IRBNet ID 2368928-2) prior to study initiation. This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. This retrospective learning-curve analysis was conducted at a single academic medical center to evaluate adoption of the TMINI® Miniature Robotic System (THINK Surgical, Fremont, CA, USA) for primary TKA. Seventy-five consecutive primary TKAs with complete intraoperative timing data, performed between January 1, 2025 and August 1, 2025, were included (Table 2). These were drawn from 93 consecutive THINK-assisted TKAs performed during the same period; 18 cases were excluded due to incomplete intraoperative timing data. All procedures were performed by a single fellowship-trained adult reconstruction surgeon who had completed over 1,500 robotic-assisted knee replacements across nine years in practice using other robotic platforms (Stryker MAKO and Zimmer Biomet ROSA), with a typical caseload of six TKAs per day, two days per week. The surgeon had no prior clinical experience with the TMINI platform. Pre-clinical preparation consisted of a single one-hour sawbones training session led by a company representative prior to the first clinical case. Case 1 of this series therefore represents the surgeon’s first clinical use of the TMINI system. During the same time period, the surgeon continued to perform TKAs using the Stryker MAKO and Zimmer Biomet ROSA platforms. The implications of this concurrent platform use are discussed in the Limitations section. All cases were performed with the operating surgeon as primary surgeon; trainees, when present, served only as first assistants and did not perform any tracked intraoperative steps.

The TMINI Miniature Robotic System is a handheld, image-based platform consisting of a graphical surgical-planning interface and an optically tracked, actively stabilized handheld pin drill. Preoperative CT-based planning defines target femoral and tibial component orientations. Intraoperatively, anatomic landmarks are registered using wireless probes, an intraoperative plan is reviewed and finalized on the planning screen, and the handheld drill places tracked pins that anchor a universal cut block on the distal femur and proximal tibia. A separate shift block then translates from robotic pin positions to the TKA-specific cut-block holes used for the 4-in-1 femoral resections. The four intraoperative procedural stages analyzed in this study (bone registration, intraoperative planning, bone resection, and time from incision to implants) correspond directly to these workflow steps.

Step-specific timing metrics

Four step-specific intraoperative efficiency metrics were analyzed: bone registration time (seconds), intraoperative planning time (seconds), bone resection time (seconds), and time from incision to implants implanted (minutes). The four step-level intervals were extracted from the TMINI system’s electronic event log, which records timestamps at each major workflow transition. Incision and final-implant times, used to compute the time-from-incision-to-implants metric, were abstracted from the intraoperative anesthesia and circulating-nurse records. Bone registration time spanned from initiation of femoral-epicondylar axis registration through completion of tibial registration verification. Intraoperative planning time spanned from initiation of intraoperative gap assessment through confirmation of intraoperative plan changes. Bone resection time encompassed the platform-specific steps of universal cut block tightening on the robotic pins for the distal femur and proximal tibia, use of the shift block to translate from robot pin positions to the TKA-specific cut-block holes for the 4-in-1 femoral resections, and the actual bony resections. Time from incision to implants implanted was defined as the interval from skin incision to placement of the final femoral component, final tibial component, and final polyethylene insert; this interval ended at definitive implant placement and did not include closure. Cases were analyzed in chronological order, with case number serving as the x-axis for all learning curve analyses.

CUSUM analysis and turning points

Learning curves were assessed with cumulative sum (CUSUM) analysis for each timing metric, an established methodology for monitoring surgical learning curves [8, 9]. The cohort mean was used as the reference target for each metric. For sequential case i, the deviation from the cohort mean was defined as d(i) = time(i) - mean(time), where positive deviations represented longer-than-average performance and negative deviations represented shorter-than-average performance. The CUSUM value for case i was computed as the running cumulative sum of deviations from case 1 through case i. For each metric, the turning point was defined as the case number corresponding to the maximum (peak) of the CUSUM curve. This peak marks the case beyond which subsequent cases tended to fall below the cohort mean for that metric, signaling the transition from a phase dominated by above-average durations to a phase dominated by below-average durations.

Early vs. later comparisons

To quantify differences before and after each CUSUM-derived turning point, cases were divided into early and later groups for each metric. Early cases were those from case 1 through the turning point; later cases were the subsequent cases through the end of the cohort. Because timing outcomes were not assumed to follow a normal distribution, early versus later comparisons were made with two-sided Mann-Whitney U tests, and results were summarized as median and interquartile range (IQR). Statistical significance was set at a two-sided α = 0.05.

Sensitivity analyses

A prespecified sensitivity analysis was performed to assess robustness against workflow-disruption cases. Extreme timing outliers were defined for each metric using a 3-standard deviation rule (values greater than mean + 3 × SD). Thresholds and the number of cases exceeding each threshold were recorded for transparency (Table 3). For the outlier-excluded analysis, any case flagged as an extreme outlier on any of the four timing metrics was excluded, leaving a 70-case subset of the primary 75-case cohort (Table 2). The outlier-excluded subset is therefore not a separate population but a nested subset of the same cases analyzed in the primary cohort. Extreme outliers in step-level intraoperative timing typically reflect identifiable workflow disruptions (for example, equipment troubleshooting, intraoperative instrument exchanges, or unscheduled pauses for soft-tissue concerns) rather than fundamental differences in the underlying procedure, and are not always reconstructable from robotic logs alone. Reporting both the primary and outlier-excluded analyses allows readers to weigh the underlying learning signal against the contribution of these rare workflow events. Because flagged cases were removed while keeping the original case numbering, the remaining case numbers in the outlier-excluded subset were not perfectly consecutive, so the early-group size could be smaller than the turning-point case number.

Results

Cohort characteristics

Cohort characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The 75-case cohort had a mean age of 68.2 ± 10.3 years (range, 43 to 86), a mean BMI of 32.6 ± 6.9 kg/m² (range, 20.0 to 50.7), and was predominantly female (n = 46, 61.3%). Preoperative coronal alignment was distributed as 53 varus knees (70.7%), 4 neutral knees (5.3%), and 18 valgus knees (24.0%), with an overall median preoperative hip-knee-ankle angle of 5.0° (range, -14.0° to 22.0°). Median preoperative flexion contracture across the full cohort was 2.0° (range, -15.0° to 20.0°), with negative values indicating hyperextension.

The initial cohort contained 75 cases for the primary learning-curve analysis. Applying the prespecified 3-SD outlier criterion to any timing metric flagged 5 unique cases, yielding a 70-case outlier-excluded subset. Outlier thresholds and metric-specific outlier counts are summarized in Table 3. Raw per-case timing distributions are shown in Online Resource 1.

Registration time

CUSUM analysis identified a turning point for registration time at case 56 in the primary cohort and case 11 in the outlier-excluded subset (Table 2; Fig. 1A, B). In the primary cohort, median registration time was 199 s in early cases (cases 1 to 56) versus 179 s in later cases (cases 57 to 75), a statistically significant 10.1% median reduction (P = 0.002). In the outlier-excluded subset (n = 70), the turning point shifted to case 11, with median registration time decreased from 233 s to 186 s between early and later cases, a 20.2% reduction (P = 0.001).

Intraoperative planning time

Intraoperative planning time showed a turning point at case 12 in both the primary and outlier-excluded subsets (Table 2; Fig. 1C, D). In the primary cohort, median planning time dropped markedly from 327 s in early cases (cases 1 to 12) to 222 s in later cases (cases 13 to 75), a 32.1% reduction (P < 0.001). In the outlier-excluded subset, results were consistent: median planning time decreased from 328 s to 221 s, a 32.6% reduction (P < 0.001).

Bone resection time

The CUSUM turning point for bone resection time occurred at case 23 in the primary cohort and case 10 in the outlier-excluded subset (Table 2; Fig. 1E, F). In the primary cohort, median bone resection time was 201 s in early cases (cases 1 to 23) versus 156 s in later cases (cases 24 to 75); this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.131). After excluding extreme outliers, the turning point shifted earlier to case 10, and median bone resection time decreased significantly from 254 s in early cases to 165 s in later cases, a 35.0% reduction (P < 0.001), confirming that the primary-cohort signal was attenuated by extreme values.

Time to implants

Time to implants showed the earliest turning point, at case 8 in both the primary and outlier-excluded subsets (Table 2; Fig. 1G, H). In the primary cohort, median time to implants decreased from 50 min in early cases (cases 1 to 8) to 44 min in later cases (cases 9 to 75), a 12.0% reduction (P = 0.006). In the outlier-excluded subset, the turning point remained at case 8, with median time to implants decreasing from 50 min to 44 min, also a 12.0% reduction (P = 0.013).

Across the primary cohort, values are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD): mean registration time was 209 ± 66 s, mean intraoperative planning time was 265 ± 106 s, mean bone resection time was 209 ± 192 s, and mean time to implants was 46 ± 8 min. The three robot-specific steps together contributed 683 s (11.4 min) to the mean 46-minute time-to-implants interval, accounting for 24.7% of the case; the remaining 75.3% reflected conventional operative steps outside the robot-assisted workflow (skin incision and exposure, capsulotomy, ligamentous releases, intermediate setup and tracker array placement, trialing and gap balancing, and definitive component cementation and impaction). For direct early-to-later interpretation, median time-to-implants decreased by approximately 6 min (50 to 44 min), whereas the summed early-to-later reductions across the three robot-specific steps totaled approximately 170 s (2.8 min); less than half of the observed time-to-implants improvement is therefore arithmetically attributable to changes in the timed robot-specific steps themselves.

Sensitivity split testing

Sensitivity split testing examined how early-versus-later comparisons evolved across multiple early-case cutoffs. In the primary cohort (n = 75), p-values for early-versus-later comparisons varied by metric and by cutoff, with full results provided in Table 4. In the outlier-excluded subset (n = 70), corresponding sensitivity split results are presented in Table 5. In the primary cohort (Table 4), the lowest p-values for planning time and time-to-implants clustered tightly near their CUSUM-defined turning points (k = 10–12 for planning, k = 7–8 for time-to-implants), and the lowest p-values for bone resection occurred near k = 7–10. Registration time was the exception in the primary cohort: the lowest p-values were observed near k = 7–8 (p = 0.002 − 0.001) rather than near the CUSUM peak at case 56, suggesting a brief early-improvement phase superimposed on a more gradual later trajectory. After 3-SD outlier exclusion (Table 5), p-value minima for all four metrics clustered tightly around their respective CUSUM turning points (k = 7–11), supporting the robustness of the step-level learning signal once a small number of extreme-duration cases were removed.

Discussion

This step-level analysis shows that adoption of the TMINI system follows a measurable but nonuniform trajectory. Across the first 75 single-surgeon cases performed by a single high-volume surgeon, efficiency gains varied by operative phase. Intraoperative planning improved early, with a primary CUSUM turning point around case 12 and corroborating sensitivity split signals generally within the first 10 to 15 cases. Time-to-implants improved even earlier, with a turning point around cases 7 to 8 and significant separation between early and later cases. Registration also improved, although the pattern appeared more gradual and possibly multi-phase. In the primary 75-case cohort the mean-target CUSUM turning point was reached at case 56. However, after excluding two extreme-duration registration cases by the prespecified 3-SD criterion, the turning point shifted markedly earlier to case 11 and the within-group reduction approximately doubled (20.2% versus 10.1%). The discrepancy is therefore largely attributable to two extreme cases in the primary cohort rather than to a uniformly slow learning trajectory, and the outlier-excluded estimate of approximately 11 cases is the more representative description of registration learning under typical workflow conditions. The “prolonged” descriptor used in our conclusion accordingly refers to registration’s longer trajectory relative to planning and time-to-implants, not to an absolute claim that registration is uniquely difficult to learn. Bone resection trended toward improvement in both median time and consistency, but the signal was more variable and sensitive to extreme values, as reflected by a nonsignificant primary early-versus-later comparison when outliers were retained. After predefined 3-SD outlier exclusion, all four step-level metrics demonstrated significant improvement, and turning points for registration and bone resection shifted earlier, supporting the robustness of the overall learning signal.

The workflow efficiency patterns observed here are consistent with prior reports of robotic TKA adoption across multiple platforms, where operative-time inflection points have typically been described as occurring early, with the precise threshold varying by platform, prior experience, endpoint definition, and analytic method [10, 11]. Most directly comparable, the recent multicenter THINK analysis by Sah et al. evaluated the first 25 robotic TKAs across 7 surgeons and found that benchmark time neutrality occurred at approximately 10 cases, with a CUSUM-defined learning curve averaging 7.9 cases [7]. The early turning points observed here for planning and time-to-implants are therefore not only plausible but well aligned with those adoption estimates. For comparison, Vermue et al. reported a 9-case total-operative-time learning curve for an imageless robotic TKA system, with the femoral cutting guide and resection step requiring the longest individual learning trajectory (16 cases) and operative time not reaching neutrality with conventional surgery [12]. The pattern of platform-specific hands-on steps maturing more gradually than cognitive planning steps therefore appears conserved across robotic platforms, even though the specific step with the longest learning curve varies by system.

Several distinctions between the present study and prior literature merit consideration. First, this was a single-surgeon analysis, which reduces inter-surgeon variation and allows assessment of within-surgeon workflow adoption. Second, the series extends beyond the first 25 cases examined in the multicenter THINK paper, which is relevant because certain operative steps may continue to mature even after global operative-time curves have stabilized [7]. Most importantly, the analysis was performed at the step level across all 75 single-surgeon cases rather than focusing on skin-to-skin time or a directly observed subset, a distinction that helps explain why the registration signal here appears later than global learning curves reported elsewhere. CUSUM at the step level identifies when a specific component consistently outperforms the series mean over time, not simply whether the earliest cases are slower than the rest. This interpretation is supported by prior work showing that operative time learning curves can exist independently of technical accuracy, with component alignment and gap balancing remaining consistent from the earliest cases even as efficiency continues to improve [13, 14].

Two complementary factors help explain why overall time-to-implants and intraoperative planning improved quickly while registration took longer to stabilize. The first is the relative time weight of each step. The three robot-specific steps contributed 24.7% of the mean 46-minute time-to-implants interval, while the remaining 75.3% reflected conventional operative steps such as exposure, soft-tissue balancing, and cementation. (Note: the time-to-implants interval ends at implant insertion; closure occurs after this measurement and is therefore not included in the 75.3%.) Because these conventional steps were already well-practiced in a high-volume arthroplasty surgeon, even modest efficiency gains within the robotic steps produced rapid stabilization of the full skin-to-implants interval. The second factor is the magnitude of improvement within each step. Planning time declined by 32.1% from early to later cases in the primary cohort, compared with 10.1% for registration; the asymmetry persisted after outlier exclusion (32.6% vs. 20.2%). Planning therefore showed not only an earlier CUSUM turning point but also a larger percent reduction than registration, so both the smaller share of total case time and the smaller percent improvement contributed to registration’s more gradual trajectory.

Of note, the observed reduction in total time to implants exceeded what the three measured robotic step improvements alone would arithmetically account for, given that those steps collectively represent only 24.7% of the total interval. This implies that a portion of the efficiency gain also occurred within the conventional operative steps, though these were not directly time-stamped in this analysis. Plausible contributors include progressive operating room team familiarity with the robotic setup workflow, more efficient bone pin and tracker array placement, and optimization of the exposure and soft-tissue assessment sequence as the surgical team developed system-specific rhythm. Future studies incorporating granular time-stamp logging for individual conventional steps would help isolate which components drove this additional improvement. From a clinical-significance perspective, the 170-second summed early-to-later reduction across robot-specific steps corresponds to approximately 6% of the mean ~ 45-minute time-to-implants interval. At the per-case level this magnitude is within the range expected from minor differences in operative setup, soft-tissue handling, and team workflow; at the program level, however, even small percentage gains aggregate across high-volume practices and may meaningfully affect operating-room scheduling, throughput, and staffing predictability over time. For context, published conventional TKA operative-time variability in a single-institution series of 738 cases was reported as 18.5 to 25.8 min (one standard deviation) on mean operative times of 69.5 to 77.3 min [15], corresponding to a case-to-case coefficient of variation on the order of 27% to 33%. The summed early-to-later 6% reduction observed in this series across robot-specific steps therefore lies well within the inherent operative-time variability reported for conventional TKA in experienced hands, suggesting that the absolute magnitude of the time savings, while statistically detectable, is unlikely to exceed normal workflow noise on a per-case basis.

A likely explanation for this asymmetry is the nature of each step. Intraoperative planning is largely cognitive and software-driven; once the surgeon becomes familiar with the user interface, the CT-based plan, and the intraoperative adjustment logic, decisions can be executed rapidly. Prior robotic experience tends to transfer well at this stage, because target alignment, gap balancing, and implant positioning cues are broadly similar across platforms. The operating surgeon in this series had extensive prior experience with other robotic TKA platforms, and that background likely contributed to the rapid early improvement observed in the planning step. Registration, by contrast, is a platform-specific, hands-on task. It depends on the mechanical handling of the TMINI system’s wireless probes, the sequence of landmark acquisitions, and verification steps unique to this platform, which do not carry over directly from fixed-arm or differently configured robotic systems. Additional deliberate, repeated practice is therefore required before registration efficiency improves, which is consistent with the more gradual trajectory observed here.

The principal contribution of this study is reframing the robotic TKA learning curve as a collection of step-specific processes rather than a single operative-time phenomenon. Rather than a single global learning curve, the data show step-specific trajectories that justify analyzing each intraoperative phase on its own terms. A second contribution is methodological: by excluding fellow-involved cases, this analysis was designed to isolate surgeon-specific learning, a distinction that has rarely been addressed explicitly in prior robotic TKA learning-curve studies. The combination of CUSUM, early-versus-later comparisons defined by CUSUM turning points, and sensitivity split testing across multiple cutoffs yields a more resilient interpretation than any single method alone, and the predefined outlier analysis confirms that the findings reflect an underlying pattern in the primary data rather than post hoc shaping.

Our findings carry several practical implications. From a scheduling standpoint, not all robotic time penalties persist uniformly across the procedure, suggesting that even as a surgeon grows comfortable with the system, operating room variability may continue to be driven by specific steps rather than the workflow as a whole. This pattern has been observed across other robotic platforms, in which registration and bone preparation have been identified as the steps most likely to drive differences in operative time during adoption [16–18].

From a training perspective, planning and registration stand out as the most productive early targets. Because registration consistently shows the longest improvement trajectory across platforms [17, 18], early adoption should prioritize deliberate, repeated practice in landmark acquisition, verification sequencing, and team coordination rather than general case volume alone. Staff and trainee involvement also deserve consideration. Because these steps do not mature at the same pace, introducing variable team members during early adoption may add noise to the least predictable steps, particularly registration. A more gradual approach to team integration may serve programs better than an abrupt transition to a fully robotic workflow.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. This was a single-surgeon, single-center study, which strengthens internal control but limits generalizability. In addition, the operating surgeon had prior experience with other robotic TKA platforms, which may have accelerated the cognitive portion of adoption and could limit the generalizability of the planning-step learning curve to surgeons without prior robotic exposure. The mean-target CUSUM is sensitive to the cohort from which the target mean is derived, and turning points can shift with inclusion or exclusion of extreme cases. Multi-surgeon validation across different practice settings benchmarked against manual TKA using time-neutrality frameworks, would help clarify not only when a system becomes more efficient, but which specific workflow components drive that transition and whether the associated gains are clinically meaningful. During the same study period the operating surgeon continued to perform TKAs using the Stryker MAKO and Zimmer Biomet ROSA platforms; concurrent multi-platform robotic use may have influenced learning rates relative to a surgeon adopting TMINI as a sole robotic system, and the present learning curve should be interpreted in that context. In addition, day-to-day variation in operating-room staffing, including scrub, circulator, and trainee personnel, was not directly modeled and represents an additional source of within-cohort timing variability.

Conclusion

Handheld robotic TKA demonstrated a measurable learning curve when evaluated with step-level metrics in a single surgeon’s first 75 cases. Planning and time-to-implants improved early; registration improved but showed a more prolonged and variable stabilization pattern; and bone resection trended toward improvement while remaining sensitive to outliers and case-specific extremes. CUSUM analysis, early-versus-later comparisons, and sensitivity split testing together provided a more complete picture of adoption than any single method alone. These data suggest that step-level learning curves may offer a practical framework for training, scheduling, and implementation of robotic arthroplasty systems. Two practical takeaways follow. First, the overall learning curve for the TMINI system is short for an experienced robotic surgeon. Second, this curve is step-specific: planning improves fastest, registration the slowest. Surgeons adopting handheld robotic TKA should plan focused early training around the registration step rather than relying on cumulative case volume alone, and device manufacturers may meaningfully shorten the learning curve in future system iterations by targeting registration usability and workflow.
