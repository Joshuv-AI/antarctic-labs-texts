Real-time visualisation of developing chick embryos cultured in transparent plastic films from the blastoderm stage until hatching
Authors: Katsuya Obara, Chizuka Obara, Mitsuru Naito, Atsushi Asano, Atsushi Tajima
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Real-time visualisation of chick embryo development occurring inside an opaque eggshell is a major goal of developmental biology. This goal was partially achieved when 3-day-old embryos recovered from fertilised shelled eggs, preincubated in a conventional incubator, hatched after the egg contents had been transferred into shell-less embryo culture systems constructed of transparent plastic film placed in a plastic cup. However, the hatchable shell-less embryo culture systems described thus far cannot be used to visualise the dynamic morphological changes associated with the emergence of major organ systems during the first 3 days of embryo development, as the blastoderm area needs to be covered with an opaque membrane. Here, we present the development of a chick embryo culture system that enables omnidirectional real-time visualisation of the developing embryo, beginning from the newly laid blastoderm stage embryo until hatching.

Introduction

A main goal of avian developmental biology is to elucidate the sequence of events that take place inside of opaque eggshells, which protect developing embryos while maintaining respiration throughout embryogenesis. Historically, Aristotle, a pioneer of multiple academic disciplines, including avian embryology, physically opened chicken eggshells to describe the macroscopic morphology of developing embryos in his historical treatise “History of Animals”1. Since that time, the largest obstacle to visualising oviparous embryos has been the presence of opaque eggshells, which are mainly composed of calcium carbonate.

The innovation of a transparent eggshell would enable real-time visualisation of embryo development in ovo. However, the preparation of a transparent eggshell with physiochemical properties identical to those of natural eggs is a long-term goal. Accordingly, considerable efforts have been made to develop shell-less culture systems (SLCSs) that use artificial transparent culture vessels.

In quail, an SLCS using an artificial culture vessel made of polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) film has been described2, which enables the hatching of embryos isolated from shelled eggs preincubated in a conventional incubator for 2 days. However, observations of embryo development in this system are limited to an angle directly above it, because the PTFE film surrounding the egg content is opaque.

In chickens, a method enabling the hatching of chicks in a transparent polymethylpentene (PMP) film has been developed by culturing chick embryos isolated from shelled eggs and preincubating them in a conventional incubator for 3 days3,4.

In the SLCSs for quail2 and chickens3,4, observations of embryo development are not possible during the preincubation period in a conventional incubator, when the dynamic morphological changes associated with the development of major organ systems occur inside an opaque eggshell.

Alternative visible culture methods have been devised using round-shaped solid plastic vessels5 or cube-shaped artificial eggshells6,7. These methods enable observations of the developing embryo from the blastoderm stage to the middle term of development, but hatching has not been confirmed.

A modified chick SLCS (cSLCS) was recently established using chick embryos beginning from the blastoderm stage to hatching8. Unfortunately, embryo development cannot be observed directly in this system because the blastoderm area needs to be covered with an opaque membrane composed of PTFE during the first 3 days of culture8.

Here, we present a visible cSLCS that enables real-time omnidirectional visualisation of developing chicken embryos starting from the newly laid blastoderm stage at oviposition until hatching (visible-cSLCS).

Discussion

We developed a chick embryo culture system using a transparent plastic film, which enabled real-time visualisation of macroscopic embryo developmental processes starting from unincubated blastoderm stage embryos at oviposition until hatching.

Experiment 1 demonstrated significantly impaired embryo development when diverting from Step 2–1 of the TOK system4 (see Methods section for details) to blastoderm stage embryos. The chorion membrane overlying the blastoderm area actively transports thin egg albumen to the sub-embryonic fluid of the embryonic body9–11. Loss of the TAL covering the vitelline membrane overlying the blastoderm area of the embryo is harmful to normal embryo development10, as demonstrated by surface wrinkles and reduced light reflections from the vitelline membranes overlying the blastoderm areas in Experiment 1. Therefore, the reduced embryo viability observed in Experiment 1 could have been attributable to the altered physical properties of the vitelline membrane overlying the blastoderm area due to the loss of the TAL.

To maintain the TAL covering the blastoderm area, culture vessels used for visible-cSLCSs were placed on the rotating shaker, and the effects of the rotation frequency on the TALs covering the vitelline membranes overlying the blastoderm areas were investigated in Experiment 2. At RF-6, insufficient TAL covering of the blastoderm area was observed due to reduced movement of the thin albumen during rotation. Conversely, at RF-28, the blastoderm area was not covered with TAL due to strong eccentric forces, leading to thin albumen accumulation at the culture vessel rim. Among the three rotation frequencies, RF-10 was considered superior, ensuring sufficient TAL coverage of the blastoderm area and supporting normal chick embryo development.

At RF-10, the potential reasons underlying the loss of the TALs in some visible-cSLCS vessels included slight variations in the configuration of hand-made culture vessels, egg yolk sizes, and blastoderm positions over the egg yolks when transferring embryos in visible-cSLCS vessels. Nevertheless, the viability of blastoderm stage embryos in visible-cSLCS vessels was maintained on a rotating shaker at 10 rpm.

Based on the results of Experiment 2, in Experiment 3 we examined the hatching rates of embryos under visible-cSLCS vessels at RF-10, with oxygen supplementation started on days 14, 16, and 17 (control). The effect of the number of days to initiate the oxygen supply was examined, considering previous studies that have demonstrated that symptoms of hypoxia develop when the oxygen supply is started on day 1712. However, no significant differences in hatching rates were observed among the treatments, suggesting that factors other than the timing of initiating the oxygen supply could influence the viability and hatching rate of embryos cultured in visible-cSLCS vessels.

Previous studies have suggested that the concentrations of oxygen and carbon dioxide gas influence the hatching rates of shelled eggs13–16. Notably, the hatching rate is approximately 50% under modified cSLCS conditions when carbon dioxide is supplied alongside oxygen17. Future studies should evaluate the optimal gas conditions for enhancing the hatching rates in visible-cSLCS vessels.

Based on the observation of TAL loss in some embryos on a rotating shaker at 10 rpm in Experiments 2 and 3, we explored methods to improve embryo viability during the initial 3 days of development. In Experiment 4, the effects of additional manual agitation were evaluated by placing the visible-cSLCS vessels on a rotating shaker set to 10 rpm until day 3. Manual agitation was performed 3–5 times per day, depending on the presence of TALs on the vitelline membrane surfaces, to ensure adequate coverage of the blastoderm areas with TALs. The rotation of visible-cSLCS vessels on the rotating shaker continued until day 4, albeit at a reduced rate of 5 rpm, to support embryo development while minimising damage to the vitelline membranes, which are crucial for the digestion of those membranes. The overall embryo viability and hatching rate with additional manual agitation were greater than those subjected to RF-10 in Experiment 3, suggesting the importance of maintaining the TAL covering the vitelline membrane overlying the blastoderm area.

The specific gravity of egg yolk is lower than that of egg albumen18. As a result, the blastoderm is located at the upper surface of the egg yolk18. Therefore, in an intact shelled egg, on earth, the egg yolk floats in the surrounding albumen, and the vitelline membrane overlying the blastoderm area on the egg yolk is in contact with the inner shell membranes that underlie the eggshell. These findings suggest that gravity plays a pivotal role in the normal development of avian embryos19.

The well-designed three-dimensional egg structure enables the exchange of respiratory gases through the eggshell‒shell membrane complex while providing a constant supply of egg albumen to the rapidly growing blastoderm area through surface tension20. The importance of the contact of the vitelline membranes overlying the blastoderm areas with the inner shell membranes in intact shelled eggs is supported by the finding of arrested embryo development in the microgravity environment in space, in which the egg yolk is located at the centre of the egg19.

In contrast, the TAL covering the blastoderm surface in the TOK system is easily depleted because of active absorption by the chorion membrane and evaporation. In this context, placing visible-cSLCS vessels on a rotating shaker and additional manual shaking are both effective in maintaining the TALs covering the vitelline membranes overlying the blastoderm areas.

In Experiments 3 and 4, the hatching rate was lower than that of conventionally shelled White Leghorn eggs21, indicating the need to optimise visible-cSLCS vessels, including the shape and embryo position within the vessel.

In summary, the development of visible-cSLCSs has opened new possibilities by facilitating omnidirectional real-time monitoring of the phenotypic outcomes of experimental treatments through a transparent plastic film from blastoderm stage embryos until hatching. This system will serve as a powerful tool in various disciplines, such as toxicology, stem cell research, bioimaging, and regenerative medicine.

Methods

Chicken eggs

Fertilised eggs from 40 White Leghorns (MB line: National Livestock Breeding Center, Okazaki, Japan) maintained at the Tsukuba Plant Innovation Research Center, University of Tsukuba, Japan, were used for the experiments. The fertilised eggs were collected daily, stored at 15 °C, and used within 9 days after oviposition.

Culture vessel

The culture vessel used in this study was previously described4. Briefly, a polystyrene plastic transparent cup (430 mL, 86 mm diameter at the top, 118 mm height) (Dixie Japan, Tokyo, Japan) was used as the frame for the cSLCS vessel. An approximately 1 cm diameter hole was made on the side, approximately 3 cm from the bottom of the cup, and a cotton ball was attached to the hole. Then, approximately 40 mL of 0.01% benzalkonium chloride solution (Osvan S, Nihon Pharmaceutical, Tokyo, Japan) diluted with distilled water was added to the cup. An approximately 10-µm-thick PMP film (F.O.R. WRAP, Riken Technos, Tokyo, Japan) was moulded into a “hammock shape” via a mould and placed on a polystyrene plastic cup to prepare the cSLCS vessel4. cSLCS4 was used as the reference method and is hereinafter referred to as the TOK system, which stands for the initials of three authors of the manuscript: Tahara, Obara, and Kamihira.

Embryo transfer into the cSLCS vessel

The embryo was transferred into the TOK system for all experiments. Briefly, the eggshell of an unincubated fertilised chicken egg was wiped with 70% ethanol and tapped gently against the side of a 4 cm diameter glass bottle to create a 2 cm linear crack at the middle transverse surface of the eggshell. Then, the eggshell was opened by applying pressure to the crack using the thumbs, and the egg contents were transferred to a cSLCS vessel. The vessel was covered with a transparent lid made of amorphous polyethylene terephthalate (86 mm diameter, 7 mm height) (Risupack, Gifu, Japan)4.

Embryo culture

Experiment 1. Application of the TOK system to unincubated blastoderm stage chick embryos

The contents of unincubated blastoderm stage chick embryos were directly translocated into the TOK system and placed on a shelf installed inside a forced-air incubator (P-008, Showa Furanki, Saitama, Japan), which was maintained at 37.8 °C and 70% relative humidity.

The surface plane of the inside shelf was inclined to 8° from the horizontal plane. The visible-cSLCS vessels in a forced-air incubator were manually turned clockwise by 120° once a day until day 10 of incubation, as described in Step 2–2 of the TOK system in Supplementary Table S1.

Blastoderm stage embryos were transferred to visible-cSLCS vessels via the methods described in Steps 2–1 and 2–2, except for the replacement of the term “embryo proper” with “blastoderm.” Fifteen visible-cSLCS vessels were prepared, and embryo viability was recorded daily until day 10.

Experiment 2. Effects of rotating visible-cSLCS vessels placed on a rotating shaker on the viability of unincubated blastoderm stage chick embryos

The contents of unincubated blastoderm stage chick embryos were directly translocated into the TOK system, as described in Experiment 1. A plastic container (length, 22.8 cm; width, 30.8 cm; height, 15.2 cm) containing six visible-cSLCS vessels was placed on a rotating shaker (BBU115, Stovall Life Science, Greensboro, NC, USA), with the top surface of the rotating shaker tilted 7° from the horizontal plane.

The visible-cSLCS vessels in the plastic container were rotated at a frequency of 6 rpm (RF-6, n = 6), 10 rpm (RF-10, n = 9), or 28 rpm (RF-28, n = 4) for 3 days (Supplementary Fig. S4a, b; Supplementary Video 3). Four water cups (8.0 cm high, 7.7 cm in diameter) containing distilled water were placed at the corners of the incubator to provide humidity.

Three days later, the visible cSLCS vessels were removed from the plastic container and cultured under the conditions described in Step 2–2 (Supplementary Fig. S4c). Embryo viability was recorded daily until day 10.

Embryo viability in Experiments 1 and 2

Living or dead embryos were determined by confirming the presence/absence of a heartbeat. Embryo viability was calculated as the ratio of the number of live embryos (numerator) to the total number of embryos in the culture vessel (denominator).

Experiment 3. Effects of the timing of oxygen supplementation initiation on the hatching rate of blastoderm stage embryos in visible-cSLCS vessels rotated at 10 rpm on a rotating shaker

The unincubated blastoderm stage embryos in visible-cSLCS vessels were incubated on a rotating shaker for 3 days, as described in Experiment 2, except that the rotation rate of the shaker was fixed at 10 rpm. On day 3 of incubation, visible-cSLCS vessels were removed from the plastic container and cultured under previously described conditions (Steps 2–2, 3, and 4). Calcium carbonate powder (250 mg; Cat. #030-00385, Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical, Osaka, Japan) was applied on the chorioallantoic membrane on day 10 of incubation, as described in Step 3 of the TOK system. Embryo viability was recorded daily.

Oxygen supplementation to visible-cSLCS vessels was initiated on days 17 (Treatment 1; n = 36), 16 (Treatment 2; n = 43), or 14 (Treatment 3; n = 30) and continued until hatching.

Experiment 4. Effects of additional manual agitation on the hatching rate of unincubated blastoderm stage embryos in visible-cSLCS vessels

The unincubated blastoderm stage embryos in visible-cSLCS vessels were prepared and incubated on a rotating shaker for 3 days, as described in Experiment 3, except that additional manual agitation of the culture vessel was applied 3–5 times every 3–10 h during the first 24–48 h of shell-less culture. Additional manual agitation was performed by holding the culture vessels and sliding the bottom of the visible-cSLCS vessels linearly at approximately 3 cm 5 times/s for approximately 3 s (Supplementary Video 4). The frequency of manual agitation varied among the culture vessels, depending on the physical condition of the embryo surface. After manual agitation, visible-cSLCS vessels were returned to the plastic container on a rotating shaker at 10 rpm.

On days 3–4, the plastic container containing visible-cSLCS vessels on a rotating shaker was rotated at 5 rpm without additional manual agitation. On day 5, the vessels were removed from the plastic container and cultured as described in Steps 2–2, 3 and 4. Calcium carbonate was supplied as described in Step 3 of the TOK system. Oxygen supplementation to shell-less culture vessels was initiated on day 16, and the embryos were cultured until hatching. Nineteen visible-cSLCS vessels were prepared as replicates. The embryo viability was recorded daily (n = 19).

A schematic diagram of the culture conditions and schedules for Experiments 1, 3 and 4 is presented in Supplementary Fig. S4d.

Viability and hatching rate of embryos in Experiments 3 and 4

Living or dead embryos were determined by the beating of the hearts or the presence of blood flow in blood vessels distributed within the vitelline membranes. Embryos in visible-cSLCS vessels were considered “hatched” when periodic oral respiration was confirmed after removal of the chorioallantoic membrane from the surface of the embryo proper.

Embryo viability was calculated as the ratio of the number of live embryos (numerator) to the total number of embryos in the culture vessel (denominator). The hatching rate was calculated as the ratio of successful hatches (numerator) to the total number of embryos set in the culture vessel (denominator).

Handling of chicks after hatching

Chickens were raised in accordance with the Japan Feeding Standard for Poultry22. All chicks were maintained in cages under a 14 L/10D photoperiod with ad libitum access to a commercial diet (ZEN-NOH, Tokyo, Japan) and water.

Euthanasia

Chickens were euthanised via decapitation under isoflurane inhalation anaesthesia in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Experimental Animals of the University of Tsukuba.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed via Easy-R (EZR ver. 1.37, Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan)23, which is a graphical user interface for R (ver. 3.4.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Embryo viability data were analysed via two-tailed Fisher’s exact probability tests. P values were adjusted via the Bonferroni method. P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Institutional approval

All procedures involving animals were performed in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines24. All animals received humane care as outlined in the Guide for the Care and Use of Experimental Animals of the University of Tsukuba. The study protocol was approved by the Animal Experiment Committee of the University of Tsukuba (approval no. 21-435).
