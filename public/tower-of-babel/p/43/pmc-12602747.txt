Body dissatisfaction and anxiety predict binge eating and eating disorder psychopathology in Malaysian adolescent girls and young women
Authors: Tang Yi-Ting, Liew Jia Xian, Chooi Weng-Tink
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEating disorders are a growing concern in adolescent mental health globally, including in middle-income countries. Understanding risk factors across developmental stages is crucial for effective prevention and intervention. This study examined how body mass index (BMI), body dissatisfaction (BD), and anxiety contribute to binge eating (BE) and eating disorder (ED) psychopathology in Malaysian female adolescents and young adults, and to investigate BE as a potential mediator. Sample 1 of 398 16-year-old girls and Sample 2 of 169 female university students, aged 18–25 (Mage = 22.15, SD = 1.22), completed self-report measures assessing BD, state anxiety, trait anxiety, binge eating symptoms, and ED psychopathology, and reported their weights and heights. Relationships between variables and test mediation effects were analyzed using structural equation modeling. Both models revealed direct effects of BMI and BD on BE and ED psychopathology. BE mediated the paths from BMI, BD, and state anxiety to ED psychopathology. Trait anxiety was not supported as a shared risk factor. The models showed high similarity across both age groups. The findings suggest similar vulnerability to maladaptive eating behaviors among female youths in both developmental stages, highlighting the robustness of BD as a risk factor. The study provides insights into the development of eating disorders in a non-Western context, contributing to the global understanding of adolescent mental health. The study highlights the need for culturally sensitive, early interventions in middle-income countries to address the rising prevalence of eating disorders.Keywords: Body image, Disordered eating, State anxiety, Path analysis, Developmental psychopathology

Introduction

Adolescence is a critical period for mental health development, characterized by significant physical, cognitive, and social changes. During this time, individuals are particularly vulnerable to the onset of various mental health issues, including anxiety disorders, depression, and eating disorders [1]. The complex interplay of biological, psychological, and social factors during adolescence can create a storm for the emergence of mental health challenges that may persist into adulthood if left unaddressed [2].

An emerging and significant concern in adolescent mental health is the development of disordered eating behaviors and associated psychopathology. Eating disorders (EDs) and patterns of disordered eating often emerge during adolescence, and these could have severe and long-lasting impacts on physical and mental health [3]. These issues are not isolated to high-income countries but are increasingly recognized as global concerns, affecting adolescents across diverse cultural and socioeconomic contexts [4].

Among the various disordered eating behaviors, binge eating (BE) behavior has gained significant attention due to its prevalence and association with other mental health issues. BE behavior, characterized by consuming unusually large amounts of food with a subjective sense of loss of control, often emerges during late childhood or early adolescence [5]. BE as a form of maladaptive eating behavior may occur occasionally (i.e., isolated episodes of BE) without meeting the full diagnostic criteria for binge eating disorder (BED), which is a clinical diagnosis characterized by recurrent onsets of BE behaviors accompanied by marked distress [6]. BED is a severe psychiatric syndrome characterized by persistent disturbances in eating or eating-related behaviors, leading to altered consumption or absorption of food, which significantly impairs physical health or psychosocial functioning [7]. It is crucial to note that although isolated episodes of BE do not necessarily lead to BED, such subclinical but maladaptive BE behaviors are prevalent in community samples, particularly among adolescents and young adults. Two peaks of BE onset are suggested: immediately after puberty [8] and in late adolescence/early adulthood [9]. The study of subclinical BE behaviors remains crucial for early identification and prevention in community samples, as such maladaptive eating behavior is independently associated with psychological distress, weight-related complications, and increased risk for progression to BED [9–12]. This pattern underscores the importance of studying BE behaviors across the developmental spectrum from adolescence to young adulthood, as they may represent early warning signs or modifiable risk factors for more severe eating pathology. This study focused on such subclinical BE behavior (or isolated episodes of BE).

Research has identified several risk factors for subclinical BE behavior and associated ED psychopathology in adolescents. These include elevated body mass index (BMI), body dissatisfaction (BD), and anxiety symptoms [13, 14]. However, much of this research has been conducted in Western contexts, leaving gaps in our understanding of how these risk factors operate in diverse cultural settings, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. Moreover, while these risk factors have been studied independently, there is a need for integrated models that examine how they interact to contribute to the occurrence of subclinical BE behavior (or isolated episodes of BE) and ED psychopathology. Such models are crucial for developing comprehensive prevention and intervention strategies that address multiple risk factors simultaneously.

The literature emphasizes that girls are more likely to be at a higher risk of subclinical BE behavior compared to their male counterparts [15]. Multiple studies have observed higher levels of BD and anxiety, which are two well-documented risk factors for subclinical BE behavior, among young females compared to males [16–18]. BD is considered a significant cognitive component of EDs, characterized by negative evaluations and attitudes toward one’s body [19], and positively associated with elevated BMI [20, 21]. Adolescent girls were more likely to report higher levels of BD [14, 22] and anxiety [17] when compared to boys. Similarly, amongst older college students, BD, anxiety, and subclinical BE behaviors were also observed to have a higher prevalence among female students [23–25]. The development and persistence of subclinical BE behaviors during adolescence, especially in girls, raise significant concerns due to their link to the development of full syndromic EDs [26, 27] and persistent ED psychopathology in early adulthood [28].

The present study

The present study aims to address these gaps by examining the relationships between BMI, BD, anxiety (both state and trait), subclinical BE behavior (isolated episodes of BE), and ED psychopathology in two samples of female youths from Malaysia: adolescent girls and young adult university students. By including both age groups, we could gain insights into how these relationships may evolve from adolescence into early adulthood. This approach aligns with developmental perspectives that emphasize the continuity of mental health issues across life stages [29].

Firstly, the study aims to verify the direct effects of these risk factors, including BMI, BD, and two different forms of anxiety (i.e., state and trait anxiety), on subclinical BE behavior and ED psychopathology. One common view on the development mechanism of subclinical BE behavior is that it serves as a maladaptive strategy to escape from negative emotions [30], particularly anxious experiences and stress [31, 32]. Nevertheless, not all individuals with high anxiety levels engage in such maladaptive behavior [33], indicating individual differences influenced by personality traits. It is helpful to incorporate different forms of anxiety in BE models to better understand the relationships between these variables. Secondly, the study seeks to investigate whether subclinical BE behavior mediates the relationship between these risk factors and ED psychopathology.

To address these aims, we employ structural equation modeling (SEM), which enables the simultaneous examination of complex pathways and mediation effects across two independent cross-sectional samples: adolescent girls (Sample 1) and young adult women (Sample 2). Methodologically, the SEM framework accommodates measurement error in self-reported constructs while testing theoretically-derived pathways. This dual-sample approach serves the additional purpose of testing the replicability of findings across developmental stages. Consistent results would suggest temporal stability in risk factor-outcome relationships, with important implications for early identification and intervention during critical developmental windows. The replication design across developmental periods may strengthen confidence in the robustness of observed relationships. Moreover, the Malaysian context provides a crucial examination of whether Western-derived risk models generalize to non-Western, rapidly developing societies. As middle-income countries undergo nutrition transitions and urbanization [34], understanding how traditional risk factors manifest in these settings becomes increasingly urgent for global mental health.

Participants and methods

Participants

Our analytical samples contained two distinct samples from our two previous cross-sectional studies [35, 36]. Sample 1 consisted of 398 16-year-old girls of Malaysian nationality from secondary schools located in Penang, Malaysia. Using a stratified cluster sampling approach, these adolescent girls were recruited from five national secondary schools in October 2020. This specific age group was selected based on evidence indicating that the first developmental peak in BE behaviors typically occurs during mid-adolescence [8]. However, the generalizability of Sample 1 may be constrained by the exclusion of private and/or international schools.

Sample 2 consisted of 169 female university students of Malaysian nationality (Mage = 22.15, SD = 1.22, range 18–25) recruited through convenience sampling from a public university in the same region in December 2021. This older cohort was selected to capture the second established risk period for BE onset during the transition to adulthood [9]. The university sample, while providing access to the target age group, inherently reflects the characteristics of tertiary-educated young adults and thus may not fully represent the broader population in terms of socioeconomic diversity and educational attainment.

Procedures

The research was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee, Universiti Sains Malaysia (USM/JEPeM/20010065). For the adolescent sample (Sample 1), recruitment was conducted through school administrators following a standardized protocol. After obtaining institutional approval, information packets containing detailed study information sheets were distributed to potential participants and their parents (or guardians). Data collection was conducted online via secured Google Forms platform, after the students’ and their parents’ (or guardians’) signed consent forms were returned. The university sample (Sample 2) was recruited through electronic announcements posted on approved institutional platforms, employing identical standardized recruitment materials across all distribution channels. Potential participants were required to complete an electronic consent procedure that included verification of comprehension prior to enrollment. After providing their e-consent forms, they completed the questionnaires via the Google Forms platform. The complete procedural details have been previously reported in the original study publications [35, 36].

Measures

Existing research [37] has empirically demonstrated that Malaysian upper primary school students (aged ≥ 11 years) possess sufficient psychosocial competence to independently complete guided self-administered questionnaires, such as self-reported BMI measurements and body image perceptions. Building upon this validated methodology, all participants in the present study autonomously completed the survey instruments under standardized guided self-administration protocols.

The following self-reported measurement tools were used: (a) the Binge Eating Scale (BES; [38]) assessing BE symptoms, including behavioral manifestations of BE and cognition following episodes of BE behaviors; (b) the Disordered Eating Examination-Questionnaire 6.0 (EDE-Q 6.0; [39]) assessing the severity of four domains (aspects) of ED psychopathology in the past 28 days, including Restraint (R), Eating Concern (EC), Shape Concern (SC), and Weight Concern (WC); (c) the Contour Drawing Rating Scale (CDRS; [40]) assessing BD; (d) the STAI-Trait subscale (STAI-T) from the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI; [41]) assessing individual anxiety tendency or proneness (i.e., trait anxiety); (e) the Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI; [42]) assessing the general symptoms of anxiety experienced (i.e., state anxiety) over the past month prior to study participation, which was the same time period eating disorders symptoms were assessed using EDE-Q 6.0. This study calculated BMI using self-reported weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared [43].

In this study, we posited conceptual and operational equivalence of the constructs of the key study variables, such as BD, BE behavior, and ED psychopathology, across our adolescent and young adult samples, supported by the consistent conceptual definitions of BD and eating pathology across developmental stages [44, 45]. Nevertheless, we acknowledge potential developmental differences in construct manifestation and address these nuances in our discussion. For the implementation of measurement, all items and instructions were presented in both the original English and professionally translated Malay versions to ensure measurement validity. The selected instruments have been previously validated for use with both adolescent and young adult populations in the Malaysian context. Specifically, in terms of disordered eating behavior, BES has shown good validity and reliability in both Malaysian adolescents [14] and university students [46]. Similarly, the EDE-Q 6.0 exhibits good psychometric properties in Malaysian samples spanning both age groups [47, 48]. Additionally, existing research supports the cross-developmental validity of the CDRS for assessing BD in Malaysian young groups, despite its Western-originated silhouettes. Prior studies have demonstrated the CDRS’s capacity to capture body image perceptions and (dissatisfaction/satisfaction) attitudinal valence among adolescents [49–51] and young adults [52, 53]. All measures in our study maintained good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α ranged 0.71–0.94; Tables 1 and 3), further supporting their reliability for cross-age comparisons.

Data analysis

Path analyses were conducted using Mplus (version 8.3; [54]), utilizing the Maximum Likelihood (ML) estimation to report standardized path estimates. The 95% bias-corrected confidence intervals from 5000 Bootstrap samples were calculated to estimate the size of total, direct, and indirect effects. The analysis specified a fully recursive SEM with unidirectional paths to maintain theoretical consistency with our hypothesized developmental sequence, where risk factors influence BE behavioral symptoms, which in turn affect cognitive-affective outcomes (DE psychopathology). This recursive parameterization intentionally prohibited feedback loops between BE behaviors and ED psychopathology as recommended by Kline [55] to avoid circular reasoning in our mediation analysis, while still allowing appropriate correlations among exogenous variables, including BMI, BD, and anxiety measures. In both models, residual covariances among the endogenous ED psychopathology subscales were freely estimated to capture their natural covariation, while the residuals of the BE mediator were constrained to be independent of outcome residuals by fixing their covariances to zero. For single-indicator constructs, error variances were calculated using the conventional formula (1—α)*σ2 based on Bollen [56]. Sensitivity analyses were conducted for both models to verify key modeling assumptions. Specifically, modification indices (MI) were examined using a conservative threshold of 3.84 (χ2(1), p < 0.05), as recommended for evaluating potential model misspecification [55]. In both models, the modification indices analysis indicated no statistically significant model misspecification (all MI values below the cutoff of 3.84), supporting the hypothesized recursive paths without the need for additional parameters [55].

The following goodness-of-fit indices were used to examine the adequacy of the model to the empirical data: (a) non-significant Chi-square (χ2) statistics (p > 0.05; [57]); (b) Comparative Fit Index (CFI); (c) Tucker-Lewis Incremental Fit Index (TLI); (d) Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA); (e) Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR). For CFI and TLI, values ≥ 0.95 were considered good (adequate if ≥ 0.90; [58]). For RMSEA and SRMR, values of 0.05 ≤ were considered good (adequate if ranged between 0.05 and 0.08; [58]). Standardized path coefficients (β) are reported to facilitate effect size interpretation, with β ≥ 0.10, β ≥ 0.30, and β ≥ 0.50 representing small, medium, and large effect, respectively, following Cohen’s [59] conventions. Descriptive statistics and correlation analysis were conducted in SPSS (version 27).

Discussion

This study examined the relationships between BMI, BD, anxiety (both state and trait anxiety), subclinical BE behavior, and ED psychopathology among young females in Malaysia, focusing on both adolescents and young adults. Overall, there is a high degree of similarity between the findings across the two samples. This high similarity not only implied that the two age groups may be similarly vulnerable to BE behaviors, but also suggested that these risk factors, including elevated BMI, BD, and recent experiences of general anxious emotions, had a similar influence on BE symptoms and potential development of ED psychopathology for young females in Malaysia, highlighting the importance of early intervention and prevention efforts targeting body image, emotion coping strategies and eating behaviors. Our findings contribute to the understanding of these issues in a non-Western context and provide some valuable insights for the promotion of mental health among Malaysian young females.

The impact of BMI, BD, and anxiety

Consistent direct effects of BMI and BD on subclinical BE behavior and ED psychopathology were observed across both age cohorts. These parallel findings suggest that elevated BMI and negative body image represent persistent and salient risk factors for dysregulated eating patterns at these developmental stages. This aligns with extensive cross-cultural evidence identifying these variables as well-documented risk factors for BE behaviors among youth populations (e.g., [14, 61, 62]). Furthermore, few studies have examined the differential effects of the two risk factors across specific ED manifestations. Our findings add understanding to the complex maladaptive eating behaviors, and demonstrate that BMI and BD may exert influence on weight-related worries (WC), shape preoccupation (SC), and eating-related anxiety (EC) among both adolescent and young adult females.

Notably, based on the BMI and reference index (i.e., for adults, healthy weight if 18.5 ≤ BMI < 25, [63]; for adolescents, healthy weight if 5th BMI percentile1 < BMI < 85th BMI percentile, [64]), many our participants fall within the healthy weight range (refer to [35, 36]). Therefore, our findings are more likely to represent characteristics within a non-clinical population of young women with average BMI levels and healthy body weight. These findings raise several concerns. First, supported by our findings, the impact of BD and BMI on BE and ED psychopathology is evident as early as adolescence, probably even earlier. Past research suggests that by age 13, girls already exhibit persistent high levels of body image concerns and dietary worries [13]. Second, similarities observed across samples may imply the robustness of the influence of BD and BMI on young female populations. Indeed, concerns about weight and shape are more pronounced in older and heavier adolescent girls, and these concerns increase over time [65]. Consequently, BD among girls intensifies with age [65]. Third, having a healthy body weight in young girls does not necessarily equate to lower risk because as age increases, healthy body weight is increasingly associated with greater weight and shape concerns among adolescent girls [66]. Furthermore, even at average BMI levels, female college students who overestimate their weight exhibit more severe ED symptoms, such as high dietary restraint [67].

Moreover, all BMI values were self-reported in this study, which may introduce systematic bias. Research consistently demonstrates that adolescents and young adults tend to underreport weight (especially in higher BMI categories) while overestimating height, potentially leading to artificially lowered BMI calculations [68]. On the contrary, some research suggests that even young adults (e.g., university students) seem likely to tend to overestimate their weight [69]. This measurement limitation suggests two further possible interpretations of our findings. Firstly, the actual proportion of participants in the overweight range may be higher than reported, potentially strengthening our observed associations given established links between higher BMI and eating pathology. Secondly and conversely, some participants with a healthy weight may have the possibility of overestimating their weight, which may indicate more severe body image disturbances than detected, as our observations might have been primarily based on two non-clinical samples of young women with healthy weights, thereby amplifying the aforementioned concerns.

As for the impact of anxiety, our study confirmed that general anxiety symptoms had a notable direct influence on restraint and shape concern in adolescent girls; these symptoms also manifested as direct effect on shape, weight, and eating concern in female university students. During adolescence, teenagers achieve physical development milestones [70], and they consequently often exhibit self-consciousness and heightened concern regarding their body shape [71], as manifested by frequent shape checking, discomfort with body, and practices such as dietary restraint [72]. In this context, anxious emotions could amplify these shape-related worries and raise the likelihood of eating behavior expression. On the other hand, social stigma/labeling, such as perceived weight/obesity stigma, may have an increasing impact on older youths in increased social interactions as they age [73]. It is plausible that general anxiety, combined with societal pressures linked to weight stigma experienced by female young adults, shapes their perceptions regarding body weight and shape significance and amplifies guilt about eating and/or eating in secret. Indeed, weight-related social stigma correlated with feelings of embarrassment concerning eating in public [74].

The mediating role of subclinical BE behavior

For both age groups, the mediating role of BE behavior in the relationship between risk factors (BMI, BD, and state anxiety) and ED psychopathology (eating, weight, and shape concern, specifically) emphasizes the potential for BE to be an early indicator of more severe eating problems. While being mindful of the modest effect sizes yielded, this finding is particularly relevant for youth’s mental health, as it suggests that the persistence of subclinical BE behavior may further lead to multiple aspects of ED psychopathology such as greater concerns about weight, shape, and eating among these young females, despite the fact that such occasional binge episodes do not necessarily progress to full-scale clinical eating disorders. Moreover, a more complex pathway is revealed: elevated BMI leads to BD, which leads to BE, eventually resulting in greater concerns about weight, shape, and eating. This indirect pathway involving multiple mediators exhibited only small effect sizes in both models (βs ranged 0.03–0.07). These findings supported the conclusion in previous studies that BE shared psychopathology with bulimia nervosa and anorexia nervosa in terms of eating, body shape, weight, and similar maladaptive eating behaviors [74, 75], although cognitive distortions related to weight and shape are not included in the diagnostic criteria for BED. Thus, this study supports previous recommendations [76–78] that research criteria for BE and BED should consider including over-importance and preoccupation with these aspects related to body image in self-evaluation. However, given the modest effect sizes, we cautiously propose this recommendation and suggest future research to further validate its clinical and diagnostic relevance.

Our findings also suggest that BE mediates the relationship between BD and restraint in adolescent girls, but not in female college students. Nuanced differential mediating paths likely reflect fundamental differences in how BD and BE constructs manifest during these developmental stages. Compared to adults, adolescents’ eating behaviors and their underlying mechanisms may be relatively more straightforward, where BD may primarily reflect peer-focused comparisons [79, 80] that may directly trigger cycles of restraint and subsequent BE. One perspective posits that BE is a compensatory behavior or a counter-regulatory effect following restraint [81, 82], where concrete body concerns resulting from peer-focused comparisons about specific features (e.g., waist size) may prompt immediate dietary restraint. We speculate that the practice of BE may, in turn, induce adolescents to engage in direct and opposite behaviors, such as restraint, in an attempt to accelerate the offset of the unpleasant consequences of BE, which includes weight gain, psychological burden, and associated guilt. In contrast, the relationship and psychological mechanisms between restraint and BE may be more complex in adults. Their BD encompasses broader sociocultural and romantic relationship ideals [83], while BE could act like generalized affect-regulation functions or coping mechanisms that may be unrelated to one’s body. Some adults engage in BE to escape from aversive self-perceptions [84] such as low self-esteem [85]. Body-related shame, envy, and embarrassment are demonstrated mechanisms underlying restraint in adults [86], suggesting more diverse psychological mechanisms underlying their eating behaviors.

Moreover, the modest variance explained in restraint (14.8% for adolescents; 21.1% for adults) further highlights the need to consider additional developmental factors. For adolescents, parental influences and school environment may play important roles, while for young adults, factors like self-concept clarity and emotion regulation capacities may be more relevant. Integrating cognitive and emotional constructs is an important step to fully comprehend BE and eating pathology across development [87]. Future research should employ developmentally-sensitive measures to better capture these age-specific manifestations of BD and BE constructs. Methodologically speaking, while our models demonstrated good fit and consistency across samples, the sample size of our study (particularly for Sample 2) warrants caution in interpreting parameter stability, especially given the number of estimated paths. SEM with limited samples is more susceptible to sampling variability, particularly in models with multiple indirect pathways (e.g., BMI → BD → BE → ED psychopathology). This could lead to overestimated or underestimated path coefficients, even if directional trends align with theory. For instance, the modest variance explained in restraint (14.8% for adolescents; 21.1% for adults) may reflect not only developmental differences but also reduced precision in parameter estimation. Future research with larger samples could employ advanced techniques, such as Bayesian SEM, to better account for uncertainty in parameter estimates.

While our mediation pathways are statistically significant, their clinical or practical significance requires careful interpretation and cautious recommendation. Modest effect sizes in indirect pathways in both models (e.g., βs ranged 0.10–0.12 for BD → BE → ED psychopathology across two cohorts; βs ranged 0.10–0.15 for state anxiety → BE → ED psychopathology across two cohorts) suggest that these relationships, though theoretically meaningful, may have limited standalone impact on eating pathology. These findings underscore the importance of contextualizing statistical results within a broader framework of risk factors and avoiding over interpretation of small effects. Clinically, interventions targeting these pathways should be integrated with other evidence-based strategies to achieve meaningful outcomes.

Implications for mental health services in middle-income countries

Our observations obtained from the Malaysian sample may reflect a broader public health challenge facing middle-income countries undergoing rapid socioeconomic transitions. With mental health disorders including eating pathology rising across these regions [88], our findings provide some practical implications for both public health initiatives and clinical practice in Malaysia and other middle-income countries.

The demonstrated associations between BD, anxiety, and BE behaviors underscore the urgency of implementing school-based universal prevention programs in Malaysia. Routine screening for body image concerns and disordered eating patterns should be integrated into standard mental health assessments within educational settings. Specifically, secondary schools and universities could administer brief self-report measures during key academic periods (e.g., semester transitions) to identify students exhibiting risk patterns such as frequent dietary restraint or negative body evaluations. Such screening tools should capture both behavioral indicators and cognitive-emotional dimensions. Early identification efforts should be coupled with classroom-based psycho-education modules for students that foster rational body image perceptions and cultivate emotional management skills and adaptive coping strategies. For instance, curriculum-integrated workshops could teach stress management through nature-based activities (e.g., mindful walking) or group sports as alternatives to maladaptive eating behaviors. Middle-income countries confront a stark mental health workforce shortage, as reported by the World Health Organization. These countries have only 2.7 mental health workers per 100,000 population, compared to 71.7 in high-income countries [89], thus necessitating innovative, low-cost prevention models. These economical measures and practices would be beneficial in resource-limited middle-income countries such as Malaysia and could play a crucial role in primary prevention, helping to prevent or reduce the occurrence of psychological disorders related to physical and eating issues. On the other hand, in many middle-income countries, less than 20% of people with mental health disorders receive any form of treatment [90]. Mental health issues, including EDs, often carry significant stigma in many middle-income countries, further limiting help-seeking behaviors [91]. Incorporating basic mental health awareness and literacy into secondary school curricula is a cost-effective way to address the lack of awareness and reduce stigma.

At the community level, public health campaigns should collaborate with media regulators to counter the proliferation of unrealistic beauty standards on social platforms, promoting culturally relevant body diversity narratives that reflect local ideals of health and well-being. This approach is particularly important in multicultural countries like Malaysia, especially considering the increasing influence of Westernized beauty ideals on contemporary Malaysian youth [92]. The Malaysian context presents unique sociocultural dynamics that may amplify vulnerability to BD and maladaptive eating behaviors. Historically, Malaysia has valorized a fuller body as a symbol of health, prosperity, and maternal capability [93], particularly among women from lower socioeconomic statuses in rural Malaysia, where a heavier weight and larger body size have historically been preferred [94]. However, rapid urbanization and exposure to globalized media have introduced competing Westernized beauty ideals centered on thinness, creating a cultural dissonance. For instance, Malay women now navigate conflicting norms due to Islamic traditions emphasizing bodily modesty and cultural pride [95], while rapid socioeconomic development, industrialization, and urbanization have eroded national identity, making Western cultural values more easily assimilated [96]. As Malaysians are increasingly more exposed to modern Western media narratives that promote the association of thinness with success and attractiveness, a qualitative thematic analysis of advertisements featuring prominent women in modern Malay magazines supports the existence of a “homogenized” aesthetic standard in contemporary Malaysian society that prioritizes absolute thinness across conditions [97]. In Malaysia, a society renowned for its rich culinary heritage where communal feasting and traditional dishes are central to cultural identity, the rise of maladaptive eating behaviors represents a paradoxical challenge. This tension is exacerbated by the proliferation of social media platforms promoting “fitspiration” and restrictive diets. These influences have not only amplified reliance on social media to shape body image ideals and fashion trends but have also magnified the impact of unrealistic beauty standards on young people’s maladaptive eating behaviors. A recent study highlights the alarmingly high rates of disordered weight control behaviors influenced by social media in Southeast Asian countries, reporting a high three-month prevalence rates of 42.3% (N = 1141) in Malaysia, 38.3% (N = 2047) in Thailand, and 25.2% (N = 1092) in Singapore [98]. Clinicians and policymakers must therefore address these dual pressures by promoting body diversity narratives that reconcile traditional values with contemporary health discourses.

While our study focused on non-clinical populations and subclinical BE behaviors, we also propose some suggestions for clinical practice. Clinicians serving adolescents and young adults should remain vigilant to the developmental progression of these risk factors. Resources for and access to treatment are often limited in low- to middle-income countries such as Malaysia; therefore, early identification of at-risk individuals is crucial. School counselors and primary care providers encountering youths with persistent BD or recurrent BE episodes, even below diagnostic thresholds, may consider brief cognitive-behavioral interventions targeting maladaptive thought patterns. Targeted interventions for high-risk subgroups, such as girls experiencing weight-related teasing or academic stress, could combine peer support groups with skills training in emotional granularity (e.g., distinguishing hunger from anxiety). In middle-income countries like Malaysia, where the national healthcare system may be underdeveloped and resources are limited, teachers and school counselors could potentially serve as the primary providers of crucial first-line interventions and preventive care. Teachers and school counselors should be educated and trained in basic mental health knowledge and first aid to start non-confrontational and non-judgmental conversations when risks or symptoms of disordered eating are detected. This approach of task-sharing where non-specialist health workers are trained to deliver basic mental health interventions could help bridge the treatment gap [99]. Moreover, given the limited mental health infrastructure in many middle-income settings, clinical interventions could consider prioritizing triage systems that refer complex cases to specialists while equipping frontline workers with basic counseling competencies through task-sharing models.

Limitations and future directions

Several key study limitations are identified. First, the present study was cross-sectional; thus, our results were only able to illustrate the relationship between these variables in a single direction and cannot provide their dynamic causal relationships along the time axis. While our models posit directional pathways from BMI through BD and anxiety to subclinical BE behaviors and eating pathology, and observed highly similar pathways across two age cohorts, longitudinal studies are needed to test whether these relationships reflect developmental cascades or bidirectional interactions over time. Second, the exclusive reliance on self-reported measures introduces potential bias. Adolescents and young adults may misreport weight and/or overestimate height, which may attenuate observed associations between BMI and eating pathology. Future investigations should combine self-reports with objective anthropometric measurements to assess the difference between self-weight/body shape perception and actual weight, thereby capturing a richer relationship between BMI, BD, and body perception. Additionally, incorporating self-reports of disordered eating symptoms with surveys for parents and teachers may be beneficial to capture more “real” eating behaviors among these young people. Third, sampling limitations constrain generalizability. Our adolescent sample from national secondary schools in Penang is not well representative of students from other regions and private schools in Malaysia. In addition, the convenience sampling method used to recruit university students from a single institution overrepresents urban, tertiary-educated youth. Replication studies should adopt stratified random sampling across Malaysia and utilize national education registries to ensure socioeconomic diversity. Furthermore, while SEM is robust for testing complex relationships, the relatively small sample sizes in our study, particularly for the young adult cohort (N = 169), may reduce statistical power, inflate standard errors, and limit the detection of smaller effects, potentially restricting the generalizability of findings. Future studies should aim for larger, more diverse samples to enhance the robustness of these findings and validate the observed relationships. Lastly, the study focused on young women, including adolescent girls and female college students (18 to 25 years of age), so the results may not generalize to their male peers. Future work may replicate our model with young male samples to better characterize sex differences in the relationship and development of these variables. Clinical samples (treatment-seeking participants) with the same age group as our samples are needed to evaluate the validity and utility of our models for intervention. Replication in clinical populations could clarify whether these pathways observed in our models hold diagnostic relevance.

Conclusion

Utilizing two independent cross-sectional samples of adolescent girls and young adult women from Malaysia, this study demonstrated that the shared risk factors, including elevated BMI, BD, and recent experiences of general anxious emotions, had a similar influence on subclinical BE behaviors across the two age groups. Additionally, these risk factors may further trigger the potential development of ED psychopathology through the occurrence of subclinical BE behaviors among these young females. Furthermore, BD seems to be a robust risk factor that influences BE behaviors and eating pathology among young females in Malaysia. Consistent observations across both sample groups emphasize the substantial influence of these risk factors among teenage and young women, highlighting the importance of early intervention and prevention efforts targeting body image concerns and disturbances, maladaptive coping strategies, and maladaptive eating behaviors. This study emphasizes the significance of nurturing emotional competence, boosting stress and anxiety coping mechanisms, and promoting rational cognitive frameworks concerning body image, eating habits, and self-perceptions, particularly among minors, as a general prevention strategy to circumvent this alarming rate of ED psychopathology among the general population in Malaysia. These practical implications are also valuable and insightful for other middle-income countries. Longitudinal studies in the future are needed to test whether these pathways detected among Malaysian young females reflect developmental cascades or bidirectional interactions over time.
