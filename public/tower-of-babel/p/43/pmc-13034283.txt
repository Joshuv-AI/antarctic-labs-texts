Experiences of internet addiction among junior college students in Taiwan: a qualitative descriptive phenomenological study
Authors: I-Ling Huang, Jin-Biau Li, Min-huey Chung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesThis study explored the lived experiences of internet addiction among Taiwanese junior college students. Specifically, it examined their perceptions of addiction, experiences of excessive use and attempts to manage their online behaviours.DesignA descriptive phenomenological approach involving one-on-one, semi-structured, in-depth interviews was employed. Data analysis was conducted using Giorgi’s phenomenological method assisted by NVivo V.12.SettingThe interviewees were from a junior college with two campuses in northern Taiwan.ParticipantsPurposive and snowball sampling of 15 junior college students. All participants met the criteria for internet addiction using the Chen Internet Addiction Scale.ResultsThree main themes and eight subthemes were identified: continuous provocation (childhood exposure and unexpected pandemic), continuous promotion (irresistible immersion and compensatory self-isolation) and continuous resistance (varied self-awareness, dilemmas, perceived insignificance and futility). This study also explored how junior college students with internet addiction repeatedly struggle to resist a seamless virtual world.ConclusionsFrom the perspective of adolescents with internet addiction, early exposure to the internet, the boost of the pandemic, the growing demand, and negative beliefs and attitudes all contribute to the inability to overcome internet addiction. These findings offer valuable insights into the underlying mechanisms of internet addiction, providing mental health professionals with a deeper understanding and establishing a foundation for the development of future intervention strategies.Keywords: MENTAL HEALTH, PSYCHIATRY, Nursing Care

Introduction

The internet’s advent has allowed people to effortlessly navigate cyberspace to meet their personal needs.1 2 Adolescents enjoy the internet’s instant accessibility, using it to quickly and efficiently gather news, entertainment and social information.3 Since January 2020, Taiwan has been significantly impacted by the COVID-19 pandemic. With home isolation, people are increasingly relying on the convenient online world. However, over-reliance can lead to a double-sided experience and internet addiction.4

In 1996, Goldberg termed abnormal internet use ‘internet addiction disorder (IAD).’ Beyond substance dependence, IAD manifests as uncontrollable, repetitive online behaviours driven by psychological stress and cognitive factors. These behaviours trigger neurobiological reactions similar to addiction, including tolerance and withdrawal.5 Due to their digital adeptness, adolescents exhibit unique vulnerability to these mechanisms.6 Globally, adolescent prevalence is estimated at 6.2–15.5%,7 8 showing a rising trend over recent years, with rates in Taiwan reaching 21.2%.9 Associated with issues such as depression, anxiety and sleep problems, internet addiction has become a critical public health priority.10

Internet addiction research has primarily relied on quantitative methods to investigate prevalence and related factors among adolescents.11 However, questionnaire surveys often miss individual differences, especially on sensitive topics. While recent qualitative studies have explored internet addiction, noting relational compensation in Turkish adolescents3 or focusing on Indonesian males,12 phenomenological research specifically examining the lived experiences of Taiwanese junior college students remains limited, particularly regarding the COVID-19 pandemic. In Taiwan, many junior high school graduates choose to continue their education in 5-year vocational programmes in junior colleges to acquire technical skills. Despite the rising severity of internet addiction among adolescents, research on this topic is limited. Although the WHO included ‘gaming disorder’ in the 11th edition of the International Classification of Diseases in 2018,13 internet addiction has not yet been recognised in the diagnostic system for mental disorders. The lack of a clear definition and diagnostic criteria has hindered our understanding of its nature and the development of effective treatment methods.14 To address this, we used a phenomenological research methodology to explore Taiwanese junior college students’ internet use experiences and addiction.

As described by Staiti, Husserl posited that the key to phenomenology is to ‘return things to themselves to understand their essence.’15 Following this philosophical viewpoint helps researchers eliminate personal biases and focus on the essence of the research topic without any preset expectations. Accordingly, we sought to explore the nature of Taiwanese junior college students’ internet addiction experiences. The research questions include: (1) What are the internet use experiences of junior college students with internet addiction? (2) What are their experiences of excessive internet use? (3) How do they manage their excessive internet use behaviours? (4) What are their perceptions regarding internet addiction?

Methods

Study design

Phenomenology is both a philosophy and a research method focused on exploring the essence of human life experiences.16 Humans interpret experiences based on their backgrounds, but the significance and feelings each phenomenon evokes can vary. Researchers must ‘bracket’ their preconceptions to understand participants’ perspectives and express their experiences accurately. This approach is often called descriptive phenomenology.17 The core of phenomenology lies in the life experiences and intentions of humans, requiring adherence to Husserl’s three principles: intentionality, essence and phenomenological reduction, which align with phenomenology’s ontological, epistemological and methodological claims.18

In summary, phenomenology reveals that the internet has profoundly impacted humanity, with modern life often intertwined with constant online activities. The internet can have both positive and negative effects, making adolescents particularly vulnerable to addiction due to their developmental processes and life experiences. Therefore, we comprehensively investigate the nature of vocational school students’ internet addiction through qualitative research.19 To enhance the study’s quality, we strictly followed the 21 key elements of the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR)20 (online supplemental file 1).

Researcher characteristics

Data collection was conducted by Huang, a teacher at the study site with over 15 years of experience in psychiatric nursing, mental health and psychology. Huang also worked in a psychiatric ward for nearly 8 years and completed a three-credit qualitative research course during doctoral studies, gaining prior research experience on internet addiction with Chung.9 During interviews, Huang maintained an open attitude and avoided leading questions to ensure the authenticity of participants’ viewpoints.

Setting and participants

We explored the subjective experiences of internet addiction among students enrolled in a junior college in northern Taiwan. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) students enrolled in the participating junior college’s daytime classes (including campuses in Xindian and Yilan); (2) willingness to share personal internet use experiences; (3) scoring ≥64 (first 3 years of the junior college) or ≥68 (last 2 years of the junior college) on the Chen Internet Addiction Scale (CIAS). These cut-offs reflect Taiwanese diagnostic norms for adolescents (equivalent to years 1–3) and university students (years 4–5), respectively.21 22 To identify information-rich cases meeting these specific characteristics, participants were recruited using purposive and snowball sampling. The researcher personally visited classrooms to explain the study purpose. Referrals from homeroom teachers and students further facilitated recruitment, resulting in 15 interviewees. This sample size was based on previous literature3 and the study’s objectives. One-on-one interviews continued until data saturation was achieved, indicated by redundant themes and no new information.23

Ethical issues

Participants were informed about the study’s purpose, procedures, compensation, risks and benefits. Informed consent was obtained from participants and their guardians (if under 18). At the study’s end, participants received a NT$500 voucher as a thank-you. Transcriptions were made according to participants’ statements, with names, classes and IDs coded for anonymity. Paper documents were stored securely, and electronic files were password-protected, accessible only to the research team.

Data collection

Before formal data collection, a pilot study with preliminary interviews was conducted with two vocational students. This led to the formulation of the official research questions, as shown in box 1. In the first half of 2022, Taiwan was still experiencing the late peak of the COVID-19 pandemic, with students attending classes remotely. The initial plan was to conduct online interviews.24 However, after the first two interviews, it became clear that unstable internet connections and participants’ reluctance to stay on camera significantly compromised interview quality and the ability to observe them effectively. Consequently, in-person interviews were instead carried out, although they were postponed and completed between September and December 2022. Each interview lasted approximately 90–100 min and was recorded using voice recognition software with participant consent. The researcher employed a passive, simple observation approach while taking field notes to record the interaction with the participant promptly. Important verbal and non-verbal information was transcribed within 3 days. Data analysis started simultaneously with a thorough review and deep examination of these materials.25

Data analysis

The formal data analysis was conducted using NVivo V.R1 software for conceptual coding and categorisation.26 The analysis followed Giorgi’s27 method of empirical phenomenology as follows: (1) Verbal data collection: Data were gathered through simple descriptions, interviews or their combination. Given the broad and open nature of internet addiction, participants were given ample opportunity to extensively express their viewpoints. (2) Reading the data: Before the analysis, the researcher read through the 15 transcripts to gain a global sense of the data and highlight content relevant to the research objectives for further analysis. (3) Dividing data into parts: Using professional standards and an open attitude, meaning units were identified and presented in the participants’ everyday language, forming several primary meaning units. (4) Organising and expressing raw data into disciplinary language: Once all meaning units were summarised into a coherent narrative, they were translated into professional terms through reflection and imaginative variation, forming secondary meaning units. (5) Expression of the phenomenon’s structure: The researcher synthesised specific structural descriptions from the participants into the situated structure’s general description. Using this, essential concepts were extracted and used to present the results.

Rigour

To establish the trustworthiness of the study results, the researcher referenced Lincoln and Guba’s criteria for rigour,28 including the following: (1) Credibility: Peer debriefing involved a PhD classmate (Ms K, a hospital-based professional) to verify transcript accuracy. Additionally, analyst triangulation was performed by the second author (Dr J, assistant professor in psychiatric nursing), who independently reviewed the coding framework and themes to ensure comprehensive, unbiased interpretation. (2) Transferability: The data were collected from students at a junior college spanning two campuses, and the researcher meticulously transformed the context into texts. Additionally, each step was thoroughly documented and detailed to serve as a comprehensive reference for similar future research. (3) Dependability: The interviewer (Huang) had extensive practical experience in mental health nursing and was a certified mental health clinical nurse specialist. Moreover, the audit trail process, involving qualitative research experts, provided diverse perspectives on the research design, transcripts and analysis, further enhancing the study’s reliability. (4) Confirmability: The researcher minimised personal subjective perspectives through continuous reflexivity. Additionally, member checking was conducted by inviting participants to review preliminary themes, ensuring that the findings accurately reflected their intentions and experiences.

Results

Online supplemental file 2 presents the participants’ demographic characteristics. The average age was 16.7 years, ranging from 15 to 19 years. The majority were female (n=12, 80%). CIAS scores ranged from 69 to 94. Freshmen to junior students (first to third years) had an average score of 85.91, while seniors (fourth to fifth years) averaged 76.75. Internet usage patterns varied individually, and students exhibited different preferences on weekdays and holidays (including winter and summer breaks).

After conducting interviews and extracting meanings, three primary themes emerged: continuous provocation, continuous promotion and continuous resistance. These themes further expanded into two, two and four subthemes (figure 1 and online supplemental file 3), outlining the framework that connects the experiences of internet addiction formation among junior college students.

Experience of internet addiction among junior college students.

Theme 1: continuous provocation

In daily life, provocation functions as an enabler, much like early exposure to 3C (computer, communication and consumer electronics) products, which connects children to the online world. Frequent 3C use naturally encourages online self-exploration. Additionally, the COVID-19 pandemic forced adolescents into isolation, further intensifying their online interactions. This theme includes two subthemes: childhood exposure and unexpected pandemic.

Childhood exposure

Five respondents indicated having access to internet-enabled devices at a very young age. One had such access during kindergarten, while two had it during elementary school. Consequently, they became familiar with the online world and could easily engage in their preferred online activities.

My mom gave me a tablet when I was in kindergarten. She knew I used it mainly to play games, and I could play not just on weekends… (P8)

I started playing online games in the first grade, and by sixth grade, I was participating in team battles… (P7)

I had a smartphone by the third grade because my dad said I could use Line to make free calls. By fifth and sixth grade, I could talk to friends for 2–3 hours… (P2)

Unexpected pandemic

All respondents mentioned that the COVID-19 pandemic led schools to introduce remote learning and increased opportunities for online classes at home. The lack of temporal and spatial constraints or effective learning further encouraged adolescents to engage in online activities, resulting in frequent human-computer interaction.

Being confined at home for online classes every day felt like a holiday. It was hard to focus on lessons with so much content online grabbing your attention, like a notification popping up with a video suggestion… (P2)

During quarantine, I binge-watched Korean dramas. Sometimes, the connection during online classes was poor, which frustrated me and made me not want to listen. I could not understand the lectures anyway, so I just kept watching videos and playing games… (P10)

I would just leave the computer on while the teacher was lecturing, and I would be on my phone pretty much all day … almost always browsing… (P15)

Theme 2: continuous promotion

Constant online exposure, especially when it consistently yields positive results and meets needs that real life fails to, leads adolescents to indulge themselves, immersing in the virtual world’s myriad attractions. This theme includes two subthemes: irresistible immersion and compensatory self-isolation.

Irresistible immersion

Different online activities, as they offer an enchanting allure and provide adolescents with the feedback they crave, like positive emotions, a sense of glory, accomplishment or even monetary rewards, lead them to invest immense time and energy, creating an addictive cycle.

Watching K-pop boy band MVs online, especially with XX being so handsome and charismatic, got me hooked. Watching their performances online just makes me inexplicably happy. Following their journey and growth makes me feel so content and touched… (P5)

When playing ‘Valorant’ and getting a chance to shine, my heart races. If I help the team win, teammates applaud or cheer, making me feel excited and accomplished… (P8)

You may think I am exaggerating, but I am currently doing a side gig as an online companion for gaming and chatting. Besides meeting new people, I also earn money through this service… (P10)

Compensatory self-isolation

Four respondents mentioned that they would rather delay real-life responsibilities and use the internet to preserve positive emotions. One noted that going online to procrastinate offered temporary relaxation, although it often led to regret afterward. Unrestricted online activities can fill their time and adequately compensate for feelings of emptiness, boredom and loneliness.

I have had procrastination issues since I was a kid. To avoid doing homework, I would start browsing social media, which felt quite relaxing, but then I would realize why I wasted so much time on my phone… (P4)

During winter and summer breaks, life feels purposeless, so I just keep playing and playing… If I play less, my gaming skills decline. On weekends, I want to kill time with games to avoid boredom… (P11)

When my close friend took a break from school, I felt really low and lonely, so I focused on Weplay (a voice chat app). It has mini-games to play with others, and they keep me company… (P14)

Theme 3: continuous resistance

Discrepant self-views, conflicting thoughts, passivity and ineffective solutions hinder behavioural change. The last theme includes four subthemes: varied self-awareness, dilemmas, perceived insignificance and futility.

Varied self-awareness

Respondents perceived a discrepancy between their self-assessments and objective CIAS scores. Viewing ‘addiction’ as subjective dependency or excessive time use rather than clinical pathology, they expressed surprise that the indicated severity did not align with their personal experience.

I was shocked by the score. I knew I was addicted, but I did not realize it was that severe. I thought the score should be lower… (P1)

I do not feel concerned about the score. I think I have a little bit of an addiction problem. Does a little bit count?… (P6)

My score on the addiction scale is too high. I just spend a lot of time on my phone, but it is not like I cannot live without it… (P9)

Dilemmas

Respondents conflicted between rational awareness and emotional dependence. The smartphone acted as a ‘psychological anchor’ for safety; thus, the fear of emptiness paralysed their self-regulation, overriding their logical understanding of the negative effects.

I have thought about deleting Instagram, but I worry I will miss out on something, get anxious about falling behind, and not know what is happening in society, which is why I have not done it yet… (P4)

I know I use the Internet too much, but going offline makes me feel uncomfortable. I always want to go back to the Internet, even just to have a quick check for peace of mind… (P11)

I know I should not play with my phone, but without it, I feel like I have nothing. Access to the Internet gives me a lot of security… (P13)

Perceived insignificance

As conflicting perceptions persisted, adolescents began experiencing various negative impacts, like reversed sleep patterns, poor mental state, physical and health changes, emotional volatility, deteriorating social relationships, compromised personal roles and impaired academic performance. However, most acknowledged these consequences but regarded them as tolerable or insignificant.

I am tired in daytime classes, which inevitably affects my studies. In fact, even though I do not really study, and my grades are somewhat bad, I do not care much about it… (P8)

My eyesight has worsened by 25–50 degrees in three years. If a game does not go well, I get upset and blame teammates, but then I just let it pass and keep going back to the game… (P11)

When I stayed up late on my phone, I would drink sweet coffee to stay awake, my weight ended up going from 46 to 53 kg… I have departed with my best friend, but I feel it is not that important… (P12)

I often forget to eat or sleep, losing track of time and responsibilities. Nevertheless, I am not very self-disciplined anyway… (P13)

Futility

Four respondents, prompted by others and their own awareness of excessive internet use, attempted to reduce their usage through actions. Nevertheless, these efforts did not meet their expected goals. They attributed this to limited self-control or insufficient positive reinforcement, reverting to their previous habits and normal internet use.

Once, I tried to go 24 hours without my phone, but by the 20th hour, I failed because everyone else was using theirs, so I wanted to do it as well… (P1)

I used to lock my screen, which would not unlock until a certain time, so as to limit my time online. However, it did not work for me… (P4)

Classmates started playing again, and I was invited to reinstall the game and join them. It felt nostalgic… (P13)

I tried to reduce game time and instead spend it studying, but my grades did not end up improving. Online games provide me with more positive feedback, so now I only think about reducing my addiction 10–20% of the time… (P8)

Discussion

This study phenomenologically examined Taiwanese junior college students’ internet addiction experiences. While acknowledging that ‘internet addiction’ remains debated and lacks formal diagnostic status, we employ the term to align with the CIAS and participants’ lived experience of compulsive usage rather than as a strict pathology. Following Giorgi’s analysis,27 three core themes and eight subthemes emerged: continuous provocation, continuous promotion and continuous resistance, highlighting their synergistic effects.

Continuous provocation

As technological products become more widespread, O’Keeffe29 warned that digital activities in childhood, without proper adult guidance, can lead to issues like internet addiction. We found that most adolescents accessed digital products early through their parents, starting internet use at a young age—consistent with findings by Martins et al30 and Messena and Everri.31

COVID-19’s sudden outbreak further amplified digital product demand, forcing adolescents to change their learning methods. However, in the absence of a mature mind and relevant video equipment, although online learning maintained educational continuity, it also increased adolescents’ chances of using the internet for non-educational purposes, thereby increasing internet addiction risk. Several studies have reported similar findings.4 12

Continuous promotion

Maslow, a humanistic psychologist, argued that human behaviour is driven by different basic need levels, while technology use has become a channel to satisfy or compensate for these needs.1

Consistent with previous research, this study supported the conclusion that digital products, both in form and function, are the preferred mobile devices. However, it is essential to recognise the heterogeneity of internet activities, such as gaming, social media, video streaming and online business. This diversity highlights a conceptual challenge with ‘internet addiction,’ as different activities serve distinct psychological functions; for instance, gaming fulfils achievement needs, while social media addresses social connection. Collectively, this versatility allows the internet to easily meet adolescents’ growing needs, such as pleasure,2 ² social,32 and self-esteem and self-actualisation needs.33 Özparlak et al3 suggested that a reason why adolescents undergoing psychiatric treatment accessed the internet was because online friends were easier to get along with than real-life friends. This partially aligns with our findings, with variations likely due to different sample attributes. Respondents were junior college students using social apps to make friends, enjoy companionship and gain financial resources, which enhanced their satisfaction of physiological and safety needs, creating strong attraction.

Moreover, during their pursuit of self-worth, adolescents face various pressures in real life. Most studies, consistent with this study, report that when adolescents lack good stress-coping skills, they intentionally choose excessive internet use as a delay tactic to escape stress and regulate negative emotions.34 35 Additionally, to compensate for unmet internal deficiencies such as pervasive feelings of emptiness, boredom and loneliness,36 37 adolescents repeatedly immerse themselves in the enjoyable online world, free from restrictions, to fill their time.

Continuous resistance

Smart digital products have become ubiquitous in daily life, especially for adolescents with immature physiological and psychological functions. This demographic is particularly susceptible to digital technology’s influence. If individuals do not recognise internet addiction’s significance, they may inadvertently develop strong resistance to ‘digital detox’ behaviours. This resistance not only increases their reluctance to attempt detox but also the likelihood of failure even if they try voluntarily, as self-control is.38

The detox goal is not to completely disconnect from the internet but to achieve manageable usage. For many individuals experiencing internet addiction, the online and physical worlds can feel equally real, allowing adolescents to satisfy various customised needs.39 Cutting off this dependency can lead to issues such as fear of missing out, cravings and nomophobia, making it hard for adolescents to quit online.40,42 Although excessive internet use negatively impacts daily routines, these changes may not significantly affect academic performance, physical health, emotional management or relationships, leading to rationalisation in favour of the immediate gratification from online activities.5

Consistent with prior research,43 participants reported voluntary digital detox as futile, discouraging further help-seeking. Therefore, clinical interventions must shift from restriction to addressing underlying psychological needs and building self-efficacy through gradual, short-term goals.44 45 Future research should prioritise longitudinal tracking and comparative studies across specific online activities to identify distinct mechanisms.

Finally, we recognised that our backgrounds in psychiatric nursing and education could influence the research process. Specifically, our clinical training might predispose us to view internet addiction solely as a pathology requiring intervention. To mitigate this bias, we employed Husserl’s bracketing and reflexive journaling to suspend professional preconceptions, ensuring that the findings remained grounded in the participants’ subjective lived experiences rather than clinical frameworks.17

Strengths and limitations

This study was reported following the SRQR guidelines.20 Our findings reveal insights into Taiwanese junior college students’ internet use and addiction experiences. Initially, the pandemic forced researchers to conduct online interviews, but connectivity and video quality issues led to a temporary halt. When students returned to in-person classes, researchers resumed face-to-face interviews, allowing for comprehensive capture of participants’ verbal and non-verbal cues.

This study has notable limitations. First, the interviews were conducted by a researcher who had teaching duties at the same institution. Some participants may feel reserved or defensive due to the teacher-student relationship, limiting their responses to surface-level information. Second, we combined participants’ internet activity experiences into themes without focusing on specific services. Different types of internet services may carry distinct addiction risks, requiring further investigation. Third, all participants were from the same junior college, thereby limiting the findings’ applicability. Future research should consider different regions and educational systems, such as high school and vocational high school students.

Conclusions

Years of unrestrained online engagement from childhood, exacerbated by the COVID-19 pandemic, have gradually induced addiction. Despite experiencing the pros and cons, cognitive conflicts, post-detox contradictions, physical discomfort and social frustrations often lead adolescents to succumb to powerful psychological needs, perpetuating a cycle of addiction. Our findings can help mental health professionals design targeted interventions for internet addiction. Understanding the developmental context of addiction, including the provocations, promotions and resistances, is crucial. Additionally, providing a comprehensive knowledge of internet addiction to parents and adolescents may help teenagers pave the way for a balanced online and offline life.
