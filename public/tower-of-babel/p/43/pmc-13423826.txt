Effects of microplastics and nanoplastics on the kidneys
Authors: Lucie Augey, Jessica Berdougo-Tritz, Aicha Mbarek, Michel Jadoul, Ziad Massy, Gilbert Deray, Sophie Liabeuf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPlastics are hydrophobic carbon polymers with a half-life of ∼500 years. The widespread production and environmental accumulation of plastics pose significant toxicity concerns. Humans are routinely exposed to micro- and nanoplastics (MNPs), which can enter rate the bloodstream and reach various organs, including the kidneys.Here, we review research on nephrotoxic effects of plastics and the underlying mechanisms. The results of several studies of kidneys in mammals and kidney cells from humans suggest that MNPs induce renal toxicity. Although the underlying mechanisms remain to be characterized in detail, the current body of evidence suggests that MNPs promote the production of reactive oxygen species and thus trigger local (renal) and systemic inflammatory responses. These processes enhance cytotoxicity and may drive MNP-induced kidney damage.This toxicity results in histopathological changes in renal tissues (including glomerular and tubular damage and fibrosis) and modifications in key biomarkers of renal function (such as the glomerular filtration rate, albuminuria, and the blood urea nitrogen level). Moreover, MNPs have been shown to induce cardiovascular damage, which may contribute to the progression of chronic kidney disease (CKD)—potentially via the activation of aryl hydrocarbon receptors. Notably, the nephrotoxic effects of MNPs appear to be exacerbated by co-exposure to other environmental contaminants and uremic toxins.CKD can impair the kidneys’ ability to eliminate MNP. Furthermore, dialysed patients are substantially exposed to MNPs during dialysis sessions, which potentially compounds their vulnerability. With a view to better understanding the effects of MNPs on renal health and the impact of CKD and dialysis on levels of exposure to plastics, further studies are essential.

INTRODUCTION

The many valuable properties of plastics mean that these hydrophobic, carbon-based polymers have been used on a massive scale in recent decades. Humans are exposed to MNPs in the environment daily. The global annual production of plastics has grown spectacularly, from a few million tonnes in the 1950s to over 360 million tonnes today [1, 2].

Micro- and nanoplastics (MNPs) are increasingly recognized as emerging environmental pollutants with potential implications for human health. These particles originate from the degradation of larger plastic materials or are directly released into the environment through industrial processes and consumer products. Absorption then occurs by inhalation, ingestion, and dermal contact (Fig. 1). Owing to their small size and physicochemical properties, MNPs can penetrate biological barriers and accumulate in various organs, including the kidneys.

MNP accumulation in the environment and human contamination.

The renal system plays a critical role in the filtration and elimination of xenobiotics, making it particularly vulnerable to the accumulation and toxicity of MNPs. Recent experimental and observational studies have highlighted the potential of MNPs to induce renal dysfunction through mechanisms such as oxidative stress, inflammation, and cellular damage. The extent of these effects appears to be influenced by particle size, surface charge, chemical composition, and exposure duration.

Despite growing concern, the nephrotoxic potential of MNPs remains incompletely understood. A comprehensive understanding of their interactions with renal tissues is essential for assessing health risks and guiding regulatory policies.

The objective of the present study is to review the properties and the of MNPs, their effects on the kidneys, to examine the mechanisms underlying this nephrotoxicity, and to explore the relationship between MNPs and CKD.

CHARACTERISTICS AND PHYSICOCHEMICAL PROPERTIES OF MNPS

Plastics are carbon polymers derived primarily from fossil fuels. Their hydrophobic surface and long half-life (often >500 years) confer high environmental stability and persistence [3]. The size, shape (fragments, fibres, spheres, etc.), surface reactivity, and density of plastic particles influence their ability to cross biological barriers, interact with cellular membranes, and enter cells [4]. Chemically, the plastics’ polymeric composition, hydrophobicity, surface charge (Zeta potential), and incorporated additives determine their interactions with organic and inorganic contaminants and the release of plastic-associated chemicals. The most widely used plastics are polyethylene (PE), polypropylene (PP), polyvinylchloride (PVC), and polystyrene (PS) [3] (Table 1).

Plastics are often present as microplastics (MPs, with a particle diameter between 1 μm and 5 mm in size) or nanoplastics (NPs, with a particle diameter below 1 µm) [5] (Fig. 2). Next, there are two types of these micro- and nanoplastics (MNPs): primary MNPs are produced intentionally by humankind (e.g. for use in cosmetics, paints, and sun creams), whereas secondary MNPs are released into the environment following the slow photochemical, physical, or biological degradation of plastics. Most plastics can accumulate in the air (because of their lightness), in water (because of their buoyancy), and in soil [6] (Fig. 1).

Diameter scale for microplastics and nanoplastics.

These omnipresent MNPs can enter the body by ingestion [7–10], inhalation [11, 12], or skin contact [13]. It has been estimated that humans ingest between 0.1 and 5 g of MNPs per week [2]. When MNPs enter the bloodstream, they can reach the organs and disrupt their function.

The detection and quantification of MNPs in human biological matrices present significant analytical challenges, due to (i) the very low concentrations of MNPs in biological fluids, (ii) the MNPs’ microscopic size (necessitating highly sensitive detection methods), and (iii) the MNPs’ diverse nature (i.e. the various types and shapes).

Deng et al. used fluorescence spectroscopy and histological techniques to study the accumulation and distribution of 5 µm and 20 µm fluorescent PS MPs in the liver, kidney, and intestine of mice. The tissue distribution was determined partly by the particle size. Deng et al.’s results suggested that MPs not only accumulate in the digestive system but are also transported to other tissues via the circulatory system [14].

To date, investigations based on micro-Raman spectroscopy [15], gas chromatography-mass spectroscopy, and infrared laser spectroscopy [16] have revealed the presence of MNPs in human urine; these particles notably include PE, polyamide 66, polyethylene-vinyl acetate, PVC, and PP. Recently, Massardo et al. used micro-Raman spectroscopy to search for MPs in human kidney tissue. The study confirmed the presence of MNPs in human urine and demonstrated their accumulation in human kidneys for the first time. In total, 26 particles (ranging from 1 to 29 µm in diameter) were identified. The polymers most frequently found in the kidneys were PE and PS [17].

The renal toxicity induced by MNPs is influenced by several physicochemical properties. Notably, the particles’ small size (especially on the nanometre scale) facilitates translocation across biological membranes, entry into the circulation, and arrival at renal tissues [17]. Furthermore, the MNPs’ surface reactivity and chemical composition enable interactions with cellular components (Table 1).

THE RENAL EFFECTS OF MNPS

The results of preclinical studies in rodents have shown that MNPs can modify histological and biological variables in the kidneys. However, these effects cannot necessarily be extrapolated to humans, due to physiological and metabolic differences. Rodents and humans differ in their renal sensitivity to substances. To the best of our knowledge, no data on the putative nephrotoxicity of MNPs in humans have been published. Studies in this area are warranted.

Glomerular lesions

In Xiong et al.’s study, mice exposed to MNPs (80 nm, 0.5 µm, or 5 µm in diameter) for 8 weeks exhibited varying degrees of glomerular basement membrane thickening and fibrotic tissue deposition, compared with control animals. The expression of WT-1 (a sensitive marker of podocyte injury) was higher in the 80 nm MP and 0.5 µm MP groups than in the control group. These results showed that exposure to plastics induces podocyte damage and changes in the glomerular basement membrane [18]. Liu et al. showed that exposure to PP MPs induced marked fusion of podocyte foot processes in the glomerulus and the degeneration of vascular granules, which were indicative of damage to the kidney filtration barrier and changes in cellular nutrient metabolism [4]. Meng et al. demonstrated that chronic (8-week) exposure to PS MNPs (50, 300, and 600 nm and 4 µm in diameter) induced mesangial matrix expansion and endocapillary proliferation in mice [19]. Moreover, in rats exposed to PS NPs (3 or 10 mg/kg/day), the renal cortex exhibited haemorrhage dilation. Congestion of glomerular capillaries and the hypotrophy of certain glomeruli were also observed [20].

Tubular lesions

Tubular lesions have been observed after MNP exposure. Wang et al. reported that PS MPs (2 µm) accumulated in mouse and HK-2 human proximal tubular epithelial cells and led to detachment of distal tubular epithelial cells [21]. The kidneys of mice exposed to PS MPs (5 μm and 20 μm, 0.1 mg/day) presented morphological changes in the area of tubular damage, such as tubular dilatation, loss of brush borders, and flattening of the tubular epithelia [22]. In rats exposed to PS nanoparticles (3 or 10 mg/kg/day), additional signs of toxicity included the loss of cellular architecture in proximal and distal convoluted tubules, the accumulation of eosinophilic material in the tubular lumen, and degeneration at higher doses. The severity of these lesions was dose-dependent [23].

Renal fibrosis

The imbalance between renal damage (glomerular or tubular) and insufficient repair processes gradually leads to fibrosis and irreversible loss of nephrons. Collagen deposition was greater in mice exposed to PS MNPs (diameter 80 nm, 5 µm, and 0.5 µm; concentration 1 mg/l) than in control mice. Elevated expression of the fibrosis biomarker alpha-smooth muscle actin was observed in the kidneys of exposed mice. Thickening of the basement membrane and increased fibrotic tissue were also reported [18].

Exposure to MNPs induced changes in the architecture of renal tissue, including the glomeruli, tubules, interstitium, and capillaries. These alterations can lead to changes in biomarkers of kidney function [24].

Biological effects

Several research groups have looked at the effects of MNPs on biomarkers of renal function (Table 1). In a recent study, Liang et al. showed that compared with controls, mice exposed to PS MNPs showed a lower blood creatinine concentration and higher levels of creatinine clearance, urine albumin, and blood urea nitrogen. These changes reflect renal hyperfiltration, which may be an early marker of the risk of developing CKD [11]. In the study by Wang et al., urine protein levels were higher in mice exposed to PS MNPs than in counterparts exposed to a placebo. Exposure to PS MPs induced albumin leakage in mice [12]. Blood creatinine concentrations were lower in mice exposed to MNPs than in control mice; this was probably related to the muscle dysfunction induced by the MNPs. Two studies of rats exposed to PS MNPs (2 mg/kg/day [21] or 3 to 10 mg/kg/day [10]) highlighted an elevation in serum creatinine and blood urea nitrogen levels.

MNPs modify biological variables in the kidneys and particularly creatininaemia, albuminuria, and blood urea nitrogen levels. The effects of MNPs on these biomarkers vary from study to study. The effects are probably dependent on the species exposed, the type of MNP, the concentration, and the mode of exposure. The histopathological lesions induced by MNPs and the changes in biomarkers observed after exposure to plastics may be regarded as early markers of kidney damage, which may progress to chronic renal failure.

MECHANISMS UNDERLYING THE RENAL TOXICITY OF MNPS

Several mechanisms appear to be involved in the nephrotoxicity of MNPs but have not yet been fully elucidated. The studies mentioned below were conducted either in vivo on rats or mice exposed to MNPs or in vitro on cultured human kidney cell lines (HK-2 or HEK-29).

MNP entry into cells

MNPs are capable of penetrating cells, where they exert their toxicity. Several mechanisms for MNP internalization have been identified. The smallest particles can be absorbed passively or internalized by clathrin- or caveolin-mediated endocytosis and then stored in intracellular vesicles. Larger particles enter through macropinocytosis or phagocytosis [4, 20, 25, 26]. The size of the MNPs therefore has a significant impact on the manner and rate of cellular uptake. Other variables may be involved, such as the type of cell exposed and MNPs’ shape, surface reactivity, dose, and duration of exposure. A study by Goodman et al. showed that HEK-293 human embryonic kidney cells are capable of internalizing plastic particles (diameter 1 µm) [27]. The cascading consequences of MNP internalization by kidney cells will be discussed next.

Reactive oxygen species and oxidative stress

Oxidative stress is a key mechanism underlying the toxicity of many substances. Environmental pollutants can induce oxidative stress, which leads to significant cellular damage in mammalian cells.

Most experts agree that the entry of MNPs into kidney cells leads to an increase in reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels and thus oxidative stress. A study by Goodman et al. showed that ROS levels were higher in HEK 293 cells exposed to PS MNPs (1 µm) than in non-exposed cells [27]. Similarly, Lu et al. observed higher ROS levels in carp kidney (CIK) cells as the concentration of PS MPs rose [28]. A study by Wang et al. found that PS MNPs exacerbated ROS production in HK-2 human proximal tubule epithelial cells, which notably induced structural changes in mitochondria and the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) [22].

In vivo, Liu et al. demonstrated that in exposed mice, PP-MNPs disrupted the cellular balance of ROS [4]. Wang et al. showed that rats exposed to PS MNPs had low levels of the antioxidant enzymes glutathione peroxidase and superoxide dismutase, which is likely to have amplified the oxidative stress [21].

The oxidative stress inhibitor N-acetylcysteine (NAC) and the ER stress inhibitor Sal dramatically reduced PSMP-exposure-induced nephrotoxicity in rats by inhibiting oxidative stress and inflammation signalling pathways [21]. This indicates that the nephrotoxicity of MNPs involves excessive ROS production.

Oxidative stress initiates ER stress, and both can lead to apoptosis. The mRNA expression levels of the ER stress-related genes IRE1, JNK, CHOP, and NFkB were considerably higher in a group of mice exposed to 2.0 mg/kg PSMPs than in the control group but were lower in exposed mice treated with NAC and Sal [21]. Wang et al. reported that oral exposure to PS MPs over 28 days induced kidney damage in juvenile rats: potentially by activating oxidative stress and ER stress [22, 29]. Many kidney diseases (such as CKD, diabetic nephropathy, and renal fibrosis) are associated with ER stress [30].

Wang et al. suggested a pro-apoptotic mechanism whereby ER stress activates the Bcl-2 associated X protein, which then forms pores in the mitochondrial membrane. This mitochondrial disruption initiates a signalling cascade that leads ultimately to cell apoptosis [21]. Lu et al. also showed that mitochondrial activity decreased significantly after PS MP treatment, which suggests that PS MPs induce mitochondrial depolarization in CIK cells [28]. The increase in the level of ROS following exposure to MNPs affected the CIK cells’ proliferation, apoptosis, and autophagy. These effects were significantly reversed by treatment with the ROS scavenger NAC. The report by Lu et al. indicated that PS MPs might stimulate apoptosis and autophagy by inducing high levels of ROS, leading to G2/M phase arrest [28].

Inflammation

Relative to non-exposed cells, HK-2 kidney cells exposed to MNP (0.4 or 0.8 mg/ml) express higher levels of inflammation-associated proteins such as phospholipase A2 and cyclooxygenase 1. These proteins are generated when the ER is stressed [22].

The results of renal biopsies from chickens and mice exposed to PS MNPs show renal inflammation. This inflammation was characterized by activation of the transcription factor NF-kB and greater secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines [tumour necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), and interleukins (IL)1β and 6] in the chickens’ kidneys [31]. Kidney biopsies from mice showed an interstitial infiltrate of mononuclear inflammatory cells [19]. Similarly, the results of a study by Xiong et al. showed that exposure of mice to MNPs promoted macrophage infiltration of the kidneys. Furthermore, exposure to 80 nm diameter MPs was associated with greater mRNA expression of monocyte chemoattractant protein 1 and the pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-1b [18]. According to Wang et al., the oxidative stress and ER stress induced by exposure to PS MNPs activate the Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK)-mediated signalling pathway. Subsequently, JNK promotes the activation of the transcription factor NF-κB and leads ultimately to inflammatory responses [29].

Last, oral ingestion of MNPs induced systemic inflammation in mice, as evidenced by elevated serum levels of three pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β) relative to non-exposed controls. When treated with N-acetyl-cysteine or salubrinal (both of which block oxidative stress pathways), the inflammatory response was attenuated and the cytokine levels were lower [22].

Exposure to MNPs therefore appears to induce both local and systemic renal inflammatory responses, elevated ROS levels, and greater oxidative stress: potentially resulting in kidney damage [19].

Cytotoxicity

Oxidative stress and inflammation are typical cytotoxic factors in mammals [31]. A number of studies have shown that MNPs induce changes in lipid or carbohydrate metabolism, alter certain cell signalling pathways, or cause ER stress as a result of oxidative stress. [28, 31]. These intracellular effects of MNPs promote the apoptosis of kidney cells, as shown by Wang et al. in rats exposed to PS MPs (1 µm, 2.0 mg/kg/d, for 28 days) [21]. Similarly, studies by Chen et al. and Lu et al. showed that high concentrations of PS MPs caused oxidative stress leading to cell death, mainly by autophagy or apoptosis [24, 28]. Lu et al. show a cell-cycle dysregulation: Flow cytometry showed that PS MPs increased the G2/M phase of CIK cells and decreased the G0/G1 phase proportion. Furthermore, Goodman et al. reported on a change in the proliferation and morphology of HEK-293 kidney cells exposed to PS MNPs (1 µm, 5 or 100 µg/ml, for 72 hours) [27]. According to Chen et al., exposure to PS MNPs (300 ng/ml) damages the renal barrier through depletion of the cell junction proteins zonula occludens 2 and alpha-1-antitrypsin [24].

The oxidative stress and inflammation caused by exposure to MNPs can induce cellular toxicity. In the kidneys, these toxicity mechanisms might modify the renal architecture and impair filtration (Fig. 3).

The cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying MNP-induced renal effects are complex and have not been sufficiently characterized. Emerging evidence indicates that MNPs interfere with specific signalling pathways and thereby perturb cellular homeostasis and compromise renal function. Indirect kidney injury

Via vascular damage

MNPs are acknowledged to be new vascular risk factors that influence vascular cell function and exacerbate atherosclerosis through various pathways [32]. These cardiovascular changes might be involved in the genesis of renal lesions and might accelerate the progression of CKD towards renal failure. The results of a recent study showed that circulating MPs are phagocytosed by immune cells, which then clog the blood capillaries [33].

Via cardiac damage

A study showed that exposure of zebrafish to PS nanoparticles (diameter 80 nm; concentration 0.1, 1 or 10 µg/ml) activated aryl hydrocarbon receptors (AhRs). Exposure to nanoparticles alters heart rate and pericardial surface area via AhR activation and greater oxidative stress. Some studies have shown that tryptophan-derived uraemic toxins (such as indoxyl sulphate) activate AhR. These uraemic toxins accumulate in non-dialysed patients with stage 4–5 CKD [34]. MNPs might therefore potentiate the renal and cardiovascular toxicity of uraemic toxins by amplifying AhR activity in general and in patients with severe CKD in particular. Given that cardiovascular damage impairs the proper functioning of the kidneys, these cardiovascular changes are likely to accelerate the progression of CKD.

Co-exposure

Living beings are constantly or regularly exposed to many potentially dangerous substances in the environment, in addition to MNPs. One can hypothesize that co-exposure to MNPs and other contaminants (such as fine particles, a constituent of polluted air) contributes synergistically to the risks of acute kidney injury and other toxic effects on the kidneys [35]. It has been shown that the vast hydrophobic surface of MNPs makes them particularly able to bind environmental pollutants. The extent of the adsorption depends on the characteristics of the plastic (the chemical formula, size, shape, density, etc.) and the medium (the salinity, pH, and temperature, etc.) [36]. According to the emerging ‘Trojan horse’ hypothesis, MNPs are vectors for toxic particles and pathogenic microorganisms [37]. It is therefore essential to study the toxicity of co-exposure to MNPs and other environmental contaminants. For example, the combination of MNPs with pesticides or phthalates has a synergistic effect.

Here, we shall focus on the nephrotoxic effects of MNP/bisphenol A (BPA) co-exposure. BPA is an aromatic organic compound at is used in the manufacture of plastics and epoxy resins and is found particularly in plastic food packaging and the inner coatings of cans. However, due to BPA’s known endocrine-disrupting effect, strict regulations have been put in place; the compound has been banned in baby’s bottles (since 2011) and till receipts (since 2020). A recent in vitro study measured the effects of exposing a proximal tubular kidney cell line (HK-2) to PE MNPs (diameter 1 to 4 µm; concentration: 0.2 mg/ml) and/or BPA (100 nmol/l) for 5 to 24 hours. These concentrations are close to those found in the environment. Although all three exposure conditions were associated with abnormally low cell viability and elevated oxidative and inflammatory effects, the impact of co-exposure was generally more pronounced [38]. Various other plastic additives exist: these include phthalates, brominated flame retardants, poly-alkyl phenols, and per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances. The results of several studies indicate that these plastic additives can interact with drug transporters, as inhibitors, substrates, or expression modulators. Renal creatinine transporters (OAT2, OCT2, OCT3, MATE1, and MATE2k) might be affected. The additives’ impact on the pharmacokinetics of certain substances (e.g. drugs) or on creatinine transport has not been extensively characterized, and there is a need for more research on these interactions and their implications [39].

These results indicate that the health risks posed by MNPs are related not only to the particles themselves but also to the bioavailability and toxicity of the environmental pollutants that they transport. It will therefore be necessary to study the mechanisms underlying the synergistic nephrotoxic effects of co-exposure.

MNP AND CKD

CKD affects ∼8.5% of the global population, and this prevalence is rising as a result of ageing, diabetes, hypertension, and obesity [40]. At the same time, human exposure to MNPs has increased significantly. Emerging evidence suggests that MNPs contribute to the development of CKD.

CKD is characterized by persistent kidney damage or impaired filtration capacity, which are often identified through albuminuria or a low estimated glomerular filtration rate. The disease typically results from chronic conditions such as diabetes, hypertension, or ageing, which cause gradual nephron loss. The kidneys respond with adaptive mechanisms—such as hyperfiltration, cellular hypertrophy, and podocyte stress—that contribute to further injury over time. This damage triggers inflammation and accelerates the decline in kidney function.

As described above, the results of animal/cell-based studies suggest that MNPs induce oxidative stress, inflammation of the glomeruli and/or tubules, and renal fibrosis. These processes are already known to be key drivers of CKD progression. MNPs might act as an initial renal insult or might exacerbate a pre-existing renal impairment. Increased susceptibility to MNPs is likely to depend on the person’s comorbidity burden and age.

Conversely, pre-existing CKD might increase susceptibility to MNP exposure and worsen its adverse outcomes. CKD is also likely to facilitate MNP accumulation in the bloodstream, although current evidence remains scarce. The urinary elimination of MNPs is poorly characterized: although faecal excretion appears to be predominant, these particles have been detected in the urine and in renal tissue, suggesting at least partial clearance through the urinary tract [14, 15, 16]. Impaired kidney function might therefore reduce this clearance and enhance systemic retention. Moreover, haemodialysis patients might face an additional risk because dialysis membranes, storage tanks, and distribution systems often contain plastic components that can release MNPs. Since haemodialysis patients cannot excrete particles in urine, systemic accumulation might be amplified. These overlapping mechanisms raise the possibility of mutual aggravation between CKD and MNP exposure.

CONCLUSION

MNPs appear to have nephrotoxic effects on kidney cells from humans and other mammals, although the mechanisms underlying this renal toxicity have not yet been characterized in detail. It appears that MNPs induce oxidative stress, both renal and systemic renal inflammation, and elevated cytotoxicity, which then lead to histopathological and biochemical changes in kidney tissues (Fig. 4). MNPs can therefore contribute to the development of CKD. Moreover, synergistic nephrotoxic effects may result from co-exposure to MNPs and other environmental contaminants.

Investigating the impact of MNPs on kidney function remains a major challenge. In animal models, accurate quantification is hampered by the particles’ low abundance, small size, diverse forms, and nonspecific chemical structures, together with the risk of contamination from plastic-based laboratory equipment. In humans, the obstacles are even greater: actual ingestion levels are difficult to estimate, interindividual variability is high and influenced by diet, water sources, and environmental conditions, and no clear toxic thresholds have been established. Most studies focused on a single type of MNP, whereas real-life exposure involves a complex mixture of shapes, sizes, and compositions. Moreover, plastics interact with other pollutants and might act as vectors that amplify toxicity through the ‘Trojan horse’ effect. In most cases, this synergistic effect increases overall toxicity. Last, ethical constraints further limit experimental designs in humans. These challenges and the scarcity of data hinder the establishment of evidence-based conclusions. To date, a tolerable threshold for MNPs in humans has not been defined.

To better understand (i) the effects of MNPs on human health in general and the kidneys in particular, and (ii) the correlation between MNPs and CKD, further studies are required. In addition to further research, the reduction of human exposure to plastics has become a public health priority. This reduction requires both limiting the everyday use of plastics (e.g. by promoting reusable glass and metal containers, biodegradable packaging, and natural-fibre textiles) and reducing overall plastic production (e.g. through sustainable manufacturing practices and the development of eco-friendly alternatives).
