The VaATG6–VaBI-1 module coordinates ER-associated autophagy and ROS homeostasis for cold tolerance in grapevine
Authors: Qiaoling Zheng, Fang Ma, Chang Liu, Qinhan Yu, Ningbo Zhang, Weirong Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cold stress is a major constraint on plant productivity, especially in perennial crops like grapevine (Vitis spp.), where it disrupts cellular homeostasis and induces oxidative damage. Here, we identify VaATG6, a key regulator of autophagy, and its interaction with VaBI-1, an ER-localized modulator, as central components of the cold stress response in Vitis amurensis. Cold exposure rapidly induced VaATG6 transcription and triggered its dynamic relocalization from the nucleus to the ER and autophagosomes, enhancing autophagic flux, reducing reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, and improving cold tolerance. In contrast, VaBI-1 acted as a modulator, fine-tuned autophagy and ROS levels through its interaction with VaATG6. Notably, cold stress strengthened the VaATG6–VaBI-1 interaction, linking a coordinated mechanism that integrated autophagy with ER homeostasis. Overexpression of VaATG6 in cold-sensitive V. vinifera enhanced antioxidant defenses and promoted soluble sugar accumulation, significantly improving cold tolerance. Conversely, VaATG6 loss-of-function mutants exhibited impaired autophagy, elevated ROS accumulation, and increased cold sensitivity. These findings establish the VaATG6–VaBI-1 regulatory module as a core mechanism for cold-induced autophagy and oxidative stress adaptation. Our results highlight VaATG6 overexpression and VaBI-1 modulation as promising genetic strategies to enhance cold tolerance in perennial crops.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s43897-025-00206-7.

Core

Our study identifies a cold-responsive VaATG6-VaBI-1 regulatory module that coordinates endoplasmic reticulum (ER)-localized autophagy to enhance cold tolerance in grapevine. These findings suggest a potential mechanism contributing to perennial stress adaptation and may provide useful genetic elements for improving crop resilience under cold stress.

Introduction

The ability of plants to withstand and adapt to cold stress is vital for their survival, productivity, and ecological significance, particularly in frost-prone regions that underpin global ecosystems and agriculture (Repo et al. 2008; Lembrechts et al. 2016). Cold stress, which includes both chilling and freezing temperatures, severely impairs plant growth, development, and reproduction, leading to significant yield losses and substantial economic impacts in major crops (Chen et al. 2015; Li et al. 2015). These detrimental effects highlight the urgent need for strategies to enhance plant resilience to low temperatures. In response to cold stress, plants initiate a complex array of physiological and molecular defense mechanisms, such as the induction of cold-responsive (COR) genes, accumulation of osmoprotectants, and activation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging systems, all of which help mitigate the cellular damage from ice formation and dehydration (Pearce, 2001, Kazemi-Shahandashti and Maali-Amiri. 2018). Nonetheless, these innate protective responses are often insufficient, as evidenced by cold-induced yield reductions in economically significant crops such as grapevine.

Recent advances in plant biology have elucidated the genetic and molecular underpinnings of cold tolerance, identifying transcription factors, signaling pathways, and autophagy as key regulatory components (Xu et al. 2017b; Shi et al. 2018; Chi et al. 2020, 2024). Among these, autophagy—a conserved mechanism for degrading and recycling damaged organelles and macromolecules—has emerged as a critical contributor to plant resilience under cold stress (Qin et al. 2007; Liu and Bassham. 2012). In plants, the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) plays a central role in the early steps of autophagosome formation, serving both as a membrane source and a platform for autophagy initiation (Zhuang et al. 2018). ER-associated autophagy is particularly important under abiotic stress, as the removal of damaged ER components and misfolded proteins contributes to the restoration of cellular homeostasis. During cold stress, autophagosomes formation facilitates the clearance of oxidized organelles and alleviates ROS accumulation, thereby enhancing cold tolerance in various crops (Zhao et al. 2023; Chen et al. 2023). Although autophagy is reportedly suppressed during cold acclimation in Arabidopsis thaliana (Sato et al. 2024), numerous studies have demonstrated its induction under acute cold stress. This response is characterized by a marked increase in autophagosome accumulation (Zhai et al. 2016; Valitova et al. 2019; Chen et al. 2023; Chi et al. 2024) and plays a pivotal role in mitigating oxidative damage through regulation of ROS homeostasis. For example, overexpression of autophagy-related genes (ATGs), such as MsATG13 in alfalfa and MdATG5 in apple, enhances antioxidant defenses and improves tolerance to cold and drought (Jia et al. 2021; Zhao et al. 2023). Furthermore, cold-induced autophagy is conserved across a broad range of plant species, with upregulation of ATGs observed in tomato, wheat, and chili pepper (Zhai et al. 2016; Valitova et al. 2019; Chen et al. 2023; Chi et al. 2024). In parallel, selective autophagy, mediated by receptor proteins such as NBR1, targets specific cellular components for degradation during cold stress (Ran et al. 2020). Despite these advances in annual crops, the regulatory mechanisms of autophagy in perennial fruit crops like grapevine—which experience recurring cold stress over multiple growing seasons—remain largely unexplored.

The autophagy-related gene ATG6 (also known as Beclin1 in mammals) is a central regulator of autophagosome formation and has been extensively characterized across eukaryotic systems (Cao and Klionsky 2007; He and Levine 2010). In plants, ATG6 has been implicated in responses to nutrient deprivation, salinity, and pathogen infection (Zeng et al. 2017; Tang and Bassham 2018; Cao et al. 2022). However, its specific role in cold tolerance remains largely uncharacterized. Prior research suggests that ATG6 plays a critical role in mitigating ROS-mediated damage under environmental stress. For instance, overexpression of ATG6 orthologs in Arabidopsis and rice enhances tolerance to salinity and oxidative stress— conditions that share overlapping mechanisms with cold-induced cellular damage (Rana et al. 2012; Zeng et al. 2017). Whether this function extends to perennial crops such as grapevine, however, remains unclear. Notably, grapevine possesses a single-copy ATG6 gene, making it an ideal model to investigate the gene’s contribution to cold stress tolerance in this economically and ecologically important perennial species.

Furthermore, the involvement of BAX inhibitor-1 (BI-1), an endoplasmic reticulum (ER)-localized regulator of programmed cell death and autophagy, introduces an additional layer of regulatory complexity. BI-1 has been shown to interact with ATG6 in other species, modulating autophagic activity and enhancing stress tolerance (Xu et al. 2017a). Importantly, BI-1 is a central regulator of ER homeostasis (Watanabe and Lam 2008) and reactive oxygen species (ROS) signaling (Kawai-Yamada et al. 2004), both of which are tightly linked to cold-induced cellular damage. Its potential interaction with ATG6 suggests a coordinated mechanism that enhances autophagic efficiency during stress adaptation (Xu et al. 2017a). However, the functional role of BI-1 in perennial crops remains poorly understood, and its interaction with ATG6 in grapevine under cold stress has yet to be investigated. Elucidating this relationship could provide valuable insights into the integration of autophagy and ER homeostasis in support of cold tolerance in perennial species.

We hypothesize that ATG6, in coordination with BI-1, plays a central role in regulating autophagy to alleviate cold-induced cellular damage in grapevine. To address the limited understanding of molecular mechanisms underlying cold tolerance in perennial crops, this study investigates the autophagy-related gene VaATG6 and its interaction partner VaBI-1 in grapevine. By integrating genetic, biochemical, and cell biological approaches, we demonstrate that cold stress induces VaATG6 expression and promotes its dynamic relocalization to autophagosomes, thereby enhancing autophagic flux. Furthermore, we show that VaBI-1 interacts with VaATG6 under cold conditions, synergistically amplifying autophagy-mediated stress responses. In transgenic grapevine lines, overexpression of VaATG6 markedly improves cold tolerance by reducing ROS accumulation, elevating antioxidant enzyme activities, and maintaining cellular homeostasis, whereas VaATG6 loss-of-function mutants exhibit impaired autophagy and increased cold sensitivity. Our results indicated that the VaATG6-VaBI-1 interaction is essential for optimal autophagic activity and adaptation to cold stress. Collectively, these findings uncover a previously uncharacterized regulatory mechanism in grapevine, highlighting the VaATG6-VaBI-1 module as a promising genetic target for enhancing cold tolerance in perennial crops.

Results

Cold stress induces the expression of autophagy-related gene VaATG6 in grape

To determine whether autophagy is activated in response to cold stress, we examined autophagic dynamics in grapevine seedlings using Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) following exposure to 2 °C for 3, 6, 12, and 24 h. Compared with control plants maintained at 25 °C, cold-treated seedlings displayed a marked accumulation of autophagosomes, peaking at 6 h and persisting through 12 h before declining at 24 h (Fig. 1A). This temporal pattern suggests that autophagy constitutes a key component of the early cold stress response, which may subsequently be downregulated under prolonged stress exposure. To elucidate the transcriptional regulation underlying this response, we analyzed the promoter sequences of 33 VvATG genes, identifying a diverse set of cis-regulatory elements associated with biotic and abiotic stress responses. Notably, 15 VvATG genes contained low-temperature responsive (LTR) motifs, with VvATG6 harboring the greatest number (five), indicating strong transcriptional responsiveness to cold stress (Fig. 1B). Expression profiling in V. amurensis under cold stress revealed that ATG6 showed the highest induction among seven significantly upregulated ATG genes (ATG1a, ATG3, ATG6, ATG8c.1, ATG13a, ATG14, and ATG20), with transcript levels rising sharply within 3 h of exposure (Fig. 1C). These findings implicate ATG6 as a major autophagy regulator during cold stress response. To assess whether regulatory divergence underlies differences in ATG6 cold responsiveness between the cold-tolerant V. amurensis and cold-sensitive V. vinifera, we cloned and compared their ATG6 promoter sequences. Alignment analysis identified five deletions (−626, −466, −372, −289, and −272 bp) and one insertion (−180 bp) in the VvATG6 promoter, resulting in altered positioning of three LTR motifs (−219, −253, and −995 bp) (Fig. 1D, S1). To test this hypothesis, we fused the VaATG6 and VvATG6 promoters to a GUS reporter gene and introduced them into grape calli. After 3 h of exposure to 2 °C, GUS staining revealed substantially stronger activity driven by the VaATG6 promoter compared to VvATG6, indicating that VaATG6 promoter mediates an enhanced transcriptional response to cold stress (Fig. 1E). In planta expression analyses further supported this observation: ATG6 was rapidly induced in V. amurensis within 3 h of cold exposure and maintained elevated expression levels for up to 48 h. In contrast, ATG6 induction in V. vinifera was delayed, with significant upregulation observed only at 12 h, peaking at 24 h before declining (Fig. 1F, G). Despite their nearly identical coding sequences (Figure S2), VaATG6 and VvATG6 exhibit markedly different expression patterns under cold stress, likely attributable to promoter divergence. These results highlight transcriptional regulation as the key factor underlying their functional differences. The faster and more sustained induction of VaATG6 in V. amurensis suggests an adaptive trait contributes to its superior cold tolerance. Together, these results identify VaATG6 as a prime candidate for functional analysis, establishing it as a transcriptionally regulated determinant of cold-induced autophagy in grapevine.

Phylogenetic relationship and subcellular localization of VaATG6 under cold stress

To explore the evolutionary conservation and potential functional divergence of VaATG6, we performed a phylogenetic analysis of ATG6 homologs across a range of plant species. A phylogenetic tree constructed from amino acid sequences of V. amurensis and related taxa, revealed distinct clustering patterns. Notably, VaATG6 clustered closely with ATG6 orthologs from other Vitis species, including V. vinifera, suggesting a conserved evolutionary lineage within the Vitis genus (Figure S3A).

The VaATG6 protein from V. amurensis shares several highly conserved domains with ATG6 homologs from V. vinifera and Arabidopsis thaliana, particularly within the ATG6_N and APG6 regions, which are critical for autophagic function (Figure S3B). To examine the subcellular localization of VaATG6 under both normal and cold stress conditions, we transiently expressed a VaATG6-GFP fusion protein in tobacco leaf cells. Under normal conditions (25 °C), VaATG6 was primarily localized to the plasma membrane, with additional distribution in the nucleus and endoplasmic reticulum (ER), as demonstrated by co-localization with the nuclear marker RK-H2B, the ER marker RK-ER, and the plasma membrane marker RK-PM (Fig. 2A). Following cold stress (2 °C, 3 h), VaATG6 exhibited reduced nuclear localization and increased accumulation in the ER, evidenced by enhanced co-localization with RK-ER and supported by quantitative analysis (Fig. 2B), indicating redistribution toward the ER. This dynamic relocalization suggests that VaATG6 may participate in cold stress responses through interactions with the ER or ER-associated signaling pathways (Fig. 2A). Additionally, some cells displayed punctate GFP fluorescence. To determine whether these puncta represented autophagosomes, we co-expressed VaATG6-GFP with the autophagy marker ATG8f-mCherry in tobacco leaves under cold stress. The observed overlap between GFP and mCherry signals confirmed that these puncta correspond to autophagosomes (Fig. 2C, D). VaATG6 overexpression enhances cold stress tolerance in V. vinifera

To explore the functional role of VaATG6 in cold tolerance, we generated transgenic V. vinifera lines overexpressing VaATG6 as well as CRISPR-Cas9-mediated knockout lines. A schematic diagram of the CRISPR/Cas9 construct used for grape transformation illustrates the targeted mutagenesis of VaATG6 (Figure S4A). Sequence analysis of the edited regions in regenerated plantlets identified two distinct mutations that introduced premature stop codons, effectively disrupting VaATG6 function (Figure S4D). In parallel, an overexpression construct driven by the CaMV35S promoter was generated for transformation (Figure S4E). PCR analysis confirmed the presence of the transgene in kanamycin- or hygromycin-resistant lines (Figure S4B, F), and qRT-PCR analysis verified significant upregulation or depletion of VaATG6 transcripts in the corresponding transgenic lines (UT) (Figure S4C, G). Additionally, Western blot analysis confirmed the expression of VaATG6-GFP in transgenic lines (Figure S4H).

Phenotypic characterization revealed that VaATG6 knockout (VaATG6-Cas9) grapevines exhibited dwarfism, smaller leaves, and impaired growth compared to UT controls. In contrast, VaATG6-overexpressing plants displayed increased height, larger leaves, and enhanced overall vigor (Fig. 3B). To evaluate the effect of VaATG6 overexpression on cold tolerance, 8-week-old transgenic grapevines were exposed to freezing conditions (−2 °C for 22 h). Phenotypic analysis demonstrated that VaATG6-overexpressing lines sustained significantly less damage than UT control, exhibiting healthier morphology and higher survival rates. Biochemical assays were performed to quantify Malondialdehyde (MDA) levels, a marker of lipid peroxidation and oxidative damage (Davey et al. 2005), and soluble sugar content, a well-established indicator of stress tolerance (Camalle et al. 2024). VaATG6-overexpressing lines exhibited significantly lower MDA levels, indicating reduced oxidative damage, along with elevated soluble sugar content, which may contribute to enhanced cold stress tolerance (Fig. 3C and D). In contrast, VaATG6 knockout lines accumulated higher MDA levels and displayed decreased soluble sugar content, further supporting the role of VaATG6 in mediating cold stress responses. These results demonstrate that overexpression of VaATG6 in V. vinifera enhanced cold tolerance, likely through mechanisms involving the mitigation of oxidative stress and the maintenance of cellular osmoprotectants.

Quantitative analysis of ROS contents and antioxidant enzyme activities further revealed differential oxidative stress responses among the transgenic lines. Under cold stress, Cas9 lines exhibited significantly higher H2O2 levels than UT, whereas OE lines maintained lower H2O2 concentrations (Fig. 4D). A similar pattern was observed for O2·− content, with elevated levels in Cas9 lines and substantial reductions in OE lines. Activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT), two key enzymes involved in ROS detoxification, were significantly increased in OE lines, particularly under cold stress, suggesting enhanced ROS-scavenging capacity. In contrast, Cas9 lines showed reduced SOD and CAT activities, correlating with elevated ROS accumulation and increased susceptibility to oxidative damage (Fig. 4D).

Furthermore, qRT-PCR analysis revealed that cold-stressed OE lines exhibited strong upregulation of VvSOD and VvCAT transcripts, whereas Cas9 lines displayed marked downregulation of these genes, consistent with the enzymatic activity profiles (Fig. 4E). These findings indicate that VaATG6 plays a critical role in maintaining ROS homeostasis under cold stress in V. vinifera. Enhanced antioxidant enzyme expression and activity in OE-VaATG6 lines contribute to their increased tolerance to cold-induced oxidative stress, whereas loss of VaATG6 function in Cas9 lines leads to excessive ROS accumulation and increased cellular damage.

VaATG6 enhances autophagy and upregulates VvATGs expression under cold stress

Under normal conditions (25 °C), autophagosome formation was limited across all genotypes, with only minor differences observed among UT, VaATG6-overexpressing (OE), and VaATG6-CRISPR knockout (Cas9) lines. However, after 3 h of exposure to chilling stress at 2 °C, OE lines exhibited a substantial increase in autophagosomes compared to UT and Cas9 lines, as indicated by arrows in TEM images (Fig. 5A). To exclude the possibility that growth differences influenced cold stress responses, plants of similar vigor and size were carefully selected from UT, OE-VaATG6, and VaATG6-Cas9 lines for cold stress assays. Despite this controlled baseline, OE-VaATG6 plants showed significantly improved cold tolerance, whereas VaATG6-Cas9 plants remained highly sensitive (Fig. 3B), supporting the hypothesis that VaATG6-mediated cold tolerance is independent of developmental phenotypes. Quantification of autophagosomes per cell further supported these observations: OE lines displayed a significant increase in autophagic structures under cold stress, whereas Cas9 lines showed a marked reduction related to UT (Fig. 5B). This elevated autophagic activity in OE lines likely facilitates the removal of damaged cellular components, thereby contributing to enhanced cold tolerance.

Furthermore, qRT-PCR analysis revealed that several core autophagy-related genes, including VvATG1, VvATG2, VvATG3, VvATG8a, VvATG10, VvATG18f, and VvATG18h, were significantly upregulated in OE lines under cold stress compared to UT and Cas9 lines (Fig. 5C-I). In contrast, Cas9 lines exhibited reduced expression of these genes, consistent with their impaired autophagic activity. These findings strongly support the hypothesis that VaATG6 enhances cold tolerance by promoting autophagy and activating downstream autophagy-related gene expression.

Discussion

Cold stress poses a major constraint on plant growth and productivity, yet the role of autophagy in mediating cold tolerance in perennial crops remains insufficiently understood. In this study, we identify VaATG6 as a key regulator of cold-induced autophagy in grapevine (Vitis amurensis) and demonstrate its functional interaction with VaBI-1, an ER-localized stress modulator. Through integrative genetic, biochemical, and cellular analyses, we show that VaATG6 is rapidly induced by cold stress, enhances autophagic flux, and attenuates oxidative damage, thereby conferring increased cold tolerance. In contrast, VaBI-1 functions as a negative regulator, modulating both autophagic activity and ROS accumulation. Notably, cold stress enhances the physical interaction between VaATG6 and VaBI-1, suggesting a coordinated mechanism that integrates autophagy and ER homeostasis during stress adaptation. We situate these findings within the broader framework of plant stress responses, delineating the distinct yet functionally interconnected roles of VaATG6 and VaBI-1, and propose the VaATG6–VaBI-1 regulatory module as a critical determinant of cold tolerance in perennial species.

VaATG6 orchestrates cold-induced autophagy and ROS accumulation for stress adaptation

Autophagy is a well-established mechanism of stress adaptation, yet its contribution to cold tolerance, particularly in perennial crops, remains insufficiently explored. Our study identifies VaATG6 as a central regulator of cold-induced autophagy in grapevine (Fig. 3), consistent with reports in Solanum lycopersicum, Medicago sativa and Brassica napus, where autophagy-related genes (ATGs) are upregulated under low temperatures (Eshkiki et al. 2020; Chi et al. 2020; Zhao et al. 2023). Notably, we extend these findings by demonstrating that VaATG6 is not only transcriptionally activated but also dynamically relocalized in response to cold stress, shifting from the nucleus to the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and autophagosomes (Fig. 2). This subcellular redistribution suggests that VaATG6 actively coordinates autophagy initiation and execution during cold exposure, potentially linking autophagy to ER stress responses—an underexplored intersection in plant stress biology. Interestingly, we observed strong punctate GFP signals in cold-treated N. benthamiana leaves, potentially representing stress granules; however, accurate identification remains technically challenging due to the large central vacuole in tobacco cells. Further studies using high-resolution imaging or co-localization with known markers are needed to clarify their identity.

The rapid induction of VaATG6 in V. amurensis, relative to V. vinifera, provides a mechanistic basis for the superior cold tolerance of the former. Promoter analysis revealed multiple low-temperature response elements (LTRs) in VaATG6, whereas V. vinifera, a cold-sensitive species, harbors deletions and rearrangements in these regulatory motifs (Fig. 1D, E, F). These findings support earlier reports that natural variations in cis-regulatory elements contributes to species-specific differences in abiotic stress tolerance (Wang et al. 2022; Hong et al. 2023; Hu et al. 2024).

Unexpectedly, despite robust cold-induced expression, VaATG6 overexpression in V. vinifera did not lead to constitutively elevated autophagy under non-stress conditions (Fig. 5). This contrasts with Arabidopsis studies where ATGs overexpression enhanced basal autophagy even in the absence of stress (Luo et al. 2017; Chiu et al. 2023). One possible explanation is that VaATG6 requires stress-activated cofactors for full autophagic activation, thereby avoiding unnecessary energy expenditure under favorable conditions. Alternatively, post-transcriptional modifications, such as phosphorylation-dependent regulation, may fine-tune VaATG6 activity, as observed in Arabidopsis (Qi et al. 2020). Elucidating these regulatory layers may shed light on how perennial crops balance growth and stress responses..

Enhanced autophagic flux mediated by VaATG6 may promote to ROS clearance through the selective degradation of damaged, ROS-producing organelles such as mitochondria and peroxisomes. This is supported by our TEM observations showing increased autophagosome formation under cold stress (Fig. 5A). Similar mechanisms have been described in Arabidopsis and apple, where autophagy facilitates organelle-specific turnover during abiotic stress (Huo et al. 2020; Oikawa et al. 2022). Autophagy may also indirectly support redox homeostasis by degrading inhibitors of antioxidant signaling. For example, the selective degradation of KEAP1-like proteins can activate Nrf2-mediated antioxidant response (Taguchi et al. 2012; Filomeni et al. 2015). Consistent with this, our OE-VaATG6 lines exhibited increased expression of VvSOD and VvCAT under cold stress (Fig. 4E), suggesting a coordinated activation of autophagy and transcriptional antioxidant defense pathways.

VaBI-1 as a modulator of cold stress and autophagy

While VaATG6 positively regulates autophagy and enhances cold tolerance, our study reveals that VaBI-1 functions as a modulator that restricts excessive autophagic activity (Fig. 8). This finding contrasts with previous reports in Arabidopsis and Wheat, where BI-1 has been implicated in promoting stress tolerance by mitigating ER stress (Ramiro et al. 2016; Lu et al. 2018). In grapevine, however, VaBI-1 overexpression resulted in elevated ROS accumulation, downregulation of autophagy-related gene, and increased sensitivity to freezing stress (Fig. 8, 9). One possible explanation for this discrepancy is that the role of VaBI-1 in stress adaptation is species-specific. In annual plants, BI-1 may enhance stress tolerance by suppressing programmed cell death (PCD) under acute stress (Watanabe and Lam 2009; Ramiro et al. 2016). In contrast, in perennial crops such as grapevine, prolonged inhibition of autophagy may be detrimental, leading to the accumulation of damaged organelles and persistent oxidative stress. This hypothesis is supported by findings in apple and citrus, where excessive suppression of autophagy has been associated with reduced long-term stress resilience (Sun et al. 2018; Fu et al. 2020).

An additional and unexpected finding was the enhanced physical interaction between VaATG6 and VaBI-1 under cold stress (Fig. 6), suggesting that VaBI-1 may not function purely as a suppressor but instead modulates the activation threshold of autophagy. While prior studies in tobacco and rice have identified ATG6-BI-1 interactions these were not shown to be temperature-dependent (Xu et al. 2017a). Our data suggest that in grapevine, cold stress induces a reorganization of protein-protein interactions, potentially shifting VaBI-1 from a static repressor to a dynamic regulator. Future studies using biochemical and structural analyses of the VaATG6-VaBI-1 interaction domain will be essential to determine whether cold-induced conformational changes influence VaBI-1 function.

Notably, VaBI-1 overexpression significantly suppressed soluble sugar accumulation under freezing conditions was particularly striking (Fig. 8F). Unlike Arabidopsis BI-1, which supports sugar metabolism under stress, AtBI-1 overexpression in sugarcane has been shown to improve drought tolerance by modulating ROS signaling and enhancing ER stress resistance (Ramiro et al. 2016b). In grapevine, however, VaBI-1 appears to disrupt sugar-dependent cryoprotection. Given the well-established roles of soluble sugars as osmoprotectants and ROS scavengers (van den Ende and Valluru. 2008; Furlan et al. 2012), our findings imply that VaBI-1 may regulate the allocation of carbon between energy metabolism and stress defense. Further investigation into the downstream metabolic pathways influenced by VaBI-1 could reveal critical mechanisms by which perennial crops balance seasonal growth and abiotic stress adaptation.

VaATG6-VaBI-1 module: a fine-tuned mechanism of ROS regulation

Autophagy plays a pivotal role in mitigating oxidative damage by degrading oxidized proteins and dysfunctional organelles. Our results demonstrate that VaATG6 enhances ROS detoxification under cold stress (Fig. 4), whereas VaBI-1 overexpression exacerbates ROS accumulation (Fig. 9). These findings are consistent with previous studies showing that ATG6 enhances antioxidant capacity in Arabidopsis, rice, and V. vinifera (Zeng et al. 2017; Tang and Bassham. 2018; Xia et al. 2024). Importantly, our study provides the first direct evidence of an antagonistic yet coordinated interaction between ATG6 and BI-1 in regulating ROS homeostasis during cold stress. Unexpectedly, while VaBI-1 overexpression led to increased ROS accumulation, it also elevated SOD and CAT activities, suggesting that VaBI-1 may activate alternative, autophagy-independent antioxidant pathways (Fig. 9). This dual regulatory effect mirrors observations in rice, where BI-1 modulates ER stress responses through non-autophagic pathways. Notably, plant BI-1 suppresses ROS-induced cell death via its conserved C-terminal domain, not by directly eliminating ROS, but by modulating intracellular oxidative stress (Kawai-Yamada et al. 2004). These results support the hypothesis that VaBI-1 functions as a regulatory hub, integrating both autophagy-dependent and independent ROS signaling to maintain redox homeostasis under cold stress.

Despite multiple transformation attempts, stable VaBI-1-Cas9 knockout lines could not be regenerated in V. vinifera cv. 'Cabernet Sauvignon', likely due to its essential role in suppressing programmed cell death. This was circumvented by targeting VaBI-1 at the callus stage. Furthermore, our data suggest that the VaATG6-VaBI-1 interaction fine-tunes the autophagic activity rather than acting as a binary switch. In double-transgenic calli (OE-VaATG6/OE-VaBI-1), ROS levels were lower than in OE-VaATG6 alone, indicating that a moderate VaBI-1 activity restrains excessive autophagy-associated cell death (Fig. 10). Such balanced regulation is crucial in perennials, where long-term survival requires tight control of degradation pathways. Similar patterns have been observed in woody species, where ATG genes exhibit seasonal oscillation (Wang et al. 2016, 2017). Collectively, our findings establish the VaATG6-VaBI-1 module as a critical integrator of autophagy, ROS homeostasis, and ER signaling in grapevine cold stress responses. Upon cold exposure, VaATG6 relocalizes to ER, where it interacts with VaBI-1 to modulate autophagic flux, reduce ROS accumulation, and maintain cellular homeostasis (Fig. 11). While VaATG6 overexpression presents a promising strategy for enhancing cold tolerance through elevated autophagy and reduced oxidative damage, VaBI-1 acts as a fine-tuning factor that optimizes the trade-off between stress adaptation and energy expenditure. These insights offer valuable targets for molecular breeding, particularly in perennial crops such as grapevine, apple, and citrus. Overexpression of VaATG6 or CRISPR-mediated attenuation of VaBI-1 activity could be leveraged in transgenic or genome-editing approaches. A working model illustrating the VaATG6-VaBI-1 regulatory module integrating ER-associated autophagy and ROS homeostasis under cold stress in grapevine. This diagram depicts how cold stress activates a coordinated ER-associated autophagy pathway governed by VaATG6 and VaBI-1, thereby enhancing cold tolerance in grapevine. Under normal conditions (left panel), VaATG6 is expressed at basal levels and primarily localizes to the plasma membrane, nucleus, and endoplasmic reticulum (ER). VaBI-1 remains constitutively localized to the ER, where it exhibits a weak interaction with VaATG6. This basal interaction mildly counteracts the autophagy-suppressing effect of VaBI-1, thereby sustaining a low level of autophagic flux, preserving ROS homeostasis, and ensuring normal cellular growth. Upon cold stress exposure (right panel), VaATG6 transcription is rapidly upregulated, and the encoded protein redistributes dynamically from the nucleus to the ER and autophagosomes. Concurrently, VaBI-1 expression is induced, and the interaction between VaATG6 and VaBI-1 is substantially strengthened. This enhanced interaction not only facilitates autophagosome biogenesis at the ER, but also appears to relieve the inhibitory effect of VaBI-1 on autophagy, leading to increased autophagic activity. In parallel, this process mitigates oxidative stress by attenuating ROS accumulation and activating antioxidant defenses. Furthermore, VaATG6 promotes the transcription of key autophagy-related genes, such as VvATG1, VvATG8a, VvATG18f, and VvATG18h, amplifying the autophagic responses. This tightly regulated mechanism, driven by the VaATG6-VaBI-1 complex, enhances ROS detoxification via upregulation of antioxidant enzymes, restores redox balance, and ultimately confers enhanced cold stress resilience in grapevine. Arrows indicate activation, blunt-ended solid lines represent experimentally validated repression, while blunt-ended dashed lines denote putative inhibitory effects that remain to be confirmed. This model underscores the dual regulatory role of VaBI-1 in environmental stress and establishes VaATG6 as a pivotal coordinator that integrates ER-associated autophagy with ROS homeostasis under cold stress

Despite these advances, key questions remain. The upstream regulatory network that activates VaATG6 and VaBI-1 under cold stress is still poorly defined. Identifying transcriptional or post-translational regulators may reveal key components of the early signaling cascade. Additionally, whether VaBI-1 functions as a scaffold protein or directly modulates autophagy via ER-specific signaling requires further elucidation. By leveraging autophagy as a dynamic and adjustable stress adaptation mechanism, our study lays the foundation for breeding climate-resilient perennial crops in an era of increasing environmental uncertainty.

Materials and methods

Plant materials, growth conditions, and cold stress treatments

Immature zygotic embryos from Vitis vinifera cv. ‘Cabernet Sauvignon’ (a cold-sensitive cultivar) were harvested 120 days after flowering from healthy, pest-free berries collected at Chateau Mihope, Ningxia, China (38◦14′25′′N, 106◦01′43′′E) on August 23, 2021, between 8:00–10:00 a.m. Grapes were transported to the laboratory in ice-packed bags, rinsed under running water to remove pulp, and air-dried in a ventilated area for 3–4 days. The dried material was sealed in tubes with color-changing silica gel and stored at room temperature to prevent fungal growth.

Cold-tolerant V. amurensis Rupr. 'Zuoshan-1' was cultivated in a greenhouse at Ningxia University, Yinchuan, Ningxia, China (38°30′13.44″N; 106°08′08.04′′E). For cold treatment, potted plants with uniform growth (substrate: vegetative soil: perlite: vermiculite = 3:1:1) were exposed to 2 °C. Leaves were collected at 0, 3, 6, 12, 24, and 48 h, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C.

ATG genes family, promoter sequence, and phylogenetic analysis

Promoter sequences (1,500 bp upstream of ATG6) were analyzed using PlantCARE (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/) and visualized in Excel and Origin2018 (OriginLab, USA). Protein sequences were aligned using CLUSTALW (https://www.genome.jp/tools-bin/clustalw), and phylogenetic trees were constructed with PhyloSuite using MAFFT and IQ-TREE. Conserved domains were identified via the SMART database (http://smart.embl-heidelberg.de/).

Total RNA isolation and gene expression analysis

Total RNA was extracted from leaves using the E.Z.N.A. Plant RNA Kit (Omega Biotech, GA, USA). First-strand cDNA synthesis was synthesized with the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (TaKaRa, Kyoto, Japan). qRT-PCR was conducted using SYBR Green on a qTOWER2.2 thermal cycler (Analytik Jena AG, Thuringia, Germany). VvActin1 (GenBank accession no. XM_002282480) was used as the internal control. Relative expression levels were calculated via the 2−ΔΔCt method (Livak and Schmittgen 2001). Three biological replicates were analyzed per sample. Statistical significance was determined by one-way ANOVA followed by LSD post hoc tests (p < 0.05 or p < 0.01). Primer sequences are listed in Table S1.

Promoter cloning and GUS staining

Genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted from V. amurensis Rupr. ‘Zuoshan-1′ and V. vinifera cv. ‘Cabernet Sauvignon’ using the Plant Genome DNA Extraction Kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China). The 1.5 kb promoter region upstream of ATG6 (NCBI accession NC_081819.1) was cloned into the pCAMBIA2301 vector, replacing the CaMV 35S promoter. The constructs were introduced into grape calli via Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain EHA105 (Wu et al. 2024). Transgenic calli were treated at 2 °C for 3 h in the dark prior to GUS staining.

Subcellular localization assays

Full-length VaATG6 and VaBI-1 cDNAs were cloned into the pCAMBIA2300-GFP. Constructs were co-transformed into tobacco (Nicotiana benthamiana) leaves with markers for plasma membrane (RK-PM), nucleus (RK-H2B), endoplasmic reticulum (RK-ER), or autophagosomes (ATG8f-mCherry). Confocal images were captured with a Leica microscope. Excitation/emission wavelengths were 488/507 nm for GFP and 588/610 nm for mCherry.

Generation of transgenic plants

A CRISPR/Cas9 system targeting VaATG6 (target: AGATGGGTCTGCCAGAACTG) was constructed using CRISPR-P (Park et al. 2015). Overexpression and knockout vectors (VaATG6-GFP, VaBI-1-GFP, and VaATG6-Cas9) were introduced into A. tumefaciens strain EHA105 and used for transformation of V. vinifera seeds (Zhang et al. 2023). Selection was performed on B5 medium with kanamycin or hygromycin. The recombinant plasmids (OE-VaATG6/OE-VaBI-1 or OE-VaATG6/VaBI-1-Cas9) were transformed into 'Cabernet Sauvignon' callus tissue following the method described (Wu et al. 2024). Following OE-VaATG6 integration, the transgenic calli were transformed with either VaBI-1-GFP or VaBI-1-Cas9. Selection was carried out on B5 medium (3.21 g·L−1 B5 Basal medium (G318, PhytoTech Labs Inc., Lenexa KS, USA), 30 g·L−1 sucrose, 7 g·L−1 agar (A8190, Solarbio, China) containing 30 mg·L−1 kanamycin or 5 mg·L−1 hygromycin. The calli were incubated in the dark for 30 days, followed by regular subculturing every two weeks to maintain stable selection.

Cold tolerance assessment

Eight-week-old transgenic and UT grapevines were pre-acclimated at 10 °C for 2 days, then exposed to − 2 °C for 22 h. Three leaves per plant were sampled before and after treatment for physiological analyses. Calli were first cultured at 23 °C for 5 days, then subjected to 10 °C in the dark for 20 days. Fresh weight was recorded post-treatment.

Physiological measurements and histochemical staining

H2O2 and O2·− were visualized with DAB and NBT staining, respectively. Quantification of the staining was performed using the FIJI software (Schindelin et al. 2012). MDA, soluble sugars, H2O2, O2·−, SOD, and CAT were quantified using commercial kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China). Fv/Fm was measured using a PlantView230F chlorophyll fluorometer. REC and Fv/Fm were calculated from triplicate measurements per genotype.

Transmission electron microscopy and MDC staining

Leaves were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde, sectioned, and imaged with a Hitachi transmission electron microscope. MDC staining of calli was performed with 100 μM MDC for 30 min, washed, and visualized via confocal microscopy (excitation: 405 nm; emission: 460–520 nm).

Y2H, BiFC, and Split-LUC Assays

Yeast two-hybrid assays used pBT3-N (bait: VaATG6) and pPR3-N (prey: VaBI-1) in yeast strain NMY51. BiFC was performed using pSPYNE-35S (nYFP) and pSPYCE-35S (cYFP). Transient expression in tobacco was analyzed by confocal microscopy 48 h post-infiltration. For split-LUC, VaATG6 and VaBI-1 were cloned into pCAMBIA1300-nLUC and pCAMBIA1300-cLUC, respectively. LUC/REN ratios were calculated from three pooled replicates using a chemiluminescence detector.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using SPSS Statistics 27. One-way ANOVA followed by LSD was used for multiple comparisons. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05 and highly significant at p < 0.01. Data are presented as mean ± SE.
