Plastic Bronchitis in a Child With Fontan Physiology After COVID-19 Infection
Authors: Helena Rae Woodhouse, Olubunmi Ogunsanya, Aaron St-Laurent, Michael Grattan, Marisha McClean
Published: 2023

Plastic bronchitis (PB) is a rare disease characterized by the expectoration of amorphous, rubbery, cohesive, and branched bronchial casts that obstruct the airway. 1 Bronchial casts are of 2 types: type 1 (inflammatory) and type 2 (acellular). 2 Most paediatric PB cases occur after the correction of congenital heart lesions and present with acellular casts. 3 PB has also been reported in paediatric patients with viral infections presenting with inflammatory casts. 3 To our knowledge, there are no reported paediatric cases associated with COVID-19 infections. Our case report describes a child with Fontan palliation who developed inflammatory PB after COVID-19 infection.

Plastic bronchitis (PB) is a rare disease characterized by the expectoration of amorphous, rubbery, cohesive, and branched bronchial casts that can partially or totally obstruct the airway.1 PB occurs at any age but is more frequent in the paediatric population.1,2 It manifests clinically with cough, wheezing, dyspnoea, hypoxaemia, chest pain, and occasionally fever. Obstruction of proximal airways may lead to respiratory failure.1

Chest radiograph and computed tomography scan typically show partial atelectasis of the segments involved with compensatory hyperinflation of the unaffected segments. Diagnosis is confirmed by rigid and/or flexible bronchoscopy or by repeated observation of the expectorated casts.1

Seear et al.2 separated bronchial casts into 2 well-defined groups based on cast histology: type 1 (inflammatory), consisting of casts composed mainly of fibrin with a dense eosinophilic inflammatory infiltrate, and type 2 (acellular), consisting of casts composed mainly of mucin with little or no cellular infiltrate. Although viral illnesses are a well-known cause of inflammatory casts, casts associated with Fontan physiology are generally of acellular type.3 Herein we present an unusual case of inflammatory cast PB in a child with Fontan physiology after COVID-19 infection.

Case Report

A 3-year-old Syrian-born boy immigrated to Canada with known but unoperated complex congenital cardiac disease: left atrial isomerism, univentricular connection of right ventricular type with absent left atrioventricular valve, double outlet right ventricle with severe pulmonary stenosis, right aortic arch with mirror image branching, interrupted inferior vena cava with azygous continuation, and bilateral superior vena cava with no bridging innominate vein. His presentation was also notable for abdominal heterotaxy. He underwent multistage Fontan palliation, initially with a bilateral bidirectional cavopulmonary connection, atrial septectomy, atrioventricular valve repair, and right pulmonary artery plasty at age 3.5 years. At age 4.5 years, he underwent an extracardiac fenestrated Fontan procedure (22 mm conduit and 4 mm fenestration). His discharge echo showed an unobstructed Fontan conduit with small fenestration shunting right to left with a mean gradient of 6 mm Hg. His postoperative course was complicated by Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus mediastinitis, chylothorax responding to dietary therapy, and atrial flutter that was successfully treated with sotalol.

During the course of his follow-up, he was noted to have increased fenestration gradients with a mean gradient of 11-12 mm Hg. At 8 months after Fontan, he presented with respiratory distress and hypoxaemia requiring supplemental oxygen, as well as cough, subjective fevers, emesis, and diarrhoea. He was found to be COVID positive and was treated with remdesivir and dexamethasone. He also received a 10-day course of cephalexin. He was discharged 5 days after admission.

He represented twice with increased work of breathing, cough, and oxygen requirements. His echocardiographic findings were stable with unobstructed Fontan and Glenn connections and a mean fenestration gradient of 11-12 mm Hg. He remained in a stable atrial rhythm. His initial presentation was thought to be related to his previous COVID-19 diagnosis. However, during his second readmission, he expectorated a bronchial cast (Fig. 1), and a diagnosis of PB was made. The cast was sent for pathology, which demonstrated proteinaceous/mucoid material with admixed inflammatory cells consisting primarily of lymphocytes and eosinophils, as well as variable organization with areas of fibrin admixed with areas of more fibrous tissue, in keeping with a type 1 inflammatory cast. Qualitative analysis of cast breakdown showed significant dissolution with tissue plasminogen activator and dornase alfa. Ultimately, he was treated with inhaled dornase alfa, inhaled budesonide, and sildenafil. The patient was discharged with a plan to have cardiac catheterization for the assessment of pulmonary hypertension and haemodynamics. Combined magnetic resonance imaging and cardiac catheterization showed an indexed pulmonary vascular resistance of 2.5 WU m2 with a Fontan pressure of 13 mm Hg. There was a small Fontan fenestration with a low indexed cardiac output. These findings combined with his previous history of elevated fenestration gradients and chylothorax put him at increased risk for poor outcomes after Fontan. The fenestration was stented up to 4 mm with increased flow seen across the fenestration into the systemic circulation, with a resultant slight decrease in systemic saturations from 92% to 90% but increased cardiac output. After catheterization and on the last follow-up, the patient was maintained on budesonide, sildenafil, and inhaled dornase alfa, and the family was provided with a suction machine to assist with mucus removal. Notably, the patient was also started on systemic steroids for a subsequent diagnosis of protein-losing enteropathy (PLE) 3 months later. A repeat cardiac catheterization resulted in occlusion of multiple aortopulmonary collaterals. Since then, he has had remission of his PB and PLE with no further hospitalizations and has been weaned off of oral steroids.

Discussion

The etiology of PB is poorly described; however, the most detailed cases of PB in the paediatric population are from surgical correction of complex congenital heart diseases with an incidence of 4% after Fontan palliation.3 The pathogenesis of PB after Fontan is thought to related to suboptimal adaptation to cavopulmonary circulation. This results in increased pulmonary artery and central venous pressures, leading to insufficient lymph drainage and accumulation of casts. The accumulation of casts and consequent airway obstruction acutely increases the pulmonary artery and central venous pressure and further propagates cast formation.1,3 After cardiac surgery, PB is associated with type 2 (acellular) casts. The described patient did have risk factors for PB based on his late initial stage of palliation, Fontan physiology, and subsequent associated complications (chylothorax and PLE). However, our case is remarkable in that our patient is presenting with type 1 inflammatory casts after COVID-19 infection, which suggests that the infection itself likely had a role in the genesis of his bronchial casts.

PB cases have also been reported in paediatric patients with bacterial and viral infections notably mycoplasma pneumonia, adenovirus serotype 7, and influenza virus causing type 1 casts.3 The pathogenesis of inflammatory casts is believed to be caused by a variety of inflammatory cell infiltrates and inflammatory mediators resulting in tracheal mucosa congestion, oedema, and necrosis and blockage of the lumen4 and decreased mucociliary clearance.3 In the literature, the nature of PB in the Fontan patient has been found to be multifactorial. In a study performed by Pałyga-Bysiecka et al.5 on a subgroup of patients with PB after Fontan, 50% were noted to have respiratory infections with positive sputum cultures in 40% of them, and 30% were also noted to have associated immune dysfunctions. In addition, although fibrin was the core component of the post-Fontan PB casts, some inflammatory cells were found in the casts.5 To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of PB associated with COVID-19 infection in a paediatric patient with Fontan physiology.

COVID-19 infection has been known to play a role in increasing pulmonary pressures. SARS-CoV-2 produces its effects through its spike protein interaction with the angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) receptors that are highly expressed in alveolar lung cells and the vascular endothelium in patients who have COVID-19 infection. The virus-receptor interaction results in a deficiency of ACE2 at the cell membrane due to the virus’s internalization along with the receptor by endocytosis, leading to deficiency/downregulation of ACE2, an enzyme that physiologically counters the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, degrades angiotensin II, and attenuates its effects on endothelial damage, vasoconstriction, and fibrosis.6 In its absence, these processes occur, which elevates pulmonary pressures.

There is a dearth of literature regarding the impact of COVID-19 in patients with Fontan palliation. In a literature review of case reports of COVID-19 in patients after Fontan palliation by Wen et al.,7 a total of 8 articles involving 14 patients (paediatric and adult) were reviewed and showed that the presenting symptoms were diverse, with cough and fever being the most common. The most common complications were thrombotic in nature, and they did not note any complications specific to Fontan circulation. Children were noted to have a lower risk of infection and a milder course than adults.7 Our index case had underlying risk factors for PB with a high fenestration gradient (suggestive of elevated pulmonary artery and central venous/Fontan pressures) and comorbid complications of chylothorax and ultimately PLE. However, we propose that his COVID-19 infection led to increased pulmonary inflammation that likely further worsened his pulmonary artery and central venous pressures, and ultimately led to his PB. Histologically, he had inflammatory casts, thought to be primarily resulting from his COVID-19, which is not commonly noted in Fontan physiology and, to our knowledge, is yet to be described in paediatric Fontan literature.

Novel Teaching Points

•COVID-19 is a possible precipitating cause of inflammatory/type 1 cast-related PB.
