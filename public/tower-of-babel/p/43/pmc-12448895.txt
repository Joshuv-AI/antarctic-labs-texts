Comprehensive analysis of desmosomal protein remodelling identifies desmocollin-2 as potential biomarker for arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy
Authors: Jie Ren, Zhenliang Hu, Lingmin Wu, Deniz Akdis, Weiquan Ye, Ardan M Saguner, Mingming Su, Hongbin Dong, Zhongli Chen, Dan Hu, Shengshou Hu, Firat Duru, Liang Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsArrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy (ACM) is a genetically-determined disease characterized by malignant arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death, particularly in young individuals. Mutations in desmosomal genes are a major cause of ACM, but the role of desmocollin-2 (DSC2) remains understudied.Methods and resultsWe conducted a multi-cohort study with 52 ACM patients, 29 with dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM), 17 with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM), and 45 controls. Immunofluorescence, immunohistochemical staining, and western blot were used to assess DSC2 expression. DSC2 expression was significantly reduced in ACM myocardium compared to controls and other cardiomyopathies (P < 0.0001), consistent across ACM subtypes and validated in an independent cohort. DSC2 down-regulation correlated with clinical characteristics such as age at onset of arrhythmia and heart transplantation. DSC2 reduction in the right ventricle effectively discriminated ACM patients from controls (ROC AUC = 0.8017, P < 0.0001) and distinguished ACM from other cardiac diseases. Immunohistochemical staining in the Swiss cohort confirmed that 11 out of 12 (91.7%) ACM cases exhibited significantly reduced DSC2 signal intensity compared to normal donors. RNA-Seq analysis revealed significant down-regulation of DSC2 genes in ACM tissues.ConclusionDSC2 remodelling is a hallmark of ACM, harbouring significant diagnostic implications. DSC2 reduction is a robust biomarker for ACM, showing high sensitivity and specificity across different subtypes. Future research should elucidate the mechanisms underlying DSC2 down-regulation.Keywords: Arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy, Desmosome, Desmocollin-2, Biomarker

Introduction

Arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy (ACM) is a genetically-determined disease that is characterized by a high risk of malignant ventricular arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death (SCD), particularly among young individuals and athletes.1,2 With a prevalence of 0.02–0.1% in the general population, ACM is one of the leading causes of SCD in individuals under the age of 35 years.3,4 The disease primarily affects the right ventricle (RV), leading to progressive dysfunction. Left ventricular (LV) involvement in ACM manifests heterogeneously across subtypes, ranging from early-onset LV dysfunction to late-stage LV disease.5–8

Pathogenic mutations in genes encoding desmosomal proteins are the primary genetic cause of ACM, accounting for ∼50% of cases. These genes include plakophilin-2 (PKP2), desmoglein-2 (DSG2), desmocollin-2 (DSC2), desmoplakin (DSP), and plakoglobin (JUP). Additionally, mutations in non-desmosomal genes, such as TMEM43, PLN, LMNA, DES, and CDH2 have also been implicated in ACM.4,9,10 Desmosomes are specialized cell–cell contact structures within the intercalated disc (ID) that allow the heart to withstand mechanical strain during contractile cycles. The desmosomal cadherins link neighbouring cells via their extracellular cadherin domains, ensuring the structural integrity of the myocardium.11

Functional studies have primarily focused on the roles of DSP, PKP2, and JUP in ACM pathogenesis.12–15 Previous research has demonstrated significant remodelling of desmosomal proteins in ACM myocardial tissue, particularly involving JUP.15 For instance, studies have shown that mutations in JUP can lead to reduced expression and mislocalization of connexin 43 (Cx43) at intercellular junctions, potentially contributing to arrhythmogenic substrates and increasing the risk of SCD.16 Similarly, immunohistochemical analyses have identified decreased expression of JUP and Cx43 in ACM patients, which can assist in clinical diagnosis.17,18

The expression and remodelling of DSC2 in ACM myocardial tissue have not been well studied. Existing studies on DSC2 are limited by small sample sizes and lack comprehensive, multifaceted analyses.19 Therefore, there is a need for large-scale studies employing multiple research techniques to elucidate the role of DSC2 in ACM.

In this study, we aim to investigate the expression and remodelling of DSC2 in ACM myocardial tissue using a variety of methodologies. We hypothesize that DSC2 remodelling may serve as a valuable marker for ACM. Our research aims to provide insights into the diagnostic implications of DSC2 remodelling in ACM

Methods

Study design and sample collection

We enrolled two cohorts of patients from China and Switzerland, respectively. The China Fuwai ACM cohort consisted of 52 formalin-fixed myocardial and frozen tissue samples obtained from explanted hearts.8 This cohort was designated as the discovery cohort to screen for desmosomal protein remodelling in ACM. Additionally, 45 normal donors and 46 non-ACM explanted hearts with dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM, n = 29) and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM, n = 17) were included as controls to explore desmosomal protein remodelling in ACM. The validation cohort from the Zurich ACM Program20 included 12 ACM patients with formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded myocardial samples obtained from SCD or heart transplantation cases (The detailed description of myocardial tissue sampling is stated in the Supplementary Methods). To address the concern regarding the specificity of DSC2 as a biomarker for ACM, we performed additional immunohistochemical (IHC) staining for DSC2 in myocardial tissues from patients with giant cell myocarditis (GCM, n = 8), lymphocytic myocarditis (LM, n = 7), and cardiac sarcoidosis (SC, n = 5). Histopathological examinations of explanted hearts and biopsies were performed according to international standards.21

Clinical data and physical examination results were collected at the time of tissue acquisition. All patients from both the Fuwai and Zurich ACM cohorts were clinically diagnosed with ACM according to the 2010 Revised Task Force Criteria and confirmed with pathological criteria.22 Myocarditis was excluded in these patients (as detailed in Supplementary Methods). For the Fuwai ACM cohort, we further classified ACM patients into four subtypes with distinct genotypes based on our previously reported Fuwai Classification.8 This classification allowed us to reveal common and distinct desmosomal protein remodelling patterns in different ACM subtypes.

Immunofluorescence

The distribution and signal intensity for intercalated proteins were examined using immunofluorescence on formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded myocardial slices. Sections were heated in EDTA (pH 8.0, ZLI-9066) to enhance specific immunostaining, followed by permeabilization and blocking with goat serum for 1 h at room temperature. Sections were then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C.23 The antibodies used in this study included: PKP2 (1:200, ab151402, abcam, UK), DSG2 (1:500, ab150372, abcam, UK), DSC2 (1:500, ab595967, abcam, UK), JUP (1:300, ab184919, abcam, UK), Cx43 (1:500, ab11370, abcam, UK), N-cadherin (1:500, ab18203, abcam, UK), and α-sarcomeric actinin (1:500, ab9465, abcam, UK). Subsequently, fluorescence-conjugated anti-rabbit or anti-mouse IgG secondary antibodies were incubated for 1 h at room temperature. Immunofluorescence images were captured using confocal microscopy (LSM780, Zeiss, Germany) under consistent screening criteria. Additionally, we tested different concentrations of the DSC2 antibody (1:200, 1:500, and 1:1000) using the same protocol to evaluate the coherence of immunofluorescence results.

Immunohistochemical staining

The signal intensity of DSC2 in validation samples was assessed using immunohistochemical staining on formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded myocardial slices. Sections were heated in EDTA (pH 8.0, ZLI-9066) to enhance specific immunostaining, followed by incubation with a peroxidase blocker for 20 min. Tissue permeabilization was performed using 0.1% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich, T9284) diluted in the blocking solution (goat serum) for 60 min at room temperature. Sections were incubated with primary antibodies, including DSC2 (1:500, ab595967, abcam, UK) and N-cadherin (1:500, ab18203, abcam, UK), overnight at 4°C. Reaction booster and secondary antibodies were incubated for 20 min each. Finally, DAB was used to develop the positive areas. High-resolution immunohistochemical images were captured using an automatic digital scanner (Axio Scan.Z1, ZEISS, Germany).

Western blot

Total protein was extracted from 30 mg of frozen tissue and denatured in loading buffer (NP0007, Invitrogen, USA) and reducing buffer (NP0009, Invitrogen, USA). A total of 20 µg of protein was separated by Bis-Tris PAGE electrophoresis and transferred to PVDF membranes using the iBlot2 dry blotting system (Invitrogen, USA). Membranes were blocked in blocking buffer (P0250, Beyotime, China) at room temperature for 1 h and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. Primary antibodies used included: PKP2 (1:300, ab151402, abcam, UK), DSG2 (1:1000, ab150372, abcam, UK), DSC2 (1:1000, ab95967, abcam, UK), JUP (1:300, ab184919, abcam, UK), DSP (1:1000, ab109445, abcam, UK), N-cadherin (1:1000, ab18203, abcam, UK), Cx43 (1:1000, ab11370, abcam, UK), and GAPDH (1:10 000, KC5G5, Aksomics, China). After washing, membranes were incubated with anti-IgG secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Enhanced chemiluminescence (34095, Thermo, USA) was used for blot imaging, and images were captured using a digital imaging system (FluorChem M system, ProteinSimple, USA). Protein expression levels were semi-quantitatively assessed by greyscale analysis using ImageJ (NIH, USA).

DSP protein quantification in myocardial tissue

The myocardial tissue samples were analysed for DSP protein expression using the human DSP enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (catalogue no.: ELK3021, ELK Biotechnology) following a standardized sandwich ELISA protocol. All tests were performed according to the manufacturers’ protocols.

Genetic testing

Genetic testing and pathogenic gene filtering were performed as previously reported.8,24 We screened 15 known ACM-related genes, including five desmosomal genes (PKP2, DSG2, DSC2, DSP, JUP), PLN, DES, LMNA, TMEM43, CTNNA3, TGFB3, SCN5A, RYR2, CDH2, and truncated mutations in TTN.25

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), and categorical variables as frequency (percentages). Comparisons between two groups were performed using Student’s t-test or Fisher’s exact test, while ANOVA was used for comparisons involving three or more groups. Paired Student’s t-test was used to compare protein expression between matched left ventricular (LV) and right ventricular (RV) samples. Relationships between variables were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation test. LASSO (least absolute shrinkage and selection operator) regularization analysis was performed using the glmnet package in R to achieve shrinkage and variable selection simultaneously.26 Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and area under the ROC curve (AUC) analyses were used to evaluate the predictive value of desmosomal proteins in diagnosing ACM. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics, version 23.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA), and R, version 4.0.0. Statistical charts were plotted using GraphPad Prism 7 (GraphPad Software Inc., CA, USA) and ggplot2 (version 3.3.3). The SHapley Additive exPlanation (SHAP) method was applied using the shap package to analyse feature importance for ACM prediction. All statistical tests were two-tailed, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Ethics statement

This study adhered to the ethical standards of the World Health Organization’s Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fuwai Hospital. Written informed consent was obtained from all heart transplant patients and donors or their families for the research use of the hearts. The myocardial samples from the Swiss cohort were collected and analysed under strict adherence to Swiss ethical regulations. The study protocol for the Zurich ACM Program was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Canton of Zurich (PB_2016-02109), and all patients provided written informed consent.

Results

Baseline characteristics of ACM patients

Detailed diagnostic criteria for ACM patients are provided in Supplementary material online, Table S1. ACM patients exhibited the largest RV size (RVEDD: ACM vs. DCM vs. HCM, 40.60 ± 1.78 mm vs. 24.66 ± 0.97 mm vs. 23.36 ± 2.63 mm, P < 0.0001). DCM patients had the most severe LV dilation with the lowest LVEF (LVEDD: ACM vs. DCM vs. HCM, 55.04 ± 1.62 mm vs. 76.38 ± 2.06 mm vs. 57.82 ± 2.37 mm, P < 0.0001; LVEF: ACM vs. DCM vs. HCM, 36.62 ± 2.01% vs. 21.83 ± 0.77% vs. 31.74 ± 2.87%, P < 0.0001). HCM patients had the highest IVS thickness and largest left atrial size (IVS: ACM vs. DCM vs. HCM, 8.66 ± 0.21 mm vs. 8.66 ± 0.46 mm vs. 11.53 ± 0.76 mm, P < 0.0001; LAID: ACM vs. DCM vs. HCM, 36.65 ± 1.22 mm vs. 49.21 ± 1.78 mm vs. 50.82 ± 2.26 mm, P < 0.0001).

ACM patients experienced arrhythmia symptoms at an earlier age (Age at onset of arrhythmia: ACM vs. DCM vs. HCM, 30.56 ± 2.15 years vs. 41.64 ± 3.01 years vs. 39.43 ± 3.64 years, P = 0.0073) and had a higher proportion of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE: ACM vs. DCM vs. HCM, 20/52 (38.46%) vs. 4/29 (13.79%) vs. 5/17 (29.41%), P = 0.0659). ACM patients also underwent heart transplantation at a younger age (Age of transplant: ACM vs. DCM vs. HCM, 37.57 ± 1.93 years vs. 43.21 ± 2.73 years vs. 47.23 ± 3.49 years, P = 0.027). Desmosomal proteins remodelling in ACM

Western blot analysis revealed a significant decrease in DSC2 levels in ACM myocardium compared to DCM, HCM, and healthy myocardium (Figure 1A and B, P < 0.0001). JUP expression was also significantly lower in ACM samples, although with substantial variation (Figure 1A and C). Immunofluorescence analysis showed reduction of signal intensity and disorganized distribution of DSC2 in ACM myocardial tissues (Figure 1D). Other desmosomal proteins (DSG2, PKP2, DSP) and the Cx43 did not show significant expression differences among the groups (see Supplementary material online, Figure S1). These results indicate that DSC2 and JUP are robustly decreased in ACM myocardium, suggesting that their remodelling may be a specific feature of ACM.

Expression levels of DSC2/JUP and clinical characteristics in ACM patients

In 52 ACM patients, DSC2 expression levels were significantly correlated with the expression levels of PKP2 (ρ = 0.293, P = 0.035), DSG2 (ρ = 0.512, P < 0.001), and Cx43 (ρ = 0.409, P = 0.003) (Table 2). DSC2 expression was associated with age at onset of heart disease (ρ = 0.418, P < 0.001) and arrhythmia (ρ = 0.381, P = 0.022) and heart transplantation (ρ = 0.331, P = 0.017). In the adjusted model (multivariable regression analysis), DSC2 expression was independently associated with clinical progression (age at heart transplantation, P = 0.033) and structural remodelling (LVEDD, P = 0.001; RVEDD, P = 0.027). While age at onset of heart disease (P = 0.062), LAID (P = 0.073) and number of precordial QRS fragmentation (P = 0.056) showed borderline significance (see Supplementary material online, Table S2). JUP expression was correlated with age at onset of arrhythmia (ρ = 0.434, P = 0.008) and heart transplantation (ρ = 0.313, P = 0.024). However, neither DSC2 nor JUP expression levels were significantly correlated with heart rate, left atrial size, left ventricular function, or right ventricular function. A majority of ACM patients (30/52) were genotype-positive, including mutations in PKP2 (n = 3), DSG2 (n = 8), DSP (n = 2), DSC2 (n = 2), and multiple pathogenic variants (n = 5) (Figure 2A). DSC2 and JUP protein levels were significantly associated with underlying genetic mutations (Figure 2B, Supplementary material online, Figure S2A and B). However, DSC2 and JUP expression levels were not significantly correlated with gender, history of arrhythmia symptoms or treatments, or NYHA functional class (Figure 2C–H, Supplementary material online, Figure S2C–H). These findings suggest that DSC2 and JUP expression levels are closely related to other desmosomal proteins and certain clinical characteristics but are not significantly influenced by gender, arrhythmia history, or NYHA classification in ACM patients.

Desmosomal proteins in ACM subtypes and biventricular distribution

Our previous research has demonstrated that ACM patients can be classified into distinct subtypes based on their pathological, genetic, and clinical characteristics (see Supplementary material online, Table S1 for detailed genetic and cluster profiles).8 DSC2 expression was significantly decreased in Cluster 1 compared to normal donors (P < 0.0001) and in Clusters 2 and 3 (P = 0.0227 for both) (Figure 3A and B). JUP expression was decreased in Cluster 1 (P = 0.0247) but not in Clusters 2 or 3 (Figure 3A and C). Cluster 4 ACM patients exhibited high heterogeneity in DSC2 and JUP expression, with no significant differences compared to normal controls (Figure 3A–C). Other desmosomal proteins (PKP2, DSG2) did not show significant expression differences across different ACM subtypes (Figure 3D and E). No significant differences in total Cx43 protein levels were observed among the groups (see Supplementary material online, Figure S3A). Immunofluorescence analysis exhibited ectopic expression in non-intercalated disc areas (see Supplementary material online, Figure S3B). In LV myocardial samples, significant reductions in DSC2, JUP, and PKP2 expression levels were observed in ACM patients compared to controls (Figure 4A–D). No significant differences were observed in the expression levels of DSC2 and JUP between the LV and RV (Figure 4E and F). DSC2 levels were comparable between different cardiac regions (as depicted in Supplementary material online, Figure S4A), supporting a global down-regulation of DSC2 in ACM. Furthermore, specific comparisons between histologically abnormal and non-affected areas revealed similar DSC2 expression patterns (see Supplementary material online, Figure S4B). In all ACM subtypes, DSC2 expression was significantly reduced, while JUP also showed a similar trend (Figure 4G and H). These results highlight the consistent remodelling of desmosomal proteins in both ventricles across different ACM subtypes.

DSC2 remodelling as a diagnostic biomarker for ACM

We investigated the potential of DSC2 reduction as a diagnostic biomarker for ACM. Reduced DSC2 expression in the RV effectively discriminated ACM patients from controls, with a ROC AUC of 0.8017 (P < 0.0001) (Figure 5A). This diagnostic capability was consistent across different ACM subtypes, including Clusters 1, 2, and 3 (Figure 5B–D). JUP expression levels provided high diagnostic accuracy for the classical subtype (Cluster 1) but had lower efficacy for other subtypes (see Supplementary material online, Figure S5). To further evaluate the diagnostic utility of DSC2 reduction in distinguishing ACM from other cardiomyopathies (HCM and DCM), we employed the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) method to rank the contribution of various predictors to the diagnostic model (Figure 5E). LASSO regression analysis identified key predictors for differentiating ACM from HCM and DCM (Figure 5F, Supplementary material online, Figure S5). Considering that antibody concentration may influence immunostaining signals,27 we tested the immunofluorescence signal for DSC2 at various antibody concentrations (1:200 to 1:1000). Reduced DSC2 signals were consistently observed across this concentration range, validating the analytical robustness of our immunostaining assay. This supports the reliability of antibody-based DSC2 detection (see Supplementary material online, Figure S6).

We further validated our findings in an independent cohort from the Zurich ACM Program, which included 12 definite ACM patients and five controls.28 Immunohistochemical staining revealed that 11 out of 12 (91.7%) ACM cases exhibited reduced or absent DSC2 signal intensity at the intercalated disc compared to normal donors (Figure 6). In contrast, non-desmosomal adhesion molecule N-cadherin showed no significant differences between ACM and control samples (see Supplementary material online, Figure S7). While GCM, LM, and SC tissues showed reductions in DSC2 signal intensity compared to healthy controls, the extent of DSC2 down-regulation was significantly less pronounced than in ACM tissues (Figure 7A, Supplementary material online, Figure S8). Also, DSC2 localization in inflammatory cardiomyopathies (GCM, LM, and SC) remained predominantly restricted to the intercalated disc. While direct tissue validation in Brugada syndrome remains elusive, transcriptomic data from iPSC-CMs (GSE221945) revealed no DSC2 dysregulation—contrasting with its reduction in ACM tissues (see Supplementary material online, Figure S9). These results confirmed that reduction of signal intensity of DSC2 is a consistent feature of ACM, highlighting its potential as a diagnostic biomarker.

DSC2 gene and protein expression similarity and functional characteristics

RNA-Seq analysis of 20 normal controls and 51 ACM RV myocardial samples revealed significant down-regulation of DSC2 and JUP genes in ACM tissues compared to controls, with no significant changes in other desmosomal genes (Figure 7B, Supplementary material online, Figure S10A–D). This highlights the specific role of DSC2 and JUP in ACM pathogenesis.

Among 30 ACM patients who underwent both RNA-Seq and western blot analyses, a significant correlation was observed between DSC2 gene expression and protein levels (P = 0.0054), indicating consistency between mRNA and protein expression. In contrast, JUP showed a trend towards similar expression patterns, but statistical significance was not reached (Figure 7C, Supplementary material online, Figure S10E). Further analysis revealed that reduced expression of desmosomal genes, including DSC2, was associated with up-regulation of genes in critical signalling pathways such as WNT, NOTCH, PPAR-γ, NF-κB, HIPPO, and TGF-β (Figure 7D). These pathways are implicated in cell adhesion, inflammation, and fibrosis, key features of ACM. These findings suggest that DSC2 down-regulation is closely linked to ACM progression through interactions with multiple signalling pathways, highlighting its potential as a biomarker.

Discussion

This study is the first to systematically explore the role of DSC2 remodelling in ACM through a multi-cohort design and comprehensive molecular analyses. By evaluating myocardial samples from discovery and validation cohorts, we demonstrated a significant reduction in DSC2 expression in ACM patients compared to controls, including those with other cardiomyopathies. This down-regulation was consistent across diverse ACM subtypes (e.g. classical and non-classical forms), underscoring DSC2 as a robust molecular hallmark of ACM.

As a core component of desmosomes, DSC2 is critical for maintaining intercellular adhesion and mechanical stability of cardiomyocytes.29 Its specific reduction in ACM may stem from multiple mechanisms. First, DSC2 is closely related to other desmosomal proteins, such as DSG2 and JUP, which are also implicated in ACM pathogenesis.30,31 The coordinated down-regulation of these proteins suggests a potential disruption in the desmosomal complex, leading to impaired cell–cell adhesion and subsequent pathological changes.32 Secondly, DSC2 may be more susceptible to degradation or misregulation due to its unique structural and functional properties.33,34 For example, DSC2 has been shown to form functional desmosomal plaques independently of DSG2, indicating its essential role in maintaining desmosomal integrity.35 Any alteration in DSC2 expression or function could therefore have a significant impact on the overall stability of the desmosome.

Our findings suggest that the loss of DSC2 may drive ACM pathogenesis through dual structural and electrical mechanisms, mediated by disrupted desmosomal integrity and altered calcium signalling. Structurally, impaired desmosomal adhesion promotes cardiomyocyte apoptosis and fibrofatty replacement, a hallmark of ACM progression.36–38 Electrically, DSC2 interacts with connexin 43 (Cx43), a gap junction protein essential for electrical coupling.39 DSC2 down-regulation disrupts Cx43 localization, leading to delayed conduction and arrhythmogenic substrate formation. The dissociation between DSC2 down-regulation and preserved Cx43 expression may result from multifaceted compensatory mechanisms at the intercalated disc. While DSC2 loss disrupts the desmosome-gap junction linkage,39 adaptive pathways, such as microtubule-dependent Cx43 trafficking via DSP-EB1 complexes,38,40 preserve Cx43 protein levels but cause pathological redistribution to lateral membranes.41 This explains the preserved immunoblot signals and ectopic Cx43 in immunofluorescence (see Supplementary material online, Figure S3B). Additionally, other desmosomal proteins, such as PKP2 and JUP, stabilizes Cx43 at the membrane,42,43 and post-translational modifications, like PKCα-induced dephosphorylation,44 alter channel conductance without affecting total protein levels. Additionally, DSC2 dysfunction destabilizes desmosomal integrity, leading to the cytoplasmic release of JUP and subsequent activation of aberrant Wnt/β-catenin signalling—a recognized ‘common final pathway’ in ACM—which exacerbates fibro-adipogenic remodelling.3,15 These findings position DSC2 as a central player in ACM’s dual pathology of structural degeneration and electrical instability.

Our findings suggest that DSC2 reduction in RV is a robust diagnostic biomarker for ACM. Compared to previous studies that focused on other desmosomal proteins such as JUP and PKP2,13,18 DSC2 showed superior diagnostic accuracy across different ACM subtypes. For example, while JUP has been widely studied as a biomarker, its expression can be affected by other conditions. In contrast, DSC2 reduction was consistently observed in ACM patients, regardless of disease subtype. Our model identified RVEDD and LAID as higher-priority diagnostic features. This likely reflects the direct measurability of structural parameters in clinical phenotyping and their strong association with organ damage. While DSC2 reduction ranked lower in our model’s feature importance, it remains biologically central to ACM pathogenesis. DSC2 reduction represents an upstream molecular event preceding structural changes, potentially serving as an early biomarker in pre-symptomatic stages. This highlights the potential of DSC2 as a reliable diagnostic marker for ACM. Furthermore, our validation in an independent cohort from the Zurich ACM Program further confirmed the consistency of DSC2 down-regulation, strengthening its diagnostic value.

Notably, a recent study demonstrated that intramyocardial delivery of Dsc2mRNA in DSC2-deficient ACM mouse models restored cardiac contractility, attenuated RV fibrosis, and prolonged survival.45 It is important to emphasize that these findings derive from a genetically specific model (Dsc2 deficiency), thus limiting extrapolation to other ACM subtypes. Further experimental studies—particularly those validating efficacy across distinct ACM subtypes—are needed to assess DSC2-targeted therapy as a precision medicine approach for broader ACM populations.

The understanding of desmosomal proteins, particularly DSC2, has advanced significantly in the past decades. Our study supports its role as a potential diagnostic biomarker for ACM, aligning with recent advancements in genetic testing and personalized management strategies for channelopathies and cardiomyopathies.46 This integration of biomarker and genetic findings into clinical practice has greatly enhanced diagnosis and management of ACM. However, as highlighted by Zeljkovic et al.,47 there is significant heterogeneity in access to genetic testing for inherited arrhythmia syndromes and cardiomyopathies, despite current recommendations for genetic testing in specific patient categories. This disparity often leads to underdiagnosis or delayed diagnosis of conditions like ACM. Our study, demonstrating the diagnostic value of DSC2 reduction as a biomarker for ACM, could potentially help bridge this gap. By identifying a molecular marker with high sensitivity and specificity, DSC2 may serve as an accessible diagnostic tool, complementing genetic testing and improving early diagnosis, especially in settings where genetic testing is less accessible.

Limitations

Our ACM-focused variant panel does not exclude contributions from other cardiomyopathies or channelopathies. Future studies should incorporate broader genetic screening (e.g. whole-exome sequencing) and multimodal phenotyping (e.g. advanced cardiac MRI and electrophysiological mapping) to disentangle overlapping pathologies. Our study did not include myocardial tissues from Brugada syndrome patients due to limited access to explanted hearts or biopsies, which restricts direct comparison of DSC2 across all arrhythmia syndromes. The clinical translation of reduced myocardial DSC2 expression is constrained by the requirement for cardiac tissue analysis in ACM. Future studies should prioritize correlating myocardial DSC2 levels with circulating biomarkers or developing patient-derived cellular model to enable non-invasive clinical application. Furthermore, longitudinal studies with larger cohorts and diverse patient populations are needed to validate the diagnostic utility of DSC2 and explore its potential as a prognostic marker.

Conclusions

Our study provides compelling evidence that DSC2 remodelling is a hallmark of ACM, harbouring significant diagnostic implications. DSC2 reduction is a robust biomarker for ACM, showing high sensitivity and specificity across different disease stages and subtypes. Future research should elucidate the mechanisms underlying DSC2 down-regulation.
