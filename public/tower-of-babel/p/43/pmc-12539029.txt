Longitudinal relationship between anxiety and sleep quality in community residents: the mediating effect of loneliness
Authors: Wenyu Wang, Zhen Wei, Meiqi Wang, Yazhuo Qi, Long Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPoor sleep quality is a critical public health concern that is highly prevalent in the general population. Although both loneliness and anxiety have been shown to be associated with sleep quality, the interactive relationship among loneliness, anxiety, and sleep quality was obscure, especially with a longitudinal design in rural community areas. To fill the gap, this study aims to investigate the longitudinal relationships among loneliness, anxiety, and sleep quality in community residents.MethodsData were collected from 483 rural residents aged 18 years or older in Shandong Province, China, across two waves (2019 and 2023). A cluster random sampling method was employed to recruit participants. The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index, Zung Self-Rating Anxiety Scale and Emotional and Social Loneliness Scale were used to evaluate sleep quality, anxiety, and loneliness, respectively. A cross-lagged path model and mediation analysis were used to explore the relationships among loneliness, anxiety, and sleep quality. All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 27 and Mplus 8.3.ResultsLoneliness in wave 1 was associated with anxiety in wave 2 (β = 0.12, p < 0.05), anxiety in wave 1 was associated with poor sleep quality in wave 2 (β = 0.18, p < 0.01), and poor sleep quality in wave 1 was associated with both loneliness (β = 0.14, p < 0.05) and anxiety (β = 0.18, p < 0.01) in wave 2. Changes in poor sleep quality were positively associated with changes in loneliness (β = 0.19, p < 0.05) and anxiety (β = 0.62, p < 0.001), with changes in loneliness partially mediating this association.ConclusionsAnxiety and poor sleep quality demonstrated a bidirectional relationship. Longitudinal analyses revealed cyclic associations among loneliness, anxiety, and poor sleep quality: poor sleep quality was longitudinally associated with loneliness, loneliness was longitudinally associated with anxiety, and anxiety was longitudinally associated with poor sleep quality. Changes in loneliness partially mediated the association between changes in poor sleep quality and anxiety. These findings indicate that anxiety, loneliness, and poor sleep quality are interrelated over time.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07474-z.

Introduction

Anxiety and poor sleep quality are common public health issues that are significantly related to individuals’ well-being [1, 2]. Studies have indicated that 32% of the Canadian population experiences inadequate sleep [3], while approximately 70 million people in the United States, 45 million in Europe [4]; and about one in five in China suffer from sleep problems [5]. Poor sleep quality can manifest as short sleep duration, long sleep onset latency, fragmented sleep, prolonged wakefulness, low efficiency, and disruptive events [6], all of which impair cardiovascular health, hormone regulation, cognition, immunity, and mental health [7]. Anxiety disorders, characterized by intense and excessive fear and worry [8], affect about 4% of the global population [9] and 4.98% in China [10], making them the most common mental health condition in the country [10]. Anxiety symptoms often co-occur with poor sleep and social withdrawal [11, 12].

Studies have shown that anxiety and sleep quality have a bidirectional relationship. Large cross-sectional studies show that higher anxiety levels were associated with poorer sleep, while poor sleep is associated with increased anxiety [13–15]. Several longitudinal studies provide additional support, but most have focused on adolescents and young adults, leaving gaps in general adult. For example, a study of 1,420 American adolescents found that generalized anxiety disorder was associated with sleep over time and vice versa [16]. Similar findings were reported among secondary students in Australia [17] and college students in China [18]. However, longitudinal studies in the general adult population remain limited.

Loneliness, another common public health issue [19], has been shown to be associated with health and health-related behaviors, particularly in East Asian contexts [20, 21], and has also been investigated in relation to sleep quality and anxiety. Cross-sectional studies consistently show that higher loneliness is linked to poorer sleep [22–24], and some even suggest reverse relationships [25]. However, a review revealed that the evidence for reciprocal associations between loneliness and sleep quality remains limited, with relatively fewer studies compared to unidirectional links [26]. Moreover, there is a notable lack of longitudinal research examining this relationship [26]. Similarly, the link between loneliness and anxiety is mixed: cross-sectional studies generally report significant associations, but longitudinal findings are inconsistent—some suggest bidirectional effects [27], whereas others support only unidirectional pathways [28, 29]. Importantly, most prior research has been conducted in Western or urban samples, often among middle-aged or older adults [25], leaving a gap in rural Chinese populations.

Taken together, these findings suggest complex interrelationships among loneliness, anxiety, and sleep quality, but very few studies have examined all three simultaneously. To our knowledge, only one cross-sectional study involving 1126 college students found that anxiety mediated the relationship between loneliness and sleep quality [30], suggesting that similar mechanisms may exist longitudinally. Moreover, evidence from rural China is limited [31], despite rural residents making up 36.1% of the national population [32] and facing higher risks of anxiety and poor sleep compared with urban residents [31, 33]. Therefore, there remains an urgent need to explore the longitudinal relationships among anxiety, loneliness, and sleep quality, particularly in the general population of underdeveloped areas.

To address these gaps, this study employs a longitudinal cross-lagged panel model to examine the temporal and causal pathways among loneliness, anxiety, and sleep quality in a sample of rural Chinese adults. The cross-lagged model is particularly appropriate as it allows the testing of directional hypotheses and reciprocal associations over time, beyond what correlational or cross-sectional designs can offer. Specifically, the study has four objectives: [1] to validate the bidirectional relationship between anxiety and sleep quality; [2] to examine the longitudinal link between loneliness and sleep quality; [3] to assess the relationship between loneliness and anxiety; and [4] to identify potential cyclic associations among loneliness, anxiety, and sleep quality. To our knowledge, this is the first longitudinal study of its kind in the general rural population in China. The findings will advance theoretical understanding of the interplay among these constructs and offer practical implications for mental health interventions in underserved communities.

Methods

Sample

The study was conducted in Shandong Province, China, in November 2019 (wave 1) and August 2023 (wave 2). Participants were recruited from Taierzhuang County. Shandong Province, located in eastern China, has a prosperous economy and agriculture. Taierzhuang County, which belongs to Shandong Province, has approximately 53.72% of its population living in rural areas [34]. For the baseline survey, a random cluster sampling method was employed. Five towns were selected, with one village randomly chosen from each town to participate [35]. Initially, 879 residents were recruited for wave 1, and the same group was invited for wave 2. However, due to unavoidable circumstances, 51 participants passed away, 145 relocated, and 194 were lost to follow-up. In addition, 5 cases with missing loneliness variables and 1 case with missing sleep quality variables were excluded. Ultimately, the follow-up survey was completed by 483 participants, all aged over 18 years. A clearer procedure for sample collection is presented in Fig. 1.

Data collection

Face-to-face interviews were conducted in both waves. Participants were invited through door-to-door recruitment, community event tables, and word of mouth, with the assistance of village committees or doctors. Trained postgraduate students conducted the interviews. Participation was voluntary, and all participants provided written informed consent. For individuals who were illiterate or semi-literate, informed consent was obtained with the assistance of their legal guardians. During data collection, interviewers completed the questionnaires based on participants’ responses. After each survey, two postgraduate students carefully reviewed the questionnaires. Any missing or unclear data were checked and completed to ensure accuracy. More detailed baseline information can be found in previous studies [35], and the questionnaire is provided in the supplementary material. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Shandong University School of Public Health (Approval No. 20190802).

Measures

Sleep quality

Sleep quality was assessed using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index in both waves [36], for which a validated Chinese version is available [37]. The scale comprises 18 items measuring seven dimensions: sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, sleep efficiency, sleep disturbance, medication use, and daytime dysfunction. Each dimension is scored on a scale from 0 to 3, and the cumulative score of all dimensions represents the total PSQI score. A greater total PSQI score indicates poorer sleep quality. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.82 in wave 1 and 0.83 in wave 2 in this study.

Anxiety

Anxiety symptoms were measured using the Zung Self-Rating Anxiety Scale [38], widely applied in China [39]. The scale contains 20 items rated on a four-point scale (1 = never or a little of the time, 4 = most of the time). Fifteen items describe negative experiences (e.g., “I feel more nervous and anxious than usual”), and five items describe positive experiences (reverse scored). The total score was obtained by multiplying the raw score by 1.25, with higher scores indicating greater severity of anxiety. Cronbach’s alpha was 0.86 in wave 1 and 0.88 in wave 2.

Loneliness

Loneliness was measured using the Emotional and Social Loneliness Scale [40], which has been validated in Chinese populations [41]. The scale includes 10 items rated on a five-point scale (1 = never, 5 = very often). Five items were reverse scored (e.g., “I belong to the group of friends”). Scores are summed, with higher scores reflecting greater loneliness. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.77 in wave 1 and 0.75 in wave 2.

Socio‑demographic variables

Socio-demographic variables included age, gender, marital status, education, employment status, and economic status. Age was calculated from birth date. Gender was categorized as male or female. Marital status was categorized as married or other. Education was categorized as illiterate, primary school, or middle school and above. Employment was categorized as yes/no. Economic status was assessed by asking, “How would you rate your family’s economic situation in the village?” and categorized as good, average, or poor.

Health status

Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height (m²). Participants were classified as underweight (BMI < 18.5), normal (18.5–24.9), overweight (25.0–29.9), or obese (BMI ≥ 30).

Statistical analysis

Preliminary analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 27. Six participants with missing values were excluded, leaving 483 participants. Continuous variables are presented as means ± standard deviations, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. To assess common method bias, Harman’s single-factor test was applied separately for each wave. The first factor explained 23.52% of the variance in wave 1 and 24.21% in wave 2, both below the critical threshold of 40%, suggesting common method bias was not a major concern. One-way ANOVA was used to examine associations between categorical and continuous variables. Pearson correlation analyses assessed associations among continuous variables. Linear regression was conducted to investigate associations between anxiety, loneliness, and sleep quality in both waves.

A cross-lagged panel model (CLPM) was applied to examine reciprocal relationships among loneliness, anxiety, and sleep quality. Analyses were performed using Mplus 8.3 with the maximum likelihood estimator with robust standard errors (MLR). Model fit indices were CFI = 1, TLI = 1, RMSEA = 0, and SRMR = 0. Due to the two-wave CLPM design, the model is saturated, and perfect fit indices are expected [42]. Based on the cross-lagged model results, mediation analysis was conducted using Model 4 of the PROCESS macro (version 4.1) in SPSS 27. To account for initial differences and capture time-series effects, mediation was tested using changes in variables from wave 1 to wave 2. Change scores were computed by subtracting wave 1 values from wave 2 values, representing changes from baseline to follow-up.

The cross-lagged path model

Figure 2 illustrates the longitudinal relationships among loneliness, anxiety and poor sleep quality. All three variables showed strong stability over the 4-year period: the autoregressive path coefficients for loneliness, anxiety, and poor sleep quality were 0.26, 0.37, and 0.30, respectively (all p < 0.001). Loneliness in wave 1 was associated with anxiety in wave 2 (β = 0.12, p < 0.05), but not with poor sleep quality (β = 0.08, p > 0.05). Anxiety in wave 1 was positively associated with poor sleep quality in wave 2 (β = 0.18, p < 0.01), but not with loneliness (β = 0.06, p > 0.05). Poor sleep quality in wave 1 was positively associated with both loneliness (β = 0.14, p < 0.05) and anxiety (β = 0.18, p < 0.01) in wave 2. These findings suggest a longitudinal cyclic relationship among loneliness, anxiety, and poor sleep quality.

Mediation model for variable changes over time

Based on the cross-lagged model results, poor sleep quality in wave 1 was longitudinally associated with loneliness in wave 2, which in turn was associated with anxiety in wave 2, suggesting a potential mediating role of loneliness. To further investigate this, a mediation analysis was conducted using change scores (wave 2 − wave 1) for each variable, representing the changes from baseline to follow-up. The results, presented in Fig. 3, showed significant coefficients for path a (β = 0.19, p < 0.05) and path b (β = 0.26, p < 0.001), indicating positive associations between changes in poor sleep quality and loneliness, as well as between changes in loneliness and anxiety. The direct effect of changes in poor sleep quality on changes in anxiety was significant (path c’ = 0.62, p < 0.001), and the total effect was also significant (path c = 0.67, p < 0.001). The indirect effect (path a*b) was 0.05, with a 95% bias-corrected bootstrap confidence interval of 0.00 to 0.11, indicating a statistically significant mediation. These findings demonstrate that changes in loneliness partially mediate the relationship between changes in poor sleep quality and anxiety.

Discussion

This study is the first to examine the longitudinal relationships among anxiety, loneliness, and poor sleep quality in the general rural population in China. Several findings have been obtained. First, anxiety and poor sleep quality had a bidirectional relationship. Second, poor sleep quality was longitudinally associated with loneliness, but not vice versa. Third, loneliness and anxiety had a unidirectional relationship, with loneliness longitudinally associated with anxiety. Fourth, loneliness, anxiety, and poor sleep quality had a longitudinal cyclic relationship. Poor sleep quality was positively longitudinally associated with loneliness, loneliness was positively longitudinally associated with anxiety, and anxiety was positively longitudinally associated with poor sleep quality. Fifth, the change of loneliness partially mediated the relationship between the changes of poor sleep quality and anxiety.

This study revealed that anxiety and poor sleep quality had a bidirectional relationship, which was consistent with previous studies [43]. This indicates that baseline anxiety may be linked to poor sleep quality, and conversely, poor sleep quality may be associated with subsequent anxiety. Clinically, sleep disturbances are included into the diagnostic criteria for anxiety disorders [44], and individuals with anxiety commonly exhibit impairments in both objective and subjective sleep measures [45, 46]. Several mechanisms may underlie the associations between poor sleep quality and anxiety. Specifically, on one hand, individuals with anxiety are vulnerable to dysregulation of the autonomic nervous system, which heightens reactivity to stress and leads to hyperarousal, thereby impairing sleep initiation and maintenance [47]. Furthermore, anxiety often triggers sleep-related worry, which prolongs sleep latency and reduces sleep propensity [46]. On the other hand, sleep quality may compromise daily functioning, induce fatigue and cognitive impairment, and contribute to negative emotions such as anxiety and depression [48]. These effects may be particularly pronounced among rural residents, who often rely on physical labor for their livelihoods; impairments in daily functioning may be perceived as a greater burden, exacerbating anxiety [49]. Additionally, limited access to healthcare and mental health resources in rural communities may further reinforce this cycle, as both sleep and anxiety difficulties may remain unaddressed.

The results also indicated that poor sleep quality at baseline was associated with greater loneliness at follow-up, whereas baseline loneliness was not significantly related to subsequent sleep quality. This wassomewhat unexpected, given prior evidence linking loneliness and sleep [50, 51]. Several explanations may account for this discrepancy. First, our sample consisted of rural residents in China. Poor sleep often involves short duration and prolonged nighttime wakefulness, resulting in more time spent alone. In rural areas, where nighttime social and recreational opportunities are limited, extended nocturnal solitude may intensify feelings of loneliness. Second, sleep is crucial for emotional information processing [52]. Poor sleep may reduce motivation for social interaction and communication, contributing to fewer close relationships, increased interpersonal strain, and heightened loneliness [53]. Third, cultural factors may also be relevant. Rural Chinese culture tends to value diligence and productivity. Individuals suffering from insomnia may appear fatigued and less able to work during the day [24], potentially being labeled as “lazy” or experiencing diminished self-esteem and shame. Such stigma may lead to further social withdrawal, reinforcing loneliness. Regarding why baseline loneliness was not significantly associated with subsequent sleep quality, one possible explanation is the relatively older average age of our participants (59 years). Due to rapid urbanization and the outmigration of younger populations, many rural residents have become accustomed to limited social contact [24], and loneliness may therefore have a weaker influence on sleep. Additionally, the high levels of physical labor common in rural settings may induce sufficient fatigue to counteract the potential impact of loneliness on sleep [54].

Inconsistent with most previous studies [55], our results suggested that the association between loneliness and anxiety was not bidirectional. Specifically, higher baseline loneliness was related to greater anxiety at follow-up, whereas baseline anxiety was not linked to in subsequent loneliness. This pattern may partly reflect cultural and contextual factors. In rural China, loneliness is not only an emotional experience but also a trigger for concrete concerns about survival and future security. Given the older age of our sample and the frequent absence of adult children due to migration [56], loneliness may be directly associated with worries about care in later life, children’s educational or marital outcomes, and other practical issues, thereby contributing to heightened anxiety. Another possible explanation relates to “performance anxiety,” a subtype of social anxiety [57]. Prior research has shown that individuals experiencing high loneliness may generate negative self-evaluations in social encounters [58], perceiving that others are judging them unfavorably. These perceptions may evoke embarrassment, tension, and heightened nervousness, which are linked to increased anxiety. Conversely, anxiety did not appear to exacerbate loneliness in this sample. This may be because anxiety in rural residents is often driven by concrete external stressors such as income instability or concerns about children. These are commonly discussed within the community, and social interactions during such discussions may mitigate the sense of isolation, thereby buffering against loneliness [59].

From a comprehensive perspective, the findings suggest that poor sleep quality, loneliness, and anxiety are interconnected, forming potential cyclical associations. Stress response mechanisms may underlie these links. High stress, stemming from various psychosocial factors [60] the adverse effects of prevalent chronic diseases [61], has been associated with reduced sleep quality. Among rural residents, heavy physical labor, economic pressures, and limited access to healthcare may further intensify sleep problems. Poor sleep often leads to prolonged nocturnal wakefulness, and in the context of limited nighttime social engagement and scarce support [62], this may accentuate loneliness. Experiences of loneliness may be accompanied by fear, dissatisfaction, and perceived stress. In cultural settings where emotional expression is suppressed and mental health services are less accessible [63], these negative experiences may undermine emotional regulation, thereby increasing anxiety. Anxiety through stress and hyperarousal mechanisms [64], may heighten tension, intrusive thoughts, and preoccupation with sleep, further affecting sleep quality. This cycle may be particularly pronounced in rural populations, given their reduced social support and relatively limited health literacy.

The mediation analyses in this study suggested that loneliness partly explained the association between poor sleep quality and anxiety. This extends prior findings from a cross-sectional study that reported generalized anxiety as a mediator between loneliness and sleep quality [30]. The reason may be that poor sleep may impair daily functioning and reduce motivation for social engagement [65], leading to social withdrawal and enhanced loneliness. Such isolation may intensify ruminative thinking about life challenges, further contributing to anxiety [66]. Neurobiologically, chronic sleep disruption has been associated with impaired prefrontal functioning and reduced emotional regulation [67], thereby weakening coping resources and increasing susceptibility to anxiety through heightened loneliness [68]. Collectively, these findings suggest that sleep quality may impair both social functioning and emotional regulation, contributing to loneliness and, in turn, anxiety, providing a nuanced framework for understanding the interrelationships among these factors in rural populations.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, all measures relied on self-reported data, which may be affected by recall bias and social desirability. Second, the sample was drawn exclusively from rural communities in Shandong Province, China, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other populations or cultural contexts. Third, although the study used a longitudinal design, unmeasured confounding factors such as genetic predispositions, physical health conditions, or major life events may have contributed to the observed associations among variables. Fourth, since the second wave of measurement data was collected after the pandemic closed, the results are likely heavily influenced by confounding factors during that period, leading to bias in the overall conclusions. Finally, owing to the two-wave design, the CLPM is saturated (df = 0), yielding perfect fit indices and constraining the examination of more nuanced longitudinal dynamics; interpretations should therefore rely on the estimated parameters rather than model fit [42].

Conclusions and implications

This study yielded five main findings: (1) Anxiety and poor sleep quality exhibited a bidirectional relationship; (2) Poor sleep quality was longitudinally associated with loneliness, but the reverse was not observed; (3) Loneliness and anxiety had a unidirectional relationship, with loneliness longitudinally associated with anxiety; (4) There were longitudinal cyclic relationships for the associations between loneliness, anxiety, and poor sleep quality; (5) The changes of loneliness partially mediated the relationship between changes of poor sleep quality and anxiety.

These findings offer important implications for practice and intervention. It is recommended that sleep education workshops be developed and implemented in rural areas to raise awareness of sleep hygiene and promote non-pharmacological interventions. Mental health screening should be implemented as a key measure in rural communities, with particular emphasis on sleep quality and loneliness among middle-aged and older adults. Furthermore, village-based social activities and support groups should be promoted to reduce isolation and strengthen social networks, especially among individuals experiencing sleep difficulties. Training in basic cognitive-behavioral techniques for insomnia and anxiety may help enhance local capacity for mental health support. By addressing sleep health, loneliness, and anxiety within an integrated framework, these strategies hold potential to disrupt the identified negative cycle and improve mental health outcomes in rural populations.
