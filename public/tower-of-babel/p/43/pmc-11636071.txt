Single Crystalline GeSe Van Der Waals Ribbons With Uniform Layer Stacking, High Carrier Mobility, and Adjustable Edge Morphology
Authors: Eli Sutter, Kim Kisslinger, Lijun Wu, Yimei Zhu, Seunghoon Yang, Fernando Camino, Chang‐Yong Nam, Peter Sutter
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPerformance of the group IV monochalcogenide GeSe in solar cells, electronic, and optoelectronic devices is expected to improve when high‐quality single crystalline material is used rather than polycrystalline films. Crystalline flakes represent an attractive alternative to bulk single crystals as their synthesis may be developed to be scalable, faster, and with higher overall yield. However, large – and especially large and thin – single crystal flakes are notoriously hard to synthesize. Here it is demonstrated that vapor‐liquid‐solid growth combined with direct lateral vapor‐solid incorporation produces high‐quality single crystalline GeSe ribbons with tens of micrometers size and controllable thickness. Electron microscopy shows that the ribbons exhibit perfect equilibrium (AB) van der Waals stacking order without extended defects across the entire thickness, in contrast to the conventional case of substrate‐supported flakes where material is added via layer‐by‐layer nucleation and growth on the basal plane. Electrical measurements show anisotropic transport and a high Hall mobility of 85 cm2 V−1 s−1, on par with the best single crystals to date. Growth from mixed GeSe and SnSe vapors, finally, yields ribbons with unchanged structure and composition but with jagged edges, promising for applications that rely on ample chemically active edge sites, such as catalysis or photocatalysis.

Introduction

Group IV monochalcogenides (MX, where M = Ge, Sn, X = S, Se) are layered van der Waals semiconductors that represent binary structural analogues of black phosphorous. This family of layered materials has been studied intensely for their potential as optoelectronic and photovoltaic materials, 2D ferroelectrics, thermoelectrics, etc. Germanium selenide (GeSe), in particular, has attracted interest for anisotropic properties such as absorbance and polarization‐dependent photoresponse,[ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 ] as well as its predicted ferroelectric and ferroelastic behavior.[ 5 , 6 ] Bilayer and few‐layer GeSe is predicted to have very high absorbance (>104 cm−1), anisotropic spin transport,[ 7 ] and emergence of 1D flat bands in twisted bilayers.[ 8 ] GeSe has a direct bandgap of ≈1.3 eV,[ 9 , 10 ] which makes it promising for optoelectronic applications such as near‐infrared photodetection and as a p‐type absorber material for photovoltaics.[ 11 , 12 ] Polycrystalline GeSe films have indeed been incorporated in solar cells with power conversion efficiency up to 5.2%.[ 11 , 12 ] The performance of solar cells, electronic, and optoelectronic devices is expected to improve if polycrystalline GeSe is replaced by high‐quality single crystalline material. However, large – and especially large and thin (i.e., few‐layer) – single crystals are notoriously hard to synthesize. Furthermore, even in bulk single crystals key properties such as the carrier mobility depend on the growth method. Physical vapor transport yields crystals with relatively low carrier concentration (≈1014 cm−3) and high mobility (128.7 cm2 V−1 s−1,[ 13 ] 60 cm2 V−1 s−1,[ 14 ] 71.5 cm2 V−1 s−1), see Table S1 (Supporting Information).[ 15 ] In chemical vapor transport, on the other hand, different transport agents appear to strongly affect the mobility, as seen for example in the use of I2 (12 cm2 V−1 s−1) versus NH4Cl (99 cm2 V−1 s−1).[ 13 ]

Large single crystalline flakes represent an attractive alternative to bulk single crystals as their synthesis may be developed to be scalable, faster, and with higher overall yield. Particularly promising are approaches that yield thin few‐layer crystals that can directly be incorporated in devices without the need for exfoliation[ 16 , 17 ] as in the case of bulk crystals. Synthesis on substrates such as mica, graphite, and silicon has so far yielded GeSe flakes with few micrometer lateral sizes[ 2 , 18 , 19 , 20 ] and limited control over thickness,[ 21 , 22 ] even though thicknesses down to 8.9 nm have been demonstrated. In addition, the transfer from growth substrates to application‐compatible supports without introducing defects remains challenging. As a result, GeSe flakes deposited on substrates such as mica and transferred to SiO2/Si[ 2 , 23 ] have consistently shown low (field‐effect) mobility (0.6 cm2 V−1 s−1,[ 23 ] 0.89 cm2 V−1 s−1,[ 2 ] 1.24 cm2V−1s⁻1,[ 24 ] 4.2 cm2 V−1 s−1).[ 25 ] GeSe and GeS1−xSex flakes grown on mica and graphite consistently show stacking faults,[ 26 ] where the change in stacking sequence likely originates during the nucleation of consecutive van der Waals layers. Interestingly, microbelts and microsheets prepared in solutions containing GeI4, SeO2, oleic acid and octadecene (i.e., without a substrate) demonstrated higher mobilities (7 cm2 V−1 s−1 to 31 cm2 V−1 s−1)[ 27 ] compared to substrate‐grown flakes. Even though solution‐synthesized flakes are not always suitable for applications requiring pristine surfaces, owing to the adsorption of organic molecules on the surface of the flakes, the higher reported mobilities point to the use of alternative synthesis approaches that do not require a substrate on which the flakes grow by consecutive layer nucleation.

One such process is vapor‐liquid‐solid (VLS) growth, where a molten metal catalyst transports precursor species from the vapor phase to the growth front of the crystal. VLS processes have been widely used for the inexpensive synthesis of single crystalline nanowires and ‐ribbons from both conventional covalent[ 28 ] and 2D layered materials.[ 29 ] Transport through the liquid catalyst can be also used for incorporating dopants[ 30 ] or for alloying.[ 31 ] We recently demonstrated the growth of layered GeSe ribbons in a VLS process in which a molten metal (Au) catalyst at the growth front transports precursor species so they are continuously incorporated into edge sites across the entire ribbon thickness, i.e., without involving layer nucleation.[ 32 ] Axial VLS growth coexists with direct lateral vapor‐solid incorporation to produce dense arrays of free‐standing, tapered GeSe van der Waals ribbons.[ 10 ]

Here, we investigate the layer stacking order and the transport properties of such VLS‐grown ribbons. Analysis of atomically resolved cross‐sectional scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) demonstrates a perfect single crystalline structure and remarkable uniformity of stacks of several tens of individual 2D layers, free of extended defects such as stacking faults. Electrical measurements show anisotropic conductivity and yield a Hall mobility of 85 cm2 V−1 s−1, confirming high‐quality GeSe ribbons whose electronic properties are on par with those of the best single crystals reported to date. Hence, the modified VLS process yields large quantities of monocrystalline flakes with controllable thickness and excellent transport properties, suitable for electronic, optoelectronic, and photovoltaic applications. Finally, we explore the possibility of synthesizing alloy ribbons from mixed vapors via the two distinct incorporation pathways, i.e., axial VLS and lateral vapor‐solid growth. While anion substitution in group IV monochalcogenides is well documented for GeSe1−xSx,[ 24 , 31 , 33 ] polycrystalline SnSe1−xSx films,[ 34 ] flakes,[ 35 ] and bulk samples,[ 36 ] examples of successful cation substitution are comparatively rare. Ge1−xSnxS and Ge1−xSnxSe alloys have been reported only for nanocrystals,[ 37 ] but studies of VLS growth have tended to show phase separation, e.g., in Ge1−xSnxS nanoribbons[ 38 ] and nanowires.[ 39 ] Here, we consider the formation of cation substitution alloys by simultaneous exposure to GeSe and SnSe. Our results show that addition of SnSe to the GeSe vapor maintains the tapered ribbon morphology obtained from pure GeSe and leads to modifications of the surface and edges of the ribbons, albeit without any detectable incorporation of Sn in the GeSe ribbons. Hence, growth from mixed GeSe and SnSe vapors appears to be no facile route toward band structure engineering in Ge1−xSnxSe alloy ribbons, but edge decoration and the formation of jagged edges of pure GeSe ribbons could be beneficial for applications requiring ample chemically active edge sites, such as catalysis or photocatalysis.

Results and Discussion

VLS growth using a pure GeSe precursor[ 10 ] on Si substrates covered with a thin Au film (see Methods for details) yields dense forests of GeSe ribbons with characteristic tapered triangular shape, length up to 100 µm and width reaching up to 30 µm near the base (Figure 1a). The thickness of the ribbons was assessed by AFM after transfer to flat SiNx/Si substrates. The GeSe ribbons exhibit flat surfaces and sharp side facets (Figure 1b and Figure 3a), and uniform thickness consistently below ≈40 nm (Figure 1b). Surface potential mapping using Kelvin probe force microscopy shows uniform potential within individual GeSe ribbons, whose magnitude relative to the potential of the surrounding substrate scales with the ribbon thickness (Figure 1c).

Micro‐Raman spectroscopy and mapping demonstrate the uniformity of the single‐crystalline GeSe ribbons (Figure 2 ). Orthorhombic GeSe has 12 Raman‐active phonon modes with Ag, B1g, and B3g symmetry.[ 40 ] The Raman spectrum of a typical nanoribbon (Figure 2e) shows peaks corresponding to the Ag, B1g, and B3g modes of GeSe. Raman intensity maps of the most intense Raman‐active Ag and B3g modes (Figure 2b–d) show a homogeneous intensity distribution throughout the entire ribbons, confirming their single crystalline structure and uniformity.

Structural investigation by TEM and nanobeam electron diffraction (NBED) further confirms that the ribbons consist of single crystalline orthorhombic GeSe (space group Pnma, JCPDS No. 48–1226)[ 41 ] with measured lattice parameters a = 0.388 nm, b = 0.455 nm (determined by NBED in plan‐view geometry, Figure 3c, Figure S1, Supporting Information), layering in the ribbon plane, and symmetry axis of the ribbons aligned along the GeSe b ‐axis (Figure 3b–d). NBED linescans obtained with 100 nm pixel spacing along entire ribbons demonstrate that the ribbons maintain a high‐quality single crystalline GeSe structure throughout (Figure S1, Supporting Information). High‐resolution TEM and diffraction along the [001] zone axis (ZA) confirms that the c ‐axis (i.e., the van der Waals stacking direction) is perpendicular to the ribbon plane, consistent with earlier findings.[ 10 ] Basal‐plane imaging, as shown in Figure 3b–d), cannot unambiguously identify the stacking order of the layered monochalcogenide crystals. GeSe, along with the other group IV monochalcogenides, usually crystallizes in an equilibrium van der Waals stacking order that consists of alternating A‐ and B‐layers (AB‐stacking), where the sequence of metal and chalcogen atoms is reversed along the b ‐axis in consecutive layers (Figure S2a,b, Supporting Information). In addition to this equilibrium stacking, group IV chalcogenides have exhibited other (non‐equilibrium) stacking registries.

Prior work showed that SnS grown on mica can crystallize with an alternative AA‐stacking order consisting of repeated A‐layers (Figure S2c, Supporting Information).[ 42 ] This AA‐stacking persists only for a few layers before being replaced by the equilibrium AB‐stacking order. The observation of ferroelectric domain structures for SnS grown on SnS2 indicates that such (ferroelectric) AA‐stacking (in contrast to the antiferroelectric AB‐stacking) can be realized on other substrates as well.[ 43 ] A different way of modifying the stacking order was discovered in GeSe1−xSx bands of multimaterial heterostructures[ 26 ] grown on mica as well as GeSe1−xSx nanowires obtained by VLS growth.[ 44 ] Here, frequent single‐layer stacking faults interrupt the equilibrium AB‐stacking sequence by spontaneously inserting an extra A‐layer (Figure S2d, Supporting Information).[ 44 ] In contrast to periodic AA‐stacking,[ 42 ] the additional A’‐layer is laterally shifted relative to the adjacent A‐layer along the b ‐axis (hence, the term AA’‐stacking, sometimes also referred to as AC‐stacking).[ 45 ] While such non‐equilibrium stacking is of potential interest as a pathway to inversion symmetry breaking and ferroelectricity, changes in the stacking sequence could be detrimental to the electronic transport properties of the GeSe ribbons. We note that the morphology studied here, free‐standing ribbons where material is incorporated via the VLS catalyst into the open edges (as well as directly from the vapor into the lateral facets, which gives rise to the characteristic triangular shape),[ 10 ] is different from the conventional case of substrate‐supported flakes where material is added by layer‐by‐layer nucleation and growth on the basal plane. This raises questions regarding the stacking order obtained in this alternative growth process of the layered crystals.

In order to determine the stacking order in the VLS‐grown GeSe ribbons, cross‐sectional TEM (or scanning TEM (STEM)) imaging has to be carried out along the [100] ZA. To this end, GeSe ribbons were transferred to a flat Si substrate followed by the preparation of cross‐sectional lamellas. In this way, cross sections were obtained for 3 different ribbons along the b ‐axis (see Figure 3a). Cross‐sectional TEM (Figure 3e,d; Figure S2a, Supporting Information) shows a uniform GeSe ribbon on the Si substrate. Chemical analysis by energy‐dispersive X‐ray spectroscopy (EDS) confirms a composition consistent with the monochalcogenide GeSe (Figure S3b–h, Supporting Information).

Atomically resolved high‐angle annular dark‐field (HAADF) STEM imaging was performed to determine the GeSe stacking order across the entire ribbon thickness (Figure 4a). The ribbon, which consists of (54 ± 2) GeSe layers (thickness ≈28 nm), is a well‐ordered single crystal. Since HAADF‐STEM images the atomic columns in cross‐sectional samples, analysis of such images can be used to directly determine the atom positions and identify the majority species in each column. Close inspection shows small but clearly identifiable intensity differences between neighboring atoms due to the difference in atomic number (Z) of the constituents, Ge (Z = 32) and Se (Z = 34). This Z‐contrast allows the unambiguous assignment of the order of cations and anions within the layers. We find a consistent reversal of the atom sequence – Ge‐Se versus Se‐Ge in the horizontal dumbbells – in consecutive layers across the entire ribbon (Figure 4b). In addition, a statistical analysis of the atom positions shows a uniform registry without any lateral shifts between consecutive bilayers (Figure 4c). These characteristics are the hallmarks of the equilibrium (AB‐stacked) crystal structure of bulk GeSe (space group Pnma). In‐plane ( b ) and out‐of‐plane ( c ) lattice parameters, again determined by analyzing atomic positions in the STEM image, are narrowly spread around average values that are consistent with bulk GeSe (Figure 4d). Thus, the VLS‐grown GeSe ribbons adopt perfect equilibrium stacking over their entire thickness, without incorporation of stacking faults or segments with non‐equilibrium stacking.

In contrast to vapor transport growth, where GeSe flakes expand laterally on an underlying substrate and increase their thickness by nucleation and growth of additional layers on the existing GeSe basal facet, the present growth process involves material incorporation into the open edges of free‐standing (≈vertical) ribbons. The triangular GeSe ribbon morphology is determined by two growth components: catalyst‐mediated fast longitudinal VLS growth in which the ribbons maintain constant lateral dimensions defined by the size of the catalyst drop, along with a lateral expansion via direct (vapor‐solid) edge incorporation into the exposed edges of the basal planes. The concurrent VLS elongation and edge incorporation from the vapor phase leads to a tapered shape due to the different exposure duration of the edges near the base and tip of the nanostructures. AFM demonstrates that the thickness remains uniform over the entire length of the ribbons, and cross‐sectional HAADF‐STEM shows a constant number of completed layers. Different from substrate‐supported flakes[ 26 , 42 ] and c ‐axis oriented VLS nanowires[ 29 , 44 ] formed via layer‐by‐layer nucleation, where changes in the stacking sequence are commonly found, the entire set of layers in the VLS edge‐growth of ribbons is nucleated at the initial growth stages and the thickness is maintained throughout the axial and lateral expansion of the ribbons. Our results show that this absence of layer nucleation during the growth process yields pure AB equilibrium‐stacking of the monochalcogenide van der Waals ribbons.

The electrical transport properties of the GeSe ribbons were determined using back‐gated field effect transistor (FET) devices and Hall‐bar structures, both prepared following the procedure outlined in Figure S7 (Supporting Information). The ribbons were transferred to 300 nm SiO2 on highly doped Si, and Au source (s) and drain (d) electrodes were positioned with electron beam lithography along the zigzag and the armchair directions (Figure S4a,b, Supporting Information). The I sd(V g) transfer characteristics at V sd = 10 V (Figure S4c, Supporting Information) and I sd(V sd) curves at gate voltages V g ranging from −80 V to +80 V (Figure S5, Supporting Information) show that the GeSe ribbons are p‐type with anisotropic conductance σ, which is larger along the armchair‐ compared to the zigzag direction of the anisotropic puckered in‐plane structure of the layered monochalcogenide crystals.[ 4 , 46 , 47 ] At V g = −20 V, for example, the ratio of conductance along [010] and [100] is σ[010]/σ[100] = 1.88, identical to results for bulk GeSe crystals[ 47 ] and consistent with the anisotropic effective hole mass in GeSe.[ 46 ] Hall‐effect measurements were performed in Hall‐bar geometry to determine the carrier concentration, resistivity, and Hall mobility along the axial ([010]) direction of the GeSe ribbons at room temperature. AFM was used to measure the thickness of the GeSe ribbon in the device, yielding a thickness of 38 nm (Figure S6a,a’, Supporting Information). The Hall measurement yielded the following results: Carrier concentration p = 9.7 × 1014 cm−3, resistivity ρ = 75.6 Ω ⋅cm, and Hall mobility µ H = 85.2 cm2V−1s−1 (see Figure S6 and Note S1, Supporting Information). Given a fixed carrier concentration, the anisotropic conductance observed on field‐effect devices translates into a Hall mobility that is also anisotropic with the same ratio of µ H,[010]/µ H,[100] = 1.88 as the conductance. Especially noteworthy is the Hall mobility of the VLS grown GeSe ribbons, which is on par with the highest Hall mobility measured in GeSe bulk single crystals (Table S1, Supporting Information). Hence, the VLS process is a competitive route toward high‐yield, high‐quality single crystalline GeSe ribbons with anisotropic transport properties and high carrier mobility.

The demonstrated growth process of single‐crystalline GeSe van der Waals ribbons raises the possibility of using the distinct incorporation pathways via the VLS catalyst and from the vapor into the edge facets for the controlled formation of alloys. Recent results on mixed‐dimensional nanowire‐nanoplate heterostructures, for example, showed the ability of combining GeSe1−xSx anion‐substitution alloys with substantially different composition via concurrent VLS and vapor‐solid incorporation from a mixed GeS and GeSe vapor.[ 32 ] Here, we investigate the possibility of obtaining similar control in Sn1−xGexSe cation‐substitution alloys by growth from a mixed SnSe‐GeSe vapor.

Similar to the growth of GeSe ribbons discussed above, adding SnSe to the GeSe vapor under identical conditions yields dense ensembles of nanoribbons, as seen in the SEM images of Figure 5a, b. The ribbons again show strongly tapered shapes and have sizes similar to the pure GeSe ribbons. The addition of SnSe vapor, however, induces marked changes to the ribbon edges. While ribbons grown from pure GeSe vapor are bounded by sharp, straight edges, the ribbons grown from mixed GeSe‐SnSe vapor have jagged edges. AFM confirms that the ribbons retain flat top/bottom facets and uniform thickness in the 50−60 nm range over the entire length (Figure 5c,d). The AFM measurements also clearly show the jagged ribbon edges.

Chemical analysis by STEM‐EDS detects Ge and Se, as well as trace amounts of S (Figure 6 ). Quantitative EDS analysis yields a composition of 49.45 at.% Ge, 49.45 at.% Se, and 1 at.% S. Importantly, EDS spectra of the ribbons show no detectable intensity at the energies of the characteristic Sn peaks. EDS analysis shows negligible Sn content (≈0.1 at.%, corresponding to the detection limit) under typical synthesis conditions at 320 °C, despite the simultaneous exposure to GeSe and SnSe vapors during growth, i.e., the ribbons consist of essentially pure GeSe.

Micro‐Raman spectroscopy confirms the pure GeSe nature of the ribbons grown from mixed precursor vapor (Figure S8, Supporting Information). The Raman spectrum of a typical nanoribbon (Figure S8e, Supporting Information) shows peaks corresponding to the Ag, B1g, and B3g Raman modes of GeSe.[ 40 ] The mode energies are found to be identical to those of ribbons grown form pure GeSe precursor vapor (Figure 2e). Raman intensity maps of the most intense (Ag, B3g) Raman‐active modes show a homogeneous intensity distribution throughout the entire ribbons (Figure S8b–d, Supporting Information), which corroborates the uniform structure and composition of the GeSe ribbons grown from mixed precursors.

Plan‐view HAADF‐STEM shows the morphology of the ribbons grown from mixed GeSe and SnSe vapor (Figure 7 ). Overall, the ribbons appear with homogeneous contrast, except for bright tips consistent with the Au catalyst particle with higher average atomic number, Z (Figure 7a–c). Electron diffraction confirms that the ribbons consist of monocrystalline layered GeSe with symmetry axis oriented along the [010] direction. Thus, the ribbons grown from mixed GeSe‐SnSe vapor are crystallographically identical to the ribbons grown from pure GeSe precursor, i.e., SnSe is not incorporated. However, the addition of SnSe transforms the sharp edges of the GeSe ribbons. In the narrower part near the VLS growth front the edges appear relatively straight, but they become increasingly more jagged towards the base of the ribbons (Figure 7a,f). The near‐edge region also appears non‐uniform in thickness (Figure 7f,g). In addition, STEM images show the presence of small bright nanoparticles on the basal plane of the ribbons (Figure 7d). The observed edge morphology can be explained by small amounts of Sn or SnSe decorating the edges, which affects the subsequent incorporation from the vapor and thus hinders the uniform lateral expansion of the ribbons.

Conclusion

Large triangular GeSe van der Waals ribbons obtained via concurrent axial Au‐catalyzed VLS and lateral vapor‐solid growth were analyzed to determine key characteristics including the stacking registry of the layered crystals and the electronic transport properties. Atomic resolution STEM, performed along the [100] zone axis on cross‐sections of the GeSe ribbons, provided evidence for a highly ordered single‐crystalline structure and a uniform (antiferroelectric) AB‐stacking across the entire thickness of the GeSe ribbons. In particular, the ribbons contained no alternative stacking registries or stacking faults found in substrate‐supported layered monochalcogenides or c ‐axis oriented VLS nanowires. This suggests that the AB‐stacking registry is defined at the onset of VLS ribbon growth and is maintained during the entire growth process, i.e., in both in the axial expansion by a VLS process and the lateral vapor‐solid growth.

Electrical measurements show outstanding properties of these single crystalline GeSe ribbons. Measurements in a field‐effect transistor (FET) geometry incorporating individual GeSe ribbons as the conducting channel show an anisotropic conductance with ratio σ[010]/σ[100] = 1.88, p‐type conduction with moderate carrier concentration (p ≈ 1015 cm−3) and a high Hall mobility of 85 cm2V−1s−1. These characteristics are comparable to those found in the highest‐quality bulk single crystals, but the advantage of the combined axial VLS and lateral vapor‐solid growth is that these properties are realized in mass‐synthesized, large, free‐standing ribbons with consistent sub‐40 nm thickness and with uniform crystal orientation – and hence easily identifiable armchair and zigzag directions – that are ready to be incorporated as the active material in electronic, optoelectronic, and energy‐conversion devices. In contrast, competing approaches either require additional process steps, such as exfoliation (in the case of growth of bulk single crystals) or yield flakes that are attached to a specific growth substrate (in the case of chemical vapor deposition or vapor transport growth).

A future challenge involves the controlled doping of such nanoribbon electronic materials. The ribbons obtained here, which were grown without intentionally supplying dopants, were found to be p‐type semiconductors as is typical for group IV monochalcogenides, which are self‐doped by native defects (e.g., cation vacancies).[ 48 ] For a related system (Au‐catalyzed SnS nanowires), n‐type doping by addition of PH3 during growth was reported,[ 49 ] which raises the prospect that ambipolar doping – for example to create pn‐junctions within individual GeSe ribbons – may also be possible for the materials reported here.

Replacing the single GeSe growth precursor by a mixed GeSe‐SnSe vapor again yields large and thin tapered ribbons, i.e., the concurrent axial VLS and lateral vapor‐solid growth is maintained. Contrary to expectations, the ribbons remain pure GeSe without any detectable Sn incorporation. However, decoration with Sn causes the formation of increasingly jagged edges, i.e., a substantial enhancement of the edge length compared to ribbons grown from GeSe alone. These findings demonstrate a route toward modifications of the GeSe ribbons for applications such as catalysis, photocatalysis, or photo‐electrochemistry, which benefit from ample undercoordinated (i.e., reactive) edge sites. Our combined results demonstrate large, thin GeSe van der Waals ribbons, obtained in large amounts via a simple and reliable growth process, as a high‐quality semiconductor materials platform for a wide range of applications.

Experimental Section

GeSe ribbons were synthesized in a pumped quartz tube reactor with two independently controlled temperature zones (see Figure S9, Supporting Information). Ribbon growth was performed by evaporation of a pure GeSe precursor (99.99%, ALB Materials), and by co‐evaporation of GeSe and SnSe precursors (99.99%, ALB Materials). In the growth from a single precursor (Figure S9a, Supporting Information), the evaporation zone containing a quartz boat (Temperature zone I) with GeSe powder was heated to 450 °C, while the zone containing the substrate (Temperature zone II) was heated to growth temperatures of 300–330 °C. For the growth from mixed GeSe and SnSe vapor, a separate quartz boat containing SnSe was placed 2 cm upstream from the GeSe boat in the evaporation zone (Figure S9b, Supporting Information). The substrates were Si(100) wafer pieces covered with nominally 2–5 nm Au films, deposited by sputtering at room temperature and dewetted at the growth temperature.[ 50 ] During growth, an Ar (99.9999%, Matheson) carrier gas flow was maintained at 60 standard cubic centimeters per minute and a pressure of 20 mTorr. Growth was typically performed for 5 minutes after which the reactor was cooled naturally to room temperature.

Images of the nanoribbon forests on the native growth substrates were obtained by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) in a FEI Helios Nanolab 660 field‐emission microscope at 5 keV primary beam energy. AFM imaging was performed in tapping mode on a Veeco Multimode AFM using commercial silicon nitride cantilevers with 300 kHz resonance frequency (Budget Sensors Tap300Al). Micro‐Raman spectra and maps were obtained on a Horiba Scientific XPlora Plus Raman microscope system with laser excitation at 532 nm at a power of 16.8 µW. Structure and morphology of the ribbons were investigated by (scanning) transmission electron microscopy ((S)TEM) and electron diffraction in an FEI Talos F200X microscope operated at 200 kV, on ensembles of ribbons dry‐transferred onto lacey carbon grids. Samples for cross‐sectional (S)TEM were prepared using the in situ lift‐out method utilizing a Hitachi NB5000 FIB and a FEI Helios G5 UX Dual Beam FIB/SEM with final Ga+ milling performed at 2 keV. Structure, morphology, and chemical composition of the ribbons were investigated by cross‐sectional (scanning) transmission electron microscopy ((S)TEM), electron diffraction, and energy‐dispersive X‐ray spectroscopy (EDS) in an FEI Talos F200X microscope operated at 200 kV. Atomic‐resolution HAADF‐STEM imaging was performed on cross‐sectional samples in a JEOL ARM200CF microscope operated at 200 kV. Atomic positions were determined from raw HAADF‐STEM images by using the built‐in Find Maxima command in the image analysis software ImageJ, followed by a statistical analysis of the bilayer registry, as well as b ‐ and c ‐axis lattice spacings in Mathematica.

Electrical measurements were carried out on devices fabricated from single GeSe ribbons transferred to 300 nm SiO2/Si supports. Deterministic nanoribbon transfers were carried out using a “hot pick‐up technique” described in the literature.[ 51 ] The detailed steps of the transfer process were summarized in Figure S7 (Supporting Information). Electrode patterns were created using electron beam lithography in a Helios G5 UX SEM/FIB equipped with a pattern generator (JC Nabity Lithography Systems). Contact metal (Au) was deposited via thermal evaporation (Kurt Lesker, PVD 75, base pressure: 10−6 Torr) with 0.1 Å s−1 deposition rate. Back‐gated field‐effect transport measurements were performed at room temperature using a Signatone probe station and a Keithley 4200A‐SCA Semiconductor Parameter Analyzer. Hall effect measurements were conducted at room temperature on GeSe ribbons fabricated into Hall‐bar structures (as shown in Figure S5, Supporting Information), using a Quantum Design Dynacool 12T Physical Property Measurement System.
