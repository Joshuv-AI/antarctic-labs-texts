Study on depression-free life expectancy in the elderly population in China
Authors: Ruirui Xie, Heting Wu, Fei Zhao, Huiling Dong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Elderly are at increased risk of certain mental health conditions, particularly anxiety, dementia-related disorders, and depression.MethodsData from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) 2011-2020 were used, comprising 2,185 participants. The multi-state life table method was applied to estimate life expectancy (LE) according to depression severity, stratified by gender and Hukou status. Z-test was used to analyze whether the differences in depression-free life expectancy (DFLE) among different genders and Hukou status were significant (p<0.05).ResultsAt age 65 in 2020, the LE of Chinese Elderly was 25.39 years, with 61.5% (15.61 years) depression-free. LE for mild and moderate-to-severe depression were 6.19 and 3.59 years, respectively. Men had an LE of 24.91 years, with a DFLE of 16.91 years (67.9%), while women had a higher LE of 25.81 years but a lower DFLE of 14.30 years (55.4%). The SDLE and MDLE of females were 7.05 years and 4.47 years, respectively, which were higher than those of males at 5.29 years and 2.71 years. The LE of urban elderly was 26.47 years, with a DFLE of 18.04 years (68.2%), both of which were higher than those of rural elderly, whose corresponding values were 24.74 years and 14.23 years (57.5%), respectively. Rural elderly had an MDLE of 6.47 years and an SDLE of 4.04 years, both higher than the corresponding values for urban elderly (MDLE 5.70 years; SDLE 2.73 years).ConclusionsCompared with their rural counterparts, urban elderly experience longer, higher-quality Lives, Likely due to better access to mental health resources..Although women live longer than men, they experience shorter DFLE, potentially indicating higher psychological burden or gender-specific health disparities. Moderate-to-severe depression significantly increased mortality risk, with lower recovery rates, especially after age 80, when health declined sharply.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24622-y.Keywords: Multi-state life table, Elderly, Healthy life expectancy(HLE), Depression-free life expectancy (DFLE)

Introduction

According to United Nations forecasts, China’s elderly population (aged 60 years and above) will increase to 424 million by 2035 and peak at 518 million in around 2054, when the proportion aged 60+ will exceed 40% [23]. This significant and progressively deepening aging trend has amplified China's societal burden of elder care and presented unprecedented challenges to the sustainability of its social security system. Moreover, high depression prevalence among seniors [11, 25] further elevates mortality and impairs healthy aging. Crucially, depression in elderly manifests in graded severity: approximately 19.05% of individuals aged 65 years and older experience mild depressive symptoms, while 12.17% exhibit moderate-to-severe symptoms [11], [25], [22].

Demographic shifts toward chronic diseases and extended morbidity have rendered traditional life expectancy inadequate. Healthy life expectancy (HLE), which integrates both mortality and health status, has thus emerged as a superior metric [19, 20]. While HLE now critically informs healthcare planning [7, 17], research disproportionately focuses on physical health assessment [8, 9], neglecting mental health indicators like depression.

Depression affects both Life expectancy and healthy life expectancy among elderly, particularly rural older women. In studies conducted both in China and abroad, depression is used as an indicator to directly measure DFLE among elderly which can more directly reflect their psychological health status. Alves et al. calculated the DFLE of the Brazilian population aged 30 years and older between 1998 and 2013 [1]; Feraldi et al. [4] utilized the multi-state Life-table approach to estimate the DFLE of married and widowed older men and women in the United States from 2002 to 2018, Huang et al. [6] measured the DFLE in Chinese elderly, Wang et al. [26] employed the multi-state Life table method to study the DFLE of rural elderly aged 60 years and above by age and gender. Although previous research has utilized depression indicators to measure the psychological aspects of healthy life expectancy, scholars have not disaggregated the severity of depression. Consequently, the derived depression-free life expectancy (DFLE), lacks specificity and detail. Depression is a complex psychological condition that varies between individuals. Therefore, failure to differentiate the severity of depression may lead to an inaccurate measurement of healthy life expectancy. This paper’s key contribution lies in distinguishing mild and moderate-to-severe depression within a multi-state life table method to more precisely estimate DFLE, thereby addressing the limitation of previous studies that treated depression as a single homogeneous state.

This study used data from the China Elderly Care and Health Longitudinal Survey (CHARLS) spanning 2011 to 2020. Depression was categorized into mild and moderate-to-severe, and the multi-state life table method was applied via the iMaCh software to assess DFLE among the Chinese elderly, and to examine the differences and characteristics of DFLE by Hukou status and gender.

Data and methods

Data

CHARLS is a project jointly conducted by the National School of Development at Peking University and the China Social Science Survey Center, with key support from the National Natural Science Foundation of China. This survey aimed to longitudinally investigate the social, economic, and health conditions of middle-aged and elderly people aged 45 years and above in China. The survey employed a stratified multistage random sampling method covering 150 counties/districts and 450 villages nationwide, with a sample of approximately 18,000 individuals. The baseline survey of CHRALS commenced in 2011, followed by biennial tracking surveys, resulting in the release of five waves of data from 2011 to 2020. CHARLS offers high-quality micro-level data with strong representativeness aimed at fostering interdisciplinary research on population aging and health issues. This survey serves as a scientific foundation for the formulation, modification, and enhancement of China’s social security policies. CHARLS survey observation data from five periods from 2011 to 2020 (2011, 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020) were used, and elderly aged 65 and above in the baseline data were selected as the research subjects. After excluding samples with missing variables and unknown status, the final study cohort comprised 2,185 participants (see Table 1 and Fig. 1). Gender, Hukou status, and educational attainment were included as covariates in all multivariable regression models. Educational attainment was measured using the CHARLS variable “Highest Level of School Attained,” which captures each respondent’s most advanced completed education level. For analysis, participants were classified as illiterate (no formal education) or literate (any completed education, from partial primary school through postgraduate degree). Hukou status was measured using the CHARLS question “What is your current hukou status?”, with respondents classified as having either urban or rural hukou.

Healthy life expectancy indicator

This study employed depression as a metric to assess health expectancy. The abbreviated version of the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D10) was used in the CHARLS dataset to assess depression levels among the elderly. The scale consists of ten items (eight negative and two positive). After reverse-coding the two positively framed questions, the scores of the ten questions were summed to calculate an individual's total depression score, which ranged from 0 to 30. Higher scores indicated greater severity of depression. According to the classification criteria of CES-D10, 0-9 points indicates no depression; if the total score falls between 10 and 15 points, the individual is categorized as having mild depression; when the total score ranges from 16 to 20 points, it indicates moderate depression; and when the total score is greater than or equal to 21 points, it signifies severe depression [30]. Based on the aforementioned criteria, this study categorized depressive states as mild depression while combining moderate and severe depression into moderate-to-severe depression.

Multi-state life table method

This study employed the multi-state life table method to measure DFLE among elderly individuals in China. Based on the Markov assumption, it posits the existence of various health states for individuals. By collecting the probability data of transitions between different health states and transition matrices and simulating the process of health state transitions, this method ultimately calculates the average lifespan of individuals in each health state and computes a weighted average to derive the expected health lifespan [2]. Based on longitudinal data, it dynamically demonstrates the transition patterns of the health states of elderly individuals, allowing for a more accurate estimation of their healthy life expectancy. Based on the classification of depressive severity described above, this study categorized life expectancy into depression-free life expectancy (DFLE), mild depression life expectancy (MDLE), and moderate-to-severe depression life expectancy (SDLE).

The multiple-state life table was constructed based on the transition probabilities between the different health states. The present study categorized the health status of elderly individuals into three non-absorbing states-no depression, mild depression, and moderate-to-severe depression- along with one absorbing state (death), resulting in four health states (Fig. 2). According to the Markov assumption, an individual's health status Y(x+n) at age x+n is solely influenced by their health status Y(x) at age x, and the transition probability pij(x) depends only on the current health state i and the time interval n experienced, independent of the previous health state sequences.

Results

Analysis of the health status transition probability of the elderly in China

This study found that from 2011 to 2020, the transition probability from no depression to mild depression in Chinese elderly aged 65-95 years increased with age, and the transition probability from no depression to moderate-to-severe depression slowly decreased (Fig. 3). The transition from mild to no depression followed an inverted "U-shaped" curve peaking at 80 years of age. Between the ages of 65 and 80 years, the probability of transitioning from mild to no depression increased continuously and declined thereafter (Fig. 4). This trend is consistent with the transition from moderate to mild depression. The probability of transitioning from moderate-to-severe depression to no depression decreased continuously with age. As age increased, the transition probabilities of depression to death and the different degrees of depression to death showed an upward trend. Compared with individuals with mild depression, those with moderate-to-severe depression were significantly more likely to have higher mortality rates, with the disparity increasing with age. The probability of maintaining mild depression showed little variation with increasing age, whereas the probability of maintaining moderate-to-severe depression decreased rapidly with age, possibly due to the higher mortality rates among elderly individuals with moderate-to-severe depression (Fig. 3).

Life expectancy of Chinese elderly without depression and with mild depression and moderate-to-severe depression

The findings of this study indicated that between 2011 and 2020, the expected survival time of 65-year-olds in China was 25.39 years (95%CI: 23.76-26.72), of which 15.61 years (95%CI: 14.75-16.76) were depression-free (61.5% of LE). Individuals with mild depression had an expected remaining Life of 5.62 (95%CI: 5.09-6.14) years (24.4% of LE), whereas those with moderate-to-severe depression had an expected remaining Life of 3.88 (95%CI: 3.51-4.26) years (14.1% of LE). As illustrated in Fig. 5, both LE and DFLE in the elderly population in China consistently decreased with advancing age, with LE experiencing a more pronounced decline than DFLE. The decreases in MDLE and SDLE were relatively uniform and showed stable trends over time. With increasing age, the proportions of DFLE and SDLE decreased, whereas those of MDLE increased proportionally. This trend was attributed to the decline in physical and cognitive functions among the elderly, which heightened their susceptibility to physical discomfort and mood fluctuations, thereby increasing the prevalence of mild depression. Moreover, as depression is not a fatal condition and minimally impacts mortality, although MDLE continues to decline, its share of LE increases progressively.

Severe depression shortened lifespan more than mild depression. Although the proportion of lifespan with moderate to severe depression (SDLE/LE) declined from 14.1% at age 65 to 9.9% at age 95, this decline reflected higher mortality rather than improved health among people with severe depression. The absolute Lifespan with severe depression declined from 3.88 years at age 65 to 0.77 years (95%CI:0.43-1.12) at age 95, consistent with the increased risk of death(See Table 2).

Life expectancy of non, mild, and moderate or severe depressed Chinese elderly by gender

Table 3 compares LE and DFLE of elderly individuals in rural China by gender. Despite having a natural survival advantage over men, women have health disadvantages. For instance, at age 65, the LE for males was 24.91 years (95%CI: 23.08-26.73), whereas that for females was 25.81 years (95%CI: 23.97-27.66). However, males had a DFLE of 16.91 years (95%CI: 15.54-18.27), accounting for 67.9% of their LE, whereas females had a DFLE of 14.30 years (95%CI: 13.08-15.51), accounting for 55.4% of their LE. This highlights that both DFLE and the proportion of healthy remaining life were higher in males, demonstrating a pronounced gender paradox. The Z-test showed a significant DFLE gap at age 65 (Z = 2.48, p<0.05). The gap stays significant through the early-old ages (for example, Z = 2.11 at age 69, p< 0.05). After age 75, the Z-values fell below 1.96, indicating non-significant differences. Additionally, females exhibited significantly longer expected survival times across various depression severities. Specifically, females had an SDLE of 7.05(95%CI: 6.24-7.87) years compared to 5.29 years(95%CI: 4.63-5.95) for males, and an MDLE of 4.47 years(95%CI: 3.94-4.99), which is markedly higher than the 2.71 years(95%CI: 2.33-3.09) observed for males. As illustrated in Fig. 6, the disparity in LE and DFLE between genders showed a narrowing trend with increasing age.

Life expectancy of non-depressed, mildly depressed, and moderately-to-severely depressed Chinese elderly by Hukou status

The LE and DFLE of elderly individuals in urban China were higher than those in rural areas. This phenomenon differed from findings in studies on physical health, suggesting that urban seniors had better access to mental health services, stronger social support, and more community activities. These factors provided them extra mental health benefits that physical health measures did not capture. In 2020, LE at age 65 was 26.47 years (95%CI: 24.32-28.61) for urban residents and 24.74 years (95%CI: 23.09-26.39) for rural residents. The DFLE for urban elderly individuals was 18.04 years (95%CI: 16.46-19.62), constituting 68.2% of the LE, whereas for rural elderly individuals, it was 14.23 years (95%CI: 13.16-15.30), constituting 57.5% of the LE. This phenomenon indicated that, in terms of mental health dimensions, elderly individuals in urban areas not only lived longer but also enjoyed a better quality of life than their rural counterparts. Under both mild and moderate-to-severe depression, urban elderly had a shorter remaining life expectancy than rural elderly. At age 65, urban elderly with mild depression were expected to Live an additional 5.70 years (95%CI: 4.87-6.53), while rural elderly with mild depression were expected to Live 6.47 years (95%CI: 5.75-7.19). Under moderate-to-severe depression, 65-year-old urban elderly were projected to survive 2.73 years(95%CI: 2.21-3.25), compared to 4.04 years (95%CI: 3.61-4.47) for their rural counterparts (Table 4). The disparity in LE and DFLE among the elderly individuals residing in different regions showed a gradual reduction with advancing age (Fig. 7). Z-tests confirmed that the urban advantage in DFLE was highly significant at age 65 (Z = 3.91, p < 0.001) and remained significant through age 85 (Z = 1.98, p < 0.05). After age 85, the Z-values dropped below 1.96, indicating no significant urban-rural DFLE gap in the oldest groups.

Discussion

Healthy life expectancy is a crucial indicator of population health and the quality of the public health system. Although extensive research has been conducted on healthy life expectancy, studies focusing on mental health, particularly among the elderly, are limited. This study used multistate life tables to estimate DFLE among the elderly in China. We subdivided the DFLE according to depression severity (mild vs moderate to severe) as well as sex and place of Hukou status. This approach allowed us to understand how different levels of depression affect the remaining healthy years and deepen our understanding of healthy life expectancy from the perspective of mental health.

Transition from mild depression to no depression: a decline in recovery probability after age 80

This study found that the probability of mild depression transitioning to no depression is 1.68-4.08 times greater than the probability of moderate-to-severe depression transitioning to no depression, with a trend that rises and then falls and age 80 as the inflection point. In the 65–80 years age group, the elderly typically maintain relatively healthy physiological functions and adapt well to lifestyle and social role changes after retirement. They are also better able to establish solid social support networks and interpersonal relationships [24]. Positive social support and a stable Living environment help to reduce mild depressive symptoms and increase the Likelihood of recovery. However, beyond the age of 80, there is a rapid decline in physiological functions and an increase in health issues, psychological stress, and anxiety [13, 28, 29]. This not only increases the risk of worsening depressive symptoms but also significantly reduces the Likelihood of recovery. For elderly individuals with moderate-to-severe depression, symptoms are more severe and recovery is more challenging, with the probability of recovery decreasing with age. In addition, after retirement, individuals may gradually form better coping strategies and emotion regulation abilities, and establish new social circles, which helps to improve depression. However, after the age of 80, the decline in physical strength and function may become the dominant factor affecting mental health, offsetting these positive effects and making depression more difficult to reverse. These findings suggest that timely health interventions for mild depression, particularly among younger older individuals, are likely to yield better health outcomes.

Variation in mortality risk associated with different levels of depression: moderate-to-severe depression poses a significantly higher mortality risk compared to mild depression

The study revealed that the risk of mortality due to moderate and severe depression among the elderly is approximately 1.2–3.8 times higher compared to mild depression, which aligns closely with findings from previous research [18], [3]. Compared to moderate-to-severe depression, mild depression typically does not significantly impair daily functioning in the elderly. Individuals with mild depression may maintain daily functioning and social engagement, which could be related to lower mortality risk. In addition, mild depression is often more readily perceived by elderly individuals, leading to the proactive acceptance of medical interventions such as psychological counseling and pharmacotherapy. Early intervention may alleviate depressive symptoms and prevent progression to more severe forms, which has been associated with improved health outcomes. Elderly individuals experiencing moderate-to-severe depression typically endure heightened psychological stress characterized by profound negative emotions, feelings of helplessness, and self-deprecation. Such psychological stress may reduce emotional regulation and increase vulnerability to social dysfunction and suicidal ideation, which have been associated with elevated mortality. Depressive symptoms may also affect physiological health, potentially contributing to increased mortality risk. In summary, the exacerbation of depression not only directly increases the risk of suicide among the elderly but may also indirectly elevate mortality risk through the initiation of physiological pathologies.

Additionally, this study found that the proportion of moderate-to-severe depression in life expectancy decreases with increasing age. This pattern may partially reflect the "selective survival effect": individuals with moderate-to-severe depression face a higher risk of Death, and thus may be underrepresented in the Elderly population [16]. In addition, long-lived survivors may have stronger coping abilities, more mature emotional regulation mechanisms, and more stable social relationships, which may help reduce the persistence or Relapse of moderate-to-severe depression.

Gender disparities in DFLE: Female elderly individuals exhibit longer lifespan yet inferior psychological well-being compared to males

This study demonstrated that while the average life expectancy among elderly women exceeded that of men, their healthy life expectancy and the proportion of those remaining healthy were lower than those of men. This characteristic does not differ from physical health life expectancy [14], however, the potential pathways of influence may diverge. Although women tend to outlive men, their healthy lifespans in terms of both psychological and physical dimensions are inferior to those of men. This suggests that the extended lifespan observed in women is often accompanied by a longer period of illness. Previous research has identified that depression indices among women in urban and rural areas of China surpass those of men, with notably higher proportions of women exhibiting moderate-to-severe depression [27].

Previous studies suggest that women may be more sensitive to external stressors compared to men [31]. Despite their longer lifespan, women’s lower mental health status and higher incidence of depression may contribute to shorter DFLE. Women’s longer lifespans increase the likelihood of experiencing life events such as widowhood, loneliness, and reduced social interaction in old age, which may place them at greater risk of psychological burden and depression. This prolonged psychological strain diminishes the life quality of women. Additionally, Long-term gender discrimination and prejudice [12], particularly in resource allocation and social status, could also exacerbate psychological distress and health issues among women. The observed lower DFLE among elderly women may reflect a "cumulative disadvantage effect" across the life course.

Significant disparities in DFLE were observed between urban and rural areas, with urban elderly individuals living longer and exhibiting better psychological well-being

This study found that when depression was used as an indicator to estimate healthy life expectancy, urban and rural areas exhibited distinct characteristics. Importantly, this pattern contrasts with findings from physical health-based studies. Research on healthy life expectancy associated with physical health indicates that rural elderly individuals are more susceptible to selective mortality. This susceptibility is attributed to the relative scarcity of medical resources and care services in rural areas, which hinders improvements in health status and consequently elevates the probability of mortality [15]. Older rural individuals who have undergone mortality selection and survive often have a higher quality of life. This results in a longer healthy life expectancy and a higher proportion of healthy life years remaining [5, 21]. By contrast, our analysis using mental health measures reveals that urban elderly not only live longer but also maintain superior mental health outcomes

China's long-standing dual economic system, combined with the scarcity of medical resources and professional mental health services in rural areas, has significantly hindered timely and effective treatment and psychological support for the elderly. This limitation of social and psychological support contributes to increased loneliness and a sense of disconnection among the elderly that subsequently increase their risk of depression. Furthermore, the low level of health awareness and education in rural areas restricts their ability to access health information and adopt healthier behaviors, resulting in a lack of serious consideration of mental health issues and a subsequent decline in quality of life.

Conclusion

Urban elderly in China Live longer and enjoy better mental-health quality than their rural counterparts, reflecting their greater access to mental-health services, stronger social support, and more community engagement. Although women Live longer than men, they experience shorter DFLE, potentially indicating higher psychological burden or gender-specific health disparities. Moderate-to-severe depression greatly increases mortality risk and lowers recovery rates, with effects intensifying after age 80, when both LE and DFLE drop sharply. Therefore, these findings highlight the need for targeted mental health programs for rural seniors, women, and individuals with severe depression, which would help them achieve more healthy years in later life.

Limitations

This study has certain limitations. The first is that important variables may not have been omitted and not considered. These variables could impact the psychological well-being of the elderly but were not included in the analysis. After further refinement of the IMaCh software, additional control variables were incorporated into the analysis. Second, because this study relied on retrospective survey data and was dependent on self-reports from the elderly, there is a possibility of self-report bias. The elderly may underestimate or overestimate their psychological health issues for various reasons, potentially leading to partial bias in the study results.
