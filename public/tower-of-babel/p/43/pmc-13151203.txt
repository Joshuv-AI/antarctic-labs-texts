There’s (no) accounting for taste: identifying and predicting subgroups of incarcerated persons based on perceived food sufficiency and personal and lifestyle characteristics
Authors: Bram de Heide, Miranda Sentse, Miranda Boone
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn recent decades, research focus on prison climate and normalization has increased, highlighting food as a particularly important dimension of prison life. This has recently led to policy reforms in Western Europe, aimed at improving dietary quality, meeting incarcerated persons’ food needs, and supporting sustainability goals. Meeting food needs can benefit health, well-being, and the overall prison experience, however, little is known about how food sufficiency may vary across the prison population. This study aims to identify subgroups of incarcerated persons based on their perceived food sufficiency and examines how personal and lifestyle characteristics predict membership of these subgroups.ResultsSurvey data from 3,010 incarcerated persons revealed three distinct subgroups, characterized by their overall level of perceived food sufficiency: Far too little (18%), Too little (44%), and Enough (38%). Several characteristics were associated with subgroup membership, including gender, age, physical activity level, health orientation, and the opportunity to self-cater. The opportunity to self-cater appeared as the strongest predictor; individuals without this opportunity were more likely to belong to the Far too little (OR = 2.2) and Too little (OR = 1.7) subgroups compared to the Enough subgroup.ConclusionsFood was regarded as insufficient by a considerable proportion of participants, particularly by those without the opportunity to self-cater. These findings suggest that the standard meal system may not accommodate the diverse needs within the prison population. Meeting these needs can offer multiple benefits, including supporting autonomy, agency and identity, promoting physical and mental well-being and social interactions, and reducing antisocial attitudes, thereby contributing to effective prison management and humane treatment.

Background

The importance of humane detention was formally recognized in 1955 with the adoption of the United Nations Standard Minimum Rules for the Treatment of Prisoners (UN Congress on the Prevention of Crime and the Treatment of Offenders, 1955). However, attention toward the lived experiences of incarcerated persons has increased in recent decades. This is reflected in the revision of these rules into the Nelson Mandela Rules in 2015 (UN General Assembly, 2015) and in the growing research focus on prison climate and normalization (Crewe, 2011; Liebling & Arnold, 2004; Van de Rijt et al., 2023). Within this body of research, both qualitative and quantitative studies frequently highlight food as a particularly important dimension of prison life (Johnson et al., 2024; Van Ginneken et al., 2018; Woods-Brown et al., 2023). In Western Europe, growing recognition of the impact of prison food has recently led to policy reforms aimed at improving dietary quality, meeting incarcerated persons’ food needs, and supporting well-being, rehabilitation, and sustainability goals (Minke & Smoyer, 2017; Pratt & Eriksson, 2013; Van der Vlist, 2022). Despite these developments, most prison systems worldwide still do not prioritize the prison foodservice and meeting food needs.

Incarcerated persons’ food needs encompass multiple aspects, including health, cultural and lifestyle preferences, self-determination, and food quality and quantity (De Heide & Ramakers, 2024), and meeting them may offer a variety of benefits. Among these needs, sufficient food quantity is central, as it is intertwined with all other aspects. For example, the availability of sufficient vegetables, vitamins, or fiber in prison meals is essential for incarcerated persons’ physical and mental health and wellbeing; reducing the risk of chronic illness and depression and antisocial or aggressive behavior (Benton, ym2007; Choy, 2023; Gangwisch et al., 2015; Ludwig, 2002; Raine & Brodrick, 2024). Meanwhile, the amount of protein in prison meals is often regarded as important by individuals whose lifestyles involve regular sports or strength training (Avieli, 2023; Condon et al., 2008). Beyond its biological aspects, food is closely tied to identity, belonging, and dignity. Sufficient cultural food products or specific herbs and spices for those with non-native cultural identities enables incarcerated persons to affirm and express their cultural and community identities, and experience a sense of normalcy and fairness (De Graaf & Kilty, 2016; Earle & Philips, 2012; Vanhouche et al., 2018; Wilson, 2023). Finally, the opportunity for individuals to determine both the type and the quantity of food they can eat can strengthen their sense of dignity, autonomy, and control and can foster agency, identity, and individuality (Chatterjee and Chatterjee 2018; Godderis 2006a, 2006b; Smith 2002; Smoyer, 2014; Ugelvik, 2011), which may help reduce negative attitudes and antisocial behavior (Giordano et al., 2002; Maruca et al., 2021; Minke, 2014; Opsal, 2012; Rowe, 2011).

While qualitative studies suggest that meeting incarcerated persons’ food needs can benefit their health, well-being, and overall prison experience, little is known about the extent to which food is considered sufficient in the broader prison population (De Heide & Ramakers, 2024; Woods-Brown et al., 2023). The present study addresses this gap by identifying subgroups of incarcerated persons based on their perceived food sufficiency (regarding specific food groups) and examining how personal and lifestyle characteristics are associated with membership of these subgroups. In doing so, the findings may support food policies that align with nutritional guidelines while accommodating the diverse needs of incarcerated persons, thereby supporting well-being and humane prison conditions.

Perceived food sufficiency

Food in prison carries meaning beyond its nutritional function. Consequently, sufficiency with prison food transcends caloric adequacy or meal size. Perceptions of food sufficiency are closely intertwined with incarcerated persons’ needs, satisfaction, and symbolic meanings of food. These needs are not uniform but are shaped by individual characteristics and lifestyle factors (De Heide & Ramakers, 2024). Qualitative research further shows that aspects such as the quality and quantity of food and the degree of autonomy incarcerated persons have over their food can shape their sense of dignity, identity, and normalcy, as well as feelings of agency, control, and individuality (e.g. Godderis 2006b; Smoyer, 2014). These factors influence how incarcerated people experience satisfaction with, and the sufficiency of, prison meals. Therefore, food sufficiency should be understood not only as an objective measure of caloric or nutritional adequacy, but as a subjective perception of whether meals are experienced as sufficient in relation to needs and (symbolic) meanings.

Because these needs and meanings vary across individuals, the same meals may be experienced differently. For example, differences in cultural preferences, physical activity, or health considerations may lead individuals to perceive identical meals as more or less sufficient. Qualitative research on the experience of food in prison and broader research on food preferences suggests that various individual and lifestyle characteristics, such as age, gender, body size, cultural identity, activity level, and health orientation are linked to how food sufficiency is experienced. Because these characteristics can be linked to multiple needs, certain subgroups of individuals may be more likely to share similar patterns of food sufficiency.

First, age could play an important role in shaping food sufficiency. While little is known in the prison context, some studies have shown that older incarcerated individuals reported specific dietary or medical requirements, such as diabetic-friendly meals, low-sodium options, and sufficient fresh fruits and vegetables (Pageau et al., 2021; Wangmo et al., 2018). Research outside the prison context suggests that food attitudes and needs change across the life course: younger adults (18–30) tend to prioritize convenience, preparation knowledge, price, and time, whereas older adults (60+) are more likely to base their choices on health considerations (Chambers et al., 2008). Age-related changes in sensory functioning, such as diminished taste and smell, can reduce appetite, dietary variety, and the perceived pleasantness of meals (Westenhoefer, 2005). Finally, declining energy expenditure after the age of 60 may lower overall appetite (Pontzer et al., 2021). Taken together, these findings suggest that older incarcerated persons may report lower perceived sufficiency for health-related food groups (e.g. fruits and vegetables), while younger individuals may report lower overall sufficiency.

Another factor potentially shaping food sufficiency and eating behaviors is gender. While little is known about gendered food patterns in the prison context, Avieli (2023) found that some incarcerated men expressed a preference for more meat, fish, and cheese, which they perceived as more masculine than the “white” and “mushy” carbohydrates provided in meals. Similar patterns have been observed outside prison: men tend to consume more red and processed meat and protein-rich foods, whereas women reported higher intake of fruits, vegetables, and fiber and place greater emphasis on healthy eating (Feraco et al., 2024; Gil et al., 2022; Wardle et al., 2004; Westenhoefer, 2005). These differences are partly shaped by cultural meanings, with meat often perceived as “masculine” and plant-based foods as “feminine” (Rogers, 2008; Rothgerber, 2013). Furthermore, men generally require more calories than women (U.S. Department of Agriculture and U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, 2020; Voedingscentrum, 2025b). Because standard prison meals are based on the (caloric) requirements of the average adult male, and women are more likely to diet or restrict food intake (Gorini et al., 2025), portions may be relatively large for women. While direct evidence on the relationship between gender and food sufficiency is lacking, these patterns indicate that women may be more likely to regard prison meals insufficient in terms of fruits and vegetables, while potentially excessive in overall size or calories. Men, however, may perceive them as insufficient both regarding meat and protein-rich foods and overall size.

Food is closely tied to cultural belonging and identity (Godderis 2006b), and the absence of culturally familiar foods in prison has been widely reported as a source of dissatisfaction. Several studies have described incarcerated persons’ frustration when unable to access dishes or products that reflect their cultural or ethnic background, which is often perceived as unfair (Godderis 2006a; Vanhouche et al. 2018; Williams et al. 2009; Wilson 2023). Individuals may rely on self-catering or (costly) canteen purchases to maintain a sense of identity and continuity with life outside (Williams et al., 2009; Wilson, 2023). While prison meals include a variety of cultural dishes, the importance of cultural dishes suggests that individuals with non-native cultural identities may perceive prison meals to be insufficient in terms of herbs and spices groups, and potentially overall, due to the limited availability of cultural products.

Body size could further shape food sufficiency in prison. Prison meals are typically designed to meet the caloric requirements of the average adult male, yet larger individuals generally have higher energy demands (Pavlidou et al., 2023; Pontzer et al., 2021; Voedingscentrum, 2025b). A common complaint in prison food studies is that portions are insufficient in quantity, as voiced by an incarcerated person: “I think there could have been a little more food portion, I mean we’re all big guys and other than eating in there there’s not a lot to do” (Struthers Montford, 2023, p. 246). This suggests that larger individuals may report overall lower perceived sufficiency, whereas those with smaller bodies could regard prison meals as excessive in size.

Another factor that could be related to food sufficiency is incarcerated persons’ physical activity level. Although inadequate portions are a common complaint, weight gain is also frequently reported during imprisonment (Choudry et al., 2019; Gebremariam et al., 2018). This may partly reflect the sedentary routines of prison life (Johnson et al., 2019), but can also result from muscle gain among (young) men in an environment where strength and physicality are tied to prison masculinity (De Viggiani, 2012; Müller & Mutz, 2022). Higher activity levels increase energy requirements (Pavlidou et al. 2023; Voedingscentrum 2025a), potentially making standard meals insufficient for those who exercise or work regularly, while they may be excessive for those with more inactive lifestyles. Beyond the quantity of portions, the perceived sufficiency may also be related to meal composition, as incarcerated persons engaged in sports or strength training often seek additional protein sources or supplements to support their fitness goals (Avieli, 2023; Condon et al., 2008). By contrast, sedentary behavior has been associated with greater consumption of energy-dense snacks and lower intake of fruits and vegetables (Pearson & Biddle, 2011). These patterns indicate that those with higher physical activity levels may perceive meals as insufficient in protein content and portion size, while those with lower physical activity levels may also regard the amount of provided food as insufficient.

Finally, many incarcerated individuals express concerns about the health implications of prison food, describing it as unhealthy, overly processed, and lacking in fresh or nutritious components such as fruits, vegetables, and whole foods (Avieli, 2023; De Graaf & Kilty, 2016; Ifeonu et al., 2023; Wangmo et al., 2018). Complaints also extend to food preparation methods: meals are frequently reheated rather than freshly prepared, with some incarcerated persons believing that repeated steaming or microwaving diminishes both nutritional value and taste (Struthers Montford, 2023; Vanhouche et al., 2018). A lack of transparency in meal preparation further undermines confidence in food safety and hygiene, reinforcing preferences for self-catering when possible (Chatterjee & Chatterjee, 2018; Smith, 2002; Vanhouche, 2015). Additionally, several studies note demands for specialized or medical diets, particularly for those with chronic conditions such as diabetes or for older individuals requiring age-appropriate meals (Pageau et al., 2021; Wangmo et al., 2018). Taken together, this suggests that individuals who place greater emphasis on nutrition and health may perceive prison meals as insufficient in health-related food groups (e.g. fruits and vegetables), whereas those less concerned about their health may regard them as adequate.

The current study

While qualitative literature suggests that food needs and sufficiency may vary across incarcerated persons personal and lifestyle characteristics (De Heide & Ramakers, 2024; Woods-Brown et al., 2023), little is known about how these differences manifest in the prison population. To address this gap, the current study aims to identify distinct subgroups among incarcerated persons based on their perceived sufficiency across several food groups. To explore the relationship between personal and lifestyle characteristics and patterns of perceived sufficiency across food groups, the current study further examines how these characteristics predict subgroup membership. This approach complements existing qualitative research by providing quantitative insights and deepening the understanding of how perceived food sufficiency varies across the prison population.

The Dutch context

Since 2022, food provision in Dutch prisons is regulated by a new national contract, in which sustainability, nutrition, and incarcerated persons’ satisfaction have become explicit criteria. Two standard meal types are available to all incarcerated persons: ready-made meals and a breakfast/lunch package. For the ready-made meals, two or three menu options are offered daily, including various cultural dishes. Incarcerated persons can also request meals that meet religious requirements, such as halal, kosher, or Hindu diets. In addition, self-catering is possible, either with an ingredient box (distributed by the supplier) or with products purchased from the canteen. However, opportunities for self-catering differ across regimes; in pre-trial units, ingredient boxes are often unavailable and limited recreational time can further restrict access to cooking facilities (Groeneveld et al., 2025). Portion sizes and nutrient compositions are designed to reflect average daily intake requirements, in line with the guidelines of the Netherlands Nutrition Centre (Voedingscentrum). Corresponding to national dietary policy, the contract also incorporates a protein transition, requiring that a proportion of meals must be vegetarian. These criteria affect the extent to which prison food can be tailored to incarcerated persons’ diverse nutritional and dietary needs.

Method

Data

The data used in this this study originates from the Dutch Life in Custody (LIC; see Van Ginneken et al., 2018) and Food in Custody (FIC) studies. In a joint effort, all incarcerated persons in all 29 Dutch prisons were approached between January and April 2024. Incarcerated persons were verbally informed about the purpose and methods of the study, how data would be used, and what rights participants had. Emphasis was placed on the voluntary nature of participating in the research, the confidentiality of their answers, and that they could withdraw from the study at any stage without any repercussions from either the researcher or prison staff. After this information was discussed, incarcerated persons were invited to participate in the study and informed consent was obtained. Two (paper) questionnaires were used to collect data: the Prison Climate Questionnaire (PCQ; see Beijersbergen, 2016; Bosma et al., 2020) and FIC questionnaire (see Nieuwbeerta et al., 2025). Although participants were invited to complete both questionnaires, they could have completed only one of them. During the researchers’ visits to the prison wings, all incarcerated persons present were offered a soda can or candy bar, regardless of intention to participate. Ethics approval was granted by Leiden University’s Ethics and Data Commission. This study’s procedure is similar to that of the earlier LIC studies; for a more detailed description see Van Ginneken et al. (2018). For more information about the FIC study, see Nieuwbeerta et al. (2025).

Sample

Across the 29 prisons included in the study, a total of 8,710 individuals were incarcerated during the data collection period. Of those, 2,511 individuals (28.8%) could not be reached (transferred or released, n = 895; unavailable due to labor or other activities, n = 388; in isolation cells, n = 98; language barriers, n = 553; behavioral or mental health concerns, n = 77; staff judged participation unsafe, n = 107; time constraints, n = 106; other reasons, n = 287). This left 6,199 individuals (71.2%) who were invited to participate. Of these, 2,022 individuals (32.6%) declined to participate (lack of interest, n = 1,136; insufficient time, n = 119; distrust of research, n = 108; imminent release, n = 47; other reasons, n = 612). Ultimately, 4,177 incarcerated persons participated, yielding a response rate of 67.4%.

Both the PCQ and FIC questionnaires were used for the present study; respondents who completed only one were therefore excluded. A total of 3,727 individuals, representing 42.8% of the total population across the 29 prisons, completed both questionnaires. Because participants were grouped based on their reported food sufficiency (see Measures), those who did not complete the food sufficiency items (n = 717) were excluded, resulting in a final sample of 3010 participants that were included in the analyses. The average age was 38 years, the majority (95%) male, and half (51%) identified with only the Dutch culture. Most of the participants were housed in pre-trial (44.5%) or prison (sentenced) (41.2%) units, the remainder in the extra care (8.7%), persistent offender (3.1%), high security (1.9%), or minimum security (0.7%) units.

The excluded sample (n = 717) did not significantly differ from the included sample (n = 3010) in age and gender, however, a lower percentage of participants in the excluded sample reported identifying with only the Dutch culture (41.9% compared to the included sample (51.2%; χ²(1) = 15.9, p < .001). Further, the distribution of participants per regime differed slightly between the excluded and included sample. In the excluded sample a higher percentage was in the pre-trial (47.3%), persistent offender (5.7%), and minimum security (1.1%), and a lower percentage in the prison (38.9%), extra care (5.6%), and maximum security (1.4%; χ²(5) = 22.1, p < .001) units. However, due to the small sizes of the differences and the excluded sample, these differences were considered negligible for the analyses.

Measures

Food sufficiency

To identify subgroups in the sample, participants were asked to rate to what extent the meals they received contained enough of eight food groups compared to their desired diet, using a 5-point Likert scale (from far too little to far too much). These food groups were based on the categories used in the healthy and sustainable food-based dietary guidelines for the Netherlands, developed by the Netherlands Nutrition Centre (Brink et al., 2019), and adjusted to the prison context: carbohydrates, meat or meat substitutes and legumes, vegetables, fruits, dairy and plant-based drinks, bread toppings/spreads, oils and fats, herbs and spices. These food groups reflect dietary categories that are familiar in the Netherlands and are easier to interpret for respondents than (macro)nutrient-based categories. This is of particular importance given the generally low educational level and high prevalence of intellectual disabilities of prison populations. Consequently, sufficiency at the level of food groups does not necessarily reflect (macro)nutrient sufficiency, as certain nutrients may be obtained from multiple food groups or they may not be present in any of them specifically (e.g. protein or sodium).

Personal and lifestyle characteristics

To examine the relationship between perceived food sufficiency and personal and lifestyle characteristics, participants’ age, gender, opportunity to self-cater, cultural identity, health orientation, body size, and activity level were measured via self-report using the PCQ and FIC questionnaires. Cultural identity was measured as a categorical variable, by asking participants with which culture they identified (e.g. only Dutch, (Dutch-) Moroccan, (Dutch-) Surinamese, (Dutch-) Antillean). Based on the qualitative literature (e.g. Vanhouche et al., 2018; Williams et al., 2009; Wilson, 2023), differences were expected between individuals identifying with only the national (Dutch) culture and those identifying with other cultures, rather than among the various non-Dutch cultural groups. Therefore, cultural identity was dichotomized into Dutch or non-Dutch. Further, participants’ health orientation was measured using the FIC questionnaire by asking them to indicate the extent to which they agreed with the following statement: ‘My health is important to me’, on a 5-point Likert scale from strongly disagree to strongly agree.

Body size

Although BMI is commonly used as a general measure to assess people’s height and weight, it does not reflect differences in energy expenditure. Since it is a relative measure, both taller and shorter individuals can have similar BMIs despite having distinctly different metabolic needs. To account for this, participants’ height, weight, and age were used to calculate their resting metabolic rate (RMR), which served as a more accurate proxy for body size in relation to energy expenditure. The RMR was calculated separately for males and females using the revised Harris-Benedict equations (Pavlidou et al., 2023): for males, RMR = (9.65 × weight in kg) + (573 × height in m) − (5.08 × age in years) + 260; and for females, RMR = (7.38 × weight in kg) + (607 × height in m) − (2.31 × age in years) + 43. To improve interpretability, the RMR (in kcal/day) was divided by 100, so that every 1-point increment in RMR reflected a change of 100 kcal/day. For reference, for a 38-year-old male, an increase of 100 kcal/day in RMR corresponds roughly to an increase of either 17 cm in height, or 10 kg in weight, or a combined increase in height of 10 cm and weight of 4.5 kg. The resulting RMR scale ranged from 11.5 to 32.4 (x100kcal/day).

Activity level

To account for participants’ level of activity, two types were considered: (1) household work and prison labor, and (2) sports. These were measured using the FIC, in which participants reported the average number of hours per week they engaged in mildly or moderately, or highly strenuous household work, prison labor, and sports, which were based on the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ; Craig et al., 2003). To combine both the duration and intensity of activity, the reported hours were weighted by multiplying them with their metabolic equivalent of tasks (MET) factors (see Ainsworth et al., 2011): household work x3, mildly or moderately strenuous prison labor x3, highly strenuous prison labor x7, mildly or moderately strenuous sports x4.5, and highly strenuous sports x8.

Because the number of hours per week were self-reported, some values were impossible or implausible, a common issue in studies using the IPAQ in the prison context (e.g. Kraft et al., 2025; Mannocci et al., 2015). Following the IPAQ scoring protocol (see IPAQ, 2022), total activity levels exceeding an average of 16 h per day were excluded. As no guidance exists for identifying implausible values for individual items, cut-off values were determined based on realistic limits within the prison context. The maximum number of prison labor hours was set at 40 h per week, reflecting a full-time work schedule. Because household work could have been interpreted to include labor as a unit cleaner, the cut-off was set at 60 h per week. While prisons offer only a few hours of organized sports per week, in-cell workouts are common. High numbers of reported sports are observed, for example, a quarter of participants reported 5 or more hours of sports per week in the study by Mutz and Müller (2023); therefore, an upper limit of 20 h per week was used. Finally, when participants appeared to have reported the same activity across multiple intensity levels (e.g. 40 h of both mildly or moderately and highly strenuous labor), identical entries were proportionally adjusted (divided evenly between the relevant items). Accounting for all implausible values and weighting them according MET factors resulted in two activity scales, MET labor and MET sports, ranging from 0 to 398 and 0 to 250 respectively.

Analyses

Identifying subgroups

Incarcerated persons’ perceived food sufficiency was analyzed to identify both latent subgroups, which are unobserved groups inferred from patterns in the data, and observed subgroups, which are derived from participants overall response profiles. Latent subgroups were examined using Latent Class Analysis (LCA), which identifies classes of individuals based on (similar) patterns of responses within participants across the eight food groups (Collins & Lanza, 2010). For example, LCA may identify a class that reports high sufficiency for carbohydrates but low sufficiency for vegetables, and another class that reports low sufficiency for both carbohydrates and protein. Observed subgroups were identified using k-means clustering, which groups participants based on similarity of overall response profiles between participants across the same variables (Hartigan & Wong, 1979). In other words, k-means clustering groups participants who have similar mean scores across the eight food groups (e.g. low, medium, or high sufficiency across all food groups). Together, these approaches capture both patterns of food sufficiency within individuals (LCA) and similarities between individuals (k-means), providing a comprehensive understanding of subgroups in the sample.

Latent class analysis

Multiple models with increasing number of classes were compared. To determine the model with the optimal number of classes, multiple fit indices were assessed. Lower values of the Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) and log likelihood (LL) indicate superior fit; these measures are comparative and not absolute measures of model fit (Weller et al., 2020). Further, entropy and average posterior probabilities (APPs) provide a measure of how accurately individuals are assigned into classes, with entropy values above 0.80 indicating well-separated classes (Weller et al., 2020). APP values < 0.70 indicated poor, 0.70–0.79 weak, 0.80–0.89 good, and > 0.90 excellent classification (Nagin, 2005).

K-means clustering

To examine the number of potential subgroups, models with two through five clusters were estimated and compared. Cases were classified into clusters by minimizing the within-cluster sum of squares (WSS), ensuring cases within a cluster were more similar to each other than to cases in other clusters (Hartigan & Wong, 1979). Model fit was evaluated by comparing the total WSS across the models, where the steepest decline indicated best model fit, which was visually verified using the elbow method (Kodinariya & Makwana, 2013).

Multinomial regression analyses

To assess how incarcerated persons’ personal and lifestyle characteristics were associated with membership of the identified subgroups, bivariate analyses were first conducted among these characteristics and between each characteristic and membership of the identified subgroups. Thereafter, multinomial logistic regression analyses were performed to examine how personal and lifestyle characteristics predicted subgroup membership. The assigned subgroup was used as the dependent variable and personal and lifestyle characteristics were used as independent variables: age, gender, cultural identity, body size (RMR), health orientation, and the two weighted activity scales (MET labor and MET sports). Lastly, rather than the regime, the opportunity for incarcerated persons to self-cater in the unit kitchen was included as a control variable, because cooking opportunities varied within regimes and are expected to be more directly related to food sufficiency. Missing data were handled using listwise deletion. Missing values were not imputed, because Little’s MCAR test was significant (p = .000), indicating data were not missing completely at random. While multiple imputation relies on the assumption that data are at least missing at random (MAR), this could not be verified, and imputation could therefore introduce bias.

Descriptive statistics

The descriptive statistics are reported in Table 1. Health orientation was rated 4.5 (SD = 0.7) on average and the majority of participants reported they had the opportunity to self-cater in the unit kitchen (76.6%). The average height and weight were 180 cm and 86 kg, which were used to calculate the resting metabolic rate (RMR), which was on average 19.1 (SD = 2.1) x100kcal/day (see Pavlidou et al., 2023). Identifying subgroups

Latent class analysis

To identify latent subgroups in the sample, a Latent Class Analysis (LCA) was conducted to examine patterns within individuals across the eight food sufficiency variables. The model fit indices for the two- to six-class solutions are reported in Table 2. While the Akaike information criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) values were lower for models with more classes, the average posterior probabilities (APPs) were very low (< 0.70 = poor separation) for all models. The entropy values for models with 2 and 6 classes indicated poorly separated classes (values above 0.80 indicate good separation). For models with 3 through 5 classes, the model did not properly converge, resulting in a lack of entropy values. Based on statistical fit and interpretability, the results did not support the presence of distinct latent classes. This indicated that, within individuals, there were no clearly distinguishable patterns of perceived food sufficiency across the eight food groups (e.g. high sufficiency for certain food groups and low sufficiency for others), suggesting that participants generally reported similar levels of sufficiency across all food groups.

K-means clustering

To identify subgroups of individuals with similar overall response profiles, k-means clustering was used, comparing solutions with two to five clusters. The total within-cluster sum of squares (WSS) decreased substantially from the 2-cluster (WSS = 10,591) to the 3-cluster solution (WSS = 8,907), indicating tighter grouping of cases within clusters. Solutions with more than three clusters did not produce meaningful improvement in WSS (4 clusters: WSS = 8,516, 5 clusters: WSS = 7,927) and resulted in very small (n = 40–47) and less interpretable clusters. The 3-cluster solution was therefore determined to be the best fitting model. Cluster sizes were reasonably balanced, (Cluster 1: n = 538, Cluster 2: n = 1314, Cluster 3: n = 1158), and ANOVA results indicated that the mean scores on all eight food sufficiency variables differed significantly between clusters (all < 0.001), suggesting that each variable contributed meaningfully to cluster separation. Each cluster represented a substantively distinct profile of food sufficiency (see Table 3), which can be interpreted as follows: cluster 1: Far too little across all eight food groups (means between 1.19 and 1.55), cluster 2: Too little across all food groups (means between 1.69 and 2.66), cluster 3: Enough across all food groups (means between 2.87 and 3.01). These results suggest that the 3-cluster model provides the most informative representation of variation in food sufficiency among the sample, both in terms of statistical fit and interpretability.

Too little cluster

Compared to both the Enough and the Far too little cluster, individuals in the Too little cluster were less likely to be men (OR = 0.384, p < .001; OR = 0.212, p < .001) and more likely to have a larger body size (RMR; OR = 1.073, p < .01; OR = 1.073, p < .05). They were also more likely to identify with only Dutch culture (OR = 1.282, p < .05) and to regard their health as less important (OR = 0.804, p < .05) compared to the Far too little cluster. In addition, those in the Too little cluster were more physically active in household work or prison labor compared to Enough cluster (MET labor; OR = 1.002, p < .01), and more physically active in sports compared to the Far too little cluster (MET labor; OR = 1.005, p < .01). Finally, they were less likely to have the opportunity to self-cater than the Enough cluster (OR = 0.598, p < .001), but more likely than the Far too little cluster (OR = 1.344, p < .05).

Far too little cluster

The Far too little cluster differed from both the Enough and Too little clusters. Incarcerated persons in the Far too little cluster were more likely to be younger compared to the Enough (OR = 0.975, p < .001) and Too little (OR = 0.981, p < .001) clusters and to regard their health as more important (OR = 1.249, p < .05; OR = 1.244, p < .05). Further, compared to the Enough and Too little clusters, they were less active in sports (OR = 0.994, p < .01; OR = 0.995, p < .05), and were less likely to have the opportunity to self-cater (OR = 0.445, p < .001; OR = 0.744, p < .05). Compared with the Too little cluster, incarcerated persons in the Far too little cluster had a greater likelihood of being male (OR = 4.706, p < .001) and of having a smaller body (RMR; OR = 0.932, p < .05). Lastly, they were less likely to identify with only Dutch culture (OR = 0.780, p < .05).

Discussion

This study set out to investigate whether distinct subgroups of incarcerated persons could be identified based on their perceived food sufficiency (across several food groups), and to explore which personal and lifestyle characteristics predicted subgroup membership. To identify potential subgroups, two approaches were applied. First, Latent Class Analysis (LCA) was used to detect latent subgroups based on patterns of perceived food sufficiency within individuals across the eight food groups. Second, k-means clustering was used to identify subgroups based on similarity in overall response patterns between individuals, indicating clusters of individuals who generally reported higher or lower food sufficiency across all food groups. Together, these approaches examine subgroups in a complementary way, capturing both distinct patterns of sufficiency with specific food groups, and patterns of overall sufficiency across all food groups. The results of the LCA indicated that no clear latent classes were present in the data, suggesting limited variation in food sufficiency patterns within individuals. The k-means clustering, however, revealed three subgroups of incarcerated persons with similar overall response profiles: Far too little, Too little, or Enough across all food groups.

As discussed in the background section, perceptions of food sufficiency reflect more than the objective quantity of the food provided, encompassing incarcerated persons’ varying needs, satisfaction, and symbolic meanings of food, such as dignity, normalcy, and autonomy (e.g. Godderis 2006b; Smoyer, 2014). Because varying needs, satisfaction, and symbolic meanings of food likely stem from personal and lifestyle characteristics, this study examined how such characteristics are linked to subgroup membership of perceived food sufficiency.

The current study identified several personal and lifestyle characteristics associated with subgroup membership, and multivariate analyses further revealed that these characteristics independently predicted membership of specific subgroups. Having the opportunity to self-cater was associated with a lower likelihood of belonging to the Too little and Far too little clusters (compared to the Enough cluster), and with a lower likelihood of being in the Far too little compared to the Too little cluster. In line with the qualitative literature, this pattern suggests that incarcerated persons without the opportunity to self-cater are more likely to perceive the prison meals as insufficient, while self-catering enables individuals to better regulate portion sizes and adjust food quantity to their personal needs (Smoyer, 2014; Ugelvik, 2011).

Women were more likely to be in the Too little cluster compared to both the Far too little and Enough clusters, while neither men nor women were more likely to be in the Far too little cluster compared to the Enough cluster. These findings are only partly in line with the hypothesis. As hypothesized, men being more likely to be in the Far too little cluster aligns with their higher average caloric needs (U.S. Department of Agriculture and U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, 2020; Voedingscentrum, 2025b) and the fact that meals are standardized to meet the average caloric need of men (around 2500 kcal/day). Moreover, women are generally more likely to engage in dieting or food restriction (Gorini et al., 2025; Wardle et al., 2004), which may shape how they perceive the sufficiency of prison food. However, that women were more likely than men to be in the Too little rather than Enough cluster, was unexpected. This may reflect prison food systems being structured around men’s eating patterns, both in composition and timing. Women typically eat more frequently and consume more snacks between meals than men (Bertéus Forslund et al., 2005; Kant, 2018), so the standard three-meal system may not align with women’s usual eating patterns. Consequently, even when the caloric intake is sufficient, women may experience the food as insufficient, suggesting that institutional food provision designed for men may inadvertently disadvantage women’s food satisfaction. However, due to the relatively small proportion of women in the sample (5.5%), these findings should be interpreted cautiously.

Incarcerated persons who rated their health as more important were more likely to belong to the Far too little cluster compared to the Too little and Enough clusters, which may reflect heightened awareness of, or concern about, their health and the intake of specific macro- or micronutrients (De Graaf & Kilty, 2016; Struthers Montford, 2023; Wangmo et al., 2018). In this study, food sufficiency was assessed for several food groups based on guidelines by the Netherlands Nutrition Centre (see Brink et al., 2019) rather than nutrient categories (e.g. carbohydrates, protein, fats, vitamins, fibers, etc.). As a result, certain nutrients are spread across multiple food groups; for example, protein from both the meat and dairy categories, and vitamins from both the fruits and vegetables categories. Individuals who actively monitor their diet, such as those exercise and track their macronutrient intake (Avieli, 2023; Struthers Montford, 2023), or older adults with specific dietary requirements (e.g. low-sodium or diabetic meals; Pageau et al., 2021; Wangmo et al., 2018), may therefore perceive nutrient insufficiencies even when the overall food group intake may be rated as adequate. Conversely, individuals who consider their health as less important may be less aware of healthy nutrient intake and potential deficiencies.

Incarcerated persons with a larger body size, as indicated by resting metabolic rate (RMR), were more likely to belong to the Too little cluster compared to both the Far too little and the Enough clusters. Similarly, higher activity levels of household work and prison labor were associated with a greater likelihood of being in the Too little cluster compared to the Enough cluster. This aligns with basic principles of energy metabolism, as both larger body size (higher RMR) and increased physical activity raise total energy requirements, which can influence perceptions of food sufficiency (Pavlidou et al., 2023; Pontzer et al., 2021). Together, these findings suggest that individuals with higher energy needs, due to a larger body size or increased physical activity, required more food than provided, though the insufficiency was moderate as there was no greater likelihood for them to be in the Far too little cluster.

Contrary to expectations, higher levels of physical activity due to sports were associated with a lower likelihood of being in the Far too little compared to the Too little or Enough clusters. Several factors may explain this finding; first, individuals who engage in sports often experience greater physical and psychological well-being and quality of life (Liguori & Calella, 2024; Marquez et al., 2020), which may foster a more positive general appraisal of daily experiences such as food sufficiency. Further, these individuals may self-cater more frequently or supplement their diet through additional canteen purchases (Avieli, 2023; Condon et al., 2008). In the current study, bivariate analyses revealed only a weak correlation between sports activity and the opportunity to self-cater; however, this does not necessarily reflect actual self-catering behavior or canteen purchases. Conversely, the discrepancy between expectations and findings may also reflect the gap between the food groups and nutrient categories, as, for example, the perceived sufficiency of meat and dairy products does not per se correspond to nutritional adequacy of macronutrients such as proteins. This could, for example, result in an experienced sufficiency of meat and dairy products, while protein intake is regarded inadequate. Lastly, lower physical activity levels have been linked to greater food intake and snacking behaviors among individuals with sedentary lifestyles (Pearson & Biddle, 2011). Incarcerated persons with limited physical activity may experience boredom or emotional distress, leading to overeating and the perception of insufficient food. However, due to potential measurement error in the activity variables, these findings should be interpreted with caution. The reported activity hours included implausible or impossible values; while these were corrected as far as possible (see Measures), some noise may have remained.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study is its large sample size, which allowed for robust statistical analyses and ensured representativeness across the Dutch prison system. By combining two complementary grouping techniques, latent class analysis and k-means clustering, the study systematically explored both latent and observed patterns of food sufficiency in the sample, enhancing the validity and interpretability of the identified subgroups. Furthermore, various personal and lifestyle characteristics that were previously identified in qualitative research as relevant to food sufficiency, were quantitatively tested, using multivariate methods, for their association with the identified food sufficiency patterns, extending on the current knowledge.

Several limitations of the questionnaire design should be acknowledged. First, all food sufficiency items were presented consecutively and response options were aligned in the same direction. This layout may have enabled homogeneous response patterns, for example straightlining, especially among less invested participants. Consequently, this could have contributed to patterns of overall (in)sufficiency across all food groups found in the data. This may explain why the identified subgroups reflected differences in overall sufficiency levels rather than distinct patterns of sufficiency among specific food groups. However, alternative options, such as reverse-worded items, could also have limitations and introduce other issues, as they can be more difficult to interpret for people in the prison system. Second, food sufficiency was assessed in terms of food groups rather than specific (macro)nutrient categories (e.g. carbohydrates, protein, fiber, etc.). While this aligns with national dietary guidelines (Brink et al., 2019), it limits the extent to which nutrient-specific sufficiency could be examined, particularly among individuals with specific dietary needs or preferences related to health or sports, such as sodium- or protein intake. Third, several implausible or impossible values were observed in the physical activity variables, suggesting potential misinterpretation of the related questions. Some participants may have double-counted activities (e.g. reporting the same hours under both mildly or moderately and highly strenuous labor) or made errors in estimating weekly totals. Although data cleaning procedures were applied to correct or remove implausible values, this may have resulted in minor distortions and additional missing values. Similar challenges have been reported in other studies using metabolic equivalent of tasks (MET) scores in prison settings, where unrealistic values were also found, though median values were comparable (e.g. Kraft et al., 2025; Mannocci et al., 2015). This indicates that inconsistent MET data may represent a broader methodological challenge in prison research, warranting a more robust approach to assessing physical activity among incarcerated persons.

Future research directions

Future studies should aim to improve the assessment of both physical activity and perceived food sufficiency in the prison context. To reduce misinterpretation, more detailed or simplified measures could be employed. Detailed approaches could include time diaries (documenting activities a number of times per day for short time intervals), which have been deemed feasible for use in prison settings (Camplain et al., 2025). Simplified approaches could involve a single measure for labor and sports, or alternatively, capturing sedentary and sleep time and assigning an average activity level for the remaining hours. For the assessment of food sufficiency, examination at the nutrient rather than at the food group level could provide a more detailed understanding of perceived nutritional adequacy, particularly for individuals with specific health- or activity-related needs.

Practical and policy implications

Prison food systems typically provide standard meals with limited options for dish type or composition. Given that over 60% of participants were classified in the Too little or Far too little subgroups, ensuring the availability of sufficient food is essential, particularly for those without the opportunity to self-cater. In line with previous qualitative research, the present findings also indicate that perceived food sufficiency varies between individuals and is related to several personal and lifestyle characteristics. This suggests that standard meal provision may not meet the various nutritional and psychological needs of incarcerated persons. Providing flexible portion options or supplementary food products could, however, help address differences in energy or nutrient requirements across individuals. Therefore, prison food policies should aim to be more responsive to differences in food requirements. A practical way to achieve this is by expanding opportunities for self-catering, enabling incarcerated persons to better meet their individual needs through greater autonomy over portion size and meal contents. Beyond meeting nutritional needs, opportunities for self-catering can foster autonomy, agency, and identity (Smoyer, 2014; Smoyer & Blankenship, 2014), mitigating the loss of control and individuality that often accompanies institutional food provision (Godderis 2006b; Smith 2002; Ugelvik 2011). Moreover, enabling incarcerated persons to make food-related choices may promote well-being, improve social interactions, and reduce antisocial attitudes (Giordano et al., 2002; Maruca et al., 2021; Minke, 2014; Opsal, 2012; Rowe, 2011), contributing to a more humane and rehabilitative prison environment.

Conclusions

This study built on qualitative insights into prison food sufficiency by identifying patterns of perceived food sufficiency among incarcerated persons using grouping techniques and examining how personal and lifestyle characteristics predicted subgroup membership. Three subgroups were identified: Far too little, Too little, and Enough across all food groups, reflecting varying levels of food sufficiency. Subgroup membership was associated with various characteristics, such as the opportunity to self-cater, gender, age, activity level, and health orientation. The findings highlighted that a substantial proportion of participants regarded the food as insufficient, particularly those without the opportunity to self-cater. This suggests that the standard meal system does not adequately meet incarcerated persons’ diverse needs; expanding opportunities for self-catering and ensuring sufficient food availability, particularly for those without the opportunity to self-cater, may better align food provision with individual requirements. Meeting incarcerated persons’ perceived food needs and enabling self-catering can offer multiple benefits, including supporting autonomy, agency and identity, promoting physical and mental well-being and social interactions, and reducing antisocial attitudes, thereby contributing to effective prison management and humane treatment.
