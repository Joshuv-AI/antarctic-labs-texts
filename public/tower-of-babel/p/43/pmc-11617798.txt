Psychopathy checklist‐youth version scores and recurring victimisation by an intimate partner in a justice‐involved sample: A comparison of young men and women using longitudinal data
Authors: Michelle N. Harris, Leah E. Daigle, Dennis Reidy, Travis Chafin, Nick D. Thomson
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeople involved in the criminal justice system are at increased risk of recurring intimate partner violent (IPV) victimisation. Experience of trauma is linked to a variety of negative outcomes, including repeated experiences, so it is important to identify factors that may distinguish non‐victims, single‐event victims and recurring victims at an early stage as this could hold potential for intervention efforts. Research studies have identified individual‐level risk factors for IPV victimisation but have not investigated psychopathy traits or sex differences.AimsTo examine sex differences in psychopathy scores and later IPV victimisation.MethodsUsing the Pathways to Desistance study, a longitudinal study of 1354 adjudicated adolescent offenders, relationships between psychopathy checklist‐youth version (PCL‐YV) scores measured during the baseline wave, independent/control variables in wave 6 and self‐reported IPV victimisation in wave 7 were explored using multinomial regression models.ResultsAmong justice‐involved young adults (average age 19), PCL‐YV scores differentiated between non‐victims, single‐event victims and recurring victims. Higher total scores and higher ratings on the antisocial behaviours facet of psychopathy significantly increased the relative risk of recurring victimisation by an intimate partner overall, and specifically of emotional or physical intimate partner violence. For the young women but not the young men, a higher scoring on the interpersonal manipulation facet of psychopathy specifically increased the relative risk of being a recurring victim of any intimate partner violence.ConclusionsSex‐specific differences in relationships between high PVL‐YV scores and recurring IPV victimisation can be used to screen for risk of repeated trauma for justice‐involved young adults, which may change a trajectory of continued involvement in the criminal justice system to a trajectory of resilience and recovery.

INTRODUCTION

Intimate partner violence (IPV) is abuse between individuals who are/have been in a relationship together, and it consists of a variety of forms, such as physical abuse, sexual violence, intimate terrorism (physical or sexual threats) and psychological/emotional abuse (Lipsky & Caetano, 2009). Approximately 1 in 3 women and 1 in 5 men worldwide are victims of intimate partner violence (IPV) (World Health Organisation, 2021), with some subgroups of the population at heightened risk. People with mental illness, for example, are almost four times more likely to be victimised by an intimate partner compared to the general population (Khalifeh et al., 2015). People in the criminal justice system are also at higher risk. Using trajectories comparing non‐, low‐rate, and high‐rate offenders, for example, Verbruggen et al. (2022) found that involvement in intimate partner violence generally and either its psychological or severe physical forms specifically, was consistently higher for justice‐involved than non‐offender groups.

Intimate partner violence is often experienced many times. After an initial victimisation, the risk of repeat victimisation is 13% within 1 month and 29% within 3 years (Xie & Lynch, 2017). Prevalence rates are higher for victims seen in hospitals, with estimates indicating 70% experience more than one event (Hayes, 2018). For justice‐involved samples, exposure to intimate partner violence is not only associated with a higher repetition rate but also increased risk of continued criminal justice system involvement (Gottlieb & Mahabir, 2021; Wagers et al., 2021). Using official police records to examine intimate partner violence, Muftić and colleagues (2015) established that a higher proportion of women and girls were revictimised than men or boys (53%: 30%, respectively).

It is important to identify risk factors for intimate partner victimisation, as doing so could hold potential for interventions. Given sex‐differences in prevalence rates of IPV revictimisation among justice‐involved samples (Muftić et al., 2015), it is plausible that there are sex differences in risk factors. Sex differences are also important as sex‐specific interventions are likely to increase responsivity (Bonta & Andrews, 2007). Advocacy for trauma responsive services (such as trauma connected to IPV) among justice‐involved people has been encouraged because of its promise in increasing responsivity and positive outcomes connected to trauma informed services (Bucerius et al., 2021; Miller & Najavits, 2012).

Many of the risk factors for IPV victimisation are the same for men and women, but there is some evidence for sex‐specific factors here, including anxiety, depression or other serious mental illness for women and comorbid substance use disorders for men (Kraanen et al., 2014; Spencer et al., 2019). Less attention has been paid to other individual‐level attributes, such as psychopathy.

Psychopathy is commonly measured using the psychopathy checklist (PCL) (Hare, 2003). Factor analyses of the PCL indicate covariation among items, which can be accounted for by two broad and moderately intercorrelated dimensions: factor 1 reflecting emotional detachment, and factor 2 reflecting social deviance. In some models, four facets are evidenced as distributed between these factors: factor 1 consists of the ‘interpersonal manipulation’ and ‘callous affective’ facets and factor 2 consists of ‘erratic lifestyle’ and ‘antisocial behaviour’ facets. The interpersonal manipulation facet includes ‘pathological lying’ as well as ‘manipulation’ while callous affect reflects difficulties with remorse, guilt and empathy; ‘erratic lifestyle’ encompasses traits like impulsivity, risk‐taking and recklessness, while ‘antisocial behaviour’ reflects, effectively, a count of such episodes.

There are several reasons to hypothesise that psychopathy and/or its facets and recurring intimate partner victimisation may be related. First, recent analyses have established that psychopathy and intimate partner violence perpetration are linked (Robertson et al., 2020; Spencer et al., 2019), and perpetration and victimisation often co‐occur and can be partially explained by the same set of factors (Daigle et al., 2008; Muftić & Hunt, 2013; Pratt et al., 2014). Secondly, psychopathy traits impact other forms of victimisation and recurring victimisation (Beaver et al., 2016; Daigle & Teasdale, 2018; Daigle et al., 2020). Thirdly, theory helps guide understanding of how psychopathy traits and recurring IPV victimisation may be linked. According to the lifestyles/routine activities perspective, a person is at risk of victimisation when they themselves engage in risky behaviours that, in turn, expose them to similarly motivated offenders who may perceive them as vulnerable (Cohen & Felson, 1979; Hindelang et al., 1978). Those with high PCL‐R scores often engage in risky behaviours linked to victimisation (Blair & Lee, 2013; Sellbom et al., 2017), and, assuming the PCL‐R scores remain stable after a victimisation event, they will keep a person at risk over time and thus vulnerable to repeated victimisation (Farrell et al., 1995). Psychopathy traits are unlikely to change without intervention (Reidy et al., 2015) and persons retaining high PCL‐R scores are less likely to learn from victim experiences in ways that could be helpful in preventing recurring IPV victimisation.

There is little research exploring any relationship between recurring intimate partner victimisation and psychopathy traits, and even less allowing for sex differences. This is important because high psychopathy ratings may be linked to greater susceptibility to violent victimisation (Daigle et al., 2020) and females with high PCL scores are more likely to engage in risky behaviours and social situations or relationships that increase their vulnerability for victimisation (Edwards & Verona, 2016). Thus, they may be more likely to seek out intimate partners with similar traits. Recent research has explored how each psychopathy facet may impact IPV‐based sexual coercion and potential sex differences; there is some evidence that interpersonal manipulative, callous and unemotional and antisocial facets predict IPV perpetration by women and the lifestyle and antisocial facets for men (Hoffmann & Verona, 2021; Mager et al., 2014; Thomson et al., 2021). Similar investigations into the role of psychopathy traits and their potentially sex‐specific connection to recurring IPV victimisation have yet to be undertaken.

Using a justice‐involved sample, we aimed to examine how aspects of psychopathy relate to recurring intimate partner violent victimisation. Our specific research questions were as follows:

Do psychopathy scores differentiate between people who have not experienced any such victimisation (non‐victims), those who have experienced one episode (single victims) and those who have experienced multiple intimate partner victimisations (recurring victims)?

Are there sex differences in the relationship between psychopathy scores and the intimate partner victimisation categories—non‐victim, single victim and recurring victim?

Given the heterogeneous nature of the findings produced by prior research studies into relationships between psychopathy facets and intimate partner violence perpetration (Robertson et al., 2020), we considered that there was insufficient evidence for specific a priori hypotheses; therefore, our questions are largely exploratory.

METHODS

Ethics

Our data were drawn from an anonymised database of an established, ethically approved study; no additional ethics approvals were required.

Data and sample

Data were drawn from the restricted version of an anonymised dataset (approved by IRB‐20‐49)—Pathways to Desistance study, a longitudinal study of 1354 adolescent serious offenders (Mulvey, 2004). Recruitment took place between November 2000 and January 2003 in two sites: Philadelphia, PA and Maricopa County, AZ. Adolescents, aged 14‐ to 17‐years, who were found guilty of a serious offence were eligible for enrolment. Participants were selected from records at each of the county court sites. They were interviewed shortly after their adjudication hearing (baseline wave) and in ten follow‐up waves. The first six waves were conducted every six months and the last four at 12 months intervals, with data collection continuing through 2010. Computer‐assisted interviews were supplemented and validated by official records and interviews with collateral informants.

The Pathways dataset is useful for our research questions because the offender‐based sample offers variation in the psychopathy measure across the sample; the average age of the sample at wave six was 19 years, when intimate partner victimisation is more likely to occur than at younger ages (Halpern et al., 2009; Peterman et al., 2015); and, the dataset includes both sexes, enabling the exploration of sex‐specific differences.

For this study, data for the dependent variable—any, physical, or emotional IPV victimisation—were taken from wave seven. The psychopathy checklist‐youth version (PCL‐YV) used to measure psychopathy traits was only administered during the baseline wave, 4 years earlier. All other independent and control variables were extracted from wave six data, about 3 years post‐baseline. We limited data extraction to the baseline wave, and waves six and seven for several reasons. First, although the domestic violence inventory was first included in wave 4, most of the samples did not complete the inventory until wave 7 (90% missing at wave 4, 87% at wave 5 and 72% at wave 6). Secondly, this allowed testing of a temporal relationship between intimate partner victimisation and previously established characteristics. Thirdly, research indicates that psychopathy does not suddenly emerge during adulthood, but rather psychopathy traits may manifest earlier in life (Forth & Mailloux, 2000). Personality traits may be more malleable in adolescence than adulthood, possibly making their treatment easier and disruption of maladaptive pathways more effective. Although the PCL‐YV was only administered at baseline, research shows the PCL‐YV can be reliabiliy assessed during adolescence and remains relatively stable across interview schedules (Lynam et al., 2009). Our use of the PCL‐YV reflects traits of psychopathy and its relationship to recurring IPV victimisation; not diagnoses or threshold scores indicative of psychopathology.

Given the nature of our dependent variable, participants who were not involved with a partner at the time of the wave 7 interviews were excluded from the analyses, resulting in 1087 young adults in the sample for our analyses.

Measures

Three dependent variables

Three dependent variables drawn from the Domestic Violence Inventory (Pathways, n.d.) were explored separately: any intimate partner victimisation, physical intimate partner victimisation (e.g. has/how many times has your partner hit, pushed, choked or thrown things at you) and emotional intimate partner victimisation (e.g. has/how many times has your partner ever called you stupid, fat or ugly). For each type, if a person did not experience any such types of victimisation during wave 7, they were coded as 0 (non‐victim); for each type of intimate partner victimisation once only during wave 7, each was coded as 1 (single‐event victim) and if there were more than one episode, they were coded as 2 for each type (recurring victim). A limitation was that the inventory did not gather information from the partners, nor relate the victimisation to a specific partner. We controlled for intimate partner violence perpetration during wave 7, which asked participants about their involvement in 19 different types of IPV perpetration (e.g. the number of times you pushed your partner was coded as 1 for each endorsed or 0 if none).

The independent variables

The psychopathy checklist‐youth version (PCL‐YV; Forth et al., 2003) was only administered at the baseline wave and included a mostly open‐ended form administered by an interviewer, validated from court records and collateral interviews, was used to examine psychopathy. The PCL‐YV includes 20 items, each rated on a 3‐point scale (0 = item does not apply, 1 = item applies to an extent, and 2 = item applies). We include a prorated score, which can accommodate up to five missing individual items without invalidating the total score (ICC = 0.91). We used Hare and Neumann's (2005) four facet model of psychopathy (PCL‐YV) reflecting: facet 1, impression management and pathological lying, α = 0.69; facet 2, callous and shallow affect, α = 0.74; facet three, erratic lifestyle, including stimulation seeking and lacking goals, α = 0.72; and facet 4 antisocial behaviour, α = 0.72.

Factors likely to be related to deviant behaviour were also included. Specifically, binge drinking, defined as a report of five or more drinks at one time during the six months before the rating (0 = no, 1 = yes). Drug use measures if participants had used any of 10 different illegal drugs during the previous six months (coded as 1 if endorsed any and coded as 0 if not). Carrying a gun within the period was also coded (1 = yes; 0 = no). Having delinquent peer(s) was rated for the same period asking participants if their peers had engaged in antisocial behaviours using a list of 19 items (rated 0 = none to 4 = all of them; e.g. how many of your friends have sold drugs?). A mean score of 0–4 was calculated for analytics purposes. Internal consistency was α = 0.94.

Demographic and control variables

Measures of age, biological sex (female coded as 1), self‐reported race/ethnicity (White, Black [referent category], Hispanic and Other), site (0 = Philadelphia; 1 = Maricopa County) and socioeconomic status (based on the parent's education and occupation level were used; Hollingshead, (1957).

Analytics strategy

Our analyses involved several steps. First, we examined descriptive statistics and any differences for all measures by male/female sex. We then examined descriptive differences between non‐victims, single‐event victims and recurring victims. Finally, we used multinomial regression models to examine if and how PCL‐SV score traits were related to recurring intimate partner violent victimisation, allowing for sex differences.

RESULTS

Almost exactly half were recruited from each study site. Characteristics of justice involved young people in the sample studied (n = 1087).

Most (n = 675, 62%) of the sample members reported no intimate partner victimisation during wave seven. Just under a quarter of the participants (n = 230, 21%) experienced one such event, whereas 185 (17%) reported experiencing two or more. Some differences were observed when considering the type of intimate partner victimisation. In the entire sample, most (n = 876, 80%) had not experienced physical violence by their intimate partner, whereas 76 (7%) were single‐event victims and 141 (13%) were recurring victims. However, emotional intimate partner victimisation was more prevalent with 163 (15%) single‐event victims, 217 (20%) recurring victims and only 65% (n = 706) non‐victims.

The average total PCL‐YV score was 16 (SD 7.62). Although standardised cut‐point scores of the PCL‐YV have not been established, some researchers argue that a total score of 25 may be indicative of psychopathy for young people (Cauffman et al., 2009). Few (186) of these participants scored in this range. Average psychopathy facet scores were as follows, with the range given in brackets: interpersonal facet 2.3 (range 0–8); affective facet 2.6 (range 0–8); lifestyle facet, 4.4 (range 0–10) and antisocial facet 5.0 (range 0–10).

Most individuals did not carry a gun (n = 982, 91%), engage in intimate partner violent perpetration (n = 703, 63%), binge drink (n = 752, 68%) or use drugs (n = 677, 62%), and most noted they had very few friends that engaged in delinquency.

Sex differences in characteristics

Sex differences on recurring IPV victimisation, psychopathy, and other characteristics were examined. Ninety‐two (58%) of the women reported no intimate partner victimisation, 33 (21%) reported a single episode and 34 reported (21%) multiple episodes; comparable numbers for the men were 583 (63%), 197 (21%) and 148 (16%), respectively. Breakdown by type of intimate partner violence suggested that among the fairly small number experiencing physical violence, there was no sex difference (females: none n = 122, 77%; one = 14, 9% and two+ = 23, 14%; males: none n = 754, 81%; one = 60, 6% and two+ = 124, 12%), but among the larger number experiencing emotional victimisation, there was a higher proportion of women than men (females: none n = 95, 60%; one = 31, 19%, two+ = 33, 21%; males: none n = 611, 66%; one = 131, 14%, two+ = 186, 20%).

The supplemental version of Table 1 shows these data in terms of averages. The average number of any IPV victimisation events was 5 for women and 4 for men (t = 2.31; p < 0.05). The average number of physical IPV victimisation events was 1.75 for women and 1.40 for men (no significant difference). Women experienced an average of 3.70 emotional IPV victimisation events and men an average of 2.79 (t = 2.68, p < 0.01). Significant sex differences emerged on the total score of the PCL‐YV (female mean: 14.33; male mean: 16.12; t = −1.79 and p < 0.01) and on the callous affect (female mean: 2.19; male mean: 2.68; t = −2.95 and p < 0.01), erratic lifestyle (female mean: 4.12; male mean: 4.51; t = −1.97 and p < 0.05) and antisocial behaviour facets (female mean: 4.36; male mean: 5.10; t = −3.49, p < 0.001); males and females were similar on the pathological lying and manipulation scale. Significantly more male than female participants had engaged in any of the deviant behaviours measured, except for drug use.

Regression models

Table 2 displays findings from the multinomial regression models examining the relationship between psychopathy scores and intimate partner victimisation in the whole sample, controlling for IPV perpetration, engagement in deviant behaviours, age, sex, and research site. Our multinomial regression models focused on characteristics that distinguish between single‐event victimisation and recurring intimate partner victimisation. As shown in model two of Table 2, the relative risk of being a recurring victim of any intimate partner violence compared to a one‐time victim in the period increased by 14% for every unit increase in the antisocial behaviour facet scale of the PCL‐YV (RRR: 1.14, p < 0.05). Similarly, the relative risk of being a recurring victim of physical intimate partner violence increased by 4% for every unit increase in the total PCL‐YV (RRR: 1.04 and p < 0.05) and increased by 20% for every unit increase in the antisocial behaviour score facet (RRR: 1.20 and p < 0.05), compared to single‐event victims (models 3 and 4). Only the total PCL‐YV distinguished between multiple and single‐event victims of emtional intimate partner violence (models 5 and 6). Across all models, intimate partner violence perpetration tripled the relative risk of being a recurring victim compared to being a single‐event victim. Factors connected to deviant behaviour, such as carrying a gun or exposure to delinquent peers, were also significant predictors in some of the models. Demographic characteristics were not significant predictors of recurring victimisation, except in some models being from the Maricopa site showed significance.

The interpersonal manipulation facet's effect (RRR: 1.52 and p < 0.05) on being a recurring victim of any IPV victimisation was stronger for females (models 1 and 2) in that the relative risk of being a recurring victim compared to a single‐event victim of any intimate partner victimisation increased by 52% for every unit increase in the interpersonal manipulation score for women. The total PCL‐YV (RRR: 1.09 and p < 0.05) and interpersonal manipulation facet (RRR: 1.42 and p = 0.10) scores increased the relative risk of being a recurring victim of emotional intimate partner victimisation compared to single‐event victimisation by 9% for every unit increase in the total PCL‐YV scale and 42% for every unit increase in the interpersonal manipulation facet for women (models 5 and 6). Intimate partner violence perpetration remained significant in these models.

DISCUSSION

Although prior research studies have generally explored aspects of psychopathy relating to single and multiple victim experiences, our study is the first to specifically explore psychopathy relating to single and multiple episodes of intimate partner violence. Further, sex differences in this respect have not previously been studied. Over a third (38%) of the total sample of young offenders had experienced IPV victimisation, with 17% experiencing two or more IPV victimisation events. Rather more of the sample were victims of emotional IPV than physical IPV victimization (35% vs. 20%). Females (42%) experienced more IPV victimization than males (37%) and there were sex differences in rates of recurring IPV victimisation, with 21% of women and 17% of men experiencing any IPV victimisation. These findings are in line with previous research studies showing that such victimisation often occurs more than once (Xie & Lynch, 2017) but builds on them by showing these sex differences.

Secondly, we found that PCL‐YV scores measured at baseline and measurement of IPV victimisation during wave seven do appear to increase the risk of repeatedly becoming an IPV victim overall, and of emotional violence and physical violence specifically. These findings are consistent with findings relating to other forms of violent victimisation (Beaver et al., 2016; Daigle et al., 2020) and relative risk of several or single events (Daigle & Teasdale, 2018). It is likely that people with high PCL‐YV scores find it difficult to establish and maintain deep interpersonal connections, which may, for example, lead to volatile relationships, relating to emotional dysregulation (Jonason et al., 2011), relationship distress (Humbard et al., 2010) and impaired relating because of ‘interpersonal manipulation’ and ‘callous affect’ (Mager et al., 2014). In this sample of young offenders, only the antisocial behaviour facet was a significant predictor of being a later repeated victim of intimate partner violence, with a 14% increase in risk of any such victimisation and a 20% increase in risk of repeated physical victimisation compared to single‐event victims. This difference may, in part, arise from the difference in samples, with the one we worked with being, by definition, particularly high in antisocial behaviours. Being, effectively, in an antisocial culture may link people to similar peers and partners who place them at risk for recurring intimate partner victimisation. This finding is consistent with the lifestyles/routine activities perspective (Cohen & Felson, 1979; Hindelang et al., 1978) with its position that engaging in risky behaviour, such as acting aggressively, may lead to victimisation. Another perspective is that engaging in antisocial behaviours may translate into having a lack of clear long‐term goals that would benefit their romantic relationships, which could lead to aggressive acting on frustrations from their partners (South et al., 2020); by the same token, lack of goals may increase the likelihood that young victims do or say things that cause harm without thought of the long‐term consequences of their actions (Reidy et al., 2017).

Our third main finding is of some evidence that high PCL‐YV scores relate to recurring intimate partner victimisation differently based on sex, type of intimate victimisation and PCL‐YV facet. The interpersonal manipulation facet, for example, seemed more relevant for female participants, for whom, unlike the male participants, it differentiated between single and recurring intimate partner victimisations overall. This may be linked to cultural norms associated with sex. Males are socialised in ways that promote dominance and freedom to dictate relationship norms, whereas females are socialised to interact in more agreeable and nurturing ways (Endendijik et al., 2020). Drawing from the social interactionist perspective (Felson, 1992)—which posits that social control attempts may occur when social norms are violated—it could be that when females engage in behaviours common in the interpersonal manipulation facet, partners view these as violating societal norms. In response, these behaviours may elicit a social control attempt that includes violence by their partner to assert dominance (McCarry, 2010; Papp et al., 2017). It is also possible that females with higher interpersonal manipulation tendencies select partners, with similar tendencies, contributing to recurring victimisation. This could be connected to harmful relationship scripts—for example, a perception that jealousy is a sign of intensely romantic attachment (Hartwell et al., 2015) and, through this romanticisation, they tolerate or even welcome behaviours that others consider unequivocally abusive (Hayes & Jeffries, 2013; Papp et al., 2017).

Our findings can be used to inform prevention efforts and clinical practices. Interventions designed to reinforce positive relationship responses related to mental health pathology, for example, may be useful. This could include disorder‐specific interventions that help couples understand and manage conflict escalation that may arise because of behaviours derived from mental health symptomatology (Baucom et al., 2014). Practitioners may also find it useful to include treatment that targets psychopathy facets. Wong and colleagues (2012), for example, suggest using a combination of (1) dynamic risk assessment tools specific to people displaying traits of psychopathy like Violence Risk Scale (Wong & Gordon, 2013) to guide treatment design, and (2) cognitive behavioural and skills‐based interventions administered by a small cohesive team (rather than individually) to proactively minimise treatment interfering behaviors like manipulation of others, lying, and antisocial behaviours more generally. As these researchers argue, though traits of psychopathy may seem static in nature, disruption of antisocial behaviours and dysfunctional lifestyles common for those high in interpersonal manipulation and antisocial behaviour facets is possible through targeted treatment models (Wong et al., 2012).

Limitations

Despite its contributions, our study does have limitations. First, the sample consists of 19‐year‐olds who were adjudicated for serious delinquent behaviour, limiting generalisability on several grounds. Not only might there be other explanations for intimate partner violence among others who are not otherwise antisocial but also our work was with people who, by virtue of their youth, were only at the beginning of trying to establish romantic relationships, particularly lasting relationships. Secondly, the number of female participants was quite small; thus, it is possible that there was insufficient power to detect other relationships between PCL‐YV scores and recurring IPV victimisation. Thirdly, the PCL‐YV was administered three years before IPV victimisation, though research indicates that scores remain relatively stable across time (Lynam et al., 2009). Although our research study finds a link between higher PCL‐YV scores and recurring intimate partner victimisation, our findings do not explicitly identify the mechanisms through which this arises.

CONCLUSION

Despite these limitations, this study contributes valuable findings in demonstrating a link between different facets of psychopathy and risk of recurring intimate partner victimisation, and disentangled sex differences. Specifically, the interpersonal manipulation facet impacting recurring IPV victimisation for women and antisocial behaviours facet heightening the risk more generally. Perhaps through disorder‐specific interventions that are designed to be reflective of sex differences and to address traits commonly found in interpersonal manipulation and antisocial behaviours facet of psychopathy, intervening during young adulthood may change the trajectory of one's life course. Thus, if clinicians can effectively target traits like manipulation, lying, and general antisociality early in adulthood, it is possible to change one's trajectory of continued IPV victimisation and (possibly) criminal justice involvement to a trajectory of resilience and recovery from trauma.
