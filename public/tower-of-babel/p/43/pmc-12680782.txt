Development and evaluation of sample preparation methods for the determination of organic micropollutants in soil samples utilizing GC-APCI-QToF MS
Authors: Nikolaos Boinis, Nikolaos Dragazis, Eleni I. Panagopoulou, Georgios Gkotsis, Nikolaos S. Thomaidis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Soil pollution poses a serious threat to terrestrial ecosystems and human health, yet studies often focus on a handful of pollutant classes per study. Wide-scope methodologies are necessary to account for the complexity and diversity of the soil matrix and the organic micropollutant mixture of both known and novel compounds it may contain. Thus, to facilitate comprehensive monitoring of the organic micropollutant burden in soil with gas chromatography coupled with high-resolution mass spectrometry (GC-HRMS), three wide-scope methods were developed and compared: modified QuEChERS (mQuEChERS), Accelerated Solvent Extraction (ASE), and Ultrasonic Assisted Extraction (UAE). Comparison of the three methods and the final choice of the modified QuEChERS were achieved via an examination of the performance characteristics, such as the number of analytes detected, recoveries, matrix effect, and precision, through a smart validation scheme that encompassed 38 analytes belonging to diverse pollutant classes. The mQuEChERS method was then fully validated for the simultaneous quantification of 75 analytes, including pesticides, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs), polychlorinated naphthalenes (PCNs), and organochlorine pesticides (OCPs). The method’s limits of detection (MLOD) ranged from 0.04 to 2.77 μg kg−1 d.w. Linearity was demonstrated within a concentration range of 30 to 300 μg kg−1 d.w. Recoveries ranged from 70 to 120%. Method precision was deemed optimal (RSD < 11%). As proof of concept, six soil samples from Greece were analyzed, with results indicative of potential temporal variations in pollutant concentration, underscoring the necessity for extensive monitoring campaigns employing GC-HRMS.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00216-025-06187-2.

Introduction

Soil is a fundamental component of terrestrial ecosystems, supporting biodiversity and playing a crucial role in maintaining environmental health. It regulates essential elements like carbon and nitrogen, thereby mitigating climate change and managing toxic substances. Furthermore, soil functions as both a receptor and a disposal site for micropollutants, absorbing them from various anthropogenic activities, including agriculture and industrial processes [1]. These pollutants can accumulate in soil and subsequently enter the food chain, posing health risks to humans and animals. Beyond its role as a receptor, soil can also contribute to environmental contamination, facilitating the release of hazardous substances, such as pesticides and microplastics, in groundwater, surface water, or the atmosphere [2]. Contaminants bind to soil through processes like electrostatic attraction or partitioning in the organic phase. Under certain environmental conditions, they can migrate back to air and water, potentially re-entering organisms through various exposure pathways [3]. Of particular concern is the increased soil sorption, which can lead to the transfer of organic micropollutants to crops, endangering health when these edible plants are consumed [4].

A thorough review of global scientific research of recent studies was performed, focusing on the appropriate methods for the extraction and purification of organic micropollutants from soil samples for efficient GC-HRMS analysis. Many of the studies employed large amounts of solvents, with chlorinated organic solvents being used frequently. Soxhlet, along with UAE and ASE, was predominantly applied. These techniques were paired with purification methods such as column chromatography and, in some cases, solid-phase extraction (SPE) or automated clean-up instrumentation [5–8]. Most studies involving the identification of micropollutants in soil are facilitated via GC-LRMS, often targeting one or two pollutant classes. Furthermore, the primary emphasis was placed on priority organic pollutants (POPs), while emerging contaminants were infrequently considered within this context [6–12]. Thus, various contaminants often go unnoticed, and their concentrations are underreported when the sample preparation method does not provide a wide accessible chemical domain. In contrast, the application of a comprehensive, wide-scope sample preparation method combined with an extensive dataset facilitates the simultaneous analysis of emerging contaminants (ECs), including pesticides, and persistent organic pollutants (POPs). This approach not only enhances analytical efficiency but also reduces environmental impact regarding the consumption of solvents and energy resources. Finally, GC-HRMS offers a powerful platform for both target and non-target screening, with the added advantage of retrospective screening capabilities in environmental studies owing to its high mass accuracy [13].

All the above dictate the critical need to develop an effective, comprehensive, and wide-scope methodology for the determination of organic micropollutants in soil. Thus, the current study aims to develop, evaluate, and implement a new sample preparation protocol designed to target a broad range of organic contaminants. The proposed method aimed to facilitate the simultaneous detection of multiple categories of organic micropollutants in soil, thereby expanding the range of chemicals that can be accessed.

The present study focuses on the development and comparison of three sample preparation protocols aimed at detecting organic micropollutants in soil samples using GC-APCI-QToF MS. Among these, the modified QuEChERS method was identified as the most effective protocol for comprehensive screening. The validation of this method broadens the accessible chemical domain by targeting various pollutant classes with differing physicochemical properties through GC-HRMS analysis. Furthermore, the validated method was applied to real-world soil samples, resulting in the identification of numerous organic micropollutants at hyper-trace concentration levels.

Existing methods often focus on subsets of pollutants with similar properties, even though they leverage GC-HRMS instrumentation [14–19]. As a result, the present study fills a critical gap in the literature by addressing the challenge of simultaneously extracting and accurately quantifying organic micropollutants spanning a wide range of polarity, volatility, and chemical stability. The developed protocol yields final extracts that encompass a wide scope of different compounds. Thus, by broadening the analytical coverage of the method, while achieving sensitivity for trace-level priority and emerging pollutants, a more reliable environmental risk assessment can be achieved by high-resolution target and non-target screening. This advancement also aligns with trends toward eco-friendly, efficient, and high-throughput sample preparation methods suited for diverse organic micropollutants in complex soil matrices [20–22].

Evaluation and modification of methods

Regarding the extraction technique, it was observed that ASE, UAE, and QuEChERS were ideal for scaling into a wide-scope approach and provided high-quality qualitative results for a broad range of pollutants. In contrast, methods such as Soxhlet extraction proved to be time- and resource-consuming, focusing only on specific compound classes outside the scope of the present study. These three techniques offer various advantages, such as the minimal use of large volumes of organic solvents, their efficiency in time, and their cost-effective, environmentally friendly solutions. Regarding the extraction and purification pairing, it was efficient to combine ASE with simultaneous solid-phase extraction (SPE) in the extraction cell, while QuEChERS and UAE were combined with traditional solid-phase extraction (SPE) employing Florisil cartridges. However, methodological improvements were performed based on an extensive study of scientific articles and their critical evaluation. Dispersive SPE (dSPE), which is traditionally applied in most QuEChERS applications, often incorporates graphitized carbon black (GCB), necessitating the use of toluene to obtain satisfactory recoveries of planar pesticides. However, toluene is not considered an environmentally friendly solvent, nor is it benign in terms of human health, given its well-documented adverse effects [23, 24]. Additionally, the QuEChERS method was modified to include an ultrasonic extraction step to enhance its capabilities [25]. For purification purposes, a solvent change was employed to transition from a polar solvent (acetonitrile) to a semi-polar or non-polar solvent (hexane and acetone), allowing for clean-up using in-house Florisil cartridges. Automated clean-up instruments were not used due to unavailability, and traditional purification methods like filtration and column chromatography were excluded due to their limited applicability for multi-class pollutants, which limits their application in more comprehensive analytical approaches.

Description of methods

Detailed descriptions, along with graphs illustrating each step of the three developed methods, can be found in the SI. In brief, all three methods used 5.00 g of freeze-dried soil sample, and the final extracts they yielded were evaporated under a gentle nitrogen stream, reconstituted in hexane, and filtered through regenerated cellulose filters to 200 μL, so their respective preconcentration factors are equal (PF = 25), which facilitated their comparison. The mQuEChERS method used 5 mL of water and 10 mL of acetonitrile with shaking and an ultrasonic bath to facilitate extraction. Then, the supernatant was collected, and the following solid reagents were added: 4 g MgSO4, 1 g NaCl. After more shaking, the supernatant was evaporated with the addition of 50 μL of isooctane, and the solvent was changed to 4 mL of 20% acetone in hexane. The purification step by Florisil cartridges that followed employed activation with 4 mL of hexane and 4 mL of 20% acetone in hexane and elution with 4 mL of the latter solvent mixture. The UAE method employed 15 mL of hexane and 15 mL of hexane/acetone (1/1, V/V) in succession with an ultrasonic bath to facilitate extraction. The collected supernatants were further purified using the same SPE methodology with the mQuEChERS method before evaporation and reconstitution in the vial. The ASE method utilized simultaneous extraction and purification inside the cell of the instrumentation. This was facilitated by placing 1 g of Florisil, 1 g of silica, and 1 g of aluminium oxide in three distinct layers at the bottom of the cell, followed by resting the sample on top. The extraction solvents used were a mixture of hexane, dichloromethane, and acetone (2:1:1 by volume). The extraction parameters of the ASE method can be found in SM Table S10. The purified extract was then reduced in volume through a rotary evaporator before evaporation until dryness.

Sample collection

A sampling campaign was conducted in November 2024 in an area of Attica, Greece, where different low-contaminated soil samples were collected and used for the development of the method. Before the preparation of samples for analysis, all specimens underwent a lyophilization process, followed by homogenization. Subsequently, the processed samples were combined to create a representative composite soil sample, ensuring the integrity and reliability of the analytical results. Furthermore, the validated method was applied to real samples, sourced from an industrial area in Attica, Greece. A total of six samples were collected from two distinct sampling locations within the region over three separate dates: December 2018, July 2019, and October 2019. Samples S1 to S3 were taken from sampling site 1, situated near a river delta and an industrial zone, corresponding to the dates December 2018, July 2019, and October 2019, respectively. Samples S4 to S6 were obtained from sampling site 2, located upstream in a mixed agricultural and industrial zone, also corresponding to the same three dates: December 2018, July 2019, and October 2019. Before the preparation of samples for analysis, all specimens underwent a lyophilization process, followed by homogenization. Subsequently, the processed samples were combined to create a representative composite soil sample, ensuring the integrity and reliability of the analytical results.

Method validation

The optimized and modified QuEChERS method was fully validated for 75 analytes, which included organic micropollutants from various classes, both CECs and POPs. Namely, the compounds included 16 PAHs, 19 PCBs, 5 PCNs, 16 OCPs, and 19 Pesticides. The selectivity of the method was ensured through successfully identifying three specific qualifier ions per analyte at a specific retention time. No interfering signals from endogenous components or other analytes were detected in the retention time range of the analyte. Fortified sample calibration curves, with concentration levels of 30, 50, 80, 100, 150, 200, and 300 μg·kg⁻1, were validated for their linearity by calculating the determination coefficient R2 and the statistical significance of the intercept (b) and the slope (a), through analysis of variance (ANOVA) at a confidence level of 95%. Recovery was evaluated across three distinct concentration levels, which included Level 1 = 30 μg·kg⁻1, Level 2 = 100 μg·kg⁻1, and Level 3 = 300 μg·kg⁻1. This systematic approach allows for a comprehensive assessment of recovery efficiency at varying concentrations, facilitating a robust analysis of the methodology employed. To evaluate the method’s precision, soil samples were spiked with all analytes in triplicate at three concentrations: the lowest concentration of 30 μg·kg−1 (Level 1), a medium concentration of 100 μg·kg−1 (Level 2), and a high concentration of 300 μg·kg−1 (Level 3). The precision of the method is expressed as the RSD of the repeated measurement in each concentration level and must not exceed 20% for analysis of organic micropollutants in environmental matrices, such as soil. Inter-day precision was determined by the analysis of three replicates of the fortified soil samples of 100 μg·kg−1 on two consecutive days, and calculating the RSD of their %R. This parameter was determined by varying process conditions, which were the different days of extraction, different solvent bottles, and different lab-made SPE cartridges.

The method’s limit of detection (MLOD) and method’s limit of quantification (MLOQ) were estimated utilizing the calibration curve of fortified soil samples within the concentration range of 30–300 μg·kg−1, at concentrations of 30, 50, 80, 100, 150, 200, and 300 μg·kg−1, for each analyte. Firstly, the MLOD was calculated using the following dependence: MLOD = (3.3 × SD/b)/PF, where b is the slope of the fortified sample curve, SD is the residual standard deviation of the fortified sample curve, and PF is the preconcentration factor of the method (PF = 25). Then, the limit of quantification was calculated as MLOQ = 3 × MLOD. To achieve optimal results, relative areas (Analyte Peak Area/IS Peak Area) were utilized for the calculation of calibration curves and spiked sample curves. This approach incorporated a weighted response from the instrumentation, which was based on the ideal internal standard corresponding to each specific analyte.

To determine the matrix effects in the soil samples using the modified QuEChERS method, all 75 analytes were spiked into soil extracts (Matrix Matched samples) and pure hexane solvent (Standard Solutions, STDs) at three concentration levels: 30, 100, and 300 μg·kg−1. The MF was, then, calculated using Formula 2, while %ME was estimated via Formula 3.

Method application

As a proof of concept, the validated method was implemented to analyze six soil samples sourced from a mixed agricultural and industrial area in Attica, Greece. The aim was to assess the efficacy of the modified QuEChERS method in detecting organic micropollutants in soil, contributing valuable insights into soil quality and environmental safety in industrial contexts. For quantification, fortified soil samples were used to compensate for potential recovery losses. Pooled soil samples were spiked with all analytes to get calibration standards at seven levels, from 30 to 300 μg·kg−1, as mentioned previously. The procedural standards were processed in the same batch and the same way as the soil samples.

Instrumentation

The soil samples were analyzed utilizing a gas chromatography system (456-GC, Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany) coupled, via an Atmospheric Pressure Chemical Ionization (APCI) source in positive ionization mode, to a hybrid quadrupole time-of-flight (QTOF) ion mass analyzer (maXis impact, Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany). The gas chromatography system used had an automatic sampler (CP-8400, Bruker Daltonics GmbH) for sample injection. The overall instrumentation consisted of a GC-APCI-QTOF MS, and the experimental parameters of the instruments are described in detail in the SI.

Data treatment

Target screening was performed using an in-house developed GC target list, which is available at the following link: https://zenodo.org/record/3753372. The data treatment was performed using TASQ Client 2025b and DataAnalysis 5.1 (Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany) software. Evaluation of the data was based on a thorough examination of Extracted Ion Chromatograms of the parent ions, as well as fragment and adduct ions. Detection was based on strict identification criteria (mass accuracy < 2 mDa, retention time shift ± 0.1 min, isotopic fitting < 100 mSigma (only for confirmation of positive findings)), whereas the presence of adduct and fragment ions confirmed the analytes [27]. More information about the identification criteria and the software used can be found in SI, Fig. S1. The contaminants that were detected in traces below the MLOQ (concentration levels between the MLOD and MLOQ values) were reported as BMQL (below the quantification limit). For statistical treatment of the results, substitution of BMQL with MLOQ/2 may be performed, as indicated by Directive 2009/90/EC [28].

Quality assurance and quality control (QA/QC)

A thorough quality assurance and quality control (QA/QC) was applied during the sample preparation and instrumental analysis. A mix of internal standards was added to each sample before extraction to ensure satisfactory recovery of the target compounds, and samples spiked with a mix of known CECs were also analyzed in each batch of samples. Moreover, a procedural blank (reagent blank) was prepared to assess any external contamination that might have been brought in during the sample preparation of the extracts and analysis. A mix of known analytes (RI calibrant substances) was used to assess the stability of retention time during instrumental analysis [29]. A QC sample was run every 10 injections to ensure the good operation and high sensitivity of the instrument. The high mass accuracy of the GC-APCI-QToF MS system was ensured via the use of perfluorotributylamine (FC43) mass calibrant, which provides stable and well-defined ions at known mass-to-charge (m/z) values that span a wide range (from 30 to 1000 Da). Both external and internal calibration were implemented, allowing the instrumentation to deliver highly precise mass measurements that enabled the confident identification and quantification of analytes with mass deviation well below 1 ppm. Specificity of the methods described in the present study was achieved via the use of GC-HRMS instrumentation that yielded high mass accuracy MS and MS/MS data. This was further enhanced through the combination of retention times provided by the GC system, along with fragmentation pattern data, providing a robust method for unambiguous identification and quantification of complex mixtures. Coeluting and isobaric compounds were distinguished based on their unique fragment ion spectra and isotopic distributions. This ensured specificity in the analysis of chromatographic peak overlaps, thereby enhancing the reliability of compound identification.

Results and discussion

Method comparison

Three different sample preparation methods (mQuEChERS, ASE, and UAE) were compared based on their performance characteristics via a smart validation scheme. For this reason, 38 compounds from 4 distinct pollutant classes were spiked into soil samples collected from an area in Attica, Greece. Detailed information regarding the analytes used, along with the full results of the smart validation of the three methods, can be found in SI Tables S1, S2, and S3.

Trueness and precision

Regarding the mQuEChERS method, all 38 compounds used in the experiments were detected at both concentration levels. With the ASE method, 5 compounds were not detected at the 100 μg·kg−1 level, while 9 were not determined at the 50 μg·kg−1 level. The UAE method failed to extract 20 and 15 analytes at the high and low concentration levels, respectively. When using the mQuEChERS method, (%R) for the 100 μg·kg−1 level ranged from 64 to 134% with a mean recovery of 105%. Sixty-three percent of the analytes displayed %R in the range of 70 to 120%, while 34% showed recoveries above 120%, and 3% were below 70%. On the other hand, with the ASE method, 29 analytes displayed acceptable quality criteria at both concentration levels, with recoveries ranging from 32 to 138% (mean %R = 87%) for the 100 μg·kg−1 level. Finally, regarding the UAE method, only 18 analytes satisfied the quality standards for the two concentration levels, with recoveries from 48 to 105% (mean %R = 77%) for the 100 μg·kg−1 level (Fig. 1). Respectively, for the 50 μg·kg−1 level, the mQuEChERS method’s recoveries ranged from 30 to 125% with a mean of 77% (Fig. 2). Fifty-eight percent of the analytes displayed %R in the range of 70% to 120%, while 37% were below 70%, and 5% were above 120%. The recoveries displayed by the ASE method ranged from 31 to 125% (mean %R = 78%) for the 50 μg·kg−1 level. Lastly, the UAE method presented recoveries varying from 28 to 129% (mean %R = 77%) for the 50 μg·kg−1 level.

All analytes, including those with recovery rates outside the 70%–120% range, exhibited consistency, as indicated by an RSD of less than 20% (Tables S2 and S4). Therefore, a recovery range of 30% to 140% was considered acceptable for the analytical validation of the three methods. The %ME of the mQuEChERS method was the most uniform, with all analytes showing response reduction in the matrix, while the ASE and UAE methods showed both enhancement and reduction.

Method selection

Considering all available data on the method quality criteria mentioned earlier, mQuEChERS was selected as the most robust and suitable method compared to the ASE or UAE sample preparation methods. The modified QuEChERS method successfully detected all analytes, which represent compounds from various pollutant classes with differing physicochemical properties, making the method ideal for the wide-scope screening of organic micropollutants in soil. Additionally, all quality criteria were met within the method’s established quality limits, with RSD < 20%, %R between 30 and 140%, and low variability in %ME (–54%). The other two methods demonstrated performance characteristics that fell outside the objectives of the current study, which focused on a wide-scope target approach involving multiple analytes from various pollutant classes. The ASE method exhibited inadequate performance in extracting PCB 153, the OCP dieldrin, and certain PAHs, while the UAE method performed even worse, with several analytes from different classes missing from both concentration levels. Finally, another major advantage of the mQuEChERS method is that it adheres to the principles of Green Analytical Chemistry, as it does not require chlorinated solvents, consumes little solvent and energy, and produces very little waste, representing a safe, nontoxic, environmentally friendly, and efficient alternative to ASE and UAE [30, 31].

Measurement of %ME is an important issue as the selectivity of a proposed method is investigated. Depending on the environment in which the ionization and ion evaporation processes take place, this competition may effectively decrease (ion suppression) or increase (ion enhancement) the efficiency of the formation of the desired analyte ions present at the same concentrations in the interface. Ideally, the %ME is equal to zero, meaning that neither ion suppression nor enhancement takes place. It must be noticed that negative values of ME% indicate ion suppression while positive values indicate ion enhancement. In total, a very high %ME (< −70% and > 70%) was observed in 32%, 31%, and 32% for the compounds extracted through the mQuEChERS, ASE, and UAE methods, respectively. A recognized approach to mitigating matrix effects in GC analysis is the addition of analyte protectants, such as D-sorbitol, L-gluconic acid γ-lactone, or 3-ethoxy-1,2-propanediol, in excess to both spiked solvent samples and matrix-matched samples. Analyte protectants can affect analytes similarly to matrix components [32]. They interact with the active sites in the liner and column, effectively masking them and increasing the amount of analytes that reach the detector, thereby enhancing the detector response. Consequently, the addition of analyte protectants in excess to both matrix-matched and solvent samples provides a viable method to equalize the detector responses of the analytes in each sample [33].

Method validation

In this study, the selected method was validated for 75 analytes, which corresponds to 18% of the National and Kapodistrian University of Athens GC-HRMS database [34]. These compounds represent almost all the classes of pollutants in the wide-scope target database and cover an extensive chemical space characterized by a varying array of physicochemical properties. All the information about the analytes, as well as the validation data, is shown in SI Tables S5 to S8. All analytes showed linear responses in concentration ranges from the lowest concentration level of 30 μg·kg−1 dry weight (d.w.) to the highest of 300 μg·kg−1 d.w. The correlation coefficients (R2), along with the respective linear equation values for each analyte, can be found in SI Table S5. The R2 factors were above 0.985 for all the tested compounds in spike samples.

The sensitivity of the method was deemed satisfactory, with the MLODs spanning from 0.04 to 2.8 μg·kg−1 d.w. Specifically, the ranges for various compounds were as follows: PAHs from 0.13 to 1.6 μg·kg−1 d.w., PCBs from 0.04 to 1.5 μg·kg−1 d.w., OCPs from 0.74 to 2.7 μg·kg−1 d.w., pesticides from 0.14 to 2.8 μg·kg−1 d.w., and PCNs from 1.7 to 2.3 μg·kg−1 d.w. The MLOQs ranged from 0.11 to 8.4 μg·kg−1 d.w. across the range of analytes. The ranges for each pollutant class were as follows: PAHs from 0.38 to 4.7 μg·kg−1 d.w., PCBs from 0.11 to 4.6 μg·kg−1 d.w., OCPs from 2.2 to 8.2 μg·kg−1 d.w., pesticides from 0.43 to 8.4 μg·kg−1 d.w., and PCNs from 5.2 to 8.4 μg·kg−1 d.w. The method’s sensitivity was deemed satisfactory, considering that pollutants from various classes can effectively be detected and quantified at ultra-trace concentration levels, nearing the low parts per billion range. The %R was calculated using Eq. 2. According to SANTE/11312/2021 [26], the recovery should be between 70 and 120%; however, a recovery in the range of 30–140% is acceptable if consistent (RSD < 20%). More than 80% of the analytes in this study had a mean recovery in the range of 70 to 120% for the three concentration levels, while 20%, 4%, and 3% showed a recovery below 70% for levels 1 to 3, respectively. %R above 120% was not recorded in Level 1, while for levels 2 and 3, it exceeded this threshold for 7% and 15% of the analytes, respectively. However, the analytes with a mean recovery outside the desired range (Table S6) showed consistency, with RSD < 15% at all levels used for recovery determination, with a maximum RSD of 11% (as shown in Fig. 8). As the concentration increased across the three concentration levels, the %ME shifted from negative values that suggest signal suppression towards positive values, thus implying signal enhancement in the ion source (Fig. 7). The method’s precision was deemed satisfactory, with the respective RSD of repeatability (intra-day precision) and reproducibility (inter-day precision) both well below the designated limit of 10%.

The lower %R and higher %ME observed in some compounds can be explained by their physicochemical properties, as well as the nature of the soil matrix. Strong absorption of planar PAHs, OCPs, PCBs, and PCNs to soil organic matter and clay particles leads to poor extractability, resulting in low recoveries for some compounds. Their planar, hydrophobic structures cause tight binding through hydrophobic and π-π interactions, while physical entrapment within soil micropores further limits solvent access. The chemical stability of these compounds reduces their desorption efficiency further [35–37]. Additionally, co-extracted soil constituents like humic acids and lipids interfere with analyte ionization, resulting in higher matrix effects from the complex soil matrix. Chromatographic overlaps with similar matrix compounds amplify these issues, complicating accurate detection and quantification [38, 39]. Careful selection of extraction parameters, along with matrix-matched calibration and high mass accuracy data acquisition, is essential to overcome these challenges for comprehensive pollutant analysis in soil samples.

Method application

In total, 31 different analytes were detected in the soil samples, with a minimum of 17 and a maximum of 27 per sample. These organic micropollutants were comprised of 16 PAH congeners, 2 PCBs (PCB 28, PCB 52), 5 OCPs, including DDT and its transformation products DDE and DDD, in addition to 8 pesticides. A comprehensive list of all analytes that were detected, along with their corresponding amounts, pollutant classes, MLODs, and MLOQs, is provided in SI Table S9. Some analytes could be detected in all samples, but just below or close to their corresponding MLOQ, representing chronic pollution in low amounts through soot, dust, air, and water. Among those analytes were two of the PAHs (Acenaphthene, Dibenzo(a,h)anthracene), the dioxin-like fungicide Hexachlorobenzene (HCB), two PCBs (PCB 28, PCB 52), and the chlorotriazine herbicides Propazine and Simazine.

The analysis indicated that PAHs exhibited the highest observed concentrations, recorded at approximately one to two orders of magnitude greater than those of other analyzed compounds. Notably, Phenanthrene exhibited a concentration of 34.4 μg·kg⁻1 d.w. in sample S3 (see Fig. S5 in SI for an Extracted Ion Chromatogram that includes comparison between real-world soil sample and standard solution). Naphthalene was recorded at a concentration of 19.5 μg·kg−1 d.w. in sample S5, while Pyrene demonstrated a concentration of 10.0 μg·kg−1 d.w. in sample S4. These findings highlight the varying distribution of PAHs within the examined samples. The second-highest concentration by classification was observed in the pesticides, and especially Pendimethalin (see Fig. S6 in SI for an Extracted Ion Chromatogram that includes comparison between real-world soil sample and standard solution), with Cmax of 3.43 μg·kg−1 d.w., and Amitraz following with Cmax of 2.87 μg·kg−1 d.w., both found in sample S4. Apart from concentration, a useful tool for understanding the risk that pollutants pose to environmental health is the frequency of appearance (FoA). Eleven out of the sixteen PAH congeners were consistently detected in all samples, achieving a frequency of occurrence (FoA) of 100%. Notably, Acenapthylene, Fluoranthene, and Benz(a)anthracene exhibited a reduced FoA of 83%. In contrast, Dibenzo(a,h)anthracene and Acenaphthene demonstrated significantly lower frequencies of occurrence, with values of 33% and 17%, respectively. This differential detection underscores the varying persistence and prevalence of specific PAH congeners in the samples analyzed. The transformation product of DDT, 4,4-DDE, appeared in all samples (100% FoA), while a-HCH was detected in 5 out of the 6 samples (83% FoA).

Most of the detected analytes are produced in very high amounts because of their use in various agricultural applications, e.g., Pendimethalin, an herbicide of the dinitroaniline group, applied for the control of weeds in cereal, legume, and vegetable crops, which was detected in concentrations ranging from BQL to 3.43 μg·kg−1 d.w. [40]. Additionally, Amitraz, a widely used insecticide and antiparasitic agent that affects various pathways of mammalian metabolism, was detected in relatively high concentrations of up to 2.87 μg·kg−1 d.w. [41]. On the other hand, several analytes are produced in large amounts as side products of industrial and urban activities, such as PAHs, which are mainly released into the environment by the combustion of coal or fossil fuel, with Phenanthrene, Naphthalene, and Pyrene reaching concentrations of 34.4, 19.5, and 10.0 μg·kg−1 d.w., respectively [42]. Furthermore, several of the analytes identified in this study, such as the banned insecticide 2,4-DDT and its transformation products 4,4-DDE and 4,4-DDD, were detected with a total concentration of DDTs ranging from 0.450 to 4.08 μg·kg−1 d.w., despite the prohibition of their manufacturing and use more than 50 years ago. These compounds are known for their persistence in the environment and exhibit lipophilicity, low rates of chemical and biological degradation, as well as low vapor pressure. Additionally, a-HCH and its isomers, all of which are well-documented for their carcinogenic properties, were also found in concentrations from to 1.06 μg·kg−1 d.w. [43, 44].

Sampling site 1, situated in proximity to industrial complexes, exhibited notably higher cumulative concentrations of PAHs, with a maximum of 59.4 μg·kg−1 d.w. observed in sample S3. Furthermore, soil sourced from sampling site 1 displayed greater accumulation of legacy pollutants, including OCPs, which reached concentration levels of 5.04 μg·kg−1 d.w. in sample S3. About sampling site 2, the samples collected in this area exhibited elevated concentrations of pesticides identified as emerging pollutants, which are actively utilized in agricultural practices. This phenomenon can be attributed to the site’s proximity to regional agricultural zones, thereby highlighting the potential for agricultural runoff to impact local environmental quality [45]. Notably, sample S4 displayed the highest cumulative concentration of pesticides with 7.97 μg·kg−1 d.w. Finally, by examining the different sampling periods, it is evident that during Attica’s rainiest season of fall, the cumulative concentrations of pollutants increased in samples collected from sampling site 1. Furthermore, in October (related to samples S3 and S6), an increase in the total concentration of pollutants at sampling site 1 coincided with a decrease at sampling site 2. This indicates the migration of pollutants, influenced by complex ecosystem mechanisms and the water cycle [46]. These results suggest a time-dependent change in the contamination of soil samples. However, these two samples across three different time periods are not sufficient to draw reliable conclusions about the actual organic micropollutant burden in soil. A study with several samples over a longer period, across numerous sampling locations, is suggested to acquire a reliable understanding of the ecosystem mechanisms that drive pollutant concentration fluctuations.

Conclusions

The present study demonstrated the effective design and comparison of three different methods of sample preparation aimed at extracting a wide variety of organic micropollutants from soil. Method comparison was facilitated via a smart validation scheme, in which characteristic compounds from various pollutant classes are used to represent the larger dataset of the target database. The smart validation included 38 organic micropollutants, including 16 PAHs, 7 PCBs, 6 PCNs, and 9 OCPs. The most suitable method was concluded to be the modified QuEChERS, which uses 5.00 g of soil sample with acetonitrile and water for solid–liquid extraction, paired with a sonication bath. Phase separation is assisted by MgSO4 and NaCl. For clean-up of the extract, a change to organic solvents, hexane and acetone, facilitated the use of Florisil SPE cartridges. The mQuEChERS method then underwent comprehensive validation encompassing a diverse range of analytes, totaling 75 distinct compounds. This set includes various organic micropollutants categorized as CECs and persistent organic pollutants (POPs). Specifically, the analytes evaluated comprised 16 polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), 19 PCBs, 5 PCNs, 16 OCPs, and 19 additional pesticides. This rigorous validation underscores the method’s applicability across a broad spectrum of environmental contaminants. The MLODs ranged between 0.04 and 2.8 μg kg−1 d.w. Linearity was shown in a concentration range of 30 to 300 μg kg−1 d.w. Recoveries ranged from 70 to 120%. Method precision was ideal (RSD < 11%). Finally, the validated mQuEChERS method was applied in real-world soil samples from Attica, Greece, and managed to pinpoint ultra-trace concentration levels of 31 organic micropollutants, consisting of 16 PAHs congeners, 2 PCBs (PCB 28, PCB 52), 5 OCPs, including DDT and its transformation products DDE and DDD, in addition to 8 pesticides.

Considering the significance of soil as a fundamental component of terrestrial ecosystems and its functions as a receptor and disposal site for micropollutants as well as a facilitator for the release of hazardous substances into groundwater, surface water, or the atmosphere, the extraction of a wide variety of organic micropollutants from soil is imperative to the goal of a holistic understanding of soil pollution, which could guide future interventions and policy making for environmental protection and the preservation of multiple ecosystems. The mQuEChERS method validated in the present study, concluded to be the most suitable among the investigated methods, adequately covers a diverse range of analytes, which could promote a significant advancement in the available research methodology on the soil matrix, offering the worldwide scientific community the possibility to widen the scope of understanding in this area of concern.
