Does one have to be queer to care for queer patients? A qualitative interview study with general practitioners in Germany
Authors: Malte Radde, Philip Oeser, Anna Sophia Wittenstein, Eric Blumenauer, Marc Sebastian Melz, Wolfram J Herrmann
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLGBT* (lesbian, gay, bisexual, trans, others) individuals are known to experience poorer health and restricted access to healthcare compared to cisgender-heterosexual individuals.ObjectiveAs General Practitioners (GPs) are a patient’s first point of contact with the healthcare system in Germany, this study examines GPs’ perspectives on LGBT healthcare.MethodsIn this qualitative study, we conducted 19 semi-structured episodic interviews with GPs in urban and rural areas of Germany. The interviews were analysed using framework analysis.ResultsOur study shows LGBT* doctors feel more responsible for LGBT* healthcare than their cis-heterosexual colleagues. There is a perceived lack of knowledge and learning opportunities on LGBT* health needs for most interviewees. Cis-heterosexual GPs often feel unprepared to provide specific healthcare interventions to LGBT* patients and believe they lack the resources to improve their education on the topic. LGBT*-specific primary healthcare seems to be delivered by a small number of GP practices and primarily by LGBT* doctors. These LGBT* doctors recommend more widespread knowledge of LGBT* primary care and lives, to allow their colleagues to provide better care for these patients.ConclusionThere is a lack of knowledge on LGBT health needs and challenges among GPs in Germany, which needs to be addressed through structured training in medical school, and on a postgraduate level. This lack of knowledge leads to cis-heterosexual GPs feeling more insecure in providing care to LGBT* patients and LGBT* GPs providing a bigger share of LGBT* healthcare than their cis-heterosexual colleagues.Keywords: LGBT health, training and teaching, access to care

Introduction

Since 2016, the NIH (National Institute of Health) recognises sexual and gender minorities as populations experiencing health disparities [1]. LGBT* (lesbian, gay, bisexual, trans, other) individuals face a diverse set of challenges in their primary healthcare. Firstly, there are common health challenges related to mental health [2,3] and restricted access to care [4]. Each group also faces a distinct set of health challenges [5–9], one example being gender affirming hormone therapy (GAHT) for trans and nonbinary individuals. They also have higher risks for specific health problems, for example, 22-43% of trans individuals have a history of suicide attempts [10]. Men who have sex with men (MSM) are at risk of contracting an STI [11].

In Germany, there is limited knowledge on LGBT* health challenges [11]. Previous research has reported an increased risk for STIs in MSM [11] and a higher prevalence of loneliness and depressive symptoms for all LGBT* individuals during COVID, with trans, nonbinary and asexual individuals being especially affected by this [12]. Also, LGBT* individuals in Germany are more likely to be affected by burnout, chronic back pain, asthma and heart diseases than cis-heterosexual individuals [13].

LGBT*-identifying patients report negative healthcare experiences at high rates. Beigang et al. showed transgender identity and homosexuality to be overrepresented as grounds for discrimination in German healthcare in 2017 [14]. Similar findings were shown for Europe by Skuban et al. [15].

The World Professional Association for Transgender Health (WPATH) states in its 8th Standards of Care that inadequate training can contribute to health disparities, particularly for transgender individuals [7].

Medical training on LGBT* healthcare is still lacking globally [7,16] and in Germany [17] with medical students advocating for improvements [18]. Literature shows that many patients do not believe that their primary care providers are fit to care for them appropriately [19]. According to WONCA Europe, General Medicine is ‘providing open and unlimited access to its users, dealing with all health problems regardless of the age, sex, or any other characteristic of the person concerned’ [20]. Also, person-centred care has been established as a central concept in primary care [21].

In Germany, there is a well-established family medicine system where patients can choose any physician. Still, most patients prefer to have a fixed General Practitioner (GP) as their first point of contact with the healthcare system [12]. LGBT* individuals in Germany have a GP as often as cis-heterosexual individuals [12], which shows the importance of GPs acknowledging their patients’ sexuality and gender and the associated healthcare needs.

While there is considerable information on LGBT* patients’ views and needs [22–24] in primary healthcare, there is limited research on the physicians’ perspectives, especially among GPs in Germany [25]. Examples from international data show that some GPs feel embarrassed when working with LGBT* patients [26,27]. Most GPs feel neither well informed on LGBT* health needs and their clinical management, nor where to refer the LGBT* patients to further [27].

Thus, this research aims to explore German GPs’ perspectives on primary care for LGBT* individuals. We would like to understand if they feel responsible and well prepared to provide primary care to LGBT* patients.

Methods

To answer the research question, we opted for a qualitative study, conducting semi-structured episodic interviews and analysing them using Framework analysis.

Sampling

To explore both rural and urban settings, we recruited GPs in Berlin and the rural state of Brandenburg using iterative sampling. Recruitment occurred from December 2022 to February 2024, targeting all regions of Brandenburg and Berlin. Possible participants were contacted personally and also by mail, telephone and email.

Of the 362 doctors’ practices contacted, 28 (7.7%) responded. Among these, 5 refused participation and 4 were lost to follow-up, resulting in 19 interviews. Reasons for non-participation were not investigated. Recruitment ended on reaching thematic saturation with no new topics emerging from the last few interviews. Saturation was discussed within the research team.

Data collection

We conducted episodic interviews, aiming to use episodes from the participants’ memories as a guiding structure to gain answers for the research questions [28].

By providing participants with storytelling prompts, abstract questions and concrete questions, episodic interviews can be used to study participants’ semantic and episodic knowledge [28]. The interviews were based on an interview guide which was first designed through deductive category formation based on literature research, interviews from other parts of the project and the researcher’s personal experiences from practical work [29]. The guide was then tested by interviewing a GP. There were minor changes throughout data collection (final version: Supplement 1). The primary author, Mr. Malte Radde, conducted all interviews. He is a male medical student and this was his first interview study. The first author was supervised by two people – a professor and a medical doctor with extensive experience in qualitative research and a medical doctor and postdoctoral researcher with experience in research and General Practice. The other members of the research team were three medical students, all of them leading similar studies interviewing lesbian, gay, and transgender patients on their primary healthcare experiences.

Interviews were conducted in German, audio recorded and then transcribed verbatim using MAXQDA Plus 24 (released November 2023) software and anonymised by the first author. Field notes describing the setting and nonverbal cues were taken after each interview by the first author. Transcripts were not returned to participants for comment or correction.

Most interviews were in-person at the doctors’ practices, with two at the research institute and one by phone due to participant constraints. Apart from the participant and the interviewer, no one was present in the face-to-face interviews. Interviews went on from April 2023 to February 2024. There were no repeat interviews.

There was no prior relationship between any participant and the interviewer. Participants were informed that this being part of the interviewer’s MD thesis.

Analysis

After transcription, analysis was done in the same software, using framework analysis by Ritchie and Lewis [30]. This method allowed for flexibility and analysis of issues at focus before starting the research and issues emerging from the interviews [30]. It also provided more transparency through structure when compared to other qualitative methods [31]. Relevant statements were coded, paraphrased, and summarised into 7 thematic charts with 4–13 subcategories (Supplement 2). They were then compiled into a master chart with 6 abstract categories to identify common patterns and typologies. Analysis was primarily done by the first author. The authors regularly consulted each other and other research institute members for feedback on preliminary results and analysis directions. The chosen categories reflect these discussions.

Quotes were translated and include the participant’s code, gender as male (M), female (F) or nonbinary (NB), and practice location in Brandenburg (BB) or Berlin (BE).

There was no participant feedback on the findings.

Data security and availability

Participants provided written informed consent for the interview, recording, transcription, and anonymised use of the data. They could withdraw consent before data analysis. The COREQ checklist was followed in writing this article [32]. The data underlying this article is stored at the research institute and will be shared on reasonable request to the corresponding author.

Ethical approval

The Ethics Commission of Charité – Universitätsmedizin Berlin approved this project (EA1/053/22).

Results

Four key topics were found to explain the current state of LGBT* primary care in Germany:

The perceived share of patients identifying as LGBT* in their practice;

Where they see responsibility for general LGBT* healthcare;

Where they see responsibility for general LGBT* healthcare;

Who they see as responsible for LGBT* specific healthcare interventions, with GAHT and STI diagnostics as examples and;

Who they see as responsible for LGBT* specific healthcare interventions, with GAHT and STI diagnostics as examples and;

GPs’ training, teaching and knowledge on LGBT* healthcare needs.

GPs’ training, teaching and knowledge on LGBT* healthcare needs.

Following a description of the study sample, each of the topics will be addressed individually.

Sample

Out of the 19 participating GPs, around a third described their practices as being specialised in HIV (2), queer patients (2), sexual and trans health (1) or as a ‘rainbow practice’ (1). All these participants also self-identified as members of the LGBT* community. Incidentally, we found five of those LGBT* participants to be part of a self-organised local network of GPs’ who share their knowledge on trans healthcare and hormone substitution therapy.

Four participants had their practices in towns in Brandenburg, ranging from 2500 to 13000 inhabitants. The other 15 were based in Berlin (Table 1).

Perceived share of patients identifying as LGBT* in the GPs’ practice

There was a wide range in the perceived share of LGBT* identifying patients in the interviewees’ practices - from believing to not have any, to estimating more than 90% of their patients to identify as queer or LGBT*.

One participant working in a small village in Brandenburg stated not to have any LGBT* patients. However, he reported speculations about possible homosexuality of some of his patients:

None. At least not from their side. You might only hear something like ‘might be’ and ‘there might be something’ or ‘possibly’ through the grapevine. But, to have someone openly say it, and I’ve been here since […] 2013, never had that. (A29, M, BB)

Other participants recalled seeing at least one trans person and multiple homosexual people in their practice.

Most doctors believed to have more LGBT* patients than they were aware of, with one doctor linking this to a possible fear of discrimination.

I guess there must be an issue, otherwise patients wouldn’t keep it behind closed doors. It would play a much more significant role otherwise, because sexuality does play a significant role. And when I’m more or less scared to tell my GP what my sexuality is like, then there’s probably barriers […]. At least in the rural areas, it was not like that at all in Berlin. (A33, F, BB)

The same participant talked about the importance of establishing a trustworthy relationship with patients to allow them to feel safe enough to come out to their providers.

So that was a long process, I’ve known that patient for a long time before doing this. He always talked about his ‘better half’. So then one day I just said ‘so what is your husband’s stance on this?’ And he was so incredibly relieved after I said that. (A33, F, BB)

Others pointed out the importance of using sensitive language in developing rapport, so that patients would feel safe enough to come out to their doctors. One example was not assuming the gender when using patients’ last name to call them in from the waiting area.

Doctors with more knowledge of LGBT* healthcare, who were usually also LGBT* themselves, reported to have more LGBT* patients in their practice, even when not publicly advertising as LGBT* friendly.

Responsibility for general LGBT* healthcare

GPs reported a wide range of opinions on their role in LGBT* healthcare. While some doctors believed general LGBT* healthcare to be specifically a task for LGBT* identifying doctors, most believed it to be every GPs’ task.

Some cis-heterosexual GPs felt less responsibility in providing care for LGBT* individuals, and perceived that doctors who are members of the LGBT* community are primarily responsible.

I don’t know how others would be doing it, I wonder how they do it, I would have to ask my gay colleague. He will for sure be open, for questions and such, in his practice. (A32, F, BE)

Others viewed all GPs as responsible in providing primary healthcare to LGBT* individuals, preferring to have a wide variety of patients in every practice and also seeing a need for further training of GPs to provide more adequate care.

Another special thing for them? No! Colourful. Mix everything up, everyone in one GPs practice. I’d rather have us train the doctor more comprehensively. (A43, F, BE)

Some participants suggested that, while there were LGBT* patients who would prefer a specialised GP, others instead might prefer to have a GP who does not focus on LGBT* healthcare.

Many people probably don’t want […] such an open GPs’ practice but would rather disappear in the crowd and be treated like every other patient. (A6, M, BE)

One queer GP stated a need for more GPs to be ‘queer-friendly’ and provide safe spaces in their practices, because GPs who provide specific care for subgroups of LGBT* patients are often overrun with LGBT* patients looking for non-discriminatory care.

Responsibility for LGBT* specific healthcare interventions

Besides responsibilities in general primary healthcare, GPs shared opinions on the responsibility to provide LGBT* specific healthcare interventions such as GAHT or STI diagnostics. Their answers fell into one of two groups:

Some participants, all of them identifying as LGBT* or queer, believed that GAHT should be able to be continued in a GPs’ practice after being started by a specialist.

Well, […] actually, it does belong into General Practice, and I am not at all in favour of opening specialised centres […]. Our task is the treatment of the people in the neighbourhood, and everyone is part of that and that’s why every GP should be familiar with it. […]. The basic care or at least continuation of hormone therapy and all that, that could actually take place at a GP very well, because the trustful relationship between GP and patient does exist and that’s important, because it’s not just about prescribing hormones, but also about looking, how are the people doing, how do they feel. (A41, F, BE)

However, almost all other doctors believed it to be too complicated to be done outside of either specialised GPs’ practices or endocrinologists’ practices, with one interviewee believing it to be within the responsibility of Gynaecology and Urology specialists.

It would be rather unusual to say: ‘I am going to do that as a GP […]’. But that’s, so I think, more of a thing for specialists. It must be done by someone who is familiar with hormone therapy in any way, it’s just, it’s too rare of a treatment for you to be able to collect enough experience to decide that for yourself. (A33, F, BB)

Participants that provided GAHT were exclusively self-identified as LGBT* or queer. Some even stated their own identity pushed them to work on aspects of LGBT* specific care:

I wanted to go there, because it was an interesting topic, uhm that I identified with as a gay man myself. It was important for me to see, what kind of epidemic is that [the HIV/AIDS pandemic], what is going on? (A48, M, BE)

The main reason cited for not providing GAHT for LGBT* patients was a combination of not having enough patients asking for it and having limited time to learn about it. GAHT was often seen as requiring a lot of experience to provide it at an adequate level by those not actively practicing it:

I believe everyone should stick to their last. Things that I am doing often, I am doing well. Those that I don’t do often, I’m not doing well. I never do [GAHT], so I wouldn’t start doing it. I also wouldn’t read up on it, but rather, my task is referring the patient to the appropriate place. (A31, M, BB)

Regarding STI diagnostics, one female participant reported that she stopped providing STI tests to young men who she believed to be gay and promiscuous because she perceived their resist to her advice on sexual health.

There are a few who take the ‘kamikaze route’, they regularly get tested for HIV, are obviously gay, um, and hang around in darkrooms. And then it’s always about getting an HIV test or hepatitis serology done here from time to time and, uh, no medical advice helps. And I’ve done that twice and now I’m not doing it anymore. As I said, [they are] resistant to advice. (A32, F, BE)

Most other GPs saw no issue in providing STI diagnostics, with some using history of sexual practices to determine individual risks:

For example, there are, because we offer STI check-ups, people coming here saying ‘I’d like to get checked out’. Of course! And then it’s […] me thinking: ‘Is this a man having sex with men?’ So, then I obviously ask before doing any testing: ‘Who do you have sex with, and what kind of sexual anatomy is involved?’ (A39, F, BE)

This indicates that, besides the level of medical knowledge on LGBT* health needs and the perceived demand for it in their patient panel, personal opinions on topics of daily LGBT* lives might affect a GPs’ feeling of responsibility for LGBT* healthcare.

Training, teaching and knowledge on LGBT* healthcare needs

Interviewees reported varying levels of experience with LGBT* healthcare. Most lacked structured training, and those with extensive knowledge often had to learn independently.

Almost none of the participants recalled learning about LGBT* patient care in medical school, except for two who remembered general information about gay men and HIV risks. Few doctors gained experience through residencies in clinics specialising in HIV or trans medicine, but most had no formal education in LGBT*-specific care.

Not in med school, no. But you can’t ignore that you’re talking to a man in his 60’s [laughing] […] But no, medically, it wasn’t taught at all. (A48, M, BE)

Many participants noted learning from their interactions with LGBT* patients, with trans patients often being described as willing to share insights about trans health.

Some participants indicated needing support on medical questions regarding trans health, but not on how to interact with their LGBT* patients in general.

I often think about how to treat those changed trans patients. Those former men or former women, is there something I need to think about? I’d need some, education or attention there. […] But I don’t need any general instructions for [interacting with] them. – A43, F, BE

Others indicated a need for information on who or where they could refer their patients to.

But then I still need information on who is specialised, who is taking care, who is doing what? Yeah, and who has experience? That’s something that’s completely missing. (A33, F, BB)

However, not all participants saw a need to improve their education. One GP mentioned that, in their practice’s urban setting, their patients could someone else, not feeling responsible herself.

So, I’d have to educate myself completely differently if I was somewhere out there in a rural area or something like that. […] Here I don’t have to do that, because they just Google what they need. And just around the corner you have the next specialised practice. – A32, F, BE

One queer participant reported sharing his extensive knowledge on LGBT* primary care through workshops for colleagues and through lectures.

We see that there is a shortage of healthcare supply […] and the doctors want to get educated, so we just started our own postgraduate training network. […] And I teach at two universities, […] and I am often being booked as a speaker […] to supply some content for postgraduate trainings. – A6, M, BE

Overall, participants gave a wide range of answers regarding their level of knowledge and education on LGBT* health, ranging from not receiving any education and having very limited knowledge of LGBT* health to being able to educate their peers and share their own, extensive, knowledge.

Discussion

Summary of main findings

Doctors who identified as LGBT* or queer felt much more responsible for LGBT* primary care than those who did not. They were almost alone in supplying specific healthcare interventions and cared more about their own education on the topics of LGBT* health challenges and healthcare. They also saw a general need for their colleagues to improve on topics like non-discriminatory communication.

A potential reason for many other GPs not feeling as responsible for improving and supplying their care for LGBT* patients was a sense of insecurity while addressing topics of LGBT* healthcare. They also believed that their number of LGBT* patients was too low to put an effort into learning more about it.

While almost all participants knew about LGBT* individuals in their practice, most reported not having had any structured training on LGBT* primary care. Although many interviewees did not view LGBT* primary care as a specialist’s issue, they often felt unqualified or not responsible enough to provide specific forms of therapy like GAHT.

While for now LGBT* primary care in Germany seems to be characterised by a small number of highly knowledgeable safe spaces, many participants still saw a need for all GPs to be better prepared to care for LGBT* patients. Establishing a trusting patient-provider relationship was seen as the important first step in allowing patients to feel safe enough to come out to their doctors, which in turn allows doctors to adapt their care to the patient’s needs.

Results in the context of existing literature

Most GPs did not receive any structured training on the issue. Our findings support Brandt, Prüll and Paslakis on the urgent need for LGBT* specific aspects of care to be taught in medical training [17]. The peer-teaching network of interested GPs we found is an example of possible postgraduate training opportunities.

While our participants did not report embarrassment when caring for LGBT* patients as reported by Hinchliffs et al. [26], many of those who did not provide specific care voiced their lack of knowledge on healthcare needs and how to address them, confirming the results of Nowaskie and Sowinski [27]. Interestingly, not all participants saw this as an issue, as many of them knew about referral options, differing from the aforementioned studies [27].

With only one participant reporting her own unwillingness to provide medically indicated diagnostics to patients whom she believed to be gay and promiscuous, our findings support data from the US. Prairie et al. found that 7 of the 25 interviewed doctors believed they had a right to refuse treatment of LGBT individuals on personal or religious grounds [33].

Despite the data suggesting providers’ deficits in acceptance of LGBT* patients and knowledge of their health needs taking a toll on quality of care [19], uptake of care [4] and general health status [4] of LGBT* patients, many cis-heterosexual participants seemed to be unaware of this link. This could potentially further compromise the quality of care being delivered.

Strengths and limitations

There was a selection bias with a high share of recruited GPs specialising or being interested in LGBT* healthcare. However, we were able to find extensive examples of GPs lacking knowledge or interest in LGBT* primary care.

Reasons for non-response in recruiting or non-participation were not actively investigated. However, some of the doctors who refused to be interviewed cited time constraints or felt ill-equipped to contribute anything meaningful due to the low numbers of LGBT* patients in their practice.

While this study was conducted in only two states of Germany, the sample included GPs from former western and eastern Germany as well as from urban and rural areas.

As with most interview-based studies, social desirability bias may have influenced responses, potentially concealing discriminatory attitudes or behaviours. Participants also often showed reluctance to discuss colleagues’ discriminatory behaviours. However, using episodic interviewing, we were able to unmask discriminatory language or behaviours.

While this was MR’s first interview study, he was supervised by researchers experienced in qualitative research and episodic interviewing.

Participants were not invited to provide feedback on our results.

Despite the aim of recruiting at least half of the sample from Brandenburg, only 21% of the participants could be recruited there. Further research could investigate the reluctance of rural GPs in either the discussion of this specific topic or in study participation.

Conclusions

Our results show that LGBT* primary care seems to be mostly delivered by LGBT* doctors. They feel more responsible for it, feel more comfortable providing care and put more effort into their own education on the topic. Some of them also educate their colleagues on LGBT* healthcare.

This implies that LGBT* primary care and LGBT* healthcare needs should be taught at medical school and in postgraduate training, focusing on communication and fostering a non-discriminatory environment. This would allow more cis-heterosexual GPs to feel comfortable when providing primary care to LGBT* patients that meets the standards set by WONCA Europe’s European Definition of General Practice, ‘dealing with all health problems regardless of the age, sex, or any other characteristic of the person concerned’ [20] and provide a care that is in line with a person-centred approach to primary care [20,21].

Further research could explore various practices for teaching GPs about LGBT*-specific care.
