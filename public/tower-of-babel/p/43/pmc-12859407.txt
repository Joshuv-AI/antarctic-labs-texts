A Case Series of Trileaflet Mitral Valves
Authors: Hamza Akhtar, Ruchika Bhargav, Gregg Pressman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

A cleft mitral valve (MV) leaflet is a rare congenital condition that can involve either the anterior or posterior leaflet of the MV. Cleft-like indentations (CLIs) are more commonly seen but typically divide the scallops of the posterior leaflet and do not reach the annulus. Three-dimensional (3D) transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) can easily visualize these abnormalities, which may not be apparent on two-dimensional imaging. We herein report several cases of MVs that appear trileaflet in nature, due to a symmetrically divided posterior leaflet, including one in a nonhuman primate. Our objectives are to (1) illustrate some of the anatomic variations found in MVs with a trileaflet appearance and (2) highlight the associated mitral regurgitation (MR), which can be severe.

Case Presentation 1

A 77-year-old patient with a history of coronary artery bypass grafting, hypertension, and persistent atrial fibrillation (AF), presented with worsening fatigue. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) demonstrated a normal sized left ventricle (LV) with a normal left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF; 60%-65% by visual estimate) and moderate MR as assessed using qualitative and quantitative measures (effective regurgitant orifice area [EROA] = 0.26, regurgitant volume [RV] = 38 mL, regurgitant fraction [RF] = 42%]. The MV had a trileaflet appearance with a symmetrically divided posterior leaflet (Video 1). Two papillary muscles were noted. The patient was evaluated by the valvular heart disease team, who recommended medical management. No intervention was undertaken, and the patient was designated for periodic surveillance echocardiography. Case Presentation 2

A 66-year-old patient with a history of chronic systolic heart failure, paroxysmal AF, chronic kidney disease, hypertension, and sarcoidosis presented to the emergency department with worsening dyspnea, edema, and 4 pillow orthopnea. A TTE revealed a dilated LV with a visually estimated LVEF of 25% to 30% and severe MR (EROA = 0.40, RV = 80 mL, RF = 54%) with a posteriorly directed jet (Figure 2). Echocardiography 9 years prior reported only trace MR. The patient underwent 3D TEE for further evaluation, which revealed a trileaflet MV with severe MR originating from a symmetrically divided posterior leaflet (Figure 3, Video 2). Two papillary muscles were visualized in their usual positions. Of note, a small commissural leaflet with chordal attachments was seen between the 2 posterior leaflets (Figures 3 and 4, Videos 2 and 3). This supports the presence of an anatomic commissure and congenitally trileaflet valve. A multidisciplinary heart team evaluation found the patient to be high risk for surgical repair, and MV transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (TEER) was recommended. Repair of the posterior leaflet was accomplished by placement of 2 MV TEER devices. The first was placed across A2-P2 just medial to the cleft, and the second device was placed just lateral to the first. Postprocedure residual MR was reported as mild (Video 4).Figure 2Case 2. Case Presentation 3

An 88-year-old patient with a medical history of paroxysmal AF, nonobstructive coronary artery disease, and chronic systolic heart failure presented to the hospital due to worsening dyspnea and edema. The TTE showed a mildly reduced LVEF (40%-45% by visual estimate) and at least moderate MR with a posteriorly directed jet (EROA = 0.38; RV = 68; RF = 52%; Figure 5). The patient underwent 3D TEE, which showed a trileaflet MV with 2 regurgitant jets originating between the divided posterior leaflet (Video 5). Three papillary muscles were noted (Video 6), although no chordae were able to be visualized within the cleft between the 2 posterior leaflets. Given the patient's age and comorbidities, surgical risk was deemed to be unacceptably high and the decision was made to proceed with MV TEER. The first device was placed at the midportion of A2-P2, with a second device placed just medial to the first. Postprocedure MR was reported as mild (Video 7).Figure 5Case 3. Case Presentation 4

A 73-year-old patient with coronary artery disease and heart failure with reduced ejection fraction developed AF. The TTE showed a mildly reduced LVEF (35% by visual estimate) and moderate MR with a posteriorly directed jet. A TEE prior to cardioversion of AF showed a trileaflet MV with a symmetrically divided posterior leaflet (Figure 6, Video 8) and associated moderate MR. Chordal attachments were noted within the cleft dividing the 2 posterior leaflets. Two papillary muscles were present in their usual positions. Three years later, the MR had progressed to severe (EROA = 0.46; RV = 85 mL; RF = 62%), and the patient was treated with MV TEER. Repair of the valve was accomplished with 2 devices. The first was attached to the posterior leaflet adjacent to the medial side of the cleft, while the second was attached to the posterior leaflet adjacent to the lateral side of the cleft; both were affixed to the midportion of A2. Case Presentation 5

This case involves a 31-year-old orangutan who underwent screening TEE as part of the Great Ape Project (www.greatapeproject.org). Although there was no clinical evidence of heart disease, a trileaflet MV was discovered with associated mild MR. The 3D imaging demonstrated an appearance quite similar to that of our human subjects, with a symmetrically divided posterior leaflet (Video 9). Three papillary muscles were noted (Video 10). No chordal attachments were able to be visualized within the cleft between the 2 posterior leaflets, but image resolution was not as good as in the human subjects. The orangutan was enrolled in a program of regular surveillance with no treatment undertaken and gave birth to a healthy male baby within the past year.

Discussion

Advances in cardiac imaging, particularly 3D echocardiography, have led to significant improvement in visualization of the MV.1 Three-dimensional TEE provides detailed visualization of the MV because the probe is positioned directly posterior to the left atrium. The en face view produced allows for accurate and complete assessment of MV leaflet morphology and is ideal for visualization of abnormalities such as clefts or CLIs, which are often difficult to visualize using two-dimensional echocardiography.

In this case series we have highlighted the echocardiographic features of what we believe to be anatomic trileaflet MVs. Two cases show what appear to be 3 papillary muscles, whereas in the other cases 2 papillary muscles were identified in their usual positions. Although there is known to be great variability in papillary muscle number and configuration, the papillary muscle tip can sometimes aid in identifying a commissure.2 Our findings are preliminary, and given the significant variation in number and configuration (number of heads) of the papillary muscles, a normal variant cannot be fully excluded. Nevertheless, the third papillary muscle was remote from the other 2 and might be the result of embryogenesis following a similar path as is typical of the tricuspid valve and its papillary muscles. In 2 cases chordal attachments were noted within the cleft between the 2 posterior leaflets, and in one of these cases there was a small commissural leaflet at the base of the cleft further supporting the presence of an actual commissure. Commissural leaflets are a well-recognized entity in normally structured bileaflet MVs.3 Our observation of a commissural leaflet with chordal attachments (case 2) strongly supports the presence of an anatomic commissure and a congenitally trileaflet valve. The literature does refer to “cleft chordae,” but this term is used to describe chordae that attach to the indentations separating the posterior leaflet segments and are located on the leaflet edges.4 This is in contrast to the findings observed in case 2, where chordal attachments are seen deep within the cleft, indicating that they likely formed during embryogenesis.

Previous reports have noted an association between trileaflet MVs and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy or left ventricular outflow tract obstruction due to a subaortic membrane.5,6 By contrast our case series suggests that a trileaflet MV can occur in the absence of left ventricular outflow obstruction or hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. As in other reports, our 3D TEE images demonstrate three well-formed leaflets of comparable dimensions.

Mantovani et al.7 reported the presence of CLIs in patients with myxomatous MV disease prior to surgical repair using 3D TEE. Cleft-like indentations were defined and differentiated from normal scallops of the posterior leaflet by having a minimum depth of 50% between adjacent scallops. The majority of CLIs were noted between the P1/P2 and P2/P3 scallops. This nonmidline position of the CLI results in unequal division of the posterior leaflet and unequal distances from the CLI to the commissures. This is in contrast to our series, where the posterior leaflet is symmetrically divided. Our images demonstrate 3 well-formed leaflets of comparable dimensions with equidistant commissures in all subjects.

Two of our patients had documented progression of MR. From a functional standpoint there are several possible reasons a trileaflet MV might develop regurgitation. First, some of our subjects appear to have had only 2 papillary muscles as opposed to the tricuspid valve, which typically has 3. The orientation of the normal left-sided papillary muscles ensures a tight seal of a bileaflet MV but might not be as effective in sealing a trileaflet valve. In addition, the normal mitral annulus folds along the intercommissural axis, thus aiding in bringing the coaptation zones of the normal leaflets in contact, again contributing to a good seal in systole. This folding motion likely reduces leaflet stress in the normal situation but may fail in a trileaflet valve. Finally, the LV develops much higher pressure than the right ventricle, and the 2 have very different shapes. We can speculate that a bileaflet MV evolved as a better adaptation to these circumstances than the trileaflet valve present in the right heart.

Variation in the number of leaflets is well established in the aortic and tricuspid valves. Given that the mitral and tricuspid valves have similar embryonic origins,2 we can speculate that on occasion the MV can differentiate into a trileaflet configuration like that of the tricuspid valve. Of note, our case series consists solely of female subjects, but other series of MV with a trileaflet appearance have reported a predominance in male subjects.

Conclusion

Our case series highlights the variable anatomic findings that may be present in subjects with a symmetrically divided posterior leaflet. Using high-quality 3D TEE, our findings strongly suggest an MV that formed as a trileaflet structure and may be at an increased risk of progressive MR.
