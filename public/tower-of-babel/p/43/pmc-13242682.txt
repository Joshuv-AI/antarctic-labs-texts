Nickel exposure disrupts epigenetic repression of developmental genes in mouse embryonic stem cells
Authors: Beatrix Bradford, David Filipovic, Xiaoru Zhang, Viktoriia Goriainova, Suresh Cuddapah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Nickel (Ni) is a naturally occurring heavy metal whose environmental levels have been steadily rising due to industrial activities and the widespread use of Ni-containing products. Ni exposure poses significant health risks, and studies in vertebrate models and human populations link Ni to developmental toxicity. However, the mechanisms by which Ni perturbs early developmental programs remain poorly understood. Here, we examined the effects of Ni exposure on pluripotency in mouse embryonic stem cells (mESCs) maintained under pluripotency-supporting conditions. Ni exposure led to aberrant upregulation of genes associated with mesodermal and endodermal lineages, while ectodermal gene expression remained largely unaffected. However, the expression of core pluripotency factors was preserved, indicating that Ni does not induce differentiation but instead disrupts normal transcriptional control within the pluripotent state. Mechanistically, Ni exposure caused a selective loss of the repressive histone modification H3K27me3 at bivalent promoters of mesoderm-associated genes without altering global H3K27me3 levels. Pharmacological inhibition of H3K27me3 demethylases attenuated Ni-induced gene activation, suggesting that localized H3K27me3 removal contributes to this aberrant activation of developmental genes. ESCs normally exist as heterogeneous populations that dynamically fluctuate between naïve and lineage-primed pluripotent states. Our findings indicate that Ni exposure perturbs this equilibrium through aberrant activation of lineage-associated genes while core pluripotency remains preserved. Such dysregulation of early transcriptional programs may predispose cells to abnormal fate decisions. These findings suggest a mechanistic link between Ni exposure and developmental abnormalities.

Introduction

Nickel (Ni) is a heavy metal of significant concern for human health due to its widespread presence in the atmosphere, groundwater, and soil (Genchi et al., 2020; El-Naggar et al., 2021). Although Ni occurs naturally through volcanic activity and rock weathering, human activities have substantially increased its environmental abundance. Major sources of Ni include industrial processes such as metallurgy and electroplating, fossil fuel combustion, and the widespread use of Ni-containing products (Eliades and Athanasiou, 2002; Khodadoust et al., 2004; Aleksandra and Urszula, 2008; Salnikow and Zhitkovich, 2008; Cameron et al., 2011). Notably, diesel exhaust as well as cigarette and e-cigarette smoke are major sources of Ni exposure in humans (Torjussen et al., 2003; Aherrera et al., 2017). Ni exposure poses a serious risk to human health, with studies suggesting that even low-level exposure to Ni could be hazardous (Rosa et al., 2014; Joh et al., 2021). Major health effects associated with Ni exposure include skin allergies, respiratory cancers, and diseases affecting the kidneys and cardiovascular system (Denkhaus and Salnikow, 2002; Gergovska et al., 2020; Nan et al., 2022; Cheek et al., 2023).

Although the adverse effects of Ni exposure on multiple organ systems, including lungs, liver, and kidneys, are well documented, its impact on early development is not well understood. Evidence from vertebrate model systems suggests that early-life exposure to Ni disrupts normal developmental processes. For instance, zebrafish embryos and larvae exposed to NiCl2 exhibited delayed hatching, reduced locomotor activity, and increased early mortality (Kienle et al., 2008). In Swiss albino mice, gestational exposure to NiCl2 resulted in increased fetal resorptions, growth retardation, and congenital malformations (Saini et al., 2013). Studies in pregnant rats have demonstrated that Ni crosses the placental barrier, as it has been detected in both amniotic fluid and fetal blood, with the fetal Ni concentrations positively correlating with maternal exposure levels (Szakmary et al., 1995; Wang et al., 2010).

Epidemiological studies similarly suggest an association between Ni exposure and developmental abnormalities in humans, including preterm deliveries (Chen et al., 2018), congenital heart defects (Zhang et al., 2019), and other birth defects (Chashschin et al., 1994; Ni et al., 2018). Moreover, Ni has been shown to cross the placental barrier in humans and was detected at elevated levels in the cord blood of mothers experiencing pregnancy complications (Ding et al., 2021). These findings raise significant concerns regarding Ni-induced developmental toxicity in humans. Despite this growing body of evidence from both animal and human studies, the molecular mechanisms underlying Ni-induced developmental toxicity remain poorly understood.

Several studies have identified the epigenome as a major target of Ni toxicity. Ni can disrupt epigenetic regulation by altering the activity of key enzymes such as DNA and histone methyltransferases and demethylases, as well as chromatin architectural proteins like CCCTC-binding factor (CTCF) (Jose et al., 2014; Gaspar and Cuddapah, 2022; Kim et al., 2024). The epigenome is especially vulnerable to environmental insults during early development. In embryonic stem cells (ESCs), the epigenome is tightly regulated to maintain the expression of pluripotency-associated genes while allowing rapid activation of lineage-specific genes in response to developmental cues (Kraushaar and Zhao, 2013). This dynamic plasticity, however, also renders the ESC epigenome highly vulnerable to environmental perturbations, which can have major developmental consequences. Therefore, we reasoned that investigating the effects of Ni exposure in pluripotent embryonic stem cells (ESCs) would provide critical insights into how Ni disrupts early developmental processes at a molecular level.

Mouse embryonic stem cells (mESCs) serve as a powerful model system for investigating early embryonic development. They efficiently differentiate in vitro through pathways that closely mimic in vivo development. Moreover, when implanted, the mESC-derived cells perform functional roles similar to their in vivo counterparts (Wichterle et al., 2002). Importantly, the chromatin-based regulatory mechanisms observed in mESCs mirror those seen during early embryogenesis in vivo (Narendra et al., 2016; Ortabozkoyun et al., 2024). Thus, mESCs faithfully recapitulate in vivo developmental processes, making them a physiologically relevant system to examine the effects of Ni on early development.

In this study, we examined the effects of Ni exposure on pluripotency in mESCs. We show that Ni exposure induces aberrant activation of genes associated with mesodermal and endodermal lineages, even under conditions that normally maintain self-renewal and pluripotency. This activation is driven by a selective loss of the repressive histone modification H3K27me3 at gene promoters. Notably, despite the induction of lineage-associated genes, core pluripotency genes were not downregulated. Previous studies have identified subpopulations of pluripotent ESCs that express differentiation-associated genes, reflecting a primed state (Trott and Martinez Arias, 2013). Our findings suggest that Ni exposure interferes with the normal regulation of this primed state by promoting aberrant activation of lineage-associated gene expression without triggering loss of pluripotency. Such dysregulation may compromise proper differentiation trajectories and contribute to the developmental abnormalities associated with Ni exposure.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

Mouse embryonic stem cell (mESC) lines E14Tg2a (CRL-1821, ATCC) and J1 (SCRC-1010, ATCC) were routinely cultured on 0.1% gelatin-coated plates (ES-006-B, MilliporeSigma) in ESGRO Complete PLUS Clonal Grade Medium (SF001–500P, MilliporeSigma). Cells were passaged every two days using 0.05% trypsin (25–052-CI, Corning).

Nickel (Ni) treatment

For Ni exposure experiments, cells were maintained in DMEM (11965–092, Gibco) supplemented with 15% Stasis Stem Cell Qualified Fetal Bovine Serum (100–525, GeminiBio), 1% MEM Non-Essential Amino Acids (11140–050, Gibco), 1% GlutaMAX (35050–061, Gibco), 1% EmbryoMax Nucleosides (ES-008-D, Millipore Sigma), 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin Solution (30–002-CI, Corning), 0.15 mM 1-Thioglycerol (M6145, MilliporeSigma), and 0.01% Leukemia Inhibitory Factor (LIF; ESG1107, MilliporeSigma). E14Tg2a cells were treated with 1, 10, 50, 100, 150, and 200 μM NiCl2 (N6136, Sigma) for 4 and 6 days. J1 cells were treated with 50, 100, 150, and 200 μM NiCl2 for 4 days. Cells were passaged every two days, and fresh NiCl2 was added to the culture medium after each passage. Time-matched untreated cells cultured under identical conditions served as controls. All cells were maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO2. For demethylase inhibitor experiments, E14Tg2a cells were treated with 150 μM NiCl2 and 3 μM GSK-J4 (HY-15648B, MedChemExpress) for 4 days. Control cells were treated with 150 μM NiCl2 without GSK-J4.

A wide range of Ni concentrations has been reported in human tissues. Ni accumulates in all major organs, with reports indicating concentrations up to 12 μM in the kidney (Dudek-Adamska et al., 2021), and over 100 μM in the lungs (Edelman and Roggli, 1989) in humans without occupational exposure to Ni. Occupational exposure can result in substantially higher tissue burdens, with lung Ni concentrations exceeding 500 μM (Andersen and Svenes, 1989; Svenes and Andersen, 1998). Most importantly, Ni has been detected in tissues relevant to embryonic development, including the placenta and ovary. Specifically, concentrations of up to 16 μM have been reported in healthy placental tissues and up to 39 μM in placental tissues of fetuses with heart defects (Zhang et al., 2019). Furthermore, Ni has been detected in healthy ovarian tissues at concentrations up to 25 μM. These findings suggest that cells in early embryonic stages, including blastocysts, may be exposed to Ni over prolonged periods. In ovarian tissues associated with borderline or malignant ovarian tumors, Ni concentrations can reach 165 μM (Canaz et al., 2017), suggesting that human tissues can accumulate very high levels of this metal. Additionally, recent studies demonstrate that primary cells take up no more than 10% of the Ni present in the culture medium (Krawic et al., 2023). Therefore, Ni concentrations employed here are physiologically relevant and appropriate for modeling Ni-induced epigenetic and transcriptional effects.

Assessment of cytotoxicity and antioxidant status

To examine cytotoxicity, 10,000 cells were seeded in triplicate in 96-well plates and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The cells were then treated with 0–500 μM NiCl2 for 72 h. Cytotoxicity was measured at 24, 48, and 72 h using the CellTiter 96 Non-Radioactive Cell Proliferation Assay Kit (G4001, Promega) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Experiments were performed in three biological replicates. Statistical significance was evaluated using a t-test. To measure antioxidant levels, E14Tg2a cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5000 cells per well in triplicate and treated with 0–200 μM NiCl2 for 4 days. The ratio of reduced (GSH) to oxidized glutathione (GSSG) was determined using the GSH/GSSG-Glo™ Assay Kit (V6611, Promega) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Statistical significance was assessed using a t-test.

RNA isolation and RT-qPCR analysis

Total RNA was isolated using QIAzol Lysis Reagent (79,306, Qiagen) and the RNeasy Mini Kit (74,106, Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA was synthesized using SuperScript III Reverse Transcriptase (18080–044, Invitrogen). Gene expression levels were analyzed using real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) with PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (A25742, ThermoFisher Scientific) on a QuantStudio 6 Flex Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems). All experiments were performed with at least three biological replicates. Statistical significance was evaluated using a t-test. Actb was used as the internal control. Primer sequences are listed in Table S1.

RNA-seq and data analysis

RNA-seq was performed using RNA isolated from two biological replicates. RNA-seq libraries were prepared using the Illumina TruSeq Stranded mRNA Library Preparation Kit (20,020,595, Illumina) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus to obtain 100 bp paired-end reads. Raw sequencing data were processed and analyzed using the Scientific Data Analysis Platform (SciDAP; Datirium, https://scidap.com/rna-seq.html). Briefly, quality-filtered FASTQ files were aligned to the mouse reference genome (GRCm38, NCBI RefSeq GCF_000001635.20) using STAR (Dobin et al., 2013). Differentially expressed genes were identified using DESeq2 (|log2FC| ≥ log2(1.5), adjusted p-value <0.05 [Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR)] and RPKM ≥1 in at least one condition) (Love et al., 2014). The RNA-Seq data were deposited in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) under the accession number GSE310598.

Western blotting

Total proteins were isolated using RIPA buffer. The proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes (1,620,115, Bio-Rad). Membranes were probed with antibodies against MEIS1 (MA5–27191, Invitrogen), OCT4 (2840, Cell Signaling), H3K27me3 (C15410195, Diagenode), and H3K4me3 (07–473, Millipore). β-Actin (ab3280, Abcam) and histone H3 (06–599, Millipore) were used as loading controls. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software. The experiments were performed with at least two biological replicates, and representative blots are shown. Statistical significance was determined using a t-test.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation

The cells were crosslinked with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min at 25 °C. The crosslinked chromatin was sonicated to obtain 200–500 bp fragments. Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) was performed as previously described (Jose et al., 2014; Jose et al., 2018) using ChIP-grade antibodies against H3K4me3 (17–614, Millipore) and H3K27me3 (C15410195, Diagenode). Enrichment of H3K4me3 and H3K27me3 at target gene promoters was assessed by RT-qPCR using the following primers:

Hand1:

F: 5′-CCAGCCCGCACCCTTAG, R: 5′-AAGCGGAAAAGGGAGTTGGT.

Meis1:

F: 5′-AAAAGTCAAAGCCAGAGCAAGAA, R: 5′-CTCGAGCGGAACCTTTTCC.

Gsc:

F: 5′-TTGGCTCGGCGGTTCTT, R: 5′-GCAGCCTTTAGGTCACCACTGT.

Overrepresentation analysis

Gene ontology (GO) overrepresentation analysis (ORA) was performed using the enrichGO function from the R package clusterProfiler (v4.12.6) (Yu et al., 2012) with a maximum gene set size set to 5000 and otherwise default settings, using the Biological Process (BP) ontology. For each experimental condition (Ni concentrations: 100, 150, 200 μM; exposure durations: 4 and 6 days), ORA was performed on the differentially expressed genes (compared to time-matched controls; |log2FC| ≥ log2(1.5), FDR ≤ 0.05, and RPKM ≥1 in at least one condition). P-values were computed via the hypergeometric test and adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. Results were visualized using the dotplot function from the enrichplot R package (part of the clusterProfiler suite), showing the top ten overrepresented BP terms with adjusted P < 0.05.

Prediction of upstream transcriptional regulators

Binding Analysis for Regulation of Transcription (BART) was performed using BART version 2.1 (Wang et al., 2018) in geneset mode with the mm10 genome reference. For each experimental condition (Ni concentrations: 100, 150, 200 μM; exposure durations: 4 and 6 days), the upregulated and downregulated genes were analyzed separately. Each predicted transcriptional regulator (TR) was ranked by its Irwin-Hall p-value within each condition, and an average rank across all conditions was computed for all TRs using upregulated and downregulated genes independently. The ten TRs with the most significant enrichment in upregulated/downregulated genes were plotted to illustrate their ranks across conditions. For each individual ChIP-seq experiment used in BART’s reference compendium, the average AUC was also computed across conditions for the upregulated and downregulated gene sets separately, and the top ten experiments with the highest average AUC were plotted.

Enrichment of the bivalent gene set within upregulated genes

The mESC bivalent gene set (promoters marked by both H3K4me3 and H3K27me3) was obtained from Liu et al. (Liu et al., 2016). For each experimental condition (Ni concentrations: 100, 150, and 200 μM; exposure durations: 4 and 6 days), the numbers of upregulated genes overlapping or not overlapping the bivalent gene set were determined. Genes that did not overlap were classified as non-bivalent, defined as those lacking both marks or carrying only H3K4me3 or H3K27me3. A 2 × 2 contingency table was then constructed to compare these counts against the background of all genes expressed in our RNA-Seq dataset. Enrichment of bivalent genes among upregulated genes was evaluated using a two-sided Fisher’s exact test.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

The mESC bivalent gene set (promoters marked by both H3K4me3 and H3K27me3) was obtained from Liu et al. (Liu et al., 2016). Gene set enrichment analysis was performed using the Python package GSEApy (v1.1.5; https://github.com/zqfang/GSEApy), which implements the standard GSEA algorithm (Subramanian et al., 2005). For each experimental condition (Ni concentrations: 100, 150, and 200 μM; exposure durations: 4 and 6 days), genes were ranked by log2 fold change, and GSEA was performed with default parameters to evaluate the enrichment of the ESC bivalent gene set.

Statistical analysis

Statistical significance of all qPCR data was evaluated using a t-test. Differentially expressed genes in the RNA-Seq data were identified using DESeq2 (|log2FC| ≥ log2(1.5), adjusted p-value <0.05 using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR), and genes with RPKM ≥1 in at least one condition) (Love et al., 2014). Gene ontology (GO) overrepresentation analysis (ORA) was performed using the enrichGO function from the R package clusterProfiler (v4.12.6). ORA was conducted on differentially expressed genes identified relative to time-matched controls; |log2FC| ≥ log2(1.5), FDR ≤ 0.05, and RPKM ≥1 in at least one condition. P-values were calculated using the hypergeometric test and adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. Upstream transcriptional regulators were predicted with BART version 2.1 (Wang et al., 2018) using the geneset mode. The predicted transcriptional regulators (TR) were ranked based on their Irwin-Hall p-value within each condition. The enrichment of bivalent genes among upregulated genes was assessed using a two-sided Fisher’s exact test, comparing the proportion of bivalent genes to all other gene classes within the upregulated gene set relative to their proportions among all genes used as the background.

Results

Ni exposure alters processes associated with cell differentiation

To assess the impact of Ni exposure on ESC pluripotency, E14Tg2a mESCs were cultured under conventional self-renewing conditions in serum- and leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF)-containing medium. Cells were treated with physiologically relevant concentrations of NiCl2 (100, 150, and 200 μM) for 4 or 6 days. These concentrations were non-cytotoxic, as assessed by the MTT assay (Fig. S1A), and did not significantly affect cellular redox status, as measured by the GSH/GSSG ratio (Fig. S1B). RNA-seq analysis of Ni-exposed cells and time-matched untreated cells revealed a dose- and time-dependent increase in the number of differentially expressed genes (|log2FC| ≥ log2(1.5), adjusted p-value <0.05, and RPKM ≥1 in at least one condition) (Fig. 1A).

To identify biological processes affected by Ni exposure, we performed overrepresentation analysis (ORA) of the differentially expressed genes using clusterProfiler (enrichGO function with ont = “BP”) (Xu et al., 2024). Our analysis identified “cell differentiation” as the most highly enriched biological process across most experimental conditions (adjusted p-value <0.05) (Fig. 1B), suggesting that Ni exposure preferentially impacts transcriptional programs associated with developmental regulation even under self-renewing conditions.

Ni exposure impacts genes associated with germ layer development

Among the top 10 upregulated genes annotated under the GO biological process “cell differentiation” (GO:0030154), Hand1 was consistently identified across all treatment conditions (Fig. 2A). Hand1 expression is first detected in vivo at days E7.5–E9.5 in extraembryonic and lateral mesoderm (Firulli et al., 1998). Its induction in LIF-maintained mESCs suggests that Ni exposure activates developmental genes that are normally repressed in pre-implantation pluripotent cells.

To further assess the impact of Ni exposure on germ layer-specific gene expression, we examined genes associated with mesoderm development (GO:0007498), endoderm development (GO:0007492), and ectoderm development (GO:0007398). Ni exposure resulted in preferential upregulation of genes associated with mesoderm and endoderm development, while ectoderm genes remained largely unchanged. Specifically, 7.1% of mesoderm genes were upregulated and 1.2% downregulated, while 11.1% of endoderm genes were upregulated and 1.9% downregulated. In contrast, no significant changes were observed among ectoderm-associated genes (0% up, 0% down). These data suggest a selective activation of mesodermal and endodermal transcriptional programs in response to Ni exposure (Fig. 2B).

We next examined whether these transcriptional changes were accompanied by alterations in cell morphology, a key feature of ESC pluripotency. Untreated cells exhibited compact, rounded colonies, characteristic of pluripotent cells maintained in LIF-containing medium. In contrast, Ni-exposed cells displayed flatter colonies with irregular edges, consistent with phenotypic changes associated with destabilization of the pluripotent state (Fig. 2C).

Ni exposure activates mesoderm- and endoderm-associated genes while preserving pluripotency

Since our global gene expression analysis indicated that Ni exposure alters the expression of genes associated with germ layer specification, we next performed a detailed examination of the germ layer-specific gene expression. We exposed E14Tg2a mESCs to a broad range of Ni concentrations (1–200 μM). Gene expression analysis using RT-qPCR revealed a dose- and time-dependent upregulation of Meis1, Hand1, and Gsc, genes commonly associated with mesodermal programs (Fig. 3A). Additionally, we observed a moderate upregulation of endoderm-associated genes (Gata4, Gata6, and Foxa1) (Fig. 3B). In contrast, no consistent changes were detected in the expression of ectoderm-associated genes (Neurod1, Msi1, and Fgf5) (Fig. 3C). These findings are consistent with the GO analysis, indicating a selective activation of mesoderm- and endoderm-associated transcriptional programs following Ni exposure. Notably, despite the upregulation of lineage-associated genes, core pluripotency factors (Oct4, Sox2 and Nanog) were not downregulated (Fig. 3D). Consistent with these results, examination of protein levels confirmed increased expression of the mesoderm-associated marker MEIS1 without the loss of the pluripotency marker OCT4 (Fig. 3E).

To determine whether this response was specific to E14Tg2a cells, we performed these experiments in another mESC line, J1. Similar to E14Tg2a cells, Ni exposure in J1 cells resulted in an upregulation of mesoderm- and endoderm-associated genes (Fig. S2A and B, respectively), with no significant changes in ectoderm-associated gene expression (Fig. S2C) and no reduction in pluripotency-associated gene expression (Fig. S2D). Moreover, Ni-exposed J1 cells exhibited a flattened colony morphology with irregular edges, consistent with altered maintenance of the pluripotency state (Fig. S2E). Together, these results indicate that Ni exposure induces an altered pluripotent state, defined by activation of lineage-associated genes without loss of core pluripotency markers.

PRC2 is predicted to bind the promoters of Ni-upregulated genes

Next, we sought to elucidate the mechanisms driving Ni-induced gene expression changes. To achieve this goal, we used BART, a computational framework that leverages publicly available ChIP-seq datasets to predict the binding of functional transcriptional regulators (TRs) to the cis-regulatory regions associated with a given gene set (Wang et al., 2018). BART analysis revealed that the cis-regulatory elements of the Ni-upregulated genes were significantly enriched for binding of the polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2) proteins, including SUZ12 and EZH2, and PRC2-associated proteins, including JARID2 (Fig. 4A). Importantly, although BART utilizes ChIP-seq data across diverse cell types to predict functional TRs, the ChIP-seq datasets that yielded the highest BART enrichment scores (AUC) for the identified regulators (e.g., SUZ12, EZH2, and JARID2) were derived from mESC datasets (Fig. 4B). This further supports the association between the Ni-induced gene upregulation in mESCs and PRC2 activity. Interestingly, BART analysis did not identify PRC2 or its associated proteins as regulators of Ni-downregulated genes (Fig. 4C and D), suggesting that distinct regulatory mechanisms govern gene activation versus repression in response to Ni exposure.

Ni-induced gene upregulation is associated with the loss of H3K27 trimethylation

Given that PRC2 is responsible for the deposition of the repressive histone modification H3K27me3, the predicted association of PRC2 with Ni-upregulated genes suggests that loss of H3K27me3 may contribute to Ni-induced gene activation. Consistent with this idea, our previous studies in human lung epithelial cells have demonstrated that Ni exposure activates the bivalent gene ZEB1 by inducing loss of H3K27me3 at its promoter, thereby resolving its bivalent chromatin state. Therefore, we reasoned that the resolution of bivalency may be a key mechanism through which Ni promotes gene activation in mESCs.

To test this idea, we analyzed previously published chromatin state annotations, which classified mESC genes into four categories based on their promoter histone modifications: bivalent, H3K4me3-only, H3K27me3-only, and unmarked (Liu et al., 2016). Although bivalent genes constitute 20% of all genes, we found that 40% of the Ni-upregulated genes belonged to the bivalent category (Fisher’s exact test, p < 0.01) (Fig. 5A), indicating a significant enrichment of bivalent genes among Ni-responsive gene targets. These findings support a model in which Ni preferentially upregulates bivalent genes through promoter-specific loss of H3K27me3, thereby targeting genes that are epigenetically poised for expression.

To determine whether the influence of Ni exposure on bivalent genes extends beyond the subset of significantly upregulated genes, we performed gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) using the previously curated set of bivalent genes identified in mESCs (Liu et al., 2016). Unlike approaches that focus only on genes that meet a defined threshold of differential expression, GSEA evaluates whether a predefined group of genes shows a consistent pattern of expression changes (upregulation in our case) across the entire ranked gene list. This allows for the detection of more subtle but coordinated shifts in gene expression across the entire transcriptome. Our analysis revealed statistically significant enrichment of the bivalent gene set in Ni-exposed ESCs compared to untreated controls, with a normalized enrichment score [NES] = 1.3 (Fig. 5B). Given that bivalent genes are often key regulators of lineage specification programs during early development, these results further suggest that Ni exposure might perturb the normal pluripotency state through bivalency resolution.

To investigate whether such bivalency resolution occurs at the promoters of Ni-activated mesoderm-associated genes, we next examined their chromatin state by assessing H3K27me3 and H3K4me3 levels using ChIP analysis. As controls, we examined the promoters of a housekeeping gene (Gapdh), and genes that were expressed at low levels in untreated cells and/or showed no increase in expression upon Ni exposure (Tuba3b, Myod1, Fgf9) (Fig. S3A). ChIP analysis revealed significant enrichment of both H3K27me3 and H3K4me3 at the promoters of Hand1, Meis1, and Gsc, consistent with a bivalent chromatin state (Fig. 5C). Upon Ni exposure, the Hand1 promoter showed over a 50% reduction in H3K27me3 levels, with no detectable change in H3K4me3 levels, indicating bivalency resolution. Meis1 and Gsc also displayed a substantial loss of H3K27me3 (>50%), accompanied by more modest reductions in H3K4me3 (~30%) (Fig. 5C). No significant changes in H3K27me3 or H3K4me3 levels were detected at the promoters of control genes (Gapdh, Tuba3b, Myod1, Fgf9) (Fig. S3B). Importantly, Ni exposure did not alter global cellular levels of H3K27me3 or H3K4me3 (Fig. 5D), suggesting that the observed alterations in epigenetic modifications are site-specific rather than global.

Ni-induced gene upregulation is dependent on H3K27me3 demethylase activity

We next sought to determine whether H3K27me3 loss contributes to Ni-induced gene activation. To this end, we exposed the cells to Ni in the presence or absence of GSK-J4, a specific inhibitor of the H3K27me3 demethylases, KDM6A (UTX) and KDM6B (JMJD3). As expected, GSK-J4 treatment increased global H3K27me3 levels (Fig. S3C). While Ni exposure alone induced expression of Hand1, Meis1, and Gsc, co-treatment with GSK-J4 significantly attenuated this induction, restoring expression to levels comparable to untreated controls (Fig. 6A). Consistent with this, GSK-J4 co-treatment partially restored H3K27me3 in Ni-exposed cells (Fig. 6B).

These results demonstrate that Ni-induced activation of mesoderm-associated genes is dependent on H3K27me3 demethylase activity. Furthermore, co-treatment with GSK-J4 partially rescued the Ni-induced morphological changes. While Ni exposure alone resulted in flatter colonies with irregular edges compared to untreated cells, co-exposed cells displayed mixed colony morphology, including both compact, rounded colonies and flatter colonies with irregular edges (Fig. 6C).

Together, these results indicate that Ni-induced activation of mesoderm-associated genes is driven by promoter-specific loss of H3K27me3 and requires H3K27me3 demethylase activity. Notably, RNA-seq analysis revealed no changes in Kdm6a or Kdm6b expression following Ni exposure (Fig. S3D), suggesting that altered recruitment or activity of these enzymes, rather than changes in their transcription, underlie the Ni-induced H3K27me3 loss and gene activation.

Discussion

Leukemia Inhibitory Factor (LIF) maintains mESC pluripotency through activation of STAT3 via the LIFR/gp130 receptor complex (Niwa et al., 1998; Graf et al., 2011). In mESCs, activated STAT3 suppresses lineage-specific differentiation programs, thereby sustaining an undifferentiated, pluripotent state. Conversely, LIF withdrawal leads to upregulation of lineage-specific genes and downregulation of pluripotency genes, reflecting exit from pluripotency and initiation of differentiation (Ohtsuka et al., 2015). In this study, we investigated how Ni exposure interferes with LIF-mediated pluripotency maintenance in mESCs. Ni accumulates in all major organs, and the concentrations used in this study were selected based on reported levels in multiple human tissues (see Methods for details). Of particular relevance to development, Ni has been detected in placenta (up to ~39 μM) (Zhang et al., 2019) and ovary (up to ~25 μM) (Canaz et al., 2017). Other tissues, such as the kidney (~12 μM) (Dudek-Adamska et al., 2021) and the lungs (>100 μM) can also accumulate Ni (Edelman and Roggli, 1989), with occupational exposures reaching several hundred μM in the lungs (Andersen and Svenes, 1989; Svenes and Andersen, 1998). Since cultured cells take up only a fraction of the Ni present in the medium (3–10%) (Krawic et al., 2023), the concentrations used here are physiologically relevant and appropriate for modeling epigenetic and transcriptional effects of Ni.

Our results show that exposure of mESCs to Ni in the presence of LIF resulted in a pronounced upregulation of mesodermal genes and a moderate increase in endodermal genes. Notably, Hand1, a gene expressed in lateral and extraembryonic mesoderm populations beginning at E7.5 (Cserjesi et al., 1995; Barnes et al., 2010), was consistently upregulated under all conditions tested. Given that mESCs are derived from the inner cell mass (ICM) corresponding to the E3.5–4.5 developmental stage (Martello and Smith, 2014), this finding suggests that Ni exposure disrupts the normal timing of developmental gene expression, allowing post-implantation genes to be expressed in pluripotent cells. Despite the induction of differentiation-associated genes, there was no detectable downregulation of key pluripotency genes, including Oct4, Sox2, and Nanog. This suggests that Ni induces an intermediate state, where mESCs activate mesoderm- and endoderm-associated genes without exiting pluripotency.

Examination of the epigenetic status of Ni-upregulated gene promoters indicates that gene activation following Ni exposure is driven primarily by bivalency resolution, specifically through the loss of the repressive mark H3K27me3. Ni-induced activation of Hand1 represents a classic example of bivalency resolution, characterized by a selective reduction in H3K27me3 levels, while the activating H3K4me3 remains unchanged. In contrast, activation of Meis1 and Gsc involves both H3K27me3 reduction and a moderate decrease in H3K4me3, suggesting additional regulatory mechanisms. However, in all cases, the relative reduction in H3K27me3 exceeds that of H3K4me3, underscoring the central role of repressive mark removal in Ni-induced gene activation. These findings are consistent with previous studies in human lung epithelial cells, which showed that Ni activated the ZEB1 gene through bivalency resolution by inducing a loss of H3K27me3 (Jose et al., 2018).

The H3K27me3 demethylase KDM6A (UTX) has been shown to be essential for retinoic acid (RA)-induced differentiation of mESCs, as it facilitates the activation of RA-inducible genes via bivalency resolution. Moreover, RA-induced bivalency resolution and activation of Hox genes are blocked in Kdm6a-null mESCs (Dhar et al., 2016), highlighting the critical role of Kdm6a in mediating bivalency resolution during differentiation. Consistent with this, our results show that pharmacological inhibition of the H3K27me3 demethylases KDM6A and KDM6B prevented Ni-induced upregulation of mesoderm-associated genes. Notably, global H3K27me3 levels remained unchanged in Ni-exposed cells, consistent with unaltered Kdm6a and Kdm6b expression. These findings suggest that alterations in chromatin localization or recruitment of KDM6A/KDM6B, rather than their expression, could be responsible for Ni-induced mesodermal gene upregulation. Further studies are needed to determine the precise mechanisms regulating KDM6A/B activity and chromatin targeting in response to Ni exposure.

During the normal course of development, dissolution of naïve pluripotency has been shown to precede lineage priming (Smith, 2017). However, pluripotent cells have also been shown to be inherently heterogeneous, with individual cells dynamically transitioning between a naïve ground state and a primed state poised for lineage commitment (Hayashi et al., 2008; Toyooka et al., 2008; Canham et al., 2010; Martinez Arias and Brickman, 2011; Trott and Martinez Arias, 2013). Single-cell transcriptomic analyses have revealed distinct subpopulations of cells within ESCs, with some expressing differentiation markers and others expressing pluripotency markers (Trott and Martinez Arias, 2013). Notably, populations of cells primed for mesodermal and endodermal fates have been identified even in mESCs maintained in N2B27 medium, a defined formulation typically used to direct differentiation toward neural lineages (Trott and Martinez Arias, 2013). Similarly, in LIF-maintained mESCs, distinct subpopulations of cells have been observed, including ICM-like cells, indicative of early stages of pluripotency, and primitive ectoderm (PrE)-like cells, indicative of later stages of pluripotency. These subpopulations coexist in a reversible and fluctuating equilibrium (Toyooka et al., 2008). It has therefore been proposed that ESCs are intrinsically primed for multiple lineages, with individual cells within the population exhibiting lineage biases at the single-cell level (Trott and Martinez Arias, 2013). Therefore, the composition and balance of lineage-primed subpopulations within the pluripotent stem cell pool may be critical for ensuring normal differentiation and proper embryonic development. Environmental perturbations that disrupt this early equilibrium may alter differentiation trajectories, leading to developmental defects.

Our findings suggest that Ni exposure disrupts the normal regulation of lineage-associated gene expression, leading to aberrant activation of mesodermal and endodermal genes while pluripotency is maintained. Since maintenance of pluripotency is incompatible with full lineage commitment, these results indicate that Ni induces expression of lineage-associated genes without driving differentiation. Such dysregulation may interfere with the fidelity of subsequent differentiation programs, potentially contributing to developmental abnormalities during embryogenesis.
