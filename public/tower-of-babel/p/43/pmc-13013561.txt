An empirical study on key drivers of blind box online purchase experience based on online reviews: integrating the Kano model and entropy weight method
Authors: Yu-Liang Feng, Ya-Qin You, Shing-Sheng Guan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
With the rapid expansion of the global blind box market and its growing share in the online cultural and creative consumption sector, understanding the factors that shape user experience in blind box online purchases has become increasingly important. This study adopts a mixed-methods approach that integrates large-scale text mining with the Kano model and the entropy weight method to systematically identify and prioritize key experience attributes. Based on 18,981 valid consumer reviews collected from major Chinese e-commerce platforms (Tao Bao, Jing Dong, and Su Ning), four core dimensions—product quality, shopping service, price value, and emotional/social interaction—along with 19 specific factors were extracted. The Kano classification results indicate that image–product consistency, logistics speed, and gift or collection-related elements function as must-be attributes; cost-effectiveness, quality consistency, workmanship, authenticity, and unboxing expectations are identified as performance attributes; design safety, packaging robustness, and transportation security are categorized as attractive attributes; while brand labeling, after-sales service, and social sharing are classified as indifferent attributes. Further entropy weight analysis reveals substantial differences in the relative importance and evaluation dispersion of attributes within and across Kano categories. By integrating asymmetric satisfaction effects with information-based importance weighting, the proposed Kano–entropy framework advances a refined prioritization perspective for blind box experience attributes, with practical relevance for decision-making in product, logistics, and service design.

Introduction

As the experience economy evolves, fashion consumption has shifted from purchasing traditional souvenirs at tourist attractions to seeking trend-driven products on e-commerce platforms, where new popular items emerge rapidly. Recently, blind boxes—characterized by randomness, entertainment, and social interaction—have become especially popular among young consumers1,2.

In China, blind boxes evolved from early prototypes in the 1990 s to a formal market launch in 2012, with rapid industry expansion after 20163. Recent data highlight swift commercial growth yet signal challenges around sustainable consumption and green marketing in the blind box segment.

As a leading industry representative, Pop Mart has expanded from the Chinese market to global distribution4. Blind boxes have also entered the collectibles sector; for example, Reuters reported that in 2025 a Labubu figurine was auctioned for RMB 1.08 million, setting a new record and further stimulating secondary-market activity5. Meanwhile, e-commerce platforms have become a dominant sales channel. In 2020, the growth rate of blind box online consumption exceeded 400%, with related products exported to more than 120 countries6. These trends suggest that online platforms not only expand consumer access to popular products7 but also play a crucial role in facilitating cross-border e-commerce and sustainable supply chain development.

Despite rapid market expansion, challenges related to user experience during blind box purchases have become increasingly prominent. Many consumers report feelings of “disappointment,” “regret,” or “expectation gaps” in online reviews, which correlate with reduced satisfaction and diminished repurchase intent, as well as perceptions of risk and distrust8. Common complaints include inconsistencies between product images and actual items, variable quality, and insufficient logistics and after-sales support, leading to shifts from anticipation to disappointment during unboxing. Frequent issues with excessive marketing and mismatches in supply and demand may also result in both resource waste and emotional frustration. This creates a paradox in which blind boxes generate excitement and surprise but can also cause loss and dissatisfaction9. These issues are supported by quantitative findings: according to iiMedia Research10, 51.11% of consumers believe blind box products need improved after-sales protection, 39.56% identify product quality as insufficient, and 47.42% indicate a lack of category innovation. Additionally, 33.72% of respondents report encountering counterfeit or imitation products, and 37.39% have experienced “secondary sales” of previously opened blind boxes10. In cosmetics blind boxes, 35.87% have received unbranded or non-compliant products, with some items near expiration or of unclear origin. A CCTV News11 survey further indicates that 93.06% of respondents express negative views toward blind boxes, citing overpricing, addictive consumption tendencies, speculative hype, and aggressive marketing strategies. Collectively, these findings suggest that dissatisfaction and declining trust in blind box consumption are closely linked to product quality, design, distribution channels, and market mechanisms.

Despite growing scholarly interest in blind box consumption, online reviews, and experience-driven productsa, several limitations remain. Existing studies have predominantly focused on behavioral motivations (e.g., impulsive buying, gambling-like mechanisms, and emotional arousal) or isolated experiential factors such as price perception and product novelty, often relying on self-reported surveys or conceptual analyses. As a result, the structural hierarchy and relative importance of multiple experience attributes throughout the online purchase process have not been systematically examined. Moreover, although online review analysis has been widely applied in e-commerce research, blind box studies rarely integrate large-scale textual evidence with theoretical demand classification models, limiting their ability to translate unstructured consumer feedback into actionable design and service priorities. In addition, while the Kano model has been extensively used to categorize customer requirements, many applications remain at the level of attribute classification and do not further quantify intra-category priority differences, constraining its practical value for resource allocation under real-world constraints.

To address these gaps, the present study integrates large-scale online review text mining, the Kano model, and the entropy weight method to construct a multi-stage analytical framework. By extracting key experience factors from authentic consumer-generated content, classifying their asymmetric effects on satisfaction, and quantifying their relative importance within each Kano attribute category, this study provides a data-driven, hierarchically structured understanding of blind box online purchase experience. This approach not only enhances methodological rigor but provides operational guidance for product design, service optimization, and sustainable experience management.

Literature review

Research on blind box consumption experience

Existing studies generally agree that blind box consumption experience is jointly shaped by uncertainty-induced excitement, emotional gratification, social interaction, and symbolic value. From a socio-cultural perspective, blind boxes are regarded as a new form of “self-rewarding” consumption among urban youth, in which emotional release and identity expression play central roles. The social circulation of blind boxes through online communities and offline exchanges further endows them with “social currency” attributes, while also raising concerns about impulsive, excessive, and speculative consumption12,13.

From a consumer behavior perspective, empirical studies have confirmed that customer experience is a key predictor of repeated purchase intention in blind box consumption. Experience dimensions such as aesthetic experience, symbolic consumption, social experience, and perceived unpredictability have been shown to significantly influence consumers’ willingness to repurchase6. In addition, research grounded in the Stimulus–Organism–Response (SOR) framework indicates that marketing stimuli affect blind box purchase intention indirectly through perceived value, highlighting the mediating role of consumers’ value perception in the formation of behavioral intention14.

However, compared with the immersive offline experience characterized by in-store browsing, shaking boxes, immediate unboxing, and instant exchanges, online blind box purchasing exposes more prominent experiential deficiencies. These include information asymmetry and opacity of probability, leading to feelings of unfairness; uncertainty in logistics and product quality; friction caused by platform rules and after-sales services; and disturbances from secondary-market speculation and scalping13,15. Studies based on online reviews further reveal that emotional value accounts for the largest proportion of user evaluations, while social value is the most critical driver, and functional value plays a relatively secondary role. Moreover, factors such as time consumption, price, appearance, quality, and operational difficulty directly affect the stability of online purchase experience16.

This observation suggests the value of employing online review data and combining the Kano model with the entropy weight method to better understand key drivers and improvement priorities in the blind box online purchase experience.

Studies on online reviews and KH coder analysis

In recent years, online reviews have become an important data source for studying consumer behavior. Scholars have examined the role of e-commerce reviews in purchase decision-making, product development, and user preference formation from multiple perspectives17. Many studies have focused on product- and service-related reviews, often employing sentiment analysis methods to uncover users’ emotional tendencies and their effects on satisfaction and purchase intention18. These findings contribute to understanding consumer perceptions and attitudes; however, systematic mining of reviews related to blind box products remains limited.

Regarding research tools, KH Coder a multilingual text-mining software—has been widely applied in the study of user reviews due to its advantages in word frequency statistics, clustering analysis, and semantic network construction19,20. Its workflow of term extraction and co-occurrence analysis enables the identification of core elements repeatedly mentioned in reviews and reveals associations among different themes. Applying the KH Coder to the blind box consumption context enables clearer identification of key dimensions, such as product quality, shopping service, price value, and emotional/social interaction. This, in turn, provides empirical support for the design of Kano model questionnaires and the subsequent classification of attribute categories.

Studies on the Kano model

The Kano model was first proposed by Kano et al., in the 1980s and aims to reveal the differentiated effects of product attributes on user satisfaction from a two-dimensional perspective of functional performance and perceived satisfaction21,22, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Unlike linear satisfaction assumptions commonly adopted in early quality models, the Kano framework emphasizes the asymmetric relationship between performance and satisfaction: certain attributes can substantially enhance satisfaction when fulfilled but may not generate equivalent dissatisfaction when absent23.

Based on this theoretical logic, the Kano model has been widely applied in product optimization, service improvement, and cross-disciplinary demand analysis, particularly in studies of consumer goods and experience-driven products24–26. Prior research indicates that the Kano model not only helps distinguish between core and peripheral needs but also supports priority setting under resource constraints, thereby improving efficiency in product development and user experience management27. These characteristics make the Kano model particularly suitable for analyzing experience-oriented and uncertainty-based products such as blind boxes. In recent years, scholars have further integrated the Kano model with sentiment analysis, topic modeling, and text-mining techniques to overcome the limitations of survey-only approaches and to identify latent needs embedded in unstructured user-generated content28.

At the application level, the Kano model typically classifies customer requirements into five categories: must-be attributes, one-dimensional attributes, attractive attributes, indifferent attributes, and reverse attributes29–31. Among these, the first three categories are generally regarded as positive satisfaction drivers, whereas the latter two are associated with neutral or adverse satisfaction effects.

In questionnaire design, each functional attribute is evaluated using a paired-question format: a functional question assesses satisfaction when the attribute is present, while a dysfunctional question assesses dissatisfaction when the attribute is absent. A standard Kano five-category functional/dysfunctional scale (e.g., like–must-be–neutral–live with–dislike) is commonly adopted, and responses are encoded using the Kano evaluation table (as shown in Table 1) to determine attribute categories. In addition, the Better–Worse coefficients (as defined in Eqs. 1 and 2) are often employed to quantify each attribute’s contribution to satisfaction enhancement and dissatisfaction reduction32. Entropy weight method

The entropy weight method is an objective weighting approach based on the principle of information entropy. Its core idea is to determine the weight of each indicator by measuring its dispersion, thereby reflecting the amount of information in the data35.

In recent years, this method has been widely applied in environmental quality assessment, industrial development evaluation, and regional economic analysis36–38. More importantly, it has also been increasingly adopted in consumer behavior and user experience research, where multidimensional indicators are weighted to construct more robust evaluation models of user satisfaction in contexts such as e-commerce logistics, tourism experience, and educational services39.

In the context of blind box consumption, consumer satisfaction is not only influenced by product quality and price but also by factors such as packaging, logistics, and after-sales service40. While the traditional Kano model reveals categorical differences in demand attributes, the entropy weight method further identifies the relative importance of these factors by quantifying their weights. The combination of these two approaches complements each other at the levels of “categorical classification” and “weight quantification,” thereby providing a more comprehensive explanation of the heterogeneity and key drivers of the blind box consumption experience. This integrated approach offers empirical support for precision marketing and product optimization.

Methodologically, this study draws on previous works41–43 and follows the standard steps of the entropy weight method: (1) constructing an evaluation matrix, (2) normalizing the original data, (3) calculating the entropy value of each indicator, and (4) deriving the weights based on the entropy values.

Research design

This study adopts a multi-stage research design integrating data collection, text analysis, questionnaire construction, and statistical testing. The overall analytical process is illustrated in Fig. 3.

First, blind box–related user review data were collected from major Chinese e-commerce platforms, including Tao Bao, Jing Dong, and Su Ning, using an automated web data collection tool.

Second, the review corpus was analyzed using the text-mining software KH Coder (version 3), through keyword extraction, co-occurrence network analysis, and topic modeling. These procedures enabled the identification of core elements emphasized by users in their consumption experiences and provided an empirical basis for the construction of attributes in the subsequent Kano model.

Based on the text-mining results, a paired questionnaire grounded in the Kano model was developed. The questionnaire was then distributed, and the collected data were classified and statistically analyzed to reveal key demand characteristics in blind box online purchase experience.

Finally, the empirical findings were synthesized to derive managerial implications and practical recommendations that inform product design, service optimization, and experience management strategies in the blind box industry.

All research procedures were conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Academic Committee of the School of Art and Design, Jiangsu University of Technology. All participants provided informed consent prior to participation. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Research process and results

Data collection and text analysis of blind box online reviews

Data collection

In the first stage, this study used an automated web data collection tool to capture blind box–related reviews from major Chinese e-commerce platforms, including Tao Bao, Jing Dong, and Su Ning. The collected information included product titles, prices, review timestamps, and review texts, resulting in a total of 28,447 raw samples. Subsequently, automated data cleaning was performed using Python scripts to remove irrelevant records, such as non–blind box reviews, emoji-only texts, duplicate entries, and corrupted data. After this preprocessing, 18,981 valid reviews were retained, providing a reliable dataset for subsequent analysis.

Text analysis

For textual processing, the valid samples were analyzed using KH Coder (version 3.0). First, tokenization and stop-word removal were performed using a standard Chinese stop-word list (the Baidu stop-word list). Second, following prior studies44,45, only nouns and proper nouns were retained to enhance conceptual clarity and capture core elements of consumer concerns (e.g., “quality,” “price,” “customer service”), while reducing syntactic noise from functional words. To balance noise reduction and semantic coverage, only terms with a minimum frequency of 50 occurrences were included in the candidate word list (as shown in Table 2).

Word frequency analysis.

The results of the word frequency analysis show that the most frequent terms include “children” (2069), “delivery” (905), “friends” (878), “blind box” (759), “authentic product” (755), “packaging” (754), “price” (580), “logistics” (553), “tactile feel” (541), and “texture” (516), among others.

To explore the complexity of the latent semantic structure in the corpus and to determine an appropriate scale for the initial topic model, the LDA topic model implemented in KH Coder was applied to the preprocessed user review data for unsupervised topic modeling. Perplexity was adopted as the evaluation metric for model fit. Specifically, multiple LDA models were trained under different topic number (K) settings across a predefined range from small to large values, and the corresponding perplexity scores were calculated on a held-out validation set. The results showed that as the number of topics increased, perplexity decreased sharply at first and then gradually leveled off. When K approached 39, perplexity reached its minimum and entered a plateau region, indicating that the model achieved an optimal balance between expressive capacity and structural stability. Based on the minimum perplexity criterion and inflection point analysis, the number of initial topics was finally determined to be 39 (See Fig. 4), which served as the basis for subsequent semantic analysis and topic integration.

After obtaining the initial topic modeling results, a structured focus group approach was used to conduct semantic induction and integrate the LDA-generated topics. The focus group consisted of five experts with backgrounds in design and user experience research. Through multiple rounds of iterative discussion, the experts compared topics in terms of semantic similarity, conceptual overlap, and experiential orientation, and merged topics that were highly related in content or pointed to the same experiential dimension. This process enabled the transformation from fine-grained “statistical topics” to conceptually coherent “experiential factors.” Ultimately, while ensuring semantic completeness and conceptual distinctiveness, the initial 39 latent topics were systematically integrated into 19 s-level thematic factors.

Co-occurrence network analysis

To further validate the above findings, this study constructed a word co-occurrence network using KH Coder. In this analysis, the Jaccard coefficient was used to measure term co-occurrence strength, and a co-occurrence network was constructed55–57. The similarity values were then transformed into distances, and the network was sparsified using the minimum spanning tree (MST) method58 to extract the core semantic structures59 (see Fig. 5).

The results show that nodes such as “children,” “quality,” and “price” are located at the center of the network and are closely connected with terms such as “gift,” “delivery,” and “packaging.” This is consistent with the four themes identified in the word frequency analysis. Specifically, the cluster “children–friends–gift–surprise” highlights the social and emotional value of blind boxes; the cluster “quality–authentic product–tactile feel–appearance” reflects users’ strong concern with the intrinsic attributes of the product; while “delivery–logistics–customer service–packaging” underscores the importance of shopping service.

Taken together, the co-occurrence network analysis not only aligns closely with the word-frequency results but also reveals semantic associations among themes. This provides more intuitive empirical evidence for the subsequent construction of the Kano model and the design of the questionnaire.

In total, 305 valid responses were collected from participants across 30 provinces and municipalities in China.

Entropy weight analysis

The positively worded items of the questionnaire were transformed into a data matrix suitable for entropy weight analysis, and Eqs. (3)–(7) were applied to calculate the weights of the 19 factors influencing online blind box purchasing experience (see Table 7). This procedure was adopted because, in the Kano model, functional and dysfunctional items are jointly used for attribute classification, whereas the entropy weight method requires indicators with a consistent evaluative direction. Positively worded items directly reflect users’ perceived contribution of each attribute to the overall experience, while negatively worded items capture dissatisfaction arising from attribute absence and are directionally inverse. Including both types would introduce directional inconsistency in indicator orientation and compromise the interpretability of entropy-based weights. Therefore, only positively worded items were retained for entropy weight calculation to ensure methodological coherence and analytical clarity.

Based on the entropy results, intra-category rankings were conducted to examine the relative importance of attributes within each Kano category. Among the must-be attributes, image–product consistency had the highest weight (7.96%), while surprise/companionship had the lowest (2.02%). Among the one-dimensional attributes, cost-effectiveness ranked highest, indicating its strong role as a satisfaction driver. Within the attractive attributes, design safety emerged as the most important factor. Notably, within the indifferent attributes, after-sales service showed the highest entropy weight, suggesting substantial heterogeneity in user evaluations. This indicates that although after-sales service does not systematically drive satisfaction at the aggregate level, it becomes critical for specific user segments in exception scenarios, reflecting segment sensitivity rather than overall priority.

Overall, the combined Kano–entropy results differentiate baseline guarantees, performance drivers, delight triggers, and low-salience contextual factors, thereby providing a structured reference for prioritizing design, production, and service improvements under limited resource conditions.

Discussion

This study integrates Kano classification with entropy-based weighting to interpret blind box user experience attributes through a dual lens: (i) asymmetric effects on satisfaction/dissatisfaction (Kano) and (ii) heterogeneity/dispersion in consumer evaluations (entropy weight). Rather than merely reporting quadrants and weights, the findings reveal how blind box consumption—characterized by uncertainty, ritualized unboxing, IP symbolism, and strong social diffusion—produces a layered demand structure that combines “trust-floor” requirements with “value-calibrated” satisfaction drivers.

Must-be attributes: trust baseline

According to the Kano classification, eight attributes are identified as must-be (M) attributes: Q1 (picture–product consistency) (7.96%), Q16 (symbolic identity) (5.21%), Q13 (logistics speed) (3.54%), Q8 (gift packaging) (3.16%), Q17 (collectible function) (2.76%), Q15 (usage enjoyment) (2.57%), Q4 (appearance design/IP) (2.09%), and Q18 (surprise/companionship) (2.02%). Together, these elements constitute the “trust-floor” of online blind box consumption under conditions of uncertainty: consumers may accept uncertainty regarding what they receive, but they do not tolerate inconsistency between presentation and delivery, unstable fulfillment, or the absence of an unboxing ritual.

Among these attributes, Q1 (picture–product consistency) carries the highest weight and ranks first within the must-be category, indicating that online blind box experience is fundamentally structured around transaction credibility. Once a discrepancy arises between displayed images and the actual product, consumers tend to reinterpret randomness as deception or misrepresentation, and the resulting unboxing gap is likely to translate directly into negative reviews and reputational risk. Therefore, picture–product consistency should be treated as an operational red line rather than a conventional quality attribute.

Must-be attributes represent consumers’ minimum psychological expectations and should be prioritized by enterprises. Efficient logistics and sturdy packaging should be maintained to avoid damage-related dissatisfaction. In addition, standardized IP design should incorporate “gift-giving rituals,” while maintaining coherent collectible logic and fundamental surprise elements. The absence of these elements often leads directly to negative reviews and reputational risks, and should be regarded as the operational “red line.”

This finding both aligns with and extends prior research on blind box consumption and experiential products. Previous studies have emphasized the role of surprise, emotional arousal, and symbolic value as key sources of blind box appeal6,9,12, often conceptualizing these elements as value-added or delight-creating factors. In contrast, our results demonstrate that in the online blind box context, such emotional and symbolic components have largely shifted into the category of must-be attributes, indicating that consumers increasingly treat them as baseline expectations rather than optional bonuses. This transformation suggests a maturation of the blind box market, in which uncertainty and surprise are tolerated only when transaction credibility (e.g., image–product consistency) and fulfillment reliability (e.g., logistics speed and packaging integrity) are secured.

One-dimensional attributes: value drivers

According to the Kano classification, five attributes are identified as one-dimensional (O) attributes: Q7 (cost performance ratio) (6.17%), Q5 (product quality consistency) (3.46%), Q6 (material/texture) (2.14%), Q3 (authenticity official product) (1.87%), and Q14 (opening expectation) (1.82%). These attributes function as linear value drivers: improvements in their performance lead to proportional increases in satisfaction, whereas deficiencies trigger pronounced dissatisfaction. Among them, cost performance ratio carries the highest entropy weight, indicating that blind box satisfaction is increasingly shaped by consumers’ calibration between perceived quality and price, rather than by novelty alone. This pattern indicates that blind box satisfaction is strongly shaped by consumers’ calibration between perceived quality and price, with economic considerations playing a more prominent role alongside novelty-driven motivations.

This finding both resonates with and extends prior research on experiential consumption and blind box purchasing behavior. Earlier studies have consistently emphasized novelty, randomness, and emotional arousal as the primary drivers of blind box appeal6,12,13, often framing satisfaction formation as a function of hedonic stimulation and uncertainty-seeking motivation. However, our results suggest a notable shift: while novelty remains relevant, consumers now place greater evaluative weight on whether the product delivers a reasonable balance between perceived quality and price. This transition indicates that blind box consumption is moving from an exploration-driven stage to a value-calibrated stage, in which emotional curiosity is increasingly filtered through economic rationality.

This pattern is also consistent with broader findings in consumer research showing that as product categories mature and become saturated, consumers’ decision criteria evolve from curiosity and experimentation toward quality assurance and cost justification46,52,54. In the context of blind boxes, emerging market signals of design fatigue, price sensitivity, and SKU saturation further reinforce this interpretation, as consumers become less driven by pure curiosity and more attentive to whether the product “feels worth it.”

Meanwhile, quality consistency and workmanship (directly perceivable during handling and display) remain salient because blind boxes are both experience products and display/collectible objects. Prior studies on aesthetic consumption and symbolic goods have noted that tactile quality and craftsmanship significantly influence perceived value and ownership pride46,47. Our Kano-based classification deepens this understanding by demonstrating that these attributes function as one-dimensional drivers, meaning that improvements directly enhance satisfaction while deficiencies trigger disproportionate dissatisfaction. In this sense, the one-dimensional category can be interpreted as the market’s “hardening” of standards: as novelty decays, tactile and quality cues increasingly determine whether consumption remains enjoyable, socially shareable, and worthy of repeat purchase.

Attractive attributes: safety-oriented satisfaction

According to the Kano classification, three attributes are identified as attractive (A) attributes: Q9 (design safety) (4.49%), Q10 (packaging robustness) (3.69%), and Q11 (transportation safety) (2.25%). These attributes function as protection-oriented satisfaction enhancers in online blind box consumption. Among them, design safety carries the highest entropy weight, indicating that consumers place particular value on avoiding safety-related risks during use. Unlike must-be or one-dimensional attributes, these attractive attributes do not constitute primary motivations for purchasing blind boxes, nor do they directly drive excitement or novelty. Instead, their presence enhances satisfaction by preventing negative incidents—such as damage, safety concerns, or unusable items—that could otherwise disrupt the anticipated emotional and symbolic experience.

Compared with prior research that conceptualizes blind box appeal largely in terms of novelty, uncertainty, and emotional stimulation, the present findings suggest that, in the online consumption context, delight increasingly emerges from the uninterrupted realization of expected experiences, rather than from intensified stimulation itself12,13. In other words, protection-oriented attributes function as experience stabilizers: they enable surprise, enjoyment, and symbolic meaning to be realized smoothly, without contributing to dissatisfaction when absent. This interpretation is consistent with the original Kano definition of attractive attributes and highlights a structural shift in satisfaction formation under conditions of perceived risk and market maturation.

Indifferent attributes: segmentation signals

According to the Kano classification, three attributes are identified as indifferent (I) attributes: Q12 after-sales service (31.97%), Q2 brand identification (8.29%), and Q19 social sharing/interaction (4.55%). Statistically, this classification indicates that, at the aggregate sample level, the presence or absence of these attributes exerts only limited marginal effects on user satisfaction or dissatisfaction. However, the entropy-based weighting analysis reveals a noteworthy structural divergence. Although after-sales service (Q12) is classified as “indifferent” in the Kano framework, it exhibits an exceptionally high entropy weight (31.97%), ranking first among all 19 evaluated attributes. This result indicates a highly dispersed distribution of user evaluations, reflecting pronounced inter-individual heterogeneity. The discrepancy between “average indifference at the Kano classification level” and “high heterogeneity at the entropy level” constitutes a key empirical finding of this study.

Methodologically, this finding provides a meaningful extension to conventional applications of the Kano model. Prior research in service management and product design has typically regarded indifferent attributes as contributing little to perceived value and therefore suitable for deprioritization in resource allocation decisions29,30. In contrast, the present results demonstrate that exclusive reliance on Kano-based average effects may obscure substantial heterogeneity across user segments. The high entropy of after-sales service suggests that user perceptions of this attribute are highly polarized: for some users, it is scarcely noticed, whereas for others it exerts a decisive influence on their overall experience. Consequently, decisions based solely on Kano classification may lead firms to underestimate this attribute as a latent high-risk touchpoint.

This pattern can be meaningfully interpreted within the specific context of blind box consumption. As highly uncertainty-driven experiential products, blind boxes entail elevated risks related to product quality, compliance, and fulfillment in online transactions. For users whose purchases are completed smoothly and who receive intact and authentic products, after-sales service is rarely activated and thus tends to be perceived as an indifferent attribute. By contrast, for specific consumer segments encountering damaged items, quality defects, or suspected counterfeits, after-sales service becomes a critical mechanism for experience recovery and trust restoration. This effect is further amplified by the prevalence of restrictive policies such as “no returns or exchanges” in the blind box market, under which the quality of after-sales handling can decisively shape users’ trust judgments and continued usage intentions once disputes arise.

Based on these findings, this study argues that in high-uncertainty, high-risk product categories such as blind boxes, indifferent attributes should not be equated with unimportant attributes. Instead, indifferent attributes characterized by high entropy should be reconceptualized as defensive critical factors, whose strategic value lies not in routine value enhancement but in buffering against experience breakdown and trust erosion under exceptional conditions. By integrating Kano classification with entropy-based weighting, the present study differentiates average satisfaction asymmetry effects from segment-specific sensitivity, thereby offering a more fine-grained analytical perspective on latent risk points in the online blind box consumption experience.

Recommendations and contributions

Managerial implications

From a managerial perspective, integrating Kano classification and entropy-weighted analysis provides a structured decision framework for allocating limited resources across blind box product design, packaging engineering, logistics configuration, and service management. Rather than offering generic operational suggestions, the findings support a layered strategy of “baseline assurance—core enhancement—differentiation—secondary maintenance,” with specific implications for blind box design and experience orchestration.

First, must-be attributes should be safeguarded as the design and delivery baseline.

Attributes such as image–product consistency, logistics efficiency, packaging integrity, and basic ritual elements (e.g., gift-giving logic and surprise structure) constitute consumers’ minimum psychological expectations in blind box consumption. From a design perspective, enterprises should establish strict visual-to-physical fidelity standards to ensure that promotional images, renderings, and concept illustrations accurately reflect the final figurine’s size, color, texture, and detailing. This includes controlling color deviation, material gloss, and facial expression consistency in mass production.

In addition, series-level structural consistency (e.g., coherent world-building, character proportion systems, and visual language) should be maintained to support collectible logic and narrative continuity. On the packaging side, standardized box structures and cushioning layouts should be adopted to reduce deformation and damage during transportation, while preserving the integrity of the unboxing ritual. These elements function as non-negotiable prerequisites and should be treated as the operational “red line” in blind box design and delivery.

Second, one-dimensional attributes should be optimized as the main satisfaction drivers through design and production control.

Attributes, including authenticity assurance, product quality consistency, workmanship, tactile experience, cost-effectiveness, and unboxing expectation management directly shape incremental satisfaction. At the design and manufacturing level, companies are advised to implement prototype-to-mass-production consistency checks, such as sample approval systems and batch comparison protocols, to minimize quality variance across different production runs.

In terms of tactile experience and workmanship, designers should pay attention to surface finishing, joint precision, painting layers, and material transitions, as these details are highly perceivable during handling and display. To manage unboxing expectations, enterprises may optimize series composition and probability structure, for example, by reducing excessive quality gaps between regular and hidden editions, or by introducing “themed sub-series” with more predictable style orientation. Pricing strategies should maintain clear quality–price alignment across entry-level, mainstream, and limited-edition products, ensuring that perceived design complexity and material investment align with price tiers.

Third, attractive attributes can be leveraged to build differentiation through protective and safety-oriented design.

Although not mandatory, attributes such as design safety, packaging robustness, and transportation protection can generate additional delight and competitive advantage. For blind box products, this can be operationalized through rounded edge design, stable base structures, and child-safe component integration, especially for series targeted at younger users or gift scenarios.

Packaging design may incorporate modular inner trays, shock-absorbing materials, and anti-scratch coatings to enhance perceived protection and opening experience. Designers can also explore gift-oriented outer packaging variants (e.g., festival editions, birthday editions) that retain the blind box format while increasing ceremonial value. These measures not only enhance perceived reliability but also support premium positioning and differentiation in a highly saturated market.

Fourth, indifferent attributes may be maintained at a basic level to ensure cost efficiency without over-design.

Attributes such as brand identification, after-sales service, and social sharing/interaction show limited marginal effects on satisfaction and dissatisfaction at the aggregate level. From a design standpoint, brand identification should remain clear and standardized without excessive logo ornamentation or costly visual treatments. After-sales service can be supported through standardized replacement or compensation processes rather than highly customized service design. Social sharing value should primarily be driven by intrinsic character design, visual appeal, and narrative concept, rather than additional functional modules or interactive mechanisms.

For resource-constrained enterprises, these factors can be treated as secondary optimization dimensions, with only basic operational and design standards maintained.

Overall, by mapping different attribute categories to differentiated design and management strategies, this study provides a design-oriented, operationally feasible reference for blind box enterprises to balance experience quality, cost control, and differentiation with limited resources. The findings suggest that effective blind box experience management is not solely a marketing issue, but a systemic design problem involving character design, series structure, packaging engineering, and production control.

Integrated contributions and research limitations

By integrating large-scale online review text mining with the Kano model of customer requirement classification and entropy-based weighting, this study proposes a systematic analytical framework for examining the structural composition of online blind box consumption experiences, making theoretical and methodological contributions. The findings not only confirm the asymmetric nature of satisfaction formation in blind box consumption but also reveal meaningful distinctions between average attribute effects and segment-specific sensitivity, thereby extending the explanatory scope of the Kano model in high-uncertainty consumption contexts.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the data were primarily derived from online reviews and survey samples collected from major Chinese e-commerce platforms, with respondents predominantly consisting of young, digitally active consumers. This sample composition may constrain the generalizability of the findings to other demographic groups and market environments. Second, the study relies on a cross-sectional design and self-reported evaluations, which limits the ability to capture temporal dynamics in consumer perceptions and to infer causal relationships. Third, although the entropy weight method effectively captures dispersion in attribute evaluations, it provides only a limited representation of dissatisfaction intensity and negative experience asymmetry.

Future research may address these limitations by extending the analysis to more diverse cultural and consumption contexts, incorporating longitudinal tracking, experimental designs, and behavioral and physiological indicators, and adopting multi-method triangulation. Such efforts would help further validate and refine the integrated Kano–entropy–text mining framework proposed in this study and deepen understanding of experiential mechanisms in uncertainty-based consumption.
