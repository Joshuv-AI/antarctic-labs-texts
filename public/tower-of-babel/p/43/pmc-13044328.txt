Avoidant‐Restrictive Food Intake Disorder Is Common in Adults With Cyclic Vomiting Syndrome and Is Associated With Malnutrition
Authors: Thangam Venkatesan, Praveen S Goday, Saranya Arumugam, Kebire Gofar, Mahima Vyas, Ana Petrova, Raul Jimenez‐Castillo, Muhammad Ayyan, Sonali Srivastava, Kristen Heitman, Peter Madril, Kristin Goheen, Kristen M Roberts
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundIn cyclic vomiting syndrome (CVS), approximately 30% of patients remain symptomatic between episodes. We hypothesized that avoidance of presumed food triggers contributes to avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder (ARFID) and is associated with nutritional consequences in adults with CVS.MethodsWe prospectively studied adults diagnosed with CVS who completed the Nine‐Item ARFID Screen (NIAS), Eating Disorder Questionnaire (EDE‐QS), Food Security Questionnaire, and the VioScreen food frequency questionnaire. Nutrition status was assessed by a registered dietitian nutritionist (RDN).Key ResultsAmong 80 participants (mean age 39 ± 16 years; 77.5% female), 30 (37.5%) screened positive for ARFID risk (NIAS+). Eight patients (10%) screened positive for eating disorder risk as well and were excluded from analyses. Those at risk for ARFID (NIAS+) were younger (32 ± 11 vs. 46 ± 16 y), had moderate–severe CVS (90% vs. 10%), anxiety (67% vs. 38%), depression (66% vs. 33%), reported more inter‐episodic symptoms, and used cannabis more often (50% vs. 30%) (all p < 0.05), though cannabis‐use disorder was not higher in this group. Those at risk for ARFID were more likely to be malnourished (73% vs. 30%) and at risk for food insecurity (30% vs. 0%) (both p < 0.001). These participants also consumed fewer calories and had several micronutrient intakes below recommendations.Conclusions and InferencesAbout a third of patients with CVS screened positive for ARFID. This was associated with malnutrition and deficient intake of several micronutrients. Screening for ARFID, disordered eating, and food insecurity is warranted. RDN involvement in developing targeted interventions may impact nutritional outcomes.Keywords: avoidant food restriction, cyclic vomiting, disorders of gut brain interaction, nutrition

Background

Cyclic vomiting syndrome (CVS) is a disorder of gut–brain interaction (DGBI) characterized by recurrent episodes of nausea, vomiting, and abdominal pain [1, 2, 3, 4]. Patients can have multiple episodes, which are often debilitating, can last several hours to days, and most patients are unable to tolerate oral intake. Patients with many disorders of gut–brain interaction (DGBI) often report food‐related gastrointestinal symptoms [5] and self‐impose dietary changes and restrictions that can potentially interfere with nutrition, though this has not been specifically studied in CVS [6].

In other DGBI, only a quarter of such patients can identify the presumed food triggers when challenged in a double‐blind fashion [7].

What is known in CVS is that some patients adopt compensatory behaviors such as drinking large amounts of water followed by self‐induced emesis to “dilute the acid,” while others restrict intake altogether [8]. Further, about 30% of adult patients with CVS have inter‐episodic nausea, low‐grade vomiting, and dyspepsia that can further limit dietary intake [9, 10]. The interplay between gastrointestinal symptoms and eating behavior also raises concerns for disordered eating. Chronic nausea, anorexia, and fear of eating can mimic or mask conditions such as avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder (ARFID). Since nutritional assessment and dietary intake have not been systematically evaluated in CVS, we sought to examine the prevalence of disordered eating risk in patients with CVS using validated questionnaires.

Methods

This study was performed at a tertiary referral center with a specialized CVS clinic. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (#2024H0003), and all patients provided written informed consent prior to study procedures. Patients received remuneration for their participation.

Study Population

All patients who were diagnosed with CVS based on Rome IV criteria were included in the study. Some patients had typical clinical features but had longer episodes that did not conform to the 7‐day criteria as per Rome, but these patients were also included. Recent Rome V criteria for CVS were modified to include patients with duration of episodes up to 10 days [11]. Inclusion criteria included the following: Age > 18 years, ability to read English, and independently operate an iPad or home computer. Exclusion criteria included 1. Inability to voluntarily provide informed consent for the study (including vulnerable populations such as prisoners). 2. Other major GI problems, such as inflammatory bowel disease, cirrhosis, or medical problems like renal failure, cardiac failure, or liver cirrhosis.

Subject Recruitment

Potential participants with CVS were recruited through the CVS clinic and consecutively offered the opportunity to participate in the study. Potential participants were also contacted through a CVS registry. An e‐consent form was used to obtain informed consent. All questionnaires were completed using REDCap, an online HIPAA‐compliant online research platform.

Clinical Data

All patients completed a standard CVS intake questionnaire that queried about disease characteristics, comorbidities, treatment, and response to treatment. This was reconciled with chart review and patient interview as needed if any discrepancies were noted.

Assessment Tools

Patient‐Generated Subjective Global Assessment (PG‐SGA)

The Patient Generated Subjective Global Assessment (PG‐SGA) is a validated, comprehensive tool used for nutrition screening, assessment, triage, and monitoring of interventions [12]. It includes a patient‐completed short form and a professional evaluation, with total scores ranging from 0 to 39, where higher scores indicate greater malnutrition risk and guide the urgency and type of nutritional intervention. The professional assessment classifies patients as well nourished (A), moderately or suspected malnourished (B), or severely malnourished (C) based on weight change, dietary intake, nutrition impact symptoms, functional status, and physical examination findings [13].

Food Frequency Questionnaire

Vioscreen Food Frequency Questionnaire (FFQ) is a web‐based dietary assessment tool that has been developed to assess dietary intake. This FFQ uniquely uses 1200 food pictures and portion sizes to assist in the collection of detailed data on foods usually consumed and automatically calculates nutrient intake and food use patterns suitable for a variety of clinical counseling and research applications [14]. By providing detailed information on nutrient consumption, the system is ideal for individual nutritional counseling, allowing a provider to deliver targeted, individualized nutrition therapy and coordinate the interventions that have an impact upon dietary behavioral change. Because this FFQ uses complex branching technology for data collection, the time for completion (26.7 ± 10.0 min) is less than a paper‐based FFQ. Participants accessed the FFQ at home or in the clinic.

Nine‐Item Avoidant/Restrictive Food Intake Disorder Screen (NIAS)

The NIAS questionnaire is a validated 9‐item tool used to identify ARFID associated eating behaviors. It is recommended that this tool be used in conjunction with another eating disorder screener, such as the Eating Disorder Examination‐Questionnaire Short (EDE‐QS), to differentiate between those at risk of ARFID and disordered eating [15]. Patients who are ARFID positive without a positive EDE‐QS (≥ 15) are considered a at risk for ARFID. There are three subscales for ARFID: picky eating ≥ 10, loss of appetite ≥ 9, and fear of eating ≥ 10, where patients can be positive on one or more of these categories [16, 17]. The NIAS questionnaire is included in Table S1.

Eating Disorder Examination—Questionnaire Short (EDE‐QS)

The EDE‐QS was developed by Gideon et al. as a 12‐item version of the EDE‐Q that captures essential symptoms of various eating disorders [18]. Scores of items are summed, ranging from 0 to 36, and higher scores indicate greater eating disorder symptoms. Eating disorder symptoms are reported for the preceding seven days. The optimal cut‐off point for the EDE‐QS was 15, which yielded the best trade‐off between sensitivity (0.83) and specificity (0.85). All patients who were EDE‐QS positive were removed from further analyses. In this paper, we refer to NIAS positive and EDE‐QS negative participants as at risk for ARFID and NIAS negative and EDE‐QS negative participants are not at risk of ARFID.

Cannabis Use Disorder Identification Test (CUDIT)

Cannabis use was assessed using the CUDIT tool, a validated tool used to screen for cannabis misuse [19]. This questionnaire consists of 8 questions that are scored from 0 to 4 based on the response. A score greater than 12 indicates possible cannabis use disorder and CUDIT, and a score of 8–12 indicates hazardous cannabis use.

Malnutrition Screening Tool (MST)

The MST is a simple 2‐item validated tool with a score of ≥ 2 indicating individuals who are at risk of becoming malnourished and who may benefit from an RDN assessment [20].

The food security questionnaire is a standard 6‐item questionnaire with households with scores of 0–1 characterized as food secure, while scores of 2–4 indicate low food security, and scores of 5–6 represent very low food security [21].

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to describe the study cohort; proportions, means, or medians of demographics and clinical characteristics were calculated for the entire cohort. Then, we compared the demographic and clinical characteristics between ARFID positive and negative groups using the Chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables, t‐test for continuous variables, and exact Wilcoxon test for count variables. We further merged the data with the FFQ data and compared energy and nutrient intake between ARFID positive and negative groups. We then performed exploratory factor analysis of responses to the NIAS 9‐item questionnaire. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted to evaluate the validity of the NIAS score within a CVS population and to ensure that the three proposed ARFID domains (picky eating, fear of eating, and loss of appetite) were preserved. Because gastrointestinal symptoms in CVS could theoretically inflate certain domains (particularly fear of eating or loss of appetite), as is seen with disorders of gut‐brain interaction (DGBI), the PCA was important to ensure that each subscale represented a distinct construct rather than symptom burden related to CVS itself.

PCA was used to extract factors, followed by a varimax rotation. A screen test and root mean square residual (RMSR) value selection suggested three meaningful factors, so only these factors were retained for rotation. Lastly, logistic regression models were developed to test the associations between ARFID status and demographic and clinical factors. Two‐sided tests were conducted, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. There was a low proportion of missing data, and hence imputation procedures were not needed. SAS Studio (SAS Institute Inc., Cary NC) was used for all analyses.

Performance of the Nine‐Item Avoidant Restrictive Food Intake Disorder Screen

Using exploratory factor analysis of 76 complete NIAS responses, the questionnaire demonstrated a stable three‐factor structure consistent with the intended ARFID subscales. Across multiple analytic approaches, items clustered into three distinct domains: picky eating (items 1–3), fear of eating due to adverse consequences (items 7–9), and loss of appetite or early satiety (items 4–6), with minimal overlap between domains. In the principal components solution with varimax rotation, these three factors (factor 1: picky eating, factor 2: fear of eating, and factor 3: loss of appetite) explained approximately 83.5% of the total variance (53.3%, 22.2%, and 8.0%, respectively), indicating excellent construct validity in the CVS population (Figure 2). Internal consistency was high for the total scale and each subscale, supporting reliable measurement of ARFID‐related eating behaviors.

Eating Disorders Risk Assessments

Of 80 participants, analysis of the EDE‐QS showed that 8 participants (10%) screened positive for eating disorder risk and were excluded from analyses. Participants with ARFID subtypes, included picky eaters (14%, 11), fear of eating (29%, 23), and loss of appetite (30%, 24), are shown in Figure 1. Demographics and clinical characteristics of patients based on ARFID+ or ARFID‐ status are shown in Table 1.

ARFID risk subtypes in patients with CVS (n = 58).

ARFID+ patients were younger (32 ± 11 vs. 46 ± 16 y), had moderate—severe CVS (90% vs. 78%), were more likely to have anxiety (67% vs. 38%) and depression (63% vs. 33%), had a greater number of inter‐episodic symptoms, and used cannabis more (50% vs. 25%) (all p < 0.05), though cannabis‐use disorder was not higher in this group. Most of the ARFID+ patients were malnourished (73% vs. 30%) by RDN assessment and screened positive for food insecurity (30% vs. 0%) (both p < 0.001).

Clinical Characteristics Associated With ARFID

On univariate analysis, younger age, presence of anxiety and depression, increased number of comorbidities, increased number of ED visits in the prior year, and increased number of symptoms that interfered with eating in the inter‐episodic phase were significantly associated with screening positive for ARFID risk (Table 2). Food insecurity and screening positive on the MST were also significantly associated with positive ARFID risk participants (Table 2). On multivariate analysis, younger age, greater number of symptoms that interfered with eating, and higher scores for food insecurity were associated with ARFID risk (Table 2).

Discussion

Our study has identified that approximately one out of every three patients with CVS screens positive for ARFID risk. These data align with a recently published population‐based study in the US and UK showing that 35% of individuals with a DGBI screened positive for ARFID [22]. Based on the EDE‐QS, the prevalence of a positive screen for eating disorder risk in these participants is ~10%, which is similar to estimates in the general population [23]. While we do not have a confirmatory diagnosis of ARFID in the participants that screened positive, patients with positive screens are more likely to be malnourished and exhibit lower intakes of a variety of nutrients.

The NIAS has been used to screen for ARFID in other gastrointestinal conditions. In a study by Murray et al. of ARFID in adult neurogastroenterology patients, fear of eating contributed significantly to ARFID risk positivity, leading to the speculation that it was the likely worsening of GI symptoms of the primary disease that led to positive screens rather than the true symptoms of ARFID itself [24]. In another study involving various GI diseases, the authors only identified two factors as opposed to the original three factors that served as the basis of the NIAS [25]. In the present study, the factor analysis identified that all three factors (fear of eating, loss of appetite, picky eating) contributed to the positive NIAS screen. Also, unlike the previous studies, in the present study, picky eating contributed to more than half of the variance, while fear of eating contributed to just less than a quarter of the variance, and the rest being contributed by loss of appetite. This suggests that the positive screens in CVS are likely a result of ARFID itself, particularly given that fear of eating and loss of appetite contributed far less to screen positivity than picky eating. This also suggests that there may be other mechanisms contributing to screen positivity rather than GI symptoms themselves.

The relationship between GI conditions and ARFID is currently controversial, especially when the NIAS is used to screen for the condition. Given that fear of eating is one of the subscales, there is a concern that overlap of primary GI symptoms may create false positives in the NIAS screen, and that patients avoid foods to prevent the emergence of GI symptoms. However, other studies have suggested that patients with a GI disorder and ARFID reported more fear of aversive consequences of eating than those without a GI disorder, while groups with ARFID without a GI disorder evidenced significantly greater sensory aversion to food and indifference to food [26]. Hence, food avoidance may be an attempt to manage fears of aversive consequences that are augmented by a history of GI symptoms, while sensory aversions and negative emotional reactions towards foods may be more elevated in ARFID without GI disorders [26]. Curiously, in the factor analysis, picky eating contributed to more than half the variance, while the two factors, which are much more likely to be associated with GI disease symptoms (fear of eating and lack of appetite), together contributed about a third. While additional research in CVS and other gastrointestinal disorders is needed to better understand and mitigate eating disturbances, consideration of ARFID in patients with GI disorders is important to optimize clinical management.

While we did not confirm the diagnosis of ARFID in the participants, the increased prevalence of malnutrition in NIAS+ participants as well as the distinctly decreased intake of several micronutrients suggests significant and meaningful differences between screen‐positive and screen‐negative participants. Three‐quarters of screen‐positive participants were malnourished, and they had lower intakes of several important micronutrients. The lower intake of vegetable protein in ARFID+ may be linked to their picky eating and should be evaluated in future clinical trials. Future research should focus on the patterns of nutrient intake in those with CVS; while this is a cyclical disease, there is a concern that inter‐episodic symptoms may play a role in the nutrient intake of these participants. At the start of this study, we speculated that nutrient intake may vary depending on the stage of the disease, with relatively normal intake in the inter‐episodic phase, significantly impaired intake during the prodromal and vomiting phases, particularly in the latter, and possibly a rebound in nutrient intake in the recovery phase. However, given the difficulty in obtaining nutrient intake records from participants during an acute illness, we were unable to obtain the data necessary to confirm or refute these speculations.

While NIAS+ participants were consuming less than the dietary reference intakes for several micronutrients, this should not in itself be correlated with micronutrient deficiencies, as dietary intake does not always correlate with biochemical evidence of micronutrient deficiency [27]. Additionally, picky eating is associated with risk for inadequate micronutrient intake which was common in this population [28].

Another important consideration in the present study was that food insecurity was associated with increased risk of ARFID‐consistent behaviors in our study. Studies of food insecurity in other GI diseases have suggested prevalences between 14% and 17%, similar to the present study [29]. Limited or unreliable access to food may amplify restrictive eating patterns and nutritional vulnerability, particularly in patients with chronic gastrointestinal disorders such as CVS. Food insecurity may also limit dietary flexibility and reinforce reliance on a narrow range of perceived “safe” foods, potentially worsening avoidance behaviors. While it is unlikely that food insecurity led to false positive screens for ARFID in our study, ARFID diagnosis needs to be confirmed by a clinician. These findings highlight the importance of routinely assessing food security when evaluating ARFID risk and nutritional status in this population.

Several important conclusions can be gleaned from the above data. Firstly, referral to an RDN is critical for participants who screen positive for disordered eating. Given that this is a cyclical condition, a careful and thorough nutrition assessment will likely help identify ways that can help individual participants both with their malnutrition and potentially provide them with tools to combat putative ARFID. Equally important will be future research into the patterns of nutrient intake in groups of participants with CVS, i.e., participants who exclude foods/food groups and those who do not. This might be helpful in differentiating avoidance that is potentially adaptive from avoidance that is problematic. This research can help advance the nutrition status of large swaths of participants with CVS and form the basis for nutritional counseling of participants with this condition. Ultimately, improvements in nutrition intake should improve their nutrition status and hopefully improve the overall health of these participants, including perhaps their CVS.

One important limitation of this study is that ARFID symptoms were assessed via self‐report and not confirmed by a clinician or psychologist. A diagnosis of ARFID should be confirmed by a psychologist or clinician based on DSM‐5 criteria, which is defined by a problematic pattern of eating that leads to inadequate intake, without body‐image concerns or other sociocultural factors that influence eating behaviors, including food insecurity [30]. In the present study, we excluded patients who screened positive for eating disorder risk. This is worth emphasizing as in clinical practice, patients with CVS are often accused of malingering or having an eating disorder such as bulimia and given implausible diagnoses [8, 10].

However, given the nutritional consequences associated with a positive screen, it appears that the results of this study are valid. The cross‐sectional nature of the study, prevalence rates may fluctuate based on disease activity and possibly proximity to an episode of CVS. Also, given that these participants were seen at a large tertiary referral center for CVS, these findings may not be applicable to participants in other settings. Finally, these data are less likely to be true in mild CVS. These findings need to be confirmed in larger studies given the moderate sample size that might have led to a type 1 error.

In summary, approximately one in three patients with CVS are at risk for ARFID, which is associated with reduced micronutrient intake and overall caloric intake. Younger age adults and those with a high burden of inter‐episodic symptoms and food insecurity are at greater risk. Picky eating, loss of appetite, as well as fear of eating were all associated with ARFID risk. Patients with CVS should be screened for malnutrition risk and ARFID risk, and an RDN and psychologist should be an integral part of the care of these patients. Further research to better understand the mechanisms underlying ARFID in CVS and strategies to manage these comorbidities in CVS are warranted.
