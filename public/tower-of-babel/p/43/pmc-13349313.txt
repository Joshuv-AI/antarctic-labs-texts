Impact of Volumetric Modulated Arc Therapy Control Point Discretisation on Dosimetry: A Comparative Evaluation of Two Treatment Planning Systems
Authors: Sankar Arumugam, Tony Young
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aim:The aim of the study was to investigate the dose calculation accuracy of volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT) control point (CP) discretisation approaches in Pinnacle and RayStation treatment planning systems (TPSs).Methods:The 6MV beam model for Elekta linear accelerator with Agility head generated in Pinnacle and RayStation was used in this study. The impact of CP discretization on dose calculation was assessed using 10 prostate and ten breast VMAT plans generated in each TPS. In Pinnacle, VMAT plans were generated using both 4° and 2° CP gantry spacing, while RayStation plans used 2° spacing. To assess the dosimetric differences due to CP discretization, VMAT plans from Pinnacle were recalculated in RayStation, and vice versa. The impact of CP discretization on target volume and critical structures was assessed by comparing dose volume histogram metrics. The agreement between ArcCheck-measured and TPS-calculated doses was assessed using gamma analysis with a 2%2 mm tolerance criterion.Results:The prostate and breast plans with 2° spacing generated in Pinnacle and recalculated in RayStation showed a mean (Std Dev) target dose agreement of 1.0 (0.4)% and 0.3 (0.8)%, respectively. The similar comparison for RayStation-generated plans recalculated in Pinnacle was 0.8 (0.2)% and 2.4 (0.2)%, respectively. The ArcCheck measurements of Pinnacle-generated prostate and breast plans showed a mean gamma pass rate of 96.5 (1.2)% and 91.5 (5.2)%, respectively. Similarly, RayStation-generated prostate and breast plans had a mean gamma pass rate of 96.7 (2.3)% and 95.4 (3.7)%, respectively.Conclusions:The CP discretization approach implemented in RayStation results in improved dose calculation accuracy of VMAT plans, particularly for plans with larger CP spacing.

INTRODUCTION

Volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT) is a radiotherapy treatment technique which delivers a dose to a patient utilizing rotation or a treatment arc. The planning and delivery of VMAT beams is highly complex, with this technique able to vary the gantry rotation speed, the dose rate and the multi-leaf collimator (MLC) position and speed.[12] As such, inverse treatment planning has been utilized in treatment planning for this technique, with the treatment planning system (TPS) provided with objectives for the treatment plan, and the TPS finding the most optimal solution.[3]

Inverse treatment planning was introduced initially for intensity modulated radiation therapy, which utilized multiple MLC apertures, or control points (CPs), at static gantry angles. VMAT built upon this technique, utilizing gantry rotation in addition to the varying beam apertures for efficient treatment delivery.[456] For VMAT purposes, the CP concept became a “small arc approximation”,[1] with the apertures calculated at fixed gantry angle spacings and summed to produce the treatment arc.

The impact of CP spacing for VMAT treatment planning has been recognized as an important user-defined parameter previously for accurate and efficient VMAT treatment planning.[78] As it is a user-defined setting, a larger spacing between CP could potentially produce a suboptimal plan, along with greater differences between planned and measured doses, while reducing the spacing between CP may increase computational time.[79]

This study investigated the impact of CP discretization on dosimetry for a range of treatment plans, comparing two commercial TPSs. This treatment planning comparison was also validated by measurement-based verification.

METHODS

Treatment planning and delivery systems

A 6MV beam model of an Elekta linear accelerator (Elekta Oncology Systems, Crawley, UK) with an Agility MLC head modeled Pinnacle v16.21 (Philips Radiation Oncology Systems, Fitchburg, WI, USA) and RayStation v2023B (RaySearch Laboratories AB, Stockholm, Sweden) TPS, is considered for this study. The beam model in the Pinnacle TPS was commissioned for clinical VMAT planning at our center in February 2014 and was used for clinical treatment until August 2023. The beam model in the RayStation TPS was commissioned for clinical use in December 2020 and is currently being used for all clinical treatment planning. The dose calculation in the Pinnacle TPS is performed using the Adaptive Convolution algorithm, while the dose calculation in the RayStation TPS is performed using the Collapsed Cone Convolution algorithm. The beam models of both these systems are commissioned and validated as per TRS 430 guidelines.

The reference dosimetry of the linacs in our center is periodically audited by the Australian Clinical Dosimetry Service (ACDS, Melbourne, Australia) Level-I audit program and found to be at an optimal level. Similarly, the beam models of both Pinnacle and RayStation systems and the delivery of modulated treatment plans are audited by ACDS through Level-II and Level-III audits at the recommended frequency and are additionally found to be at an optimum level.[101112]

Multi-leaf collimator model differences

The intra-leaf transmission and tongue and groove characteristics of MLC leaves are modeled and accounted for in dose calculation in a similar manner in both Pinnacle and RayStation. However, the rounded leaf tip design of MLC leaves is handled differently. In Pinnacle, the round leaf tip is managed by using off-axis distance-dependent position offset MLC leaves in the leaf movement direction.[13] In RayStation, the rounded leaf tip is primarily modeled by increasing transmission (the square of intra-leaf transmission) up to the user-defined leaf tip length and geometrical position offset in the leaf movement direction.[1415]

Control points discretization for volumetric modulated arc therapy dose calculation

Pinnacle

In Pinnacle, VMAT dose calculation is divided into segments represented by CPs. Each CP is treated as a static beam, with three-dimensional (3D) dose calculation computed individually for CPs defining the dynamic VMAT plan delivery. CP weights are averaged for subsequent CPs to accommodate dynamic changes in beam parameters such as gantry angle and leaf position during delivery.

RayStation

In RayStation, VMAT dose calculation also involves segmenting the delivery into CPs. However, additional sub-segments are introduced between CPs based on the magnitude of leaf motion, ensuring leaf travel between sub-segments never exceeds 1.5 cm. Leaf positions and monitor units (MUs) are linearly interpolated between these sub-segments. Fluence from all segments, including sub-segments, is applied to the mean gantry position of subsequent CPs. Total energy released per unit mass (TERMA) is calculated for each segment by raytracing fluence into the patient and accumulated for each 10° section of the VMAT arc. The 3D dose deposited for each 10° arc section is computed by convolving the point dose kernel with the computed TERMA. Finally, the dose resulting from the VMAT arc is calculated by summing the doses from all arc sections.

Standard pattern volumetric modulated arc therapy arcs

Uniform fluence arcs

The study examined the impact of CP discretization on VMAT calculation by generating VMAT arcs using an in-house script that produces standard fluence patterns in the beam’s eye view. VMAT arcs with an arc length of 28° and a rectangular aperture sweeping from −12 cm to 12 cm were created in both Pinnacle and RayStation. Four aperture sizes (1 cm × 15 cm, 2 cm × 15 cm, 3 cm × 15 cm, and 4 cm × 15 cm) were considered. For each aperture size, arcs were generated with CP gantry spacings of 4° (resulting in 8 CPs) and 2° (resulting in 15 CPs), with equal weights assigned to all CPs. Sine modulated volumetric modulated arc therapy fields

The relative weights of the CPs of uniform fluence VMAT arcs with 2° CP spacing and aperture sizes 1 cm × 15 cm, 2 cm × 15 cm, 3 cm × 15 cm, and 4 cm × 15 cm were adjusted using an in-house script to produce a sine-modulated fluence with four peaks and three troughs in the beam’s eye view. Figure 1b illustrates the electronic portal imaging device (EPID) measured fluence of the sine-modulated VMAT arc generated with a 1 cm × 15 cm aperture. Whilst the first three peaks of the generated fluence are identical in width and magnitude, the last peak has a larger width due to the residual weight of the arc after four peaks is assigned to the last CP. In this case, the weight assigned to the last CP is high, resulting in the broadening of the peak. The modulation was applied to arcs with CP spacings of 2° and aperture sizes of 1 cm × 15 cm, 2 cm × 15 cm, 3 cm × 15 cm, and 4 cm × 15 cm.

Measurement of standard pattern volumetric modulated arc therapy arcs

The uniform fluence and sine-modulated VMAT arcs were measured using the EPID available on the Elekta Versa HD Linac. The EPID images were converted to a planar dose image at a depth of 5.4 cm in a homogeneous water equivalent phantom with a 100 cm source-to-axis distance (SAD) geometry, utilizing a previously published EPID image dose conversion method.[161718] The planar dose is calculated at a depth of 5.4 cm, as this methodology was validated against 2D ion chamber array-based measurements performed at the same depth in our institute. The calculated dose in EPID geometry from Pinnacle and RayStation using the collapsed VMAT arc QA methodology was compared using profile comparison with in-house developed software.[1920] The measured and calculated dose profiles were quantitatively compared using the mean absolute percentage difference (MAPD) and the standard deviation of signed percentage differences (SDSPD). In the point-by-point comparison, signed percentage differences may include both positive and negative values, which can cancel when averaged and thereby underestimate the true magnitude of disagreement. To address this limitation, MAPD was used to quantify the overall magnitude of deviation independent of direction. In addition, SDSPD was used to characterize the spread of signed differences, providing a measure of variability around the mean signed agreement between measured and calculated profiles.

RayStation plans calculated in Pinnacle

Similar to Pinnacle-generated plans, the VMAT plans created in RayStation were calculated using the beam model and dose engine available in the Pinnacle planning system. The CT and RT structure dataset were imported using DICOM import functionality, while the RT plans were imported using in-house software.[21] Figure 2b illustrates the study schema for comparing RayStation-generated plans calculated in Pinnacle. A cohort of 10 prostate and 10 breast patients was considered for this comparison. Clinically, a 2° CP spacing for VMAT plans is used with RayStation at our center, as plan optimization and dose calculation are faster, and this setting does not impact plan generation efficiency. All plans were optimized with the leaf motion constraint value set to 4.6 mm/deg gantry angle. Furthermore, in RayStation, there is no “Interpolate” option available to interpolate plans generated with coarse CP spacing to fine CP spacing. Hence, only the plans generated with 2° CP spacing in RayStation are compared with the dose calculation in Pinnacle. The RayStation and Pinnacle dose calculations are compared using the DVH metrics shown in Table 2. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to assess the difference between RayStation and Pinnacle calculated DVH metrics. For the consistency in dose comparison purpose the RayStation calculated dose was considered as the reference dose in the determination for dose difference for both Pinnacle and RayStation generated plans.

Measured dose comparison

The prostate and breast VMAT plans generated in both Pinnacle and RayStation planning systems were evaluated using the ArcCheck helical diode array detector. The measured dose distributions were compared with the TPS-calculated dose on the digital image dataset of the ArcCheck phantom. For all Pinnacle-generated patient plans (dose calculated with 2° and 4° CP gantry spacing), ArcCheck QA plans were calculated in both Pinnacle and RayStation and compared against the measurements. Similar comparisons were performed for RayStation-generated plans.

The measured and TPS-calculated dose matrices were compared using 3% global dose tolerance and 3 mm distance-to-agreement (3%/3 mm) criteria, as well as 2%/2 mm tolerance criteria with a 10% low dose threshold.

Uniform fluence volumetric modulated arc therapy arc

Pinnacle’s 4° CP spacing resulted in 8 distinct dose bands, halving the dose between adjacent bands [Figure 3a]. The 2° CP spacing showed double the bands [Figure 3b]. For the 1 cm × 15 cm aperture, Pinnacle 4° CP spacing exhibited large discrepancies (MAPD: 72.1%, SDSPD: 80.1%), which decreased substantially with 2° CP spacing (MAPD: 20.2%, SDSPD: 22.6%). In contrast, RayStation TPS showed much smaller differences for both 4° (MAPD: 5.4%, SDSPD: 4.2%) and 2° CP spacing (MAPD: 4.7%, SDSPD: 3.5%), indicating consistently better agreement with measurements.

For the 2 cm × 15 cm aperture, Pinnacle with 4° CP spacing showed higher discrepancies (MAPD: 38.3%, SDSPD: 42.0%), whereas RayStation demonstrated lower differences (MAPD: 2.4%, SDSPD: 3.6%). With 2° CP spacing, Pinnacle showed slightly improved agreement (MAPD: 9.7%, SDSPD: 10.5%) compared to RayStation (MAPD: 9.8%, SDSPD: 11.1%), with both systems performing comparably.

For the 3 cm × 15 cm aperture, discrepancies for Pinnacle at 4° CP spacing (MAPD: 4.9%, SDSPD: 6.5%) were markedly lower than those observed for smaller apertures. RayStation again showed lower differences (MAPD: 2.7%, SDSPD: 3.5%). With 2° CP spacing, Pinnacle demonstrated slightly better agreement (MAPD: 3.2%, SDSPD: 3.9%) compared to RayStation (MAPD: 4.1%, SDSPD: 4.7%).

For the 4 cm × 15 cm aperture, Pinnacle at 4° CP spacing showed higher discrepancies (MAPD: 16.7%, SDSPD: 17.9%), whereas RayStation maintained lower differences (MAPD: 4.3%, SDSPD: 4.9%). With 2° CP spacing, both systems showed good agreement, with Pinnacle (MAPD: 2.4%, SDSPD: 3.3%) performing slightly better than RayStation (MAPD: 2.9%, SDSPD: 3.9%).

Overall, RayStation demonstrated consistently better agreement with EPID measurements for 4° CP spacing across all aperture sizes. For 2° CP spacing, RayStation showed significantly better agreement for the smallest aperture (1 cm × 15 cm), while for larger apertures (≥2 cm × 15 cm), both systems showed comparable performance, with Pinnacle exhibiting marginally better agreement with measurements.

DISCUSSION

In current clinical radiotherapy planning systems, dose calculation for dynamic radiotherapy delivery is approximated using multiple equidistant static beams. While increasing the number of static beams can intuitively improve dose calculation accuracy, a trade-off must be made to ensure optimization and dose calculation times remain practical without compromising accuracy. Various strategies have been implemented to optimize the optimization time of VMAT plans. For instance, in RapidArc plan generation using Eclipse, optimization begins with 5 equidistant CPs for a full arc VMAT plan and progressively adds more CPs during optimization, with a final gantry spacing of 2°.[22] In Pinnacle and RayStation, optimization starts with fluence-based optimization of CPs defined at 24° angular separation over the defined arc in the first level of optimization, followed by introducing user-defined gantry spacing CPs between the initial ones for further optimization.[23] While these approaches affect optimization time and plan quality to some extent, the accuracy of the generated plan primarily depends on the final resolution of the CPs and leaf travel distance between subsequent CPs.[82425]

In one of the earliest VMAT optimization papers, Otto postulated that a CP gantry angle spacing of 1° and leaf motion not exceeding 5 mm per gantry angle are necessary to achieve dose calculation accuracy in VMAT planning.[2] However, the majority of commercial planning systems offer user-defined CP spacing in the range of 2°–6°, along with user-defined leaf motion constraint parameters. Both CP gantry spacing and leaf travel per gantry angle play vital roles in determining dose calculation accuracy in VMAT.

Feygelman et al. studied the dosimetric accuracy of VMAT plans generated for TG119 test cases with 2°, 4°, and 6° CP spacing in Pinnacle and concluded that plans with 4° CP spacing provided acceptable dosimetric accuracy with reasonable optimization and dose calculation time.[7] Generally, studies have shown acceptable dose accuracy with 2° CP spacing in RapidArc and 4° CP spacing in Pinnacle plans.

In this study, we examined the VMAT CP discretization implemented in both Pinnacle and RayStation using VMAT arcs that generate standard fluence, as well as clinical prostate and breast VMAT plans. The primary aim of utilizing standard fluence VMAT arcs was to assess the impact of CP discretization on dose calculation in both systems, with a focus on CP spacing, which determines the leaf travel distance between CPs (also known as leaf travel per degree of gantry rotation).

The introduction of sub-segments between CPs by RayStation significantly improved dose agreement between measurement and calculation for both uniform and sine-modulated VMAT arcs, compared to Pinnacle-calculated doses, for both 2° and 4° CP spacing. This improvement is expected to enhance the accuracy of patient dose calculations. In Pinnacle calculations, the 2° CP arc consistently exhibited better agreement with measurements compared to 4° CP plans, likely due to the increased number of static arcs representing dynamic delivery.

In RayStation calculations, the agreement between measured and calculated doses was primarily determined by the leaf travel distance between CPs rather than CP spacing. RayStation defines a leaf travel threshold of a minimum of 1.5 cm between CPs for the introduction of interpolated sub-segments. This approach may yield better dose agreement for VMAT arcs with coarse CP spacing, where the leaf travel between subsequent CPs exceeds the threshold, resulting in the introduction of multiple sub-segments. However, for arcs with fine CP spacing, dose discrepancies may arise due to the absence of sub-segments, as the leaf travel is below the threshold.

For example, in a uniform fluence VMAT arc with a 2 cm × 15 cm aperture, the 4° VMAT arc in RayStation demonstrated improved agreement with measured dose compared to its equivalent 2° arc. This was primarily due to the 4° arc resulting in a leaf travel of 3.14 cm between successive CPs, leading to the addition of three additional sub-segments between CPs. Consequently, 29 static beams (comprising 21 sub-segments and 8 CPs) were used for the arc to maintain leaf travel of <1.5 cm between adjacent segments. In contrast, the 2° arc had a leaf travel between successive CPs of 1.47 cm, which was less than the leaf travel threshold required for the introduction of sub-segments. Therefore, the arc was effectively calculated with 15 static beams (defined by CPs). Implementing sub-segments based on leaf travel distance per degree rather than per CP in RayStation would likely result in more consistent dose calculations. The dose profile for uniform fluence calculated using a 3 cm × 15 cm aperture with 4° CP spacing shows better agreement with the measurements compared to 2° CP spacing for both Pinnacle and RayStation calculations [Figure 4c]. This is attributed to the favorable alignment of the aperture edges in the leaf motion direction defined by the 8 CPs. For the same reason, the dose calculated for the 4 cm × 15 cm aperture with 2° CP spacing by both planning systems also shows close agreement with the EPID measurements [Figure 4d].

In general, at coarser angular resolution (4° CP spacing), differences in how the two systems model leaf motion and dose interpolation between CPs can lead to larger discrepancies, particularly for larger leaf motion between CPs. With finer sampling (2° CP spacing), the discretization error is reduced, allowing both systems to better approximate continuous delivery, thereby minimizing differences. At the finer CP spacing, Pinnacle calculation agreed marginally better for the uniform fluence VMAT arc for the aperture size ≥2 cm × 15 cm than RayStation [Table 3]. However, in sine modulated VMAT arcs, RayStation consistently showed better agreement with the measurement than Pinnacle [Table 4]. For the 2 cm × 15 cm sine-modulated arc, the differences observed with RayStation are higher than those for the 1 cm × 15 cm aperture. This is likely due to the introduction of sub-segments in the 1 cm × 15 cm case, where leaf travel between CPs exceeds 1.5 cm. These additional sub-segments improve the approximation of continuous delivery and reduce discretization error. In contrast, for the 2 cm × 15 cm aperture, only the CP-defined segments are used, resulting in comparatively higher discretization effects and dose differences.

In clinical VMAT plans, Pinnacle dose calculations exhibited relatively larger differences between 2° and 4° CP plans. For breast plans, the mean dose and D2 to PTV showed a mean (SD) difference of 0.4 (0.2) % and 0.9 (0.6) %, respectively, and these differences were statistically significant. The 2° CP plan resulted in higher doses compared to the 4° CP plan.

Chen et al. investigated dose variations in target volumes and OARs in prostate VMAT plans optimized with 10° CP and then recalculated with linearly interpolated 2° CP spacing. They also explored the effect of the leaf motion constraint in both scenarios.[9] Their results indicated that when the leaf motion was constrained to 2 mm/deg, the PTV and rectum doses agreed within 1.4% for both 10° and 2° CP plans. However, with a relaxed leaf motion constraint of 20 mm/deg, dramatic differences in dose to target volumes and OARs were observed between the 10° and 2° CP plans. Specifically, the mean dose to PTV increased by 5.3% in the 2° CP plan compared to the 10° CP plan, with a 150% increase in the standard deviation of PTV dose, indicating dose heterogeneity within the PTV. Similarly, the mean dose to the rectum and bladder increased by 20% and 7.7%, respectively, in the 2° CP plans compared to the 10° CP plan.

Our study revealed a similar trend in mean dose to target volumes between 2° and 4° CP prostate and breast plans calculated in Pinnacle. However, the magnitude of dose differences observed was small, as the plans were generated with a leaf motion constraint of 0.46 cm/deg and relatively less difference between the CP gantry spacing among the studied cohorts.

The DVH metrics difference between 2° and 4° CP plans calculated in RayStation showed good agreement, with a maximum mean (Std Dev) difference of − 0.1 (0.1) % observed for PTV D2 in breast plans. Understandably, the fixed number of static beams representing CPs resulted in dose differences between 2° and 4° CP plans in Pinnacle. In contrast, the sub-segments approach implemented in RayStation resulted in additional static beams and less dose difference between 2° and 4° CP plans.

The 4° CP Pinnacle-generated prostate plans recalculated in RayStation exhibited a maximum target dose difference between the calculations of the same plan in two systems. RayStation calculation resulted in a higher PTV mean dose, with a mean (Std Dev) difference of 1.5 (0.4)%, compared to Pinnacle calculations. The 2° CP plans showed a similar trend with a relatively reduced dose difference. The reduction in dose difference with reduced CP spacing is attributed to the better approximation of the arc by static beams with high-resolution CPs in Pinnacle. Overall, Pinnacle-generated plans calculated in RayStation showed a consistently higher mean dose, by 0.7 (0.6)%, to the target volumes for combined prostate and breast plans with 2° CP spacing.

The difference in OARs DVH metrics between Pinnacle and RayStation also reduced in 2° CP plans compared to 4° CP plans for both studied treatment sites. The agreement of PTV D2 and D0.01cc of the trachea and spinal canal, parameters that represent the maximum dose in the volumes, between Pinnacle and RayStation calculations improved in 2° CP plans. However, the mean dose to the heart and liver showed relatively higher percentage differences between the doses calculated by both systems. These structures are at the edge of the treatment field and receive a mean dose in the range of 3% to 12% of the target prescription dose. The observed dose differences are in the range of 0.3% to 1.5% of the prescription dose. Nevertheless, the dose calculated by RayStation for these structures was systematically lower compared to Pinnacle calculations. A small difference in out-of-field beam modeling between Pinnacle and RayStation could be the reason for this observed systematic difference.

The RayStation-generated prostate plans calculated in Pinnacle showed better agreement with the mean dose to target volumes compared to the Pinnacle-generated prostate plans calculated in RayStation [Table 5]. In contrast, RayStation-generated breast plans calculated in Pinnacle showed higher dose differences compared to the 2° CP Pinnacle breast plans calculated in RayStation. A similar trend was observed for OAR dose agreement for both prostate and breast plans.

Overall, the target volume dose calculated by RayStation was generally higher than that calculated by Pinnacle, irrespective of whether the plans were generated in Pinnacle or RayStation. The largest dose differences were observed for breast plans optimized in RayStation. The observed differences between Pinnacle and RayStation calculations may be partly attributed to variations in VMAT segment complexity; however, this was not explicitly quantified in the present study and warrants further investigation. In particular, the relatively complex segments associated with breast VMAT plans optimized in RayStation may contribute to the observed discrepancies when recalculated in Pinnacle, as the MLC modeling parameters in Pinnacle are primarily tuned for plans generated within its own optimization framework. In contrast, Pinnacle-optimized plans recalculated in RayStation showed comparatively better agreement, suggesting that the RayStation dose calculation model is more robust to variations in segment characteristics originating from Pinnacle-generated plans.

The ArcCheck gamma analysis of measured dose against TPS-calculated dose shows that with a 2%/2 mm tolerance criterion, the plans calculated with 4° CP spacing in RayStation showed better agreement with measurements compared to Pinnacle calculations, supporting the increased dosimetric accuracy with the introduction of sub-segments in the 4° CP plans calculated in RayStation. Increased dosimetric accuracy with high-resolution CPs has been demonstrated previously by other studies.[272627] While increasing the sub-segments better approximates the dynamic delivery, it does not model all the characteristics of dynamic treatments, such as acceleration and deceleration of leaf and gantry motion, and ramp-up and ramp-down of dose rate during the change in dose rate. Boylan et al. modeled the leaf and gantry motion characteristics, including the gravity effect on these parameters, of the VMAT delivery in Monte Carlo-based dose calculation and found improved agreement of the calculated dose with the measured dose.[28] In commercial clinical planning systems, the leaf latency of binary leaves is accounted for in the Tomotherapy dose calculation;[29] however, aspects of dynamic VMAT delivery are yet to be implemented for improved dose calculation accuracy.

In this work, the clinical beam models used in two commercial planning systems that have two distinct approaches to model VMAT delivery have been studied. Our results suggest that in clinical prostate and breast VMAT plans, the plans generated with 2° CP spacing showed minimal dose difference between them, which is comparable to the results of other studies.

CONCLUSIONS

The CP discretization approaches implemented in the Pinnacle and RayStation planning systems are characterized, and the dosimetric differences arising from these two different approaches are studied. Pinnacle exhibited dramatic dose differences for the collapsed arc dose calculations of the VMAT arcs, generating uniform and sine-modulated fluence, while RayStation calculated doses for these arcs closely matched the measured dose. Pinnacle-generated VMAT plans calculated in RayStation demonstrated that the VMAT dose calculation accuracy in RayStation is less affected by CP spacing. However, in Pinnacle, improved dose calculation accuracy is achieved with 2° CP gantry spacing compared to 4° CP gantry spacing. Small dose differences to the target volume were observed between Pinnacle and RayStation for Pinnacle-generated prostate and breast VMAT plans; however, the breast VMAT plans generated in RayStation showed a higher PTV mean dose difference, up to 2.4 (0.2)%, compared to the same plans calculated in Pinnacle. The sub-segment-based VMAT dose calculation approach implemented in RayStation results in improved dose calculation accuracy of VMAT plans compared to the Pinnacle dose calculation.
