Double-chambered left ventricle as a novel cause of aneurysm formation at the apex of accessory chamber
Authors: Michiko Suzuki, Makoto Orii, Ryo Sasaki, Takuya Chiba, Tsuyoshi Sugawara, Yohei Uchimura, Yoshihiro Morino, Kunihiro Yoshioka
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis case report shows unique aneurysm at the left ventricular apex associated with a double-chambered left ventricle (DCLV) in asymptomatic 69-year-old female. The aneurysm was located at the apex of an accessory chamber, which was formed by hypertrophied muscle band and the interventricular septum, along with a pronounced jet flow directed towards the apex aneurysm. These finding suggests that the obstruction at the base of the accessory chamber caused a pressure overload at the apex, ultimately leading to the development of the aneurysm. To best of our knowledge, this is the first case to prove the underlying cause of aneurysm formation in DCLV.Keywords: Double-chambered left ventricle, Apex aneurysm, Mid-ventricular obstruction, Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, Cardiac magnetic resonance, ECG-gated 4-dimensional CT

Introduction

Double-chambered left ventricle (DCLV) is a rare congenital heart disease, and it is characterized by a division of the ventricular cavity by an anomalous septum or muscle bundle [1,2]. DCLV is usually asymptomatic and has a benign prognosis. A few cases with coronary embolism and with nonsustained ventricular tachycardia, ventricular fibrillation have been reported [1,3]. When symptomatic or when associated with other cardiac anomalies, surgical treatment is recommended [1,4].

Although various causes exist for left ventricular aneurysms, those associated with a DCLV are particularly rare. A recent case report has noted left ventricular aneurysms in patients with DCLV, however, the etiology of aneurysmal formation in DCLV has not fully investigated [5]. In this report, we have demonstrated that DCLV is a contributing factor in the development of left ventricular apical aneurysm using advanced cardiac imaging.

Case presentation

The patient is a 69-year-old female diagnosed with apical hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) 20 years ago and atrial fibrillation 2 years prior. A recent transthoracic echocardiogram showed apical akinesia, severe tricuspid regurgitation, and moderate mitral regurgitation. Invasive coronary angiography showed no significant stenosis. Her condition was monitored with B-type natriuretic peptide levels ranging between 300-400 pg/mL. She was referred to our hospital for a more comprehensive evaluation of the cardiomyopathy using advanced cardiac imaging.

Cine MRI in cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) identified an accessory chamber in the left ventricle formed by hypertrophied muscle band extending from the mitral valve chordae tendineae and the interventricular septum (Fig. 1). Cine MRI also demonstrated an aneurysm at the apex of the accessory chamber, and accelerated blood flow at the obstructive accessory chamber (Figs. 1B and D, and Supplementary movie 1). These processes are very similar to the formation of apical aneurysm in HCM with mid-ventricular obstruction (MVO). Late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) images revealed dense enhancement in the area of the anterior and inferior septal insertion of the right ventricle, suggesting the presence of underlying HCM (Figs. 2A and B). Notably, LGE was observed in the aneurysm wall and at both ventricular apices, indicating long-standing myocardial injury (Figs. 2C and D). Elevated ventricular pressures due to the obstructive accessory chamber may be attributable to wall stress. ECG-gated 4-dimensional CT more clearly demonstrated hypertrophied of the muscle band extending from the chordate tendineae, forming an accessory septum and thus creating the accessory chamber (Fig. 3). The apex aneurysm of the accessary chamber was continuous with the obstructive accessary chamber by the hypertrophied muscle band and interventricular septum (Figs. 3B and C, and Supplementary movie 2). There was no evidence of thrombus formation in the aneurysm.

The ongoing management plan includes vigilant monitoring for potential enlargement of the apical aneurysm, thrombus formation, and the onset of ventricular arrhythmias originating from the region of the myocardial damage.

Discussion

The DCLV is a rare condition, with only a handful of cases reported since its first description in 1981 [[6], [7], [8]]. It is characterized by division of left ventricle chamber into 2 chambers by abnormal muscular tissue or accessary septum which by nature cloud be muscle band most of the time or it could be a membranous structure, fibromuscular ridge or prominent trabeculations [9]. It is infrequently associated with other congenital diseases and often presents asymptomatically [7].

Aneurysm at the left ventricular apex is relatively common, typically arising from myocardial infarction. Other causes include HCM, arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy, myocarditis, sarcoidosis, trauma, and Chagas disease [10]. On the other hand, aneurysms due to the DCLV are extremely rare. A recent case report has reported 2 cases of intraventricular flow abnormalities in DCLV leading to aneurysm formation [5]. However, the etiology of aneurysmal formation in DCLV has not fully investigated.

Echocardiography is the first modality for cardiac imaging, but detecting DCLV can be challenging as in our case [11]. Advanced cardiac imaging has an important role in the accurate assessment of morphologic characteristics of DCLV [12]. CMR is a better modality to confirm the diagnosis because of its better spatial resolution and tissue characterization ability [10]. Cine MRI can directly visualize the hemodynamics of the heart. Additionally, LGE allows for visualization of fibrosis in the ventricular wall, enabling the assessment of myocardial damage. ECG-gated cardiac CT offers high spatial resolution, making it valuable for the assessment of anatomical structure. Especially, ECG-gated 4-dimensional CT has the potential to assess the motion of heart walls, papillary muscles, and valves in 4D multiplanar reformations, with high spatial resolution than that of echocardiography [13].

In our case, LGE of CMR suggested the presence of underlying HCM. CMR and ECG-gated 4-dimensional CT demonstrated hypertrophied muscle band, forming an accessory septum between the interventricular septum, divided the left ventricle into 2 chambers. Hypertrophied muscle band and the interventricular septum obstructed the accessory chamber during systole, creating a jet flow to the apex. Due to the blockage by the abnormal muscle bundles, the accessory chamber needed enhanced contraction to push blood into the main chamber during systole, which resulted in a chronic increase in contractive pressure in the distal of accessary chamber and led to the formation of the apical ventricular aneurysm. Actually, LGE in the aneurysm wall and at both ventricular apices indicates chronic myocardial damage from increased ventricular pressure and wall stress. This process is very similar to the formation of apical aneurysm in HCM with MVO [5].

In patients with HCM and left ventricular apical aneurysms, primary prevention strategies may include implantable cardioverter-defibrillators and anticoagulation therapy for stroke prevention [14]. However, there are no established treatment protocols or reported outcomes for patients with DCLV and apex aneurysms. In our patient's case, she remained asymptomatic with normal left ventricular systolic function and a small aneurysm without thrombus formation. Thus, a conservative observational approach was adopted. We recommend vigilant monitoring for any enlargement of the apical aneurysm, thrombus formation, and the emergence of ventricular arrhythmias from this region.

Conclusions

DCLV with apical aneurysm due to intraventricular hemodynamic disturbances is extremely rare in the clinical setting. In our case, obstruction of the accessory chamber by the hypertrophied muscle bundles and the interventricular septum elevated intracavitary systolic pressure. Moreover, increased ventricular wall stress at the apex of accessory chamber led to the formation of the apical aneurysm and regional myocardial scarring. Thus, advanced cardiac imaging including CMR and ECG-gated 4-dimensional CT plays a crucial role in making the correct diagnosis and revealing the etiology of aneurysmal formation in a patient with DCLV.
