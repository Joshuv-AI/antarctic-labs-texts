Pear scion genotypes shape rhizosphere fungal communities in association with photosynthetic sugar metabolism
Authors: Hao Xu, Han Yang, Yujie Shi, Xiao Hu, Liping Kan, Tianjie Yang, Yangchun Xu, Caixia Dong, Qirong Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe effects of scion and rootstock combinations on rhizosphere microbial communities in grafted pear remain poorly understood. This study aims to investigate whether the genotypes of rootstocks or scions influence the rhizosphere fungal community and to elucidate the potential mechanisms involved. We investigated two scion cultivars: the European pear 'Hongqie' (Pyrus communis L.) and the Asian pear 'Sucui' (Pyrus pyrifolia (Burm. F.) Nakai), in combination with two widely utilized Chinese rootstocks 'Shanli' (Pyrus ussuriensis Maxim) and 'Duli' (Pyrus betulifolia Bunge). Four distinct grafting combinations were used and we measured photosynthetic capacity and its products, as well as physical and chemical properties of the soil and rhizosphere fungal community under identical environmental conditions. Transcriptomic analyses revealed significant scion-driven remodeling of gene expression, with 11,230 to 11,170 genes activated, primarily in carbohydrate metabolism pathways (e.g., Calvin cycle, nucleotide sugar metabolism). Notably, 'Hongqie' scions accumulated higher levels of leaf fructose (P < 0.05), sorbitol (P < 0.001), and sucrose (P < 0.001), exhibiting distinct source-sink carbon allocation patterns. Rhizosphere fungal communities displayed significant scion-specific differentiation, 'Hongqie'-associated communities exhibited a higher abundance of Basidiomycota and were enriched in Fusarium species (P < 0.01), while 'Sucui' favored Cladosporium (P < 0.05). Fungal co-occurrence networks indicated that 'Hongqie' rhizospheres had denser interaction networks, with core ASVs positively correlated with photosynthetic parameters (e.g., Pn, Tr) and available potassium (AK). These findings suggest that scion genotypes are associated with differences in rhizosphere fungal ecology and are consistent with a role for photosynthate-mediated signaling, providing mechanistic insights that can inform the optimization of graft combinations to enhance soil–plant-microbe interactions in sustainable pear production.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08139-z.Keywords: Grafting, Scion cultivar, Rootstock genotype, Rhizosphere fungus community, Photosynthetic carbohydrates

Introduction

Similar to the intestines in animals, the roots of plants growing in soil are inhabited by microbial communities with different taxonomic structures [1]. These microbial consortia perform essential biological functions for their plant hosts, including nutrient acquisition, immune regulation, and stress responses [1–3]. A significant proportion of the carbon fixed (over 20% in some cases) through photosynthesis is translocated to plant roots and released into the rhizosphere as exudates. These root-derived exudates contain various compounds, including sugars, organic acids, phenolic compounds, and proteins [4, 5]. Research has demonstrated that these exudates serve as both substrates and signaling molecules for soil microorganisms, influencing the recruitment and colonization of beneficial microbial taxa [6]. These microbiota, shaped by plant-derived exudates, are often referred to metaphorically as the plant's "second genome" [6–8]. The assembly and composition of rhizosphere microbial communities are shaped by a combination of environmental factors, including soil properties, agricultural practices, and the host plant genetic background. After soil type, the host plant genotype plays a particularly significant role in determining the structure of the rhizosphere microbiome [9]. In recent years, there has been growing interest in understanding the rhizosphere microbial communities associated with horticultural crops, especially perennial species, due to their unique ecological and agricultural significance [5].

Grafting, one of the oldest horticultural techniques, remains widely utilized in modern agriculture to improve crop resilience, yield, and disease resistance [10]. This method connects a vigorous root system (rootstock) and an above-ground portion (scion) capable of producing commercial crops. The union creates a vascular bridge, forming a physiological chimera characterized by a symbiotic relationship that enhances plant survival [11]. This union exchanges water, nutrients, hormones, photosynthetic products, proteins, mRNA, and small RNA (sRNA) between the rootstock and scion via the xylem and phloem. This exchange influences leaf morphology, root system development, and overall plant performance [12]. The mechanisms by which rootstocks mediate effects on scion phenotypes have been extensively studied, including responses to abiotic stress, nutrient mobilization, and regulation of circadian rhythms [13–15]. Conversely, scion traits can also impact rootstock characteristics and development by modulating sugar metabolites and hormone signaling [16]. Nevertheless, the influence of the scion on the structure of the rootstock rhizosphere microbial community has received little attention. Advances in high-throughput sequencing technology have enabled researchers to explore microbial taxonomic diversity in greater detail, particularly in grafting-related research [17]. While numerous studies have demonstrated that rootstocks play a critical role in shaping rhizosphere bacterial communities [18, 19], the reciprocal interaction between the scion and rootstock microbiomes remains understudied within grafted plant systems. Further research is needed to explore how scion-derived signals may influence rootstock-associated microbial communities.

Pears are one of the most economically significant fruit trees and have become one of the most widely cultivated fruits in temperate regions of China [20]. Due to their potent antioxidant properties, pear peels have become increasingly utilized in the production of nutritional and healthcare products [21]. Globally, there are over 5,000 types of pear germplasm resources [22]. Based on geographical distribution, pears can be classified in European and Asian varieties. China is recognized as the primary center of origin for pears, with more than 2,000 cultivated varieties [22, 23]. Among the main edible pear varieties, 'Sucui' pear (Pyrus pyrifolia (Burm. F.) Nakai) is a newly developed early-maturing sand pear variety, created by hybridization between 'Huasu' pear (female parent) and 'Cuiguan' pear (male parent). 'Hongqie' pear (Pyrus communis L.), an early-ripening variety originating from the United States, was introduced in China from Yugoslavia in 1977. These two pear varieties are extensively utilized as scion and rootstock materials in commercial fruit production across China [24]. The above-ground (scion) and below-ground (rootstock) genotypes in grafted pear trees differ, influencing performance. The compatibility between scion and rootstock, as well as their nutrient uptake capabilities and disease resistance, can vary depending on environmental factors [22]. Additionally, different scion varieties exhibit distinct photosynthetic efficiencies, which directly affect the regulation of root system architecture. However, it remains unclear whether distinct pear scion cultivars influence the assembly and regulation of rhizosphere fungal communities.

Pear production underpins farming economies with diversity for crop type and dietary nutrition in several countries [25]. However, lower input efficiency and productivity increasingly hinge on root-soil microbial interactions in perennial orchards. Grafting is the foundation of modern pear cultivation; however, most microbiome-oriented research has focused on rootstock effects [19, 26], leaving a critical gap: whether scion genotypes actively contribute to rhizosphere fungal communities. In this study, we selected two rootstock varieties with distinct geographical distributions; 'Duli' (Pyrus betulifolia Bunge) and 'Shanli' (Pyrus ussuriensis Maxim). 'Duli', a wild pear variety native to China, highly tolerant to biotic and abiotic stresses and exhibiting excellent compatibility when European and Asian pear scions are grafted onto it. It has been identified as an exceptional dwarfing rootstock [27]. In contrast, 'Shanli' pear demonstrates strong cold resistance, drought tolerance, and adaptability to poor soil conditions, making it a widely used rootstock variety in colder regions of China [28]. We employed 'Hongqie', representing European pears, and 'Sucui', representing Asian pears, as scions. These were grafted onto 'Duli' and 'Shanli' rootstocks, respectively. We analyzed tree growth conditions, rhizosphere soil characteristics, and fungal communities across the four scion-rootstock combinations to evaluate comprehensively their performance. The primary objective of this study was to elucidate the contributions of pear scions and rootstocks to the diversity of rhizosphere fungal communities and to infer functional differences in rhizosphere fungi between European and Asian pears.

Materials and methods

Experimental design and sample collection

Samples were collected from four different grafting combinations involving two scion varieties ('Sucui' and 'Hongqie') and two rootstock varieties ('Duli' and 'Shanli'). In 2018, seeds were surface-sterilized by soaking in 75% ethanol for 30 s, followed by immersion in 5% sodium hypochlorite solution for 10 min. After thorough rinsing with sterile deionized water 5 times to remove residual disinfectants, the sterilized seeds were placed on sterile moist filter paper in Petri dishes and incubated at a controlled temperature of 25 ± 1 ℃ in a dark, humid environment (relative humidity 80–85%) for germination. All seeds were provided by Jiangsu Academy of Agricultural Sciences, located at Nanjing City, Jiangsu Province, China. Upon germination, the seedlings were transferred to a seedling-raising substrate with a composition of peat moss: perlite: vermiculite = 3: 1: 1 (v/v/v). When the seedlings developed 4–6 leaves, they were transplanted into soil. The soil had a pH of 6.4 and contained 15.32 g kg−1 organic matter (SOM), 64.80 mg kg−1 alkali-hydrolyzable nitrogen (AN), 42.43 mg kg−1 available phosphorus (AP), and 297.38 mg kg−1 available potassium (AK). After one year, pear seedlings of various genotypes were selected for grafting using a single-bud splice grafting method. A completely randomized experimental design was implemented to ensure statistical independence among treatments. Throughout the entire study, consistent disease control measures were applied to all treatments. This experiment focuses on the rootstock-scion combination system and does not include self-rooted treatments. Rootstocks of P. ussuriensis and P. betulifolia were obtained as seedling populations; thus, each rootstock individual represents a genetically distinct genotype rather than a clonal line. This heterozygosity is typical of seedling rootstock germplasm widely used in pear production and breeding. In all subsequent analyses, scion and rootstock were included as fixed factors to account for variation attributable to each partner in the grafted combination. The experiment was conducted at Lishui District, Nanjing City, China (31°23′ N, 118°51′ E). At the time of sampling, the trees had been maintained for six years. Five independent biological replicates were collected for each grafting combination to account for natural variability.

Leaf samples were collected from the tree and stored at −80 ℃ for subsequent analysis. Another portion of leaf tissue was dried to a constant weight by first being subjected to 105 ℃ for 30 min followed by air-drying at 70 ℃.

Rhizosphere soil sampling was conducted during the growing season using a sterile shovel to carefully excavate soil around the roots to a depth of 0–20 cm, minimizing root system disturbance. The rhizosphere soil was carefully collected from the root surface and lateral roots. Both the roots and associated rhizosphere soil were placed into sterile centrifuge tubes, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen at the field site, and transported to the laboratory for further fungal community analysis. Additional rhizosphere soil samples collected as previously described were air-dried under shaded conditions and sieved (< 2 mm) for subsequent analyses of soil physical and chemical properties. Four replicates of rhizosphere soil samples from distinct plants were collected for each scion-rootstock combination, allowing for the identification of various soil characteristics.

Root samples were washed with sterile distilled water, rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen to preserve their integrity before storage at −80 ℃ for future sugar degradation-related experiments.

Determination of plant and soil properties

The height of a grafted pear tree is defined as the vertical distance from the ground to the apex of the tree. For each scion/rootstock combination (treatment), tree height was measured on five randomly selected plants, with one consistent measurement taken per plant to ensure data reproducibility. To measure trunk circumference, tape measures at a point 15 cm above the graft junction were acquired. Stem thickness was assessed with a vernier caliper at the section of the trunk that is in contact with the ground. The leaf area of individual leaves was quantified using a specialized leaf area meter (ImageJ-assisted digital leaf area meters).

Leaf gas exchange parameters were measured during the sample harvest stage with a portable photosynthetic system (LI-COR, USA). For each scion-rootstock combination, each leaf was measured three times consecutively, and the average value of the three repeated measurements was used for subsequent statistical analysis (12 average values per treatment in total). During the measurement, the leaf temperature (25 ± 0.5 ℃), relative humidity (65 ± 1%), and photosynthetic photon flux density (1000 μmol m−2 s−1) were controlled [29].

Non-structural carbohydrate (NSC) concentrations were analyzed using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) with four replicates. Briefly, roots or leaves were weighed and ground with 10 mL of pre-cooled 80% ethanol. The homogenate was transferred to a centrifuge tube, and centrifuged at 4 ℃ for 10 min at 13,000 rpm. The supernatant was collected, and the extraction process was repeated once using additional 80% ethanol. The extracts were pooled, concentrated under nitrogen gas at 40 ℃, reconstituted with distilled water to a final volume of 5 mL, filtered, and stored for sucrose, fructose, glucose, and sorbitol analysis [30].

The determination of soil indicators was carried out according to the description in Chai et al. [5]. The soil pH value was assessed by measuring a 1: 2.5 (w/v) aqueous suspension using the FE28 digital pH meter provided by Mettler-Toledo Instruments (Shanghai) Co., Ltd., China. Soil organic matter (SOM) was determined through a redox titration method involving potassium dichromate and sulfuric acid. Alkali-hydrolysable nitrogen (AN) in the soil was quantified using a diffusion absorption approach. Available phosphorus (AP) content was extracted from the soil samples using a 0.5 mol L−1 NaHCO3 solution and analyzed following the methodology outlined by et al. For available potassium (AK), the extraction process involved a 1 mol L−1 ammonium acetate (CH3COONH4) solution, with subsequent quantification conducted via inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES).

Transcriptome sequencing, data processing, and differential expression analysis

In this study, total RNA was extracted from the leaf of the two scion varieties ('Sucui' and 'Hongqie') using the CTAB method [31]. The quality of the RNA was assessed with an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer and a NanoDrop One spectrophotometer. Libraries were prepared with the VAHTS mRNA-Seq v6 Library Prep Kit and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq X Ten. Initial sequencing raw reads underwent quality assessment and filtering via the fastp tool to generate high-quality clean reads [32]. To eliminate ribosomal RNA (rRNA) contamination, the short-read aligner Bowtie2 was utilized to map clean reads against the species-specific ribosomal sequence database, with strict alignment parameters that prohibited any base mismatches[33]; reads successfully mapped to ribosomal sequences were subsequently excluded from further analysis. Remaining high-quality reads were aligned to the reference genome of Chinese white pear (Pyrus bretschneideri; NCBI assembly accession GCF_000315295.1) using HISAT2. We chose this genome as a common reference for both scion genotypes, 'Hongqie' (P. communis) and 'Sucui' (P. pyrifolia), in order to quantify scion-dependent transcriptional responses within a single coordinate system. This strategy follows previous pear transcriptome studies, in which RNA-seq data from different Pyrus species, including European pear cultivars, were mapped to the Pyrus bretschneideri reference genome for differential expression and pathway analyses [34–36].

Gene expression abundance was quantified using the RSEM (RNA-Seq by Expectation–Maximization) program, with expression levels normalized as FPKM (fragments per kilobase of transcript sequence per million base pairs sequenced). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified by applying two stringent criteria: an absolute log2-transformed fold change (log2|FC|) greater than 1 and a false discovery rate (FDR) less than 0.05. For functional annotation of DEGs, the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database and Gene ontology (GO) database were employed. Hierarchical cluster analysis of DEG expression patterns was conducted using Java Treeview software to visualize expression trends. Additionally, KOBAS (KEGG Orthology Based Annotation System) software was used for KEGG pathway mapping, leveraging both P-values and BLAST alignment results. To pinpoint biologically relevant metabolic and signal transduction pathways associated with DEGs, hypergeometric tests were conducted (FDR < 0.05 and p-value < 0.05) to assess the significance of pathway enrichment, ensuring the identification of pathways with statistically meaningful associations.

DNA extraction, PCR amplification, and sequencing of fungal Internal Transcribed Spacer (ITS) rRNA gene

DNA was extracted from 0.5 g of rhizosphere soil samples using the HiPure Soil DNA Kits (Magen Biotech), following the manufacturer protocol [37]. The purity and concentration of the extracted DNA were assessed using a NanoDrop ND-1000 UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). A portion of the DNA was aliquoted and stored at −80 ℃ for subsequent analyses. Genomic DNA was amplified to target the ITS2 region of the ribosomal RNA gene using specific primers with unique barcodes. The primer sequences used were: ITS3_KYO2 (GATGAAGAACGYAGYRAA) and ITS4 (TCCTCCGCTTATTGATATGC) [38]. Following amplification, the amplicons were purified and ligated to sequencing adapters. Library quality was evaluated using qPCR on the ABI StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System to ensure proper concentration and size distribution. Paired-end sequencing was performed using a NovaSeq 6000 system (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) with the NovaSeq S2 Reagent Kit v1.5 (PE250 mode).

Bioinformatics analysis

After downloading the sequencing data, FASTP (version 0.18.0) was employed to filter the original data obtained from the Illumina platform [32]. Subsequently, the DADA2 algorithm was first applied to the filtered clean tags to resolve amplicon sequence variant (ASVs) by correcting sequencing errors and distinguishing single-base differences. The resulting ASVs were further subjected to chimera filtering using the UPARSE process (version 9.2.64) to remove chimeric sequences. These high-quality ASVs were then used for abundance statistics and other subsequent analyses. The tag sequence with the highest abundance was selected as the representative sequence for each ASV. For species classification annotation, the ASVs were aligned against the SILVA database (version 138.1) or the UNITE database (version 8.3) using the naive Bayes model implemented in RDP annotation software (version 2.2), with a confidence threshold set between 0.8 and 1 [39–41]. The abundance statistics for each species taxonomy were visualized using Krona (version 2.6) [42]. Furthermore, fungal functional groups (guilds) were inferred using FUNGuild database (version 1.0) and PICRUSt2 [43].

Co-occurrence network analysis

Co-occurrence network analysis was performed on the rhizosphere-associated microbiota of each pear scion variety to investigate significant relationships between ASVs. To construct the network [5], we first excluded ASVs with a relative abundance of less than 0.05% that were detected in fewer than 20% of the replicates for each scion variety. After this filtering step, pairwise correlation coefficients were calculated using Spearman's rank correlation coefficient. P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure to account for multiple testing. We retained correlations with values greater than 0.6 and adjusted P-values less than 0.05 to represent statistically significant relationships. Network topological properties, including degree distribution, average clustering coefficient, average path length, and network density, were calculated using the R package igraph for both observed and random networks (1,000 randomizations). Visualization of the network was conducted using Gephi version 0.9.1 with ForceAtlas2 layout to highlight key connections. The top 20 nodes with the highest connectivity in the network were identified as hub ASVs based on degree centrality. Taxonomic annotations for these hub ASVs revealed potential keystone taxa shaping microbial community structure.

Statistical analysis

Data on plant traits, soil properties, and bacterial alpha diversity and composition were generated using Origin 2024. A two-way ANOVA model was conducted to analyze the effects of pear scion variety and rootstock genotype on seedling traits, soil properties, and the sugar content in roots and leaves. If either the scion or rootstock variety emerged as a statistically significant predictor, an independent samples t-test was subsequently performed. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software version 20 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Scion genotype dominates rhizosphere soil chemical properties in pear graft combinations

In this study, we established a system to investigate the effects of different pear scions and rootstocks on graft combinations. After six years of growth in natural soil, pear scion-rootstock combinations exhibited plant heights ranging from 1.24 to 1.43 m, trunk circumferences of 7.22–9.27 cm, and leaf areas of 20.01–77.20 cm2. While plant height varied substantially between the genotypes, trunk circumference and leaf area showed minimal rootstock-driven variation (Tables S1–S2). Specifically, 'Sucui' and 'Hongqie' scions differed significantly in plant height (P < 0.05), with scion type also influencing trunk circumference (P < 0.01) and leaf area.

Building on these aboveground findings, we next investigated whether scion and rootstock genotypes differentially shaped the rhizosphere microenvironment (Fig. 1, Tables S3–S5). Rootstock genotypes significantly impacted SOM content and alkali-hydrolysable nitrogen. Notably, scion genotypes emerged as critical modulators of rhizosphere chemistry: 'Hongqie' scions elevated rhizosphere pH, available phosphorus (vital for photosynthesis), and available potassium (essential for stomatal function) relative to 'Sucui'.

Transcriptomic and metabolic evidence reveals scion-driven regulation of carbohydrate metabolism in pear grafts

Based on the phenotypic observations that scion varieties dominate aboveground growth (Fig. 1, Tables S1–S2), we explored the molecular mechanisms underlying this divergence through leaf transcriptome analysis. This study obtained 12 leaf RNA-seq libraries, yielding a total of approximately 3.80 × 107 – 4.70 × 107 clean reads (Table S6). The total mapping rate (Total_Mapped) of all samples to the reference genome of Pyrus bretschneideri (NCBI assembly accession number GCF_000315295.1) ranged from 73.13% to 77.54%, among which uniquely mapped reads (Unique_Mapped) accounted for 64.41% – 69.97%, and unmapped reads (Unmapped) accounted for 22.46% – 26.87%. Calculated by scion, the average total mapping rate of 'Hongqie'-related samples (HB and HU) was 73.32%, while that of 'Sucui'-related samples (SB and SU) was 76.19%.

Transcriptome analysis revealed massive gene expression remodeling between 'Hongqie' and 'Sucui' scions: when grafted onto P. betulifolia, 'Hongqie' exhibited 11,230 upregulated and 7,342 downregulated genes, a pattern recapitulated in P. ussuriensis-grafted (11,170 up/7,233 down; Fig. 2a, Fig. S1). In contrast, rootstock-induced differentially expressed genes were negligible, and principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) confirmed sample clustering by scion genotype (Fig. 2b–d), supporting the notion of scion dominance at the transcriptional level. Gene ontology (GO) enrichment showed significantly different of scions in carbohydrate metabolism-related functions, including hexosyltransferase (GO:0016758) and glucan metabolism (GO:0044042)—pathways directly linked to photosynthate synthesis. KEGG analysis further identified the Calvin cycle (ko00710) and nucleotide sugar metabolism (ko00520) as core pathways for scion-mediated carbon fixation, mechanistically aligning with the higher net photosynthetic rate in 'Hongqie' (Fig. 3a).

Two-way ANOVA corroborates the functional implications of transcriptomics data (Table S7): scion variety significantly influenced net photosynthetic rate (Pn, P < 0.01), transpiration rate (Tr, P < 0.001), and stomatal conductance (gs, P < 0.01), while rootstocks only affected intercellular CO₂ concentration (Ci, P < 0.05). This indicates scions dominate physiological differences by regulating photosynthetic apparatus efficiency, whereas rootstocks primarily impact local carbon balance. In leaves (Fig. 3e, S1), all non-structural carbohydrates are significantly affected by scion variety (Table S8): fructose (P < 0.05), glucose (P < 0.01), sorbitol (P < 0.001), and sucrose (P < 0.001). Notably, the contents of fructose, sorbitol, and sucrose in leaves are significantly higher in pear tree with 'Hongqie' as the scion in comparison to those from 'Sucui'. Additionally, differences in fructose (P < 0.05) and glucose (P < 0.01) are observed between rootstock genotypes. Specifically, glucose content is significantly greater in leaves of pear tree with 'Hongqie' as the scion compared to those with 'Sucui'. In roots (Fig. 3f, S2), scion variety has a significant effect on glucose content (P < 0.05). Similar to leaf tissue, glucose, fructose, and sorbitol contents are significantly higher in pear tree with 'Hongqie' as the scion compared to those with 'Sucui'. However, sucrose content shows the opposite trend. Rootstock genotype significantly affects fructose content (P < 0.05). Furthermore, a significant interaction between scion and rootstock is observed for sorbitol and sucrose levels in both leaves and roots (Table S8).

Pear scion cultivars alter the characteristics of the rhizosphere fungal community

In total, we obtained 1,742,580 high-quality sequences from 16 soil samples and identified 7,419 ASVs (Table S9). Mantel test analysis and redundancy analysis (RDA) revealed significant correlations between fungal microbial communities and leaf metabolite concentrations, specifically fructose (P < 0.05), sorbitol (P < 0.01), and sucrose (P < 0.05) contents (Fig. 4a, Fig. S3). In this study, no significant differences in rhizosphere fungal alpha diversity were observed between rootstock genotypes; however, significant differences were detected between scion varieties (Shannon index: P < 0.05, Table S10). Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) further demonstrated that the rhizosphere fungal communities of scion-rootstock combinations formed two distinct clusters along the first principal axis (Fig. 4b–e, Table S11). PERMANOVA analysis revealed that scion variety alone explained 11.6% of the total variance in fungal community structure (P < 0.05), while rootstock genotype accounted for 7.82% of the variability, which was not statistically significant (P > 0.05). Additionally, the interaction between scion variety and rootstock genotype explained 4.40% of the total variance; however, this effect was also not significant (P > 0.05).

According to the classification of ASVs, eight fungal phyla were identified in the rhizosphere of grafted pear tree: Ascomycota, Basidiomycota, Glomeromycota, Chytridiomycota, Mortierella, Mucor, Blastomycota, and Rhododendron. Among these, Ascomycota, Basidiomycota, and Glomeromycota were the most dominant, collectively accounting for 90.86–91.57% of the total relative abundance. A Manhattan plot revealed that 113 ASVs exhibited significant differences between the two scion varieties, with the majority belonging to the phyla Ascomycota, Basidiomycota, Chytridiomycota, and Glomeromycota (Fig. 4f-h, S4). Further analysis of fungal community composition among scion varieties indicated that the rhizosphere associated with 'Hongqie' as the scion exhibited a higher abundance of Basidiomycota compared to 'Sucui', while Mortierella showed an inverse trend.

We classified the relative abundance of fungal taxa at the genus level. The ten most abundant fungal genera identified were Aspergillus, Spizelella, Basiliella, Glomus, Cladosporium, Fusarium, Penicillium, Mortierella, Preosia, and Cladosporium. Significant differences were observed between the two scion varieties for the genera Cladosporium (P < 0.05) and Fusarium (P < 0.01). Specifically, the abundance of Cladosporium in 'Hongqie' was significantly lower than that in 'Sucui', while Fusarium was more abundant in 'Hongqie' compared to 'Sucui'. No significant differences were found among rootstock genotypes at either the phylum or genus levels. These findings suggest that pear scion varieties, as the above-ground component of the grafting combination, exert a significant regulatory influence on rhizosphere fungal diversity and composition compared to rootstock genotypes.

Predictive functional analysis of rhizosphere fungal communities

This study aimed to predict rhizosphere fungal functions using FungalTraits (Fig. 5). Our results revealed that the rhizospheres of pear tree with 'Hongqie' and 'Sucui' as scions were predominantly associated with wood saprotrophs, soil saprotrophs, and plant pathogens. Notably, plant pathogens exhibited significantly higher abundance in the rhizosphere of pear tree with 'Hongqie' compared to those with 'Sucui'. Functional predictions using PICRUSt2 further highlighted metabolic differences between the two scion-associated rhizospheres. In 'Hongqie', we observed significant enrichment of pathways related to L-arginine biosynthesis I, L-ornithine biosynthesis, and 5-aminoimidazole ribonucleotide biosynthesis. These findings suggest potential variations in nitrogen metabolism or nucleotide biosynthesis between the two systems. Conversely, the rhizosphere of 'Sucui' tree showed elevated abundance of functions associated with glucose metabolism (e.g., glucose degradation), sucrose invertase activity, and starch degradation. These differences may influence carbon cycling dynamics in the rhizosphere.

Scion genotype shapes fungal asv co-occurrence networks and their correlations with plant physiological parameters in pear rhizosphere

The scion-driven changes in rhizosphere soil properties (Fig. 3) further shaped the fungal community assembly, as revealed by co-occurrence network analysis (Fig. 6). The fungal microbiome co-occurrence network in the rhizosphere of the pear grafting combinations revealed distinct patterns between 'Hongqie' and 'Sucui', as shown in Fig. 6a. Across all networks analyzed, co-occurring interactions were significantly more prevalent than mutual exclusions. A comparison between the two networks revealed that despite having a similar number of nodes, the rhizosphere network of 'Hongqie' exhibited higher interaction counts (edges) and a greater average clustering coefficient compared to 'Sucui'. In contrast, the number of mutual exclusions (edges) and the average path length were lower in the 'Hongqie' rhizosphere network than in that of 'Sucui' (Table S12). Furthermore, there were notable differences in the classification of highly connected nodes between the two varieties (Fig. 6b, c). In both 'Hongqie' and 'Sucui' rhizospheres, central ASVs characterized by high abundance and prevalence predominantly belonged to the phylum Ascomycota (Fig. 5b). At the genus level, however, distinct patterns emerged: the central ASVs in 'Hongqie' were primarily associated with Talaromyces, Chaetomium, Cistella, Fusarium, Olivenia, and Stalk, while those in 'Sucui' were mainly linked to Cladosporium, Chaetomium, Thielavia, Pterospermia, Exophyllum, Humicola, Leuspora, Microcoelium, and Stachybotrys.

Mantel test analysis revealed a significant impact of the grafting combination on the network topology linking the rhizosphere fungal community structure to plant physiological parameters, including photosynthetic physiology, leaf sugar content, and soil chemical properties. In 'Hongqie'-grafted pear tree, core ASVs (excluding ASV57, ASV59, ASV92, and ASV94) exhibited positive correlations with photosynthetic rate, transpiration rate, stomatal conductance, leaf fructose, sorbitol, sucrose, and available potassium (AK). The positive correlation between available potassium and Fusarium abundance in 'Hongqie' rhizospheres (Fig. 6c, d) suggests that scion-mediated potassium mobilization selects for specific fungal taxa. Notably, ASV4 was significantly more abundant in 'Hongqie' and negatively correlated with pH. Conversely, all these parameters were positively correlated across the dataset. Of particular interest, ASV1 and ASV25 were significantly enriched in 'Sucui' and exhibited strong negative associations with the aforementioned parameters.

Discussion

The significant role of scion genotype in determining plant phenotypes and shaping rhizosphere dynamics has often been underestimated in grafting studies, which have predominantly emphasized rootstock-mediated traits [19, 26]. Our four-year field experiment provides compelling evidence that different pear scion varieties substantially influence both aboveground growth and the regulation of belowground fungal communities, while the effects of rootstock genotype are comparatively minor. This finding underscores the previously overlooked role of the scion in mediating interactions among plants, soil, and microorganisms.

Scion genotype as the primary determinant of aboveground growth

Due to the extended juvenile period, the intrinsic challenges of traditional breeding methods, and the high associated costs, grafting has become a cornerstone of commercial pear cultivation to enhance profitability [22, 23]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that grafting not only improves the adaptability of woody perennials in diverse environments [12], but also modifies scion phenotypes, including early maturity, yield, disease resistance, and fruit quality [44]. In this study, while rootstock genotype exerted some influence on the plant height, stem diameter, and leaf area of graft combinations [45, 46], these effects were not statistically significant. Instead, consistent with the phenotypic observations (Tables S1–S2), the scion variety emerged as the primary regulator of these traits, which are critical for light capture and photosynthetic efficiency. The significant divergence in plant height, trunk circumference, and leaf area between 'Hongqie' and 'Sucui' correspnd with transcriptomic evidence revealing massive gene expression remodeling. These traits are regulated by scion-specific gene expression programs (e.g., Calvin cycle-related genes) that are independent of rootstock background. Such genetic-driven aboveground traits subsequently mediate belowground rhizosphere chemistry and fungal community assembly.

Photosynthesis is universally acknowledged as foundational to plant growth and yield enhancement [47, 48], producing carbohydrates such as sucrose, glucose, fructose, and sorbitol—the primary sugars in pear tissues [29]. In this study, the scion variety had a significant impact on the levels of non-structural carbohydrates in both leaves and roots: 'Hongqie' as a scion significantly increased leaf fructose, sorbitol, and sucrose contents compared to 'Sucui'. The efficient transport of these carbohydrates from source (leaves) to sink (fruits and roots) is crucial for tree development and plays a vital role in influencing fruit quality, depending on the regulated distribution of sucrose and sorbitol [30]—compounds closely associated with flavor, texture, and dry matter content [49–51].

Scion-mediated regulation of rhizosphere chemistry

The root system of grafted combinations serves as the central interface with soil, playing a pivotal role in mediating plant responses to both biotic and abiotic stresses, such as water availability, nutrient acquisition, toxic compounds, and pathogen attacks [52]. Previous studies have demonstrated that the root exudate profile influenced by scion-specific carbohydrate metabolism may drive roots to secrete more organic acids (such as malic acid and citric acid) and sugars, which in turn regulate soil pH and nutrient solubility. This explains the increase in rhizosphere pH, available phosphorus, and available potassium in the 'Hongqie' treatment [53]. Importantly, the ANOVA results for non-structural carbohydrates (Table S7) reveal that this scion effect on carbon status is not transmitted to the roots in a simple one-to-one manner. While leaf sorbitol and sucrose are strongly controlled by scion genotype, root sorbitol and sucrose are dominated by the Scion × Rootstock interaction and do not show a significant scion main effect [11, 12, 47]. This interaction implies that any influence of scion genotype on the rhizosphere must be understood within the context of the grafted scion – rootstock combination, and our results support the view that scion – dependent leaf carbon traits respond in a coordinated, but rootstock-modulated, network.

The observed effect of scion on available potassium is particularly noteworthy, as potassium plays a critical role in stomatal function and stress tolerance [54]. 'Hongqie' ability to elevate rhizosphere potassium levels may explain its higher transpiration rate and stomatal conductance (Fig. 3b,d), creating a positive feedback loop in which enhanced nutrient availability supports photosynthetic efficiency. This finding extends beyond previous reports that rootstocks primarily regulate nutrient uptake [55], demonstrating that pear scions actively shape the belowground chemical environment to fulfill their physiological demands.

Scion-driven carbohydrate metabolism shapes rhizosphere fungal communities

Plant genotype significantly influences root metabolism and exudate composition, directly affecting the establishment and maintenance of interactions between host plants and soil microorganisms [9, 56]. Previous studies have demonstrated that plant genotype can profoundly alter microbial community structure and abundance, particularly impacting fungal communities [57]. In grafting systems, the regulatory role of scions (representing aboveground genotypes) in shaping rhizosphere microorganisms has garnered increasing attention. For instance, the composition of arbuscular mycorrhizal fungal communities in citrus is strongly influenced by scion genotype [55], whereas apple rhizosphere fungal communities are primarily regulated by rootstocks [58], indicating species-specific differences in the regulatory roles of scions and rootstocks on microbial communities.

Soil physicochemical properties such as pH, structure, and organic matter content shape microbial community structure by creating specific niches [56, 59]. These properties also alter root exudate composition, thereby regulating microbial colonization. Among root exudates, sugars—as the main component—significantly impact various microorganisms (including pathogens, beneficial bacteria, and fungi) by modulating nutrient availability and soil pH [59]. It has been confirmed that fungi predominantly utilize soluble sugars and lipids derived from sucrose, and differences in root-secreted sugar components significantly affect fungal recruitment and enrichment [60, 61].

Mantel test analysis in this study further supports this mechanism. The scion-mediated carbon allocation plays a central role in structuring rhizosphere microbiomes, which likely drive distinct root exudation patterns, selecting for specific fungal taxa—consistent with findings that plant-derived sugars are primary drivers of fungal recruitment, with different monosaccharides and polyols favoring divergent taxonomic groups [60, 61].

Notable genomic differences exist between European and Asian pears due to divergent selection pressures [62]. Consequently, 'Hongqie' (a representative of European pears) and 'Sucui' (a representative of Asian pears) exhibit variations in genetic lineages, which could indirectly influence their rhizosphere fungal communities. Our results reveal significant differences in fungal enrichment between the two scion varieties, with the majority of operational taxonomic units (ASVs) belong to Ascomycota, Basidiomycota, Chytridiomycota, and Glomeromycota. Among these, Ascomycota play a critical role in acquiring essential elements such as nitrogen, potassium, and iron [63]. Additionally, symbiotic relationships between Glomeromycota and vascular plants are widespread, primarily associated with enhanced phosphorus uptake and improved nutrient acquisition capabilities [64].

Ascomycota, Basidiomycota, and Glomeromycota collectively account for 90.86%–91.57% of the relative abundance in both scion-associated rhizospheres, reflecting their ecological versatility in carbon utilization. However, 113 differentially abundant ASVs were identified between 'Hongqie' and 'Sucui' (Fig. 4f–h), highlighting scion-specific filtering: 'Hongqie' enriched in Basidiomycota, known for lignocellulose degradation, while 'Sucui' favored Mortierella, a genus linked to soil organic matter decomposition [64]. These differences may arise from distinct exudate profiles—'Hongqie' higher sucrose content could support Basidiomycota, whereas 'Sucui' sorbitol may preferentially fuel Mortierella.

Functional implications of scion-associated fungal communities

According to Fig. 5, The primary functional role of the fungal community in the rhizosphere of grafted pear trees were different, and this functional divergence aligns with the distinct evolutionary backgrounds of the two varieties: 'Hongqie', a European pear, may lack co-evolved defense mechanisms against Asian soil-borne pathogens [65], while 'Sucui', an Asian pear, has likely developed strategies to suppress local pathogenic taxa. Notably, our findings reveal that ASV4 (a Fusarium genus member) is significantly enriched in 'Hongqie' rhizospheres compared to 'Sucui'. This observation is ecologically relevant, as Fusarium causes root rot—a disease particularly prevalent in Asian pear orchards [65, 66]—and underscores the vulnerability of European pears to region-specific pathogens. The mechanism underlying this pattern may involve root exudates Fusarium spore germination, a critical initial step in colonization, is typically inhibited by antifungal compounds [67] but can be triggered by nutrient-rich exudates, with the response dependent on exudate composition and quantity [65]. This scion-mediated regulation of rhizosphere pathogen populations contrasts with observations in other crops: in tomato, rootstocks rather than scions have been shown to influence Fusarium abundance in the rhizosphere [10], whereas in banana, expressing cell-death-inducing fungal elicitors specifically in the xylem enhances resistance to Fusarium wilt by increasing jasmonic acid (JA) accumulation, reinforcing vascular cell walls and upregulating defence-related genes, including a VND-type NAC transcription factor that participates in a positive feedback loop on xylem defence [68]. These hormone-dependent and NAC-linked defence responses illustrate how aboveground genotypes can systemically influence plant-pathogen interactions along the root-xylem continuum. Together with our data in pear, these comparative examples support the notion that scion genotype and its associated hormone and carbon signalling networks can play an active role in steering rhizosphere communities in perennial fruit crops.

Future directions

A methodological limitation of our study is that all leaf RNA-seq reads from both scion genotypes were mapped to the P. bretschneideri reference genome, even though 'Hongqie' belongs to P. communis. This cross-mapping strategy is widely used in pear and other woody perennials and, in our case, yielded comparable and generally high mapping rates for both scions [34–36]. Nevertheless, expression of P. communis-specific genes or highly diverged genomic regions is likely underestimated in the present analysis [22, 62]. As a result, the DEG lists and pathway enrichments reported here should be regarded as a conservative estimate of scion-dependent transcriptional changes, especially for the European pear scion. Future work should re-analyse these datasets using a high-quality P. communis reference genome (e.g. ‘Bartlett’ DHv2.0) or a pan-Pyrus genome, in order to validate and refine the key scion-associated pathways identified in this study [24].

Our findings open several directions for future research. First, combining leaf transcriptomics with direct measurements of root exudates and rhizosphere metabolomes would help to more explicitly link scion-dependent carbon allocation to belowground chemical gradients and fungal niche partitioning [1, 3, 7]. Second, experiments using clonal or genotyped rootstocks, and extending the graft combinations to additional pear and rootstock genotypes, could further dissect the relative contributions of rootstock diversity and Scion × Rootstock interactions to rhizosphere assembly [11, 58]. Third, targeted inoculation studies with enriched taxa such as Mortierella and Fusarium, under controlled environmental conditions, may clarify the functional consequences of scion-associated fungal communities for pear growth, nutrient use efficiency and disease risk [8, 65, 67]. In particular, the scion-responsive genes highlighted in the carbohydrate metabolism heat map (Fig. S1, Supplementary 3), including sorbitol transporters, sorbitol dehydrogenases and sucrose-phosphorylating enzymes such as sucrose synthases and hexokinases/fructokinases, represent promising candidates for overexpression or silencing in transgenic or gene-edited lines to test their causal roles in linking leaf carbon status to rhizosphere fungal community assembly. Together, these approaches may provide a more mechanistic and predictive framework for using scion-rootstock combinations to steer rhizosphere microbiomes in sustainable pear production.

Conclusion

Pear scion genotype plays a central role in the grafted system, exerting a primary influence on aboveground growth, photosynthetic performance and leaf sugar profiles, and is associated with differences in rhizosphere fungal communities. Scion-dependent combinations are further linked to shifts in rhizosphere chemistry (pH, available P and available K), which together are consistent with a nutrient–photosynthesis feedback between the scion and the belowground environment rather than demonstrating a simple linear causal chain. Scion-mediated carbon allocation appears to contribute to fungal community divergence, with differential ASVs suggesting scion-related filtering: 'Hongqie' tends to enrich Basidiomycota and putative pathogens (e.g. Fusarium), whereas 'Sucui' more frequently favors Mortierella and Saprotrophs. Overall, our findings highlight scion genotype as a key component of plant – soil – microbe associations in grafted pear systems and suggest that scion selection may offer a promising strategy to support more sustainable pear production.
