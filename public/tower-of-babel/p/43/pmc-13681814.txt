Breastfeeding after early-onset breast cancer: a scoping review
Authors: Jennifer G Taylor, Tisha Felder, Karen Wickersham, Michael D Wirth, Linda Hazlett
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo synthesize the current evidence examining the breastfeeding experiences of women with a history of early-onset breast cancer.Data sourcesA scoping review of the literature was conducted using the following databases: CINAHL, PubMed, Psych Info and Web of Science. Citations of relevant articles were also reviewed.Study selectionWe included articles published in English from 2000 to 2023 that were primary empirical full-text articles with objectives examining breastfeeding in women with a history of breast cancer under the age of 45. Thirty-seven full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, and 11 articles met criteria.Data extractionThe Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) was used to organize the search results. Information extracted from the articles included: country of origin, objectives of the study, demographics of participants, types of support and breastfeeding challenges.Data synthesisAll articles reported cases of successful breastfeeding after breast cancer. Common challenges of breastfeeding after breast cancer included insufficient milk supply, nipple pain, and little knowledge on the topic from the clinical team.ConclusionPotential challenges women may experience with breastfeeding after breast cancer are insufficient milk supply, engorgement, and lack of support from their family and their healthcare team. Discussion of infant-feeding goals during the antepartum period with continued support immediately and well into the postpartum period is crucial to assist with breastfeeding issues and prolong success.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12884-026-09351-3.Keywords: Breastfeeding after breast cancer, Breast cancer survivors

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most common type of cancer diagnosed in young women with a rate of 23.4 new cases per 100,000 in the United States [1]. Approximately 10% of incident breast cancer cases are diagnosed in women under the age of 45 which defines early-onset breast cancer (EOBC) [2]. EOBC incidence has been steadily increasing over the last decade which, in part, has resulted in a change to 2016 breast cancer screening guidelines currently in progress from the United States Preventative Services Task Force [3–5]. The draft guidelines recommend all women, regardless of their family history, begin biennial mammograms at age 40 [3].

EOBC cases include more aggressive subtypes less likely to be amenable to treatment resulting in a significant emotional, physical, and economic burden to those diagnosed and their families [6]. These burdens can be magnified in Black women and their families because of the disparities in health care that exists within the U.S. However, even with the increasing incidence in this subgroup of women, young women have not been the focus of breast cancer screening and prevention strategies and interventions for this phenomenon remain limited [7, 8].

Fertility and motherhood after breast cancer

Women in their reproductive ages are often focusing on starting their careers, thinking about settling down with their long-term partners, and about starting a family [9]. Young women who have not started or completed their families often look forward to the bond that comes along with pregnancy and breastfeeding their newborns, not the thought about the possibility of being diagnosed with breast cancer [10]. For women who are diagnosed with breast cancer, the timeframe to begin treatment is often rapid and the discussions of fertility preservation and breast-preservation can be easily missed [7–10]. To ensure that women receive education on fertility preservation within a timely manner of cancer treatment, the American Society for Clinical Oncology (ASCO) and the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) now mandate counseling for fertility preservation for those receiving chemotherapy [11]. Treatments like chemotherapy and radiation can negatively affect a woman’s future fertility and breastfeeding efforts [12–14]. Chemotherapy and radiation can negatively affect younger women more than older women because these treatments often bring on early menopause symptoms years before they have reached actual menopause [5, 15]. Breastfeeding is contraindicated while on chemotherapy because of the potential for it to transfer from breastmilk to the infant so current recommendations agree that women are safe to conceive and breastfeed one to two years after their completed breast cancer treatment [9, 12, 14, 16, 17].

Breastfeeding and breast cancer

In the general population, breastfeeding has been shown to be a protective factor against triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) and even for women who are carriers of the BRCA1/2 gene [4, 18–22]. In fact, breastfeeding in women with a history of breast cancer may even reduce the risk for recurrence, but more research is needed [21]. The Society of Obstetricians and Gynecologists of Canada clinical practice guidelines state that there is no evidence that breastfeeding increases the risk of recurrence of breast cancer and carries no risk to the infant in women with a history of breast cancer [23, 24]. Women with a history of breast cancer who breastfeed have been shown to benefit emotionally from support given by a lactation consultant or maternity nurse as well as learn proper breastfeeding techniques to help with longer duration [25].

Research question

Despite the increasing incidence and worse outcomes, EOBC remains poorly understood and considerable gaps exist in the literature regarding the risk factors, diagnosis, prognosis, and screening measures [8]. The purpose of this scoping review is to synthesize the current evidence examining the breastfeeding experiences of women with a history of early-onset breast cancer. We aim to answer two questions: (1) how many studies reported women who can successfully breastfeed, defined as their infant being able to effectively latch and swallow at either the affected breast or the unaffected breast, after breast cancer with details on their experiences, (2) what the challenges were these women experienced when attempting to breastfeed after breast cancer.

Methods

Design and inclusion/exclusion criteria

The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) was used to guide the article search and reporting of the findings [26]. A scoping review of the literature was completed with studies published from January 2000 through July 2023. Keywords for the article search included the following: breastfeeding after breast cancer, motherhood after breast cancer, early-onset breast cancer and breastfeeding. Articles were selected if the publication documented on some aspect of breastfeeding in women 45 years or younger with a history of breast cancer. Researchers who reported measuring pregnancy-associated breast cancer and postpartum breast cancer were excluded from the search because of the emerging evidence of the pathophysiological differences as well as the variety of definitions surrounding those diagnoses. Articles were extracted and organized by database into Endnote where duplicates were then removed.

Literature search, screening, and article selection

The keywords and MESH terms best relevant for the topic as well as ensuring each database search was accurate and consistent. The databases for this review were: CINAHL, PubMed, Psych Info and Web of Science with filters for articles after 2003 through July 2023. Searches from each database were imported into Endnote totaling 480, after which duplicates were removed and articles considered ineligible leaving 61 articles. The 61 articles with abstracts and titles were reviewed for relevance to the research question. Of these, 37 articles were retrieved for full-text and reviewed, and their list of references were reviewed for further studies relevant to the search. Research studies that were published before January 2000 were removed because of the significant advances discerning EOBC as its own entity over the last two decades. Titles and abstracts were screened for their relevance to the overall research question in that they reported breastfeeding experiences after breast cancer, with 61 records screened by reviewing abstracts in detail and 37 reports were reviewed to determine their eligibility. From the 37 reports that were eligible, 14 articles were reviewed from the citations, and 26 were excluded. Chosen articles were included if the study population included females with a history of breast cancer ≤ 45 years of age and if any type of breastfeeding after breast cancer outcomes were examined, measured, or discussed. Eleven articles in total were deemed relevant for this review.

Data extraction and analysis

Articles were then organized into an evidence table presented Table 1. Information extracted from each of the articles included: country of origin, objectives of the study, demographics of participants, breastfeeding challenges as well as types of support. Results

Overview of included studies

There were 11 articles that gave descriptive evidence of the experiences of mothers who were able to successfully breastfeed after breast cancer. Table 2 depicts the results of the selection process for this review. In terms of study designs, there were two case studies (Michaels and Wanner [25]; Poupas-Martins and Sim-Sim [30]), two case–control studies (Lambertini et al. [28]; [31]), three retrospective cohort studies [5, 27, 29], one prospective cohort study [32], two exploratory qualitative studies [9, 13], and one qualitative study guided by social constructionism [15].

The articles by Stopenksi et al. 2017 [32] and Smorti et al. 2020 [31] documented outcomes of breastfeeding in mothers with any cancer, not just breast cancer, and were deemed appropriate for this review because of the documented breastfeeding experiences from the breast cancer participants.

Common findings among all articles included positive breastfeeding support, misinformation on breastfeeding after breast cancer, challenges with breastfeeding techniques, as well as small sample sizes with a lack of diversity across most of the selected studies. Only two articles reported participant demographics in which all women identified in their samples as being white. Age of participants were reported in all articles and ranged from 20 to 46 years. All 11 reports in this review documented participants who expressed an interest in wanting to breastfeed their newborn after their breast cancer and many were able to initiate and successfully breastfeed, even if for only a small amount of time. Four studies reported participants who were able to breastfeed for six months or longer [5, 9, 13, 25]. Being able to successfully breastfeed allowed participants to feel a sense of normalcy as specifically reported in at least two of the studies [9, 13]. Positive psychological effects from being able to breastfeed and bond with their newborn were reported across the articles. Participants often described their breast cancer had limited them from having a normal pregnancy and breastfeeding experience. Being able to breastfeed, even if for a short period of time, helped them to forgive their body and offered them a sense of hope and normalcy [9, 13].

Successful breastfeeding was seen in all 11 reports. Using the contralateral or unaffected breast was attributed to successful breastfeeding and longer duration. Most recommendations advise not to attempt breastfeeding on the affected/irradiated breast because of the decrease supply or potential for complications such as mastitis which may be harder to treat [5, 9, 13]. Three studies reported participants breastfeeding using the affected or irradiated breast and only 2 participants were able to actually breastfeed but stopped after two weeks [5, 9, 29]. Concerns about breast cancer recurrence or difficulty of detecting a new onset of breast cancer during or after breastfeeding was also a common theme reported by participants in three of the studies [9, 13, 15]. Although rare, Azim et al. 2010 [5] reported two relapses in breast cancer in their participants with the first relapse in a participant that did not attempt breastfeeding nine years after delivery and the second relapse found in a participant was a local recurrence 2 years after delivery who had breastfed her baby for 11 months. The fear of cancer recurrence was so great in some of the participants that they chose not to breastfeed at all or only for a small amount of time [13, 15]. Other participants were afraid of recurrence because of previous dismissal of breast lumps as a normal presentation of pregnancy and lactation that caused a delay in their previous breast cancer diagnosis from their clinical team [9, 13]

Lambertini et al. 2018 focused on the safety of pregnancy according to ER subtypes of breast cancer [28]. When comparing women who were able to get pregnant with a history of breast cancer to the non-pregnant cohort with a history of breast cancer, no difference in disease-free survival was observed in participants who breastfed their newborns (HR = 0.70, 95% CI = 0.26 to 1.94, p = 0.50) [32]. These results suggest that pregnancy after breast cancer is safe and should not be discouraged.

Breastfeeding support

Seven articles mentioned some type of positive or negative professional support among participants. The types of support received by the lactating mothers varied across publications. Professional support, which can be defined as practical breastfeeding techniques and monitoring for complications from some type of clinical expert, such as an International Board-Certified Lactation Consultant (IBCLC) or by other nursing staff, was seen in seven of the studies [5, 9, 15, 25, 30, 31]. Overall, support received by the participants ranged from a variety of sources including oncologists, obstetricians, IBCLC’s, maternity nurses, breastfeeding support groups, private consultations, and spousal or partner support.

Two studies reported participants having positive experiences with receiving practical support on breastfeeding techniques from IBCLC’s and maternity nurses which helped them to be able to successfully breastfeed [15, 21]. These practical breastfeeding techniques included having the infant placed skin-to-skin soon after delivery, engaging in frequent feedings, helping with pumping after breastfeeding sessions using a hospital-grade electric breast pump, close observation of each twin’s latch in order to protect the single breast and nipple to prevent complications like mastitis, as well as supplementing with formula [13, 25].

The timing of when participants received support greatly affected their ability to successfully breastfeed. Michaels and Wanner 2013 was the only study to specifically document practical breastfeeding support as well as setting breastfeeding goals beginning in the antepartum period and ending well into the postpartum period [25]. Two articles documented practical breastfeeding support during delivery, and two studies documented practical and emotional support from maternity nurses and IBCLC’s during the postpartum period [13, 25, 30]. Active participation and goal setting in the antepartum period allowed participants and the IBCLCs to set practical breastfeeding goals together which can help ease disappointment if the participant is not able to accomplish her goals. Two studies had participants that reported feelings of guilt and disappointment for not being able to breastfeed as they originally intended to [9, 13]. Support beginning in the antepartum period can help achieve and sustain successful breastfeeding in the mother with a history of breast cancer, as well as decrease disappointment and guilt if she is not able to achieve her original goal. The IBCLC in Michaels & Wanner’s study followed the participant closely after she was discharged home and because of the continued close support, was able to successfully breastfeed her set of twins for over two years well past her initial goal of three months on the unaffected breast [25]. Azim et al. 2010 reported that lactation counseling was offered to five participants in which all were able to successfully breastfeed for a median duration of 12 months and a range from nine to 17 months but did not give specifics about when lactation counseling was started [5]. Stopenski et al. 2020 did not specifically measure or report outcomes on support but did acknowledge the importance of women with a history of breast cancer seeking out assistance early when lactation problems begin [32]. These examples further emphasize the fact that women with a history of breast cancer can greatly benefit from practical support beginning in the antepartum period to well into the postpartum period.

Support from maternity nurses and IBCLC was different compared to support from other clinical staff in that they gave practical breastfeeding technique support whereas support from obstetricians and oncologists were found to give more emotional and encouragement kind of support. Social pressures to breastfeed was documented in four articles and participants discussed how their physicians did not pass judgment regardless of their decision to pursue breastfeeding or not [9, 13, 15, 31]. Smorti et al. 2020 discussed the phrase “warm chain of support” first implemented by Kaiser, AM in 1994, which is a skilled care network that aims to assist mothers build their confidence with breastfeeding [31, 33]. This concept can be helpful for women with a history of breast cancer who want to breastfeed. They argue for the need for mothers to be able to freely express their concerns with their clinical team.

Breastfeeding challenges

Across the publications, technical breastfeeding challenges were well documented in six studies and included nipple pain, mastitis, insufficient milk supply, difficulty with infant latching, newborn not gaining weight, and engorgement [5, 9, 13, 15, 25, 29, 32]. Psychosocial issues were also well documented in six of the studies and documented confusion about breastfeeding, insecurity fulfilling infant’s nutritional needs, guilt from using formula, exhaustion due to time commitment, feelings of being overwhelmed, and worries about detecting a recurrence of their breast cancer [5, 9, 15, 30, 31].

Three studies reported a lack of provider support or knowledge about breastfeeding after breast cancer from physicians, IBCLC’s and maternity nurses [5, 9, 13]. Reasons for the clinical team not providing support include uncertainty of safety of breastfeeding, unfeasibility of breastfeeding, and not having substantial knowledge on breastfeeding after breast cancer [5, 9, 13]. Smorti et al. 2020 was the only article that acknowledged the lack of clear and specific guidelines for clinicians of breastfeeding after breast cancer [31].

Discussion

In this scoping review, many women were able to successfully breastfeed after their breast cancer. Participants expressed a strong desire to attempt breastfeeding using the unaffected breast or even attempt using both breasts. Successful breastfeeding or attempting to breastfeed helped instill a sense of normalcy for the women, providing them with confidence that they were doing what they thought was best for their infant as well as themselves [9, 13]. Many participants across the publications reported being happy to have had the breastfeeding experience and partake in the special bond breastfeeding provides with their newborn. Decisions to breastfeed often changed over time due to difficulty with latching, relying on just a single breast instead of two breasts, insufficient milk supply, and fear of not being able to detect recurrence in the lactating breast [15].

Although most participants were able to successfully breastfeed, limited information was available in the studies about specific techniques used to assist with breastfeeding, including early skin-to-skin contact after delivery, using a hospital-grade electric breast pump for pumping after breastfeeding sessions, close follow-up with an IBCLC after discharge home, and supplementation with either formula or expressed breast milk if milk supply is low. Successful breastfeeding and longer duration of breastfeeding was possible if breastfeeding was done using the unaffected breast [5, 9, 13, 15, 25, 27, 29, 30]. Most participants did not have any milk expression from the affected breast because of their past treatment. Other reasons for successful breastfeeding were largely due to the amount of professional support received, particularly from IBCLCs and close follow-up with women after discharge from the hospital [5, 9, 13, 15, 25, 30]. The documented of experiences of participants who were able to engage in successful breastfeeding after breast cancer and treatment is encouraging and should promote the concept that breastfeeding after breast cancer is possible and feasible with close nursing support which can be further defined as consistent monitoring and help with breastfeeding techniques from a nurse-midwife, IBCLC, or a registered nurse.

Some women, including women who have undergone a bilateral mastectomy, are physically unable to breastfeed but may still desire their infant to be fed human milk [9]. Other feeding methods include the use of formula and donor breast milk. Donor breast is.

human milk from donors who are screened for any blood-borne diseases [34]. Access to donor milk can be somewhat limited but it can be found in major cities across the U.S. from accredited milk banks like the Human Milk Banking Association of North America [34]. One receiver of donor milk from the Mothers Milk Bank Austin described how access to donor breast milk was a relief to her after she was diagnosed with breast cancer and her daughter had an allergic reaction to an ingredient in formula [35]. Most donor milk received from milk banks goes through a pasteurization process, which can decrease the milk’s nutritional content however, donor milk is still widely used by many mothers and even neonatal intensive care units for the exceptional nutritional values breast milk still can possess [36].

Having access to an encouraging and knowledgeable support network of various clinicians that helped with breastfeeding techniques, was reported beneficial to participants and their breastfeeding success across the articles of this review. Smorti et al. 2020 [31] advocated for the need for what they called a “warm chain” of support that consisted of a skilled care network to assist women with a history of cancer in building their confidence with attempting breastfeeding [33]. Best practices for breastfeeding education for the general population is recommended to start during the antepartum period and the same education and conversations should be done for young women with a history of breast cancer that are interested in breastfeeding their newborn [37]. If women with a history of breast cancer do not have access to professional lactation support or other types of support such as spousal/familial or breastfeeding support groups, breastfeeding after breast cancer can be difficult and result in frustration and feelings of failure. A systematic review conducted by Bhurosy et al. 2021 also acknowledged the importance of more research being needed for supporting antepartum and postpartum women who decide to breastfeed after breast cancer [38].

Limitations

This review has several limitations. Even with the assistance of a librarian for refining the search strategy, finding empirical articles of breastfeeding after breast cancer was challenging due to the multiple ways to categorize breast cancer in young women including EOBC, pregnancy-associated breast cancer, and postpartum breast cancer. Although similar, there are pathophysiological differences between terms. The definition of early-onset breast cancer ranges across public health organizations and research studies based on the age. The small sample sizes and lack of diversity among participants in the studies demonstrates the gaps in the literature regarding breastfeeding after breast cancer and potential next steps for future research. Even though breast cancer incidence is increasing among women of reproductive age, it is still generally considered to be uncommon and that may be one reason for the small sample sizes. Because of the increasing incidence of EOBC, more empirical research should be conducted in this population. Young Black women also have reported worse health-related quality of life after a breast cancer diagnosis furthering the need to include diverse sample sizes to address these concerns [39].

Implications for nursing

New mothers with a history of breast cancer experience unique challenges when attempting breastfeeding. There is a lack of clear guidelines for women who would like to attempt breastfeeding after their breast cancer and because of this, these mothers tend to experience a variety of information that may seem misleading or incorrect [31]. There is also a lack of diversity among the samples of the studies even though Black women are more likely to have higher rates of triple negative breast cancers but also have lower rates of breastfeeding. Registered nurses (RNs) are considered the primary caregivers for new mothers and their infants in the hospital setting and outpatient setting due to their close and consistent interaction [37]. A substantial part of the RN’s profession is to provide up-to-date, quality education supported by evidence-based practices which includes breastfeeding techniques such as initiating skin-to-skin contact after delivery, observing proper latch and sucking from the infant, teaching about infant feeding cues, preventing breast engorgement and clogged milk ducts that can lead to mastitis, and teaching self-efficacy which has been associated with a longer duration of breastfeeding [17, 40, 41]. RNs are also responsible for helping to establish breastfeeding plans with expectant mothers to help them achieve their breastfeeding goals. Support from expertly trained healthcare providers, such as RNs, IBCLCs, and nurse midwives, was important for successful breastfeeding in many of the studies listed in this review [5, 9, 12, 16, 28, 30]. Without the close support from RNs, new mothers, especially new mothers with a history of breast cancer, are more likely to experience breastfeeding challenges [27].

Lack of knowledge or support from health care providers was a common theme seen in the challenges that women with a history of breast cancer experienced. Many RNs have previously reported they lacked the knowledge necessary to support breastfeeding in the general population and is not extensively taught in nursing programs or hospital orientation programs. In this scoping review, we found that new mothers with a history of breast cancer reported that they did not have adequate time or education from hospital staff [5, 9, 13]. Reasons for not being able to provide education and support to breastfeeding mothers in the general population reported by RNs was due to staff shortages and increased workloads, which has been a longstanding issue for RNs across specialties, not just in maternal health [37].

Conclusion

In summary, breastfeeding after breast cancer is feasible among many breast cancer survivors of reproductive age. As more women develop EOBC during their reproductive years, it is crucial to examine this phenomenon from multiple perspectives including examining risk factors, creating interventions to assist with breastfeeding afterwards, and to raise awareness that breastfeeding is possible after breast cancer. Studies should be done exploring breastfeeding techniques and methods to best provide education and support to this population. Guidelines should be developed for women with a history of breast cancer who would like to attempt breastfeeding.
