Glycated haemoglobin for diagnosing and monitoring diabetes in Indian populations-authors' reply
Authors: Shambo Samrat Samajdar, Shashank R. Joshi, Anoop Misra, Naval K. Vikram
Published: 2026

We read with great interest the correspondence from Dr. Sutirtha Chakraborty1 regarding our article. While we appreciate the emphasis on assay standardization and biological variation (BV), we believe this viewpoint—largely derived from clinical settings such as Norway and other developed countries—does not fully capture the distinctive hematological complexities and realities encountered in India.

Our central argument is that although HbA1c may demonstrate lower biological variation in healthy, standardized cohorts, this advantage diminishes in populations with a high prevalence of conditions affecting red blood cell dynamics. In India, iron-deficiency anemia remains highly prevalent, while G6PD deficiency and hemoglobinopathies are also common. Under such circumstances, the “biological” component of HbA1c often reflects alterations in RBC lifespan rather than true ambient glycemia. Laboratory standardization alone cannot overcome the fundamental impact of shortened or prolonged RBC survival on glycation equilibrium, which underlies the clinical fallacies we described.

Importantly, we do not advocate replacing HbA1c. Rather, we propose a pragmatic multiparametric strategy in which HbA1c is corroborated by complementary tests, particularly when results appear discordant. Such an approach is feasible even in low-resource settings, as outlined in Table 3 of our study. For example, our findings indicate that when simple, low-cost indices such as Red Cell Distribution Width (RDW) are markedly elevated (>17%), the reliability of HbA1c declines substantially, warranting greater reliance on glucose-based diagnostics. Despite its recognized variability, oral glucose tolerance test and self-monitoring of blood glucose (SMBG) are easy-to-do, inexpensive, clinically informative for detecting dysglycemia in Asian Indians and method of doing these is easily known by physicians. Moreover, SMBG captures glycemic excursions—such as postprandial spikes (commonly seen in Asian Indians) and daytime variability—that HbA1c inherently cannot reflect. Other clinically relevant patterns, including daytime and nocturnal hypoglycemia, also fall outside the scope of HbA1c assessment.

Regarding concerns about blood glucose measurement, the pre-analytical issues cited (e.g., glycolysis) are largely technical and can be mitigated through appropriate collection protocols, such as the use of fluoride tubes. These solutions are considerably more accessible in many Indian settings than high-end, NGSP-certified HPLC platforms. In practical terms, blood glucose testing continues to represent the most cost-effective and dependable test in this context. Exclusive reliance on HbA1c in populations with substantial hematological heterogeneity may be clinically hazardous in India. Recognizing these limitations and incorporating corroborative testing should be viewed not as regression, but as a necessary step toward greater diagnostic precision in Asian Indian populations. Indeed, this discussion is long overdue and merits broader debate, both within India and across other South Asian countries where anemia is prevalent.

We also wish to make it clear that we are not advocating the “abandonment” of HbA1c.2 Rather, we are arguing for its selective and context-sensitive use, particularly in populations where biological and analytical limitations are common. If our article is read carefully, it is clear that we have emphasized this point at several places.

Our estimates of anaemia prevalence were based on robust and nationally representative data from the National Family Health Survey (NFHS). Chadwick et al. are under the false impression that only severe anaemia alters HbA1c interpretation; while iron deficiency itself—even when associated with mild anaemia—can meaningfully influence HbA1c values. Interestingly, in a robustly conducted Indian study by Hardikar et al.,3 (quoted in our article) the mean hemoglobin level of whole group was 13.0 g/dL (±2), that of anemic individuals was 12.1 + 2.3 g/dL, and curiously, 17 individuals had normal hemoglobin but low iron levels (non-anaemic iron deficiency). The authors, based on their data in mildly anemic and iron deficient population clearly stated: “The use of HbA1c to diagnose prediabetes and diabetes in iron-deficient populations may lead to a spuriously exaggerated prevalence.” To re-emphasize, in a country where iron deficiency is highly prevalent, regardless of the severity of anaemia, this has important implications for both diagnosis and monitoring.

In resource-constrained settings with financially strapped populations, routine reliance on HbA1c may not always be practical. The test remains relatively costly, and assay standardisation across laboratories is variable, especially in suburban and rural areas where, iron deficiency is also prevalent. Under such conditions, over-reliance on HbA1c risks both financial burden and misclassification.

We emphasise that HbA1c should be used selectively, where appropriate and feasible, and ideally interpreted alongside SMBG for monitoring response to therapy. Capillary glucose monitoring is inexpensive, empowers patients, and can be performed at home once or twice weekly (or every 7–10 days) without requiring hospital visits even in underserved populations. Maintaining a structured logbook of fasting and postprandial values is highly feasible and often more immediately actionable for therapeutic decisions.

We state, in strong difference to the authors, that for most Indian patients, regardless of location of residence and socio-economic strata, simple and low-cost blood glucose monitoring remains the most pragmatic and feasible tool for longitudinal tracking of glycaemic control, and oral glucose tolerance test (fasting and 2-h post 75 gm glucose samples) remain the gold standard for diagnosing diabetes.
