Incidence of hematologic cancer types in Japan by ICD-O-3 code: analysis of National Cancer Registry
Authors: Ayako Muramatsu, Tetsuji Kawakami
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAim: We classified subtypes of hematologic cancer in Japan’s National Cancer Registry (NCR) in 2016 by ICD-O-3 code and compared numbers with the Japanese Society of Hematology’s Blood Diseases Registry (BDR).Materials & methods: We reviewed data for individual cases, and calculated incidence per 100,000.Results: NCR reported 67,919 cases of hematological cancer, including diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, Not Otherwise Specified (NOS) (11.07 per 100,000), plasma cell myeloma (5.26/100,000), myelodysplastic syndrome, NOS (3.92/100,000), malignant lymphoma, NOS (3.03/100,000), marginal zone B-cell lymphoma, NOS (2.52/100,000) and follicular lymphoma, grade 1 (1.43/100,000). Around 48% of NCR cancers were also reported in the Blood Diseases Registry.Conclusion: ICD-O-3 codes revealed 12,365 additional cases to the 55,554 reported publicly. Accurately updated data are necessary for medical resource planning.Keywords: : blood diseases registry, hematologic cancer, japan, national cancer registry, rare cancer

Introduction

The ICD-O-3 code was specifically designed to categorize tumors and is primarily used in tumor or cancer registries to code the site (topography) and histology (morphology) of neoplasms, usually as obtained from pathology reports and research, what brings advantages over the traditional ICD codes, that in most cases reports only one of them (topography or histology). The ICD-O-3 was developed by a joint group of the WHO and the International Agency for Research on Cancer in 2000 and revised again 2011 to the ICD-O-3.1. This version is currently used by cancer registries globally, including the NCR in Japan. The NCR was established in 2016 as a key element of the Cancer Control Act of Japan, and reporting of all diagnosed cases to the NCR system is now a legal requirement for medical institutions across the country [1,2]. This allows an understanding of the actual incidence of diagnosed cancers in Japan in place of estimates. Specific data in the NCR include incidence by age, sex and ICD-O-3 code. Since 2019, NCR individual data have been accessible for public use.

The incidence of hematologic cancers in Japan was reported from 1975 until 2015 by the Regional Cancer Registry, and then from 2016 by the National Cancer Registry (NCR) [1–3]. Publicly, however, these registries report hematologic cancers in three broad categories only, namely malignant lymphoma (ICD-10: C81-C85, C96), multiple myeloma (ICD-10: C88-C90) and leukemia (ICD-10: C91-C95) [2–4]. These data lack sufficient detail for pharmaceutical companies aiming to understand patient numbers for clinical trial design, resource allocation and other purposes in the development of new treatment options. This is particularly important given that drugs for cancer treatment are selected by specific cancer type, necessitating an understanding of incidence by detailed cancer subtype. Although several previous studies have analyzed specific hematologic tumors by ICD-O-3 code based on Regional Cancer Registry data, no analysis of all cases diagnosed across Japan based on the NCR has yet appeared, and the specific incidences of all hematological tumors has not been reported.

In this study, utilizing data for individual patients in the NCR to analyze the incidence of 157 subtypes of hematologic cancer by ICD-O-3 code, we first performed a descriptive epidemiological analysis of hematologic cancers by ICD-O-3 code to categorize detailed subsets. We then compared the NCR data with those reported in the Blood Diseases Registry (BDR) of the Japan Society of Hematology (JSH) [5]. The BDR contains records from 535 institutions and covers all hematological diseases; it is the most widely used data register for hematologic conditions in Japan, and reports not only incidence but also details of treatment and outcome.

Materials & methods

Data sources

The information used in this study was provided in accordance with Japanese law and was independently created and processed by the authors. Specifically, using the established procedure [6], we submitted an application to the Council of the National Cancer Registry Provision and its Provision Office for the use of NCR data covering all individual cases of hematologic tumors diagnosed in 2016. We then identified the type of each hematological tumor using the ICD-O-3 code regarding: sex, age at diagnosis, laterality, topography (ICD-O-3) and morphology (ICD-O-3) data. Finally, we identified 157 hematological sub types of cancer. Mortality-to-incidence ratio, death certificate only% and morphologically verified% were 0.37, 3.2 and 85.4%, respectively. Incidence rates per 100,000 population were calculated by dividing the number of incident cases by the corresponding population by sex and age groups, as reported by the Population Project for Japan (Statistics Bureau of Japan) [7].

We also grouped indications based on the WHO terms used in the International Classification of Diseases for Oncology Third Edition (ICD-O-3), First Revision.

We then matched diagnosed cases reported by JSH’s BDR for 2016 with the NCR dataset. As BDR reports use the WHO Classification 4th edition (2008) [6], we developed the list to enable comparison between ICD-10 codes, ICD-O-3 codes and the WHO Classifications. The list contains the ICD-O-3 codes and corresponding names of histologic diagnoses. We then added the categories of major-medium-minor classifications which are stated in the WHO Classification [8], as well as the corresponding ICD-10 codes. For comparison to allow understanding of the BDR’s national coverage, we used eight tumors which had been subject to at least some level of drug development, using the medium classification.

Statistical analysis

Data were explored using descriptive statistics. Incidences of respective cancers were categorized according to sex and age groups by ICD-O-3 code.

Results

Supplementary Tables S1 & S2 show the number of cases and incidence rate (per 100,000 population) for all 157 cancer types. Incidence was reported for 109 cancer types, but not for 23 types having fewer than 10 cases (not identified as incidence numbers in Supplementary Table S2) or 25 having no reported incidence.

Total incidence of the 157 hematologic cancers in 2016 was 67,919 cases (males: 37,916; females: 30,003), or 53.51 per 100,000 inhabitants.

The top six cancers in Japan by ICD-O-3 code accounted for 51% of total hematologic cancers. These were diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, NOS (ICD-O-3 code: 9680/3, 11.07 per 100,000), plasma cell myeloma (9732/3, 5.26 per 100,000), myelodysplastic syndrome, NOS (9989/3, 3.92 per 100,000), malignant lymphoma, NOS (9590/3, 3.03 per 100,000), marginal zone B-cell lymphoma, NOS (9699/3, 2.52 per 100,000) and follicular lymphoma, grade 1 (9695/3, 1.43 per 100,000).

Table 1 shows the ratio of male and female patients by tumor. Although most tumors were more prevalent in males (male: female = 1:0.75), there was little difference between sexes in the incidence of malignant lymphomas, NOS or diffuse (1:0.95) and plasma cell tumors (1:0.92). The average age at diagnosis across all hematological tumors was 69.5 years old; however, Hodgkin lymphoma (9650-9667) and lymphoid leukemia (9811-9837) were first identified at the slightly younger age of 57.4 and 57.2 years, respectively. Looking closer at Hodgkin lymphoma, age at diagnosis varied depending on subtype (Table 2). While people presenting with most Hodgkin lymphoma subtypes were over 60 years old, those with two subtypes, nodular sclerosis and nodular lymphocyte predom, which account for 30 and 5% of Hodgkin lymphoma, were on average much younger, at 45.8 (males: 49.2; females: 41.9) and 52.0 years (males: 48.1; females: 61.5), respectively.

Discussion

In this study, we found that the most frequent types of hematologic cancer by ICD-O-3 code among 67,919 cases reported in Japan's NCR in 2016 were diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, NOS (11.07 per 100,000), plasma cell myeloma (5.26 per 100,000) and myelodysplastic syndrome, NOS (3.92 per 100,000). As the total number of cases of malignant lymphoma (ICD-10: C81-C85, C96), multiple myeloma (ICD-10: C88-C90) and leukemia (ICD-10: C91-C95) publicly reported by the National Cancer Registry [2–4] was 55,554, this study revealed a further 12,365 unreported cases. Hodgkin lymphoma and lymphoid leukemia occur at a younger age than most other hematological tumors. Within Hodgkin lymphoma, age at diagnosis differed by subtype. Consistent with previous findings [9,10], 34% of two types of Hodgkin lymphoma – nodular sclerosis and nodular lymphocyte-predominant Hodgkin lymphoma – occurred at a younger age while other subtypes occurred at older age. Further, around 48% of hematological cancers in the NCR were also reported in the BDR, at consistent ratios with the NCR for nearly all subtypes. This study provides a detailed understanding of the incidence of hematological cancers by ICD-O-3 subtype and is suitable for understanding patient numbers in the development of innovative drugs in Japan.

In these Japanese patients, diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, NOS (ICD-O-3: 9680/3) had the highest incidence, at 11.07 per 100,000, while malignant lymphoma, NOS (ICD-O-3:9590/3) at 3.03 per 100,000 also ranked in the top 5. As Chihara et al. (2013) [11] indicated, however, more detailed pathological review of NOS cases may provide more accurate diagnosis and consequent allocation to other specific subtypes, in turn increasing the reported incidence of these tumors [12]. Given Clarke and colleagues' report that types included in NOS tend to be reclassified to the minor subtypes of hematologic cancers [13], NCR is likely to underreport these minor subtypes of hematologic cancer.

Based on previously published statistical data [2], the rate of cancer is known to be higher in males than females, and average age at diagnosis is over 60 years [14]. Leukemia and malignant lymphoma were no exception. However, the more detailed analysis in this study revealed that the incidence of some types of hematological tumor do not differ by sex, and that some cancers have a typically younger age at diagnosis. Hodgkin lymphoma shows a bimodal pattern: with a high proportion of young and elderly patients [15–19], most patients with nodular sclerosis subtypes are younger while those with the mixed cellularity subtype are older. Similar results were reported by SEER in the United States [20]. While treatment choices for cancer can differ greatly depending on subtype, age and sex of the patient can also be major factors in treatment choice. Knowing the age and sex composition of cancer patients is critical to predicting numbers in the longer term.

To date, JSH’s BDR has been the single source of data for understanding the number of hematologic cancer patients and their treatment and outcomes data in Japan. Coverage in BDR was almost the same for both major and minor cancers, which validates the reliability of the NCR data. This finding also indicates that BDR's treatment and outcome data can be linked to NCR data. However, among eight specific cancers we compared, CTCL had notably lower coverage, at only 12.5%. This could be because CTCL can be diagnosed and treated by dermatologists, rather than only by hematologists, as with most other hematological cancers. Because BDR data are gathered from 535 volunteer institutions, all members of the Japan Society of Hematology, coverage of cases may be lower if the condition can be diagnosed by specialists other than hematologists. Data background – in particular, who diagnoses and who participates in the study – is critical to any formal analysis of disease incidence and categorization using registry-derived data.

This study reports the incidence of 157 different hematologic cancers in Japan as classified using ICD-O-3 codes. Since drug development companies need to understand patient numbers to determine the target patient population for a drug, detailed information on subtypes is critical. For cancer, understanding incidence by ICD-O-3 code provides a maximum level of detail and allows group indications to suit clinical needs, which are now rapidly changing in the oncology treatment field. As seen in the case of Hodgkin lymphoma, differences in patterns by age and sex are important to understand, as these provide more accurate projection of patient numbers, and in turn help decision making regarding drug development and investment.

The NCR data on individual patients was first opened to public use in 2016. Although it may overestimate incidence as a result of being the first year of the system, our present study serves as an example of the use of these data. Similar studies in other cancer types are warranted, and should ideally be reported in other countries, including Japan. This requirement for detailed information is useful not just for hematologic cancers, but also for other cancer types in each country. Indeed, we believe that it should be considered a minimal level of information required for developing new therapies, including designing clinical trials, determining the necessary amount of investment, and applying for drug/therapy approval. Greater accessibility to nationally representative databases will benefit not only medical researchers but also private companies aiming to develop innovative and more effective treatments.

Conclusion

This study reports the incidence of 157 different hematologic cancers in Japan as classified using ICD-O-3 codes. An understanding of incidence by ICD-O-3 code provides maximum detail and allows group indications to suit rapidly changing clinical needs. Similar studies for other cancer types and in other countries are warranted and will benefit not only medical researchers but also private companies aiming to develop innovative and more effective treatments.
