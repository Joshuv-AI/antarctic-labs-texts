Associations of adverse childhood experiences with midlife cardiovascular health in women
Authors: Grace Wang, Sheryl L. Rifas-Shiman, Izzuddin M. Aris, Li Yi, JoAnn E. Manson, Jorge E. Chavarro, Marie-France Hivert, Emily Oken, Amy R. Nichols
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiovascular health (CVH) may be influenced by early life factors, such as adverse childhood experiences (ACEs). Prior work suggests social stressors may particularly influence CVH trajectories across the lifecourse in women; however, this relationship remains poorly understood. We used data from a prospective longitudinal cohort study to evaluate associations of ACEs with CVH and its components among midlife women (mean 51.3y).MethodsWe included 588 women from Project Viva who delivered a singleton livebirth between 1999 and 2003. The primary exposure was number of ACEs experienced (continuous and by domain) and the primary outcome was Life’s Essential 8 (LE8) CVH score, an average of eight behavioral and biomedical factors (0–100 points, higher score = better CVH). Exclusion criteria included missing data for the primary exposure or all LE8 components.ResultsAt least one ACE was reported by 63 % of participants. Mean (SD) LE8 scores were 73.7 (13.9) overall, 73.4 (17.4) for the behavioral domain, and 71.6 (21.7) for the biomedical domain. A higher number of ACEs was associated with lower overall (β=-0.7, 95 %CI: -1.3, -0.1) and behavioral (β=-0.9 points, 95 %CI: -1.7, -0.2) LE8 scores. Higher continuous ACEs score was associated with lower LE8 scores for physical activity (β=-1.8 points, 95 %CI: -3.6, 0.0) and blood glucose (β=-1.5points, 95 %CI: -2.4, -0.5).ConclusionsExperiencing more ACEs was associated with lower overall and behavioral LE8 scores at midlife. These findings provide evidence for the long-term impact of ACEs on women’s CVH and underscore the importance of trauma-informed care in cardiovascular disease prevention.

Introduction

Cardiovascular health (CVH) [1] includes biological and behavioral factors [2] and provides guidance for prevention of outcomes such as cardiovascular disease (CVD) and mortality [2]. Women’s CVH trajectories differ from men’s, with sex-specific differences in biological factors including blood pressure and cardiac troponin trajectories [[3], [4], [5]]. Prior literature suggests that the impact of social stressors on health trajectories may be stronger in women; however, the specific stressors contributing to women’s CVH across the lifecourse remain poorly understood.

Early life exposures may contribute to adult CVH [[6], [7], [8]]. Specifically, adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) are strong predictors of lifetime physical and mental health [9,10]. Evidence demonstrates that women are more likely to experience ACEs and more vulnerable to negative health outcomes downstream of ACEs [11]. Though prior research indicates associations between number of ACEs experienced and cardiometabolic dysfunction, [[12], [13], [14]] little is known about the associations of ACEs with CVH, a modifiable construct that not only predicts, but enables prevention of future adverse health outcomes. Understanding this relationship is particularly important for women at midlife, when biological increases in dyslipidemia and endothelial dysfunction may result in heightened sensitivity to the effects of earlier stressors [15]. Additionally, evidence suggests ACEs may have prolonged effects on stress responses from the humoral, nervous, and immune systems, potentially compounding declines in CVH across midlife [16].

The goal of this study was to examine associations of ACEs with women’s CVH at midlife. We assessed CVH using the American Heart Association’s (AHA) Life’s Essential 8 (LE8), a validated composite score of CVH that includes diet, physical activity, sleep, exposure to inhaled nicotine products, body mass index (BMI), blood glucose, blood lipids, and blood pressure. We hypothesized that women with greater exposure to ACEs would have poorer CVH at midlife, as indicated by lower LE8 scores.

Methods

Study cohort

As detailed in other sources [[17], [18], [19], [20]], Project Viva enrolled pregnant individuals during initial prenatal visits at a multispecialty group practice in eastern Massachusetts between 1999 and 2002. Eligibility criteria for the cohort included enrollment prior to 22 weeks of gestation, singleton pregnancy, ability to communicate in English, and plans to stay in the study area through delivery. The study continues to follow participants via periodic in-person study visits and annual mailed questionnaires.

For this analysis, we used data collected during enrollment, CVH assessed at the in-person Mid-Life Visit (2017 to 2021), and ACEs assessed in the 2021 annual questionnaire or the Women’s Health Visit 1 in 2022 to 2024. Exclusion criteria included participants missing primary exposure (ACEs) data or all LE8 components, preventing calculation of scores (n = 1512, 72 % excluded). Of 2100 enrolled participants, 681 (32 %) provided recalled information on ACEs. Of 681 with exposure data, 588 (86 %) reported at least one measured component of the LE8, and 397 (58 %) reported all eight LE8 components (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Study sample flow chart.Fig 1

Study sample flow chart.

Participants provided written informed consent at enrollment and subsequent study visits. All study procedures were approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Harvard Pilgrim Health Care Institute.

Adverse childhood experiences

Our primary exposure was ACEs score, derived from the ACEs questionnaire administered in 2021, and if missing, the Women’s Health Visit 1 in 2022 to 2024 [9,21]. The series of yes/no questions refers to specific experiences in the first 18 years of life (Supplemental Table 1). The 10 items are categorized into three domains: abuse (3 items), household challenges (5 items), and neglect (2 items). The ACEs score is a summation of these 10 questions. We also examined scores for each individual ACEs domain.

Cardiovascular health at midlife

Our primary outcome was CVH as assessed by LE8 scores. The LE8 is an AHA framework [2] that includes 8 components in two domains, behavioral and biomedical. The behavioral domain includes diet quality (Healthy Eating Index), physical activity (minutes of light, moderate, and vigorous activity), sleep health (duration), and nicotine exposure. The biomedical domain includes measured BMI, blood pressure, blood lipids (non–HDL cholesterol), and glycemia (fasting glucose, hemoglobin A1c, or diabetes status). We described details regarding assessment of each LE8 metric and calculation of each LE8 component score in Supplemental Table 2.

We calculated scores for overall LE8, each of the two domains, and the eight components on a scale from 0 to 100 points [2], with higher scores indicating better CVH. We averaged component scores for overall, behavioral, and biomedical domains. Our primary outcomes were overall LE8 score and the behavioral and biomedical domain scores. Secondary outcomes were the eight individual LE8 component scores.

Assessment of covariates

At enrollment, participants self-reported sociodemographic characteristics using interviewer-administered questionnaires that we operationalized as follows: age (years), race and ethnicity (Hispanic, non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, non-Hispanic Asian, or Multiracial/Other), nativity (U.S. born or not), highest level of education (college graduate or not), marital status (married/cohabitating or not), household income (>$70,000 per year or not), and their parents’ highest level of education (college graduate or not). We examined race and ethnicity as a social construct, not a biological variable, since race and ethnicity are associated with ACEs and lifetime health outcomes.

Analyses

We used multivariable linear regression to estimate associations of ACEs with continuous midlife LE8 scores. We evaluated ACEs as a continuous number (0 to 10) of experiences and categorical variable (0, 1–3, 4+ ACEs), as in prior studies [22]. We further examined associations of domain-specific ACEs (abuse, household challenges, neglect) with LE8 scores. For individuals with complete LE8 data, we assessed overall LE8 score utilizing all eight measured components. Among individuals with incomplete LE8 data (<8 components), we used a validated machine learning model previously outlined by Zheng et al. [23] to provide an LE8 overall score based on non-missing LE8 components. Details of the estimation procedure are described in the Supplemental Methods.

Since advancing age is an independent risk factor for poorer CVH [24], we adjusted all models for age at outcome (Model 1). We then adjusted for participant nativity and parents’ education (Model 2), as these factors may have influenced the number of ACEs experienced [25]. Lastly, we adjusted for race and ethnicity (Model 3), which tends to correlate with both ACEs experienced and midlife CVH [25,26].

In primary models, we did not account for any socioeconomic or health characteristics in adulthood, since they may be influenced by exposure to ACEs and lie on the causal pathway between ACEs and midlife CVH. Nevertheless, to ensure the robustness of our results, we conducted a sensitivity analysis adjusting for participant’s age at outcome, education, marital/cohabitation status, and household income at enrollment. Lastly, we examined effect modification by menopause status.

All analyses were conducted using SAS Enterprise Guide, Version 8.3 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

Results

Study population

Mean participant age (SD) was 51.3 (4.9) years at the Mid-Life Visit, and individuals were mainly US-born (80 %) and had annual household income >$70,000 (70 %) at enrollment (Table 1). At midlife, 73 % identified as non-Hispanic White, 11 % as non-Hispanic Black, 6 % as non-Hispanic Asian, 7 % as Hispanic, and 3 % identified as Multiracial or Other. Mean (SD) overall LE8 score was 73.7 (13.9), 73.4 (17.4) for the behavioral domain, and 71.6 (21.7) for the biomedical domain (Table 1). Compared to those excluded because of missing exposure or outcome data, included participants were more likely to identify as non-Hispanic White (73 % vs. 64 %) and have parents who had graduated A majority (63 %) of participants (n = 369) reported at least one ACE; about half (53 %) reported at least one household challenge, but fewer experienced physical/sexual abuse (33 %) or neglect (20 %) (Supplemental Table 4). Compared to those with 0 ACEs, those who experienced 4+ ACEs (n = 98) were more likely to have household income ≤$70,000 at enrollment (21 % with 0 ACEs vs. 51 % with 4+ ACEs), have parents who did not graduate college (mother did not graduate: 54 % with 0 ACEs vs. 73 % with 4+ ACEs; father did not graduate: 42 % with 0 ACEs vs. 62 % with 4+ ACEs), and identify as Hispanic or non-Hispanic Black (10 % with 0 ACEs vs. 37 % with 4+ ACEs) (Table 1).

Associations of ACEs with overall LE8 score

A higher continuous ACEs score was associated with lower overall LE8 score in all models (Table 2). In Model 1 adjusting for age at outcome, a higher number of ACEs was associated with lower LE8 score (β=−1.0 points, 95 %CI: −1.6, −0.5). These associations were attenuated but remained significant after additional adjustment for nativity and parents’ education (Model 2: β=−1.0 points, 95 %CI: −1.6, −0.4), and race and ethnicity (Model 3: β=−0.7 points, 95 %CI: −1.3, −0.1) (Table 2, Fig. 1). Overall, 1 in 6 women (17 %) reported having experienced 4+ ACES, one in five (20 %) reported neglect, and one in 3 (33 %) reported physical or sexual abuse. In categorical ACEs analyses, experiencing 4+ ACEs, physical or sexual abuse, or neglect were each associated with lower LE8 scores (Table 2). Effect sizes attenuated from Model 1 to Model 3, and only neglect remained significantly associated with LE8 when adjusted for race and ethnicity (β=−4.4, 95 %CI: −7.3, −1.5).

In analyses examining effect modification by menopause status, we observed no difference in strength of ACEs and LE8 associations between pre-menopausal and menopausal women (data not shown).

Associations of ACEs exposures with behavioral LE8 score

In Model 1 adjusting for age at outcome, a higher continuous ACEs score was associated with lower LE8 behavioral domain score (β=−1.2 points, 95 %CI: −1.9, −0.5). These associations were consistent after additional adjustment for nativity and parents’ education (Model 2: β=−1.2 points, 95 %CI: −1.9, −0.4) and attenuated but remained significant after adjustment for race and ethnicity (Model 3: β=−0.9 points, 95 %CI: −1.7, −0.2) (Table 2, Fig. 1).

In categorical analyses, experiencing 4+ (vs. 0) ACEs was associated with a lower LE8 behavioral score across Models 2 and 3; in Model 2, 4+ ACEs was associated with a 5.1-point lower LE8 score (95 % CI: −9.4, −0.8). (Table 2). Further, reporting any ACEs of neglect was associated with lower midlife LE8 behavioral domain score in Model 3 [any (vs. no) neglect (β=−6.0 points, 95 %CI: −9.6, −2.4)] (Table 2).

Associations of ACEs exposures with biomedical LE8 score

Associations of ACEs score with the biomedical LE8 score mostly contained the null (Table 2, Fig. 2). Discussion

In this cohort study, we observed an association of experiencing ACEs with poorer midlife CVH in women, as demonstrated by a 0.7-point lower overall LE8 score and 0.9-point lower behavioral domain score. In our sample of midlife women, prevalence of ACEs was similar to that of the general U.S. adult population: 63 % of our participants experienced at least one ACE (vs. 63.9 % in U.S. adults [27]), 17 % experienced 4+ ACEs (vs. 17.3 % of U.S. adults [27]), and 33 % experienced physical or sexual abuse (vs. 23.3 % of U.S. adults experiencing physical abuse and 12.6 % experiencing sexual abuse [27]).

In categorical analyses, experiencing 4+ ACEs predicted 4-point lower overall LE8 and behavioral domain scores in fully adjusted models. Our secondary results indicated that reporting more ACEs are associated with lower physical activity and blood glucose scores, indicating that these components may drive the relationship between ACEs and lower overall LE8 score.

Though our effect sizes are modest, prior literature suggests a dose-response relationship between LE8 score and mortality: a one-point increase in overall LE8 score has been associated with an 11 % reduced risk of all-cause mortality (95 %CI 0.86–0.93) [28,29]. A 2019 Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) report showed that as ACEs score increased, the association with adverse cardiometabolic outcomes became more pronounced [30]. Less is understood about the relationship between ACEs and CVH. A study using data from the 2019 Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System found inverse associations of ACEs with CVH as measured by the AHA’s Life’s Simple 7, the precursor to the LE8 [1,31] However, this study was cross-sectional, precluding analysis of the cumulative effect of health behaviors over time, and did not include sleep behaviors.

Our study builds upon these findings, indicating that experiencing ACEs may predict poorer midlife CVH in women, with individuals who have experienced 4+ ACEs at particular risk. Currently, little is known about the association of ACEs with CVH according to sex, despite evidence that women are more susceptible to the health consequences of ACEs [32]. In the Nurses’ Health Study II, women who reported abuse had an increased risk of developing hypertension compared to those who had not (27 % increased risk among women reporting physical abuse; 95 %CI 17–38 %). However, this association was no longer significant after adjusting for adulthood covariates [33]. Our study found a similar association that remained robust after adjustment for adulthood covariates, suggesting that experiencing childhood abuse may be associated with midlife CVH even after accounting for socioeconomic differences in adulthood.

The association of higher ACEs score with lower physical activity and blood glucose scores appeared to drive the relationship between ACEs and CVH. Of the LE8 behavioral components, reduced physical activity is the major contributor to both all-cause and CVD-specific mortality [[34], [35], [36]]. Proposed mechanisms for the relationship between ACEs and physical activity suggest that childhood adversity may induce physical inactivity [[37], [38], [39]] or that individuals with more ACEs may be structurally disadvantaged in adulthood, residing in neighborhood environments less conducive to physical activity due to higher crime rates or poorer built environment characteristics [40]. While increased activity is beneficial for CVH in the general population, prior work indicates that it may serve even greater importance for individuals who have experienced ACEs: increased physical activity was observed to attenuate associations between ACEs and adulthood adverse health outcomes [[41], [42], [43]]. Thus, though physical activity is especially beneficial for those with a history of ACEs, experiencing more ACEs may lead to reduced physical activity in later life. This finding underscores the need for clinicians to assess and counsel female patients with a history of ACEs on physical activity, and the importance of more research into effectively promoting physical activity in this population.

Higher ACEs score was also associated with higher blood glucose, supporting previous findings that ACEs are associated with increased diabetes risk [14,44,45]. The Whitehall II study among British civil servants found that for each ACE, the risk of diabetes increased by 11 % (95 %CI: 1.00, 1.24) [14], an effect size that is consistent with other reports. Our findings suggest that ACEs are correlated with poorer glycemic control, even prior to development of diabetes. Several mechanisms for this relationship have been proposed. One key factor may be the increase in chronic stress resulting from a history of ACEs. This may lead to a sustained increase in cortisol, resulting in subsequent increases in blood glucose and triglyceride levels [46]. Further evidence suggests that ACEs may precede increased production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, resulting in an inflammatory state that heightens risk for developing insulin resistance [[47], [48], [49]] a pathophysiologic driver of progression towards diabetes.

When approaching treatment of women’s CVH, our results inform the practice of trauma-informed, “whole-person” care. Trauma-informed care is a framework for providing healthcare based on the understanding of trauma and its implications on lifecourse mental and physical health [50]. Preliminary research indicates that trauma-informed care may reduce likelihood of future cardiovascular events [51,52]. By integrating ACEs screening into routine CVH assessments for women, clinicians may more effectively target early screening and interventions for at-risk individuals [53,54]. Given that over half of the U.S. population has experienced at least one ACE [10], it is important to ensure that interventions are holistic and consider past trauma, particularly for populations that hold intersectional identities according to race, class, and gender identity [55]. The National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute, the AHA, and CDC have all emphasized the need to comprehensively examine how social determinants of health influence CVH disparities [52,[56], [57], [58]]; our findings suggest that early-life circumstances that may predispose individuals to poorer CVH across the lifespan. One area of potential clinical intervention is physical activity, a behavioral factor that may be both cardioprotective and underutilized for individuals who have experienced ACEs. Further evidence is needed to develop interventional strategies in clinical practice, especially for vulnerable individuals that bear the greatest risk burden.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this investigation include use of a well-characterized cohort with collection of both CVH and relevant covariates. To our knowledge, this is the first analysis of how ACEs predict CVH in midlife women as measured by the LE8, a validated assessment for CVH on an individual and population level. Use of the LE8 allows for analysis of behavioral and biomedical elements, enabling identification of specific risk factors that may act as interventional targets. Likewise, the ACEs survey is validated for assessment in adulthood, providing a valuable screening tool for clinicians. However, our study has its limitations. At enrollment, all participants had health insurance, access to healthcare, and were mostly college educated, which may limit generalizability to other populations. There is also potential selection bias given the loss to follow-up in our cohort, as well as recall bias given that ACEs were collected at midlife. Additionally, the majority of our participants identified as non-Hispanic White, although the proportion in Project Viva was similar to proportions in Massachusetts according to the 2000 United States Census [59]. Lastly, we acknowledge that our findings demonstrate an association rather than causation between experiencing more ACEs and lower LE8 scores in midlife women. While we adjusted for key measured covariates, unmeasured confounding and potential biases remain possible.

Conclusion

Experiencing more ACEs was associated with lower overall and behavioral LE8 scores at midlife in women. These results were primarily driven by associations with physical activity, a potentially modifiable risk factor for CVD that may be especially protective for those with a history of ACEs. The AHA supports universal screening for ACEs in clinical practice, noting the importance of early intervention to prevent downstream health implications [60].
