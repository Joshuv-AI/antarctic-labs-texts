A phase 2 trial of a “sandwich” strategy: Sequential CD22/CD19 chimeric antigen receptor T‐cells therapy combined with autologous hematopoietic stem cell transplantation in patients with Philadelphia chromosome–negative B‐cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia
Authors: Chong‐Sheng Qian, Zi‐Hao Wang, Zheng Li, Zhen Yao, Wen‐Jie Gong, Yan‐Jun Wu, Hai‐Xia Zhou, Ming‐Zhu Xu, Yan Qiu, Shun‐Zhe Xu, Kai‐Wen Tan, Fang‐Tong Liu, Si‐Man Huang, Han‐Yu Cao, Hai‐Ping Dai, De‐Pei Wu, Sheng‐Li Xue

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe relapse after chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T‐cell therapy remains a critical challenge, and the optimal timing and treatment strategies for CAR T urgently need to be explored. Autologous hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (auto‐HSCT) demonstrates comparable leukemia‐free survival (LFS) and overall survival (OS) in patients who rapidly achieve MRD‐negative complete remission (CR) compared with allogeneic HSCT (allo‐HSCT). Thus, combining CAR T cells with auto‐HSCT may represent a promising treatment strategy.The trial registration is ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT05470777.MethodsThis phase 2 trial evaluated the safety and efficacy of sequential CD22/CD19 CAR T cells combined with an auto‐HSCT “sandwich” strategy in patients with Philadelphia chromosome–negative (Ph‐negative) B‐cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B‐ALL), including adolescents and young adults (AYA) as well as adults who were unable or declined to allo‐HSCT. The primary and secondary end points were OS and LFS, respectively.The trial registration is ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT05470777.ResultsAt a median follow‐up of 28 months, the median OS and LFS were not reached. The 2‐year OS and LFS rates were 97% (95% confidence interval [CI], 90%–100%) and 72% (95% CI, 58%–90%), respectively. All 35 patients who completed the sandwich strategy survived. Continuous MRD‐negative CR rates after the second CAR T‐cell infusion were 80% by multiparameter flow cytometry and 70% by next‐generation sequencing of immunoglobulin H rearrangements. OS and LFS did not differ between poor and standard genetic risk groups. Compared with the allo‐HSCT external control group, the sandwich strategy showed improved OS and comparable LFS. No cases of immune effector cell–associated neurotoxicity syndrome or severe cytokine release syndrome were observed.The trial registration is ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT05470777.ConclusionThe CD22/CD19 CAR T‐cell and auto‐HSCT sandwich strategy represents a promising approach for the treatment of Ph‐negative B‐ALL in AYA and adult patients, offering high efficacy and a favorable safety profile.The trial registration is ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT05470777.Keywords: autologous hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, chimeric antigen receptor T cells, leukemia‐free survival, overall survival, Philadelphia chromosome–negative B‐cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia

INTRODUCTION

With the emergence of antigen‐targeting immunotherapies, the prognosis of patients with B‐cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B‐ALL) has improved significantly. Blinatumomab, inotuzumab ozogamicin, and chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T‐cell therapy have been approved for treating relapsed or refractory (R/R) B‐ALL. 1 , 2 , 3 Recent clinical studies have demonstrated favorable efficacy and safety profiles of blinatumomab and inotuzumab ozogamicin in measurable residual disease (MRD) positive/negative and newly diagnosed patients with B‐ALL. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 Although immunotherapy has been incorporated, the current treatment backbone still requires multi‐course cytotoxic regimens, leading to unavoidable cumulative toxicity. 8 , 9 In addition, allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo‐HSCT) remains the primary treatment for Philadelphia chromosome–negative (Ph‐negative) B‐ALL in adolescents and young adults (AYA) and adult patients with MRD‐positive and/or high‐risk features. 10 However, the overall survival (OS) and quality of life of patients undergoing allo‐HSCT are significantly affected by complications‌ and nonrelapse mortality (NRM). 11 , 12 , 13

CAR T‐cell therapy is currently approved only for R/R B‐ALL. Although approximately 80% of patients with R/R B‐ALL achieve complete remission (CR), 14 40%–60% of patients relapse after CD19 CAR T‐cell therapy. 15 The optimal timing and strategy for CAR T‐cell administration therefore require further investigation. Unlike blinatumomab and inotuzumab ozogamicin, CAR T‐cell applications during the induction or consolidation phases are rarely reported. Zhang et al. 16 reported encouraging efficacy, with a 76% complete molecular remission (CMR) rate achieved by dasatinib and CAR T‐cell therapy in newly diagnosed patients with Ph‐positive B‐ALL (n = 28); only one patient received consolidative allo‐HSCT, all patients received single‐agent dasatinib maintenance therapy after CAR T‐cell therapy, and the 2‐year OS and leukemia‐free survival (LFS) rates were 92%. Our group reported a phase 1 clinical trial involving patients with de novo Ph‐positive B‐ALL. Patients received induction and two courses of systemic chemotherapy, followed by a single cycle of CD19 CAR T‐cell infusion (CAR‐T1) and then three additional cycles of CD19 CAR T cells combined with CD19+ feeder T cells (CAR‐T2–T4). None of the patients underwent allo‐HSCT. With a median follow‐up of 27 months (range, 7–57 months), the relapse‐free survival (RFS) and OS rates were 84% and 83%, respectively. 17 Another clinical trial reported induction therapy with venetoclax and azacitidine (VA) followed by CD19/CD22 CAR T‐cell therapy in newly diagnosed Ph‐negative B‐ALL (n = 12); the MRD‐negativity rate after CAR T‐cell therapy was 83.3%, eight patients subsequently underwent allo‐HSCT, and the estimated 1‐year OS and LFS rates were 81.8%. 18 Preliminary evidence indicates that the use of CAR T‐cell therapy in the frontline setting could reduce the need for multi‐course intensive chemotherapy and/or allo‐HSCT.

Multiple studies have suggested that, in patients with B‐ALL who rapidly achieve deep remission, there is no significant difference in LFS or OS between autologous HSCT (auto‐HSCT) and allo‐HSCT. 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 We previously reported the use of sequential CD22 and CD19 CAR T‐cell therapy bridged with auto‐HSCT as consolidation therapy in patients with AYA and adult B‐ALL (n = 12), achieving an MRD‐negativity rate of 100% after CAR T‐cell therapy. At a median follow‐up of 24 months, 10 of 12 (83.3%) patients maintained MRD‐negative CR. 24 However, the myeloablative conditioning regimen used before auto‐HSCT may eliminate CAR T cells. A second CAR T‐cell infusion after auto‐HSCT could compensate for this elimination. Moreover, the myeloablative regimen may establish a favorable immune environment for the second CAR T‐cell infusion, potentially enhancing CAR T‐cell persistence. 25

Therefore, we conducted a phase 2 clinical trial to assess the safety and efficiency of sequential CD22/CD19 CAR T‐cell therapy bridging auto‐HSCT followed by a second CD22/CD19 CAR T‐cell sandwich strategy in AYA and adult patients with B‐ALL and compared the outcomes with those of an allo‐HSCT external control group. This strategy synergistically integrates four key advantages: (1) multi‐course intensive chemotherapy is reduced by incorporating CAR T‐cell therapy and auto‐HSCT; (2) the first CAR T‐cell therapy effectively eliminates MRD before auto‐HSCT; (3) auto‐HSCT avoids graft‐versus‐host disease and reduces NRM; and (4) the second CAR T‐cell therapy compensates for the loss of the first CAR T cells induced by myeloablative conditioning and establishes long‐term immune surveillance.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and participants

This was a phase 2 single‐arm, open‐label, single‐center study approved by the institutional review board of the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University (approval no. 2019‐188). Newly diagnosed AYA and adult patients with Ph‐negative B‐ALL expressing CD19 and CD22 by multiparameter flow cytometry (MFC) were screened. Eligible patients were those unable to undergo allo‐HSCT or who declined allo‐HSCT. Detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria are provided in the Supporting Information protocol (File S1). This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients or their guardians provided written informed consent before study participation. The primary end point was OS of the sandwich strategy, measured from the first day of the first CAR T‐cell (CAR‐T1) infusion to death from any cause. Secondary end points included LFS (measured from the date of remission after CAR‐T1 relapse from CR, CR with incomplete recovery, or death from any cause), MRD‐negativity rate and duration, incidence of adverse events (AEs; assessed after the CAR‐T1), and NRM. Exploratory end points included comparisons of LFS and OS between the sandwich strategy and an external control group of patients who underwent allo‐HSCT.

Procedures

According to the study protocol, the patient received induction and consolidation therapy (File S4). Following induction therapy and lymphocyte recovery (>0.5 × 109/L), peripheral blood lymphocytes were collected via leukapheresis for CAR T‐cell preparation, regardless of remission status. The first and second CD22 and CD19 CAR‐T cells (CAR‐T1 and CAR‐T2) were administered sequentially at a doses of 5 × 106/kg each. Autologous stem cell mobilization and collection were performed 6–8 weeks after CAR‐T1. A modified BuCy 26 regimen was used as conditioning for auto‐HSCT approximately 4 weeks after successful stem cell collection. The second CD22 and CD19 CAR T cells (CAR‐T2) were infused at the same doses as CAR‐T1, 2 days after autologous stem cell infusion (Figure S1). Patients did not receive maintenance treatment after CAR‐T2, except for those with Ph‐like ALL harboring ABL‐class fusions, who received tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs). The complete sandwich strategy pathway is shown in Figure S2. All patients were followed up, and MRD was monitored using multiparameter flow cytometry (MFC‐MRD). Next‐generation sequencing (NGS)‐based MRD assessment by detection of immunoglobulin H (IgH) rearrangements was performed in 28 of 37 patients. In cases of MFC‐MRD progression (>0.01%) after CAR‐T1, the sandwich strategy was discontinued, and allo‐HSCT was recommended for patients who had previously declined transplantation. Individualized treatments such as blinatumomab or inotuzumab ozogamicin were recommended for patients without suitable donors.

Outcome measurements and AEs

Efficacy was assessed according to the manufacturer’s protocol. MFC‐MRD–negative CR was defined as CR with MRD <0.01% in the bone marrow by MFC. NGS‐MRD–negative CR was defined as CR with an IgH rearrangement clone ratio <0.0001% by NGS. AEs were evaluated according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5.0, and cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and immune effector cell‐associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) were graded according to National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines. 27 Severe CRS and ICANS were defined as grade ≥3 events.

Statistical analysis

The 2‐year OS rate for patients receiving allo‐HSCT was approximately 60%. 28 The sample size was determined using a one‐sample test for exponential hazard rate, assuming an expected 2‐year OS rate of 85% in this study. The optimal outcome was defined as a 2‐year OS significantly higher than that reported for allo‐HSCT (60%). Assuming a dropout rate of 10%, a two‐sided significance level (α) of 0.025, and 80% power, the required sample size was 37. The calculation was performed using NCSS PASS 15 software (NCSS LLC, Kaysville, Utah; ncss.com/software/pass).

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26.0 (SPSS Corp., Armonk, New York). Categorical variables were expressed as percentages. The Kaplan‒Meier method was used to estimate survival, and differences between groups were compared using the log‐rank test. Propensity score matching (PSM) analysis was used to compare the “sandwich” and allo‐HSCT control groups (Figure S3). Graphs were generated using Prism version 10.1.2 (GraphPad Software Inc). Statistical significance was set at p < .05.

RESULTS

Patient characteristics

Thirty‐eight patients were screened and 37 patients were enrolled, one patient did not proceed to the study because of active hepatitis B. The median age was 28 years (range, 15–60 years), and 13 patients (35%) were older than 35 years. The white blood cell (WBC) count of six patients (16%) at initial diagnosis was >30 × 109/L. Thirty‐three patients (89%) were diagnosed with Ph‐negative B‐ALL, and four patients (11%) had Ph‐like B‐ALL (ABL class: n = 2; JAK‐STAT: n = 2). According to the NCCN genetic risk stratification, 10 21 patients (57%) had high‐risk features (Table 1). Thirty‐five patients completed the sandwich strategy, including one patient with Ph‐like B‐ALL (ABL class) who did not receive TKI (protocol deviation) (Figure 1). Two patients who relapsed after the CAR‐T1 did not complete the sandwich strategy and underwent allo‐HSCT.

Efficacy

With a median follow‐up of 28 months (range, 10–50 months), the median OS and LFS were not reached. The 2‐year OS and LFS rates were 97% (95% confidence interval [CI], 90%–100%) and 72% (95% CI, 58%–90%), respectively (Figure 2). MFC‐MRD assessments were performed in all patients, and NGS‐MRD assessments were performed in 28 of 37 patients (27 patients completed the sandwich strategy). After induction chemotherapy, 92% of patients (34 of 37) achieved CR, 54% achieved MFC‐MRD–negative CR, and none achieved NGS‐MRD–negative CR. During consolidation chemotherapy, 80% of patients (28 of 35) remained in CR, 71% (22 of 35) had MFC‐MRD–negative CR, and 23% (5 of 22) had NGS‐MRD–negative CR. After CAR‐T1 (response assessments performed on day 30 ± 5), all patients achieved MFC‐MRD–negative CR, and 68% (19 of 28) achieved NGS‐MRD–negative CR. Two patients relapsed after CAR‐T1 therapy (discontinued the sandwich strategy) and underwent allo‐HSCT. After auto‐HSCT and CAR‐T2 (response assessments performed on day 30 ± 5), all patients remained in MFC‐MRD–negative CR and 93% (25 of 27) achieved NGS‐MRD–negative CR (Table S1). During follow‐up, all 35 patients who completed the sandwich strategy remained alive. Of these, 28 (80%) maintained continuous MFC‐MRD–negative CR after CAR‐T2. Among these 28 patients, 20 remained in remission for >1 year, and 11 for >2 years. Twenty of 27 patients (74%) maintained continuous NGS‐MRD–negative CR after CAR‐T2. Among these 20 patients, 14 remained in remission for >1 year and seven for >2 years (Figure 3). OS and LFS did not differ between the poor and standard‐risk genetic groups (Figure 4A,B). Before auto‐HSCT, 21 patients achieved NGS‐MRD–negative CR, and six achieved NGS‐MRD–positive CR. OS did not differ between the NGS‐MRD–negative and NGS‐MRD–positive groups; however, a trend toward shorter LFS was observed in the NGS‐MRD–positive group, although the difference was not statistically significant (Figure 4C,D).

Relapse

Twelve of 35 patients (34%) presented with R/R disease before CAR‐T1 during chemotherapy and continued the sandwich strategy according to the protocol (10 of 12 R/R patients received bridging chemotherapy before the first CAR T cells and two did not). Three of these 12 patients (25%) relapsed after completing the sandwich strategy. Twenty‐three patients remained in CR before CAR‐T1, among whom four (17%) experienced relapse after completing the sandwich strategy. The relapse rate after the complete sandwich strategy did not differ significantly between R/R and non‐R/R patients before CAR‐T1 (25% vs. 17%, p = .362). Two patients relapsed after CAR‐T1 therapy and did not complete the sandwich strategy; both underwent allo‐HSCT. One died of disease progression after allo‐HSCT, whereas the other one remained in CR.

Among the 35 patients who completed the sandwich strategy, seven (20%) experienced relapse, including one with concurrent central nervous system (CNS) relapse (Ph‐like B‐ALL [ABL class] who had refused TKI therapy. All relapsed cases were CD19‐positive. No significant baseline differences were observed between relapsed and nonrelapsed patients, except for median WBC count at diagnosis (26 × 109/L vs. 7 × 109/L, p = .015) (Table S2). The median relapse time after completing the sandwich strategy was 5 months (range, 1–10 months). All relapse patients achieved CR with blinatumomab (n = 5) or chemotherapy (n = 2). Five patients underwent allo‐HSCT and achieved CR. Two patients declined allo‐HSCT; one remained in CR and the other (Ph‐like B‐ALL [ABL class]) subsequently agreed to receive dasatinib and multiple intrathecal therapies, achieving CR again.

Toxicities

All patients experienced grade 3‐4 hematotoxicity (Table S3). The median duration of granulocyte deficiency was 11 days (range, 9–23 days) after CAR‐T2, and the median duration of platelet count ≤50 × 109/L was 15 days (range, 10–158) after CAR‐T2. Grade 1–2 CRS occurred in eight (22%) and 12 (34%) patients after CAR‐T1 and CAR‐T2 therapy, respectively. No ICANS or severe CRS (grade ≥3) occurred. None of the patients experienced severe organ toxicity. B‐cell aplasia occurred in all patients, with a median duration of 170 days after CAR‐T2 (range, 68–502 days). Two patients experienced sepsis (post–CAR‐T2), and two patients experienced lung infections (one post–CAR‐T1 and one post–CAR‐T2). No NRM was observed.

CAR T‐cell persistence and subgroups

CAR T‐cell expansion was detected in blood using real‐time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qRT‐PCR) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The median CAR‐T1 peak was 9.5 × 104 copies/µg of genomic DNA (range, 1.6 × 103–7.5 × 105), with a median peak time of 10 days (range, 5–14 days). The median CAR‐T2 peak was 1.3 × 103 copies/µg of genomic DNA (range, 9.4 × 101–1.5 × 105), with a median peak time of 7 days (range, 2–28 days). CAR T cells remained detectable in all patients who completed the sandwich strategy, with a median duration of 204 days (range, 14–715 days) after CAR‐T2 infusion. The median proportions of stem cell memory T‐cell (Tscm) subsets in CD19 CAR T cells and CD22 CAR T cells were 36% (range, 5%–74%) and 29% (range, 4%–55%), respectively (Tables S4a and S4b). Other CAR T‐cell subsets are listed in Tables S4a and S4b. Interestingly, three patients demonstrated MRD increase coincident with CAR T‐cell expansion (Figure S4).

Exploratory analysis

The Ph‐negative B‐ALL in individuals who underwent allo‐HSCT during the same period at our center served as the external control group. A PSM analysis compared the sandwich strategy group (n = 37) and the allo‐HSCT group (n = 52) (Figure S4). Patient characteristics were not significantly different between groups (Table S5a). The OS of the sandwich strategy group was longer than that of the allo‐HSCT group (p = .021) and the LFS was comparable between the two groups (p = .576) (Figure 5A,B). Relapse rates in the sandwich strategy and allo‐HSCT groups were 24% and 19%, respectively (p = .607). The NRM rate was lower in the sandwich strategy group than in the allo‐HSCT group (0% vs. 15%, p = .019).

DISCUSSION

In the era of molecular and immunotherapies, the prognosis of patients with B‐ALL has significantly improved. 4 , 7 However, long‐course chemotherapy or allo‐HSCT still serve as the backbone of these novel protocols, potentially increasing NRM and negatively affecting patients’ quality of life. Multiple studies have shown that auto‐HSCT in early MRD‐negative patients results in no significant differences in LFS and OS compared with allo‐HSCT. 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 A retrospective study analyzed the outcomes of 21 patients with R/R Burkitt’s lymphoma; patients receiving auto‐HSCT followed by CAR T‐cell therapy (n = 8) demonstrated superior outcomes compared with those receiving CAR T‐cell therapy alone (n = 13). 29

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first phase 2 trial of a sandwich strategy using CAR T cells combined with auto‐HSCT in B‐ALL. Compared with chemotherapy, more patients achieved deep remission. MFC‐MRD–negative and NGS‐MRD–negative CR rates reached 100% and 93% after CAR‐T2, respectively. The 2‐year OS and LFS rates were 97% and 72%, respectively. Among the 35 patients who completed the sandwich therapy, all survived; 80% maintained continuous MFC‐MRD–negative CR, and 70% maintained continuous NGS‐MRD–negative CR. Twelve patients with R/R B‐ALL (34%) received the sandwich strategy, with a relapse rate of 25% that was lower than previously reported. 15 These results indicated that CAR T cells and auto‐HSCT sandwich strategy may be a promising alternative treatment to improve the OS and LFS of patients with Ph‐negative B‐ALL and may provide allo‐HSCT–free survival for some patients.

In our study, 57% of patients had poor genetic risk features, and OS and LFS were comparable between the poor and standard genetic risk groups. A previously reported case described a patient with R/R TP53‐mutated Burkitt’s lymphoma who achieved long‐term disease‐free survival following sandwich therapy with subsequential CD19/CD22 CAR T‐cell therapy combined with auto‐HSCT. 30 This suggests that the sandwich strategy may overcome poor genetic risk factors. A trend toward shorter LFS was observed in patients who were NGS‐MRD–positive (MFC‐MRD–negative CR) before auto‐HSCT, indicating that patients with NGS‐MRD–negative CR may have a better response to CAR T cells and potentially benefit more from the sandwich strategy. Seven of 35 patients experienced CD19‐positive relapse after the sandwich strategy, and a high WBC count at initial diagnosis may be an unfavorable factor for relapse. These preliminary data require confirmation in larger cohorts. Relapsed patients could achieved CR after treatment with blinatumomab (n = 5), suggesting that blinatumomab may be an effective salvage therapy for patients with R/R B‐ALL who experienced CD19‐positive relapse after CAR T‐cell therapy.

As previously reported, high rates of CRS have been observed across clinical trials of CD19 CAR T‐cell therapy for R/R B‐ALL (any‐grade CRS, 56%–100%) including grade ≥3 CRS (3%–71%). The rates of ICANS range from 29% to 72% across clinical trials of CD19 CAR T‐cell therapy for R/R B‐ALL, including grade ≥3 ICANS (6%–56%). Bone marrow tumor burden has been strongly associated with toxicity in most studies. 31 In contrast to the more robust cytokine response observed in R/R B‐ALL patients, CAR T‐cell therapy yielded only mild cytokine responses and CRS in MRD‐positive patients. 32 , 33 In our study, ICANS and severe CRS (grade 3–4) were not observed; only grade 1–2 CRS occurred in 22% and 34% of patients after CAR‐T1 and CAR‐T2 therapy, respectively. Based on the study design (Figure S1) and protocol, most patients in our study were in CR or had a low tumor burden status before CAR T‐cell infusion. Therefore, compared with CAR T‐cell therapy in R/R patients, the sandwich strategy could improve safety.

The OS of the sandwich strategy group was longer than that of the allo‐HSCT control group, with comparable LFS. The NRM rate was significantly higher in the allo‐HSCT group than in the sandwich strategy group, whereas relapse rates were comparable between the two groups. The sandwich strategy demonstrated high efficiency and low toxicity. Possible advantages of this approach include the following: (1) CD22/CD19 CAR T cells efficiently eliminated MRD before auto‐HSCT; (2) low NRM rate and comparable relapse rate compared with allo‐HSCT, contributing to improved OS and LFS; (3) the myeloablative conditioning regimen (modified BuCy) for auto‐HSCT served as a lymphodepleting conditioning regimen for CAR‐T2; and (4) potential engraftment of stem‐like CAR T‐cell subsets into the bone marrow. The tumor microenvironment (TME) plays a crucial role in terms of response to CAR T‐cell therapy. 34 , 35 We hypothesized that a myeloablative conditioning regimen may eliminate the immunosuppressive TME, including macrophages, regulatory T cells, and myeloid‐derived suppressor cells, 35 thereby establishing a favorable environment for CAR T‐cell expansion. Effector T‐cell subsets have limited expansion capacity and are prone to exhaustion, whereas Tscm subsets exhibit longevity and self‐renewal capacity. Clinical studies have demonstrated that infusion of stem‐like CAR T cells results in favorable outcomes. 36 In our study, the median proportions of Tscm in CD19 CAR T cells and CD22 CAR T cells were 37% and 29%, respectively. CAR‐T2 was infused after the myeloablative conditioning regimen and autologous stem cell infusion, which may have promoted engraftment of stem‐like CAR T‐cell subsets. We observed long CAR T‐cell persistence and synchronization between CAR T‐cell copy number and MRD in three patients, which may have been induced by engrafted CAR T cells.

In conclusion, the CD22/CD19 CAR T‐cells and auto‐HSCT sandwich strategy is a promising approach for treating Ph‐negative B‐ALL in AYA and adult patients, offering high efficacy with a favorable safety profile. Future studies with larger sample sizes and longer follow‐up are warranted to further validate these findings and further explore allo‐HSCT–free strategies.
