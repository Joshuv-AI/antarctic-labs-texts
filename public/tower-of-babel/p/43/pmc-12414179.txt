Precision medicine in acute spinal cord injury: moving beyond static hemodynamic targets
Authors: Tej D Azad, Gary Schwartzbauer, Nicholas Theodore
Published: 2025

The Eastern Association for the Surgery of Trauma (EAST) multicenter cohort of 164 patients with blunt spinal cord injury (SCI) demonstrates that maintaining mean arterial pressure (MAP) ≥85 mm Hg for the first 96 hours failed to increase early American Spinal Injury Association (ASIA) Impairment Scale (AIS) conversion rates despite achieving >80% adherence to protocol targets.1 This finding exposes the potential limitations of uniform vasopressor strategies and aligns with recent critiques highlighting the limited evidence base underlying current MAP recommendations.

Contemporary physiologic research provides evidence for more nuanced hemodynamic management. Analysis of 200 000 intraoperative datapoints revealed that elevated MAP correlated with motor gains, but only within the first 48 hours postinjury.2 Subsequent topological network analysis defined a non-linear ‘sweet spot’ of MAP 76–104 mm Hg, with time spent outside this therapeutic window independently predicting failure to improve neurologically.3

Direct monitoring of spinal cord perfusion pressure (SCPP = MAP − intraspinal pressure) demonstrates superior prognostic capability compared with systemic MAP alone. Maintaining SCPP >50 mm Hg doubled 6-month AIS conversion rates in a prospective observational cohort study,4 and porcine contusion experiments confirmed that cerebrospinal fluid drainage combined with modest MAP elevation effectively raises SCPP and reduces ischemic penumbra volume.5

These physiologic insights are driving clinical innovation. Protocols replacing traditional MAP goals with SCPP targets via lumbar CSF drainage are emerging in specialized centers. Empirical modeling in 92 catheter-monitored patients identified an optimal bedside SCPP range of 60–90 mm Hg for neurologic recovery.6 In addition, early evidence suggests patient-specific SCPP goals may further optimize outcomes.7

Molecular biomarkers offer additional refinement for patient triage. Serum neurofilament light and glial fibrillar acidic protein measured within 24–96 hours accurately mirror baseline injury severity and predict 6-month outcomes with reasonable accuracy. Recent work from our group combined spinal cord-derived cell-free DNA with plasma protein panels to diagnose injury, stratify SCI severity, and predict AIS conversion with an area under the curve of 0.91.8

The neutral EAST results should not be interpreted as evidence against hemodynamic optimization in SCI management. Rather, these findings signal that simply ‘pushing MAP’ represents an insufficient therapeutic strategy. Future neurologic gains will likely emerge from integrating continuous SCPP monitoring, early cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) diversion, and dynamic biomarker panels into adaptive clinical trials that treat perfusion pressure as a patient-specific variable rather than a universal target.
