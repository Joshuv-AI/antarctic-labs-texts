Introduction

Priapism is defined as a persistent erection lasting more than 4 h that is, unrelated to sexual stimulation or interest [1]. It is classified into three types: ischemic (low‐flow), nonischemic (high‐flow), and stuttering priapism [2]. Ischemic priapism is a urological emergency, as prolonged corporal hypoxia can lead to irreversible erectile tissue damage and subsequent erectile dysfunction [3].

The etiology of ischemic priapism includes medications (particularly psychotropic drugs, anticoagulants, and anesthetic agents), hematological disorders, malignancies, and idiopathic causes [4]. Among anesthetic agents, propofol has been infrequently reported as a cause of priapism [5, 6, 7].

The management of ischemic priapism follows a stepwise approach, beginning with corporal aspiration and irrigation, followed by the intracavernous injection of sympathomimetic agents. If these measures fail, surgical interventions such as distal shunts (e.g., Winter's procedure or T‐shunt) or proximal shunts may be required [1].

Herein, we present a rare case of recurrent ischemic priapism following living‐donor kidney transplantation that required multiple interventions, including bilateral T‐shunt procedures for resolution.

Case Presentation

A 44‐year‐old man with end‐stage kidney disease secondary to malignant nephrosclerosis and IgA nephropathy presented for living‐donor kidney transplantation with his mother as the donor. His medical history included hypertension, dyslipidemia, and hyperuricemia. His medications comprised tacrolimus extended‐release, mycophenolate mofetil, extended‐release nifedipine, azilsartan, carvedilol, febuxostat, atorvastatin, alfacalcidol, daprodusate, sennoside, and lubiprostone. He also had a 4‐year history of smoking (20 cigarettes daily) and habitual alcohol consumption. Prior to undergoing renal transplant surgery, the patient had erectile capacity. History of peritoneal dialysis catheter surgery; anesthesia details unknown, prior propofol exposure possible; no priapism history.

The patient underwent living donor kidney transplantation under general anesthesia, with propofol (total dose: 1810 mg) used for induction and maintenance. Immunosuppression was induced with basiliximab and subsequently maintained under tacrolimus extended release, mycophenolate mofetil, and methylprednisolone. Tacrolimus trough levels (ng/mL): pre‐op day −1, 2.3; Day 0, 4.4; after surgery, 11.6; POD1, 10.2; POD5, 6.3; POD10, 8.8—generally near the 8–12 target range. On POD1, moderate penile pain was attributed to catheter, likely masking priapism. After catheter removal on POD5, persistent erection was noted, prompting urology consult.

Physical examination revealed a fully erect and rigid penis on palpation, with spontaneous pain and dark red discoloration.

Initial management involved bedside corporal aspiration using a butterfly needle, which led to temporary resolution of the priapism. However, recurrence occurred on Day 6. Contrast‐enhanced CT revealed distention of the corpora cavernosa, suggestive of blood flow stasis (Figure 1a,b). Bilateral Winter shunt was performed under propofol 120 mg. This involved inserting an 18G needle through the glans penis into each corpus cavernosum, followed by irrigation with normal saline (Figure 1c,d), resulting in transient improvement.

Despite these measures, the priapism recurred on Day 7. Blood gas analysis of the corporal aspiration confirmed severe ischemia, with the following values: pH 6.90, PO2 22.1 mmHg, PCO2 78.9 mmHg, HCO3− 14.7 mEq/L, base excess −18.8 mEq/L, lactate 9.1 mmol/L, and oxygen saturation 36.6%. Bilateral T‐shunt was performed under remimazolam 98.669 mg instead of propofol. This technique involved making a stab incision with a #11 scalpel through the glans penis into each corpus cavernosum, rotating the blade 90° away from the urethra, and withdrawing it to create a T‐shaped communication between the glans and the corpus cavernosum (Video S1). Each intervention achieved detumescence, but priapism recurred—after aspiration on Day 6 and Winter's shunt on Day 7—posing a clinical challenge.

The priapism resolved after the T‐shunt procedure. The patient experienced no postoperative infections and was discharged on postoperative Day 21. At the 6‐month follow‐up, the patient had no recurrence of priapism with good graft function (serum creatinine 1.26 mg/dL and eGFR 50 mL/min/m2); however, he had developed erectile dysfunction.

Discussion

This case report presents a rare occurrence of ischemic priapism following kidney transplantation. Although the exact cause was not definitively identified, propofol, which was used during general anesthesia, was suspected to be a possible etiological factor. Although timing and literature suggest propofol as the likely cause, other factors cannot be excluded. Transplantation stress, hemodynamic changes, altered vascular reactivity, tacrolimus, antihypertensives, and surgical stress may have collectively predisposed to priapism. Ischemic priapism is characterized by complete rigidity of the corpora cavernosa, penile pain, and dark discoloration. Blood gas analysis typically reveals hypoxemia, hypercapnia, and acidosis, as was confirmed in our patient [1, 2].

Propofol is a commonly used intravenous anesthetic agent; however, priapism is a rare adverse effect. A review of the literature revealed only a few reported cases of propofol‐induced priapism (Table 1). The exact mechanism remains unclear; proposed theories include inhibition of sympathetic‐mediated vasoconstriction, enhancement of parasympathetic‐mediated vasodilation, or possibly fat embolism [5, 6, 7]. Of note, priapism has been reported with both low and high doses of propofol, including in our patient, who received a total dose of 1810 mg.

The management of ischemic priapism follows a stepwise approach. First‐line therapy involves corporal aspiration with irrigation, followed by intracavernous injection of sympathomimetic agents [2]. Surgical shunting procedures are indicated in refractory cases. Distal shunts, such as Winter's procedure and the T‐shunt, create communication between the corpus cavernosum and the glans penis, allowing drainage of deoxygenated blood. More invasive proximal shunts may be necessary in cases that fail to respond to distal shunting [8].

In our case, treatment progressed from corporal aspiration to bilateral Winter's shunts and ultimately to bilateral T‐shunts due to recurrent episodes. The T‐shunt, described by Brant et al. [9], provides a larger communication channel than Winter's procedure and was ultimately successful in resolving our patient's priapism.

One of the most significant long‐term complications of prolonged ischemic priapism is erectile dysfunction, which was observed in our patient. The risk of erectile dysfunction increases with the duration of priapism and has been reported in up to 90% of cases lasting longer than 24 h [9]. Our patient's priapism had likely been present for several days before it was identified, contributing to the development of erectile dysfunction despite the successful resolution of the condition.

This case highlights the importance of vigilance for priapism in patients receiving propofol during surgical procedures, particularly when typical signs such as pain may be masked by postoperative discomfort or misattributed to other causes, such as urethral catheter irritation. Early recognition and timely escalation of management are crucial for preventing long‐term sequelae.

Conclusion

We report a case of recurrent ischemic priapism following living‐donor kidney transplantation that required multiple interventions, including bilateral T‐shunt procedures. Propofol is suspected as a likely etiological agent. This case emphasizes the importance of recognizing this rare complication in the posttransplantation setting, and the need for prompt and aggressive management to preserve erectile function.
