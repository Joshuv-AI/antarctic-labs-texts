Sound-induced visual motion perception in older adults: aging enhances audiovisual motion integration
Authors: Shinya Harada, Ryo Teraoka, Naoki Kuroda, Wataru Teramoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigated audiovisual integration in visual motion perception among both younger and older adults. Participants were asked to discriminate the visual motion direction of a random dot kinematogram in two experiments. The visual motion coherence of the random dot kinematogram varied across trials. Two experimental conditions were established: the lateral-shift and the one-sided sound condition. In the lateral-shift sound condition, the auditory motion stimulus based on interaural level differences was presented simultaneously with the visual stimuli. The auditory stimulus moved horizontally in either a rightward or leftward direction. In the one-sided sound condition, an auditory stimulus was presented through only one side of the headphones, simultaneously with the visual stimuli. The velocity of the dot motion differed between the two experiments (Experiment 1: 8°/s; Experiment 2: 6°/s). The results showed that the point of subjective stationarity was biased in the opposite direction to the auditory motion in the lateral-shift sound condition compared to the one-sided sound condition, regardless of age. Only in the Experiment 2, the point of subjective stationarity in the lateral-shift sound condition was more biased in older adults than in younger adults. These findings suggest that, in slow visual motion, age-related differences influence audiovisual motion integration.

Introduction

Aging impairs various functional abilities, affecting both higher-order functions (visual attention; Trick and Enns 1998; short-term memory; Craik and McDowd 1987) and fundamental perceptual functions (visual acuity; Milne 1979; contrast sensitivity; Lord et al. 1991; and hearing; Wiley et al. 1998). Age-related changes in fundamental visual functions are a risk factor for falls (Black and Wood 2005), while hearing degradation increases the risk of dementia (Livingston et al. 2017, 2020), potentially reducing healthy life expectancy. Therefore, investigating and identifying age-related decline in perceptual function is crucial for deepening our understanding of human perceptual and cognitive capabilities and enhancing the well-being of older individuals.

Motion perception—an individual’s ability to detect the movement of objects and their own movement in space—is crucial for appropriately perceiving the external world and for ensuring personal safety. Aging deteriorates visual motion perception (Billino and Pilz 2019). Age-related decline in global visual motion perception is well documented (Arena et al. 2012; Billino et al. 2008; Bocheva et al. 2013; Bogfjellmo et al. 2013; Hutchinson et al. 2014; Snowden and Kavanagh 2006; Trick and Silverman 1991; Yan et al. 2023). Numerous studies have examined this decline in global visual motion perception using random dot kinematograms in which dots move translationally in specific directions while varying the motion coherence of the visual stimulus (Arena et al. 2012; Hutchinson et al. 2014; Snowden and Kavanagh 2006; Trick and Silverman 1991; Yan et al. 2023). Researchers typically asked participants to discriminate the motion direction of the visual stimulus and examined whether aging affected the motion coherence threshold (Fig. 1A). Most studies have reported that the coherence threshold is higher in older adults than in younger adults (Billino and Pilz 2019), suggesting that aging impairs global visual motion perception.

Conversely, few studies have examined auditory motion perception in aging adults (Harada et al. 2025; Saberi et al. 2022). These studies found that aging did not affect auditory motion perception, at least when using broadband noise. The reason for this asymmetry between age-related changes in auditory and visual motion perception remains unclear (Harada et al. 2025).

Motion perception appears to be shaped not only by information from individual sensory modalities but also by the integration of information across multiple sensory modalities. Several psychophysical studies have investigated audiovisual integration in motion perception in younger adults. Their results suggest that auditory motion information influences visual motion perception and vice versa (Hidaka et al. 2009, 2011; Kim et al. 2012; Soto-Faraco et al. 2002, 2004). Although studies have yet to examine whether auditory motion information affects visual motion perception in older adults, some studies have suggested greater multimodal integration gain in older adults across various tasks (de Boer-Schellekens and Vroomen 2014; Laurienti et al. 2006; McGovern et al. 2014; Peiffer et al. 2007). For example, in a temporal order judgment task, older adults exhibited greater multimodal gain than younger adults (de Boer-Schellekens and Vroomen 2014). Additionally, older adults showed greater performance gain in speeded discrimination tasks owing to greater audiovisual integration (Laurienti et al. 2006). These studies suggest that multimodal integration may compensate for age-related decline in sensory sensitivity.

The principle of inverse effectiveness and the maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) model within the Bayesian inference theory may account for these age-related changes in multimodal integration. The principle of inverse effectiveness suggests that weaker unimodal input elicits greater multimodal gains (Meredith and Stein 1986). The MLE model argues that an unreliable signal in one modality and reliable signal in another modality result in a multimodal bias toward reliable input (Ernst and Banks 2002). Consequently, age-related decline in one modality may elicit multimodal gains. However, several studies have reported no age-related differences in multimodal integration or decreased multimodal integration among older adults (Atkin et al. 2024; Jones et al. 2019; Ren et al. 2021; Riemer et al. 2019). These findings cannot be fully explained by the abovementioned theories. Thus, the conditions or tasks that elicit multimodal gains in older adults remains unclear.

This study investigated whether age differences affect audiovisual integration in visual motion perception using the paradigm of sound-induced visual motion perception (SIVM; Hidaka et al. 2011). In SIVM, a blinking visual stimulus at a fixed location was perceived as lateral motion when its onset was synchronized to a sound alternating between left and right ears. Previous studies on SIVM have shown that auditory motion information affects visual motion perception in peripheral vision, both in static visual stimulus and in random dot kinematograms (Hidaka et al. 2009, 2011, 2017; Teramoto et al. 2010, 2012). Based on Hidaka et al. (2011), we used random dot kinematograms as visual stimuli and varied their motion coherence. We also used pink noise as an auditory stimulus, which was presented in synchrony with visual stimuli.

In the lateral-shift sound condition, we gradually modulated the interaural level difference of the auditory stimulus to induce the perception of auditory translational motion (Fig. 1B, a). In the one-sided sound condition, auditory stimuli were presented on only one side of the headphones; thus, participants did not perceive auditory motion (Fig. 1B, b). Participants were asked to discriminate visual motion direction while ignoring auditory motion direction. We hypothesized that participants would be more likely to report motion directions corresponding to auditory motion (or position) in the lateral-shift than in the one-sided sound condition, regardless of age. Previous studies have shown age-related decline in visual motion perception but no decline in auditory motion perception (visual motion; Arena et al. 2012; Hutchinson et al. 2014; Snowden and Kavanagh 2006; Trick and Silverman 1991; Yan et al. 2023; auditory motion; Harada et al. 2025; Saberi et al. 2022). Based on the principle of inverse effectiveness and the MLE model within the Bayesian inference theory, we hypothesized that older adults’ visual motion perception would be more influenced by auditory motion information than younger adults.

Experiment 1

Methods

Transparency and openness

Our study was pre-registered on the Open Science Framework before data collection (URL: https://osf.io/m2pgh/). We determined the sample size through a priori power analysis using Power Analysis for General ANOVA Designs (PANGEA; Westfall 2015). The analysis indicated that for a medium effect size (d = 0.45), a sample size of 16 participants per age group was required (α error probability = 0.05, power [1-β error probability] = 0.80). Owing to typically greater variability in data among older individuals, we terminated data collection upon reaching 16 available participants in the younger group and 20 available participants in the older group after excluding participants who either misunderstood instructions or whose data did not fit the psychometric function (see below). To improve clarity and readability, portions of the manuscript were edited using ChatGPT (ChatGPT, OpenAI), an AI-based language model. No content was generated by the tool; it was used solely for language editing.

Participants

Sixteen younger adults (eight women, mean age ± SD: 20.6 ± 1.00 years) and 24 community-dwelling older adults (13 women, mean age ± SD: 76.9 ± 3.73 years) participated in the experiments. We excluded data from four participants who either misunderstood instructions or whose data did not fit the psychometric function.

We recruited all the younger adult students from Kumamoto University and all older participants from the Senior Citizens’ Employment Guidance Center of Kumamoto City, a temporary employment organization for older adults. For older adults, hearing thresholds were tested at 500, 1000, 2000, and 4000 Hz using pure-tone audiometry and the average across ears; these frequencies (PTA4) were calculated before the experiment. We recruited older individuals with a PTA4 of less than 40 dB (i.e., normal hearing to mild hearing loss based on the Japan Audiological Society criteria, 2014). The Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE; Folstein et al. 1975) score was also used as a criterion; we recruited older individuals who scored more than 23. All the participants were naïve to the purpose of the experiment. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Kumamoto University (Reference number: 51; Title: Lifelong development of human perceptual-cognitive mechanisms and their neural basis), and the study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (1964). Written informed consent was obtained from all the participants.

Apparatus and stimulus

The experiments were controlled and visual stimuli were presented using a Windows PC (DELL Precision 3650 Tower) and a cathode ray tube (CRT) monitor (SONY CPD-E230). The screen resolution was 1024 × 768, and the refresh rate was 60 Hz. Auditory stimuli were delivered through headphones (SONY MDR-CD900ST) via an audio interface (Roland QUAD-CAPTURE). An experimental program was developed using Python (version 3.8.19) with the PsychoPy (version 2024.1.4) and Python-sound device library (version 0.4.6). Participant responses were obtained via a numeric keyboard.

Figure 1 shows schematic illustrations of visual and auditory stimuli. In the experiment, a random dot kinematogram was presented at 5° lateral to the center of the CRT monitor; that is, the distance from the center of the random dot kinematogram to the center of the CRT monitor was 5°. A previous study varied the location of visual stimuli at 5°, 10°, and 20° (Hidaka et al. 2011). In this study, we only used 5° as the stimulus location, considering age-related changes in attentional function in the peripheral visual field (Sekuler et al. 2000). Based on Hidaka et al. (2011), the position of the kinematogram depended on the participant’s dominant eye; if the right eye was dominant, a random dot kinematogram was presented at 5° to the right of the center and vice versa. A total of 200 dots were presented inside an invisible circle with a diameter of 10°, and each dot was 0.1° in size. The lifetime of the dots was 10 frames, and the displacement per frame was 0.13°. The random dot kinematogram was generated using the DotStim function in PsychoPy. The signalDots parameter in DotStim was set to “different,” such that the assignment of signal and noise dots was randomized on each frame. The noiseDots parameter was set to “direction,” meaning that noise dots moved in random but constant directions. The dots were light gray, and their luminance was 74.4 cd/m2. The direction of the dot motion, either rightward or leftward, was randomly determined for each trial. The velocity of the dot motion was 8°/s and the stimulus duration was 400 ms. The background was dark gray, and their luminance was 0.02 cd/m2. Dot motion coherence varied across trials, with values of 0.08, 0.16, 0.32, and 0.64. In each trial, a fixation circle appeared at the center of the display. Participants were instructed to maintain their gaze on the fixation point. The fixation circle was 0.4° × 0.4°, red in color, and had a luminance of 16.2 cd/m2.

A pink noise burst was used as the auditory stimulus (sampling rate: 48000 Hz, duration: 400 ms; including 5 ms rise/fall ramps) and was presented simultaneously with the visual stimuli. We established two sound conditions: lateral-shift and one-sided sound. For the lateral-shift sound, the auditory stimulus was panned between the two ears using constant-power panning, with gains following complementary square-root trajectories so that the overall signal power remained constant throughout the transition. For the one-sided sound, the signal was presented to one ear only throughout the 400-ms duration, with no interaural panning and thus no perceived auditory motion. We confirmed that the onsets of the visual and auditory stimuli were synchronized using a digital oscilloscope. Before the practice trials, the participants were instructed to adjust the sound of the auditory stimuli to a comfortable level.

Procedure

A two-way mixed-design experiment was conducted to estimate the point of subjective stationarity. We defined the point of subjective stationarity as the motion coherence at which the visual stimulus was perceived as stationary. The between-participant factor was age (2; older and younger adults), and the within-participant factor was the auditory condition (2; lateral-shift and one-sided sound). Participants were asked to discriminate the motion direction of a random dot kinematogram (right or left). The main session consisted of 320 trials (2 auditory conditions × 2 auditory motion directions (locations) × 2 visual target directions × 4 motion coherence levels × 10 repetitions). Thus, we manipulated the following variables: auditory condition (2; lateral-shift and one-sided sound), auditory motion direction or location (2; right (ward) and left (ward)), visual target direction (2; rightward and leftward), and coherence (4; 0.08, 0.16, 0.32, and 0.64). We classified stimulus conditions as either consistent or inconsistent based on the combination of visual and auditory stimuli. For example, in the consistent situation, leftward visual motion was presented along with leftward or left-sided auditory stimulus (lateral-shift or one-sided condition, respectively). In the inconsistent situation, leftward visual motion was presented along with rightward or right-sided auditory stimulus (lateral-shift or one-sided condition, respectively). The order of trials was randomized. Before the main session, participants completed a practice session. The practice session consisted of 16 trials (2 auditory conditions × 2 auditory motion directions (or locations) × 2 visual target directions × 2 motion coherence levels). Thus, we manipulated four variables: auditory condition (2; lateral-shift and one-sided sound), auditory motion direction or location (2; right (ward) and left (ward)), visual target direction (2; rightward and leftward), and coherence level (2; 0.32 and 0.64).

The experiments were conducted in a quiet, dimly lit room. Participants were seated in a chair positioned 57 cm from the display, with their heads stabilized using a chin rest. At the beginning of each trial, the fixation circle appeared at the center of the display before the response display. Participants were instructed to maintain their gaze on it for as long as it was presented. One thousand milliseconds after the fixation appeared, a random dot kinematogram with 0 coherence was presented for a randomly determined duration between 166 and 283 ms. Immediately afterward, we presented the participants with another random dot kinematogram, serving as the visual motion stimulus. The auditory stimulus was presented simultaneously with the visual stimulus. The duration of each stimulus was set to 400 ms. The response display appeared 283 ms after the stimulus disappeared. Participants indicated the motion direction of the visual stimuli via a key press. Upon pressing the response key, the display disappeared immediately and the next trial began (Fig. 2). Prior to the main experiment, participants engaged in a practice session that comprised 16 trials.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.2.1; R Core Team 2022) and anovakun package (version 4.8.7; Iseki 2022). We fitted a psychometric function to the proportion of visual motion direction perceptions consistent with the lateral shift of sound in each condition (Fig. 3). We used the maximum likelihood method to fit a cumulative Gaussian distribution function to each participant’s data. If the coefficient of determination (McFadden’s Pseudo-2) in a given condition was less than 0.1, we excluded the individual data from the analysis. We estimated the point of subjective stationarity based on each participant’s 50% threshold in the psychometric function, at which the visual stimulus was perceived as stationary. A Shapiro-Wilk test for each condition confirmed the normality of the data (ps > 0.05). A two-way mixed-design analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed on the estimated point of subjective stationarity. In this ANOVA, the between-participant factor is age (2: older and younger adults) and the within-participant factor is the auditory condition (2: lateral-shift and one-sided sound).

A previous study suggested that auditory information is utilized only under difficult task conditions, specifically when visual motion coherence is low (Meyer and Wuerger 2001). This tendency in participants’ judgment is referred to as response bias. To examine response bias effects, we calculated just noticeable differences (JND) using the following formula: (75% threshold – 25% threshold) / 2 based on an earlier study (Hidaka et al. 2011). We performed a two-way mixed design ANOVA on JND. As the Shapiro-Wilk test did not confirm the normality of the data (ps < 0.05), we applied an aligned rank transform (ART) to the JND data using the ARTool package in R (Elkin et al. 2021; Wobbrock et al. 2011). In this ANOVA, the between-participant factor was age (2; older and younger adults), and the within-participant factor was the auditory condition (2; lateral-shift and one-sided sound).

Results and discussion

Data from three older participants were excluded based on the criterion of each condition’s average ± 2SD. Consequently, the final analysis included 16 younger (8 women, M = 21.0, SD = 0.75) and 17 older participants (8 women, M = 76.3, SD = 3.35). Figure 3 presents the psychometric functions fitted to the proportion of visual motion direction perception consistent with sounds as a function of motion coherence. Figure 4a presents the point of subjective stationarity. For older adults, the mean value was − 0.048 (SD = 0.042) in the lateral-shift sound condition, and 0.032 (SD = 0.041) in the one-sided sound condition. For younger adults, the mean value was − 0.027 (SD = 0.033) in the lateral-shift sound condition, and 0.024 (SD = 0.043) in the one-sided sound condition. A two-way ANOVA for mixed design revealed that the main effect of the auditory condition was significant (F(1, 31) = 55.00, p < .001, ηp2 = 0.39); however, the main effect of age and the interaction between auditory condition and age were not significant (age: F(1, 31) = 0.32, p = .577, ηp2 = 0.003; age × auditory condition: F(1, 31) = 2.63, p = .115, ηp2 = 0.018). For both younger and older participants, the point of subjective stationarity in the lateral-shift sound condition was lower than in the one-sided sound condition. This result suggests that auditory motion information captured visual motion perception, regardless of age. Contrary to our hypothesis, the point of subjective stationarity in the lateral-shift sound condition did not differ between the age groups.

Figure 4b presents the JNDs. The y-axis represents the proportion of visual motion direction responses consistent with the auditory motion direction (or position), and the x-axis represents relative audiovisual congruency in the psychometric function of our experiment (Fig. 3). Negative x-axis values are expected to correspond to lower proportions of congruent responses, whereas positive x-axis values are expected to correspond to higher proportions. Thus, we defined the JND as an index of the sensitivity of participants’ responses to changes in visual motion coherence. If response bias (i.e., decisional bias) influenced the results, participants’ decisions would be expected to rely more strongly on sound motion information, particularly under low-coherence conditions in which visual motion direction is difficult to discriminate. In such a case, the presence of response bias would be reflected in a shallower slope of the psychometric function in the lateral-shift sound condition compared with the one-sided sound condition, and there should be differences in JND between the conditions. For older adults, the mean value was 0.137 (SD = 0.053) in the lateral-shift sound condition, and 0.115 (SD = 0.043) in the one-sided sound condition. For younger adults, the mean value was 0.132 (SD = 0.069) in the lateral-shift sound condition, and 0.148 (SD = 0.068) in the one-sided sound condition. A two-way ANOVA with ART for mixed design was conducted on the transformed data, which revealed that the main effects of age and auditory condition were not significant (age: F(1, 31) = 0.37, p = .540, ηp2 = 0.011; auditory condition: F(1, 31) = 0.12, p = .726, ηp2 = 0.004); however, the interaction between auditory condition and age was significant (age × auditory condition: F(1, 31) = 6.60, p = .015, ηp2 = 0.017). We performed a Wilcoxon rank-sum test to examine the differences between age groups within each sound condition and between sound conditions within each age group. Corrections were applied using the Holm–Bonferroni method. The results revealed no significant differences (younger-shift vs. younger-onesided: padjusted = 0.80; older-shift vs. older-onesided: padjusted = 0.62; younger-shift vs. older-shift: padjusted = 0.80; younger-onesided vs. older-onesided: padjusted = 0.47). These results suggest that the difference in the point of subjective stationarity between auditory conditions was not influenced by response bias. Additionally, age differences might not have affected sensitivity to visual motion coherence in this experiment. However, this point is discussed further in the General Discussion.

In Experiment 1, we investigated whether age differences affect audiovisual integration in visual motion perception. Our results revealed no age-related difference in the point of subjective stationarity under lateral-shift sound conditions. This finding suggests that audiovisual integration in visual motion perception is comparable between older and younger adults. Additionally, the JND analysis revealed no age-related differences in sensitivity to visual motion coherence. Based on the principle of inverse effectiveness and the MLE model within the Bayesian inference theory, we hypothesize that the absence of age difference in audiovisual integration in this experiment may be attributed to the lack of age differences in sensitivity to visual motion perception. Previous studies have suggested that age-related differences in motion perception depend on the speed of the visual stimulus (Billino and Pilz 2019). Several studies have reported that age-related impairments are greater for slower motion than for faster motion (Bocheva et al. 2013; Bogfjellmo et al. 2013; O’Connor et al. 2010; Snowden and Kavanagh 2006).

Experiment 2

In Experiment 2, we reduced the speed of the visual motion from 8°/s to 6°/s. We used visual motion speed of 8°/s in Experiment 1, following a previous study (Hidaka et al. 2011). In Experiment 2, we used a motion speed of 6°/s because it is slower compared to 8°/s in Experiment 1 and is not far from 8°/s. Previous studies suggested that spatiotemporal differences between auditory and visual stimuli affect audiovisual integration (Park et al. 2021; Soto-Faraco et al. 2002). We were concerned that large speed modifications between two experiments could degrade audiovisual integration in both age groups, so we chose a motion speed of 6°/s. We predicted that the point of subjective stationarity in the lateral-shift condition would be lower in older adults than in younger adults, and JND in each sound condition would be higher in older adults than in younger adults.

Methods

The procedure was the same as in Experiment 1, except for the following modifications. The dot motion velocity was varied from 8°/s to 6°/s by reducing the spatial displacement. The dot displacement per frame was 0.1°. Sixteen younger adults (6 women, mean age ± SD: 21.0 ± 1.22 years) and 22 community-dwelling older adults (11 women, mean age ± SD: 77.2 ± 3.88 years) participated in the experiment. Data from two older participants were excluded owing to misunderstanding instructions or failure to fit the psychometric function. Consequently, we terminated data collection upon reaching 16 available participants in the younger group and 20 available participants in the older group.

Transparency and openness

Our study was pre-registered on the Open Science Framework before data collection (URL: https://osf.io/udj86/). We determined the sample size and terminated data collection using the same criteria as in Experiment 1.

Results and discussion

Data from one younger and one older participant were excluded based on the criterion of each condition’s average ± 2SD. Consequently, the final analysis included 15 younger (6 women, M = 21.1, SD = 1.15) and 19 older participants (8 women, M = 76.9, SD = 3.86). Figure 5a presents the point of subjective stationarity in Experiment 2. For older adults, the mean value was − 0.067 (SD = 0.054) in the lateral-shift sound condition, and 0.023 (SD = 0.056) in the one-sided sound condition. For younger adults, the mean value was − 0.028 (SD = 0.033) in the lateral-shift sound condition, and 0.013 (SD = 0.037) in the one-sided sound condition. A two-way mixed-design ANOVA revealed that the main effects of age were not significant (F(1, 32) = 1.06, p = .311, ηp2 = 0.01); however, the main effect of the auditory condition and the interaction between the auditory condition and age were significant (auditory condition: F(1, 32) = 1.06, p < .001, ηp2 = 0.030; age × auditory condition: F(1, 32) = 6.35, p = .017, ηp2 = 0.043). A simple main effect test revealed that the difference in the point of subjective stationarity between the age groups was significant only in the lateral-shift sound condition (lateral-shift: F(1, 32) = 5.52, p = .025, ηp2 = 0.147; one-sided: F(1, 32) = 0.34, p = .566, ηp2 = 0.010). Additionally, the difference between the lateral-shift and one-sided sound conditions was significant in both younger and older participants (younger: F(1, 14) = 13.80, p = .002, ηp2 = 0.265; older: F(1, 18) = 38.43, p < .001, ηp2 = 0.402). For both younger and older participants, the point of subjective stationarity in the lateral-shift sound condition was significantly lower than in the one-sided sound condition, suggesting that auditory motion information captured visual motion perception regardless of age. More importantly, in the lateral-shift sound condition, the point of subjective stationarity in older participants was lower than in the younger participants (lateral-shift: F(1, 32) = 5.526, p = .003, ηp2 = 0.147; one-sided: F(1, 32) = 0.337, p = .566, ηp2 = 0.010). Thus, older participants’ visual motion perception was more influenced by auditory motion information than that of younger participants.

Figure 5b presents the JND in Experiment 2. For older adults, the mean value was 0.145 (SD = 0.062) in the lateral-shift sound condition, and 0.158 (SD = 0.059) in the one-sided sound condition. For younger adults, the mean value was 0.124 (SD = 0.064) in the lateral-shift sound condition, and 0.129 (SD = 0.077) in the one-sided sound condition. We conducted a two-way mixed-design ANOVA with ART on the transformed data, which revealed that the main effects of age and auditory condition were not significant (age: F(1, 32) = 3.63, p = .007, ηp2 = 0.006; auditory condition: F(1, 32) = 0.19, p = .663, ηp2 = 0.006). Additionally, the interaction between the auditory condition and age was not significant (age × auditory condition: F(1, 32) = 0.59, p = .045, ηp2 = 0.018). As in Experiment 1, the difference in the point of subjective stationarity between auditory conditions did not depend on response bias. Furthermore, age differences in sensitivity to visual motion coherence did not reach significance. In other words, older participants’ motion coherence sensitivity may have been comparable to that of younger participants, contrary to our hypothesis. However, this point is discussed further in the General Discussion.

In Experiment 2, we investigated age differences in audiovisual integration in visual motion perception, using a slower stimulus speed than in Experiment 1. The results revealed that the point of subjective stationarity in lateral-shift conditions was lower in older adults than in younger adults, suggesting greater audiovisual integration in older adults than in younger adults. However, the results also showed that the JND of older adults was comparable to younger adults in each sound condition. Contrary to our hypothesis, we could not confirm any differences in visual motion coherence sensitivity between older and younger adults.

Exploratory analysis

An exploratory analysis was conducted to compare data from Experiments 1 and 2 and to investigate why age-related differences emerged only in the slower-motion condition (6°/s) and not in the faster-motion condition (8°/s). Independent-samples t-tests were performed to compare the results of Experiments 1 and 2 within each age group and sound condition. These analyses revealed no significant differences between experiments in any condition (older-shift-Exp. 1 vs. older-shift-Exp. 2: t = 1.11, p = .28; older-onesided-Exp. 1 vs. older-onesided-Exp. 2: t = 0.51, p = .61; younger-shift-Exp. 1 vs. younger-shift-Exp. 2: t = 0.08, p = .94; younger-onesided-Exp. 1 vs. younger-onesided-Exp. 2: t = 0.71, p = .48). Most importantly, there was no significant effect in the lateral-shift sound condition for older adults between Experiments 1 and 2, contrary to the findings of the within-experiment analyses.

In addition, we conducted an exploratory analysis of the slope at the 50% point of the psychometric function to examine potential effects of response bias. Because the psychometric function may be asymmetric or have a shallow slope, 75%–25% JND estimates could be biased. Therefore, we calculated the slope at the 50% point of the psychometric function and re-examined whether response bias affected the results in Experiments 1 and 2.

For older adults in Experiment 1, the mean value was 0.137 (SD = 0.053) in the lateral-shift sound condition, and 0.115 (SD = 0.043) in the one-sided sound condition. For younger adults in Experiment 1, the mean value was 0.132 (SD = 0.069) in the lateral-shift sound condition, and 0.147 (SD = 0.068) in the one-sided sound condition. A two-way ANOVA with ART for mixed design was conducted on the transformed data, which revealed that the main effects of age and auditory condition were not significant (age: F(1, 31) = 0.39, p = .537, ηp2 = 0.012; auditory condition: F(1, 31) = 0.26, p = .614, ηp2 = 0.007); however, the interaction between auditory condition and age was significant (age × auditory condition: F(1, 31) = 5.89, p = .021, ηp2 = 0.016). We performed a Wilcoxon rank-sum test to examine the differences between age groups within each sound condition and between sound conditions within each age group. Corrections were applied using the Holm–Bonferroni method. The results revealed no significant differences (younger-shift vs. younger-onesided: padjusted = 0.80; older-shift vs. older-onesided: padjusted = 0.62; younger-shift vs. older-shift: padjusted = 0.80; younger-onesided vs. older-onesided: padjusted = 0.47).

For older adults in Experiment 2, the mean value was 0.145 (SD = 0.062) in the lateral-shift sound condition, and 0.157 (SD = 0.059) in the one-sided sound condition. For younger adults in Experiment 2, the mean value was 0.124 (SD = 0.064) in the lateral-shift sound condition, and 0.129 (SD = 0.077) in the one-sided sound condition. We conducted a two-way mixed-design ANOVA with ART on the transformed data, which revealed that the main effects of age and auditory condition were not significant (age: F(1, 32) = 3.62, p = .007, ηp2 = 0.010; auditory condition: F(1, 32) = 0.28, p = .600, ηp2 = 0.001). Additionally, the interaction between the auditory condition and age was not significant (age × auditory condition: F(1, 32) = 0.24, p = .879, ηp2 = 0.001).

Similar to the results for the original JND (75%–25%), these results suggest that the difference in the point of subjective stationarity between auditory conditions was not influenced by response bias, and age differences might not have affected sensitivity to visual motion coherence in Experiments 1 and 2. Additionally, the JND values obtained using the two approaches (i.e., values calculated at 75% and 25% points of the psychometric function and those calculated at 50%) were nearly identical. This pattern suggests that the shape of the psychometric function did not substantially influence the results or their interpretation.

We fitted a linear mixed-effects model with the point of subjective stationarity in Experiment 2 as the dependent variable, auditory condition and age group as fixed effects, the 75%–25% JND as a covariate, and participant as a random intercept. This approach allows us to examine whether JND affected the point of subjective stationarity. The analysis revealed that the main effect of age was not significant (β = −0.01, SE = 0.02, t = − 0.723, p = .473); however, the main effect of auditory condition was significant (β = −0.09, SE = 0.01, t = − 6.13, p < .001). The interaction between auditory condition and age group was also significant (β = 0.05, SE = 0.02, t = 2.19, p = .035). In contrast, the effect of JND was not significant (β = 0.03, SE = 0.97, t = 0.34, p = .74). Importantly, the effects of auditory condition and its interaction with age group remained significant after controlling for JND, suggesting that the shift in the point of subjective stationarity cannot be explained by differences in JND. This pattern of results remained the same even when the slope at the 50% point of the psychometric function at the 50% threshold was included as a covariate.

General discussion

We investigated whether age differences affected audiovisual integration in motion perception. The results of Experiments 1 and 2 revealed that audiovisual integration was greater in older adults than in younger adults, but only in slower motion (6°/s in Experiment 2). Some studies have examined age-related differences in audiovisual integration in motion perception. Puschmann et al. (2014) reported that changes in auditory frequency disrupted visual motion perception only in older adults with high-frequency hearing loss. Other studies examined age-related changes in visual motion perception using the stream-bounce effect (Bedard and Barnett-Cowan 2016; Roudaia et al. 2013); however, they did not focus on auditory motion information. This study is the first to demonstrate that aging enhances the integration of auditory and visual motion information.

One might argue that auditory motion information distracted visual motion discrimination among older adults in our experiments. However, we believe this is unlikely for two reasons. First, we included a one-sided sound condition in which auditory stimuli were presented to only one ear and compared the results with those of the lateral-shift sound condition. Similar to the lateral-shift sound condition, one-sided sound could have acted as a distractor for visual motion discrimination; therefore, this comparison allowed us to rule out the potential distracting effect of auditory stimulation. Second, if auditory motion was a distractor only among older adults, we would have observed significant age differences in Experiment 1. Consequently, we could not conclude that older adults were more distracted by auditory motion than younger adults.

Another possible interpretation is that older adults were more vulnerable to response bias (i.e., decisional bias) than younger adults. However, we consider this explanation less likely. Following a previous study (Hidaka et al. 2011; see the Statistical Analysis section), we used the JND as an index of response bias. No significant differences in JND were observed between sound conditions in either experiment, suggesting that the age-related differences observed in the lateral-shift sound condition in Experiment 2 are less likely to be driven by response-related strategies or decisional biases.

Together, these considerations suggest that the age-related difference observed in Experiment 2 is better interpreted as reflecting a change in the audiovisual integration process. Furthermore, this difference is more likely to reflect a change in integration strategy rather than a change in visual sensitivity, given that no age-related differences were observed in JND. However, it is not clear why age-related differences emerged only in the slower motion condition (6°/s) and not in the faster motion condition (8°/s). As noted above, the principle of inverse effectiveness argues that weaker unimodal input elicits greater multimodal gain (Meredith and Stein 1986), and the MLE model within the Bayesian inference theory indicates that an unreliable signal in one modality and reliable signal in another modality predict a multimodal bias toward reliable signal (Ernst and Banks 2002). Several studies have suggested that the age-related decline in motion perception is greater for slower motion than for faster motion (Bocheva et al. 2013; Bogfjellmo et al. 2013; O’Connor et al. 2010; Snowden and Kavanagh 2006). However, our results revealed that the JND of older adults was comparable to younger adults even in the slower motion condition (Experiment 2), suggesting no age-related difference in visual motion coherence sensitivity. Therefore, we cannot conclude that the observed age-related differences in the slower motion condition were due to weaker unimodal input in the slower condition. Alternatively, these findings could be explained by age-related changes in the audiovisual integration process or strategy (Park et al. 2021; Yan et al. 2023). Previous studies have suggested that age-related changes in motion perception may result from age-related alteration in brain activity within regions responsible for global motion processing, particularly in the V5/MT area (Biehl et al. 2017). The V5/MT area is activated by auditory motion (Alink et al. 2008). Hidaka et al. (2017) suggested that illusory motion perception in sound-induced visual motion is associated with the V5/hMT, as indicated by an fMRI study. Furthermore, Yan et al. (2023) demonstrated that neural substrates associated with global motion perception in older adults differ partially between the slow (1.8°/s) and fast (7.5°/s) motion (slow: bilateral V4v, V5/MT+, left V7, V8, LO; fast: the right V7 and left V5/MT+). Speculatively, our findings of age-related differences only in the slower motion condition may reflect age-related structural or functional change in the brain regions involved in visual and auditory motion processing. Alternatively, a recent study using the Bayesian causal inference model suggested that the audiovisual integration strategy differs between older and younger adults (Park et al. 2021). This age-related difference in audiovisual integration strategy may also be related to our findings.

This study has some limitations. First, we did not track participants’ eye movements or include filler trials to confirm fixation. Thus, we could not confirm whether the participants maintained fixation on the cross. However, previous studies have shown that SIVM is more pronounced when a global motion stimulus is presented in the peripheral visual field (Hidaka et al. 2011). Therefore, even if older participants had difficulty maintaining fixation, we believe that the impact of fixation on the age-related difference in audiovisual integration would be minimal. Second, contrary to the findings of Experiments 1 and 2, the exploratory analysis showed no significant effect in the lateral-shift sound condition for older adults across both experiments. This apparent discrepancy can be explained by the relative ordering of the condition means. Performance of older adults in the lateral-shift sound condition in Experiment 1 fell between that of younger adults in the lateral-shift sound condition in Experiment 2 and that of older adults in the same condition in Experiment 2. Consequently, the difference between Experiments 1 and 2 within the older adult group was attenuated, despite the presence of a clear age-group difference in Experiment 2. Alternatively, the results of this exploratory t-test may have been influenced by limited statistical power, as the analysis involved subdividing the sample into smaller subgroups. Importantly, because this analysis was exploratory and not preregistered, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and await confirmation in future studies specifically designed to compare these metrics. Third, following Hidaka et al. (2011), we used the JND as an index of response bias using the following formula: (75% threshold – 25% threshold) / 2. This is an indirect and nonstandard index of response bias, and response bias could also be manifested as a shift in the point of subjective stationarity in our experimental setting. An alternative method to exclude response bias is to include an audiovisual asynchrony condition, as in Experiment 3 of Hidaka et al. (2009). However, a long experiment such as that of Hidaka et al. (2009) could introduce confounds due to fatigue in older adults, making it difficult to extract the pure effects of audiovisual integration. Future studies should address this issue by using alternative approaches. Fourth, we examined only two speed conditions (8°/s and 6°/s). Therefore, it remains unclear whether age-related differences in SIVM exist under conditions with speeds faster or slower than those used in our experiments. If stimulus speeds exceed 8°/s, age-related differences may not be significant, as observed in Experiment (1) Conversely, at speeds slower than 6°/s, age-related differences may be larger than those observed in Experiment (2) Put simply, there may be a threshold between 6°/s and 8°/s at which the age-related differences becomes statistically significant or nonsignificant. However, previous studies have suggested that spatiotemporal discrepancies between auditory and visual stimuli affect audiovisual integration (Park et al. 2021; Soto-Faraco et al. 2002). Therefore, large changes in stimulus speed may attenuate audiovisual integration in both age groups. Future studies should conduct more comprehensive experiments with a wider range of stimulus speeds. Fifth, the position of the sound source in the one-sided condition may influence visual motion discrimination performance, because lateralized sounds can affect spatial attention or bias perception. However, comparing the effects of the lateral-shift and one-sided sound conditions allows us to isolate the contribution of auditory motion information from potential attentional biases. Therefore, we consider the one-sided sound condition to have adequately served as a control condition. Sixth, large individual differences were observed in both the point of subjective stationarity and JND across experiments, suggesting that the effect is not consistent across individuals. Alternatively, there may be distinct subgroups (e.g., some older adults integrate audiovisual motion information, whereas others do not). The task or measurement used in the present experiments may not be suitable for all participants, who may possess diverse characteristics. Future studies should investigate the factors underlying these individual differences.
