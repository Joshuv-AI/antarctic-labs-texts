A case of pigmented epithelioid melanocytoma in a pediatric patient with reflectance confocal microscopy
Authors: Randy F Ko, Shelly Stepenaskie, John R Durkin
Published: 2024

Clinical presentation

A 4-year-old female patient was referred to our clinic by a pediatrician to evaluate a lesion, which appeared a year prior, situated on the right cheek/temple area. The patient’s mother reported that the lesion had grown and exhibited a speckled appearance. Upon physical examination, we observed a 3 mm exophytic dark blue papule surrounded by similarly colored, grouped macules (Fig 1, A). The patient’s mother indicated that there was no known family history of skin cancer. Clinical suspicion primarily included spitz nevus, atypical spitzoid neoplasm, agminated/segmental blue nevus, congenital nevus with satellite lesions, nevus spilus, and venous malformation.

A, Clinical image of an exophytic dark blue papule surrounded by grouped macules at the right cheek/temple area. B, Dermatoscopic image showing a 3 mm dark brown to black papule with homogenous pigment and dark brown to black macules surrounding the papule.

Dermoscopic appearance

A dermoscopic examination revealed a dark brown to black papule with homogenous pigment and dark brown to black macules surrounding the papule (Fig 1, B).

Confocal microscopy appearance

Images were acquired with reflectance confocal microscopy (RCM; VivaScope 1500, Caliber Imaging & Diagnostics), which revealed an atypical honeycomb pattern featuring disarray within the epidermis of the lesion (Fig 2, A). The epidermis further displayed an abundance of hyper-refractile atypical epithelioid and dendritic cells, with pagetoid spread throughout the lesion (Fig 2, A). The dermis revealed plump and highly refractile epithelioid cells (Fig 2, B). The dermal-epidermal junction presented numerous hyper-refractile atypical spindle cells. Based on these RCM findings, there was a heightened suspicion of an atypical spitzoid neoplasm, with melanoma as a diagnosis that could not be ruled out.

Reflectance confocal microscopy image. A, Atypical honeycomb pattern (orange aHC) featuring disarray within the lesion. The epidermis displayed an abundance of hyper-refractile atypical epithelioid (orange arrow) and dendritic cells (blue arrow). B, The dermis revealed plump and highly refractile epithelioid cells (orange arrow).

Histologic diagnosis

Histologic examination of two punch biopsies revealed two distinct but adjacent nevi (Fig 3). In the laterally located lesion, hematoxylin-eosin stain of a pigmented epithelioid melanocytoma (PEM) lesion revealed a heavily pigmented epithelioid, spindled, and dendritic cell appearance that is round-to polygonal-shaped. In the medial lesion, deeper tissue levels (located medially) were also investigated to confirm the diagnosis of a blue nevus with PEM features, which were noted to track along adnexal and vascular structures.

Histologic image. Hematoxylin-eosin-stained image of a pigmented epithelioid melanocytoma lesion (A) at low magnification and (B and C) at higher magnification of the dermis showing heavily pigmented and pleomorphic epithelioid, spindled, and dendritic cell appearance that are round-to polygonal-shaped.

Key message

We present the RCM findings of a PEM, also known as an epithelioid blue nevus or a low-grade “animal-type melanoma,” in a pediatric patient.1 PEM is a rare subtype of blue nevus characterized by epithelioid and dendritic melanocytes with a dispersed distribution pattern throughout the dermis.2,3 Due to its scarcity, diagnosing and managing PEM can be challenging and can appear with or without the Carney complex.4,5 The differentiative diagnosis includes other pigmented lesions including melanoma, blue nevus, Spitz nevus, and other benign melanocytic neoplasms. RCM features of blue nevi typically reveal dense melanocytic nests within the dermis. Spitz nevi show pagetoid infiltration, spindled cells, and plump, bright cells corresponding to melanophages in the dermis. Melanoma will show the presence of pagetoid, atypical large, and dendritic cells at the dermoepidermal junction and epidermal architecture with disrupted honeycomb or cobblestone pattern of epidermal keratinocytes. Benign melanocytic neoplasms vary in appearance but will lack the cellular atypia seen in malignancies and maintain normal epidermal architecture patterns. This report demonstrates the potential role of RCM in differentiating PEM from other pigmented skin lesions. However, mosaic images reveal that PEM is indistinguishable from melanoma on RCM, making RCM unsuccessful in avoiding a biopsy in this case. One potential limitation of this report is the lack of molecular studies for PEM. RCM allows for early recognition of potentially malignant lesions and can serve as a helpful tool in guiding the diagnostic pathway for complex cases, further emphasizing the need for a multidisciplinary approach in dermatology practice.
