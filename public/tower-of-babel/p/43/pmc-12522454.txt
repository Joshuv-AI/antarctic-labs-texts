Dietary phytochemical index and sleep quality: a cross-sectional study from Turkey
Authors: Cansu Memiç İnan, Ayşe Özfer Özçelik
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPoor sleep quality is an increasing public health concern. Phytochemicals are suggested to have beneficial effects on sleep due to their antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties. The aim of this study was to investigate the relationship between the Dietary Phytochemical Index (DPI), an alternative method for estimating dietary phytochemical intake, and sleep quality.MethodsThe sample of this cross-sectional study consisted of university students between the ages of 19–29 studying at Ankara University. The study data were obtained by face-to-face interview using a questionnaire form. The questionnaire form included general information, anthropometric measurements, Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), International Physical Activity Questionnaire (short form) and a 24-hour dietary recall. DPI was calculated as the percentage of daily energy intake from phytochemical-rich foods: DPI = (kcal from phytochemical-rich foods / total kcal) × 100.ResultsThis study included 472 university students. The participants were classified according to their DPI scores as < 10.00 first tertile (T1), 10.00–27.14 s tertile (T2), > 27.14 third tertile (T3). The mean DPI score of the participants was 20.45. The frequency of individuals aged ≥ 22 years, overweight, non-smokers, and those with moderate physical activity was significantly higher in the lowest DPI tertile (p < 0.05). Total PSQI, sleep quality, habitual sleep efficiency, use of sleeping medication, and daytime dysfunction scores were found to be lower in participants in the highest DPI tertile. (p < 0.05). Participants in the highest tertile of the DPI had a 14% lower odds of poor sleep quality than those in the lowest tertile (OR = 0.861, 95% CI, 0.757–0.980).ConclusionsAs a result, this study found that sleep quality was better in those in the highest DPI tertile. Longitudinal studies with large samples are needed to confirm the relationship between DPI and sleep quality.Keywords: Dietary phytochemical index, Phytochemicals, Sleep quality, University students

Introduction

Sleep is defined as a complex and reversible behavioural state in which the individual perceptually disconnects from the environment and does not react to the environment. Sleep, which is important for physical and mental health, is a homeostatic process involving active and periodic biological conditions [1]. Sleep quality includes both subjective satisfaction with the sleep experience and quantitative components of sleep such as sleep duration, sleep onset, sleep maintenance and sleep efficiency [2]. Although sleep disorders related to poor sleep quality are common at all ages, they are recognised as an important public health problem especially in university students [3]. Poor sleep quality has been reported in 30–70% of university students [2]. Poor sleep quality is associated with cancer, cardiovascular diseases, diabetes and all-cause mortality [4]. Various factors such as demographic characteristics, environmental factors, and the level of physical activity can affect sleep quality [5]. Additionally, recent studies have indicated that dietary intake also influences sleep quality [6].

Diet is indicated as a key factor that can modulate sleep disorders and influence sleep quality [5]. The relationship between diet and sleep quality is explained by factors such as inflammation, oxidative stress, gut microbiome, epigenetic modifications, and the impact of nutrients on cognitive function [7, 8]. Diets rich in foods such as fruits, vegetables, nuts, legumes, whole grains, and olive oil, which have properties that can improve mental health, are also reported to have positive effects on sleep quality [8, 9]. The foods consumed richly in these diets contain rich bioactive components that play an important role in reducing or preventing inflammation that may pose a risk for poor sleep quality [10].

Phytochemicals are plant-based, non-nutritive bioactive compounds, including phenolic compounds (flavonoids, phenolic acids, hydroxycinnamic acids, lignans, tyrosol esters, stilbenoids), isoperenoids, organosulfur compounds (allyl sulfides, isothiocyanate) [11]. Although they are not considered as nutrients, it is estimated that these compounds may have positive effects on sleep disorders due to their antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anti-estrogenic, immunomodulatory, cardioprotective and/or anti-carcinogenic properties [12].

Phytochemicals are notable for their health-protective and inflammation-reducing properties; however, limited information about dietary phytochemical intake and the difficulties in analyzing it make studying these relationships challenging [13]. The concept of ‘phytochemical index’ proposed by McCarty in 2004 is an alternative and practical method for determining dietary phytochemical intake [14]. Defined as the percentage of energy from phytochemical-rich foods, this index is considered an appropriate indicator for assessing the total phytochemical content of the diet despite its limitations. Several epidemiological studies have shown that dietary phytochemical index (DPI) is inversely associated with obesity [15], depression [16], hypertension [17], oxidative stress [13], inflammation [18], cancer [19] and poor mental health [20].

When the literature was examined, no study examining the relationship between DPI and sleep quality was found. Poor sleep quality is an increasing public health concern [1]. Phytochemicals are thought to have potential beneficial effects on sleep quality due to their antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties [12]. In this context, investigating the potential effects of dietary phytochemical intake on sleep quality may provide important scientific insights not only for individual health but also for improving public health at the population level. The aim of this study was to evaluate the relationship between the Dietary Phytochemical Index and sleep quality among university students.

Methods

Study design and sample

The sample of this cross-sectional study consisted of university students aged between 19 and 29 years studying at Ankara University between December 2021 and April 2022. Students of the Department of Nutrition and Dietetics were not included in the study to prevent conflict of interest. The sample size for the study was determined to be at least 400, considering relevant literature [20, 21] with a confidence interval of 95% and a margin of error of 5%, calculated using the G-Power program. Pregnant and lactating women, individuals under 19 and over 29 years of age, those with a daily energy intake below 1000 kcal or above 6000 kcal [22], participants with any acute or chronic diseases such as hypertension, cancer, thyroid disorders, cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, kidney or liver diseases, individuals taking any medication that may affect sleep, those following a special diet, users of dietary supplements containing phytochemicals, participants diagnosed with sleep disorders such as sleep apnea, and shift workers were excluded from the study. Within the scope of the study, 650 university students were reached. However, 221 of them were not included in the study for various reasons (incomplete response to the questionnaire (n = 67), chronic disease (n = 20), energy intake < 1000 kcal (n = 58), age > 29 years (n = 33)). As a result, 472 university students constituted the sample of the study.

The study data were obtained by face-to-face interview using a questionnaire form. The questionnaire form included general information, anthropometric measurements, Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index, International Physical Activity Questionnaire (short form) (IPAQ) and a 24-hour dietary recall. Permission was obtained from Ankara University Ethics Committee (No. 334168) before collecting the study data. All participants gave voluntary consent to the study, which was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

General information

Information on participants’ sex, age, socioeconomic status, smoking, and alcohol consumption was collected in the general information section of the questionnaire.

Anthropometric measurements

As anthropometric measurements, body weight and height measurements of the participants were taken by trained researchers in accordance with the rules. Body Mass Index was calculated using these measurements (Body Mass Index = Weight (kg) / Height (m²)). When categorising the Body Mass Index, according to the classification of the World Health Organization, those below 18.5 kg/m² were classified as underweight, 18.5–24.9 kg/m² as normal body weight, 25.0–29.9 kg/m² as overweight, and 30.0 kg/m² and above as obese [23].

Pittsburgh sleep quality index

Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), developed by Buysse et al. [24] and validated in Turkish by Ağargün et al. [25], was used to determine the sleep quality of the participants. This index is a self-report-based assessment tool that helps to give detailed information about the quality of sleep in the last month and the severity and type of sleep disturbance. The form consists of a total of 18 questions and includes scores for 7 components. These are habitual sleep efficiency, subjective sleep quality, sleep duration, time to fall asleep (sleep latency), daytime dysfunction, sleep medication use and sleep disturbance. Each question is evaluated with a number from 0 to 3. The sum of the scores of the seven components includes the total PSQI score, and the total PSQI score can take a value between 0 and 21. Those with a total score of less than 5 are considered to have ‘good’ sleep quality, while those with a score of 5 and above are considered to have ‘poor’ sleep quality.

International physical activity questionnaire short form

In order to determine the physical activity levels of the participants, the International Physical Activity Questionnaire Short Form (IPAQ) developed by Craig et al. [26] and validated in Turkish by Öztürk [27] was used. In the evaluation of the scale, MET-minute score was calculated after obtaining the information of the physical activities performed in days (frequency) and hours (minutes). MET-minute score is the value obtained from the product of the duration of physical activity and MET score. According to the total MET value, activity levels of < 600, 600–3000, > 3000 MET/min/week were evaluated as low, moderate and high, respectively.

24-hour dietary recall

Dietary intake was assessed using the retrospective 24-hour dietary recall method. The types and amounts of each food and beverage consumed on the previous day were recorded. Portion sizes of consumed foods were determined using the ‘Food and Nutrient Photo Catalog: Measures and Quantities’ catalog [28]. The ‘Standard Meal Recipes in Institutional Food Services’ book was used to identify the content of prepared or out-of-home meals [29]. The average energy and nutrient values of consumed foods were calculated using the Nutrition Information Systems Package (BEBIS) program [30].

Phytochemical index

The ‘Phytochemical Index’ method developed by McCarty was used to determine the total dietary phytochemical intake of individuals [14]. DPI value was calculated as the percentage of energy from foods rich in phytochemicals in daily energy intake [DPI=(daily energy from foods rich in phytochemicals kcal/total energy kcal) × 100]. The category of foods rich in phytochemicals included fruits and vegetables, legumes, whole grains, oilseeds, soya products, olives and olive oil, while potatoes were not considered as vegetables due to their high starch content. At the same time, foods with rich phytochemical content such as 100% natural fruit and vegetable juices and tomato sauce were included in the fruit and vegetable groups.

Statistical analysis

The data obtained from the study were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of the variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov or Shapiro-Wilk tests, histogram curves, coefficient of variation, and skewness and kurtosis values. Normally distributed variables are presented using mean and standard deviation, and non-normally distributed variables are presented using median, minimum and maximum values. In comparing the three groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used when the normal distribution was provided, and the Kruskal-Wallis test was used when it was not. A χ2 test was performed to examine the difference between categorical variables. Dietary intakes across tertiles of DPI were compared using ANCOVA and all values were adjusted for energy intake. Logistic regression was used to estimate OR and 95% CI for sleep quality across tertiles of DPI in crude and multivariable-adjusted models. P < 0·05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Results

In this study, DPI score was organised as an ordinal variable and grouped according to 33rd, 66th and 99th percentile values. Afterwards, the participants were classified according to their DPI scores as < 10.00 first tertile (T1), 10.00–27.14 s tertile (T2), > 27.14 third tertile (T3). The mean DPI score of the participants was 20.45.

General characteristics of the study participants according to DPI tertiles are given in Table 1. Individuals in the lowest DPI tertile were found to have a higher mean age and BMI compared to the other tertiles (p < 0.05). Age, BMI classification, smoking and physical activity status showed a statistically significant difference according to DPI tertiles (p < 0.05). In individuals in the lowest DPI tertile, the frequency of individuals aged ≥ 22 years, overweight, non-smokers and individuals with moderate physical activity was higher (p < 0.05). Morever, the average age and BMI are higher in the lowest DPI tertile (p < 0.05).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the relationship between DPI, an index of the phytochemical content of the diet, and sleep quality. The findings of this study showed that individuals in the highest DPI tertile were significantly more likely to have good sleep quality.

In this study, the differences in daily energy and nutrient intake with diet according to DPI tertiles are noteworthy. McCarty predicted that the DPI value of a typical American diet could not even exceed 20 points [14]. In this study, it was found that some individuals in the second tertile and all individuals in the last tertile had DPI values above 20 points. Similarly, studies conducted in Iran found that only individuals in the first tertile had DPI values below 20 points [20, 31]. This situation may provide insights into the lower consumption of refined grains, added sugar, potato products, and fats compared to Western-style diets in these countries. In this study, individuals in the highest DPI tertile had higher dietary carbohydrate intake, percentage of energy from carbohydrate, total fibre, thiamine, vitamin B6, magnesium and potassium. In contrast, the percentage of dietary fat and energy from fat is lower. Mofrad et al. [20] found that dietary carbohydrate, fiber, magnesium, potassium, and potassium intake were higher and fat was lower in individuals in the highest DPI tertile. In another study, the percentage of dietary energy from carbohydrate, magnesium and potassium intake was higher in individuals in the highest DPI tertile, while energy intake was higher in those in the lowest DPI tertile [31]. Changes in energy and nutrient intake according to DPI classification may be related to differences in the types and amounts of phytochemical-rich foods consumed.

The relationship between the Mediterranean diet, which is rich in phytochemicals, and sleep quality has been investigated in previous studies. In a study conducted in Spain with an average follow-up period of 2.8 years, it was determined that adherence to the Mediterranean diet was associated with less risk of changes in sleep duration and better sleep quality [32]. In another study, adherence to a Mediterranean-style diet was found to be associated with sufficient sleep duration, fewer symptoms of insomnia, and a lower likelihood of insomnia accompanied by short sleep [33]. In a study conducted by Godos et al. [34], individuals with the highest adherence to the Mediterranean diet were found to have a higher frequency of good sleep quality, while the frequency of short sleep duration, long sleep latency, daytime dysfunction due to napping, and very low sleep efficiency was lower.

In this study, total PSQI, sleep quality, habitual sleep efficiency, use of sleeping medication and daytime dysfunction scores were found to be lower in individuals in the highest DPI tertile. The frequency of individuals with sufficient sleep quality was higher in the group of individuals in the highest DPI tertile. The frequency of individuals with good sleep quality is higher in the group with individuals in the highest DPI tertile. Although there are similarities between DPI and the Mediterranean diet adherence scale, the score for adherence to the Mediterranean diet al.so includes questions related to meat and fish consumption, as well as questions about some unhealthy components that are reverse scored [35]. Furthermore, because diets with the same DPI score may contain different phytochemical components, different results could be obtained regarding the relationship between DPI and sleep quality. Therefore, prospective longitudinal studies are needed to investigate the causal relationships between DPI and sleep quality.

Previous studies have investigated sleep quality with polyphenols, a subgroup of phytochemicals. In a study with adult individuals in Italy, a higher intake of certain polyphenols (flavonoid subclasses (flavanones and flavones) and phenolic acids (hydroxycinnamic acids and lignans)) from the diet was found to be associated with better sleep quality [7]. In a study conducted in women in England, it was determined that polyphenol classes taken with fruit and vegetable consumption in the diet were associated with sleep duration [8]. Golmohammad et al. [36] found that individuals in the highest tertile of dietary polyphenol intake had significantly lower PSQI scores. In this study, after adjusting for all confounding factors, individuals in the highest DPI tertile had a 14% lower PSQI score than those in the lowest DPI tertile.

The relationship between DPI and sleep quality can be explained by various possible mechanisms. Sleep is a physiological recovery and process of clearing free radicals. Therefore, sleep disturbances can negatively affect the endocrine system, immune system, and metabolism [37]. Sleep disorders have been reported to lead to increases in various inflammatory markers such as CRP, IL-6, and TNF-α [38]. On the other hand, inflammation can lead to negative outcomes related to mental health, exacerbating poor sleep quality [39]. Foods rich in phytochemicals have been reported to reduce neuronal damage caused by oxidative stress and conditions related to inflammation [40]. Apigenin and hydroxycinnamic acid have been reported to enhance cellular antioxidant activity against oxidative stress in the central nervous system [41, 42]. Lignans have been reported to protect the blood–brain barrier against inflammatory cells by reducing oxidative stress, inflammation, and permeability [43, 44]. In addition, some polyphenols have been reported to influence sleep by modulating neurotransmitter systems [45, 46]. Ferulic acid, a derivative of hydroxycinnamic acid, has been reported to exert sedative effects by interacting with the GABAergic and serotonergic systems, shortening sleep onset latency and prolonging sleep duration [45]. Similarly, rosmarinic acid has been shown to facilitate sleep initiation by inhibiting the enzyme GABA transaminase, thereby enhancing GABAergic activity [46]. Furthermore, polyphenols have been suggested to play a role in regulating sleep patterns and quality by modulating tryptophan metabolism and serotonin production through their effects on the gut microbiota [47]. The microbiota–gut–brain axis enables bidirectional communication via neurological, endocrine, and inflammatory pathways. Particularly in cases of dysbiosis, modulation of the gut through dietary polyphenols may influence mental health outcomes, including sleep disturbances [7, 48].

Strengths

This study has several strengths. To the best of our knowledge, it is one of the first to examine the relationship between the DPI, which reflects the phytochemical content of the diet, and sleep quality. Assessing the associations of DPI not only with the total PSQI score but also with its individual components allowed for a comprehensive evaluation of different aspects of sleep quality. The findings highlight a significant association between DPI and sleep quality, contributing novel insights to the existing literature in this field.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. Firstly, DPI is calculated according to the amount of energy from foods rich in phytochemicals. Therefore, the impact of foods that are high in phytochemical content but do not contribute significantly to energy intake, such as tea, coffee, tomato paste, garlic, and onions, which are consumed in large quantities in Turkey, not being reflected in this index may have affected the results. Secondly, since the DPI score is not specific according to phytochemical components, it is thought that two diets with the same DPI score may have different health effects with different phytochemical component contents. Third, different cooking techniques and food consumption patterns may change the phytochemical content in foods. Fourth, the cross-sectional nature of the study limits the establishment of a cause and effect relationship between variables Fifth, since this study was conducted with individuals aged 19–29 years, it may not be generalised to a wider age range. Aging may modify the relationship between diet and sleep quality. Therefore, investigating the association between DPI and sleep quality in older age groups is important for future research. Finally, dietary intake was assessed using a single 24-hour dietary recall. Although this method is widely used in cross-sectional studies due to its practicality, it may limit the ability to capture long-term dietary behaviors. Moreover, considering that the PSQI evaluates sleep quality over the past month, this temporal mismatch should be taken into account when interpreting the relationship between diet and sleep quality. Future studies using multiple-day dietary assessments are recommended to provide a more comprehensive understanding of this relationship.

Conclusion

In conclusion, in this study, total PSQI, sleep quality, habitual sleep efficiency, use of sleeping medication, and daytime dysfunction scores were lower in those in the highest DPI quartile. Those in the highest DPI tertile were more likely to have good sleep quality. Therefore, increasing dietary phytochemical intake may be beneficial to improve sleep quality. Longitudinal studies with large samples are needed to confirm the relationship between DPI and sleep quality.

These results also carry potential implications for public health strategies. In particular, promoting the consumption of phytochemical-rich foods such as fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and legumes with high polyphenol content may serve as an effective approach to improving sleep health at the population level. Emphasizing these components more prominently in dietary guidelines could contribute not only to enhanced sleep quality but also to overall improvements in quality of life.
