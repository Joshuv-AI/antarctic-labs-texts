Health care assistants in out of hours community palliative care: multiple qualitative organisational case studies
Authors: Felicity Hasson, Sonja McIlfatrick, Sheila Payne, Anne Fee, Paul Slater, Christine Shannon, Dori-Anne Finlay, Tracey McConnell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGlobally, the role, title and preparation of the unregulated worker is unstandardized, yet increasing evidence suggests they play an important role in the provision of palliative care. In the United Kingdom, a Health Care Assistant (HCA) is an unregulated healthcare worker who provides support to patients under the supervision of registered professionals across clinical settings. Whilst the provision of hospice out-of-hours (OOH) community palliative care is considered a key priority internationally; little is known about the role and contribution of the HCA in the provision of such care.AimThis study aimed to understand the HCA’s role, responsibilities, and contribution to out-of-hours community palliative care.MethodsAn organisational qualitative case study approach was adopted, studying six out-of-hours palliative care service models across the UK. Data collection used interviews from multiple perspectives (carers, managers, specialist nurses, and HCAs). In total, 59 semi-structured interviews were held between 2021 and 2022. Framework analysis was adopted to analyse the interviews.ResultsTwo overarching themes were identified, relating to service and role complexity and impact of the HCA within OOH community palliative care. Both illustrate the variable environment of OOH community palliative care within which the role of the Health Care Assistants operates. Health Care Assistants were found to provide care for the dying patient and psychosocial support for wider family members. They were often the first to detect changes in patient conditions and played a key role, with recognising, responding and escalating care. Participants believed this helped to contribute to patients remaining in the home environment.ConclusionUnderstanding both the nature of out of hours palliative care delivery and the work delivered by Health Care Assistants, is key to developing organisational structures that support patient care. Further studies to better understand how modification of these factors can be used to enhance care delivery and the effectiveness of the Health Care Assistants role in palliative care are warranted given the limited evidence base.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12904-026-02057-5.

Introduction

Globally healthcare systems have faced staff shortages in the face of rapidly aging populations, which has seen an increasing reliance on the role of the unregulated health care worker [1–3]. Internationally the role has evolved from supportive, to encompass traditionally complex nursing responsibilities [4–6]. However, there is no universal standard to licencing or regulation and no consensus regarding the title, remit, or preparation for this role internationally [4, 7]. The designation for the healthcare assistant role varies across countries, ranging from nurse care workers in China [8] to personal care assistants and nursing assistants in Australia [9]. Throughout Europe, a variety of titles is in use, yet the term Health Care Assistant (HCA) is most frequently applied [10].

In the United Kingdom (UK), the HCA title is used to refer to the unregulated worker. Statutory regulation, however, overseen by the Care Quality Commission, an independent regulator for the quality and safety of care, does not exist. Despite a reported lack of recognition as a team member [11, 12], HCAs play an integral role across a range of care settings, including home-based community palliative care [13–15]. In the UK, community palliative care refers to care being delivered outside of the hospital and outpatient settings, such as a patients’ home [16].

Within palliative care, most research on the HCA role has focused on the community, suggesting that they provide most of the direct patient care [17], and are often the first to recognise and alert specialists to patient changes [18, 19]. Research suggests they can make highly accurate observations of less conspicuous changes to the patient’s condition, allowing them to predict a transition towards the end of life [20] up to, and at the point of death of the patient [21, 22]. Tending to develop more of a ‘friendship’ with patients and family caregivers [18], they have been recognised as playing a critical role in the end-of-life discussions and the provision of psychosocial support [23, 24]. This proximity also increases their exposure to the emotional intensity of witnessing patient suffering, decline, and family distress. Such repeated exposure places HCAs at risk of experiencing secondary traumatic stress and vicarious trauma, particularly in palliative and end-of-life settings where death, loss, and anticipatory grief are routine [25–27]. However, whilst evidence suggests HCAs are an important component of the OOH community palliative care workforce [28], their role and contribution, is relatively unknown [29].

Out-of-hours care provision across the world is variable, in the UK it refers to the period between 18.30 and 08.00 on weekdays and across the weekends and bank holidays. OOH represents 63% of the week when normal in-hour primary care services are not available [30, 31]. Given that many palliative care patients wish to be cared for, and to die, in their home [32, 33], OOH has been identified this as a key research and policy priority both in the UK and Ireland [34, 35].

Whilst similarities exist between HCAs working in inpatient settings and the patient’s own home in the OOH period, there are key distinctions. OOH community palliative care delivery faces unique challenges such as unpredictable home environments, geographical variation of services, co-ordination difficulties between services; variable training in end-of-life practices; as well as balancing the wishes of the patient and family [31, 36]. In the OOH period, palliative care patients have been reported to face an increased risk of harm, particularly related to assessment and management of symptom control [37]. Finally, HCAs in inpatient settings, are directly supervised and work alongside members of the multi-disciplinary team, thus ensuring access to continuous specialist support. Whereas HCAs in the OOH period tend to be lone, independent workers with supervision delivered at a distance, leaving HCAs having to deal with highly variable and changing environments [7, 38].

Key challenges associated with OOH community palliative care provision are the identification of symptom complexity and prognostic judgements as well as the timely escalation of concerns [31, 39]. In the UK, in the OOH period, a regulated health professional such as District Nurse (DN) or specialist practitioner often administers medication, however the administration can be severely delayed by travel time, and/or unavailability of anticipatory medication in the home, even with dedicated OOH community ‘rapid response’ nursing services [40]. Long waits may mean that symptoms are not adequately managed in the home setting [41] however, it also means that staff, potentially HCAs, are placed in a demanding position to manage these situations whilst being exposed to a variety of emotions and stresses [42–44].

Understanding the responsibilities of HCAs as team members is vital in improving multidisciplinary collaboration and efficient care delivery [1]. This study seeks to understand HCAs role and responsibilities and provide a clearer understanding of their contribution in OOH community palliative care.

Methods

Study design and setting

Six organisational case studies [45], across adult hospices located within the UK were used to examine the role and contribution of the HCAs in OOH community hospice care. The presence of `HCAs in each hospice service’ was determined as `the case.’ From a previous national survey [28] of adult hospices providing OOH community palliative care, four providers opted to be included who met inclusion criteria (see Table 1) [46]. OOH service types included multi visiting (n = 1), Planned Variable (n = 1), Rapid Response (n = 1) and Hospice at Home (n = 3). The OOH service, size and composition of the teams varied, however all employed HCAs in care delivery. A multiple organisational case study design enabled comparison to be made across several cases to help to “generate in-depth, multi-faceted understanding of a complex issues in its real-life context” [47, p1]. Appropriate to palliative care contexts [48, 49], analysis was ‘embedded’ within several sources of data (HCAs, specialists nurses, family carers and managers) from within and across the case study sites. To ensure rigour in reporting, COREQ guidelines were adhered to [50] (Supplementary file 2).

Inclusion criteria

Whilst attempts were made to recruit patients, the implications of the COVID-19 pandemic—particularly the increase in late referrals, which resulted in patients being too unwell to participate in the OOH service—meant that this was not achieved.

Data collection

In this study, data collection methods included semi-structured one-to-one online interviews, and documentary analysis. In total, 59 online interviews were undertaken with administrators/managers (n = 8), HCAs (n = 24), registered nurses (n = 16), active carers (n = 6), and bereaved carers (n = 5). All participants were provided with study information, which outlined the nature of the study and provided written consent. A semi-structured interview guide oriented to the research aim and analysis of data from previous phases of the study [28, 29, 51] was developed. The interview guides were flexible to elicit participants’ backgrounds and enable them to describe their experiences of OOH community palliative care provision (see supplementary file 1). Interviews were digitally recorded (with written consent) and lasted between 18 and 67 min, undertaken by three female investigators. Investigators included two researchers (AF, CS) and one senior academic (FH), all trained in qualitative research and recorded field notes after each interview.

Although the data collectors were not clinically trained, their prior hospice interview experience supported sensitive engagement, and reflexive practices were used to minimise potential bias. Data were collected between July 2021 – April 2022. All interviews were transcribed verbatim. Data saturation was reached by ongoing analysis during data collection, as successive interviews confirmed existing themes without contributing novel information. Case study sites were offered an amount of £1000 each for participation in interviews, all hospices decided to offer a proportion of this to staff as gift vouchers.

Data analysis

Data were subject to a framework approach to analysis to enable comparison to be undertaken across and within cases [52]. Transcripts were checked against audio recordings to ensure accuracy and then read and re-read for familiarisation. Following this, an initial coding framework was developed because of line-by-line coding; and data were imported into ‘NVivo 12’ qualitative data analysis software [53] for management. From this process, an initial thematic framework was developed to capture concepts relevant to the HCA role and contribution in out-of-hours community palliative care. Transcripts were then indexed using this framework. Using this approach, codes and themes that were relevant for the HCA role and contribution in OOH community palliative care were identified and compared across cases to identify relevant contextual factors.

The research team (FH; AF & CS) collaboratively refined and agreed the coding framework; any discrepancies or disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus. Indexed data were subsequently charted into framework matrices to support systematic comparison. Within-case analysis enabled in-depth understanding of each case, while cross-case analysis facilitated the identification of cross-cutting themes and contextual influences on the HCA role [49]. Through an iterative process of mapping and interpretation, two overarching themes were developed and refined. Direct quotations are presented to illustrate and reflect typical participant experiences.

Rigour

Rigour was assured in line with the principles of trustworthiness [54]. To enhance confirmability, interview transcripts were repeatedly read and independently reviewed by three members of the research team, supporting transparency and reducing individual researcher bias. Credibility was strengthened by recruiting participants who had direct experience of the phenomenon under investigation and by using an agreed and consistently applied interview schedule. Dependability was supported through maintaining a clear audit trail of analytic decisions, and transferability was facilitated by providing detailed descriptions of the study context and participant characteristics.

Theme: service and role complexity

The ability to support the patient and manage symptoms to enable them to stay at home was considered a fundamental role of OOH hospice community palliative care provision, across all sites. It was recognised that to achieve delivery of such care, the co-ordination of other services across acute, community and social services was vital. However, participants identified contrasts between OOH community palliative care and in-hours practices, which provided specific differences and challenges such as accessibility of services, medication, and availability of staff.“I know that we have a thing that you can ring and talk or an on-call Manager for out of hours, but you are ringing someone from Wales, who doesn’t even know where (place name) is, they don’t know your stuck on top of a Mountain, you know”. Whilst all OOH staff played a role in the delivery of care, there were notable differences in the role and availability between specialist nurses and HCA staff in the OOH period. Most specialist nursing staff, particularly those delivering rapid response or supporting hospice at home services commented on the pressures they encountered. These included rising demands for palliative care, increased complexity of care, whilst balancing staff shortages. These, combined with uncertainty surrounding visit and travel times between homes, accumulated in greater demands on their role. As illustrated:“So, out of hours, they are kind of a very precious resource, because there is less availability of staff to do that, but with more patients with complex conditions. Demand is rising”. HCAs were recognised by managers and registered nurses as being integral to the delivery of OOH hospice community palliative care. Unlike registered specialist healthcare staff who have undertaken additional specialist education in palliative care, HCAs reported spending the greatest amount of time with patients and their family caregivers. This enabled HCAs to develop detailed knowledge of patients’ care needs, routines and patterns of behaviour which was then used to support the delivery of care. Furthermore, it enabled HCAs to build rapport and trusting relationships with patients and families. Active and bereaved carers commented on the importance of having this time with one staff member to allow for individualised care of the patient, respite to be provided and a safe platform in which their worries, fears and wishes could be expressed. Several active and bereaved carers reported that, once the patient was settled, they had time with HCAs, to discuss their own issues surrounding death, dying and bereavement.

HCAs initially described their role as including tasks such as mobilisation, personal hygiene, infection control, monitoring equipment (i.e., syringe drivers), care after death (i.e., washing, positioning, and dressing the body, and tending to any medical equipment) and honouring the spiritual and cultural wishes of the deceased person and their family. They also reflected that they played a key role in educating, signposting, and providing emotional support to patients and family carers; and managing conflicts and grief within the wider family. Combined with the routine activities described above, HCAs played a key role in assessing signs and symptoms (such as constipation, breathing, delirium, pain, nausea, and fatigue), that suggest physical and/or emotional rapid decline. No HCAs mentioned the use of assessment tools to guide decisions or actions, rather they continually observed behavioural, verbal, and nonverbal cues to guide their reactions.“Sometimes people can’t tell you that they’re in pain sometimes, so you have to be aware of that. Sweating sometimes, uncomfortableness, just their breathing as well, you know. Within Hospice at Home services HCAs played a significant role in managing pain. Initially they provided reassurance and repositioning to alleviate pain. It was recognised by managers, HCAs, and registered nurses that that the onus of responsibility for assessing and escalating concerns mainly resided with HCAs.

Given that medication administration was outside their scope of practice, HCAs reported they had to prompt families for pain relief. This frequently required them to wake exhausted family members to ask them to administer pain relief. Some HCAs voiced relief that they were not responsible for directly administering medication, whilst others expressed frustration and questioned the implications of this on patients, families, perceptions of their role and the service. Some openly questioned such restrictions, viewing this as poor practice and highlighted this as different to other settings, such as nursing homes, where they had delegated authority for the administration of medications.

Theme: Impact of HCAs within OOH care

Across all sites, it was reported that OOH community palliative hospice care was integral to the care of patients and their families, in the community. Several examples of the impact of OOH community palliative care on the patient outcomes were also cited including helping to prevent hospital admissions, reducing the risk of admission to residential care, improved wellbeing, and reduction in social isolation. Combined, it was recognised by many participants that OOH community palliative care helped to reduce pressure on other NHS resources. However, all participants believed that the help received enabled the patient to continue to live and fulfil their wishes to remain and to die at home.

Discussion

The study highlights the importance of the HCA role, against the backdrop of the complexity and challenges of providing OOH community palliative care. Despite OOH community palliative care being advocated [34, 35], increasing demand, combined with managing uncertainties and limited resource availability, ultimately place pressure on its delivery and the workforce.

Consistent with other studies [7, 24, 29], the result of this study indicates that that HCAs are frontline staff in OOH community palliative care, providing an array of physical and emotional support to both the patient and the caregiver. Their role was viewed as integral to alleviating carer stress and enabling the patient to fulfil their wishes to remain, and to die, at home. The findings of the present study are like previous research, especially regarding the hidden complexity and ever evolving role of the HCA in healthcare [5, 55, 56]. Reflecting previous research [17, 20, 22, 23], it is the HCA who are spending a considerable amount of time with patients and carers and hence are the first to notice changes that signify the need for a response in community palliative care. In the out-of-hours (OOH) period, findings indicate the complexity of care is heightened by the inherent unpredictability of OOH environments, combined with limited resources and increasingly overburdened services and staff [57]. This is further compounded by the tendency for HCAs to work as lone, independent practitioners with minimal access to clinical or peer supervision [7]. Additional challenges arise from reduced accessibility to other key resources—such as clinicians, specialist advice, and timely medication—during the OOH period. These findings underscore the need for improved access to resources for staff working during the out-of-hours period, to ensure safe and responsive palliative care delivery.

Data in this study adds to the evidence base [20], demonstrating that HCAs are being placed in increasingly challenging situations, requiring them to assume the responsibility of assessing and monitoring patients and caregiver status, that previously would have been the remit of registered professionals. Findings indicate that HCAs in the OOH period, were often solely responsible for observing, understanding, and interpreting vital signs and escalating care appropriately. However, escalation of care was complex, due to anxiety about the legitimacy of need and a reluctance to bother the family, brokered with the realisation of potential delays in patients being able to access specialist care from services such as rapid response teams. All staff (regulated and unregulated) expressed concern about delivering good palliative within the wider constraints of the health care system and specifically regarding access to specialist palliative care support in the OOH period. These findings highlight the need for clearly defined clinical support pathways and decision-making protocols for OOH staff.

This raises questions about patient safety and the quality of care provided. Moreover, the impact on family carers—who witness the patient’s physical and cognitive decline while experiencing delays in accessing assistance—requires careful consideration, as does the effect on the OOH community palliative care workforce. In this study, the data reinforce that engagement with dying patients and distressed family members has a direct impact on HCA wellbeing, echoing findings [7, 43, 44]. Such work involves considerable emotional labour, as HCAs must manage and display appropriate emotions in highly charged situations. Additionally, instances where HCAs recognised the need for action but were constrained by limited authority or resources point towards moral distress, reflecting tensions between knowing what is ethically required and being unable to act. Compounding these pressures, previous nursing literature identifies non-clinical staff as being at risk of vicarious trauma due to repeated exposure to distressing or traumatic events [25–27], which can result in longer-term psychological consequences [27]. Combined, this evidence suggests that organisational improvements are needed. Although the HCA role will continue to evolve, McPherson et al. [23] caution that such evolution must not result in unregulated workers absorbing responsibilities beyond their scope. Strengthening support structures and role clarity is therefore essential to safeguard both workforce wellbeing and the quality of OOH palliative care.

Strengths & limitations

Our study has several notable strengths. To our knowledge, it is the first to examine the role of the HCA in delivering out-of-hours (OOHs) community palliative care. It was informed by a large and diverse sample of participants with substantial experience across multiple levels of seniority, representing perspectives from a wide range of services. This breadth of insight strengthens the credibility of the conclusions and offers a nuanced understanding of the complexities inherent in providing palliative care outside normal working hours.

This study has however several limitations. First, the findings are based on a mainly white purposive sample, which, although appropriate for qualitative inquiry, may not fully capture the breadth of experiences across all settings or staff groups. The perspectives of participants who chose not to take part remain unknown, and it is possible that those who participated had particularly strong views or experiences that influenced the themes identified. In addition, recruitment challenges—particularly those arising from the COVID-19 pandemic, increased service pressures, and wider contextual constraints—limited the inclusion of patients in this study. Second, the study was conducted within a specific organisational and geographical context, which may influence the transferability of the findings to other palliative care services, especially those operating with different staffing configurations, referral pathways, or out-of-hours infrastructures. Four of the services are in Northern Ireland which operates a different health and social care system than the rest of the UK.

Conclusion

This study highlights the complexity of out-of-hours (OOH) community palliative care and the broad, evolving role of unregulated HCAs within this context. HCAs provide key support to patients, carers, and wider OOH teams despite limited resources, high service demand, and minimal supervision. They frequently witness significant patient and family distress, contributing to a notable emotional burden that requires organisational recognition and support to protect staff wellbeing and care quality. The findings add to growing evidence calling for a regulatory framework for HCAs and emphasise the need to better understand OOH palliative care delivery. Further research is recommended to explore the changing nature of HCA work and its implications for safe, sustainable care.
