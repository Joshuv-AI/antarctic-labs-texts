Effect of resistance training combined with carbohydrate and protein supplementation on the HOMA-IR, glycemic, lipid profile and hypertrophy of older adults with Type II Diabetes: secondary data analysis of a triple-blind RCT
Authors: Juliana Leme Rosa, Matheus Henrique dos Santos Lino, Marcus Vinicius Grecco, Adriana Machado Saldiba de Lima, Joselma Rodrigues dos Santos, Vanderlei Carneiro da Silva, Julia Maria D’Andréa Greve, Félix Salvador Chavane, Guilherme Eustáquio Furtado, Angelica Castilho Alonso
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionType 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a prevalent chronic disease associated with major health complications, including cardiovascular risk.ObjectiveTo evaluate the effects of resistance training (RT) combined with protein or carbohydrate supplementation on muscle strength, hypertrophy, and glycemic and lipid profiles in older adults with T2DM.MethodsA triple-blind randomized clinical trial was conducted with 60 men with T2DM, allocated into three groups: Protein Group (20 g whey protein), Maltodextrin Group (20 g maltodextrin), and Control Group (colored water). All participants completed a 12-week RT program performed twice weekly. Each session included six exercises, with three sets of 8–12 repetitions, guided by subjective perception of effort. Muscle strength progression was monitored through training loads. Glycemic response, HOMA-IR, and lipid profile were analyzed, while muscle thickness of the biceps brachii and vastus lateralis was measured using ultrasound.ResultsThe Protein Group showed a significant reduction in HOMA-IR (p = 0.03), indicating improved insulin resistance. Lipid profile variables showed no significant changes across groups. Most exercises demonstrated significant load progression, except rowing and standing curls. Muscle thickness did not show significant differences in either muscle evaluated. RT improved muscle strength regardless of supplementation type but did not produce significant hypertrophy. Protein supplementation significantly reduced HOMA-IR, indicating early improvements in insulin sensitivity, whereas carbohydrate supplementation showed no metabolic effect. Lipid profile parameters remained unchanged, and no significant changes were observed in HbA1c or fasting glycemia.Keywords: Older adults, Resistance Training, Type 2 Diabetes, Supplementation, Glycemia

Introduction

Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM) is a metabolic condition characterized by an imbalance in glucose levels, with multifactorial causes including obesity, physical inactivity, insulin resistance, genetic factors, and aging [1]. The latter is not a direct cause of T2DM but is associated with an increased predisposition to the disease due to a series of factors that tend to accumulate with age, such as greater insulin resistance, metabolic changes, accumulation of body fat, physical inactivity, and hormonal changes [2]. This disease is becoming a global epidemic, with projections indicating that by 2045, 783 million people will be affected, representing 12.2% of the world’s population. Factors such as social inequality, urbanization, family history, sedentary lifestyle, and hypertension contribute to this high prevalence, accounting for 90% of cases [3].

The disease represents a significant public health challenge, leading to substantial costs for healthcare systems [4]. Older adults living with T2DM experience a decline in muscle mass (sarcopenia) that is two to four times greater compared to those without the disease [5]. This occurs because muscle tissue plays an essential role in the absorption and utilization of blood glucose [6]. With the muscle loss associated with aging and the presence of T2DM, the body’s ability to effectively absorb glucose is compromised, leading to elevated blood glucose levels [7]. Both aging and T2DM are associated with increased inflammation and oxidative stress, which contribute to muscle loss and deterioration of muscle function [8]. This also impairs the body’s ability to properly metabolize fatty acids, resulting in fat accumulation and a higher cardiovascular risk [9].

Muscle contraction plays an important role in glucose elimination, demonstrating anti-inflammatory activity, improving glucose uptake, fatty acid oxidation, and promoting mitochondrial biogenesis. Muscle tissue is the primary site for postprandial glucose disposal [10]. In T2DM, the ability to restore postprandial blood glucose levels decreases, with low protein concentration impairing muscle contraction capacity, glucose transport, and mitochondrial proteins, leading to an imbalance between protein degradation and synthesis [11].

Regular physical exercise, especially resistance training (RT) or combined exercise, has proven effective in improving muscle function, cardiovascular risk management, and glycemic control in older adults living with T2DM [11, 12]. Different types of exercise have been shown to significantly improve glycemic control and cardiovascular risk factors in individuals with type 2 diabetes. Supervised aerobic and RT result in significant reductions in HbA1c when compared to no exercise, with combined exercise producing even greater reductions ranging from 0.17% to 0.23%. Additionally, supervised RT has been associated with improvements in total cholesterol, a reduction in systolic blood pressure of 3.90 mmHg, and a decrease in total cholesterol of 22.08 mg/dL [13].

In addition to the beneficial effects described above, RT reduces inflammatory and oxidative stress effects, decreases adiposity and lipotoxicity in tissues, including pancreatic beta cells, and lowers inflammation markers in the body [14], such as pro-inflammatory cytokines like TNF-α, IL-6, and C-reactive protein (CRP), thereby reducing chronic inflammation [15]. It also stimulates the secretion of antioxidant peptides, which mitigate oxidative damage and reduce oxidative stress in pancreatic cells, improving insulin function and aiding glucose metabolism [16]. However, it is important to emphasize that the reduction of glucotoxicity and lipotoxicity is best observed when combined with a balanced diet and the adoption of healthy lifestyle habits [17].

Considering the consensus on a healthy diet combined with physical exercise to achieve better quality of life, carbohydrates stand out, breaking the idea that they are strictly contraindicated for individuals with diabetes [18]. This notion is not entirely accurate. The dietary approach for people with diabetes has progressed over time, focusing more on the quality of carbohydrates and portion control rather than total restriction. Carbohydrates play a key role in regulating glucose metabolism and physical performance [18]. Consuming adequate amounts of carbohydrates can improve monosaccharide oxidation and postprandial protein absorption. Furthermore, they influence the release of incretin hormones, such as GLP and GLP-1, which play a role in pancreatic insulin secretion and appetite regulation [19]. This can contribute to better glycemic control and muscle function in older adults with type 2 diabetes.

Previous studies have demonstrated that exercise training combined with nutritional supplementation can modulate adipokines, inflammatory markers, and cardiometabolic risk factors in populations with obesity or metabolic dysfunction, suggesting potential synergistic effects between physical training and bioactive compounds [20–22]. However, most of these investigations have been conducted in younger or non-diabetic populations, and evidence in older adults with T2DM remains scarce.

The effects of RT combined with protein and carbohydrate supplementation within a balanced diet appears to be a promising non-pharmacological strategy for managing individuals with T2DM. However, current evidence is insufficient to determine the optimal supplementation protocol, and existing studies show only trends rather than consistent benefits [23–25]. Therefore, further well-designed research is needed. This study aimed to evaluate the effects of maltodextrin and whey protein supplementation combined with RT on the lipid and glycemic profiles of older adults with T2DM, hypothesizing that the combined intervention would improve both glycemic control and lipid profile.

Methods

This study is based on secondary data from a previously conducted randomized clinical trial approved by the Ethics Committee of São Judas Tadeu University (protocol #7.480.364). The trial was carried out at São Judas Tadeu University in collaboration with the Movement Study Laboratory (LEM) of the Institute of Orthopedics and Traumatology (IOT) at the Hospital das Clínicas, Faculty of Medicine, University of São Paulo (FMUSP). The study is also registered in the Brazilian Clinical Trials Registry (ReBEC) under the identifier [RBR-2m7w87r].

Participants

The population of the present study consisted of 60 male older adults diagnosed with T2DM, aged between 65 and 79 years. Participants were recruited through social media channels, WhatsApp groups, and senior citizen groups associated with the Movement Study Laboratory (LEM). They were categorized into three groups: (a) protein group, (b) maltodextrin (carbohydrate) group, and (c) placebo group without supplement intake. The sample size was determined based on a pilot study involving five older adults with T2DM. The chosen outcome variable was the strength of the dominant quadriceps muscle. The following two-tailed hypotheses were adopted: alpha (Type I error probability) of 5%; beta and test power of 80%. Based on these values, 13 participants were considered for each group.

The following inclusion criteria were adopted: individuals with a stable dose of medication for T2DM (oral antidiabetics, insulin, or a combination of both) for three months or more; renal function assessed by the Modification of Diet in Renal Disease (MDRD) above 60 ml/h; aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels up to 2.5 times the upper limit of the method; absence of musculoskeletal impairments, debilitating diseases, or previous surgeries that could prevent the performance of exercises; absence of untreated or decompensated chronic non-communicable diseases, especially arterial hypertension. Exclusion criteria included participants unable to undergo evaluation and reevaluation or with attendance below 85%.

Instruments and procedures

All participants signed an informed consent form, completed a questionnaire containing sociodemographic information and medical history, and were evaluated by a physician to confirm eligibility according to the inclusion criteria. All assessments were performed at baseline and at the end of the study.

A 20 mL peripheral blood sample was collected on a separate scheduled day after an 8–12 h overnight fast using EDTA-containing tubes. Plasma obtained from EDTA-treated whole blood was used to assess glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) and the circulating lipid profile, including non-HDL cholesterol, HDL, LDL, total cholesterol, triglycerides, and VLDL, using commercial kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Labtest, Minas Gerais, Brazil). The thickness of the biceps brachii and vastus lateralis muscles was assessed using a portable B-mode ultrasound device (DP 10, Mindray, Shenzhen, China) with a 10 MHz frequency probe. Muscle thickness was defined as the distance between the muscle tissue and the subcutaneous fat interface (i.e., aponeuroses) to the corresponding bone. Images were obtained 48–72 h before the start of the study and 48–72 h after the final training session to mitigate muscle swelling caused by training.

To begin the RT, a 1-Repetition Maximum test (1RM) was conducted to assess maximum muscle strength in specific exercises [27]. In this study, the exercises included leg press, calf raise, leg curls, chest press, abdominal crunch, and rowing. The test began with a warm-up, followed by a gradual increase in load, performing single repetitions until failure, and recording the heaviest weight lifted with proper form.

After the test, 70% of the 1RM was calculated for the initial training load. However, load progression was guided by the OMNI Resistance Exercise scale, as it has good validity for controlling intensity in RT [28]. The scale was explained in detail to the participants and applied at the end of each set. If participants were able to perform more than 12 repetitions in two consecutive training sessions, the weight was adjusted following the guidelines of the American College of Sports Medicine position stand [12]. The load values were recorded individually in all sessions to track progress over the three-month period.

Dietary assessment

Dietary intake was evaluated using multiple 24-hour dietary recalls administered at two time points. Three recalls were collected before the initiation of the resistance training program and three additional recalls were obtained at the end of the intervention period. The recalls were conducted on non-consecutive days, including both weekdays and weekend days, in order to capture habitual intake variability. All interviews were performed by trained professionals using standardized procedures.

Reported food items and portion sizes were entered into the DIETPRO software, which converted household measures into quantitative estimates of daily intake. The analysis focused on total energy intake (kcal/day), carbohydrate intake (g/day), and protein intake (g/kg/day).

Randomization, allocation, blinding, and follow-up losses

Group allocation followed a stratified, randomized numerical sequence generated before the first assignment. The study included three parallel groups: control (water with edible dye), carbohydrate supplementation (maltodextrin), and protein supplementation (whey protein), all undergoing the same RT protocol. An independent researcher generated the random sequence in variable-sized blocks and stored it in opaque, sequentially numbered envelopes, inaccessible to blinded evaluators. Another independent researcher, also blinded to group codes, performed the allocation.

The study was triple-blind: the evaluators, the intervention researcher, and the participants were unaware of the assigned supplementation. A nutritionist administered the supplements and was the only person who knew each participant’s group. Data tabulation and statistical analysis were performed by a separate blinded researcher.

Losses during follow-up were handled using the intention-to-treat approach, analyzing participants according to their original allocation. In the maltodextrin group, three participants were lost; in the protein group, four; and in the control group, four, due to health issues, withdrawals, work commitments, or dissatisfaction with training (Fig. 1).

All participants received nutritional guidance to standardize daily protein intake throughout the experimental protocol period (1.0 to 1.2 g of protein/kg per day). According to the recommendation of the PROT-AG Study Group, older adults (> 65 years) require higher protein intake compared to younger adults [29]. Four dietary recalls (24 h and 48 h) were conducted by nutritionists to guide the older adults in maintaining their regular eating routine during the study period.

Supplementation

Daily supplementation was administered by nutritionists after the training sessions. The supplements were provided immediately after strength training, according to the group, through the intake of 20 g of whey protein diluted in water or 20 g of maltodextrin. Finally, the control group received a cup of water mixed with food coloring of the same color as the other supplements. This mixture was prepared immediately after the strength training session.

Resistance training

The RT sessions were conducted twice a week for 12 weeks. The exercise protocol targeted major muscle groups: machine bench press, articulated rowing, leg press, standing knee flexion, calf raises, and machine abdominal exercises. Each exercise consisted of 3 sets of 8 to 12 repetitions. The intensity ranged between 7 and 8 according to the Subjective Perception of Effort (SPE) using the OMNI scale from 0 to 10. The rest interval between sets and exercises was one to two minutes. Following previous studies, all sessions were directly supervised by a trained and experienced professional in resistance exercise prescription [18, 30].

Statistical analysis

The data were stored and analyzed using the Jamovi. The descriptive analysis of the sample was performed using mean and standard deviation. Qualitative variables were presented in terms of their absolute and relative values. To assess the association between qualitative variables, the Chi-square test and/or Fisher’s exact test were used. Normality and homogeneity of variances, as well as adherence to the Gaussian curve, were assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk and Levene tests, respectively. All data were analyzed using a generalized linear mixed model (GLMM), with group as random effects, time as repeated measures, and group-by-time interactions as fixed effects. No data imputation was performed, as the GLMM can handle missing data. The accepted level of significance was set at 5% (p ≤ 0.05).

Results

Most volunteers did not have cardiovascular or coronary disease, nor conditions related to T2DM, such as retinopathy, neuropathy, or altered foot sensitivity. They were aged between 69.5 and 71.6 years, with blood pressure ranging from 120/76 to 129/81 mmHg. Additionally, they had been living with the disease for over 13 years. More than half of the participants engaged in some type of physical activity before the study, particularly walking. However, none were practicing RT. Most were former smokers, consumed alcohol, were of white ethnicity, married, had higher education levels, and an income between 2 and 5 minimum wages. They used hyperglycemic agents from the sulfonylurea class and others, as well as antihypertensive and hypoglycemic agents (dyslipidemia) (Table 1).

Discussion

The main findings of this study indicate that the addition of whey protein or maltodextrin supplementation did not promote further improvements in muscle strength or hypertrophy in older adults living with T2DM. However, a significant improvement in insulin resistance was observed in the protein-supplemented group, while glycemic control and cardiometabolic risk markers remained unchanged.

HOMA-IR reflects hepatic insulin resistance and is particularly sensitive to early changes in insulin sensitivity, whereas HbA1c represents longer-term glycemic exposure over approximately 8–12 weeks. Baseline HbA1c values were relatively controlled, and participants were under stable pharmacological treatment, which may have limited the magnitude of detectable changes.

From a clinical perspective, the significant reduction in HOMA-IR observed in older adults with long-standing T2DM is relevant, as it suggests that resistance training combined with whey protein supplementation may promote improvements in insulin sensitivity even in individuals with established metabolic dysfunction. Considering that participants had been living with the disease for more than 13 years, were under stable pharmacological treatment, and presented relatively controlled HbA1c levels, the detection of measurable changes in insulin resistance represents an important metabolic response in a population typically characterized by progressive metabolic decline [31].

HOMA-IR is sensitive to early alterations in insulin metabolism and may reflect improvements in hepatic insulin sensitivity that precede detectable changes in HbA1c. Evidence indicates that reductions in this index are associated with more favorable glycemic trajectories in individuals with type 2 diabetes [31, 37, 38]. However, based on the design of the present study, it is not possible to determine whether these early improvements translate into sustained long-term clinical benefits.

From a nutritional perspective, although a significant reduction in total energy intake was observed over time, protein intake (g/kg/day) and the proportion of carbohydrates remained stable between groups, with no significant group × time interaction. This pattern suggests that the observed metabolic effects do not appear to be explained solely by modifications in dietary intake, and that the combination of resistance training and whey protein supplementation may have additionally contributed to the metabolic adaptations identified. Moreover, the changes in dietary intake were not accompanied by significant alterations in body.

Thus, the study hypothesis was rejected. Although previous evidence has demonstrated that protein supplementation combined with resistance training (RT) improves glycemic control, body composition, cardiometabolic risk, inflammation, and muscle strength in non-diabetic older adults [32], these benefits were not observed in the population with T2DM investigated in the present study. These findings are consistent with previous studies in which RT combined with whey protein (with or without vitamin D) did not result in significant improvements in glycemic control, muscle strength, cardiometabolic risk, or muscle cross-sectional area compared with isocaloric carbohydrate controls [24, 25, 30]. Therefore, current evidence does not indicate a consistent additive effect of protein supplementation beyond that promoted by RT alone in older adults with T2DM.

Considering the anabolic resistance commonly observed in older adults with T2DM, supplementation provided only twice weekly may not have been sufficient to substantially augment muscle protein synthesis beyond the effects of RT alone [31]. Moreover, total daily protein intake was standardized according to current recommendations, which ensured nutritional adequacy but may have reduced the contrast between groups. Individuals with T2DM present chronic low-grade inflammation, insulin resistance, oxidative stress, and impaired mTOR signaling, factors that may blunt anabolic responsiveness and muscle adaptation [30, 33]. In this context, higher doses or more frequent supplementation strategies may be required to elicit more robust hypertrophic or metabolic effects in this population.

In the maltodextrin-supplemented group, no significant changes were observed in any metabolic or muscular outcomes. Although carbohydrates play an essential role in energy provision and insulin secretion [24], their metabolic effects depend strongly on carbohydrate type. Evidence indicates that resistant dextrin improves insulin resistance and inflammatory markers in women with T2DM, likely due to its prebiotic properties [33]. In contrast, maltodextrin lacks such functional characteristics, which may explain the absence of metabolic benefits observed in the present study. These findings reinforce that carbohydrate quality, rather than quantity alone, is critical for metabolic regulation in T2DM.

Regarding muscle strength, all groups showed significant improvements over time, with no between-group differences, indicating that RT alone accounted for the observed gains. Improvements were evident in most exercises, except rowing and standing knee flexion, and neither protein nor carbohydrate supplementation provided additional benefits. These results are consistent with previous evidence demonstrating the effectiveness of RT in improving muscle strength in individuals with T2DM [30, 31].

Although adequate protein intake is recognized as an important strategy for preserving muscle mass in older adults, its synergistic effects when combined with RT in individuals with T2DM remain inconclusive. In contrast, evidence from metabolically healthy older adults suggests more favorable outcomes [32], although the applicability of these findings to individuals with T2DM remains uncertain.

Muscle thickness of the vastus lateralis and biceps brachii did not increase in any group, although no reductions were observed, indicating that RT was effective in preserving lean mass regardless of supplementation. The lack of hypertrophy may be explained by T2DM-related mechanisms, including chronic inflammation, insulin resistance, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction, which impair protein synthesis and muscle adaptation [30, 34]. The dissociation between strength gains and muscle thickness likely reflects neural adaptations, particularly in previously untrained individuals. Improvements in motor unit recruitment, synchronization, and firing frequency may explain the observed increases in load progression without measurable hypertrophy [30]. Additionally, ultrasound-derived muscle thickness reflects localized structural assessment and may not fully capture subtle changes in total muscle cross-sectional area or muscle quality. Training frequency (twice weekly), combined with the metabolic environment associated with T2DM, may have further limited hypertrophic responses.

No improvements in cardiometabolic risk markers were observed, consistent with evidence showing that shorter RT protocols yield inconclusive effects on lipid profile [35]. Longer supervised interventions have been associated with improvements in lipid parameters, highlighting the importance of intervention duration in high-risk populations [13]. Whey protein supplementation may improve insulin sensitivity through insulinotropic mechanisms mediated by amino acids and incretins such as GIP [25, 36]. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses indicate that milk protein supplementation improves fasting insulin and HOMA-IR and reduces postprandial glycemia in individuals with T2DM, whereas changes in HbA1c typically require longer intervention periods [37, 38].

Strengths and limitations

The main limitations of this study include the absence of analyses exploring different doses of protein and carbohydrate supplementation, which may not have been sufficient to elicit significant metabolic or muscular adaptations in older adults living with T2DM. In addition, the intervention duration may have been too short to induce changes in outcomes such as lipid profile and glycated hemoglobin. The presence of multimorbidity and polypharmacy, participants used an average of four medications and presented with at least two chronic conditions, may also have influenced the physiological responses to exercise and supplementation. The inclusion of only older men limits the generalizability of the findings to women and other age groups. However, this approach was intentionally adopted to reduce physiological heterogeneity, particularly considering sex-specific hormonal influences on muscle metabolism and glycemic regulation. Future studies should investigate similar interventions in women and mixed populations to broaden external validity.

Future directions and clinical relevance

These limitations highlight the need for future studies employing higher or individualized supplementation doses, longer intervention periods, and stratification according to disease severity, medication use, and metabolic control. Further research should also investigate optimal dosing strategies, frequency, volume, and intensity of RT combined with nutritional supplementation. Despite these limitations, this study contributes to the growing body of evidence by examining a clinically representative population of older adults with T2DM. Improving the understanding of how protein and carbohydrate supplementation can be optimally integrated with RT may support the development of more effective, safe, and personalized non-pharmacological strategies aimed at improving muscle strength, glycemic control, and cardiometabolic health, ultimately enhancing quality of life in this rapidly growing population.

Conclusion

Independent of protein or carbohydrate supplementation, RT promoted significant improvements in load progression in older adults living with T2DM, even in the absence of measurable increases in muscle thickness. Protein supplementation was associated with a significant reduction in HOMA-IR, suggesting early improvements in insulin sensitivity without concomitant changes in longer-term glycemic markers. No additional metabolic or musculoskeletal benefits were observed beyond resistance training alone, and no significant changes were detected in lipid profile variables across groups.
