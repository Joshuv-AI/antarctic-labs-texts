Radiological diversity in rheumatoid arthritis associated with usual interstitial pneumonia (RA-UIP) and its therapeutic implications: towards a generalizable framework of interstitial lung disease treatment
Authors: Hideaki Yamakawa, Yasuhiko Yamano, Hiroto Tsuboi, Shigeki Makino, Tamiko Takemura, Takashi Ogura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRheumatoid arthritis-associated interstitial lung disease (RA-ILD) is a major extra-articular manifestation of rheumatoid arthritis and a key determinant of patient outcomes. Among its radiologic subtypes, the usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP) pattern is the most frequent and clinically important; however, the definition and conceptual use of “RA-UIP” differ among disciplines and across countries, reflecting variations in how radiologic and pathologic features are integrated into clinical practice. RA-UIP shares several features with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) but also differs in pathogenesis, imaging characteristics, and clinical behavior, resulting in more heterogeneous prognoses and therapeutic responses. Although a recent international update proposed a unified classification encompassing both idiopathic and secondary interstitial pneumonias, secondary UIPs such as RA-UIP often show overlapping or combined morphologies that remain challenging to categorize and manage. This review emphasizes the dynamic interplay between joint and lung disease activity in RA-ILD conceptualized as a “virtuous” or “vicious” cycle. Stable control of arthritis can promote pulmonary stability, whereas uncontrolled systemic inflammation and treatment limitations—often complicated by infection—can destabilize both. While antifibrotic therapy can slow fibrotic progression, sustained inflammatory control remains essential for maintaining a favorable cycle. Integrating these dual perspectives, we outline a conceptual clinical-radiologic framework for interpreting UIP-like patterns in RA-ILD in relation to the balance between inflammation and fibrosis. Background and diagnostic frameworks

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA), a systemic autoimmune disease classified as one of the connective tissue diseases (CTDs), is characterized by chronic synovitis that extends beyond articular manifestations [1]. Interstitial lung disease (ILD) is one of the most serious extra-articular complications of RA and a major determinant of morbidity and mortality [2].

RA-associated ILD (RA-ILD) comprises diverse radiological and histopathological subtypes, among which the usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP) pattern is the most frequent and clinically relevant, followed by nonspecific interstitial pneumonia (NSIP), which also represents a common subtype [2, 3]. The UIP pattern plays a crucial role in prognostic assessment and therapeutic decision-making, especially when evaluating the risks associated with RA pharmacotherapy [3]. However, RA-UIP is not identical to idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) [4–6]. Histopathologically, airway lesions such as follicular bronchiolitis and lymphocytic infiltration extending into the alveolar regions are often intermingled, and against this background, air trapping and bronchiolocentric clustering of cystic changes are frequently observed on high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT), complicating radiologic classification [2–9].

Classification frameworks originally developed for the idiopathic interstitial pneumonias (IIPs) have profoundly influenced how clinicians understand and classify fibrotic ILDs, including CTD-ILDs. The 2011 ATS/ERS/JRS/ALAT [American Thoracic Society/European Respiratory Society/Japanese Respiratory Society/Latin American Thorax Association] guideline for IPF defined HRCT patterns as UIP, possible UIP, or inconsistent with UIP, and these were later refined in the 2018 update to UIP, probable UIP, indeterminate for UIP, and alternative diagnosis. Further, defined levels of diagnostic confidence were subsequently introduced in the 2022 update [2, 10–12]. These frameworks were originally developed to distinguish IPF from other causes of ILD. Over time, they have also been applied to describe radiologic patterns across various fibrotic ILDs. However, their direct application to RA-ILD remains challenging because the current definitions of UIP are largely based on IPF-specific guidelines and may not fully capture the pathological and radiological heterogeneity observed in RA-ILD [4, 13, 14]. Several studies have reported poor outcomes in RA-UIP, but others show more heterogeneous results, suggesting that disease-specific biology, rather than imaging findings alone, determines the clinical course [4, 9, 13–15]. In RA-ILD, radiologic features often deviate from the typical UIP pattern described in IPF, reflecting airway-centered and/or mixed inflammatory-fibrotic changes. Temporal evolution of patterns is also common—for example, NSIP may evolve into a UIP-like pattern—underscoring the limitations of static HRCT categorization for prognosis or guiding therapy [4, 9, 15]. Recent observational data further caution against interpreting radiologic phenotype as a stand-alone therapeutic stratifier in RA-ILD. In a multisite retrospective cohort, immunosuppressive therapy was associated with improved pulmonary function trajectory, and the presence of a UIP pattern did not significantly modify treatment response [16]. In addition, a recent quantitative imaging study found that, in the RA-ILD cohort, baseline ground-glass-to-fibrosis ratio and fibrosis extent were not associated with differential response to immunomodulatory therapy [17]. These findings suggest that, although radiologic appearance may provide important prognostic and pathophysiologic context, it should not by itself be assumed to determine treatment responsiveness. Accordingly, the present review is intended to offer a conceptual and hypothesis-generating framework for multidisciplinary interpretation rather than a stand-alone treatment algorithm.

Recently, the 2025 ERS/ATS Update of the International Multidisciplinary Classification of the Interstitial Pneumonias proposed a unified framework that encompasses both IIPs and interstitial pneumonias with identifiable causes, integrating them within a single classification system rather than separating them into distinct categories [18]. This integrative approach reflects the recognition that etiologic distinctions are often unclear at initial presentation and that similar morphologic patterns may arise from diverse clinical contexts. However, in practice, grouping secondary UIPs such as RA-UIP into a single category remains challenging as RA-related disease frequently exhibits combined or overlapping morphologies (e.g., UIP + NSIP or pleuroparenchymal fibroelastosis + UIP) [2–4, 19]. Indeed, the updated classification acknowledges that “patients who have combined patterns are often challenging to diagnose and manage” [18]. Thus, while the new classification provides an important conceptual step toward unifying idiopathic and secondary forms, RA-UIP exemplifies the complexity of applying morphology-based systems to diseases driven by systemic autoimmunity and evolving inflammatory-fibrotic interplay.

The RA-ILD interaction cycle and its therapeutic implications

While antifibrotic therapy has demonstrated benefit in IPF, treatment decisions in RA-ILD, particularly for the UIP-like subtypes, remain more complex and should not be based on direct therapeutic extrapolation from IPF alone [20]. At the same time, consideration of antifibrotic therapy in RA-ILD is not based solely on analogy with IPF, as subgroup analyses and emerging real-world data have begun to provide RA-ILD-specific support for this approach in selected patients with progressive fibrosing disease [21, 22]. Effective RA control requires immunosuppression, which may worsen fibrosis, whereas antifibrotic agents can stabilize ILD but have little effect on systemic inflammation [2]. This duality produces a therapeutic dilemma unique to RA-UIP. Management must therefore integrate both articular disease activity and ILD severity [4]. High RA activity has been recognized not only as a predictor of overall mortality but also as an independent risk factor for ILD progression [4, 23, 24], implying that adequate control of systemic inflammation may reduce pulmonary deterioration. At the same time, contemporary RA-ILD-specific treatment studies suggest that pulmonary disease behavior cannot be inferred solely from baseline radiologic pattern. In a multisite retrospective cohort, immunosuppressive therapy was associated with improvement in post-treatment trajectories of lung function compared with pretreatment decline, and neither UIP pattern nor the choice of immunosuppressive agent significantly modified that response [16]. In addition, prospective and real-world observational data on abatacept have reported improvement or stabilization of pulmonary disease in a substantial proportion of patients, including across both UIP-like and non-UIP-like phenotypes [25, 26]. These studies do not establish an optimal treatment algorithm, but they do indicate that RA-ILD treatment response may be more heterogeneous, and less tightly linked to radiologic phenotype alone, than would be suggested by direct analogy with IPF.

The pathophysiologic interplay between systemic inflammation associated with RA and ILD can be conceptualized as a cycle characterized by mutual amplification between inflammation and fibrosis (Fig. 1). Well-controlled RA may lead to ILD stability (Fig. 2; Scenario A, cases (A) and (B)), whereas uncontrolled or flaring RA can destabilize ILD (Fig. 2; Scenario B, case (C)). Superimposed infections may further trigger disease exacerbation (Fig. 2; Scenario C, cases (D) and (E)), and limitations in RA drug use due to pulmonary toxicity or infection can lock both diseases into a self-perpetuating vicious cycle. Even in patients with well-controlled RA, a subset exhibits progressive or unstable ILD (Fig. 3; Scenario D, cases (A) and (B)), which may worsen systemic condition and increase the risk of drug-related toxicity, again predisposing to a negative feedback loop. Once this vicious cycle has become established, RA-targeted therapies may increase the risk of infection further, leading to therapeutic restrictions and perpetuating clinical deterioration. Therefore, close multidisciplinary collaboration between rheumatologists and pulmonologists is essential.

Addressing these questions requires recognition of the heterogeneity of UIP-like patterns in RA-ILD and development of a flexible clinical framework that integrates radiologic features with longitudinal disease behavior, pulmonary physiology, systemic RA activity, infection risk, and treatment tolerance. This review aims to summarize the radiologic diversity and therapeutic implications of UIP-like patterns in RA-ILD, discuss how IPF-derived frameworks can be adapted cautiously to autoimmune contexts, and propose a patient-centered, hypothesis-generating perspective that may help structure multidisciplinary discussion across idiopathic and autoimmune ILD.

Epidemiology and prognosis of RA-ILD

To further contextualize these conceptual perspectives, it is important to review the epidemiological characteristics, radiologic subtypes, and survival outcomes of RA-ILD reported across cohorts [4]. RA-ILD is observed in approximately 10–30% of patients with rheumatoid arthritis, depending on the study population and diagnostic criteria [13, 14, 24, 27–35]. Among radiologic subtypes, the UIP pattern is the most frequent, accounting for 20–70% of cases in large cohort studies, followed by NSIP (12–40%), whereas a substantial proportion remain unclassifiable (Table 1).

Early observational studies reported extremely poor survival in RA-ILD, with median survival times of only 2.6–3.0 years after diagnosis and 5-year survival rates of 35–39%, comparable to those of IPF [24, 27]. Similarities have also been noted in genetic background as RA-ILD has been associated with the MUC5B promoter variant and leukocyte telomere shortening—both known risk factors for IPF [36, 37]. Based on these findings, RA-UIP has consistently been regarded as a subtype with a worse prognosis than non-UIP patterns [2, 24, 27].

Despite consistent evidence that the UIP pattern is generally associated with poorer outcomes than NSIP, survival data among studies vary widely. Reported median survival times for RA-UIP range from 3 to over 10 years, with 5-year survival rates spanning approximately 35% to 70% [24, 27–35]. Early cohorts demonstrated a striking gap between UIP and NSIP (for example, 3–4 years vs. 17 years, respectively) [24, 27], whereas more recent studies, incorporating contemporary diagnostic criteria and improved disease management, have reported longer survival and narrower differences between subtypes [30–34]. Japanese studies have further supported this heterogeneity, showing median survival times of 6–9 years across definite, probable, and mixed UIP/NSIP patterns, with some patients remaining stable for extended periods despite radiological UIP features [13, 14].

Collectively, several common observations emerge. First, UIP is the predominant radiologic pattern in RA-ILD, accounting for roughly half to two-thirds of cases [13, 14, 27–35]. Second, RA-UIP generally carries a worse prognosis than NSIP, although the magnitude of the difference varies across studies [4]. Third, unlike in IIPs—in which the UIP pattern typically portends an unfavorable outcome despite individual variability—the prognosis of RA-UIP is considerably more heterogeneous, with median survival ranging from 3.2 to over 10 years [13, 14, 24, 29]. This variability may reflect differences in HRCT classification criteria (2002 ATS/ERS vs. 2011 IPF guidelines vs. 2018 Fleischner criteria), treatment strategies (immunosuppressive vs. antifibrotic therapy), and ethnic or genetic backgrounds. Finally, several studies have noted that even patients with typical honeycombing—representing the classical UIP phenotype—may remain clinically stable for years, suggesting that not all cases of RA-UIP inevitably progress as in IPF [38]. These observations highlight the heterogeneity that underlies RA-UIP and provide the clinical background for considering more nuanced clinical-radiologic interpretation.

Recent longitudinal studies further highlight that progression in RA-ILD is heterogeneous rather than uniform. In a retrospective cohort with a median follow-up of 6.5 years, over half of patients showed a relative decline in percent predicted forced vital capacity (FVC) > 10% within 24 months, and trajectory modeling identified rapidly declining, slowly declining, and stable/improving groups, with the rapidly declining group showing a markedly increased risk of lung transplant or ILD-related death [39]. In a large national Veterans Health Administration cohort, England et al. further demonstrated that distinct longitudinal FVC decline trajectories were independently associated with mortality and respiratory-related hospitalization in RA-ILD, reinforcing the clinical importance of trajectory-based heterogeneity beyond static baseline assessment [40]. Likewise, a prospective Korean cohort identified distinct trajectories of lung function, and importantly, lung function trajectory was not directly comparable to the trajectory of RA disease activity [41]. Together, these findings support the view that RA-ILD progression is dynamic and heterogeneous, and that longitudinal functional monitoring may provide prognostic information beyond static baseline HRCT categorization.

Proposed radiological subtypes of RA-ILD with a UIP pattern

Considering the marked variability in the reported prevalence and prognosis of RA-UIP across studies, it is clear that RA-UIP cannot be regarded as a single, homogeneous entity. Rather, RA-UIP encompasses multiple radiologic subtypes, each with distinct clinical characteristics and therapeutic implications. Although presented here as discrete categories for clarity, these subtypes should not be viewed as fixed morphologic entities. In RA-ILD, radiologic and pathophysiologic changes occur along a continuous and dynamic spectrum, and transitions between subtypes—reflecting shifts between inflammatory and fibrotic dominance—are not uncommon over time. Accordingly, any correspondence between our therapeutic subtypes and morphology-based taxonomies should be considered as many-to-many rather than as one-to-one.

In this section, we outline these major subtypes and discuss their potential clinical and therapeutic implications (Fig. 4). These categories are not intended as validated treatment classes, but rather as dynamic clinical-radiologic descriptors that may help structure multidisciplinary interpretation in practice. These categories are not mutually exclusive but represent dominant radiologic tendencies seen in clinical practice. As a note of caution, imaging features that deviate from the typical UIP pattern described in IPF and dynamic changes in radiologic appearance are frequently observed in RA-ILD, and these subtypes often overlap rather than exist as clearly separable categories. Therefore, these patterns should be interpreted flexibly in light of the prevailing radiologic and clinical features at each point in time.

Typical UIP (IPF-like UIP)

This subtype is characterized by a fibrotic pattern predominantly involving the subpleural and basal regions, with variable degrees of honeycombing. Radiologically, it is indistinguishable from idiopathic UIP and would fulfill the diagnostic criteria for IPF when occurring in an idiopathic context. Ground-glass opacities and peribronchovascular fibrosis are minimal or absent [11]. Histopathologically, it shows patchy fibrosis accentuated in the subpleural and lobular periphery and involvement of interlobular septa, with intervening areas of relatively preserved lung parenchyma and fibroblastic foci (Fig. 5A) [10, 11].

Patients with this pattern may overlap with the subgroup of RA-ILD at higher risk of fibrotic progression or acute exacerbation; however, outcomes in RA-UIP remain heterogeneous, and a radiologic UIP-like appearance alone should not be assumed to define prognosis or treatment responsiveness [4, 13–17]. Although antifibrotic therapy may become an important consideration, particularly in patients with objectively progressive disease or fulfillment of PPF criteria [21, 42–45], recent multisite RA-ILD data suggest that patients with UIP patterns may still exhibit improvement or stabilization with immunosuppressive therapy and that UIP pattern itself does not necessarily predict poorer immunosuppressive response [16]. Prolonged use of moderate-to-high doses of corticosteroids should be approached cautiously given potential toxicity and infectious risk (Fig. 3; scenario D, case (A)) [4, 45]. Likewise, decisions regarding methotrexate should be individualized according to pulmonary reserve, prior drug tolerance, infection risk, and the need for articular disease control, rather than being based on radiologic pattern alone [4, 16, 23, 46, 47].

Notably, a subset of patients with this subtype demonstrates coexisting emphysematous destruction, consistent with combined pulmonary fibrosis and emphysema (CPFE), likely reflecting the strong pathogenic influence of cigarette smoking on both RA and ILD [48]. Such cases—referred to here as UIP consistent with CPFE—are commonly associated with a history of smoking but can also occur in non-smokers [49–51]. Radiologically, they show coarse cystic lucencies in the upper or lower lobes, reflecting confluent wall destruction and paraseptal emphysema-like morphology (Fig. 4). These patients typically maintain preserved vital capacity (VC) or FVC, whereas the diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide is markedly reduced. Pulmonary hypertension is common in this subgroup, and the risk of lung cancer is also increased [52]. Regardless of smoking status, this subtype carries a high risk of chronic infection within emphysematous areas—particularly with Pseudomonas aeruginosa, nontuberculous mycobacteria, or fungal pathogens—necessitating close monitoring and preventive management [4, 52]. Although CPFE is best regarded as a distinct comorbid condition rather than a separate ILD subtype, its recognition within the UIP spectrum is clinically important as it further worsens prognosis and complicates therapeutic decision-making.

NSIP evolving to UIP-like

It has long been suggested that, in some cases, NSIP may represent an early or transitional stage that naturally progresses into a UIP-like pattern as PPF [53, 54]. Chung et al. [54] identified several CT findings that are more frequently observed in CTD-associated UIP than in idiopathic UIP, including the “anterior upper lobe” sign (concentration of fibrosis within the anterior aspects of the upper lobes with relative sparing of the posterior and lateral regions), the “exuberant honeycombing” sign (honeycomb-like cysts occupying more than 70% of the fibrotic portions of the lung), and the “straight-edge” sign (fibrosis confined to the lung bases with a sharp craniocaudal demarcation and minimal lateral extension on coronal images) (Supplemental Fig. 1). These distinctive findings are thought to reflect the potential evolution of previously homogeneous NSIP lesions into a more heterogeneous UIP-like fibrotic pattern. In clinical practice, long-term follow-up HRCT often shows such transitions, where areas initially showing NSIP gradually develop honeycombing, and a single time-point image may resemble idiopathic UIP (Fig. 4).

From a pathophysiological standpoint, it is plausible that inflammatory signals driving NSIP progression may eventually trigger fibrotic pathways resembling those of UIP-like fibrosis. Therefore, in the early stages, adequate anti-inflammatory therapy may merit consideration, particularly when inflammatory activity appears clinically relevant. However, when longitudinal assessment suggests objective fibrotic progression, antifibrotic therapy may become an important topic for multidisciplinary discussion; such decisions should not be based on radiologic evolution alone.

Mixed NSIP/UIP

This pattern likely overlaps substantially with “NSIP evolving to UIP-like,” but not all cases can be explained by a clearly sequential temporal progression. In a subset of patients, NSIP and UIP features are observed concurrently at the same time point, reflecting heterogeneity in disease evolution [4, 13, 14]. Specifically, reticular opacities with subpleural predominance and associated traction bronchiectasis—typical of UIP—may be seen alongside peribronchovascular ground-glass attenuation consistent with NSIP. Consequently, a considerable number of cases cannot be clearly classified as either NSIP or UIP (Fig. 5B, C).

From a therapeutic standpoint, the relative prominence of inflammatory-appearing and fibrotic-appearing features may inform clinical discussion, but should not be interpreted as a validated basis for assigning anti-inflammatory versus antifibrotic therapy [17]. Accordingly, management in mixed NSIP/UIP should integrate radiologic appearance with longitudinal behavior, pulmonary physiology, extrapulmonary RA activity, infection risk, and treatment tolerance, with consideration of both anti-inflammatory and antifibrotic approaches as clinically appropriate [4]. Even when one component appears visually dominant, clinicians should remain mindful that inflammatory and fibrotic processes may coexist and evolve over time.

Airway-predominant UIP-like

Airway involvement is common in RA, and radiologic findings such as air trapping and centrilobular micronodules reflecting small airway inflammation are frequently observed [2, 8, 55]. In a pathological study by Lee et al. [7], coexistence of airway disease and ILD was often noted in RA-ILD. Moreover, some honeycomb-like cysts appear to originate from airway lesions rather than from the typical parenchymal remodeling seen in idiopathic UIP [8, 56]. Histopathologically, these cystic spaces have been described as “multiple cystic lesions corresponding to the honeycombing on CT, mainly composed of dilated bronchioles with abundant inflammatory cells, dense fibrous tissue, and loss of elastic lamina in the walls, accompanied by vanishing alveolar structure” [8, 56, 57]. This mechanism contrasts with the formation of honeycombing in idiopathic UIP/IPF, in which perilobular fibrosis leads to alveolar collapse and dilation of alveolar ducts with minimal inflammation that results in multiple cysts [10, 12]. In contrast, airway-predominant honeycombing in RA may represent a mimicker of idiopathic UIP, arising from airway-centered inflammation and bronchus-associated lymphoid tissue hyperplasia [8, 56–58]. Pathologically, it is characterized by peribronchiolar fibrosis extending into adjacent alveolar septa with variable inflammatory infiltration (Fig. 5D). Akiyama et al. [9] also reported cases in which honeycomb-like cysts appear to develop from airway lesions rather than from peripheral alveolar collapse. Taken together, these observations suggest that a substantial subset of patients may exhibit an airway-predominant UIP-like pattern. Notably, this bronchiolocentric remodeling process may also account for some of the clustered cystic changes observed in the “anterior upper lobe sign” or “exuberant honeycombing” described earlier in cases evolving from NSIP to UIP-like. We speculate that these findings could, at least in part, reflect trans-bronchiolar fibrotic remodeling rather than purely subpleural alveolar destruction (Supplemental Figs. 2–4).

From a physiological standpoint, patients with this pattern often maintain relatively preserved VC or FVC despite a honeycombing-like lesion, reflecting the bronchiolar origin of the fibrosis. Because airway lesions may track with systemic RA activity in at least a subset of patients, stabilization of articular disease may in some cases be accompanied by pulmonary stability; however, this observation should be interpreted cautiously and not as subtype-specific proof of treatment responsiveness [2, 8, 9]. However, because of airway involvement, these patients may also have an increased susceptibility to chronic lower respiratory infections [54] (Fig. 2; Scenario C, cases (D) and (E), and Supplemental Fig. 4)—including P. aeruginosa, nontuberculous mycobacteria, and fungal pathogens—necessitating careful clinical monitoring.

UIP with inflammatory change

This pattern can be conceptually recognized radiologically by transient wall thickening or surrounding ground-glass opacities superimposed on a UIP-like background and histologically by inflammatory cell infiltration within fibrotic areas. In contrast to idiopathic UIP/IPF, for which the trajectory of FVC typically shows a steady decline, many patients with UIP-like features in RA-ILD exhibit transient improvement in FVC following anti-inflammatory therapy [28, 59]. This clinical course likely reflects the coexistence of inflammatory cell infiltration within the UIP framework [59]. Radiologically, this pattern is characterized by thickened cyst walls of honeycomb-like structures that may partially regress after intensification of anti-inflammatory treatment (Fig. 4 and Supplemental Figs. 5, 6). Histopathologically, a UIP background is often accompanied by interstitial and peribronchiolar inflammatory cell infiltrates, including lymphoid follicles (Fig. 5C) [4]. It is crucial, however, to differentiate these inflammatory changes from those of chronic lower respiratory infections as similar radiologic findings may be observed in cases complicated by P. aeruginosa, nontuberculous mycobacteria, or fungal infections. Recognition of this subtype is important because it may suggest that inflammatory modulation remains relevant in selected RA-UIP cases; however, this possibility should be interpreted as a clinical-radiologic hypothesis and weighed carefully against the need to exclude chronic or superimposed infection. Pathologically, we speculate that airway-predominant UIP-like and UIP with inflammatory change share certain key features, such as hyperplasia of inducible bronchus-associated lymphoid tissue and destruction of pre-existing elastic fibers, both of which may contribute to cyst formation within the UIP framework. However, they may differ in the relative predominance of airway remodeling and alveolar inflammation. Specifically, airway-predominant UIP-like is characterized by bronchiolar destruction accompanied by peribronchiolar fibrosis and dense inflammatory infiltration, whereas UIP with inflammatory change shows more extensive alveolar inflammation within a similar structural context.

Conceptual therapeutic considerations across radiological subtypes

Importantly, these proposed subtypes were not derived from a formal cohort-based classification exercise and are not accompanied by prevalence estimates, outcome analyses, or treatment-response data specific to each category. In addition, these categories were not designed as operational imaging definitions, and no explicit thresholds, quantitative criteria, or interobserver agreement data are currently available for their application. Rather, they are presented as conceptual clinical-radiologic descriptors intended to organize recurrent patterns observed in practice. Their epidemiologic validity and clinical utility remain to be established in future cohort-based studies. Radiological subtypes of ILD with a UIP or UIP-like pattern and their conceptual therapeutic implications are illustrated in Fig. 6. Importantly, radiologic appearance alone should not be used as a stand-alone determinant of treatment selection, as available RA-ILD data do not support radiologic pattern alone as a reliable predictor of response to immunomodulatory therapy. Accordingly, the subtypes proposed here are intended to support multidisciplinary interpretation rather than to prescribe a fixed treatment algorithm.

In this context, a UIP (IPF-like) phenotype may raise consideration of antifibrotic therapy, particularly when there is objective fibrotic progression or fulfillment of PPF criteria, whereas NSIP-like features may raise consideration of anti-inflammatory therapy. For intermediate or overlapping patterns—including airway-predominant UIP-like, UIP with inflammatory change, mixed NSIP/UIP, and NSIP evolving to UIP-like—therapeutic discussion should take into account the balance of inflammatory and fibrotic features together with pulmonary physiology, longitudinal behavior, systemic RA activity, infection risk, and treatment tolerance. Because infection risk may be particularly relevant in CPFE and airway-predominant UIP-like phenotypes, careful monitoring and infection control remain essential.

These subtypes are not mutually exclusive; overlap and coexistence are common in clinical practice. Therefore, therapeutic decisions should be individualized and multidisciplinary, with this framework serving as a conceptual aid rather than a stand-alone treatment algorithm.

Future directions and perspectives

Although the presence of distinct subtypes within RA-ILD is increasingly being recognized, evidence to guide therapeutic decision-making remains limited. Most existing data are derived from retrospective studies involving heterogeneous populations, and robust prospective evidence is still lacking. Consequently, the choice and timing of immunosuppressive and antifibrotic therapies continue to rely largely on clinical experience rather than high-level evidence [4, 18, 44, 45]. At present, however, the proposed subtypes have not been systematically applied to a defined cohort, and their prevalence, reproducibility, and associations with clinical outcomes or treatment response remain unknown. Their current utility is therefore conceptual and heuristic, intended to organize clinical heterogeneity rather than to define validated prognostic or therapeutic classes. Future studies will need to determine whether these proposed subtypes can be translated into reproducible imaging categories by establishing explicit radiologic criteria, testing interobserver agreement, and evaluating their performance in cohort-based analyses. Recent RA-ILD studies suggest that treatment responsiveness cannot be inferred from UIP pattern or baseline quantitative radiologic features alone [16, 17]. This reinforces the need to view the present framework as conceptual and hypothesis-generating pending future validation. Real-world comparative effectiveness data in RA-ILD remain limited but are beginning to emerge. For example, a US veterans cohort did not identify significant differences in survival or respiratory hospitalization between patients initiating tumor necrosis factor (TNF) inhibitors and those receiving non-TNF biologic or targeted synthetic DMARDs [60]. More recent comparative observational data have also suggested broadly similar effectiveness and safety between tocilizumab and rituximab in RA-ILD [61]. Although such studies are still sparse and do not yet define optimal treatment selection, they suggest that contemporary RA-ILD management is moving toward a more evidence-informed approach beyond simple extrapolation from IPF alone.

Nevertheless, the therapeutic paradigm is rapidly evolving. Novel antirheumatic agents, including Janus kinase inhibitors, are now widely used in routine practice, raising important questions about their efficacy and safety in RA-ILD [62]. Moreover, emerging agents such as nerandomilast, a preferential phosphodiesterase 4B inhibitor, which demonstrated efficacy in IPF and PPF, possess both antifibrotic and immunomodulatory properties, potentially challenging the traditional dichotomy between “anti-inflammatory” and “antifibrotic” therapy [63, 64]. How such agents should be integrated into multidisciplinary treatment strategies in relation to radiologic or histopathologic subtypes of RA-ILD remains a major area of future investigation.

Future research should therefore aim not only to validate the prognostic significance of radiologic and histopathologic subtypes but also to establish evidence for the optimal sequencing or combination of immunosuppressive and antifibrotic therapies. In particular, future studies should clarify whether UIP-like phenotypes within RA-ILD represent a distinct biological end-stage of inflammation or a heterogeneous continuum that remains modifiable by anti-inflammatory intervention. In this context, the present radiologic classification—developed as a conceptual clinical-radiologic framework—may be useful for refining hypotheses and structuring future treatment studies across broader ILD phenotypes, including idiopathic and fibrotic hypersensitivity pneumonitis.

Even though the establishment of prospective international registries and collaborative studies is indispensable for generating high-quality evidence, it is equally important to continue learning from real-world clinical practice. Retrospective reflection on existing data and thoughtful integration of evolving evidence into patient-centered care will remain crucial as we strive to optimize treatment for this complex and heterogeneous disease spectrum.

Finally, while our therapeutic subtypes were developed from a clinical and pathophysiologic perspective, their potential correspondence with morphology-based frameworks such as the 2025 ERS/ATS classification of interstitial pneumonias [18] is summarized in Table 2. For example, emerging concepts within the ERS/ATS framework—such as bronchiolocentric interstitial pneumonia (BIP) may partially overlap with airway-centered features seen in RA-ILD. In this context, airway-predominant UIP-like may correspond to bronchiolocentric fibrosis within the BIP spectrum. This comparative view highlights that our categories, although presented discretely for clarity, should not be regarded as fixed morphologic entities. In RA-ILD, radiologic and pathophysiologic features evolve along a continuous and dynamic spectrum, and transitions between subtypes—reflecting shifts between inflammatory and fibrotic dominance—are common over time. Accordingly, any correspondence between our therapeutic subtypes and morphology-based taxonomies should be interpreted as many-to-many rather than as one-to-one, thus bridging static morphology with dynamic clinical behavior. Ultimately, this integrative framework aims to provide a conceptual bridge between idiopathic and autoimmune forms of fibrosing interstitial pneumonia by linking radiologic diversity with dynamic clinical interpretation and multidisciplinary discussion; however, its therapeutic relevance should be regarded as hypothesis-generating pending future validation.
