Management of infected periprosthetic fracture of hip and knee: a systematic review of the literature
Authors: Luigi Zagra, Mattia Alessio-Mazzola, Giulia D’Andrea, Giacomo Placella, Francesco Anzano, Vincenzo Salini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe coexistence of periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) and periprosthetic fracture (PPFx) is one of the most challenging complications after total hip arthroplasty (THA) and total knee arthroplasty (TKA). Evidence regarding the management and outcomes of concomitant PJI and PPFx remains limited. This systematic review aimed to evaluate treatment strategies for infected PPFx in order to optimise infection eradication, fracture healing, implant survival, and limb function.MethodsA systematic review was conducted according to PRISMA and Cochrane guidelines and registered in the PROSPERO database (CRD420251051853). Studies of level II–V evidence reporting on the management of infected PPFx following THA or TKA were included. Extracted data comprised patient demographics, fracture classification, pathogens, treatment strategies, complications, infection control, and fracture healing outcomes.ResultsThirteen studies involving 210 patients were included, with a percentage of hip fractures of 81.9%. The most frequently isolated pathogens were Staphylococcus epidermidis, Staphylococcus aureus, and Cutibacterium acnes. Treatment strategies varied and included revision arthroplasty, plating, intramedullary fixation, distally locked stems, and staged reconstruction with antibiotic-loaded cement or spacers. Fracture union rates ranged from 58 to 100%, while reinfection or recurrence occurred in 6–28% of cases. Stable fixation was associated with improved outcomes, particularly in Vancouver B1 fractures, whereas B2 and B3 fractures showed higher mechanical complication rates. Mortality was reported up to 22% predominately in frail populations.ConclusionInfected periprosthetic fractures remain rare but involve severe complications. Staged protocols combining infection eradication with stable fixation provide the most consistent outcomes, although treatment should be individualised.

Introduction

The increasing number of joint arthroplasties performed in recent years, although highly effective and satisfactory, has been accompanied by a growing incidence of periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) and periprosthetic fracture (PPFx), which now represent the leading causes of revision surgery (1, 2, 3) after total hip arthroplasty (THA) and total knee arthroplasty (TKA) (1). The incidence of PJI is expected to rise, particularly among younger patients (2), while PPFxs predominantly affect individuals over 70 years old (3).

The coexistence of PJI and PPFx is a rare but likely underestimated complication, as no large registry studies can easily address this scenario with two concomitant reasons for failures and case series are limited due to small numbers (4). Moreover, the lack of a universally accepted definition may contribute to underdiagnosis. Combined PJI and PPFx represent one of the most challenging conditions in orthopaedics and traumatology, as infection eradication, fracture stabilisation, and implant stability must be achieved simultaneously; treatment failure can result in high mortality and severe joint dysfunction (5). The therapeutic goal is to preserve limb function, eradicate infection, and promote fracture healing and arthroplasty durability.

Moreover, PPFxs and PJIs may occur together during surgery. Removal of infected implants may cause iatrogenic fractures, and revision procedures for PPFx are associated with an increased risk of PJI, with rates ranging from 7 to 11% (6). Patients with Vancouver type A fractures or those on dialysis are particularly at risk (7). For Vancouver type B2/B3 fractures associated with loose stems and high suspicion of infection, routine evaluation for PJI is recommended (4).

Diagnostic workup often begins with white blood cell (WBC) count, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), and C-reactive protein (CRP), an inexpensive and widely available screening tool (8). However, WBC, ESR, and CRP have limited specificity and may be elevated in the setting of acute fractures (9, 10). Therefore, additional investigations, such as joint aspiration (which is problematic to perform in acute fractures), deep tissue cultures, implant sonication, and histopathological analysis or leukocyte esterase, should be employed, as they provide higher positive predictive values (11). Intraoperative cultures may unexpectedly yield positive results in more than 6% of PPFx cases and are associated with elevated CRP and stem loosening (12).

Fractures can also occur below the level of a spacer in cases of persistent prosthetic infection (13). Hardware retention and osteosynthesis in such conditions may be detrimental, as plates or cerclages can become infected, impairing antibiotic efficacy and promoting the development of multidrug-resistant bacterial strains (13).

Drawing from experience with infected non-unions and PJIs with bone loss, plating techniques combined with antibiotic-loaded cement have shown promising results, achieving normalised laboratory parameters and mechanical stability (14, 15). Case reports have described successful outcomes with this approach in both total hip arthroplasty (THA) (16) and TKA (17).

A two-step technique involving hardware removal, temporary stabilisation with plates and spacers, and final reimplantation has demonstrated good overall results in a small series (18). However, complications related to prolonged procedures were reported (18). Modular megaprostheses have also been used successfully to manage concomitant infection and fracture, with cemented spacers between stages, although infection recurrence remains a concern (19). Megaprosthesis implantation in previously infected sites carries a fourfold increased risk of reinfection (20), and although silver coating may reduce infection rates (21), its use requires caution.

Other strategies include long, distally locked femoral stems that bypass the fracture site to provide immediate stability. In a cohort of 17 patients, fracture healing and callus formation were achieved in all cases, and 10 patients subsequently underwent definitive revision within seven months, while the others remained on oral antibiotic therapy (22). Additionally, local antibiotic delivery with calcium sulphate beads has been reported as a potential adjunctive measure (21, 22).

The aim of this study is to identify an appropriate treatment approach for infected periprosthetic fractures based on the type of pathogen involved, the patient’s functional demands, and the specific fracture pattern.

Materials and methods

This review was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews: CRD420251051853.

Eligibility criteria

The review included level II–V studies reporting the management of infected periprosthetic fractures following total hip arthroplasty (THA) or total knee arthroplasty (TKA). Extracted data included fracture type, management strategy, isolated pathogens, complications, and fracture healing outcomes.

Search strategy

The study was conducted according to the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (Fig. 1). A comprehensive literature search was performed in the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), MEDLINE/PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Science Citation Index Expanded (Web of Science), ScienceDirect, CINAHL, and LILACS databases, covering publications from 1 January 2000 to 1 September 2025.

PRISMA flow chart for study selection.

The following keywords and their combinations were used: ‘periprosthetic joint infection’, ‘management of periprosthetic joint infection’, ‘periprosthetic joint fracture’, ‘management of periprosthetic joint fracture’, and ‘infected periprosthetic joint fracture’.

Study selection followed the PICOS (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcomes, and Study design) framework. Two reviewers (GD and FA) independently screened all titles and abstracts retrieved from the initial search. Full-text articles were obtained for potentially relevant studies, and reference lists were examined for additional eligible works. Disagreements were resolved by consensus with a third reviewer (MAM).

Data extraction and synthesis

Data were extracted and are summarised in Tables 1 and 2. A total of 13 studies met the inclusion criteria: 5 retrospective case series (7, 12, 18, 19, 22), 6 case reports (5, 16, 17, 23, 24, 25), 1 retrospective cohort study (10), and 1 prospective study (26). Overall, 210 patients were included, 172 with hip and 38 with knee infected PPFx. Reported treatments included surgical revision, plating, use of distally locked stems, intramedullary rods or pins, strut grafts, open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF), and revision prostheses with or without cerclages.

Risk-of-bias assessment

The risk of bias of the included non-randomised studies was independently assessed by two reviewers (MAM and GD) using the ROBINS-I (Risk of Bias in Non-Randomised Studies of Interventions) Version 2 tool. This instrument evaluates bias across seven domains: bias due to confounding, selection of participants, classification of interventions, deviations from intended interventions, missing data, measurement of outcomes, and selection of the reported result. Disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus with a third reviewer (FA). The overall risk-of-bias assessment for the included studies is summarised in Fig. 2.

Outcomes

The main outcomes assessed were management strategy, postoperative complications, fracture healing rate, and the most frequently isolated pathogens.

Results

Thirteen studies met the inclusion criteria: five retrospective case series (7, 12, 18, 19, 22), six case reports (5, 16, 17, 23, 24, 25), one retrospective cohort study (10), and one prospective study (26). Overall, 210 patients were included, 172 with hip (81.9%) and 38 (18.1%) with knee infected periprosthetic fractures.

Two studies (7, 12) included mixed cohorts of infected and aseptic PPFxs; for the purpose of this review, only data specifically referring to infected cases were extracted. In both studies, outcomes of infected PPFXs were reported separately from aseptic cases, allowing selective extraction of healing rates, complications, and reinfection data exclusively for infected cases.

Isolated pathogens

Across studies, Staphylococcus epidermidis, Staphylococcus aureus (including MRSA), and Cutibacterium acnes were the most frequently identified pathogens, while Gram-negative organisms such as Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Klebsiella pneumoniae were less commonly reported and typically associated with chronic or previously treated infections. Differences in microbiological findings largely reflected variability in diagnostic protocols, as some authors did not systematically record intraoperative cultures or distinguish true infection from contamination.

Treatment modalities

Considerable heterogeneity was found in treatment strategies. Müller et al. (18) described a structured three-step approach within a two-stage revision, consisting of implant removal, extensive debridement, and temporary fixation with plate osteosynthesis and antibiotic-loaded spacers. All patients were infection-free after reimplantation. Alvand et al. (19) used modular endoprosthetic reconstruction following radical debridement in cases with substantial bone loss; despite satisfactory functional outcomes, reinfection remained a concern even with the use of silver-coated implants. Barberis et al. (12) highlighted the diagnostic relevance of intraoperative cultures by identifying unsuspected infections in presumed aseptic fractures; patients undergoing targeted revision and antibiotic therapy demonstrated no recurrence at follow-up.

Konan et al. (22) achieved reliable fracture healing and infection control using long distally locked revision stems placed after extensive debridement as permanent treatment, which provided stable fixation and allowed early weight-bearing. Matsuno et al. (7) reported that treatment choice depends primarily on fracture classification and implant stability: stable components were managed with open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF), whereas loose stems required revision arthroplasty, with satisfactory outcomes when infection was managed with target antibiotic therapy according to the isolated pathogen. Bonacker et al. (23) presented a complex tibial case involving chronic infection and repeated mechanical failure, ultimately resolved through staged debridement, appropriate antibiotic therapy, and modular revision total knee arthroplasty.

Schwab et al. (24) described a two-stage protocol using custom antibiotic-loaded cement rods to achieve infection control and provide temporary stabilisation until definitive reconstruction. Bottner et al. (5) reported two severe cases in which hip disarticulation was required to eradicate infection, followed by reconstruction with modular proximal femoral replacements, resulting in pain relief and restored ambulation. Maceroli et al. (16) combined antibiotic-coated plates, cement spacers, and intramedullary fixation in patients with significant bone loss, achieving stable fixation, early weight-bearing, and effective infection management. Pang et al. (25) described a two-stage approach using a stemmed articulating spacer to maintain joint mobility and alignment during infection treatment; after normalisation of inflammatory markers, definitive reconstruction was done, resulting in full infection eradication and satisfactory long-term function.

Hao et al. (17) used a two-stage protocol for an MRSA-infected periprosthetic knee fracture, initially managing the infection and fracture with a handmade vancomycin-loaded T-shaped cement spacer, followed by delayed fixation and revision arthroplasty with bone grafting and cortical splints. This resulted in infection resolution and restoration of joint stability. Van den Kieboom et al. (10) evaluated a cohort of 395 revision cases and found that revision arthroplasty combined with additional fixation was the most common approach, with PJI representing the leading cause of re-revision, highlighting the persistent infection risk after fracture-related revision procedures. Jaschke et al. (26) analysed 41 patients who developed PPFx during staged treatment for pre-existing infection. Most fractures occurred during explantation and were treated with cerclages or plating. Reinfection-free survival was 68.3% at two years, while mortality reached 22%, primarily among frail individuals.

Overall, the evidence, although low, seems to indicate that staged surgical management, characterised by radical debridement, temporary antibiotic-loaded fixation and spacer with implant removal, and delayed reimplantation, represents the main way for achieving both infection eradication and mechanical stability in infected PPFxs. Studies consistently showed improved outcomes when aggressive debridement and stable fixation were combined with targeted local and systemic antibiotic therapy.

Outcomes and complications

Fracture union rates varied across the included studies, ranging from 58 to 100%; this could be associated with differences in fixation techniques and follow-up duration, as observed in the series by Matsuno et al. (7), Konan et al. (22), Bonacker et al. (23), Pang et al. (25), and Maceroli et al. (16). Infection eradication was achieved in approximately 70–80% of cases, while reinfection rates ranged from 6 to 28%, as observed from the cohort and case series published by Jaschke et al. (26), Van den Kieboom et al. (10), Müller et al. (18), and Alvand et al. (19).

Persistent infection was more frequent in patients treated with megaprosthetic reconstruction, particularly in situations where complete debridement was not feasible, a pattern highlighted in the study of Alvand et al. (19). Conversely, studies employing staged revision procedures with antibiotic-loaded materials (16, 18, 22, 24, 25) demonstrated the achievement of infection-free survival and improved mechanical stability.

Mortality was reported by a few studies, reaching up to 22% and predominantly occurring in frail and complex patients, as described by Jaschke et al. (26).

Only four studies reported detailed functional recovery (5, 16, 23, 25), describing improvements in mobility and pain control once infection had been successfully eradicated.

Complications such as recurrent infection, mechanical failure, non-union, and implant loosening were described across several studies, highlighting the multifactorial nature of postoperative adverse events and underscoring the challenges associated with achieving long-term stability and successful clinical outcomes in these patients (10, 19, 23, 26).

Reinfection represents the principal cause of treatment failure and, in the most severe cases, leads to further revision surgery or even amputation. This issue was especially concerning in patients treated with megaprostheses (19, 20), which suggests a substantially higher risk of recurrent infection compared with non-infected settings.

The use of silver-coated implants showed potential in reducing reinfection rates, but evidence remains limited and inconclusive, as suggested by the small case series reported by Alvand et al. (19).

Discussion

The incidence of primary joint arthroplasty is projected to rise exponentially over the coming decades, and the number of PPFx is expected to increase proportionally. This trend is largely attributed to age-related factors, including decreased bone quality and a higher prevalence of fall-related risk drivers, such as obesity, functional decline, dementia, and growing multimorbidity burdens. A particularly complex clinical challenge is the management of PPFx occurring in the presence of PJI, a scenario that is being encountered with increasing frequency by orthopaedic surgeons (4).

Although national joint registries and large observational cohorts document this coexistence after both hip and knee arthroplasty, the true incidence of simultaneous PPFx and PJI remains inadequately quantified and is likely underestimated due to non-uniform diagnostic thresholds and inconsistent reporting (4). Registry-based data likely underestimate the actual burden of disease due to non-uniform diagnostic thresholds, inconsistent reporting, and potential misclassification of early failures as either septic or aseptic events. Furthermore, fracture-related revisions may not always capture infection status in the registry dataset, contributing to further under-reporting. These limitations must be acknowledged when interpreting epidemiological data.

The diagnosis of infected PPFx represents a dual challenge that can significantly affect clinical outcomes: the accurate assessment of implant stability, for which diagnostic algorithms have been proposed (27), and the reliable evaluation of infection, including the determination of appropriate markers and threshold values in the specific setting of PPFx. On the latter, one of the most extensive institutional analyses was performed by Chevilotte et al. (28), using fracture datasets from the Mayo Clinic. Fractures were stratified through the Vancouver Classification System, including types A, B, and C. Confirmed infection was identified in 11.6% of cases, commonly accompanied by elevated inflammatory markers, including serum C-reactive protein (CRP) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), but without explicit guidance on a standardised treatment pathway.

The core difficulty in diagnosing infected periprosthetic fractures lies in the inflammatory overlap created by fracture trauma and infection, which compromises the specificity of serum tests. In this setting, synovial fluid biomarkers show superior diagnostic value.

Shah et al. (29) evaluated 121 fracture cases investigated for infection and found that synovial WBC count and polymorphonuclear percentage reached 100% sensitivity using thresholds of 2,707 cells/mL and 77% neutrophils, with specificities of 65 and 63%, respectively. These values are consistent with the thresholds proposed by the International Consensus Meeting 2018 (30). According to these criteria, synovial fluid analysis is fundamental, with leukocyte esterase and alpha-defensin recognised as validated diagnostic markers. The 2025 Consensus document (31) further supports the diagnostic reliability of these synovial biomarkers, particularly LE and AD, but does not introduce new cut-off values for synovial WBC count or PMN%. Serum ESR and CRP continue to provide supportive diagnostic information due to their acceptable sensitivity, although their limited specificity prevents them from serving as standalone diagnostic tests.

Van de Kieboom et al. (10) further validated this diagnostic challenge in a 144-case registry of hip and knee PPFxs, identifying 41 concurrent infections using similar laboratory parameters, again without defining a reproducible treatment algorithm. This confirms that, despite progress in diagnostic definitions, fracture-specific infection protocols remain poorly standardised in the clinical literature.

An additional and highly relevant clinical dilemma concerns the time-sensitive management of early PPFx. In acute postoperative or early traumatic fractures, prompt surgical intervention is often required to restore mechanical stability and prevent further morbidity. However, the diagnostic work-up required to confirm or exclude infection, including synovial aspiration, advanced laboratory testing, and culture incubation, is time-consuming. This creates a tension between the urgency of fracture fixation or revision and the need for diagnostic certainty regarding infection status. In some cases, proceeding with fixation without adequately ruling out infection may compromise long-term outcomes, whereas delaying surgery to complete infection diagnostics may expose frail patients to additional systemic risks. This time-pressure conflict underscores the need for streamlined diagnostic pathways, and careful intraoperative sampling for culture is recommended, in particular, when infection cannot be confidently excluded preoperatively.

From a surgical perspective, treatment requires balancing two competing priorities: fracture stability often favours internal fixation or implant retention in case of a stable implant, while infection eradication may demand removal of foreign material and concerns in positioning of internal fixation devices. Current evidence heavily favours staged and hybrid surgical strategies (4) as infection eradication has the priority.

Hao et al. (17) reported complete limb preservation in a prospective 8-patient cohort managed with a three-step protocol prioritising interval stabilisation. Biological bridging using intramedullary articulated antibiotic spacers has also been described, including internal fracture spanning with a femoral stem antibiotic spacer, which enables mechanical support while delivering high-dose local antibiotics, as reported by Jaske et al. (26).

Additional reconstruction pathways include plate-based or rod-assisted osteosynthesis constructs augmented with local antibiotic-eluting cement or coated implants, including antibiotic-coated plates, which provided reliable fracture control in reported case series but demonstrated variable healing, frequent need for secondary reconstruction, and occasional prolonged or repeated spacer retention (4, 16). Even though there is a general trend in the increasing use of single-stage procedures for the treatment of PJI, evidence does not currently support routine single-stage revision when infection and fracture coexist due to insufficient comparative outcomes data.

A major limitation emerging from the available literature is the heterogeneity in outcome definitions. Key endpoints, such as ‘infection eradication’, ‘reinfection’, ‘fracture union’, and ‘treatment success’, were inconsistently defined across studies. Some authors reported reinfection-free survival at predefined time points, others defined eradication based on normalisation of inflammatory markers or the absence of clinical signs, while several studies did not provide explicit operational criteria. Similarly, fracture union was variably defined as radiographic consolidation, painless full weight-bearing, or the absence of mechanical failure, with substantial differences in follow-up duration. Whenever possible, infection-related outcomes were interpreted in light of established consensus definitions, such as those proposed by the International Consensus Meeting; however, most included studies did not explicitly adopt or reference these criteria. This lack of standardisation limits direct comparability between cohorts and precludes meaningful quantitative pooling of data. Consequently, the reported reinfection and union rates should be interpreted as descriptive ranges rather than strictly comparable metrics. The variability in definitions likely contributes to the broad range of reported infection eradication (approximately 70–80%) and fracture union (58–100%) rates.

Clinical outcomes remain heterogeneous and generally guarded. In larger institutional cohorts (7, 18, 22) evaluating infected fracture candidate THA populations, reinfection-free survival was approximately 75%, with overall combined fracture and infection protocol success reported near 70%, an early reinfection risk of about 19–20% at one year, and mortality as high as 22% in one follow-up cohort (7), underscoring the frailty of this population. Fracture union was achieved inconsistently and often required further revision to address persistent mechanical or biological compromise.

The predominant drivers of failure were recurrent or persistent infection, impaired fracture consolidation, bone loss during spacer intervals, and pathogens classified as difficult-to-treat organisms, particularly when iterative debridement or spacer exchange was required between initial implant removal and reimplantation (16, 17, 18).

Uniformly across studies, authors emphasise that optimal care requires individualised mechanical and biological planning, complete foreign body removal when infection control outweighs mechanical priorities, targeted tissue debridement, fracture spanning through internal or external antibiotic elution constructs, pathogen-guided antibiotic delivery, and multidisciplinary coordination involving infectious disease and soft tissue management expertise.

Conclusion

Infected periprosthetic fractures are an increasingly common but inconsistently reported complication of joint arthroplasty. Epidemiological data remain uncertain due to non-standardised reporting frameworks and biomarker variability after fracture trauma. Synovial fluid biomarkers currently provide the most sensitive and clinically validated diagnostic thresholds, while serum inflammatory markers remain supportive but non-specific in the acute fracture setting.

Treatment evidence favours staged or combined surgical strategies that integrate infection control, including implant removal with individualised fracture stabilisation and high-dose local antibiotic delivery. Outcomes remain unpredictable and are heavily influenced by host comorbidity and pathogen risk profile. High complication and mortality rates reveal clear evidence gaps, highlighting the need for periprosthetic fracture-specific infection protocols, comparative clinical trials, and consensus studies to define algorithms for optimal sequencing of implant removal, fixation, reconstruction, and antimicrobial therapy.
