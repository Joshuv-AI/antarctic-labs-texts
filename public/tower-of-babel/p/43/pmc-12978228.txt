Enhancing plant antiviral responses and productivity through the elimination of m5C methyltransferase
Authors: YaoYao Jiang, Peng Liu, MeiChen Zhang, Bin Yong, JingLiang Yin, LiWen Chen, ChaoNan Shi, Ping Li, YunFei Guo, HaiChao Hu, LiXiao Feng, Lu Chen, TianYe Zhang, JiaQian Liu, KaiLi Zhong, XuGuo Zhou, Feng Chen, JianPing Chen, Jian Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
RNA 5-methylcytosine (m5C) plays an undefined role in plant antiviral defense. Here, we reveal that the wheat methyltransferase NOP2/Sun RNA methyltransferase 2 (TaNSUN2) is an RNA m5C methyltransferase recruited by eukaryotic elongation factor 1-alpha (TaeEF1A) into Chinese wheat mosaic virus (CWMV) replication complexes (VRCs). TaNSUN2 promotes the m5C modification of CWMV RNAs to stabilize them and enhance their translational efficiency. Moreover, m5C modification in RNA 3′ untranslated region strengthens both TaeEF1A binding, promoting viral replication, and viral coat protein interaction, facilitating viral assembly. A major allele of TaNSUN2 prevents its interaction with TaeEF1A and hinders its entry into the VRCs to suppress m5C modification of viral RNAs. TaNSUN2 knockout enhances wheat resistance to CWMV and increases the weight and size of wheat grains. Our findings provide mechanistic insights into the function of RNA modifications in plant virus infection and a valuable genetic resource for future wheat breeding projects.

INTRODUCTION

RNA methylation is a common posttranslational modification process and plays a key role in numerous biological processes (1). Of more than 170 identified types of RNA methylation, 5-methylcytosine (m5C) is notably prevalent and is generated through transferring a methyl group from S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) to the carbon-5 position of cytosine in RNA (2). To date, multiple m5C regulators have been reported, including m5C methyltransferases (writers) such as NOP2/Sun RNA methyltransferase 2 (NSUN2) and NSUN6, and demethylases (erasers), such as ten-eleven translocation family member 2 (TET2) and alkylation repair protein B (AlkB) homolog 1 (ALKBH1) (3). In addition, Aly/REF export factor and Y-box binding protein 1 (YBX1) have been reported to function as “reader” proteins to detect m5C and facilitate the export of mRNA from the nucleus to stabilize RNA (4, 5). mRNA m5C modifications have been shown to regulate many biological processes and responses to multiple diseases through the functions of reader proteins. For example, YBX1 has been shown to recognize and maintain the stability of its target m5C-marked mRNAs to mediate oncogene activation in the pathogenesis of human bladder urothelial carcinoma (6). However, how m5C methylation regulators function in m5C methylation in plants remain largely unclear. The host m5C regulator is also involved in the m5C modifications of viral genomic RNA, which play a crucial role in the viral life cycle, influencing viral infection and participating in the host antiviral innate immune response (7–10). For example, NSUN2 has been shown to serve as a primary methyltransferase responsible for the m5C modification of HIV RNAs. Disabling NSUN2 function prevents the addition of m5C to HIV-1 transcripts to inhibit viral replication (8). Similarly, NSUN2 deficiency impedes the replication of murine leukemia virus (MLV), while introducing the m5C modification into MLV RNA enhances viral gene expression (9). For hepatitis B virus (HBV), the absence of NSUN2 leads to a reduced HBV accumulation, while the lack of the m5C demethyltransferase TET2 function results in increased HBV expression (7). Furthermore, NSUN2 expression correlates with the m5C modification of retinoic acid–inducible gene I (RIG-I) binding to suppress interferon-β (IFN-β) mRNA in host cells, enhancing IFN expression. Thus, regulation of NSUN2 expression not only affects HBV mRNA through m5C modification but also reduces the m5C modification levels of host IFN mRNA, two crucial factors in the HBV life cycle (10). Although the current knowledge on mammalian viruses has underscored the significance of the m5C modification during viral infection (11–13), the studies on the roles of m5C modification and its regulators on plant RNA virus infection are largely unclear. Chinese wheat mosaic virus (CWMV) is a member of the genus Furovirus, family Virgaviridae. It is a positive-sense, single-stranded RNA virus that predominantly infects cereal crops (14). CWMV genome consists of two RNA segments: (i.e., RNA1 and RNA2). RNA1 encodes a viral replication–associated protein (p153), an RNA-dependent RNA polymerase protein (RdRp), and a movement protein (MP). RNA2 encodes a major coat protein (CP) and two minor CP-related proteins, translated through a noncanonical initiation (N-CP) or a read-through strategy (CP-RT). RNA2 also encodes a cysteine-rich protein (CRP) that functions as an RNA-silencing suppressor (14–16). The two untranslated regions (UTRs) are located at the 5′ and 3′ ends of two RNAs. The 3′UTR of the two RNAs has a highly conserved tRNA-like structure and has been shown to interact with Nicotiana benthamiana eukaryotic elongation factor 1-alpha (NbeEF1A) specifically. These 3′UTRs are crucial for CWMV replication (17). CWMV infection in plants involves multiple complex interactions between the CWMV-encoded proteins and host factors (18, 19). In addition, the interactions between viral proteins and host cellular factors can suppress or circumvent the multilayered host antiviral responses (20, 21). Previous studies have also shown that several host factors, such as heat shock protein 70 (HSP70) and eEF1A, can be recruited into CWMV viral replication complexes (VRCs) to promote infection (17, 22). To date, how many host factors can be targeted into the CWMV VRCs remains unclear. Because VRCs are critical for plant RNA virus replication (23), we hypothesize that the m5C machinery is likely involved in the assembly of plant RNA virus VRCs to regulate viral pathogenesis. In this work, we found that TaNSUN2, a wheat m5C methyltransferase, can promote CWMV infection. Inside the CWMV-infected cells, TaNSUN2 is hijacked into VRCs through interaction with Triticum aestivum eukaryotic elongation factor 1-alpha (TaeEF1A) to stabilize CWMV RNAs and to increase translational efficiency. The TaNSUN2-mediated m5C modification can enhance the interaction between TaeEF1A and the 3′UTR of RNA2 to promote CWMV replication. In addition, this modification strengthens the binding affinity of the viral CP for the 3′UTR, thereby facilitating viral assembly. Moreover, we identified a natural variant of TaNSUN2 in CWMV-resistant wheat cultivars that can inhibit the interaction between TaNSUN2 and TaeEF1A. Therefore, we consider that m5C methylation plays a key role in RNA virus infection in plants

RESULTS

TaNSUN2 is a putative mRNA m5C methyltransferase

To identify mRNA methyltransferases in wheat, we used the amino acid sequence of the human m5C methyltransferase NSUN2 to align with the amino acid sequences of 21 predicted members in the NSUN RNA methyltransferase family retrieved from the wheat annotation project database (Ensembl Plants) (table S1). Phylogenetic analyses using the NSUN family members of wheat, rice, Arabidopsis, and humans showed that these proteins could be divided into six subclasses (Fig. 1A). This finding suggests that the NSUN family members are likely well conserved among different species. Because TraesCS5A02G249700, TraesCS5B02G247600, and TraesCS5D02G256900 were found to cluster with NSUN2, Arabidopsis thaliana tRNA methyltransferase 4B (AtTRM4B), and Oryza sativa NOP2/Sun RNA methyltransferase 2 (OsNSUN2) in subclass I, we named these genes as TaNSUN2-5A, TaNSUN2-5B, and TaNSUN2-5D, respectively. Our analysis indicated high sequence conservation in the coding regions of TaNSUN2-5A, -5B, and -5D (fig. S1A). The promoter of TaNSUN2-5A contains potential NAM, ATAF1/2, and CUC2 (NAC) transcription factor binding sites, suggesting distinct pathways for its transcriptional regulation (fig. S1B). Analysis of CWMV-infected wheat plants through quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) showed that only the expression of TaNSUN2-5A responsed to CWMV infection, suggesting a critical role of TaNSUN2-5A in the wheat response to CWMV invasion (Fig. 1B). We also analyzed the expression of the three TaNSUN2 homeologs in wheat roots, stems, and leaves without viral infection. TaNSUN2-5A expression was significantly higher in leaves, while TaNSUN2-5B was markedly up-regulated in roots. In contrast, TaNSUN2-5D showed only a modest increase in leaves and was lower in roots (Fig. 1C). Consequently, TaNSUN2-5A (GenBank accession no. XM_044567543.1) was selected for our further studies and is abbreviated as TaNSUN2 thereafter. In addition, we found that two key cysteine residues involved in the methyltransferase activity of TaNSUN2 are highly conserved (fig. S2).

To determine the subcellular localization of TaNSUN2, we cotransfected plasmid pNSUN2:TaNSUN2-GFP and p35S:H3-RFP were cotransfected into wheat protoplasts to coexpress TaNSUN2–green fluorescent protein (GFP) and histone 3 (H3)–red fluorescent protein (RFP). Wheat protoplasts cotransfected with plasmid pNSUN2:GFP and p35S:H3-RFP were used as controls. The results revealed that TaNSUN2-GFP mainly colocalized with H3-RFP in the nucleus, where mRNA methylation is known to occur. In the control protoplasts, GFP was localized in both the cytoplasm and nucleus (Fig. 1D and fig. S3A). To determine whether the m5C modification requires TaNSUN2, we overexpressed TaNSUN2-GFP in wheat protoplasts. The protoplasts expressing GFP were used as controls. After extraction of total RNA from the assayed protoplasts, mRNA was isolated from each total RNA sample, followed by dot-blot assays. The results showed that in the presence of TaNSUN2-GFP, the level of m5C modification on mRNAs was significantly increased when compared to the control protoplasts (Fig. 1E and fig. S3B). In a separate experiment, we inoculated wheat plants using a barley stripe mosaic virus (BSMV)–based virus-induced gene silencing (VIGS) vector (BSMV:TaNSUN2). Analysis of these wheat plants through qRT-PCR showed that the expression level of TaNSUN2 was significantly reduced in the BSMV:TaNSUN2-infected plants compared to that in BSMV:00-infected wheat plants (Fig. 1F and fig. S3C). As expected, the level of m5C modification on mRNAs in the TaNSUN2-silenced wheat plants was much lower than that in the BSMV:00-infected control plants (Fig. 1G).

To determine whether TaNSUN2 itself has the m5C methyltransferase activity, we performed in vitro methylation assays using an RNA probe with the most enriched m5C motifs reported previously (24) or its derivatives without cytosine. These RNA probes were coincubated with glutathione S-transferase (GST) or TaNSUN2-GST, followed by dot-blot assay to measure the m5C modification level. In the presence of GST, no m5C modification on the RNA probe was detected. In the presence of TaNSUN2-GST, however, the m5C modification on the RNA probe was detected, confirming that TaNSUN2-GST does have m5C methyltransferase activity (Fig. 1, H and I). Also in this experiment, two conserved cysteine residues in TaNSUN2 were substituted with alanine to create a TaNSUN2C285/335A-GST mutant. Dot-blot assay results revealed that in the presence of TaNSUN2C285/335A-GST, no detectable m5C modification was detected on the RNA probe (Fig. 1, G and H). In addition, it has been reported that NSUN2 can perform N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modification on RNA (25–27). We further verified whether TaNSUN2 has the ability of m6A modification using in vitro methylation experiments. Methyltransferase B, which functions as m6A methyltransferase in wheat, was used as a positive control (28). The results demonstrated that TaNSUN2 does not have the capacity for m6A modification (fig. S4).

TaNSUN2 promotes CWMV infection through its RNA m5C methyltransferase activity

To investigate the function of TaNSUN2 in wheat resistance to CWMV infection, we obtained a TaNSUN2 mutant (m-tansun2) wheat line, with a nonsynonymous G to A mutation at position 203 inside the coding region, from the wheat cv. KN9204 TILLING Database. This mutation leads to premature termination of protein translation (fig. S5A). Moreover, the accumulation of TaNSUN2 was significantly reduced in the m-tansun2 determined by Western blot assay using a TaNSUN2-specific antibody (fig. S5B) and did not alter the plant phenotype under normal growth conditions (fig. S5C). Field trial using this m-tansun2 wheat line in a nursery with a long CWMV infection history revealed that this mutant exhibited milder mosaic symptoms and more stunted plant growth than its parental KN9204 plants (fig. S5D). The results of qRT-PCR and Western blot assay confirmed that the CWMV-infected m-tansun2 plants accumulated significantly less CWMV RNA and CP than the CWMV-infected KN9204 plants (fig. S5, E and F). To further confirm the above finding, we overexpressed TaNSUN2 in wheat cv. Fielder plants through stable transformation. Analysis through qRT-PCR showed that the expression levels of TaNSUN2 in the TaNSUN2-L3-T0 and TaNSUN2-L6-T0 plants were approximately 56-fold higher than that in the wild-type (WT) Fielder plants (fig. S6A). The results of Western blot using TaNSUN2-specific antibody in TaNSUN2-L3-T0 and TaNSUN2-L6-T0 were much higher compared to that in control plants (fig. S6B). Under standard greenhouse conditions, the transgenic expression of TaNSUN2 in wheat resulted in a normal developmental phenotype (fig. S6C). Meanwhile, we analyzed the RNA m5C levels in overexpressing lines and found that the modification levels in L3 and L6 plants were significantly elevated compared with the control Fielder (fig. S6D). After inoculation with CWMV in the laboratory, we found that the TaNSUN2-L3-T3 and TaNSUN2-L6-T3 plants, with about 50-fold higher TaNSUN2 expression, enhanced viral infection (Fig. 2A and fig. S6E). In a separate field trial, TaNSUN2-L3-T3 and TaNSUN2-L6-T3 also displayed more severe mosaic symptoms than the WT Fielder plants (Fig. 2B). As expected, the accumulation levels of CWMV RNA and CP in the two transgenic lines were significantly increased (Fig. 2, C and D).

We also generated TaNSUN2 knockout (KO) Fielder lines by targeting a conserved region in the three copies of TaNSUN2 (on chromosome 5A, 5B, and 5D), respectively, using CRISPR-Cas9 technology. A total of six lines was initially identified by PCR using Cas9-specific primers (fig. S6F). Hi-TOM sequencing analysis of these plants revealed a comprehensive set of mutant genotypes, resulting in a T0 editing efficiency of 62.5% (table S2). Bulk seeds collected from CWMV-free plants were advanced to the T1 generation. The final genotypic distribution was as follows: 88 plants edited only in the A homoeolog, 33 plants edited only in the B homoeolog, 31 edited only in the D homoeolog, 46 plants coedited in A and B homoeologs, 33 plants coedited in A and D homoeologs, 37 plants coedited in B and D homoeologs, 33 plants coedited in all three homoeolog, and 11 plants were unedited. All mutations led to premature stop codons in the corresponding genes. For subsequent experiments, we selected eight distinct editing types. Because TaNSUN2-5A is specifically induced by CWMV infection, two different editing types were chosen and designated as KO-1 and KO-2. The edited types on each homoeolog were also selected and named as tansun2-B, tansun2-D, tansun2-AB, tansun2-AD, tansun2-BD, and tansun2-ABD (table S2). Then, these lines were inoculated with CWMV. After 21 dpi, we found that KO-1 and KO-2 significantly reduced disease symptoms (fig. S6E). This resistance phenotype was observed in all editing combinations that included the A homoeolog (A-only, AB, AD, and ABD) (fig. S6E), indicating that TaNSUN2-5A plays an important role in plant viral infection. Consistent with this, field-grown plants of KO-1 and KO-2 also showed markedly attenuated symptoms despite accumulating higher levels of both CWMV RNA and CP (Fig. 2, F to H). Moreover, KO-1 and KO-2 did not affect the plant phenotype under normal growth conditions but significantly reduced the RNA m5C modification level (fig. S6, B to D). Subsequently, we constructed the expression vectors of TaNSUN2-RFP and TaNSUN2C285/335A-RFP and transfected them into the protoplast of KO-1 plants. The m5C modification level of the transfected TaNSUN2 increased significantly. However, transfection of the TaNSUN2C285/335A-RFP expression vector showed no significant difference from the control RFP (fig. S6G). Subsequently, they were cotransfected with CWMV respectively into the protoplasts of KO-1 plants. Protoplasts cotransfected with the empty vector (RFP) and CWMV were used as the control, and after transfection, total RNA and protein were extracted from the assayed protoplasts and analyzed for CWMV accumulation through qRT-PCR and Western blot assays. The results showed that in the presence of TaNSUN2-RFP but not the TaNSUN2C285/335A-RFP or RFP, the accumulation levels of CWMV RNA and CP were significantly increased (Fig. 2, I and J).

TaNSUN2 enhances the m5C modification of CWMV RNAs

Because NSUN2 can introduce m5C modifications to animal viral genomic RNAs (7), we hypothesized that the CWMV genome may also be modified in cells. To investigate this hypothesis, we extracted and purified CWMV virions from infected wheat leaves, followed by m5C modification assays. The dot-blot assay using an m5C-specific antibody showed a positive reaction in the dots with the virions, and the labeling signal increased as the concentrations of CWMV virions increased (fig. S7A). Meanwhile, in vitro transcribed (IVT) CWMV RNA was used as a negative control. The results of dot-blot analyses showed that IVT RNAs contain negligible m5C signals (fig. S7B). We then analyzed the leaf samples collected from the CWMV-infected Fielder, TaNSUN2-overexpressing, or TaNSUN2 KO plants. The results showed that the virions from the TaNSUN2-overexpressing transgenic plants exhibited a greater m5C modification level, whereas the TaNSUN2 KO plants showed a lower m5C modification level than the Fielder plants (Fig. 3A). We also performed Northern blot assay to further confirm m5C modification on CWMV genomic RNAs and subgenomic RNAs. In addition, the m5C modification level of RNA in viral genomic and subgenomic RNAs extracted from the transgenic overexpressed TaNSUN2 was significantly enhanced, while it was decreased in the TaNSUN2 KO lines (Fig. 3B). To further confirm the m5C modification of CWMV RNAs, we conducted an RNA bisulfite sequencing (RNA-BisSeq) using purified bisulfite-treated CWMV RNAs extracted and purified from KO-1, L3-T3, and Fielder leaves, followed by bisulfite treatment and next-generation sequencing. IVT viral RNAs were used as negative controls. Several false-positive sites were identified in IVT RNAs using RNA-BisSeq assay (fig. S7, C and D). After calibrating the sequencing data, a total of 158 m5C sites was identified in the CWMV RNAs, which were extracted from Fielder plants. Among them, the methylation ratio of 15 m5C sites was significantly induced in L3-T3 but significantly reduced in KO-1 plants. The cytosine at nucleotide position 4438 (C4438) in RNA1 and the cytosine at nucleotide position 3322 (C3322)in RNA2 were specific targets regulated by TaNSUN2 (Fig. 3C and table S3). To confirm this finding, we performed m5C–immunoprecipitation (IP)–qRT-PCR (m5C-IP-qRT-PCR) assays using an m5C-specific antibody. The results confirmed that m5C enrichment at segment 1 (S1) containing C4438 and segment 2 (S2) containing C3322 had an increased m5C modification fold when compared to the control reactions using an immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibody (Fig. 3D). In addition, the results of RNA IP (RIP)–quantitative PCR (qPCR) demonstrated that TaNSUN2 could directly bind to S1 and S2 in vivo (Fig. 3E). To validate this finding, we selected 50-base sequences containing the C4438 site from S1 (designated as S1-P) and the C3322 site from S2 (designated as S2-P) as RNA probes, respectively. Meanwhile, we selected a segment of unrelated RNA as a control (fig. S7E). These probes were then used for microscale thermophoresis (MST) experiments. The MST assay results showed that the reaction containing GST and the RNA probes did not exhibit a significant dissociation constant (Kd). However, the complexes formed by TaNSUN2-GST with S1 or TaNSUN2-GST with S2 had Kd values of 28.64 and 7.04 nM, respectively. In contrast, the interaction between TaNSUN2-GST and the unrelated RNA showed no measurable Kd (Fig. 3F). We also performed RNA pull-down and Western blot assays using a GST antibody, indicating that both S1 and S2 interacted with TaNSUN2 (Fig. 3G). The results of m5C-IP-qRT-PCR showed that the m5C modification level was significantly greater in the TaNSUN2-overexpressing wheat plants than that in the Fielder plants (Fig. 3H). As expected, the m5C modification level in the TaNSUN2 KO plants was significantly reduced (Fig. 3I).

Inactivation of the m5C site of CWMV destabilizes viral RNA

To investigate the effects of m5C modification at C4438 in RNA1 and C3322 in RNA2 on CWMV infection, we introduced substitutions in infectious clones of CWMV RNA1 and RNA2. Specifically, we replaced C4438 with U in the RNA1 of CWMV infectious clone, designated it as CWMVm1, and replaced C3322 with G in the RNA2 of CWMV infectious clone, designated it as CWMVm2 (fig. S8A). RNA fold predictions showed that these mutations did not significantly alter the RNA secondary structure of CWMV (fig. S8B). We then inoculated Fielder seedlings with the IVT CWMV, CWMVm1, or CWMVm2 RNA transcripts. After 21 days, CWMV RNAs were isolated from the assayed wheat plants, digested to ribonucleosides, and analyzed for the m5C modification levels using the liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). IVT viral RNAs served as a negative control. The results of LC-MS/MS showed that IVT RNAs contained negligible m5C signal, whereas viral RNA exhibited a substantial m5C signal. Moreover, the m5C modification level in the viral RNAs from the CWMVm1- or CWMVm2-infected wheat plants was significantly lower than that from the CWMV-infected wheat plants (Fig. 4A). Overexpressing of TaNSUN2 in wheat plants (L3 and L6 lines) did not restore the m5C modification level in the S1 of CWMVm1 and S2 of CWMVm2 (Fig. 4B). MST assay was used to evaluate the binding affinity of TaNSUN2 with mS1-P (the 4438nd C base was mutated to U) and mS2-P (the 3322nd C base was mutated to G) (fig. S8C). The binding affinities of TaNSUN2 with S1-P/S2-P (Fig. 3F) served as positive controls, while GST binding to mS1-P/mS2-P acted as negative controls. The results showed that C4438 and C3322 mutations in RNA1 and RNA2 decreased the binding affinity of TaNSUN2 with viral RNAs (Fig. 4C). To further investigate the function of m5C modification on viral RNA stability, we substituted C4438 in RNA1 and C3322 in RNA2 to U and G, which designated it as RNA1(m4438) and RNA2(m3322), respectively, and then performed a transcription inhibition assay to measure the lifetime of RNA1, RNA1(m4438), RNA2, and RNA2(m3322) using actinomycin D. The results showed that RNA1(m4438) or RNA2(m3322) transcripts degraded more rapidly than RNA1 and RNA2 transcripts (Fig. 4D). It was reported that the NSUN2-catalyzed m5C modification can affect the translation efficiency of viral RNAs (29). We observed that the translation efficiencies of RdRp and CP were significantly reduced when directed by viral RNA from CWMVm1- and CWMVm2-infected plants, respectively, compared to the WT CWMV control (Fig. 4E). In addition, either the CWMVm1-or CWMVm2-infected wheat plants exhibited milder mosaic symptoms with lower viral RNA accumulation and CP expression compared to that in the CWMV-infected wheat plants (Fig. 4, F to H). These results were further confirmed in the N. benthamiana (fig. S9). In addition, CWMV, CWMVm1, and CWMVm2 were inoculated into the leaves of KO-1 plants, respectively. After 21 days, these plants all displayed identical mosaic symptoms (Fig. 4I). qRT-PCR and Western blot analyses showed no significant differences in viral RNA accumulation or CP protein levels between CWMV, CWMVm1, and CWMVm2 in KO-1 plants (Fig. 4, J and K). Moreover, we investigated whether TaNSUN2 regulated m5C modification on host genes. A dot-blot assay revealed that m5C modification levels were significantly elevated in TaNSUN2-overexpressing lines but reduced in the KO and mutant lines compared to control plants. Following CWMV infection, m5C modification levels were decreased across all materials compared to those without infection (fig. S10A). We further investigated the changes in m5C methylation levels of genes associated with CWMV infection (18, 22) in Fielder and KO-1 plants using RNA-BisSeq (table S4). A total of 16,901 m5C sites in 3927 wheat genes was identified using RNA-BisSeq, based on the criteria of ratio = 0 in KO-1 and ratio ≥ 0.0002 in Fielder. Among these genes, TaHSP70 has been demonstrated to participate directly in CWMV replication (22). Therefore, we selected TaHSP70 for further study. The m5C-IP-qRT-PCR analysis showed that TaHSP70 methylation levels were higher in L3 and L6 lines than in Fielder plants, while they were lower in KO-1 and KO-2 lines compared to Fielder plants (fig. S10B). In addition, TaNSUN2 induced TaHSP70 mRNA modification level (fig. S10C), and mutating the m5C modification sites in the TaHSP70 reduced the stability of TaHSP70 transcripts (fig. S10D).

TaNSUN2-mediated m5C modification was involved in viral life cycle

To determine how TaNSUN2 affects CWMV infection in plants, we investigated the subcellular localization pattern of TaNSUN2. TaNSUN2-GFP was expressed alone or together with CWMV in N. benthamiana leaves via agroinfiltration. After growing at 15°C for 6 days, the infiltrated N. benthamiana leaves were harvested and examined under a confocal microscopy. The result showed that TaNSUN2-GFP was localized predominantly in the nucleus. However, in the presence of CWMV infection, TaNSUN2-GFP is localized in both the nucleus and cytoplasm as punctate (Fig. 5A). These punctate appeared similar to the VRCs. To elucidate the composition of these punctate, we further expressed TaNSUN2-RFP transiently in the B2:GFP double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) reporter N. benthamiana leaf tissues (30–32). The result showed that TaNSUN2-RFP colocalized with B2-GFP in the punctate in CWMV-infected cells (Fig. 5B). To investigate how TaNSUN2 enters these VRCs, we analyzed the interactions between TaNSUN2 and CWMV-encoded proteins through yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) assays. The results showed that TaNSUN2 did not interact with the CWMV-encoded proteins in yeast cells (fig. S11). We then investigated TaNSUN2-interacted proteins in the CWMV-inoculated wheat protoplasts through LC-MS/MS with five independent biological replicates. TaeEF1A (TraesCS4D02G197200) was coimmunoprecipitated with TaNSUN2 across all five biological replicates (table S5). Then, we focused on verifying interactions between TaeEF1A and TaNSUN2. We used a split luciferase complementation imaging (LCI) assay and GST pull-down assay to confirm the interaction between TaeEF1A and TaNSUN2 (Fig. 5, C and D). It is known that NbeEF1A (XM_009784954) shares 98% amino acid identity with TaeEF1A (17) (fig. S12A). Our LCI assay result showed that TaNSUN2 and NbeEF1A interacted in N. benthamiana leaf cells (fig. S12B). To investigate whether TaeEF1A could target TaNSUN2 into CWMV VRCs, we transgenic silenced the NbeEF1A in the dsRNA reporter N. benthamiana B2-GFP plants and constructed a B2:GFP/nbeef1aRNAi line. Next, we expressed TaNSUN2-RFP in the CWMV-infected B2:GFP/nbeef1aRNAi N. benthamiana leaves. Six days later, TaNSUN2-RFP was found to localize separately from B2-GFP under the confocal microscope (Fig. 5E and fig. S12, C and D). In contrast to the coexpression of β-glucuronidase (GUS)–Flag and TaNSUN2-RFP in CWMV-infected B2:GFP/nbeef1aRNAi N. benthamiana leaves, coexpression of TaeEF1A-Flag and TaNSUN2-RFP led to punctate colocalization of TaNSUN2-RFP with B2-GFP (Fig. 5E). Analysis of the function of m5C modification on the interaction between TaeEF1A and the RNA2 3′UTR through MST showed that the Kd value of the TaeEF1A and S2-P interaction was 15.57 nM, while the Kd value of the TaeEF1A and S2-P (C3322 with m5C) (fig. S12E) interaction was 2.48 nM. The above results indicate that the m5C modification of S2 enhances the interaction between TaeEF1A and S2 (Fig. 5F). As CP could specifically bind to RNA elements in the viral genome to regulate viral infection process (33), we then investigated the interaction between CP and S2. RIP-qRT-PCR analysis demonstrated that CP interacts with S2, but this interaction was significantly attenuated by the C3322 mutation (Fig. 5G). The result of MST showed that CP–maltose-binding protein (MBP) could directly interact with S2-P derived from S2 (Kd value = 43.69 nM). However, C3322 labeled with m5C in S2-P derived from S2 enhanced the interaction between CP and S2 (Kd value = 4.56 nM) (Fig. 5H). Moreover, we performed RNA structural probing assays on synthetic S1 and S2 RNAs with and without m5C modification at specific sites (C4438 for S1 and C3322 for S2). The RNAs were treated with nucleases T1, S1 (single-strand selective), and V1 (double-strand selective). The results revealed that m5C modification significantly altered the digestion patterns of both S1 and S2, particularly with nuclease S1. Notably, m5C modification in S2 also affected the digestion pattern with nuclease V1 (Fig. 5I). Furthermore selective 2′-hydroxyl acylation analyzed by primer extension and mutational profiling (SHAPE-MaP) (34) was used for the analysis and identification of RNA structures. Wheat plants were infected with CWMV, CWMVm1, or CWMVm2 and treated with the structure-probing reagent 2-methylnicotinic acid imidazolide (NAI). RNA was then extracted, followed by reverse transcription, library construction, and deep sequencing. The resulting reads were aligned to the CWMV genomes. As expected, NAI-treated samples exhibited elevated mutation rates compared to untreated controls (Fig. 5, J and K). To characterize the folding patterns of the CWMV genome in vivo, we calculated median SHAPE reactivities and Shannon entropies using sliding windows and constructed a genome-wide SHAPE structure map (Fig. 5, J and K). The results revealed that the median SHAPE reactivity and Shannon entropy in the RdRp region containing the m5C site C4438 in RNA1 (0.48 and 0.09, respectively) were lower than in the RNA1(m4438) (3.15 and 0.12, respectively) (Fig. 5J). A similar pattern was observed in the RNA2 3′UTR compared with RNA2(m3322) 3′UTR (raised from 0.44 and 0.08 to 0.47 and 0.18, respectively), which contains the m5C site C3322 (Fig. 5K).

A natural variant of TaNSUN2 confers resistance to CWMV infection

To investigate whether TaNSUN2 plays a role in wheat resistance to CWMV in the field, we first analyzed TaNSUN2 sequences in 406 wheat varieties. The results showed a total of five nonsynonymous single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in the TaNSUN2 gene: (i) a G to A substitution at the position 1462 and causes a valine to isoleucine change, (ii) a C to T substitution at the position 1667 and causes a proline to leucine change, (iii) a C to G substitution at the position 2275 and causes a glutamine to glutamate change, (iv) a C to T substitution at the position 2464 and causes a leucine to phenylalanine change, and (v) an A to G substitution at the position 2566 and causes a methionine to valine change (Fig. 6A). The TaNSUN2_Hap1 (named as TaNSUN2S) exhibited much stronger susceptibility to CWMV infection than the TaNSUN2_Hap2 (named as TaNSUN2R) (Fig. 6B). On the basis of the genotypic variations identified, we performed a genome-wide association study (GWAS) to pinpoint candidate genes associated with CWMV resistance. The analysis revealed a significant association between TaNSUN2 and CWMV resistance across two independent association panels. Population 1 consisted of 243 wheat varieties genotyped using the Wheat 660K SNP array, while population 2 included 163 varieties genotyped with the Wheat 90K SNP array (Fig. 6C). The consistent association signals observed in both populations strongly implicate TaNSUN2 as a key gene involved in CWMV resistance. To assess the interactions between TaeEF1A and the two TaNSUN2 variants (TaNSUN2S and TaNSUN2R), we performed LCI and MST assays. The results indicated that TaNSUN2S, but not TaNSUN2R, interacts with TaeEF1A (Fig. 6, D and E). Further analysis using the pNSUN2:TaNSUN2R-RFP vector showed that TaNSUN2R-RFP did not colocalize with B2-GFP in the CWMV-infected cells, possibly because TaNSUN2R-RFP was unable to enter the CWMV VRCs (Fig. 6F). We selected three CWMV-susceptible (CH-3, CH-55, and CH-75) and three CWMV-resistant (CH-17, CH-47, and CH-186) wheat varieties for inoculation with CWMV under controlled conditions. At 21 dpi, CH-17, CH-47, and CH-186 displayed significantly milder disease symptoms than the susceptible varieties (Fig. 6G). Subsequently, m5C-RIP-PCR experiments were conducted on these six wheat varieties. The result showed that the m5C modification only occurred on the RNAs from the three CWMV-susceptible wheat varieties (Fig. 6H). To investigate the roles of the five SNPs in the two haplotypes, we performed site-directed mutagenesis using the TaNSUN2 from the TaNSUN2S haplotype. The resulting five mutants were (i) a G to A substitution at the position 1462 (named as TaNSUN2S-1462G>A), (ii) a G to T substitution at the position 1667 (TaNSUN2S-1667G>T), (iii) a C to G substitution at the position 2275 (TaNSUN2S-2275C>G), (iv) a C to T substitution at the position 2464 (TaNSUN2S-2464C>T), and (v) a A to G substitution at the position 2566 (TaNSUN2S-2566A>G). Subsequently, the TaNSUN2 of the TaNSUN2S haplotype or the TaNSUN2R haplotype and each of the five site-directed mutants were cloned into the C-terminal fusion RFP vector to express TaNSUN2S-RFP, TaNSUN2R-RFP, TaNSUN2S-1462G >A-RFP, TaNSUN2S-1667G>T-RFP, TaNSUN2S-2275C>G-RFP, TaNSUN2S-2464C>T-RFP, and TaNSUN2S-2566A>G-RFP, respectively. These recombinant expression vectors were individually cotransfected with the full-length CWMV infectious clone into wheat protoplasts via polyethylene glycol (PEG)–mediated method. Then, the protoplasts were harvested and extracted for total RNA or protein, followed by qRT-PCR and Western blot assays, respectively. The result of qRT-PCR revealed that in the presence of TaNSUN2R-RFP, the accumulation level of CWMV RNA was reduced when compared to that in the protoplasts overexpressing TaNSUN2S-RFP. Among the five site-directed TaNSUN2S-RFP mutants, the accumulation level of CWMV RNA in the protoplasts expressing TaNSUN2S-2566A>G-RFP was the lowest (Fig. 6I). The same result was also found for the CWMV CP (Fig. 6J). Expression of the other four site-directed TaNSUN2S-RFP mutants also inhibited the accumulation of CWMV RNA and CP in wheat protoplasts, indicating that these five SNP sites, especially the SNP at the position 2566, play important roles in wheat resistance to CWMV infection. Furthermore, in the protoplasts expressing one of the five site-directed mutants, especially the TaNSUN2S-2566A>G-RFP, the level of m5C modification in the S2 was much lower than that in the protoplasts expressing the TaNSUN2S-RFP (Fig. 6K). In the presence of TaNSUN2S-RFP, the lifetime of S2 was longer than that of the S2 coexpressed with TaNSUN2R-RFP. Among the treatments using each of the five site-directed mutants, the S2 transcripts showed the shortest lifetime when they were coexpressed with TaNSUN2S-2566A>G-RFP (Fig. 6L).

Mutation of TaNSUN2 improves seed quality

Previous study showed that loss of function of NSUN2 in Arabidopsis or rice leads to shorter root lengths (24, 35, 36). Accordingly, KO-1 and KO-2 or tansun2-D revealed normal root length compared to control plants (fig. S13, A and B). However, the root length in the tansun2-B was significantly reduced compared to control plants (fig. S13, A and B). This phenotype variation was observed in all editing combinations that included the B homoeolog (fig. S13, A and B). In crop breeding, high levels of stress resistance may reduce grain yield. Thus, we investigated the function of TaNSUN2 on wheat development and productivity. In the trial nurseries, the T3 generation of KO-1 and KO-2 exhibited similar plant height, panicle length, and spikelet number per panicle to Fielder plants after CWMV infection (Fig. 7, A to F). The T3 generation of KO-1 and KO-2 lines was found to produce grains with higher thousand-grain weight and larger size than the grains produced by Fielder plants in viral infection filed (Fig. 7, G to J). For example, the CWMV-infected KO-1 and KO-2 T3 generation lines produced an additional 5.86 and 5.92 g of thousand-grain weight (Fig. 7G). The grain width of the two mutant lines was also significantly increased than that from the Fielder plants (4.09 or 4.16 mm versus 3.72 mm) (Fig. 7, H and I). Moreover, the grain length of the two mutant lines increased by 0.74 and 0.81 mm, respectively (Fig. 7, J and K). In addition, compared to the WT Fielder, the KO-1 and KO-2 T3 generation lines exhibited increases in yield per plant of 14.58 and 11.82%, respectively (Fig. 7L). Yield per square meter at the Yangzhou site also increased significantly by 13.76 and 13.58% (Fig. 7M), respectively. These results suggested that KO of TaNSUN2 expression can not only enhance wheat resistance to CWMV infection but also improve grain yield in the trial nurseries.

DISCUSSION

Multiple members of human NSUN2, Arabidopsis TRM4B, and rice NSUN2 have been reported as m5C methyltransferases (24, 35, 36). Through phylogenetic analysis, we found that wheat TaNSUN2 shares a high degree of identity with human NSUN2, Arabidopsis TRM4B, and rice OsNSUN2 (Fig. 1A). Because the expression of TaNSUN2-5A was significantly induced in the CWMV-infected wheat (Fig. 1B), we speculated that TaNSUN2 might have a role in wheat response to CWMV infection. Through a dot-blot assay using a m5C antibody, we found that transient overexpression of TaNSUN2 in wheat protoplasts increases the m5C modification level in mRNAs (Fig. 1E and fig. S6D), while silencing of TaNSUN2 expression decreases the m5C modification level in mRNAs (Fig. 1, F and G). In addition, our in vitro methylation assay result demonstrates that TaNSUN2-GST catalyzes the m5C modification in an RNA probe with the most prevalent m5C motifs reported in a previous study (24), indicating that TaNSUN2 has the m5C methyltransferase activity (Fig. 1, H and I). Previous reports have indicated that the methyltransferase activity of NSUN2 depends on two conserved sites (4). Mutations introduced to either of the two cysteine residues abolish the methyltransferase activity of NSUN2 (37, 38). In this study, we also found two conserved cysteine sites in TaNSUN2, and the TaNSUN2 with mutations at these two cysteine sites loses its m5C methyltransferase activity (Fig. 1I and fig. S2). In mammals, it has been reported that NSUN2 has m6A modification ability for pre–microRNA (miRNA), affecting pre-miRNA function (25–27). However, TaNSUN2 does not have the capacity for m6A modification (fig. S4). This result might be due to low activity level of TaNSUN2 in m6A modification or difference function of NSUN2 between mammal and plant cells. Many reports have indicated that the loss or depletion of NSUN2 inhibits the infections of many mammalian viruses (7, 8, 10, 39). However, the function of NSUN2 in plant viral infection remains unknown. In our study, we found that overexpression of TaNSUN2 in wheat promoted CWMV infection, while TaNSUN2 KO in wheat significantly suppressed CWMV infection (Fig. 2 and fig. S5). In addition, we found that in the presence of TaNSUN2, but not TaNSUN2C285/335A, the accumulation of CWMV in wheat protoplasts was restored (Fig. 2, I and J). Consequently, we conclude that TaNSUN2-mediated m5C modification plays a crucial role for plant viral infection.

Although the function of NSUN2-mediated m5C modification in plant viral infection was still unknown, RNA methylation in mammalian viruses, especially in nuclear-replicating viruses, is well documented (39). However, recent study showed that N6-methyladenosine modification is not a general trait of viral RNA genomes (40, 41) and indicated that the results of m5C on viral RNA may be false positive too. In this study, we confirmed that TaNSUN2 mediated m5C modification of the CWMV genome through multiple methods, including RNA-BisSeq, Northern blot, dot-blot, and mass spectrometry assays (Figs. 3, A to C, and 4A). These methods have been widely used for functional analysis of m5C in mammalian viruses (7–10, 42). We used IVT viral RNAs as negative controls to calibrate false-positive signals. Our results showed that, compared to the IVT controls, CWMV genomic RNA exhibited significant m5C modification characteristics (Figs. 3C and 4A and fig. S7C). Accordingly, these results suggested that m5C modification is also present in plant RNA virus, at least in the CWMV genome that replicates in the cytoplasm. Then, we analyzed the distribution of m5C methylation on CWMV genomic RNAs and identified two sites, i.e., C4438 site in RNA1 and C3322 site in RNA2, with higher methylation rate (Fig. 3C and table S3). Subsequent functional analysis showed that TaNSUN2 not only interacts with S1 and S2, which contain the C3322 and C4438 sites, respectively, but also promotes m5C enrichment at these segments. It has been reported that m5C plays an important role in RNA export, stability, and translation (43). We then introduced mutations into CWMV infectious clones to investigate the potential roles of m5C-modified sites in the CWMV life cycle. The results showed that mutation at these two sites reduced TaNSUN2 binding ability, RNA stability, and translational efficiency, thereby slowing viral infection (Fig. 4, B to H). These findings indicate that TaNSUN2-mediated m5C modification enhances plant virus replication by stabilizing viral RNA and improving translation efficiency. In mammalian viruses, the role of m5C modification mediated by NSUN2 is paradoxical. NSUN2 depletion impairs replication of HIV-1, HBV, and Enterovirus 71 (EV71) by reducing RNA stability and translation efficience (7, 8, 10, 39). Conversely, NSUN2 KO reduces m5C levels in severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 RNA but enhances viral replication (42). These contrasting effects highlight the complex regulatory roles of m5C modification in different viral systems and underscore the need for further investigation into the underlying mechanisms. Moreover, no significant differences in viral RNA accumulation or CP levels were observed among CWMV-, CWMVm1-, and CWMVm2-inoculated leaves of KO-1 plants. T. aestivum heat shock protein 70 (TaHSP70), a known promoter of CWMV infection, undergoes m5C methylation in a TaNSUN2-dependent manner. On the basis of these findings, our findings demonstrate that TaNSUN2-mediated m5C modification plays a dual role in viral infection and directly enhanced viral RNA m5C modification to facilitate viral pathogenesis while indirectly regulating viral infection through host RNA metabolic modulation.

Under the confocal microscope, TaNSUN2-RFP was found to colocalize with B2-GFP in VRCs in the CWMV-infected cells (Fig. 5, A and B). Previous reports have indicated that eEF1A can recruit viral proteins to form VRCs (44) and can bind directly to the 3′UTRs of CWMV RNAs (17). Moreover, RNA viruses are also known to hijack host factors to remodel organelle membranes to form multifunctional VRCs during virus infections (45). Our results showed that TaNSUN2 interacts with TaeEF1A and NbeEF1A both in vivo and in vitro (Fig. 5, C and D, and fig. S12B). Coexpression of TaeEF1A-Flag and TaNSUN2-RFP in B2:GFP/nbeef1aRNAi plants resulted in colocalization of TaNSUN2-RFP and B2-GFP in punctate structures (Fig. 5E). However, TaNSUN2 is spatially separated from B2-GFP in transgenic plants with silenced NbeEF1A (Fig. 5E). These results indicated that TaNSUN2 is likely targeted to the CWMV VRCs through direct interaction with TaeEF1A. Moreover, the binding of TaeEF1A to the CWMV RNA2 3′UTR is enhanced by m5C modification to promote CWMV replication in cells (Fig. 5F). According to the published mammalian transcriptomes, histone H3 lysine 36 trimethylation (H3K36me3) is recognized and bound by methyltransferase-like 14 (METTL14), a crucial component in the m6A methyltransferase complex (MTC), which in known to facilitate the binding of m6A MTC to RNA polymerase II. This binding allows m6A MTC to actively transcribe the nascent RNAs and to deposit m6A cotranscriptionally (46). The m6A modification of coding sequences (CDSs) and 3′UTR is dynamically and precisely regulated through the cross-talk between histone modifications and RNA methylation. As the m5C sites have been found to distribute across different coding regions and the UTRs of CWMV RNAs (Fig. 3C), we speculated that m5C modification at these sites may rely on interactions between TaNSUN2 and other host factor(s). It has been confirmed that the interaction between CP and RNAs can regulate RNA synthesis, translation, RNA encapsulation, and the formation of viruslike particles (33). The C3322 mutation in RNA2 reduced the interaction between CP and S2, while m5C modification enhanced this interaction (Fig. 5, G and H), suggesting that TaNSUN2-mediated m5C modification is involved in CP-regulated viral infection processes. Previous studies have shown that RNA epigenetic modifications can regulate the structural shaping of RNA-protein interactions (47). RNA structural probing assays and SHAPE-MaP showed that TaNSUN2-mediated m5C modification in S1 and S2 alters the secondary structures of viral genomic segments (Fig. 5, I and J). Accordingly, our results suggest that TaNSUN2-mediated m5C modification may alter the structure of CWMV genomic segments, thereby facilitating interactions between TaeEF1A or CP and viral RNAs and promoting viral infection.

In general, plant genes that facilitate pathogen infection and support compatibility between plant and pathogen can considered susceptibility (S) genes (48). Because overexpressing of TaNSUN2 in plants increases their susceptibility to CWMV infection while KO lines of TaNSUN2 expression results in a reduced viral infection (Fig. 2, B to G), we propose that TaNSUN2 is an S gene in wheat and can mediate wheat resistance to CWMV infection. The S genes are often known to affect plant growth and development. Therefore, disruption of S gene functions may cause yield penalties (49). However, in this study, we found that the TaNSUN2 KO lines are more resistant to CWMV infection and produce higher yield, due mainly to higher thousand-grain weight and larger grain size (Fig. 7, A to J). Wheat genome has three copies of TaNSUN2 (i.e., TaNSUN2-5A, TaNSUN2-5B, and TaNSUN2-5D). However, upon CWMV infection, only the expression of TaNSUN2-5A was induced (Fig. 1B), suggesting that TaNSUN2-5A is also involved in wheat response to CWMV infection. Notably, we identified potential NAC transcription factor binding sites predominantly in the promoter of TaNSUN2-5A, which are largely absent in the promoters of -5B and -5D (fig. S1B). As NAC transcription factors are well-established mediators of transcriptional reprogramming in response to various biotic stresses, including viral infections (50), we hypothesize that a virus-responsive NAC factor activates the expression of TaNSUN2-5A specifically. Because TaNSUN2-5A is also involved in grain development, it should consider as two major targets for wheat breeding (51). Natural disease resistance in many crops is controlled by multiple alleles in the genome. For example, a natural allele of the rice C2H2-type transcription factor confers an enhanced resistance to the blast disease (52). In this study, we also identified a natural variant (TaNSUN2R) of TaNSUN2 in the CWMV-resistant wheat cultivars. This variant does not interact with TaeEF1A and is unable to enter the VRCs during CWMV infection. The lack of entering into the VRCs prevents the m5C modification of the viral RNAs, thereby strengthening the host defense against CWMV (Fig. 6, G to L). A significant discovery made in this study is that KO of TaNSUN2-5A expression in wheat increases the size and weight of wheat grain. This important finding has not been reported previously. Previous studies have established that loss of function of TRM4B in Arabidopsis and NSUN2 in rice results in impaired root growth. Our findings reveal that TaNSUN2-5A is preferentially expressed in leaves, whereas TaNSUN2-5B is markedly up-regulated in roots. In contrast, TaNSUN2-5D showed only a modest increase in leaves and remained at relatively low levels in roots (Fig. 1C). Consistent with these expression patterns, root length was predominantly controlled by the B homoeolog (TaNSUN2-5B), as KO of TaNSUN2-5B significantly reduced root length, whereas disruption of the A or D homoeolog had no measurable effect (fig. S13). By contrast, the A homoeolog (TaNSUN2-5A) plays a critical role in viral infection, as its KO conferred strong resistance to CWMV (fig. S6E). Together, these results indicate that the functional specialization of TaNSUN2 homoeologs enables targeted KO of TaNSUN2-5A to enhance virus resistance while avoiding the adverse effects on root growth associated with loss of TaNSUN2-5B, thereby providing a favorable strategy for improving disease resistance and yield-related traits. Moreover, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes analysis of the RNA-BisSeq data from KO-1 and Fielder linked several of these genes to seed development pathways, such as photosynthesis, the calvin cycle, and the pentose phosphate pathway (table S5). These results indicate that TaNSUN2 is involved in plant seed development pathways. However, the molecular mechanisms involved in TaNSUN2 regulating plant development are largely unknown and need to be further investigated. Consequently, we believe that this natural TaNSUN2 variant has great potential for use in breeding for CWMV resistance.

In conclusion, our findings establish TaNSUN2-mediated m5C methylation as a previously uncharacterized regulatory strategy in the arms race between wheat and RNA viruses. In susceptible cultivars cell, TaNSUN2 is recruited into the CWMV VRCs through a direct interaction with TaeEF1A. Inside VRCs, TaNSUN2-mediated m5C modification in CWMV RNAs could stabilize viral RNA and increase translational efficiency. Meanwhile, the m5C methylation in 3′UTR of CWMV RNA also promotes viral assembly and genome packaging. On the other hand, the natural variants of TaNSUN2 in the CWMV-resistant cultivars have lower binding affinities for TaeEF1A and are correlated with differing levels of resistance to CWMV infection in wheat (Fig. 7L). The findings described in this paper provide important insight into the function of RNA m5C modification in plant RNA virus infections. The molecular mechanism underlying this RNA m5C modification–mediated plant resistance requires further investigations.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Plant growth conditions

N. benthamiana plants were grown in a controlled growth chamber maintained constantly at 25° ± 2°C, 65 ± 5% relative humidity, and a 16-hour light and 8-hour dark photoperiod. Wheat cv. Fielder (CWMV susceptible) was used as this study and was grown inside a greenhouse maintained at 25° ± 2°C. After rub inoculation of CWMV, the four-leaf–stage wheat seedlings were grown inside a growth chamber maintained at 15°C and a 16-hour light and 8-hour dark photoperiod. Field trials were conducted using cv. Fielder and transgenic wheat lines in a nursery at Yangzhou, Jiangsu Province. This nursery is known to have several CWMV outbreaks in 2021 to 2024.

Plasmid constructions

Full-length CDSs of CWMV p153, RdRP, MP, N-CP, CP, CP-RT, and CRP were individually PCR amplified from a CWMV infectious clone (15). The full-length TaNSUN2S and TaeEF1A sequences were individually PCR amplified from a Fielder-derived cDNA, while the full-length TaNSUN2R sequence was amplified from a wheat cv. CH186–derived cDNA. The mutated CWMV RNA1 (called m4438), CWMV RNA2 (called m3322), TaNSUN2285/335C-A, and several other TaNSUN2S mutants (i.e., TaNSUN2S-1462G>A, TaNSUN2S-1667G>T, TaNSUN2S-2275C>G, TaNSUN2S-2464C>T, and TaNSUN2S-2566A>G) were individually generated using an overlapping PCR technique. The CDSs of individual CWMV proteins and TaNSUN2 were individually amplified and cloned into the pGADT7/pGBKT7 vector using a one-step PCR cloning method. The TaeEF1A-His–expressing vector was generated through integration of its CDS into the pET-32a vector via a one-step PCR cloning method, while the TaNSUN2-GST and TaNSUN2285/335C-A-GST expression vectors were generated through insertion of their CDSs into the pGEX4T-2 vector, respectively. In addition, the CDSs of TaeEF1A, TaNSUN2, and TaNSUNR were individually inserted into the pCambia1300-n/cLUC vector to produce pTaeEF1A-nLUC, pTaNSUN2-cLUC, and pTaNSUNR-cLUC, respectively, through a streamlined one-step cloning process. An ~2-kb sequence, corresponding to the upstream region of TaNSUN2 promoter, was used to replace the 35S promoter in the p35S:TaNSUN2-GFP (pGWB505) vector to create pNSUN2:TaNSUN2-GFP.

Wheat protoplast isolation and transfection

Wheat protoplasts were isolated from the leaves of 1- to 2-week-old Fielder seedlings as previously described (18). Construct pNSUN2:TaNSUN2-GFP and pNSUN2:GFP were individually cotransfected with p35S-H3-RFP (a nuclear marker vector) into wheat protoplasts. After overnight incubation at room temperature (RT), the transfected protoplasts were examined under a Leica TCS SP8 confocal laser scanning microscope as described (18, 28).

Virus inoculation and agroinfiltration

Wheat seedlings were rub inoculated with IVT CWMV RNAs as previously described (18). Briefly, the second leaf of individual 1- to 2-week-old seedlings was rub inoculated with CWMV transcripts (1 μg of RNA transcript per leaf). The inoculated plants were then grown inside a controlled climate house maintained at 15° ± 2°C. In other experiments, wheat seedlings were inoculated with a CWMV infectious clone through agroinfiltration as previously described (18).

m5C dot-blot assay

Quantification of m5C levels was performed using a reported method (36, 53). Briefly, RNA samples or purified CWMV virions were denatured for 5 min at 95°C and then transferred onto Hybond-N+ membranes. The membranes were cross-linked using ultraviolet light. After 10 min of incubation in a 5% nonfat milk solution, the membranes were individually probed with an anti-m5C antibody (Abcam, catalog no. ab10805, Cambridge, UK), followed by 60 min of incubation in a 1:500 diluted horseradish peroxidase (HRP)–conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody solution (Zsbio, catalog no. ZB2305, Beijing, China). The detection signal was visualized using the Immobilon Western HPR Substrate Luminol Reagent (Merck Millipore, Burlington, US). RNA samples stained with 0.1% Methylene Blue (Solarbio, catalog no. G1300, Beijing, China) were used to show sample loadings. The accumulation level of CWMV CP in samples was determined using a CWMV CP antibody. Each experiment was repeated at least three times with similar results.

Western blot assay

Leaf samples were homogenized in a lysis buffer composed of 10% SDS, 2% β-mercaptoethanol, and 100 mM tris-HCl (pH 8.8). Individual leaf samples were mixed with a SDS loading buffer, boiled for 10 min, and analyzed in SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) gels through electrophoretic separation, followed by transferring onto nitrocellulose membranes. The membranes were probed with specific primary antibodies and then an HRP-conjugated anti-mouse or anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody as instructed (Abbkine Scientific, California, USA). All primers used in this study are listed in table S4.

RNA extraction and qRT-PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from individual tissue samples using the plant RNA extraction kit (Magen, Guangzhou, China), followed by cDNA synthesis using the ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Master Mix (TOYOBO, Osaka, Japan). The resulting cDNAs were further analyzed through qRT-PCR using the ABI Q5 Sequence Detection System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) along with the SYBR Green qRT-PCR kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Each qRT-PCR reaction included at least three biological replicates. The expression level of wheat cell division cycle (CDC) gene (XM_020313450) in each sample was also detected and used as the internal control. The fold changes of the assayed genes were individually determined using the 2–ΔΔCt method. The primers used in the qRT-PCR assays are listed in table S6.

In vitro methylation assay

The methylation assay was conducted according to the established protocols (28). Two RNA probes were synthesized for this assay. One probe is enriched with the m5C motifs as previously described (24) (i.e., AAC CAA ACC AAA CCA AAC CAC UCC CUC CCU CCC UCC UCC), while the other probe is without cytosine (i.e., AAG GAA AGG AAA GGA AAG GAG UGG GUG GGU GGG UGG UGG). The full-length CDSs of TaNSUN2 and TaNSUN2285/335C-A were amplified and cloned, respectively, into the pGEX-4 T-2 vector (GE HealthCare, Beijing, China). These two vectors were transformed individually into Escherichia coli strain BL21 cells to express TaNSUN2-GST and TaNSUN2285/335C-A-GST fusions. The expressed proteins were purified via affinity chromatography. Each methylation assay was done in a 50-μl reaction containing 0.15 nmol of each RNA probe (synthesized by Sangon, Shanghai, China), 0.15 nmol of purified GST (control), TaNSUN2-GST or TaNSUN2285/335C-A-GST, 0.8 mM SAM (Sigma-Aldrich, Burlington, US), 80 mM KCl, 1.5 mM MgCl2, RNasin (0.2 U/μl), 10 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), 4% glycerol, and 15 mM Hepes buffer (pH 7.9). Before the initiation of the methylation reaction, the samples were heated at 90°C for 3 min, and 40 cycles of gradual cooling (cooling 2°C per cycle) within 30 min, followed by an incubation at 16°C for 12 hours. RNA was extracted from each sample using the phenol/chloroform method and then the ethanol precipitation method.

Generation of transgenic wheat lines

Plasmid pNSUN2:TaNSUN2 and pGWMB110 were cointroduced into immature wheat embryos through particle bombardment. Plasmid pGWMB110 harbors a bialaphos resistance gene (bar) that can be used as a selection marker as described (54). To generate TaNSUN2 KO mutant wheat lines, single guide RNAs (sgRNAs) specific to the three copies of TaNSUN2 were synthesized and cloned into a CRISPR-Cas9 expression vector. The resulting vector was used to transform Fielder immature embryos. Transgenic wheat seedlings were selected through a combination of PCR and restriction enzyme (RE) assays as described (55). Briefly, genomic DNA was isolated from the assayed plants and analyzed through PCR using a high-fidelity DNA polymerase and primers specific for TaNSUN2-5A, TaNSUN2-5B, and TaNSUN2-5D, respectively. The amplified segments were cleaved using the restriction enzyme NcoI. The presence of uncleaved PCR products indicates the successful deletions of the NcoI site in the gRNA-targeted regions in the original TaNSUN2 open reading frame. The selected wheat lines were further confirmed through DNA sequencing. The primers are also listed in table S3.

Subcellular localization

Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 cultures, harboring specific expression vectors, were individually pelleted through 30 s of centrifugation at 8000 rpm. The pelleted cells were resuspended in a solution containing 10 mM MES (pH 5.6), 10 mM MgCl2, and 200 mM acetosyringone till optical density at 600 nm = 0.4. After infiltration of A. tumefaciens cultures into the leaves of N. benthamiana plants, the plants were incubated at 15°C for 72 hours. Fluorescent signals from the expressed proteins were examined under a Leica TCS SP8 confocal laser scanning microscope as described (28).

Ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography–multiple reaction monitoring (UHPLC-MRM)–MS/MS analysis of m5C level

To quantify the m5C/C ratio, purified RNA was enzymatically digested into individual ribonucleosides using the method reported previously (24). After the digestion, the samples were cleaned using the spin-filter cleanup method, followed by the analyses using an Agilent 6490 Triple Quadrupole mass spectrometer in the LC-MS/MS mode. A mass transition of 258.1 to 126.1 was considered as the ribonucleoside m5C, while a mass transition of 244 to 112 was considered as the ribonucleoside C.

IP of m5C mRNA

The m5C RIP assay was conducted as described previously (24). Initially, 200 μg of total RNA extracted from CWMV-infected wheat plants was fragmented into ~300–nucleotide (nt)–long pieces in the fragmentation buffer (Ambion, catalog no. AM8740, Austin, USA) at 70°C for 15 min. The fragmented RNA was precipitated in ethanol. Five milligrams of anti-m5C antibody (Abcam, catalog no. AB10805) was preincubated with 40 μl of Dynabeads Protein A (Invitrogen, catalog no. 10001D, Carlsbad, USA) in a 500 μl of immunoprecipitation (IP) buffer [150 mM NaCl, 0.1% NP-40, and 10 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.4)] for 1 hour at RT. The fragmented RNA samples were pretreated at 70°C for 15 min and then added individually into the bead-antibody mixture, followed by incubation at 4°C for 4 hours with gentle rotation. The beads were washed three times with the IP buffer and then eluted twice with the IP buffer containing m5C (0.5 mg/ml) and RNasin (Promega, Madison, USA) at 4°C for 1 hour with gentle rotation. The m5C-enriched RNAs were extracted using the phenol/chloroform extraction and ethanol precipitation methods. The untreated fragmented RNA (20 μg per sample) was used as the input. Reactions without the m5C antibody was used as the negative controls. The enrichment of an immunoprecipitated RNA sample relative to the input was presented as the fold enrichment.

TaNSUN2-RIP-qRT-PCR assay

The CWMV-infected leaves were fixed in a 1% formaldehyde solution for 30 min on ice and under vacuum. The fixation was halted through addition of 150 mM glycine to the reaction and then incubated for 5 min on ice. The fixed leaf samples were pulverized in liquid nitrogen and then individually homogenized in 5 ml of lysis buffer [50 mM Hepes, (pH 7.5), 150 mM KCl, 0.5% NP-40, 2 mM EDTA, 0.5 mM DTT, 1× protease inhibitor cocktail, and ribonuclease (RNase; 200 U/ml) inhibitor]. The homogenized samples were rotated for 2 hours at 4°C, followed by 20 min of centrifugation at 15,000g at 4°C. An aliquot of supernatant (500 μl per sample) was collected as the input, while the remaining supernatant was subjected to an overnight IP at 4°C using an anti-TaNSUN2 polyclonal antibody or a rabbit IgG as instructed (Invitrogen). Approximately 60 μl of Dynabeads Protein-A (Synaptic Systems, Göttingen, Germany) was added to each IP mixture, followed by 2 hours of incubation at 4°C. The beads were washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and the RNA-protein complexes were digested for 1 hour with proteinase K (Takara, Kusatsu, Japan) at 55°C. The immunoprecipitated RNAs and the input RNAs were reverse transcribed, followed by qRT-PCR. The results were normalized against the input levels. The IgG-immunoprecipitated RNAs were used as the negative controls.

RNA-BisSeq

CWMV RNA1 and RNA2 were transcribed in vitro using the Message T7 in vitro transcription kit (Ambion, Austin, TX, Promega, China) and then used as negative controls. High-confidence m5C sites were initially identified through comparative analysis with IVT RNA. To ensure measurement accuracy, m5C methylation levels were normalized using a computational correction method as previously described (56), and the normalized m5C methylation levels are shown in table S2. Statistical validation was performed through Fisher’s exact test. The final identification of high-confidence m5C sites followed rigorous multistage filtering criteria: (i) demonstrated false discovery rate (FDR) ≤ 0.2 in negative control comparisons, (ii) achieved statistical significance (P ≤ 0.05) in Fisher’s exact test, (iii) maintained a minimum nonconversion rate of 0.002, and (iv) had ≥10× sequencing depth across replicates.

RNA pull-down assay

The sense and anti-sense RNA 3′UTR were synthesized using the MEGAscript T7 Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and then labeled with biotin using the Pierce RNA 3′ End Desthiobiotinylation Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For RNA pull-down assay, the biotin-labeled RNAs were incubated with the purified TaNSUN2-GST or GST (control) using the Magnetic RNA-Protein Pull-Down Kit as instructed (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Translation efficiency assay

Measurement of translation efficiency was done as previously described (17) with minor modifications. Ten grams of wheat leaf tissues is ground in liquid nitrogen, followed by total RNA extraction using the plant RNA extraction kit (Magen). Eight grams of tissue from the same sample was mixed with 20 ml of polyribosome extraction buffer [200 mM tris-HCl (pH 9.0), 35 mM MgCl2, 200 mM KCl, 25 mM EGTA, 1% Triton X-100, 200 mM KCl, 1% IGEPAL CA-630, 5 mM DTT, 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, chloramphenicol (50 μg/ml), and actinomycetin (100 μg/ml)] and gently shaken at 4°C for 20 min. The samples were centrifuged twice at 4°C at 16,000g, and each time was 20 min. Supernatant (16 ml) of each sample was slowly transferred onto a 15-ml sucrose cushion [1.75 M sucrose, 400 mM tris-HCl (pH 9.0), 35 mM MgCl2, 5 mM EGTA, 200 mM KCl, 5 mM DTT, chloramphenicol (50 μg/ml), and actinomycetin (50 μg/ml)], followed by 4 hours of centrifugation at 200,000g and 4°C. The resulting supernatant was carefully removed, and each pellet was resuspended in 300 μl of diethyl pyrocarbonate–treated water. Multiribosomal RNA was isolated using the TRIzol reagent (Life Technologies) and then analyzed through qRT-PCR.

Wheat protoplast IP–LC-MS/MS analysis

Wheat protoplasts were isolated from 10- to 14-day-old seedlings as previously described (18). The CWMV infectious clones pCB-35S-R1 and pCB-35S-R2 were cotransfected with pNSUN2:TaNSUN2-GFP into wheat protoplasts using a PEG-mediated method. After overnight incubation in the dark, protoplasts were harvested, and total proteins were extracted. TaNSUN2-GFP–associated proteins were enriched by IP using anti-GFP agarose beads. Immunoprecipitated proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and visualized by Coomassie Brilliant Blue staining. Gel bands corresponding to TaNSUN2-GFP were excised and subjected to in-gel trypsin digestion, followed by LC-MS/MS analysis. Peptide separation was performed on an EASY-nLC 1200 system coupled to an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operated in data-dependent acquisition mode. Tandem mass spectra were processed using Proteome Discoverer software (version 3.1; Thermo Fisher Scientific) and searched against the wheat protein database with trypsin specified as the digestion enzyme. Carbamidomethylation of cysteine was set as a fixed modification and oxidation of methionine as a variable modification. Peptide and protein identifications were accepted at a false discovery rate of less than 1%. The IP-LC-MS/MS experiment was performed with five independent biological replicates. The candidate interacting proteins were required to be consistently detected across all five biological replicates in copurifications with TaNSUN2-GFP.

Y2H assay

An Y2H assay was used to investigate the potential interactions between different CWMV-encoded proteins and TaNSUN2. Plasmid pAD-TaNSUN2 was coinfiltrated with DNA-binding domain (BD)–P153, BD-RdRp, BD-MP, BD–N-CP, BD-CP, BD–CP-RT, or BD-CRP into yeast cells. The transformed yeast cells were cultured on the synthetic dropout (SD)/−Leu/−Trp medium for 72 hours. The transformed yeast cells were then selected and cultured on the SD/−Trp/−Leu/-His/−Ade medium for 5 to 7 days to determine the interactions. The yeast cells cotransformed with pAD-T and pBD-Lam or pAD-T and pBD-53 were used as the negative and positive controls, respectively.

LCI assay

The CDSs of TaeEF1A and TaNSUN2 were individually amplified and cloned into the Luciferase reporter vector pCambia1300-nLuc or pCambia1300-cLuc to generate the pTaeEF1A-nLuc and pTaNSUN2-cLuc vectors. Agrobacterium culture harboring the pTaeEF1A-nLuc vector was mixed with an equal volume of Agrobacterium culture carrying the pTaNSUN2-cLuc vector. The mixture was infiltrated into the leaves of N. benthamiana plants. Two days later, the leaves were infiltrated again with a 0.2 mM Luciferin solution prepared using ddH2O and 0.01% Triton X-100 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The Luciferase activity was detected using a low-light cooled charge-coupled device imaging apparatus (NightOWL II LB 983 equipped with the indiGO software). The leaves expressing empty vectors (cLUC or nLUC) were used as the negative controls. The leaves coexpressing SGF-nLUC and SAR-cLUC were used as the positive controls.

Pull-down assay

The CDSs of TaeEF1A and TaNSUN2 were individually cloned into the pET-32a or pGEX4T-2 vector to express the TaeEF1A-GST and the TaNSUN2-His fusions in E. coli strain BL21. After purification, the TaNSUN2-GST fusion was incubated with 100 ml of glutathione agarose beads (GE HealthCare) for 2 hours at 4°C. After three washes with the phosphate-buffered saline containing Tween-20 (PBST) buffer, the beads were incubated with an equal volume of bacterial lysate containing the TaeEF1A-His fusion for 2 hours at 4°C. After three washes with PBST, the proteins were detected through Western blot assays using an anti-GST or an anti-His antibody (1:5000 diluted; TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China) to determine the interaction between the two proteins.

RNA structure analysis

The RNA fold web server was used to predict the secondary structures of RNAs. The minimum free energy and partition function fold algorithms with avoiding isolated base pairs were selected.

For RNA structural probing, synthetic S1 and S2 with or without m5C modification were labeled with biotin (Pierce RNA 30 End Biotinylation Kit, 20160). A structural probing assay with RNase T1, nuclease S1, and RNase V1 was performed as previously described (47).

In vivo SHAPE-MaP in plants

The SHAPE reagent, NAI, was synthesized as previously described (57). We collected wheat leaves infected with CWMV, CWMVm1, or CWMVm2 and immediately immersed them in either a fresh 100 mM NAI solution or a dimethyl sulfoxide control. To enhance reagent permeability, samples were vacuum infiltrated and incubated at RT for 20 min. The reaction was quenched by adding dithiothreitol to a final concentration of 0.5 M, followed by a 2-min incubation. Tissues were then washed three times with water, flash frozen in liquid nitrogen, and used for subsequent virus particle purification. CWMV RNA was extracted from the purified virions, followed by reverse transcription with random primers and sequencing as described previously (34).

SHAPE-MaP data analysis

SHAPE-MaP data analysis was performed using RNA Framework v2.6.9, adapted from established protocols (58, 59). The process consisted of three main steps: (i) After the raw data underwent quality control via fastp [removal low-quality bases (Phred < 20) or reads containing internal Ns], it was aligned to the CWMV reference genome using the rf-map module (-ctn -cmn 0 -cqo -cg5 20 -b2). (ii) The CWMV genome index was built by Bowtie2, and nucleotide mutation signals were counted to generate a file containing mutation counts, coverage, and aligned fragments through rf-count module (-m -rd). (iii) The rf-norm module was used to standardize the data of the rc binary file generated in the previous step (-sm 3 -nm 3 -rb AC).

RNA secondary structure modeling

The secondary structure of the CWMV genome was folded using rf-fold module. A windowing approach was used, with 300-nt increment of the genome being slid into the analysis. SHAPE reactivities were incorporated as soft constraints to guide the structure prediction. The first and last 100 nt of each window were excluded to minimize end biases. This analysis provided secondary structures, base-pairing probabilities, and Shannon entropy to assess structural diversity.

Microscale thermophoresis assay

RNA probes used in this assay are listed in table S3. The TaNSUN2-RNA probe, TaeEF1A-TaNSUN2S, and TaeEF1A-TaNSUN2R binding affinities were individually monitored using the Monolith NT.115 (NanoTemper Technologies, Munich, Germany). MST was used to label TaeEF1A using the Monolith NT Protein Labelling Kit RED diluted in a PBS solution containing 0.05% Tween 20. The labeling protocol was described by the NanoTemper Technologies. The RNA probes were labeled with Cy5 as instructed by the Sangon. For further analysis, the samples containing the labeled protein, RNA probes, and different concentrations of ligands were loaded into the hydrophilic-treated capillaries (NanoTemper) and assessed using the NanoTemper analytical software to determine the equilibrium dissociation constants (Kd values) for the protein-RNA probes interactions.

mRNA stability assay

Specific expression vectors were transfected into wheat protoplasts, followed by 18 hours of incubation at RT. Actinomycin D (20 μg/ml; Sigma-Aldrich) was dissolved in ddH2O and then applied to the transfected protoplasts. After 30 min, the protoplasts were collected once every 3 hours, and at each collection time, three samples were taken. The samples collected immediately after the application of actinomycin D (0 hours) were used as controls. The amount of target gene mRNA was determined through qRT-PCR.

Genotyping and GWAS

A total of 243 wheat accessions in the panel I was genotyped using the Wheat 660K SNP array as reported (28), while the 163 wheat accessions in the panel II were analyzed using the Wheat 90K SNP array as reported (60). After stringent quality control measures, only the SNPs had a minor allele frequency greater than 0.05, and less than 20% missing data were retained for the further analysis (61). The GWAS was conducted using the PLINK software in the reported protocols (60). In this GWAS, the GAPIT package in the R software was used and used a mixed linear model that incorporate both the principal components analysis and the kinship matrix (K) as covariates. The significance threshold for the P values was set at 1 × 10–4 for the panel I and 1 × 10–3 for the panel II across all replications. In addition, the Haploview 4.2 software was used to analyze the haplotypes associated with the significant SNPs identified during this study.

Statistical analyses

Statistical evaluations were carried out with GraphPad Prism. Pairwise contrasts relied on two-tailed Student’s t tests, whereas multigroup comparisons were assessed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s correction. The symbol “n” indicates biological replicates unless stated otherwise. Outcomes were deemed significant at P < 0.05 and marked as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001; n.s. denotes not significant; distinct lowercase letters flag statistically separable means.
