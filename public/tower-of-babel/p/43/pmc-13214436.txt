Effects of virtual reality and robot-assisted therapy on rehabilitation outcomes in cerebral palsy: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Hang Zhou, Yunyun Zhang, Miao Nie, Lina Ge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCerebral palsy (CP) is a leading cause of lifelong physical disability in children, necessitating long-term rehabilitation. Advanced technologies such as virtual reality (VR) and robot-assisted therapy are emerging as promising adjunctive interventions. However, existing research evidence remains fragmented, with most studies focusing on a single technology or lacking comprehensive subgroup analyses. This systematic review and meta-analysis aims to synthesize current evidence regarding the efficacy of VR and robot-assisted technologies versus conventional therapy for key outcomes including motor function, balance, spasticity, joint range of motion, and activities of daily living in children with CP.MethodsThis systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook guidelines and the PRISMA statement, with a retrospective registration on PROSPERO. We searched electronic databases including PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Library, Web of Science, and CINAHL, together with clinical trial registries and grey literature sources, to identify randomized controlled trials published from 2016 to 2025. Two reviewers independently performed study selection, data extraction, and risk of bias assessment using the RoB-1 and RoB-2 tools. The GRADE approach was applied to assess the certainty of evidence. Meta-analyses were performed for primary outcomes including gross motor function, balance, spasticity, activities of daily living, and joint range of motion. Subgroup analyses were further conducted based on intervention type and CP subtype.ResultsOf the 1720 identified records, 17 randomized controlled trials were initially included. Pooled analyses of the 17 studies demonstrated that compared with conventional therapy, VR and robot-assisted therapies exerted positive effects on patients’ gross motor function (GMFM-88: SMD = 0.53, 95% CI = 0.32 to 0.75), balance ability (PBS: MD = 2.06, 95% CI = 1.17 to 2.95), and spasticity level (MAS: MD = -0.49, 95% CI = −0.82 to −0.15). A small yet statistically significant improvement was observed in the outcome of activities of daily living (PEDI-CAT Daily Activity: MD = 0.51, 95% CI = 0.27 to 0.74). No significant effect on joint range of motion was detected. Subgroup analyses suggested potential differences between VR and robot-assisted therapies. These findings were exploratory given the limited number of included studies. According to the GRADE approach, the certainty of evidence ranged from very low to moderate.ConclusionVR and robot-assisted therapies represent promising adjuncts to conventional rehabilitation for children with cerebral palsy, demonstrating benefits in gross motor function, balance, spasticity reduction, and activities of daily living. To facilitate the broader and optimal clinical application of these technologies, future research should prioritize high-quality, large-scale randomized controlled trials with long-term follow-up. These studies will help confirm these positive findings, establish optimal treatment protocols, and determine the efficacy of such therapies across different CP subtypes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12984-026-01973-0.

Introduction

Cerebral palsy (CP) is the most common cause of lifelong physical disability in children across most countries [1]. Recent epidemiological studies estimate a prevalence of approximately 1.6 cases per 1,000 live births in high-income countries, while the prevalence is considerably higher in low- and middle-income regions [1–3]. At present, this disease has become the most common cause of childhood neurological dysfunction worldwide, imposing a substantial health burden globally [4]. Due to deficits in the normal muscle growth capacity of children with CP, the increase in their body weight is associated with the manifestation of motor function abnormalities, such as abnormal muscle tone, muscle contractures, and poor motor control [5, 6]. These symptoms severely hinder the daily activities of children with CP, including walking and maintaining balance. Long-term physical contractures can lead to physical deformities and reduce the independence of daily living [7]. Therefore, patients with CP require continuous and effective rehabilitation interventions to increase muscle mass, enhance motor ability, improve autonomy in daily living and optimize quality of life [8].

Rehabilitation for patients with CP is necessary and often a prolonged and arduous process. Conventional rehabilitation exercises include active training, passive flexion and extension of joint range of motion, as well as motor strength training [9]. Traditional rehabilitation relies on one-on-one manual intervention by therapists, who stimulate coordinated muscle movement through repetitive training. The rehabilitation of CP patients requires long-term and continuous intervention, usually lasting for years or even a lifetime [10]. While this approach confers benefits on patients with cerebral palsy, it is also confronted with numerous challenges. Such monotonous and repetitive training is often tedious for pediatric patients, which in turn compromises treatment compliance. Meanwhile, due to limitations in the professional competence of family caregivers and the accessibility of rehabilitation resources, approximately 60% of CP patients worldwide fail to receive standardized and adequate rehabilitation training. They ultimately miss the critical window period for motor function remodeling [11].

In recent years, rehabilitation technologies represented by virtual reality (VR) and robot-assisted therapy have developed rapidly, offering effective alternatives to traditional interventions [12]. VR creates immersive, game-like environments. It can enhance engagement and compliance among children with cerebral palsy by transforming therapy into an enjoyable activity [13, 14]. Robot-assisted therapy delivers highly precise, consistent and quantifiable repetitive movements. It enables standardized, high-dosage training that may be difficult to achieve manually, thus addressing the limitations associated with the professional competence of family caregivers [15]. Consequently, robotic therapy and VR have been increasingly applied in clinical rehabilitation practice. Through these technologies, targeted treatment plans can be formulated for patients, helping them perform accurate and repetitive training to improve motor control ability, which is more conducive to the recovery of patients’ muscle control [16].

Currently, several existing systematic reviews and meta-analyses have only explored the efficacy of a single technology. For instance, one meta-analysis focusing exclusively on VR interventions reported a moderate effect on gross motor function with an SMD of 0.41 (95% CI, 0.16 to 0.66), and it did not report findings related to balance [17]. However, most meta-analyses on robot-assisted therapy have demonstrated effects on gross motor function and joint improvement, with limited data available on activities of daily living [18–20]. However, these reviews either focus on a single technology or lack comprehensive subgroup analyses such as those stratified by CP subtypes or comparisons between VR and robot-assisted therapies. Furthermore, the existing evidence remains controversial. Meyns et al. [21] reported that VR did not improve balance, whereas Choi et al. [22] found that technology-based interventions favorably enhanced motor control in children with CP. These conflicting conclusions, coupled with the lack of integrated evidence across technologies, make it difficult for clinicians to reach a consensus on the effectiveness and applicability of such interventions, thereby hindering their widespread application.

To address these gaps, we conducted this review. We aimed to explore the comparative effects of VR and robot-assisted therapy versus conventional rehabilitation on gross motor function, balance, spasticity, range of motion (ROM), and activities of daily living in children with CP. This study seeks to provide evidence-based recommendations for clinicians to formulate targeted rehabilitation plans, offer a basis for future large-sample and long-term randomized controlled trials (RCTs), and ultimately promote the more precise and extensive application of VR and robot-assisted therapy in the physical rehabilitation of patients with CP.

Literature search

We systematically searched PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Library, Web of Science, and the Cumulative Index to Nursing & Allied Health Literature (CINAHL) using a combination of subject terms and free-text terms. To identify unpublished or ongoing trials, we searched two international clinical trial registries, namely ClinicalTrials and the World Health Organization International Clinical Trials Registry Platform. The search terms were identical to those used in electronic databases, and no restrictions were imposed on trial status. Meanwhile, we conducted a search for grey literature to avoid publication bias. This search covered theses and dissertations and unpublished technical reports from relevant research institutions, with the time frame consistent with that applied to the electronic database search. We searched for relevant studies published from 2016 to 2025.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

This study was conducted in strict adherence to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Population (P): Children or adolescents with CP aged ≤ 18 years, regardless of gender or CP type; (2) Intervention (I): Robot-assisted therapy or virtual reality (VR) intervention in the experimental group; (3) Comparison (C): Conventional rehabilitation therapy in the control group; (4) Outcomes (O): Primary outcomes included motor function (measured using the GMFM-88 Scale), balance ability (assessed via the PBS), spasticity level (evaluated with the MAS), joint range of motion, and activity ability (measured using the PEDI-CAT Daily Activity Scale); (5) Design (D): Randomized controlled trial (RCT).

The exclusion criteria: (1) Non-empirical studies, including reviews, conference abstracts, case reports, and retrospective analyses; (2) Studies with unavailable or non-convertible outcome data (incomplete indicators or non-standardized reporting); (3) Studies published before January 1, 2016; (4) Non-English studies.

Two researchers independently screened the literature and extracted data in accordance with the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Any discrepancies arising during the screening process were resolved through repeated discussions until a consensus was reached. Literature quality assessment

Two trained researchers independently assessed the methodological quality of the included RCTs using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool [23]. To adopt a more comprehensive assessment framework and strictly adhere to the registered PROSPERO protocol, we employed both the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool (RoB-1) and ROB-2. RoB-1 was used to assess the risk of bias across seven standard domains for all included trials. In accordance with our protocol, ROB-2 was specifically applied to the primary outcome measures of this review to provide a more detailed evaluation of trial integrity for key results. To ensure objectivity, the assessors cross-checked the evaluation results. Any discrepancies identified were resolved through repeated discussions; if a consensus still could not be reached, a third independent reviewer was consulted for arbitration.

Statistical methods

The meta-analysis was conducted using RevMan 5.4 software. For continuous outcomes, we extracted the final scores and their standard deviations from each study. Where studies reported both change-from-baseline and final scores, we prioritized final scores for consistency across the included trials. When the included studies evaluated the same outcome but employed different measurement scales, the pooled effect estimate was calculated as the standardized mean difference (SMD) with a 95% confidence interval (95% CI). In contrast, when the same outcome was assessed using identical measurement scales across the included studies, the pooled effect estimate was computed as the mean difference (MD) with a 95% CI. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for intergroup differences. Model selection was determined by both statistical and clinical heterogeneity: A fixed-effects model was employed only when statistical heterogeneity was low (I2< 50% and P > 0.05) and clinical homogeneity was confirmed. Clinical homogeneity was defined as consistent intervention protocols, homogeneous participant characteristics, and uniform outcome assessment time points across included studies. A random-effects model was utilized if either statistical heterogeneity was significant (I2≥ 50% or P ≤ 0.05) or clinical heterogeneity existed. Clinical heterogeneity was reflected in variations such as different VR intervention durations, variable robot-assisted therapy dosages, or disparate participant age ranges across trials, even when the I2 value was < 50%. For the Modified Ashworth Scale (MAS), an ordinal scale used to assess spasticity, we treated it as a continuous variable in the meta-analysis. This approach was rationally justified based on two key considerations. First, it aligns with clinical and methodological precedents: the MAS has been widely treated as a continuous variable in previous meta-analyses of cerebral palsy rehabilitation interventions [24–26]. Second, it conforms to the applicability of statistical methods. The MAS adopted in the included studies was an ordinal scale with no fewer than five grades, and the data distribution of the studies showed an approximately normal pattern with no severe skewness [23]. This approach is quite common in meta-analysis practice, and we will discuss it in the limitations section. Publication bias was evaluated using Egger’s linear regression test and funnel plots exclusively for outcomes with ≥ 10 included studies. For outcomes with < 10 included studies, publication bias could not be reliably assessed due to insufficient statistical power [23], and this limitation is explicitly acknowledged in the limitations section. Sensitivity analysis was performed by sequentially excluding individual studies to test the robustness of the pooled results.

Grading of recommendations assessment, development, and evaluation

To appraise the certainty of evidence, we applied GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation) guidance (GRADE Working Group) to the main outcomes (GMFM-88, PBS, REDI-CAT Daily Activity, ROM, MAS). Two reviewers independently rated evidence across risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias, and resolved disagreements by consensus. Appendix A presents a summary table of the results.

Results

Literature search and screening results

A total of 1720 relevant studies were initially identified through database searches. After screening based on titles and abstracts, 66 studies met the eligibility criteria. Following full-text review, 17 studies were finally included in the analysis. Figure 1 illustrates the detailed literature screening process.

Basic characteristics of the included trials

Table 1 summarizes the basic characteristics of the 17 clinical trials included in this study. The included studies spanned from 2016 to 2025, covering multiple countries and regions such as South Korea, Germany, India, China, Iran, Turkey, the United States, Australia, and Saudi Arabia. These studies involved a total of 578 patients with CP, with 13 studies focusing on children (aged ≤ 12 years). The proportion of male participants was relatively high (n = 322, 55.7%). Spastic CP was the predominant type, with a small number of studies involving dyskinetic CP, ataxic CP, and other types. The severity of motor function in children with CP was classified using the GMFCS, ranging from Level I to IV, with Levels II and III accounting for the largest proportion. The interventions were categorized into two types, 12studies adopted VR, and 5 used robotic devices. The study outcomes covered core scales and tests in the field of CP rehabilitation, including motor function assessments (GMFM-88, PDMS-2), balance capacity measurements (PBS, BBS), gait performance evaluations (6MWT, 10MWT), activities of daily living assessments (PEDI-CAT, WeeFIM), ROM and muscle function evaluations (MAS, grip strength), as well as trunk control and upper limb function assessments (TCMS, JHFT, ABILHAND-Kids).

Bias risk assessment results

Detailed results of the risk of bias assessment are summarized in the supplementary materials for RoB-1 and RoB-2. Firstly, all 17 included studies were evaluated using the RoB-1 tool. The results indicated that, due to the inherent characteristics of the interventions, blinding of participants and intervention providers could not be implemented, resulting in all studies in this field being rated as having a high risk of bias. Most studies reported the implementation of blinding for outcome assessors, while only two studies did not provide detailed descriptions of relevant blinding procedures. All studies were rated at low risk concerning incomplete outcome data, selective reporting and other potential sources of bias. On this basis, we further applied the RoB-2 tool to rate the primary outcomes and provide descriptions for studies rated as high risk or some concerns in the RoB-1 assessment. The study by Uysal presented significant baseline differences and imbalances across groups, making it difficult to reliably attribute the observed effects to the interventions themselves.

Sensitivity analysis and publication bias test

Given the heterogeneity observed in some outcome measures, this study adopted the one-by-one exclusion method with reference to relevant comprehensive research methodologies to assess the impact of each study on the overall effect size, thereby verifying the robustness of the pooled results. Detailed results are presented in Multimedia Appendix 2.

For the GMFM-88, the virtual reality subgroup included 5 studies, with a pooled SMD = 0.49 (95% CI, 0.17 to 0.81). The robot-assisted subgroup included 6 studies, with a pooled SMD = 0.64 (95% CI, 0.23 to 1.05). The spastic subgroup included 7 studies, with a pooled SMD = 0.44 (95% CI, 0.20 to 0.69). The bilateral spasticity subgroup included 2 studies, with a pooled SMD = 0.46 (95% CI, -0.12 to 1.04). The diplegia subgroup included 2 studies, with a pooled SMD = 1.04 (95% CI, −0.02 to 2.09). The results of GMFM-88 were robust in most subgroups. Due to insufficient sample size, the analytical results of the latter two subgroups lacked statistical validity and had limited result robustness. For the PBS, the VR subgroup included 5 studies, with a pooled MD = 2.75 (95% CI, −0.16 to 5.66). The robot-assisted subgroup included 3 studies, with a pooled MD = 3.36 (95% CI, 0.05 to 6.68). For PBS, a certain degree of instability was observed in both the VR and robot-assisted subgroups. In particular, the SMD changed significantly after excluding the study by Uysal (2016), indicating that these results may be greatly affected by individual studies. For the MAS, a total of 4 studies were included, with a pooled MD = -0.49 (95% CI, -0.82 to -0.15). For the PEDI-CAT Daily Activity Dimension, three studies were included, with a pooled MD = 0.51 (95% CI, 0.27 to 0.74). When the number of included studies for a certain outcome measure was ≥ 10, funnel plots and Egger’s linear regression test were used to analyze small-sample study effects. Asymmetry in funnel plots may reflect publication bias, or it may be caused by heterogeneity between studies, random errors or differences in study characteristics. For the GMFM-88 measure, visual inspection of the funnel plot (see Multimedia Appendix 2) and Egger’s test (P = 0.082) showed no clear evidence of small-sample study effects. However, it should be noted that the number of included studies in some subgroups was limited, so the explanatory power of this result is subject to limitations.

GRADE assessment

The results of the GRADE evidence quality assessment showed that the certainty of evidence for each outcome measure ranged from very low to moderate (see Multimedia Appendix 2 for details). For GMFM-88 score, we downgraded the evidence by one level due to the risk of bias in some trials, where most studies had insufficient blinding of participants, therapists, and outcome assessors that was prone to inducing assessor subjective bias, resulting in a final moderate certainty of evidence for this measure. For the PBS outcome, the certainty of evidence was downgraded by one level due to inadequate blinding, then further downgraded by one level owing to baseline imbalances in gender and MACS grade and elevated risk of bias in some studies, and additionally downgraded by one level for imprecision because the 95% CI of the effect estimate was below the minimal clinically important difference of the PBS scale, resulting in a final rating of very low certainty of evidence for this outcome. For the PEDI-CAT Daily Activities scale and MAS scale, the certainty of evidence was initially downgraded by one level for the same inadequate blinding, and then further downgraded by one level because the lower bounds of the 95% CI for their pooled effect sizes did not reach the respective widely accepted minimal clinically important differences, leading to a final rating of low certainty of evidence for both outcomes. For the ROM outcome, the certainty of evidence was first downgraded by one level for the same inadequate blinding, and then further downgraded by one level because the 95% CI of the effect estimate crossed the null value, resulting in a final rating of low certainty of evidence for this outcome. These GRADE assessment results suggest that caution should be exercised when interpreting the pooled effect sizes of this study (see Multimedia Appendix 2 for details).

System evaluation and meta-analysis results

Among the included studies, some outcomes could not be pooled for meta-analysis; therefore, a descriptive summary of these results is provided. Most studies indicated that compared with the control group, patients receiving novel rehabilitation technologies showed improvements in walking distance, stride length, and the quality of upper limb motor function. Regarding walking speed measurements, studies by Sajan et al. [31] and Choi et al. [22] confirmed that novel rehabilitation technologies significantly improved the walking speed of patients with CP. Meanwhile, the findings of Choi et al. [22] demonstrated that robot-assisted therapy could enhance the walking distance of patients with CP. Avcil et al. [43] used the NHPT and the DHI to reveal that VR technology exerted significant benefits on hand dexterity and hand function-related abilities. In addition, the study by Moll et al. [28] found that as an innovative and technologically advanced device, robot-assisted technology was perceived as an interesting tool by children with CP. Compared with the repetitive movements of traditional rehabilitation, it was more effective in stimulating children’s willingness to participate actively and reducing treatment resistance.

Discussion

Through systematic review and meta-analysis, this study evaluated the effectiveness of virtual reality and robot-assisted technologies for rehabilitation in patients with cerebral palsy. Overall, the evidence indicates that these novel rehabilitation technologies exert positive effects on improving patients’ motor function, balance capacity, activities of daily living, and reducing muscle spasticity. However, the clinical significance of some findings requires cautious interpretation.

First, our comprehensive analysis confirms that the integration of VR or robot-assisted technologies, compared with conventional rehabilitation, improves gross motor function (assessed via GMFM-88) in patients with CP. This finding aligns with most included studies and the general consensus in this field [17, 43, 44]. Notably, subgroup analysis by intervention type in this study further reveals potential differences in the focus of action between the two technologies. Both VR technology (SMD = 0.49) and robot-assisted training (SMD = 0.64) enhance gross motor function, suggesting that both are reliable options for improving this outcome in patients with CP. Robot-assisted training shows a slightly larger effect magnitude, which may indicate marginally superior efficacy. This could be attributed to the ability of robot-assisted rehabilitation to deliver precise, repetitive movement training, thereby effectively promoting the recovery of gross motor function in patients with CP [45]. However, due to the limited number of included trials and corresponding statistical power, these interpretations are based on exploratory findings from limited subgroup data. Future targeted studies are needed to verify these potential mechanisms.

For balance capacity assessed using the PBS, the overall pooled MD was 2.06. This effect size indicates that patients with cerebral palsy achieved an average increase of approximately 2.06 points in PBS scores, suggesting a positive facilitative effect of the intervention on balance capacity. Regarding the clinical significance of this improvement, we interpreted the results by referring to the minimal clinically important difference threshold derived from general methodological approaches [46]. The observed improvement met the minimal clinically important difference threshold, which ranges from 3 to 4 points.Both the observed improvement and the upper bound of its confidence interval were below this threshold, indicating that although the intervention significantly improved balance ability statistically, the magnitude of improvement may not have reached a clinically meaningful enhancement. However, we must clarify that this comparison was based on an approximate value extrapolated from general methodologies rather than an exact threshold specific to the cerebral palsy population [47]. This finding should therefore be regarded as positive and indicative evidence. Future studies designed to establish a cerebral palsy-specific minimal clinically important difference are still needed to confirm this observation.

For spasticity reduction, the pooled MD was -0.49. The overall analysis indicated that the intervention exerted a positive effect on alleviating muscle spasticity. Subgroup analysis revealed that the VR intervention yielded a significant effect. (MD = -0.61, P < 0.05) In contrast, robot-assisted intervention failed to demonstrate superiority with a non-statistically significant result.(MD = 0−.12, P > 0.05 ) This discrepancy might stem from the distinct mechanisms of action underlying the two technologies. The visualized environment and task-oriented training incorporated in VR technology facilitate cortical reorganization and neuroplasticity, which in turn promotes selective muscle activation and relaxation [48, 49]. On the other hand, robot-assisted training may focus more on motor skill acquisition and joint range of motion improvement, with limited integration of sensory feedback and regulation of neural pathways associated with spasticity [50]. Given the limited number of studies included in this subgroup analysis and the presence of clinical heterogeneity, the results should be regarded as exploratory findings. They provide preliminary clues suggesting potential differences in the mechanisms of action between the two technologies.

Subgroup analyses stratified by CP type provided evidence for the efficacy of interventions in specific patient populations. For spastic CP, the pooled SMD was 0.44 (95% CI, 0.20 to 0.69), confirming that VR and robot-assisted technologies could effectively improve gross motor function in patients with this most common CP subtype. In contrast, results for bilateral spastic CP (SMD = 0.46, 95% CI, -0.12 to 1.04) and diplegia (SMD = 1.04, 95% CI, −0.02 to 2.09) were not statistically significant. This indicates that the analysis lacks statistically robust evidence and current findings are insufficient to verify the efficacy of VR or robot-assisted technologies in improving gross motor function for these specific CP subtypes. Variations in point estimates such as the relatively high SMD observed in the diplegia subgroup should be interpreted with extreme caution. Such variations may be influenced by small sample sizes and specific study characteristics rather than reflecting consistent true treatment effects. These findings suggest that future targeted studies with larger sample sizes are required to validate the efficacy of novel technologies in these specific CP subtypes.

For activities of daily living assessed via the PEDI-CAT Daily Activity domain, the intervention group achieved higher scores than the control group with a pooled MD of 0.51 (95% CI, 0.27 to 0.74). It should be noted that for standardized scales such as the PEDI-CAT which are reported as T-scores with a mean of 50 and a standard deviation of 10, the observed between-group difference of 0.51 points represents a very small effect size. This is compounded by the small number of included studies [51]. Therefore, despite the statistical significance of the 0.51 mean difference, it may not represent a clinically meaningful improvement for individual patients. In rehabilitation research, a clinically meaningful change on such scales is generally considered to require a magnitude of 6 to 15 points, corresponding to an approximate 11% change in the PEDI score [52]. Thus, even though the results are statistically significant, the magnitude of improvement may not reach the level of minimal important change perceptible to children with CP or their families in daily life. This finding suggests that while the interventions are statistically effective, their actual clinical benefits for activities of daily living may be very limited. For joint ROM, the pooled SMD was 0.46 (P > 0.05) with high heterogeneity. This indicates that novel rehabilitation interventions did not show a statistically significant improvement in joint ROM. We attribute this to three main reasons. First, the inclusion of two intervention measures with distinct core technical mechanisms contributed to the heterogeneity of ROM outcomes. Second, there were discrepancies in the specific joints assessed across patients, and advanced technologies may exert differential effects on the ROM of different joints. Third, patients might have been at different stages of rehabilitation, leading to variations in their potential for joint recovery [53], for instance, patients with already good baseline ROM have limited room for improvement.

Study limitations and future directions

Although this study provides evidence for the application of VR and robot-assisted technology in CP rehabilitation through systematic review and meta-analysis, several limitations remain. First, the protocol of this study was registered as a partially retrospective registration, which introduces the potential risk of selective reporting bias. Although all subsequent data extraction, analysis and result interpretation strictly adhered to the finally registered and publicly available protocol, and outcome-based selective reporting was avoided to minimize the impact of such bias, partially retrospective registration itself is not a methodological best practice and may theoretically carry a potential risk of bias. Second, interpretation of the spasticity outcome measured by the MAS requires caution. Although we pooled data from this ordinal scale as continuous variables following established practices and the data distribution was generally reasonable, this approach essentially constitutes a methodological approximation. It assumes equal intervals between scale grades, which may exert a subtle impact on the accuracy of the estimated pooled mean difference. Last, multiple subgroup analyses conducted in this review are exploratory in nature and some subgroup includes a small number of studies. This results in insufficient statistical power for these analyses, and their results should be interpreted with caution. We have refrained from making definitive comparative claims based on these analyses, which cannot be used as the basis for drawing comparative conclusions. In addition, the evidence base of this study ranges from moderate to very low. This indicates that the current findings are mainly derived from preliminary, exploratory evidence. Therefore, a high degree of caution should be exercised when interpreting these pooled effect sizes, especially for outcomes with low or very low evidence levels. They should not be regarded as definitive conclusions but rather as references for designing large-scale, high-quality studies and generating hypotheses in the future. For future reviews, we recommend conducting prospective registration and prioritizing blinded study designs and objective continuous measurement tools to improve the quality of evidence. Promoting the establishment of a patient-centered core outcome set with well-defined minimal clinically important differences and facilitating the translation of high-quality evidence into individualized clinical practice will contribute to a more comprehensive integration of the rapidly evolving fields of VR technology and robot-assisted rehabilitation.

Conclusions

This systematic review synthesizes the current evidence on the effects of virtual reality and robot-assisted rehabilitation technologies on children with cerebral palsy. Pooled analyses indicate that VR and robot-assisted rehabilitation technologies offer promising benefits in improving gross motor function, balance, reducing spasticity, and enhancing activities of daily living.

However, these positive findings must be interpreted with caution due to several key limitations. The certainty of the evidence is compromised by multiple factors. Many of the included studies are still subject to potential biases including inherent detection bias associated with unblinded intervention designs, small sample sizes and short follow-up durations in most studies, heterogeneity in study designs, and the exploratory nature of subgroup analyses.

Therefore, although VR and robot-assisted therapies appear to be promising adjuncts to conventional rehabilitation, current evidence is insufficient to support their widespread and unconditional application in clinical practice. High-quality research in the future is imperative. Specifically, there is a need for large-scale, rigorously designed randomized controlled trials with adequate blinding, long-term follow-up, and patient-centered core outcome sets. These trials will help clarify the relative effectiveness, optimal application protocols, and sustained functional benefits of these technologies for different CP subtypes.
