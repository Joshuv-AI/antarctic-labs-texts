Knowledge, attitude and willingness towards hospice care among older adult patients and their family members in the geriatric department in Hubei Province, China: a multi-centre cross-sectional study
Authors: Pan Du, Jianqiang Zhu, Ying Yang, Xue Zhou, Hua Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo investigate the knowledge, attitude and willingness (KAW) towards hospice care among older adult patients and their family members within the geriatric department of Hubei province, and to situate these findings within the broader context of public awareness and the health system’s multidisciplinary approach to end-of-life care.DesignA cross-sectional survey.SettingDemographic information and KAW scores were collected through a self-made questionnaire. Data were analysed using descriptive statistics, t-tests, ANOVA and path analysis.ParticipantsThis study was conducted across five tertiary hospitals in Hubei province between June and December 2023.Primary outcome measureWillingness towards hospice care.Secondary outcome measuresKnowledge and attitude towards hospice care, and their relationships with demographic factors.ResultsA total of 390 patients participated in this study, with 205 (52.56%) being male with a mean age of 69.75±8.01 years. The KAW scores were 4.88±5.14 (range: 0–14), 33.04±4.60 (range: 11–55) and 20.59±2.70 (range: 9–45), respectively. Family members scored 7.04±5.20 (range: 0–14) for knowledge, 37.20±3.61 (range: 11–55) for attitude and 22.35±3.59 (range: 6–30) for willingness. Path analysis revealed that in patients, knowledge (β=0.33, p<0.001) and attitude (β=0.11, p<0.001) directly affected willingness. Family members manifested a more proactive willingness to engage in hospice care. Based on these findings, targeted educational interventions are recommended, particularly focusing on improving hospice care knowledge among patients with lower education levels and those residing in rural areas. Additionally, involving family members more actively in care discussions may enhance acceptance and utilisation of hospice care services.Keywords: Health Services for the Aged, PUBLIC HEALTH, Aged

Introduction

Hospice care, established in the 1960s,1 offers significant benefits to terminally ill patients, enhancing quality of life and alleviating symptom burden.2 Despite these advantages, global access to hospice care remains limited; approximately, 40 million patients require such care annually at the end of life, yet only 14% receive it.3 In China, while there has been increased governmental support for hospice care in recent years, its development remains inadequate. This inadequacy is evidenced by a lack of education about hospice care, a hospice care team lacking diversity in professional roles (eg, doctors, nurses, social workers and psychological counsellors) and an uneven geographical distribution of services.4

The Knowledge-Attitude-Willingness (KAW) theory, pivotal in shaping health behaviours,5,7 is commonly employed in conjunction with questionnaires to comprehensively assess knowledge, attitude and practices within healthcare domains, gauging the demand and acceptance levels of relevant content.8 Focusing on this demographic is imperative due to the unique complexities associated with caring for ageing individuals, including intricate healthcare needs and multiple comorbidities. Research conducted within geriatric departments offers invaluable insights into the perspectives of those directly affected by ageing-related health issues.

Additionally, highlighting Hubei Province as the study locale is crucial owing to its significant older adult population, providing a localised lens to comprehend the challenges encountered by geriatric departments in regions with high concentrations of older adult individuals. Despite the importance of this perspective, such research targeting this group is lacking; previous studies have primarily focused on healthcare professionals.9 10 Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the knowledge, attitude and willingness (KAW) towards hospice care among older adult patients and their family members within the geriatric department in Hubei Province.

Methods

Patient and public involvement in research

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional survey was conducted across five tertiary hospitals in Hubei Province between June and December 2023. The selected hospitals were Xiangyang Central Hospital, Taihe Hospital in Shiyan City, Central Hospital of Huangshi City, First People’s Hospital of Jingzhou City and Enshi Central Hospital. Participants were recruited from outpatient clinics or geriatric departments and included both patients and their accompanying family members. Inclusion criteria: (1) participants who have capacity for independent decision-making; and (2) provide voluntary consent for participation in data collection and analysis. Exclusion criteria: (1) participants who failed quality control assessments (eg, incomplete questionnaires, patterned responses or logical inconsistencies); (2) provided responses inconsistent with the study objectives. Ethical approval for this research was obtained from the Medical Ethics Committee of Xiangyang Central Hospital, and written informed consent was obtained from all study participants.

Questionnaire introduction

The initial draft of the questionnaire was developed based on a review of existing literature on hospice care KAW. The draft was then reviewed by 10 experts, including five specialists in geriatric medicine and five specialists in hospice care. Based on their recommendations, the questionnaire underwent revisions and was subsequently pilot-tested on a small-scale sample of 88 respondents, achieving a reliability coefficient of 0.880. These respondents were independent of the patients and family members included in the main study. The final questionnaire, administered in Chinese, encompasses four dimensions: demographic information and KAW dimensions.

For patients’ questionnaires

The knowledge dimension comprises seven questions, with correct responses scoring 2 points and incorrect or unclear responses scoring 0 points, resulting in a score range of 0–14 points. The attitude dimension consists of 11 questions, using a five-point Likert scale ranging from ‘extremely positive’ (5 points) to ‘extremely negative’ (1 point), yielding a score range of 11–55 points. The willingness dimension includes nine questions, with items rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from ‘always’ (5 points) to ‘never’ (1 point), resulting in a score range of 9–45 points. The overall Cronbach’s α coefficient for the questionnaire is 0.89, indicating satisfactory internal consistency.

For family members’ questionnaires

The knowledge dimension comprises seven questions, with correct responses scoring 2 points and incorrect or unclear responses scoring 0 points, yielding a score range of 0–14 points. The attitude dimension comprises 11 questions rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from ‘extremely positive’ (5 points) to ‘extremely negative’ (1 point), resulting in a score range of 11–55 points. The willingness dimension includes six questions rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from ‘always’ (5 points) to ‘never’ (1 point), yielding a score range of 6–30 points. The overall Cronbach’s α coefficient for the questionnaire is 0.891, indicating good internal consistency. Attainment of scores above 70% of the maximum in each section denotes adequate knowledge, positive attitude and proactive willingness.11 Questionnaires were distributed through in-person surveys conducted in clinic rooms, with both patients and their accompanying family members completing the questionnaires separately during their visit.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using Stata 17.0 (Stata Corporation, College Station, TX, USA). Continuous data are presented as means and SD, while categorical data are expressed as n (%). Prior to conducting parametric tests, continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Continuous variables that followed a normal distribution were analysed using the independent samples t-test, while the Wilcoxon Mann-Whitney test was applied for those that did not. Levene’s test was employed to assess the homogeneity of variances. For comparisons among three or more groups with normally distributed continuous variables and equal variances, ANOVA was used, while the Kruskal-Wallis test was employed for non-normally distributed data. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using the Tukey test for ANOVA and Dunn’s test for Kruskal-Wallis. Spearman correlation analysis was utilised to determine the correlation between KAW scores. To quantify the strength and direction of associations, correlation coefficients were interpreted as weak (0.1–0.3), moderate (0.3–0.5) or strong (0.5+). Path analysis was used to explore the relationships between knowledge (K), attitude (A), willingness (W) and demographic information. Model fit was evaluated using the χ2 test, RMSEA, CFI and TLI, with acceptable model fit indicated by RMSEA < 0.08 and CFI/TLI > 0.90. A two-sided P-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The path analysis model was specified according to the hypothesised relationships between KAW and key demographic variables, as supported by existing literature on hospice care perceptions. In addition to the path analysis, we conducted univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses to explore factors associated with proactive hospice care willingness. For this analysis, a mean item score above 3.5 on the willingness dimension was classified as ‘proactive willingness’. Variables with p < 0.10 in univariate analysis were included in the multivariate model. ORs with 95% CIs were reported, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Demographic characteristics

A total of 390 patients were enrolled in this study, 205 (52.56%) were males, with a mean age of 69.75±8.01 years. 205 (52.56%) had junior high school education or below, 195 (50%) had suffered from hypertension, 342 (87.69%) had experienced the death of a close family member, and 253 (64.87%) had cared for a dying family member or relative. 214 (54.87%) self-rated their health as fair. Their KAW scores were 4.88±5.14 (possible range: 0–14), 33.04±4.60 (possible range: 11–55) and 20.59±2.70 (possible range: 9–45), respectively. Analysis of the relationship between knowledge scores and demographic variables showed that patients' knowledge scores varied significantly by age, residence, education, occupation, monthly income, health insurance, experience with the death of a close family member and self-rated health status. Among these, education (p<0.001), residence (p<0.001) and age (p<0.001) showed the most significant associations with knowledge levels, suggesting that knowledge scores tended to be higher among younger, more educated and urban-dwelling patients. Also, patients with different education, occupation, monthly income and self-rated health status were more likely to have different attitude scores (all p<0.005) (online supplemental table 1). Among the 416 family members who participated in this study, 217 (52.16%) were females, with a mean age of 42.98±15.72 years. 238 (57.21%) had a college/bachelor’s degree or above, 317 (76.2%) had experienced the death of a close family member and 218 (52.4%) had cared for a dying family member or relative. Their KAW scores were 7.04±5.20 (possible range: 0–14), 37.20±3.61 (possible range: 11–55) and 22.35±3.59 (possible range: 6–30), respectively. Analysis showed that knowledge scores differed within certain demographic groups of family members, for example, by age, education and monthly income. Concurrently, religious beliefs were associated with differences in attitude. Further, education and experience of having cared for a dying family member or relative were associated with differences in willingness scores (all p<0.05) (online supplemental table 1). The path analysis indicated a good fit for both patients and family members, as demonstrated by good model fit indices (online supplemental table 4). Path analysis found that knowledge (β=0.33) and attitude (β=0.11) had direct effects on willingness, while knowledge (β=0.09), residence (β=−0.55), education (β=0.51), occupation (β=0.50) and age (β=−0.23) had indirect effects on willingness (table 2 and figure 3A). In family members, knowledge (β=0.35), attitude (β=0.20), and experience of caring for dying family members (β=−0.65) had direct effects on willingness, while knowledge (β=0.04), education (β=0.33), and religious beliefs (β=0.11) had indirect effects on willingness (all p<0.05) (table 3 and figure 3B).

Mediation analysis in family members

Logistic regression analysis of factors associated with hospice care willingness

Logistic regression was performed to examine the associations between demographic and KAW dimensions with proactive hospice care willingness (defined as average item score>3.5).

Among patients (n=390), multivariate regression showed that higher knowledge scores (OR=0.113, 95% CI 0.072 to 0.153, p < 0.001) and attitude scores (OR=0.339, 95% CI 0.294 to 0.384, p < 0.001) were significantly associated with proactive willingness. Additionally, patients with lower education levels (junior high school and below) had significantly lower odds of proactive willingness (OR = –0.656, 95% CI –1.266 to –0.047, p=0.035).

Among family members (n=416), knowledge (OR=0.214, 95% CI 0.156 to 0.272, p<0.001) and attitude (OR=0.352, 95% CI 0.268 to 0.436, p<0.001) were also positively associated with willingness. Similarly, lower education level was negatively associated with proactive willingness (OR = –1.167, 95% CI –2.020 to –0.315, p = 0.007).

Discussion

Main findings/results of the study

Both older adult patients and their family members demonstrated insufficient knowledge, yet a moderate attitude and willingness towards hospice care. Targeted educational interventions should be implemented for both older adult patients and their family members to enhance understanding and acceptance of hospice care. Since the findings indicate that knowledge levels are significantly influenced by education, targeted interventions should focus on providing accessible educational resources, especially for patients with lower education levels and those from rural areas. Additionally, given that family members exhibited a higher willingness towards hospice care, involving them more actively in the decision-making process may foster a more supportive environment for older adult patients. Furthermore, policies should aim to improve access to hospice care, particularly in underserved rural regions, where knowledge and attitudes towards hospice care are less positive, as identified in this study.

What this study adds

This study provides key insights into the KAW regarding hospice care among older adult patients and their family members. Family members exhibited limited knowledge and moderate attitude and willingness towards hospice care, which is consistent with their crucial role in decision-making and caregiving.12 However, both groups displayed insufficient knowledge yet a positive attitude towards hospice care, indicating the need for comprehensive education and awareness programmes.

Education plays a crucial role in shaping perceptions and behaviour towards hospice care among older adult patients. Both the significant differences analysis and the SEM results emphasise the association of education with KAW. Higher levels of education consistently correlate with greater knowledge and a more positive attitude towards hospice care, as evidenced by both analyses. This suggests that interventions focusing on improving hospice care knowledge, particularly among patients with lower education levels, could effectively bridge knowledge gaps and foster a supportive attitude. Similarly, income is another critical factor, with the SEM findings indicating direct effects of income on attitude, suggesting that financial resources may influence perceptions of hospice care.13 The SEM results also reveal direct and indirect effects of residence on knowledge and attitude, highlighting potential disparities in healthcare access and awareness between urban and rural areas.14 Additionally, age plays a nuanced role in hospice care knowledge and attitude, with younger patients showing different patterns in these aspects.

Education and income were associated with the perceptions and behaviour of family members towards hospice care for older adults. Both the significant differences analysis and SEM results underscore the critical role of education in influencing knowledge levels, with higher education correlating consistently with greater knowledge about hospice care. Additionally, income levels were correlated with knowledge, indicating a possible association between financial resources and access to hospice care information among family members. Although residence also influences knowledge and attitude towards hospice care among family members, its effect may be less pronounced compared with its impact on patients. Finally, the experience of caring for dying family members emerges as a significant factor influencing the willingness of family members to consider hospice care. Interventions should focus on caregiver support and training programmes to address emotional and practical barriers, thereby enhancing caregivers’ confidence and ability to deliver high-quality hospice care to their loved ones.15 16

Spearman correlation analysis sheds light on the relationship between KAW regarding hospice care among older adult patients and their family members. Among patients, positive correlations are observed between knowledge and attitude, knowledge and willingness, and attitude and willingness. This suggests that patients with higher knowledge levels tend to exhibit more positive attitudes and hold more favourable attitudes towards hospice care. Similarly, family members show positive correlations between knowledge and attitude, knowledge and willingness, and attitude and willingness, indicating that greater knowledge is associated with more positive attitudes and increased willingness to support hospice care. Addressing knowledge gaps through educational interventions is crucial, as improved knowledge can lead to more positive attitudes and behaviours towards hospice care17 18

Analysis of patients’ knowledge levels reveals varying understanding of hospice care. While most patients understand that hospice care focuses on improving quality of life, there are misconceptions, such as believing that choosing hospice care means discontinuing medical treatments. Targeted educational campaigns, including informational sessions and educational materials, can help correct these misunderstandings. Integrating hospice care education into medical and nursing curricula can ensure that future healthcare providers convey accurate information to patients.19 20

Family members demonstrate a slightly higher level of knowledge than patients, particularly understanding hospice care’s holistic approach. Healthcare professionals can leverage this existing knowledge to engage family members as allies in patient care. Providing tailored educational resources, such as online modules or support groups, can further empower family members to advocate for comprehensive hospice care for their loved ones. Additionally, fostering partnerships between healthcare providers and community organisations can extend educational initiatives beyond clinical settings, reaching a broader audience.21 22

Attitudes towards hospice care among patients reveal nuanced perspectives. While the majority express support for its implementation, substantial proportions harbour concerns regarding its ethical implications and impact on quality of life. These findings mirror the ambivalence observed in previous studies, where individuals simultaneously recognise the benefits of hospice care while harbouring reservations about its implications for end-of-life decision-making. Engaging patients in open dialogue about their concerns can help dispel myths and alleviate anxieties. Additionally, using patient testimonials and success stories can humanise the hospice care experience, illustrating its positive impact on quality of life. Offering decision aids, such as informational videos or decision-making tools, can empower patients to make informed choices aligned with their values and preferences.23,25

Family members exhibit a more positive attitude overall, with stronger agreement regarding hospice care’s role in facilitating peaceful deaths. This may reflect their firsthand experiences, witnessing the benefits of hospice care in alleviating suffering and promoting comfort at the end of life. However, concerns about the adequacy of hospice care services persist, highlighting the need for continuous improvement and expansion of services to meet evolving patient and family needs. To capitalise on this support, healthcare systems can implement family-centred care models that actively involve family members in care planning and decision-making. Providing psychoeducation sessions tailored to family members’ needs can address concerns and equip them with coping strategies to navigate the challenges of caregiving.26 27

The willingness to engage with hospice care services varies among patients, reflecting individual preferences and perceptions of care. Patients express varying degrees of openness towards hospice care, with a substantial proportion indicating a preference for aggressive treatment despite approaching the end of life. Healthcare providers can initiate discussions about hospice care early in the disease trajectory, emphasising its role in enhancing quality of life and symptom management. Offering hospice care consultations as a routine part of care can normalise its inclusion in treatment plans and encourage proactive decision-making. Furthermore, facilitating shared decision-making processes that prioritise patient autonomy can ensure care aligns with patients’ values and preferences.28

Conversely, family members demonstrate greater willingness to consider hospice care for their loved ones, particularly if it can be provided in a home setting. Concerns about the quality and accessibility of hospice care services emerge as significant influencers of decision-making, highlighting the importance of addressing structural barriers and enhancing service delivery models to improve uptake and satisfaction among patients and families. Healthcare systems can support family members by providing comprehensive information about hospice care options and resources. Establishing caregiver support programmes that offer practical assistance and emotional support can alleviate the burden of caregiving and enhance family members’ willingness to engage with hospice care services. Additionally, implementing flexible care delivery models that accommodate family preferences, such as home-based hospice care programmes, can optimise comfort and convenience for both patients and their families.29 30

Strengths and weaknesses/limitations of the study

Despite the insightful findings, this study has several limitations. first, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish causal relationships between variables. Second, the study was conducted in a specific geographic region (Hubei Province), potentially limiting the generalisability of the findings to other populations. Third, the reliance on self-reported data through questionnaires may introduce response bias and affect the accuracy of the results. Fourth, although the sample size of 390 patients and 416 family members provided sufficient power for the analyses performed, it may still limit the detection of smaller effect sizes, and future studies with larger and more diverse populations are warranted to enhance generalisability. Moreover, although some subgroup differences were statistically significant, the mean differences were small and may have limited practical implications. In addition, the clinical significance of these findings should be interpreted with caution, as statistical significance does not always translate into clinical relevance. Furthermore, while the use of KAW was suitable for evaluating KAW, it does not establish randomness and therefore the generalisability of the results to clinical practice should be regarded as limited. Finally, the statistical analyses, though appropriate, should be interpreted within the constraints of the study design, and the generalisability of the findings remains cautious. However, despite these limitations, the strengths of this paper lie in their comprehensive examination of KAW towards hospice care among both older adult patients and their family members. Additionally, the utilisation of structural equation modelling provides a robust analytical framework for understanding the complex interplay of factors influencing hospice care perceptions and behaviours. Furthermore, the inclusion of both patients and family members offers a holistic perspective on hospice care perceptions within the geriatric department, which can inform targeted interventions to improve the delivery and acceptance of hospice care services. Future interventions could include community-based educational programmes, integration of hospice care knowledge into routine clinical consultations and public health campaigns aimed at improving awareness in both patients and family members. In addition, future research could also incorporate the perspectives of health professionals to better understand their perceptions and barriers regarding hospice care.

Conclusions

In conclusion, while family members demonstrate slightly higher levels of KAW towards hospice care compared with older adult patients, both groups exhibit insufficient knowledge and moderate attitude. Consequently, interventions should prioritise educating both patients and families, emphasising hospice care’s significance, and actively involving family members in the care process to enhance patient outcomes and improve quality of life.
