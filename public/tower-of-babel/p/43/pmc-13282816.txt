A Bleeding Risk Score for Cancer-Associated Venous Thromboembolism in Patients Receiving Direct Oral Anticoagulants
Authors: Tomoyuki Nagai, Naohiko Nakanishi, Yugo Yamashita, Takeshi Morimoto, Seiichi Hiramori, Ryuki Chatani, Yuji Nishimoto, Nobutaka Ikeda, Yohei Kobayashi, Satoshi Ikeda, Kitae Kim, Masayuki Shiba, Toru Takase, Shuhei Tsuji, Maki Oi, Takuma Takada, Kazunori Otsui, Jiro Sakamoto, Yoshito Ogihara, Takeshi Inoue, Shunsuke Usami, Po-Min Chen, Kiyonori Togi, Norimichi Koitabashi, Kosuke Doi, Hiroshi Mabuchi, Yoshiaki Tsuyuki, Koichiro Murata, Kensuke Takabayashi, Hisato Nakai, Daisuke Sueta, Wataru Shioyama, Tomohiro Dohke, Takeshi Kimura, Satoaki Matoba, COMMAND VTE Registry-2 Investigators

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with cancer-associated venous thromboembolism (VTE) are at a high risk of major bleeding during anticoagulant therapy, emphasizing the need for bleeding risk assessment in the era of direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs). However, few bleeding risk scores have been specifically developed and validated for this population.ObjectivesThe aim of this study was to develop and validate a novel bleeding risk score (ONCO-DOAC BLEED score) for patients with cancer-associated VTE receiving DOAC therapy.MethodsWe derived the bleeding risk prediction score using 1,166 patients with cancer-associated VTE treated with DOAC therapy from the COMMAND VTE (Contemporary Management and Outcomes in Patients with Venous Thromboembolism) Registry-2 and externally validated its performance in an independent cohort from the ONCO deep vein thrombosis and ONCO pulmonary embolism studies.ResultsOver a median follow-up of 163 days, 127 patients (10.9%) experienced major bleeding. In multivariable analysis, a history of major bleeding, chronic kidney disease, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug use, distant metastatic cancer, terminal cancer, upper gastrointestinal cancer, pancreatic cancer, and uterine cancer were independently associated with major bleeding. Based on these risk factors, the ONCO-DOAC BLEED score was constructed. This score demonstrated moderate discrimination for major bleeding in the derivation cohort (Harrell's C-index 0.68; Uno's C-index 0.67) and validation cohort (Harrell's C-index 0.62; Uno's C-index 0.63), with higher discrimination in the derivation cohort and comparable performance in the validation cohort compared with the VTE-BLEED, RIETE, CAT-BLEED, and Perform scores.ConclusionsThe ONCO-DOAC BLEED score provides clinically relevant stratification of major bleeding risk in patients with cancer-associated VTE receiving DOAC therapy and may support individualized therapeutic decision-making in routine practice. (Optimal Duration of Anticoagulation Therapy for Isolated Distal Deep Vein Thrombosis in Patients With Cancer Study [ONCO DVT]; NCT03895502 and Optimal Duration of Anticoagulation Therapy for Low-risk Pulmonary Embolism Patients With Cancer [ONCO PE]; NCT04724460)Key Words: bleeding, bleeding risk score, cancer, cancer-associated thrombosis, direct oral anticoagulants, DOAC, DVT, major bleeding, NOAC, novel oral anticoagulants, risk prediction, thrombosis, venous thromboembolism, VTE

The global burden of cancer continues to increase, and advances in anticancer therapies have increased the number of cancer survivors worldwide. However, venous thromboembolism (VTE) remains a common and serious complication in patients with active cancer and is associated with substantial morbidity and mortality. Accordingly, the optimal management of cancer-associated VTE has emerged as a major clinical challenge.1,2 Patients with active cancer are at high risk of both recurrent VTE and major bleeding during anticoagulant therapy.3,4 Although anticoagulation is essential to prevent thrombotic recurrence, its continuation in patients with cancer requires careful consideration of competing thrombotic and bleeding risks.5 Recently, direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) have become widely used for the treatment of cancer-associated VTE.6 Nevertheless, robust bleeding risk stratification tools tailored to patients with cancer receiving DOACs are limited. Consequently, a reliable bleeding risk prediction model specifically designed for this population is warranted.

Several bleeding risk scores have been proposed for patients receiving anticoagulation therapy, including the HAS-BLED,7 VTE-BLEED,8 and RIETE scores.9 However, these scores were not specifically developed for patients with cancer-associated VTE treated with DOACs and may provide limited applicability or insufficient risk stratification. More recently, cancer-specific scores, such as the CAT-BLEED10 and Perform scores,11 have been proposed; however, concerns remain regarding their complexity, limited external validation, and modest discriminative performance. Taken together, existing bleeding risk scores have demonstrated only modest discrimination and limited applicability in patients with cancer-associated VTE receiving DOAC therapy, underscoring the need for a simple and clinically feasible risk score for bleeding risk stratification in contemporary practice.

In this study, we aimed to develop a simple and clinically applicable bleeding risk prediction score for patients with cancer-associated VTE receiving DOAC therapy using data from the Contemporary Management and Outcomes in Patients with Venous Thromboembolism (COMMAND VTE) Registry-2,4 a large real-world cohort in the DOAC era. We further evaluated the discriminative performance and risk stratification ability of this novel score and compared it with existing bleeding risk scores for major bleeding in an external validation cohort.

Methods

Ethical approval

All studies were conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and were approved by the Kyoto University institutional review board, and the institutional review boards of all participating institutions (Supplemental Appendix 1). In the COMMAND VTE Registry-2, the requirement for written informed consent was waived because the study used clinical information obtained from routine clinical practice, and none of the patients declined to participate when contacted for follow-up. In the ONCO deep vein thrombosis (DVT)12 and ONCO pulmonary embolism (PE) studies,13 written informed consent was provided by all the study participants. The ONCO DVT and ONCO PE studies were registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT03895502 and NCT04724460).

Study design and population in the derivation cohort

The COMMAND VTE Registry-2 is a physician-initiated, multicenter, retrospective cohort study that enrolled consecutive patients with acute symptomatic VTE from 31 centers across Japan between January 2015 and August 2020. Details of the registry design have been reported previously.4 After screening 51,313 patients with suspected VTE, 5,197 patients with acute symptomatic VTE were enrolled in the COMMAND VTE Registry-2. Among these patients, 1,507 had active cancer at the time of VTE diagnosis. After excluding 144 patients who did not receive oral anticoagulant therapy, 164 who received warfarin therapy, and 33 who received thrombolytic therapy, 1,166 patients were included in the derivation cohort (Figure 1).

Data collection

Data collection and definitions have been described previously.4,14 In brief, data on patient characteristics were collected from hospital charts or hospital databases according to prespecified definitions using an electronic case report form in a web-based database system. Physicians at each participating institution were responsible for data entry, and the data were automatically checked for missing or contradictory entries and values outside the expected range. Additional data quality checks were performed at the registry's coordinating center. Follow-up information was collected through a review of hospital charts, with additional information obtained by contacting patients, relatives, and/or referring physicians. In this cohort study, final data collection for follow-up events was performed between September 2021 and April 2022.

Definitions of patient characteristics

Patients with active cancer were defined as those receiving treatment for cancer, such as chemotherapy or radiotherapy, those scheduled to undergo cancer surgery, those with metastasis to other organs, and/or those with terminal cancer (expected life expectancy ≤6 months) at the time of diagnosis. Anemia was defined as a hemoglobin level of <13 g/dL in men and <12 g/dL in women according to the World Health Organization criteria.15 Thrombocytopenia was defined as a platelet count of <10 × 103/μL.16 Chronic kidney disease (CKD) was diagnosed based on persistent proteinuria or an estimated glomerular filtration rate <60 mL/min/1.73 m2 for more than 3 months. Detailed definitions of remaining characteristics are provided in Supplemental Appendix 2.

Endpoint

The primary endpoint was the occurrence of major bleeding events during DOAC therapy. Major bleeding was defined according to the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis criteria, including fatal bleeding, symptomatic bleeding in a critical area or organ, and bleeding reducing hemoglobin levels ≥2 g/dL or requiring transfusions of ≥2 units of whole blood or red blood cells.17 Considering the potential different impact of thrombolytic therapy on bleeding risk in the acute phase, patients receiving thrombolytic therapy were excluded. Although major bleeding events in the acute phase can also be influenced by acute treatment strategies for VTE other than DOAC therapy, we aimed to assess bleeding risk after initiation of DOAC therapy, including the acute phase. Therefore, major bleeding events occurring during the acute phase of DOAC therapy were included as outcomes in the development of the bleeding risk score.

Study design and population in the validation cohort

The ONCO DVT study is an investigator-initiated, multicenter, open-label, adjudicator-blinded randomized clinical trial conducted at 60 institutions in Japan, designed to compare 12-month and 3-month edoxaban treatment regimens in patients with cancer-associated isolated distal DVT. The study details have been described previously,12,18 and detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria are provided in Supplemental Appendix 3. The ONCO PE study is an investigator-initiated, multicenter, open-label, adjudicator-blinded randomized clinical trial conducted at 32 institutions in Japan, designed to compare 18-month and 6-month rivaroxaban treatment regimens in patients with cancer and acute low-risk PE. The trial design has been previously reported in detail,13 and detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria are provided in Supplemental Appendix 4. For external validation, the performance of the bleeding risk score developed in the derivation cohort was evaluated in a validation cohort of 779 patients with cancer-associated VTE, including 601 patients from the intention-to-treat population of the ONCO DVT study and 178 patients from the intention-to-treat population of the ONCO PE study. Major bleeding in the validation cohorts was also defined according to the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis criteria.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are presented as numbers and percentages. Continuous variables are expressed as the mean and SDs or median and 25th-75th percentiles (Q1-Q3), depending on the distributions. Categorical variables were compared using the χ2 or Fisher exact tests, and continuous variables were compared using Student's t-test or the Wilcoxon rank sum test.

To develop the bleeding risk prediction score, a comprehensive set of 22 candidate variables was selected based on clinical relevance and prior reports of bleeding risk. These variables were then evaluated using a univariable Cox proportional hazards model to estimate the hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for major bleeding. Variables associated with major bleeding at a significance level of P < 0.10 were subsequently entered into the multivariable model. Finally, the ONCO-DOAC BLEED score was constructed using variables independently associated with major bleeding in the multivariable analysis (P < 0.05). Given the exploratory nature of this study, formal sample size or power calculation was not performed; however, the number of candidate variables was selected to ensure an adequate events-per-variable ratio. For simplicity and clinical applicability, each variable was assigned 1 point regardless of the magnitude of the regression coefficient, and the total score was calculated as the sum of individual components. Weighted scoring based on regression coefficients was explored in sensitivity analyses.

To assess the score's performance, the cumulative incidence of major bleeding was estimated for each total score considering death as a competing risk, and differences between groups were compared using Gray's test. Patients were stratified into 3 bleeding risk categories as follows: low risk (0 points), intermediate risk (1 point), and high risk (≥2 points). Cumulative incidence curves were also constructed according to these bleeding risk categories. The discriminative ability of the model was assessed using Harrell's concordance index (C-index) and Uno's C-index, with the ONCO-DOAC BLEED score treated as a continuous (ordinal) variable. The performance of the developed model was compared with that of existing bleeding risk scores, including the VTE-BLEED, RIETE, CAT-BLEED, and Perform scores.

In the validation cohort, the predictive performance of the developed bleeding risk score was evaluated without model modification. Risk stratification was assessed using cumulative incidence curves, and differences between risk groups were compared using Gray's test. Discriminative performance was further evaluated using Harrell's C-index and Uno's C-index, and results were compared with those of the VTE-BLEED, RIETE, CAT-BLEED, and Perform scores. Model calibration of the prediction model was evaluated by comparing predicted and observed risks of major bleeding across score levels. Predicted risks were estimated using cumulative incidence based on the Fine-Gray method, with the ONCO-DOAC BLEED score as a continuous variable. Patients were grouped by score value, and the estimated risk and observed event rate were calculated for each score level. For external validation, regression coefficients from the derivation cohort were applied to the validation cohort to estimate predicted risks. Calibration of the predicted 1-year risk of major bleeding was evaluated separately in the derivation and validation cohorts using cumulative incidence–based calibration plots.

Missing data (<1% for all variables) were handled using a complete-case analysis. This study was conducted in accordance with the TRIPOD (Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis Or Diagnosis) statement, and the corresponding checklist is provided in the Supplemental Material. All analyses were performed using the JMP Student Edition (version 18; JMP), R Statistical Software (version 4.5.2; R Core Team, 2026), and GraphPad Prism (version 9.3.1; GraphPad Software). Statistical significance was defined as a 2-sided P value of <0.05.

Results

Patient characteristics in the derivation cohort

Of the 1,166 patients with cancer-associated VTE who received DOAC therapy, 127 experienced major bleeding during DOAC therapy (major bleeding group) and 1,039 did not (non-major bleeding group) (Figure 1). The cumulative incidence of major bleeding is shown in Supplemental Figure 1. Baseline characteristics of patients with and without major bleeding are presented in Table 1. Patients with major bleeding were more likely to have CKD, history of major bleeding, distant metastatic cancer, and anemia than those without major bleeding. Patients with major bleeding also tended to have a lower body weight and a higher prevalence of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and proton pump inhibitors use than those without major bleeding. With regard to cancer type, patients with major bleeding had a higher prevalence of upper gastrointestinal tract and uterine cancers than those without major bleeding, whereas lung and hematologic malignancies were less frequent in patients with major bleeding. Details of the bleeding sites are provided in Supplemental Table 1. Gastrointestinal and genitourinary bleeding were the most common bleeding site.

Univariable and multivariable analyses for the development of the ONCO-DOAC BLEED score

Univariable Cox proportional hazards analysis identified several factors associated with major bleeding: age ≥75 years, low body mass index <18.5 kg/m2, history of major bleeding, CKD, anemia, NSAID use, distant metastatic cancer, terminal cancer, upper gastrointestinal cancer, pancreatic cancer, and uterine cancer (Supplemental Table 2). In contrast, lung cancer and hematologic malignancy were inversely associated with major bleeding. Thrombocytopenia, antiplatelet drugs use, proton pump inhibitor use, lower gastrointestinal cancer, and bladder cancer were not significantly associated with major bleeding. The multivariable Cox proportional hazards analysis identified a history of major bleeding, CKD, NSAID use, distant metastatic cancer, terminal cancer, upper gastrointestinal cancer, pancreatic cancer, and uterine cancer as independent predictors of major bleeding (Table 2).

To develop a bleeding risk prediction score, each variable independently associated with major bleeding was assigned 1 point (Table 3). The ONCO-DOAC BLEED score was calculated as the sum of these points. Based on a median score of 1 point in the derivation cohort, patients were classified into 3 risk categories: low risk (0 points), intermediate risk (1 point), and high risk (≥2 points). The cumulative incidence of major bleeding is shown in Figure 2. One-year cumulative incidences of major bleeding were 5.7% in the low-risk group, 8.9% in the intermediate-risk group, and 21.2% in the high-risk group. The cumulative incidence of major bleeding stratified by the total ONCO-DOAC BLEED score is illustrated in Supplemental Figure 2.

Discriminative performance of the ONCO-DOAC BLEED score

Harrell's C-index and Uno's C-index of each bleeding risk score in the derivation cohort are shown in Figure 3. Harrell's C-index was 0.68 (95% CI: 0.62-0.73) for the ONCO-DOAC BLEED score, 0.55 (95% CI: 0.50-0.61) for the VTE-BLEED score, 0.58 (95% CI: 0.53-0.63) for the RIETE score, 0.58 (95% CI: 0.53-0.64) for the CAT-BLEED score, and 0.54 (95% CI: 0.48-0.59) for the Perform score. Accounting for censoring, Uno's C-index of the ONCO-DOAC BLEED score was 0.67 (95% CI: 0.62-0.72), showing higher discrimination compared with existing bleeding risk scores in the derivation cohort.

In the validation cohort, 53 of the 779 patients experienced major bleeding during DOAC therapy, whereas 726 patients did not (Figure 4). The cumulative incidence of major bleeding in the validation cohort stratified by the ONCO-DOAC BLEED score is presented in Figure 5 and Supplemental Figure 4. A stepwise increase in the cumulative incidence of major bleeding was observed across category, with the highest incidence in the high-risk group. In contrast, cumulative incidence curves based on the VTE-BLEED, RIETE, CAT-BLEED, and Perform scores demonstrated limited separation among risk groups in the validation cohort (Supplemental Figure 5). In the validation cohort, Harrell's C-index of the ONCO-DOAC BLEED score was 0.62 (95% CI: 0.54-0.70), which was numerically higher than those of the VTE-BLEED score (0.60 [95% CI: 0.52-0.68]), RIETE score (0.60 [95% CI: 0.53-0.68]), CAT-BLEED score (0.55 [95% CI: 0.47-0.63]), and Perform score (0.53 [95% CI: 0.46-0.60]). Accounting for censoring, Uno's C-index of the ONCO-DOAC BLEED score was 0.63 (95% CI: 0.54-0.71), showing similar discrimination to existing bleeding risk scores in the validation cohort (Figure 6). Calibration plots based on individual ONCO-DOAC BLEED scores are presented in Figure 7, demonstrating acceptable agreement between the predicted and observed risks of major bleeding at 1 year in both the derivation and validation cohorts. In sensitivity analyses, a weighted scoring system based on regression coefficients did not improve predictive performance compared with the unweighted model and showed less stable risk stratification in the validation cohort (Supplemental Figure 6).

Discussion

In this study, we developed a simple and clinically applicable bleeding risk score tailored to patients with cancer-associated VTE receiving DOAC therapy (Central Illustration). The novel bleeding risk score, ONCO-DOAC BLEED score, demonstrated moderate discriminative performance and improved risk stratification for major bleeding during DOAC therapy in patients with cancer-associated VTE compared with existing bleeding risk scores. Furthermore, the predictive performance of the ONCO-DOAC BLEED score was externally validated in an independent cohort.

A Bleeding Risk Score for Patients With Cancer-Associated VTE Receiving DOAC Therapy

This illustration summarizes the development and external validation of the ONCO-DOAC BLEED score for predicting major bleeding in patients with cancer-associated VTE receiving DOAC therapy. The score incorporates 8 independent predictors: history of major bleeding, chronic kidney disease, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug use, distant metastatic cancer, terminal cancer, upper gastrointestinal cancer, pancreatic cancer, and uterine cancer. The score stratified patients into low-, intermediate-, and high-risk groups with distinct cumulative incidences of major bleeding. Discrimination indices are also presented.

Several bleeding risk scores have been developed and validated in various clinical settings for patients receiving anticoagulant therapy. The HAS-BLED7 score was primarily designed to assess bleeding risk in patients with atrial fibrillation treated with warfarin. For patients treated with anticoagulation therapy for VTE, several bleeding risk scores, including the VTE-BLEED8 and RIETE9 scores, have been proposed; however, these scores were not specifically designed for patients with active cancer nor specifically for DOAC therapy. More recently, CAT-BLEED10 and Perform11 scores have been developed to predict bleeding risk during DOAC therapy in patients with cancer-associated VTE. Although these scores represent important advances and provide useful tools for predicting major bleeding in high-risk populations, their application in routine clinical practice may be limited by complex scoring calculation, insufficient discriminative performance, and limited external validation. On the other hand, the ONCO-DOAC BLEED score integrates cancer-related characteristics, such as cancer type and disease status (including metastatic and terminal cancers), together with patient comorbidities and concomitant medications. This approach was intended to balance simplicity with clinical relevance and to provide a simple, clinically meaningful tool for bleeding risk stratification that aligns with the routine management of patients with cancer-associated VTE receiving DOAC therapy. In the present study, the ONCO-DOAC BLEED score showed higher discrimination than existing bleeding risk scores in the derivation cohort (Harrell's C-index 0.68 vs 0.54-0.58), whereas differences were attenuated and not statistically significant in the validation cohort (Harrell's C-index 0.62 vs 0.53-0.60). Overall, these findings suggest modest but consistent improvements in risk stratification.

In the ONCO-DOAC BLEED score, a history of major bleeding and CKD were identified as patient-related independent risk factors for major bleeding, whereas NSAID use represented a treatment-related risk factor. In addition, cancer-related characteristics, including distant metastatic and terminal cancers, as well as cancer site factors such as upper gastrointestinal, pancreatic, and uterine cancers, were also independently associated with an increased risk of major bleeding. A history of major bleeding represents one of the strong predictors of bleeding events during anticoagulant therapy, as consistently demonstrated in previous studies.7,8 CKD is also a well-established risk factor for bleeding, particularly in patients receiving DOAC therapy, because impaired renal function alters drug clearance and increases plasma concentrations of DOACs, thereby increasing bleeding risk.19 NSAIDs increase the risk of gastrointestinal bleeding through cyclooxygenase-1 and cyclooxygenase-2 inhibition, which suppresses the synthesis of prostaglandin E2 in the gastric mucosa. This reduction in prostaglandin-mediated mucosal protection predisposes patients to gastric ulcer and upper gastrointestinal bleeding.20 A distinctive feature of the ONCO-DOAC BLEED score is the incorporation of cancer-related characteristics, including cancer type and disease status. Upper gastrointestinal and uterine cancers are anatomically predisposed to bleeding because of their proximity to luminal surfaces, which may increase vulnerability to hemorrhagic complications during anticoagulant therapy.21 Regarding pancreatic cancer, duodenal invasion has been reported as a potential cause of gastrointestinal bleeding,22 and biliary or gastrointestinal endoscopic procedures performed during DOAC therapy may further increase the risk of procedure-related bleeding.23 In addition, distant metastatic and terminal cancers have been associated with an increased risk of bleeding, potentially reflecting deterioration in clinical condition and cancer-related cachexia.24 Although overlap between distant metastatic cancer and terminal cancer was present, it was limited, and additional analyses supported their inclusion as separate variables, as described in Supplemental Appendix 5. Taken together, the ONCO-DOAC BLEED score comprehensively includes patient characteristics and cancer-related factors that have been consistently associated with an increased risk of bleeding during anticoagulant therapy.

Anticoagulant therapy plays a central role in the management of VTE; however, patients with active cancer face substantially higher risk of both recurrent thrombosis and bleeding during anticoagulation, making careful balancing of these competing risks particularly important. Assessment of bleeding risk of individual patients before initiation of anticoagulant therapy can support clinicians in decision-making regarding continuation, dose adjustment, or discontinuation of DOAC therapy, thereby facilitating safer long-term anticoagulation in patients with cancer-associated VTE. Moreover, detailed assessment of bleeding risk during DOAC therapy provides clinically meaningful information for routine practice. Identifying high-risk patients may prompt closer clinical follow-up, careful review of concomitant medications, particularly the use of NSAIDs, and monitoring of changes in patient condition, thereby supporting uninterrupted cancer treatment and optimal VTE management. Importantly, the ONCO-DOAC BLEED score was derived from a large real-world registry, allowing incorporation of patient characteristics, cancer-related factors, and treatment patterns that may be under-represented in randomized clinical trials. The score was externally validated in an independent cohort and is simple to use, supporting its potential utility as a practical tool for routine clinical decision-making.

Study Limitations

This study has some limitations. First, because both cohorts consisted of Japanese patients, external validation in Western and other populations is essential, given known ethnic differences in DOAC clearance and bleeding risk. Second, the validation cohort was derived from the ONCO DVT and ONCO PE randomized controlled trials, which excluded patients with a short life expectancy (within 3 months in the ONCO DVT study and within 6 months in the ONCO PE study), resulting in limited representation of patients with terminal cancer (an independent predictor of major bleeding in the derivation cohort) in the validation cohort. In addition, patients with PE or proximal DVT were excluded from the ONCO DVT study, yielding a lower-risk validation cohort and potentially contributing to lower bleeding rates and calibration differences. Therefore, the performance of the score, particularly in the highest-risk group, should be considered carefully, and further validation in broader real-world cohorts is warranted. In addition, the derivation and validation cohorts differed in study design and follow-up duration; therefore, the long-term incidence of major bleeding should be interpreted with caution. Finally, we attempted to incorporate all clinically relevant variables that could be reliably collected; however, some components of existing bleeding risk scores were unavailable in the registry, introducing potential risk of underfitting or overfitting.

Conclusions

The ONCO-DOAC BLEED score enables clinically relevant stratification of major bleeding risk in patients with cancer-associated VTE receiving DOAC therapy and may serve as a practical tool to support therapeutic decision-making in clinical practice.

COMPETENCY IN MEDICAL KNOWLEDGE: In patients with cancer-associated venous thromboembolism (VTE) receiving direct oral anticoagulant (DOAC) therapy, a history of major bleeding, chronic kidney disease, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug use, distant metastatic cancer, terminal cancer, and specific cancer types—including upper gastrointestinal, pancreatic, and uterine cancers—were independently associated with an increased risk of major bleeding. The ONCO-DOAC BLEED score was developed based on these factors and provides clinically relevant stratification of bleeding risk in this high-risk population.

TRANSLATIONAL OUTLOOK: The ONCO-DOAC BLEED score may facilitate individualized bleeding risk assessment in patients with cancer-associated VTE receiving DOAC therapy and may inform clinical decision-making regarding anticoagulant management in clinical practice. Future studies are warranted to validate its performance in various populations and to evaluate whether the clinical application of this score can improve patient outcomes.
