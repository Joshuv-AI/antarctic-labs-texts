MyClone: rapid and precise reconstruction of clonal population structures for tumors
Authors: Qihan Guo, Dan Lv, Bo Lan, Haili Qian, Fei Ma, Ting Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUnderstanding tumor heterogeneity is essential for advancing cancer treatment. Clonal reconstruction methods play a pivotal role in deciphering this heterogeneity. Our goal is to develop a clonal reconstruction approach that is clinically applicable, easy to implement, and capable of delivering both high-speed performance and excellent reconstruction accuracy.ResultsWe present MyClone, a probabilistic method designed to reconstruct the clonal composition of tumors using deep sequencing genomic data. MyClone processes read counts and copy number information of single nucleotide variants derived from deep sequencing data, enabling it to determine the mutational composition of clones and the cancer cell fractions of these mutations. Compared to existing clonal reconstruction methods, MyClone enhances clustering accuracy and cancer cell fraction prediction when applied to deep-targeted sequencing data and bulk tumor sequencing data with deep sequencing coverage. Additionally, MyClone achieves a substantial improvement in computational speed. We rigorously validated MyClone’s performance using both simulated and real clinical datasets and applied it to analyze a circulating tumor DNA sequencing dataset from 139 metastatic breast cancer patients. In this analysis, we explored mechanisms of drug resistance in metastatic breast cancer and identified 10 mutated genes potentially associated with drug resistance or sensitivity.ConclusionsFor deeply sequenced data, MyClone outperforms existing methods on both targeted sequencing and bulk tumor data. Its high computational efficiency and reconstruction accuracy position MyClone as a promising tool for broad clinical application in cancer treatment. Background

Modern research in cancer genetics has established that cancer development follows a Darwinian evolutionary process [1, 2]. This process is driven by sequential somatic mutations and subclonal selections, resulting in tumors composed of multiple genetically distinct cell populations [3, 4]. These populations, referred to as clones, exhibit complex phylogenetic relationships and contribute to tumor heterogeneity. Tumor heterogeneity poses a significant obstacle to cancer treatment, increasing the risk of malignant progression [5, 6] and serving as a major driver of treatment resistance [7–10]. Reconstructing the clonal architecture of tumors can provide critical insights into how tumors respond to treatments [11, 12], identify mutations that drive cancer progression or cause therapeutic resistance [13–15], and inform the design of more effective treatment strategies [8].

Accurate reconstruction of tumor clonal architecture often requires spatially diverse tumor sampling [16] and advanced single-cell sequencing techniques [17–19]. Hence, precise clonal reconstruction through clinical measurement remains challenging due to high costs and current technological limitations [20]. Current strategies for clonal reconstruction typically combine next-generation sequencing (NGS) with probabilistic computational methods. The standard workflow is as follows: (1) performing bulk sequencing to identify genetic mutations, including single nucleotide variations (SNVs) with allele frequencies and copy number alteration (CNA) regions; (2) applying computational methods to cluster mutations into clones and reconstruct the clonal structure from the bulk sequencing data [21–23]. These methods model the clonal evolution of tumors as a probabilistic mixture model, where the clonal structure is treated as unknown parameters within a probability distribution, and sequencing data are considered samples drawn from this distribution. Bayesian inference is then employed to infer the unknown clonal architecture from the sequencing data.

We formally define the clonal reconstruction problem as follows. The cancer cell fraction (CCF) of a mutation represents the proportion of cells carrying this mutation in all tumor cells. A tumor may comprise multiple mutation clusters, with mutations in each cluster sharing similar CCFs. Genomic sequencing detects all mutations present in a tumor, along with their variant allele frequencies (VAFs), defined as the ratio of mutant allele reads to total reads at a given locus. Sequencing also provides copy number information for each mutation locus, indicating the average number of alleles per cell (typically 2 in the absence of copy number abnormalities). Clonal reconstruction involves inferring the clonal composition of a tumor from sequencing data, including the number of clones, the set of mutations each clone contains, and the CCF of each mutation.

Many methods have been developed to infer clonal population structure from genomic sequencing data. We briefly review some of the most widely used methods [24–38]. Most clonal reconstruction methods rely on Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) techniques, with PyClone [28] being a prominent example widely used in clinical settings. However, MCMC-based methods are computationally intensive and slow. Another common approach is the Expectation-Maximization (EM) method, exemplified by SciClone [25], which can handle multi-sample tumor data but is limited to SNVs in regions with normal copy numbers (i.e., copy number = 2). Other methods employ different techniques: FastClone [32] uses a Gaussian kernel function to improve computational speed but is restricted to single-sample tumor data, while cfdna-wgs [39] utilizes K-means clustering to circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) sequencing data but suffers from a relatively low clustering accuracy.

Here, we present MyClone, a novel probabilistic method for clonal reconstruction from deep sequencing genomic data. MyClone offers several key advancements:

Expanded data compatibility: MyClone enables precise clonal reconstruction not only for targeted sequencing data but also for deeply sequenced bulk tumor data. While current methods mainly evaluate their performance on bulk tumor data, MyClone demonstrates superior clustering accuracy and CCF prediction accuracy on targeted sequencing data, which is widely adopted in clinical settings due to its low sequencing costs and high sequencing depth [40–42]. This makes MyClone particularly valuable for identifying driver mutations and guiding treatment decisions.

Expanded data compatibility: MyClone enables precise clonal reconstruction not only for targeted sequencing data but also for deeply sequenced bulk tumor data. While current methods mainly evaluate their performance on bulk tumor data, MyClone demonstrates superior clustering accuracy and CCF prediction accuracy on targeted sequencing data, which is widely adopted in clinical settings due to its low sequencing costs and high sequencing depth [40–42]. This makes MyClone particularly valuable for identifying driver mutations and guiding treatment decisions.

Dynamic clonal reconstruction: MyClone can process multiple genome sequencing data from a single patient at different time points, reconstructing the complete dynamic clonal landscape of the patient. This feature is especially relevant for liquid biopsy data, which allows for repeated, non-invasive sampling over time [43].

To evaluate the performance of MyClone, we conducted experiments on simulated tumor sequencing data, demonstrating its superior accuracy and speed. We further validate MyClone using a clinical breast cancer dataset that includes both bulk sequencing and single-cell sequencing data, demonstrating that it outperforms current commonly used clonal reconstruction methods. Finally, we applied MyClone to the deep-targeted ctDNA sequencing data from 139 patients with metastatic breast cancer. By analyzing clone status and CCF changes of mutations in patients treated with specific drugs, we partially decoded the mechanism of drug resistance in metastatic breast cancer and identified 10 mutated genes that showed a significant response to the treatment (resistant or sensitive). These findings highlight MyClone’s potential to advance precision oncology by providing clinical insights into tumor evolution and treatment resistance.

For the sake of simplicity, VAF is used in the figure to represent the combination of variant read count and total read count. The basic clustering module uses a mixture statistical model for initial clustering, assuming that all mutations are heterozygous with a copy number of 2. The clonal segmentation module then segments out mutations affected by CNAs from the initial clustering to prevent these mutations from being incorrectly clustered. The tumor purity correction module uses the CCF of the Main clone to predict tumor purity. The input data is then modified to reflect a tumor purity of 1 and re-submitted into the basic clustering module. The data is then passed through the clonal segmentation module again and input into the clonal merging module. The clonal merging module traverses the possible genotypes for each CNA-affected mutation. The genotype whose CCF matches a clone with a normal copy number is taken as the inferred genotype, and the mutation is then assigned to the corresponding clone. If none of the genotypes can match a clone with a normal copy number, the mutation is assigned to a separate clone only containing CNA-affected mutations. CN, copy number; VAF, variant allele frequency; CCF, cancer cell fraction

In the first round, we infer tumor purity by estimating the CCF of the main clone (i.e., the clone with the highest CCF). As the starting clone, the CCF of the main clone should align with the tumor purity. The process involves three modules: (1) the basic clustering module, which performs initial clustering without considering CNAs; (2) the clonal segmentation module, which removes CNA-affected mutations from the main clone; and (3) the tumor purity correction module, which assigns correct mutations to the main clone based on copy number data (fewer input mutations) or examines CNA-unaffected mutations in the main clone (more input mutations) and calculates the CCF of the main clone.

Once tumor purity is inferred (or provided), we normalize the data to a tumor purity of 1 by adjusting reference read counts. The second round begins with the basic clustering module (same as Round 1) and proceeds through two additional modules: (1) the clonal segmentation module, which isolates CNA-affected mutations; and (2) the clonal merging module, which predicts true CCFs for these mutations using their copy number information and assigns them to the correct clusters. In Methods section, we will introduce the four modules utilized in the computational process in detail.

MyClone significantly outperforms current clonal reconstruction methods for deep targeted sequencing data

To demonstrate MyClone’s ability to perform rapid and precise clonal reconstruction on targeted sequencing data, we conducted experiments using simulated data of the cfdna-wgs model [39]. By introducing random somatic mutations and copy number alterations, this simulated data mimicked a family of phylogenetically related cancer cell populations. The simulation tracked the copy numbers of parental and maternal alleles at each genomic locus, as well as the number of mutant allele copies for each somatic mutation, so that copy number changes affected the mutant allele counts realistically. In Methods section, we will introduce the generation methods of this simulated data in detail. We compared MyClone with nine other methods on the simulated targeted sequencing data: PyClone-VI [24], PhyloWGS [26], FastClone [32], Pairtree [33], CONIPHER [34], DeCiFer [35], Sclust [36], CALDER [37] and CSR [38]. Among them, CSR is a consensus approach proposed by PCAWG, which aggregates the inference results of all the other methods except MyClone.

We first conducted experiments on simulated single-sample deep-targeted sequencing data. In this dataset, we generated 100 patients with a single tumor sample. Each simulated patient has 9–14 mutations, which originated from 4 clones. The average sequencing depth is 2,000. The parameter settings for this dataset referred to the characteristics of deep-targeted sequencing data in clinical settings [43]. Figure 2 summarizes the performance of the ten methods on this simulated dataset.

The V-measure was used to assess the accuracy of clustering (Fig. 2a), with values ranging from 0 to 1, where a value of 1 indicates perfect clustering [44]. V-measure is the harmonic mean of homogeneity (how uniform the clusters are) and completeness (how well all data points of a class are assigned to the same cluster), providing a balanced evaluation of clustering quality. MyClone achieves a median V-measure score of 0.893, outperforming all other methods, with the closest competitor attaining a median score of 0.886. The average absolute deviation between the predicted and actual CCFs for each mutation was calculated to evaluate the accuracy of CCF estimation (Fig. 2b). The median deviation of MyClone is 0.014, while the lowest median deviations among the other methods is 0.019. Additionally, MyClone’s median runtime is 0.204 s, significantly faster than the next fastest method, which takes a median of 2.269 s (Fig. 2c). We excluded CSR’s runtime from the comparison since it directly utilizes the results of other methods for inference. These results demonstrate that MyClone outperforms all competing methods in clustering accuracy, CCF estimation accuracy, and runtime on simulated single-sample targeted sequencing data.

Furthermore, we simulated multi-sample deep-targeted sequencing data. In this dataset, we generated 100 patients with three temporally different tumor samples. Each simulated patient has 13–20 mutations, which originated from 6 clones. The average sequencing depth is 2,000. Since FastClone and Sclust can only run on single-sample sequencing data, we ran MyClone, PyClone-VI, PhyloWGS, Pairtree, CONIPHER, DeCiFer, CALDER and CSR on this simulated dataset. Figure 3 summarizes the performance of the eight methods on this simulated dataset.

MyClone achieves a median V-measure score of 0.949, while the median score of other methods ranges from 0.688 to 0.942 (Fig. 3a). For CCF estimation, its median deviation of 0.011 is lower than the range of median deviations (0.014 to 0.181) reported by other approaches (Fig. 3b). MyClone still significantly outperforms other methods in clustering accuracy, CCF estimation accuracy, and runtime. These results confirm that MyClone is the top-performing clonal reconstruction method for deep targeted sequencing data, offering high-speed and high-accuracy reconstruction in clinical settings.

MyClone also performs best on bulk tumor data with deep sequencing coverage

To show the versatility of MyClone, we further simulated bulk tumor data using the simulation framework of TRACERx [45]. The TRACERx simulation framework generates realistic tumor phylogenies and bulk sequencing data by modelling the evolution of various genetic alterations, including SNVs, CNAs, and Whole Genome Doublings, and their impact on mutation multiplicities and losses. It leverages detailed statistical distributions from the TRACERx 421 cohort to ensure simulations reflect real-world genomic characteristics, such as mutation burden, sequencing coverage, and CNA frequency. In this simulated dataset, we generated 100 simulated patients, each with 3 tumor samples, 3–8 clones, and 178–3,788 mutations. The average sequencing depth is 2,000. In addition to the methods compared on simulated multi-sample targeted sequencing data, we further included comparisons with SciClone [25] and cfdna-wgs [39], which are specifically designed for datasets with a larger number of mutations. Figure 4 provides an overview of the performance of the ten methods on the simulated bulk tumor data.

Since SciClone does not predict the CCF of mutations, it was not compared in the accuracy metric of the CCF estimation. On the V-measure metric, MyClone achieves a median score of 0.802, surpassing the highest median score of 0.742 among competing methods (Fig. 4a). In terms of CCF estimation accuracy, MyClone exhibits a median deviation of 0.037, outperforming the lowest median deviation of 0.058 achieved by other methods (Fig. 4b). Concerning running speed, MyClone’s median runtime of 3.741 s is faster than the next fastest method, which takes a median of 6.971 s (Fig. 4c). The results show that on the simulated deep-sequenced bulk tumor data, MyClone still outperforms current methods across all the three metrics.

It is worth noting that bulk tumor data with sufficient sequencing depth can be obtained by performing deep resequencing on the mutations detected from low-depth sequencing. This approach has been widely used in research on cancer genome remodeling [46–48]. Furthermore, previous research showed that ensuring sufficient sequencing depth for tumor data is necessary for correct clonal reconstruction [25, 27].

Through the simulation experiments, we have demonstrated that MyClone has the best performance currently available, whether on targeted sequencing data or bulk tumor data with high sequencing depth. Therefore, given sufficient sequencing depth, MyClone will be the optimal choice among clonal reconstruction methods.

MyClone is only suitable for high-depth sequencing data

To investigate the applicability of MyClone across sequencing data with varying depths, we generated two simulated patient cohorts using the TRACERx simulation framework, each comprising 100 patients with 3–8 clones per patient. The average sequencing depths of the two cohorts are 200 and 60, representing typical depths for whole-exome sequencing (WES) and whole-genome sequencing (WGS) data, respectively [23]. We applied MyClone, PyClone-VI, and Pairtree to both simulated patient cohorts and compared the experimental results with their performance on the TRACERx-simulated data with an average sequencing depth of 2,000 described in the previous section, as shown in Fig. 5.

Consistent with the previous section, we employed the V-measure to assess clustering accuracy (Fig. 5a) and CCF error to evaluate CCF prediction accuracy (Fig. 5b). At an average sequencing depth of 2,000, MyClone exhibits the highest median V-measure and the lowest median CCF error among the three methods. Robustness of myclone to mutation loss caused by sparsity

When the sequencing depth is insufficient, some SNVs may not be detected, thereby affecting the accuracy of clonal reconstruction. This is known as the sparsity issue [26, 28]. To validate the robustness of MyClone against mutation loss caused by sparsity, we performed downsampling on the simulated bulk tumor data from TRACERx with 2,000x coverage in the previous section. For each simulated tumor, we randomly removed 80% of the mutations and compared the clonal inference results of MyClone on the retained mutations before and after downsampling. We still used V-measure and CCF error to characterize MyClone’s clustering accuracy (Fig. 6a) and CCF prediction accuracy (Fig. 6b). Changes in these two metrics before and after downsampling are very small, proving that MyClone is robust to mutation loss caused by sparsity. It should be noted that in this simulation experiment, we still employed mutation data with sufficient sequencing depth, which in real-world scenarios could be achieved by performing deep resequencing on mutations obtained from sparse sequencing data [46–48].

We used seven cases: SA429, SA494, SA500, SA501, SA530, SA531, and SA535, each containing 2–6 tumor samples, totaling 29 samples. MyClone helps to discover new drug-resistant and drug-sensitive genes in metastatic breast cancer

We applied MyClone to a cohort of 327 metastatic breast cancer samples from 139 patients, profiled using targeted sequencing at approximately 2,000× depth. We firstly analyzed the drug responses of frequently mutated genes to commonly used drugs. We examined all pairwise combinations of 22 genes (mutated in at least 10 individuals) and 29 drugs (administered to at least 10 individuals). For each gene-drug pair, a contingency table was constructed to record treatment receipt and changes in mutation CCF. Significant correlations were found in 15 combinations, involving 10 genes (e.g., TP53, ESR1, PTEN). Eight combinations linked treatment to increased mutation CCF, while seven showed decreased CCF. Our MyClone analysis discovered that chemotherapeutic drugs such as paclitaxel might be associated with TP53 mutations, and resistance to fulvestrant might also be linked to TP53. Previous studies have confirmed that breast cancer patients with TP53 mutations have a worse prognosis when treated with paclitaxel or fulvestrant [50–52], which is consistent with our MyClone findings. Additionally, MyClone identified ESR1 mutations as potentially associated with resistance to toremifene, a selective estrogen receptor modulator (SERM) with a structure and efficacy nearly identical to tamoxifen [53]. Furthermore, MyClone also identified new resistance-related genes: DDR2 may be related to fulvestrant resistance, and DNMT3A may be associated with resistance to bevacizumab. These findings, however, require further validation through additional fundamental experiments in the future.

MyClone helps to Decode the mechanism of drug resistance in metastatic breast cancer

We further investigated the relationship between the clonal status of frequently mutated genes and their drug response. Similar to the previous analysis, we analyzed all pairwise combinations of 22 recurrently mutated genes and 29 widely administered drugs. For each gene-drug pair, a contingency table was compiled to record the clonal status (clonal or subclonal) and changes in CCF (increase or decrease) of the mutation. Among these, 37 combinations showed significant correlations, with all cases consistently linking subclonal mutations to increased CCF, consistent with prior research suggesting a strong link between subclones and treatment resistance [57].

Our previous studies on patients treated with pyrotinib and capecitabine indicated that those with TP53 mutations had poorer prognoses [58, 59]. In our study, MyClone analysis revealed a significant increase in the CCF of TP53 subclones following the treatment with these drugs, suggesting that TP53 subclones become more dominant and contribute to resistance compared to the original master clone, aligning with Darwinian evolution theory [57]. Similarly, PIK3CA mutations have been reported as major causes of resistance to letrozole and fulvestrant [60, 61]. Our MyClone analysis showed an increase in the CCF of PIK3CA subclones after treatment with these drugs, indicating that PIK3CA subclones gain dominance in drug resistance.

These findings suggest that clonal reconstruction can more clearly elucidate the mechanisms of drug resistance. Selective pressure from treatment promotes the survival and proliferation of resistant subclones, contributing to treatment failure and disease progression. Discussion

In this work, we have introduced MyClone, a versatile clonal reconstruction method suitable for deep sequencing data. Compared to other existing methods, MyClone demonstrates significant performance advantages on targeted sequencing data and bulk tumor data with deep sequencing coverage. Notably, MyClone’s rapid runtime suggests its potential for assisting in inferring tumor heterogeneity within very large-scale datasets.

Our method outperforms existing approaches due to MyClone’s straightforward inference mechanism, which is particularly effective when sequencing depth is sufficient and measured VAF closely matches true VAF. First, when the number of clones is not large and mutation VAFs are accurate, they naturally form distinct groups with clear boundaries, enabling precise initial clustering with K-means (MyClone’s initialization), which improves subsequent inference accuracy. Second, current Bayesian methods employ complex probabilistic modeling based on genetic lineage to ensure inference performance with low-quality data. While this complex modeling safeguards the lower limit of inference accuracy, it compromises the upper limit, making it challenging for tumor parameters to converge to true values. For example, PhyloWGS [26] models SNVs in a tree structure, where the CCF of descendant nodes depends on parent nodes. While this reduces errors with low sequencing depth, deeper nodes’ CCFs struggle to converge, limiting performance. In contrast, MyClone’s simple statistical assumptions allow it to converge to accurate tumor parameters when VAFs are precise, yielding better results. Third, MyClone handles CNAs more effectively when VAFs are accurate. Current Bayesian methods integrate CNAs into statistical models. For example, PyClone [28] considers various possible copy number states of SNVs when calculating CCFs and provides a comprehensive prediction based on probabilities. While this prediction does not deviate significantly from the true value, it rarely achieves accurate approximation. However, if VAFs are accurate, the copy number state of mutations can be precisely calculated. For instance, when the VAF of a mutation with a total copy number of 3 is approximately two-thirds of the VAF of mutations with a copy number of 2 in a clone, it can be deduced that this mutation affected by CNAs belongs to the clone and the mutation copy number is 1. In summary, MyClone’s simplicity achieves higher inference accuracy than complex models when VAFs are precise. Consequently, MyClone is well-suited for targeted sequencing data due to the typically high sequencing depth associated with such datasets. Furthermore, when the measured VAFs are not sufficiently accurate due to inadequate sequencing depth, the performance of MyClone falls short compared to certain clonal reconstruction methods, such as PyClone-VI [24].

The methodological framework of MyClone also endows it with robustness to sparsity. Firstly, MyClone can process data from multiple tumor samples simultaneously during clonal inference, which to some extent mitigates the problem of undetected mutations in individual sequencing runs, as mutations missed in one sample may be captured in another. Furthermore, MyClone primarily relies on the VAF of mutations for inferring clonal clustering, and has little dependence on the number of mutations. Thus, the absence of some mutations does not affect the overall clonal inference. While the loss of mutations due to sparsity does not significantly affect MyClone’s performance, the large measurement deviations in mutation VAFs caused by low coverage can significantly impact clonal inference results. This is a common challenge for clonal reconstruction methods, and previous studies have emphasized the necessity of sufficient sequencing depth for accurate clonal reconstruction [25, 26]. In clinical settings, deep sequencing is widely adopted because of its ability to detect low-frequency mutations and its high sensitivity [62, 63]. And mutations initially identified through low-coverage methods, such as WGS, can be subjected to deep resequencing to improve their coverage [46–48]. Therefore, our current analysis focuses on the application of MyClone to deep sequencing data, where its performance is optimized. Future work could explore the extension of MyClone to low-coverage data scenarios.

MyClone offers numerous additional advantages. Firstly, MyClone’s runtime is faster than that of existing clonal reconstruction methods. This is mainly because, unlike other methods that use computationally less efficient Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) techniques (e.g., PyClone [28], PhyloWGS [26]), we employ a more computationally efficient variational EM algorithm. Moreover, our method can automatically infer the number of clusters without the need for setting various assumptions and selecting from different clustering results (e.g., PyClone-VI [24], PhyloWGS [26]). Besides, MyClone is fully implemented in Python, making it easy to deploy.

Additionally, circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) is an emerging tool in clinical cancer genotyping and treatment [64]. It offers advantages such as minimally invasive, low-cost, and the ability to collect dynamic tumor samples over time. It has been widely used in cancer clinical applications [65–67], and increasing research has proven that ctDNA can be used to analyze tumor heterogeneity [43, 68, 69]. In this work, we have used ctDNA simulation data from the cfdna-wgs model [39] and applied MyClone for medical analysis on real ctDNA samples. The excellent performance on the simulated data suggests that MyClone is applicable to ctDNA data, further demonstrating its versatility in handling various data types. Our medical analysis based on ctDNA sequencing data also demonstrates that the combination of ctDNA sequencing data and MyClone can effectively monitor treatment response, assess prognosis, and identify potential drug-resistant mutations to adjust treatment strategy.

A limitation of MyClone is that it relies on some strong assumptions in its methodology. We assume that the VAF of mutations follows a Binomial distribution, and in some modules of MyClone we assume that mutations are heterozygous with a copy number of 2. These assumptions facilitate the implementation of the method but may lead to some biases when encountering genomes with extremely complex mutation patterns. In our future work, we plan to incorporate biological priors into the statistical assumptions to enhance the stability and interpretability of the method.

Conclusions

MyClone, as a clonal reconstruction method, possesses comprehensive capabilities. It can handle data from multiple samples, automatically infer tumor purity, and correct for CNAs. It shows state-of-the-art performance on both targeted sequencing data and bulk tumor data with deep sequencing coverage. It can run on sequencing results from both tissue biopsies and ctDNA for clonal inference. With its fast runtime and high reconstruction accuracy, MyClone has the potential for widespread use in clinical cancer treatment.

Module 2. Clonal segmentation module of myclone

This module’s input consists of several clones. The module aims to segment the clones to separate mutations that may have been incorrectly clustered together. Initially, each mutation is marked with its sample vector. If a clone contains mutations with different sample vectors, it is segmented into several clones, ensuring that each clone contains a unique sample vector and that different clones contain different sample vectors. It is noted that mutations belonging to the same clone are co-present, so we consider that two mutations with different sample vectors do not belong to the same clone. After segmenting the clones based on the sample vectors, the copy-number vectors for each mutation are then annotated. The copy number vector for mutation is an -dimensional vector where the -th element records the total copy number of the mutation locus of in . This step is performed to prevent incorrect clustering due to CNAs, as two mutations from different clones may exhibit similar VAF due to CNAs. Finally, the module outputs all clones resulting from the two rounds of segmentation.

Module 3. Tumor purity correction module of myclone

The module’s input consists of several clones, each with a unique copy number vector and sample vector. The module operates in two modes: a simple mode and a complete mode. The simple mode is more computationally efficient than the complete mode, but it will produce significant errors when the total number of mutations is low, preventing accurate inference of tumor purity. Therefore, it is only used when the total number of mutations is relatively high. When the total number of mutations is low, we use the complete mode to accurately infer tumor purity.

When the total number of mutations exceeds 100, the simple mode is used. In simple mode, the module first discards clones with too few mutations (specifically, < 5 mutations) and clones with copy number abnormalities (i.e., those with non-2 elements in their copy number vectors). Module 4. The clonal merging module of myclone

The module’s input consists of several clones, each with a unique copy number vector and sample vector. The module first extracts all clones without copy number aberrations and then merges clones with close CCF vectors among them. Specifically, if the Euclidean distance between the CCF vectors of two clones is less than (We set in our experiment), they are merged, and the CCF vector is updated based on the new mutation contents until no such clones exist. After the merging process completes, these clones without copy number abnormalities are used as reference clones, and each copy number abnormal clone is attempted to be matched to one of these reference clones using the same method as the complete mode of the tumor purity correction module. However, unlike the tumor purity correction module, for copy number abnormal clones that fail to match, their CCF vectors are set to the closest probable CCF vector to the reference clones’ CCF vectors during the matching process. Finally, the module outputs the remaining clones as the final clustering outcome of MyClone after the matching process completes, along with the CCF vectors of these clones.

Simulated data generation methods

Based on the simulation framework of cfdna-wgs model [39], we simulated targeted sequencing data obtained from a patient through multiple ctDNA liquid biopsies. For a simulated patient, we first determined the number of detections , which can be arbitrarily specified. Then, for each genome sequencing, we randomly generated a cancer fraction (i.e., the proportion of ctDNA in cell-free DNA), with this value ranging between 0.3 and 0.8. Given the total number of clones for the patient, we randomly generated a phylogenetic tree for these clones, which remains consistent across each genome sequencing, representing the overall phylogenetic tree of the patient. We then randomly generated the mutations contained within each clone, with the number of mutations specified in advance. For each mutation, we randomly determined the affected genomic region, with a SNV to CNA occurrence ratio of 199:1. Next, we assigned a CCF to each clone in the phylogenetic tree, with this CCF value representing the CCF of all mutations contained within the clone. At this point, we have completed the simulation of the patient’s precise clonal evolutionary process.

Parameter settings of the compared methods

In this work, we compared MyClone with eleven other clonal reconstruction methods: PyClone-VI [24], SciClone [25], PhyloWGS [26], cfdna-wgs [39], FastClone [32], Pairtree [33], CONIPHER [34], DeCiFer [35], Sclust [36], CALDER [37] and CSR [38]. To balance runtime and inference accuracy, we set burnin-samples to 20 and mcmc-samples to 100 when running PhyloWGS, and --iterations to 100 when running Pairtree. We set the initial_clusters parameter of cfdna-wgs to 100 and the _SMALLEST_CLONE parameter of FastClone to 1, ensuring their compatibility with the mutation count in the simulated data. For DeCiFer, we set -k to 2 and -K to 10 to align with the number of clones in the simulated data. For the other methods, we used the default running parameters.

Patients and sample collection

This study included patients diagnosed with metastatic breast cancer who were treated at the National Cancer Center/National Clinical Research Center for Cancer/Cancer Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences, and Peking Union Medical College from April 25, 2016, to May 31, 2021. All patients enrolled in this study voluntarily participated in biomarker research.

The following inclusion criteria for patients were used: (1) Confirmed diagnosis of metastatic breast cancer through cytological or histological examination at the specified center. (2) Availability of at least two ctDNA tests, requiring sufficient peripheral blood specimens. (3) Inclusion of patients with complete clinical and pathological data. (4) Participation is voluntary upon signing an informed consent form. Exclusion criteria included: (1) Patients with incomplete clinical or pathological data. (2) Patients undergoing neoadjuvant therapy at the time of enrollment. (3) Patients without recurrence or metastasis following breast cancer surgery.

To assess treatment response, Computed Tomography (CT) scans were conducted either after every two treatment cycles or upon observing signs or symptoms indicative of disease progression. Response to treatment was evaluated using the RECIST 1.1 criteria.

CtDNA analysis

The sequencing of 1021 genes commonly mutated in breast cancer was performed, as detailed in Supplementary Table 3. Somatic mutations were identified by analyzing paired germline DNA and plasma isolated from blood cells. The process involved DNA extraction, library preparation, hybrid capture, sequencing, and analysis, following previously described methods [43, 70].

Statistical information

Statistical analysis of the contingency table was performed using the Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test, both of which were two-tailed. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. All statistical analyses were conducted using Python (3.9.13).
