Sex-specific transcriptome dynamics of Anopheles gambiae during embryonic development
Authors: Agata Izabela Kalita, Eric Marois, Frank Rühle, Claudia Isabelle Keller Valsecchi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In this study, Kalita et al. detail the sex-specific transcriptomes and gene expression dynamics during Anopheles embryogenesis, highlighting the differences in alternative polyadenylation and splicing during zygotic genome activation. Furthermore, they report that the sexually dimorphic regulator SOA mediates X-linked dosage compensation that underpins sex-biased gene expression, representing a mechanism of collective, chromosome-level, gene regulation.Keywords: malaria mosquitos, embryogenesis, zygotic genome activation, sex-specific gene expression, alternative polyadenylation, dosage compensation, X chromosome

In many species, males and females display differences in their physiology that are not limited to the gonads. For example, sex differences in adult insects comprise body size, ornamentation, coloration, and feeding behavior, as only female, but not male, mosquitoes are blood feeding in order to produce eggs (Hopkins and Kopp 2021). This is relevant because the malaria-causing parasite Plasmodium is transmitted by a female mosquito after a previous blood meal taken on an infected individual. Thus, understanding sex differences may have a big impact on the fight against malaria by inspiring new vector control strategies.

Sexually dimorphic traits are determined by complex gene-regulatory networks operating during the embryonic, larval, and pupal developmental stages. The current understanding of the expression dynamics along Anopheles mosquito embryogenesis is based on a mixed-sex microarray data set, which lacks information on sex differences, noncoding transcripts, and splicing isoforms (Goltsev et al. 2009). Another study has investigated the transcriptome of male embryos in comparison with a mixed-sex embryo population, limited to a single replicate per stage (Hall et al. 2016). Others have studied sex-specific transcriptomes at postembryonic stages (Jiang et al. 2015; Hall et al. 2016; Rose et al. 2016; Papa et al. 2017). Thus, the sex-specific Anopheles gene regulation in embryos, especially at the stage where the embryo switches from maternal to zygotic transcription, is largely uncharacterized to date.

The initiation of sex-specific gene expression in the malaria mosquito Anopheles gambiae is conferred by the Y-linked maleness gene termed Yob (Krzywinska et al. 2016). Yob expression starts in early embryos at zygotic genome activation (ZGA) and triggers a largely unknown cascade of molecular events ultimately leading to the establishment of distinct phenotypes in males and females (Scali et al. 2005). Two recent studies (Kalita et al. 2023; Krzywinska et al. 2023) identified the SOA/007 gene (referred to here as SOA), encoding a male-specific DNA binding protein expressed after Yob, as the master regulator of Anopheles dosage compensation (DC). We showed that the SOA protein associates specifically with the X chromosome, where it mediates the upregulation of X-chromosomal genes in XY males in order to match their expression levels in XX females. SOA binding sites in pupae are predominantly found at gene promoters and are enriched in a simple CA dinucleotide repeat DNA sequence (Kalita et al. 2023). How SOA binding is established during Anopheles development and what sequence preference underlies its targeting remain unknown to date. The striking process of collective gene regulation spanning an entire chromosome renders the study of the developmental onset and targeting principles of DC complexes particularly interesting.

The DC mechanism is well characterized in another dipteran insect, the model organism Drosophila melanogaster. Here, this mechanism is established in a two-step fashion during embryogenesis (Lott et al. 2011; Prayitno et al. 2019; Samata et al. 2020). First, the master regulator of DC, the MSL2 protein, becomes expressed during the blastoderm stage, whereupon it binds to the X chromosome at ∼300 high-affinity sites (HASs). In the second step, the multisubunit MSL complex assembles and spreads to all X-linked genes. Through histone H4 lysine 16 acetylation along the entire X chromosome, the MSL complex mediates upregulation of expressed X-linked genes. Acetylation gradually increases over several hours during embryogenesis, and some genes (e.g., developmental regulators and genes that are located further away from the HASs) get compensated with a delay. Notably, because of the properties of (1) HAS targeting and (2) spreading, the compensation mechanism can act in principle on any gene located on the X chromosome. Accordingly, when autosomal genes are artificially inserted on the X chromosome, they can become targeted by the MSL complex and acquire DC (Gorchakov et al. 2009). However, in another dipteran, Lucilia cuprina, the DC mechanism cannot compensate transgenes and appears to be specific for genes native to the X chromosome (Linger et al. 2015). Although the DC mechanism in L. cuprina is not well understood, it suggests that DC in insects is not necessarily based on the same universal molecular mechanism.

SOA's direct binding at promoter regions of expressed genes at pupa stage (∼10 days of development) (Bayoh and Lindsay 2003) is remarkably distinct from Drosophila MSL2. However, the dynamics of SOA binding—whether it initially targets HASs or whether specific gene groups are particularly sensitive to its loss—are currently unknown.

Here, we used single-embryo RNA-seq to identify the sex-specific transcriptome and onset of the DC mechanism along Anopheles embryogenesis. We characterized stage- and sex-specific RNA-processing events and identified the expression onset of sexually dimorphic protein-coding and noncoding RNA transcripts. Genome-wide profiling of SOA by CUT&Tag in embryos and analysis of its loss reveal that, as in pupae, SOA (1) directly localizes to promoters and (2) readily establishes DC when genes become expressed. Thus, we propose that the SOA pathway operates “gene by gene” yet in a chromosome-wide fashion. Our atlas provides a framework to explore sex differences and X-chromosome regulation in development and evolution and thereby enables future functional analyses of sex-specific expression patterns and isoform usage.

Results

The developmental transcriptome of male and female Anopheles embryos

We isolated RNA from single A. gambiae embryos before and at eight different time points after ZGA as well as from neonate first instar larvae (Fig. 1A). Because male and female embryos cannot be visually distinguished, we genotyped single embryos prior to generating libraries for RNA-seq (see the Materials and Methods; Fig. 1B; Supplemental Fig. S1A). We then performed poly(A)+ RNA-seq followed by quality control (QC), including assessing the mapping rates, 3′ end bias indicative of degradation, library complexity, or clustering (Supplemental Table S1). To the best of our knowledge, our atlas is the first RNA-seq data set of single male and female embryos of any mosquito species. It represents a significant improvement over a microarray study on a mixed-sex A. gambiae population (Goltsev et al. 2009) that contains information on ∼10,000 protein-coding genes (Supplemental Fig. S1B) and a previous RNA-seq study (Hall et al. 2016) where only males in comparison with a mixed population (single replicate) were investigated.

We mapped our data to a custom genome annotation (n = 16,725 genes), which is based on the current AgamP4.14 Ensembl annotation but additionally contains 3171 putative long noncoding (lnc)RNA genes identified by Jenkins et al. (2015) (Supplemental Fig. S1B). The additional genes included in our data display on average lower expression levels and higher variability across developmental stages compared with the gene set described by Goltsev et al. (2009) (Supplemental Fig. S1C,D).

To validate that our transcriptome accurately captures the intended developmental time points, we scrutinized genes with well-established dynamics in Drosophila and Anopheles (Goltsev et al. 2004, 2009). This confirmed the expected expression patterns of RPL19 (constitutive), mat α-tubulin (maternal), even-skipped (early embryonic), fushi tarazu (ftz), and hunchback (segmentation and ZGA), as well as the Hox gene Antennapedia (gastrulation) (Fig. 1C; Supplemental Fig. S1E). To further confirm that we accurately captured the transition before and after ZGA, we investigated the expression patterns of A. gambiae orthologs of previously characterized bona fide early zygotic A. stephensi genes (Materials and Methods; Supplemental Fig. S1F; Wu et al. 2018). Although some genes behaved differently (i.e., were maternally deposited or not expressed at all), approximately half of the A. gambiae orthologs showed an induction after ZGA (Supplemental Fig. S1F).

When plotting the expression dynamics of all transcripts along the developmental time points, we noticed that they are similar between males and females (Fig. 1D,E). A principal component analysis (PCA) showed that most of the variance in our data set can be explained by differences between developmental stages rather than sexes (Fig. 1D). We thus focused first on analyses of developmental changes (for sex differences, see below) and used unsupervised clustering for gene classification according to their expression dynamics (16 clusters) (Supplemental Fig. S2A). Those were then further grouped into nine clusters (A–I) based on whether they were stable or dynamic and whether they were only maternal, only zygotic, or both (Supplemental Fig. S2B; Supplemental Table S2). We investigated them for (1) regulatory patterns using motif enrichment analysis in promoters (400 bp upstream of and 100 bp downstream from the annotated transcription start site) and (2) enriched gene functions using a gene ontology (SLIM-GO) enrichment analysis (Supplemental Fig. S2B; Heinz et al. 2010). Promoters of genes in cluster B (exclusively early zygotic) are enriched in the Zelda binding motif, which is consistent with findings in Drosophila, where Zelda is known to drive early zygotic transcription by binding these DNA motifs (Harrison et al. 2011). The Trl/GAGA factor binding motif is enriched in clusters C, D, and E (transcripts that are not maternally deposited and expressed in mid and late embryos or larvae). Similar to Drosophila (Li et al. 2023), Trl/GAGA factor could establish faithful 3D organization for proper activation of these genes during embryogenesis. Other enriched motifs were typical of general transcription factors (TATA box or initiator motif), and few clusters were also enriched for an E-box binding motif. The latter is noteworthy because E-box transcription factors regulate circadian rhythm genes, and oviposition in female Anopheles occurs preferentially at dusk. Therefore, those transcription factors could intrinsically ensure appropriate timing starting from egg laying to larval hatching.

The GO term analysis revealed expected patterns such as, for example, “housekeeping” functions in the stable cluster I, “muscle process” for the larval stage (cluster E), “mitotic cell cycle” (mid-embryo; cluster G), or “ribosome biogenesis” (semistable; cluster H). Interestingly, cluster F is characterized by “protein folding” as a GO term specifically enriched in maternal and early embryonic stages, indicating that, prior to ZGA, maintaining a faithful proteome appears critical for supporting early developmental processes.

After investigating global expression patterns, we turned our attention to individual genes. Besides the expected developmental genes (e.g., hunchback) (see above), clustering also identified lncRNAs with dynamic expression patterns across embryogenesis. For example, the spliced lncRNA merged.4615 in cluster B is maternally deposited but then also becomes actively and highly transcribed in the early embryo, reaching maximum expression in 5 h embryos (Supplemental Fig. S3A). Its expression pattern differs from the adjacent genes (AGAP001238 and AGAP001239), suggesting active transcriptional regulation, and thus unlikely functions by transcriptional interference with the neighboring genes. Tfsitescan prediction revealed a Grainy head binding motif in the upstream region of the gene (Supplemental Fig. S3B), and comparisons of the expression dynamics between the two implied that Grainy head could be a negative regulator of merged.4615 (Supplemental Fig. S3A). Based on synteny analysis, we found that this lncRNA is also highly expressed in Aedes aegypti embryos and A. albimanus adults (Giraldo-Calderón et al. 2022). As expected for a lncRNA, merged.4615 exhibited overall low sequence conservation, apart from two conserved stretches (25 nt at 88% identity and 23 nt at 65% identity across three mosquito species) (Supplemental Fig. S3C). This suggests that merged.4615 could encode a precursor for a small RNA. Consistently, we observed two peaks with high read counts in publicly available small RNA sequencing experiments (Supplemental Fig. S3D; Biryukova et al. 2014). The peak heights decrease following Ago1 RNAi treatment in adult mosquitoes, indicating that these small RNAs are likely microRNAs.

Our study was not intended to provide a functional exploration of these new transcripts and transcription factors, but these proof of concept analyses underscore the quality and usefulness of the first single-embryo transcriptome atlas of any mosquito species presented here. The normalized expression of each A. gambiae gene in male and female embryos as line plots over developmental time is provided in Supplemental File S1.

Developmental differences in alternative polyadenylation sites

Using RNA-seq can reveal isoform diversity generated through alternative splicing, TSS selection, and polyadenylation site variation. We performed extensive analyses comparing the different sampled time points as well as sexes (see below) using rMATS (alternative splicing events) (Supplemental Table S3; Shen et al. 2014), DEXSeq (differential exon usage) (Supplemental Table S3; Anders et al. 2012), IRFinder (intron retention) (Supplemental Table S4; Middleton et al. 2017), and APAtrap (alternative polyadenylation [APA]) (Supplemental Table S5; Ye et al. 2018). When manually inspecting the events in the genome browser, we found that a majority of them appear false positive, have very low inclusion level difference (<20%), or are of unclear relevance; for example, because they affect only few nucleotides outside coding regions. Nevertheless, DEXSeq revealed that the highest numbers of differential exon usage between time points occur early in embryogenesis, with a second wave between 14 and 20 h (Supplemental Fig. S4A). To further interpret these isoform switching events, we stratified them based on their position with regard to the first ATG start codon and last stop codon. Approximately one-third of the events affect either the 5′ untranslated region (UTR; exons before or overlapping with the first ATG), 3′ UTRs (exons after or overlapping with the last stop), or other exons, the latter of which may affect the coding sequence (Supplemental Fig. S4A). Although some of these changes could be caused by alternative splicing, they may also be a consequence of alternative transcription start site selection and alternative poly(A) site usage. Indeed, we noticed upon manual inspection in the genome browser that maternal transcripts (pre-ZGA) often have shorter 3′ UTRs compared with post-ZGA samples (Fig. 2A–C). To quantify and score these differences, we used APAtrap, revealing that 5176 out of 13,100 expressed genes in any of our samples (more than one transcript per million [TPM]) are predicted to use more than one polyadenylation site (Supplemental Table S5). A comparison between the time points revealed a progressive shift in poly(A) site usage upon ZGA, with decreased proximal and increased distal sites usage (Fig. 2D). The most pronounced change occurs between pre-ZGA and early ZGA, with 1245 genes showing significantly more distal poly(A) site usage, whereas only 94 show more proximal usage (P < 0.01) (Fig. 2E). These APA sites uncovered by APAtrap result in elongated 3′ UTRs, which affect 14% of all expressed genes at these stages (Fig. 2F). This suggests the existence of a global shift in poly(A) site usage after ZGA. A GO term analysis of genes with significant APA between pre-ZGA and early ZGA samples revealed that these were enriched in biological processes related to chromatin remodeling, translation, and protein folding (Supplemental Fig. S4B, top). The molecular function of the genes was related to ATP and RNA binding, as well as protein kinase activity (Supplemental Fig. S4B, bottom). The prevalent 3′ UTR extension upon ZGA observed here is consistent with recent discoveries in Drosophila, where analysis of 3′-seq data sets across tissues and developmental stages revealed the short 3′ UTR as a feature of the maternal transcriptome and 3′ UTR elongation as a zygotic signature (Lee et al. 2022).

Besides ZGA, we observed a second wave of isoform switching between 14 and 20 h embryos (Supplemental Fig. S4A), which coincides with the onset of expression of neurogenesis genes such as AGAP029808 (fd3F), AGAP001883 (elav-like), or AGAP008274 (wor) (Supplemental Fig. S4C,D). 3′ UTR extension was uncovered and characterized during neurogenesis in Drosophila embryos for ∼100 genes (Hilgers et al. 2011; Carrasco et al. 2020). We identified 94 orthologs in A. gambiae. Out of those, 67 genes had alternative polyadenylation sites identified by APAtrap, with 48 showing a significant 3′ UTR extension (APAtrap, P < 0.05), indicating that this process is conserved (Fig. 2G; Supplemental Fig. S4E). This includes genes like elav-like (Fig. 2H) and its paralog, fne/Rbp9-like (Supplemental Fig. S4C), which are positive regulators of 3′ UTR extension by binding the proximal poly(A) sites (Hilgers et al. 2012; Carrasco et al. 2020; Wei et al. 2020).

The conservation of 3′ UTR extension during embryogenesis in Drosophila (Hilgers et al. 2011; Lee et al. 2022) and Anopheles (this study) suggests its functional importance during the maternal-to-zygotic genome transition as well as neurogenesis. A mechanistic exploration of this phenomenon will be best pursued in Drosophila, given its superior genetic and biochemical tools compared with Anopheles.

Sex-biased gene expression during embryogenesis

Having analyzed the stage-specific transcriptome, we focused on the differences between sexes. For this, we first performed differential gene expression analysis between males and females (Fig. 3A; Supplemental Table S6). Excluding the late ZGA time point (see the next section on X-chromosome DC), this revealed a total number of 111 sex-biased protein-coding genes (Padj < 0.05), as well as nine sex-biased putative lncRNAs (Jenkins et al. 2015). The sex-biased genes in embryos were significantly overrepresented on the X chromosome, especially among the female-biased transcripts, where approximately two-thirds of the differentially expressed (DE) genes are located on the X chromosome (Fig. 3B). This is in line with earlier findings suggesting a “feminization” of the Anopheles X chromosome (Hahn and Lanzaro 2005; Papa et al. 2017) but could also be caused by inefficient DC (see below). GO term analysis revealed an enrichment of biological processes such as “sex differentiation” or “RNA splicing” (Fig. 3C). Similarly, “channel regulator activity” and “protein–DNA complex” were enriched as GO molecular functions and GO cellular components, respectively (Supplemental Fig. S5A). This is consistent with the view that sex differentiation pathways are often exerted by a combination of sequence-specific transcription factors, splicing regulators, and developmental regulatory proteins (e.g., membrane receptors) (Salz and Erickson 2010).

Compared with the several hundreds of sex-biased genes present at adult stages (Papa et al. 2017), our embryonic data set reveals a relatively small number of sex-biased genes. Notably, most genes reported as fully sex-specific in adult somatic tissues (Papa et al. 2017) were either not expressed in embryos or not sex-specific during embryogenesis (Supplemental Fig. S5B), suggesting that most adult sexually dimorphic gene expression arises after embryogenesis. However, some genes identified as sex-biased during embryogenesis remain biased in adults (Supplemental Fig. S5C,D), likely reflecting common upstream regulators. These findings are not completely unexpected because the morphological and behavioral differences of adult mosquitoes are dramatic compared with embryos, where males and females are phenotypically indistinguishable.

Among the sex-biased genes were the maleness gene Yob (AGAP029221) (Krzywinska et al. 2016), the DC master regulator SOA (AGAP005748) (Kalita et al. 2023), Doublesex (AGAP004050) (Scali et al. 2005), and femaleless (Fle; AGAP013051) (Fig. 3D; Krzywinska et al. 2021). Fle has been proposed to be a regulator of DC and sex determination, responsible for establishing the female splicing pattern of doublesex. Interestingly, although femaleless plays a crucial role in female sexual development, we scored femaleless expression as male-biased (Fig. 3D, middle). However, a recent study proposed a post-transcriptional regulation of Fle (Krzywinska et al. 2023), whereby the transcript but not the protein is expressed in both sexes. One example of a novel sex-biased gene is AGAP000430, which was scored as DE at 9–14 h after egg laying (Fig. 3A,D). AGAP000430 encodes a C2 domain-containing membrane protein, whose Drosophila ortholog, Synaptotagmin 14, is involved in nervous system development and synapse function. AGAP000430 is also expressed in a male-biased fashion at the adult stage (Supplemental Fig. S5E; Papa et al. 2017). It is possible that AGAP000430 expression bias contributes to the generation of male-specific neurons, for example, to initiate male-specific behavioral patterns. Apart from protein-coding genes, there were also lncRNAs scored as differentially expressed between the sexes (Fig. 3E). Notably, despite extensive searches and de novo transcript discovery approaches, we were unable to find any X-linked lncRNA transcripts akin to roX1/2 or Xist that is expressed in only one of two sexes.

Emergence and prevalence of sex-specific alternative splicing

Alternative splicing plays a critical role in sex determination and differentiation across development by enabling the generation of sex-specific transcripts from a shared genome. To investigate this, we conducted splicing analyses with rMATS (Supplemental Table S3), DEXSeq (Supplemental Table S3), and IRFfinder (Supplemental Table S4). When checking events individually, we found that most alternative splicing or intron retention events were misidentified due to overlapping transcripts, unannotated UTRs, or low transcript coverage, highlighting the challenges of studying alternative splicing in nonmodel organisms at understudied developmental stages. To narrow down the candidates, we focused on sex-specific splicing events detected by rMATS at more than one time point (Supplemental Fig. S5F). Compared with the relatively large extent of sex-specific splicing in adults (e.g., the ones conserved among four Anopheles species) (Papa et al. 2017), this revealed only a few events in our data set, the majority of which showed minor differences between males and females. This suggests that most of the adult patterns are established after embryogenesis.

Nevertheless, careful examination of all statistically significant events revealed a few with potential biological relevance. The most striking example revealed by IRFfinder was SOA (AGAP005748), which we previously reported to produce two protein isoforms: a truncated, nonfunctional isoform in females and a male full-length isoform capable of inducing DC (Kalita et al. 2023). Apart from SOA, we also found a previously unreported female-specific retention event in AGAP000171 intron 3 (Fig. 3F) affecting a subset of the AGAP000171 transcripts. The retained intron 3 contains an in-frame premature stop codon, resulting in a protein truncation after 224 amino acids (Fig. 3G). This is predicted to produce a protein lacking critical domains of the UBR-1-like E3 ubiquitin ligase encoded by AGAP000171. Comparison with publicly available data sets revealed that the female-specific intron retention remains substantial in adult females, especially in antennae (Supplemental Fig. S5G; Pitts et al. 2011; Giraldo-Calderón et al. 2022).

Another example of a gene with sex-specific isoforms is AGAP000874 (Fig. 3H). The gene is a duplication of AGAP000875, which encodes a protein predicted to be involved in transcription regulation. The gene is not expressed until 20 h into embryogenesis, at which point exon 2 is spliced out in males but not females. The second exon is part of the 5′ UTR, indicating that the sex-specific splicing does not affect the coding potential of the transcript but might result in changed translation efficiency or transcript stability.

Together, these findings highlight the minor yet noteworthy role of sex-biased gene expression and alternative splicing in establishing sex-specific differences during embryogenesis. The fact that most sex-specific splicing patterns emerge postembryonically likely reflects the increasing complexity and phenotypic manifestation of sex-specific functions in later developmental stages.

The onset of DC during embryogenesis

One gene-regulatory process relevant to sex differences is DC, and we next investigated its establishment, particularly when zygotic transcription begins. Before ZGA, all transcripts are maternally deposited and therefore equally expressed between the sexes (no differentially expressed genes; median log2FC of 0.0735 for the X chromosome) (Fig. 4A,B; Supplemental Table S6). Early at ZGA (∼3 h after oviposition), expression is still balanced between the sexes and between chromosomes. Shortly thereafter (late ZGA; 3.5 h), we detected a striking imbalance because males express X-linked genes only from a single copy, compared with the double dose in females and autosomal genes (Fig. 4B,C). When comparing males with females, this translates to a log2FC of −0.8859 for the X-linked genes, meaning that the level of X-linked genes in the males is 53.74% of the level in females. Out of 541 DE genes, 189 were X-linked (significant overrepresentation; P = 1.80 × 10−77) (Fig. 4C). In 5 h embryos, this imbalance is already partially compensated for (log2FC of −0.2044, 86.8%) with further fine-tuning later in development. This corresponds to the appearance of the master regulator of DC: the male-specific SOA transcript isoform (Fig. 4D), whose physiological roles we dissected earlier (Kalita et al. 2023).

To investigate whether there is a difference in the timing and extent of DC between different gene groups, we turned back to the developmental clusters (Supplemental Fig. S2B). Comparing the log2FC between males and females revealed that X-linked genes in clusters with maternal contributions show less expression imbalance compared with genes in clusters consisting of purely zygotic genes, suggesting that maternal factors help mitigate DC imbalances during early development (Fig. 4E). Of note, the chromosomal distribution of genes within clusters is not random (Fig. 4F): Maternal and early embryo clusters show a paucity of X-linked (e.g., cluster B; early embryo; only 10 out of 290 genes are X-linked). Because DC is not yet established at this early developmental stage, X-chromosomal location could cause male–female differences for an early embryonic gene expressed at this stage. In Drosophila, early developmental regulatory X-linked genes such as giant, vnd, nullo, btd, tsg, and sog are compensated in a noncanonical fashion despite the absence of MSL-H4K16ac (Lott et al. 2011). Anopheles apparently solves this issue in a different way by encoding at least some of these genes on autosomes (e.g., giant and vnd) (Supplemental Fig. S5H). Others show distinct expression dynamics compared with Drosophila (Supplemental Fig. S5I). They are either maternally deposited or expressed later in development when DC is established. Both solutions mitigate the risk of imbalance.

SOA binding during embryogenesis

SOA binds promoters of X-linked genes to induce DC (Kalita et al. 2023). To examine the onset of this process, we analyzed SOA occupancy during embryogenesis by generating SOA CUT&Tag data sets from pooled embryos across three developmental stages using a homozygous SOA-null mutant line (SOA-KI, extensively characterized by Kalita et al. 2023) as a negative control (Supplemental Fig. S6A). Significantly bound sites and their associated genes were identified with DiffBind (Supplemental Table S7). Our earliest time point captured 3–4 h embryos, where, consistent with very low SOA mRNA expression (only ∼10 TPM) (Fig. 4D), we did not observe the characteristic SOA binding pattern. Several lines of evidence suggest that these 3–4 h peaks, rather than being very early binding sites, reflect technical artifacts such as phantom peaks (Jain et al. 2015): (1) They did not show the CA-rich motif (Supplemental Table S8); (2) their coverage in later time points was similar between wild-type (WT) and SOA-KI mutant samples, indicative of false positives (Fig. 5A); (3) they were not enriched on the X chromosome (Fig. 5B); and (4) only four genes were bound between all three time points (Fig. 5C). Even for these four, the actual peak positions differed between 3–4 h and the other time points, indicating that the initial binding sites do not persist (Supplemental Fig. S6B). Considering that there is a lack of DC in 3.5 h embryos (late ZGA) (Fig. 4B), we conclude that these 3–4 h peaks are background and do not fulfill the criteria of HASs like in Drosophila (Alekseyenko et al. 2008; Samata et al. 2020).

Therefore, we focused on the SOA binding sites in 6–7 and 16–20 h embryos, where SOA becomes highly enriched on the X chromosome (Fig. 5B). The timing is consistent with the onset of DC (Fig. 4B) and the high expression of SOA transcript starting at 5 h after oviposition (∼150 TPM) (Fig. 4D). As in pupae, most of the SOA peaks at these two time points were located at promoter regions (Fig. 5D). Of the 171 genes bound at 6–7 h, 139 (81%) remain bound at 16–20 h, and 204 new genes become bound at the later time point (Fig. 5C). However, closer inspection of these newly bound genes revealed that enrichment was already detectable at 6–7 h, though at weaker levels than at 16–20 h, and likely for this reason did not reach significance in DiffBind (Supplemental Fig. S6C). In contrast, sites significant only at 6–7 h or at both time points showed lower enrichment at 16–20 h. This suggests that there are no early sites like HASs, as these are expected to remain strongly bound throughout development.

Characterization of SOA-bound sites suggests a nonhierarchical, expression-correlated binding

To gain further support for this hypothesis and insights into the mechanism, we wanted to test whether SOA binding at late sites occurs in a manner dependent on the distance to early sites. Such correlation would be indicative of a two-step model, where peaks that gain strong binding at 16–20 h are located close to the 6–7 h sites, whereas those with weaker binding are farther away (scheme in Supplemental Fig. S6D). We therefore plotted the distances from 16–20 h peaks to the nearest significant 6–7 h peak, stratified by SOA binding strength (Supplemental Fig. S6D). Although there was a weak trend where late sites strongly bound by SOA (Q4 quartile) had on average a slightly shorter mean distance to 6–7 h sites (Supplemental Fig. S6D), the same trend was observed when we investigated distances based simply on gene expression. Indeed, genes on the X chromosome and autosomes that are highly expressed (20 h) tend to be located closer to previously highly expressed genes (9 h), indicative of gene expression “hubs” (Supplemental Fig. S6E), and thus provide a likely explanation for the distance effects observed for SOA. These results are different from those in Drosophila (Prayitno et al. 2019) and do not support a 2D distance relationship.

Next, we examined the DNA sequence motifs associated with early and late sites. If putative HASs were bound first, followed by low-affinity sites at the later stage, we would expect strong motif enrichment and high information content at the early sites. Late sites would then show either two distinct motifs (HASs + low-affinity sites) or weaker motifs (if low-affinity sites are the major population). However, we observed the opposite pattern in a MEME (Bailey et al. 2015) motif analysis: The characteristic CA motif observed in our previous experiments in pupae and cultured cells (Kalita et al. 2023) was detected as significant only in the 16–20 h sites, whereas the early sites did not contain any significant motifs (top three hits in Fig. 5E,F). Moreover, when we searched for CA motif occurrences in peak sequences with FIMO (Bailey et al. 2015), we observed that only the 16–20 h significant peaks have a higher fraction of sites that contain the CA motif compared with the background (Supplemental Table S8). Peaks common between 6–7 and 16–20 h samples had a slightly lower CA motif enrichment and less-defined resulting consensus motif compared with peaks identified uniquely in one time point (Supplemental Fig. S7A–C).

To evaluate whether strong SOA binding is associated with specific histone modifications rather than motifs or distance, we profiled the repressive mark H3K27me3 and the active marks H3K27ac and H3K36me3 in Ag55 cells (Supplemental Fig. S7D,E). The global gene expression profile of Ag55 cells resembles that of 20 h embryos. For example, genes in clusters G, H, and I (Supplemental Fig. S2B) exhibit similar on/off expression patterns in Ag55 and 20 h embryos (Supplemental Fig. S7D). Ag55 cells are female and normally do not express SOA, but ectopic expression results in SOA binding specifically on the X chromosome in a pattern that closely matches the endogenous SOA binding in male tissue (Kalita et al. 2023). Confirming the quality of these new ChIP-seq and CUT&Tag data sets, we found that H3K27me3 was associated with repressed gene bodies, whereas H3K27ac localized to promoters, and H3K36me3 localized to gene bodies of actively transcribed genes (Supplemental Fig. S7E). As expected, these patterns were observed on X-linked and autosomal genes. We then asked whether there are any differences for X-linked genes with strong compared with weak SOA binding (Supplemental Fig. S7F). Interestingly, this revealed that genes that become strongly bound upon ectopic expression of SOA tend to show higher levels of H3K27ac and H3K36me3 already prior to SOA binding—features that are correlated with a high gene expression level of the corresponding gene (Supplemental Fig. S7F).

This was also reflected in the genes with increased SOA binding at 16–20 h (Supplemental Table S7), which tended to show an increase in expression levels at the 20 h embryo stage (Fig. 5G). In contrast, genes with decreased binding (fold change < 0) or no change in binding (DiffBind Padj > 0.05) did not show changes in overall RNA expression (Fig. 5G). Altogether, this argues against a “hierarchy” of SOA sites. Instead, SOA's binding at promoters is best correlated with the onset of expression of a given gene and associated histone modifications rather than proximity to early-bound sites with high-affinity motifs.

DC progression for different gene groups

Next, we were interested in how SOA binding is linked to DC onset and used k-means clustering to separate all X-linked genes into three groups based on their SOA binding strength across all time points (Fig. 6A). The genes with the strongest SOA binding by CUT&Tag (cluster 1) (Fig. 6A) were the most imbalanced in RNA-seq in 5 h embryos (Fig. 6B). However, this group was fully compensated for by 9 h after oviposition, whereas establishment of DC was more gradual for the remaining two groups with weaker SOA binding (Fig. 6B). The genes with strongest SOA binding in embryos displayed constitutive and high expression compared with genes with intermediate or weak SOA binding (Supplemental Fig. S8A). A similar pattern was observed in the expression clusters (A–I) derived from the transcriptomic data across embryogenesis (Supplemental Fig. S2B). Constitutively expressed genes with rather stable expression patterns (clusters G, H, and I) exhibited the strongest SOA signal (Supplemental Fig. S8B). The few early zygotic X-linked genes (cluster B) showed minimal SOA binding, likely due to the transient nature of their expression, which is induced ∼5 h into development, prior to the 6–7 h time point sampled by CUT&Tag. Similarly, genes with larval expression (cluster E) showed no SOA binding, which suggests that SOA binding co-occurs with the activation of a gene rather than preceding it (Supplemental Fig. S8B). Overall, these data indicate that specificity of SOA to X-linked gene promoters tends to correlate best with the onset of the corresponding gene's expression and, accordingly, the presence of active chromatin marks. This is in contrast to the progressive, HAS distance-dependent binding by the MSL complex and DC establishment over different gene groups along Drosophila embryogenesis (Prayitno et al. 2019; Samata et al. 2020).

Inefficient DC contributes to sex-biased expression

To examine the interplay between SOA binding and the extent of expression imbalance in embryos lacking SOA, we conducted RNA-seq in 18 h WT and SOA-KI male and female embryos (Supplemental Fig. S8C; Supplemental Table S6). As expected, the expression of X-linked genes was substantially reduced in SOA-KI male but not female embryos (Fig. 6C; Supplemental Fig. S8D,E). Similar to the pupa stage (Kalita et al. 2023), this effect depends on SOA binding strength irrespective of whether this analysis was conducted globally (Fig. 6C,D right) or at an individual gene level (Fig. 6D, left). Because SOA binding is tightly linked to gene expression (Supplemental Fig. S8A), we asked whether it might be possible to distinguish direct, gene-by-gene compensation from broader expression effects. To test this, we selected the significantly downregulated genes in SOA-KI mutants based on RNA-seq data and selected 43 that also had a significant SOA peak in CUT&Tag. We then sampled 43 X-linked genes with similar RNA expression levels but no detectable SOA binding. Strikingly, although the “top SOA targets” exhibited a substantial loss of expression, the expression of nonbound genes was only mildly affected in SOA-KI mutants, suggesting an overall and strong dependence of SOA binding at a given gene (Fig. 6E).

Finally, because female-biased genes tend to be overrepresented on the X chromosome (Fig. 3A,B; Papa et al. 2017), we wondered whether the female-biased expression of some X-linked genes could be explained by inefficient DC. SOA binding, as assessed by CUT&Tag, is significantly weaker at female-biased X-linked genes compared with unbiased genes (Supplemental Fig. S8F). This is also reflected by the extent of imbalance scored after perturbation of the SOA pathway: The X-linked female-biased genes tend to be SOA-resistant compared with unbiased genes, which is apparent in SOA-KI mutant males as well as females ectopically expressing SOA (SOA-R) (Supplemental Fig. S8G; Kalita et al. 2023). Because the latter are XX females, this finding also implies that SOA DC, rather than genotypic or phenotypic sex, contributes to the observed sex-biased expression. Taken together, the female bias of some X-linked genes arises from weaker upregulation in males due to “resistance” to SOA-mediated DC.

Discussion

Our single-embryo sex-specific RNA-seq data set provides significantly more information compared with previously published embryonic transcriptomes. First, we provide sex-specific data across multiple time points, increasing our understanding of the establishment of DC across development and the onset of sex-specific expression patterns. Additionally, profiling embryos by RNA-seq allowed us to understand the features of the embryonic transcriptome beyond the mere expression levels; for example, identifying the differential usage of APA sites across development. Moreover, analyzing single embryos enabled a detailed understanding of rapid developmental transitions that are often overlooked in bulk collections of embryos. Consequently, this data set offers a valuable resource for, for example, evolutionary studies, enabling direct comparisons of embryonic transcriptomes across insect species.

The cases of 3′ UTR extension in our data set include previously reported transcripts (Hilgers et al. 2011; Carrasco et al. 2020). However, apart from these dozens of neuronal extension events, we have also observed more than a thousand genes having their 3′ UTRs extended upon ZGA. This suggests that this regulation mechanism is also a general ZGA feature. Recently, the global shift in 3′ UTR length upon maternal-to-zygotic transition (MZT) has also been observed in Drosophila (Lee et al. 2022). Our data imply that this phenomenon might be conserved through Dipterans or even more insect groups. Of note, dynamic changes in 3′ UTR length have also been shown to play a role in zebrafish MZT (Li et al. 2012; Mishima and Tomari 2016).

3′ UTR extension may be linked to the rapid developmental changes occurring in the embryo. Although 3′ UTRs are noncoding sequences, their length could affect transcript stability, translational efficiency, and availability of miRNA binding sites. The increased 3′ UTR length allows inclusion of additional binding sites for regulatory proteins and miRNAs that can control gene expression post-transcriptionally. This mode of gene regulation is more rapid compared with transcriptional regulation, which might be especially important for embryonic development (Chen et al. 2014).

The prediction of APA sites in our analysis relied solely on RNA-seq data. Such computational prediction can have its limitations; however, these data allow for insights into APA usage in the absence of 3′-end-seq information in a species of interest. Improved annotation of mosquito 3′ UTRs would aid in correct prediction of regulators acting on a given transcript (e.g., microRNA binding prediction or RNA binding protein motif search). Further investigation of this feature will require experimental mapping to unambiguously annotate APA sites, as well spatially resolved transcriptomics to understand whether this phenomenon is more prevalent in certain tissues of Anopheles embryos.

Sequencing of male and female embryos separately allowed us to discover genes that are expressed in a sex-biased manner in early embryogenesis. Compared with adult mosquitoes (Papa et al. 2017), the sex bias in expression and splicing is modest in embryos but includes key upstream regulators of sex-specific development (e.g., Yob, SOA, doublesex, and femaleless). This reinforces the value of analyzing sex-specific gene expression during early developmental stages, where key regulatory factors can be identified without being obscured by the extensive number of sex-biased genes later in development (Kalita and Keller Valsecchi 2025). To get further insights into the gene-regulatory dynamics during the MZT and the associated timing of DC in this species, which is apparently very rapid, it will be necessary to design experiments using crosses of diverging strains (Lott et al. 2011; Liang and Sharakhov 2019). In their hybrid offspring, any transcripts aligning to only the paternal genome must be of zygotic origin.

Analysis of our data set together with SOA CUT&Tag binding profiles and expression in SOA mutants allowed us to gain deeper insights into the dynamic onset and mechanism of DC. We discovered several features that are unusual compared with known regulators in, for example, mammals (Cecalev et al. 2024), Drosophila (Prayitno et al. 2019; Samata et al. 2020), and nematodes (Ercan et al. 2009). (1) There was no other, consistent sex-specific putative SOA cofactor in our data set. This indicates that SOA may co-opt a conserved, non-sex-specific machinery to regulate its target genes. We also did not find any evidence for an X-linked lncRNA akin to Drosophila roX1/2 (Meller and Rattner 2002), Anolis lizard MAYEX (Tenorio et al. 2024), or mammalian Xist (Brockdorff et al. 1992), though it is formally possible that such a sex-specific transcript is nonpolyadenylated (Pan et al. 2024) and thus was not captured by our data set. (2) We did not observe initial HASs. Instead, the SOA binding pattern was established rapidly between 3–4 h (where no SOA DC occurred) and 6–7 h (where both binding and DC were prominent). (3) Although additional genes became bound at 16–20 h, there was no distance relationship to early sites. Association with promoters upon expression of a given X-linked gene was the main predictor of DC efficiency and occurred acutely and precisely. (4) As in pupae, binding in 16–20 h embryos occurred at CA motifs. The early sites were less well defined by sequence motifs, reinforcing the idea that they lack the typical features of HASs. Because we have previously shown that the SOA territory is resistant to RNase A treatment (Kalita et al. 2023), we favor the idea of a DNA-guided mechanism, potentially involving CA motifs at X-linked genes. This is in contrast to the progressive establishment over different gene groups seen in Drosophila (Prayitno et al. 2019; Samata et al. 2020) that correlates with accumulation of roX1/2 RNA. (5) Our ChIP-seq data indicate that histone modifications could contribute to an open chromatin landscape, allowing faithful SOA recruitment. Other mechanisms potentially involving DNA shape (Villa et al. 2016), 3D genome architecture (Giorgetti et al. 2014; Crane et al. 2015), or condensate formation (Valsecchi et al. 2020) could additionally aid in defining SOA target sites.

What are the actual promoter features that are sufficient to specifically attract SOA to a target gene? This is an exciting question for future investigation that will necessarily need to involve transgenic tools (i.e., translocations of genes from X chromsome to autosome and vice versa). Independent of this experimental validation, which is challenging in nonmodel organisms such as Anopheles, this direct binding mechanism of SOA becomes interesting when analyzing distinct gene groups: Early in development, maternal contributions appear to partially mitigate X-linked imbalances, as evidenced by a lower imbalance in genes with maternal deposits compared with purely zygotic genes. In addition, maternally deposited and early zygotic genes seem to be depleted from the X chromosome, providing Anopheles a solution to avoid imbalance due to X dosage differences before DC starts. The gene-specific role of SOA in inducing upregulation is also reflected in the group of female-biased X-linked genes, which show a paucity of SOA binding and resistance to DC in males.

More generally, an extensive discussion of the Anopheles-specific developmental patterning programs is provided by Goltsev et al. (2007), but our data may further help to expand our understanding of insect embryo development and gene regulation beyond Drosophila. For instance, unlike Drosophila, mosquito embryos develop on water surfaces, exposing them to fluctuating temperatures and water conditions, which likely influence gene-regulatory networks and developmental timing. Comparing them in A. gambiae and D. melanogaster can provide insights into how environmental factors shape developmental pathways and evolutionary differences between different insect embryos.

Materials and methods

Mosquito rearing and staging

A. gambiae mosquitoes were maintained in standard insectary conditions (26°C–28°C, 75%–80% humidity, and 12/12 h light/dark cycle). Egg development was induced by blood feeding, and egg laying was started by presenting a wet egg dish to the mosquito females for ∼20 min. Individual eggs were collected at designated time points after presenting the egg dish (note that egg laying and fertilization in mosquitoes are synchronized). Because the dipteran development and transcriptome at zygotic genome activation (ZGA) undergo very rapid changes, the 20 min collection time window was not sufficient to appropriately stage ZGA. For the ZGA time point, we therefore sampled a total of 10 embryos, which were then allocated to early and late ZGA based on data from Wu et al. (2018). For this, the A. gambiae orthologs were retrieved on Ensembl BioMart, and the embryos were clustered based on the expression levels of the early ZGA genes. In addition, we manually inspected several early zygotic genes according to Goltsev et al. (2009) and allocated the embryos to early and late time points.

Cell culture

Ag55 cells (kindly provided by Professor M. Adang) were cultured in Leibovitz L15 media with 10% FBS (Gibco 10270106) and 1× penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco 15140122) at 27°C and 80% humidity.

Genotyping

Pre-ZGA embryos were genotyped by PCR on DNA isolated using the TRIzol–chloroform method for RNA and DNA isolation from one lysate. PCR was performed with FastStart Universal SYBR Green master (ROX) mix (Roche 04913850001) using Y-linked repeats (S23) (Krzywinski et al. 2004) as template and autosomal repeats (Ag404) (Krzywinski et al. 2005) as control. In brief, DNA and RNA were extracted simultaneously from single embryos collected before zygotic genome activation (ZGA). The DNA fraction was then subjected to quantitative PCR (qPCR), where a Y chromosomal satellite repeat was amplified, allowing us to identify males (Krzywinski et al. 2004). For embryos after the ZGA stage, the RNA levels of Yob, a Y-linked sex determination gene, were determined by subjecting 10% of the isolated RNA to reverse transcription (RT)-qPCR (RNA-seq confirmation of correct sexing is shown in Fig. 1B).

Post-ZGA samples were genotyped based on expression of Yg2 (i.e., Yob) mRNA by qPCR with Rp49 as a control. qPCR was performed on a Roche LightCycler II using FastStart Universal SYBR Green master (Roche 04913914001) in a 7 µL reaction at 300 nM final primer concentration. Cycling conditions were as recommended by the manufacturer.

Embryogenesis transcriptome RNA isolation, library generation, and sequencing

RNA was extracted using TRIzol (Fisher Scientific 15-596-026) and the Direct-zol RNA MicroPrep kit (Zymo Research R2062). For pre-ZGA samples, only the aqueous phase formed after phenol–chloroform separation was loaded onto the column after mixing with 100% EtOH. The phenol–chloroform phase was used for DNA isolation following the manufacturer's instruction manual (Fisher Scientific 15-596-026). RNA integrity was assessed with high-sensitivity RNA ScreenTape (Agilent 5067-5579 and 5067-5580). The library preparation was performed with Illumina's stranded mRNA preparation ligation kit following the stranded mRNA preparation ligation reference guide (June 2020, document 1000000124518 v00) using 13 PCR cycles. The samples were sequenced in paired-end mode twice for 75 cycles each on an Illumina HiSeq 4000 or a NextSeq 500 (see Supplemental Table S1 for details).

Embryogenesis transcriptome data processing and visualization

Reads were trimmed with cutadapt (1.18). To filter out rRNA reads, these reads were mapped to the ribosomal RNA sequences extracted from the Ensembl AgamP4.14 genome using the Ensembl AgamP4 annotation (release 48) with STAR (v. 2.7.3a) with the following parameters: outFilterMultimapNmax 1000000, outFilterMismatchNoverLmax 0.04, and outFilterMismatchNmax 999. Unmapped reads were used in downstream processing. Reads were mapped to the Ensembl AgamP4 genome using the Ensembl AgamP4 annotation (release 48) together with lncRNA annotation from Jenkins et al. (2015) with STAR (v. 2.7.3a) using the following parameters: outFilterMismatchNoverLmax 0.04 and outFilterMismatchNmax 999. Only uniquely mapped reads were used for downstream analysis. Coverage signal tracks (bigWigs) of primary alignments were generated using SAMtools (v1.9) and deepTools (v3.1.0). Primary alignments were assigned to features using subread 1.6.5 with the AgamP4 annotation (release 48) combined with lncRNA annotation (Jenkins et al. 2015) as a reference. Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (1.26.0), and only genes with Padj < 0.05 were considered as differentially expressed. To compare our transcriptome atlas with the microarray data generated by Goltsev et al. (2009), we downloaded the expression data provided in Supplemental Table 4 of that study and converted the AGAP GeneIDs to the current annotation available at https://vectorbase.org (“Searches” tab) (Giraldo-Calderón et al. 2022). Genome browser snapshots were generated with IGV (https://software.broadinstitute.org/software/igv) (Robinson et al. 2011).

ZGA orthologs

A stringent list of early zygotic transcripts described in A. stephensi was used to find their orthologs. Out of 71 genes, 49 had assigned orthologs in A. gambiae. Next, after visual inspection, genes with expression in pre-ZGA samples were filtered out from the list (AGAP006073, AGAP009974, AGAP002361, AGAP029670, AGAP007651, and AGAP00369). Additionally, X-linked genes that would presumably differ in their expression between sexes upon ZGA due to lack of DC at this time point were excluded (AGAP000819, AGAP000215, AGAP000014, AGAP000317, and AGAP000714), resulting in a final list of 38 autosomal genes.

Clustering analysis

Gene expression was normalized to transcript per million (TPM). We calculated the mean gene expression for a given time point (combining both male and female samples). Next, only genes with mean expression per group of more than five TPMs at any time point were selected to avoid noise in our clustering analysis, resulting in 10,841 genes passing the filtering. The expression profiles were scaled from 0 to 1, where 1 was the highest expression of a given gene across all time points. We used the R package kohonen (3.0.12) (Wehrens and Kruisselbrink 2018) to construct self-organizing maps, resulting in expression profile clustering into 16 groups. Next, we inspected these clusters and assigned them a biologically relevant category based on the timing of the highest expression.

HOMER, MEME, and FIMO motif enrichment analysis

The promoters of manually grouped expression clusters were analyzed using the HOMER motif discovery tool. In brief, regions 400 bp upstream of and 100 downstream from the annotated transcription start site were input to find enriched motifs 6, 8, or 10 bp in length from the insect motif database. HOMER 1 was used to search only through annotated, known motifs.

For de novo motif discovery with MEME, the sequences of significant SOA peaks were extracted with BEDtools, and motif discovery was performed with the default MEME settings, where the 100 bp centered sequence of the peak was used for the motif search. For the targeted motif search with FIMO, the top 16–20 h motif file (consensus: CACACAC) was used, and only motif occurrences with a P-value of <10−3 were categorized as matching. The common and unique peaks were identified with BEDtools overlap, where we considered peaks with any overlap between the samples as common.

Tfsitescan

The sequence of the putative promoter of Merged.4615 (−1400 bp) was extracted in IGV and analyzed with Tfsitescan against a Drosophila site database.

DEXSeq2

Read counts by exon were counted with HTSeq (v. 2.0.3) (Putri et al. 2022). The results were then processed with DEXSeq (v1.44.0) with a Padj cutoff value of 0.01.

To assess the positional distribution of differentially used exons relative to the start and stop codons of protein-coding genes, we used an annotation-based classification strategy. For this, a GTF file (AgamP4.8) was parsed, and features/positions corresponding to “start_codon” and “stop_codon” were extracted. gene_id and exon_number information was generated using the cSplit function. For each gene_id, the minimum exon number associated with “start_codon” and the maximum exon number associated with “stop_codon” were identified, representing the first and last translated exons, respectively. This yielded a reference table of codon-related exon positions for each gene. Differential exon usage results were obtained using DEXSeq across multiple developmental time points. The featureID field was parsed to extract exon numbers, and the gene identifier was used to merge with the codon-based annotation. Only exons with Padj < 0.05 were used for plotting.

rMATS

Alternative splicing analysis between sample groups was performed with rMATs (v.4.1.0) on BAM files setting the flags ‐‐allow-clipping ‐‐variable-read-length ‐‐novelSS. Low-coverage events with coverage of less than five reads were removed for downstream analysis. Furthermore, statistically significant events were selected using a Padj cutoff of 0.01 and minimum PSI change of 10% (ΔPSI = 0.1). Splicing events detected with reads that spanned splicing junctions and reads on target (junction–exon counts [JCECs]) were considered in the analysis. Only events with a significant female or male bias in more than one time point were plotted.

IRFinder

IRFinder (v. 2.0.0) was run on BAM files, and the differential intron retention analysis was performed with DESeq2 (1.26.0) without filtering and with a Padj cutoff of <0.05.

APAtrap

We analyzed all embryogenesis time-course RNA-seq samples to identify the APA sites and their usage. First, we used BEDtools (2.29.2) function genomeCoverageBed to generate the bedGraph files of our RNA-seq alignments. Next, we used the identifyDistal3UTR script from APAtrap (Ye et al. 2018) with bedGraphs of all the files and a BED file of gene locations generated based on the Ensembl AgamP4 annotation (release 48) as input. We used the default settings for genomes with short UTRs (50 window size and 5000 pre-extension 3′ UTR size). Next, using a BED file with newly identified 3′ UTR coordinates, we ran predictAPA for all samples together, grouped by sex and time, using the following settings: coverage drop cutoff 0.2, coverage threshold 20, APA min dis 100, and scan window size 50. After inspection revealed little difference between the sexes, we assigned all the samples at a given time point to one group regardless of sex. We then ran deAPA to score differential usage of APA sites between the time points, setting the minimum number of replicates showing APA change to three. This allowed us to identify transcripts that change APA usage during development, which we identified based on the Padj < 0.01. In Figure 2G, we plotted the smoothed average of APA usage for the 48 orthologs of genes previously identified in D. melanogaster as elongating their 3′ UTRs and scored as significantly changing in Anopheles embryogenesis. Because genes have a different number of APA sites, the approximate APA usage across genes was calculated with a polynomial regression.

CUT&Tag library generation and data analysis

Mixed-sex embryos from homozygous wild-type and SOA-KI loss-of-function lines were collected at the following time spans after egg laying: 3–4, 6–7, or 16–20 h. Embryos were flash-frozen and stored at −80°C. After thawing, embryos were homogenized in DPBS and filtered through a strainer. Cells were washed twice with PBS and resuspended in wash buffer. After DAPI staining, the cells were counted and adjusted to 100,000 cells per reaction (for 3–4 h samples) and 400,000 cells per reaction (6–7 and 16–20 h samples). The cells were bound to activated concanavalin A beads and incubated with the primary antibody (SOA or IgG; 1:100 dilution) overnight in antibody buffer. The cells on beads were rinsed with Dig wash buffer and incubated for 1 h at room temperature with the secondary antibody (guinea pig antirabbit IgG; ABIN101961). After a rinse and a wash with Dig wash buffer, 1:100 pA-Tn5 (prepared by the Institute of Molecular Biology Protein Production Core Facility) in Dig-300 buffer was added and incubated on a nutator for 1 h at room temperature. After a rinse and a wash with Dig-300 buffer, 150 µL of tagmentation buffer containing MgCl2 was added, and the samples were incubated for 1 h at 37°C. Next, 5 µL of 0.5 M EDTA, 1.5 µL of 10% SDS, and 1.25 µL of 20 mg/mL Proteinase K (Invitrogen 10665795) were added, and the samples were incubated for 1 h at 55°C. After Proteinase K digestion, DNA was extracted using the DNA Clean&Concentrator-5 Zymo kit. Libraries were generated by PCR amplification for 15 cycles with NEBNext Ultra II Q5 master mix with custom primers. Amplified libraries were purified with SPRI magnetic beads and analyzed using the DNA high-sensitivity bioanalyzer. The libraries were pooled in equimolar ratios aiming at 10 Mio reads per library. The pool was sequenced on a NextSeq 2000 P3 (200 cycles) flow cell twice for 111 cycles each for paired ends plus twice for eight cycles each for the index reads. The analysis was performed using the NGSpipe2go ChIP-seq pipeline (available at https://gitlab.rlp.net/imbforge/NGSpipe2go) with modifications. Briefly, the reads were trimmed using cutadapt (v.4.6) to remove Illumina adapter sequences and subsequently mapped to the AgamP4 reference genome with bowtie2 (v.2.4.5). Multimapping and duplicate reads were removed. We then called peaks using macs2 (v.2.1.2) with the corresponding IgG samples as control using the effective genome size of 300 Mb. The resulting consensus peak set containing peaks called in at least two samples was used for downstream analysis with DiffBind (v.3.8.4) to identify sites that were significantly (Padj < 0.05) differentially bound between wild-type and SOA-KI samples (results are shown in Supplemental Table S7). Peaks were assigned to neighboring genes with ChIPseeker (1.34.1). Mean coverage of replicates was calculated with WiggleTools (v.1.2.8), and downstream visualization of differentially bound peaks (e.g., heat maps) was generated using deepTools (v.3.5.1).

The histone modification CUT&Tag was prepared and analyzed in the same way with the following differences: Ag55 cells were grown in L-15 medium with 10% FBS, and 50,000 cells were used per CUT&Tag reaction. H3K27me3 was profiled with a polyclonal rabbit antibody (Active Motif 39055).

RNA-seq of SOA-KI embryos

RNA was isolated as described above in “Embryogenesis Transcriptome RNA Isolation, Library Generation, and Sequencing.” Genotyping was performed as described above in “Genotyping” for post-ZGA samples. Two embryos of the same sex and genotype were pooled together prior to library generation and were prepared with Illumina's stranded mRNA preparation ligation kit following the stranded mRNA preparation ligation reference guide (April 2021, document 1000000124518 v02). Libraries were prepared with a starting amount of 4 ng and amplified with 13 PCR cycles. Two post-PCR purification steps with AMPure XP beads were performed. Libraries were pooled together in equimolar ratio aiming for at least 10 million reads per sample and sequenced on a NextSeq 2000 P3 (200 cycles) flow cell twice for 108 cycles each for paired end plus twice for 10 cycles each for the index reads and one dark cycle upfront for R1 and R2. Reads were mapped to the AgamP4 genome using the Ensembl AgamP4 annotation (release 48) with STAR (v. 2.7.3a) using the following parameters: outFilterMismatchNoverLmax 0.04 and outFilterMismatchNmax 999. Only uniquely mapped reads were used for downstream analysis. Coverage signal tracks (bigWigs) of primary alignments were generated using SAMtools (v1.10) and deepTools (v3.5.1). Primary alignments were assigned to features using subread 2.0.0 with the AgamP4 annotation (release 48) as a reference. Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (1.26.0), and only genes with Padj<0.05 were considered differentially expressed. One replicate per group (except SOA-KI males) was excluded because of significant deviation from the other samples. The exclusion was based on high Euclidean distances and clustering separately from all other samples in PCA plots (the following samples were excluded: 24_M_WT_18h_Rep4, 18_F_WT_18h_Rep2, and 28_F_SoaKI_18h_Rep4).

ChIP-seq library generation and data analysis

Ag55 cells were cultured in Leibovitz L15 media with 10% FBS (Gibco 10270-106, lot: 2260092) and 1× penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco 15140122) at 27°C and 80% humidity. Fifty Mio Ag55 cells were seeded in a 15 cm dish. After 16 h, 9 mL of an empty baculovirus in Sf-900 media was added to the cells. Cells were collected for further processing 48 h later. The cells were detached, washed in DPBS, and counted. Next, 50 Mio cells were resuspended in 1 mL of PBS with 1.8% formaldehyde and incubated for 12 min at room temperature with occasional mixing by inversion. After 12 min, glycine was added to a final concentration of 0.25 M to stop the fixation. The cells were pelleted at 3000 rpm and washed twice: once with PBS and 0.25 M glycine and once with PBS only. Cell pellets were then flash-frozen before further processing. Cell pellets were resuspended in 0.6 mL of Farnham buffer (10 mM Tris at pH 8, 85 mM KCl, 0.5% IGEPAL-CA630) with a protease inhibitor cocktail and transferred to a 1 mL AFA Fibre Covaris tube. Nuclei were isolated in Covaris S200 with the following settings: 2% duty cycle/factor, 200 bursts, and 75 W for a total of 14.5 min at 6°C. Pellets were centrifuged at 2000g for 2 min and resuspended in 500 µL of chromatin shearing buffer (10 mM Tris at pH 8, 0.2% SDS, 1 mM EDTA, 50 mM NaCl). Chromatin was sheared with Covaris S200 using the following settings: 20% duty factor/cycle, 200 bursts, and 150 W in a 1 mL AFA Fibre tube in 0.5 mL of buffer for a total of 27 min. Ten microliters of the sheared chromatin was taken as input for later processing. Samples were flash-frozen. Upon thawing, chromatin was centrifuged at full speed for 5 min at 4°C. The supernatant was diluted to 20 µg of chromatin in final buffer with 0.05% SDS, 125 mM NaCl, 10 mM Tris (pH 8), and 1 mM EDTA. Next, the material was incubated with 1 µL of the respective histone modification antibodies overnight (H3K27ac [Abcam ab4729, lot GR243994-1] and H3K36me3 [Abcam ab9050, lot GR20245-2]). The bound antibody–protein–DNA complexes were captured using Protein G Dynabeads for 1 h at 4°C followed by three washes in RIPA (25 mM HEPES at pH 7.6, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1% SDS, 0.1% DOC, protease inhibitors), one wash in LiCl buffer (250 mM LiCl, 10 mM Tris-HCl, 1 mM EDTA, 0.5% NP-40, 0.5% DOC), and two washes in TE buffer. The immunoprecipitated DNA was decross-linked by an overnight incubation at 65°C with shaking. The next day, samples were incubated with 1 µL of RNase A for 30 min at 37°C and then with 2.5 µL of 20% SDS and 1 µL of Proteinase K for 1 h at 65°C. DNA was extracted using magnetic beads, and the ChIP-seq libraries were prepared using the NEBNext Ultra II DNA library preparation kit with standard protocols and 12 PCR cycles. Libraries were cleaned up using 1.8 vol and pooled in equimolar ratios aiming at 10 Mio reads per library. The pool was sequenced on a NextSeq 500 (high output, 150 cycles) flow cell twice for 79 cycles each for paired end plus eight cycles for the single index read. The analysis was performed using the NGSpipe2go ChIP-seq pipeline. Briefly, the reads were mapped to the AgamP4 reference genome with bowtie2 (v.2.3.4). Multimapping and duplicate reads were removed. Mean coverage of replicates was calculated with WiggleTools (v.1.2.8), and downstream visualization of active and inactive genes with heat maps was generated using deepTools (v.3.5.1).

Statistics and web resources

All statistics were calculated with R version 3.6.1 for the time-course RNA-seq (DESeq2) analysis, and later analyses were done with R version 4.2.2. In the box plots, the line that divides the box into two parts represents the median, the box bottom and top edges represent interquartile ranges (IQRs; 0.25th to 0.75th quartiles [Q1–Q3]), and whiskers represent Q1 − 1.5 × IQR (bottom) and Q3 + 1.5 × IQR (top). Bar plots represent the mean ± SEM, with overlaid data points representing independent experiments. Results were considered significant at Padj or P-value of >0.05. In the figures, “ns” indicates not significant, “NA” indicates not analyzed, and “SEM” indicates standard error of the mean. Unless indicated otherwise, statistical tests were two-sided. Gene and protein sequences were retrieved from FlyBase (http://flybase.org) and VectorBase (https://www.vectorbase.org). Lists of 1:1 orthologs were obtained via the BioMart tool on VectorBase. The GO term analysis was performed on VectorBase. No custom codes were used in this study.
