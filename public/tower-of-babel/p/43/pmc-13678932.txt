The relationship between C-reactive protein-triglyceride-glucose index and incident cardiometabolic multimorbidity in postmenopausal women: insights from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS)
Authors: Liu Wang, Chun Luo, Yanxiao Shao, Xiaohui Xie, Xiaoqin Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM), defined as the coexistence of at least two cardiometabolic diseases, is an increasing public health burden. Postmenopausal women may be particularly vulnerable because menopause-related metabolic and inflammatory changes can intensify cardiometabolic risk. However, simple tools for early CMM risk prediction in this population remain limited. The C-reactive protein-triglyceride-glucose index (CTI) integrates inflammatory status and insulin resistance, but its association with incident CMM in postmenopausal women has not been fully evaluated. We therefore investigated the relationship between CTI and new-onset CMM and assessed its predictive performance and potential clinical utility when combined with other clinical variables.Methods Data were obtained from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). The study included 2972 postmenopausal women (aged ≥45 years) without CMM at baseline, with a mean follow-up duration of 103.53 months. The association between CTI and the risk of CMM was assessed using a multivariate Cox regression model. The dose-response relationship was examined through restricted cubic splines (RCS), and the cumulative incidence of CMM across different CTI level groups was compared using the Kaplan-Meier curve. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses, which included repeated validation with the third wave as the baseline, reanalysis after excluding missing values, and stratification by age and menopausal age, were conducted to confirm the robustness of the findings. The predictive performance and incremental value of CTI were evaluated using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, net reclassification improvement index (NRI), and integrated discrimination improvement index (IDI). Additionally, a nomogram was constructed based on variables selected through LASSO regression.Results A total of 395 participants (13.3%) developed new-onset CMM during follow-up. Multivariate Cox regression analysis indicated that for each 1-unit increase in CTI, the risk of CMM rose by 95% (HR=1.95, 95%CI: 1.66-2.30). In comparison to the lowest quartile group of CTI, the highest quartile group had a higher risk of CMM (HR=3.91, 95%CI: 2.62-5.82, P for trend <0.001). RCS analysis suggested a positive linear association between CTI and the risk of CMM (P-overall <0.001, P-nonlinear = 0.577). The Kaplan-Meier curve showed a higher cumulative incidence of CMM in the high CTI group (log-rank P <0.001). Subgroup analysis indicated that the association between CTI and CMM was more pronounced in postmenopausal women under 60 years of age (interaction P=0.003). The results of the sensitivity analysis were consistent with those of the primary analysis. The AUC of CTI for predicting CMM was 0.649 (95%CI: 0.621-0.678), higher than that of TyG (0.616), CRP (0.612), FBG (0.638), and TG (0.582). Following the inclusion of CTI, the NRI was 0.230 (P <0.001) and the IDI was 0.225 (P <0.001). The nomogram constructed from the six variables selected by LASSO (self-rated health, hypertension, dyslipidemia, CTI, FBG, and HbA1c) showed acceptable discrimination in the training set (AUC=0.742) and the validation set (AUC=0.703).Conclusion Elevated CTI levels were positively associated with the risk of new-onset CMM in postmenopausal women, with a clear dose-response pattern. CTI showed moderate standalone discrimination and added incremental predictive value to traditional risk models. The nomogram incorporating CTI showed acceptable discrimination and calibration. As a simple inflammation-metabolic biomarker, CTI may help identify postmenopausal women who warrant closer cardiometabolic risk assessment when interpreted together with other clinical variables.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12872-026-06150-8.

Background

Cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM) is commonly defined as the coexistence of at least two cardiometabolic diseases, mainly diabetes, coronary heart disease, and stroke [1–4]. Its burden is increasing with population ageing and lifestyle changes [5, 6]. In China, CMM affects a substantial proportion of adults, with prevalence exceeding 30% among people aged 70–79 years [7, 8]. Among Chinese women aged 45 years or older, the prevalence of CMM has been reported to be 33.1% and to increase with age [9]. CMM is associated with higher mortality, cognitive decline, frailty, depressive symptoms, disability, poorer quality of life, and greater medical costs [10–17]. Therefore, early identification of individuals at elevated risk of developing CMM is important for healthy ageing and for reducing the burden on health-care systems.

The early identification and risk stratification of CMM remain challenging in routine practice, highlighting the need for simple composite biomarkers. The C-reactive protein-triglyceride-glucose index (CTI) combines C-reactive protein, a marker of systemic inflammation, with the triglyceride-glucose index, a surrogate of insulin resistance [18–20]. Previous studies have linked CTI with cardiovascular disease, stroke, diabetes, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, mortality, and CMM in general middle-aged and older populations [21–39].

However, most available evidence comes from mixed populations. Its role in postmenopausal women, who experience menopause-related cardiometabolic changes and a substantial multimorbidity burden, remains insufficiently studied [9, 40–42].

Several research gaps remain. First, prior CTI-CMM studies have mainly examined general middle-aged and older adults rather than postmenopausal women specifically [24, 25, 43–45]. Second, estrogen decline during and after the menopausal transition is associated with adverse changes in adiposity, inflammatory and adipokine profiles, insulin resistance, and cardiovascular risk [40, 41, 46, 47].

Whether CTI captures CMM risk in this physiological context is unclear. Third, the incremental predictive value of CTI over common inflammation-metabolic indicators and traditional risk factors has not been systematically assessed in postmenopausal women, and no individualized risk quantification tool incorporating CTI has been developed for this group.

Using prospective CHARLS data, this study evaluated the association between baseline CTI and incident CMM in postmenopausal women. We assessed robustness through sensitivity analyses, including validation using Wave 3 as a new baseline, complete-case reanalysis, and stratification by age and menopausal age. We compared the discriminatory performance of CTI with traditional indicators using ROC curves, assessed incremental value with NRI and IDI, and developed a nomogram that incorporated CTI and clinically available variables.

Methods

Study design and participants

The data for this study were derived from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a national, prospective cohort study of Chinese adults aged 45 years or older and their spouses [48]. CHARLS uses a multi-stage probability proportional to size sampling method, covering 150 counties (districts) and 450 village or residential committees across 28 provinces, autonomous regions, and municipalities. The baseline survey was conducted from June 2011 to March 2012 (Wave 1), with follow-up assessments every 2 to 3 years. The publicly available data used here included Wave 1 (2011–2012), Wave 2 (2013–2014), Wave 3 (2015–2016), Wave 4 (2018–2019), and Wave 5 (2020–2021). CHARLS collected information on demographic characteristics, health status, lifestyle factors, biochemical indicators, chronic diseases, and health-care use. All participants provided written informed consent. The study was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Committee of Peking University (IRB00001052-11015).

This study used the 2011 baseline survey and follow-up data from 2013 to 2020 (Waves 2–5) to identify incident CMM events, thereby establishing a longitudinal temporal sequence between baseline CTI and subsequent CMM occurrence. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Women aged 45 years or older at the baseline survey conducted in 2011; (2) Completion of tests for C-reactive protein (CRP), triglycerides (TG), and fasting blood glucose (FBG) at baseline; (3) Absence of CMM at baseline; and (4) Being in a postmenopausal state. The exclusion criteria were: (1) Male participants; (2) Age at baseline < 45 years; (3) Diagnosis of CMM at baseline; (4) Diagnosis of a malignant tumor at baseline; (5) Non-menopausal status or absence of information regarding menopausal status; (6) Missing data on key covariates (CRP, TG, FBG, BMI); (7) Absence of diagnostic information for CMM; (8) Loss to follow-up.

Menopausal status was determined from self-reported CHARLS questionnaire items [49, 50]. Participants were classified as postmenopausal women if they answered yes to the question, “Have you already gone through menopause?“, and reported natural menopause as the cause. Women whose menstruation ceased because of surgical procedures, such as hysterectomy or oophorectomy, were excluded. This approach is consistent with reproductive ageing frameworks that define menopause around the final menstrual period and with prior CHARLS-based studies of menopausal women [49–51].

Following rigorous data cleaning and sample screening, a total of 17,708 participants were included at baseline in 2011. Based on the established inclusion and exclusion criteria, the following groups were sequentially excluded: individuals aged < 45 years (n = 648), male participants (n = 8322), those with missing information on menopausal status (n = 727), and non-menopausal individuals (n = 2001). Additionally, data on CRP, TG, FBG, and BMI were missing for 2234 participants. Diagnostic information for CMM was absent for 23 individuals, while 151 participants had preexisting CMM at baseline. Furthermore, 37 participants had a baseline malignant tumor, and 593 were lost to follow-up. Ultimately, 2972 postmenopausal women were included in the analysis, of whom 395 participants (13.3%) developed new-onset CMM during follow-up. Calculation of CTI

CTI is a composite biomarker reflecting systemic inflammation and insulin-resistance-related metabolic status. CTI was calculated using the formula established in prior literature. The specific calculation formula is as follows: [18–20, 22]\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{CTI}\:=\:0.412\;\mathrm x\;\log10(\mathrm{CRP}\;\lbrack\mathrm{mg}/\mathrm L\rbrack)\;+\;\log10(\mathrm{TG}\;\lbrack\mathrm{mg}/\mathrm{dL}\rbrack\;\mathrm x\;\mathrm{FBG}\;\lbrack\mathrm{mg}/\mathrm{dL}\rbrack\;/\;2)$$\end{document}

CRP refers to C-reactive protein (mg/L), TG denotes triglyceride (mg/dL), FBG indicates fasting blood glucose (mg/dL), and log10 represents the common logarithm.

Because logarithmic transformation requires positive inputs, CTI was calculated only for participants with positive CRP, TG, and FBG values after data cleaning. All blood biochemical indicators were assessed using morning fasting venous blood samples collected during the 2011 baseline survey. CRP was measured once at baseline.

Participants with baseline malignant tumors were excluded, but CHARLS did not provide repeated CRP assays or sufficiently detailed information on acute infection or recent surgery; this residual acute-phase variation is acknowledged as a limitation [52].

Assessment of incident CMM

The primary outcome of this study was new-onset CMM. In accordance with definitions established in prior research, CMM is characterized by the presence of at least two cardiometabolic diseases (CMDs) within the same individual, specifically including diabetes, heart disease, and stroke [43–45]. Thus, participants are classified as having experienced a CMM event if they exhibit any of the following combinations: diabetes with heart disease, diabetes with stroke, heart disease with stroke, or the simultaneous presence of all three conditions.

The diagnosis of diabetes employs a composite criterion that integrates laboratory test results, medication history, and self-reported information. A diagnosis is established if any of the following conditions are met: (1) FBG ≥ 7.0 mmol/L; (2) glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) ≥ 6.5%; (3) the use of oral hypoglycemic agents or antidiabetic medications, such as insulin, at the time of assessment; or (4) an affirmative response to the standardized survey question, “Has any doctor ever told you that you have diabetes or elevated blood sugar?“. This composite definition encompasses biomedical testing and clinical diagnostic records, thereby maximizing the identification of diabetes cases and minimizing the risk of missed diagnoses.

The diagnosis of heart disease and stroke primarily relied on participants’ self-reported physician diagnoses, supplemented by medication information. Heart disease was assessed using the standardized question: “Has any doctor ever told you that you have coronary heart disease, angina pectoris, congestive heart failure, or other heart problems?” Participants who responded yes or reported the use of cardiovascular medications, such as anti-angina or anti-heart failure drugs, were classified as having heart disease. Stroke was determined using the question: “Has any doctor ever told you that you have had a stroke?” An affirmative response indicated stroke. These self-reported physician diagnoses have been widely used in CHARLS-based epidemiological studies [43–45, 48], although potential misclassification is acknowledged in the Discussion.

The determination of the occurrence time of the outcome adheres to the following principle: For participants who developed new CMM during the follow-up period, the event time is defined as the interval between the baseline survey date and the first follow-up survey date at which new CMM was identified. If a participant meets the diagnostic criteria for CMM for the first time during a specific follow-up, the date of that survey is considered the occurrence time of the event. For participants who did not experience CMM throughout the entire follow-up period, the follow-up duration is defined as the interval between the baseline survey date and the date of their last completed survey, and this duration was treated as censored follow-up time.

Additionally, for participants who died during the follow-up period, their disease status was determined based on the last available health survey information prior to death.

Missing data processing

The proportions of missing values for study variables were low and are summarized in the supplementary materials. Among the 2,972 postmenopausal women included in the analysis, missingness was below 10% for all variables.

Variables with the highest missingness included marital status (8.07%), menopausal age (2.66%), dyslipidemia (2.43%), sleep duration (1.77%), ADL6 (1.29%), depressive symptoms (1.20%), SBP (1.05%), and DBP (1.06%). Overall, missing data accounted for less than 5% of all data items.

This study assumed a missing-at-random (MAR) mechanism. To mitigate bias introduced by missing data and enhance statistical efficiency, we used the MissForest algorithm for missing value imputation [53]. MissForest is an iterative random forest-based method that can handle mixed numerical and categorical variables and capture nonlinear relationships. All variables were incorporated as predictors during imputation and iterated until convergence. The fully imputed dataset was used for the primary analyses. Because the MAR assumption cannot be proven empirically, a complete-case analysis was also performed as a sensitivity analysis, and the possibility of missing-not-at-random bias is acknowledged in the Discussion.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses in this study were conducted using R software (version 4.3.0) and EasyR software. A P value < 0.05 in the two-sided test is defined as statistically significant.

Continuous variables that follow a normal distribution are reported as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD), whereas those that do not conform to a normal distribution are presented as median with interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables are expressed in terms of frequency and percentage. Inter-group comparisons were performed using appropriate methods based on the variable types: independent sample t-tests were applied for continuous variables with normal distribution, while Mann-Whitney U tests were utilized for non-normal distributions.

Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test (chi-square test) or Fisher’s exact test.

Cox regression analysis was conducted to assess the relationship between CTI and the risk of CMM occurrence. Both CTI, treated as a continuous variable and as a quartile categorical variable, were evaluated using a multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression model. The hazard ratio (HR) and its 95% confidence interval (CI) were computed. Dose-response relationship analysis

To investigate the potential nonlinear dose-response relationship between CTI and CMM risk, restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression was employed. Three knots were used to provide flexible modeling while limiting overfitting, and were placed at the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles of CTI. The Wald test was used to assess nonlinearity (P for nonlinearity).

Cumulative disease risk analysis

The cumulative incidence of CMM across various quartile groups of CTI was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier survival curve.

The log-rank test was employed to assess differences in survival distributions among the groups. Follow-up time was calculated as the interval from the baseline survey date to either the date of the CMM event or the date of the last completed follow-up survey (censoring).

Subgroup analysis and interaction test

To assess the consistency of the association between CTI and CMM across populations, predefined subgroup analyses were performed by age (< 60 years vs. ≥60 years), marital status, educational attainment, place of residence, drinking status, and smoking status. Cox models were used within each subgroup, and interaction terms between CTI and subgroup variables were introduced to test for effect modification. Because these subgroup analyses were exploratory and no multiplicity correction was applied, interaction P values were interpreted cautiously.

Sensitivity analysis

To assess the robustness of the research findings, a series of sensitivity analyses were performed. Sensitivity Analysis 1 involved repeated validation using the year 2015 (the third wave, Wave 3) as the new baseline time point.

Postmenopausal women without CMM at this time were included in the analysis. The CTI value was recalculated, and its association with the risk of subsequent CMM occurrence was examined to assess whether the CTI-CMM association was consistent across baseline time-point selection. Sensitivity Analysis 2 involved reanalysis after excluding all missing values. A complete case analysis was conducted, incorporating only participants with no missing values across all variables. The Cox regression model was re-fitted to mitigate any bias that could arise from missing value imputation. Sensitivity analysis 3 (stratified by age): The samples were categorized into two subgroups: 45–60 years old and ≥ 60 years old, according to the participants’ ages. The relationship between CTI and CMM was assessed separately to evaluate whether the CTI-CMM association was consistent across age groups. Sensitivity analysis 4 (stratified by menopausal age): Participants were divided into three categories (< 45 years old, 45–55 years old, and ≥ 55 years old) based on menopausal age. The association between CTI and CMM was analyzed independently to examine whether the CTI-CMM association was consistent across menopausal age groups. All sensitivity analyses were adjusted using the variables from Model III.

Predictive performance assessment

To assess the discriminatory performance of CTI for CMM, a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was generated, and the area under the curve (AUC) along with its 95% confidence interval was computed.

The optimal cutoff value was identified at the point where the Youden index reached its maximum. Additionally, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and accuracy were calculated as evaluation metrics.

To compare the strengths and weaknesses of CTI against traditional indicators in predicting CMM, the AUC values for the TyG index, CRP, FBG, and TG were calculated and subsequently compared with the AUC of CTI.

Incremental predictive value assessment

To further assess the incremental value of the CTI in the prediction model, we employed the Net Reclassification Improvement (NRI) index and the Integrated Discrimination Improvement (IDI) index. Initially, we constructed a baseline Cox regression model that included traditional risk factors, encompassing all covariates except for CTI as specified in Model III. Subsequently, we incorporated CTI, TyG, CRP, FBG, and TG into the baseline model sequentially. After the addition of each index, we calculated the NRI and IDI values and their P values. An NRI greater than 0 indicates improved reclassification, while an IDI greater than 0 indicates improved overall discriminative ability of the model.

Construction and verification of the nomogram

To create a risk-prediction nomogram for estimating CMM risk in postmenopausal women, we initially employed LASSO regression to screen the features of all covariates in Model III.

LASSO regression identifies the optimal regularization parameter λ (lambda.min) through 10-fold cross-validation, thereby controlling model complexity and mitigating the risk of over-fitting. Utilizing the non-zero coefficient variables identified by LASSO regression, we constructed a multivariate logistic regression model, which subsequently facilitated the development of a nomogram. This nomogram translates the regression coefficients into a visual scoring system. By summing the scores of each variable, we derive a total score that is then mapped to the predicted probability of CMM.

All samples were randomly allocated into a training cohort and an internal validation cohort in a 7:3 ratio. The nomogram model was developed using the training set, followed by internal validation in the validation set. The model’s discrimination ability was assessed using the ROC curve and AUC. Calibration of the model was evaluated through a calibration plot to determine the alignment between predicted probabilities and actual observed probabilities. Additionally, the clinical net benefit of the model was analyzed using decision curve analysis (DCA).

Results

Baseline characteristics of participants

A total of 2,972 postmenopausal women without CMM at baseline were included, with a mean follow-up of 103.53 months (approximately 8.6 years). During follow-up, 395 participants (13.3%) developed new-onset CMM. As shown in Table 1, participants in the CMM group were older and had higher blood pressure, BMI, FBG, HbA1c, TG, CRP, and CTI, as well as lower HDL-C, than those without CMM (all P < 0.001). The TyG index showed a similar pattern. The proportions of hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, heart disease, and stroke were higher in the CMM group than in the non-CMM group (all P < 0.001). Association between the CTI and the CMM incidence

The relationship between CTI and the risk of new-onset CMM in postmenopausal women was assessed using a multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression model, with the findings presented in Table 2. In Model I, which did not adjust for any covariates, each additional unit of CTI as a continuous variable was associated with a 69% increase in the risk of CMM occurrence (HR = 1.69, 95%CI: 1.53–1.88, P < 0.001). In Model II, adjusted for age, place of residence, marital status, and educational attainment, each additional unit of CTI corresponded to a 66% increase in the risk of CMM (HR = 1.66, 95%CI: 1.50–1.85, P < 0.001). In Model III, which included comprehensive adjustments for all covariates, every 1-unit increase in CTI was linked to a 95% increase in the risk of CMM (HR = 1.95, 95%CI: 1.66–2.30, P < 0.001). The CTI was categorized into quartiles (Q1: 5.85–8.22, Q2: 8.22–8.73, Q3: 8.73–9.27, Q4: 9.27–13.16), with the lowest quartile (Q1) serving as the reference group. In Model III, no significant difference in the risk of CMM was observed between group Q2 and group Q1 (HR = 1.28, 95% CI: 0.87–1.88, P = 0.209). However, the risk of CMM in group Q3 increased significantly (HR = 2.23, 95% CI: 1.54–3.22, P < 0.001). Furthermore, the risk of CMM was higher in group Q4 (HR = 3.91, 95% CI: 2.62–5.82, P < 0.001). The trend test indicated a significant increase in the risk of CMM occurrence with rising quartiles of CTI (P for trend < 0.001).

Subgroup analysis and interaction test

To further assess the consistency of the association between CTI and CMM across predefined populations, we performed subgroup analyses by age, marital status, education level, place of residence, drinking status, and smoking status. The results are presented in Fig. 4. In most subgroups, higher CTI was associated with increased CMM risk. The age subgroup analysis suggested a stronger association among women aged < 60 years than among those aged ≥ 60 years, with a statistically significant interaction between age and CTI (P for interaction = 0.003). No significant interactions were observed for marital status, educational level, place of residence, drinking status, or smoking status (all P for interaction > 0.05). The former-smoking subgroup was small (n = 49), and this subgroup estimate should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

Sensitivity analysis

To verify the robustness of the research results, we conducted a series of sensitivity analyses. (1) Repeated verification using the third Wave as the baseline: The year 2015 (the third wave, Wave 3) was established as the new baseline time point to recalculate the CTI and examine its association with the risk of subsequent CMM occurrence. The results indicated that for each additional unit increase in CTI, the risk of CMM rose by 86% (HR = 1.86, 95%CI: 1.56–2.22, P < 0.001). In comparison to the lowest quartile group, the risk of CMM in the highest quartile group increased by 240% (HR = 3.40, 95%CI: 2.23–5.18, P < 0.001), with a trend test yielding P < 0.001 (Supplementary Table 1). (2) Reanalysis following the exclusion of all missing values: To mitigate potential bias associated with missing value imputation, we employed a complete case analysis, including only participants with no missing variables for model refitting. The results indicated that for each additional unit of CTI, the risk of CMM increased by 91% (HR = 1.91, 95%CI: 1.61–2.25, P < 0.001). In comparison to the lowest quartile group, the risk of CMM in the highest quartile group rose by 312% (HR = 4.12, 95%CI: 2.73–6.22, P < 0.001), with a trend test P < 0.001 (Supplementary Table 2). (3) Sensitivity analysis stratified by age involved dividing participants into two groups: those aged 45–60 years and those aged ≥ 60 years. The association between CTI and CMM was evaluated within each group. In the 45–60 age group, each additional unit of CTI was associated with a 143% increase in the risk of CMM (HR = 2.43, 95%CI: 1.87–3.17, P < 0.001). In the ≥ 60 years age group, each additional unit of CTI corresponded to a 79% increase in the risk of CMM (HR = 1.79, 95%CI: 1.44–2.22, P < 0.001) (Supplementary Table 3). (4) Sensitivity analysis stratified by menopausal age: Participants were categorized into three groups based on menopausal age: <45 years, 45–55 years, and ≥ 55 years. In the early menopause group (< 45 years), each 1-unit increase in CTI was associated with a 154% increase in the risk of CMM (HR = 2.54, 95%CI: 1.53–4.20, P < 0.001). In the normal menopause group (45–55 years), a 1-unit increase in CTI corresponded to a 94% increase in CMM risk (HR = 1.94, 95%CI: 1.62–2.33, P < 0.001). In the late-onset menopause group (≥ 55 years), the association was also statistically significant (HR = 4.63, 95%CI: 1.28–16.75, P = 0.019), but the small sample size and wide confidence interval require cautious interpretation (Supplementary Table 4). Overall, the sensitivity analyses were broadly consistent with the main analysis.

Predictive performance assessment

To assess the discriminatory performance of CTI for CMM, we constructed ROC curves and calculated AUC values (Table 3; Fig. 5). The AUC for CTI was 0.649 (95% CI: 0.621–0.678), and the optimal cutoff was 8.739. The detailed sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and accuracy are presented in Table 3. CTI had a higher AUC than the TyG index (0.616, 95% CI: 0.587–0.645), CRP (0.612, 95% CI: 0.582–0.641), FBG (0.638, 95% CI: 0.607–0.668), and TG (0.582, 95% CI: 0.553–0.612). These results indicate that CTI showed the highest AUC among the evaluated single indicators; however, its limited sensitivity and positive predictive value restrict its use as a standalone clinical screening tool.

Construction and verification of the nomogram

A risk-prediction nomogram was developed to estimate CMM risk in postmenopausal women. The samples were randomly split into a training set (n = 2080) and an internal validation set (n = 892) at a 7:3 ratio. In the training set, LASSO regression was initially applied to identify features among all covariates in Model III. The optimal regularization parameter λ was determined (lambda.min = 0.0276) using 10-fold cross-validation. Six non-zero coefficient variables were identified through LASSO regression, including self-rated health (SRH), hypertension, dyslipidemia, CTI, FBG, and HbA1c (Fig. 6; Supplementary Table 5).

Fig. 6Lasso regression cross-validation plot (A) and coefficient path plot (B). (λ = 0.0276446348971309)

Lasso regression cross-validation plot (A) and coefficient path plot (B). (λ = 0.0276446348971309)

A multivariate logistic regression model was constructed using the six variables in the training set, leading to the development of a nomogram (Fig. 7). This nomogram visualizes risk scores by summing the score for each variable and mapping the total score to the predicted probability of CMM. In the training set, the AUC of the nomogram for predicting CMM was 0.742. In the internal validation set, the AUC was 0.703 (Fig. 8), indicating acceptable discrimination. The calibration curve showed reasonable agreement between predicted and observed CMM probabilities (Supplementary Fig. 6). Discussion

Using national prospective cohort data from CHARLS, this study evaluated the association between CTI and new-onset CMM among postmenopausal women.

Higher baseline CTI was associated with increased CMM risk and showed a dose-response pattern. After adjustment for demographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, physical measurements, laboratory parameters, and comorbidities, each 1-unit increase in CTI was associated with a 95% higher risk of CMM (HR = 1.95, 95% CI: 1.66–2.30). Compared with the lowest CTI quartile, the highest quartile was associated with higher CMM risk (HR = 3.91, 95% CI: 2.62–5.82). RCS analysis supported a positive linear association (P-overall < 0.001, P-nonlinear = 0.577), and the Kaplan-Meier curve showed a higher cumulative incidence of CMM in participants with higher CTI levels (log-rank P < 0.001). Subgroup analysis suggested a stronger association among women aged < 60 years (P for interaction = 0.003), while other subgroup interactions were not significant. Sensitivity analyses using Wave 3 as a new baseline, complete-case analysis, and stratification by age and menopausal age were broadly consistent with the main findings. CTI showed moderate standalone discrimination for CMM (AUC = 0.649), and adding CTI to the traditional risk factor model improved NRI and IDI. The nomogram incorporating CTI and clinical variables showed acceptable discrimination in the training and validation sets. Overall, CTI may help identify postmenopausal women at elevated CMM risk when interpreted together with other clinical information.

This study provides evidence of a positive association between CTI and CMM risk in postmenopausal women. The finding is consistent with previous work in mixed middle-aged and older populations. Lin et al. reported that each 1-unit increase in CTI was associated with a 71% higher risk of CMM in CHARLS and ELSA (HR = 1.71, 95% CI: 1.39–2.11) [24]. Chen et al. also found a positive dose-response association between CTI and new-onset CMM in CHARLS [25]. The higher point estimate in the present study may reflect the specific postmenopausal population and longer follow-up. Menopause-related cardiometabolic changes, including adverse adiposity, inflammatory and adipokine changes, and insulin resistance, provide a plausible context for the heightened risk observed in this group [9, 40–42].

As a composite biomarker that integrates inflammation and insulin resistance, the predictive value of CTI has been validated across various cardiometabolic outcomes. Regarding cardiovascular diseases, Zhang et al., utilizing the CHARLS cohort (n = 8,084), reported that for each additional unit of CTI, the risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD) increased by 44% (HR = 1.44, 95% CI: 1.17–1.78), with a stronger association observed in the 45–60 age group [22]. Additionally, Xu et al. found in CHARLS that CTI is positively correlated with CVD risk, highlighting a nonlinear relationship between the two variables [26]. In a population with CKM syndrome, Ou et al. determined that for every additional unit of CTI, the risk of all-cause mortality rose by 111% [27]. Lu et al. indicated that the risk of CVD in the highest quartile of CTI increased by 42% [28]. Furthermore, Han et al. confirmed a positive correlation between CTI and CVD risk, noting that accelerated biological aging mediates part of this association [29]. Xian et al. demonstrated that each additional unit of CTI corresponded to a 15% increase in stroke risk, with a notably stronger effect observed in individuals with mild renal insufficiency (HR = 2.73) [23].

Zhou et al. reported that the combined CTI and waist-height ratio (CTI-WHTR) exhibited superior predictive accuracy for stroke compared to CTI alone [30]. Tang et al. revealed that individuals in the highest quartile of CTI among hypertensive patients had a 66% higher risk of stroke [31]. Huo et al. uncovered a significant positive relationship between CTI and stroke in individuals with normal blood glucose levels and prediabetes, whereas this association was not statistically significant in diabetic patients [32]. Moreover, CTI has been linked significantly to diabetes, metabolism-related fatty liver disease (MASLD), and mortality risk. Sun et al. calculated cumulative CTI (cumCTI) and found that for each 1-unit increase in cumCTI, the risk of diabetes increased by 22%. Furthermore, a consistently high CTI trajectory was linked to a 162% rise in diabetes risk [33]. Shan et al. also established that CTI serves as an independent predictor of diabetes risk, demonstrating predictive efficacy that surpasses that of the TyG index [34]. Regarding MASLD, Jin et al. utilized the NHANES and CHARLS dual cohorts to confirm that elevated CTI was significantly associated with the prevalence of MASLD and the long-term risks of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, with diabetes mediating a substantial portion of this association [35]. Ni et al. demonstrated, using data from the NHANES, CHARLS, and Gaoyou cohorts, that the CTI exhibits a J-shaped nonlinear relationship with all-cause and cardiovascular mortality among the elderly population, with a turning point at CTI = 8.31. Furthermore, they found that its predictive efficacy surpasses that of other metabolic indicators [36]. Song et al. validated the predictive value of CTI for mortality in the CKM syndrome population through machine learning techniques, achieving an AUC of 0.866 with the random survival forest model [37]. This evidence suggests that CTI is associated with both cardiometabolic events and long-term adverse outcomes, supporting its potential role in cardiometabolic risk assessment. The present study extends this evidence to postmenopausal women and suggests that CTI may contribute to identifying individuals at elevated risk of progression toward CMM.

This study showed that the AUC of CTI for predicting CMM was 0.649, exceeding the AUCs of TyG, CRP, FBG, and TG. Addition of CTI to the traditional risk factor model also improved reclassification and discrimination, with an NRI of 0.230 and an IDI of 0.225. These findings align with prior studies showing incremental value of CTI for cardiometabolic outcomes [22, 24, 34, 38]. CTI may be useful because it integrates two pathological pathways relevant to CMM: low-grade inflammation reflected by CRP and insulin resistance reflected by the TyG component.

Nevertheless, the standalone AUC was moderate, and the limited sensitivity and positive predictive value indicate restricted clinical utility if CTI is used alone. CTI should therefore be interpreted as an initial risk marker or component of a broader prediction model rather than as an independent diagnostic indicator.

This study focused on postmenopausal women, a population with important clinical and public health implications. Menopause is accompanied by a decline in estrogen and changes in adiposity, inflammatory activity, insulin resistance, and cardiovascular risk [40, 41, 46, 47]. Estrogen has cardioprotective effects, including regulation of vascular endothelial function, lipid metabolism, inflammatory responses, and insulin sensitivity. Estrogen withdrawal may promote inflammatory signaling, visceral fat redistribution, lipolysis, and free fatty acid release, which can aggravate insulin resistance [50, 54]. In this pathophysiological context, CTI may be particularly sensitive to early inflammation-metabolic changes. Our subgroup analysis showed a stronger association between CTI and CMM among women aged < 60 years than among those aged ≥ 60 years (P for interaction = 0.003), suggesting that early postmenopause may represent a useful window for risk assessment. The association was also stronger among women with menopause before age 45 years, although the late-menopause subgroup had few events and wide confidence intervals. These subgroup findings should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

This study has several strengths. First, the national CHARLS cohort supports risk stratification within Chinese postmenopausal women. Second, the study focused on postmenopausal women, a group in which evidence on the association between CTI and CMM remains limited. Third, multiple sensitivity analyses, including Wave 3 baseline validation, complete-case analysis, and stratification by age and menopausal age, were used to assess the consistency of the findings. Fourth, the study evaluated both the association of CTI with CMM and its incremental predictive value when added to traditional risk factors. Finally, the nomogram provides a potential tool for individualized risk estimation, although external validation is still needed.

The findings have clinical and public health implications. CTI is derived from routine CRP, TG, and FBG tests, so it can be implemented without additional specialized testing. The nomogram developed in this study integrates CTI with readily available variables, including self-rated health, hypertension, dyslipidemia, FBG, and HbA1c, enabling individualized estimation of future CMM probability in postmenopausal women. However, as shown in Table 3, the sensitivity and positive predictive value of CTI alone were low (sensitivity = 0.286, PPV = 0.075), indicating that CTI should not be used as a standalone diagnostic or screening tool. A sensitivity of 0.286 means that nearly three-quarters of postmenopausal women who will eventually develop CMM would be missed if CTI were used alone. Similarly, a PPV of 0.075 indicates that only about 7.5% of individuals identified as high-risk by CTI alone would actually develop CMM, leading to a high false-positive rate. These limitations restrict its utility in clinical practice for individual risk prediction.

Therefore, CTI is better suited as an initial risk marker that should be combined with other clinical variables (such as age, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and HbA1c) rather than used in isolation. The nomogram showed acceptable predictive performance (training-set AUC = 0.742) and may support a more individualized risk assessment approach when externally validated.

This study has several limitations. First, heart disease and stroke were primarily identified using self-reported physician diagnoses, which may introduce misclassification or recall bias. Although this approach has been widely used in CHARLS-based studies [43–45, 48], outcome misclassification remains possible.

Second, we did not distinguish specific CMM combinations, such as diabetes plus heart disease, diabetes plus stroke, or heart disease plus stroke. Third, CTI and CRP were measured once at baseline. Because CRP is an acute-phase reactant, transient infection, recent surgery, or other acute inflammatory conditions could influence baseline CRP values, and CHARLS did not provide repeated CRP measurements to confirm persistent inflammation [52]. Additional extreme-value sensitivity analyses for CRP were not performed in the present revision. Fourth, deaths before CMM were treated as censoring events in the Cox models; formal competing-risk analyses were not performed, so competing mortality may have affected hazard estimates. Fifth, the primary imputation assumed MAR, and missing-not-at-random mechanisms cannot be fully excluded despite the complete-case sensitivity analysis. Sixth, serum creatinine and uric acid were included in Model III, but albumin and bilirubin were not available in the analytic dataset used for the present revision; this may limit assessment of endogenous antioxidant status [55, 56]. Seventh, the cohort included only Chinese postmenopausal women, so generalization to other ethnic groups, men, or premenopausal women should be cautious. Eighth, this observational study cannot establish causality. Finally, the late-menopause subgroup had a relatively small number of CMM events, requiring cautious interpretation and external validation.

In conclusion

Using the CHARLS national prospective cohort, this study found that higher CTI levels were positively associated with incident CMM in postmenopausal women and showed a dose-response pattern. CTI provided moderate standalone discrimination and incremental predictive value beyond traditional risk factors. A nomogram incorporating CTI and clinical variables showed acceptable discrimination and calibration in the training and validation sets. CTI may therefore serve as a simple reference biomarker for early CMM risk identification in postmenopausal women when interpreted together with other clinical information.
