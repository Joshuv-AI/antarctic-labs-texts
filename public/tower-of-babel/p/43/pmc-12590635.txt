CACNB4 attenuates cardiac dysfunction by regulating calcium and ATP levels via interaction with RyR2
Authors: Shan Jiang, Ming Hong, Jingbo Zhang, Fei Xu, Xingqian Zhang, Guanghao Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCACNB4 is suspected to be involved in cardiac contraction, its specific role and the underlying mechanisms are not elucidated. We analyzed gene expression profiles of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from heart failure patients using the GSE235757 data set from the NCBI GEO database. KEGG enrichment analysis was performed to identify pathways associated with differentially expressed genes. Analysis of the GSE235757 data set revealed a significant downregulation of CACNB4 expression in PBMCs of heart failure patients, particularly in the “Cardiac muscle contraction” pathway. Western blot analysis confirmed a reduction in CACNB4 expression in hypoxic myocardial cells and heart tissue from heart failure mice. Overexpression of CACNB4 improved cardiac function, reduced infarct size, alleviated myocardial damage, via enhancing ATP and Ca2+ levels. Further analysis identified an interaction between CACNB4 and RyR2, which improve Ca2+ and ATP levels. This study demonstrated that CACNB4 interacts with RyR2 to regulate intracellular Ca2+ and ATP levels, which are critical for maintaining cardiac function.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03322-8.Keywords: Myocardial contraction, Cardiac dysfunction, CACNB4, RyR2, Ca2+

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) is a complex clinical syndrome characterized by structural or functional cardiac abnormalities leading to impaired ventricular filling or ejection capacity [1]. With a high incidence rate and poor prognosis, HF is a common consequence of various cardiovascular diseases. Clinical interventions for HF aim to improve cardiac function, alleviate symptoms, enhance quality of life, and prolong lifespan [2]. The heart’s contraction and relaxation functions are crucial in HF. Impaired systolic function leads to decreased pumping capacity, which impact systemic organ perfusion, and trigger various clinical symptoms and complications. Cardiac contractility modulation (CCM) is a novel treatment approach for HF that utilizes non-excitatory electrical stimulation of the myocardium during the absolute refractory period of the cardiac cycle. This stimulation has been shown to enhance contractility, improving HF symptoms, functional status, and quality of life [3], but it is very expensive. Therefore, comprehensively addressing systolic heart function is essential in HF management.

HF pathogenesis is complex, involving multiple cellular and molecular mechanisms, including electrophysiological abnormalities of myocardial cells, organelle dysfunction, myocardial remodeling, and inflammatory response [4]. Studies have shown that the concentration of cytosolic Ca2+ plays an important role in the effect of myocardial contractility [5]. Exploring the pathogenesis of HF and searching for new targets improving Ca2+ is crucial for advancing clinical HF management. To investigate key factors affecting myocardial contraction, we conducted differential expression analysis on the GSE235757 data set, identifying 184 upregulated and 140 downregulated genes. KEGG analysis of these 324 differentially expressed genes revealed that ENSG00000182389 (Calcium voltage-gated channel auxiliary subunit beta 4, CACNB4) is significantly enriched in the Cardiac muscle contraction pathway, suggesting a critical role for CACNB4 in cardiac contractility during heart failure. The CACNB4 gene encodes the auxiliary β4 subunit of the calcium channel subunit Cav1.2 expressed in the heart, which has the ability of nuclear translocation [6] and is essential for myocardial contraction [7]. Tammineni et al., has demonstrated that the auxiliary β4 subunit of the myocardial Cav1.2 channel enhances antiviral immune response in H9c2 cardiomyocytes by promoting the expression of interferon-β-related genes [8]. Previous research has linked a novel variant of the CACNB4 gene to African American idiopathic dilated cardiomyopathy (IDC), impacting myocardial contractility [9]. Cao et al. identified 2502 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) by integrating four acute myocardial infarction (AMI) expression profiles. Their findings indicated that EHF, KRT6A, and DSG3 were upregulated in AMI, while CACNG4 was downregulated [10], providing potential targets for early AMI diagnosis and intervention.

These studies suggest that CACNB4 plays a vital role in cardiac contraction during HF, although its underlying mechanism remains unclear. Therefore, this study aims to investigate how CACNB4 regulates cardiac contraction and affects heart failure.

Materials and methods

The experiments conformed to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals published by the US National Institutes of Health and The second qilu hospital of Shandong University (Jinan, China). The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Shandong University and The second qilu hospital of Shandong University (Jinan, China).

Data acquisition and analysis

Expression profiling by high throughput sequencing was obtained from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) data set GSE235757. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were obtained from 4 patients who had heart failure with atrial fibrillation (HF/AF group) and 4 matched healthy subjects. Differential expression genes (DEGs) were analyzed using GEO2R Significance level cutoff 0.05. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis was performed by R language software package clusterProfiler.

Establishment of a mouse model of heart failure following myocardial infarction

Considering the high homology (99.4%) between mouse and human CACNB4 proteins, we used a mouse model to validate the function of CACNB4. 8-week-old male SPF grade C57/BL6 mice were purchased from Jiangsu Huachuang Xinnuo Pharmaceutical Technology Co., Ltd., and housed in an SPF animal laboratory at 21–23 °C and 60–65% humidity for 1 week. Mice were maintained on a 12/12-h light/dark cycle with ad libitum food and water access. Following 2.0% isoflurane inhalation anesthesia. Multi-parameter acquisition (MPA) biological signal acquisition analysis system and respiratory anesthesia machine was used. After successful intubation and anesthesia, hair was removed from the left chest. Fully expose the heart. Using the lower edge of the left atrial appendage as a landmark, a 6–0 non-invasive suture was used to ligate the left anterior descending branch of the coronary artery 2–3 mm below the root of the left atrial appendage, with a needle depth of 0.8 mm. After ligation, local myocardial changes (left ventricular outer membrane appeared pale for about 1 min) and ECG monitoring of ST segment elevation were observed to confirm successful ischemic model establishment. Sham group mice underwent anesthesia and heart exposure, but only sutures were placed without ligation. Then chest cavity was closed. The ventilator was removed when the heartbeat returned to normal and spontaneous breathing occurred. Mice were kept in a warm environment and monitored for infection. Twenty-four hours after MI modeling, mice were injected with either AAV virus containing an empty vector or AAV virus containing overexpression of CACNB4 via tail vein injection at a dose of 10^12 VP. Mice were pretreated with TMDJ-03 (0.1 mg/kg/day) for 1 week before MI modeling, while control mice received an equal volume of DMSO. After 4 weeks of routine feeding, mice were euthanized using ultrasound examination, and heart appearance was photographed, and serum was collected. Heart tissues were collected for histological analysis and other experiments.

Echocardiography

Echocardiography was performed on anesthetized mice using an isoflurane anesthesia machine. A Vevo2100 small animal ultrasound machine (Vevo2100, FUJIFILM Visualsonics) with an MS-550D probe was used for ultrasound detection. M-mode ultrasound recordings of left ventricular motion in the long-axis section were acquired to measure the left ventricular end diastolic diameter (LVID; d), left ventricular end systolic diameter (LVID; s), calculate the left ventricular ejection fraction (EF), and assess left ventricular short axis systolic rate (FS).

Histochemical staining analysis

Following euthanasia, heart tissue was harvested and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (44124, Sigma). The tissue was then dehydrated, cleared, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned to 4 µm thickness. Sections were deparaffinized by immersing in xylene for 20 min, followed by rehydration in a gradient ethanol series and three washes in PBS solution. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and Masson staining were used to examine tissue structure and fibrosis. Sections were then mounted with neutral gum and observed under a microscope (IX71, Olympus).

2,3,5-Triphenytetrazolium–chloride (TTC) staining

Following anesthesia, the heart was quickly excised and stored in a − 20 °C freezer for 1 h. The heart was then placed in a dedicated heart slot and sliced vertically and horizontally into 5 pieces (approximately 1 mm thick) along the long axis using a thin blade. The slices were immersed in 2% TTC staining solution (G3005, Servicebio) and incubated at 37 °C for 15 min. After staining, the slices were rinsed with clean water and fixed in 4% formaldehyde for 24 h. Stained red areas represent normal heart tissue, while white areas represent infarcted heart tissue. The stained heart slices were arranged in order. ImageJ image processing software was used to calculate the volume of cardiac infarction. First, the infarct area of each heart slice was calculated, and then the sum of the infarct areas of each slice was multiplied by the slice thickness (1 mm) to obtain the total infarct volume. Infarct volume (%) was calculated as (infarct volume/total volume) × 100%.

Isolation and culture of mouse cardiomyocytes

Mouse cardiomyocytes were isolated and cultured as follows: briefly, after anesthesia, the skin on the chest and abdomen was moistened with alcohol spray. The apex of the heart was excised and rinsed with 3–4 mL of D-Hank’s solution in an ice bath to remove blood. The D-Hank’s solution was aspirated, and the apex was cut into 1 mm3 pieces using ophthalmic or microscopic scissors. The tissue was then digested in a water bath for 30–60 min with collagenase (LS004196, Worthington). The digested tissue block was blown into a single-cell suspension. Cells were centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant was removed, and the cell sediment was resuspended in complete myocardial cell culture medium (PriMed-iCell-022, iCell). After incubation for 1.5–2 h, adherent cells (mainly fibroblasts and endothelial cells) were discarded. The unattached cell suspension was removed, and complete myocardial cell culture medium was added to adjust the cell concentration. The medium was changed the following day.

Transfection and culture of mouse cardiomyocytes

Experimental groups included a control group, where mouse cardiomyocytes were cultured under normal conditions, and a hypoxia group, where mouse cardiomyocytes were cultured in an anoxic incubator with a mixture of 94% N2, 5% CO2, and 1% O2 gases. For overexpression of CACNB4 and RyR2, hypoxia-induced myocardial cell injury was observed in mice, and Lipofectamine 2000 transfection reagent (11668–027, Thermo Fisher) was used to transfect overexpressing CACNB4 or RyR2 plasmid. The negative control (NC) plasmid was used as a control.

Western blot

To detect protein expression levels in heart tissue or myocardial cells, we performed Western blot experiments. Tissues or cells were lysed in RIPA lysis buffer (BL504A, Biosharp), homogenized, and protein concentrations were determined by BCA Protein Assay Kit (BL521A, Biosharp). Twenty micrograms of protein were loaded onto SDS–PAGE gels and transferred to PVDF membranes (IPVH00010, millipore). Immunoblotting was performed using antibodies against β-actin (GB11001, Serivicebio), TNC (DF6697, Affinity), TNI (ab209809, Abcam), BNP/NPPB (DF6902, Affinity), CACNB4 (17770–1-AP, Proteintech), RYR2(ab302716, Abcam), β-mhc (22280–1-AP, Proteintech), a-mhc(22281–1-A, Proteintech). HRP-conjugated secondary antibody anti-rabbit IgG (BL003A, Biosharp) were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system (5200, Tanon) with ECL (WBKLS0100, Millipore). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software and normalized to β-actin levels for relative quantification.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

Co-IP was performed using Pierce® Co-Immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) Kit (26149, Thermo Fisher) according to the operating manual. Briefly, logarithmically growing mouse cardiomyocytes were cultured in a 10 cm dish and transfected with the corresponding vector as described previously. After 48 h of transfection, cells were harvested and washed three times with cold PBS (pH 7.4). Cells were then lysed in IP lysis buffer (BL504A, Biosharp) containing 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) and 1 mg/ml protease inhibitor cocktail at 4 °C for 30 min. The clarified lysate was precleared with protein A/G beads and then incubated with protein A/G beads plus monoclonal antibodies against CACNB4 (17770–1-AP, Proteintech) and RYR2 (ab302716, Abcam) for 6–8 h. The beads were then washed with IP lysis buffer, boiled in sample buffer, and the proteins were subjected to SDS–PAGE, followed by immunoblotting analysis with corresponding antibodies.

GST-pull down assay

To investigate protein–protein interactions, we performed GST pull-down assays using Pierce™ GST Protein Interaction Pull-Down Kit (21516, Thermo Fisher) according to the operating manual. Myocardial cells were co-transfected with GST-tagged proteins or empty vectors as negative controls. After 48 h of protein expression, cells were lysed in 1% NP-40 lysis buffer, 5 mM sodium pyrophosphate, 5 mM NaF, 2 mM sodium orthovanadate, 10 mg/L aprotinin, 10 mg/L leupeptin, and 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride) at 4 °C for 30 min, followed by centrifugation at 14,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The clarified lysate was incubated with glutathione sepharose 4B beads at 4 °C for 3 h for routine pull-down assays. After incubation, beads were washed three times with 1% NP-40 lysis buffer, eluted by boiling in SDS–PAGE loading buffer, and analyzed by Western blotting.

Ca2+ concentration detection

Ca2+ concentration in tissues and cells was detected by Fluo-3/AM (Powder, Cell Permeant, 40703ES50, Yeasen). For cell staining, after processing, cells were incubated with 5 μM Fluo-3 dye diluted in HBSS at 37 °C for 30 min. Following incubation, hoechst33342 (C1025, Beyotime) was then added. The cells were observed and photographed under a microscope (IX71, Olympus). For animal tissue staining, heart tissue from each group of mice was collected and frozen sectioned to a thickness of 8 µm. After processing the slices were incubated with 5 µM Fluo-3 dye (40703ES50, Yeasen). Hoechst 33342 dye (C1025, Beyotime) was added. They were observed and photographed under a microscope (IX71, Olympus).

ATP content determination

The contents of ATP in heart tissues or cells were detected by ATP assay kit (A095-1–1, Nanjing Jiancheng) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Tissue homogenate and cell lysis buffer was prepared. The absorbance was detected use an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay reader (DR-3518GL, Diatek) at 636 nm to detect.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 22.0 (IBM, Armonk). Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 9.3 (GraphPad, La Jolla, CA, USA). Measurement data were compared using the t test and presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Each experiment was performed in triplicate and repeated at least three times.

Results

Downregulation of CACNB4 expression in PBMCs of heart failure patients is associated with cardiac contractility

To investigate the potential role of CACNB4 in heart failure, we analyzed the GSE235757 data set, which contains gene expression profiles of PBMCs from heart failure patients, retrieved from the NCBI GEO database. GEO2R software revealed 324 differentially expressed genes in PBMCs from heart failure patients compared to the normal group, including 184 upregulated and 140 downregulated genes (Fig. 1A, B). To further investigate the signaling pathways involved in these differential gene expression changes, KEGG enrichment analysis was performed. The analysis identified enrichment of differentially expressed genes in the “Cardiac muscle contraction” pathway (ko04260). Notably, ENSG00000182389, encoding the calcium voltage-gated channel auxiliary subunit beta 4 (CACNB4), was significantly enriched in this pathway (Fig. 1C, D), suggesting a potential role for CACNB4 in cardiac contractility during heart failure. To validate this observation, we established myocardial cell hypoxia models and mouse models of heart failure. Western blot (WB) analysis demonstrated a significant reduction in CACNB4 expression in hypoxic myocardial cells and heart tissue of heart failure mice compared to control groups (Fig. 1E, F). These findings indicate that CACNB4 may play a protective role in heart failure.

Overexpression of CACNB4 alleviates cardiac function

To investigate the role of CACNB4 in myocardial contraction during heart failure, we established a mouse model of heart failure and overexpressed CACNB4 in the model. Western blot analysis revealed a significant reduction in CACNB4 protein expression in the MI group compared to the sham group, while overexpression of CACNB4 significantly increased CACNB4 protein levels (Fig. 2A). Echocardiography results showed that LVIDd and LVIDs were significantly increased, while EF% and FS% were significantly decreased in the MI group compared to the sham group, indicating successful establishment of the heart failure model after myocardial infarction (Fig. 2B). Importantly, overexpression of CACNB4 significantly improved cardiac function. Grossly, sham group mice exhibited a relatively uniform heart size, normal morphology, and a conical shape. The ventricular walls were of consistent thickness and size, and the heart texture was tough and elastic. In contrast, the overall heart volume increased in the MI and MI + OE–NC groups, showing significant dilation, and the heart texture was relatively hard and brittle. Overexpression of CACNB4 led to a decrease in cardiac texture hardness and a reduction in infarct volume (Fig. 2C). TTC staining revealed significantly increased infarct volume in the MI group compared to the sham group, while overexpression of CACNB4 resulted in a significant decrease in infarct volume and some relief of myocardial infarction (Fig. 2D). Histological analysis revealed a clear cardiac structure, orderly arrangement of myocardial cells, uniform cytoplasm, and no inflammatory cell infiltration in the sham group. MI and MI + OE–NC groups exhibited irregular cardiac tissue morphology, coagulation necrosis of myocardial fibers in the infarcted area, significant reduction in myocardial cells, and obvious infiltration of inflammatory cells. After CACNB4 overexpression, cardiac tissue lesions improved significantly, with relatively intact tissue structure and local inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig. 2E). Masson staining showed significant collagen fiber deposition in the MI and MI + OE–NC groups compared to the sham group, but CACNB4 overexpression significantly reduced collagen fiber deposition in the heart (Fig. 2F).

Further analysis of myocardial injury markers demonstrated that compared to the sham group, TNI protein expression was significantly reduced, and BNP protein expression was increased in the MI and MI + OE–NC groups. Overexpression of CACNB4 significantly reduced BNP protein expression, while there was no significant difference in TNI protein expression. TNC changes were not significant in any group (Fig. 2G). The results showed a decrease in ATP content in the MI and MI + OE–NC groups compared to the sham group. However, ATP content was increased in the MI + OE–CACNB4 group compared to the MI + OE–NC group (Fig. 2H). Fluo-3 staining showed a decrease in Fluo-3 fluorescence intensity in the MI and MI + OE–NC groups compared to the sham group. Conversely, the MI + OE–CACNB4 group exhibited an increase in Fluo-3 fluorescence intensity compared to the MI + OE–NC group (Fig. 2I). These findings suggest that overexpression of CACNB4 alleviates heart failure by improving cardiac function, reducing myocardial damage, and promoting ATP production.

Overexpression of CACNB4 enhances intracellular Ca2+ and ATP levels in cardiomyocytes

To further investigate the impact of CACNB4 on heart failure, we isolated mouse cardiomyocytes and overexpressed CACNB4. Western blot analysis revealed a significant reduction in CACNB4 protein expression in the hypoxia group compared to the control group. Overexpression of CACNB4 restored CACNB4 protein levels to those observed in the control group (Fig. 3A). Analysis of myocardial injury markers demonstrated a significant reduction in TNI, α-actin, and α-MHC protein expression in the hypoxia group compared to the control group, while β-MHC protein expression was significantly increased. Compared to the Hypoxia + OE–NC group, the Hypoxia + OE–CACNB4 group showed increased α-actin and α-MHC protein expression, a significant decrease in β-MHC protein expression, and no significant difference in TNI protein expression. TNC protein expression did not differ significantly among the groups (Fig. 3B). Fluo-3 staining revealed a significant reduction in Fluo-3 fluorescence intensity in cardiomyocytes of the hypoxia group compared to the control group. However, compared to the Hypoxia + OE–NC group, Fluo-3 fluorescence intensity increased in cardiomyocytes of the Hypoxia + OE–CACNB4 group (Fig. 3C), indicating an increase in intracellular Ca2+ concentration. The results showed a significant reduction in ATP content in cells of the hypoxia group compared to the control group. Compared to the Hypoxia + OE–NC group, the ATP content of cells in the Hypoxia + OE–CACNB4 group was significantly increased (Fig. 3D). These findings demonstrate that CACNB4 promotes Ca2+ and ATP content in cardiomyocytes, potentially improving cardiac contractility and slowing down the progression of heart failure.

Interaction between CACNB4 and RyR2 in the regulation of heart failure

To elucidate the underlying mechanism of CACNB4 in heart failure, we investigated potential protein interactions. Using STRING analysis, we identified a potential interaction between CACNB4 and RyR2 (Fig. 4A). Western blot analysis confirmed a significant reduction in RyR2 expression in the hypoxia induced myocardial cell and heart tissue of the heart failure model, while overexpression of CACNB4 increased RyR2 expression (Fig. 4B, C). Pull-down assays demonstrated an interaction between CACNB4 and RyR2 in vitro, as RyR2 was detected in the GST–CACNB4 group but not in the GST control group (Fig. 4D). Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) results further confirmed an interaction between CACNB4 and RyR2 (Fig. 4E).

To explore the functional consequences of this interaction, we overexpressed RyR2 in a myocardial cell heart failure model. Western blot analysis revealed a reduction in RyR2 protein expression in the hypoxia group compared to the control group. Overexpression of RyR2 (Hypoxia + OE–RyR2 group) significantly increased RyR2 protein expression compared to the Hypoxia + OE–NC group (Fig. 4F), indicating successful overexpression. Further analysis of myocardial injury markers demonstrated a significant reduction in TNI, α-actin, and α-MHC protein expression, and an increase in β-MHC protein expression in the hypoxia group compared to the control group. However, the Hypoxia + OE–RyR2 group showed significantly increased expression of TNI, α-actin, and α-MHC proteins, while β-MHC protein expression decreased compared to the Hypoxia + OE–NC group. TNC protein expression did not differ significantly among the groups (Fig. 4G). Staining results showed a decrease in Fluo-3 fluorescence intensity in the hypoxia group compared to the control group. However, the Hypoxia + OE–RyR2 group showed a significant increase in Fluo-3 fluorescence intensity compared to the Hypoxia + OE–NC group (Fig. 4H), indicating an increase in intracellular Ca2+ concentration. The results revealed a decrease in cellular ATP content in the hypoxia group compared to the control group. The Hypoxia + OE–RyR2 group showed an increase in cellular ATP content compared to the Hypoxia + OE–NC group (Fig. 4I). These findings suggest that CACNB4 interacts with RyR2, and overexpression of either CACNB4 or RyR2 can enhance intracellular Ca2+ levels and ATP production, ultimately improving cardiac function and mitigating heart failure progression.

CACNB4 regulates heart failure by influencing RyR2 to modulate Ca2+ and ATP levels

To investigate the role of RyR2 in CACNB4-mediated heart failure regulation, we treated a heart failure model overexpressing CACNB4 with the RyR2 inhibitor TMDJ-035. Western blot analysis confirmed that TMDJ-035 treatment significantly reduced RyR2 expression (Fig. 5A). Echocardiography results revealed that LVIDd and LVIDs were significantly increased, while EF% and FS% were significantly decreased in the MI group compared to the sham group, demonstrating successful establishment of the myocardial infarction heart failure model. Overexpression of CACNB4 significantly improved cardiac function. However, cardiac function indicators were significantly worse in mice treated with TMDJ-035 compared to the MI + OE–CACNB4 + DMSO group (Fig. 5B). Histological analysis revealed a significant increase in myocardial fiber coagulation necrosis and inflammatory cell infiltration in the infarcted area of mice treated with TMDJ-035 compared to the MI + OE–CACNB4 + DMSO group (Fig. 5C). Masson staining showed significantly increased collagen fiber deposition in the heart tissue of mice treated with TMDJ-035 compared to the MI + OE–CACNB4 + DMSO group (Fig. 5D). Western blot analysis showed that BNP protein expression was significantly increased after TMDJ-035 treatment compared to the MI + OE–CACNB4 + DMSO group, while TNI protein expression remained unchanged. TNC protein expression did not differ significantly among the groups (Fig. 5E). In addition, ATP content and Ca2+ fluorescence intensity were significantly reduced in the heart tissue of mice treated with TMDJ-035 compared to the MI + OE–CACNB4 + DMSO group (Fig. 5F, G).

Discussion

HF is characterized by the heart’s inability to effectively pump enough blood to meet the body’s oxygen and nutritional demands, leading to functional impairment [11]. Despite advancements in treatment strategies, HF prognosis remains challenging due to a limited understanding of its pathogenesis. Ca2+ and ATP play pivotal roles in myocardial contraction, working together to enable effective heart contraction [12]. This study identifies a previously underappreciated role for CACNB4 in the pathogenesis of heart failure. We demonstrated that the CACNB4–RyR2 axis as a crucial regulator of cardiac contraction and a potential target for slowing HF progression by modulating Ca2+ concentrations. This research highlights CACNB4 as a promising target for improved diagnostics and therapeutics in heart failure. The significant downregulation of CACNB4 suggests its utility as a minimally biomarker, while functional studies demonstrate that restoring CACNB4 expression improves cardiac function and reduces infarct size. The CACNB4–RyR2 pathway offers new mechanistic insights into calcium and metabolic dysregulation in HF, paving the way for precision medicine strategies.

HF is categorized into heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF),heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) and heart failure with mildly reduced ejection fraction (HFmrEF) [13]. HFrEF, typically caused by heart injury (myocardial infarction) or chronic hypertension [14] is characterized by impaired cardiac contractility. Improving contractility is, therefore, a central therapeutic aim, with the potential to alleviate symptoms, slow progression, and enhance quality of life for HF patients [15]. A central challenge in HF research is unraveling how dysregulated molecular pathways converge to impair cardiac contraction. Current and future research on inotropes in HF management aims to improve cardiac function in HF patients by delving deeper into the mechanisms of impaired myocardial contractility, identifying new therapeutic targets, and developing novel therapeutic drugs. Our bioinformatics analysis revealed the involvement of CACNB4 in the myocardial contraction signaling pathway, suggesting its importance in HF caused by myocardial contractile dysfunction. While CACNB4, encoding a VDCC β4 subunit involved in calcium regulation and cellular signaling [16], has known roles in the myocardium and nervous system, with mutations causing various channelopathies and neurological disorders [17–19]. Its specific function in regulating heart contraction in HF remains unclear. Unlike previous work focusing on neuronal effects, we revealed CACNB4’s involvement in the myocardial contraction signaling pathway, suggesting a key role in HF caused by contractile dysfunction. Supporting this, we found that CACNB4 overexpression significantly improved cardiac function in a heart failure model, reducing inflammation, fibrosis, and infarct size, demonstrating its critical role in slowing HF progression.

Effective Ca2+ -dependent mitochondrial energy supply is essential for normal cardiac contractile activity, and disruption of Ca2+ homeostasis contributes to the pathogenesis of various human diseases, particularly HF [20, 21]. Myocardial systolic dysfunction involves reduced contractility and delayed relaxation due to altered intracellular Ca2⁺ transients [22, 23]. While existing strategies, like sodium calcium exchanger (NCX) inhibition, aim to improve Ca2⁺ handling and cardiac function [24], a comprehensive understanding of Ca2⁺ and energy regulation in HF is lacking. Research has shown that SAR340835 can improve cardiac function in both normal dogs and dogs with HF, including increasing stroke volume, enhancing contractility, and restoring sympathetic vagus nerve balance and blood pressure reflex sensitivity. The drug also exhibits antiarrhythmic properties [25]. Ca2+ acts as the “trigger” for myocardial contraction, while ATP serves as the “energy source.” Together, they ensure the normal contraction and relaxation of the myocardium. Myocardial contraction requires energy supply, and HF can lead to energy metabolism disorders, resulting in insufficient ATP supply to myocardial cells, thereby weakening myocardial contractility [26, 27]. The observed decrease in ATP levels in HF can impair cardiac contractility and lead to impaired pump function [28]. VDCC is an important ion channel on the cell membrane responsible for the transmembrane transport of calcium ions. This study focuses on VDCCs, key channels for transmembrane Ca2⁺ transport. This study found that overexpression of CACNB4 significantly increased Ca2+ concentration and ATP content in cardiomyocytes, indicating that CACNB4 slows down HF by regulating Ca2+ and ATP content in cardiomyocytes. This novel finding suggests that CACNB4 slows HF progression, not just by regulating Ca2⁺, but also by impacting energy metabolism, positioning it as a crucial regulator of both key factors for myocardial contractility.

To investigate the mechanism of action of CACNB4 in HF, we conducted protein interaction analysis using STRING and found that CACNB4 can interact with RyR2. This finding was validated by Co-IP and GST pull-down assays. RyR2 is a calcium release channel on the sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR), responsible for the release of calcium from SR to the cytoplasm [29]. During myocardial contraction, the myocardial cell membrane undergoes depolarization, and the L-type calcium channels on the cell membrane open, triggering Ca2+ influx [30]. These influx Ca2+ ions act on the ryanodine receptor (RyR) on the sarcoplasmic reticulum, causing calcium release and promoting the mutual sliding between thick and thin muscle filaments, triggering the contraction of cardiac muscle cells [31]. Numerous studies have found that alterations in RyR2 contribute to the occurrence and development of HF by participating in the inhibition of key cell contraction indicators such as [Ca2+]i transients and promoting arrhythmogenic Ca2+ waves during diastole [32]. While RyR2 is known to be crucial for myocardial contraction, with alterations contributing to HF through disrupted [Ca2+]i transients and arrhythmogenic Ca2⁺ waves [33], and strategies targeting RyR2, like Danqulin, improving outcomes in ischemic heart disease [33], the specific role of CACNB4 in modulating RyR2’s function in this context was previously unknown. This study demonstrated that overexpression of RyR2 significantly improved cardiac function, reduced inflammatory cell infiltration, collagen fiber deposition, and infarct size in a heart failure model, highlighting the crucial role of RyR2 in slowing HF progression. Further research revealed that overexpression of RyR2 can promote Ca2+ influx and ATP content. These findings suggest that CACNB4 participates in the regulation of the HF process by interacting with RyR2 to modulate Ca2+ influx and ATP content, highlighting a previously unappreciated link between these key factors in myocardial contractility.The study is limited by its reliance on a single data set (GSE235757), which may not represent the diverse heart failure population and lacks generalizability. The results need validation across multiple data sets and populations. The experimental models used, including myocardial cell hypoxia and mouse models, have limitations in fully replicating human heart failure pathophysiology, necessitating caution in extrapolating findings to humans and highlighting the need for further clinical studies. In addition, the study’s focus on the CACNB4 gene and its interaction with RyR2 may overlook other contributors to the multifactorial disease of heart failure, suggesting a need for broader investigation of genetic and molecular networks to fully understand disease mechanisms and identify more therapeutic targets. In addition, extrapolating PBMC-derived CACNB4 to cardiac tissue in the MI model faces significant limitations. First, the cellular heterogeneity and functional differences between PBMCs and cardiac tissue make it difficult to accurately represent the complex molecular mechanisms within the heart. Second, the MI model may not fully capture the entire progression of heart failure, and the temporal relationship between molecular changes in PBMCs and cardiac tissue can be complex. Third, the intricate molecular network and interactions within cardiac tissue suggest that extrapolating PBMC-derived features could oversimplify the mechanisms of heart failure. Finally, the high heterogeneity of human heart failure and individual variability in PBMC molecular features necessitate further validation in larger and more diverse patient populations to assess the clinical relevance and applicability of PBMC-derived molecular features in predicting or reflecting cardiac tissue pathology.

In addition, there are still some shortcomings in this study. First, Cardiac function is divided into systolic and diastolic dysfunction, myocardial infarction model mainly affects systolic function, whether CACNB4 affects diastolic function is not clear. Second, overexpression of CACNB4 improves calcium and ATP levels in heart failure. Studies have shown that CACNB4 can increase the release of calcium from the endoplasmic reticulum through RYR2, but how it affects ATP levels is not clear.

Conclusion

In summary, this study demonstrates that CACNB4 plays a critical protective role in heart failure by regulating intracellular Ca2⁺ and ATP levels through interaction with RyR2. We found that CACNB4 expression is significantly downregulated in heart failure patients and experimental models. Overexpression of CACNB4 improved cardiac function, reduced infarct size, alleviated myocardial injury, and enhanced Ca2⁺ and ATP levels in vitro and in vivo. Mechanistically, CACNB4 physically interacts with RyR2 to modulate calcium handling and energy metabolism, thereby preserving myocardial contractility. Inhibition of RyR2 abolished the beneficial effects of CACNB4 overexpression, confirming the functional importance of this interaction. These findings suggest that targeting this axis may offer promising therapeutic strategies for heart failure.
