A clinical analysis of 19 cases of chordomas with poor differentiation treated using diverse therapeutic modalities
Authors: Wei Wang, Yajie Cui, Renkun Nie, Lirong Mao, Yue Su, Chao Liu, Wenhuan Chen, Jie Ding, Zhengzheng Shen, Jianzhi Gao, Jingli Du
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThere is an urgent need to enhance clinicians’ ability to diagnose and treat chordomas with poor differentiation, particularly poorly differentiated chordomas (PDCs), through clinical case analysis.MethodsA retrospective review was conducted on the clinical data of 19 patients with pathologically confirmed chordomas with poor differentiation, treated at Hebei Yizhou Cancer Hospital between July 2021 and May 2024. All patients underwent surgery. 9 received proton radiation, with 4 also having conventional chemotherapy. 3 patients had both conventional chemotherapy and targeted therapy after proton radiation, while another 3 received chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy. The progression of the disease was primarily monitored through follow-up telephone consultations. The study used polytomous logistic regression to assess the impact of age and gender on chordoma lesion sites, and ordinal logistic regression to evaluate the effects of age, gender, and lesion sites on chordoma differentiation. The COX Proportional Hazards Model analyzed the influence of these factors and surgical resection ratio on chordoma prognosis. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis assessed the impact of surgical resection ratio on prognosis.ResultsPostoperative diagnoses revealed 12 cases of PDC, 4 of dedifferentiated chordoma (DDC), and 3 of low differentiated chordoma (LDC). Some patients experienced tumor reduction and symptom improvement. By August 2024, 8 patients had died, while 11 were still alive, with follow-up durations ranging from 12 to 65 months and an average of 29.45 months. Based on these limited 19 cases, COX regression analysis showed that sex, differentiation degrees, chordoma locations, treatment regimens, and surgical resection ratio were not independent prognostic factors (all P > 0.05). Younger age was linked to higher differentiation (P < 0.05), and chordomas on the clivus had the worst differentiation and highest malignancy (P < 0.001).ConclusionsBased on the analysis of 19 chordoma cases, it appears that for chordomas with poor differentiation, the completion of surgical resection followed by adjuvant chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and targeted therapy may enhance patient prognosis to some extent. The efficacy of immunotherapy for chordomas with poor differentiation warrants further investigation. Additionally, potential prognostic factors influencing chordoma outcomes should be validated in studies involving larger populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03667-w.Keywords: Poorly differentiated chordomas, Treatment modalities, Case analysis

Introduction

Chordomas with poor differentiation are rare and aggressive neoplasms originating from the axial skeleton, characterized by notochordal differentiation. These tumors are predominantly observed in pediatric and young adult populations, with a relatively higher incidence in females [1]. Recent advancements in molecular pathological diagnostics and therapeutic technologies have facilitated an increase in the identification of chordomas with poor differentiation. The implementation of multidisciplinary treatment strategies has extended the median survival time of chordomas patients with poor differentiation, leading to a rise in reported clinical cases. Nonetheless, there is a paucity of systematic reviews and analyses concerning the clinical characteristics, diagnostic approaches, and therapeutic experiences related to chordomas with poor differentiation. This study retrospectively examines 19 cases of chordomas with poor differentiation, including 12 cases of poorly differentiated chordoma (PDC), treated with proton radiation therapy at our institution from 2021 to 2024, with the objective of enhancing clinicians’ understanding of this condition.

Methods

Patient enrollment

We obtained comprehensive case data for 19 chordomas patients diagnosed with poor differentiation who received proton radiation therapy at our institution between July 2021 and May 2024. The cohort comprised 11 female and 8 male patients, ranging in age from 3 to 60 years, with disease durations spanning from 6 months to the present (Table S1). The inclusion criterion was a postoperative pathological confirmation of chordomas with poor differentiation. Exclusion criteria encompassed a poor general condition, indicated by a performance status (PS) score of 3–4, and intolerance to proton radiation therapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy. This study adheres to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki by the World Medical Association.

Pathological diagnosis

The diagnosis of all patient specimens was conducted by two experienced pathologists. The confirmation of chordomas with poor differentiation was based on morphological criteria, including cell morphology and arrangement, as well as immunohistochemical criteria, such as positivity for markers including S-100, cytokeratin (CK), epithelial membrane antigen (EMA), and Brachyury.

Imaging examination

All 19 patients underwent preoperative imaging using either computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). The CT scans were conducted utilizing a GE Revolution 256-slice CT scanner, operating at a tube voltage of 120 kV, a tube current ranging from 100 to 280 mA, and a scan slice thickness between 1.23 and 5 mm. MRI scans were performed with a GE Discovery S/MR750W superconducting MRI scanner, employing sequences such as transverse T1-weighted imaging (T1WI), transverse T2-weighted imaging (T2WI), and either sagittal or coronal T2WI. For contrast enhancement, iodixanol was used in CT imaging at a dosage of 1.2 ml/kg with an injection flow rate of 2.0 to 3.0 ml/s, while gadopentetate dimeglumine was utilized in MRI imaging at a dosage of 0.1 mmol/kg with a flow rate of 0.5 ml/s.

Therapeutic regimens

All 19 patients underwent surgical resection followed by a variety of adjuvant treatment modalities. Specifically, 9 patients received proton radiation therapy; 3 patients were treated with a combination of targeted therapy, chemotherapy, and proton radiation therapy; 4 patients received chemotherapy in conjunction with proton radiation therapy; and 3 patients underwent a regimen comprising targeted therapy, chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and proton radiation therapy, as detailed in Table S1. The chemotherapy regimens utilized included one or more of the following drugs: vincristine, doxorubicin, cyclophosphamide, carboplatin, ifosfamide, etoposide, cisplatin, vindesine sulfate, and dacarbazine. When necessary, glucocorticoids such as prednisone and dexamethasone were used in combination, as indicated in Table S2. The targeted therapies administered included imatinib, apatinib, and anlotinib (Table S2). Immunotherapeutic agents employed were sintilimab or camrelizumab [2, 3]. Proton radiation therapy was delivered using the Belgian IBA Proteus PLUS proton therapy system, as illustrated in Fig. 1.

Analysis of risk factors associated with the differentiation degrees and prognosis in patients with chordomas

Utilizing the demographic and clinical characteristics of the patient cohort, we performed an analysis to examine the relationship between lesion sites and variables such as age and gender. Subsequently, we investigated the correlations between the degrees of chordoma differentiation and the aforementioned factors. Furthermore, we evaluated the associations among age, gender, differentiation degrees, lesion sites, treatment regimens, and the surgical resection ratio for patient prognosis.

Data analysis

Polytomous logistic regression analysis was employed to assess the impact of age and sex on chordoma locations. Additionally, ordinal logistic regression analysis was conducted to investigate the influence of age, sex, and chordoma locations on differentiation degrees. The Cox Proportional Hazards Model was employed to conduct an in-depth analysis of the prognostic effects of variables such as age, sex, differentiation degrees, chordoma locations, treatment regimens, and the surgical resection ratio. Additionally, the survival proportions were calculated to assess the impact of chordoma differentiation degrees on prognosis. The Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was specifically applied to examine the influence of the surgical resection ratio on prognosis. These analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics (version 27.0; IBM Inc., Armonk, NY, USA) and GraphPad Prism (version 9.5.0; GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). A 5% significance level was used to determine statistically significant associations.

Results

Clinical symptoms and signs

The initial symptoms observed in the cohort of 19 chordomas patients with poor differentiation predominantly manifested as space-occupying effects. Specifically, eight patients reported recurrent neck pain, while five patients initially experienced symptoms such as headache, dizziness, decreased vision, and blurred vision, which were subsequently followed by restricted extraocular movement. One patient presented with limb weakness persisting for over a month, and three patients reported symptoms of snoring, choking, and difficulty swallowing during sleep. Additionally, two patients experienced sacrococcygeal pain without any apparent cause. Post-treatment evaluations showed that some individuals experienced significant symptom improvement after surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and targeted therapy. Furthermore, the patient encountered notable adverse reactions during the chemotherapy and targeted therapy phases (refer to Table S2).

Pathological grading

The postoperative pathological diagnoses comprised 12 cases of PDC, 4 cases of dedifferentiated chordoma (DDC), and 3 cases of low differentiated chordoma (LDC), as detailed in Table S1.

Imaging findings

Preoperative computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) examinations were conducted on all 19 patients. The lesions were identified in the clivus in 10 patients, the cervical vertebrae in 6 patients, the sacral vertebrae in 2 patients, and the skull base in 1 patient. Notably, 3 patients presented with multiple bone metastases. CT imaging revealed that the tumors exhibited low, slightly high, or mixed density, frequently accompanied by irregular calcification and bone destruction. MRI findings indicated that the tumors demonstrated slightly low or isointense signals on T1-weighted images (T1WIs) and isointense, slightly high, or mixed signals on T2-weighted images (T2WIs). Following the administration of contrast agents, the lesions showed heterogeneous enhancement. Posttreatment MRI, in comparison to pretreatment images, demonstrated a reduction in lesion size, with a slightly lower signal intensity on T1WI and slightly higher or mixed signal intensity on T2WI. Contrast-enhanced images revealed heterogeneous enhancement, with an increase in nonenhanced areas within the lesions (Fig. 2).

Prognosis and follow-up

All 19 patients were primarily monitored through telephone follow-up. Of the 9 patients who underwent proton radiation therapy post-surgery, 3 achieved remission, 2 maintained stable disease, 1 experienced disease progression, and 3 succumbed to the disease. Among the 4 patients who subsequently received conventional chemotherapy following proton radiation therapy, 1 achieved remission, 1 remained stable, and 2 died. The 3 patients who received both conventional chemotherapy and targeted therapy after proton radiation therapy are currently stable or in remission and continue to be monitored. Conversely, all 3 patients who underwent chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy both before and after proton radiation therapy died, with an overall survival of 45.67 months and a progression-free survival of 12.77 months. The total follow-up duration spanned from 6 to 72 months, measured from the initial hospital visit to the time of follow-up. The treatment outcomes of the 11 surviving patients remain under observation. By the conclusion of the follow-up period in August 2024, the duration of follow-up for the 11 surviving patients varied from 12 to 65 months, with a mean follow-up duration of 29.45 months.

Risk factors affecting the differentiation degrees and prognosis of Chordomas

In this study, which included 19 cases, no statistically significant effects on prognosis were identified with respect to age, sex, degrees of differentiation, chordoma locations, treatment regimens, or the ratio of surgical resection, as all P-values exceeded 0.05 (refer to Table 1 and Table S3). Additionally, there was no statistical significance between gender and chordoma locations or differentiation degrees (all P > 0.05) (Tables 2 and 3). In this study involving 19 patients, independent variables, such as age and lesion sites were found to significantly influence the differentiation degrees of chordoma (P < 0.001) (Table 3). Regarding age as an influencing factor, within the limited sample size of this study, a younger age was associated with a higher degree of differentiation (all P < 0.05), although age did not significantly affect the lesion site (all P > 0.05) (Tables 2 and 3). Furthermore, no significant association was found between prognosis and the differentiation degrees of pathological grades or treatment regimens (all P > 0.05) (Table 1 and Fig. S1). The surgical resection ratio did not demonstrate statistical significance in prognostic evaluation, either when incorporated into COX regression analysis alongside other factors or when assessed independently using Kaplan-Meier survival analysis (all P > 0.05) (refer to Table 1 and Table S3). Notably, the location of the chordoma significantly influenced the differentiation degrees (Table 3), with chordomas located in the clivus exhibiting the worst differentiation and highest malignancy (P < 0.001) (Table 3).

Discussion

In accordance with the 2020 World Health Organization Classification of Tumors of Soft Tissue and Bone, chordomas are categorized into three pathological subtypes: conventional chordoma, DDC, and PDC, the latter representing a novel classification distinguished by its high invasiveness and metastatic potential [4]. The molecular pathology of PDC is predominantly characterized by the loss of function of the SMARCB1/INI1 protein [5, 6], which may lead to the disruption of SWI/SNF complex function, thereby impairing the regulation of gene expression, cell proliferation, and differentiation [7]. Chordomas most frequently manifest in the clivus, followed by the cervical spine, with sacrococcygeal chordomas being relatively uncommon. This observation aligns with the statistical distribution of lesion sites observed in our cohort of 19 patients. In this study, cases were stratified into PDC, LDC, and DDC groups based on pathological grade and malignancy severity. Among these, PDC represents the most severe form, followed by LDC and DDC.

Surgical intervention continues to be the primary clinical strategy for managing poorly differentiated spinal cord tumors. Palliative surgery, which involves the piecemeal resection of the majority of the tumor, is occasionally employed to mitigate neurological symptoms arising from tumor invasion and spinal compression. Given the invasive characteristics and metastatic potential of PDC, alongside its responsiveness to chemotherapy, some experts advocate for the administration of chemotherapy and radiotherapy following surgical procedures. The chemotherapy regimen for PDC is typically informed by protocols established for soft tissue sarcoma [2, 3]. Postoperative radiotherapy targeting residual tumor lesions has been shown to significantly extend progression-free survival in patients with chordomas. Furthermore, recent studies underscore the enhanced therapeutic efficacy of proton knife and heavy ion therapy in the treatment of PDC, compared to other radiotherapy techniques [8]. Additionally, targeted therapy or immunotherapy may be viable systemic treatment options for patients with SMARCB1/INI1-deficient PDC [9, 10]. In this study, 19 patients diagnosed with chordomas underwent diverse post-surgical treatment protocols. These included proton radiation therapy alone, proton radiation therapy combined with chemotherapy, proton radiation therapy in conjunction with both chemotherapy and targeted therapy, and a regimen comprising proton radiation therapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy.

In this study, we did not observe a significant impact of age or sex on the prognosis of chordomas (Table 1). However, the results of the ordered regression analysis indicate that age significantly influences the degree of chordoma differentiation, with younger patients exhibiting a higher degree of differentiation (Table 3). Generally, a higher degree of differentiation is associated with a more favorable prognosis. Previous studies have reported that children with chordoma tend to have a better prognosis compared to adults [11, 12], which aligns with our findings. Additionally, it has been reported that children under the age of five have a poorer prognosis [11], as corroborated by another review [13]. Our findings for the under-five age group contradict this conclusion, possibly due to the limited sample size of this subgroup in our study. Regarding the influence of gender on prognosis, an earlier large-scale population follow-up study in the United States found a higher incidence of chordoma and a lower 5-year survival rate in men compared to women [14]. However, Smoll et al. suggested that gender affects only the incidence and not the prognosis [15]. None of these findings were observed in this study, and existing research does not support the same conclusion. Therefore, these associations require further validation in a larger population.

In previous studies, Zhao et al. reported that LDC was associated with the poorest prognosis; however, their analysis did not account for the differentiation degree of PDC [16]. Similarly, Nachwalter et al. demonstrated that the prognosis for DDC was significantly worse compared to conventional chordoma [17]. Furthermore, the degree of surgical resection and the adequacy of marginal resection are considered the primary determinants influencing the prognosis of chordoma [18, 19]. However, our results suggest that the location of the lesion, the level of differentiation, the treatment protocols, and the surgical resection ratio do not have a significant impact on the prognosis of chordoma (refer to Table 1 and Table S3). This discrepancy may be attributed to the variability in treatment implementation. Given that the cases were collected from multiple hospitals nationwide, there was an absence of standardized evaluation criteria for formulating treatment regimens. In clinical practice, treatment decisions are primarily based on the severity of the condition and the overall health status of the patients, which may result in more aggressive adjuvant therapies for patients in poorer health [12]. Consequently, it can be inferred that comprehensive treatment regimens may mitigate the impact of differentiation degrees and lesion sites on the prognosis of chordoma to some extent. This may explain why our research found that chordoma patients with tumors on the slope exhibit greater poor differentiation (Table 3), although the tumor location does not significantly affect prognosis (Table 1). Notably, none of these patients underwent multiple treatment regimens, including targeted therapy and immunotherapy (Table S1). This may be attributable to the potential for serious adverse reactions associated with some targeted therapies and immunotherapies, which could adversely impact prognosis [20, 21].

Proton therapy offers a notable clinical advantage due to the unique depth-dose characteristics of protons, which allow for a substantial reduction in normal tissue exposure both proximal and distal to the target volume [22]. Consequently, this approach is advantageous for increasing tumor doses, preserving healthy tissues, and improving the management of chordoma [22]. In our study, proton radiation therapy, as a fundamental component of all treatment regimens, significantly reduced chordoma size (Fig. 1). The application of proton therapy in pediatric patients with chordoma has demonstrated favorable therapeutic outcomes [23]. A recent large-scale cohort study conducted in the United States has demonstrated that proton radiotherapy enhances the overall survival rate in adults with chordoma compared to conventional radiotherapy; however, the optimal dosage has yet to be established [24]. Furthermore, Rotondo et al. suggested a treatment protocol involving initial proton therapy, followed by surgical intervention, and subsequent proton therapy, which yielded optimal control of chordoma [25]. This strategy may serve as a valuable reference for future clinical practice.

Previous research has indicated that traditional chemotherapy agents have minimal impact on chordoma [19, 26]. In a study by Guiu et al., the combination of carboplatin with epinephrine was employed to augment the concentration and antitumor efficacy of carboplatin, thereby enhancing therapeutic outcomes through intratumoral chemotherapy [27]. Furthermore, Cao et al. identified that chordoma patients exhibit resistance to chemotherapeutic drugs due to insufficient DNA damage accumulation, with the PARP DNA repair pathway playing a pivotal role in this resistance mechanism [28]. The application of PARP inhibitors in conjunction with chemotherapeutic agents in chordoma patients not only increased DNA damage accumulation, cell cycle arrest, and apoptosis in vitro, but also inhibited the growth of chordoma xenografts in vivo [28].

In the context of targeted therapy, Albarran et al. suggest that, beyond the several drugs that performed poorly as identified in this study, other tyrosine kinase receptors, including VEGFR, RET, MET, AXL, PDGFR, KIT, FGFR, and IGF-1R, hold therapeutic potential for chordoma treatment, particularly when used in conjunction with other systemic therapies [29]. Additionally, kinases such as AURA, CDK9, CDK4, and MOK are considered to have significant potential applications in targeted therapy [30, 31]. Al Shihabi et al. employed high-throughput screening utilizing organoid technology and identified the therapeutic potential of targeted drugs, including PI3K/mTOR, EGFR, and JAK2/STAT3 inhibitors, for chordoma [32]. Furthermore, Yang et al. demonstrated the therapeutic potential of targeting the Shh-Gli1 signaling pathway in chordoma treatment [33].

For patients with advanced chordoma, systemic treatments such as immunotherapy are essential to prevent distant metastasis and recurrence [34–37]. Previous studies have indicated that immunotherapies, including therapeutic cancer vaccines and immune checkpoint inhibitors, or their combinations, may serve as potential treatment options for chordoma [35, 38]. Furthermore, chordoma cells subjected to proton radiotherapy exhibit increased sensitivity to T-cell-mediated cytotoxicity [39]. However, it is noteworthy that all patients who received immunotherapy succumbed to the disease (Table S1). This outcome warrants further investigation. As previously documented, this patient cohort is often in a compromised health state [12], which negatively impacts treatment efficacy. Additionally, immunotherapy may induce adverse reactions [20, 21]. Moreover, a review has suggested that the combination of immunotherapy with other treatments is less effective than monotherapy, with PD-1/PD-L1 immune checkpoint inhibitor monotherapy being recommended for patients with advanced chordoma [40]. The potential application of natural killer (NK) cells or high-affinity NK (haNK) cells in conjunction with cetuximab for the treatment of chordoma, via antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC), has been suggested as a feasible therapeutic approach [41]. Furthermore, recent research underscores the importance of considering the tumor immune microenvironment in the context of chordoma immunotherapy [26, 42]. These findings offer valuable insights for the development of future treatment regimens for advanced chordoma.

It is important to underscore that due to the relative rarity of chordoma in clinical practice and the limitations of current diagnostic techniques, the sample size in this study is small. Consequently, the conclusions derived from this study are based solely on the 19 cases examined and require validation through further research involving a larger cohort of chordoma patients.

Chordoma, a distinct neoplasm originating from the axial skeleton, is typically classified as a low-grade tumor histologically derived from notochordal remnants. Despite this classification, chordomas exhibit aggressive growth patterns [43]. Due to limitations in current diagnostic technologies, chordomas are infrequently encountered in clinical settings, and standardized treatment protocols have yet to be established globally. PDC has recently been identified as a tumor with high malignancy and a poor prognosis. Critical factors influencing the clinical management of PDC include molecular pathological classification, tumor size, tumor location, local compression, and distant metastasis [2, 44]. The aggregation and analysis of additional PDC cases will contribute to a deeper understanding of the disease’s characteristics, as well as the development of diagnostic and therapeutic strategies and comprehensive management approaches.

Conclusions

Among the cohort of 19 patients examined, several exhibited partial improvement in both symptomatic presentation and imaging outcomes of chordoma following the administration of surgical intervention, chemotherapy, proton radiotherapy, and targeted therapy. Nonetheless, the efficacy of immunotherapy requires further investigation. It is imperative to validate the correlation between age and sex with the prognosis of chordoma in larger populations. The prognosis of chordoma appears to be unaffected by the lesion locations and degrees of differentiation, potentially due to the influence of treatment regimens. The variation in severity and baseline condition prior to treatment may account for the lack of significant impact of treatment regimens on prognosis.
