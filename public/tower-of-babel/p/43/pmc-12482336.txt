Investigation of potentially zoonotic Rickettsia species in dogs and their attached ticks in Malawi through the lens of One Health
Authors: Elisha Chatanga, Henson Kainga, John Kothowa, Michael Luwe, Richard Ssuna, Tinotenda Razemba, Laston Chimaliro, Naoki Hayashi, Yuki Ohsugi, Yongjin Qiu, Kyoko Hayashida, Nariaki Nonaka, Ryo Nakao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe genus Rickettsia in the order Rickettsiales (Alphaproteobacteria) consists of gram-negative obligate intracellular bacteria that infect a wide range of hosts. Epidemiological studies on the occurrence of Rickettsia spp. and their associated tick vectors are essential to understand their distribution, host range, and transmission mechanisms in nature. This is particularly relevant within the One Health framework, which emphasizes the interconnectedness of animal, human, and environmental health.MethodsTo investigate the presence of Rickettsia spp. in dogs and their ticks in Malawi, a molecular survey was conducted. A total of 209 dog blood and 259 tick samples of the species Haemaphysalis elliptica (n = 16) and Rhipicephalus linnaei (n = 243) were screened using real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR) targeting the citrate synthase (gltA) gene. Positive samples were further characterized via the conventional PCR and Sanger sequencing of gltA and the outer membrane protein A (ompA) genes.ResultsRickettsia DNA was not detected in any dog samples. However, the detection rates in R. linnaei and H. elliptica were 2.5 % (n = 6) and 6.3 % (n = 1), respectively. The obtained sequences showed 100 % identity with Rickettsia conorii subsp. conorii (n = 4), 99 %–100 % with Rickettsia massiliae (n = 2), and 100 % with Rickettsia rhipicephali (n = 1). Phylogenetic analysis clustered these sequences with the corresponding sequences of R. conorii subsp. conorii, R. massiliae, and R. rhipicephali reported from other countries in both gltA and ompA gene-based phylogenetic trees. The detection of R. massiliae and R. rhipicephali in southern Africa suggests expansion of the geographical distribution of these potentially zoonotic Rickettsia species.ConclusionThis is the first report of Rickettsia species detection in ticks collected from dogs in Malawi. The findings highlight the need for further surveillance, including humans and other animals, to better assess the public and veterinary health risks. Public engagement is needed to raise awareness on the role of dogs and their ticks in the transmission of Rickettsia within the One Health approach.Keywords: Dog, Haemaphysalis elliptica, Malawi, Rhipicephalus linnaei, Rickettsia conorii subsp. conorii, Rickettsia massiliae, Rickettsia rhipicephali

Introduction

Tick-borne rickettsioses are among the oldest known vector-borne zoonotic diseases caused by obligate intracellular bacteria belonging to the spotted fever group (SFG) [1]. The increase in the number of studies on tick-borne rickettsial pathogens by employing new diagnostic methods such as next-generation sequencing technologies has facilitated the simultaneous identification of novel and previously known rickettsiae in ticks [2]. A wide range of tick-borne rickettsiae that were previously considered nonpathogenic have recently been associated with human infections [3]. Further, novel Rickettsia spp. whose pathogenicity is not known are also being reported in or isolated from ticks worldwide [3]. There are five reported tick-borne zoonotic Rickettsia species that have been documented in southern Africa; Rickettsia africae (responsible for African tick bite fever), Rickettsia conorii subsp. conorii (responsible for Mediterranean spotted fever), Rickettsia aeschlimannii (responsible for innominate rickettsiosis), Rickettsia sibirica subsp. mongolitimonae (responsible for lymphangitis-associated rickettsiosis), and Rickettsia massiliae (responsible for mild spotted fever rickettsiosis) [4].

Rickettsiosis is one of the neglected zoonotic diseases on the African continent, although it is not included on the World Health Organization list of neglected tropical diseases [5]. An increase in tick bites and tick-borne rickettsioses among tourists and travelers visiting tourism resorts and wildlife reserves in Africa may negatively impact the continent's tourism industry [6]. Rickettsia pathogens have gained increasing attention as reflected by greater research collaboration, heightened clinical interest, and advances in diagnostic technologies [3]. However, little new data exist on antimicrobial therapy for tick-borne SFG rickettsioses. Over the past two decades, several publications have emerged, though progress remains limited partly due to the lack of tick exposure reports and the absence of pathognomonic signs, such as an inoculation eschar or characteristic skin rash, which are often overlooked due to low clinical suspicion. This lack of awareness may contribute to delays in initiating appropriate antimicrobial therapy [7], thereby affecting treatment regimen and outcomes [3]. This is corroborated by the finding that, although rickettsioses are endemic in sub-Saharan Africa, most diagnoses of human cases are made outside the continent, particularly among travelers returning to Europe and other non-endemic regions [6,8]. In regions where malaria is the most frequently considered cause of fever, the non-specific signs of rickettsiosis in the initial stages can complicate differential diagnosis and be misdiagnosed as malaria [9].

Malawi is located in the Southeastern Africa, sharing borders with Mozambique to the east, south, and southwest, Tanzania to the north and northeast, and Zambia to the west. No previous study has reported the occurrence of Rickettsia species in animals, humans, or their vectors in Malawi. However, in neighboring countries such as Mozambique, Tanzania, and Zambia, several studies have investigated rickettsial pathogens and infections in arthropod vectors, as well as animal and human hosts. In Mozambique, a study by Magaia et al. [10] showed that the prevalence of Rickettsia species ranged from 43 % to 100 % in ticks infesting domestic animals. A study conducted in Tanzania by Heinrich et al. [11] reported a seroprevalence of 43 %–91 % against SFG rickettsioses in human blood samples collected from an apparently healthy population. The prevalence of rickettsial infection in Amblyomma variegatum ticks in Zambia was 35.7 % [12]. Although human cases are rare in southern Africa, mainly due to limited diagnostic capacity, underreporting, underfunding, and unrecognition as a public health threat, cases have been documented in Botswana, Mozambique, South Africa, and Zimbabwe [13].

Dogs have been described as good sentinels for human diseases due to their unique characteristics and close association with humans [14]. High populations of free-roaming dogs with limited veterinary care in developing countries increase their exposure to vectors and pathogens [14]. In some countries, people sleep in the same house with their pet dogs, while others travel with them. This exposes humans to several pathogens and parasites that the dogs harbor [15]. There are reports of a strong correlation between the infection in domestic animals and the humans who keep them [14], supporting the One Health framework that emphasizes the interconnectedness of animal, human, and environmental health. Accordingly, pathogen monitoring in dogs may offer a valuable approach for anticipating zoonotic transmission risk to human populations. To address this knowledge gap, this study was undertaken to (1) detect Rickettsia species infecting dogs and their attached ticks, (2) identify the tick species infesting dogs, and (3) genetically characterize the detected Rickettsia species in Malawi.

Methodology

Sample collection

A total of 209 dog blood samples for DNA testing were obtained from a previous study [16]. In brief, sampling was done during free rabies and neutering campaigns by the Lilongwe Society for the Protection and Care of Animals and All Creatures Animal Welfare League from February 2018 to March 2019 in three major cities of Malawi: Blantyre (15.78° S, 35.00° E), Lilongwe (13.90° S, 33.79° E), and Mzuzu (11.47° S, 34.02° E). Some blood samples were collected in ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) vacutainer tubes (n = 163), while others were collected on Flinders Technology Associates (FTA) cards (n = 46). The blood samples in EDTA tubes were kept at −20 °C, while the FTA cards were kept at room temperature until required for DNA extraction.

Further, 259 ticks were collected from 115 of the 209 dogs during the same sampling period as the blood samples. The ticks were collected during physical examination of the dogs before vaccination or neutering by checking the body surface from the head to the tail, including the ears, axilla, groin, paws, and the tail. The observed ticks were picked using forceps or tick twisters. After collection, the ticks were preserved in 50 mL centrifuge tubes containing dry silica and kept at room temperature until required for identification and DNA extraction. The ticks collected from each dog were kept in one tube.

All the procedures were conducted in accordance with the guidelines of Hokkaido University, Japan and approved by the Animal Health Research Committee of the Department of Animal Health and Livestock Development (DAHLD) in the Ministry of Agriculture, Irrigation, and Water Development (MoAIWD), Malawi with a reference number of 10/15/32/D.

Tick species identification

Ticks were identified using morphological features under stereomicroscope using the identification keys described previously [[17], [18], [19]]. Further, the 16S ribosomal RNA gene (16S rDNA) was amplified in randomly selected ticks from each species to complement the morphological identification results, as mentioned below. In this study, we also aimed at identifying Rhipicephalus sanguineus sensu lato using molecular methods because of the two lineages that have been recently reclassified [20].

DNA extraction

The DNA from blood samples collected in EDTA tubes was extracted using the Quick Gene DNA whole blood kit S (DB-S) (Kurabo Industries Ltd., Osaka, Japan), while samples collected on Whatman FTA cards were done as per manufacturer's recommendations with minor modifications. In brief, a single 10.0 mm circle punch was made from the center of the blood spot using a sterile chisel into a sterile 1.5 mL tube. The chisel was sterilized between each sample using 70 % ethanol, followed by flaming under a Bunsen burner until red hot. Thereafter, it was also immersed in fresh 70 % ethanol to cool down followed by making three punches on fresh sterile filter paper before being used to make a new punch on FTA cards containing the blood sample. Thereafter, 400 μL of molecular grade water was used for DNA elution.

DNA extraction from ticks was done using DNAzol (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). We placed one tick sample in a sterilized 2 mL tube containing two stainless steel beads and washed the sample twice with 500 μL of distilled water. After adding 200 μL of DNAzol reagent into the tube containing the ticks, the ticks were crushed using Micro Smash MS-100R (TOMY, Tokyo, Japan) at 2500 rpm for 30 s. This was followed by centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 30 s, and incubated at room temperature overnight. Each tube was filled with 800 μL of DNAzol and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min. Then, 750 μL of the supernatant was transferred into a new tube. This was followed by adding 375 μL of 100 % ethanol into the tube and mixed. The mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was discarded and the DNA was washed twice using 75 % ethanol. Thereafter, the tube containing DNA was allowed to air dry at room temperature. After drying, 200 μL of 0.8 mol/L NaOH was added into the tube for DNA elution and incubated at 4 °C overnight. Finally, 2 μL of 1 mol/L HEPES was added to the elute. The extracted DNA was stored at −20 °C until required for use.

Preparation of plasmid standards

The DNA of Rickettsia asiatica strain Maytaro1284 [21] was used to generate a plasmid containing rickettsial citrate synthase (gltA) gene. PCR was carried out using the CS-F and CS-R primers (Table 1) and KOD-Plus Ver 2 DNA Polymerase (Toyobo, Kyoto, Japan). The amplified PCR product was A-tailed with 10 × A-attachment mix (Toyobo) and cloned into a pGEM-T vector (Promega, Madison, WI, USA), followed by transformation into the competent Escherichia coli DH5 alpha (Toyobo). The measurement of the plasmid DNA concentration and calculation of the corresponding copy numbers were done using Quant-iT dsDNA BR assay kit (Invitrogen) and Qubit Fluorometer (Invitrogen).

Real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR)

All samples were first screened for Rickettsia gltA gene using real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR). The primers and probe used are shown in Table 1. The reactions were conducted in a reaction mixture containing THUNDERBIRD Probe qPCR mix (Toyobo), 300 nmol/L of each primer, 200 nmol/L of probe, template DNA (or water as a negative control), and molecular grade water. A QuantStudio 12K Flex Thermal Cycler (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) was used to conduct the reaction. The reaction conditions were 50 °C for 3 min, 95 °C for 1 min, 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s, and 60 °C for 1 min. Each run included a negative control and serially diluted plasmid standards (106, 104, and 102 copies/reaction).

Conventional PCR

Molecular identification of ticks was performed on the selected samples including damaged ones to complement the morphological identification. The partial sequence of tick 16S rDNA was amplified by conventional PCR. To further characterize Rickettsia qPCR-positive samples, rickettsial gltA and outer membrane protein A (ompA) genes were amplified by semi-nested PCR and single PCR, respectively. All the primers used are shown in Table 1. The PCR reaction was carried out in a reaction mixture containing 2× Gflex PCR Buffer (Mg2+, dNTP plus) (TaKaRa Bio Inc., Shiga, Japan), Tks Gflex DNA Polymerase (TaKaRa Bio Inc.), 200 nmol/L of each primer, template DNA (or 1st PCR product for 2nd ompA PCR), and molecular grade water. The cycling conditions were set with initial denaturation at 94 °C for 1 min, followed by 45 (for tick 16S rDNA and ompA PCRs) or 30 (for 1st and 2nd gltA PCRs) cycles of denaturation at 98 °C for 10 s, annealing at 55 °C (for tick 16S rDNA PCR), 48 °C (for ompA and gltA 1st PCRs), or 54 °C (for gltA 2nd PCR) for 15 s, extension at 68 °C for 30 s, and final extension at 68 °C for 5 min. The amplicons were electrophoresed in 1.5 % agarose gel stained with Gel-Red (Biotium, Hayward, CA, USA) and visualized under UV light.

Sanger sequencing and sequence data analysis

The amplicons were purified using the NucleoSpin Gel and PCR Clean-up Kit (TaKaRa Bio Inc.). Sanger sequencing was conducted using the BigDye Terminator version 3.1 Cycle Sequencing Kit (Applied Biosystems) and an ABI Prism 3130xl genetic analyzer (Applied Biosystems) according to the manufacturers' instructions. The obtained sequence data were analyzed using GENETYX version 9.1 (GENETYX Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) and were trimmed on both the 5′ and 3′ ends to remove the primer annealing sites.

The obtained sequences were compared with those available in public databases using BLASTn at NCBI (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi). Tick 16S rDNA and the rickettsial gltA and ompA gene sequences obtained in this study and those obtained from the NCBI database were aligned with ClustalW in MEGA (version 7.0) [27]. DAMBE (version 7.0) software [28] was used for the tests of substitution saturation and confirmed that there is no saturation in the datasets. MEGA software was used to find the best-fit models for the phylogenetic analysis. GTR + G model was selected as the best model for tick 16S rDNA. T92 and T92+G were selected as the best-fit models for Rickettsia gltA and ompA genes, respectively. The maximum likelihood method was used to construct a phylogenetic tree for tick 16S rDNA, while the neighbor-joining method was employed for Rickettsia species due to the limited number of informative sites. Ornithodoros moubata (L34328), Rickettsia felis (MG818715 and JQ674484), and R. felis (DQ102710) were used as outgroups for the tick 16S rDNA, rickettsial gltA, and rickettsial ompA genes, respectively. Rickettsia felis was selected as an outgroup since previous studies have shown that the species is at the basal position of the SFG rickettsiae, including R. africae, R. conorii, Rickettsia japonica, and R. massiliae [29]. The sequences obtained in this study were submitted to the DNA Data Bank of Japan (http://www.ddbj.nig.ac.jp) under accession numbers LC799759 to LC799767 for tick 16S rDNA, LC865674 to LC865677 for rickettsial ompA gene, and LC865678 to LC865680 for rickettsial gltA gene.

Results

Tick species identification

A total of 259 ticks from two genera Haemaphysalis (n = 16, all females) and Rhipicephalus (n = 243, 167 males and 76 females) were identified in this study. In female Haemaphysalis elliptica, the following distinct morphological features were observed; punctations on the scutum were moderately dense, denser on lateral fields, absent in cervical grooves; medium-sized, discrete, relatively deep. Posterior lip of genital aperture was broadly U-shaped. The basis capituli was broader than long and dorsally subrectangular; cornua slightly bulging [19]. For R. sanguineus (s.l.), male ticks showed the distinct characteristic features of hexagonal basis capituli, distinct festoon with the central one protruding, sub-triangular adanal shields, the adanal shields, and plates present as shown in Fig. S1. Female R. sanguineus (s.l.), showed unornate scutum that was punctated, the basis capituli had hexagonal dorsal profile, broad U-shaped genital aperture, pronounced broad but shallow cervical grooves.

A selected subset of ticks identified morphologically, three H. elliptica (all females) and 10 R. sanguineus (s.l.) (five male and five female), were used for molecular identification. The Sanger sequencing analysis yielded two unique sequences for H. elliptica and seven unique sequences for R. sanguineus (s.l.). The phylogenetic analysis showed that two sequences of H. elliptica obtained in this study clustered together with H. elliptica reported from South Africa (Fig. 1). All seven sequences obtained from R. sanguineus (s.l.) ticks collected in Malawi clustered together with the tropical lineage of R. sanguineus (s.l.) (MF351591) reported from Colombia, which has been reclassified as Rhipicephalus linnaei [30]. These sequences were separated from R. sanguineus (s.l.) temperate lineages reported from USA (L34302), Afghanistan (KY111474), Turkmenistan (KY111473), and Spain (JX997393) (Fig. 1).

Maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree of Haemaphysalis elliptica and Rhipicephalus linnaei ticks collected from dogs in Malawi based on 16S rRNA gene sequences. The bootstrap values >0.75 were indicated as black dots. Ornithodoros moubata was used as an outgroup.

Real-time qPCR for Rickettsia in dogs and ticks

The gltA qPCR results showed that none of the dogs were positive for Rickettsia infection. A total of six R. linnaei (four males and two females) and one H. elliptica (female) were positive for Rickettsia spp. (Table 2). The estimated total copy numbers of rickettsial DNA in the tick samples ranged from 41 to 560,000 per reaction.

Characterization of rickettsiae

The sequences of the seven Rickettsia-positive samples, one H. elliptica and six R. linnaei, resulted in three and four unique sequences for gltA and ompA genes, respectively. By BLAST search analysis, three gltA sequences were 100.0 %, 100.0 %, and 99.7 % identical to R. conorii subsp. conorii strain Malish 7 (AE006914 and U59730), R. massiliae (CP000683, U59719), and Rickettsia rhipicephali (CP003342), respectively. Among the four unique sequences of ompA gene, two were 99.8 % identical to R. conorii (OP985642 and KT368818), one was 100.0 % identical to R. massiliae (OK205204, KR401145), and the remaining one was 100.0 % identical to R. rhipicephali (EU109175). Two samples, DT120 and DT238, were not successfully sequenced on gltA gene because of mixed signals, while one sample DT56 was negative on ompA gene PCR. The H. elliptica tick sample was positive for R. conorii subsp. conorii, while R. linnaei samples were positive for R. conorii subsp. conorii, R. massiliae, and R. rhipicephali (Table 3).

The evolutionary analysis and expansion of geographical distribution of Rickettsia species

To investigate the relationship with Rickettsia species reported from other countries, phylogenetic analysis including other sequences available in the NCBI database was conducted. The sequences obtained in Malawi clustered with corresponding sequences of R. conorii subsp. conorii, R. massiliae, and R. rhipicephali reported from other countries in both gltA and ompA gene-based phylogenetic trees (Fig. 2A and B, respectively). The first detection of R. massiliae and R. rhipicephali in southern Africa shows the expansion of the geographical distribution of zoonotic Rickettsia species.

Neighbor-joining phylogenetic trees of Rickettsia spp. detected in ticks based on gltA (A) and ompA (B) genes. The countries where the sequences were reported from have been indicated in parenthesis. The bootstrap values >0.75 were indicated as black dots. Rickettsia felis was used as an outgroup.

Discussion

This study reports that domestic dogs in Malawi are infested with the southern Africa yellow dog ticks, H. elliptica, and the brown dog tick tropical lineage, R. linnaei. Further, it has been found that the ticks attached to dogs were infected with Rickettsia species. However, no Rickettsia DNA was detected in any of the dogs examined in this study. This may reflect the absence of active bacteremia at the time of sampling, low rickettsial load in the blood, or that dogs are not the primary vertebrate hosts for these Rickettsia species in Malawi. The detection of Rickettsia DNA in ticks suggests that the bacteria may be maintained primarily within tick populations through transstadial and transovarial transmission. Despite the absence of detectable infection in dogs in Malawi, there are several studies from neighboring countries, such as Mozambique [31], Tanzania [32], and Zambia [33], reporting high infection rates of Rickettsia pathogens in ticks and dogs. These results may highlight the important role that dog-associated ticks may play in the maintenance, distribution, and transmission of rickettsial pathogens with zoonotic potential.

The correct identification of ticks is very important in the understanding of the phylogenetics, systematics, and vector competence of the pathogens that ticks harbor. The separation of the R. sanguineus (s.l.) into two clades representing the tropical and temperate lineages in the phylogenetic tree supports the current reclassification of the tropical lineage into R. linnaei [20] as being separate from the latter. Haemaphysalis elliptica was also identified as another tick infesting dogs in Malawi. This finding is not unexpected as other countries in the surrounding region have also reported its occurrence in domestic dogs [34,35]. The molecular identification of the randomly selected ticks to check the two lineages of R. sanguineus (s.l.) ticks confirmed the morphological identification of R. linnaei, indicating that these two techniques can complement each other and need to be used concurrently, particularly when resolving species within this complex.

In this study, we have detected Rickettsia species in H. elliptica and R. linnaei attached to dogs at a positive rate of 6.3 % and 2.5 %, respectively. The detection of Rickettsia infection in ticks attached to dogs from all the three regions of Malawi shows that rickettsial pathogens are distributed throughout the country. This finding is contrary to a previous study that looked into tick-borne infections in dogs and attached ticks in Malawi that did not detect rickettsial pathogens in all the samples examined [36]. This discrepancy may be due to the low sensitivity of the conventional PCR used previously when compared with the qPCR used in this study. The low Rickettsia DNA copy numbers detected in this study may also support these findings.

R. conorii subsp. conorii has been reported in the vector ticks R. linnaei [37], Rhipicephalus evertsi evertsi [3], Rhipicephalus simus [3], Rhipicephalus mushamae [3,37], Haemaphysalis punctaleachi [37], and Haemaphysalis leachi [3]. R. conorii subsp. caspia has also been reported in R. linnaei and Rhipicephalus pumilio [3]. The clinical disease in dogs is characterized by fever, intermittent vomiting, anorexia, and lethargy [38]. Human infection is characterized by fever, headache, myalgias, a maculopapular rash, and a single inoculation eschar at the site of the tick bite [13]. In southern Africa, human cases of Mediterranean spotted fever characterized by acute disseminated encephalomyelitis was reported in South Africa [39] and in a Japanese tourist returning from Zambia [40]. It has been documented that domestic dogs are the reservoir hosts for R. conorii subsp. conorii and R. conorii subsp. israelensis, and can act as the source of infection to ticks attached to the dogs [41]. The findings of these studies suggest that the infected ticks that are attached to dogs can transmit Rickettsia to the humans that they can also infest [7,37,41].

R. massiliae has been reported in sub-Saharan Africa in the vector ticks A. variegatum [37], Amblyomma marmoreum complex species [37], R. evertsi [3], Rhipicephalus senegalensis [3], Rhipicephalus guilhoni [3], R. linnaei [37], and Haemaphysalis paraleachi [3]. In humans, the disease is presented with a generalized rash with fever, myalgia, skin or scalp eschar, and neck lymphadenopathy [42]. The documented human cases have been reported in South America and Europe, but not in Africa. However, the bacterium has been detected or isolated in ticks in northern and western Africa [43,44]. H. elliptica is one of the ticks commonly reported to infest people in southern Africa [45], and it has been reported to harbor R. conorii subsp. conorii, and R. massiliae [4]. In this study, we detected R. conorii subsp. conorii in an H. elliptica, which is in accordance with the earlier study. The present study also reports the detection of R. massiliae in R. linnaei ticks in Malawi. These findings indicate both geographical expansion of the pathogen into southeastern Africa and extension of its host tick range.

R. rhipicephali has been reported in R. sanguineus (s.l.) in the Central African Republic [46]. There are no reports on human infections, suggesting that R. rhipicephali may be of no known public health significance. However, it has been reported to inhibit the colonization of tick ovaries by pathogenic Rickettsia species [47]. The detection of R. rhipicephali in Malawi also shows its wide geographical distribution.

There is a correlation between the serological detection of SFG rickettsial pathogens in humans, dogs, and ticks in endemic areas [48]. Hence, the diagnosis of Rickettsia in dogs has a public health significance, as it can aid in assessing the risk of human rickettsial infection, with dogs serving as sentinels for humans [49]. In southern Africa, increased time spent by dogs indoors with people has heightened household exposure to zoonotic pathogens, including Rickettsia species carried by ticks. These facts underscore the need for a One Health approach to clarify the public health significance of Rickettsia in under-reported countries such as Malawi.

Conclusion

This study provides the first molecular evidence of zoonotic Rickettsia spp. in ticks collected from dogs in Malawi. These findings expand the known geographical range of R. massiliae and R. rhipicephali in southern Africa and underscore the potential role of dog-associated ticks in the maintenance and transmission of tick-borne pathogens. The detection of these species, particularly those of public health relevance, demonstrates the need for integrated surveillance approaches targeting vectors, animals, and humans. Adopting a One Health strategy will be critical for understanding the epidemiology of rickettsial infections and assessing their true burden in Malawi. Future research should include human serosurveys, vector competence studies, and evaluations of awareness among healthcare providers to guide diagnostic and control strategies.
