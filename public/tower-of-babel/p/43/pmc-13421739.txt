Ocimum basilicum essential oil controls Haemaphysalis longicornis via multi-modal toxicity, enzyme inhibition, and synergy with hexythiazox
Authors: Zhihua Gao, Feidi Guo, Xuecheng Song, Xintong Liu, Pengxu Zhu, Haokun Feng, Chenxiao Lu, Zihan Liang, Xiaolong Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHaemaphysalis longicornis is an important ectoparasite infesting livestock, particularly cattle and sheep, causing significant economic losses and serving as a vector for multiple zoonotic pathogens. The widespread development of acaricide resistance necessitates the exploration of alternative control strategies with novel mechanisms of action.ResultsThe essential oil (EO) of Ocimum basilicum L. demonstrates potential as a multi-target botanical acaricide against the hard tick Haemaphysalis longicornis. GC-MS analysis identified eugenol (47.75%) as the major constituent. Bioassays revealed significant, dose-dependent contact toxicity against larvae (LC₅₀ = 18.47 mg/mL), nymphs (LC₅₀ = 34.93 mg/mL), and adults (LC₅₀ = 27.75 mg/mL). Purified eugenol exhibited 1.3–1.9-fold greater potency than the whole EO. The EO also showed strong fumigant activity and significantly suppressed fecundity by inhibiting oviposition and reducing egg hatchability. Mechanistic studies indicated that the acaricidal effect may be associated with the inhibition of key metabolic and neurological enzymes, including acetylcholinesterase (AChE), carboxylesterase (CarE), and glutathione S-transferase (GST). Furthermore, a 2:8 (hexythiazox: oil) formulation exhibited significant synergism against nymphs (Co-toxicity coefficient = 128.35), enabling a reduction in synthetic acaricide dosage.ConclusionsThis work elucidates a multi-modal action mechanism of O. basilicum EO against H. longicornis through contact toxicity, fumigation, reproductive suppression, and enzyme inhibition. The synergistic interaction with hexythiazox provides a sustainable, plant-based strategy for controlling tick infestations in livestock, showing potential for addressing the critical challenge of acaricide resistance in animal production systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-026-05711-5.Keywords: Livestock ectoparasite, Animal health, Tick control, Sustainable parasite management

Background

Tick-borne infectious diseases, due to their complex transmission chains and wide host adaptability, have posed a significant threat to global public health and animal husbandry. Haemaphysalis longicornis is an important blood-sucking ectoparasite that is widely distributed in East Asia. According to the latest report, the geographical distribution of H. longicornis has expanded from East Asia to North America [1]. With a broad host range, its bites can cause not only host anemia, emaciation, and reduced productivity but also transmit various zoonotic pathogens, including Anaplasma bovis, Borrelia burgdorferi, Bartonella henselae, and Coxiella burnetii [2]. Some viruses can persist long-term within ticks [3] and may even be transovarially transmitted [4], thereby facilitating disease spread and posing substantial risks to human health and livestock development. Global economic losses attributable to ticks reach 18 billion USD annually, with their impact on human health second only to mosquitoes [5]. The prolonged application of conventional chemical acaricides (e.g., pyrethroids and macrolides) has accelerated the development of tick resistance [6]. Current tick control strategies remain heavily reliant on chemical agents [7]. Therefore, developing safe, efficient, and environmentally sustainable control measures for H. longicornis carries significant economic, medical, and epidemiological importance and holds considerable practical value for preventing and managing tick-borne virus transmission.

In this context, plant-derived compounds, particularly EOs, present a compelling avenue for research. Ocimum basilicum L., a member of the Lamiaceae family, is an economically important herbaceous and shrubby plant with diverse biological applications. Characterized by its distinct chemotypes and aromatic fragrance, O. basilicum is widely utilized as a supplement in the perfume, pharmaceutical, food, aromatherapy, and cosmetics industries. The EO of O. basilicum exhibits potential antimicrobial, antifungal, antioxidant, antiviral, antiproliferative, anti-inflammatory, antispasmodic, and sedative properties [8]. In the field of pest control, plant EOs have gained significant research attention owing to their high safety profile, low propensity for resistance development, and biodegradable nature [9–11]. Previous studies have demonstrated that plant-derived EOs possess significant acaricidal activity against H. longicornis. For instance, essential oils from Ilex purpurea and Cinnamomum cassia have been reported to exhibit notable toxicity against this tick species [12, 13]. In addition to direct toxicity, plant EOs can interfere with insect reproductive physiology, as evidenced by their ability to significantly suppress oviposition and reduce egg hatching rates [14, 15]. Recent studies have shown that combining EOs with chemical agents can reduce the required dosage of synthetic acaricides and delay the development of resistance [16]. However, current research remains largely focused on single modes of action, and a systematic understanding of stage-specific synergistic mechanisms is still lacking.

To address this, the present study employs a multi-disciplinary approach integrating chemical analysis, classical bioassays, and biochemical profiling. This study specifically aims to characterize the chemical composition of O. basilicum EO via GC-MS, evaluate its multi-modal (contact, fumigant, reproductive inhibition) toxicity against H. longicornis across larval, nymphal, and adult stages. This study elucidated the biochemical mode of action by measuring its impact on key neuronal (acetylcholinesterase, AChE), metabolic (carboxylesterase, CarE; glutathione S-transferase, GST) enzymes, and investigated the potential for stage-specific synergy between the EO and the conventional acaricide hexythiazox. This work seeks to provide a foundational mechanistic framework for the rational development of novel, plant-enhanced acaricidal formulations for integrated tick management.

Methods

Essential oils and acaricides

The EO and eugenol were obtained from the Ji’an Huaxin Natural Plant Essential Oil Mall (China). O. basilicum EO (batch No. HX26060101, steam-distilled) was applied in this study. It passed full quality inspection and was stored sealed at room temperature, protected from light and moisture, within its 3-year shelf life. Hexythiazox and pyridaben were purchased from the Shanghai Ruifeng Chemical Co., Ltd. (China).

Ticks

Unfed adult H. longicornis were collected by flag dragging from vegetation in the Xiaowutai National Nature Reserve Area (39◦50′ to 40◦07′ N, 114◦47′ to 115◦30′ E) of Hebei Province, North China, during the spring season (April to May). The dominant vegetation included Hippophae rhamnoides, Ostryopsis davidiana, Corylus mandshurica, and Spiraea spp., among others. Ticks were pooled by collection date and site, and subsequently reared on New Zealand white rabbits in the laboratory for colony maintenance. The rabbits were obtained from a local farm. All animal procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of Hebei Normal University (Licence 2023LLSC040). All ticks were maintained under laboratory conditions (27 ± 1 °C, RH ≥ 80%). For the bioassays, unfed larvae, nymphs, and adults were used. Each tick was used only once in the experiments and was subsequently euthanized in an oven post-testing.

Phytochemical analysis

The chemical composition of O. basilicum EO was analyzed by gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) using an Agilent 5975 C system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, USA), and the total ion chromatogram (TIC) was obtained. The retention time of each peak was recorded. The volatile constituents were identified by comparing the mass spectrum and retention index (RI) of each peak with those in the NIST mass spectral library and relevant literature data. The relative content of each compound was calculated using the area normalization method.

The gas chromatography conditions were as follows: injection volume, 1 µL; split ratio, 10:1; injector temperature, 250 °C; and carrier gas flow rate, 1 mL/min. The temperature program was set as follows: initial temperature held at 80 °C for 2 min, then increased to 150 °C at a rate of 4 °C/min, further raised to 200 °C at 3 °C/min, subsequently ramped to 260 °C at 10 °C/min, and finally held at 260 °C for 15 min.

MS Conditions: Ionization: EI; Electron Energy: 70 eV; Ion Source Temperature: 230 °C; Interface Temperature: 260 °C; Quadrupole Temperature: 150 °C; Tuning: Standard Autotune; Scan Mode: Full Scan; Mass Range: 50–550 amu.

Larval Packet Test (LPT)

The contact toxicity of the EOs against larvae was evaluated using the larval packet test (LPT) as described by Stone and Haydock (1962) [17]. The EOs were diluted with 2% Tween 80. By setting a concentration gradient with equal difference (from 15% of the highest concentration to 0.0625% of the lowest concentration), the semi lethal concentration is screened out. The concentrations were delimited by referring to a large number of literature and conducting extensive preliminary experiments. For each concentration, approximately 500 larvae (counted individually) were enclosed in a filter paper packet (Whatman No. 1), with its edges sealed with clips. The packet was uniformly impregnated with 800 µL of the test solution. After 5 min of exposure, the larvae were transferred to a new, untreated filter paper packet, which was then sealed and placed in an incubator maintained at 27 ± 1 °C and ≥ 80% relative humidity. Mortality was assessed 24 h post-treatment under a stereomicroscope; larvae that were completely immobile were considered dead. Each concentration was tested in three independent replicates (each replicate containing 500 larvae), and a 2% Tween 80 solution served as the negative control.

Nymph and adult immersion test

The mortality of EOs against nymphs and adults was assessed using the adult immersion test (AIT) as described by Drummond et al. (1973) [18]. The EOs were diluted with 2% Tween 80. By setting a concentration gradient with equal difference (from 15% of the highest concentration to 0.0625% of the lowest concentration), the semi lethal concentration is screened out. The concentrations were delimited by referring to a large number of literature and conducting extensive preliminary experiments. Unfed nymphs and adults, which had molted two weeks prior, were selected for the experiment. For each concentration, A group of 50 ticks was immersed in 5 mL of the test solution within a 5 mL centrifuge tube for 5 min. Subsequently, the ticks were removed, briefly placed on filter paper to absorb excess liquid, and then transferred to a new, clean centrifuge tube. The tube was maintained in an incubator at 27 ± 1 °C and ≥ 80% relative humidity. Mortality was recorded 24 h post-treatment under a stereomicroscope; individuals showing no movement were considered dead. Each concentration was tested in three independent replicates (each replicate containing 50 nymphs or adults), and a 2% Tween 80 solution served as the negative control.

Fumigation test

The fumigant activity of the EOs against ticks was evaluated using a glass vial method. The EOs were dissolved in acetone and prepared as stock solutions at various concentrations. The concentration of essential oil in the fumigation assay was expressed as µg of EO per cm² of internal vial surface area (µg/cm²). By setting a concentration gradient with equal difference (from 10% of the highest concentration to 0.0625% of the lowest concentration), the semi lethal concentration is screened out. The concentrations were delimited by referring to a large number of literature and conducting extensive preliminary experiments. Briefly, the EO solutions was added to 20 mL glass vials. The vials were immediately covered with perforated aluminum foil and placed on a roller apparatus for 2 min to ensure uniform distribution of the solution on the inner surface. The vials were then left open under a fume hood for 15 min to allow complete evaporation of acetone, leaving a homogeneous residue of EO on the vial walls. Subsequently, a specified number of ticks (50 unfed nymphs or unfed adults, or 30 engorged females) were introduced into each vial, which was then resealed with the perforated foil lid. All vials were placed in an incubator maintained at 27 ± 1 °C and ≥ 80% relative humidity. Mortality was assessed 24 h after treatment. Individuals that showed no movement were recorded as dead. Each concentration was tested in three independent replicates, each containing 50 unfed nymphs (or 50 unfed adults, or 30 engorged females). A acetone solution was used as the negative control.

Assessment of oviposition and hatching in engorged females

Engorged female ticks that survived the fumigation bioassay were individually transferred to separate test tubes. These tubes were maintained in an incubator (27 ± 1 °C, RH ≥ 80%) until oviposition was complete. The total egg mass laid by each female was then recorded. The eggs were carefully collected into new test tubes and returned to the incubator to monitor hatching. The experiment included three replicates, with untreated engorged females serving as the control group.

Synergistic effect of EOs and acaricides

Treatment solutions were prepared by mixing the LC₅₀ concentration of the plant EO with the LC₅₀ concentration of hexythiazox at ratios of 10:0, 9:1, 8:2, 7:3, 6:4, 5:5, 4:6, 3:7, 2:8, 1:9, and 0:10. The actual mortality of unfed H. longicornis nymphs and adults was determined using the Nymph and adult immersion test mentioned above. The expected mortality was calculated to determine the toxicity ratio and identify the optimal mixing ratio between the EO and the acaricide. Enzyme activity assays

Unfed adults (30 per group) were exposed to the LC₅₀ concentrations of O. basilicum EO or eugenol for 24 h using the immersion method described above. After treatment, the ticks were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and ground to a fine powder. The powdered samples were homogenized with 0.1 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.2) at a ratio of 1:10 (w/v). The homogenates were then centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatants were collected, filtered, and kept on ice for subsequent enzyme activity measurements.

GST activity was determined using a Glutathione S-transferase Assay Kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China; Catalog No. BC0350) strictly according to the manufacturer‘s instructions. One unit (U) of GST activity was defined as the amount of enzyme catalyzing the formation of 1 µmol of CDNB-GSH conjugate per minute per gram of tissue protein at 37 °C. Absorbance values of the treatment and blank groups were measured at 340 nm at 10 s and 310 s after the reaction initiation. GST activity was calculated as follows: GST activity (U/g prot) = 0.38×[(A2 treated group - A1treated group)/(A2 blank group - A1 blank group) ] ÷ Sample mass×Dilution ratio.

CarE activity was determined using a Carboxylesterase Assay Kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China; Catalog No. BC0840) strictly in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. One unit (U) of CarE activity was defined as the amount of enzyme causing a 0.5 increase in absorbance per minute per mg of tissue protein in a 1 mL reaction system at 37 °C. Absorbance values of the treatment and blank groups were measured at 450 nm at 10 s and 310 s after the reaction initiation. CarE activity was calculated as follows: CarE activity (U/g prot) = 4 × [(A2 treated group - A1treated group)/(A2 blank group - A1 blank group)] ÷ Sample mass × Dilution ratio.

AChE activity was determined using an Acetylcholinesterase Assay Kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China; Catalog No. BC2020) strictly according to the manufacturer‘s instructions. One unit (U) of AChE activity was defined as the amount of enzyme catalyzing the formation of 1 µmol of TNB per minute per gram of tissue protein at 37 °C. Absorbance values of the treatment and control groups were measured at 412 nm at 10 s and 130 s after the reaction initiation. AChE activity was calculated as follows: AchE activity (U/g prot) = 2.225×(A treated group - A control group)÷ Sample mass×Dilution ratio.

CAT activity was determined using a Catalase Assay Kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China; Catalog No. BC0200) according to the manufacturer‘s instructions. One unit (U) of CAT activity was defined as the amount of enzyme catalyzing the degradation of 1 µmol of H₂O₂ per minute per mg of tissue protein in the reaction system. Absorbance values were measured at 240 nm at 0 s and 60 s after the reaction initiation. CAT activity was calculated as follows: CAT activity (U/g prot) = 678×(A treated group - A control group) ÷ Sample mass×Dilution ratio.

Each treatment was performed in three independent biological replicates. Ticks treated with 2% Tween 80 alone served as the control group.

Data analysis

The median lethal concentration (LC₅₀), 95% confidence intervals (95% CI), regression equation, and coefficient of determination (R²) for the experimental EOs were calculated using Polo Plus 1.0 software. Chi-square (χ²) values and degrees of freedom (df) were determined with SPSS Statistics. All figures were generated using GraphPad Prism version 8.0.2.

Contact toxicity

The acaricidal efficacy of O. basilicum EO against unfed larvae was evaluated using the larval packet test. The results demonstrated a significant lethal effect (p = 0.00041), with LC50 and LC90 values of 18.47 mg/mL and 81.68 mg/mL, respectively (Table 2). The toxicity of O. basilicum EO and eugenol against unfed nymphs and adults was assessed via the adult immersion test. Both substances exhibited marked acaricidal activity against unfed nymphs, with LC50 values of 34.93 mg/mL for the EO and 27.09 mg/mL for eugenol, and corresponding LC90 values of 71.47 mg/mL and 73.65 mg/mL (Table 2). Against unfed adults, the LC50 values were 27.75 mg/mL for the EO and 14.56 mg/mL for eugenol, with LC90 values of 69.94 mg/mL and 43.54 mg/mL, respectively (Table 2). According to LC50 data, eugenol exerted 1.3-fold higher activity against unfed nymphs and 1.9-fold higher activity against unfed adults compared with the whole O. basilicum EO. Overall, eugenol exhibited 1.3–1.9-fold greater potency than the whole EO. Mortality rates for H. longicornis increased progressively with higher concentrations of the EO (Figs. 1 and 2). At equivalent concentrations, eugenol demonstrated greater toxicity than the whole EOs against both unfed nymphs and adults (Fig. 2).

Fumigant toxicity

The fumigant efficacy of O. basilicum EO against H. longicornis nymphs and adults was evaluated (Table 3). The experiments demonstrated significant fumigant activity against nymphs. The LC₅₀ values for unfed and engorged nymphs were 26.92 µg/cm² and 20.58 µg/cm², respectively, with corresponding LC₉₀ values of 71.92 µg/cm² and 34.11 µg/cm². Similarly, the EO exhibited notable fumigant toxicity against adult ticks. The LC₅₀ values for unfed and engorged adults were 25.58 µg/cm² and 73.22 µg/cm², respectively, while the LC₉₀ values were 57.71 µg/cm² and 353.79 µg/cm². As the EO concentration increased, a progressive rise in mortality was observed in H. longicornis. Comparative analysis indicated that engorged nymphs were the most sensitive stage among the four tested, whereas engorged adults were the least sensitive (Fig. 3).

Effects on tick reproduction

The impact of O. basilicum EO on the reproductive function of engorged H. longicornis females was investigated using the fumigation bioassay. The results indicate that the EO effectively disrupts the tick life cycle by reducing oviposition and hatchability, thereby suppressing tick reproduction and population transmission. The inhibition of oviposition increased progressively with higher concentrations of EO, while the egg hatching rate correspondingly declined. At concentrations of 48.08, 96.15, and 192.31 µg/cm², the oviposition inhibition rates were 62.14%, 76.83%, and 100%, respectively. The hatching rates of eggs laid by treated females at these same concentrations were 52.00%, 20.50%, and 0%, respectively (Table 4).

Synergistic effects of O. basilicum EO and acaricides

In the contact toxicity assays, both hexythiazox and pyridaben demonstrated significant acaricidal activity. The LC₅₀ values of hexythiazox against unfed nymphs and adults were 13.40 mg/mL and 8.35 mg/mL, respectively, with corresponding LC₉₀ values of 73.91 mg/mL and 18.28 mg/mL (Table 5). Pyridaben exhibited LC₅₀ values of 4.00 mg/mL and 0.64 mg/mL against unfed nymphs and adults, respectively, and LC₉₀ values of 9.59 mg/mL and 4.35 mg/mL (Table 5).

This study revealed that mixing the EO with hexythiazox enhanced the acaricidal effect. Screening of different mixing ratios showed that the 2:8 (hexythiazox : O. basilicum EO) ratio yielded a toxicity ratio of 1.21 against unfed nymphs, indicating a synergistic effect (Table 6). The LC₅₀ of this 2:8 mixture against unfed nymphs was 10.44 mg/mL, which is significantly lower than that of the EO alone (34.93 mg/mL) (p = 0.0024), with a co-toxicity coefficient (CTC) of 128.35 (Table 7). The lethal efficacy of the 2:8 mixture against unfed nymphs was also markedly higher than that of hexythiazox alone (LC₅₀ = 13.40 mg/mL). For unfed adults, the 1:9 (hexythiazox : O. basilicum EO) ratio showed a toxicity ratio of 2.18, suggesting a potential synergistic interaction (Table 8). The LC₅₀ of the 1:9 mixture against unfed adults was 35.08 mg/mL, which was not significantly different from that of the EO alone (27.75 mg/mL) (p = 0.2984), consistent with the antagonistic CTC value (38.20) (Table 9).

The effects of O. basilicum EO and eugenol on the activities of GST, CAT, AChE, and CarE in H. longicornis were investigated after a 24-hour exposure at their respective LC₅₀ concentrations. Discussion

This study systematically evaluated the acaricidal activity and mechanism of action of O. basilicum EO and its primary component, eugenol, against H. longicornis, and further investigated their synergistic effects with chemical acaricides. The results demonstrate that O. basilicum EO exhibits significant contact toxicity, fumigant activity, and reproductive inhibition. Furthermore, it exerts its acaricidal effect by disrupting key enzymatic activities within the tick, thereby providing a scientific basis for the development of botanical acaricides.

The EOs from Lamiaceae plants often possess insecticidal properties. For instance, volatiles from Mentha species exhibit various effects against multiple pests. These EOs are utilized for fumigation, suppression of pest oviposition, interference with egg hatching, inhibition of insect development, or as repellents. The volatiles and EOs from the genus Ocimum play a significant role in agriculture and pest management. GC-MS analysis in this study revealed that O. basilicum EO primarily contains active components such as eugenol (47.75%) and isopropyl palmitate (40.12%). Isopropyl palmitate is a synthetic skin softening emollient, so all subsequent bioassays in this study focused on eugenol, the native bioactive constituent naturally produced by basil plants. Contact toxicity assays demonstrated significant efficacy against ticks at different developmental stages, with LC₅₀ values of 18.47 mg/mL for larvae, 34.93 mg/mL for nymphs, and 27.75 mg/mL for adults. Notably, purified eugenol exhibited 1.3–1.9 times higher toxicity than the whole O. basilicum EO, suggesting potential synergistic or antagonistic interactions among other constituents in the crude oil. Some studies indicate that different components within an EO may contribute diversely to its bioactivity. For example, the major component of Ilex purpurea EO showed significantly higher toxicity than the EO itself [13], whereas Cinnamomum cassia EO demonstrated greater biological activity than its individual constituent, trans-cinnamaldehyde [16]. This highlights the importance of investigating interactions among components in EOs for pest control applications. Research suggests that eugenol may exert its insecticidal effects by interfering with physiological processes in insect eggs, such as respiration, nutrient uptake, or the regulation of developmental hormones, as observed in Tribolium castaneum [19]. A deeper understanding of eugenol’s mechanism of action helps elucidate its pathways within organisms and provides valuable insights for future insecticide development.

This study demonstrated that O. basilicum EO exhibits a dual “lethal+reproductive inhibition” effect against H. longicornis in fumigation assays, substantially enhancing its control efficacy. The remarkable fumigation activity suggests its suitability as a fumigant acaricide against this tick species. Beyond its fumigant action, EOs and volatiles from Ocimum species possess multiple modes of action. Suppressing pest oviposition reduces population growth, while disrupting egg hatching effectively impedes reproductive cycles, thereby minimizing crop damage [20]. These results showed pronounced oviposition deterrence in engorged female ticks, with 76.83% oviposition inhibition and only 20.50% egg hatching rate. Plant EOs play pivotal roles in pest management by modulating insect sensory receptors, leading to feeding deterrence that ultimately disrupts development and life cycles. This mechanism holds significant implications for crop protection by potentially reducing synthetic pesticide usage, mitigating environmental contamination, and preserving ecological balance [21]. For instance, 1,8-cineole in eucalyptus oil inhibits feeding in Aedes aegypti adults by interacting with mosquito sensory receptors, triggering food rejection behavior and compromising normal feeding patterns [22].

AChE, CarE and GST are crucial detoxification enzymes in living organisms [23, 24]. These enzymes function cooperatively to mitigate the effects of various toxic substances and maintain internal homeostasis. This study found that O. basilicum EOs and its primary active components significantly inhibited the activities of AChE, CarE, and GST. This observation aligns with previous reports on other EOs [16, 25–27]. These findings confirm that AChE, CarE, and GST are key detoxification enzymes in H. longicornis and are involved in the metabolic response to EOs. Given that detoxification enzymes and related metabolic mechanisms contribute to insecticide resistance in pests, combining plant extracts with synthetic insecticides has been proposed as a potential resistance management strategy. This integrated approach could also reduce environmental toxic load over the long term [28, 29]. Reports indicate that toxic compounds present in plant extracts can disrupt specific targets beyond nicotinic acetylcholine receptors and vanilloid-type transient receptor potential channels in insects [30]. Furthermore, these compounds interfere with insect cytochrome P-450, glutathione S-transferase, and esterase activities [31, 32].

The presence of multiple active compounds in plant extracts and their synergistic interactions with chemical insecticides are key factors in evaluating insecticide-plant extract combinations [28]. Previous studies have reported similar synergistic phenomena: moringa root extract enhanced the efficacy of flupyradifurone against Bemisia tabaci [33], synthetic coconut oil nanoliposomes showed synergy with cyfluthrin in controlling Ostrinia furnacalis [34], and salicylic acid, a major plant-derived component, exhibited synergistic effects with flonicamid and diafenthiuron against whiteflies [29]. Based on our findings regarding the impact of O. basilicum EO on detoxification enzymes in H. longicornis, we hypothesize that the interference of O. basilicum EO with the activities of AChE, CarE, and GST may represent an underlying mechanism for the observed synergy.

While the LC₅₀ values of O. basilicum EO are higher than those of many commercial synthetic acaricides, several considerations should be taken into account when evaluating its practical applicability. First, studies have shown pests are not easily resistant to plant EOs, and they are less harmful to non-target organisms (26, 27). Second, the EOs exhibits a dual “lethal+reproductive inhibition” effect against H. longicornis, substantially enhancing its control efficacy. Third, the primary value of EO lies not in its standalone toxicity but in its role as a synergist, as demonstrated by the strong synergism with hexythiazox (CTC = 128.35) that enables significant dosage reduction. Finally, the formulation technologies such as microencapsulation and nanoemulsions could enhance stability and bioavailability under field conditions.

Conclusions

This study confirms that O. basilicum EO can effectively control H. longicornis through multiple pathways, including contact toxicity, fumigation, and enzyme inhibition, while its combination with chemical acaricides demonstrates stage-dependent synergistic effects. Future research should focus on: structural optimization of active constituents to enhance efficacy against adult ticks; development of formulated mixtures that balance synergistic potency with environmental safety; and field validation to facilitate the practical application of plant-based acaricides. In summary, O. basilicum EO, as a low-cost and readily biodegradable control option, shows significant potential for use in integrated tick management programs and may contribute to delaying the development of acaricide resistance, while supporting ecological conservation.
