Locoregional recurrence in carcinoma of the ophthalmic sites: Primary cancer and secondary invasion from adjacent regions
Authors: Aniwat Berpan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:To evaluate locoregional recurrence in patients treated with local treatment.Methods:This was an institutional-based retrospective observational cohort study including 36 patients with ophthalmic plus nasal cavity (NC) and paranasal sinus carcinoma invading ophthalmic structures. Patients underwent treatment with surgery and radiotherapy. The primary outcome measure was the frequency and pattern of treatment failure.Results:Seventeen patients with ophthalmic cancer were identified with a median follow-up of 3.5 years. Most originated from the eyelid (64.7%), followed by the globe and lacrimal gland, each at 17.6%. Sebaceous carcinoma was found the most (35.3%), followed by squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) (29.4%). 23.5% had nodal involvement, precisely within the parotid group. All patients underwent surgery, including exenteration (n = 6). Local recurrence (LR) developed in two patients, both with SCC who underwent exenteration without postoperative radiotherapy (PORT). Regional recurrence (RR) occurred at 17.6%. None had LR and RR together. Nineteen patients with NC and paranasal sinus cancer were identified, including cancer of maxillary sinus (n = 9), ethmoid sinus (n = 7), and NC (n = 6), with a median follow-up of 8.4 months. SCC was found in 73.7%. None had parotid metastasis. Of five patients who underwent surgery, three patients required exenteration. Orbital recurrence was found in two out of five patients who developed LR, both with SCC. One underwent exenteration without PORT (the other two received PORT). The other received definitive radiotherapy. RR occurred in three patients.Conclusions:Patients requiring exenteration probably need PORT to minimize LR.

Ophthalmic carcinoma is a rare malignancy primarily managed through surgical intervention.[12] Radiotherapy serves as a definitive treatment when surgery is not feasible or as postoperative radiotherapy (PORT) for patients with high-risk factors for recurrence.[3456] Due to their proximity, cancers of the nasal cavity (NC) and paranasal sinus (PNS) frequently invade ophthalmic structures. Orbital exenteration may be necessary to achieve optimal disease control and improve survival outcomes, albeit at the cost of blindness and significant disfigurement.[7] The present study aimed to evaluate locoregional recurrence in patients with carcinoma of the ophthalmic sites, and NC and PNS accompanied by ophthalmic involvement.

Methods

Patients

Data from patients with carcinoma of the ophthalmic sites, together with NC and PNS involving ophthalmic structures who were treated between January 2008 to December 2023, were retrospectively reviewed. Inclusion criteria were patients undergoing curative local treatment including surgery or radiotherapy. Chart review was performed in order to collect patient information. When survival details were not sufficiently reported, death certificates were obtained. Patients with metastatic disease, sarcoma, malignant melanoma, or hematologic malignancies were excluded because metastatic lesions may alter locoregional recurrence patterns, and it is often impossible to distinguish whether local or lymph node failure originates from the primary tumor or from metastatic disease. In addition, sarcoma, malignant melanoma, and hematologic malignancies have distinct cellular origins, resulting in different patterns of treatment failure and therapeutic approaches. Individuals with a prior history of head and neck cancer or incomplete data were also excluded. This study was approved by the institutional review board.

Procedures

After diagnosis of carcinoma of the ophthalmic sites, patients were generally advised to undergo surgery with the type of operation determined by disease extent. If patients declined surgery, radiotherapy would be provided as an alternative. PORT was performed in cases with closed or positive surgical margins. Concurrent cisplatin was occasionally administered for cases with microscopic or gross disease.

For patients with NC or PNS cancers, resectability and operability were assessed first. Surgery would be performed if patients were suitable for resection, with the type of surgery mainly specified by the extent of tumor spread. Patients deemed unsuitable for surgery were evaluated by radiation and medical oncologists for either definitive radiation with or without concurrent chemotherapy, or preoperative chemotherapy. If surgery remained infeasible following chemotherapy, definitive radiotherapy was delivered. PORT with or without concurrent chemotherapy was usually provided in case of positive surgical margin, extranodal extension, T3-4 disease, multiple lymphadenopathy, perineural invasion, or lymphovascular invasion. Concurrent chemoradiation was routinely given for gross disease, surgical margin involvement, or extranodal extension. Chemotherapy is always a platinum-based regimen, with single-agent regimens normally used during concurrent chemoradiotherapy, while doublet regimens were reserved for neoadjuvant chemotherapy. Platinum-based chemotherapy with fluorouracil was mainly prescribed, followed by platinum-based chemotherapy with paclitaxel.

For radiotherapy, the technique and dose depended on the availability of machines at the time of treatment, the patient’s performance status, disease characteristics, and the preference of the radiation oncologist. Electrons and megavoltage (MV) photons were used. Radiation techniques were composed of two-dimensional (2D), three-dimensional conformal (3DCRT), and intensity-modulated radiation (IMRT). The radiation dose for R0-1 resection ranged from 50 to 66 Gy in 25 to 33 fractions, while 66 to 70 Gy in 33 to 35 fractions was typically given in R2 resection or definitive radiotherapy.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as mean with standard deviation (SD) or median with range, depending on the data distribution. Categorical variables were presented as number and percentage. Disease-free interval (DFI) was calculated from the initiation of the first treatment to the occurrence of any recurrence. OS was calculated from the initiation of cancer treatment to death from any cause. Patients without any events were censored at the date of their last follow-up. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan–Meier method. Differences between survival curves were analyzed by the log-rank test. The impact of prognostic factors on survival outcomes was examined using the Cox regression model. Statistical significance was set at P value ≤ 0.05. PASW Statistics (SPSS) 28.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL., USA) was used for the analysis.

Results

A total of 35 patients with carcinoma of the ophthalmic sites were assessed for eligibility; 18 were excluded (2 for diagnosis of sarcoma, 5 for malignant melanoma, 7 for lymphoma, 3 for incomplete data, and 1 for no cancer treatment given). Therefore, 17 patients met the inclusion criteria. The mean age of the included patients was 59 years (SD 12) with 21.8% having an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status score of at least 1, indicating that they were not fully active and were unable to carry on all predisease performance without restriction. The eyelid was the most common primary tumor location (64.7%), followed by the globe and lacrimal gland, each at 17.6%. Sebaceous carcinoma was the most frequently diagnosed histology (n = 6), followed by squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) (n = 5) and adenoid cystic carcinoma (ACC) (n = 2). The rest were eyelid Merkel cell carcinoma, ocular malignant teratoid medulloepithelioma, lacrimal mucoepidermoid carcinoma, and lacrimal polymorphous adenocarcinoma. Tumor staging revealed equal frequencies of T1 and T4 (41.2%). Four patients presented with nodal involvement, specifically within the parotid group, including those with eyelid sebaceous carcinoma, conjunctival SCC, ocular malignant teratoid medulloepithelioma, and lacrimal adenoid cystic carcinoma. All patients underwent surgery, with orbital exenteration performed in six cases. One received salvage exenteration on account of stable disease after definitive radiotherapy, while the rest underwent definitive surgical procedures. Three patients underwent parotidectomy, two of whom also had cervical lymph node dissection. Radiotherapy was administered in seven patients with postoperative and definitive radiotherapy performed in six and one patients, respectively. Four patients received nodal irradiation. The median follow-up time was 3.5 years. Orbital recurrence developed in two patients. DFIs ranged from 1.2 to 25.5 months. The 10-year orbital recurrence rate was 18.9%. Both patients (one with eyelid SCC and one with conjunctival SCC) underwent exenteration without PORT. Notably, two patients who underwent exenteration without PORT and did not experience orbital recurrence were diagnosed with eyelid sebaceous carcinoma. Regional recurrence (RR) occurred in three patients, with DFIs ranging from 0.5 to 12.1 months, yielding a 10-year RR rate of 18.9%. One patient with malignant teratoid medulloepithelioma experienced ipsilateral supraclavicular lymph node recurrence despite receiving parotidectomy and neck node dissection, followed by radiotherapy for parotid lymph nodal metastases. The other two patients had no regional involvement at baseline; therefore, neither lymphadenectomy nor nodal irradiation was performed. Recurrence in cervical lymph node level Ib and II occurred in a patient with eyelid sebaceous carcinoma, while cervical lymph node level Ib and II, and intraparotid lymph node recurrence arose in a patient with eyelid SCC. None of the patients developed simultaneous local and regional recurrences, and no distant metastasis (DM) were reported as the first site of failure.

Out of 130 patients with NC and PNS cancer evaluated for eligibility, 19 met the inclusion criteria. A total of 111 patients were excluded due to reasons including lack of cancer treatment (n = 10), metastatic stage at diagnosis (n = 5), pathology of sarcoma (n = 2) and malignant melanoma (n = 1), incomplete data (n = 29), and absence of ophthalmic involvement (n = 64). The mean age of the eligible cohort was 57 years (SD 16) with about 12% having an ECOG performance status score greater than 0. Primary tumor locations included the NC (26.3%), maxillary sinus (36.8%), and ethmoid sinuses (36.8%). The most frequent histopathological subtype was SCC, observed in 14 patients. Other subtypes included ACC, sinonasal undifferentiated carcinoma (SNUC), small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (SNEC), SMARCB1-deficient carcinoma, and large cell carcinoma. Surgery was performed in five patients, including orbital exenteration in three cases. One of these patients underwent a modified radical neck dissection, while another received orbital exenteration without concurrent surgery on the NC and PNS, or neck node dissection. Baseline nodal involvement was present in three patients, but none had intraparotid nodal metastasis. Radiotherapy was delivered to 17 patients with postoperative and definitive radiotherapy in 4 and 13 patients, respectively. Nodal irradiation was performed in 7 patients. Table 1 summarizes the clinical and treatment characteristics of eligible patients with carcinoma of the ophthalmic sites, and NC and PNS. With a median follow-up of 8.4 months, residual disease was identified in 8 patients, leaving 11 patients evaluable for disease failure. Recurrence at orbit was found in 2 out of 5 patients who developed local recurrence. The DFIs were 3.5–15.8 months. The 2-year orbital recurrence was 33.3%. Both patients had ethmoid SCC. One underwent orbital exenteration without PORT, while the other received definitive radiotherapy. The two patients who underwent exenteration with PORT did not develop orbital recurrence. RR occurred in three patients with DFIs of 2.8–8.9 months, corresponding to a 2-year RR rate of 28%. Neck node dissection and nodal irradiation were performed in none of them. One patient experienced local recurrence without orbital recurrence. No cases of intraparotid recurrence or DM as the first site of failure were reported.

In the multivariable analysis, carcinoma of the ophthalmic sites (compared to carcinoma of the NC and PNS), and radiotherapy (HR 0.44; 95% CI 0.19–0.99 and HR 0.06; 95% CI 0.004–0.81, respectively) were significantly associated with OS [Table 2]. Patients with cancer of the NC and PNS had a median OS of 1.6 years, while those with cancer of the ophthalmic sites had a median OS of 4.7 years (P value < 0.001). The 5-year OS rates were 12.8% and 83.6%, respectively [Fig. 1]. Among all patients, those who received radiotherapy had a median OS of 4.7 years, compared to 6.3 years for those who did not receive radiotherapy (P = 0.43), corresponding to 5-year OS rates of 41.8% and 61.1% [Fig. 2]. Discussion

The present study demonstrated that orbital recurrence could develop in both patients with cancer of the ophthalmic sites, and NC and PNS involving ophthalmic structures. However, the incidence was relatively low, at 11.8% in ophthalmic cancers and 18.2% in NC and PNS cancers. Similarly, RR seldom occurred, observed in 17.6% and 27.3% of cases, respectively. Independent favorable factors included primary tumor location as ophthalmic sites and radiotherapy.

Indications of orbital exenteration comprise tumors involving the orbital apex, periorbita, retrobulbar fat, extraocular muscles, conjunctiva, or sclera, together with orbital invasion of malignancy, eyelid skin cancer with conjunctival involvement, or tumor in close proximity to the orbital contents necessitating adequate surgical margins.[7] Exenteration is generally indicative of advanced disease. In the present study cohort, six patients with ophthalmic cancer and three patients with NC and PNS cancers underwent definitive exenteration, with four patients receiving PORT. Among the four patients who developed orbital recurrence, all were diagnosed with SCC and three had received exenteration without PORT. These findings imply that patients who underwent exenteration, especially those with SCC, whether originating from ophthalmic sites or as a result of direct invasion from PNS, might benefit from PORT in minimizing the risk of orbital recurrence.[36]

Parotid lymph node metastases were detected in all patients with regional involvement due to carcinoma of ophthalmic sites. This finding aligns with a study by Nijhawan et al., which evaluated lymphatic drainage patterns of the eyelids using lymphoscintigraphy. The study identified the preauricular lymph node as the first-order sentinel node in 72% of cases, regardless of location on the eyelid. The submandibular and deep cervical groups were defined as the first-order sentinel node in the remaining cases.[8] Similarly, Soysal and Markoç assessed the clinical features of eyelid SCC and found regional lymph node metastases in 5 of 76 patients, all within the preauricular region. Cervical lymph node metastasis occurred later in one patient.[1] In another study by Faustina et al., involving 27 patients who had nodal metastases (either initially or at recurrence), the affected lymph node basins included the parotid/preauricular in 12 patients, submandibular/submental in 9 patients, submaxillary in 3 patients, cervical in 3 patients, and subdigastric in 2 patients. Two patients exhibited metastasis in multiple regional nodal basins.[2] In contrast, ophthalmic involvement, which may alter nodal failure patterns in NC and PNS cancers, demonstrated a distribution distinct from that of primary ophthalmic carcinoma, particularly with respect to intraparotid lymph node metastasis. This suggests that prophylactic parotidectomy or elective parotid irradiation is not necessary for these patients. Avoiding these treatments is particularly beneficial as both can negatively impact quality of life due to treatment-related xerostomia.

Patients with cancer of the NC and PNS with ophthalmic involvement had worse OS compared to those with ophthalmic cancers. This inequality is attributed to NC and PNS cancer patients being classified as T4 under the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging system, whereas only approximately 41% of ophthalmic cancer patients were categorized as T4. Radiotherapy also emerged as an independent favorable factor for OS. In several studies, radiotherapy for ophthalmic cancer has been described as definitive and postoperative treatments. Most patients in those studies received superficial orthovoltage or kilovoltage (kV) x-rays, or electrons to the ophthalmic regions. On the other hand, in the present study, electrons were delivered to only one patient, while the rest were irradiated with MV photons. Petsuksiri and colleagues evaluated outcomes for patients with eyelid SCC treated with either definitive radiotherapy or PORT following wide excision. Either superficial X-rays or electrons were applied during radiation in most patients. The median tumor size was 1.4 cm (range, 0.3–4 cm) and 5% had initial nodal involvement. With a median follow-up of 76 months, the 5-year local control rates for definitive and postoperative radiotherapy were 89% and 86%, respectively. The regional control rates at 5 years were 93% and 100%, respectively. The 5-year OS rate was 71% for all patients.[3] PORT for patients with cancers of the eyelid and conjunctiva was studied by Hsu et al., excluding those who underwent orbital exenteration. Most patients were irradiated with either electrons or kV photons. After a median follow-up of 21 months, no patients experienced local or regional recurrence.[4] Hata and colleagues investigated outcomes for patients with eyelid sebaceous carcinoma treated with curative radiotherapy, primarily using electrons. Upfront surgery was done in 61.5% of cases, and the median tumor size of 1.3 cm (range, 0.3–3 cm) was recorded. At a median follow-up of 55 months, the 5-year local progression-free survival and OS rates were both 100%.[5] The results of radical radiotherapy using electrons in patients with eyelid SCC were studied by Inaba et al. In this study, 39.5% had tumors larger than 2 cm, invasion of adjacent ocular or orbital structures, or perineural invasion. Additionally, 55.3% had a tumor diameter exceeding 1.5 cm, and 10.5% had pretreatment regional involvement. Nevertheless, no patient required exenteration. The 5-year local relapse-free, nodal relapse-free, and OS rates were 71.8%, 77.5%, and 79.5%, respectively.[6] The outcomes of the present study were comparable to the previous findings, with no local recurrence or death reported, and only one patient experiencing regional recurrence during the follow-up period.

Conclusion

Ophthalmic and regional recurrences were infrequent among patients with carcinoma of the ophthalmic sites, and NC and PNS involving ophthalmic structures. However, when they occurred, these recurrences were associated with significant morbidity and mortality. Ophthalmic recurrence was more commonly observed in patients who underwent orbital exenteration without PORT, suggesting that radiation might enhance local control in these cases. Besides, patients who received radiotherapy demonstrated improved OS, compared to those who did not. Notwithstanding, local recurrence in ophthalmic cancer patients in the present study was confined to the orbit; the potential routes of tumor spread, as outlined in Table S2, could serve as a valuable guide for determining the extent of surgical resection and delineating target volume in radiotherapy planning.
