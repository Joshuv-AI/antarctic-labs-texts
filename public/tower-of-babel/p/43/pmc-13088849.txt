Association between estimated glucose disposal rate and heart disease events in adults with overweight or obesity: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Yingxiu Huang, Guosong Jiang, Ting Ao, Ming Hu, Peng Zhen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRecent research indicates that insulin resistance (IR) plays a role in the progression of cardiovascular diseases (CVD), with the estimated glucose disposal rate (eGDR) recognized as a dependable proxy for measuring IR. Nonetheless, there is a scarcity of studies concentrating on overweight and obese populations. Consequently, this study aims to explore the correlation between eGDR and the risk of developing CVD among individuals who are overweight or obese.MethodsIn the cohort study, participants were selected from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), encompassing those without a history of heart disease and possessing complete baseline data on the estimated glucose disposal rate (eGDR). Participants were stratified into three groups based on the tertiles (T) of eGDR values. Cox proportional hazards models were used to evaluate the association between eGDR and the incidence of heart disease. Furthermore, a multivariate adjusted restricted cubic spline (RCS) model was utilized to assess the linearity of the association. Robustness of the results was examined through subgroup and sensitivity analyses.ResultsA cohort of 2,217 participants were enrolled, with a mean age of 56.9 ± 8.0 years, including 839 males (37.8%). Throughout a follow-up duration of up to 9 years, 600 participants (27.1%) developed heart disease events. After controlling for all covariates (Model 4), the association between eGDR and incidence heart disease persisted as significant (adjusted HR: 0.90; 95% CI: 0.86–0.94; P < 0.001). When analyzed as a categorical variable, eGDR tertiles 2 and 3 demonstrated a significant reduction in the risk of heart disease compared to tertile 1, with adjusted HRs of 0.76 (95% CI: 0.62–0.92) and 0.56 (95% CI: 0.44–0.71), respectively, in Model 4. RCS analysis indicated an inverse linear relationship was observed between eGDR and the occurrence of heart disease. Subgroups and sensitivity analysis both verify the robustness of the findings.ConclusionseGDR is a practical and informative marker for identifying individuals with overweight or obesity who are at higher risk of heart disease. Incorporating eGDR into routine risk assessment may help improve early prevention strategies. Further validation in external cohorts is warranted.Keywords: Heart disease, Insulin resistance, Estimated glucose disposal rate, Overweight, Obesity

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVD) remain the leading cause of mortality worldwide, accounting for approximately 17.9 million deaths annually [1]. China bears a disproportionate burden as CVD account for 32% of all national deaths [2]. This growing epidemic is largely driven by rapid urbanization and changes in lifestyle, particularly the increasing prevalence of overweight and obesity. According to Global Burden of Disease (GBD) data, China has the largest population of overweight and obese adults globally, with an estimated 402 million individuals (397–407 million) [3]. Data from the China Chronic Disease and Risk Factor Surveillance (CCDRFS) study indicate that 41.2% of Chinese adults have a body mass index (BMI) ≥ 24 kg/m², a threshold associated with a 34% increased risk of coronary heart disease compared to individuals with normal BMI [4]. Notably, the metabolic consequences of excess adiposity tend to manifest at lower BMI thresholds in Asian populations than in Western counterparts, largely due to ethnic differences in fat distribution. Chinese adults, in particular, exhibit greater visceral adiposity per unit of BMI [5]. Mechanistically, obesity-induced insulin resistance promotes endothelial dysfunction, chronic inflammation, and atherogenic dyslipidemia—together contributing to approximately 63% of obesity-related cardiovascular disease (CVD) deaths [6]. Furthermore, longitudinal data from the China Cardiometabolic Disease and Cancer Cohort have shown that metabolically unhealthy individuals with overweight or obesity face a 2.1-fold increased risk of developing CVD compared to their metabolically healthy peers, highlighting the urgent need for effective and individualized cardiovascular risk stratification tools [7].

Recent studies have highlighted the estimated glucose disposal rate (eGDR) as a clinically meaningful and non-invasive surrogate marker of insulin resistance (IR) for cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk assessment [8–11]. Calculated based on waist circumference, hypertension status, and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), eGDR offers a practical alternative to the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp, with advantages in terms of cost-effectiveness, feasibility, and reproducibility [8]. eGDR has been robustly associated with a variety of cardiovascular outcomes [12]. It has been shown to outperform other IR-related indices in predicting CVD-particularly stroke- and to mediate a substantial proportion (up to 66%) of the association between cardiovascular health metrics and congestive heart failure (CHF) [13]. In population-based studies, eGDR has shown demonstrated superior predictive performance for cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome when assessed using machine learning models algorithms [14]. Moreover, higher eGDR levels have been significantly associated with reduced risks of CVD (21.2%), heart disease (18.3%), and stroke (39.5%) [15]. Although eGDR is widely utilized to predict micro-vascular complications in diabetes, its utility in CVD risk stratification among overweight or obese populations remains inadequately investigated.

To address this knowledge gap, we utilized data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a nationally representative prospective cohort of middle-age and older adults in China. The study aimed to investigate the association between eGDR and the risk of heart disease. We hypothesized that lower eGDR levels would be independently associated with an increase incidence of heart disease among individuals with overweight or obesity.

Methods

Study design

This study utilized data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a nationally representative survey of Chinese adults aged 45 years and older [16]. It provides a broad spectrum of data covering economic status, health (both physical and psychological), demographic profiles, and social connectivity. Additionally, it includes assessments of anthropometric measures and cardiometabolic conditions. The CHARLS baseline survey involved 17,708 participants from 150 counties and 450 villages. The baseline survey (Wave 1) was conducted between June 2011 and March 2012, followed by four subsequent waves: Wave 2 (2013–2014), Wave 3 (2015–2016), Wave 4 (2017–2018), and Wave 5 (2020). Blood specimens were gathered during the first and third waves. The CHARLS study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Peking University (IRB00001052-11015), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment [17]. This investigation was executed in strict adherence to the guidelines set forth by the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) initiative [18].

Study population

The study cohort included participants from the national baseline survey who met the following criteria: (1) overweight defined as BMI ≥ 24 kg/m², obesity defined as BMI ≥ 28 kg/m² [19]; (2) with no documented heart disease at baseline; (3) with no missing values for eGDR; (4) completion of follow-up; and (5) age ≥ 45 years; (6) complete data for covariates. A total of 2,217 participants without heart disease at baseline in 2011 were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1).

Ascertainment of exposure

Venous blood samples were collected by trained personnel during the baseline survey. The samples were centrifuged within 2 h of collection to separate serum and plasma, which were then stored at − 80 °C at the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention Biobank. Subsequent assays were conducted at the Youanmen Center for the Clinical Laboratory of the Capital Medical University [20]. For HbA1c measurement, whole blood samples were used and analyzed using boronate-affinity high-performance liquid chromatography. The coefficient of variation for HbA1c measurement was 1.9% [21]. A stadiometer and weighing scale were used to measure height and weight, respectively, with participants assessed barefoot and in light attire. Waist circumference was measured by trained personnel using a flexible tape placed at the level of the navel. Anthropometric data, including height, weight, and waist circumference, were measured in triplicate, with the mean values used for subsequent analyses. Outcome measures and follow-up duration

Heart disease status was ascertained through participants’ self-report using a standardized set of questions from the CHARLS questionnaire [23]. Specifically, participants were asked, “Have you ever been told by a doctor that you have had a heart attack, coronary heart disease, angina, heart failure, or other heart problems?” Those who responded “yes” at baseline or during follow-up waves were classified as having heart disease. The follow-up period started from the baseline survey in 2011 and continued until the first reported diagnosis of heart disease or the last survey date in 2020, whichever came first.

Covariates

Covariates included sociodemographic factors, vital signs, lifestyle behaviors, laboratory test results, and history of diseases. Information on demographic characteristics, lifestyle habits, and disease history was collected via standardized questionnaires. Sociodemographic characteristics comprised age, gender, and marital status, education attainment (elementary school or below, secondary school, high school, university/college) and living in rural (no/yes). Lifestyle factors included smoking status (never, ever, and current) and alcohol consumption (never, ever, and current). Disease history included hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia. Hypertension was defined as self-reported diagnosis history of hypertension. Dyslipidemia was defined as a self-reported physician diagnosis. Diabetes was defined as self-reported diagnosis history of diabetes. Systolic blood pressure (SBP) was calculated as the average of the second and third systolic measurements, and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) was obtained using the same method.

At the 2011 baseline, laboratory measurements included white blood cell count (WBC), glucose, serum creatinine, total cholesterol, triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c). Medical professionals collected blood samples on-site, which were promptly transported to the local partner hospital laboratory for analysis. Standardized testing protocols and equipment were employed in the laboratory to guarantee data accuracy and reliability.

Statistical analysis

Normally distributed variables were summarized using mean ± standard deviation (SD), and variables without normal distribution were described by median (interquartile range, IQR), whereas categorical variables were summarized as proportions. For comparisons between groups, the independent samples Student’s t-test was applied to normally distributed continuous variables, and the Mann–Whitney U test was used for those with skewed distributions. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test where applicable.

Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HR) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI) for the risk of heart disease. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed through log-minus-log survival plots and interaction terms with survival time. eGDR was examined both continuously and in tertile-based categories. Selection of covariates for adjustment was based on clinical relevance and evidence from prior research. Model 1: Unadjusted; Model 2: Adjusted for sex, age; Model 3: Adjusted for sex, age, marriage, rural, education; Model 4: Adjusted for sex, age, marriage, rural, education, smoking status, drinking status, diabetes, dyslipidemia, SBP, DBP, WBC, total cholesterol, HDL-C, Triglycerides. To investigate possible linear relationships associations between eGDR and on-set of heart disease, smooth curves were fitted using a restricted cubic spline (RCS) model.

Kaplan–Meier curves were used to illustrate the cumulative incidence of heart disease, and differences between different tertiles of the eGDR were assessed using the log-rank test. The robustness of the results was further examined through subgroup analyses. Stratified analyses with multiplicative interaction terms were performed according to different subgroups of age (< 65 or ≥ 65 years), sex (female or male), diabetes (No/Yes), hypertension (No/Yes), smoking status (never/ever/current), drinking status (never/ever/current), and BMI (overweight or obesity). E-values were used to assess the impact of unmeasured confounders on outcomes in a sensitivity analysis.

All statistical analyses were performed using R Statistical Software (Version 4.2.2, http://www.R-project.org, The R Foundation) and the Free Statistics Analysis Platform (Version 2.1.1, Beijing, China, http://www.clinicalscientists.cn/freestatistics) [24]. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participants characteristics

Following the selection criteria presented in Fig. 1, a total of 2,217 participants were enrolled, having an average age of 56.9 ± 8.0 years, including 839 males (37.8%). Over a follow-up span of no more than 9.0 years, 600 participants (27.1%) developed onset of heart disease. Table 1 provides an overview of participants’ demographic characteristics across different eGDR strata. Participants in the higher eGDR group were found to be younger, had lower white blood cell (WBC), and exhibited lower prevalence rates of hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia (P < 0.001). As shown in Fig. 2, lower eGDR values were observed in heart disease patients as a continuous variable (mean eGDR: 8.22 vs. 9.0).

The association of eGDR with incident heart disease

Among the participants, 600 (27.1%) developed new-onset heart disease. The Kaplan–Meier analysis revealed a significant stepwise decrease in incidence across tertiles 1 to 3 (log-rank test P < 0.001; Fig. 3).

Cumulative incidence of heart disease separated by the three tertile of estimated glucose disposal rate (eGDR). T: tertile

According to eGDR tertiles, the incidence of heart disease was 36.6% (n = 276) in tertile 1, 25.4% (n = 191) in tertile 2, and 18.7% (n = 133) in tertile 3, indicating a stepwise decrease in heart disease risk with increasing eGDR levels. In the unadjusted model, each 1-unit increase in eGDR was associated with a 14% lower risk of heart disease (HR: 0.86; 95% CI: 0.83–0.89; Table 2). After controlling for all potential confounders (Model 4), the association remained significant, with an adjusted HR of 0.90 (95% CI: 0.86–0.94; P < 0.001).

Subgroup analysis sensitivity analysis

Subgroup analyses were conducted by stratifying participants according to sex, age, smoking status, alcohol consumption, hypertension, diabetes and BMI to evaluate the consistency of the association between eGDR and heart disease risk across different population subgroups. Additionally, interaction analyses were performed to assess potential effect modifications. The results demonstrated that the inverse association between eGDR and heart disease remained significant across all subgroups, with all interaction P-values exceeding 0.05, indicating no significant interactions (Fig. 5), indicating a consistent effect of eGDR on heart disease risk. To verify the robustness of the study outcomes, an E-value calculation was performed based on the fully adjusted model (Model 4). The resulting E-value was 1.46, suggesting that an unmeasured confounder would need to be strongly associated with both eGDR and heart disease to fully account for the observed association.

Discussion

The findings indicate that higher eGDR levels were independently associated with a reduced risk of heart disease, even after adjusting for a comprehensive set of potential confounders. This association remained robust in both continuous and categorical analyses of eGDR. Furthermore, the RCS regression confirmed a linear inverse relationship between eGDR and cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk. Subgroup and interaction analyses demonstrated consistent associations across various demographic and clinical strata. Our study is among the first prospective cohort studies to examine this association in adults with overweight or obesity in a large community-based Chinese population, highlighting the potential of eGDR as an accessible surrogate of insulin resistance for cardiovascular risk stratification.

eGDR has been validated as a reliable surrogate marker of insulin resistance and has demonstrated substantial prognostic value in the context of cardiovascular disease [9, 23, 25, 26]. Prior studies in Sweden and China have demonstrated that lower eGDR levels are associated with increased risks of cerebrovascular and coronary events in patients with type 2 diabetes [15, 27, 28] and elevated stroke risk [22]. Additionally, eGDR has shown superior predictive ability for cardiovascular disease compared with conventional insulin resistance measures in patients with stage 0–3 cardiometabolic kidney syndrome [14, 29]. Population-based analyses further revealed inverse associations between circulating eGDR and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease [11], as well as reduced risk of cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality in non-diabetic chronic kidney disease populations [10]. Despite these findings, evidence regarding the relationship between eGDR and cardiovascular risk in adults with overweight or obesity remains limited. To address this gap, the present study analyzed 2,217 adults aged ≥ 45 years with overweight or obesity from a nationwide Chinese prospective cohort. Our results demonstrated a marked inverse association between baseline eGDR and incidence of heart disease, consistent with previous reports. These findings underscore that eGDR is not only a reliable marker of insulin resistance but also a practical tool for predicting and stratifying cardiovascular risk in overweight or obese populations.

Current research has yet to completely clarify the causal relationship between eGDR and CVD, though numerous explanatory frameworks have been suggested. First, eGDR serves as a reliable proxy for IR, which plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis-the primary driver of heart disease [30]. Second, IR is strongly associated with three major pathological pathways: inflammation, hemostatic dysfunction, and oxidative stress [31–33]. These interconnected mechanisms synergistically impair endothelial integrity, ultimately mediating the development of CVD. Third, IR reduced the bioavailability of nitric, compromising endothelial function and contributing to vascular injury, which ultimately heightens the risk of CVD [34, 35]. Therefore, lower eGDR levels may reflects underlying vascular and organ damage [36, 37]. In clinical practice, these findings suggest that maintaining eGDR within an optimal range may be essential for CVD prevention. Additionally, understanding the relationship between eGDR and CVD risk can refine risk assessment practices, facilitating more precise clinical decision-making and personalized therapeutic strategies. Such an approach may aid in the early identification and management of high risk of individuals. Nevertheless, the exact mechanism by which eGDR influences CVD warrants further investigation.

This study has several strengths and offers novel contributions. Using data from a large nationwide cohort with long-term follow-up, it provides robust and generalizable evidence on the association between eGDR and incident heart disease. Its prospective design reduces reverse causality, and the focus on overweight and obese middle-aged and older Chinese adults addresses an understudied high-risk population. By analyzing eGDR both continuously and categorically, and employing advanced modeling such as restricted cubic splines, we offer a nuanced understanding of dose-response relationships. Comprehensive adjustment for confounders and multiple subgroup analyses confirm the robustness of our findings. To our knowledge, this is the first large-scale cohort study to elucidate a significant inverse relationship between estimated glucose disposal rate (eGDR) and the risk of heart disease among individuals with overweight or obesity.

However, several limitations should be considered. First, eGDR was assessed only at baseline, limiting the ability to evaluate temporal changes in insulin sensitivity and their impact on heart disease risk. Incorporating longitudinal measurements of eGDR would provide more comprehensive risk stratification. Second, detailed data on certain lifestyle factors, such as dietary intake, were unavailable. Although major lifestyle variables were included and E-value analyses suggested robustness to unmeasured confounding, residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded. Third, given the large sample size, some statistically significant associations may lack clinical relevance. Lastly, the study population comprised exclusively middle-aged and older Chinese adults, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings to other ethnic or age groups.

Conclusions

In conclusion, eGDR is a simple, non-invasive marker predicting heart disease risk in overweight and obese middle-aged and older adults, with potential to improve cardiovascular risk stratification and prevention. Further studies are needed to confirm its utility across diverse populations and clinical settings.
