Mapping the transcriptional landscape of algal resistance to viral infection reveals a core expression program
Authors: Talia S. Shaler, Amir Fromm, Daniella Schatz, Shifra Ben‐Dor, Ester Feldmesser, Assaf Vardi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Summary Algal blooms and their demise by viruses drive global‐scale ecological processes in the ocean. These blooms form the foundation of marine food webs, regulate microbial communities, and shape biogeochemical cycles. Although algal populations are constantly infected by viruses, resistant subpopulations frequently emerge after the infection. Yet, antiviral molecular mechanisms of marine microalgae are underexplored.We used a model system of the ubiquitous microalga Gephyrocapsa huxleyi and its giant virus, Emiliania huxleyi virus (EhV), to study how resistant populations evolve and to identify their transcriptional programs. We generated a detailed temporal transcriptomic dataset during a viral infection, covering the stages of lysis and the recovery of a resistant subpopulation.Viral infection triggered prominent transcriptome changes to support viral propagation, followed by a unique transcriptional response in resistant cells. Both infected and resistant cells highly expressed innate immune response genes, notably those with Toll/interleukin‐1 receptor (TIR) domain. Additionally, resistant cells expressed genes involved in membrane‐bound glycan remodeling, sphingolipid metabolism, and nutrient uptake.Using comparative transcriptomics across diverse resistant G. huxleyi strains, we identified a core group of resistance‐related genes, providing a set of gene markers to detect this rare phenotype during the host‐virus arms race in algal blooms.

Introduction

Phytoplankton are unicellular photosynthetic microorganisms that are key primary producers in aquatic ecosystems. Under specific environmental conditions, some species can rapidly proliferate, forming dense and large‐scale algal blooms (Winter et al., 2006; Sarkar, 2018; Schleyer & Vardi, 2020). The algal blooms are frequently terminated by lytic virus infections (Bratbak et al., 1993; Tarutani et al., 2000; Martínez Martínez et al., 2007; Vardi et al., 2012; Laber et al., 2018). The viral‐mediated lysis of the cells redirects the algal biomass into the dissolved phase through a process known as the viral shunt (Wilhelm & Suttle, 1999). This flux of nutrients and metabolites is a key ecological process that fuels marine food webs, microbial communities (Buchan et al., 2014), biogeochemical cycles (Simó, 2001; Rost & Riebesell, 2004; Taylor et al., 2017), and carbon export to the deep ocean (Laber et al., 2018). Although algal blooms demise, they recur annually, and resistant cells were isolated during the demise phase of the blooms (Tarutani et al., 2000; Schleyer et al., 2023), indicating a stable coexistence of hosts and viruses in the environment (Waterbury & Valois, 1993; Carlson et al., 2022). Laboratory experiments in several microalgae species show that resistant populations emerge after viral infection (Thyrhaug et al., 2003; Tomaru et al., 2009; Thomas et al., 2011; Frada et al., 2017; Joffe et al., 2024). Therefore, studying how resistance to viral infection is acquired in natural populations is key to understanding bloom dynamics and their ecological consequences.

In the host‐virus evolutionary arms race, host cells develop multiple layers of defense, and viruses evolve counter‐defenses to overcome them. Defense mechanisms can act at the cell surface level to prevent viral attachment and entry by mutating membrane receptors (Stoddard et al., 2007; Avrani et al., 2011; Kolan et al., 2024). Alternatively, they can act intracellularly by interfering with various stages of the viral life cycle, such as gene transcription, translation, genome replication, or virion packaging. In response, viruses evolve to overcome host defense in many ways, from mutation‐based evasion (Stokar‐Avihail et al., 2023) to direct inhibition of immune proteins (Leavitt et al., 2022; Yirmiya et al., 2025) and restoration of molecules depleted by defense systems (Osterman et al., 2024).

New antiviral defense systems are constantly being discovered in bacterial model systems like Escherichia coli (Doron et al., 2018; Millman et al., 2022), with some even functionally conserved in human immune systems (Wein & Sorek, 2022; Bernheim et al., 2024). However, for marine microalgae, where host‐virus dynamics have large‐scale ecological implications, most defense strategies remain underexplored. Using homology to known defense systems is a common practice for detecting resistance mechanisms, but it may not fully capture the diversity of algal resistance. For example, a recent study tested two putative defense systems encoded by a viral‐resistant strain of the cyanobacterium Synechococcus. Surprisingly, they did not protect against a cyanophage infection (Zborowsky et al., 2025). Instead, resistance was conferred by a constitutive absence of specific tRNAs in the host cells, preventing the translation of key cyanophage proteins. Therefore, elucidating the molecular mechanisms contributing to algal resistance phenotypes requires comparative approaches and functional assays.

To explore genes involved in algal resistance to viral infection, we used the bloom‐forming Gephyrocapsa huxleyi (formerly Emiliania huxleyi, (Bendif et al., 2019)) and its specific virus, Emiliania huxleyi virus (EhV), a lytic nucleocytoplasmic large DNA virus (NCLDV). When EhV infects G. huxleyi cells, it reprograms the cell transcriptome and metabolome into a virocell state that functions to support viral replication (Rosenwasser et al., 2014; Ku et al., 2020). Infection ultimately leads to cell lysis and bloom demise (Bratbak et al., 1993; Wilson et al., 2002; Martínez Martínez et al., 2007). But, resistant isolates occur in natural populations, and infections under laboratory conditions often result in cell regrowth and the recovery of a resistant population (Thyrhaug et al., 2003; Joffe et al., 2024). The recovered population exhibits a spectrum of resistance levels, enabling a dynamic balance that supports host‐virus coexistence (Joffe et al., 2024).

Resistance in G. huxleyi was previously associated with life cycle transitions and morphological changes (Frada et al., 2008, 2017). Specifically, haploid (1n) flagellated cells are resistant to viral infections (Frada et al., 2008). When susceptible diploid (2n) cultures are infected, resistant cells that resemble haploid cells emerge (Frada et al., 2008). However, these cells are diploid (2n‐flagellated) or may have increased ploidy levels (Frada et al., 2017). In addition, compared to susceptible cells, the resistant cells contain different compositions of glycosphingolipids (GSL) (Hunter et al., 2015; Schleyer et al., 2023) and show significant changes in gene expression (Feldmesser et al., 2021). With > 18 000 genes differentially expressed (DE) between resistant and susceptible strains (Feldmesser et al., 2021), it is challenging to determine which specific genes are involved in the resistance phenotype and which may be related to any existing strain variability, such as morphology or cell physiology.

In the current work, we aimed to identify the specific genes associated with resistance. We analyzed the transcriptome of G. huxleyi with high temporal resolution, tracking the response to viral infection and the transition from sensitivity to resistance. Our findings outlined a unique transcriptomic profile of resistant cells across multiple strains of G. huxleyi. This core group of resistance‐related genes includes those involved in remodeling membrane‐bound glycans, sphingolipid metabolism, and nutrient uptake. Additionally, it involves genes with domains related to innate immune responses, such as Toll/interleukin‐1 receptor (TIR) domains. These core resistance‐related genes offer a set of potential markers for identifying G. huxleyi resistance in complex natural populations.

Materials and Methods

Algal and viral strains

Gephyrocapsa huxleyi (formerly Emiliania huxleyi Lohmann) strain RCC6946 and the Emiliania huxleyi virus (EhV) strain EhVM1 were isolated from a G. huxleyi bloom during a mesocosm experiment in Raunefjorden, Bergen, Norway (60.27°N; 5.22°E) in May 2018.

Culture growth conditions and experimental setup

Gephyrocapsa huxleyi RCC6946 cultures were grown in filtered autoclaved seawater (FSW) supplemented with a modified K/2 medium (Keller et al., 1987) (18‐μM KH2PO4 instead of organic phosphate), and the antibiotics Ampicillin (100 μg ml−1) and Kanamycin (50 μg ml−1). Cultures were incubated at 18°C with a 16 h : 8 h, light : dark cycle and 100 μmol photons m−2 s−1 light intensity provided by white light‐emitting diodes. EhVM1 was propagated on G. huxleyi RCC6946 until the algal culture lysed to clearance. The viral lysate was filtered through a 0.45 μm pore size Nalgene Rapid‐Flow filter unit with PES (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and stored in the dark at 4°C until the infection assay. In the time series infection assay, exponential phase RCC6946 cultures (c. 106 cells ml−1, volume of 1.4 l) were infected with EhVM1 in a 5 : 1 viral particles‐to‐cell ratio. The infectivity of EhVM1 is c. 1.4%, which results in a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of c. 0.07 infectious particles per cell at the time of infection. Abundances of algal cells and extracellular viral particles were monitored for 23 d through the infection and recovery phases by flow cytometry (Supporting Information Fig. S1; Methods S1, S2). After 23 d, the recovered cultures were diluted weekly with fresh medium. The other culture of recovered RCC6946 (Rec II), which was used to validate the transcriptomic profile of the resistance phenotype, originated from another EhVM1 infection but was maintained under standard growth conditions for several months before RNA extraction. Growth curves were generated with GraphPad Prism (v.10.3.1; GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA).

RNA extraction and quality evaluation

Pellets for RNA extraction were collected by 2‐step centrifugation at 4°C (Methods S3). Since the infection time series was done in the same flasks, we were limited in the available volume for RNA extraction. Depending on cell abundance, we pelleted 50–200 ml of each culture, where larger culture volumes (150–200 ml) were used when cell abundance was low. Cell pellets from the uninfected treatment were collected until day 7, after the cultures reached stationary growth. Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer's protocol, followed by a double Turbo DNase treatment (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Invitrogen). RNA concentration and integrity were assessed using the Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and TapeStation assay (Agilent Genomics, Santa Clara, CA, USA). During the advanced stages of infection (days 4–15), where live cell counts were low (103–104 cells ml−1), high‐quality RNA extraction and library construction were unsuccessful. In the second infection assay, cell pellets for RNA extraction from a single time point during exponential growth were collected from G. huxleyi RCC6946 culture and a resistant recovered culture (Rec II).

MARS‐seq library preparation and sequencing

cDNA libraries were prepared according to a bulk adaptation of the MARS‐seq protocol (Keren‐Shaul et al., 2019). A starting material of 50 ng clean RNA from each sample was reverse transcribed to cDNA using oligo(dT) adaptors containing molecular barcodes, allowing enrichment for mRNA and uniquely tagging each fragment. The tagged cDNA was pooled together, converted into double‐stranded DNA (dsDNA), and linearly amplified using T7 RNA polymerase in vitro transcription (IVT). The amplified RNA was fragmented to 200–500 bp, ligated to partial Illumina primers, and reverse transcribed to cDNA before final amplification using 10‐cycle PCR. The library size distribution and concentration were measured using Qubit and TapeStation assays. Quality control scores during library preparation were evaluated by qPCR of the actin gene using 3′ directed primers: forward 5′‐GACCGACTGGATGGTCAAG‐3′; and reverse 5′‐GCCAGCCTTCTCCTTGATGTC‐3′ (Nam et al., 2018). cDNA libraries were sequenced using an Illumina NextSeq500 (75 cycles) or NovaSeq 6000 SP (100 cycles) platforms, aiming for 10 M reads per sample. BCL files were demultiplexed based on molecular barcodes and converted to Fastq files using the UTAP2 transcriptome analysis pipeline (Lindner et al., 2025).

Read processing and mapping to reference

Reads were processed using the UTAP2 transcriptome analysis pipeline (Lindner et al., 2025). The reads were assessed with FastQC (Andrews, 2010) and MultiQC (Ewels et al., 2016), trimmed with Cutadapt (Martin, 2011) to remove Illumina adapters and polyA tails, and filtered by Q20 quality and a minimal length of 25 bp. The processed Fastq files were downsampled to a depth of 10 M reads and mapped to a dual reference of G. huxleyi and EhVM1 using Star v.2.7.10a (parameters: ‐‐alignEndsType EndToEnd ‐‐outFilterMismatchNoverLmax 0.05 ‐‐twopassMode Basic ‐‐alignSoftClipAtReferenceEnds No) (Dobin et al., 2013).

The reference EhVM1 genome has 489 predicted coding sequences (Fromm et al., 2022), to which we added 306 potential ORFs (in total, 795 transcripts). Since G. huxleyi RCC6946 has no available genome, we used a pan‐transcriptome built from long‐ and short‐read sequencing of several G. huxleyi strains (Feldmesser et al., 2021). The pan‐transcriptome comprises 65 391 sequences and represents a broad repertoire of genes, comparable to genomes available for other G. huxleyi strains (Read et al., 2013; Skeffington et al., 2023; Kao et al., 2024). Gene annotations were obtained from Feldmesser et al., 2021, and additional KEGG annotations at the protein level from blastkoala (v.3.1, eukaryotes database) and ghostkoala (v.3.1, Eukaryotes + Prokaryotes + Viruses databases, score ≥ 100) (Kanehisa et al., 2016). Conserved domains were predicted using HHsuite v.3.3.0 (Söding, 2005) against the Pfam database (E‐value <0.05).

Genes encoding carbohydrate‐active enzymes were predicted with the Carbohydrate Active Enzymes database (Drula et al., 2022) (CAZy, http://www.cazy.org/) based on the G. huxleyi CCMP1516 draft genome (JGI Portal: Emihu1, NCBI Taxonomy ID: 280463). Annotated CAZy proteins of the draft genome (c. 800) were blasted against the pan‐transcriptome reference to convert JGI accession numbers to protein ID (BlastP, identity ≥ 95%, length ≥ 100). Cellular localization was predicted with Deeploc‐2.1(Ødum et al., 2024) (default parameters for high‐throughput analysis: high‐quality model (slow) and short output format) with the recommended probability thresholds: cytoplasm (0.4761), nucleus (0.5014), extracellular (0.6173), cell membrane (0.5646), mitochondrion (0.6220), plastid (0.6395), endoplasmic reticulum (0.6090), lysosome/vacuole (0.5848), golgi apparatus (0.6494) peroxisome (0.7364), peripheral membrane (0.60), transmembrane (0.51), lipid anchor (0.82), and soluble (0.50). Gene expression levels were evaluated with HTSeq‐count v.2.0.2 (Anders et al., 2015), and duplicate reads were removed using the Unique Molecular Identifiers (UMIs, molecular tags).

Differential gene expression analysis

Gephyrocapsa huxleyi genes with a minimum of 10 UMI counts in at least three samples were considered significantly expressed and were included in the DE analysis conducted with the deseq2 package (v.1.38.3) (Love et al., 2014). Since RNA‐seq provides compositional data representing relative abundances of genes, we excluded viral reads to prevent false identification of DE genes by introducing another organism (virus) into a finite pool of reads. Each time point under both conditions (infected and uninfected) was compared to the reference level of the uninfected sample at T0 (0.5 h post‐infection). Genes identified as significantly DE had baseMean expression ≥ 5, absolute log2(FC) ≥ 1 (Wald test: LFC threshold = 1, alpha = 0.05, altHypothesis = ‘greaterAbs’), and an FDR adjusted P‐value < 0.05 in at least one comparison. For principal component analysis (PCA) and K‐means clustering, we used normalized counts after variance stabilizing transformation (VST) using deseq2 and R (v.4.2.2). The optimal number of clusters was determined by complementary statistical methods. For heatmap visualization, we applied row (gene) standardization, scaling the means of each row to zero with a SD of one.

The proportion of viral RNA transcripts was calculated from the total transcripts mapped to the dual reference of G. huxleyi and EhVM1. Statistical significance was determined by a one‐way repeated measures ANOVA and Dunnett's post hoc multiple comparison tests using GraphPad Prism 10.3.1 (GraphPad). Asterisks represent a statistically significant increase (P‐value < 0.05). Some viral RNA transcripts exist in uninfected and recovered cultures. Since uninfected cultures were virus‐free, the signal likely resulted from pipetting carryover or incorrect read assignment to samples rather than viral expression. In the recovered cultures, the viral transcripts may reflect the background noise as in uninfected cultures, suggesting recovered cultures are free of viral expression. Alternatively, they could originate from a small, infected subpopulation that expresses viral genes. This issue remains unclear, and future single‐cell approaches could provide insights into it.

Functional annotation and enrichment analysis

Gephyrocapsa huxleyi Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG annotations were adapted from Feldmesser et al. (2021). GO enrichment was computed by the topgo package (v.2.50.0) with Fisher exact test, weight algorithm, and biological process ontology. KEGG pathway enrichment was computed by applying a hypergeometric test in R. Enrichment of specific GO terms or KEGG pathways for each cluster was determined by P‐value ≤ 0.01 when compared to all expressed genes.

Meta‐analysis of resistant strains

Published RNA‐seq datasets comparing resistant and sensitive G. huxleyi strains were downloaded from Feldmesser et al. (2021), Keeling et al., (2014), and Bousquet et al. (2025). The transcriptomes included four resistant G. huxleyi strains: CCMP373, CCMP379, LC5‐11A (1n‐flagellated), and LC5‐12A (2n‐flagellated). For CCMP373 (Feldmesser et al., 2021) and CCMP379 (Keeling et al., 2014), processed Fastq files were mapped to the dual reference of G. huxleyi and EhVM1 using RSEM (Li & Dewey, 2011). DE analysis was conducted with the deseq2 package (v.1.38.3). Data from Keeling et al. (MMETSP0994‐0997, MMETSP1006‐1009) had no replicates, so infected and non‐infected samples were pooled together. For LC5‐11A and LC5‐12A (Bousquet et al., 2025), we used the available DE tables. Gene sequences from Bousquet et al. were compared to our pan‐transcriptome reference to convert gene annotations (BlastN, identity ≥ 95%, length ≥ 100). In some cases, several genes in this database matched the same gene in the pan‐transcriptome.

Genes from each dataset were defined as DE when absolute log2(FC) > 1 and FDR‐adjusted P‐value < 0.05. From each dataset, DE genes that were shared with the time series transcriptome were organized according to the four clusters. Each gene was assigned a ‘conservation score’ ranging from 1 to 5, reflecting the number of resistant strains in which it was upregulated. Similarly, downregulated genes were scored −1 to −5 based on the number of transcriptomes.

Structural predictions and structural search

AlphaFold 3 (Abramson et al., 2024) was used to model the structure of the core resistant group genes and TIR‐containing genes. Structural models were searched in the AlphaFold/UniProt50 database using Foldseek (van Kempen et al., 2024). Protein descriptions for each UniProt50 match were fetched using the UniProt API. Top UniProt50 hits for each structural model were examined manually and further analyzed for functional annotation.

TIR gene definition, phylogenetic, and structural analyses

To find TIR domain‐containing proteins, conserved domains were predicted using HHsuite v.3.3.0 (Söding, 2005). TIR domain‐containing proteins were defined as such if they contain at least one of the TIR‐domain protein families: pfam13676, pfam08937, pfam01582, pfam10137, pfam08357, and pfam18567 (pfam probability score ≥ 50%). Expressed TIR domain‐containing genes were manually examined for domain completeness and then structurally modeled as described above. Transmembrane helices were predicted using DeepTMHMM (Hallgren et al., 2022), and additional encoded domains were searched with the CDD database (Wang et al., 2023). PyMol (v3.7; Schrödinger LLC) was used to visualize the predicted structures and superimpose them with the human and Arabidopsis TIR domains.

Results

Host cells recovered from viral infection and acquired resistance

To investigate the host cellular response that facilitates resistance to viral infection, we infected G. huxleyi (strain RCC6946) with Emiliania huxleyi virus (EhV; strain EhVM1) (Fromm et al., 2022) and sampled the cultures over a detailed time series across various phases of interactions. This host‐virus pair was isolated from induced blooms of G. huxleyi in the fjords of Norway and presents an ecologically relevant host‐virus model system (Fig. 1a).

Gephyrocapsa huxleyi exponential cultures were infected with EhVM1 and monitored by flow cytometry over 23 d, through the stages of lytic infection and recovery (Fig. 1b). Uninfected G. huxleyi cultures reached a cell density of c. 5 × 106 cells ml−1 within 5 d and remained steady until day 23. By contrast, the virus‐infected cultures experienced a profound decline during days 2–7 post‐infection, whereby only < 0.5% of the culture survived (Fig. 1b). The fraction of dying cells was measured by Sytox Green staining and reached 80% by day 4 (Fig. S2). Cultures remained at a minimal cell density of c. 103 cells ml−1 for 4 d. From day 11, cell abundance increased, signifying the recovery phase of the cultures. Recovery dynamics varied between the biological replicates (n = 3), but eventually, all recovered. Replicate A steadily grew and reached the starting point concentration on day 21, but replicates B and C fully recovered only on day 37 (Fig. S3). A divergence between the replicates during the recovery phase is expected, given that we are comparing processes that occur after almost complete lysis of the culture. Deviation between the replicates in the number of surviving cells, which are the foundation of the new resistant population, can lead to less synchronization in the recovery dynamics. After the last sampling point (day 23), the three recovered cultures were diluted into fresh media and kept under standard growth conditions.

The onset of virus release (extracellular virions) was already apparent at 8 h post‐infection, and virus abundances increased over 100‐fold within the first 2 d (Fig. 1b). This preceded the significant rise in cell death, suggesting viruses were released by budding (Vincent et al., 2021). A constant high viral particle load, over 108 viral particles ml−1, was measured through the recovery of host cultures, suggesting the cells developed resistance to EhVM1 and transitioned to a co‐existence state. To validate the acquisition of resistance to viral infection in the recovered host cultures, we challenged them with a fresh EhVM1. Cell abundance of the challenged recovered cultures was similar to that of uninfected controls (Figs 1c, S4), meaning the recovered cultures gained resistance to EhVM1.

To determine the morphological properties and life cycle phase of the recovered cultures, we examined calcification and ploidy using flow cytometry analyses. The RCC6946 strain was initially isolated as a calcifying strain; however, over time, under laboratory conditions, the proportion of calcified cells decreased and reached c. 4% at the time of infection. Notably, the recovered cells were non‐calcified (Fig. S5). Additionally, nuclear DNA content analyses to assess ploidy levels indicated that recovered cells are haploid (1n) (Fig. S6; Methods S4), whereas the original RCC6946 strain was diploid (2n).

The transcriptomic response of G. huxleyi to viral infection

To understand the phenotypic switch from sensitivity to resistance to viral infection, we conducted a detailed transcriptomic analysis during the 23‐d infection time series. Following the same cultures of one evolving strain from sensitive to resistant, we aimed to identify the genes contributing to the resistance phenotype. We collected RNA samples from 9 time points (0.5 h; 1–4, 7, 15, 19, and 23 d post‐infection) throughout the time series, with an additional sampling point on day 50, after the recovered cultures grew in fresh media (Fig. 1b).

In total, 48 samples underwent 3′‐end RNA sequencing (RNA‐seq) using a bulk adaptation of the MARS‐seq protocol (Keren‐Shaul et al., 2019). Since there is no available genome for this specific host strain, the reads were mapped to a pan‐transcriptome reference containing transcripts from several G. huxleyi strains (Feldmesser et al., 2021) and EhVM1 transcripts (Fromm et al., 2022). This pan‐transcriptome represents a broad repertoire of genes, comparable to the available genomes of other G. huxleyi strains (Read et al., 2013; Skeffington et al., 2023; Kao et al., 2024). To evaluate the pan‐transcriptome as a suitable reference, we performed single‐nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) calling and assessed the genetic diversity of the RCC6946 strain. The reads from each replicate were mapped to the reference, and SNPs were identified using the GATK tool (McKenna et al., 2010). We found a high similarity between the mapped reads and the pan‐transcriptome, with an average of 0.003 SNPs per mapped base and 2.6 SNPs per gene (Table S1; Methods S5). These results align with the global SNP calling conducted by Feldmesser et al., 2021, which analyzed reads from four strains and reported an average of 0.003–0.005 SNPs per mapped base and 5.06–6.07 SNPs per gene.

The sequenced reads capture both host and virus gene expression and enable linking the host transcriptional state with the progression of infection. To profile the transcriptional signature of infected and resistant cells, we focused on the samples from the infection and recovery phases. We included time points from 0.5 h post‐infection (T0), and days 1, 2, 3, 19, 23, and 50 post‐infection (T1, T2, T3, T19, T23, and T50). Respectively, the relevant time points from uninfected samples were included (T0 to T3). The transcriptomic analysis yielded 19 816 expressed host genes and 270 virus genes (Dataset S1). The number of expressed G. huxleyi and EhV genes aligns with findings from previous transcriptomic studies (Rosenwasser et al., 2014; Ku et al., 2020; Feldmesser et al., 2021; Bousquet et al., 2025).

Principal component analysis (PCA) of host gene expression revealed distinct transcriptional profiles of recovered and infected populations. PC1, which accounted for 46.7% of the variance (Fig. 2a), separated the recovery phase from the uninfected and infected phases. The recovery phase samples (T19, T23, and T50) are distributed along the PC1 axis but do not form time‐specific clusters, similar to the observed asynchrony in recovery dynamics (Fig. 1b). PC2 accounted for 22.3% of the variance and captured the temporal progression in the infection phase, based only on the host response to infection. Uninfected samples clustered closely, displaying relatively low variation throughout the sampling period. Together, these results highlight the transcriptional plasticity during infection and recovery phases.

The proportion of viral RNA transcripts out of the total mapped transcripts increased from c. 0.03% at T0 to 14% at T3 (Fig. 2b). EhVM1 is a slow‐infecting strain, which is reflected by its slower infection dynamics. Host lysis occurs at c. 1–2 d post‐infection, with low infectivity, c. 1.4% infectious particles. The reduced virulence of EhVM1 may account for the relatively low proportion of viral RNA transcripts, compared to the more virulent EhV201 strain. In the case of EhV201 infection of G. huxleyi CCMP2090, host lysis occurs within 24 h, with 20% infectivity. Within those 24 h, c. 80% of the virocell transcriptome is viral (Rosenwasser et al., 2014), and c. 90% of the cells become infected (Ku et al., 2020). Additionally, variations in the response to infection or susceptibility levels between the host strains may also explain the observed differences in the viral takeover of the mRNA pool. Thus, the slower infection dynamics and limited viral dominance in the global transcriptome offer an opportunity to study the transcriptional response of G. huxleyi throughout infection, including gene mining for resistance signaling pathways.

Gene expression analysis along the time series revealed 2174 DE host genes in at least one comparison (absolute log2(Fold Change) ≥ 1, FDR P‐value < 0.05; Pass = ‘True’ in Table S2). We conducted the DE analysis on G. huxleyi genes only since introducing another organism (virus) into a finite pool of reads can generate false identification of DE genes, as RNA‐seq is compositional data representing relative abundances of genes.

Unsupervised k‐means clustering (k = 4) highlighted temporal expression patterns with significant enrichment in specific GO terms (Fig. 2c; Table S3) and KEGG pathways (Table S3). The host response to infection is represented by clusters 1–3 and includes an early response detected after 0.5 h (T0) and an extensive reprogramming of the virocell transcriptome at T1–T3. The virocell early response cluster (cluster 2; 548 genes) captured an immediate and transient gene upregulation, marked by genes involved in cellular signaling pathways and the synthesis of signaling molecules (Fig. S7). Among them are phosphatidylinositol (PtdIns) kinases, Ras family GTPases, lipoxygenases producing oxylipins, and isoprenoid synthesis genes. PI3K, a PtdIns kinase, is known to be activated as a stress‐induced response in higher eukaryotes (Munnik et al., 1998; Ramanan et al., 2018), and oxylipins are released in diatoms in response to viral infection (Edwards et al., 2024), grazing stress (Johnson et al., 2020), or disrupted membranes (Pohnert, 2000, 2002). Such oxylipins act as a chemical signal to induce programmed cell death in neighboring diatom cells (Vardi et al., 2006). Additionally, genes encoding proteins with domains homologous to Toll/interleukin‐1 receptor/resistance (TIR) domains, a signaling component of the eukaryotic immunity system (Nimma et al., 2021), were detected.

Virocell clusters 1 and 3 reflect a global remodeling of core metabolic pathways to support viral propagation, in corroboration with previous findings (Rosenwasser et al., 2014) (Table S4). The virocell downregulated cluster (cluster 1; 406 genes) was enriched with pathways associated with core cellular processes such as photosynthesis, amino acid metabolism, pyruvate metabolism, and terpenoid synthesis via the 2‐C‐methyl‐d‐erythritol‐4‐phosphate (MEP) pathway. The virocell upregulated cluster (cluster 3; 554 genes) involved pathways of DNA break repair and DNA replication, TCA cycle enzymes, and cytoskeleton organization.

The recovery cluster (cluster 4; 666 genes) grouped genes upregulated during the recovery phase. It highlighted a unique transcriptional program of the cells that acquired resistance, distinct from the gene expression patterns of uninfected or infected cells. This cluster was enriched with inorganic anion transmembrane transport pathways and DNA replication initiation (Fig. 2c).

The unique transcriptional state of recovered cells

To validate the transcriptional profile of recovered cells that gained resistance, we analyzed an independent culture of G. huxleyi RCC6946, which recovered from EhVM1 infection and was maintained under standard growth conditions for several months before the transcriptome profiling (RCC6946 Rec II). The culture was sampled for RNA‐seq and compared to the sensitive ancestor strain at a single time point during the exponential growth phase. The analysis resulted in 787 DE genes (absolute log2(FC) ≥ 1, FDR P‐value <0.05; Pass = ‘True’ in Table S5). Approximately half of the detected DE genes (391 out of 787) were also DE in the time series transcriptome and were mainly from the recovery cluster (351 genes). The likelihood of observing 351 or more overlapping genes with the recovery cluster by chance is extremely low (hypergeometric test, P‐value < 10−5). Therefore, we focused on the genes shared between the recovery phase of the two independent experiments, as they have a stronger association with the resistant phenotypic state.

Among the shared upregulated genes, we detected enzymes involved in lipid modification and sphingolipid metabolism (Table 1). These include sphingolipid desaturase 4 (sld4), sphingoid base hydroxylases 4 and 5 (sbh4‐5) (Schleyer et al., 2023), and several CRAL/TRIO domain‐containing proteins with putative roles in lipid intracellular trafficking (Panagabko et al., 2003). We also found an upregulation of several sugar modification enzymes that can remodel membrane‐bound glycans (Table 1). This group includes glycosyltransferases (GT), sugar methyltransferases, and glycoside hydrolases (GH) (Table 1). Specifically, xylanase and cellulase enzymes from GH families specializing in cell wall polysaccharide degradation were upregulated (GH5, GH10, determined by the CAZy Database (Drula et al., 2022)). Additionally, some genes from the GT and GH families were predicted to localize extracellularly, according to Deeploc (Ødum et al., 2024), indicating that they may modify cell surface glycans.

Furthermore, we detected higher transcription of genes involved in nutrient uptake and metabolism, particularly of nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), carbon dioxide (CO2), and sulfate (S). Among these are ammonium transporters, glutamine synthetase, urocanase, phosphate transporters, sulfite exporters, carbonic anhydrase, and bicarbonate transporters (Table 1). Additionally, we detected the expression of SANT/Myb transcription factors (TFs). Myb TFs are abundant in plants and microalgae (Thiriet‐Rupert et al., 2016) and are involved in regulating nutrient stress responses to P, N, and CO2 in several microalgae lineages (Yoshioka et al., 2004; Kumar Sharma et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2022). Specifically, genes associated with flagellar functions, which are known to be upregulated in resistant cells (Frada et al., 2017; Feldmesser et al., 2021; Bousquet et al., 2025), were not detected here (Fig. S8).

Interestingly, among the upregulated genes in the recovered‐resistant population were genes potentially involved in pathogen‐recognition signaling pathways. The proteins encoded by these genes contain motifs or domains that are conserved in eukaryotes and activate an innate immune response upon pathogen recognition (Richter & Levin, 2019). The immune response typically includes activation of transcription factors that initiate the transcription of immunity genes. Here, we observe that the recovered cells highly transcribe genes encoding Toll/interleukin‐1 receptor/resistance (TIR) domains, leucine‐rich repeat (LRR) motifs, and NACHT domains (NTPase) (Table 1).

Resistant G. huxleyi strains share a conserved transcriptional state

Next, we asked whether the transcriptional state of recovered populations is similar to that of other resistant G. huxleyi strains. We performed a meta‐analysis comparing the gene expression profiles of multiple G. huxleyi strains that exhibit resistance to different EhVs. These strains were either isolated as resistant against all tested EhVs or recovered from EhV infections. With this approach, we aimed to identify a core set of genes associated with resistance to viral infection. This could provide insights into the molecular mechanism underlying resistance and serve as potential biomarkers for identifying resistant G. huxleyi cells.

We compared the DE genes identified in this study with expression patterns from available published transcriptomes of resistant and sensitive G. huxleyi strains (Keeling et al., 2014; Feldmesser et al., 2021; Bousquet et al., 2025) (see the Materials and Methods section). Among the 2174 DE genes in the time series dataset, we identified those significantly DE (upregulated or downregulated) in the other resistant strains. These shared DE genes were then grouped based on the cluster affiliation from Fig. 2(c) (Table S7).

Our analysis revealed that most genes in the recovery cluster were also upregulated in other resistant strains (Fig. 3a, height of the bars, cluster 4). By contrast, the virocell clusters (clusters 1–3), representing a response to infection, had lower similarity with the transcriptome of resistant strains. The upregulated genes in resistant strains also showed higher fold change (FC) values, with expression levels ranging from 25 to 215 times higher than those in sensitive strains. The extreme FC values suggest these genes are not expressed by the sensitive strains (Fig. 3b). These findings strengthen the functional importance of genes from the recovery cluster in the phenotypic state of resistance.

Next, we assessed if the DE genes from each transcriptome formed a mutually shared group across the transcriptomes. We assigned each gene a score ranging from 1 to 5, reflecting the number of resistant strains in which it was upregulated, serving as a conservation level across the resistant phenotype. Similarly, downregulated genes were scored −1 to −5 based on the number of transcriptomes (Fig. 3a, color gradient). We identified a core group of 206 genes (‘resistance genes’) within the recovery cluster that were upregulated in all tested resistant strains (conservation level = 5). This conserved group of genes represents a potential molecular signature of G. huxleyi resistance.

The core resistance group covers most of the genes discussed above, mainly the immune signaling pathways, glycan and lipid remodeling and metabolism, and nutrient uptake (Table 1, bolded genes). Using AlphaFold 3 (Abramson et al., 2024), we generated structural predictions for a better functional annotation of genes with no sequence‐based homologs. This revealed new functional groups, highlighted by genes involved in RNA/DNA processing and calcium (Ca2+) binding and transport (Table S8). Among them are genes with zinc‐finger domains, EF calcium‐binding motifs, and bestrophins, which function as anion channels in humans. Interestingly, the only process not conserved with other resistant strains is the DNA replication initiation (Table 1), suggesting it is a unique transcriptional signature of recovery in the G. huxleyi RCC6946 strain.

Exploring the functional role of TIR domains in algal resistance

Due to the principal role of TIR domain‐containing proteins in the immunity of animals, plants, bacteria, and archaea (Essuman et al., 2017; Fitzgerald & Kagan, 2020), we focused on elucidating their transcriptional patterns during alga‐virus dynamics. Based on a conserved domains search, we identified the TIR‐containing genes encoded by G. huxleyi. In total, we found 15 genes predicted to encode proteins with a TIR domain that were DE during the time series transcriptome (Fig. 4a). The predicted TIR domain‐containing proteins exhibit temporal expression patterns. Some were upregulated exclusively in the recovered phase (T19–T50), some immediately after viral inoculation, as part of the virocell early response phase (T0), and some transcribed mainly during the infection (T1–T3).

To characterize the expressed TIR domain‐containing genes, we generated structural predictions using AlphaFold 3 (Abramson et al., 2024). Searching the predicted structural models of G. huxleyi TIR domains using Foldseek (van Kempen et al., 2024) showed structural similarity to known, functionally active TIR domains found in bacteria, plants, and humans (Nimma et al., 2021). Notably, while the length of the detected TIR domains varied (ranging from 76 to 128 amino acids) and their sequences differed, the putative catalytic glutamic acid residue in the active site (Essuman et al., 2017; Wan et al., 2019) was conserved (Fig. S9; Table S9). Additionally, TIR domain‐containing genes are typically associated with other domains, such as nucleotide‐binding sites (NBS) and leucine‐rich repeat (LRR) domains. However, the TIR domain‐containing genes in G. huxleyi did not encode such additional domains. It is important to note that there are known TIR domain‐containing proteins that possess only the TIR domain (‘truncated’ TIR) and still function effectively as immune receptors (Nishimura et al., 2017; Santamaría et al., 2019; Tian et al., 2021; Ogden et al., 2023).

To assess whether the temporal expression patterns (Fig. 4a) imply different functions, we examined shared features among the cluster members, such as additional encoded domains and predicted transmembrane regions (Table S9). We found that TIR domain‐containing genes in the virocell clusters (clusters 2 and 3) were predicted to have an extracellular region or multiple transmembrane helices at the N‐terminus. Following these regions was a TIR domain, with structural homology to signal‐producing TIRs. For example, the cogent1101_i1 gene from cluster 2 consists of three main components: an extracellular region, a transmembrane helix, and an intracellular TIR domain (Fig. 4b). The extracellular region showed structural similarities to ligand‐binding domains, such as those found in leucine‐binding and epidermal growth factor (EGF)‐like domains. The TIR domain shared structural homology to signal‐producing TIRs, like the TIR domain from Arabidopsis thaliana disease resistance protein, RPS4. Furthermore, the early expression (0.5 h post‐infection) of TIR domain‐containing genes from cluster 2 resembles the early activation of TIR signaling in plants in response to pathogen‐associated molecular patterns (PAMP) (Bjornson et al., 2021; Tian et al., 2021). This provides initial evidence that TIR domain‐containing genes from the virocell clusters may have a receptor‐like function to generate signaling molecules in response to viral infection.

The TIR domain‐containing genes from the recovery cluster – particularly the two expressed by all resistant strains (Fig. 4a, marked by asterisks) were predicted to localize intracellularly in the cytoplasm (Table S9). These two genes (373c_47547, cogent2074_i1) show high structural homology to signal‐producing TIRs and the Human SARM1 TIR domain (Fig. 4b). SARM1 functions as an NAD+ hydrolyzing enzyme that triggers cell death and neuronal degradation, effectively halting the progression of viral infections (Sundaramoorthy et al., 2020). This supports the potential role of TIR domain‐containing genes from the recovery cluster in facilitating defense to viral infection through NAD+ depletion.

Discussion

Viral infections play an important role in shaping the dynamics of algal blooms by regulating host cell fate (Wilson et al., 2002; Martínez Martínez et al., 2007; Laber et al., 2018; Kranzler et al., 2019). Recent studies have advanced our understanding of viral takeover mechanisms and the extensive reprogramming of the host's metabolome, transcriptome, and genome during infection (Rosenwasser et al., 2014; Yau et al., 2016; Moniruzzaman et al., 2018; Ku et al., 2020). However, the molecular mechanisms underlying algal resistance and anti‐viral response remain largely unknown. By contrast, a wide range of resistance mechanisms has recently been identified in bacteria (Doron et al., 2018). In some cases, dozens of defense systems can be found in a single cyanobacterial genome (Millman et al., 2022).

Resistant phenotype is an outcome of G. huxleyi‐EhV infection

In the G. huxleyi‐EhV system, viral infections often lead to the recovery of resistant host populations that maintain a stable resistant phenotype (Fig. 1b,c) (Frada et al., 2017; Joffe et al., 2024). The recovery process can take several weeks to over a month, depending on the algal and virus strains (Frada et al., 2017; Joffe et al., 2024). Recovered cells may arise through a life cycle transition to haploidy (Fig. S6) or through an alternative mechanism that includes morphological changes and results in diploid, non‐calcified (scale‐bearing) biflagellate cells (Frada et al., 2017; Bousquet et al., 2025). Moreover, even within isogenic populations, phenotypic heterogeneity in resistance levels to viral infection can be observed (Joffe et al., 2024). This highlights the complexity of this host‐virus interaction and the plasticity of host resistance to viral infection.

Our study provides a detailed mapping of the transcriptional landscape during the transition from algal sensitivity to resistance in response to viral infection. At the cellular level, viral infections trigger significant remodeling of the transcriptome, particularly affecting key pathways that are likely to support viral replication. The infection imposes selective pressure, revealing the transcriptional profile associated with resistance. This transcriptional profile was consistently observed across various resistant strains of G. huxleyi, indicating a core expression program. It includes genes involved in signaling pathways related to innate immune responses, genes that participate in the remodeling of lipids and membrane glycans, as well as those associated with nutrient uptake and metabolism (Table 1).

Proposed functional roles of the core resistance genes

The group of 206 resistance genes identified in our study represents a potential molecular signature of G. huxleyi resistance. Among these genes are the three lipid modification enzymes predicted to be involved in synthesizing specific glycosphingolipids (GSL) of resistant cells (resGSLs) (Schleyer et al., 2023) (Tables 1, S6). Sphingolipids (SLs) belong to a lipid class that is enriched in microdomains within cell membranes (lipid rafts). They are essential in pathogen recognition and signaling, in maintaining membrane structure, and are conserved in all eukaryotes and some bacterial taxa (Aguilera‐Romero et al., 2014). Modifying the sphingolipid composition may provide resistance by activating cellular signaling pathways, or by directly preventing viral adsorption and entry to the cells through membranal lipid raft remodeling, the possible mediators of viral entry and exit (Mackinder et al., 2009; Rose et al., 2014).

The resistance genes group also includes several sugar modification enzymes, such as glycosyltransferases (GTs) and glycoside hydrolases (GHs). Membrane‐bound glycans (glycolipids and glycoproteins) are found on both the host cell surface and viral particles. Viruses utilize these glycans for attachment and entry into the cell. Furthermore, giant viruses (NCLDV) are known to encode enzymes for polysaccharide synthesis and modification of sugar nucleotides and glycans (Speciale et al., 2022). In particular, chloroviruses can remodel the cell walls of their host during infection. They encode GTs that synthesize the glycans attached to viral major capsid glycoproteins (DeAngelis et al., 1997; Van Etten et al., 2017). EhV encodes multiple GTs and GHs, suggesting their significance in the viral life cycle. Thus, altering glycan structures on the host cell surface can influence host‐virus interactions and contribute to host resistance. Similarly, in the green alga Ostreococcus tauri, resistant cells that recovered from infection by a giant virus (OtV) upregulated numerous GTs (Yau et al., 2016). Notably, two of those highly expressed GTs (ostta19g00630, ostta19g00120) share sequence homology with sugar modification genes from the resistance genes group. This further supports that glycan modifications are a common metabolic strategy to alter susceptibility to viral infection.

We also detected genes involved in nutrient uptake and metabolism. High transcription levels of nutrient uptake genes usually reflect nutrient‐limited cells (Rokitta et al., 2014; Alexander et al., 2020). Under nutrient limitation, cells induce high‐affinity uptake systems and allocate nutrients from internal storage to handle the scarcity, which might result in a transient resistant state. For example, under P stress, G. huxleyi cells exhibit membrane remodeling by substituting membrane phospholipids with non‐phosphorus lipids (Shemi et al., 2016). EhV infection under P limitation resulted in lower viral production, without apparent bloom demise (Bratbak et al., 1993). The regulation of nutrient‐stress gene expression in resistant G. huxleyi cells might involve SANT/Myb TFs. Myb TFs are known to be expressed in resistant haploid cells (Von Dassow et al., 2009) and during the recovery phase of resistant cells (Table 1) (Frada et al., 2017).

It remains to be determined whether the processes discussed above represent the molecular mechanisms that confer resistance or if they indicate a unique metabolic state of recovered cells. For instance, the expression of nutrient uptake genes, even under replete growth conditions, can arise from an induced response that actively prevents the virus from utilizing these nutrients. Alternatively, this expression could be a secondary effect caused by changes in the cell membrane that block viral entry but also impact nutrient uptake. This upregulation of nutrient uptake genes may reflect a trade‐off in the growth fitness of recovered cells. Indeed, resistance often comes with a fitness cost, which is reflected through reduced growth rates and lower carrying capacity (Lennon et al., 2007; Frickel et al., 2016; Frada et al., 2017; Bedi de Silva et al., 2024; Joffe et al., 2024).

The transcriptional profile of resistant G. huxleyi strains could provide valuable insights into how microalgae defend themselves against giant viruses. Further transcriptional profiling of resistant strains from different microalgae species (Yau et al., 2016) may help determine whether the observed transcriptional changes are general molecular components of resistance to giant virus infections. Resistance may be manifested through diverse cellular mechanisms, and the identified group of resistance‐related genes presents promising candidates for future mechanistic studies.

The potential role of TIR domains in G. huxleyi resistance

Microalgae and macroalgae (multicellular algae) share immune motifs that are common in both plants and animals, such as TIR, nucleotide‐binding site (NBS), and leucine‐rich repeat (LRR) domains (Richter & Levin, 2019; Kloareg et al., 2021). In the red macroalga Pyropia yezoensis, the transcriptional response to infection by the pathogen Pythium porphyrae involves upregulation of genes with LRR, NBS, and TIR domains (Tang et al., 2019). However, to date, TIR‐based anti‐viral systems have not been described in microalgae. Notably, we report for the first time that genes encoding TIR‐containing proteins are involved in the immune response of algal cells.

The temporal expression of TIR domain‐containing proteins during different phases of EhV infection and recovery may suggest various roles. TIR domain‐containing proteins have diverse functions, acting in immune signal transduction or nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) hydrolysis. Recently, studies have shown that TIR domains are involved in bacterial immune response by recognizing phage infection (Ofir et al., 2021). Upon infection, a TIR‐based signaling pathway activates an effector protein that depletes cellular NAD+ levels, leading to cell death that aborts phage replication (Ka et al., 2020; Ofir et al., 2021; Tamulaitiene et al., 2024). In the context of the ongoing evolutionary host‐virus arms race, other studies suggest that phages can overcome bacterial defense by sequestering the TIR‐derived signaling molecule (Leavitt et al., 2022) or by restoring NAD+ levels in infected cells (Osterman et al., 2024).

Depletion of NAD+ levels can abort viral infections, limit the spread of viruses, and protect neighboring cells from infection. However, this mechanism can lead to cell death. In other words, the expression of TIRs prevents the infection but does not save the cell itself. By contrast, resistant G. huxleyi cells express TIR domain‐containing genes without experiencing cell death, even when challenged with EhV. One possible explanation for this phenomenon is that the predicted TIR domain‐containing proteins act only as a secondary line of defense. According to this hypothesis, resistant cells first try to prevent infection through morphological changes, such as glycan and lipid modifications in the cell surface. But should this strategy fail, the cells also pre‐activate transcription of TIRs in a self‐inhibited state to prevent unnecessary cell death. If a virus successfully invades the cell, those intracellular TIRs will be activated, leading to cell death and inhibition of viral spread within the population. Another possible explanation is that these TIRs do not cause cell death; they may function as transcriptional regulators of stress responses. Research in plants has shown that adding 2′,3′‐cyclic adenosine monophosphate (2′,3′‐cAMP), which is synthesized by TIR proteins (Yu et al., 2022), can activate transcriptional reprogramming related to stress responses (Chodasiewicz et al., 2022). Furthermore, another defense system that involves NAD+ depletion without causing cell death has been demonstrated in Bacillus subtilis (Garb et al., 2022). Defense‐associated sirtuins 1 (DSR1) induces a transient and reversible NAD+ depletion, which inhibits phage replication while still allowing bacterial growth (Garb et al., 2022).

Interestingly, TIR domains are relatively rare in viral genomes (Spear et al., 2009; Iyer et al., 2022). However, several NCLDVs that infect eukaryotic microalgae from the haptophyta phylum do encode TIR domain‐containing genes (Gallot‐Lavallée et al., 2015; Bhattacharjee et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025). Among these are Haptolina ericina virus (GenBank: XPO57143.1), Chrysochromulina ericina virus (ALH23162.1), Prymnesium kappa virus (XPO57813.1), and an unidentified species within the Nucleocytoviricota phylum (UZT28792.1). It is known that bacterial pathogens of plants produce bacterial TIR‐like virulence effectors that resemble plant‐derived TIR products (Eastman et al., 2022; Hulin et al., 2023). These effectors could negatively regulate host immunity or manipulate NAD+ metabolism. Therefore, the function of viral‐encoded TIRs in giant viruses in the marine environment is still to be explored, but it has the potential to influence host immune pathways.

Using the core resistance genes as sensitive gene markers

The specific expression patterns of the resistance genes group could be used to study how resistance evolves within a population. Do resistant cells exist before viral infection, persisting as a small subpopulation due to the associated costs of resistance? (Joffe et al., 2024). Alternatively, is resistance induced in infected cells that abort the infection cycle or in uninfected bystander cells as a response to chemical cues released by infected cells? Applying single‐cell RNA‐sequencing (scRNA‐seq) techniques will enable sensitive detection of resistant cells using these potential resistance marker genes (Fromm et al., 2025). Then, combining the simultaneous view of the viral and host transcriptomes at the individual cell level can provide valuable insights into the origin and development of resistant cells in the population.

The resistance genes identified here can provide sensitive gene markers to detect resistant G. huxleyi cells in natural oceanic blooms. Resistant cells may have a significant ecological role in bloom succession as the survivors of viral infections and the seed population of the following year bloom. However, since resistant cells have thus far been identified as non‐calcified, detection and quantification methods are limited, hindering our ability to track the complete life cycle in the natural environment. We propose that using the newly identified marker genes of resistance can provide a sensitive tool to assess the host‐virus arms race during bloom succession and how environmental factors control it.
