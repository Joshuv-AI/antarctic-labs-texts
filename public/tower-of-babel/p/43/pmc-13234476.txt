Commentary on “Opioid-use disorder and reported pain after spine surgery: Risk-group patterns in cognitive-appraisal processes in a longitudinal cohort study”
Authors: Tejas Purimetla, Zorica Buser
Published: 2026

Skolasky et al. recently published a aimed to analyze the potential impact of different cognitive appraisal processes as moderators of opioid-use disorder (OUD) risk during postoperative recovery [1]. This is a highly relevant topic, as long-term opioid use, particularly for managing postoperative pain associated with spinal disease, has been linked to poor outcomes in patients undergoing spine surgery [[2], [3], [4]]. As a result, there is growing emphasis on minimizing postoperative narcotic use while still optimizing adequate pain control following spinal surgery.

The authors reviewed the electronic medical records of patients undergoing treatment for back and/or leg problems, neck or arm/shoulder conditions, or isolated spinal deformities. Surgical procedures included discectomy, decompression, and fusion. Data on patient-reported outcomes, cognitive factors, and pre- and postoperative narcotic use, were collected both preoperatively and at 3 months postprocedure.

In total, 342 patients met eligibility criteria. The most frequently represented spinal condition was lumbar disease (70%), followed by cervical pathology (45%) and deformity. On average, participants demonstrated severe disability, with Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) or Neck Disability Index (NDI) scores around 44. They also had below-average PROMIS physical and mental health scores and above-average PROMIS pain interference scores.

In bivariable analyses, several notable differences emerged across OUD-risk groups. Notably, the high-risk group exhibited the greatest levels of disability prior to surgery, the highest levels of pain interference presurgically, and the highest pain scores at 3 months postoperatively. Interestingly, the low-to-moderate risk group reported the highest presurgical pain scores. With respect to cognitive appraisal processes, differences across OUD-risk groups were observed both preoperatively and at 3 months postoperatively. Patients in the high-risk group were more likely, prior to surgery, to endorse problem-resolution goals and emphasize recent health problems, while being less likely to focus on recovery or habituation and less likely to compare themselves to others.

Overall, this study represents a moderately large sample and is among the few to investigate cognitive appraisal processes in relation to OUD risk. Its longitudinal design strengthens the analysis by allowing for the assessment of changes in pain perception and cognition over time. Furthermore, the emphasis on cognitive processes, rather than solely numerical pain intensity, offers more clinically meaningful insight and may help inform interventions such as cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT).

However, an important limitation is the study’s retrospective design, which limits control over patient selection and introduces the potential for bias. Despite this, the findings are thought-provoking, as they help clarify the relationship between how patients cognitively process pain and how those patterns influence reported pain levels. These insights should encourage clinicians to more carefully consider the role of psychological interventions, such as CBT, in modifying maladaptive cognitive appraisal patterns and potentially reducing both pain and opioid-related risk following spine surgery.
