Engineering trispecific IL-2 receptor agonistic antibodies through geometry optimization for enhanced Treg targeting
Authors: Valentina Lykhopiy, Emilie Pollenus, Laurie Rangan, Michelle Stakenborg, Tugsan Tezil, Michiel Varheust, Giel Tanghe, Nina Lambrechts, Vanshika Malviya, Eva De Langhe, Gianluca Matteoli, Christophe Blanchetot, Luc Van Rompaey, Susan M Schlenner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRegulatory T cells (Tregs) are central to maintaining immune tolerance, and their selective activation via interleukin-2 (IL-2) signaling presents a promising therapeutic strategy for autoimmune diseases and transplant rejection. Here, we develop IL-2 receptor (IL-2R) agonists, employing trispecific antibodies that simultaneously engage all three IL-2R subunits. This design preferentially activates and expands Tregs over conventional T cells and natural killer cells that express the dimeric IL-2R (CD122 and CD132). Incorporation of a second CD25-targeting VHH domain confers further enhanced specificity and potency for CD25⁺ Tregs. Extensive engineering of antibody geometry was then critical to maximize Treg selectivity, highlighting the importance of spatial configuration in receptor engagement. This study reports the successful development of trispecific IL-2R-targeting antibodies and significantly expands the potential of antibody-based immunomodulation. By selectively activating the high-affinity trimeric IL-2R on Tregs, this versatile platform offers a differentiated and promising strategy for the treatment of autoimmune diseases and transplant rejection.Subject terms: Antibody therapy, Interleukins, T cells, Autoimmunity

Introduction

Autoimmune diseases are characterized by an aberrant immune response against self-antigens, leading to chronic inflammation and tissue damage. Central to the prevention of such pathological conditions are FOXP3+ regulatory T cells (Tregs), which play an indispensable role in maintaining immune homeostasis1.

Interleukin-2 (IL-2) is a critical cytokine for Treg biology, supporting their differentiation, survival, and suppressive function, and maintaining the balance between regulatory and effector T cells2. IL-2 regulates immune responses by engaging the IL-2 receptor (IL-2R), which exists in two forms distinguished by their affinity. The intermediate-affinity IL-2R (Kd ~ 1 nM) is composed of IL-2Rβ (CD122) and common γ chain (IL-2Rγ, CD132, γc), and is widely expressed, particularly on CD8+ memory T cells and natural killer (NK) cells. Upon IL-2 binding, IL-2Rβ and IL-2Rγ (βγ) dimerization activates downstream signaling cascades, including JAK1-JAK3-STAT5, PI3K-AKT-mTOR, and MAPK pathways, driving the transcription of IL-2-responsive genes3. Cells can enhance their sensitivity to IL-2 by expressing IL-2Rα (CD25), which associates with βγ to form the high-affinity trimeric receptor complex (αβγ; Kd ~ 10 pM). While CD25 itself does not mediate signaling, it acts as a cell-specific subunit that enhances IL-2 capture and responsiveness4,5. Treg cells constitutively express high levels of CD25, enabling efficient formation of the high-affinity IL-2R complex, whereas other immune cells, such as activated CD4+ and CD8+ effector T cells, NK cell subsets, and NKT cells, maintain high expression of IL-2Rβ and γc but only transiently upregulate CD25 upon activation.

Clinical trials have shown that low-dose IL-2 therapy preferentially expands Tregs and ameliorates autoimmune and inflammatory conditions, including type 1 diabetes (T1D), systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) and graft-versus-host disease (GvHD)6–8. Nevertheless, IL-2 therapy is limited by short serum half-life and off-target activation of non-Treg populations9,10. Strategies to overcome these limitations include extending IL-2 half-life through immunoglobulin fusion11 or PEGylation12, and enhancing Treg selectivity by engineering IL-2 variants with altered receptor affinities13,14, or complexing IL-2 with antibodies that bias its binding toward the high-affinity trimeric IL-2R15–18. Given its critical role in defining Treg identity and function, CD25 has also emerged as a key therapeutic target for Treg-focused interventions19,20.

Obligate multispecific antibodies are engineered molecules whose activity depends on the simultaneous engagement and physical linkage of multiple targets. Bispecific antibodies, and by extension obligate multispecific antibodies, are exquisite therapeutic entities that have been developed to expand the scope of therapeutic application of antibodies21. Multispecific antibodies offer the ability to simultaneously engage multiple targets, potentially improving the therapeutic index of IL-2-based therapies for autoimmune and inflammatory indications. Notably, bispecific anti-IL-2Rβγ antibodies that mimic IL-2 activity can preferentially stimulate effector T cells22–24. Building on these concepts, we hypothesized that an antibody-based IL-2 mimetic capable of simultaneously engaging all three IL-2 receptor subunits could selectively and potently activate Tregs, offering a superior strategy for immune modulation.

Here, we describe the generation of single-domain antibodies (VHHs) against the three human IL-2R subunits. By fusing these VHHs, we engineered bispecific antibodies targeting the dimeric IL-2R and trispecific antibodies incorporating a CD25-targeting domain to enhance Treg specificity. We evaluated these antibodies in ex vivo assays and a humanized in vivo expansion model to assess their binding affinities and functional activities in promoting selective Treg proliferation. Subsequent optimization efforts focused on further enhancing Treg-biased activity, yielding trispecific antibody candidates with improved therapeutic potential for the treatment of autoimmune diseases.

Results

Development and characterization of antibodies binding to the IL-2R subunits

VHHs specific for the IL-2R subunits were generated by DNA immunization of outbred llamas with plasmids expressing the full-length human IL-2R subunits allowing for the generation of diverse VHH libraries (Fig. 1a). We confirmed strong immune responses against each receptor subunit by testing llama serum before and after the immunizations (Supplementary Fig. 1). The isolated lymphocytes from immunized llamas were used to construct phage display complementary DNA (cDNA) libraries encoding immunoglobulin VHH regions. After three rounds of phage selections and screening on human recombinant proteins by ELISA and SPR, we identified 85 IL-2Rα/CD25-, 153 IL-2Rβ/CD122-, 92 IL-2Rγ/CD132-specific clones with unique VHH sequences belonging to 15, 38 and 7 VHH complementarity-determining regions (CDR)3 families, respectively (Fig. 1b–d). Mouse cross-reactivity was assessed; however, most VHHs did not exhibit cross-reactivity with murine IL-2R subunits (Supplementary Table 1). IL-2 blocking and non-blocking VHH families were identified for IL-2Rα (data not shown). Furthermore, epitope binning using SPR determined that IL-2Rα-, IL-2Rβ-, IL-2Rγ-specific clones bound to 3, 4 and 5 distinct epitopes on their respective targets (Supplementary Fig. 2,3).

To generate antibody formats for functional characterization, the VHH cDNAs from 10-13 clones per IL-2 receptor subunit were selected to represent diverse affinities, IL-2-blocking capacities and CDR3 families (Fig. 1b–d). Monospecific anti-human IL-2Rα, IL-2Rβ and IL-2Rγ VHH were expressed as Fc fusions in mammalian cells. Upon selection of one representative clone per epitope bin for IL-2Rβ and IL-2Rγ binders, 16 bispecific bivalent anti-IL-2Rβγ antibodies (βγVHH) were also generated. To enable controlled Fab-arm exchange (cFAE) technology25, anti-IL-2Rα VHHs (αVHH) were fused to a human IgG1 Fc domain harboring the K409R mutation, while monospecific anti-IL-2Rβ and IL-2Rγ VHHs, as well as the bispecific βγVHHs, were fused to an Fc domain containing the F405L mutation (Fig. 1e, Supplementary Fig. 4). All Fc constructs additionally incorporated LALAPG mutations (L234A, L235A and P329G) to eliminate immune effector functions26. Using cFAE, αVHH and βγVHH molecules were recombined to efficiently generate trispecific anti-IL-2Rαβγ (αβγVHH) antibodies.

Bispecific anti-IL-2Rβγ & trispecific anti-IL-2Rαβγ antibodies induce dose-dependent STAT5 phosphorylation

The anti-IL-2R antibodies were further tested in cell-based assays for binding strength, interference with IL-2 binding, and functional activity demonstrating agonism using HEK293 cells that express all three IL-2R subunits (hereafter referred to as HEKαβγ) (Fig. 2a). Monospecific bivalent antibodies–the building blocks of bi- and trispecific cross-linking antibodies–varied in binding strength (Fig. 2b, c). Monospecific bivalent αVHH and βVHH displayed high and intermediate cell binding, as measured by flow cytometry using a mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) readout, while cell binding was low for γVHH, likely reflecting the low CD132 expression on these cells (Fig. 2a–c).

Upon incorporation of an anti-IL-2Rα-binding VHH to generate trispecific antibodies, the αVHH became the primary determinant of binding strength (Fig. 2d, and Supplementary Fig. 5). Trispecific antibodies containing the strongest anti-IL-2Rα binders (αVHH-2, αVHH-8 and αVHH-10), which exhibited the lowest EC50 values in monospecific binding assays (Fig. 2c), showed correspondingly strong cell binding. In contrast, trispecific clones incorporating weaker anti-IL-2Rα binders, such as αVHH-3, αVHH-5 and αVHH-11, displayed reduced binding potency (Fig. 2d, and Supplementary Fig. 5b).

Notably, differences in maximal binding responses (Emax) observed in the cell-based assays likely reflect factors beyond affinity alone, including epitope accessibility, receptor presentation at the cell surface, and the stability of antibody-receptor interactions during the assay time frame. Consistent with this interpretation, surface plasmon resonance (SPR) measurements revealed broadly comparable association rate constants (kon) across the αVHH panel, whereas differences in dissociation kinetics (koff) correlated with the apparent Emax values observed in the cell-binding assay (Fig. 2c, Supplementary Table 1). Specifically, αVHH constructs exhibiting lower Emax values, including αVHH-11, αVHH-1, αVHH-6 and αVHH-2, displayed faster dissociation rates by SPR, consistent with reduced signal accumulation during the flow cytometric binding assay despite similar apparent affinities. In addition, several monospecific VHHs targeting each IL-2R subunit interfered with IL-2 binding to its receptor to varying degrees (Supplementary Fig. 6a). Strong anti-IL-2Rα binders, such as αVHH-8 and αVHH-10, efficiently inhibited IL-2 binding, and this neutralization property was retained in trispecific antibodies incorporating these αVHH (Supplementary Fig. 6b).

We next evaluated the ability of our antibody constructs to induce IL-2R signaling in HEKαβγ cells by measuring STAT5 phosphorylation (pSTAT5), the principal downstream event of both the dimeric and trimeric IL-2R engagement3. As expected, none of the monospecific antibodies alone, nor combinations of αVHH with either βVHH or γVHH, triggered pSTAT5 activation (Supplementary Fig. 7a). In contrast, most antibodies incorporating both IL-2Rβ- and IL-2Rγ-specific VHH domains effectively activated the JAK/STAT5 signaling pathway, with some of the βγVHH constructs inducing pSTAT5 levels comparable to those triggered by recombinant IL-2 (Fig. 2e). Although IL2Rβγ engagement is sufficient to induce signaling, the bispecific βγVHH-11 did not achieve the maximal pSTAT5 responses observed with native IL-2 in CD8⁺ T cells and NK cells. This likely reflects differences in how synthetic βγ-targeting antibodies stabilize the signaling-competent IL2Rβγ complex, as native IL-2 is evolutionarily optimized to promote productive receptor geometry and sustained signaling. Consistent with previous reports on IL-2Rβγ-targeting surrogate agonists23, antibody-mediated βγ cross-linking can result in attenuated signaling relative to IL-2 despite engaging the same receptor subunits. Only constructs incorporating the non-functional γVHH-35, which exhibited no detectable activity across all assays, failed to induce pSTAT5 (Supplementary Fig. 7b). Remarkably, several βγVHH constructs demonstrated robust signaling activity (Fig. 2f, yellow box, Supplementary Fig. 7b) despite exhibiting relatively low binding potency (high EC50) (Fig. 2d, yellow box, and Supplementary Fig. 5a). These results demonstrate that heterodimerization of IL-2Rα with either IL-2Rβ or IL-2Rγ is insufficient for signaling induction and underscores the necessity for a cis-targeting approach of dual engagement of IL-2Rβ and IL-2Rγ for receptor activation in agreement with previous literature27.

It is interesting to note that the efficacy of the pSTAT5 activation, i.e. maximum induced signal (Emax), of trispecific αβγVHH was correlated solely with the potency of the corresponding parental bispecific βγVHH clone (Fig. 2f, and Supplementary Fig. 7b) even though the maximum cell binding in general correlated with the potency of the αVHH clones (Fig. 2d). These results are in line with the mechanism of cytokine receptor activation, whereby the high-affinity receptor subunit–here IL-2Rα or CD25–dictates cellular specificity as well as the dose sensitivity, while the intermediate affinity receptor subunits–IL-2Rβγ or CD122/CD132–are responsible for the complex assembly and signaling duration and strength4.

Consequently, we identified βγVHH /αβγVHH antibody pairs, where the addition of an anti-CD25 VHH improved the potency or dose-sensitivity on HEKαβγ, demonstrated by the fold change between the EC50 of trispecific αβγVHH and the EC50 of their parental bispecific βγVHH, meaning that the trispecific αβγVHH would be more selective for CD25+ cells (Fig. 2g, and Supplementary Fig. 8). Remarkably, all the αβγVHH containing βγVHH-11 demonstrated the highest improved potency (Fig. 2g, yellow box, Supplementary Fig. 8b). Although several αβγVHH containing strong αVHH binders had improved potency over their bispecific parental antibody, the addition of αVHH-2 resulted in the greatest fold change in potency for all trispecific αβγVHH (Fig. 2g, orange box, and Supplementary Fig. 8). This implies that addition of the αVHH-2 improves the pSTAT5 activation potency of any bispecific βγVHH and could be more universally applied to engineer Treg-biasing antibodies19,20. Accordingly, we selected αβγVHH-48 (comprising αVHH-2, βVHH-19, γVHH-27) as our lead molecule for further ex vivo and in vivo evaluation of Treg selectivity, based on its potent activity on HEKαβγ cells (EC50 = 0.015 nM), which was comparable to that of human IL-2 (EC50 = 0.010 nM; Supplementary Fig. 7, 8).

Trispecific anti-IL-2Rαβγ antibodies preferentially activate and expand Tregs ex vivo

Although the HEKαβγ and HEKβγ reporter cell lines provide an excellent and controlled platform for ranking ligand binding and agonist activity, they do not reflect physiological IL-2 receptor subunit densities, as illustrated by comparison of Fig. 2a and Fig. 3a. To account for these non-physiological expression levels, we next evaluated Treg selectivity and functional efficacy of the anti-IL-2R antibodies in ex vivo assays using rested human PBMCs. Tregs, distinguished by their high CD25 expression, were the intended target population, whereas CD3negCD56+ NK cells, which express high levels of the dimeric IL-2Rβγ but lack IL-2Rα, served as a key population for assessing potential off-target dimeric IL-2 receptor activation5,28 (Fig. 3a). To comprehensively evaluate off-target effects, we analyzed responses to CD8+ T cells, conventional CD4+ T cells, and CD3+CD56+ NKT cells, which express substantially lower CD25 per cell levels (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Fig. 9a). αβγVHH-48 was compared with its parental bispecific βγVHH-11, with other αβγVHHs sharing the same βγVHH but differing in their αVHH domains, and with recombinant human IL-2 (Fig. 3b).

Both the bispecific βγVHH-11 and the trispecific αβγVHH constructs, including αβγVHH-48, induced JAK/STAT5 signaling predominantly in Tregs, with lower activation observed in non-Treg populations (Fig. 3c, gating strategy shown in Supplementary Fig. 9a). The activation profiles of the trispecific antibodies closely resembled that of IL-2 but displayed a markedly increased potency toward Tregs, as reflected by lower EC50 values relative to other lymphocyte subsets. Tregs were particularly dose-sensitive to trispecific constructs incorporating high-affinity CD25-binding VHHs that target the IL-2 binding site (αVHH-2, -8 and -10), whereas constructs containing a low-affinity CD25 binder (αVHH-5) or high-affinity CD25 binders recognizing non-IL-2 epitopes (αVHH-3, -4 and -11) exhibited reduced potency (Fig. 3c, first graph; and Supplementary Fig. 6). This difference correlated with CD25-binding potency (EC50) but not with maximal signaling amplitude (Emax) (Fig. 2c).

The tested molecules activated conventional CD4+ T cells with a similar qualitative pattern but with lower potency and efficacy (Emax), consistent with their lower CD25 expression, whereas IL-2Rβhigh NK(T) cells did not display comparable activation. Although αβγVHH-61 (containing αVHH-10) was more potent on Tregs than αβγVHH-48 (containing αVHH-2), both constructs showed similarly increased potency (reduced EC50) on conventional CD4⁺ T cells. When selectivity was assessed using ΔAUC, which integrates both potency and maximal response, αβγVHH-48 and αβγVHH-61 exhibited the highest Treg-to-non-Treg ratios and thus greater Treg selectivity than other trispecific constructs and IL-2 (Fig. 3d). In terms of efficacy, reflected by the maximum pSTAT5 signal, trispecific antibodies were comparable to their parental bispecific βγVHH-11, highlighting that overall efficacy is governed by the βγVHH component rather than modulation through the CD25-binding domain. Together, these results demonstrate that the trispecific constructs, like IL-2, preferentially and potently activate Tregs, and that incorporation of a high-affinity anti-CD25 VHH substantially enhances both the potency and selectivity of IL-2R agonism towards the Treg compartment.

To test whether αβγVHH-48 not only agonises but also expands Tregs ex vivo, we purified and expanded Tregs from total PBMCs and treated them for 7 days in the presence of αβγVHH-48, its parental clone βγVHH-11 or human IL-2 (Fig. 3e, for gating strategy see Supplementary Fig. 9b). Although Tregs expanded less with αβγVHH-48 than with human IL-2, αβγVHH-48 was more potent than the βγVHH-11, again demonstrating the importance of CD25 targeting.

To assess whether preferential STAT5 activation in Tregs drives their selective proliferation within human PBMC cultures, PBMCs were labeled with a proliferation dye and cultured for 7 days with αβγVHH-48, αβγVHH-61, their parental clone βγVHH-11, or human IL-2 (Fig. 3f, g, for gating strategy see Supplementary Fig. 9c). Immuneprofiling of Treg after 7-day culture in the presence of αβγVHH-48 for surface proteins whose expression marks immunosuppressive capacity indicates that suppressive capacity in Treg is retained (Supplementary Fig. 10). The trispecific anti-IL-2R antibodies αβγVHH-48 and αβγVHH-61 acted as IL-2 mimetics, inducing robust Treg proliferation with potency and efficacy comparable to native IL-2, whereas the bispecific βγVHH-11 elicited minimal Treg proliferation. Expansion of Tconv, CD8, NK and NKT cells was minimal and dose-dependent, similar to native IL-2. Thus, trispecific antibody formats confer enhanced Treg selectivity at the level of signaling while maintaining IL-2-like proliferative activity across lymphocyte populations. At higher antibody concentrations, a modest reduction in proliferation was observed, consistent with non-productive receptor over-engagement in dense cellular systems rather than reduced intrinsic agonistic potency. The enhanced Treg selectivity of trispecific antibodies relative to βγVHH-11 is attributed to their CD25-targeting VHH domain, which increases potency. Importantly, the differential response of Tregs to trispecific versus bispecific antibodies is not due to sequestration of βγVHH by CD122high non-Tregs, as this would have shifted non-Treg potency, which was not observed. Furthermore, Tregs cultured with trispecific antibodies or IL-2 exhibited a significantly higher proliferation index than those treated with βγVHH-11 (Fig. 3f, g).

The trispecific anti-IL-2Rαβγ antibody, αβγVHH-48, with higher potency and specificity for Treg than IL-2 was selected as the lead candidate for preclinical in vivo testing.

The trispecific anti-IL-2Rαβγ antibody αβγVHH-48 preferentially increases Tregs in a xenogeneic NSG T cell expansion model in vivo

Given the established association between Treg expansion and clinical benefit8,29, we investigated whether αβγVHH-48 could preferentially expand Tregs in vivo. To this end, we employed a humanized NSG mouse model, in which human PBMCs from healthy donors were engrafted into immunodeficient NSG mice, leading to rapid activation and expansion of human T cells. NSG mice were injected with human PBMCs, then 1 day after the graft and 7 days thereafter the mice were injected with antibodies or phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (Fig. 4a). We measured the proportions and numbers of human lymphocyte subsets at day 7 (before the antibody injection) and day 14 by flow cytometry. As a first step, we titrated our lead antibody αβγVHH-48 to determine the dose at day 1 that would maximize Treg expansion at day 9 while minimizing the expansion of non-Treg cell subsets. The optimal dose was established as 0.03 µg, at which the Treg-to-non-Treg ratios were highest (Supplementary Fig. 11).

Treatment with αβγVHH-48 selectively increased frequency of Tregs without altering the frequencies of conventional CD4+ T cells or CD8+ T cells (Fig. 4b). This selective expansion led to a significant increase of Treg-to-CD4+ and Treg-to-CD8+ T cell ratios (Fig. 4c). While absolute cell counts correlated with higher overall engraftment in αβγVHH-48-treated mice (Fig. 4d, and Supplementary Fig. 12a), the increased Treg-to-non-Treg ratios were consistent (Fig. 4e).

Further analysis revealed an increase in Ki-67+ Treg following αβγVHH-48 treatment, indicative of enhanced proliferation. In contrast, non-Treg cell populations exhibited greater Ki-67 expression in response to βγVHH-11 (Fig. 4f). Additionally, a trend toward increased CD25 MFI was observed selectively in Tregs after treatment with αβγVHH-48, while FOXP3 expression remained stable (Supplementary Fig. 12b–c). These data were confirmed in two additional independent experiments (Supplementary Fig. 12d–s).

Further, we compared the performance of αβγVHH-48 to low dose (LD) IL-2 and an Fc-fused IL-2 mutein (V91K)9 (Supplementary Fig. 13) and included bodyweight and GvHD disease scoring. Interestingly, while LD IL-2 expanded Tregs significantly at day 7, there was also substantial expansion of effector T cells ultimately resulting in rapid disease progression. In comparison, the IL-2 mutein and αβγVHH-48 (as well as DC41 – an optimized geometry of αβγVHH-48 presented in the next paragraph) performed comparably in Treg specificity and disease outcome. Notably, the anti-CD25 VHH used to construct αβγVHH-48 competes at the IL-2R with IL-2 binding which may reduce Treg suppression through IL-2 sequestration. Usage of non-competing anti-CD25 VHHs may further improve the performance of the trispecific antibody.

Together, these findings demonstrate that IL-2R crosslinking by the trispecific antibody αβγVHH-48 selectively targets CD25-expressing Treg cells in vivo, promoting their preferential expansion over other T cell subsets.

Optimized geometries of αβγVHH-48 further increase Treg specificity and potency

The antibody format plays a crucial role in its functional properties21. The trispecific antibody αβγVHH-48 is composed of an anti-CD25 VHH (αVHH-2) on one arm, and of two VHHs (βVHH-19 and γVHH-27) on the other arm, linked to a human Fc domain via a (G4S)3 linker. While superior to its parental CD25-deficient bispecific antibody, αβγVHH-48 can still activate CD25-negative or CD25-low T and NK cells albeit at higher concentrations (Fig. 3b–d). CD25, the high-affinity receptor subunit predominantly expressed on Tregs, dictates cellular specificity as well as dose-sensitivity (EC50) of IL-2R ligands4,20. We hypothesized that increasing the avidity for CD25 could further improve Treg selectivity of IL-2R agonists. To test this, we engineered an additional anti-CD25 VHH (αVHH-2), rendering the final constructs bivalent for CD25. We then evaluated the impact of antibody format on function by designing five αβγVHH-48 variants with identical specificity and valency but differing geometries (Fig. 5a, and Supplementary Fig. 14, 15). These geometry variants differ exclusively in the relative spatial arrangement and ordering of the VHH domains, while linker length, valency, and overall domain composition were kept constant across all constructs. All variants were produced with good expression yield and high purity and displayed favorable early-stage developability characteristics, enabling a direct functional comparison of geometry-dependent effects (Supplementary Figs. 14, 15).

The optimized trispecific formats were next evaluated in functional ex vivo assays using rested PBMCs (Fig. 5b–d, and Supplementary Fig. 16,17). All variants exhibited greater potency (lower EC50) than the original αβγVHH-48, as expected due to their bivalency for CD25 (Fig. 5b). Notably, the efficacy (maximum pSTAT5 signal, Emax) varied between constructs and this could be attributed to the distinct spatial positioning of the VHHs that bind IL-2Rβ and IL-2Rγ, suggesting a key role of the geometry of the VHH between each other in the antibodies.

Antibodies DC41 and DC44 exhibited potent Treg activation, achieving maximal STAT5 phosphorylation at single-digit picomolar or even femtomolar concentrations (Fig. 5b, and Supplementary Fig. 16,17). However, this marked increase in potency on Tregs was not accompanied with a proportional gain in selectivity, as these variants also induced STAT5 signaling in non-Treg populations, albeit only at 100- to 1000-fold higher concentrations compared to Treg cells (Fig. 5b, and Supplementary Fig. 16,17). In contrast, geometry variants DC43, DC45 and DC60 acted as partial agonists but showed the greatest differential potency between Treg and non-Treg subsets (Fig. 5b, and Supplementary Fig. 16, 17). Interestingly, despite a different strategy of reaching Treg selectivity, the Fc-fused IL-2 mutein V91K displayed a partial agonism comparable to DC43 and DC60. The V91K mutation decreases binding to CD122 and increases dependence on CD259. This demonstrates that increasing CD25 binding can be an equally performing strategy to reducing CD122 binding. Because EC50 values could not be reliably determined for all constructs, we quantified activity using fold change in area under the curve (ΔAUC), a parameter that integrates both potency (EC50) and efficacy (Emax) into a single measure. Notably, the AUC was calculated directly from the experimental data range, without extrapolation to lower concentrations, and thus underestimates both AUC values and their differences. Despite this, DC41 and DC44 displayed the highest ΔAUC between Treg and non-Treg populations, underscoring their superior selectivity and therapeutic promise (Fig. 5c). Furthermore, at picomolar concentrations, DC41 and DC44 elicited stronger pSTAT5 responses in Treg than IL-2 and other constructs, while non-Treg subsets showed minimal per-cell STAT5 phosphorylation (MFI) (Fig. 5d).

To evaluate the ability of αβγVHH-48 variants to promote Treg proliferation ex vivo, purified Tregs were cultured for seven days in the presence of either αβγVHH-48, its geometry-modified variants, Fc-fused IL-2 mutein or recombinant IL-2 (Fig. 5e). Among the tested constructs, DC41 expanded Tregs as effectively as αβγVHH-48, whereas other variants – such as DC43 – induced robust pSTAT5 signaling per cell (MFI) but were less effective in supporting proliferation. Notably, Treg expansion correlated more closely with the maximal pSTAT5 signal per cell (MFI) than with the percentage of pSTAT5-positive cells, consistent with the idea that stronger intracellular signaling promotes greater proliferative responses (Fig. 5d, e). This was further demonstrated by the IL-2 mutein V91K inducing selective STAT5 phosphorylation (Fig. 5b), however at per cell levels that were too low to induce Treg proliferation (Fig. 5d, e). All trispecific formats revealed a trade-off between Treg potency and off-target activation, highlighting the inherent challenge of enhancing efficacy while preserving selectivity (Fig. 5a–d).

Discussion

Recent advances in IL-2 immunotherapies for autoimmune and inflammatory diseases have demonstrated encouraging results, particularly with low-dose IL-2 regiments that preferentially expand Treg cells and improve clinical outcomes7,8. However, next generation engineered IL-2 analogs continue to face significant challenges, including narrow therapeutic windows and off-target toxicity9,10. In this study, we developed trispecific VHH-based agonistic antibodies that selectively target Tregs through engagement of the high-affinity trimeric IL-2 receptor. Our lead antibody selectively activated and expanded Tregs ex vivo and in vivo. Notably, Treg selectivity was markedly enhanced by incorporating an additional CD25-targeting VHH. Building on this finding, we optimized antibody geometry, generating variants with improved Treg specificity and increased Treg-to-non-Treg ratios in both ex vivo and in vivo models.

Receptor architecture – specifically, the proximity and orientation of receptor subunits – is a crucial determinant of cytokine-mediated signal transduction30,31. In the context of multispecific antibody agonists, effective receptor activation depends not only on the spatial orientation of the targeted epitopes but also on the structural flexibility of the antibody itself. Excessive separation or suboptimal alignment between extracellular domains can impair receptor clustering and reduce signaling efficiency30,31. Accordingly, engagement of IL-2Rβ and IL-2Rγ alone is not sufficient to predict signaling potency, as receptor geometry and complex stability critically determine downstream signal strength. Synthetic βγ-directed agonists, including bispecific antibodies, may therefore exhibit reduced efficacy on βγ-expressing effector cells compared to IL-2, even when binding the same receptor chains. In our study, bispecific anti-IL-2Rβγ constructs successfully induced IL-2 signaling by physically approximating IL-2Rβ and IL-2Rγ subunits, enabled by the flexible linkers connecting the target-binding VHH domains. Several constructs elicited STAT5 phosphorylation with potency (EC50) and efficacy (Emax) comparable to recombinant IL-2.

Receptor signaling strength did not correlate with the individual affinities of the IL-2Rβ- and IL-2Rγ-specific VHHs, suggesting that epitope location–and consequently the geometry of receptor engagement–plays a more decisive role than binding strength alone. Similarly, no correlation was observed between VHH affinity and cell-type selectivity. In contrast, only the incorporation of an anti-CD25 VHH conferred pronounced Treg selectivity. Trispecific constructs bearing this additional specificity demonstrated over 100-fold greater potency on Tregs ex vivo compared to IL-2, with minimal activation of CD25-negative cells. Notably, anti-CD25 VHHs that competed with IL-2 binding exhibited both the strongest CD25 binding and superior function, potentially by promoting an IL-2 receptor that more closely resembles the native IL-2-bound state than non-competitive CD25 binders. Although IL-2 sequestration via CD25 is a key mechanism of Treg-mediated suppression, particularly of CD8 T cells32, and may therefore represent a therapeutic limitation for IL-2-competitive designs, we demonstrate that this constraint can be mitigated through the incorporation of additional CD25-binding VHH and optimization of antibody geometry. Use of IL-2-non-competitive CD25 binders may further preserve IL-2 capture while maintaining high on-target activity.

In vivo, the lead trispecific αβγVHH-48 selectively expanded Tregs in a humanized mouse model, significantly increasing both Treg-to-CD4⁺ T cell and Treg-to-CD8⁺ T cell ratios without stimulating conventional T cells. Constructs lacking CD25-binding capacity failed to replicate this selectivity, underscoring the essential role of CD25 engagement in directing Treg-biased responses. This finding underlines the role of CD25 as a key cell-specific receptor subunit that serves to target the cytokine4,5,20.

Previous studies have shown that engineering ligand geometry can strongly influence signaling strength and cell-type bias. For instance, re-orienting erythropoietin receptor dimers using diabodies was shown to modulate signaling output30, an approach later extended to IL-2 and type I interferons23, as well as to soluble cytokine adapters that redirect IL-10, TGF-β, IL-23, or IL-17 signaling toward IL-2 or IL-10 receptors33. Related strategies have also been applied to generate antibody-based cytokine mimetics, such as for IL-1834. Building on this concept, we refined Treg targeting by incorporating a second anti-CD25 VHH and empirically modified the antibody geometry by rearranging the positioning of individual VHH domains within the molecule. The resulting geometric variants exhibited variable efficacy, as measured by maximum pSTAT5 signaling, compared to αβγVHH-48 and recombinant IL-2. Among these variants, several trispecific antibodies – now bivalent for CD25 – achieved pico- to femtomolar potency on Tregs while maintaining minimal activity on CD25-negative cells. Consistent with this, a similar strategy of leveraging avidity to enhance efficacy has been successfully applied in second-generation anti-CD20 T cell engagers35. These findings highlight the critical role of trispecific VHH avidity, conferred by CD25 bivalency, in enhancing both the potency and efficacy of trispecific antibodies. The combination of exceptional potency and the extended serum half-life of monoclonal antibodies could yield favorable pharmacokinetics, making these molecules promising candidates for the treatment of autoimmune diseases. Notably, despite identical domain composition and linker lengths, no clear structural features explained the observed differences in activity. SPR analyses using individual IL-2R subunits revealed no significant differences in binding kinetics or maximal binding across geometries, indicating that paratope accessibility was not impaired (Supplementary Fig. 18 and Supplementary Table 2). This suggests that the functional differences arise from geometry-dependent effects at the assembled receptor complex on the cell surface, i.e., how the distinct VHH domains engage IL-2Rβ and IL-2Rγ subunits, rather than from steric interference detectable in single-receptor assays. Together, these findings underscore the need for empirical screening and antibody engineering approaches to identify optimal multispecific designs36.

To advance this research towards clinical translation, several key areas should be further investigated. Although Treg IL-2R signaling predominantly activates the STAT5 pathway37–consistent with our data demonstrating preferential Treg activation via STAT5 in primary cells–additional non-STAT pathways, including PI3K-AKT, MAPK, and mTOR, are also implicated in modulating Treg stability, metabolism, and suppressive function3,23. Elucidating the contributions of these alternative pathways could provide further insights into the mechanisms underlying in vivo efficacy. Additionally, more detailed phenotypic and functional characterization of in vivo-expanded Tregs could provide insight into their activation profile, suppressive potency, and lineage stability. In parallel, the VHH-based antibodies developed in this study should be evaluated in disease-relevant in vivo models. As these antibodies lack cross-reactivity with the murine receptor, such studies will necessitate the use of fully humanized IL-2/IL-2R models.

In the broader context of IL-2-based therapies, several strategies have been developed to bias IL-2 activity toward Tregs, including low-dose IL-2 regimens, IL-2 muteins with reduced IL-2Rβγ engagement, and antibody-IL-2 complexes that modulate receptor affinity or cytokine exposure9. While these approaches have demonstrated clinical promise, they frequently involve trade-offs between selectivity, signaling strength, pharmacokinetics, and residual activation of effector T or NK cells, resulting in narrow therapeutic windows. In contrast, the trispecific antibodies described here achieve Treg bias primarily through CD25-directed avidity and geometry-dependent receptor engagement rather than by attenuating cytokine activity, thereby preserving IL-2-like proliferative capacity while enhancing Treg-focused signaling. A limitation shared with other Treg-biased IL-2 modalities is that complete exclusion of effector cell engagement remains challenging, particularly at higher exposures; however, the modularity of our VHH-based antibody engineering platform also enables the generation of multispecific constructs with additional Treg-specific antigens to further enhance Treg selectivity or target specific Treg subsets. For instance, existing trispecific anti-IL-2R formats could be expanded to tetraspecific designs by incorporating moieties targeting additional Treg-enriched surface markers38–40. This combinatorial avidity strategy could further refine targeting precision and cell specificity, making our platform well-suited for the development of next-generation immunotherapies.

In conclusion, we have developed potent CD25-targeting IL-2R agonistic trispecific antibodies that selectively activate and expand Tregs with pico- to femtomolar potency. These findings present a promising approach to Treg-targeted immunotherapy, establishing a strong foundation for future research and clinical development aimed at unlocking its therapeutic potential for autoimmune and inflammatory diseases.

Llama immunizations were performed at the Animal Facility ‘De Ark’, Department of Veterinary Sciences, Laboratory for Veterinary Physiology and Biochemistry, University of Antwerp, Antwerp, Belgium, under the supervision of Prof. Dr. Jo Leroy and Dr. Inne Xhonneux. All experimental procedures involving mice were approved by the Animal Care and Animal Experiments Ethical Committee of KU Leuven (P135/2021). Sex was not considered in the study design.

All experiments involving materials from human samples were performed under the guidelines and with the approval of the Ethical Committee of the University Hospital Leuven (S65883). Consent was given by the blood donors. Buffy coats were supplied by the Red Cross Flanders Blood Service, Belgium. No personal data were processed. Sex/gender and age were not considered in the study design.

Llama immunization and library construction

Two llamas were immunized with a mix of three pUNO1 plasmids (pUNO1-hIL02 RA, pUNO1-hIL02 RB, pUNO1-hIL02 RG) encoding the human IL-2Rα, IL-2Rβ and IL-2Rγ proteins under control of constitutively active EF-1α/HTLV promoter (Invivogen) in a 2:1:2 mass ratio. Intramuscular DNA injections were repeated a total of 6 times with 2-week intervals. Blood samples of 10 mL were collected pre- and post-immunization to investigate immune response. Specific immune responses to human IL-2Rα, IL-2Rβ and IL-2Rγ were measured by ELISA on coated recombinant proteins. Four days after the last immunization, 400 mL blood from each immunized llama was collected to isolate PBMCs using Ficoll-Paque gradient and used for RNA extraction. Total RNA was then converted into random primed cDNA using reverse transcriptase, and gene sequences encoding for VHH regions of llama heavy chain-only antibodies were amplified by PCR and subcloned into a phagemid vector as described previously41.

Selection of VHH binding to IL-2Rα, IL-2Rβ and IL-2Rγ

Llama VHH phage display libraries were used for selections against the different subunits of the human IL-2R. The VHH-pDCL1 phage display libraries passed the QC criteria of size above 1.0E + 08 and showed 100% VHH insert percentage. Specific VHH antibody fragments were identified by selecting and screening using recombinant human and mouse IL-2Rα, IL-2Rβ and IL-2Rγ proteins (Sino Biological) as antigens. Two parallel phage display selection strategies were used to identify antibodies binding to the different subunits of the IL-2 receptor: either in-solution selections on pre-captured antigens or panning on antigens coated on a plate. In-solution selections were performed using the KingFisherTM Flex system (Thermo Scientific). In the first round the human proteins were used, in rounds two and three both human and mouse IL-2R proteins were used. Very high phage enrichments were observed for selections performed on both human and mouse IL-2Rα, IL-2Rβ and IL-2Rγ for one of the animals; single-dose binding studies on mouse and human receptor subunits indicated presence of cross-reactive binders. Selections using a library derived from the second llama resulted in high enrichment only for human IL-2Rβ.

VHH screening and characterization

Individual clones were isolated and periplasmic extracts (P.E.) containing soluble VHH fragments were produced and screened in a binding ELISA and by surface plasmon resonance (SPR). In a binding ELISA, human or mouse IL-2Rα, IL-2Rβ and IL-2Rγ proteins were coated directly on MaxiSorp microtiter plates (Nunc) overnight at 4 °C. Free binding sites were blocked using 4% Marvel in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 1 h. Next, 1:5 dilution of P.E. in 1% Marvel/PBS were added to wells and incubated for 1 h. After incubation and an extensive PBS washing step, VHH binding was revealed using anti-c-myc IgG and anti-mouse IgG-HRP antibodies. Binding specificity was determined based on O.D. at 450 nm values compared to negative controls. SPR was performed to determine dissociation rates using a Biacore 3000 instrument (GE Healthcare). Briefly, human and mouse IL-2R subunit proteins were immobilized on a carboxylmethylated dextran sensor chip (CM5) at ~2500 RUs using amine coupling in acetate buffer (GE Healthcare). The VHH-containing P.E. were loaded with a flow rate of 30 µL/min and the off-rates were measured over a 120 s period. Complementary DNA corresponding to VHH clones binding to IL-2R subunits were Sanger sequenced (LGC Genomics) and divided into families based on the sequence of the VHH CDR3 region.

Monospecific antibody purification and characterization

For each IL-2R subunit, 10 clones displaying varying characteristics were reformatted as VHH-human Fc fusion molecules. For this purpose, the cDNA encoding the VHH of each clone was engineered into a mammalian expression vector comprising the cDNA encoding the CH2 and CH3 domains of human IgG1 and containing mutations that abrogate antibody effector functions mediated by the Fc receptor. Particularly, the molecules comprised the amino acid substitutions L234A, L235A and P329G (EU numbering) in the immunoglobulin heavy chains. Antibody molecules were subsequently produced by transient transfection in HEK293E cells and purified from cell supernatant by protein A affinity chromatography. Finally, the purity of the VHH-human Fc molecules was assessed by SDS-PAGE analysis, and was estimated to be >90%.

Epitope mapping by SPR

Biacore 3000 system (GE Healthcare) was used to determine whether IL-2Rα-, IL-2Rβ- and IL-2Rγ-specific antibodies compete for the same epitope. A CM5 sensor chip (GE Healthcare, #BR-1003-99) was coated with human IL-2Rα, IL-2Rβ and IL-2Rγ proteins (Sino Biological) at ~100 RU using standard amine coupling. Antibodies were diluted in HBS-EP pH 7.4 buffer to a concentration of 100 nM. Antibodies binding to the same IL-2R subunit were injected pairwise using the Biacore COINJECT method and a flow rate of 30 µL/min. An increased signal observed after the injection of a second antibody indicated that it bound to another epitope on its target, as compared to the first antibody. The same setup was used to test whether anti-IL-2Rα antibodies blocked human IL-2 binding to human IL-2Rα (CD25). Here, each antibody injection was followed by injection with 100 nM human IL-2 (proteintech).

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR)

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) measurements were performed on a Biacore 8 K (Cytiva) at 25 °C using 1× HBS-EP+ (pH 7.4) as running buffer, on a Series S Sensor Chip CM5 (Cytiva, 29127556). His-tagged human ectodomains from Sino Biological CD25/IL-2Rα (10165-H08H), CD122/IL-2Rβ (10696-H08B), and CD132/IL-2Rγ (10555-H08H) were captured via an anti-histidine capture format using an anti-histidine antibody kit (His Capture Kit, Cytiva, 29234602) and injected at ligand concentrations that yielded low-density capture surfaces for kinetic fitting (typical ligand injections 0.15-1.15 µg/mL with capture levels ~11-33 RU, depending on receptor), while higher ligand injections (20 µg/mL) were included for selected specificity/negative-control surfaces (capture levels in the ~350-1400 RU range). Analyte antibodies (VHH-based constructs) were injected at 30 µL/min with 120 s association and 180 s dissociation, using five-point concentration series tailored per interaction: for CD25, typically 0.39-6.25 nM or 0.78-12.5 nM; for CD122, typically 12.5-200 nM; for CD132, typically 0.78-12.5 nM or 1.56-25 nM; and for non-cognate specificity controls high analyte ranges of 62.5-1000 nM were applied. Sensorgrams were processed in Biacore 8 K Evaluation Software v5.0 with double referencing (flow cell 1 and buffer-only blank injections) and globally fit using a 1:1 Langmuir binding model to obtain kon, koff, KD and Rmax.

Construction of bispecific and trispecific antibodies

Five VHH clones specific for IL-2Rβ and 5 VHH clones specific for IL-2Rγ were selected and used to construct bispecific bivalent anti-IL-2Rβγ antibodies. Two VHH fragments were linked to an IgG1 backbone Fc region, while a (G4S)3 linker between the two VHH fragments and between the anti-IL-2Rβ VHH and the Fc region was used. The molecules comprised the amino acid substitutions L234A, L235A and P329G (LALA-PG) (EU numbering) in the immunoglobulin heavy chains, known to abrogate Fc-mediated effector functions. The Fc regions of the antibodies also included the mutations necessary for Fc domain heterodimerization by controlled Fab arm exchange (cFAE)25. In particular, the anti-IL-2Rβγ antibodies contained the F405L CH3 domain mutation. Antibody molecules were produced by transient transfection in HEK293E cells and purified from cell supernatant by protein A affinity chromatography. Five VHH clones specific for anti-IL-2Rα were produced as monospecific bivalent VHH-hFc fusion proteins. The Fc portions of the antibodies comprised LALA-PG mutations and the cFAE K409R CH3 domain mutation. Trispecific anti-IL-2Rαβγ antibodies were obtained using the cFAE method25. Monospecific bivalent anti-IL-2Rα antibodies containing K409R mutation and bispecific bivalent anti-IL-2Rβγ antibodies containing F405L mutation were mixed with a reducing agent at equimolar quantities. The resulting heterodimerization of the Fc domains yielded trispecific monovalent anti-IL-2Rαβγ antibodies.

Size-exclusion chromatography

SEC-HPLC analysis was performed using a TSKgel G3000SWxl column (7.8 × 300 mm, 5 µm; Tosoh) equipped with a SWxl guard column (6.0 × 40 mm; Tosoh). Samples were filtered through a 0.22 µm membrane prior to analysis, and ~20 µg of protein was injected per run. Chromatography was carried out on an Agilent 1260 or Waters Arc HPLC system at a flow rate of 0.7 mL/min using 0.1 M Na₂SO₄ in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 6.7 ± 0.3) as the mobile phase. Elution was monitored by UV absorbance at 280 nm.

nanoDSF and DLS thermal stability analysis

Thermal stability and aggregation behavior of trispecific antibodies were analyzed using a Prometheus Panta instrument (NanoTemper Technologies), combining nano differential scanning fluorimetry (nanoDSF) and dynamic light scattering (DLS). Antibodies were measured at a concentration of 0.48 mg/mL in PBS. Samples were loaded into standard capillaries and subjected to a thermal ramp from 25 °C to 95 °C at 1 °C/min. Protein unfolding was monitored by intrinsic tryptophan fluorescence using the F350/F330 ratio and its first derivative. Aggregation onset was assessed by backreflection (turbidity), scattering intensity, and changes in hydrodynamic radius during the thermal ramp. Baseline colloidal properties, including hydrodynamic radius (rₕ) and polydispersity index (PDI), were determined from cumulant analysis of DLS measurements under native conditions. Data were analyzed using the manufacturer’s software (Panta analysis software v1.10.2).

Binding and IL-2 inhibition assays in HEK-Blue IL-2 cells

The ability of anti-IL-2R antibodies to bind the human IL-2 receptor was analyzed using a HEK-Blue IL-2 recombinant cell line (Invivogen, #hkb-il2) overexpressing the three IL-2R subunits. Cell culture was performed following the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were seeded at 100 000 cells/well in a 96-well plate, washed with FACS buffer (PBS with 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Sigma-Aldrich)), and incubated with antibodies diluted in FACS buffer at the concentration of 10 nM for 1 h at 4 °C, washed with FACS buffer and stained with anti-human IgG PE (eBioscience) for 1 h at 4 °C. The same cell line was used to assay the ability of monospecific antibodies to compete with the human IL-2 binding to its receptor. Cells were seeded at 100,000 cells/well in a 96-well plate, washed with FACS buffer, and preincubated with antibodies diluted in FACS buffer at the concentration of 10 nM for 20 minutes at 4 °C, after which biotinylated human IL-2 (proteintech) at 2 nM was added for another 1 h at 4 °C. Cells were washed again with FACS buffer and stained with Streptavidin PE (eBioscience) for 1 h at 4 °C. Dead cells were excluded from the analysis by using a fixable viability dye (eFluor780, eBioscience). Stained cells were analyzed on an BD LSRFortessa X-20 Cell Analyze (BD FACSDiva software version 9.7, BD Biosciences). Final analysis and graphic output were performed with FlowJo v10.7.1 software (BD Biosciences) and GraphPad Prism version 8 (GraphPad Software).

pSTAT5 activation assay in HEK-Blue IL-2 recombinant cells

The potency of bispecific and trispecific anti-IL-2R antibodies to induce IL-2 signaling was analyzed by determining the level of STAT5 phosphorylated by the HEK-Blue IL-2 recombinant cell line (Invivogen, #hkb-il2) in the presence of the antibodies. Cell culture was performed following the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were seeded at 200 000 cells/well in a 96-well plate in RPMI medium including 0.1% bovine serum albumin (BSA, Sigma-Aldrich) and treated with antibodies at a concentration of 50 nM for 1 h at 4 °C. Several clones were further tested at multiple concentrations to determine the antibody concentration that gives half-maximal response (EC50). Cells were further treated with IC Fixation buffer (eBioscience) for 15 min at room temperature, washed with BSA buffer (PBS with 0.2% BSA), and incubated with BD Phosflow Perm Buffer III (BD Biosciences) for 30 min on ice. After washing with BSA buffer the cells were stained with anti-human STAT5 (pY694) PE antibody (pY694, BD Biosciences) overnight at 4 °C. Dead cells were excluded from the analysis by using a fixable viability dye (eFluor780, eBioscience). Flow cytometric measurements were performed on an BD LSRFortessa X-20 Cell Analyzer (BD FACSDiva software version 9.7, BD Biosciences).

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells isolation

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from human healthy donor buffy coat donations (supplied by the Red Cross Flanders Blood Service, Belgium). Buffy coats were diluted 1:5 with PBS (Gibco). For PBMC separation, 35 mL of the diluted buffy coat was gently layered over 15 mL of Lymphocyte Separation Medium (LSM; MP Biomedicals) in a 50 mL tube. The tubes were centrifuged at 900 g for 30 min at room temperature without brake. After centrifugation, the PBMC interphase was carefully collected. To deplete platelets, the cell suspension in PBS was subjected to three rounds of centrifugation at 100 g for 15 min each. Isolated PBMCs were used immediately or frozen and stored in liquid nitrogen.

pSTAT5 activation assay in human PBMCs

Freshly isolated or thawed PBMCs were seeded at 1 × 106 cells/well in a 96-well plate in BSA buffer (PBS with 0.2% BSA) and rested for 30 min at 37 °C. Then cells were treated with native IL-2, Fc-fused IL-2 mutein (V91K) or test antibodies for 45 min at 37 °C with addition of fixable yellow dead cell stain (Invitrogen) for the last 15 min. Cells were further treated with Fix/Perm buffer (from the Transcription Factor Phospho Buffer Set from BD Biosciences) for 50 min at 4 °C, washed with Perm/Wash buffer (from the Transcription Factor Phospho Buffer Set from BD Biosciences), and incubated with BD Phosflow Perm Buffer III (BD Biosciences) for 30 min on ice followed by storage at -20 °C. The next day, cells were washed with Perm/Wash buffer followed by staining for 1 h at room temperature with the following anti-human antibodies: CD3 AF532 (UCHT1, eBioscience), CD127 PE-Cy5.5 (eBioRDR5, eBioscience), CD4 PE-Cy7 (SK3, BioLegend), CD8 BV785 (SK1, BioLegend), CD19 PE-Fire640 (HIB19, BioLegend), CD56 BV650 (5.1H11, BioLegend), FOXP3 PE-CF594 (259D/C7, BD Biosciences), STAT5 (pY694) AF488 (pY694, BD Biosciences). CD25 staining was performed by combining anti-human CD25 BV421 clone M-A251 and clone BC96 (BioLegend). Antibodies were titrated prior to use in experiments. The acquisition was performed on a BD LSRFortessa X-20 Cell Analyzer (BD FACSDiva software version 9.7, BD Biosciences) or CYTEK Northern Lights (SpectroFlo software version 3.1.0). Data were analyzed with FlowJo 10.9.0 software (BD Biosciences).

Purified Treg expansion assay

CD4 T cells were enriched from freshly isolated PBMC using the MojoSort Human CD4 T Cell Isolation Kit (BioLegend) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Subsequently, CD127+ cells were stained with anti-human CD127 PE (A019D5, BioLegend) and depleted using MojoSort Human anti-PE Nanobeads (BioLegend). The enriched cell suspension was stained with the following anti-human antibodies: CD4 APC (RPA-T4, BioLegend), CD127 PE (A019D5, BioLegend), and CD25 BV711 (M-A251, 2A3 and BC96, BioLegend) for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark. The CD4+CD25hiCD127lo/- population was sorted using the Sony MA900 Cell Sorter (Sony Biotechnology Inc.) at the KU Leuven Flow and Mass cytometry core facility. Sorted Treg cells were seeded in 96-well U-bottom plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well with Dynabeads at a 1:1 bead-to-cell ratio (Dynabeads Human T-Activator CD3/CD28, Gibco) in 200 μL of complete medium (RPMI-1640, 10% FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin) supplemented with IL-2 (final concentration 500 U/mL, Proleukin, KU Leuven Hospital). The cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2. IL-2 was refreshed every other day, and Dynabeads were renewed every 7 days. After 10-14 days, Dynabeads were removed using a magnetic separator. The cells were washed and seeded in a 96-well U-bottom plate with fresh medium and incubated overnight at 37 °C for starvation. The following day, the cells were washed, counted, and seeded in triplicate at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well in a final volume of 200 μL. The cells were treated with test antibodies starting at a concentration of 10 nM with serial 1:10 dilutions for 7 days. On day 4, half of the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing the respective treatments. On day 7, the triplicates were pooled and stained using LIVE/DEAD Fixable Blue Dead Cell Stain Kit (ThermoFisher) and anti-human antibodies: CD3 APC-Cy7 (UCHT1, BioLegend), CD4 EF450 (RPA-T4, Invitrogen), CD8 AF700 (SK1, BioLegend), CD25 BV711 (BC96, BioLegend), FOXP3 AF488 (206D, BioLegend), CD127 PE-Cy7 (eBioRDR5, eBioscience) and Ki67 PE (B56, BD Biosciences). Antibodies were titrated prior to use in experiments. After staining, counting beads (Spherotech) were added to each sample. Samples were acquired using a Sony ID7000 spectral flow cytometer (Sony Biotechnology Inc.) and analyzed with FCS Express software (version 7, De Novo Software).

Ex vivo human PBMC expansion assay

Test antibodies were assayed for their ability to preferentially expand CD25+ Treg in human PBMC culture. PBMC were thawed and rested at high density (1 × 107 cells/ml) for 48 h to restore T cell reactivity, as previously described42,43. Cells were then seeded at 200 000 cells/well in 96-well U-bottom culture plates in RPMI-1640 culture medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco), 2 mM L-glutamine (Gibco) and freshly added 1:1000 β-mercaptoethanol (Gibco). Cells were labeled with CellTrace Violet (CTV; Invitrogen; 5 µM for 10 min at 37 °C)44 and treated with IL-2 or test antibodies starting at a concentration of 100 nM with serial 1:10 dilutions. On day 4, fresh antibodies were added and cells were stimulated for another 3 days. On day 7, cells were stained with antibodies against CD45 BV605 (HI30, BioLegend), CD3 APC-Cy7 (UCHT1, BioLegend), CD4 BUV496 (SK3, BD Biosciences), CD8 AF700 (SK1, BioLegend), CD56 BV750 (5.1H11, Sony), FOXP3 AF488 (206D, BioLegend), CD127 PE-Cy7 (eBioRDR5, eBioscience), CD25 BV711 (M-A251, BioLegend) and Ki67 PE (B58, BD Biosciences). Antibodies were titrated prior to use in experiments. Dead cells were excluded from the analysis by using a fixable viability dye (LIVE/DEAD Fixable Blue Dead Cell Stain Kit, ThermoFisher). Flow cytometric measurements were performed on a Sony ID7000 spectral flow cytometer (Sony Biotechnology Inc.). Cell expansion was assessed by measuring absolute cell counts and CTV proliferation profiles.

To immunophenotype Treg after extended culture in presence of various multispecific antibodies, total PBMC were rested at high density (1 × 10⁷ cells/mL) for 48 h, followed by incubation with IL-2 or test antibodies (all 1 nM) for an additional 96 h. Cells were harvested on day 4 for phenotypic analysis by flow cytometry. Viable cells were identified using Live/Dead Blue viability dye (ThermoFisher, 20 min, 4 °C), followed by surface staining for CCR8 PE (L263G8, BioLegend), CCR4 PE-Fire700 (L291H4, BioLegend), GITR APC-Fire750 (108-17, BioLegend), LAG-3 BV421 (11C3C65, BioLegend), CD25 BV711 (M-A251, BioLegend), CD4 BUV496 (SK3, BD Biosciences) and CD39 BUV563 (TU66, BD Biosciences) for 1 h at 4 °C. Cells were fixed and permeabilized using either the FOXP3 Transcription Factor Buffer Set (eBioscience) or the BD Cytofix/Cytoperm kit (BD Biosciences), followed by intracellular staining for FOXP3 AF647 (206D, BioLegend), Ki67 AF700 (Ki-67, BioLegend), CTLA-4 BV605 (BNI3, BioLegend), LAP Viobright FITC (Miltenyi Biotec) and IL-10 PE-Dazzle594 (JES3-19F1, Sony) overnight at 4 °C. Antibodies were titrated prior to use in experiments.

In vivo NSG T cell subset expansion

NOD/Scid/IL2Rγ-/- (NSG; Charles River) mice at 8-12 weeks of age received tail vein injections of human PBMC (20 × 106 per mouse) on day 0. Mice received intraperitoneal injections of PBS or 0.03 µg antibody (100 µL, diluted in PBS) on day 1 and day 8. To evaluate the human leukocyte engraftment and the dynamics of T and NK(T) cell subsets over time, immunophenotyping was performed on freshly isolated human PBMC, as well as on peripheral blood collected at days 1 and 7 post-engraftment. For the dose-optimization study (see Supplementary Fig. 11), spleens were harvested on day 9. For blood sampling, ~150 µL of blood from each mouse was collected into EDTA-coated tubes (Sarstedt Microvette®). After red blood cell lysis, samples were stained with a viability dye (Live/Dead Blue, ThermoFisher) for 20 min at 4 °C followed by blocking with Human Fc Block (BD Biosciences) for 10 min at 4 °C. The following staining antibodies were used: (1) antibody to mouse protein: CD45 BUV395 (30-F11, BD Biosciences), and (2) antibodies to human proteins: CD45 BV605 (HI30, BioLegend), CD3 APC-Cy7 (UCHT1, BioLegend), CD4 eFluor450 (RPA-T4, eBioscience), CD8 AF700 (SK1, BioLegend), Ki67 PE (B56, BD Biosciences), CD127 PE-Cy7 (eBioRDR5, eBioscience), FOXP3 AF488 (206D, BioLegend), CD56 BV750 (5.1H11, BioLegend), CD45RO BUV805 (UCHL1, BD Biosciences), CD45RA PerCP-eFluor710 (GRT22, eBioscience), CD25 BV711 (BC96, BioLegend). Antibodies were titrated prior to use in experiments. The engraftment of the human cells was calculated using %hCD45/(%mCD45 + %hCD45) from total live cells. Flow cytometry was performed on a Sony ID7000 spectral flow cytometer (Sony Biotechnology Inc.) and data were analyzed using FCS Express v7 (De Novo Software).

In experiments including clinical GvHD scoring, female NOG (NOD.Cg-Prkdcscid Il2rgtm1Sug, Taconic) mice were engrafted with human PBMC (20 × 106 per mouse) on day 0. Mice were dosed on days 1, 8, 15 and 22 with vehicle (PBS), Abatacept (10 mg/kg bodyweight), Fc-fused IL-2 mutein (V91K) (0.03 µg), tsVHH-48 (0.03 µg) or DC41(0.03 µg); or were dosed with low-dose- (LD-) IL-2 at 25.000IU days 0 through 5 and then every three days through day 22. Blood samples were analysed by flow cytometry on days 7, 14 and 21 to evaluate the dynamics of T and NK(T) subsets over time. Clinical scoring of GvHD development included the evaluation of hunching posture, fur ruffling, overall activity, anemia and weight loss. Animals were weighted prior to treatment, at the end of the study, 3x per week during the first week, then daily until the end of the study. For other parameters, first scoring took place prior to treatment and last scoring at the end of the study. Scoring frequency was 3x per week. The scores ranged from 0, 1 and 2, with 0 in case all parameters appeared normal. A score of 1 was applied for hunching posture and fur ruffling when ‘mild to moderate’, for overall activity when ‘mild to moderate decrease in activity and isolation’, for mild signs of anemia, and >10% but less than 20% weight loss. A score of 2 was applied for severe hunching posture (movement distinctly impaired), severe fur ruffling and poor grooming as well as moderate to severe loss of fur, when overall activity showed lethargy, paralysis or staggering, for severe signs of anemia, and a weight loss of >20%. The clinical score was the sum of these criteria. Any individual criterion score of 2 or the sum of clinical scoring >/=5 constituted a humane endpoint necessitating euthanasia of the mouse.

Statistics and reproducibility

Flow cytometry data analyses were performed in FlowJo v.10.7.1 and v10.9.0 (BD Biosciences) or FCS Express v7 (De Novo Software). Statistical analyses were performed in Prism version 8 and 9 software (GraphPad). P values were determined by one or two-way ANOVA with Tukey post-tests (NS, P > 0.05, *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001) or one-way ANOVA with multiple comparisons (Tukey-Kramer’s test). *P ≤ 0.05, **P ≤ 0.01, ***P ≤ 0.001, ****P ≤ 0.0001. Analysis of Kaplan-Meier survival curves was performed with two-sided log-rank test. EC50 values were calculated in Prism 9 software (GraphPad) using the inbuilt nonlinear regression curve fit (log (agonist) versus response, variable slope, 4 parameter) or (log (agonist) versus response, standard slope, 3 parameter).
