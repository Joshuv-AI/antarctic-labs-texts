MRI-derived three-dimensional modelling reveals cervicothoracic subarachnoid space narrowing in syringomyelia-affected cavalier king charles spaniels
Authors: Ryan Jones, Srdjan Cirovic, Clare Rusbridge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSyringomyelia (SM) is a disorder of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) circulation strongly associated with Chiari-like malformation (CM) in Cavalier King Charles Spaniels (CKCS). Although CM is nearly ubiquitous in the breed, not all affected dogs develop signs of CM-related pain (CM-P) or Syringomyelia (SM), raising questions about underlying mechanisms. CM-P without SM has been linked to brachycephaly, whereas SM involves additional craniocervical conformational changes. This study tested whether CM-P affected CKCS with and without SM were more likely to have reduced cisterna magna volume and cervicothoracic subarachnoid space (SAS) narrowing compared to each other and with CKCS with CM but no clinical signs (CM-N).ResultsA retrospective dataset of 168 CKCS was reviewed. After applying exclusion criteria and matching for body size, three groups were analysed: CM-N, CM-P without SM, and CM-P with SM (n = 10 per group). Three-dimensional SAS models from the foramen magnum to T3/T4 were generated by segmenting MRI data in 3D Slicer and lofting contours in ANSYS SpaceClaim. Whole SAS volume was lower in SM cases than in both comparison groups (ANOVA p = 0.0033, η² = 0.344). Cisterna magna volume and surface area did not differ, but the volume-to-surface area ratio was reduced in SM affected dogs (p = 0.011, η² = 0.274), consistent with a relatively constricted SAS. Slice-by-slice analysis revealed focal reductions in annular area and hydraulic diameter at the occiput, C2–C6, and T1–T3, with hydraulic diameter more sensitive to local change. Corresponding decreases in Womersley number indicated a viscous-dominated CSF flow regime and loss of pulsatile compliance. Principal Component Analysis confirmed distinct clustering of SM cases, while CM-P and CM-N dogs overlapped; displacement along the principal axis correlated with syrinx diameter, linking morphology to clinical severity.ConclusionsCKCS with CM-P and SM have global SAS volume loss with focal constrictions, whereas CKCS with CM-P without SM does not differ from CM-N. These findings support a model in which SM develops from limited cranial and spinal SAS capacity to accommodate and redistribute pulsatile CSF flow, altering pressure transmission along the neuraxis. The 3D reconstruction method offers a reproducible framework for quantifying these changes and linking structure to flow dynamics. Limitations include incomplete neuroaxis coverage, post-SM analysis (cause versus effect), small sample size, and single-operator measurements.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-026-05435-6.Keywords: Canine, Chiari-like malformation, Cerebrospinal fluid dynamics, Spinal Subarachnoid Space, Compliance, Cisterna magna, Annular area, Hydraulic diameter, Womersley number

Background

Syringomyelia (SM) is a disorder of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) circulation, a concept first proposed by Gardner more than 75 years ago [1]. Since then, a series of evolving theories, ranging from Gardner’s “water-hammer” hypothesis and Williams’ “suck” theory to Oldfield’s “piston” model and contemporary computational and in vitro biomechanical studies, have sought to explain how alterations in CSF dynamics lead to syrinx formation [1–12]. Despite these advances, the pathogenesis remains incompletely understood. Current evidence indicates that it cannot be attributed to a single factor, such as hindbrain herniation in Chiari malformations, but emerges from a complex interplay between multiple elements. These include the mechanical properties of the brain, spinal cord, nerve roots and their coverings, the presence of CSF flow obstruction, and the generation of abnormal pulsatile pressure waves within the subarachnoid space (SAS) [13–16].

In the dog, SM is most associated with the developmental abnormality Chiari-like malformation (CM)[17–19]. What remains unclear is why some dogs with CM go on to develop clinical disease, such as CM-related pain (CM-P) and syringomyelia (SM), while others with similar anatomical abnormality remain clinically unaffected. Knowler et al. found that CM-P in the absence of SM was associated with brachycephaly, whereas SM was associated with brachycephaly and additional conformational changes at the craniocervical junction [20]. This points to a multifactorial process in SM development involving neuroparenchyma overcrowding and reduced compliance, restriction to outflow of CSF movement into the SAS possibly influenced by reduced circulation of CSF into nasal and skull-based lymphatics associated with brachycephaly and loss of surface area of this tissue [18, 21].

The Cavalier King Charles Spaniel (CKCS) breed is ubiquitous for CM [22]. Even dogs regarded as clinically normal and free of pain demonstrate some degree of this malformation (CM-N). The bony changes at the skull and craniocervical junction only partly explain the condition. There is also evidence that the CKCS brain including the cerebellum is disproportionately large relative to their skull volume [23–27]. This may help explain why other brachycephalic breeds such as the Shih Tzu and Japanese Chin, which are objectively more extreme in skull shortening, do not typically develop CM associated SM. A larger brain in a smaller “skull box” would be expected to reduce cranial subarachnoid space, alter conformation, and decrease intracranial compliance.

An intriguing question is whether this relative “overcrowding” is confined to the brain or reflects a more generalised disproportion of the central nervous system. Sparks et al. found that CKCS have more caudal termination of the spinal cord and dural sac compared with other small-breed dogs. This low-lying conus medullaris was associated with the presence of lumbar SM but not pain. Shorter filum terminale internum length (but not filum externum) correlated with pain but not SM [28, 29]. These findings suggests that clinical disease could be associated with altered spinal biomechanics and reduced mobility or functional tethering of the caudal spinal cord. A low-lying conus and short filum terminale may also reflect a spinal cord–vertebral column mismatch, paralleling the breed’s relatively large brain within a small skull. Such a disproportion could reduce the volume of lumbar SAS and thereby restrict spinal cord mobility and CSF dynamics.

Focal stenosis of the SAS is a well-documented risk factor for SM in both experimental and clinical contexts. Experimental and computational studies [13–16] show that stenosis amplifies pulsatile CSF pressures, generates valve-like and resonance effects, and imposes abnormal mechanical stress on the spinal cord. In dogs, small syrinxes or intramedullary fluid accumulations characteristic of presyringomyelia are typically found immediately cranial to the site of spinal cord stenosis, such as that caused by arachnoid adhesions [30] or by intervertebral disc herniations [31]. This parallels the pattern seen in humans, where post-traumatic syrinxes most commonly extend cranially from the site of injury (cranial in 81%, caudal in 4%, and both cranial and caudal in 15% of cases) [32, 33]. Arachnoiditis or a true stenosis reduces the ability of the local SAS to accommodate pulsatile CSF displacement. Cranially, this reduced capacity enhances the transmission of cardiac- and respiratory-related pressure waves, producing larger local pressure oscillations. Caudally, the more capacious lumbar cistern provides a reservoir that dampens these pulsations. The resulting cranio-caudal pressure gradients favour the movement of fluid into the spinal cord parenchyma through perivascular pathways cranial to the stenotic site [8, 34–36].

Based on evidence that SM develops from a combination of neuroparenchymal overcrowding and alterations in CSF flow and pressure transmission within the craniospinal system, we hypothesised that CKCS with SM would demonstrate a narrower cervicothoracic SAS compared to CKCS with CM-associated pain and clinically normal CKCS. A reduction in the SAS would reflect diminished capacity of the SAS compartment to accommodate pulsatile CSF displacement. We further hypothesised that cisterna magna volume would be reduced in CKCS with SM, consistent with impaired CSF reservoir function at the craniocervical junction and reduced capacity for pressure-volume buffering within the cranial compartment.

Methods

Aim, design, and setting

The aim of this study was to compare cervicothoracic SAS geometry in three CKCS cohorts: (1) Clinically normal CKCS with minimal or no CM and no signs of pain (CM-normal); (2) CKCS with CM related pain but no syringomyelia (CM-P); and (3) CKCS with CM related pain and CM associated syringomyelia (CM-P + SM). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-based three-dimensional (3D) modelling was used for all assessments. We hypothesised that dogs with CM-P + SM would show greater SAS narrowing and reduced cisterna magna volume, reflecting reduced compliance within the spinal canal. We further hypothesised that CM-related pain, even in the absence of SM, would be associated with reduced cisterna magna volume. The study was designed as a retrospective, case–control analysis using a pre-existing database of clinical MRI scans from a referral veterinary neurology service.

Study population

A total of 168 Cavalier King Charles Spaniels (CKCS) were classified into three groups based on MRI findings and clinical history.

Chiari normal (CM-N)

Dogs graded CM-0 or CM-1 under the Royal Kennel Club/British Veterinary Association (RKC/BVA) CM/SM Health Scheme [37] and in addition had no other CSF-associated abnormalities such as ventriculomegaly or quadrigeminal cistern expansion. These dogs had undergone MRI screening prior to breeding and had no history or clinical signs of CM-associated pain (n = 21; 16 males, 5 females).

Chiari-related pain (CM-P)

Dogs graded CM-2 under the RKC/BVA CM/SM scheme, with clinical signs consistent with CM-associated pain and no syrinx ≥ 2 mm in transverse diameter (central canal diameter < 2 mm) (n = 52; 17 males, 35 females).

Chiari-related pain with syringomyelia (CM-P + SM)

Dogs graded CM-2 with CM-associated pain and a syrinx ≥ 2 mm in transverse diameter (n = 77; 44 males, 33 females). No dogs in this cohort had MRI-confirmed SM in the absence of clinical signs; although these categories do not represent every theoretical clinical combination in the CM and SM spectrum, they collectively describe all dogs included in this dataset prior to exclusion.

Dogs were excluded if they were younger than 3 years (to allow sufficient time for clinical sign and syrinx development), had incomplete MRI coverage (not extending from the foramen magnum to T3–T4), or lacked adequate clinical data. To minimise body size as a potential confounder, dogs were matched using a reproducible linear measure, the distance between the most dorsal point of the odontoid process and the dorsal midline of the C2–C3 intervertebral disc (Dens–C2/3 distance) on mid-sagittal T2-weighted MRI (Fig. 1; slices 17–24). These landmarks were selected for their consistent visibility across scans. An L2 vertebral reference was not feasible because this region was inconsistently captured in the imaging field of view. Body weight was excluded from matching, as it is affected by condition and activity level; for example, obese or sedentary dogs may weigh more despite being skeletally smaller, and clinically affected dogs may gain weight due to reduced exercise or gabapentinoid therapy. All measurements were performed by a single operator (R.J.) to ensure consistency.

Spline contours were placed at key anatomical landmarks (red): the occipital bone, atlas, dens, axis, and each intervertebral disc from C2/3 to T3/4. Additional intermediate slices (green) were added between discs, within the cisterna magna, and in defined subregions to improve geometric fidelity. A total of 30 contours were generated per dog. Body “size” was defined as the linear distance between the most dorsal point of the odontoid process and the dorsal midline of the C2–C3 intervertebral disc (Dens–C2/3 distance; slices 17–24). This measurement was used to match dogs of similar body size across the three groups. These landmarks were selected for their consistent and reproducible visibility across dogs

Mean (± SD) Dens–C2/3 distances were: CM-N 32.2 ± 3.1 mm, CM-P 31.3 ± 2.3 mm, and CM-P + SM 29.5 ± 2.1 mm. Matching was anchored to the CM-N group: each control was paired with a CM-P dog within ± 0.5 mm (or the closest available match if none fell within that range). The Dens–C2/3 distances of each CM-N/CM-P pair defined the upper and lower limits for selecting CM-P + SM dogs; if several candidates qualified, the one with the largest syrinx diameter was chosen. MRI acquisition and segmentation

All study dogs were imaged using a standardised SM protocol [38] (Siemens Magnetom Symphony, A Tim System, 1.5 T, Erlangen, Germany) and positioned with the head extended in dorsal recumbency. Images from the T2-weighted sagittal MRI (Table 2) from the foramen magnum to the level of the T3/T4 intervertebral disc space were imported into 3D Slicer (version 5.8.0). Transverse contours of the subarachnoid space (SAS) were manually delineated at 30 anatomical levels per dog (Fig. 1). Contours were drawn in the transverse plane, orthogonal to the sagittal imaging plane, to capture cross-sectional geometry at specific landmarks and to define the inner (spinal cord) and outer (dural) boundaries. The central canal and any syrinx were excluded from the analysis. To improve geometric fidelity, additional intermediate contours were also placed (Fig. 1; Table 3).

Each spline, representing a smooth curve fitted through user-defined control points, was resampled to 20 equally spaced points per contour, an example is provided in Fig. 2. These were imported into ANSYS SpaceClaim (ANSYS Inc., Canonsburg, PA, USA), where surfaces were lofted to generate continuous 3D reconstructions, a geometric reconstruction process in which a continuous, smooth surface is created by interpolating between a series of ordered cross-sectional contours, thereby generating a three-dimensional surface that represents the anatomical structure between adjacent slices. Principal component analysis (PCA)

To explore patterns of spatial variation in the SAS along the cervical and cranial thoracic spine, a Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed on the annular area measurements and hydraulic diameter obtained at the 30 anatomical slice locations per dog (Table 3; Fig. 1). PCA is a dimensionality reduction technique that transforms correlated variables into a smaller set of uncorrelated variables known as principal components (PCs). Each PC represents a linear combination of the original variables, capturing the major axes of variance within the dataset.

Prior to analysis, all annular area values were standardised (z-score normalisation) to remove scale differences. The first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) were retained for visualisation, as they captured the largest proportions of variance. Each dog was represented as a single point in the two-dimensional PC space defined by PC1 and PC2. Confidence ellipses (95%) were fitted around each diagnostic group—CM-N, CM-P and CM-P + SM—to illustrate within-group variability and group separation. Group centroids were plotted to represent the mean position of each cohort in PC space.

A separate loadings plot was generated to visualise how individual spinal slices contributed to the two principal components. The direction and length of each loading vector represented the correlation between a given slice and the PCs: slices oriented in similar directions exhibited correlated variation, while longer vectors indicated slices contributing more strongly to the variance captured by the PCs. To improve interpretability, an angle-threshold filter was applied, displaying only those loadings within 5° of the vectors representing the major or minor ellipse axes or the line connecting group centroids. This filtering step reduced visual noise and highlighted the spinal levels most strongly associated with group separation.

Statistical analysis

Normality was assessed using the Lilliefors test, and homogeneity of variance using Levene’s test. Group comparisons were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Bonferroni-adjusted post-hoc tests. When assumptions were not met, non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis tests were applied. Effect sizes (η²) were calculated. Analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was used to account for covariates (age, weight, body size). Slice-by-slice comparisons were performed using ANOVA with Tukey post-hoc testing. PCA was used for dimensionality reduction and visualisation of group clustering. No a priori power calculation was performed due to the retrospective and matched nature of the dataset.

Results

Whole cervicothoracic subarachnoid space volume

Mean whole SAS volumes (± SD) were CM-N: 7789.8 ± 1114.1 mm³, CM-P: 7421.7 ± 1180.3 mm³, and CM-P + SM: 5860.1 ± 1343.9 mm³ (Fig. 3a). Levene’s test confirmed homogeneity of variance (p = 0.52). One-way ANOVA showed a significant group effect (p = 0.0033). Bonferroni-adjusted post-hoc comparisons showed no difference between CM-N and CM-P groups (p = 1.0), but the CM-P + SM group had lower SAS volumes compared with both CM-N (p = 0.0043) and CM-P (p = 0.0236). Effect size was large (η² = 0.344). Kruskal–Wallis testing supported these results (p = 0.0064). After adjusting for Dens–C2/3 distance, age, and weight (ANCOVA), group differences in SAS volume remained significant (F [5, 24] = 11.5, p < 0.001).

These findings support our first hypothesis that cervicothoracic SAS volume is significantly reduced in dogs with CM-P + SM compared to both CM-N and CM-P groups, whereas no difference was observed between CM-N and CM-P.

Cisterna magna volumes

Mean cisterna magna volumes (± SD) were CM-N: 1468.3 ± 286.4 mm³, CM-P: 1307.9 ± 270.0 mm³, and CM-P + SM: 1223.4 ± 243.6 mm³ (Fig. 3b). Variances were equal across groups (Levene’s test, p = 0.987). One-way ANOVA showed no overall group difference (p = 0.134), and Bonferroni-adjusted post-hoc comparisons confirmed that all pairwise comparisons were non-significant (CM-N vs. CM-P: p = 0.572; CM-N vs. CM-P + SM: p = 0.151; CM-P vs. CM-P + SM: p = 1.000). Non-parametric testing (Kruskal–Wallis, p = 0.151) gave consistent results.

When covariates (Dens–C2/3 distance, age, weight) were included in an ANCOVA, the model reached significance (F[5, 24] = 3.97, p = 0.0092), suggesting that these factors contributed to variability in cisterna magna volume. Residuals were normally distributed (Lilliefors p > 0.05), and the effect size was moderate (η² = 0.138).

Cisterna magna volume did not differ significantly between groups; therefore, our second hypothesis was not supported.

Cisterna magna surface area

Mean cisterna magna surface area (± SD, excluding end “caps” i.e. the flat top and bottom surfaces that close off a 3D volume when it is reconstructed from a series of cross-sectional contours) was CM-N: 1197.6 ± 126.3 mm², CM-P: 1155.0 ± 178.3 mm², and CM-P + SM: 1231.7 ± 229.9 mm² (Fig. 3c). Variances were equal across groups (Levene’s test, p = 0.224). One-way ANOVA found no group differences (p = 0.649), and Bonferroni post-hoc tests confirmed that all pairwise comparisons were non-significant (all p = 1.0).

When covariates (Dens–C2/3 distance, age, weight) were included in an ANCOVA, the model did not reach significance (F[5, 24] = 2.10, p = 0.101). Residuals were normally distributed (Lilliefors p > 0.05). The effect size was small (η² = 0.032). A non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis test supported these findings (p = 0.618).

These findings demonstrate that cisterna magna surface area did not differ significantly between groups.

Cisterna magna volume-to-surface area ratio

Mean volume-to-surface area ratio (± SD) was CM-N: 1.221 ± 0.178 mm, CM-P: 1.130 ± 0.133 mm, and CM-P + SM: 1.003 ± 0.146 mm (Fig. 3d). Variances were equal across groups (Levene’s test, p = 0.477). One-way ANOVA showed a group effect (p = 0.013), with post-hoc analysis confirming a difference between CM-N and CM-P + SM (p = 0.011); other comparisons were non-significant. ANCOVA adjusting for covariates (Dens–C2/3 distance, age, weight) also detected a group effect (F[5, 24] = 2.69, p = 0.046). Residuals were normally distributed (Lilliefors p > 0.05). Effect size was large (η² = 0.274). A non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis test gave consistent results (p = 0.024).

These findings demonstrate that the volume-to-surface area ratio was lower in CM-P + SM dogs compared with CM-N, indicating a relatively narrower or more constricted cisterna magna/SAS, a difference not evident from volume or surface area alone.

Local slice-by-slice analysis

Slice-by-slice comparisons (ANOVA with Tukey post-hoc testing) showed that annular area in CM-P + SM was lower than in CM-N and CM-P at multiple levels. Differences were most evident at T1–3 (slices 1–6), C5–6 (slices 10–12), C2–4 (slices 14–18), and the occipital plane (slice 30), with mean differences ranging from ~ 7–15 mm² at T1-T3 (p < 0.01) and 8 to 25 mm² (p < 0.01) in the occipital and cervical regions. No differences were detected between CM-N and CM-P at any slice (Fig. 4a; Table 4).

No slices showed a difference in hydraulic diameter between CM-N and CM-P (Fig. 4b; and Table 5).

The hydraulic diameter proved more sensitive to such local changes, identifying a greater number of slices with significant group differences, consistent with its ability to capture both area and shape-related variations in subarachnoid geometry.

Discussion

Global and regional alterations in subarachnoid space geometry

This study provides quantitative evidence that CKCS with CM-P and SM have reduced SAS volume along the cervical and cranial thoracic spine compared with both clinically normal dogs (CM-N) and those with clinical signs of pain without SM (CM-P). Whole SAS volume was significantly lower in CM-P + SM dogs (p = 0.0033), and this difference persisted after adjustment for body size, age, and weight. In contrast, CM-N and CM-P dogs did not differ, suggesting that syrinx formation, rather than CM-associated pain alone, is linked to measurable loss of SAS capacity. Large effect sizes and consistent outcomes across both parametric and non-parametric analyses support the robustness of this association despite modest sample size.

Localised morphometric analysis identified focal reductions in annular area and hydraulic diameter, parameters reflecting the cross-sectional and effective flow dimensions of the SAS, particularly at the occipital region, cranial to mid-cervical levels (C2–C6), and cranial thoracic spine (T1–T3). These regions coincide with zones of spinal curvature and correspond to the anatomical sites where syrinx cavities most commonly arise in CKCS [40]. Finite-element simulations by Cirovic et al. demonstrated that such curved segments experience the greatest shear and axial normal stresses when CSF motion is restricted, suggesting that focal SAS narrowing amplifies local mechanical stress and disturbs pulsatile flow [40].

While the persistence of group differences after covariate adjustment and the consistent regional pattern of narrowing suggest a possible predisposing role for reduced SAS volume, causality cannot be confirmed. It remains equally plausible that syrinx expansion secondarily compresses the SAS, or that both processes interact in a self-reinforcing cycle of narrowing and pressure amplification.

Cisterna magna volume and surface area did not differ significantly between. However, compared to CM-N dogs, those with CM-P + SM exhibited a lower cisterna magna volume-to-surface-area ratio, consistent with a relatively narrower or more constricted geometry. A “tight” cisterna magna without hindbrain herniation has been recognised in human SM as a source of impaired CSF compliance [41]. The cisterna magna serves as a principal compliance chamber within the craniospinal system, accommodating the basal cistern pulse generated during systolic brain expansion and transmitting CSF between cranial and spinal compartments. When this reservoir is constricted, its pressure-buffering capacity is diminished, amplifying pulsatile pressure gradients and promoting syrinx formation [41, 42].

Taken together, these findings support a two-stage model for SM development in CKCS:

Stage 1 – Predisposition: Global factors—such as brachycephaly, craniocervical crowding, a relatively large hindbrain, and reduced capacity of the cranial and spinal SAS to accommodate and redistribute pulsatile CSF flow—establish a “pro-syringomyelia” state.

Stage 2 – Initiation and progression: Local factors such as focal SAS narrowing and regional spinal canal curvature can alter cord–CSF mechanical coupling (e.g., reducing the ability of the cord to translate or deform with pulsatile CSF loads). These changes distort CSF wave propagation, generating resonance and valve-like effects, dissociated pressures, and increased mechanical stresses on the spinal cord.

CM-P dogs may therefore represent the predisposed state (pain without syrinx), whereas SM dogs progress to syrinx formation once additional local flow constraints develop. Given these global and regional differences, further analysis focused on the local flow geometry of the SAS to clarify how specific spinal regions might modulate CSF movement and pressure transmission.

Local flow geometry and functional implications

Slice-wise morphometric analysis demonstrated distinct, region-specific alterations in SAS geometry in CM-P + SM dogs. The annular area was consistently smaller in CM-P + SM dogs, particularly at the occipital region, mid-cervical (C2–C4, C5–C6), and cranial thoracic (T1–T3) levels, with mean reductions of 7–25 mm² relative to the other groups.

Hydraulic diameter, which integrates both area and perimeter, showed an even broader pattern of reductions, revealing additional constrictions that area alone could not detect. This parameter is more physiologically relevant, capturing irregular or elongated deformations that affect resistance and compliance. Clusters of reduced hydraulic diameter at T1–T3, C4–C5, and C2–C3 correspond to areas of canal curvature where CSF shear and axial stresses are greatest [43].

The Womersley number, which relates pulsatile flow characteristics to the geometry of the space and the driving frequency, followed a similar distribution to the hydraulic diameter, with the lowest values occurring at the cervicothoracic junction (T1–T3). Lower Womersley numbers in CM-P + SM dogs indicate a viscous-dominated flow regime, in which resistive forces outweigh fluid inertia. As a result, the total impedance of the system is increased, requiring greater pressure gradients to drive flow.

These findings are consistent with recent computational fluid dynamics (CFD) analyses in human models of SM. Edrisnia and Sharbatdar (2024) demonstrated that spinal cord enlargements producing 40–60% canal stenosis caused local reductions in Womersley number exceeding six units, particularly between T1 and T5, indicating a marked shift toward viscous-dominated flow [15]. Their simulations showed that such constrictions disrupt the normal oscillatory velocity profile and reduce the effective inertial coupling between adjacent canal segments, amplifying local pressure gradients and promoting jet-like velocity peaks near the stenotic region. In their model, these hydrodynamic changes were most pronounced in the thoracic spine, coinciding with the same region where syrinx cavities commonly form in both humans and CKCS.

These results suggest that SM is associated with geometric constriction of the spinal SAS, resulting in a reduced capacity of the space to accommodate pulsatile CSF flow. The combined effects of decreased SAS volume and altered channel geometry therefore provide a plausible biomechanical pathway by which anatomic crowding can promote syrinx initiation.

Principal component analysis

Principal Component Analysis provided an integrated view of SAS morphology, highlighting clear group-level trends, and individual level variation. The CM-N and CM-P groups showed similar overall morphometric patterns, with CM-P exhibiting greater isotropy, consistent with increase variability in hydraulic diameter and annular area in CM-P. In contrast, the CM-P + SM group occupied a distinct region of PCA space, reflecting the structural alterations associated with SM. Furthermore, syrinx diameter was associated with a further distance from the CM-P/N clusters in PCA space.

One CM-P + SM case, however, clustered more closely with the CM groups rather than with the other CM-P + SM individuals. This apparent outlier can be explained anatomically: the dog lacked a syrinx within the analysed region, so the local spinal morphology remained largely unaffected. Its PCA position likely reflects either the absence of subarachnoid space narrowing, which may explain why SM did not develop in this segment, or the lack of syrinx-related deformation of the surrounding SAS. This observation reinforces the interpretation that the PCA separation between groups reflects localised structural effects of SM rather than broader inter-group variability. In other words, PCA was sensitive to the presence and regional extent of syrinx pathology.

Taken together, these results suggest two complementary patterns. SM is associated with focal, directional deformation centred around C2 (and with contributions from the C5/6 level), consistent with local cord expansion or distortion in the cranial cervical region. CM-P does not produce the same focal displacement but instead is associated with increased, more isotropic variability across cranial cervical levels; the CM-N group’s constrained variance around C2–C3 highlights that the CM-P pattern is a departure from the normal, tightly constrained morphology at this junction. The single CM-P + SM case that clustered with the CM groups (noted previously) further supports a local interpretation: that dog lacked a syrinx at the analysed levels, and therefore its local morphology resembled CM morphology rather than the CM-P + SM signature.

These findings motivate targeted analyses at the cranial cervical spine, including direct group comparisons and correlation with clinical severity measures. Such focused testing will determine whether the directional PCA signals observed here reflect consistent, statistically robust anatomical differences with clinical relevance, or whether they are driven by a subset of cases.

Methodological significance

Beyond its biological findings, this study introduces a 3D MRI reconstruction technique for quantifying SAS morphology. By segmenting serial MRI slices and generating continuous lofted models, this method captures subtle geometric variations not discernible on conventional two-dimensional imaging. Derived parameters such as hydraulic diameter and volume-to-surface-area ratio serve as functional surrogates for CSF flow impedance and compliance, linking structural geometry directly to physiological function.

With standardised image acquisition, automated segmentation, and multi-observer validation, this technique could provide a reproducible framework for assessing CSF pathways in vivo. It is applicable not only to CM and SM but also to other neurological conditions involving altered CSF dynamics and may assist in surgical planning, postoperative evaluation, and longitudinal monitoring.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. This was a retrospective study, and MRI acquisition parameters varied between cases, including slice thickness, orientation, and sequence weighting. Transverse sequences were not consistently available, necessitating manual reconstruction of cross-sectional contours from sagittal images. Although this approach provided satisfactory geometric fidelity, minor distortions may have occurred in regions of pronounced curvature.

Where available, alternative imaging modalities (for example, T1-weighted MRI) were used to distinguish cerebrospinal fluid from epidural fat within the subarachnoid space, as these tissues may appear similar on T2-weighted MRI. However, such alternative sequences were not available for all subjects or across the entire modelled domain. As a result, some contoured regions may be subject to uncertainty due to partial volume effects and limited tissue contrast. This potential source of error was minimised through reconstruction performed in consultation with an experienced veterinary neurologist (C.R.); nevertheless, it remains an inherent limitation of the present study.

All spline delineations were performed by a single operator to ensure consistency; however, inter-observer variability was not assessed. The lofting procedure used to generate 3D models assumes smooth continuity between slices and may underestimate abrupt anatomical transitions, while the fixed use of 30 contours per dog could have missed subtle local variations. Group matching was based on a single linear measure (dens–C2/3 distance) rather than comprehensive morphometrics, which may not have fully accounted for conformational differences between dogs.

The modest sample size (n = 10 per group) limited statistical power and prevented analysis of sex or age interactions. As in most canine CM/SM studies, recruitment was constrained by the scarcity of clinically normal CKCS for comparison. Nonetheless, the large effect sizes and concordant results across parametric and non-parametric analyses support the robustness of the principal findings. Because all CM-P + SM dogs were studied after syrinx formation, temporal causality could not be determined—that is, whether SAS reduction precedes or results from syrinx development.

Despite these constraints, the strong internal consistency of the dataset and convergence of morphometric and flow-related metrics provide confidence in the validity of the conclusions. Future studies should employ standardised imaging protocols, multi-observer validation, and prospective longitudinal designs to clarify causality and refine understanding of regional flow–structure interactions. Determining whether subarachnoid narrowing represents a primary predisposing factor or a secondary consequence of syrinx expansion remains a key question. Addressing this will require longitudinal imaging of CKCS before syrinx formation (e.g., at six months of age) and repeated follow-up (e.g., at 18 months and three years old).

Conclusion

CKCS with CM-P + SM show reduced cervicothoracic SAS volume and altered spinal canal geometry compared with both CM-N and CM-P dogs. They also exhibit a lower cisterna magna volume-to-surface-area ratio relative to CM-N dogs. Together, these findings suggest a reduced capacity of the cranial and spinal CSF spaces to accommodate and redistribute pulsatile flow, supporting a model in which SM arises from the combined effects of global neural overcrowding and focal disturbances to CSF movement. The 3D MRI reconstruction technique presented here provides a novel, non-invasive method for visualising and quantifying these geometric alterations in vivo. By linking morphology to potential CSF flow impedance, this approach offers a powerful framework for future studies exploring disease mechanisms, refining diagnostic precision, and guiding surgical or therapeutic interventions in both veterinary and human neuropathology.
