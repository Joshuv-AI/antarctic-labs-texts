How to distinguish opioid toxicity from natural dying in patients with advanced illness and how to manage opioid toxicity?
Authors: Jason W Boland, Elaine G Boland
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Opioids, commonly used in patients with advanced illness, have a range of toxicities, some of which overlap with signs of naturally dying from advanced illnesses.•Distinguishing opioid toxicity from natural dying is crucial to avoid over- or under-treatment with opioids, thus ensuring patient comfort and minimising distress.•Adjusting the opioid regimen can usually enable a balance of the beneficial effects and minimise the burden of toxicities; this involves a change in opioid in patients with pain and opioid toxicity.•Life-threatening opioid-induced respiratory depression (evidenced by a decrease in both respiratory rate and oxygen saturations) is rare in patients on regular opioids for symptom management.•Opioid-induced respiratory depression is managed with stimulation and oxygenation; rarely, low-dose IV naloxone (20–100 μg every 1–2 min) is needed in this setting.Keywords: Opioid, Natural dying, Advanced illness, Respiratory depression, Naloxone, Pain, Sedation

Introduction

Opioids are commonly used to manage pain and breathlessness in advanced illness.1, 2, 3 Opioids have a range of toxicities, which include neurotoxicities such as sedation, respiratory depression, myoclonus and delirium (Table 1).1,2,4 Sedation and delirium are also common in patients who are naturally dying,5 but might be misattributed to opioids. Toxicities are more likely during increases in opioid plasma concentration, such as during initiation, titration, changes in opioid type or altered metabolism/excretion.6, 7, 8 Toxicities are more likely with rapid dose increases and in patients with renal or hepatic failure (although there are differences between opioids), as well as with other medications with overlapping toxicities.2,4,8

Toxicities need to be assessed in the patient’s individual context. Distinguishing opioid toxicity from natural dying avoids over- or under-treatment with opioids, ensuring comfort and minimising distress. Adjusting the opioid regimen usually enables a balance of the beneficial effects and minimise the burden of toxicities. In patients with pain and opioid toxicity, a change in opioid and/or adding non-opioid analgesics might be needed to optimise comfort.9

Natural dying is the process of death occurring as a result of the body’s natural disease progression or aging, without medical interventions aimed at artificially prolonging or shortening life. This is marked by end-of-life signs, indicating the final stage of a disease trajectory.10

The aim of this review is to discuss natural dying, opioid use in patients with advanced, life-limiting illness, the manifestations of opioid toxicity, and factors helping to distinguish opioid toxicity from natural dying. It also provides a guide to managing opioid toxicity, including opioid-induced respiratory depression, in the context of advanced illness. The review is focused on patients taking opioids for symptom management. There are many definitions and meaning of end of life;11 it is thus best to define this by using a time frame of estimated prognosis. This could be the last months, weeks, days or hours of life. These timeframes are useful for end-of-life care planning and communication with the patient and family.11 This article is about patients in the last days of life and about differentiation of opioid toxicity from natural dying.

Natural dying

Natural dying is a core part of clinical care that physicians and healthcare professionals (HCPs) have the opportunity to witness and understand during routine clinical practice.11 The natural process of dying, although variable in terms of rapidity and symptomatology, usually involves a predictable progressive clinical and functional deterioration that can often be recognised, albeit with a degree of prognostic uncertainty, especially in the last weeks of life.12, 13, 14, 15 There are clinical signs and observations that change in the final days/weeks of life.10 Although it is a clinical diagnosis, many tools exist to help guide prognostication, such as the Palliative Prognostic Score (PaP) and the ‘3-day surprise question’, which, along with a multimodal multidisciplinary team (MDT) approach, can lead to planning.10,12,13,16 Recognition can be taught.17

A comprehensive assessment should include collateral history from the patient, family and HCPs to understand the full context.10 When planning care, it is important to clarify the goals (the overall priorities and desired outcomes – what is important for the patient/family, within the context of clinical realities) and ensure that the patient/family share a clear understanding of expectations. The patient’s medical history and the broader context of their illness, overall prognosis and likely course of deterioration need to be considered.10 For example, in a patient with advanced heart failure on maximal treatment who has become increasingly bedbound and breathless, HCPs should confirm whether the patient and family understand that the decline is part of the natural disease trajectory, rather than opioid toxicity or reversible illness. Goals of care might then focus on comfort rather than repeated hospital admissions or invasive interventions.

Signs of natural dying include reduced consciousness, altered breathing patterns (eg Cheyne–Stokes respirations) and reduced oral intake. These signs are normal in the last days of life.15 Early indicators of dying such as decreased level of consciousness, a Palliative Performance Scale ≤20%, and dysphagia of liquids are common but lack specificity.10 In contrast, late signs including pulselessness of the radial artery, respiration with mandibular movement, decreased urine output, Cheyne–Stokes respiration and death rattle occur shortly before death and demonstrate very high specificity and positive likelihood ratios for predicting death within 3 days.10 Management of natural dying generally does not need pharmacological intervention, although all patients in the last weeks/months of life need access to anticipatory medications (subcutaneously medications, including opioids, prescribed in anticipation of symptoms or inability to swallow oral medication, designed to enable rapid symptom relief).10 Patients might also need regular medication administration via a continuous subcutaneous syringe driver, if they needed several doses of anticipatory medications or were previously on oral symptom control medications (such as opioids for pain). The mainstay of management is advance care planning and explanation to family. This includes educating the family on natural dying and, if there is a question of opioid toxicity, the difference between opioid toxicity and natural signs of death. After assessment, it might be appropriate to maintain or even increase opioids, if signs and symptoms of pain or breathlessness, to ensure comfort.

Family members (and HCPs) might be concerned that opioids are hastening death, particularly if they are unfamiliar with the process of natural dying.18,19 It is essential to provide clear explanations about the role of opioids in relieving suffering and the difference between opioid toxicity and the expected symptoms of dying. When death is expected, there may be a reluctance to escalate opioids for fear of making the situation worse.12, 13, 14 If the plan is to decrease opioids, ensure it is clearly communicated in the context of patient and families understanding. Patients who are naturally dying might have symptoms that overlap with the signs of opioid toxicity, such as decreased consciousness, irregular breathing and somnolence.15 It is important to consider if there is anything reversible in someone who is dying (eg opioid/drug toxicity, dehydration, hypercalcaemia or infection), its potential causality to dying and its appropriateness to intervene in natural dying.

Opioids in patients with advanced life-limiting illness

Opioids are a key treatment for moderate to severe pain and breathlessness in palliative care. Even at the end of life, opioids must be used appropriately and monitored. They should not primarily be used for sedation or management of tachypnoea (in the absence of breathlessness). Morphine, fentanyl, oxycodone and hydromorphone are among the commonly prescribed opioids, with differences between their side effect profile, metabolism and excretion.2,8

Opioids are often used in patients who are naturally dying. Fear about opioids is common among patients, relatives and HCPs.18,19 This is partly stems from the association of opioid use and dose increases near death.18 There might also be lack of clear communication from HCPs about this being an association (more opioids are needed for comfort in some people who are dying), rather than causation (opioids causing the dying in patients at the end of life).20,21 No robust clinical evidence suggests that opioids in the last days/weeks of life hasten death.20,21 Nevertheless, patients and relatives might not want opioids or opioid dose increases, and HCPs might be reluctant to prescribe or adjust opioids leading to increased suffering.18,19

Despite their benefits in patients with advanced illnesses, there are risks of opioid use, including a range of toxicities.2,22 These can occur with opioid dose increases and potentially in patients with renal or hepatic impairment, as opioids and their metabolites can accumulate (although there are differences between opioids).4,7,8 There is usually a temporal relationship between the opioid plasma concentration change (based on dose and metabolism) and toxicity.23 Balancing symptom control with the risk of toxicity is essential, particularly at the end of life.24,25

Opioid toxicities

Opioids have a range of toxicities that can affect multiple organs and systems (Table 1).26 Opioid neurotoxicity might resemble natural dying by causing sedation and/or delirium, causing diagnostic and management uncertainty. Common opioid toxicities which can overlap with natural dying are primarily neurotoxicities which include sedation (drowsiness to unresponsiveness) and delirium (confusion, hallucinations and agitation or somnolence).5,27

Toxicities from opioids often occur during opioid initiation, dose increase or metabolism/excretion decrease. Opioid-induced neurotoxicity arises via mechanisms that are less directly related to μ-opioid receptor activation and may involve accumulation of opioid metabolites (eg morphine-3-glucuronide), interactions with other receptor systems such as N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptors, or alterations in neurotransmitter release.4 Management of opioid-induced toxicity involves dose reduction or changing opioids.1 These can also be associated with dying or metabolic issues like dehydration, hypercalcaemia or infection, as well as other medications.28,29

Albeit rare in this setting, severe opioid toxicity has the potential to cause dying by inducing respiratory depression, requiring urgent assessment and management.1,30,31

Opioid-induced respiratory depression

A feared and often inappropriately managed opioid toxicity in patients on long-term opioids for chronic pain from advanced illness is opioid-induced respiratory depression.2,30,32 This is primarily mediated via µ-opioid receptors in the pre-Bötzinger complex, a respiratory rhythm generating area in the pons,33 whereby opioids reduce the sensitivity of respiratory centres in the brainstem to carbon dioxide, leading to slowed (respiratory rate < eight breaths/min) and then shallow breathing, resulting in hypoxia and hypercapnia. This rare toxicity, in patients on long-term opioids for pain, needs careful assessment and management to avoid harm.1,30,31 Even in chronic breathlessness, there is no evidence of significant or clinically relevant respiratory adverse effects of opioids.30 Although naloxone is sometimes given, this is often inappropriately so, as it is not without risk in patients on long-term opioids for pain (Table 2).28,34 Naloxone is very appropriately used and lifesaving in people with opioid-induced respiratory depression who are not on long-term opioids for chronic pain.35,36 However, this practice can be harmful to patients on long-term opioids for chronic pain, especially when they do not have opioid-induced respiratory depression or when larger than necessary doses are administered (Table 2, Table 4).30,32,37 The initial step in the management of opioid-induced respiratory depression is an urgent assessment.

Assessment

In opioid-induced respiratory depression, urgent assessment and management are paramount. Opioid-induced respiratory depression leads to reduction in both respiratory rate and oxygen saturations, so it is vital that both respiratory rate and oxygen saturations are contemporaneously measured and both are decreased before diagnosing opioid-induced respiratory depression.26,37

Otherwise (and after managing opioid-induced respiratory depression), there is time for a detailed assessment to differentiate opioid toxicity from natural dying; this might involve longitudinal assessments and collateral histories.32,37 The purpose of this assessment is to determine the likelihood of opioid toxicity causing the symptoms resembling natural dying.32 As there is potential overlap of opioid toxicity with signs of natural dying, as opioids might have been increased due to pain severity in someone approaching the end of life, and as there are risks from decreasing opioids indiscriminately, this highlights the importance of a rigorous assessment and clinical judgement in these situations (Table 3).32

Assess other health issues that might contribute to sedation, confusion or respiratory changes, such as infections, metabolic imbalances (eg dehydration, renal impairment), or other new medications that may potentiate opioid effects, or cause sedation in their own right.28,29 It should be noted that there is no evidence to show an association between decreased survival and benzodiazepines in patients with cancer.38 Although there is increased risk of respiratory depression and death with coadministration of opioids and central nervous system depressants across different clinical and non-clinical settings,39 there is no evidence for this in a hospice setting.39

In the assessment, it is essential to get a clear understanding of the temporal picture of symptoms and signs and the relationship of these with recent changes in opioids (as well as other medications, either starting or stopping, as often happen when patients change their setting of care and/or are in the last days of life). Assessing the temporal relationship with opioid dose changes (initiation, increases or changes), PRN (pro re nata – as needed) use, errors (prescription/administration/accidental), opioid metabolism and excretion is needed to ascertain the likelihood of opioids causing any signs resembling natural dying (Table 3). Checking renal and hepatic synthetic function, as well as drug interactions that may increase opioid levels, any unprescribed drugs being used (or stopped) and treatment that might have reduced pain and opioid need (eg nerve blocks, radiotherapy), is crucial. Opioid toxicity may develop rapidly, especially with high doses or combinations with other sedative medication. Patients naturally dying often show a gradual decline in responsiveness. They may become increasingly unresponsive, but can still occasionally respond to verbal cues or gentle stimulation. Symptoms typically evolve over days to weeks, with a gradual decline in physical and cognitive function, often culminating in a comfortable death.11

Managing opioid toxicity requires a thorough approach, focusing on promptly identifying symptoms, evaluating the severity, and providing appropriate interventions to counteract the effects and stabilise the patient.

Address contributing factors – patients with advanced illness are at risk of infection, dehydration, hypercalcaemia or hyponatraemia, which can worsen delirium and sedation. These potentially mimic (or exacerbate) signs of opioid toxicity and even dying. Where appropriate, these should be assessed for and corrected if appropriate and it fits with the goals of care. If multiple factors are deemed likely for the deterioration, assessment and correction of these should be done at the same time as any necessary opioid changes.

Conclusion

A careful clinical assessment is needed to differentiate opioid toxicity from natural dying in patients who have advanced illness, as both share overlapping symptoms. The exact clinical presentation (eg respiratory patterns), the timing of symptoms onset in relation to opioid exposure (dose/route, metabolism and excretion) and the response to opioid adjustments guide the diagnosis and management. Opioid toxicity is usually reversible with reducing the dose or changing the opioid, allowing patients to maintain comfort without undue harm. Ultimately, compassionate care, prioritising the patient’s comfort and dignity at the end of life, is paramount.
