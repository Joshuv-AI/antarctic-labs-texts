Diagnostic performance of procalcitonin for predicting bloodstream infection and guidance on microbial aetiology: a prospective study at an Egyptian tertiary care hospital
Authors: Amira Muhammad Abbas, Heba Sherif Abdel Aziz, Reham Mohammad Raafat Hamed, Soliman Belal Soliman, Sara El-Sayed Abd El-Ghani, Noha Salah Soliman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBlood stream infection (BSI) is a life-threatening condition that implies prompt diagnosis and management. With the time-consuming traditional microbiological culture and limited provision of molecular diagnostics, procalcitonin (PCT) has emerged as a rising rapid biomarker for prediction of BSI.AimWe aimed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of PCT in predicting BSI as well as in tentative differentiation of the possible underlying microbial etiology.MethodsBlood samples were collected from total 328 patients suspected of having BSI and were measured for levels of procalcitonin, C-reactive protein (CRP) and total leucocyte count (TLC). Concomitant microbiological culture was done for detection and identification of microbial etiology.ResultsMicrobial cultures were positive in 47.9% and negative in 52.1% of patients. Bacterial and fungal growth accounted for 87.8% and 12.1%, respectively, where bacterial growth was differentiated as Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. Culture-positive group displayed significantly higher median values of PCT (2.69 ng/ml) and CRP (117.2 ng/ml) than culture-negative group. PCT levels were significantly higher among Gram-negative than Gram-positive cultures, however were non-significantly higher among bacterial than fungal positive-cultures. Compared to other biomarkers, PCT displayed the highest diagnostic accuracy in differentiating culture-positive and -negative BSI (ROC-AUC: 0.706, P < 0.001) at cut-off value of 0.675 ng/ml, as well as in discriminating Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria (ROC-AUC:0.636, P: 0.004) at cut-off value of 2.135 ng/ml.ConclusionPCT exhibited higher diagnostic accuracy than other biomarkers in BSI and in differentiating Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacterial etiology, which may offer a modest guidance in sepsis management and optimization of antibiotic intake.Clinical trialNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13794-6.Keywords: Blood stream infections, Sepsis, Procalcitonin, Biomarkers

Background

Bloodstream infection (BSI) poses a significant threat to public health, especially in hospitalized patients, resulting in economic burdens on healthcare systems [1]. Surveillance studies indicate that BSI represents a substantial public health burden in Egypt, accounting for approximately 30–56% of healthcare-associated infections in intensive care units [2, 3]. Despite significant focus on BSI and advancements in laboratory diagnostics, this infection remains a serious healthcare emergency, with reported mortality rates reaching up to 43% among patients with healthcare-associated infections in Egyptian setting and a range of 10% to 20% in global literature [2, 4].

Early diagnosis of BSI and identification of the causative bacterial or fungal pathogens is essential for the commencement of appropriate antimicrobial treatment, which significantly affects patient clinical outcomes and survival rates [5]. A one-hour delay in initiating antimicrobial treatment increases mortality rates by 7.6% [5]. Conversely, the indiscriminate prescription of broad-spectrum antibiotics can contribute to the emergence of antimicrobial resistance, opportunistic infections, and increased financial burden on healthcare systems [6]. This renders ruling out BSI as important as its confirmed early detection. Microbiological culture is the gold standard for diagnosis of BSI and underlying pathogens. However, it is time-consuming and exhibits low sensitivity, possibly due to prior antibiotic use, low bacterial load, or the presence of non-cultivable fastidious bloodstream pathogens [5]. Over the past decade, several technologies have emerged to swift the diagnosis of BSI, such as automated blood culture systems and molecular methods. Nevertheless, these techniques are relatively expensive and require technical expertise [7].

The assessment of sepsis biomarkers is increasingly recognized as an efficient approach for the early identification of BSI, aiding clinicians in evaluating the risks and benefits associated with initiating or holding unnecessary antibiotic treatment [8]. While widely utilized, conventional biomarkers of sepsis, including C-reactive protein (CRP) and leucocytic count, exhibit reduced specificity. Procalcitonin (PCT) is a promising biomarker whose elevated levels correlate significantly with systemic bacterial infections [9]. PCT can significantly increase in response to bacterial endotoxin entering the bloodstream [8]. Furthermore, blood levels may vary based on different etiological factors [10]. Therefore, PCT may serve as a valuable tool for predicting the diagnosis of BSI and guiding for the presumed microbial etiology, which enables tailored antibiotic treatment [8, 11, 12]. Nevertheless, elevated levels of PCT in non-infectious systemic inflammations may raise concerns regarding its diagnostic performance in BSI [8]. There is no currently universal consensus on the optimal use of PCT, compounded by the limited published data from our country concerning its diagnostic role in BSI, optimal cutoff values, and guidance on potential underlying microbial etiology for appropriate antibiotic use [8, 13]. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic performance and optimum cutoff values of PCT biomarkers in the detection of BSI and differentiate the potential underlying microbial etiology.

Methods

The present study was conducted on patients clinically suspected of having BSI admitted to Cairo University tertiary-care hospitals over a period between first May 2024 to 30 November 2024. The study enrolled adult patients experiencing their first episode of suspected bacteremia presenting with acute fever (≥ 38 °C) with no apparent signs of other types of infections. The study excluded children, pregnant females, and patients with a history of organ transplantation, surgery or trauma within 3 months, autoimmune diseases, or intake of immune-suppressive drugs. All study subjects concomitantly underwent laboratory investigations that included a microbiological blood culture and measurement of serum levels of PCT, CRP, and TLC biomarkers at the time of clinical suspicion of BSI. Patients’ data was collected, including demography, admission location, length of hospital stay, and survival outcome. Patient samples were de-identified and anonymously analyzed to ensure blind randomization.

Ethical approval

The Research Ethics Committee (REC) of the Faculty of Medicine, Cairo University approved this study under the reference number (N-88-2024).

Microbiological blood culture and identification of microbial isolates

Under strict aseptic conditions, we obtained two whole blood samples from each patient, each containing 8–10 ml. The collected samples were inoculated into blood culture bottles and incubated in an automatic blood culture system BACTEC9240 (Becton Dickinson, SARKS, MD, USA) for 5- days of continuous monitoring. Specimens from positive-signaled bottles were cultured on blood agar, chocolate agar, and MacConkey agar media (Oxoid, Egypt) and incubated overnight at 37 °C. The microbial isolates grown in culture were identified by Gram stain microscopic examination and conventional biochemical reactions in accordance with standard microbiological procedures [14].

Measurement of biomarkers (PCT, CRP, and TLC) for BSI

PCT and CRP biomarker levels were determined by analyzing patients’ sera from blood samples. Serum PCT was measured by electro-chemiluminescent immunoassay using Elecsys BRAHMS PCT kit operated on automated device COBAS e 601 (Roche, Switzerland) according to manufacturer’s instructions. PCT was detected by combining the one-step sandwich method and the final fluorescence detection (ELFA) method, where values < 0.5 ng/mL represent a low risk of severe sepsis and/or septic shock, whereas > 2.0 ng/ml represent a high risk of severe sepsis and/or septic shock [15]. CRP serum levels were measured using a nephelometric technique on the COBAS b 101 instruments (Roche, Switzerland). Briefly, serum collected from patients was incubated with antisera containing specific human CRP antibodies, and the formation of agglutinates was monitored for 6 min. The concentration of CRP protein was accurately determined from a standard curve by utilizing the formation rate of agglutinates. The expected value in adults is < 5 mg/dL [16]. Additionally, the count of total blood leucocytes was measured using a Sysmex XN-1000™ Hematology Analyzer (Lincolnshire, IL, USA), as per the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical methods

Statistical analyses were performed using the statistical package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 28 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data was summarized using mean, standard deviation, median, minimum, and maximum in quantitative data, as well as frequency (count) and relative frequency (percentage) for categorical data. Quantitative variables were compared using non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis and Mann-Whitney tests. The Spearman correlation coefficient was utilized to assess correlations between quantitative variables. The Receiver-operating characteristic curve was constructed using the area under the curve (ROC-AUC) to determine the optimal cutoff value of significant parameters for detecting BSI and differentiating etiological microbial groups. P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Multivariate Logistic regression was done to adjust for confounders that may influence the use of PCT in prediction of BSI using significant parameters.

Results

Demographic and clinical data

The study included 328 patients who met the inclusion criteria. The average age of the patients was 64 years (± 15.26), with 59.1% identifying as male and 40.9% as female in the study. The median length of stay (LOS) was 15 days, with 86.9% of the patient population residing in the intensive care unit (ICU) and 13.1% in the ward. The patient population was located within the hospital. The clinical outcome was a hospital discharge for 60.1% (n = 197) of patients, while 29.9% (n = 131) of patients died.

Blood culture results and microbial profiles

Among total cultured samples of 328 patients, microbial growth was observed in 47.9% (n = 157) of cultured samples, whereas 52.1% (n = 171) exhibited no microbial growth. Bacterial and fungal growth was identified in 87.8% (n = 138) and 12.1% (n = 19) of positive cultures (n = 157), respectively (Fig. 1). Isolated bacteria were differentiated as Gram-positive (n = 72) and Gram-negative organisms (n = 66). The growth outcomes of cultured blood samples and the types of isolated organisms are presented in Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 1.

Measurements of Procalcitonin among culture results

Of the total patient cohort (n = 328), the PCT exhibited median (IQR) values of 1.44 (0.32–5.17) ng/ml (95% CI: 0.85–1.89). The median (IQR) values for CRP and TLC were recorded as 108.4 (46.82–172.2) (95% CI: 95.5-117.2) and 12.05 (8.35–17.15) (95% CI: 11.1–12.9), respectively. Diagnostic performance of PCT as a predictive biomarker of BSI compared to other markers

The Receiver Operating Characteristic curve (ROC) curve was constructed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of PCT, TLC, and CRP biomarkers in BSI. The diagnostic accuracy for differentiating between culture-positive and culture-negative BSI was modest for PCT (ROC-AUC: 0.706) at the cutoff value of 0.675 ng/ml, with sensitivity and specificity of 78.1% and 54.5%, respectively, however was significantly the highest among other biomarkers (P < 0.001). At the cut-off > 0.675 ng/mL the PCT remained a significant independent predictor of BSI (adjusted OR = 4.422, 95% CI: 2.674–7.313, p < 0.001), by adjusting for potential confounders using multiple log regression (Table 4). The CRP displayed a significant ROC-AUC of 0.573 at a cutoff value of 104.585, yet weaker than PCT (P-value of 0.02) (Table 5). The diagnostic accuracy of TLC demonstrated no significant differentiation between culture-positive and culture-negative BSI, with a P-value of 0.483. It did not yield relevant cutoff, sensitivity, or specificity values regarding the bacterial or fungal etiology of BSI. The PCT demonstrated the least effective performance in differentiating bacterial and fungal infections (ROC-AUC: 0.589, P: 0.179). Conversely, CRP and TLC exhibited a significant discriminatory performance (Table 5). However, PCT was the only biomarker demonstrating significant efficacy in differentiating between Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria at a cutoff value of 2.135 ng/mL (ROC-AUC: 0.636, P: 0.004), with a sensitivity and a specificity of 66.7% and 55.7%, respectively (Table 5; Fig. 5).

Discussion

The global burden of mortality associated with bloodstream infections (BSI) is significant, making early diagnosis essential for better enhance clinical outcomes [17]. Recent studies indicate that blood biomarkers outweigh timely microbiological culture in early diagnosis of BSI [5]. The present study aimed to evaluate the performance of PCT in the diagnosis of BSI and in tentative differentiation of the potential microbial etiology.

We analyzed blood samples from a total of 328 potentially BSI patients predominantly in ICUs (86.9%). The demographic data matched previous studies [18, 19]. Blood cultures were positive in 47.9% of patients, with bacterial growth rates (87.8%) surpassing fungal growth (12.1%). The finding of predominant Gram-positive bacteria aligns with the microbial profile of BSIs reported by prior studies [5, 20]. The observed over-representation of Gram-negative bacteria in other studies [19, 21] may be attributed to differences in study populations and local microbial ecology of different healthcare settings [22].

In this study, the median value of PCT was 2.69 ng/ml, with higher levels in culture-positive patients compared to culture-negative patients (P < 0.001). This confirms recent reports of PCT values ranging from 1.79 to 3.2 ng/ml [12, 21], highlighting high PCT serum levels in BSI [8, 23, 24]. Excessive production of PCT in bacteremia results from the interaction between bacterial endotoxins and host immune mediators [23, 25]. Well-known biomarkers for predicting infectious diseases are CRP and TLC, however, they frequently exhibit low specificity due to other non-infectious inflammatory disorders [12, 20]. Other biomarkers demonstrated elevated median values in culture-positive patients compared to culture-negative patients, although their significance was lower than that of PCT.

Our study observed that the median values of PCT were higher in bacterial (3.11 ng/ml) than fungal BSI (2.12 ng/ml). Previous reports, including a recent meta-analysis, indicated that PCT levels are significantly higher in bacteremia than in candidemia [12, 26, 27], likely attributed to distinct microbe-specific signaling, which induces distinct cytokine responses [12]. Reagrding bacterial aetiology, patients with isolated Gram-negative bacteria exhibited significantly elevated PCT (5.26 ng/ml) than Gram-positive bacteria (2.06 ng/ml). This can be explained by variations in bacterial cell membrane composition, which results in the activation of distinct host cell Toll-like receptors and subsequent release of different immune mediators [28]. The CRP demonstrated comparable findings to PCT, suggesting that the reasons outlined above for PCT may also apply to CRP, since both markers are assigned as acute phase reactants [12].

The demographic and clinical data revealed a negative correlation between the three biomarkers and patient age, with PCT showing the highest significance (P < 0.001). This may be linked to the more aggressive sepsis immune responses, which decline with age [29]. Another study found no correlation between age and serum PCT levels [29]. Nevertheless, PCT is considered a valuable tool for predicting BSI in both young and old age [8]. This study witnessed a positive correlation between PCT levels and length of stay (LOS), suggesting that extended hospitalizations may increase infection risk [30]. Furthermore, ICU patients had higher PCT levels than ward patients, corroborating previous studies, probably due to their original critical state [31, 32].

Our study demonstrated significantly higher median values of PCT (P < 0.001) in death compared to survival outcomes, in agreement with previous studies which have shown that rising PCT values anticipate adverse outcomes, and vice-versa [33]. This signifies the role of PCT in sepsis follow-up for enhancing clinical outcomes, through PCT-guided antibiotic de-escalation [34–36]. According to a recent meta-analysis, PCT-guided management of sepsis decreased antibiotic therapy duration from 10.4 to 9.3 days, and reduced mortality rates from 23.7% to 21.1% [35], supporting prior studies [34, 36]. Evidence increasingly supports the role of PCT in antibiotic de-escalation. Nevertheless, according to the guidelines from the Infectious Disease Society of America (IDSA) and the Surviving Sepsis Campaign (SCC), PCT is not yet recommended for therapeutic escalation or antibiotic initiation, due to the potential for adverse outcomes associated with the drug toxicity burden on human organs [23, 37, 38].

Our study revealed that PCT showed a modest, yet higher diagnostic accuracy than CRP and TLC in differentiating culture-positive from culture-negative patients (ROC-AUC: 0.76, cut-off: 0.67, P < 0.001)), with a sensitivity and a specificity of 78.1% and 54.5%, respectively. Previous research on the diagnostic accuracy of PCT in sepsis has yielded divergent outcomes [23]. Our findings align with multiple studies that indicate diagnostic accuracies of PCT, with sensitivity ranging from 74% to 84% and specificity from 53% to 79%, respectively [8, 19, 23, 39]. Other studies reported higher PCT ROC-AUC values (0.85–0.96) than ours [20, 25, 40]. PCT diagnostic accuracies may vary due to multiple factors, such as sample size, study population, critical illnesses, presence of concurrent systemic inflammations, and research design, whether encompassing both bacteremia and fungaemia or only bacteremia [23]. The multiple log regression in our study supports that PCT above the 0.675 ng/mL cut-off is an independent predictor of BSI, with an adjusted odds ratio of 4.42 after controlling for age, sex, and ICU admission (P < 0.001). However, several caveats must be emphasized, such as not including severity scores which, were not available in our dataset, thus a possibility of a residual confounding by illness severity remains. Moreover, it is worth to note that despite of the observed significantly higher PCT levels in culture-positive than culture-negative patients (P < 0.001), yet the diagnostic performance of PCT in this study was only modest (AUC 0.706), with a low specificity of 54.5%, indicating that PCT should be interpreted strictly as an adjunct to clinical assessment and blood cultures, not as a standalone diagnostic test. This supports the assertion of not over-relying on this biomarker for clinical decision making.

Our study found that PCT exhibited greater sensitivity than specificity at an optimal threshold of 0.67 ng/ml, suggesting it may be better at ruling out BSI than confirming it. Other studies similarly ruled out BSI at cutoff values of 0.3 to 0.5 ng/ml [8, 23]. A recent study found that the risk of BSI is considered high at PCT levels > 2.0 ng/ml, intermediate between 0.5 and 2.0 ng/ml, and low at < 0.5 ng/ml [20]. Other studies established a higher PCT cutoff of > 3.2 ng/ml for the prediction of bacteremia with 81.1% sensitivity and 63.3% specificity [19, 41]. The discrepancies in cutoff values across various studies may be attributed to the measurement ranges of PCT observed in those studies, influenced by factors, such as study population and critical illnesses [23]. The low specificity of sepsis biomarkers often arises from the difficulty in distinguishing infections from systemic inflammations [23]. However, in literature, PCT demonstrated higher performance over other biomarkers in differentiating BSI from non-infectious inflammatory disorders [8, 20, 23, 40, 42]. A recent study indicated that the combined application of PCT and CRP may improve the diagnostic accuracy of BSI [20].

In our study, PCT had a significant performance in distinguishing between Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteremia (ROC-AUC: 0.636). Likewise, a consensus has emerged across several studies, with comparable ROC-AUCs (0.58–0.92) [43–45]. The diagnostic performance of PCT was lower than that of CRP at distinguishing bacterial and fungal BSIs, contrary to earlier studies. This discrepancy may be attributed to varying PCT levels associating different distributions of Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteremia in those studies [12, 45].

In underdeveloped nations and resource-limited settings, PCT assessment is introduced as a fast, practical, and economical alternative to time-consuming standard blood cultures and limited advanced diagnostics for early BSI prediction [7]. Our study demonstrated that PCT showed higher diagnostic accuracy than other biomarkers in predicting BSI at an optimal cutoff value of 0.675 ng/ml. The greater analytical sensitivity compared to specificity suggests that PCT is potentially useful adjunct as a rule-out test. In clinical practice, this could support withholding or discontinuing antibiotics in patients with low suspicion of BSI, thereby reducing unnecessary antimicrobial exposure [8, 20]. Conversely, PCT levels > 2.0 ng/ml, as previously suggested should raise high suspicion for BSI [15]. Additionally, the significantly elevated PCT values in deceased patients versus survivors may offer modest prognostic guidance in BSI. In this view, PCT-guided algorithms could be integrated into daily rounds of ICUS, with serial measurements to monitor treatment response [34, 35].

The implementation of PCT-guided management for sepsis may enhance antimicrobial stewardship programs (ASP), leading to improved clinical outcomes. PCT may inform antimicrobial therapeutic decisions in ASP by the following: (a) de-escalation – stopping antibiotics when PCT declines from peak or falls below 0.5 ng/mL; (b) avoidance of initiation – when PCT remains low in suspected BSI; and (c) modest guidance to Gram-negative bacteremia is more likely at PCT > 2.1 ng/ml, though cultures remain mandatory [23, 34]. In settings, where broad-spectrum antibiotics are overused, PCT monitoring could curb carbapenem and glycopeptide overprescription, with significant cost savings and reduced resistance pressure. However, it is crucial for the cutoffs to be locally validated before deployment, since optimal thresholds vary by populations [8, 19].

While acknowledging its advantages, over-reliance on PCT may entail certain risks. Potential concerns include false positive results that may occur in non-infectious systemic inflammation (e.g., major surgery, trauma, autoimmune flares, or cardiogenic shock) [23]. Furthermore, insufficient information is available regarding the reliability of the PCT biomarker in immunocompromised patients, and the effective implementation of PCT-guided management in chronic inflammatory conditions [23]. In our study, some patients with negative blood cultures, still had minor PCT elevations – which could be due to non-infectious conditions. Conversely, fungal BSI may provide false negative results due to lower PCT levels than bacterial BSI. This indicates that a low PCT levels must be interpreted with caution to prevent overlooking fungal BSI, which are especially prevalent in immunocompromised and critically ill patients [12, 26]. Therefore, PCT is encouraged to be integrated in diagnostic algorithms to supplement and not replace clinical judgment and microbiological cultures [8, 23, 46].

Early diagnosis of BSI remains challenging since blood cultures take 24–72 h with limited sensitivity, especially in case of prior antibiotic intake [5]. Our study provides highlights on the potential contribution of rapid PCT measurement in early BSI diagnosis and management, that include the following: (1) alerting clinicians to a high likelihood of BSI in case of elevated PCT above the set cut-off value; (2) identifying low-risk patients with low serum levels (PCT < 0.5 ng/mL), who may safely avoid antibiotics while awaiting cultures, reducing antibiotics overuse and adverse drug events; (3) guiding selection of anti-Gram-negative or anti-Gram-positive antibiotics– when PCT exceeds 2.1 ng/mL, anti-Gram-negative coverage becomes more appropriate, whereas lower levels with high clinical suspicion might favor anti-Gram-positive agents; (4) facilitating antimicrobial de-escalation upon observing declines in PCT during patient follow-up. In settings with limited resources, PCT may play a rapid potential role in prioritizing patients in need of immediate blood cultures and intensive monitoring, ultimately saving lives and reducing healthcare expenditures.

While numerous studies have evaluated PCT for predicting BSIs, yet most of the data is reported from other high-income countries and regional areas of different populations, with striking paucity of data from developing nations including Egypt and other areas in the Middle-East North-African (MENA) region. Population-specific variations can significantly influence PCT kinetics in response to BSI, thus affecting serum elevations, and recommended cut-off values for early BSI prediction. Our study provides inaugural comparative evaluation of PCT in relation to other biomarkers within an Egyptian tertiary care cohort, generating locally validated cut-off values for BSI prediction. Additionally, we emphasize the optimum cutoff levels for preliminary microbial etiology distinction, which has been a relatively underexplored area in literature. This can inform similar low-resource settings on integrating PCT into diagnostic algorithms for the early BSI prediction, in adjunct to clinical diagnosis and standard blood culture. Furthermore, it promises to enhance ASP by advising on antimicrobial de-escalation, thus improving therapeutic outcomes and mitigating antimicrobial resistance.

Limitations

Few limitations of this study should be acknowledged. Initially, biomarker samples were drawn simultaneously with blood cultures, rather than prior to the peak of clinical suspicion, which would have enhanced their clinical relevance for early prediction. However, such a design was not feasible in our acute care setting. Secondly, the data on the precise time of blood sample collection in relation to the initiation of empirical therapy was not systematically documented, probably due to insufficient reliable records and the variability in timing of antibiotic administration prior to hospital admission. Third, severity scores (SOFA) in sepsis patients, as well as the diagnoses of blood culture negative cases were not accessible, likely due to lack of consistent documentation in the medical records of our tertiary care hospital. Consequently, our findings primarily support the utilization of PCT as a complementary measure alongside concurrent blood cultures and in adjunct to clinical assessment. Furthermore, our reported cut-off values must be regarded with caution and may not be immediately applicable to different contexts without prior local validation. It is recommended for future prospective studies to integrate sepsis severity scores into biomarker analyses, and to include data like onset to blood collection and prior administration of antibiotics, to enhance validation of marker cut-off values.

Conclusion

PCT serves as a rapid and useful biomarker for predicting BSI and offers early indications of microbial etiology. In our Egyptian cohort, it demonstrated a modest diagnostic accuracy with higher performance compared to CRP and TLC. PCT is considered a rapid adjuvant tool to clinical judgement and blood cultures, rather than a standalone test, that can enhance antimicrobial stewardship programs and improve sepsis outcomes, particularly in settings where advanced diagnostics are limited.
