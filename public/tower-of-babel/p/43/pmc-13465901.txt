Comment on: Can depression, anxiety, and stress predict smoking?
Authors: Parthkumar Dhameliya
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

The February 2026 issue of the Journal of Family Medicine and Primary Care featured a compelling study by Shah et al.[1] titled “Can depression, anxiety and stress predict smoking? An analysis of mental health correlates among university young adults in Saudi Arabia”. By investigating the intersection of psychological distress and tobacco use among Saudi undergraduates, the authors have contributed a vital perspective to our understanding of regional public health dynamics.

A primary takeaway from their analysis is the positive correlation between anxiety and smoking behaviors. This reinforces a global body of literature suggesting that nicotine is frequently utilized as a form of self-medication to manage anxious symptoms.[2] Recognizing anxiety as a significant predictor provides a clear mandate for university health centers to merge psychiatric screening with tobacco cessation programs.

Perhaps most striking is the finding that stress was inversely related to smoking, a result that stands in stark contrast to most Western epidemiological trends. Shah et al. prudently attribute this to local “cultural buffers,” specifically the protective roles of religious observance and robust familial networks, which appear to mitigate the impact of stress without a reliance on tobacco. This highlights the necessity of designing interventions that are culturally resonant rather than merely adopting universal models.[3]

Furthermore, the lack of a significant association between depression and smoking suggests a nuanced diagnostic landscape. While historical data often link the two,[4] the authors’ findings may reflect a high degree of symptomatic overlap with anxiety or perhaps a shifting social awareness of depression that encourages healthier coping strategies. Such distinctions are theoretically vital for the development of targeted, evidence-based mental health support.

From a methodological standpoint, the study’s cross-sectional framework precludes any definitive claims regarding causality. Additionally, the reliance on self-reported data may introduce social desirability bias, and the absence of variables like peer influence or socioeconomic status leaves room for further exploration. However, these points do not overshadow the study’s utility; rather, they serve as a roadmap for future inquiry.

In summary, Shah et al. have successfully mapped the unique psychological correlates of smoking in a specific cultural context. Their work underscores that while anxiety remains a driver for tobacco use, factors like stress and depression may manifest differently across cultures. Moving forward, longitudinal research will be essential to untangle these causal threads and refine integrated clinical approaches.
