Olanzapine Induced Necrotizing Pancreatitis and Pancreatico-Pleural Fistula Mandating Robotic Pancreatectomy
Authors: Rania Atto, Emanuel A Shapera
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction:Olanzapine as a cause of necrotizing pancreatitis is very rare. Unheard of in the literature is the development of a pancreatico-pleural fistula following olanzapine induced necrotizing pancreatitis requiring minimally invasive pancreatectomy to resolve the disease. This report seeks to publish the history, findings and strategies used to resolve this extremely unique case.Case Description:A 53-year-old male presented with over a year of chronic abdominal pain. He was diagnosed with pancreatitis. His presentation worsened, leading to necrosis, peripancreatic fluid collections and ultimately a pancreatico-pleural fistula. Despite a low-fat diet, confirmation of alcohol abstinence, absence of gallstones, pancreatic duct stenting, peritoneal and pleural drainage, the fistula persisted and his weight loss worsened. Hepatobiliary surgery was consulted, performed a successful minimally invasive pancreatectomy. His drains were removed sequentially and at final follow up was free of symptoms, regaining weight.Discussion:Olanzapine as a cause of acute necrotizing pancreatitis is rare. For it to result in necrosis, peripancreatic fluid collections and a pancreatico-pleural fistula is even rarer. This case failed to resolve with nonsurgical management. Robotic technology combined with appropriate endoscopic, interventional radiologic and nutritional therapy resolved this case, highlighting strategies that will be useful to physicians and their patients, as well as being of great interest.Keywords: Fistula, Olanzapine, Pancreatectomy, Pancreatic pseudocyst

INTRODUCTION

Adverse drug reaction is an uncommon cause of acute pancreatitis, overshadowed by gallstones and alcohol abuse.1,2 There are numerous mechanisms for this disease: ischemia, ductal stricture, accumulation of end products, direct toxicity, and metabolic disease. The latter two maybe the mechanism by which Olanzapine, a rare cause of this phenomenon, induces pancreatitis.3 Rarer still is a severe necrotizing pancreatitis resulting from this drug leading to pancreatic leak, fistulization into the pleural space and pancreatico-pleural fistula.4 Unheard of and hitherto unreported is a case of Olanzapine induced acute on chronic pancreatitis with subsequent development of pancreatio-pleural fistula mandating resection.5 We hope the publication of this unique occurrence will educate providers and patients in all aspects of this interesting, multifaceted case.

CASE REPORT

A 53-year-old male presented with persistent epigastric pain in November 2024. He had a past medical history of hyperlipidemia, diverticulosis, and bipolar I disorder, for which he was on Olanzapine monotherapy for approximately 4 months at the time of presentation. He underwent computed tomography (CT) scanning and lab work; the former identified stranding around the pancreatic tail and calcifications, suggestive of chronic pancreatitis, while the latter demonstrated a markedly elevated serum lipase >10,000 IU/L (Figure 1) but a normal triglyceride level of 215 mg/dL. Right upper quadrant ultrasound confirmed the absence of gallstones and yielded no murphy sign. Having no gallstones on imaging and no clinical or laboratory evidence of alcohol abuse, no definitive etiology of his pancreatitis was determined. After conservative management with a low-fat diet, he was discharged home. He had recurring bouts of pain for a few months, with CT scan in late November and early December of 2024 demonstrating similar findings (Figure 2). Urine toxicology was persistently negative for alcohol abuse.

The patient remained with chronic smoldering pain that worsened to a severe level mandating emergency room (ER) representation in early August 2025. A large, simple and walled-off peripancreatic fluid collection developed in the left retroperitoneum (Figure 3). Due to the size and presence of pain, an interventional-radiologic guided drain was placed with fluid analysis returning a thin, amylase rich result (>16,000 IU/ml) (Figure 4). Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) was performed with pancreatic duct stent placement to promote forward drainage and resolution of the leak. Interventional radiology (IR) drain was removed 2 weeks later and pancreatic duct stent replaced. At this point, thorough history taking confirmed the cause to be Olanzapine, with imaging and labs continuing to show no signs of biliary or alcoholic disease.

Interventional radiologic guided drain into the pseudocyst.

Despite its cessation and the maximal application of these nonsurgical methods to reduce pancreatitis and the leakage, the patient had a subsequent bout of pain and ER presentation 19 days after the ERCP, and a second IR-guided retroperitoneal drain was placed high up on the left side in early September 2025 for a large peripancreatic fluid collection (Figure 5). The patient did well with this drain but continued with pain and developed dyspnea in November 2025, mandating another ER presentation. A third ERCP with pancreatic duct stent replacement failed to resolve the pain or continued drain output. CT scan of the chest, abdomen and pelvis demonstrated marked ascites (Figure 6) and a pleural effusion (Figures 7 and 8). Two thoracentesis returned amylase rich fluid, so a chest tube was placed for a laboratory confirmed pancreatico-pleural fistula.

At this point, the patient had reached a nadir weight of 140 pounds from his baseline of 195 pounds and had undergone several weeks of low-fat diet, endoscopic stenting and radiologic drainage with a worsening pancreatic leak that had now manifested into a pancreatico-pleural fistula. Given these findings, hepatobiliary surgery team was consulted. The patient was offered and agreed to undergo Robotic-assisted laparoscopic distal pancreatectomy and splenectomy. Due to the extremely thick nature of the pancreatic neck, electrosurgical energy was used to transect the pancreas here, proximal to the suspected area of leak (Figure 9), which was identified based on the area of calcification and where the fluid on CT appeared to strand from. The cut edge of the pancreas was reinforced with multiple topical hemostatic absorbable cloths, sealants and two rows of running suture to minimize the risk of leak. Given the presence of a preoperatively placed forward draining pancreatic duct stent, this was determined to be sufficient to prevent recurrence of leak. The operation took 300 minutes and resulted in 200 ml of estimated blood loss (Figure 10).

Distal pancreas and spleen.

Following the operation, the patient was started on a clear liquid diet on postoperative day 3 and a low-fat full liquid diet on postoperative day 4. On postoperative days 5 and 6 his first and second left chest tubes were removed when fluid amylase studies returned <100 IU/L. Finally, his abdominal drain was removed on postoperative day 7. He was seen in clinic on postsurgery day 50 due to a prolonged stay in acute rehab, where he did well but required intense physical therapy to recover his resilience. His weight recovered to 165 pounds. He was administered appropriate vaccinations by his primary care physician against encapsulated organisms and influenza. His last CT scan in January 2026 demonstrated a clean abdominal (Figure 11) and thoracic cavity (Figure 12) free of any leak or fluid collection. He reported substantial satisfaction with his life and gratitude for freedom from pain.

DISCUSSION

Olanzapine-induced acute on chronic pancreatitis is a rare phenomenon. Most cases are self-limiting, but rarely necrosis—hypothesized to occur due to autodigestion—can ensue, leading to peripancreatic fluid collections which can cause a myriad of nutritional, infectious and painful issues. While pancreatic leak remains rare, a pancreatic leak from olanzapine induced pancreatitis resulting in a pancreatico-pleural fistula requiring operative correction is unreported and unheard of in the literature—although certainly plausible.

Nonetheless the management principles remain the same: remove the offending pancreatitic agent, control the source of sepsis, optimize nutrition and perform procedures that will eliminate fistulizing factors (“FRIENDS”) or the fluid collections themselves.

In this patient, the surgeon suspected that the calcified component in the pancreas (visible in some of the above cited CT scan images) was a cause of nonhealing, or may have been a calcified pseudocyst that ruptured, from which a constant stream of pancreatic fluid emanated.

Due to the chronic nature of the leak and the lymphatic stomata of the diaphragm, a pancreatico-pleural fistula developed. Multiple weeks of ERCP drainage procedures with pancreatic stent promoting forward drainage and nonoperative interventions touching upon the principles of sepsis and nutritional optimization failed to resolve the issue and the placement of multiple chest tubes to drain the left pleural effusion signaled an urgent need for a radical solution.

During the operation, a stapler could not be secured on the thick pancreatic tissue, necessitating a cutting-transection with surgical electricity (bipolar device). This risked recreating immediately a leak and a new fistula, however the application of suturing on the transected pancreatic tail with directed topical hemostatic agents and elimination of the suspected ruptured pseudocyst/calcified lesion in the pancreas gave the surgeon in the case cause to proceed. In addition, the existence of the pancreatic duct stent was extremely useful in promoting forward drainage. Removing the most distal drains (the chest tube) occurred only when both the chest tube and the surgically placed abdominal drain were biochemically confirmed to be free of pancreatic leak. This ensured the possibility of a “plan B” where an abdominal drain, removed last, could control any future leak and prevent pleural fistulization if evidence of a leak manifested after chest tube removal.

Determining the etiology of this presentation was challenging; pancreatitis is often attributed to gallstone disease or alcoholic abuse. The gallbladder on final pathology had no gallstones or thick sludge, ruling out biliary pancreatitis. Alcoholic pancreatitis was determined less likely due to the absence of both laboratory evidence and clinical history. Although hypertriglyceridemia is often found in patients with olanzapine induced pancreatitis, He et al found over a third of patients had normal triglyceride levels in a case series of this disease.3 The timing of the use of the drug and the patient’s prolonged current freedom from issues postpancreatectomy strongly suggests that this was the true etiology.

CONCLUSION

Olanzapine as a cause of acute pancreatitis is rare. A very severe case resulting in a substantial pancreatic leak, peripancreatic fluid collections, and a pancreatico-pleural fistula mandating resection is unheard of, and worthy of reporting. Additionally, the nonsurgical and subsequently surgical tactics that successfully eliminated this disease will be helpful for physicians and patients in the management of similar cases.
