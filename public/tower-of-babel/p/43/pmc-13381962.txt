Electrosynthesis of 1,1-Bis(difluoromethyl)alkenes via Twofold C−H Difluoromethylation as Bioisosteres of Elusive 1,1-Enediols
Authors: Seonyoung Kim, Eunji Kwon, Sung Bum Park, Areum Park, Hyuk Lee, Hyunwoo Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGeminal bis(difluoromethyl)alkenes, featuring two CF2H groups as lipophilic hydrogen-bond donor surrogates, are highly coveted yet rarely accessible bioisosteres of 1,1-enediols that remain nearly theoretical due to their inherent instability. Here we report a catalyst-free electrosynthetic strategy that enables the direct conversion of 1,1-diarylalkenes into geminal bis(difluoromethyl)alkenes under mild oxidative conditions. This transformation relies on the use of Zn(CF2H)2(DMPU)2 as a difluoromethyl radical precursor in combination with ZnCl2, allowing twofold C(sp²)–H difluoromethylation without transition-metal catalysis. The method exhibits broad functional-group tolerance and accommodates complex molecular scaffolds. Mechanistic studies indicate that the reaction proceeds through sequential CF2H radical additions followed by oxidation-induced carbocation formation and elimination. Voltammetric analysis further reveals that ZnCl2 moderates electrode potentials and suppresses side reactions, facilitating sequential difluoromethylation. This electrosynthetic platform provides a general and practical entry to stable 1,1-bis(difluoromethyl)alkene architectures, expanding the toolbox of CF2H-based bioisosteres for medicinal chemistry.Subject terms: Synthetic chemistry methodology, Reaction mechanisms

Introduction

The strategic replacement of a functional group with a bioisostere is a cornerstone strategy in modern medicinal chemistry, enabling deliberate modulation of physicochemical and biological properties while preserving key recognition elements1,2. Among the broad repertoire of bioisosteric strategies, carboxylic acids and their structural surrogates represent prototypical examples that demonstrate how rational functional-group design can balance potency, selectivity, and metabolic stability. As ubiquitous pharmacophores, carboxylic acids form essential hydrogen-bonding and electrostatic interactions with biological targets; however, their ionized nature often limits permeability and metabolic robustness3–5. Classic replacements such as tetrazoles, phosphonates, sulfonamides, and hydroxamates have provided durable scaffolds for drugs including Losartan, Celecoxib, and Vorinostat, whereas more recent surrogates such as sulfonimidamide, oxadiazolone, and boron-based analogues have extended this concept to further enhance physicochemical and pharmacokinetic profiles6–8. Nevertheless, even these classical replacements cannot always reconcile hydrogen-bonding strength with membrane permeability3,5,9. Within this context, the difluoromethyl (CF2H) group has emerged as a distinctive modern bioisostere, combining a balanced polarity–lipophilicity ratio with the ability to act as a polarized C–H hydrogen-bond donor10–16.

In particular, CF2H substitution becomes advantageous when hydrogen-bond donors impart chemical instability or undesired biological interactions (Fig. 1a)1. For instance, CF2H analogues of lysophosphatidic acid (LPA) were designed to suppress acyl migration while retaining productive nuclear hormone receptor interactions. In these analogues, replacing a labile hydroxyl group with CF2H maintained agonism at PPARγ (a nuclear receptor) but attenuated unwanted binding to LPA1–3 G-protein-coupled receptors, thereby improving both chemical stability and target selectivity17,18. This example illustrates a broader design principle guiding our approach: the CF2H group can replace a hydrogen-bond donor to preserve effective intermolecular interactions while decoupling unwanted reactivity or off-target recognition pathways. Such a strategy is especially valuable when the original donor functionality is conformationally labile or prone to tautomerization.

On the other hand, enol motifs (hydroxyl–alkene units) are prevalent in bioactive scaffolds, where conjugation and a directional hydrogen-bonding geometry often underpin key molecular recognition events (Fig. 1b)19. This prevalence highlights the synthetic and functional importance of hydroxyl-bearing C = C motifs in medicinal chemistry. Structurally related yet far less accessible are 1,1-enediols, the dihydroxylated analogues of alkenes that retain the bidentate hydrogen-bonding geometry while introducing a second donor20,21. In practice, however, true 1,1-enediol tautomers are essentially hypothetical entities, as they rapidly revert to thermodynamically favored keto form under ambient conditions. Indeed, observation or isolation of 1,1-enediols has only been achieved in rare, substrate-specific cases under harsh conditions, with extremely limited generality.

Rather than asserting a direct functional equivalence, we approached this challenge from a bioisosteric design perspective, seeking to emulate the spatial hydrogen-bonding disposition of 1,1-enediols while avoiding their intrinsic instability. Specifically, replacement of both hydroxyl groups with CF2H units was envisioned to preserve directional hydrogen-bond donor capability and local polarity, while eliminating the driving force for tautomerization. Such a strategy does not imply strict chemical equivalence but instead offers a functional mimicry of hydrogen-bonding geometry within a more stable framework. Embedding this putative “1,1-enediol-mimetic” CF2H motif within a fully substituted alkene further confers conformational rigidity and geometric fixation, features that are often associated with reduced entropic penalties upon target binding and improve metabolic stability22–25.

Early examples of 1,1-bis(difluoromethyl)alkenes were reported using prefunctionalized substrates through multistep sequences26. However, these approaches rely on multistep substrate preparation and harsh reaction conditions, and do not provide a general or direct strategy for accessing geminal bis-CF2H alkenes. In addition, geminal bistrifluoromethylated alkenes have been accessed through photoredox-enabled double C–H trifluoromethylation or copper-catalyzed stepwise olefination strategies27,28. While these approaches elegantly enable CF3-rich alkene architectures, direct access to CF2H-based enediol mimics via catalyst-free C(sp²)–H functionalization remains limited. In recent years, significant advances in selective fluorination and difluoromethylation chemistry have greatly broadened the range of strategies available for introducing fluorine-containing groups into organic molecules15,29–47. However, despite the promise of geminal bis-CF2H motifs, most existing difluoromethylation methods focus on installing only a single CF2H group on sp2 carbons, and only limited examples of bis-CF2H alkanes have been reported48–51. While recent studies have introduced bis(difluoromethyl) phosphorane reagents as CF2H transfer agents, their application has largely been confined to mono-difluoromethylation or carbene-type reactivity, leaving the direct construction of geminal bis-CF2H units on alkenes unresolved52. Very recently, a photocatalytic consecutive difluoromethylation strategy was reported for the construction of tetrafluoroisopropyl motifs via a triple radical cascade process50. While this approach enables stepwise assembly of (CF2H) 2CH units from simple precursors, it does not address the direct formation of geminal bis-CF2H motifs on C=C double bonds, which remains unresolved. Several elegant photo- and electrochemical approaches have mainly been reported for mono-difluoromethylation53–56. These strategies, however, are unable to install geminal bis-CF2H units on a C−C double bond. Consequently, a general, mild, and operationally simple route to achieve 1,1-bis(difluoromethylation) of alkenes, particularly one that affords stable 1,1-enediol mimics, has remained elusive.

Here, we report a catalyst-free electrochemical approach that addresses this challenge (Fig. 1c). In our design, the electrode serves as the sole redox mediator and Zn(CF2H)2(DMPU)2 is employed as an efficient CF2H radical source. Zn(CF2H)2(DMPU)2—well known as a nucleophilic CF2H⁻ donor and more recently recognized as a viable CF2H radical source48—is leveraged here under anodic activation. Leveraging the radical-generating capacity of Zn(CF2H)2(DMPU)2, we develop an electrochemical protocol that enables direct C(sp2)–H 1,1-bis(difluoromethylation) of alkenes under mild oxidative conditions. This method directly constructs CF2H bioisosteres of 1,1-enediols embedded in tetrasubstituted alkene frameworks in a single step. Mechanistically, the transformation proceeds via anodic generation of CF2H radicals, followed by anti-Markovnikov addition to the alkene (I), then oxidation and E1 elimination from corresponding carbocation II to yield a difluoromethylated Heck-type intermediate (III). A second CF2H radical addition (IV), oxidation (V), and elimination sequence on this intermediate constitutes a double C(sp2)–H difluoromethylation, affording the geminal bis(difluoromethyl)alkene product. Voltammetric analysis further verifies the role of ZnCl2 additive, which effectively buffers the required overpotentials, allowing the reaction to proceed under mild conditions. Overall, this electrochemical platform provides an operationally simple route to stable 1,1-enediol mimics, which have long been deemed inaccessible due to tautomeric instability, and offers a blueprint for the construction of densely substituted, functionally rich alkene architectures.

Results and Discussion

Reaction discovery and parameter optimization

We commenced our investigation with 1-methoxy-4-(1-phenylvinyl)benzene (1a) as a model substrate to explore the feasibility of twofold electrochemical C–H difluoromethylation. This electron-rich 1,1-disubstituted styrene was chosen to test our mechanistic hypothesis, as stabilization of a carbocationic intermediate was anticipated to be important for the transformation. After systematic optimization, we found that the application of constant current of 2 mA enabled the formation of the desired 1,1-bis(difluoromethylated) product 3a in 82% yield along with 7% of the mono-CF2H product 3a′ (Fig. 2a, entry 1). The optimal conditions employed Zn(CF2H)2(DMPU)2 (2, 3.3 equiv.), ZnCl2 (2.0 equiv.) as an additive, LiClO4 as the electrolyte, carbon felt and platinum electrodes as the anode and cathode respectively, in DMSO. On the other hand, when the loading of Zn(CF2H)2(DMPU)2 was reduced in the absence of ZnCl2, the reaction predominantly furnished 3a′ (70% yield, entry 2). Notably, the effect of ZnCl2 was marginal for electron-rich substrate 1a (entry 3), whereas for the electronically neutral 1b, the absence of ZnCl2 led to a significant decrease in efficiency (entry 4). Increasing the amount of ZnCl2 to 6.0 equivalents proved beneficial, leading to an improved yield with this substrate (entry 5). And the use of a divided cell setup enabled identification of the optimal conditions (entry 6). This enhancement is attributed to the spatial separation of anolyte and catholyte, which suppresses crossover of electrodeposited Zn(0) from the cathode to the anode, thereby preventing short-circuiting of the reaction system. Given the clear role of additional Zn salts, a range of zinc additives with different counteranions as well as MgCl2 were screened, with ZnCl2 providing the best performance (entries 7–11). DMSO was identified as the uniquely effective solvent (entries 12–13). Finally, control experiments confirmed that no product formation occurred in the absence of applied current (entry 14). Alternative difluoromethyl radical precursors were also evaluated; however, these predominantly led to mono-difluoromethylation (3a′), likely reflecting differences in radical generation profiles, potentially associated with the lower oxidation potential of Zn(CF2H)2(DMPU)2 (Supplementary Table 1, entry 4–5).

During the optimization studies, hydroxydifluoromethylated byproducts 4 and 4′ were also observed, particularly under conditions lacking Zn salts (vide infra). Their formation implies a radical–polar crossover mechanism, wherein a benzylic radical is converted into its carbocation and subsequently trapped by residual water. These side reactions further highlight the role of ZnCl2 in modulating the cell potential and thereby kinetically suppressing undesired byproduct formation.

To verify the involvement of radical intermediates, a set of radical clock experiments was carried out under our optimized reaction conditions (Fig. 2b). (1-cyclopropylvinyl)benzene 5 underwent rupture of the cyclopropyl ring to furnish 6 upon electrolysis in the presence of PhSH as a hydrogen-atom transfer reagent. In contrast, in the absence of PhSH, 5 delivered the hydroxylated analogue 6′ in 75% yield, presumably via overoxidation of the ring-opened radical intermediate. Additionally, radical clock substrate 7 afforded cyclized product 8 under standard reaction conditions. These results highlight the benzylic radical intermediacy of the reaction. Furthermore, the addition of TEMPO under the standard electrolysis conditions completely suppressed the formation of both 3a and 3a′, while affording the TEMPO–CF2H adduct 5, as confirmed by high-resolution mass spectroscopy (HRMS) (Fig. 2c). These observations corroborate a mechanistic scenario in which anodically generated CF2H radicals add to the alkene substrate to initiate the desired transformation.

Mechanistic investigation

To gain further insight into the mechanism of this twofold C−H difluoromethylation, we suspected that the transformation proceeds through two discrete CF2H radical additions. We therefore sought to identify and characterize the intermediate generated after the first CF2H radical addition in order to elucidate the subsequent course of the reaction (Fig. 3a). During the optimization studies, we hypothesized that 3a′ could serve as an intermediate in the overall transformation. To test this hypothesis, we reduced the loading of 2 to 1.1 equivalents, which allowed selective formation of 3a′ in 70% yield. Subsequent electrolysis of the isolated 3a′ under the standard conditions afforded the final product 3a in 74% yield, thereby validating the proposed stepwise mechanism. Having established this sequence, we next performed Hammett analysis to probe the electronic nature of the two elementary steps (Fig. 3b). Step 1 exhibited an essentially flat correlation (ρ = –0.05, R2 = 0.08), indicating that substituent effects are minimal at this stage. In contrast, step 2 displayed a pronounced negative correlation (ρ = –1.21, R2 = 0.71): electron-donating substituents such as p-OMe accelerated product formation by up to six-fold relative to the unsubstituted analogue, whereas electron-withdrawing substituents (p-/m-Br) noticeably deteriorated reactivity. Consistent with these findings, a time-course reaction profile revealed transient accumulation of the mono-difluoromethylated intermediate 3a′, which reached its maximum yield at 6 h before gradually converting to the bis-difluoromethylated product 3a (Fig. 3c). The rapid formation of 3a′ followed by its slow consumption indicates that the initial CF2H radical addition is kinetically facile, whereas the subsequent CF2H addition–oxidation–elimination sequence–corresponding to the second C(sp2)–H difluoromethylation event–constitutes the rate-determining step. This result implies substantial positive charge development in the transition state of step 2, thereby highlighting the second C(sp2)–H difluoromethylation as the kinetically decisive event in the overall transformation.

The role of ZnCl2 was further examined using substrate 1b (Fig. 3d). Whereas ZnCl2 efficiently promoted the desired transformation (48% yield), neither Zn(OTf)2 nor Et4NCl alone induced productive reactivity, and their combination only partially restored product formation (42%). These observations indicate that both Zn2+ and Cl- ions are required for optimal activity, with ZnCl2 providing the most effective synergistic environment.

The voltaic profiles during electrolysis further clarified this dual function (Fig. 3e). In addition to suppressing water-derived hydroxydifluoromethylation via water sequestration, ZnCl2 also influences the electrochemical response of the system. Voltammetric analysis indicates that ZnCl2 moderates anodic polarization under constant-current conditions, thereby reducing non-productive oxidative pathways such as overoxidation of reactive intermediates. In the presence of ZnCl2, the anodic potential was maintained at 0.4–0.6 V vs Fc/Fc⁺, closely matching the oxidation window of Zn(CF2H)2(DMPU)2, while the cathodic potential stabilized near –1.5 V, corresponding to Zn²⁺ → Zn⁰ reduction. By contrast, reactions with Zn(OTf)2 required a higher anodic bias (~1.0 V), and in the absence of ZnCl2, both electrodes shifted toward more extreme potentials—conditions that favor overoxidation and the generation of hydroxylated byproducts (4 and 4′). Thus, ZnCl2 moderates excessive cell polarization and effectively suppresses undesired side reactions (Supplementary Fig. 11).

Finally, cyclic voltammetry confirmed that Zn(CF2H)2(DMPU)2 undergoes preferential oxidation at approximately 0.4 V, substantially below the oxidation onset of the styrene substrates (≥ 0.7 V) (Fig. 3f). Mixtures containing 2, ZnCl2, and the alkene substrates (1a and 1b) exhibited enhanced catalytic currents in this potential region, consistent with anodic CF2H radical generation followed by addition to the olefin. Collectively, these findings disfavor a direct alkene oxidation pathway and instead support an electrochemically mediated CF2H radical mechanism.

Mechanistic rationale

To rationalize these mechanistic findings, we propose the mechanistic scenario depicted in Fig. 4a. First, anodic oxidation of Zn(CF2H)2(DMPU)2 (2) generates CF2H radicals (10), which add to styrene derivatives to afford benzylic radicals (11). Subsequent oxidation furnishes benzylic cations (12) that undergo elimination to give mono-difluoromethylated alkenes (3′). A second CF2H incorporation via radical addition to 3′, followed by oxidation and deprotonation, yields the 1,1-bis(difluoromethylated) product (3). In parallel, competitive interception by water gives rise to hydroxydifluoromethylated side products (4 and 4′). These pathways are particularly pronounced under conditions without ZnCl2, consistent with the observation that the absence of ZnCl2 leads to larger overpotentials and accelerates overoxidation, thereby allowing carbocation intermediates to engage in competing nucleophilic trapping pathways. ZnCl2 thus exerts a dual function: cathodic reduction of Zn²⁺ maintains charge balance of overall electrolysis, while Cl⁻ oxidation moderates the anodic potential. It is plausible that ZnCl2 additionally suppresses side-product formation by sequestering adventitious H2O through hydrate formation57–59.

A second CF2H incorporation furnishes the 1,1-bis(difluoromethylated) product (3), while water interception leads to hydroxylated byproducts (4, 4′). ZnCl2 mitigates these side reactions by modulating electrode potentials and sequestering adventitious H2O. b Control experiments showing that added H2O promotes exclusive formation of hydroxydifluoromethylated products, whereas increasing ZnCl2 loading restores selectivity toward 3. c 1H NMR analysis in DMSO-d6 demonstrating that Zn2+ salts induce downfield shifts of the H2O resonance, consistent with Zn–hydrate formation irrespective of the counteranion, thereby supporting the proposed water-sequestration role of ZnCl2.

Control experiments further substantiated this rationale (Fig. 4b). Deliberate addition of water led exclusively to hydroxydifluoromethylated alcohols (4b + 4b′), whereas incremental inclusion of ZnCl2 progressively restored selectivity toward the desired difluoromethylated alkenes (3b + 3b′). To probe the origin of this effect, we examined Zn–H2O interactions by 1H NMR spectroscopy in DMSO-d6 (Fig. 4c). In the presence of either ZnCl2 or Zn(CF2H)2(DMPU)2, the water resonance shifted downfield, consistent with the formation of Zn–hydrate complexes. Notably, this shift was independent of the counteranion or ligand environment, indicating that Zn2+ itself is responsible for hydrate formation. This observation directly supports the notion that Zn salts suppress hydroxylated byproducts by binding adventitious water into stable hydrates, thereby preventing nucleophilic trapping of carbocationic intermediates. Taken together, these results establish that ZnCl2 plays a dual role by modulating electrode potentials and suppressing hydrate-derived side reactions, thereby reconciling the electroanalytical data with the outcomes of control experiments.

Substrate scope

The generality of this 1,1-bis(difluoromethylation) protocol was next investigated across a broad range of substrates (Fig. 5a). As confirmed in the preceding optimization study, p-methoxy-substituted and unsubstituted styrenes furnished the 1,1-bis(difluoromethylated) adducts 3a and 3b in 90 % and 58 % yield, respectively. Even substrates containing oxidatively sensitive N,N-dimethylamino groups furnished the desired products 3c (42%) and 3 d (58%) in moderate yields. Electronically neutral substituents, including methyl and para-halogen groups such as bromo and fluoro, were well tolerated (3e-g), while the meta-bromo analogue provided 3 h in diminished yield. Heteroaryl substrates, including thiophene and methoxythiophene derivatives, smoothly underwent the desired reaction to afford 3i and 3j, with their structures unambiguously confirmed by single-crystal X-ray diffraction (SCXRD). The benzofuran derivative 1k displayed distinctive reactivity, undergoing three-fold CF2H incorporation to give the tris(difluoromethylated) product 3k, while 1,3-benzodioxole-containing substrate furnished desired 3 l in somewhat diminished yield. In stark contrast, a sterically congested polymethylated arene afforded only the mono-difluoromethylated product 3m′ in 67% yield, suggesting that steric hindrance can prevent the second CF2H incorporation.

Late-stage functionalization of structurally complex pharmaceutical scaffolds proceeded smoothly under the optimized electrochemical conditions (3n–y). Notably, both protected and unprotected derivatives performed well: protected Ospemifene and Ketoprofen derivatives afforded 3t and 3 v, while the corresponding unmasked alcohol- and carboxylic-acid-containing substrates (Ospemifene and Ketoprofen) likewise underwent efficient transformation to deliver 3 u and 3w without difficulty, demonstrating that labile protic functionalities are well tolerated without the need for protection–deprotection sequences. Cyclic substrates such as Doxepin, Olopatadine, Dosulepin and Amitriptyline derivatives (3z–3ac) also participated well in the reaction, affording the desired products in slightly reduced yet synthetically useful yields. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the electrochemical 1,1-bis(difluoromethylation) tolerates diverse electronic and steric environments, including heteroaryl and drug-like frameworks, enabling direct access to bis- and even tris(difluoromethylated) motifs under mild oxidative conditions.

To illustrate the structural relevance of the 1,1-bis(difluoromethylated) motif to bioactive frameworks, we compared representative products with pharmacologically known scaffolds (Fig. 5b). Under slightly modified electrochemical conditions, the hydroxydifluoromethylated analogue 4b (72%), which was initially observed as a minor side product (vide supra), was deliberately obtained demonstrating the accessibility of bioisosteric structures corresponding to Benactyzine and Trospium derivatives. These comparisons highlight the capacity of this approach to access fluorinated architectures that emulate classical hydrogen-bond donor motifs in medicinal chemistry. To further demonstrate the practical utility of this method, a gram-scale reaction was performed (Fig. 5c). The reaction of 1r on a 6 mmol scale delivered product 3r in 66% isolated yield (1.34 g) under slightly adjusted electrochemical conditions, confirming the scalability and robustness of the protocol.

Finally, to directly explore the therapeutic relevance of the 1,1-bis(difluoromethyl)alkene scaffold, we assessed its pharmacological performance through a series of biological assays (Fig. 6). Specifically, the anti-proliferative activity of the bexarotene derivative 3y was examined in Jurkat cells (T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia). The IC50 value of 3y (43 μM) was lower than that of bexarotene (61 μM), indicating enhanced efficacy against this leukemic cell line. Notably, both compounds exhibited comparable passive membrane permeability, as assessed by parallel artificial membrane permeability assay (PAMPA), while the incorporation of the bis(CF2H) unit in 3y led to a modest decrease in lipophilicity (CLogP: 7.414 vs. 8.194). Importantly, plasma stability was markedly improved in 3y (>99% in both mouse and human) relative to bexarotene (77% and 99%, respectively). In contrast, microsomal stability decreased significantly for 3y across all species tested, suggesting increased susceptibility to hepatic metabolism. Taken together, these results validate the geminal bis(difluoromethyl)alkene motif not only as a synthetic and structural bioisostere but also as a pharmacologically privileged scaffold with experimentally verified biological activity and tunable physicochemical properties. The data highlight the potential of this underexplored fluorinated architecture to impact medicinal chemistry applications by enabling access to conformationally defined, lipophilic hydrogen-bond donor functionality within drug-like molecules.

In conclusion, we have developed a catalyst-free electrosynthetic strategy that enables the twofold C(sp2)–H difluoromethylation of alkenes to access 1,1-bis(difluoromethyl)alkenes, an elusive class of functional groups long regarded as synthetically inaccessible. This transformation proceeds under mild conditions, tolerates diverse aromatic, heteroaromatic, and drug-like substrates, and avoids the need for transition-metal catalysis. Mechanistic studies reveal that the transformation proceeds via two discrete CF2H radical additions followed by carbocation-mediated eliminations, with ZnCl2 playing a critical dual role in controlling cell polarization and suppressing side reactions. The resulting geminal bis(difluoromethyl) products act as conformationally defined surrogates of 1,1-enediols, introducing lipophilic dual hydrogen-bond donor functionality while circumventing the instability of classical enol systems. This work not only expands the synthetic repertoire for constructing densely substituted, fluorine-rich alkenes but also establishes a versatile platform for bioisosteric design in medicinal chemistry. Notably, preliminary cytotoxicity screening of a representative product revealed promising antitumor activity, hinting at the therapeutic relevance of this emerging fluorinated motif.

Methods

General procedure for electrosynthesis of 1,1-bis(difluoromethyl)alkenes

An oven-dried, 10 mL two-neck glass tube was equipped with a magnetic stir bar, a rubber septum, a threaded Teflon cap fitted with electrical feed-throughs, a carbon felt anode (1.0 * 0.5 cm2) (connected to the electrical feedthrough via a 9 cm in length, 2 mm in diameter graphite rod), and a platinum plate cathode (1 × 0.5 × 0.02 cm3). In a nitrogen-filled glove box, substrate (0.2 mmol) was added, followed by the sequential addition via syringe of degassed DMSO (4.0 mL), 2 (185.7 mg, 0.44 mmol, 2.2 equiv), LiClO4 (85.1 mg, 0.8 mmol), and ZnCl2 (55 mg, 0.4 mmol, 2.0 equiv.). The cell was sealed and removed from the glove box. After stirring the reaction mixture for 5–10 min, electrolysis was initiated at a constant current of 2.0 mA at r.t. for 20 h. After completion of the reaction, the mixture was diluted with DCM (30 mL) and then washed with water, brine, dried over anhydrous MgSO4, and concentrated under reduced pressure. The residue was subjected to flash column chromatography on silica gel (eluted with hexanes/ethyl acetate) to yield the desired product.
