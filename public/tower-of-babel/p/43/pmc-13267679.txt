Interstitial Lung Disease in Rheumatoid Arthritis: Incidence and Outcomes in Western Australia
Authors: Hari Prasanna, Charles Inderjeeth, David B Preen, Johannes Nossent
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo determine the incidence, risk factors for, and mortality from interstitial lung disease (ILD) in patients with Rheumatoid Arthritis (RA) in Western Australia (WA).MethodsA retrospective statewide cohort study of ILD in patients with RA (n = 5971) and age/sex matched controls (n = 20 830) hospitalized between 1985 and 2014. ILD was identified based on validated algorithms. ILD incidence (IR) per 1000 person‐years (PY), odds ratio (OR) for risk factors and respiratory complications, and mortality rate (MR) per 1000 PY were estimated with 95% confidence intervals (CI).ResultsThe IR of ILD in patients with RA (RAILD+) was increased (IR ratio 8.02, CI 6.28–10.32) and stable over 30 years. Age (OR 1.02, CI 1.01–1.03), smoking (OR 1.97, CI 1.48–2.63), and scleritis (OR 3.84, CI 1.86–7.94) were independent risk factors for ILD. Pulmonary infections, oxygen dependence, and lung cancer occurred as frequently in RAILD+ and CoILD+ but at higher rates than in participants without ILD in each group. MR for RAILD+ patients was higher than for RA patients not developing ILD (99.9 vs. 50.8; p < 0.01), but lower than for CoILD+ (166.3 vs. 99.9, p < 0.01).ConclusionThe incidence of ILD among patients with RA was stably increased over time. ILD increased respiratory morbidity and mortality in both RA and controls. Smoking cessation and early detection could reduce the risk and severity of ILD.Keywords: epidemiology, interstitial lung disease, mortality, rheumatoid arthritis, risk factors

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic, autoimmune disease that affects approximately 0.5% of adults worldwide [1]. While RA is typically characterized by chronic synovial inflammation, it can also cause a range of extra‐articular manifestations [2, 3]. Interstitial lung disease (ILD) is characterized by inflammation and fibrosis of the lungs [4]. There is evidence that ILD is more prevalent in patients with RA than in the general population [5, 6], but the exact prevalence of RA‐associated ILD is highly variable with estimates ranging from 2% to 60% [6, 7]. ILD can severely impair lung function, reduce quality of life, increase healthcare resource utilization, and mortality [8, 9, 10, 11]. As regional data on RA‐associated ILD are scarce, we performed a long‐term cohort study comparing ILD incidence, risk factors, and mortality between hospitalized patients with RA and matched controls over a long observation period.

Methods

Design

This was a population wide observational study of persons > 15 years of age with an incident diagnosis of RA over the period 1st January 1985 and 31st December 2014 and age and sex matched controls in Western Australia (WA).

Data Sources

Data were derived from the Western Australia Rheumatic Disease Epidemiological Registry (WARDER), that contains routinely collected and validated health data for patients with rheumatic diseases seen at public and private healthcare organizations in WA and similar data from a control group of age and sex‐matched hospitalized patients free of inflammatory rheumatic disease during the study period. WARDER data are sourced from four different datasets: the WA Health Hospital Morbidity Data Collection (HMDC), Emergency Department Data Collection (EDDC), WA Cancer Registry and the WA Death Registry with individual patient data linked through a validated process of probabilistic matching and clerical review to provide de‐identified longitudinal health data for each participant. All data are based on the clinical diagnoses made by physicians and then translated to International Classification of Disease (ICD) codes by trained clinical coders [12, 13]. The final dataset for this study contained longitudinal health data for each participant, including sociodemographic data, hospital location, length of admissions, principal and secondary diagnoses (up to 20) for all hospital contacts linked to death and cancer registrations in WA. The WARDER database has been successfully applied to the clinical‐epidemiological study of rheumatic diseases and controls have been demonstrated to reflect the general population well [14, 15].

Definitions

We selected patients with two or more admissions recording any RA diagnosis through ICD discharge codes 714.× (ICD‐9‐CM), M.05.× and M.06.× (ICD‐10‐AM) following standardized Australian Classification Index pathways and coding rules (Table S1). Two ICD discharge codes have been found to have a high positive predictive value for identifying patients with RA in administrative databases [16]. The index date was the date of the first RA admission for patients with RA and the date of the nearest admission in that year for controls. The observation ended at 1‐1‐2015 or the date of death. We defined ILD as two or more admissions recording an ILD diagnosis with the following ICD discharge codes: 515, 516.3, 516.8, 516.9 (ICD‐9‐CM), J84.1, J84.8, J84.9 (ICD‐10‐AM) (Table S1). The requirement of multiple admissions and the use of these specific codes are based on coding algorithms proposed by England et al. and Albrecht et al. that have 96% specificity for the identification of RAILD+ after exclusion of other potential underlying causes of ILD [11, 17].

For all study participants, we also registered comorbidities and lifestyle factors (Table S1) and estimated accrual of overall organ damage according to the validated and prognostically important updated Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), modified by omitting the rheumatic disease score (m‐CCI). We then grouped patients as those fulfilling our ILD criteria in the RA (RAILD+) and control group (CoILD+) and those not fulfilling our ILD criteria (RAILD‐ and CoILD‐).

Outcomes

The primary outcomes of this study were annual incidence rates (IR) per 1000 person‐years (PY), point prevalence of RAILD+ on 31st December 2014 expressed per million population and mortality rate (MR) per 1000 PY. Secondary aims included risk factors for ILD, the subsequent development of O2 dependence, pulmonary infections, lung cancer, changes in m‐CCI and primary causes of death (Table S1).

Statistics

Patients' baseline characteristics were described with median and interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables, and non‐parametric methods like the Kruskal‐Wallis and Mann–Whitney U tests were used for comparison. Categorical data were described with frequencies and proportions, and groups were compared using Fisher's exact test and odds ratios (OR). ILD IRs are presented with 95% confidence intervals (CI) derived from the Poisson distribution. Changes in ILD IR over time were assessed by the linear least squares regression using the coefficient of determination (R [2]) as the goodness of fit measure. To examine the association between risk factors and ILD occurrence, we applied logistic regression with variables statistically significant in univariate analysis further included in age and sex adjusted Wald backward multivariate models (p to enter < 0.1, p to stay < 0.05). Survival data were described with Kaplan–Meier estimates with log‐rank testing for subgroup comparisons. To determine the robustness of our findings, we performed additional analyses in a larger cohort of patients registered with a single diagnostic code for RA, applying two codes or a single diagnostic code for ILD. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software v28.0 (IBM, USA) and OpenEpi software with two‐sided p‐values (p) < 0.05 considered to be statistically significant.

Results

Following exclusion of patients with other ILD causes and patients with one ILD diagnosis (Figure 1), We studied 5971 patients with RA and 20 830 controls. A total of 220 patients with RA (3.7%) and 87 controls (0.4%) met our ILD definition. The ILD IR was significantly higher among patients with RA (3.68, CI: 3.21–4.20) than for controls (0.46, CI: 0.36–0.57), resulting in an IR ratio of 8.02 (CI: 6.28–10.32) (Table 1) with a slightly divergent statistically non‐significant trend over time (Figure 2).

During a mean follow‐up of 9.3 years (±7.3) RAILD+ patients were as likely as CoILD+ patients to become O2 dependent (OR 0.98, CI 0.19–7.47, p = 0.99) (Table 3) with similar pulmonary infection rates (7.72, CI: 6.5–9.1 versus 5.4, CI: 3.6–7.9) (p = 0.42). Both outcomes were significantly higher compared to those without ILD in each group (p < 0.01). Lung cancer frequency in the CoILD+ group (9.2%) was not significantly higher than in RAILD+ patients (5.5%) (OR, 1.71, 0.65–4.36, p = 0.27). The odds for lung cancer in RAILD+ patients were borderline higher than for RA‐ILD‐ patients (OR 1.71, CI 0.89–3.026, p = 0.08), while the odds were significantly higher for CoILD+ patients than CoILD‐ (OR 4.74, CI 1.96–8.73, p < 0.001).

Discussion

This is the first study to report the population‐wide epidemiology and mortality for ILD in Australian patients with RA and reaffirms that ILD incidence and prevalence are higher in patients with RA and stable over time. Smoking, scleritis, obesity, male sex and older age were the main risk factors for RA‐associated ILD that led to worse respiratory outcomes and survival than patients without ILD.

The period prevalence of RA‐associated ILD (3.68%) was lower than in the one other Australian study that found clinically significant ILD in 5/36 (14%) of patients with early RA [18]. Similarly, a recent meta‐analysis found significant differences in the case definitions for RA‐ILD where high‐income countries had a pooled prevalence of 13.3% for RA‐ILD versus a global prevalence of 21.4% [5]. Our study improves the specificity of an ILD diagnosis. As our algorithm excluded patients with just one ILD admission, it is highly specific but less sensitive and could underestimate ILD prevalence in RA. Including patients with one ILD code raised the RA‐ILD period prevalence to 6.96% and 0.94% for controls, still supporting the increased prevalence in RA. Over the study period significant changes have occurred in the management of RA with Methotrexate becoming the anchor drug, the use of a treat to target model and the introduction of biological drugs. Despite this, ILD incidence in RA patients did not change notably over time suggesting that these developments and especially therapy with TNF inhibitors have not affected ILD occurrence [19, 20].

Patients with RA were significantly more likely to be diabetic and obese with obesity as one of the independent risk factors for RA‐ILD. This concurs with previous studies by Cacciapaglia et al. and Guimaraes et al., which found that obesity is highly prevalent in patients with RA and that underlying systemic inflammation in these patients may be driving both RA symptoms and type 2 diabetes [21, 22]. Increased prednisolone use may contribute to these morbidities [23]. We also found that RAILD+ patients were significantly younger at diagnosis, with one study suggesting this is because patients with RA have more frequent screening tests (chest xray, pulmonary function test) prior to starting drug treatment and ILD is thus captured earlier [24]. We also found that smoking, male sex and age independently contributed to the risk for developing ILD in both patients with RA and controls. This is in line with other studies that found that both obesity and smoking 30‐pack years had a strong association with RA‐ILD [25]. RA is predominant among women, but ILD is more prevalent among male patients with RA and while this could be attributed to the higher smoking rates among men, hormonal and genetic variations between the sexes can also impact ILD development [26, 27]. Overall, this data suggests that smoking cessation and weight loss may help prevent the development of ILD in both patients with RA and controls. Of note, scleritis was the strongest predictor for developing ILD in both RA and controls (OR of 3.84 and 4.72 respectively). Scleritis is another extra‐articular manifestation of RA and, similarly to ILD, occurs in patients with a more severe, often seropositive phenotype. Although we lacked reilable data on serostatus and disease activity, this could explain the relation between scleritis and RA‐ILD [3, 28]. However, its role among the Co‐ILD group is unexpected as there is no clear association in the literature between scleritis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), the most common ILD in patients without RA [29]. Given the low absolute numbers (three scleritis patients in CoILD+ versus nine in RAILD+), it is difficult to draw strong conclusions. Potential reasons could be infectious scleritis, the rare association with lung malignancies [30, 31, 32] or underlying Sjogren's disease not formally diagnosed [33].

Our data confirm that ILD significantly worsens the survival of patients with RA and controls with respiratory disease as the most frequent cause of death in both groups. However, RAILD+ patients had a better prognosis compared to CoILD+ patients. This is inline with other studies like that of Dubey et al. who reported 5‐year survival rates of 87.5% for RA‐ILD patients vs. 40.4% for IPF (the most common cause of ILD in controls) [24]. Dubey et al. suggest that the survival benefit may be due to earlier diagnosis rather than aetiological differences due to more frequent lung screening in patients with RA [24]. This would fit with the younger age in RAILD+ patients observed in our study. To explore whether this could be the case, further analysis was conducted. Survival of RAILD+ and CoILD+ patients were considerably similar when stratified by age (MRR = 1.17 in the> 70 group vs. MRR = 1.66 for all ages), with the difference in mortality among the > 70 cohorts not approaching statistical significance (MRR = 1.17 (0.84–1.63), p = 0.35). This supports Dubey et al.'s conclusion that RA patients may be captured earlier, deflating the mortality rates.

The mortality rate of RAILD+ patients was nearly two times greater than RAILD‐ with half the survival time post‐index admission. This likely relates to the increased rate of O2 dependency and pulmonary infections in ILD patients and is reflected in the high rate of pulmonary causes of death. As similar results were seen among the controls, this reiterates the importance of early ILD capture, so that steps can be taken to reduce these poor health outcomes. While we acknowledge that our study's case definition requiring hospital admissions may bias the sample towards patients with severe and complicated RA resulting in higher mortality estimates, sensitivity analysis comparing one vs. two ILD codes reaffirmed that RAILD+ MR remained higher than RAILD‐ and lower than CoILD+ (Table S3).

While previous studies have highlighted links between cancer and RA‐associated ILD, our data did not show any clear associations with overall cancer development in either group. However, there was a significant increase in the prevalence of lung cancer among CoILD+ patients (9.2% vs. 2.3%, p < 0.01) and a borderline significant association in RAILD+ patients as shown by Brooks et al. [34].

The strengths of this study are the population‐based approach, resulting in a large RA and control cohort with a long observation period. Also, our study followed the validated algorithms proposed by England et al. and Albrecht et al. and we stringently excluded other causes of ILD. Finally, in sensitivity analyses, the direction of all outcome measures remained the same, confirming the robustness of the data. Limitations of this study regard the lack of clinical detail in our deidentified dataset. This precluded analysis of results from blood investigations, pulmonary function testing (PFT), CT scans or biopsies to confirm ILD, which meant we could not verify the sensitivity/specificity of the ICD‐based algorithms on our patient population. The deidentified dataset also did not include clinical details relating to RA serostatus (RF/ACPA), RA treatment or ILD subtypes like UIP or NSIP. This meant the effects of DMARDs/biologic agents on ILD, the distribution of ILD subtypes and the seropositivity of patients could not be reliably identified. The WARDER dataset does not capture non‐hospitalized patients who may have milder RA and the study period was only up to 2015, which may reduce the generalisability of these results to the current population. Lastly, risk factors identified in this study are associations and do not imply causation.

Conclusion

The incidence and prevalence of ILD in Western Australia remained significantly higher among patients with RA than matched controls over time with no obvious effect of newer RA therapies. ILD development significantly worsened pulmonary outcomes and mortality in both RA patients and controls. While smoking cessation and weight loss could help prevent ILD, earlier diagnosis of ILD is associated with reduced mortality in RA patients with ILD.
