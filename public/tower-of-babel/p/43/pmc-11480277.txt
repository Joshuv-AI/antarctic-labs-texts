Impact of Disease Burden of Patients with Psoriasis on Biologic Therapy Switching: Real-World Evidence from the CorEvitas Psoriasis Registry
Authors: Andrew Blauvelt, Robert R McLean, Silky W Beaty, Adam P Sima, Robert Low, Jeffrey L Stark, Laura McClung, Jerry Bagel
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDue to variable psoriasis symptoms, disease progression, and individual responses to therapy, patients may start, stop, or switch biologic therapies. Real-world data on the associated disease burden of patients with psoriasis who do and do not switch biologics are incomplete.MethodsThis study compared disease burden among patients from the CorEvitas Psoriasis Registry (July 2017–December 2021) who switched biologics and those who did not within 4–12 months following initiation. Disease-related patient-reported outcomes (PROs) were recorded, including skin pain, itching, activity impairment, and effects on health-related quality of life (HRQoL). Disease severity was measured by body surface area (BSA) and Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI). Unadjusted and adjusted regression models were used to compare study outcome measures between the two groups.ResultsThis study included 2145 patients, with 159 classified as switchers and 1986 as non-switchers. The most common reason for switching therapy was failure to maintain initial response (51.7%; n = 78). Moderate-to-severe disease (BSA ≥ 3) was found among 83.0% (n = 132) of switchers versus 26.1% (n = 516) of non-switchers. PASI > 5 was reported among 49.7% (n = 79) of switchers versus 8.6% (n = 171) of non-switchers. Differences in skin pain, itching, and effects on HRQoL between switchers and non-switchers were larger in magnitude for bio-experienced patients.ConclusionsPatients who switched biologic therapy experienced a greater disease burden of psoriasis across PROs than non-switchers. Patient-centered factors may be important drivers of biologic switching. Our findings suggest the association between switching and disease burden may be stronger among patients with prior biologic therapy experience.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13555-024-01257-2.Keywords: Psoriasis, CorEvitas Psoriasis Registry, Biologics, Real-world evidence, Therapy switch, Psoriasis treatment patterns, WPAI

Key Summary Points

Patients with psoriasis may initiate, stop, or switch biologic therapies due to variations in symptoms and treatment responses.There is limited understanding of the differences in disease burden among patients that may drive biologic switching.The objective of this study was to compare patient-reported disease burden among patients with psoriasis from the CorEvitas Psoriasis Registry who switched biologic therapy versus those who did not.In this analysis of patients with psoriasis treated in real-world clinical practice, patients who switched biologic therapy experienced greater disease burden than those who did not switch.Patient-reported outcomes and clinical measures such as disease severity should be viewed as potential targets for clinical interventions designed to optimize treatment strategies in psoriasis.

Introduction

Due to its chronicity, clinical management of psoriasis often requires long-term treatment [1, 2]. For moderate-to-severe psoriasis, common systemic treatments include biologic and non-biologic therapies [2, 3]. Systemic biologic therapies prescribed for psoriasis include inhibitors of key cytokines involved in the pathophysiology of psoriasis, such as tumor necrosis factor (TNF), interleukin (IL)-17, and IL-23 [4–6]. Given the variability of psoriasis symptoms, disease progression, and individual responses to therapy, patients often start, stop, or switch therapies during the normal course of treatment [2, 7].

Recent studies have identified common reasons behind switching and modeled switch trends among biologic therapies. However, measures of association between health-related quality of life (HRQoL) or psoriasis disease severity and biologic therapy switching patterns have not been fully explored [8–11]. Evidence supports an association between switching biologic therapy and factors such as unmet expectations for therapy or poor long-term efficacy of biologic therapy [12, 13].

While studies often focus on the economic and cost implications of biologic switching as outcomes [2, 10, 14–16], less evidence is available regarding the disease burden experienced by patients who have switched biologics, and current knowledge regarding the relationship of patient-centered factors and psoriasis disease measures with switching patterns is incomplete [10]. Real-world data on biologic therapy changes for patients with psoriasis, and the associated clinical characteristics establishing differential disease burden of patients that do and do not switch biologics, are needed to better assess risk factors associated with biologic switching [17, 18].

In this study of patients from the CorEvitas Psoriasis Registry, patient-reported disease burden was compared between those who switched biologic therapies versus those who did not. As a secondary objective, trends between the two groups were evaluated on the basis of the number of prior biologic therapies used and presence of concomitant psoriatic arthritis.

Methods

CorEvitas Psoriasis Registry Population and Study Design

This study utilized data from the CorEvitas Psoriasis Registry, an independent, prospective, multicenter, observational North American registry launched in April 2015 in collaboration with the National Psoriasis Foundation [19]. In the registry, adult patients diagnosed with psoriasis by a dermatologist and starting an eligible systemic therapy for the treatment of psoriasis are enrolled. Patients are assessed via patient and provider questionnaires during routine dermatology visits occurring at approximately 6-month intervals.

At registry enrollment, providers record psoriasis-related medication history, including changes in usage for any biologic or non-biologic therapy. These changes in therapy may be planned (e.g., biologic therapy is prescribed but the first dose is not administered) or observed. Reasons for any change in biologic therapy use are reported from a selection of common options listed on the provider questionnaire.

This study was cross-sectional at registry enrollment and used data from patients between July 2017 and December 2021 who had a history of plaque psoriasis and demonstrated persistent use of an eligible biologic therapy (Supplementary Table 1) prior to registry enrollment. Persistent use was defined as having initiated a biologic 4–12 months prior to enrollment and remaining on the initiated biologic ± 30 days of enrollment (Fig. 1). Patients were excluded if they discontinued a biologic therapy without switching to a new biologic ± 30 days of enrollment, initiated a biologic > 12 months before enrollment, had missing or uncertain data precluding accurate determination of the initiation date of a biologic, had a missing psoriasis disease burden indicator, or had a potential confounding variable.

This study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki [21] and the Guidelines for Good Pharmacoepidemiology Practice [20]. All participating investigators were required to obtain full board approval for conducting noninterventional research involving human subjects with a limited dataset. Sponsor approval and continuing review were obtained through a central Institutional Review Board (IRB), (Advarra, protocol number Pro00051221). For academic investigative sites that did not receive authorization to use the central IRB, full board approval was obtained from their respective governing IRBs, and documentation of approval was submitted to CorEvitas, LLC, before the site’s participation and initiation of any study procedures. All patients in the registry were required to provide written informed consent and authorization before participating in the CorEvitas Psoriasis Registry.

Study Measures

Outcome Measures

The disease burden of psoriasis and related effects were characterized by patient-reported outcome (PRO) measures assessing psoriasis-related symptoms and HRQoL. Patient perception of skin pain, fatigue, and itching/pruritus was assessed by a 100-point visual analog scale (VAS), with 0 indicating no pain/fatigue/itching and 100 indicating most severe pain/fatigue/itching [22]. The Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI) questionnaire assessed HRQoL domains, including symptoms and feelings, daily activities, leisure, work and school, personal relationships, and treatment. Total DLQI score was calculated by adding the score of each of the 10 items and ranged from 0 (no impact) to 30 (high impact), with higher scores indicating poor HRQoL [23]. DLQI ≤ 5 was characterized as small or no effect on the patient’s life, while DLQI > 5 was considered a moderate to severe (extremely large) effect [24]. The Work Productivity and Activity Impairment (WPAI) questionnaire gathered employment status and assessed the percent of daily life and work activities impacted by psoriasis [25]. The WPAI questionnaire covered four domains, which included activity impairment, absenteeism (defined as work time missed), presenteeism (defined as reduced on-the-job effectiveness), and work productivity loss; the latter three domains were restricted to patients who were employed. Scores ranged from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating greater impairment and less productivity (worse outcomes). The Patient Global Assessment of psoriasis (VAS-PGA) scored overall patient perception of HRQoL with respect to impact due to psoriasis and ranged from 0 (very well) to 100 (very poor) [26]. The Euro-Quality of Life Visual Analog Score (EQ-VAS) assessed each patient’s self-rated health state on a vertical visual analogue scale, which ranged from 0 (worst imaginable health state) to 100 (best imaginable health state) [27].

Exposure Measure

The primary exposure for this study was an indicator of whether a patient switched from one biologic therapy to another at registry enrollment. Patients were classified as either switchers or non-switchers. Switchers were defined as patients who discontinued a biologic systemic therapy and initiated or were prescribed a different biologic systemic therapy ± 30 days of registry enrollment. The reason for starting a new biologic therapy was recorded with the following possible responses: failure to maintain initial response, primary non-response (defined as inadequate initial response or continued active disease), or other. If multiple reasons were provided, the first reason corresponded to the primary reason for switching and was selected for inclusion in this analysis for patients who switched biologic therapy. Non-switchers were defined as patients with confirmed use of a biologic therapy at enrollment without any indication of intent to stop therapy ±30 days of registry enrollment.

Covariates

All covariates were collected at registry enrollment. Demographic and socioeconomic characteristics included age (years), sex at birth (male, female), race (white, Black, Asian, other/unknown), ethnicity (Hispanic, yes/no), type of health insurance (any private, non-private), education (high school or less, any college), and employment (full time, not full time). Lifestyle characteristics included smoking status (never, former, or current), body weight (kg), body mass index (BMI; kg/m2), concomitant cardiovascular disease (diabetes mellitus, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, obesity), history of anxiety or depression (yes, no), and number of other comorbidities (0, 1, > 1). Disease characteristics included psoriasis duration (years), history of psoriasis in challenging treatment areas or non-plaque morphologies (scalp, nail, genital, palmoplantar, inverse/intertriginous, guttate, pustular, erythrodermic), concomitant psoriatic arthritis as identified by a dermatologist (yes, no), body surface area (BSA; percentage) [28], worst BSA ever (percentage), Psoriasis Area Severity Index (PASI; scored 0–72) [29], and disease severity as measured by BSA (BSA <3 [mild], BSA ≥3 [moderate to severe]) and PASI (PASI ≤5 [mild], PASI >5 [moderate to severe]). Treatment characteristics included current use of a non-biologic systemic therapy (yes, no), current use of topical therapy (yes, no), current use of phototherapy (yes, no), number of prior unique biologics, prior systemic biologic experience (bio-naïve, bio-experienced), and prior therapy use (non-biologic systemic therapy, topical therapy, phototherapy [separately]).

Statistical Analysis

Means (standard deviations [SDs]) and frequencies (percentages) were reported for characteristics measured at enrollment. Enrollment characteristics were presented for the overall cohort, as well as separately by switch group classification, with standardized differences (std diff) reported to evaluate the magnitude of between-group differences. Std diff values of 0.30, 0.50, and 0.80 represented small, moderate, and large differences, respectively, between switchers and non-switchers.

Unadjusted and adjusted regression models were used to compare study outcome measures between switchers and non-switchers, with mean differences (95% confidence intervals [CIs]), odds ratios (ORs; 95% CIs), and rate ratios (RRs; 95% CIs) reported where applicable. Mean differences and ORs were calculated from linear or logistic (for DLQI only) regression models. Additionally, mean differences and ORs were reported for adjusted covariates selected a priori on the basis of their suspected associations with disease burden (age, sex, race, ethnicity, employment status, history of anxiety or depression, BMI, PASI, concomitant psoriatic arthritis, biologic experience, time on current therapy, and current use of a non-biologic systemic therapy, topical therapy, or phototherapy [separately]).

Outcomes of WPAI domains were analyzed using zero-inflated negative binomial (ZINB) regression models [30] due to the proclivity of the WPAI scores being 0, which exceeded 80% in some cases. These models produced ORs that measured the odds of switchers having a score of 0 compared with non-switchers along with a RR that compared the means of those with a non-zero score between the switch groups to jointly obtain an OR of having a WPAI score of 0 and RR of having a WPAI ≥ 0. Separate analyses considered biologic experience (bio-naïve, bio-experienced) and psoriatic arthritis status (yes, no) as potential modifiers of the association between switch group classification and outcome measures. Separate mean differences, ORs, or RRs comparing the switch groups were reported for each level of the modifying variable. A difference-in-difference (DID) measure was used to quantify how much the mean differences, ORs, or RRs differed between the levels of the modifying variable. These quantities were calculated as the difference in mean difference for continuous disease burden of psoriasis measures, ratios of ORs for binary measures, and ratios of RRs from the ZINB models.

Results

Baseline Demographics and Lifestyle Characteristics

Upon screening the CorEvitas Psoriasis Registry for initial inclusion criteria, 8647 patients were identified. Among those identified, 6502 patients were excluded on the basis of the study criteria, leaving a final analytic cohort of 2145 patients (Fig. 2). Within the included patients, 159 were classified as switchers and the remaining 1986 patients as non-switchers.

Patient selection criteria and attrition for study analysis

In the final cohort, 394 (18.4%), 235 (11.0%), 816 (38.0%), and 700 (32.6%) of the patients initiated a TNF inhibitor (i), IL-12/23i, IL-17i, and IL-23i, respectively. Although baseline demographics and lifestyle characteristics did not vary widely between switch groups, mean body weight and BMI were substantially greater among switchers (std diff = 0.39 for both groups) compared with non-switchers. Mean (SD) body weight and BMI for switchers were 100.9 (29.1) kg and 34.2 (8.4) kg/m2, respectively. For non-switchers, mean (SD) body weight and BMI values were 91.8 (22.9) kg and 31.3 (7.3) kg/m2, respectively. A higher proportion of switchers (40.3%; n = 64) had a BMI ≥ 35 compared with 24.6% (n = 489) of non-switchers (std diff = 0.34; Table 1). Concomitant psoriatic arthritis diagnosis was identified in 48.4% (n = 77) of switchers compared with 32.6% (n = 647) of non-switchers (std diff = 0.33; Table 2).

Reasons for Switching Biologic Therapy

Among those patients that switched biologic therapy and provided a reason for switching (n = 151), the most common reason for starting a new therapy was failure to maintain the initial response (51.7%; n = 78). Additional reasons for switching biologic therapy included inadequate initial response (25.2%; n = 38) and active disease (6.0%; n = 9). The remaining patients that switched biologic therapy (17.2%; n = 26) indicated other reasons (including: serious side effect, co-pay/patient cost, minor side effect, patient preference, alternative mechanism, denied by insurance, fear of future side effect, and frequency of administration). A small number of patients (n = 8) had a missing reason for switching.

Severity of Psoriasis by Switch Classification Group

Switchers exhibited greater disease severity of psoriasis compared with non-switchers, as measured by BSA and PASI at enrollment. The mean (SD) BSA (% involvement) was 13.1 (16.0) for switchers and 3.0 (7.0) for non-switchers (std diff = 1.26). The mean (SD) PASI was 7.3 (8.2) for switchers and 1.8 (3.6) for non-switchers (std diff = 1.34). Likewise, a larger proportion of switchers reported moderate-to-severe disease status compared with non-switchers, as indicated by BSA ≥ 3 and PASI > 5. Disease severity, as measured by BSA involvement, was 83.0% (n = 132) for switchers, compared with 26.1% (n = 516) for non-switchers (std diff = 1.39). PASI measures indicating moderate-to-severe psoriasis were observed in 49.7% (n = 79) of switchers versus 8.6% (n = 171) of non-switchers (std diff = 1.01; Table 2).

Disease Burden of Psoriasis by Switch Classification Group

Switch status was associated with worse disease burden across PRO measures investigated at baseline. A greater proportion of switchers reported effects of psoriasis on WPAI domain measures (activity impairment, absenteeism, presenteeism, and work productivity loss) compared with non-switchers. Activity impairment as measured by WPAI was reported by 68.6% (n = 109) of switchers, with a mean (SD) score of 25.7 (27.7), compared with 40.5% (n = 804) of non-switchers, with a mean (SD) score of 9.9 (20.7). Absenteeism as measured by WPAI was reported by 16.5% (n = 17) of switchers, with a mean (SD) score of 3.4 (12.6), compared with 9.9% (n = 123) of non-switchers, with a mean (SD) score of 2.1 (10.8). Presenteeism as measured by WPAI was reported by 57.3% (n = 59) of switchers, with a mean (SD) score of 15.8 (22.3), compared with 32.7% (n = 405) of non-switchers, with a mean (SD) score of 5.7 (14.4). Work productivity loss as measured by WPAI was reported by 59.8% (n = 61) of switchers, with a mean (SD) score of 17.5 (23.6), compared with 35.9% (n = 443) of non-switchers, with a mean (SD) score of 6.8 (16.1; Table 3).

Of the PROs evaluated, switchers noted worse itching than non-switchers as measured by VAS-Itch. Following regression analysis adjustment for disease severity measures and additional study covariates, mean itch was nearly 30 points greater in switchers (27.3; 95% CI: 23.0, 31.5; p < 0.001; Fig. 3). Moreover, switchers reported worse HRQoL due to psoriasis compared with non-switchers as reflected by an adjusted mean difference in VAS-PGA of 20.7 points (95% CI: 16.7, 24.7; p < 0.001). VAS measures of skin pain and fatigue indicated a higher magnitude of these variables in switchers as well, with adjusted mean differences of 18.0 (95% CI: 14.7, 21.3; p < 0.001) and 10.2 (95% CI: 5.8, 14.6; p < 0.001) points, respectively. Similarly, mean EQ-VAS was 4.7 points lower (95% CI: −8.0, −1.5; p = 0.004) for switchers compared with non-switchers, indicating worse self-rated health. Switchers had approximately threefold greater odds (OR [95% CI]=2.8 [2.0, 4.1]; p<0.001) of skin problems as measured by DLQI >5, compared with non-switchers (data not presented in figure). Among the disease burden of psoriasis measures reported, patients that switched biologic therapy had significant twofold increased odds of activity impairment as reported by WPAI with OR=2.2 (95% CI: 1.4, 3.3; p < 0.001) and RR = 1.4 (95% CI: 1.1, 1.8; p = 0.005) compared with non-switchers (Fig. 4).

Biologic History as a Modifier

The difference in skin pain between patients that switched and did not switch biologic therapies was larger for bio-experienced patients (difference [95% CI]=21.9 [17.5, 26.3]) than the bio-naïve patients (difference [95% CI]= 13.5 [8.9, 18.2]; DID [95% CI]= 8.4 [2.2, 14.7]; p = 0.008). A similar pattern was observed with itching, with the magnitude of difference between switchers and non-switchers being more pronounced for bio-experienced patients (difference [95% CI]= 33.2 [27.6, 38.9]) than bio-naïve patients (difference [95% CI]=20.6 [14.6, 26.5]; DID [95% CI]= 12.7 [4.7, 20.7]; p = 0.002). The relationship between effect on HRQoL and switching indicated higher rates of moderate/severe effects based on DLQI > 5 for both bio-naïve and bio-experienced patients. However, the OR was nearly twice as large (OR [95% CI]= 3.9 [2.3, 6.7]; p < 0.001) for the bio-experienced group, with a DID measure of 1.9 (95% CI: 1.0, 4.0; p = 0.070) comparing the bio-naïve and bio-experienced groups (Supplementary Table 2).

Psoriatic Arthritis Status as a Modifier

Results suggested that the association between switching and DLQI > 5 may have been stronger in patients with concomitant psoriatic arthritis than in those with only psoriasis (Supplementary Table 3), though the CI included the null (DID = 2.0; 95% CI: 1.0, 4.1; p = 0.067). The associations of switching with activity impairment (OR = 2.7; 95% CI: 1.3, 5.4) and absenteeism (OR = 1.6; 95% CI: 0.7, 3.9) tended to be stronger in those with psoriatic arthritis, though CIs were overlapping (Fig. 5).

Discussion

The findings of this study demonstrated that in adult patients with psoriasis treated in real-world clinical practice, patients who switched biologic therapy 4–12 months following initiation experienced a greater disease burden of psoriasis at the time of switching than those who did not switch therapy, regardless of biologic history. Switchers generally recorded worse disease-related PROs, particularly skin pain and itching, as well as a higher prevalence of work and activity impairment and greater effects on HRQoL compared with non-switchers. Importantly, these differences were observed following adjustment for patient demographics, disease severity, and prior treatment history.

Previous studies have demonstrated that bio-experienced patients may have altered responses to subsequent psoriasis therapies and present with different patient- and disease-related characteristics compared with bio-naïve patients [31–34]. The results from our analysis suggest that the magnitude of association between switching and disease burden of psoriasis might be stronger among bio-experienced patients as well. Thus, switching behaviors may be influenced by characteristics such as high disease burden and prior biologic therapy use that potentially impact biologic efficacy for some patients. As treatment response can relate to overall patient satisfaction with psoriasis management [35], these results may provide additional insight into specific factors associated with treatment efficacy and switching patterns.

Certain disease factors and patient characteristics have also been linked to biologic response, including disease severity and BMI [36, 37]. Notably, the disease severity measures evaluated in our study (BSA and PASI) indicated substantially higher psoriasis severity for switchers compared with non-switchers, suggesting an association with switch status. As clear skin has been found to be an important treatment goal for patients with psoriasis [38, 39], those with more severe disease may repeatedly switch biologic therapies due to the desire to achieve complete skin clearance. In addition to more advanced disease, mean body weight and BMI at baseline were substantially greater among switchers in this study, further suggesting a potential influence of body weight/BMI on the incidence of biologic switching.

Our findings suggest the need for wider implementation of tailored approaches in care for patients with psoriasis that directly target features affecting HRQoL, such as skin pain and itching. Since patients with insufficiently controlled psoriatic disease are more likely to experience a greater degree of psychological and social stressors [35, 40], it is important to also address factors extending beyond skin symptoms that possibly contribute to frequent biologic switching. For instance, the increased odds of work impairment reported by switchers versus non-switchers have broad economic implications. In a retrospective, observational cohort study that examined healthcare utilization and costs associated with biologic treatment patterns, switchers were found to have higher adjusted mean total healthcare costs than non-switchers [41].

The data presented in this analysis describe real-world patient-centered factors, including daily effects on HRQoL, skin pain, and itching, as common characteristics that may be connected to biologic switching. As more biologic therapies become available, clinical measures relating to disease severity in addition to PROs should be taken into consideration to optimize broader treatment strategies for patients with psoriasis. In this study, a higher proportion of switchers had moderate-to-severe disease severity compared with non-switchers. Since patients with moderate-to-severe psoriasis may be undertreated [42], it will be important to focus efforts on developing effective long-term therapies that reduce biologic switching to improve patient outcomes and HRQoL.

Strengths and Limitations

Utilizing data collected at registry enrollment leveraged inclusion criteria to ensure capture of psoriatic disease measures at the time of biologic therapy switch. Furthermore, the use of registry data enabled documentation of specific reasons for switching biologic therapy, which allowed for identification and analysis of predictors coinciding with switch occurrence.

As it has previously been shown that skin clearance is an important consideration for switching and is related to patient perception of HRQoL [7, 43], the models used in this study were adjusted for disease severity to properly evaluate differences in the disease burden of psoriasis between switchers and non-switchers. This analysis comprehensively evaluated outcomes and assessed various effect modifiers, thus providing insight on PROs and clinical characteristics that may influence treatment switching patterns.

This study relied on the duration of biologic therapy prior to registry enrollment, and the dates of initiation and/or discontinuation of an eligible biologic treatment were required for inclusion. Consequently, data were captured for individuals treated between 4 and 12 months and therefore may be limited in generalizability to patients treated for shorter or longer durations. As both providers and patients volunteer to participate in the registry, the study cohort may not be a representative sample of all patients receiving biologic treatments in the US and Canada.

Due to the cross-sectional design of this study, any change or reduction in the disease burden of psoriasis from initiation could not be determined, as patients started biologic therapy prior to registry enrollment. Additionally, there may have been a lack of robust information regarding baseline disease activity at the time of therapy initiation, which forced reliance on worst BSA value ever achieved as a baseline measure of severity.

Conclusions

In this study of adults with psoriasis treated with a biologic in real-world clinical practice for 4–12 months, we observed greater disease severity and associated effects on HRQoL among patients who switched therapy compared with those who did not switch, even following adjustment for covariates. These data suggest that patient-centered factors such as effects on daily living and HRQoL, reported skin pain, and itching may be particularly important drivers of medication switch behaviors. PROs, as well as clinical measures connected to overall disease severity, should be evaluated as potential targets for clinical interventions aimed at optimizing treatment responses in patients with psoriasis. The findings warrant additional studies to further investigate the modifying effect(s) of characteristics, such as prior biologic exposure and concomitant psoriatic arthritis, to guide development of more tailored therapeutic strategies available to patients with psoriasis.
