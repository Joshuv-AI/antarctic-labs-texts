CitrusGS: 3D Gaussian splatting for sparse-view CT reconstruction and precise morphological phenotyping of citrus fruit
Authors: Zilong Xu, Wenhao Li, Xihang Gu, Qinyi Yu, Shanjun Li, Yaohui Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Computed tomography enables non-destructive phenotyping of fruit internal structure but traditionally requires hundreds of projections, limiting throughput. Under sparse-view conditions, conventional and learning-based methods both suffer from streaking artifacts and regional distortions that degrade trait quantification. This study present CitrusGS, an integrated framework that achieves high-fidelity 3D reconstruction and precise morphological phenotyping of citrus fruit from only 15 projections using radiative 3D Gaussian splatting. Our method employs sparse-point initialization, optimized loss composite, and dual-stage pruning to suppress artifacts while preserving anatomically critical details with significantly higer convergence efficiency. In the citrus fruit datasets, CitrusGS achieves 29.78 dB PSNR and 0.870 SSIM, outperforming corresponding baseline method by 1.58 dB and 0.067 in SSIM, and enables automated extraction of ten external and internal phenotypic traits with R2 larger than 0.944. Moreover, the framework shows initial zero-shot transferability across pathological citrus samples and additional horticultural specimens without retraining. By reconciling acquisition efficiency with anatomical fidelity using low-cost X-ray hardware, CitrusGS provides a promising framework for high-throughput, non-destructive phenotyping in breeding and grading applications.

Introduction

Citrus is among the most economically important fruit crops worldwide, and improving fruit quality assessment is central to both industrial grading and breeding programs [1,2]. In practice, market value and consumer acceptance are determined not only by external appearance but also by internal traits such as peel-layer thickness, pulp development, and cavity geometry [3]. These internal phenotypes directly relate to eating quality, storability, and resistance to physiological disorders or pest damage [4]. However, routine evaluation of internal traits still relies heavily on destructive sampling, which is labor-intensive, low-throughput, and incompatible with continuous monitoring or large-scale screening. Consequently, there is a strong demand for non-destructive phenotyping technologies that can quantify internal morphology accurately while remaining practical for high-throughput deployment.

Existing internal quality estimation methods fall broadly into destructive and non-destructive approaches [2]. Destructive measurements provide rich information on chemical composition and ripeness indicators [5], but they are inherently terminal and therefore unsuitable for longitudinal observation and large-scale sorting [6,7]. In contrast, many non-destructive techniques used in commercial settings, such as near-infrared spectroscopy or ultrasound, can estimate certain chemical properties and detect some defects [8], yet they provide limited spatial resolution and do not capture three-dimensional anatomical structure [9]. For applications that require explicit 3D morphology, imaging-based methods are more appropriate. X-ray computed tomography (CT) is particularly attractive because it can directly visualize internal structure by reconstructing a 3D volume from 2D projections acquired at multiple angles, enabling precise measurement of both external morphology and internal anatomy. Lu et al. [10] used CT to non-destructively assess internal durian traits, achieving mean absolute percentage error of 3.09% for sarcocarp and 1.14% for fruit volume. Wood et al. [11] used CT to evaluate early physiological disorders in pome fruits, showing that mean greyscale values could predict core and flesh browning in ‘Braeburn’ and ‘Golden Delicious’ apples.

Despite these advantages, conventional CT imaging typically requires hundreds to thousands of projections per sample. This results in long acquisition times, elevated radiation dose, and substantial computational cost, which collectively restrict throughput and limit field or production-line adoption. Sparse-view CT offers a promising path forward by reconstructing useable 3D volumes from a drastically reduced number of projections (often fewer than 50) [12,13], thereby improving scanning speed and reducing dose. However, sparse-view reconstruction is severely ill-posed: limited angular sampling leads to streaking artifacts, missing high-frequency details, and structural distortions especially in regions with subtle density variations [14,15]. In fruit CT, these fine structures, including thickness gradients within peel layers, delicate pulp boundaries, and cavity morphology, are precisely the features required for reliable phenotyping and defect assessment [16]. Importantly, reconstruction artifacts do not remain confined to image quality; they propagate to downstream tasks such as semantic segmentation and quantitative trait extraction, introducing systematic bias and reducing phenotyping accuracy [17,18].

A wide range of learning-based approaches has been developed to address sparse-view CT reconstruction. Supervised networks, including convolutional neural networks (CNN) and transformer-based models, can suppress noise and reduce artifacts [19], but their performance often depends strongly on the training distribution and may degrade under variations in cultivar, size, or morphology conditions that are common in agricultural datasets. Generative models like generative adversarial networks (GAN) and diffusion models may further enhance perceived fidelity [20,21], yet they can introduce hallucinated structures that are physically inconsistent with the underlying object, which is unacceptable when the goal is measurement. Implicit neural representations and neural radiance field (NeRF)-style methods have demonstrated impressive detail preservation under sparse measurements [22], but they are typically computationally expensive and slow to converge, making real-time or high-throughput industrial use challenging. As a result, current sparse-view CT pipelines still face a persistent trade-off among reconstruction fidelity, physical consistency, and computational efficiency, while also needing to support reliable downstream segmentation and phenotyping. This downstream-oriented requirement suggests that reconstruction quality in CT-based phenotyping should be evaluated not only by image-wise similarity metrics, but also by anatomical fidelity and structural consistency, as tissue boundaries and internal anatomical structures directly influence segmentation and trait quantification.

To address these challenges, we propose CitrusGS, an integrated framework for sparse-view citrus CT reconstruction, organ-level segmentation, and automated phenotyping. We aim to achieve high-throughput CT reconstruction with high accuracy and computational efficiency under even extremely sparse X-ray projections like 15, whose data acquisition can be conveniently accomplished using low-cost X-ray machines instead of CT scanners. Our key idea is to improve a radiative 3D Gaussian splatting (3DGS) representation that is explicit, compact, and well-suited for projection-based optimization, enabling high-fidelity reconstruction from limited views while maintaining practical runtime. First, CitrusGS introduces a sparse-point initialization (SPI) strategy that encourages stable, coarse-to-fine optimization, as the model captures global geometry early and progressively refines local detail without being dominated by redundant primitives. Second, we design an optimization objective that combines photometric and structural constraints with a composite structural loss to better preserve boundaries and fine internal textures critical for phenotyping and defect analysis. Third, we incorporate an enhanced densification rule together with a two-stage pruning mechanism to suppress floaters and redundancy, improving both reconstruction quality and computational efficiency. Building on the reconstructed volumes, we employ a CT-pretrained vision foundation model with a lightweight task-specific decoder to segment fruit anatomy into peel layers and internal tissues, enabling robust organ delineation with limited manual annotations. Finally, the segmentation outputs support automated extraction of ten phenotypic traits spanning external morphology, such as length, width, height, surface area, and total volume, and internal structure, including peel-layer thickness and tissue volumes.

In summary, this work makes three main contributions:1.A sparse-view CT reconstruction module based on radiative Gaussian splatting, featuring sparse initialization, multi-term optimization, and efficient densification–pruning strategies for artifact suppression and detail preservation.2.A segmentation–phenotyping module that leverages a CT foundation model to produce organ-level masks from reconstructed volumes and to extract external and internal traits automatically.3.Task-oriented validation demonstrating that the proposed pipeline improves reconstruction quality and supports accurate, scalable phenotyping under sparse-view acquisition, highlighting its potential for industrial grading systems and portable phenotyping devices.

Materials and methods

Overall pipeline

Fig. 1a summarizes the proposed CitrusGS framework, which integrates sparse-view CT reconstruction, organ-level segmentation, and automated phenotyping into a unified pipeline. Given a small number of cone-beam X-ray projections, the reconstruction module recovers a high-fidelity 3D fruit CT volume using a radiative Gaussian-splatting formulation, as shown in Fig. 1b. This reconstructed volume is then fed into the segmentation module, which produces voxel-wise masks of the main anatomical components, including the flavedo, albedo, pulp, and locular cavity, using a CT-pretrained foundation model with a lightweight task-specific decoder, as shown in Fig. 1c. Finally, the phenotyping module converts the predicted 3D masks into quantitative measurements by computing trait values directly from voxel geometry and calibrated spatial resolution, as shown in Fig. 1d. This enables automated extraction of key phenotypic traits covering both external morphometrics and internal structural parameters. In addition, the reconstructed volumes and derived masks support internal defect visualization and analysis, providing a practical basis for non-destructive grading and high-throughput phenotyping.Fig. 1The overall pipeline of CitrusGS. Data acquisition, preprocessing, and experimental setup

A total of 300 fruit samples (C. reticulata cv. ‘Yunnanmiju’) were harvested from local orchards in Yuxi, Yunnan, China in 2025 and scanned for CT-based phenotyping. Ground-truth (GT) CT data were acquired using a microfocus CT scanner (NanoVoxe 2000, Tianjin Sanying Co., Ltd.) equipped with a microfocus X-ray source, a high-contrast flat-panel detector, and a rotation stage. The scanning protocol was configured as listed in Tables S1–S2. For each fruit, 360 projections were reconstructed into a 3D CT volume using Voxel Studio, and the resulting volumes were treated as GT for subsequent experiments. The original reconstructed volume size was 960 × 960 × 768 voxels. To standardize the data and reduce background redundancy, the window width/level was adjusted to remove empty margins, after which each volume was cropped to 512 × 512 × 512 voxels and downsampled to 256 × 256 × 256 for model training and evaluation. All experiments were conducted on a system with Windows 10, an Intel Core i9-13900K processor, and an NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPU (48 GB memory).

3D reconstruction

Sparse Gaussian point initialization strategy

High-quality initialization is critical for stable convergence and final reconstruction fidelity in Gaussian-splatting–based CT reconstruction [23]. Existing approaches often sample initial primitives from FDK-reconstructed priors [[24], [25], [26]]; however, under sparse-view conditions these priors are highly contaminated by streaking artifacts and noise, which can mislead the early optimization trajectory. To improve robustness and efficiency, we propose a sparse Gaussian initialization that allocates primitives to informative regions while maintaining global structural coverage.

Concretely, we uniformly distribute M = 5, 000 seeds throughout the volume. Uniform coverage is achieved via farthest point sampling over an initially oversampled set of candidate points, ensuring an approximately even spatial distribution and preventing clustering artifacts in the initial Gaussian layout. The isotropic scale σi is adaptively initialized for each center μi based on local density, ensuring comprehensive spatial coverage across the volume. The process begins by calculating the average k-nearest-neighbor distance ri:(1)ri=1k∑μj∈Nk(μi)μi−μj2,where Nk denotes the set of k nearest neighbors. The scale vector si is then initialized isotropically as:(2)si=(σi,σi,σi),withσi=clip(ασ⋅ri,σmin,σmax),where ασ is a dilation factor that controls the initial overlap between Gaussians. By setting ασ = 0.6, we encourage a low-frequency-first optimization bias, where broad kernels capture coarse structures before refining details. The hyperparameters are empirically set to k = 3, σmin = 0.05, and σmax = 1.2. Finally, the opacity of each Gaussian is initialized to a small constant with slight random perturbation to avoid early saturation, providing a stable starting point for subsequent optimization.

Optimization

Loss function. We optimize Gaussian parameters using a composite objective that combines photometric, structural, and volumetric regularization terms. The original loss of Gaussian Splatting LGS integrates the photometric loss L1, the structural dissimilarity loss Lssim, and 3D total variation (TV) regularization Ltv. Beyond these fundamental constraints, a composite structural loss LPET is incorporated, comprising perceptual (Lperc), edge (Ledge), and texture (Ltex) components. This combined loss function enables the precise capture of intricate morphological features within the fruit internal structure. The perceptual loss Lperc is defined as:(7)Lperc=Φˆ(Ir)−Φˆ(Im)22+121−Φˆ(Ir)⋅Φˆ(Im)Φˆ(Ir)2Φˆ(Im)2,where Ir and Im denote the rendered and measured images, respectively. Φˆ(⋅) denotes a fixed deep feature extractor used to map images into a perceptual feature space.

The edge loss Ledge is defined as:(8)Ledge=(Ir∗Kx)2+(Ir∗Ky)2−(Im∗Kx)2+(Im∗Ky)21,where Kx and Ky are standard Sobel filters.

The texture loss Ltex is defined as:(9)Ltexture=|Ir∗L|−|Im∗L|1,where L is the standard 2D Laplacian kernel.

The overall trainning loss Ltotal is defined as:(10)Ltotal=L1+λssimLssim+λtvLtv⏟LGS+λpercLperc+λedgeLedge+λtexLtex⏟LPETwhere λssim, λtv, λperc, λedge and λtex are the D-SSIM weight, TV weight, perceptual weight, edge weight, and texture weight, respectively.

Enhanced densification. To improve robustness and physical plausibility, we refine the standard densification process. During the densification stage, the subdivision of an oversized Gaussian Gp into N newly generate Gaussians {Gc,i} is constrained by mass and volume conservation. The density of the oversized Gaussian dp is distributed among itself and newly generate Gaussians, and the scaling of the new Gaussians sc is reduced accordingly. This conservation-centric approach ensures that the updated density dp′ and new Gaussian scaling sc,i satisfy:(11)dp′=dc,i=dpN+1(N+1)1/3(12)sc,i=sp(N+1)1/3where dp and sp denote the density and scaling vector of the oversized Gaussian, respectively. dp′ denotes the updated density of the oversized Gaussian, while {dc,i} and {sc,i} represent the density and scaling factors assigned to the N newly generate Gaussians.

Pruning strategy. To keep the Gaussian representation compact and to avoid redundant growth after densification, we adopt a two-stage pruning scheme that combines gradual attenuation with strict removal. This design reduces the risk of deleting potentially useful primitives too early, while reliably eliminating persistent outliers and physically implausible Gaussians.

We first apply soft pruning to Gaussian primitives whose survival iterations exceed a preset threshold of 10. We define a dynamic pruning threshold ρmin as half of the 5th percentile of all Gaussian densities:(13)ρmin=0.5⋅quantile({ρj},0.05)where {ρj} represents the set of densities of all Gaussians at the current iteration.

For any Gaussian Gi whose density ρi falls below the dynamic threshold ρmin, we enforce an exponential decay rather than immediate removal:(14)ρi←ρi⋅(1−γ)where γ = 0.05 is the decay coefficient. This attenuation phase allows stable but low-density primitives to potentially recover their contribution via subsequent gradient signals, mitigating premature information loss in intricate citrus textures.

Hard pruning is further implemented to permanently remove primitives that are clearly uninformative or physically implausible. A primitive Gi is permanently discarded from the primitive set P when either its density ρi falls below a threshold τh or its maximum spatial extent exceeds smax:(15)Prune(Gi)=True,if ρi<τh∨max(si)>smaxFalse,otherwisewhere τh = 0.2 ⋅ ρmin represents the minimum density required for a primitive to remain in the scene. ρmin is the dynamic threshold derived from the density distribution as previously defined. By imposing scale constraints (smax = 0.3), this stage effectively eliminates ‘floaters' and ensures that each primitive remains physically plausible within the reconstructed scene.

Fruit segmentation

Accurate organ-level segmentation is required to enable reliable fruit phenotyping from reconstructed CT volumes. In this study, we define the fruit internal anatomy as four components: flavedo, albedo, pulp, and locular cavity. All four anatomical components were delineated by trained raters using 3D Slicer, producing voxel-wise GT masks for supervision. We build our fruit organ segmentation model upon MedDINOv3 [27], a vision foundation model pretrained on a large corpus of CT slices. The segmentation model was trained using a dataset of 75 manually annotated citrus CT volumes, while 15 additional volumes were utilized for validation.

The MedDINOv3 backbone is self-supervised pretrained using a student-teacher framework with Gram branches, as shown in see Fig. 2a. To adapt the foundation model to the citrus phenotyping task, we employ a frozen-backbone fine-tuning strategy. Specifically, the MedDINOv3 encoder remains frozen to preserve its generalizable feature space, while a task-specific decoder and multi-scale segmentation heads are optimized to map these features to the citrus domain (see Fig. 2b). For the segmentation task, the reconstructed fruit CT volume by CitrusGS is fed into the encoder-decoder network, which outputs segmentation masks for flavedo, albedo, pulp, and cavity (see Fig. 2c). Phenotypic traits extraction

Comprehensive and accurate phenotyping is essential for citrus breeding and grading. Using the organ-level segmentation outputs, we first generate high-fidelity 3D masks for each anatomical component and for the whole fruit. These masks are then used to compute phenotypic traits in a fully automated manner.

For volumetric traits, we quantify the volume of each structural component by combining its voxel count with the corresponding spatial resolution. Because all CT volumes are normalized, the conversion from voxel count to physical volume is consistent across samples. For morphometric traits and layer-thickness traits, measurements are derived directly from the geometry of the corresponding masks. Overall, the pipeline extracts ten key phenotypic traits as summarized in Table S3, including five exterior traits (fruit length, fruit width, fruit height, fruit surface area, and fruit volume) and five interior traits: flavedo thickness, albedo thickness, pericarp volume, pulp volume, and cavity volume, where pericarp volume was calculated as the sum of the flavedo and albedo volumes.

Evaluation metrics

We evaluated the proposed pipeline from three perspectives: (i) reconstruction fidelity, (ii) organ-level segmentation accuracy, and (iii) phenotyping accuracy. For reconstruction evaluation, sparse-view reconstructed volumes were compared with the corresponding GT CT volumes reconstructed from 360-view projections using the traditional FDK algorithm [28].

For reconstruction fidelity, peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) and structural similarity index (SSIM) were used to quantify reconstruction quality between the reconstructed volume Vrec and the GT volume Vgt. Let N be the numbers of voxels and(16)MSE=1N∑i=1NVrec(i)−Vgt(i)2,PSNR is then computed as(17)PSNR=20log10ImaxMSE,where Imax is the maximum possible voxel intensity. SSIM was computed between Vrec and Vgt using the standard formulation based on local means, variances, and cross-covariance; higher PSNR/SSIM indicates better reconstruction fidelity.

Organ-level segmentation performance was evaluated on anatomical components. For each component, we computed the Dice coefficient and intersection-over-union (IoU) between the predicted mask P and GT mask G:(15)Dice=2|P∩G||P|+|G|=2TP2TP+FP+FN,(16)IoU=|P∩G||P∪G|=TPTP+FP+FN.Here, TP, FP, FN are voxel-wise counts defined with respect to the foreground of the target component. Multi-class performance was summarized by averaging Dice/IoU across the four components.

The accuracy of the extracted phenotypic traits was validated using the root mean square error (RMSE) and the coefficient of determination R2.

Reconstruction results

Based on our constructed dataset, we benchmarked CitrusGS against three representative baselines: the conventional FDK algorithm, the NeRF-based SAX-NeRF [22], and the 3D Gaussian splatting based R2-Gaussian [26]. All methods were evaluated under 15-, 25-, and 50-view settings using both quantitative metrics and qualitative visual comparisons.

Quantitative comparison. As summarized in Table 1, CitrusGS achieves the highest PSNR across all view, reaching 29.778, 32.801, and 35.516 dB under the 15-view, 25-view, and 50-view, respectively. Compared with R2-Gaussian, the largest improvement occurs in the most challenging 15-view regime, where CitrusGS increases PSNR by 1.58 dB and improves SSIM by 0.067. At 25- and 50-view, CitrusGS consistently maintains the best PSNR while also yielding higher SSIM than R2-Gaussian and substantially outperforming FDK, demonstrating robust reconstruction fidelity under increasing projections. At 15-view, SAX-NeRF achieves an SSIM that was 0.049 higher than that of CitrusGS; however, CitrusGS achieves a PSNR that was 1.59 dB higher than that of SAX-NeRF. Under the 50-view, the SSIM difference between SAX-NeRF and CitrusGS decreases to 0.002, whereas CitrusGS retained a 0.262 dB PSNR advantage over SAX-NeRF. Model compactness and convergence. In addition to reconstruction quality, CitrusGS produces a more compact Gaussian representation. Ablation study

To validate the individual contributions of our key components, we conducted an ablation study on the 15-view fruit dataset. The experiments evaluated the impact of three critical elements in the proposed reconstruciton framework: the sparse point initialization (SPI) module, the proposed loss composite LPET, and the soft and hard pruning (SHP) strategy. The original R2-Gaussian was taken as the baseline method for comparison. The quantitative results are summarized in Table 2. Our complete framework achieves the highest reconstruction quality with PSNR of 29.778 dB and SSIM of 0.870, representing improvements of 1.58 dB and 0.067 over the baseline method, respectively. In the ablation removing the SPI module while retaining the loss composite LPET and pruning strategies, a significant performance degradation is observed, with PSNR of 28.635 dB and SSIM of 0.822, and prominent streaking artifacts are visible in the reconstructed volumes as shown in Fig. 4. Removing the LPET component results in PSNR of 29.156 dB and SSIM of 0.850, while ablating the SHP strategy yields PSNR of 29.051 dB and SSIM of 0.845, both leading to noticeable degradation in reconstruction quality, particularly in preserving fine structural details. These results collectively demonstrate that each component plays an essential role in our framework. The SPI module establishes an effective initialization that guides optimization toward meaningful geometry. The loss composite LPET ensures both global fidelity and local detail preservation, while the dual-stage pruning strategy maintains a compact yet expressive Gaussian representation. The synergistic combination of these components enables high-fidelity reconstruction from extremely sparse views. In addition, the effects of initialization and the two-stage pruning strategy on reconstruction stability were tested through ablation experiments under the 15-view setting. The results showed that SPI achieved higher reconstruction quality than FDK initialization, with PSNR and SSIM improvements of 0.945 dB and 0.045, respectively. The SHP pruning strategy further improved reconstruction performance while preserving anatomical structures, with PSNR and SSIM improvements of 0.109 dB and 0.08, respectively. Segmentation results

To further validate the fidelity and accuracy of our reconstructed CT volumes, we employed organ-level segmentation as a downstream task that is highly sensitive to reconstruction artifacts and structural distortions. For comparison, we benchmarked another two representative segmentation architectures, nnUNet [29] and DINOUNet [30] to CT volumes reconstructed from 15-view projections using CitrusGS. nnUNet and DINOUNet are finetuned following the same process of MedDINOv3. The 15-view setting was selected as it provides a practical trade-off between acquisition sparsity and anatomical boundary visibility.

Quantitative evaluation shown in Table S7, demonstrates that finetuned MedDINOv3 achieves the highest segmentation performance across all metrics, with a Dice coefficient of 0.9168 and an IoU score of 0.8512. Although the numerical gains are modest, they are consistent across both metrics. Qualitative results shown in Fig. 5 further support these findings. Discussion

Reconstruction quality under sparse acquisition conditions

Sparse-view CT reconstruction is fundamentally ill-posed, as limited angular sampling creates an underdetermined inverse problem where conventional FDK amplifies noise and learning-based approaches introduce hallucinate structures. Our results demonstrate that CitrusGS overcomes this challenge through a coherent optimization strategy that aligns initialization, loss design, and representation management toward physically plausible solutions.

The sparse-point initialization avoids propagating FDK artifacts into the optimization trajectory. By starting with only 5000 uniformly distributed Gaussians rather than sampling from artifact-contaminated priors, the model first captures low-frequency geometry before refining details, leading both the 6× faster convergence and the 2.29% improvement in ROI coverage efficiency. The loss composite LPET addresses a key limitation of single-metric optimization: PSNR/SSIM alone cannot guarantee anatomically meaningful fidelity. By jointly optimizing perceptual similarity, edge sharpness, and texture preservation, CitrusGS specifically targets structures essential for phenotyping, particularly the flavedo–albedo interface and tissue–cavity transitions where density contrast is inherently low. Finally, the dual-stage pruning mechanism maintains representation compactness without sacrificing expressiveness. Soft pruning's gradual attenuation preserves potentially useful low-density primitives, while hard pruning eliminates physically implausible “floaters” that degrade structural coherence. The 14% reduction in Gaussian count relative to R2-Gaussian without quality degradation demonstrate that CitrusGS concentrates model capacity on biologically relevant structures rather than background noise or artifacts.

The reconstruction–segmentation–phenotyping cascade

A critical insight from our work is that reconstruction artifacts propagate systematically through downstream tasks, introducing measurement bias that cannot be corrected later. Comparative study shows that only CitrusGS preserves both global geometry and local texture details required for precise organ delineation. As CitrusGS specifically preserves structures relevant to trait extraction, it achieves moderate absolute PSNR gains but delivers substantially larger improvements in organ segmentation and phenotyping accuracy. Critically, our pipeline differentiates flavedo and albedo layers, enabling peel-stratification phenotyping, whereas recent approaches treat the pericarp as homogeneous [21]. This capability directly supports breeding programs targeting peel-related traits that depend on layer-specific morphology.

The error propagation analysis was conducted on 75 samples by comparing reconstruction, segmentation, and phenotypic results between R2-Gaussian and CitrusGS. CitrusGS achieved higher PSNR and SSIM than R2-Gaussian, with improvements of 1.01 dB and 0.036, respectively. Downstream segmentation results further support this trend, with CitrusGS improving performance across all four anatomical classes. The mean Dice increased from 0.965 to 0.968, IoU increased from 0.934 to 0.940, and ASSD decreased from 0.362 to 0.333 (detailed results in Table S8). The segmentation gains propagated to downstream phenotyping, where CitrusGS demonstrated consistently higher R2, lower RMSE and MAE, and reduced absolute mean bias across all ten traits against ground-truth measurements. On average, R2 increased from 0.967 to 0.981, RMSE decreased by approximately 24.0%, and MAE decreased by approximately 29.2%. Furthermore, CitrusGS reduced absolute mean bias in 8 of 10 traits, indicating more consistent phenotypic estimation with fewer systematic errors (as illustrated in Fig. S10). This error propagation pattern is consistently observed in both thin-layer and cavity-related structures. For albedo thickness estimation, improvements in segmentation quality were accompanied by substantial gains in phenotypic prediction performance, with R2 increasing from 0.849 to 0.944, RMSE decreasing from 0.071 to 0.043, and MAE decreasing from 0.045 to 0.031.

Overall, these results suggest that reconstruction errors in CT-based phenotyping should not be interpreted solely as image-level degradations. Instead, they may propagate through anatomical segmentation and affect mask-derived phenotypic measurements, consistent with recent extremely sparse-view CT studies emphasizing downstream task-based evaluation [32].

Generalization across defect conditions and biological variability

Practical agricultural phenotyping requires robustness across natural biological variation and pathological conditions. To evaluate generalization capability, we tested CitrusGS on two challenging scenarios beyond our training distribution: pest-infested fruit exhibiting internal tissue damage and fruit samples from different cultivars with significant anatomic differences.

For pest-infested samples, CitrusGS achieved a 1.120 dB PSNR gain and a 0.037 SSIM improvement over R2-Gaussian. More importantly, qualitative assessment revealed that CitrusGS preserved boundary definition in damaged regions where structural continuity is disrupted, a scenario where other methods easily generate hallucinated structures or severe blurring, as shown in Fig. 8a. Overall, CitrusGS is evaluated on 97 scenes across four datasets (YNMJ = 70, six citrus cultivars = 6, pest-infested samples = 11, horticultural plants = 10), consistently outperforming the baseline with a mean PSNR improvement of +1.161 dB and SSIM gain of +0.040 (see Supplementary Table S9). This generalization capability has direct implications for real-world deployment, as breeding programs routinely encounter novel genotypes with unexpected morphologies, while grading lines must handle fruit exhibiting various physiological disorders or pest damage. A reconstruction method requiring extensive retraining for each new condition would be impractical at scale. CitrusGS's ability to handle such variation in 3D reconstruction without training supports its viability for both research and industrial applications.

Practical trade-offs and deployment considerations

The 15-view acquisition protocol employed in our experiments represents a strategic balance between scanning efficiency and phenotyping accuracy. Compared to conventional CT protocols requiring hundreds to thousands of projections, the proposed sparse-view strategy reduces the number of acquired projections from 360 to 15. In our CT system, acquiring 360 projections requires approximately 14 min; therefore, reducing the acquisition protocol to 15 projections corresponds to an estimated acquisition time of approximately 35 s. Under unchanged tube voltage, tube current, and exposure time per projection, this corresponds to an estimated 95.8% reduction in projection-related X-ray exposure. This is consistent with previous sparse-view CT studies showing that reducing the amount of projection data can provide advantages such as dose reduction and shorter scanning time [35], and that radiation exposure is closely related to the number of acquired X-ray projection views under comparable acquisition conditions [36]. These efficiency gains come with only minor accuracy penalties, resulting in pehnotyping accuracy comparable to that of destructive methods [31]. Moreover, sparse projections can be conveniently obtained using X-ray machines, whose cost is typically 5% of CT machines.

Nevertheless, application-specific requirements should guide view-count selection. Breeding programs prioritizing precision for subtle trait variation may benefit from even more views for higher anatomic accuracy, while industrial grading lines emphasizing throughput might operate effectively at fewer. This flexibility, coupled with the method's compatibility with existing X-ray hardware, facilitates adoption potentially across diverse operational contexts.

Limitations and future work

Despite its advantages, our framework has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the current pipeline requires manual triggering of segmentation and phenotyping stages following reconstruction. Future integration with intelligent workflow agents could enable fully automated end-to-end processing from raw projections to trait reports. Second, while reconstruction time (2 min at 15-view) is practical for many applications, further acceleration through hardware-aware optimization or model compression would benefit ultra-high-throughput scenarios such as commercial packing lines processing thousands of fruits hourly.

Conclusion

This study presents CitrusGS, a unified framework that enables high-fidelity 3D reconstruction and precise morphological phenotyping of citrus fruit from sparse X-ray projections. By reformulating radiative 3D Gaussian splatting with sparse-point initialization, composite structural loss optimization, and dual-stage pruning, our method achieves artifact-suppressed reconstructions from as few as 15-view, substantially outperforming conventional FDK and learning-based baselines. These reconstructions support organ-level segmentation with Dice scores of 0.917 and enable automated extraction of ten external and internal phenotypic traits with high accuracy, including previously inaccessible peel-layer stratification. Critically, CitrusGS demonstrates robust zero-shot generalization across pathological conditions and diverse horticultural specimens without retraining, while remaining compatible with low-cost X-ray hardware. By reconciling acquisition efficiency with anatomical fidelity, this work establishes a practical pathway toward non-destructive, high-throughput phenotyping for both breeding programs and industrial grading systems.
