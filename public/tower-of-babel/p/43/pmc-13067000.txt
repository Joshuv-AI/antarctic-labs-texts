Prognostic value of DNA methylation in head and neck cancer: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Mingyang Che, Dongyuan Tang, Rongjun Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe clinical relevance of DNA methylation in head and neck cancer (HNC) is increasingly recognized, yet controversies persist regarding its prognostic significance. We conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis adhering to the PRISMA 2020 guidelines to synthesize evidence on this association.MethodsA comprehensive literature search was performed across five databases (PubMed, Web of Science Core Collection, Cochrane Library, Embase, and Scopus) from inception to October 31, 2025. Eligible studies included original clinical investigations (prospective/retrospective cohort, case-control) reporting prognostic outcomes [overall survival (OS), disease-free survival (DFS)] stratified by DNA methylation status in histologically confirmed HNC patients. Pooled hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using random-effects models.ResultsNineteen studies involving 3,251 patients were included. High DNA methylation was significantly associated with poor prognosis in HNC: OS (HR =1.50, 95% CI: 1.19–1.90, P=0.001) and DFS (HR =2.44, 95% CI: 1.74–3.47, P<0.001). Significant heterogeneity was observed for OS (I2=71%, P<0.001) and moderate heterogeneity for DFS (I2=42%, P=0.10), partially attributed to regional differences and variability in detection methods. Confounding adjustment status confirmed the robustness of the results.ConclusionsDNA methylation plays a pivotal role in HNC carcinogenesis and progression, emerging as a robust and promising prognostic biomarker. However, its predictive value for treatment response remains unproven, requiring further investigation in treatment-stratified cohorts.

Highlight box

Key findings

• A PRISMA 2020-compliant systematic review and meta-analysis of 19 studies [3,251 head and neck cancer (HNC) patients] demonstrated that high DNA methylation correlates significantly with poor overall survival (OS: HR =1.50, 95% CI: 1.19–1.90, P=0.001) and disease-free survival (DFS: HR =2.44, 95% CI: 1.70–2.97, P<0.001). Substantial heterogeneity was observed for OS (I2=71%, P<0.001) and moderate heterogeneity for DFS (I2=42%, P=0.10), attributable to regional differences and variable methylation detection methods. Inclusion of only multivariate-adjusted hazard ratios ensured the robustness of this prognostic association.

What is known and what is new?

• DNA methylation is clinically relevant to HNC, yet its prognostic significance remains controversial owing to inconsistent study evidence.

• This study provides a rigorously adjusted quantification of the methylation-prognosis link in HNC, identifies key sources of between-study heterogeneity, and explicitly distinguishes the validated prognostic value from the unvalidated predictive utility of DNA methylation.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• High DNA methylation can serve as a robust prognostic biomarker for HNC risk stratification. Future studies should standardize detection methods, stratify patients by HPV status, and conduct large, prospective, treatment-stratified cohorts to validate its predictive potential for therapeutic response.

Introduction

Head and neck cancer (HNC) represents a heterogeneous group of lethal human malignancies arising from anatomical sites that include the oral cavity, pharynx (nasopharynx, oropharynx, and hypopharynx), larynx, paranasal sinuses, nasal cavity, thyroid, and salivary glands (1). Head and neck neoplasms are a critical health problem that endanger human life worldwide by resulting in impaired dietary intake, breathing, and appearance (2). Additionally, despite advancements in early diagnostic and therapeutic modalities, the 5-year overall survival (OS) rate persists at about 50%, and the prognosis for most patients remains dismal and is difficult to predict (3-5). Accordingly, there is an urgent need to establish robust and reliable prognostic biomarkers for early detection, diagnosis, and prediction of the clinical outcome to develop effective therapeutic interventions for patients with HNC (6).

DNA methylation is a major epigenetic mechanism during the pathogenesis of human cancers and can affect chromatin structure, gene expression, and tissue development by resulting in heritable transcriptional gene silencing (7). Gene methylation affects key cellular functional pathways that control the cell cycle and proliferation; response to genotoxic stress and apoptosis; metastasis and invasion; and terminal differentiation, which indicates that it is likely to have a close relationship with cancer prognosis (8). Accumulating clinical evidence indicates that DNA methylation is an important diagnostic hallmark in a substantial number of cancers, including HNC (9,10). Although numerous prior association studies have investigated the prognostic biomarker role of DNA methylation in HNC, its clinical prognosis remains controversial and ambiguous. some studies link high methylation to adverse prognosis. Noorlag et al. concluded that DNA methylation is associated with prognosis in HNC (11). Sun et al. and Langevin et al. believed there is a possible relationship between the DNA methylation of some genes and HNC (12,13).

DNA methylation has emerged as a pivotal epigenetic regulator in the carcinogenesis and progression of HNC, making it a promising prognostic biomarker. However, extensive research in this field has yielded inconsistent findings: while some studies link high methylation to adverse prognosis, others report null associations—such as Padin-Iruegas et al. (14), who demonstrated no correlation between MGMT/MLH1 methylation and OS in oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC). A minority of studies even suggest protective effects, exemplified by Roh et al. (15), who identified p16 (CDKN2A) methylation as a protective factor in human papillomavirus (HPV)-negative HNC patients with disruptive TP53 mutations.

This controversy is primarily driven by four interrelated factors, with HPV status being the most critical confounder: (I) gene-specific functional heterogeneity, as methylation of distinct genes (tumor suppressors like MLH1/p16 vs. DNA repair genes like MGMT) exerts divergent prognostic effects; (II) gene-specific functional, given the etiologic differences between HPV-driven oropharyngeal carcinoma and tobacco/alcohol-related oral cavity carcinoma, which most studies fail to stratify by; (III) methodological heterogeneity in methylation detection [e.g., methylation-specific polymerase chain reaction (MSP) vs. pyrosequencing] and inconsistent definitions of hypermethylation, complicating cross-study comparisons; (IV) the unaddressed modifying role of HPV status—the strongest prognostic determinant in HNC. As highlighted by Marsit et al. (16), etiologic heterogeneity (including HPV status) is a key driver of inconsistent methylation-prognosis relationships, yet only 30% of prior studies incorporate HPV stratification, and no meta-analysis has systematically integrated this factor to resolve the ambiguity. These gaps raise two critical unanswered questions: (I) does HPV status explain the heterogeneity in methylation-prognosis associations? (II) Are the prognostic effects of methylation gene- or subsite-specific, independent of HPV? To address these, we pre-specified two core hypotheses: (I) HPV status acts as a key effect modifier—high methylation of target genes (MGMT/MLH1/p16) correlates with poorer prognosis in HPV-negative HNC but is attenuated or absent in HPV-positive patients; (II) heterogeneity is further driven by gene type and anatomical subsite.

Against this backdrop, the present meta-analysis aims to systematically integrate eligible studies to resolve the existing controversy. We will conduct pre-specified subgroup analyses (by HPV status, gene type, and anatomical subsite) and meta-regression (incorporating HPV status, detection method) to test the above hypotheses, we performed a comprehensive literature review in this meta-analysis of epigenome-wide association studies to clarify the correlation between DNA methylation as a sensitive prognostic biomarker and the survival rate in patients with HNC. We present this article in accordance with the PRISMA reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2025-1502/rc).

Methods

Study design

All methods and analytical plans were predefined based on the research question and PRISMA recommendations, with detailed procedural documentation to ensure reproducibility. The study selection process is outlined in the PRISMA flow diagram (Figure 1).

Literature search strategy

Databases and search period

A comprehensive search was performed across five biomedical databases (PubMed, Web of Science Core Collection, Cochrane Library, Embase, and Scopus) to minimize selection bias. All databases were searched from inception to October 31, 2025 to capture the latest evidence on DNA methylation as a prognostic biomarker in HNC.

Search terms and strategy

The search strategy was designed using the Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcomes and Study design (PICOS) framework, integrating database-specific medical subject headings (MeSH, exclusive to PubMed) and free-text keywords. Core search dimensions included: (I) HNC and subtypes [e.g., head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), oropharyngeal cancer, nasopharyngeal cancer]; (II) DNA methylation-related terms (e.g., hypermethylation, CpG island methylation, epigenetic modification); (III) prognostic endpoints [e.g., OS, disease-free survival (DFS), recurrence, prognostic biomarker]. Supplementary filters included English language, publication timeframe, and restriction to original research. Full search strings for each database are provided in Appendix 1.

Literature screening process

Literature screening was performed independently by two reviewers (M.C. and D.T.) in two stages: (I) title/abstract screening to exclude irrelevant studies (e.g., non-HNC populations, preclinical studies, reviews); (II) full-text screening to confirm compliance with inclusion/exclusion criteria. Discrepancies were resolved via discussion or consultation with a third reviewer (R.Z.).

Risk of bias (RoB) assessment

In compliance with PRISMA guidelines and Cochrane recommendations, we adopted the RoB framework (replacing “Quality assessment”) using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS, 9-star scale) for cohort/case-control studies. RoB levels were defined as: low risk (NOS ≥7 stars), moderate risk (4–6 stars), high risk (≤3 stars). Special emphasis was placed on the “comparability” domain (critical for confounding control); studies with ≤1 star in this domain were classified as moderate/high risk regardless of total score. RoB assessment was performed independently by two reviewers, with discrepancies resolved via discussion. A narrative summary of RoB findings is provided in the Results section.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Review Manager 5.4 and RStudio. A random-effects model was used for pooled effect size calculation to account for inter-study heterogeneity.

Heterogeneity and subgroup analyses

Heterogeneity was evaluated using the I2 statistic and Q test; I2>50% indicated significant heterogeneity. Predefined subgroup analyses were conducted stratified by gene type, anatomical subsite, and detection method to explore heterogeneity sources.

Sensitivity analysis

Due to the absence of moderate/high-RoB studies (all NOS ≥7 stars with ≥2 stars in “comparability”), RoB-stratified analysis was unfeasible. Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis (sequentially excluding each study and recalculating pooled HR) was performed to verify result robustness.

Publication bias assessment

Publication bias was evaluated separately for OS and DFS endpoints based on the number of included studies. For OS, Egger’s linear regression test was performed to quantitatively assess publication bias (P<0.05 indicated statistical significance), as the number of included studies was sufficient to ensure adequate statistical power. For DFS, given the small number of included studies (n<10), Egger’s test was deemed methodologically inappropriate due to low statistical power (Higgins et al., 2022). Instead, a qualitative assessment of funnel plot symmetry was adopted to visually evaluate potential publication bias. The limitations of this visual approach for small study sets are explicitly discussed in the Limitations section. Additionally, the trim-and-fill method was applied to further assess and correct for potential publication bias where applicable.

Results

Study selection and characteristics (PRISMA Items 9–10)

The PRISMA-guided study selection process yielded 19 eligible studies (Figure 1). Initial searches across five databases identified 5,833 records; 2,149 duplicates were removed, leaving 3,684 records for initial screening. A total of 3,304 records were excluded at title/abstract screening, and 361 at full-text review, resulting in 19 studies for inclusion (17-35).

All included studies were retrospective cohort studies published between 2015 and 2024, with sample sizes ranging from 30 to 528 participants (total n=3,251). Among them, 12 studies investigated OS and 8 investigated DFS (Table 1). Primary tumor sites included the oral cavity, oropharynx, hypopharynx, larynx, and nasopharynx, with TNM stages ranging from I to IV. HPV status was evaluated in 13 studies [detection methods: p16 immunohistochemistry, HPV in situ hybridization, polymerase chain reaction (PCR)] with positive rates of 0–70.1%, but insufficient detailed data (e.g., HPV typing, viral load) precluded HPV-stratified subgroup analyses. DNA methylation detection methods included Q-MSP, Illumina Infinium HumanMethylation450 BeadChip, MSP, bisulfite pyrosequencing, and EpiTYPER MassARRAY technology. Discussion

HNC remains a clinically heterogeneous disease with substantial variability in patient outcome. DNA methylation is an important epigenetic modification that has been investigated extensively, and mainly manifests in decreased whole-genome methylation levels and the abnormal increase of the local CpG methylation levels (35), which causes gene transcriptional silencing and expression, and subsequent cell fate (36-39). Given the encouraging results of clinical trials evaluating the prognostic value of the DNA methylation of numerous tumor-associated genes, such as p16 (40), IGF1R (41) and RASSF1A (42), DNA methylation is being closely studied as a potential prognostic marker in cancer (30,43,44). The most recent phase I/II clinical trial evaluating DNA methylation profiling—particularly circulating tumor DNA (cfDNA) epigenetic analysis—highlights a promising future for both advanced detection of HNC (45). In the present study, we found that numerous studies on HNC reported relatively higher DNA methylation levels (9,46,47). However, some findings remain to be investigated (46,48). Accordingly, we summed the DNA methylation level profile, consistent with other results (9). Tumor-suppressor genes had increased DNA methylation levels at varying frequencies. Interestingly, the DNA methylation analysis showed that gene methylation status correlated negatively with the clinical outcome of head and neck tumors relatively strongly (49). Against this backdrop, our meta-analysis synthesizes data from 19 high-quality cohort studies, providing the most comprehensive evidence to date that DNA methylation serves as a robust adverse prognostic marker for OS and DFS in HNC.

Our analysis confirms that aberrant DNA methylation is consistently associated with poor clinical outcomes in HNC: the pooled HR for OS was 1.50 (95% CI: 1.19–1.90, P=0.001), and for DFS was 2.24 (95% CI: 1.70–2.97, P<0.001). These findings align with a well-established mechanistic paradigm wherein locus-specific hypermethylation of CpG islands within tumor suppressor gene promoters induces transcriptional silencing, disrupting key pathways governing cell cycle control, apoptosis, and DNA repair. Notably, our subgroup analyses highlight that genes such as NDN (HR =2.35, 95% CI: 1.40–3.94), PTGDR1 (HR =1.58, 95% CI: 1.06–2.36), and SALL3 (HR =1.91, 95% CI: 1.16–3.16)—consistently hypermethylated across included cohorts—are critical mediators of this prognostic effect. This is consistent with prior functional studies demonstrating that silencing of NDN promotes tumor cell proliferation via deregulation of the p53 pathway, while PTGDR1 methylation enhances HNC invasiveness by modulating epithelial-mesenchymal transition. Collectively, these data underscore DNA methylation’s role not only as a prognostic correlate but also as a functional driver of HNC aggressiveness.

Strikingly, subgroup analyses stratified by geographic region revealed pronounced disparities in prognostic effect sizes. North American cohorts showed no statistically significant association between DNA methylation and OS (HR =1.23, 95% CI: 0.96–1.57, P=0.11), whereas Asian and European cohorts exhibited strong negative correlations with DFS (HR =1.90, 95% CI: 1.48–2.44, P<0.01; HR =2.49, 95% CI: 1.39–4.45, P<0.01, respectively). These regional differences are likely multifactorial: first, HPV prevalence varies dramatically across cohorts [0% in the Serbian Military Medical Academy (MMA) hospital cohort vs. 70.1% in the UK Head and Neck 5000 cohort], and HPV-driven HNC is known to harbor distinct epigenetic profiles characterized by hypomethylation of specific loci. Second, lifestyle factors—such as smoking and alcohol consumption, which are potent modifiers of DNA methylation—differ substantially by region: for example, high rates of alcohol and tobacco use in Asian and European HNC populations may exacerbate epigenetic dysregulation, amplifying its prognostic impact. Third, technical variability in methylation detection methods (e.g., high-throughput Illumina BeadChip arrays in TCGA-derived cohorts vs. targeted Q-MSP in single-center studies) may contribute to heterogeneity, as these approaches differ in CpG site coverage, quantification accuracy, and ability to capture global vs. locus-specific methylation changes.

To address substantial heterogeneity (OS: I2=71%, P<0.001; DFS: I2=42%, P=0.10), we conducted comprehensive subgroup analyses and meta-regression. For DFS, tumor type emerged as a key contributor to heterogeneity: studies focusing on multiple HNC subtypes (oral cavity, oropharynx, larynx, hypopharynx) showed a stronger prognostic effect (HR =2.31, 95% CI: 1.65–3.24) than those targeting single subtypes [e.g., laryngeal squamous cell carcinoma (LSCC), nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC); HR =1.76, 95% CI: 1.28–2.42]. This likely reflects the broader spectrum of epigenetic alterations across diverse tumor sites, as distinct HNC subtypes arise from unique cell-of-origin and etiological factors. Meta-regression further identified detection method (P=0.003) as a significant modifier of OS heterogeneity, with high-throughput arrays yielding more consistent results than targeted PCR—possibly due to their ability to capture genome-wide methylation patterns rather than single-gene alterations. Critically, leave-one-out sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of our findings: sequential exclusion of any single study—including Shen et al.’s 2017 LSCC cohort (HR =2.71, 95% CI: 1.02–7.17)—did not substantially alter pooled HRs for OS (range, 1.42–1.58) or DFS (range, 2.01–2.47) (31). This resilience to individual study removal underscores the reliability of our conclusions and mitigates concerns about the impact of outlier cohorts [e.g., Burgener et al.’s 2021 HPV-negative circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) cohort, HR =7.55, 95% CI: 0.95–60.00] (18).

Based on the above results, our meta-analysis indicates the potential prognostic utility of DNA methylation levels in HNC. Notably, OS rates of HNC vary significantly by HPV status (50). and existing literature has hypothesized that HPV-associated tumors are more strongly driven by methylation alterations compared to HPV-negative tumors (49). Our findings confirm that high DNA methylation is associated with poor prognosis in HNC, providing preliminary evidence for its potential as a prognostic biomarker. However, its strengths—including strict adherence to PRISMA guidelines, inclusion of only high-quality studies (all NOS=8 stars, low RoB), and comprehensive subgroup and sensitivity analyses—this meta-analysis has several limitations that inform future research. First, the confounding and interactive effects of HPV status could not be fully disentangled. While 13 of 19 studies reported HPV status (positive rates: 0–70.1%) and performed stratified analyses, 6 studies did not specify HPV detection methods or subgroup results. Given that HPV-positive HNC is associated with improved survival and distinct epigenetic landscapes—including hypermethylation of p16 and hypomethylation of oncogenes—this data gap may obscure subtype-specific associations. A primary limitation is the inability to fully address the confounding and interactive effects of HPV status, despite our efforts to include studies published in the past decade. As the most critical prognostic determinant in HNC, HPV status not only independently influences patient survival but also exhibits close crosstalk with DNA methylation. Previous studies have demonstrated that HPV-positive and HPV-negative HNCs possess distinct epigenetic profiles, with HPV-driven tumors often characterized by specific methylation patterns. Regrettably, the paucity of detailed HPV status data (e.g., HPV typing, infection load) in the included studies precluded us from performing HPV-stratified subgroup analyses. This deficiency may obscure the true association between DNA methylation and prognosis across different HPV subgroups, particularly given the increasing incidence of HPV-associated HNC in recent years. Thus, the pooled results may not be universally applicable to all HNC populations, highlighting the need for future meta-analyses or primary studies to prioritize the collection and reporting of detailed HPV data. Integrating HPV status into analytical frameworks is essential to clarify its confounding role and interactive effects with DNA methylation, thereby enhancing the clinical relevance and reliability of DNA methylation as a prognostic biomarker in HNC. Future studies must prioritize detailed reporting of HPV typing (e.g., HPV16 vs. non-16 high-risk types), viral integration status, and p16 expression, and integrate HPV status into multivariable models to clarify its interaction with DNA methylation.

Second, publication bias may have influenced our results. Funnel plot asymmetry and Egger’s test indicated potential bias, while trim-and-fill analysis imputed 3 missing studies for OS and 4 for DFS—suggesting underrepresentation of smaller studies with non-significant findings. This bias is exacerbated by the relatively small sample sizes of individual included studies (range, 30–528 patients) and the field’s emphasis on statistically significant results. To address this, large-scale prospective consortia studies—such as the Head and Neck Cancer Genome Atlas (HNCGA)—are needed to validate methylation signatures in diverse populations and ensure generalizability.

Third, technical heterogeneity in methylation detection methods (e.g., Illumina 450K/EPIC arrays, Q-MSP, pyrosequencing) introduces variability in CpG site selection and quantification. Standardization of these methods—including harmonization of reference standards, quality control metrics, and data analysis pipelines—is critical to enable cross-study comparisons and accelerate clinical translation. Additionally, most included studies focused on tissue-derived methylation; future research should prioritize ctDNA-based methylation assays, which offer non-invasive prognostic monitoring and potential for early detection of recurrence—an unmet need in HNC management.

Our findings have important clinical implications for HNC management. DNA methylation signatures could be integrated into existing prognostic models (e.g., TNM stage, HPV status, smoking history) to enhance risk stratification: for example, patients with high methylation levels could be assigned to more intensive treatment regimens or closer surveillance. Furthermore, ctDNA methylation profiling—already validated in early HNC detection—could serve as a dynamic prognostic tool, enabling real-time monitoring of treatment response and early identification of recurrence. Finally, the regional disparities observed in our analysis highlight the need for personalized epigenetic biomarkers tailored to specific populations, accounting for etiological factors such as HPV prevalence and lifestyle exposures.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this meta-analysis provides robust evidence that aberrant DNA methylation is a consistent and reliable adverse prognostic biomarker for OS and DFS in HNC. While limitations exist—primarily related to HPV status confounding and publication bias—our findings underscore the potential of DNA methylation to address unmet needs in HNC risk stratification and clinical decision-making. Future research should prioritize large-scale prospective studies, standardization of methylation detection methods, and functional validation of key methylated genes to advance the translational potential of epigenetic biomarkers in HNC.
