Synergistic activation of dendritic cells by Lycium barbarum polysaccharide and tumor endothelial progenitor cell lysate enhances anti-tumor responses
Authors: Lifang Wang, Ziye Wang, Changchang Zhu, Wenhu Chen, Hongguang Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dendritic cells (DCs) play a pivotal role in initiating robust T cell responses against tumors, yet effective strategies to enhance DC activation remain limited. This study explores the synergistic effects of Lycium barbarum polysaccharide (LBP) and the tumor cell lysate of endothelial progenitor cells (EPC-TCL) to boost DC activation and anti-tumor immunity. Using flow cytometry, the enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, western blot analysis, and in vivo tumor models, we demonstrate that the combination treatment significantly enhanced expression of co-stimulatory molecules (CD40, CD80, CD86, and MHC-I) in DCs and elevated cytokine levels (IL-6 and IL-12). Co-culture experiments revealed that T cells primed with LBP and EPC-TCL-modified DCs increased proliferation and reduced exhaustion, characterized by upregulated CD69 and downregulated PD-1. Mechanistically, activation of the MAPK and STING signaling pathways was confirmed by phosphorylation of key proteins. Moreover, T cells activated by DCs treated with LBP and EPC-TCL exhibited potent anti-tumor effects, significantly reducing invasiveness of mouse lung carcinoma cells and impairing angiogenesis in vitro. In a mouse axillary tumor model, the combination treatment markedly suppressed tumor growth and induced apoptosis of tumor cells. These findings highlight the potential of LBP and EPC-TCL as novel immunotherapeutic agents targeting DCs to enhance anti-tumor immunity. Future studies will focus on clinical validation and integration into combination therapies for broader cancer applications.

Introduction

The activation of dendritic cells (DCs) is a pivotal process in the initiation and regulation of immune responses, particularly in the context of cancer immunotherapy1. DCs serve as the primary antigen-presenting cells that bridge the innate and adaptive immune systems, orchestrating T cell activation and subsequent immune responses against tumors. However, enhancing the activation and functionality of DCs remains a significant challenge in the fields of immunology and cancer therapy. Current strategies for DC activation include the use of various immunomodulatory agents; nevertheless, there is a critical gap in understanding the synergistic effects of these agents when used in combination, particularly regarding the mechanisms of action and suspected signaling pathways2,3.

Recent studies have highlighted the importance of specific signaling pathways in DC activation, especially the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) and stimulator of interferon genes (STING) pathways. Activation of these pathways is essential for the maturation and functional enhancement of DCs, which in turn can significantly influence the efficacy of T cell responses and overall anti-tumor immunity4,5. Despite advancements in elucidating the role of individual agents, the lack of consensus on the most effective combinations of agents to activate DCs indicates a pressing need to explore novel combinatory approaches to improve immunotherapeutic outcomes6.

The main objective of this study was to investigate the potential of Lycium barbarum polysaccharide (LBP) and the tumor cell lysate (TCL) of endothelial progenitor cells (EPC-TCL) as a combined treatment to enhance DC activation. Both LBP and EPC-TCL have been reported to possess immunomodulatory properties, yet the synergistic effects on DC activation and underlying molecular mechanisms remain largely unexplored7,8. A comprehensive methodology that included flow cytometry, the enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), Western blot analysis, and in vivo tumor models was employed to elucidate the effects of LBP and EPC-TCL on DC activation and the subsequent impact on T cell responses and anti-tumor efficacy. In addition, the effects of LBP + EPC-TCL on the expression of key activation markers of DCs, T cell proliferation in response to modified DCs, and activation of relevant signaling pathways were assessed. The results of this study provide insights into the molecular mechanisms employed by LBP and EPC-TCL to enhance DC activation and T cell responses, ultimately contributing to the development of novel cancer immunotherapy strategies to effectively harness the power of the immune system against tumors. Moreover, these findings address critical gaps in the current understanding of DC activation and present a promising avenue to improve immunotherapeutic approaches for cancer treatment.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

Lewis lung carcinoma (LLC) cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Gibco®; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; HyClone Laboratories, Inc., Logan. UT, USA), 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco®), and 2 mM L-glutamine (Gibco®) at 37 °C under an atmosphere of 5% CO2/95% air. The medium was replaced every 2–3 days. At approximately 80% confluence, the LLC cells were trypsinized with 0.25% trypsin-ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid solution (Gibco®) for subculture.

Isolation and culture of primary DCs

DCs were isolated from the bone marrow of C57BL/6 mice (age, 8–12 weeks) by density gradient centrifugation. After euthanizing the mice in accordance with ethical guidelines, the bone marrow of the femurs and tibias was flushed with Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium. The collected cells were resuspended in complete RPMI 1640 medium containing 10% FBS, 100 ng/mL mouse granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor, and 20 ng/mL interleukin (IL)-4 (PeproTech, Inc., Cranbury, NJ, USA) to promote DC differentiation at 37 °C under an atmosphere of 5% CO2/95% air for 7 days, with half of the medium replaced every 3 days.

Isolation and culture of T cells

Spleens from C57BL/6 mice were harvested and mechanically dissociated to obtain single-cell suspensions. Red blood cells were lysed using ammonium-chloride-potassium lysis buffer (Gibco®), and splenocytes were washed with RPMI 1640 medium. For T cell isolation, splenocytes were subjected to negative selection using a CD3+ T cell isolation kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. Isolated T cells were cultured in complete RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 2 mM l-glutamine, then stimulated with antibodies against CD3 and CD28 (eBioscience, San Diego, CA, USA) for 48 h at 37 °C prior to further analysis.

Flow cytometry

Flow cytometry was performed to analyze the expression levels of the surface markers of DCs and T cells. Harvested DCs were stained with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies against cluster of differentiation (CD)40 (fluorescein isothiocyanate), CD80 (phycoerythrin), CD86 (allophycocyanin), and major histocompatibility complex class I protein (MHC-I) (PerCP-Cyanine5.5) (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA). The cells were incubated with antibodies for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark, washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 2% FBS, and analyzed using a flow cytometer (LSRFortessa™; BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). The isolated T cells were stained with antibodies against CD69 (phycoerythrin) and PD-1 (allophycocyanin) (BioLegend) under similar conditions as for the DCs. Flow cytometry was performed to quantify the proportion of activated T cells. Data were processed using FlowJo software (Tree Star, Inc., Ashland, OR, USA).

T cell proliferation assay

To assess proliferation, T cells were labeled with carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester (CFSE) using a commercial kit (InvivoGen, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, T cells (1 × 107/mL) were resuspended in PBS and incubated with 5 µM CFSE for 5 min at 37 °C. The reaction was stopped by adding an equal volume of FBS. Labeled T cells were then washed twice with RPMI 1640 medium and co-cultured with activated DCs at a ratio of 5:1 for 72 h. The dilution of CFSE was confirmed by flow cytometry to evaluate the proliferation of T cells.

ELISA

The secretion of cytokines and chemokines from DCs and tumor cells was quantified using commercial ELISA kits (eBioscience) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocols. Supernatants from DC and LLC cell cultures were collected at 24 h post-treatment. The concentrations of IL-6, IL-12, CCL3, and CCL9 in DC supernatants, as well as VEGF and FGF in LLC cell supernatants, were quantified at an absorbance of 450 nm using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Transwell assay

To create a more physiologically relevant environment for cell migration, the membrane of the Transwell insert was pre-coated with Matrigel (Corning, Inc., Corning, NY, USA). Specifically, Matrigel was diluted to an appropriate concentration in cold serum-free RPMI 1640 medium (50 µl) and applied to the upper chamber, then allowed to solidify at 37 °C for 30 min. Subsequently, LLC cells (1 × 10⁵) were seeded into the upper chamber, while complete culture medium was added to the lower chamber. The Transwell apparatus was then incubated at 37 °C under an atmosphere of 5% CO2/95% air for 24 h. Following incubation, non-invasive LLC cells remaining in the upper chamber were gently removed with a cotton swab, allowing for collection of invasive cells that migrated to the lower chamber. To evaluate cell viability, the invasive cells were stained with crystal violet and counted under a microscope.

Angiogenesis

To evaluate the angiogenic potential of the conditioned media, human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were plated on Matrigel (Corning, Inc.) in the wells of 96-well plates and treated with conditioned media from either tumor cells or activated DCs for 6 h. Tube formation was visualized using an inverted microscope and the number of branch points was quantified using ImageJ software (https://imagej.net/ij/).

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release assay

Cytotoxicity was evaluated using a LDH release assay. Conditioned media from co-cultures of T cells and LLC cells were collected, and LDH activity was measured using an LDH cytotoxicity detection kit (Promega Corporation, Madison, WI, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was measured at 490 nm, and the percentage of cytotoxicity was calculated based on the formula: % cytotoxicity = [(experimental LDH release − baseline LDH release)/(maximum LDH release − baseline LDH release)] × 100%.

Western blot analysis

Cells were lysed in radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined using a bicinchoninic acid protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Equal amounts of protein (30 µg) were separated by electrophoresis and electroblotted onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (EMD Millipore Corporation, Billerica, MA, USA), which were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in tris-buffered saline/Tween 20 for 1 h and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against ERK1/2, JNK, P38, STING, TBK1, IRF3, and related phosphorylated forms (Cell Signaling Technology, Inc., Danvers, MA, USA) at appropriate dilutions. For Western blotting, membranes were cut to the appropriate molecular-weight regions prior to antibody hybridisation to enable probing of different targets. Subsequently, the membranes were washed and incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence detection system (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and densitometric analysis was performed using ImageJ software.

Animal experiments

C57BL/6 mice (male, 6–8 weeks old, 18–22 g) were purchased from Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). This study is reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org), ensuring rigorous standards for the design, conduct, and reporting of animal experiments. All animal experiments were conducted under approved protocols. Anesthesia was induced with intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (40 mg/kg), and euthanasia was performed by CO₂ inhalation, in accordance with institutional ethical guidelines. C57BL/6 mice bearing LLC tumors were randomly divided into five groups: PBS control (CON), TCL, EPC-TCL, and LBP + EPC-TCL. DCs obtained from each treatment group were reinfused into the tail vein of the mice every other day for a total of five injections. Tumor dimensions were measured weekly using calipers, and tumor volumes were calculated using the formula: volume = (length × width²)/2. The survival of mice was monitored, and Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated to illustrate differences among treatment groups.

Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) assay

Tumor tissues from LLC-bearing mice were harvested, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and cut into 5 μm-thick sections, which were assessed using a TUNEL apoptosis detection kit (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Tissue sections were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (Sigma-Aldrich Corporation, St. Louis, MO, USA) to visualize the cell nuclei. Apoptotic cells were identified as TUNEL-positive and quantified by counting positive cells per field by fluorescence microscopy.

Immunohistochemical analysis

Immunohistochemical staining of formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded tumor tissue sections was performed to assess expression of Ki67 and CD34. Sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated, followed by antigen retrieval using citrate buffer (pH 6.0) in a microwave. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched using 3% hydrogen peroxide. Sections were blocked with 5% goat serum (Vector Laboratories, Inc., Newark, CA, USA) for 1 h at room temperature before incubation with primary antibodies against Ki67 (1:200; Abcam Limited, Cambridge, UK) and CD34 (1:100; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Inc., Dallas, TX, USA) overnight at 4 °C. After washing, the sections were incubated with biotinylated secondary antibodies and developed using a 3,3’-diaminobenzidine substrate kit (Vector Laboratories, Inc.). Slides were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, and mounted. Positive cells in random fields were quantified under a light microscope.

Statistical analysis

All experiments in this study were independently repeated three times. All quantitative data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). The t-test was used to compare differences between two groups, while one-way analysis of variance, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test, was conducted for multiple comparisons of three or more groups. A probability (p) value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Synergistic effects of LBP and EPC-TCL on DC activation

Morphological observations confirmed successful extraction and induction of DCs (Fig. 1A). The results of the Cell Counting Kit-8 assay indicate that the maximum safe concentration of LBP for DCs was 100 µg/mL (Fig. 1B). The DCs were subsequently divided into four distinct treatment groups: CON, TCL, EPC-TCL, and LBP + EPC-TCL. Following treatment, flow cytometry was conducted to evaluate the expression levels of the key activation markers CD40, CD80, CD86, and MHC-I. Statistical analysis revealed that combined treatment with LBP + EPC-TCL significantly enhanced functional activation of DCs as compared to the CON and other treatment groups, while TCL and EPC-TCL also demonstrated notable activation (Fig. 1C, D). To further investigate the functional outcomes of these treatments, supernatants of the treated DC cultures were collected. ELISAs were used to quantify levels of the cytokines IL-6 and IL-12, as well as the chemokines CCL3 and CCL9. The results confirmed the flow cytometry findings, indicating that LBP + EPC-TCL treatment significantly induced production of these immunoregulatory molecules as compared to the other treatments (Fig. 1E, F). These results suggest that LBP + EPC-TCL effectively promoted DC activation and cytokine secretion, underscoring the potential of this combinatory therapy to enhance immunotherapeutic strategies.

DC-mediated enhancement of T cell activation and proliferation by LBP + EPC-TCL

DCs were divided into four distinct treatment groups (CON, TCL, EPC-TCL, and LBP + EPC-TCL) and co-cultured with T cells. To track proliferation, T cells were stained with CFSE. After 5 days, flow cytometry revealed that LBP + EPC-TCL significantly enhanced T cell proliferation (Fig. 2A, B) and DCs induced by LBP + EPC-TCL significantly promoted upregulation the T cell activation marker CD69 (Fig. 2C), while expression of the T cell exhaustion marker PD-1 was suppressed (Fig. 2D). Collectively, these results suggest that LBP + EPC-TCL not only boosts T cell proliferation but also enhances T cell activation, while mitigating exhaustion signals, highlighting the potential of this combinatory approach to improve immune responses.

DCs treated with LBP and EPC-TCL augmented tumor suppression by T cells

To explore the activation status of T cells by DCs treated under different conditions, DCs were divided into four distinct treatment groups (CON, TCL, EPC-TCL, and LBP + EPC-TCL) and co-cultured with T cells to evaluate the effects on T cell functionality. The induced T cells were co-cultured with mouse LLC cells. The Transwell chamber assay revealed that T cells co-cultured with DCs treated with LBP + EPC-TCL significantly reduced the invasive capability of LLC cells (Fig. 3A). In addition, the tube formation assay demonstrated that T cells co-cultured with LBP + EPC-TCL-treated DCs significantly inhibited the angiogenic potential of LLC cells (Fig. 3B). The LDH assay confirmed that the T cells could effectively kill LLC cells following co-culture with DCs treated with LBP + EPC-TCL (Fig. 3C). Furthermore, the ELISA results indicated that T cells co-cultured with LBP + EPC-TCL-treated DCs significantly decreased secretion of the pro-angiogenic factors VEGF and bFGF by LLC cells (Fig. 3D, E). Collectively, these findings highlight the potential of LBP and EPC-TCL-modified DCs to enhance T cell-mediated anti-tumor activities against lung carcinoma.

LBP and EPC-TCL enhanced MAPK and STING pathway activation in DCs

In this exploration, we delved into the activation mechanisms of DCs prompted by the synergistic effects of LBP in conjunction with EPC-TCL to scrutinize the activation states of the MAPK and STING signaling pathways. The experimental cohorts comprised DCs subjected to CON, TCL, EPC-TCL, and LBP + EPC-TCL. Western blot analyses illuminated that amalgamated treatment with LBP and EPC-TCL significantly elevated the phosphorylation levels of the ERK1/2, JNK, and P38 proteins within the MAPK signaling cascade (Fig. 4A, B). Moreover, we discerned pronounced augmentation in phosphorylation of the STING, TBK1, and IRF3 proteins, pivotal constituents of the STING signaling pathway (Fig. 4C, D). These revelations imply that LBP, when administered alongside EPC-TCL, not only invigorates the MAPK signaling pathway but also robustly stimulates the STING pathway, thereby promoting profound activation of DCs. This dual activation through both signaling pathways may illuminate the immunomodulatory ramifications of LBP and EPC-TCL within the realm of DC biology.

Suppression of tumor growth by DCs treated with LBP and EPC-TCL

To investigate the anti-tumor efficacy of modified DCs, a mouse axillary tumor model was established using LLC cells. DCs were subsequently allocated into five treatment groups (PBS, CON, TCL, EPC-TCL, and LBP + EPC-TCL) and intravenously injected via the tail vein. Over a period of 5 weeks, DCs treated with LBP + EPC-TCL significantly inhibited tumor growth, as demonstrated by the reduced tumor volume (Fig. 5A, B) and tumor weight (Fig. 5C). At the experimental endpoint, tumor diameters ranged from 0.56 to 1.32 cm, as measured with calipers, and did not exceed the maximum size permitted by institutional ethical guidelines. Histological analysis (TUNEL immunofluorescence assay) of the tumor tissue revealed that DCs treated with LBP + EPC-TCL markedly enhanced apoptosis of tumor cells as compared to the other groups (Fig. 5D). Furthermore, immunohistochemical staining of KI67 and CD34 indicated that this treatment effectively suppressed tumor cell proliferation and intra-tumoral angiogenesis (Fig. 5E). These findings collectively underscore the potent anti-tumor effects of LBP + EPC-TCL on modified DCs, highlighting their potential in cancer immunotherapy.

Discussion

Lung cancer, which remains a leading cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality globally, is characterized by a complex interplay of genetic, environmental, and immunological factors that contribute to disease development and progression9,10. The tumor microenvironment is particularly significant, as it encompasses various immune cells that can either promote tumor growth or facilitate anti-tumor immunity. Recent research has highlighted the potential of immunotherapy as a promising treatment strategy for lung cancer, aiming to enhance the immune response against tumor cells11,12. However, the effectiveness of immunotherapy is often limited by the immunosuppressive nature of the tumor microenvironment and the ability of cancer cells to evade immune detection, underscoring the need for innovative therapeutic approaches that can effectively activate and modulate immune responses13.

In this context, our study investigated the synergistic effects of LBP and EPC-TCL on DC activation: a crucial step to initiate robust T cell responses against tumors14. We aimed to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms and signaling pathways involved in DC activation, particularly focusing on the MAPK and STING pathways15–17. Our findings reveal that LBP + EPC-TCL significantly enhanced DC activation, leading to increased T cell proliferation and improved anti-tumor efficacy. This research contributes not only to the understanding of DC biology but also proposes a novel therapeutic strategy that holds promise to enhance cancer immunotherapy outcomes.

The significant enhancement of DC activation through the synergistic combination of LBP and EPC-TCL provides a substantial contribution to our understanding of the underlying molecular mechanisms of immune activation. Specifically, our findings elucidate how elevated expression levels of co-stimulatory molecules, such as CD40, CD80, CD86, and MHC-I, indicate robust maturation of DCs18. This is further supported by increased production of the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6 and IL-12, alongside chemokines CCL3 and CCL919. Activation of the MAPK and STING signaling pathways has emerged as a crucial aspect of this process, confirming the role of these pathways in mediating DC activation and enhancing immune responses20,21. Such insights provide a theoretical foundation for the development of novel immunotherapeutic strategies aimed at targeting DCs to improve cancer treatment outcomes.

In addition to the molecular mechanisms, the results of this study also highlight the importance of gene function and cellular behavior in the context of T cell activation22. The observed reduction of PD-1 expression and upregulation of CD69 in T cells co-cultured with LBP and EPC-TCL-modified DCs suggest enhanced T cell activation and reduced exhaustion23,24. This dual effect underscores the potential of employing combinatory treatments to amplify anti-tumor immunity. Moreover, the implications of these findings extend to our understanding of how DCs can be harnessed to shape T cell responses in tumor environments, thus paving the way for innovative approaches in cancer immunotherapy that could leverage this synergy to improve therapeutic efficacy.

Furthermore, in vivo evaluation of tumor growth inhibition by DCs co-treated with LBP and EPC-TCL reinforces the potential of this approach in cancer therapy. The significant reduction in tumor volume and weight observed in the mouse tumor model validates the efficacy of this combinatory treatment. It is essential to further explore the mechanisms underlying the potent anti-tumor effects mediated by activated T cells, including the capacity to suppress tumor invasiveness and angiogenesis. These findings suggest that the enhanced anti-tumor activity of T cells may arise from robust activation of DCs, which then primes T cells for an effective immune response against tumors. Future studies should aim to optimize treatment protocols and investigate the clinical relevance of these findings in diverse cancer types25,26.

This study is subject to certain limitations that warrant consideration for future research. First, the absence of clinical validation restricts the applicability of these findings to human populations, as the results derived from mouse models may not fully encapsulate the complexities of human tumor biology and immune responses. Furthermore, the reliance on a single tumor model may not adequately represent the heterogeneity of various cancer types, which could influence the efficacy of the combinatory treatment. Additionally, the sample size utilized in this research may limit generalizability of the outcomes, necessitating further studies with larger cohorts and diverse tumor settings to establish the robustness of these findings in a clinical context.

In conclusion, LBP + EPC-TCL has demonstrated significant potential to enhance DC activation, T cell responses, and anti-tumor efficacy. These findings highlight the promise of innovative immunotherapeutic strategies that target DCs, suggesting that further exploration of this combinatory approach could pave the way for improved cancer treatment outcomes. Future studies should aim to address the limitations identified in this research while also investigating the translational applicability of these findings in clinical settings.
