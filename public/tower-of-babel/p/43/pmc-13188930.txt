Initial testing of a mobile app behavioral pain coping intervention for patients with cancer: Results of a pilot randomized controlled trial
Authors: Sarah A. Kelleher, Hannah M. Fisher, Natalie Chou, Allison K. Diachina, Joseph G. Winger, Hope E. Uronis, Francis J. Keefe, Tamara J. Somers
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objectives:Behavioral management is recommended for cancer pain, yet not well integrated into clinical practice. PainPac is an asynchronous mobile application that delivers behavioral pain coping strategies. We evaluated PainPac’s feasibility, acceptability, and impact on pain compared to a videoconference-delivered protocol (PCST-Video).Methods:Participants (N = 62) were adults with a stage I-IV diagnosis of colorectal cancer within the past two years and pain ≥4 on 0-10 scale from an academic cancer center. Participants were randomized 1:1 to PainPac or PCST-Video. Feasibility (accrual [N = 60/15 months]; attrition at the post-treatment assessment [primary endpoint; <25%]; adherence to all assessments and intervention modules/sessions [>75%]), burden (number of modules/sessions completed; time from baseline to completion of first module/session; time to complete all sessions/modules), engagement (skills practice and PainPac log in >3 times/week), and acceptability (≥80% of participants reporting ≥75% satisfaction on Client Satisfaction Questionnaire) were assessed. Changes in pain and pain-related outcomes were explored at baseline (A1), post-treatment (A2), and 1-month post-treatment (A3).Results:Feasibility exceeded prespecified benchmarks (N = 62/10 months; 92% A2 retention). Ninety percent of PainPac participants completed all modules (Msessions=3.77, SD=.82); 80% of PCST-Video participants completed all sessions (Msessions=3.37, SD = 1.33). Participants moved from A1 to completion of first module/session in 1 day for PainPac and 12 days for PCST-Video. Number of days to complete all modules/sessions was M = 23.57 (SD = 5.31) for PainPac and M = 27.63 (SD = 6.70) for PCST-Video. PainPac engagement exceeded prespecified benchmarks (M = 9.22 app logins per week). Satisfaction was high with PainPac (89.3% ≥24; M = 28.11, SD = 3.74) and PCST-Video (92.6% ≥24; M = 28.93, SD = 3.57). Pain outcomes changed in the expected direction for both conditions, improving from A1 to A3.Discussion:PainPac is a promising approach for improving cancer pain management. Technology-based behavioral interventions can decrease barriers and improve intervention reach, scalability, and sustainability in clinical practice. Larger, well-powered randomized trials are needed to test PainPac’s efficacy.Clinical Trial Registration Number:ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT05686122

Introduction

Pain is common, interfering, and distressing for patients with cancer.1,2 A meta-analysis reported that over half of patients with cancer have pain, with over one third of patients endorsing moderate to severe pain.3 Pain is common across cancer types and the symptom burden for patients with colorectal cancer is particularly concerning. Colorectal cancer is one of the most frequently diagnosed cancers, common across age groups and sex, and the second leading cause of cancer mortality.4,5 Research has shown patients with colorectal and other cancers reporting pain are more likely to experience psychological distress, physical disability and symptoms (e.g. fatigue), lower quality of life, and increased financial difficulty than patients without pain.6-11

Decades of work have shown behavioral pain interventions are efficacious for pain in patients with cancer.12-14 Teaching patients cognitive and behavioral strategies (i.e. relaxation, activity pacing, cognitive restructuring) for managing psychosocial factors related to pain can lead to decreased pain and psychological distress, and increased pain coping self-efficacy.15 These interventions have traditionally been delivered in-person at a medical center by highly trained behavioral pain specialists over 8–12 weekly sessions. This type of intervention protocol presents patient, provider, and system access barriers (e.g. time, cost, availability, burden). Our group and others have found brief (3–6 sessions), therapist-led, cognitive-behavioral pain interventions delivered in-person enhance the ability of patients with chronic disease to manage their pain, leading to improvements in pain and other important pain-related outcomes.9,16-21 Still, access barriers remain, including time and financial cost to the patient, limited availability of behavioral pain specialists, and a high need for such services that place a significant strain on resources within the healthcare system.

To mitigate barriers, we have designed and tested behavioral pain interventions that use mobile health (mHealth) technology for patients with diverse cancers and pain.22-26 In a large trial (N = 178), we showed a 4-session behavioral cancer pain intervention delivered with homebased videoconferencing was just as efficacious as a protocol delivered in-person at the medical center.25 Still, videoconference-delivered protocols retain some access barriers due to the therapist-led delivery model and resources required (e.g. training and ongoing fidelity monitoring of behavioral pain specialists, scheduled appointments). More innovation is needed to improve access to behavioral symptom management for the greater than 22 million cancer survivors expected over the next 10 years.27,28

One potential solution is delivering behavioral pain interventions via a mobile application that is readily downloadable to a patient’s smartphone device. There is a maturing body of literature on the use of mobile apps developed specifically for supportive care throughout the cancer experience.29-31 Several systematic reviews32-34 have found that web- and mobile app-based symptom management interventions for patients with cancer can reduce pain and overall physical symptom burden. Most existing mobile apps have been examined in breast cancer populations and primarily aimed at improving medication adherence and delivering health educational content (e.g. nutrition, physical activity, smoking/alcohol use) and/or a focus on disease self-management.34-41 While pain management may be a singular component of some mobile apps, there are few multi-module, mobile app protocols for patients with cancer that solely focus on efficacious cognitive-behavioral pain management strategies.29-31,42-44

This paper examines a standalone behavioral cancer pain mobile application we developed (PainPac) that includes four modules of cognitive-behavioral therapy-based pain management techniques. PainPac rectifies shortcomings of existing pain apps as it is closely linked to scientifically proven concepts for pain management, uses empirically tested strategies for pain management, and has been developed by behavioral pain experts. We describe a pilot randomized controlled trial whose goal was, 1) to test whether PainPac is feasible (primary aim; target accrual N = 60 within 15 months, <25% attrition at the post-treatment assessment (primary endpoint), >75% adherence to all three assessments and four intervention modules/sessions), low burden (number of modules/sessions completed, time from baseline assessment to completion of first module/session, and time to complete all sessions/modules), engaging (PainPac logins and coping skills practice >3 times per week), and acceptable (≥80% of participants reporting at least 75% satisfaction with their respective intervention protocol); 2) to examine PainPac’s impact on pain (primary outcome), emotional distress, pain self-efficacy, and quality of life; and 3) to gather qualitative post-treatment data on patients’ perspectives on PainPac to update and refine PainPac for a future large-scale randomized clinical effectiveness trial. We hypothesized that PainPac would meet or exceed pre-specified benchmarks for feasibility, burden, engagement, and acceptability, and describe outcomes for PainPac and a videoconferencing protocol (PCST-Video).

Methods

Participants

This study was approved by the Duke University Institutional Review Board (IRB#: Pro00112397) and registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05686122). Participants were recruited from the Duke Cancer Institute. Study enrollment occurred between September 2023 and August 2024. Participants (N = 62) were/had: 1) ≥18 years, 2) a diagnosis of stages I–IV colorectal cancer within the past 2 years, 3) self-reported pain ≥4 on 0–10 scale, and 4) English-speaking. Exclusion criteria included: 1) cognitive impairment, 2) brain metastases, 3) severe psychiatric condition, or 4) behavioral pain management engagement (<6 months).

The preliminary support for PainPac has been informed by our team’s work with patients with diverse cancers (e.g. breast, prostate, lung, colorectal, advanced stage cancer, stem cell transplant patients).25,45-48 Due to the pilot nature of the current work, we enrolled a homogenous opportunity sample of colorectal cancer patients based on our team’s prior work in the gastrointestinal cancer clinics and the fact that colorectal cancer impacts men and women, and spans a large age range, making it an appropriate population for this early work. Future PainPac work will involve efficacy testing in patients with diverse cancer types.

Procedures

Potentially eligible patients were identified by electronic medical record screening and mailed a letter introducing the study and including an opt-out option. Patients completed telephone screening. Interested and eligible patients completed informed consent and an electronic baseline assessment including demographic, clinical, and psychosocial questionnaires. Participants were then randomized (1:1) to either: 1) a mobile app, behavioral cancer pain intervention (PainPac) or 2) a therapist-led telehealth behavioral cancer pain intervention (PCST-Video). All participants were re-assessed 5–8 wk (i.e. post-treatment) and 9–12 wk (i.e. 1-month follow-up) following randomization. Randomization and electronic assessments were administered by staff not involved in intervention delivery.

Intervention conditions

PainPac protocol

PainPac is based on 1) Pain Coping Skills Training (PCST) and 2) similar cognitive-behavioral interventions designed and tested by our group19,22,25,49 and others.29-31 PainPac uses Social Cognitive Theory50 to promote behaviors to improve self-efficacy for pain management and, in turn, improve pain and pain-related outcomes.51 PainPac is an asynchronous app that used a modular format to introduce a new skill(s) across 4 modules: 1) progressive muscle relaxation (PMR), 2) guided imagery and brief relaxation, 3) activity rest cycling and pleasant activity planning, and 4) cognitive restructuring and coping statements. Patients were prompted to complete one module per week for 4 wk. Each module required patients to watch a didactic video (10–12 min) including text, video, and audio presentation of skill rationale and expected outcomes, skill modeling, and tips for daily use. Table 1 shows PainPac content and features.

PainPac was designed to help patients integrate learned pain coping skills into their daily lives through interactive components such as symptom tracking and text messaging. Participants rated their average pain, fatigue, and stress (0–10 scale) across the day on 3 days per week. These symptom ratings were averaged to obtain an aggregate symptom rating that was categorized as low (0–3), moderate (4–7), or high (8–10). Based on this aggregate symptom rating, participants were sent text messages with content ranging from general encouragement for lower symptom ratings (e.g. “Keep doing what you’re doing”) to more directive recommendations for skill practice and app engagement (e.g. “Take a few moments to practice relaxation in your PainPac app”) for higher symptom ratings. Tailored text messages were developed by behavioral pain clinicians on the study team. Reminders to view module content were sent to participants via text and push notifications, and a progress tracker reinforced engagement. PainPac was developed and hosted by Greenlight Health Data Solutions Inc. and downloaded for free by participants through iOS or Android on a smartphone device.

PCST-Video protocol

Participants randomized to PCST-Video received a four-session, telehealth behavioral cancer pain intervention. Sessions were delivered one-on-one by a doctoral-level clinical psychologist specializing in behavioral cancer pain management. Telehealth sessions were scheduled weekly for 45–60 min. Manualized PCST-Video content was based on PCST and matched the PainPac modules, and included interactive, dynamic discussion of coping skills’ rationale, use, and participants’ home practice experience.

Measures

Demographic and clinical variables

Demographic (e.g. age, race, ethnicity, partner status, education, income) and clinical (e.g. cancer type and stage, surgeries and treatments received, medication use) variables were collected by participant self-report and confirmed through medical record review.

All feasibility, burden, engagement, and acceptability metrics were chosen based on pilot trial guidelines52 for RCTs of behavioral interventions and extensive prior work developing and pilot testing behavioral interventions.22,24,45,49,53-57

Feasibility (primary aim)

Feasibility benchmarks included: 1) target accrual (N = 60) within 15 months; 2) <25% attrition, defined as the percentage of participants who completed the post-treatment assessment (primary endpoint); and 3) >75% of participants adhering to all 3 assessments (baseline, post-treatment, 1-month follow-up) and all 4 intervention modules/sessions.

Burden

Benchmarks for burden included: 1) number of modules/sessions completed; 2) time from baseline assessment to completion of first module/session; and 3) time to complete all sessions/modules.

Engagement

Process data to assess participants’ engagement with PainPac (e.g. number of logins) were downloaded from the app, and cleaned and tabulated in Excel using time and date stamps. Participants’ self-reported skill use (i.e. “On how many days in the past week have you used any skill from the PainPac program?”) was assessed at post-treatment and 1-month follow-up. Engagement benchmarks for logins and self-reported skill use were >3 times per week.

Acceptability

The 8-item Client Satisfaction Questionnaire (CSQ)58 was administered post-treatment to measure participants’ satisfaction with their intervention protocol. Responses were rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“Quite Dissatisfied”) to 4 (“Very Satisfied”) and summed to obtain a total score with higher scores indicating higher satisfaction. The benchmark for intervention satisfaction was ≥80% of PainPac and PCST-Video participants reporting a total CSQ score of at least 24 (i.e. 75% satisfaction).

At post-treatment, PainPac participants also completed Atkinson’s59 30-item tool on perceived attributes (e.g. advantages, usability, innovation) of mHealth interventions. Participants rated their level of agreement with statements such as “Using PainPac made learning about health a better experience than I would have otherwise.” Items were rated on a 6-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“Strongly Disagree”) to 6 (“Strongly Agree”) and then averaged, with higher scores indicating a more positive assessment of PainPac.

Outcome measures (appendix)

The primary outcome of pain severity was assessed with the Brief Pain Inventory (BPI).60 Secondary outcomes and measures included: 1) pain interference (BPI60); 2) emotional distress (Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System [PROMIS] Depression-Short Form61) 3) self-efficacy for pain management (self-efficacy subscale of Chronic Pain Self-Efficacy Scale62) 4) self-efficacy for using new technology to receive a behavioral cancer pain intervention (Computer Self-Efficacy Scale63) and 5) quality of life (The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention’s Health-Related Quality of Life Measure64).

PainPac preferences, barriers, and facilitators

At post-treatment, PainPac participants were asked 28 items modeled from our past work23,49 assessing preferences regarding referral to use the PainPac app and content within the app, as well as potential barriers (e.g. no internet, time commitment) and facilitators (e.g. more user instruction) for individuals using PainPac in the future. Items were answered on an 11-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (“Low Preference/Barrier/Facilitator”) to 10 (“High Preference/Barrier/Facilitator”).

Qualitative data

At post-treatment, a clinical research coordinator (AD) conducted semi-structured exit interviews (20-30 min) with PainPac participants (N = 17) via telephone. The interview guide (Appendix) was based on the Health Belief Model65-67 and queried participants about: 1) perceived importance of cancer pain management (e.g. “How important is it for you to learn ways to manage your cancer pain?”); 2) perceived benefits and barriers to using the PainPac app; 3) cues to action (e.g. “What would make it easier to use the PainPac app?”); 4) self-efficacy (e.g. “How confident do you feel that you could use the PainPac app to manage your cancer pain?”); and 5) cultural tailoring of the PainPac app. Semi-structured interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim to ensure all participant feedback was documented.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics were calculated using SPSS (Version 29). Evaluation of intervention conditions was based on a conservative approach focused on assessing descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations). Consistent with guidelines for early stage studies,52,68,69 we did not power for hypothesis or efficacy testing. Rather, sample size was informed by established guidelines for behavioral intervention development and early feasibility testing,52,70,71 and past similar studies23 that suggest 30 participants per condition is sufficient for determining feasibility and acceptability, and readiness for a future definitive trial.

Qualitative analysis of interview transcriptions was conducted using rapid analysis.72,73 Three coders (Clinical Research Coordinators: NC, AD; Clinical Psychologist: HF) were assigned so each transcription was reviewed by two coders to summarize participant responses within a coding framework that directly followed the domains outlined by the semi-structured interview guide (see Appendix). Coders independently completed assigned summarizations in separate Excel sheets (Rows = participant ID, Columns = domain; Cells = summarization of participant feedback). Summarizations for each participant from both coders were placed side-by-side in a new Excel sheet, and coders met via teleconference to discuss initial summaries and rectify discrepancies. A final Excel sheet was created including all agreed upon summarizations. Our team has previously used this approach to analyze qualitative focus group and semi-structured interview data.45,74

Results

Participant characteristics

Demographic and clinical characteristics are reported in Table 2. Participants were on average 58.37 years, 61% were female, and the majority were White (66.1%). Most participants (77.4%) had advanced (Stage III and IV) disease.

Feasibility

Accrual

A total of 199 patients were screened for eligibility (n = 82 eligible, n = 117 ineligible). Primary reason for ineligibility was self-reported pain severity less than 4 (n = 95). Of the 82 eligible patients, 62 participants were enrolled from 9/28/2023 to 8/14/2024 (10 months, 16 days). Nine of the screened patients declined consent (i.e. coping well, time commitment, no longer interested) and eleven were lost to follow-up. Accrual exceeded our benchmark of N = 60 in 15 months. Of the 62 participants enrolled, 60 were randomized to PainPac (n = 30) and PCST-Video (n = 30). Two participants were lost to follow-up after consent (see Figure 1 for CONSORT).

Attrition

Of the 60 participants randomized, 55 (91.7%) completed the post-treatment assessment (primary endpoint). Five participants (8.3%) did not complete the post-treatment assessment (2 PainPac, 3 PCST-Video); this met our benchmark of <25% attrition at the post-treatment timepoint.

Adherence

Adherence to individual baseline (n = 60, 96.8%), post-treatment (n = 55, 88.7%), and 1-month follow-up (n = 57, 91.9%) assessments was high. Fifty-five participants completed all three assessments (88.7%). Adherence to PainPac modules was also high, with 27 of 30 participants completing all 4 modules (90.0%). Twenty-four participants in the PCST-Video condition completed all 4 sessions (80.0%). Overall, adherence to all assessments and modules/sessions exceeded our benchmark (i.e. 75% completion).

Burden

The average number of sessions completed across PainPac was M = 3.77 (SD=.82) and PCST-Video was M = 3.37 (SD = 1.33). The number of days from the baseline assessment to completion of the first module of PainPac was M = 1.32 (SD = 2.28) and the first session of PCST-Video was M = 12.25 (SD = 10.92). The number of days to complete all modules for PainPac was M = 23.57 (SD = 5.31) and all sessions for PCST-Video was M = 27.63 (SD = 6.70).

Engagement

The average number of PainPac logins per week was 9.22 (SD = 4.20). This is well above our benchmark of >3 logins per week. At post-treatment, number of days with use of “any skill” in the past week for PainPac (M = 5.21, SD = 1.91) and PCST-Video (M = 5.52, SD = 1.63) was above our benchmark of >3 days per week. Results were similar at 1-month follow-up (Table 3). Number of days with use of individual skills ranged from 4 to 5 days at post-treatment and 3-4 days at 1-month follow-up.

Acceptability

Eighty-nine percent (89.3%) of PainPac participants reported an acceptability score ≥24 (M = 28.11, SD = 3.74). For PCST-Video, 92.6% of participants reported an acceptability score ≥24 (M = 28.93, SD = 3.57). Frequencies for PainPac and PCST-Video were well above the benchmark of ≥80% of participants reporting a total score of ≥24.

Intervention outcome patterns

Descriptive statistics for outcome variables show favorable trends for each condition and at each assessment timepoint (Table 4). Overall, mean levels of pain (severity, interference), emotional distress, pain self-efficacy, and quality of life changed in the expected direction, improving from the baseline assessment to the 1-month follow-up. These results should be considered exploratory since no formal statistical analyses were conducted, consistent with guidelines for early-stage pilot studies.52,68,69

Post-treatment perspectives on PainPac

Comfort with PainPac and acceptability

At post-treatment, PainPac participants reported high computer self-efficacy (M = 83.71, SD = 19.82; Possible Range: 0–100). Acceptability of the PainPac protocol and app (i.e. advantage, simplicity, innovation), specifically, was also very positive (M = 4.82, SD = 0.52, Possible Range = 1–6). Participants largely agreed the PainPac protocol was user-friendly and included content that was informative and engaging.

Facilitators, barriers, and preferences

Figure 2 shows the distribution and average score for items assessing participants’ impressions of facilitators and barriers to PainPac use, as well as preferences for content and referral to use PainPac. The highest rated facilitator was video instruction on how to use the PainPac app. The highest rated barrier was lack of internet access. The lowest rated barrier was lack of in-person interaction with a clinician. Content preference items indicated that participants were in favor of keeping PainPac content as currently designed, though would prefer more audio relaxation recordings. Participants most preferred being referred to use the PainPac app through a direct text message to their phone.

Exit interviews

Participants described managing pain as “really important,” yet challenging even after treatment (Domain: Perceived Importance). PainPac helped participants learn techniques for pain management and relaxation and participants appreciated that the app held them “accountable each day” (Domain: Perceived Benefit/General). The self-directed format was particularly valued, as it allowed participants to fit skills modules into their daily routine without the constraints of scheduled sessions (Domain: Perceived Benefit/Most Liked). Many participants reported the relaxation techniques (e.g. progressive muscle relaxation, guided imagery) were practical and effective, and also described the activity-rest cycle as a particularly helpful reminder to “take it easy [and] not push it” (Domain: Perceived Benefit/Most Liked). Some reported confusion regarding required tasks in the app and when to complete assigned activities (Domain: Perceived Benefit/Least Liked). While most participants encountered few barriers, some noted that limited internet access or personal and work schedules could pose challenges (Domain: Perceived Barriers). Suggestions for improvement included adding more audio relaxation recordings, providing additional examples of skills use, and enhancing the organization of app tasks (Domain: Suggested Changes). Discussion

This was a randomized pilot trial assessing the feasibility, burden, engagement, and acceptability of a standalone mobile app behavioral cancer pain intervention (PainPac) for patients with colorectal cancer. PainPac exceeded all pre-specified feasibility benchmarks. We met our accrual goal approximately five months ahead of schedule, reflecting a high level of interest in the study, and appropriate recruitment procedures. Retention at post-intervention was excellent in the PainPac condition (28/30; 93.3%) and the PCST-Video condition (27/30; 90.0%). Adherence to study assessments and modules/sessions across both conditions was robust and exceeded our benchmark. Adherence to PainPac modules was strong wherein 27 out of 30 participants (90%; vs. 80% of PCST-Video participants) completed all 4 modules. These data suggest participants were able to complete self-paced modules in the PainPac app. Indeed, participants verbalized as such during exit interviews, noting they appreciated the self-paced nature of PainPac that allowed them to complete modules at their convenience.

Importantly, this is one of the first studies to examine two mobile pain management strategies. Past work has compared different types of in-person interventions20,75-77 and newer work has compared mobile to in-person interventions.23-25 We have previously shown that a videoconference-delivered, behavioral pain intervention resulted in less attrition and better adherence than an in-person protocol.25 The current findings extend this work, suggesting that an app-delivered, theory-based pain intervention may have low attrition and high adherence. Given the trend toward greater use of telehealth in pain management, comparing telehealth intervention strategies is an important next step in behavioral pain intervention research.

Measures of burden revealed that PainPac participants began the first module one day after enrollment and completed all modules in 27 days; PCST-Video participants began their intervention 10 days following enrollment and completed all modules in 31 days. This is noteworthy and indicates that with an app-based cancer pain intervention like PainPac, patients with cancer experiencing pain may be able to begin behavioral pain treatment rapidly while the need is urgent. Similarly, patients with cancer using PainPac may complete the protocol in a short timeframe, potentially experiencing benefits earlier. Although promising, these findings are preliminary due to the pilot design and require confirmation in a larger, fully powered trial. Importantly, PainPac participants completed one module per week, matching PCST-Video weekly sessions and closely approximating real-world clinical practice. Interestingly, our findings regarding barriers to treatment indicated that most participants did not view the lack of a clinician (e.g. pain psychologist, oncologist) as something that prevented them from using PainPac. These data suggest interfacing with a clinician in-person and/or via videoconference may not be a high priority for some patients when engaging with supportive care services. A mobile app intervention allows for increased privacy for patients and furthermore may reduce feelings of stigma, factors that may contribute to this finding.

In general, PainPac participants reported high satisfaction with the mobile app intervention that exceed the prespecified benchmark; mean satisfaction was also high for the PCST-Video condition. Engagement with the PainPac app was good; participants logged into the app approximately 9 times per week and reported using a skill from the PainPac app approximately 5 times per week. Engagement data observed here was in line with or higher than that observed in other studies testing mobile app-based supportive interventions for patients with chronic disease.78-80 Participants shared they especially found the activity-rest cycle and relaxation techniques (e.g. progressive muscle relaxation, guided imagery) to be helpful. Quantitative and qualitative findings suggest participants found the PainPac app user-friendly and included content that was informative and engaging, despite not receiving the more detailed and interactive therapist-delivered protocol. Notably, all feasibility, burden, acceptability, and engagement benchmarks were met, confirming our first hypothesis.

Overall, changes in outcomes were favorable for both conditions. Conclusions regarding patient-reported outcomes, however, need to tempered by the fact that this was a small, feasibility trial not powered to detect significant differences between conditions across time. Participant heterogeneity on sociodemographic and/or medical variables may influence mean symptom levels, and this effect can be amplified in modest samples. A future, larger-scale trial might stratify randomization on relevant variables (e.g. cancer stage) to ensure conditions are balanced on key outcome variables at baseline. Also of note is that pain severity increased slightly from baseline to post-intervention in the PainPac condition, though reduced to its lowest level at the 1-month post-intervention assessment. Pre-post changes patterns presented here are exploratory and should be interpreted with caution given the pilot nature of this work.

Results should be considered in light of limitations. Consistent with guidelines for early-stage studies, our sample size was small and we did not power for hypothesis or efficacy testing; this is a limitation of pilot work. The next step efficacy trial will include a larger sample that provides adequate power for testing group differences across time and detecting minimum clinically important difference in pain and pain-related outcomes. Our sample was recruited from a large academic medical center (61% female; 85% some college or more) and may not be representative of individuals with limited access to technology. The heterogeneity of our sample—with participants spanning stages I–IV and various points in the treatment trajectory—introduces variability in symptom burden, treatment context, and capacity for intervention engagement. Differences in treatment intensity, recovery demands, and symptom profiles could affect both adherence and perceived usefulness of the PainPac and PCST-Video interventions. This heterogeneity reflects real-world clinical diversity, which may enhance ecological validity but complicates interpretation in a small pilot sample. Average time since diagnosis was about one year; it is possible that enrolling participants closer in time to their diagnosis may yield a sample with worse pain and more acute need for a behavioral cancer pain intervention. In exit interviews, several participants mentioned PainPac would have been particularly useful during active treatment. Alternatively, future work might examine pain interference as the primary outcome. Pain interference may be more likely to change in a sample of patients with mixed cancer types where pain type and severity vary greatly depending on cancer stage and treatment (e.g. surgery, chemotherapy).

Implications for psychosocial providers and future directions

There is a time sensitive and critical need to optimize the impact of modern technology-based behavioral pain interventions to reach the growing number of patients with cancer in the US. Numerous clinical trials show behavioral pain management approaches are efficacious. Yet, data indicate most patients are not aware, not offered, and/or cannot access efficacious pain management strategies. PainPac, a standalone mobile app pain intervention, offers an innovative solution and method that could reduce strain on the healthcare system by increasing availability of behavioral pain management strategies to patients with cancer. Furthermore, exciting opportunities exist for additional innovation and automation, including use of artificial intelligence. For example, PainPac could be connected to the electronic medical record system and when a patient’s reported pain score reaches a predefined threshold, the automated system could trigger sending PainPac directly to the patient, further decreasing burden on the healthcare system and its providers.

Our findings support a next step in this program of research, specifically, a large-scale trial that is well-powered to assess the efficacy of PainPac compared to active and non-active control groups. Qualitative data from exit interviews will be used to refine PainPac for future testing (e.g. more audio relaxation recordings and examples of skill use, improved flexibility and organization of app tasks, cultural tailoring). If future efficacy testing is successful, our efforts will shift to effectiveness and implementation testing. A standalone behavioral cancer pain mobile app that uses efficacious cognitive and behavioral pain management strategies will dramatically increase access to behavioral pain management for patients with cancer. Specifically, a standalone app eliminates the need for a therapist, is always available to the patient, and is easily accessible on a smartphone or tablet. To address barriers such as lack of smartphone or limited data plan, PainPac could be moved to a free YouTube platform to further increase accessibility in rural and/or medically underserved regions located some distance from well-resourced, tertiary cancer centers.
