Functional ability profiles in beta-propeller protein-associated neurodegeneration (BPAN)
Authors: Francesco Gavazzi, Samuel R. Pierce, Vanessa Smith, Eric Yang, Julie Skorup, Kristy Pucci, Emma Kotes, Allan M. Glanzman, Stacy V. Cusack, Todd Levy, Holly Dubbs, Emma Wiener, Sarah Woidill, Joseph Vithayathil, Abbas Jawad, Nivedita Thakur, Laura A. Adang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Beta-propeller Protein-Associated Neurodegeneration (BPAN) is a rare neurodevelopmental degenerative disorder caused by pathogenic variants in WDR45 leading to brain iron accumulation. Its rarity and complex clinical course make it difficult to select appropriate clinical outcome assessments (COAs). This study evaluates established COAs for feasibility and effectiveness in capturing BPAN’s functional ability profiles exploring cognitive, motor, and behavioral features.Methods:We performed an observational study. Children were recruited as part of the Myelin Disorders Biorepository Project at the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia. We administered the Gross Motor Function Measure-88 (GMFM-88), the Leiter International Performance Scale (Leiter-3), and the Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scale (VABS-3). A Rasch validated version of the GMFM-88, the GMFM-66, was derived from the GMFM-88 data. Descriptive statistics and Spearman’s rank correlation were used to compare assessments. Statistical analyses were performed to compare performance across cohorts and assess correlations, with significance defined as p < 0.05.Results:Fifty-three individuals (43 females, 10 males) with molecularly confirmed BPAN participated. The VABS-3 (n = 53) showed a decline in adaptive skills over time, with significant differences between Communication and Socialization Domain performance (Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s correction p < 0.0001). GMFM-88 assessments (n = 32) showed a median performance of 33.4 %. Patient participation/behavior affected data completeness. The more limited GMFM-66 correlated better with the VABS-3 Gross Motor subdomain than the GMFM-88 (r = 0.94, r = 0.73, respectively). The Leiter-3 (n = 36) demonstrated significant non-verbal cognitive impairment, although there were behavioral challenges which impacted implementation. Longitudinal VABS-3 data revealed a median − 2.9 points/year decline in Adaptive Behavior Composite scores, reflecting progressive functional loss.Discussion:This study highlights key considerations for selecting COAs in BPAN. The panel of COAs should accommodate the behavioral challenges associated with BPAN that can limit participant compliance with testing. The abbreviated GMFM-66 was a more reliable tool to capture motor skills. Similarly, behavioral difficulties impacted Leiter-3 performance, which demonstrated general impairment in non-verbal cognitive skills. The VABS-3 effectively tracked adaptive function decline, demonstrating feasibility for longitudinal monitoring. Future studies should expand cohort size, refine assessment strategies, and align measures with disease progression to optimize clinical trial readiness.

Introduction

Beta-propeller Protein-Associated Neurodegeneration (BPAN), also known as neurodegeneration with brain iron accumulation type 5 (NBIA5), is a rare neurodevelopmental and neurodegenerative condition characterized by iron buildup in the brain over time. [1,2] The disorder arises from pathogenic variants in the WDR45 gene on the X chromosome, which codes for a beta-propeller protein that plays a role in essential cell functions like iron metabolism, autophagy, and endoplasmic reticulum stability. [3–5]

As described predominantly in females, BPAN typically progresses in two stages: an initial phase marked by developmental delays, followed in adolescence or early adulthood by a period of regression. [1,6] In early childhood, those affected by BPAN often experience developmental delays, seizures, sleep issues, and behaviors commonly associated with autism spectrum disorder. [1,7] In the later stage, young adults lose milestones and develop severe neurodegenerative symptoms like parkinsonism, dystonia, and dementia. [8,9] During early childhood, brain imaging reveals atypical myelination with progression to the characteristic patterns of iron accumulation in the basal ganglia and substantia nigra and atrophy later in life. [10] [11,12] BPAN was previously considered prenatally lethal in males, but affected males both with and without somatic mosaicism have now been reported, expanding the clinical spectrum. [13,14]

At present, there are no FDA-approved treatments to modify the progression of BPAN. Fundamental to future trial design, however, is establishing fit-for-purpose Clinical Outcome Assessments (COAs) with demonstrated measurement properties, including feasibility, reliability, and sensitivity to change in this population. A robust characterization of these assessments is essential to ensure that future patient-centric clinical trials can meaningfully capture disease progression and treatment effects, as per the FDA Breakthrough Therapy guidelines. [15–20]

This study aims to evaluate established COAs that can effectively capture BPAN’s cognitive, motor, and behavioral aspects, and support better understanding of BPAN functional trajectory.

Methods

Study design

We performed an observational study.

Standard protocol approvals, registrations, and patient consents

Participants were recruited as part of the Myelin Disorders Biorepository Project (MDBP; IRB 14–011236) at the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia. Written informed consent (consent for research) was obtained from all participants (or their legally authorized representatives if unable to provide consent because of age or diminished capacities in the study. Enrollment occurred between 03/2021 and 12/2024.

Cohort

Fifty three individuals (43 female, 10 male) with a molecular and clinical diagnosis of BPAN attributable to pathogenic variants in WDR45 and available medical records were included.

Clinical outcome measures selection

The selection of outcome measures to evaluate the psychometric function of individuals with BPAN was conducted through a process of face validity by a group of experts on outcome measures and BPAN (L.A., S.R.P, F.G, A.M.G., A.J., N.T., K.P.). The minimum agreement required for approval was set at 80 %. Consensus was achieved upon the following tools: the Gross Motor Function Measure-88 (GMFM-88), the Leiter International Scale, third edition (Leiter-3), and the Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scale, third edition (VABS-3).

Materials

All prospective outcome measures were assessed by providers trained in their administration.

The Leiter International Scales, 3rd edition (Leiter-3) is a non-verbal Performance Outcome measure (PerfO) [21] that evaluates non-verbal intelligence quotient (IQ), Attention and Memory, Cognitive/Social functioning, and emotional regulation abilities of an individual from age 3 years onwards. The Cognitive Composite domain was administered in this study, which is expressed as Non-Verbal IQ (NVIQ), with a testing range from 30 to 170 (Average 100, Standard Deviation 15). The average range of performance is between 85 and 115. Scores between 70 and 84 fall within the borderline range. Mild intellectual disability corresponds to scores between 50 and 69, while moderate intellectual disability is indicated by scores between 35 and 49. Severe intellectual disability is defined by scores below 35. The standard administration of the Leiter-3 takes between 30 and 60 min. In this population, breaks were incorporated as needed between subtests due to concerns about endurance and attention in the BPAN population. Failed administration was defined as the inability of an individual to complete any item of any of the mandatory cognitive composite subtests. The floor was defined as the minimum score achievable in the assessment (Non-verbal IQ = 30).

The Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scale, 3rd edition (VABS-3) is an Observer-Reported Outcome (ObsRO) widely used to assess adaptive behavior in children with neurodivergent development, cerebral palsy, and autism spectrum disorder, and is administrable to all ages. [22–24] It comprises eleven subdomains, organized into four domains: Communication domain (Receptive, Expressive, Written); Daily Living Skills domain (Personal, Domestic, Community), Socialization domain (Interpersonal Relationships, Play, and Leisure, Coping Skills), and Motor Skills domain (Gross Motor and Fine Motor). Communication, Daily Living Skills, and Socialization domains provide an Adaptive Behavior Composite (ABC). Subdomain raw scores are normalized by age, providing a v-score (range 1–24), while each domain and the ABC can be measured as Standard Scores (median 100, range 20–140) and percentiles. The floor was defined as the minimum score achievable in the assessment (Standard Score [SS] = 20). This assessment was administered to the caregivers of the enrolled individuals in the context of in-person or remote research assessments. The time of administration ranged from 30 to 60 min.

The Gross Motor Function Measure-88 (GMFM-88) is a performative Clinician-Reported Outcome (ClinRO) designed to capture changes in gross motor function over time or with intervention in children with CP. [25] It is comprised of 88 items (score range 0–3) organized in 5 dimensions: A (Lying and Rolling), B (Sitting), C (Crawling and Kneeling), D (Standing), E (Walking, Running, and Jumping). Each of the five dimensions is calculated as a percentage, and the average of all percentage scores for the five dimensions is calculated as the overall GMFM-88 total score (range 0–100 %). The allotted time for administration of the GMFM-88 was 60 min. The GMFM-88 was administered to all subjects enrolled in the study. Both in-person and remote evaluation approaches were used. [26] All subjects were barefoot without bracing for the assessments. The GMFM item set scores were abstracted from the existing data and the Gross Motor Ability Estimator software (GMAE-3) was used to estimate the GMFM-66 score with associated confidence interval based on the existing item set data. GMFM-66 is a 66-item subset of the original 88 items identified through Rasch analysis. [25] The GMFM-66IS uses 3 entry items related to sitting, walking and stair climbing to determine which of four different item sets (Item Sets 1–4) to enter into the algorithm that calculates an estimate of the GMFM-66 representing the child’s level of functioning. [27]

Data extraction and collection

Psychometric assessments (GMFM-88, Leiter-3, VABS-3) were performed by a trained provider and documented in paper assessment forms. The forms were filed in binders and scanned into the study’s electronic database (REDCap software). [28,29] Demographic information, including sex, race, ethnicity, and date of birth, was extracted from the medical documentation available. Age at disease onset was collected based on available medical records, which align with recent guidelines on natural history studies in rare disorders. [30]

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to describe the population demographics and performance at the different assessments. GraphPad Prism software (version 10) and R (Version 4.4.0) were used for all statistical analysis. Descriptive statistics (including median and interquartile range [IQR]) and Spearman’s rank correlation [31] were used to compare assessments. Wilcoxon rank sum tests and Kruskal-Wallis tests with correction with Dunn’s multiple comparison tests were used to compare test performance between subcohorts and subtest domains, respectively. Significance was defined as p-value <0.05.

Results

Cohort description

Overall, 53 individuals participated in outcome assessments (43 females and 10 males). Of these, 43 subjects (81.1 %) self-identified as Caucasian/White, 3 (5.7 %) as Black/African American, and 7 (13.2 %) identified as belonging to other racial or ethnic groups. Not all subjects completed all assessments (sensitivity analysis is shown in Supplemental Table 1). Genotype details are included in Supplemental Table 2.

Outcome measures performance (cross-sectional analysis)

Leiter-3

The Leiter-3 was used to capture non-verbal intelligence. Overall, the Leiter-3 was administered to 36 subjects (29 females, 80.6 %; 7 males, 19.4 %), of whom 31 completed it. The five unsuccessful administrations occurred because the participants were unable to complete any subtest items due to difficulty understanding the task requirements. As part of the Leiter-3 administration, behaviors that could impact performance were recorded. This included stereotypies, perseverative behaviors, and odd verbalizations or sounds. Additionally, limited attention and task persistence were observed. The median age at the time of assessment was 6.58 years (IQR 8.49). Median NVIQ performance was 36.5 SS (IQR 23.25; Table 1, Fig. 1). Overall, N = 2 (6.5 %) participants scored within the average range of performance, and N = 2 (6.5 %) demonstrated borderline performance. Mild intellectual disability was observed in N = 6 (19.3 %) subjects, moderate intellectual disability in N = 10 (32.2 %) subjects, and severe intellectual disability in N = 11 (35.5 %) participants. Out of the 31 successful administrations, floor performance was observed in N = 3 participants (9.7 %). There was no significant difference by sex (Mann-Whitney test, p = 0.11). There was no correlation between performance and age at the assessment time (Spearman’s rank Correlation, r = −0.28, p = 0.12).

VABS-3

The VABS-3 was used to capture the family’s perspective of skills and function. Overall, caregivers of 53 subjects completed the VABS-3 (43 females, 81.1 %; 10 males, 18.9 %, Fig. 2A; Table 2; Supplemental Table 3). Median age at the time of first assessment was 6.35 years (IQR 8.5). Median performance at the ABC was 44 (IQR 26), with no sex difference (Wilcoxon rank sum test, p = 0.23). Overall, N = 4 (7.6 %) subjects performed within −1 and − 2 SD from normalized data, N = 13 (24.5 %) subjects performed within −2 and − 3 SD, N = 16 (30.2 %) subjects performed within −3 and − 4 SD, N = 14 (26.4 %) subjects performed within −4 and − 5 SD, and N = 6 (11.3 %) subjects performed below −5SD. Floor performance was observed in 3 participants (5.7 %). When considering domain performance, we observed a significant difference between Communication and Socialization [respective medians 28 (IQR 28.5) and 54 (IQR 24.5); Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s correction, p < 0.0001)] and between Communication and Daily Living Skills [respective medians 28 (IQR 28.5) and 47 (IQR 29); Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s correction, p = 0.03)]. No difference in performance was observed between male and female participants (Wilcoxon rank sum test, Communication: p = 0.2821, Socialization: p = 0.0921, DLS: p = 0.4857, Motor: p = 0.1794, ABC: p = 0.2280, Table 3). When considering subdomain performance (v-score), expressive language was more strongly impacted compared to Socialization subdomains (Interpersonal, Play and Leisure, Coping Skills, Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s correction, p < 0.0001; Fig. 2B).

GMFM-88

The GMFM-88 was used to capture gross motor performance (Fig. 3, Table 3). Overall, 32 subjects attempted the assessment (25 females, 78.1 %; 7 males, 21.9 %). The median age at the time of assessment was 6.07 years (IQR 8.08). We observed a median performance at the GMFM-88 of 33.4 % (IQR 30.8, Fig. 3A). There was no difference in performance between sexes in total GMFM-88 score or individual dimensions (Wilcoxon rank sum test, p = 0.25), except for dimension A (Wilcoxon rank sum test, p = 0.03). There was no correlation between performance and age at the assessment time (Spearman’s rank Correlation, r = 0.09, p = 0.64).

Compliance was recorded during the administration, with frequent occurrences of items not tested due to behavioral limitations or lack of adherence to instructions (Supplemental Table 4). Due to concerns about compliance, a score using a reduced number of items was calculated (GMFM-66 and GMFM-66IS, Fig. 3B). The median performance on the GMFM-66 was 62.35 (IQR 17.42), while the median performance at the GMFM-66IS was 62.2 (17.65). We then explored the correlation between GMFM-88, GMFM-66, and GMFM-66IS with the VABS-3 Gross Motor subdomain (expressed as raw score, Fig. 4A–C). A total of 25 concurrent VABS-3/GMFM-88 encounters (defined as assessments completed within one month) were included in this analysis. The strong correlation between the VABS-3 and the GMFM-88 (R = 0.73, p < 0.0001) was improved with the Rasch validated versions: GMFM-66 (R = 0.94, p < 0.0001) and GMFM-66IS (0.93, p < 0.0001).

Longitudinal administration of the VABS-3

The mean time between longitudinal administrations of the VABS-3 was observed as 0.91 years (between encounters 1–2; n = 31), 0.91 years (between encounters 2–3; n = 20), 0.56 years (between encounters 3–4; n = 5), and 0.69 years (between encounters 4–5; n = 2). We observed a median change of −2.9 points/year (IQR −6.45) in the overall cohort by ABC standard scores (Fig. 5A). Individual performance is shown in Fig. 5B.

Discussion

As we anticipate future clinical trials for BPAN, it is critical to identify feasible COAs that capture motor performance and parental insights into the priorities of affected individuals and their caregivers. [15–20,32] In this study, we sought to identify COAs capable of capturing function and change in individuals with BPAN. Because of the profound behavior and attention issues of the BPAN population, it is critical to identify reliable measures that reflect real-world function. This work also helps to inform our understanding of the natural history of BPAN neurologic function.

In this pilot study, we focused on three key metrics: gross motor function (GMFM-88, a clinician-administered battery of tasks), non-verbal IQ (Leiter-3, a clinician-administered performance measure), and an objective measure of adaptive function via parental assessment (VABS-3). Notably, our cohort included ten males whose performance was comparable to that of females, suggesting an expanded, milder phenotype in males compared to previously published cases. We hypothesize that this is due to mosaicism, although this could not be consistently confirmed with the available clinical testing results. Although we found that non-verbal IQ and communication skills are profoundly affected in BPAN, there was a small subset of individuals who performed within the expected range for their age.

This study also underscores the challenges of administering tests in populations with a strong behavioral component. We encountered barriers with both provider-administered measures (GMFM-88 and Leiter-3). While the GMFM-88 could be administered in all participants, notable data was missing due to inconsistent compliance. We hypothesize that this was due to challenges associated with the ability to understand and follow instructions and a lack of desire to perform specific tasks and for the participants to perform tasks below their level of function, such as movements when lying on their backs or stomachs. This was most evident in Dimension C (crawling and kneeling), where children frequently refused to participate, whereas better performance was observed in Dimension D (walking). Such behavioral limitations complicate the interpretability of GMFM-88 results due to a potential discrepancy between functional capacity and typical performance.

Post-hoc calculations using the GMFM-66 and GMFM-66IS [33] (specifically designed to account for uncompleted items) yielded more reliable estimates of gross motor function. These tools appeared less sensitive to compliance variability and demonstrated stronger convergence with the VABS-3 than the GMFM-88, suggesting better alignment with caregiver-reported functional ability. The GMFM-66 item sets are tailored to the child’s current functional level and eliminate items much below the child’s functional level increasing compliance with testable items. These findings underscore the importance of adapting assessment tools to specific populations’ behavioral characteristics to enhance interpretability and utility.

The selection of the Leiter-3 as a performance-based outcome (PerfO) measure was driven by the limited verbal abilities observed in a significant proportion of this population. [7] While most participants were able to complete the assessment, many children exhibited severe behavioral challenges during the testing period, including limited attention span and behaviors commonly associated with autism (such as stereotypies, perseverative behaviors, and odd verbalizations or sounds). These behavioral characteristics may have influenced performance outcomes and, similarly, would affect cognitive function in the home and school. Results indicated a general impairment in non-verbal cognitive skills within the cohort, with a small subset performing within age-appropriate norms. Future studies should assess the tool’s broader applicability, identify subgroups for whom it is most suitable, and clarify the apparent lack of sex-related performance differences, which may reflect the smaller male sample size linked to disease pathogenesis. Further work is also needed to characterize BPAN’s behavioral features better, both to aid differential diagnosis and to inform appropriate medical and rehabilitative care, as well as to track their evolution over time.

We also captured the caregiver’s assessment of critical adaptive skills through the VABS. [34,35] Unlike provider-administered assessments, the VABS-3 relies on caregiver-reported observations, making it independent of direct task performance and potentially more reflective of the typical performance of this population. Furthermore, its capacity for remote administration ensures accessibility for participants facing barriers such as geographic distance, disease severity, or economic constraints. In the BPAN population, parental reports indicated a decline in normalized performance of adaptive skills during early childhood, reflecting limited acquisition of new abilities rather than an actual loss of previously acquired ones, thereby broadening the gap between observed and age-expected skills. Notably, the magnitude of this decline compared to age-matched peers aligns with the minimally clinically significant differences established in autism spectrum disorder (VABS-II). [36] Although the findings in the BPAN population represent a small cohort, they underscore the promise of the VABS-3 as a longitudinal outcome measure. Expanding this research to a larger cohort will be essential to confirm these observations and further evaluate the tool’s potential utility in future clinical trials. This also suggests an early regression in BPAN before the well-known late phase degeneration as previously described.

This study provides insights into the function and change in BPAN, although several limitations should be noted. The small sample size limits the generalizability of findings, and the cohort’s demographic homogeneity potentially restricts the representation of BPAN’s diverse clinical presentation. The VABS-3, though advantageous for its caregiver-reported format, is subject to observer bias, which could over or underestimate adaptive behaviors. Comparative studies in similar populations suggest a strong correlation between the VABS and provider-administered measures.

As we move towards patient-centric clinical trial development, it is critical to recognize the importance of capturing the family’s lived experience, balanced with the feasibility of multi-hour assessments in a population with severe motor, behavioral, and cognitive impairments. The progressive decline in adaptive behavior scores observed with the VABS-3 correlates with known disease trajectories, underscoring its potential as a measure for following disease progression. Future research can address these limitations by expanding the cohort size, exploring alternative tools like the GMFM-66 and GMFM-66IS, and extending follow-up periods to improve the characterization of disease trajectories. Determining the fit-for-purpose of these tools in the context of clinical trials will require future natural history studies and alignment with existing and future studies to identify additional health concepts in BPAN. [32]
