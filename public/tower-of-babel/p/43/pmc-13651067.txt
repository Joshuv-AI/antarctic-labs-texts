Motivation, capacity and opportunities for cervical cancer home-based vaginal self-sampling in Réunion Island: a qualitative study
Authors: Dolorès Pourette, Amber Cripps, Amir Hassine, Eric Opigez, Marc Bardou, Alexandre Dumont
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCervical cancer remains a major public health concern. In France, women in deprived areas face a 62% higher risk, yet nearly 40% of eligible women are under-screened. While HPV self-sampling can double or triple screening rates, these rarely exceed 30%. Prior studies have overlooked women experiencing both insufficient screening and socioeconomic disadvantage. The RESISTE trial addressed these gaps using combined quantitative and qualitative methods. In this work, we evaluated behavioural strategies targeting both women’s capacity to perform self-sampling and their perception of screening opportunities, in Réunion Island.Method35 semi-structured interviews were conducted among women who had received a RESISTE self-sampling kit: 16 were respondents who had returned the kit and 13 were non-respondents. These participants came from across the island and were of varying age groups. We also interviewed health professionals who had been contacted by women during the trial (6). Following an inductive thematic analysis, the Behaviour Change Wheel was used to identify factors relating to capability, motivation and external opportunities.ResultsAfter receiving the kit, several women underwent screening either by self-sampling or by visiting a health professional. Ease of use, convenience and privacy were key factors in self-sampling uptake. Receiving the kit at home creates a sense of being targeted which reinforces the perceived relevance of screening. Relatives generally had a facilitating effect, encouraging self-sampling and follow-up. An HPV positive result was a strong motivator to undergo a follow-up smear test.Nevertheless, a lack of prior knowledge of vaginal self-sampling led to a lack of trust in the programme amongst women. This and the perceived complexity of the procedure were the main demotivating factors. The financial incentive contributed to doubts regarding authenticity. In addition, the health professionals contacted rarely encouraged women to self-sample.ConclusionsWomen appreciated receiving an HPV vaginal self-sampling kit at home. Their motivation and capability could be improved by increasing prior communication – to both women and health professionals – on self-sampling in general, and more specifically on reliability, sample preservation and ease of use. Background

Cervical cancer remains a major public health concern worldwide. With an estimated 604,127 new cases and 341,931 deaths, it ranks fourth both in incidence and mortality among cancers affecting women globally [1, 2]. Despite progress in prevention, the World Health Organization (WHO) has highlighted the persistence of a marked socioeconomic gradient—even in high-income countries—as one of the main challenges to cervical cancer control [1–4].

In France, cervical cancer is the form of cancer most strongly linked to socioeconomic deprivation. Women living in the most deprived areas (Q5) have a 62% higher risk of developing cervical cancer compared with those living in the least deprived areas (Q1) (95% CI: 1.40–1.88) [5]. Furthermore, screening coverage remains suboptimal: 4 in 10 women—equivalent to nearly 7 million individuals—are either not screened or under-screened [6]. Screening participation is, therefore, a major factor in these social inequities relating to cervical cancer outcomes.

Such disparities are partly explained by reduced access to healthcare and preventive services among women of low socioeconomic status, leading to lower screening uptake and later cancer detection [7]. Cultural and social barriers have also been identified, including difficulties navigating the healthcare system, arranging medical appointments or engaging with healthcare providers [8]. Together, these structural and psychosocial barriers contribute to the persistence of gaps in screening participation.

Since 2019, HPV testing has been introduced in France as the primary screening test for women aged 30–65 years. This shift toward HPV-based screening has created new opportunities for alternative collection strategies, particularly self-sampling. The French National Authority for Health has highlighted self-sampling as a relevant approach for under-screened women, offering an alternative to clinician-collected samples, and has recommended further studies to demonstrate its effectiveness [8–10].

Previous studies have shown that making self-sampling available to under-screened women can lead to a twofold or threefold increase in screening uptake. However, participation rates rarely exceeded 30% [9–12], suggesting persistent barriers despite the availability of this solution. Moreover, very few studies have specifically focused on women affected by both low screening participation and social deprivation [10]. It is important to note that these interventional studies largely lacked a qualitative component capable of providing valuable insights into the viewpoints of women and healthcare providers to help contextualise and better understand intervention outcomes.

To address these gaps, the RESISTE trial was conducted using a combination of quantitative and qualitative methods to assess the effectiveness of self-sampling incentives and tackle behavioural barriers to screening participation [13]. The study was implemented across four French departments, including two overseas territories.

The French overseas territories experience some of the widest health inequities in France. Among them, Réunion Island was selected as a key study location. In this territory, cervical cancer incidence and mortality are approximately twice as high as in mainland France (13.9 and 4.8 per 100,000 vs. 6.1 and 1.7, respectively), despite a slightly higher average screening rate compared with the national average [6]. This epidemiological paradox suggests that screening coverage alone does not fully reflect underlying inequalities in prevention and early detection.

One possible explanation lies in the magnitude of social inequities observed both between and within territories. A recent survey conducted among 1,000 screening-eligible women identified low socioeconomic status, limited awareness of cervical cancer screening, low literacy levels and being a migrant from the neighbouring Indian Ocean islands as key non-participation factors [14]. In addition, 36% of Réunion Island’s population lives below the poverty threshold—2.5 times higher than in mainland France (14%)—and 23% report difficulties reading, writing or understanding simple texts. These inequities are further compounded by geographic isolation, particularly for individuals living in remote mountainous areas [15]. Households in rural and disadvantaged urban areas are those most subject to poverty, whereas the west and the north of the island are generally less economically disadvantaged [16].

Being a former French colony, the population is composed of people who arrived on the island during colonial times (of African, Indian, or French descent), and people who have arrived more recently (from Europe - mostly France - or from other Indian Ocean islands - mostly the Comoros, Madagascar, and Mauritius). Furthermore, several publications suggest that women living in Reunion Island have imperfect control over their sexuality and more generally over the prevention of associated risks such as unwanted pregnancies or sexually transmitted diseases [17, 18].

In this context, self-sampling appears to be a particularly relevant strategy. A survey conducted among 200 women on Réunion reported an acceptability rate of 80% [11]. It was therefore highly pertinent to evaluate the RESISTE intervention in this location.

Following a trial, the study was conducted among women who had received the self-sampling kit, whether they had returned the kit or not. The objective was to provide a comprehensive overview of the acceptability of this innovative approach from the women’s perspective. From a public health standpoint, these insights are essential to contextualise key results of the intervention in terms of effectiveness and to better understand the factors underpinning its potential success or failure.

Study context

In January 2022, 2,564 HPV self-sampling kits were mailed to women living in deprived areas of Réunion Island. Return methods were randomised between 4 trial groups: (1) Return by post; (2) Return by post with the financial incentive of €20 paid upon receipt of the collected sample; (3) Return to a healthcare professional of the woman’s choice (gynaecologist, general practitioner, midwife or pharmacist); (4) Return to a healthcare professional of the woman’s choice with a €20 incentive.

The kit contained a detailed presentation of the study, illustrated instructions, a self-sampling tube, a zip-lock bag and a pre-stamped return envelope. Prior to the trial, an introductory letter had been sent out to targeted women and to all health professionals in the department who might come into contact with them as part of the study (gynaecologists, general practitioners, midwives and pharmacists). Women who returned a sample received their HPV results by text message and by post within 3 months. These documents are presented in additional files [see Additional files 1, 2, 3, 4, 5].

This qualitative study was conducted after completion of the RESISTE trial [12] and included participants from Réunion Island regardless of whether they had returned the self-sampling kit.

Purpose of the study

Using an integrated behavioural–anthropological framework, this study aimed to identify the barriers and drivers in the adoption of a new screening method—home self-sampling—by closely examining the reasons why women did or did not perform self-sampling (motivations), as well as their feelings, difficulties and questions on receiving or using the kit, and when they received the results.

Methods

Study design

This study was based on two key scientific implementation frameworks. Firstly, we used the social anthropological framework to capture the positive and negative experiences and feelings of women and healthcare workers, without “false familiarity” or “false evidence” [19]. Given the sensitivity of the subject (health, cancer, self-testing, gynaecological health) and the study population (women from disadvantaged backgrounds who do not necessarily speak French and are not necessarily literate), conducting in-depth semi-structured interviews seemed to be the most appropriate methodology. This method gave women the freedom to express themselves in an open interview, without being limited by a closed questionnaire. The interviews were based on general and follow-up questions that allowed the women to express their feelings and experiences without fear of judgement. They also made it possible to gather precise information about the women’s living conditions, their biographical and health history, their past experiences, etc. These elements are important in understanding the factors that motivate and demotivate women, and their feelings towards a new screening solution. The interviews were conducted by two anthropologists accustomed to conducting interviews on sensitive topics related to women’s health, who were able to conduct the interviews in Reunionese Creole and had good knowledge of the Reunionese context. Secondly, the Behaviour Change Wheel (BCW) was selected to guide the qualitative analysis because it provides a comprehensive and systematic framework for understanding and influencing health-related behaviours, particularly through its central COM-B model, which conceptualises behaviour as the interaction between capability, opportunity and motivation. In the context of a home-based self-sampling method to increase participation in cervical cancer screening, the BCW is especially suited to identifying multilevel determinants of screening uptake and to mapping these determinants onto appropriate intervention functions and implementation strategies. This theoretical structure enables a rigorous assessment of both feasibility (practical capability and access) and acceptability (motivational and social influences), thereby aligning closely with the study’s objectives.

Context

Fieldwork was conducted on Réunion Island between January and November 2022 as part of the RESISTE trial.

Study population

The study targeted disadvantaged women on Réunion Island who were not up-to-date with their CC screening and who had received an HPV-HR vaginal self-sampling kit under the RESISTE trial.

Sample and sampling approach

The RESISTE programme involved a cluster-randomised trial targeting women living in low socioeconomically deprived IRIS areas (categories 4 and 5), IRIS being the smallest geographical unit used for statistics in France). The sampling for this qualitative study aimed to select women of broad-ranging personal characteristics in terms of age group (3 age groups were defined − 30 to 39, 40 to 54, and over 55) and place of residence. For sampling purposes, the island was divided into 6 geographical areas: North, West, South, East, “Les Hauts” (literally “high places”, a term used locally to refer to the central, higher parts of the island, ) and “Cirques” (corries created by the collapse of extinct volcanoes) (Fig. 1). Access to health care is more problematic in the latter two geographical areas, due to their remoteness.

Geographic distribution of study participants

Secondly, women were selected on the basis of the RESISTE trial: their assigned trial group (4 groups); whether or not they returned the kit (responders and non-responders); how long they took to return the kit (‘normal’ response within 2 months, ‘late’ response between 2 and 6 months); and, finally, their HPV test results (negative or positive).

Health professional (HP) sampling targeted the doctors and pharmacists through whom the women had returned their samples.

Data collection process

The women were contacted by telephone (using details provided on the return form for responders and from the Regional Cancer Screening Coordination Centre (RCSCC) for non-responders). We contacted responders with negative HPV results 1 month after sending out their results, to allow time for postal delivery from mainland France. This was the case for both ‘normal’ and ‘late’ responders. Women with positive results were contacted 3 months after their results were posted out to them, to allow them time to see a doctor and undergo additional screening before the interview. Women were considered non-responders if their sample was not received within 6 months after home delivery, estimated at 2 weeks after posting. Most of the women’s interviews (25/29) were conducted in their homes. A few women (4/29) chose to be interviewed by telephone. It was the opposite for HPs: most preferred a telephone interview (5/6).

Anthropologists conducted the semi-structured interviews based on interview guidelines for specific target groups (women, doctors and pharmacists - see Additional Files 6 and 7). Interviews were conducted in French or Reunionese Creole, according to participant preference.

Data analysis

Each interview was then transcribed into French and then coded inductively. First, a thematic analysis, a key component of the social anthropological approach adopted in this study, was used to both compare the participants’ responses and to identify overarching trends and discrepancies [20]. Secondly, to understand the implementation process at the individual level, we used the foundational behaviour science framework COM-B model [15]. This provides a comprehensive framework for understanding behaviour by positing that behavioural change results from the interaction of three essential components: capability, opportunity and motivation. This model guided our assessment of how women engage with the intervention and how they feel about it. By applying COM-B, we explored the capability, motivation and opportunity of women to take up the kit and to navigate referral pathways. This behavioural lens helped identify implementation barriers and facilitators, and inform targeted strategies to support change.

Measures undertaken to ensure quality

The qualitative research was conducted by two expert anthropologists with in-depth knowledge of the Réunion context and the subject. During the course of this research, they spent several months working in the field. In order to triangulate the data, the team presented and discussed the findings with various panels of health professionals specialising in cervical cancer and gynaecological health on Réunion Island. These factors contribute to the credibility of the research. Its reliability is based on rigorous documentation of the various phases of the project, methodological choices and data collection and analysis procedures. Transferability is ensured by a precise and comprehensive description of the study framework, participant characteristics and procedures. Confirmability was achieved through regular debriefings between the two anthropologists and with other team members, as well as with experts on the subject in Réunion Island.

Reflexive work based on discussions between the two anthropologists was carried out throughout the data collection and analysis process in order to limit the influence of their respective subjectivity.

Ethical considerations

Before the interviews, participants were informed of the purpose of the study and of its non-binding and confidential nature. They were provided with an information sheet and a consent form to sign. Interviews were recorded with participant prior consent. To ensure confidentiality, no names were mentioned or recorded at any time during the interviews. All research material was anonymised and password protected.

The only risk identified for participants was the emotional impact of discussing sensitive topics. We ensured that participants felt comfortable choosing not to respond to any questions they found unsettling.

The RESISTE protocol was approved by the “Sud-Ouest et Outre-Mer II” ethics committee on 6 February 2020 and is currently being conducted in accordance with the fourth version of 10 June 2022 - ID-RCB no.: 2020-A0002237 (File 2-20-006 id6698) 2°HPs.

The RESISTE trial registration number is: NCT04312178.

Results

Women study participants

A total of 29 women of various age groups and from all six study areas (see Fig. 1 below) participated.

Women’s socio-economic status varied considerably (see Fig. 2). Most women [15] were unemployed, all of whom had access to benefits and National Health Insurance. Of these women, 10 lived in social housing and 9 considered that they had difficulties “making ends meet” at the end of the month. The other 13 women were employed or retired and had private health insurance. We documented 4 women living with a disability and 3 others caring for a disabled family member.

Women participants’ social status by age

In terms of their origin, most women were born on Réunion Island [21], 2 were born in France, 1 in Madagascar (of Mahoran parents), and the last was from Vietnam.

In terms of how women responded to the trial, 16 were respondents, 8 with negative and 8 with positive HPV results, and 13 were non-respondents. Among the respondents, 10 women returned the sample within 2 months (normal response) and 6 within 2 to 6 months (late response). Of the 13 RESIST HBVSS non-respondents, 5 were screened under other Organised Cervical Cancer Screening (OCCS) services. Indeed, 1 had just been screened when she received the kit, 4 were screened by a health professional after receiving the kit and 1 was waiting for an appointment to be screened. Another non-respondent was not eligible for screening due to a total hysterectomy. Thus, only 6 of the 13 non-respondents to home-based self-sampling can be considered OCCS non-participants.

Health professional study participants

In this study, we interviewed 6 health professionals who collected women’s self-samples and whose contact details were provided to the coordinating centre and those who refused to receive self-samples and whose contact details were provided by women who called the RESISTE team to enquire about the programme.

Capability, opportunity and motivation to perform vaginal self-sampling

Capability: knowledge and practice

More than half of the women doubted their capability to perform the self-sampling correctly. Self-sampling initially seemed complex to several women [13], many of whom (9/13) did not return the kit. The women said the accompanying documents gave the impression that the procedure was “long and complicated”. Some women [5] had to read the letter several times and said that the terminology was “too medical”. Others [3] had a low level of education or were not used to reading “official documents”, while 1 was visually impaired. Most of these women (11/13) decided to put the kit aside until they had more time and only just over half (6/11) later returned the kit (late response).

“When I receive something like this, I put it on the table. But I wait before I go and do it! I have the impression that it’s long, complicated and all that, so I don’t read it, I don’t look at it too much and then when I have really made up my mind, that’s when I go back to it.” (respondent, late returnee, aged 56, tested 1 month earlier).

Conversely, the illustrated instructions were considered clear [19] and user-friendly. However, the procedure remained unclear to some non-respondents who were not used to self-sampling.

“The booklet was well designed for someone who is used to doing it alone, but for someone who is not used to it, it doesn’t work” (non-respondent woman, aged 47, screened 12 years earlier).

More than half of the responders expressed feeling at ease with self-sampling [7]. However, vaginal self-sampling for the first time caused anxiety for both responders and non-responders who doubted their capacity to insert the swab correctly and produce a sample of sufficient quality without hurting themselves [12]. Some women [5] tried to imitate the position for undergoing a smear test believing this would provide a better sample.

“I thought about how the doctor does it… the position and I tried to do it, reading the instructions carefully… I lay on my back, spread my legs apart, and I put the little stick in, turned and turned and looked to see if there was enough secretion on it to make sure it worked!” (Respondent, late return, 38 years old, tested 2 years earlier).

Most women understood that the kit was sent to them as part of the organised cervical cancer screening programme. They considered the self-test to be equivalent to a smear test (the same test but done at home rather than by a professional). Several women [6] confused the test with other forms of self-testing: either a colorectal cancer screening device or a Covid nasal swab. Two other women did not understand how to use the device and thought they had to take it to a healthcare professional, which they didn’t do. Because self-sampling was considered the same as a smear test, several women considered it “too technical” for home use [7]. None of them returned the kit.

Another important factor in women’s capacity to self-sample was finding time at home in an already busy schedule [4].

“When I received the test, and it has to be done, but after work… I put it off, I put it off… it’s not easy. The doctor told me to go to the gynaecologist to have the test done. But it’s really far away and I put it off each time because I’ve got other things to do too. I don’t have time to read too much (non-respondent who made an appointment after receiving the kit, 43 years old, screened 7 years earlier).

Finally, 2 women felt ashamed to carry out self-sampling, or even to consider doing so, one returned the kit, while the other did not do so for this very reason.

Opportunity: external factors

Receiving the kit at home provides a real opportunity to access screening. Several women [5] found the kit more encouraging than a reminder letter. Having the kit “at hand” made screening easier. Others booked an appointment for a cervical smear test, whereas they had not done so after receiving a reminder letter. Some also felt better informed after receiving the kit than after receiving a reminder letter, which encouraged them to be screened.

“If I’d received a letter instead of the kit, it would have gone in the bin”. (Respondent, late returnee, aged 44, tested 8 years earlier)

“I often receive letters at home but you had to go to a gynaecologist to have the sample taken, which I didn’t do (…) I thought it was a good thing to be able to do it myself (…) I said to myself, it’s there anyway, I’ll do it and then I’ll see. (Respondent, age 50, tested 8 years earlier)

Offering multiple options for returning the kit also provides women with an opportunity to access screening. The women interviewed appreciated the postal return option, particularly the pre-stamped return envelopes [10], noting that it was “simple”, “free” and “anonymous”. Ensuring that the sample arrived quickly and safely so that it could be processed rapidly and their results sent out as fast as possible was a key concern. Several women [5] made sure to send off the sample quickly after self-collection. One did not return the kit, for fear the sample would deteriorate before she could post it. Some [5] preferred to return the sample to a health professional (HP) where they felt it would be processed more rapidly.

However, a lack of prior information on self-screening was a strong barrier to screening. Some non-respondents [3] would have participated if they had heard about the programme beforehand, from their GP or on the radio or television. After receiving the kit, 13 women contacted their GP [9] or the RESISTE project [4]. None of the GPs contacted by women participants were aware of the intervention, and most discouraged them from self-testing, having no knowledge of the project or of HPV self-sampling in general.

“She [the doctor] said she didn’t dare! Because now they’ve changed the model (…) Because they’ve done it like the PCR test there, the rod. She said that she’d never seen it before. She said she’d never seen it come to your house.” (respondent who asked a home nurse to take the sample, age 57, not tested for “a long time”).

Most of the women who received such feedback did not self-sample. Some of them were offered a smear test instead and chose this option [3] or asked an HP to perform the test [1].

When the kit had to be returned to a health professional, as was the case for 2 of the 4 randomised trial groups, the pharmacists and GPs contacted by the women (8/9) were not aware of the study and refused to take the sample, which made the programme appear “less credible”.

“The fact that you arrive at the pharmacy and that the pharmacist discovers the scheme and says, ‘What the hell is this?’ And that he’s taken the document to read it and all that, well that makes it a little less credible (respondent, late returnee, 44 years old, tested 8 years earlier).

The vast majority of health professionals contacted during the trial reported having had no prior knowledge of the RESISTE study or, in some cases, of HBVSS more broadly. Health professionals uniformly requested more direct communication, favouring concise, practical guidance on what to do when a woman returns a kit, alongside a short introductory phone call or visit rather than a letter alone. Despite these gaps, all expressed willingness to receive and process kits, though only some pharmacists saw this as a natural extension of their existing involvement in cancer prevention; others were willing to act as a passive relay point but were reluctant to proactively raise the topic with clients.

The women highlighted the influence of health professionals on their decision to self-screen. Several women [6] would have self-sampled if a health professional had reassured them about the device. Following explanations given at the end of their interviews, 5 non-respondents would now like to self-sample. Others (11 respondents and non-respondents) would prefer to be screened by a doctor. Pharmacists emphasised the convenience and accessibility of pharmacies as a trusted first point of contact. One pharmacist felt that returning the kit to a health professional even gave value to the act itself:

“It’s not great just doing the sampling and putting it in a post box, I don’t know, as a patient, that gives less importance to what they’ve done.” (Pharmacist).

Participants appreciated receiving their results by text message and by post. Receiving results by SMS was appreciated for its speed - a delay of several months was mentioned for receiving letters from mainland France. The text of the SMS, “You performed a self-test on (date)…” reassured women that the information was genuine. Some women [3] preferred to receive the results by post which allowed them to decide when to read the letter and provided them with an official document for their records. Ten participants felt that the 3-month wait for results was too long. In terms of social opportunities, women’s relatives and close friends generally encouraged them to screen [18].

Motivating factors

Most of the women interviewed (20 out of 29) appreciated receiving the kit at home. They felt self-sampling at home was more convenient than visiting a doctor [18]. These women mentioned the time gain and not having to wait for a doctor’s appointment.

“Personally, I would never have gone for screening if it hadn’t arrived, this home kit. I wouldn’t have made an appointment to do it because we don’t have time, and personally, it’s true that I’m not concerned about my health.” (Respondent, late returnee, age 44, tested 8 years earlier).

Several [7] also compared their self-sampling experience with conventional screening which they presented as a violation of their privacy, emphasising their discomfort during gynaecological examination. They also described a sense of urgency and importance on receiving the kit at home. The fact that it came from mainland France and bore the Dijon University Hospital logo added to this perception. Women interpreted receiving the kit as a sign that they were more at risk of cervical cancer than others due to being “late for screening”, being at an age where they considered themselves “more vulnerable” to CC (identified as around 50) or having gynaecological symptoms. Some women felt that receiving the kit meant they must “already be contaminated”. These perceptions motivated women to self-sample or go for professional screening, as did the feeling that “things could get worse” over time. This feeling was shared both by those who returned the kit immediately and by those who initially put the kit aside and did the self-sampling later.

“When it comes from over there (mainland France), it’s not the same (…) Because it’s far away. How can they know? So, there’s something wrong. They want in-depth results to make sure! (Respondent, aged 51, screened 4 years earlier)

Two women who had recently undergone a cervical smear test returned the HBVSS sample, because they thought it was a more in-depth test aiming to investigate a suspicion of cancer found on the pap-smear test.

“I said to myself, “Was something wrong with the test ? Why are they sending me back another one? Do I have cancer? I had a smear test in the hospital in September 2021”. (Respondent, age 42, HPV positive, negative pap-smear test 6 months earlier)

Prior knowledge of self-sampling or of other self-screening programmes increased women’s motivation to return the kit. Some women said they “automatically” respond to all screening invitations [2]. Finally, several women [6] said the quality of the kit, and in particular of the swab, “reassured” them and made them “want to take part”.

Doubts and discouraging factors

The main emotions experienced by women upon receiving the kit were fear and surprise. While fear of cancer was a motivating factor, fear of pain and fear of discovering a disease were discouraging factors for 3 women.

Women were surprised because of their unfamiliarity with cervical cancer self-screening, which they described as “strange”. The usefulness of the RESISTE programme was questioned by women with a regular screening history - they felt it was the role of their general practitioner (GP) to invite them to be screened. Other women were concerned about the reliability of self-sampling as compared to HP-led screening, about the kit’s shelf life, sample contamination or deterioration (over time or due to heat), and sample damage or loss in the post.

“It says it’s not the same method as a smear test, but is it still reliable? I don’t know, the [smear] it pinches, it goes right to the bottom.” (respondent, age 46, screened more than 3 years earlier).

Another concern voiced by women [8] related to how they had been selected and how their personal information (name and address) had been accessed, given that the kit had been sent directly from mainland France rather than through the Réunion social security system. A significant number of women [12] considered the intervention to be a “scam”.

“I was surprised. I didn’t expect to get this kit because I know that it’s usually the doctor who takes the samples. But I was reluctant to do it! (…) Isn’t it a scam? Did it exist and why did it come from mainland France? It was bizarre! How did they get my address? Why me? (…) I hesitated for at least two months, I think. (Respondent, late returnee, aged 38, tested 2 years earlier)

For some women, the financial incentive contributed to their perception that it was a scam [6] while others appreciated receiving the incentive [9] but did not consider it to be a determining factor in their decision to self-test or not.

Finally, the fact that the kit was sent out as part of a trial had an impact on how the purpose of the intervention was perceived. Some women thought that the aim was to produce “statistics” rather than to improve women’s health. This discouraged them from taking part.

Post-HBVSS monitoring

Capacity to understand test results

Receiving a positive result was a “shock” to the women, many of whom thought that a positive HPV result was a cancer diagnosis [7]. The need for medical follow-up and further testing was recognised, although the participants did not always understand why. Some women [3] thought their partner had been unfaithful when they tested positive. One woman, who had prior suspicions, split up with her partner after receiving a positive test result.

“Even if they say no, I immediately think of cancer, it makes you think of difficult treatment, and of death. Injections, chemotherapy, and more (…) I don’t know exactly why I need to go and see a gynaecologist.” (Respondent, HPV positive, age 44, screened 5 years earlier).

Regarding the CC screening calendar, women knew that screening had to be done regularly, but many did not know exactly when they needed to be screened again [16]. There was also some confusion about the age at which screening should be done. I It was considered more important after the age of 40 or 50, and sometimes less important after 60 when sexual activity was considered less regular.

Opportunities for post-HBVSS monitoring

Half of the HPV-positive respondents (4/8) would have preferred to receive a phone call informing them of their results, so as to be able to ask questions. One HPV-positive woman deleted the text message as soon as she received it and only informed her family and consulted a doctor after receiving confirmation by post.

“I didn’t think it was a good idea to receive this kind of result by text message. I was shocked. (…) I deleted the text message, I didn’t want my children to see, not really knowing what was wrong with me. (…) A month later, I told my children about it because I received a letter at home saying that I should contact a health professional as soon as possible. It’s easier to read than a text message. For me, a text message is more familiar”. (Respondent, HPV positive, aged 50, screened 8 years earlier)

In some cases, there was confusion between the trial HPV results and organised screening pap-smear results. One woman who received a positive HPV result from a self-sample immediately went to a gynaecologist for a smear test. She had not yet received the results of this smear test when her GP, to whom she had returned the self-sample, phoned to tell her about the positive results, without specifying that it was the HPV test. She thought it was the result of the smear test. Another woman received the kit when she had just had a smear test, the results of which she had not yet received. She thought this meant her results must be positive and she needed further screening. Both women were very concerned.

Motivation to undergo additional screening

Receiving a positive result is a “scary” experience for women. All 8 HPV-positive respondents went to a doctor for a smear test. Many (6/8) went straight to their GP or immediately made an “urgent” appointment with a gynaecologist. The other two women with positive HPV results consulted a month after receiving their initial results by text message, one because she had to wait to see a female gynaecologist, the other because she did not trust the text message and was waiting for more official confirmation of the results by post.

Women with positive results discussed them with friends and family, who encouraged them to have further tests. Others looked for information on the internet, which generally reassured them.

Discussion

Key findings

This qualitative study aimed to identify the main capability, opportunity and motivation factors relating to home-based HPV vaginal self-sampling.

The results show that receiving the kit encouraged women who had not been screened for several years to undergo screening, either by self-sampling or with a practitioner.

In terms of capability, both non-respondents and respondents did not feel entirely comfortable with self-sampling, concerned that they would not be able to collect a sample of sufficient quality or that they would hurt themselves. Several women did not feel capable of performing what they perceived as a medical procedure. A few felt ashamed. The results also reveal a lack of understanding of the screening schedule and confusion between a positive result and a cancer diagnosis.

In terms of opportunity, receiving the kit at home and having multiple return options encouraged use of the kit. Sending a kit to women’s homes does more to encourage screening than a simple reminder. Receiving results directly and quickly is considered by both respondents and non-respondents as an important factor in uptake. Relatives generally have a facilitating effect, encouraging the practice of self-sampling at home. But the lack of prior information about vaginal self-sampling and the lack of participation by health professionals, particularly doctors and pharmacists, represent missed opportunities and major barriers to HBVSS. The financial incentive does not appear to encourage response; on the contrary, in some cases it feeds doubt about the authenticity of the programme.

In terms of motivation, receiving the kit at home creates a sense that one is being targeted as potentially affected by cervical cancer. Respondents were motivated by their time gain and preserved intimacy. Prior familiarity with self-sampling or other self-screening programmes increases women’s motivation to return the kit. The non-respondents were discouraged from participating by fear of pain or disease (cancer), lack of trust in the programme or insufficient information about vaginal self-sampling. Finally, a positive result provided strong motivation for women to seek advice and have a smear test. All women who received a positive result consulted a healthcare professional, the time taken to do so depending on their preferences (sense of urgency, availability of appointments with a female HP, receipt of the official letter, etc.).

Comparison with other literature

Our results are consistent with several studies that have shown high acceptance of vaginal sampling by women [8–10]. Another randomised trial has also shown increased participation amongst women who receive an HBVSS kit by post compared to those who receive a reminder letter or an HBVSS kit order form [22].

Previous studies also identified user-friendliness and privacy as key factors in self-sampling uptake [23–25]. Quick reception of results has also previously been identified as being highly valued by women [24]. Perceived relevance of screening is another key determinant of uptake. As in our pre-intervention study [13] and other prior research, we found that having symptoms [22], being over 50 years of age [21] or engaging in risky sexual behaviour, defined on Réunion Island as having multiple partners [13], meant that women considered screening to be more relevant to their personal circumstances. To our knowledge, no prior studies have documented that receiving the kit at home creates a sense of being targeted. This is an important motivational factor.

Other studies have highlighted the influence of peers [24] and husbands [26–28], particularly in cultural contexts of close family proximity. In one study, women who could discuss screening with friends or family members were three times more likely to participate in screening than those who could not [29]. The suggestion of several women that the opinions of women who had carried out HBVSS should be shared (so-called “community champions”) [30] seems justified. Conversely, misconceptions about the screening programme, especially by close relatives, could be a major barrier to home screening [22].

A lack of self-confidence in sample collection has been observed in other contexts [22–24, 30, 31] and can lead to low uptake and poorer quality self-collection [22]. As in our pre-intervention study, women emphasised their need for support [24].

The pre-intervention study [13] and other studies conducted in mainland France [32] also identify the lack of prior information as one of the reasons women do not consider HBVSS a viable option, highlighting the direct (on the woman) and indirect impact (on next of kin) on uptake [22].

Several studies have highlighted women’s expressed need to be accompanied by a practitioner in self-sampling [11, 13, 32]. Chemineau et al.’s study found that women on Réunion preferred to receive and return the kit via a practitioner [11]. Our study found that women preferred the anonymity and facility of receiving the kit at home and returning it by post.

To our knowledge, there has been no qualitative study of a screening programme that included a financial incentive. The importance of free screening has been documented among women of low socioeconomic status [33].

The concerns expressed by health professionals during the pre-intervention study regarding the loss of HPV-positive women after self-testing [13] do not appear to be founded. HBVSS can therefore encourage women to reintegrate organised cervical cancer screening systems.

Strengths and limitations

RESISTE is the first trial in which self-sampling kits were sent to women’s homes. This study is innovative in that it includes both non-respondents and respondents with positive and negative HPV results. It is also the first study to analyse the impact of providing a financial incentive.

In terms of limitations, the study was unfortunately unable to sufficiently represent women over 55, nor were we able to track all the health professionals contacted by women during the trial.

Implications

Providing HBVSS motivates women to screen. However, health professionals need to be involved to provide better guidance for the women, who need to have the option of self-sampling at a practitioner’s or at home and receiving a self-sampling kit at home or from their GP.

Both health professionals and the general public, especially women, need to be better informed about HPV self-sampling. Mass media communication must target women and their relatives. Financial incentives did not seem to be a key motivating factor in this study, suggesting that they could be dropped from future home-based vaginal self-sampling programs in this territory.

To ensure that women feel confident in performing the HBVSS, the information provided with the kit should be brief, clear, accurate and free from medical jargon. Women also need to be reassured about the reliability of the results, the preservation of the sample and the ease of the procedure.

User-friendliness would also be enhanced by a shorter time frame for receiving the results. For speed and convenience, results should be emailed to the women’s GPs and a link to access results sent directly to the women via text message and email, so that they can view them at their convenience. For optimal communication in the event of a positive result, immediate feedback should be sent by email to the GP, followed by a phone call to the woman concerned, thereby reducing anxiety and ensuring the best possible follow-up.

Both the women and the professionals requested that outreach activities be carried out in more geographically and socially isolated neighbourhoods.

Conclusions

The anthropological approach, combined with the Behaviour Change Wheel, enabled us to identify drivers of improved access to cervical cancer screening for under-screened women on Réunion Island. Participants in our study appreciated receiving a VSS kit at home and would like to do so in the future. Some women even wanted the kit to be sent out with initial screening campaigns (not just with the reminder letter). However, to increase awareness, ease of use and support from health professionals, the system needs to be adapted. Greater coverage before introducing the system would contribute to increased awareness and motivation.

Future research should be conducted among healthcare professionals, including general practitioners, gynaecologists and midwives. The aim would be to identify the obstacles and barriers to integrating self-sample screening, information and support for women into their practices using this method.
