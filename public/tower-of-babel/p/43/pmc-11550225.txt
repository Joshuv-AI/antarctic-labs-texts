Novel dual inhibitor targeting CDC25 and HDAC for treating triple-negative breast cancer
Authors: Bidyadhar Sethy, Richa Upadhyay, Iin Narwanti, Zih-Yao Yu, Sung-Bau Lee, Jing-Ping Liou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTriple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) presents a significant challenge for treatment due to its aggressive nature and the lack of effective therapies. This study developed dual inhibitors against cell division cycle 25 (CDC25) and histone deacetylases (HDACs) for TNBC treatment. CDC25 phosphatases are crucial for activating cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs), the master regulators of cell cycle progression. HDACs regulate various biological processes by deacetylating histone and non-histone proteins, affecting gene expression, chromatin structure, cell differentiation, and proliferation. Dysregulations of HDAC and CDC25 are associated with several human malignancies. We generated a group of dual inhibitors for CDC25 and HDAC by combining the molecular structures of CDC25 (quinoline-5,8-dione) and HDAC (hydroxamic acid or benzamide) pharmacophores. The newly developed compounds were evaluated against various solid-tumor, leukemia, and non-malignant breast epithelial cells. Among the synthesized compounds, 18A emerged as a potent inhibitor, demonstrating significant cytotoxicity against TNBC cells, superior to its effects on other cancer types while sparing non-malignant cells. 18A possessed similar HDAC inhibitory activity as MS-275 and potently suppressed CDC25 activity in vitro and the CDK1 dephosphorylation in cells. Additionally, 18A hindered the progression of S and G2/M phases, triggered DNA damage, and induced apoptosis. Introduction

Cancer remains a significant global health burden, with nearly 20 million new cases reported in 2022, leading to approximately 9.7 million cancer-related deaths worldwide. Among all cancer types, breast cancer stands out as one of the most prevalent, with 2.3 million new cases in 2022, making it the second most commonly diagnosed cancer globally. Within the spectrum of breast cancer subtypes, triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) accounts for approximately 10–15% of all breast cancer cases [1]. TNBC is characterized by the absence of estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) expressions, presenting as a particularly aggressive form of the disease with a poorer prognosis compared to other subtypes. Research efforts are ongoing to develop targeted therapies tailored to address the unique challenges posed by TNBC and improve treatment outcomes for individuals affected by this subtype. Conventional chemotherapy and radiation are the primary systemic therapeutic strategies, and no other Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved targeted therapies are yet available for TNBC. Due to these features and the lack of targeted therapies, numerous attempts have been made to discover potential molecular targets for TNBC [2].

Histone deacetylases (HDACs) and histone acetyltransferases (HATs) are crucial enzymes in the epigenetic regulation of gene expression through post-translational modifications of histone proteins. HDACs remove acetyl groups from histones, leading to chromatin condensation and gene repression, whereas HATs add acetyl groups, resulting in chromatin relaxation and gene activation [3]. Besides histones, HDACs deacetylate non-histone substrates, including polyamines, tubulin, structure maintenance of chromosomes 3 (SMC3), p53, and HSP90. The deacetylation activity of HDACs guides gene expression, chromatin structure, and cellular processes such as differentiation and proliferation, thereby significantly regulating multiple biological functions [4, 5]. In humans, HDACs comprise a family of eighteen members divided into four classes. Among all, eleven members known as the “classical” HDACs with a zinc-dependent active site are Class I (HDAC1, HDAC2, HDAC3, and HDAC8), Class II (HDAC4, HDAC5, HDAC6, HDAC7, HDAC9, and HDAC10), and Class IV (HDAC11) [6]. Class I HDAC1-3 mainly deacetylate histones H3 and H4 but also target non-histone proteins [7]. It is generally agreed that up-regulation of HDACs in malignant tumor cells leads to the deregulation of homeostasis, differentiation, cell apoptosis, and inactivation of tumor suppressors [8]. Since epigenetic changes are highly implicated in tumorigenesis, inhibitors of histone deacetylases could represent a promising therapeutic strategy [9].

To date, the U.S. FDA has approved four HDAC inhibitors (HDACi) for treating hematological malignancies. These are Vorinostat (SAHA; 1), Belinostat (PXD-101; 2), Romidepsin (FK228; 3), and Panobinostat (LBH589). The most recently approved one is Givinostat (4), which is used for the treatment of Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy (DMD) (Fig. 1A) [10, 11]. The China FDA and Japanese Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency (PMDA) have also authorized Tucidinostat to treat peripheral T-cell lymphoma [12]. Entinostat (MS-275; 5), a widely used benzamide group inhibitor targeting HDAC1-3, has been demonstrated to help treat hematological malignancies, while its inhibitory effectiveness against solid tumors is modest [13]. Nevertheless, many other HDAC inhibitors are in clinical development, including Vorinostat-based long-chain hydroxamic acids (Ricolinostat, Citarinostat, CUDC-101), Belinostat-based cinnamic hydroxamic acids (Resminostat, Pracinostat, Dacinostat), Givinostat-based aromatic hydroxamic acids (Abexinostat, Bisthianostat, Quisinostat, Nanatinostat, Fimepinostat), and lastly Entinostat-based ortho-aminoanilides (Mocetinostat, Domatinostat, CXD101) etc [11, 14]. Many clinical researches have shown that HDAC inhibitors exploit synergistic effects with other anticancer agents [15]. However, multicomponent drugs raised the risks involved in complex pharmacokinetic properties, unpredictable drug-drug interactions, and different drug solubilities. Developing HDACi-based multitargeting drugs may thus provide an effective and practical strategy to overcome the limitations of single and multicomponent agents [16].

One notable factor contributing to the development of malignant tumors is the disruption of cell cycle control. Disordered cell cycle, unlimited proliferation, and mutations in tumor suppressors and oncogenes are common characteristics all malignancies share [17]. Cell division cycle 25 (CDC25) phosphatases are central to the regulation of the cell cycle, in which CDC25 dephosphorylates the threonine and tyrosine residues of cyclin-dependent kinases (CDK), promoting CDK activation and thus cell cycle progression. CDC25 dysregulation has been implicated in various malignancies, highlighting their potential as therapeutic targets in cancer treatment [18]. The CDC25 family comprises three isoforms - CDC25A, CDC25B, and CDC25C. These isoforms function on distinct CDK/cyclin complexes at various cell cycle phases. CDC25A mainly activates the CDK2/cyclin E and CDK2/cyclin A complexes during the G1/S transition, while CDC25B and C primarily regulate the CDK1/cyclin B1 activity to promote the progression of mitosis [19].

Targeting CDC25 has shown promise in preclinical studies, with inhibitors demonstrating the ability to induce cell cycle arrest and promote apoptosis in cancer cells [20]. Figure 1B shows the typical quinone and non-quinone moieties carrying CDC25 inhibitors. Through high-throughput screening (HTS), a sulfur-containing vitamin K analog, NSC95397 (6), was found to be a potent irreversible CDC25 inhibitor, while it also targets MKK7 and other kinases [21, 22]. Moreover, naphthoquinone derivatives, such as NSC95397, UPD-140, and Cpd-5, are the most numerous and can interfere with CDC25 activity and cell cycle progression potentially through irreversibly oxidizing the cysteine residue of the CDC25 catalytic domain [21, 23, 24]. Additionally, compound-13 (7), a new class of non-quinoid derivatives containing the 3-aminoisoquinolin-1(2H)-one pharmacophore, was reported by the Lazo and Wipf group as a reversible and competitive CDC25B inhibitor with a Ki 1.9 µM [25]. In a recent study by Song et al., a novel imidazopyridine-based non-quinone derivative, CHEQ-2 (8), was discovered as a potent CDC25A/B inhibitor that triggered cell cycle arrest in the S phase, accompanied by ROS generation with enhanced mitochondrial dysfunction and apoptosis [26]. Furthermore, the research group led by Zwergel et al. identified novel coumarin- and quinolinone-based polycycles with potent CDC25s inhibition activity, such as SV37, compound-1a, and 2c [9]. The same research group recently also discovered Compound-2c (9) as a potent CDC25C inhibitor that could trigger a pro-apoptotic effect [27, 28]. Likewise, the quinone-containing compounds NSC668394 and DA3003-1/NSC663284/6a (11) were shown to be sub-micromolar half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of 0.64 and 0.25 µM, respectively [22]. DA3003-1/NSC663284/6a (11) is one of the most potent CDC25 inhibitors reported and has been shown to block cell cycle progression at both G1 and G2/M and causes hyperphosphorylation of CDK1/CDK2 [29]. In addition, Jing research group discovered a quinoline-5, 8-dione derivative M2N12 (10), which potently inhibited CDC25C with IC50 of 0.09 µM and exhibited remarkable suppression on KB-VIN cell growth (IC50 = 6.81 µM) [30]. Furthermore, our earlier work demonstrated 6-regioisomeric quinoline-5, 8-dione containing compound DA3003-2/NSC663285/6b (12) as a potent CDC25 inhibitor that induced genome instability and killed colorectal cancer cells through apoptotic pathways [31].

In recent years, advancements have made it possible to target multiple pathways implicated in the progression of cancer therapy with combinatorial techniques that combine numerous targeted therapies [32]. Therapies targeting dual or multiple pathways have shown high efficacy and can reduce therapeutic doses and side effects compared to single-target treatment [33]. One of the mechanisms of action of HDAC inhibitors is to increase the acetylation of histones, which leads to the reactivation of tumor suppressor genes, such as p21, in tumor cells. This, in turn, inhibits CDK/cyclin complexes and ultimately causes cell cycle arrest and cell differentiation. On the other hand, CDC25 inhibitors have also been shown to restrain the activation of CDKs, resulting in cell cycle arrest and apoptosis in cancer cells [34]. Therefore, we hypothesized that simultaneous CDC25 and HDAC inhibition may create synergistic effects on the growth of cancer cells. A promising strategy in drug design involves incorporating HDAC inhibitory pharmacophores, such as hydroxamate or o-phenylenediamine, into the structure of inhibitors targeting other molecules. This approach aims to create dual or multi-target inhibitors that leverage HDAC inhibition [35–37]. Consistent with our hypothesis, HDAC/CDK dual inhibitors have demonstrated strong enzymatic inhibition and potent anti-proliferative effects [37]. Moreover, recent studies suggest that CDK inhibition can enhance the sensitivity of cancer cells to HDAC inhibitors, potentially leading to increased cancer cell death [38].

In this study, we designed and synthesized potential CDC25-HDAC dual inhibitors by combining CDC25 and HDAC pharmacophores and explored their structure-activity relationships (SARs). We tested these hybrid molecules on various cancer cell lines and further examined their CDC25 and HDAC inhibitory activities in cells. We also biologically investigated the effects of these compounds on cell cycle progression, DNA damage, and apoptosis. Our findings suggest that CDC25-HDAC dual-targeting inhibitors could have therapeutic advantages for developing anti-TNBC drugs.

Chemistry

Study rational design

To guide the design of potent anti-cancer agents with a thorough understanding of related proteins, such as CDC25 and HDAC in various cancer progression, we applied the principle of molecular hybridization combining structural features of CDC25 and HDAC inhibitors to create dual-target molecules. According to the classical HDAC inhibitors model, HDAC inhibitors should have a (i) zinc-binding group (ZBG) which is important for the HDAC catalytic function (hydroxamic acid and benzamide groups), (ii) a spacer mimicking the lysine side chain of the substrate such as an aromatic or aliphatic linker, and (iii) a capping group to effectively inhibit the hydrolysis of acetyl-lysine residues on histone tails and non-histone proteins (Fig. 2) [11, 15]. The ZBG is a hydrophilic domain, including hydroxamic acid and 2-aminobenzamide groups interacting with the active zinc cation (Zn2+) at the HDAC site. Furthermore, as discussed earlier, the cap group is a crucial unit susceptible to chemical modifications to enhance selectivity and assisted pharmacokinetic properties. The cap group can be used with some conventional cytotoxic agents or drugs to generate HDAC-based multi-target agents without compromising the HDAC enzyme inhibition activity. Here, we introduced quinoline-5, 8-dione moiety as the cap (CDC25i core scaffold), which may cause of improving the effectivity by interacting with the aromatic amino acid residues located either close to the outer domain of the active site or at the external surface of the enzyme, as well as by facilitating its ability to form redox biological reaction and DNA damage. Therefore, based on the principle of molecular hybridization, by considering the structural analogous of the core scaffold of CDC25 inhibitors (quinoline-5, 8-dione moiety), we designed and synthesized multiple inhibitors of CDC25-HDAC combined via different aliphatic or aromatic linkers with HDAC pharmacophores including hydroxamic acid or benzamide moieties (Fig. 2), which may provide reasonable pharmaceutical properties and expecting improved efficacy for cancer treatment.

Cytotoxicity

To evaluate the potential of compounds 13A ~ 18A and 13B ~ 18B for cancer treatment, we firstly tested their cytotoxicity against the triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) cell line, MDA-MB-231, to understand the structure-activity relationships (SAR) within this series. Cells were treated with the compounds for 48 h, and their viability was determined using the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay (Fig. 3A). In this assay, we also included CDC25 inhibitors (CDC25i) 6a (DA3003-1/NSC663284) and 6b (DA3003-2/NSC663285) [31], and HDAC inhibitors (HDACi) MS-275 and SAHA as reference controls for comparison. Our results revealed that compounds 15A, 15B, 16B, and 17B exhibited almost no cytotoxicity, while compounds 13A, 13B, 14B, 16A, and 17A showed moderate cytotoxic effects. Notably, compounds 14A, 18A, and 18B demonstrated the most significant cytotoxicity among the tested series. The SAR analysis shows that compound 13A bearing N-benzyl hydroxamic acid motif with quinoline-5, 8-dione conjugated via an aromatic linker at the C7-position showed comparable cytotoxicity with the reference control groups (6a, 6b, SAHA, and MS-275). However, its 6-regioisomer compound 13B treatment displayed a lower potency. Interestingly, the compound bearing N-phenyl benzoyl (14A) hydroxamic acid motif with quinoline-5, 8-dione connected through the same aromatic linker at the C7-position demonstrated improved cytotoxicity compared to the 7-regioisomer compound 13A and all control groups. Contrastingly, the N-phenyl benzoyl hydroxamic acid group at C6-substitution (14B) largely reduced its cytotoxicity. Meanwhile, the use of an alkyl chain (n = 6 ~ 8; compounds 15A ~ 17A and 15B ~ 17B) in place of an aromatic N-benzyl linker failed to get potential antiproliferative effects. Among the aliphatic alkyl chain linking derivatives, compounds 16A (n = 7) and 17A (n = 8) demonstrated moderate cytotoxicity by 23% and 28% of MDA-MB-231 cells growth reduction, respectively, while their regioisomers compounds 16B (n = 7) and 17B (n = 8) were not poisonous. Furthermore, we introduced an N-benzyl-O-amino benzamide motif (18A and 18B) replacing the N-benzyl hydroxamic acid (13A, 13B, 14A, and 14B) and following the same aromatic linker conjugated to quinoline-5, 8-dione. Our results showed that the accommodation of N-benzyl-O-amino benzamide motif (18A and 18B) resulted in the most substantial cytotoxic effects against MDA-MB-231 cells (Fig. 3A).

Subsequently, we compared the cytotoxic potential of the most potent compounds, 14A, 18A, and 18B against MDA-MB-231 cells by determining the values of half maximal inhibitory concentrations (IC50) (Fig. 3B). We showed that compounds 14A and 18B showed strong cytotoxic inhibitory potency with IC50 values of 2.50 and 2.44 µM, respectively. Among these, compound 18A emerged as the most cytotoxic with an IC50 value of 1.07 µM compared to other derivatives 14A, 18B, and the reference control groups 6a (IC50 = 5.75 µM) and MS-275 (IC50 = 2.93 µM). Moreover, the hybrid molecule 18A exhibited superior cytotoxicity compared to the combination of the individual CDC25 and HDAC inhibitors (Fig. S1A), likely due to its capability to efficiently and simultaneously target both the CDC25 and HDAC pathways within a unified molecular framework. These findings underscore the significant potential of compound 18A as a promising candidate for further exploration.

To extend our analysis, we screened all the synthesized compounds across six different cell lines derived from solid tumors. These included triple-negative breast cancer (MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-436), PR/ER-positive breast cancer (MCF-7), pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PANC-1), glioblastoma (U87MG), and colorectal cancer (CRC; DLD-1). Notably, 18A treatment showed a prominent reduction in cell viability of DLD-1 cells, comparable to the effect of 6b (Fig. S1B), the compound identified as a potent CDC25 inhibitor specifically targeting CRC cells in our previous study [31]. Additionally, 18A exhibited superior cytotoxic activity against TNBC cells (MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-436) compared to ER/PR positive breast cancer cells (MCF-7) (Fig. 3C, and S1C-E). Furthermore, we observed lower cytotoxicity of 18A in U87MG and PANC-1 cells (Fig. S1F-G). In contrast to 18A, the remaining compounds in the series displayed relatively weak cytotoxic effects across all tested cell lines. Most importantly, we further assessed the effect of 18A on a non-malignant breast epithelial cells and found that 18A exhibits minimal cytotoxicity to the M10 cells (Fig. 3C). Furthermore, our assessment of the impact of synthesized compounds on leukemia cell lines revealed that 18A demonstrated substantial effectiveness against HL60 (acute promyelocytic leukemia) and K562 (chronic myelogenous leukemia) (Fig. S2). However, it did not exhibit varying levels of toxicity in KG-1 (acute myelogenous leukemia) and MV4-11 (biphenotypic B-myelomonocytic leukemia) compared with reference and other compounds (Fig. S2). Together, these findings underscore the potential selectivity of 18A for TNBC over non-TNBC and normal breast cells.

HDAC inhibitory activity

Given that 18A was the most poisonous among the synthesized compounds, we determined its inhibitory activity towards the potential target, HDACs. Our results indicated that treatment with 18A dose-dependently increased the acetylation levels of histone H3/H4 (Fig. 4), the canonical substrates of the class I HDAC1-3 (Fig. 4). The increased level was similar to that observed in cells treated with the reference compound MS-275 (Fig. 4). In contrast, we did not detect significant changes in SMC3 and alpha-tubulin acetylation, indicating that 18A may not target HDAC8 and HDAC6. These findings strongly suggest that 18A is a class I HDAC inhibitor possessing comparable activity and specificity as MS-275 in cells.

CDC25 inhibitory activity

We next analyzed the impact of 18A treatment on the activity of another potential target, CDC25. To this, we used a high concentration of thymidine to synchronize cells in the early-S phase (Fig. 5; T0) and then allowed the cells to progress into mitosis after being released from the thymidine block. To minimize the potential secondary effect from defective cell-cycle progression caused by compound treatment, we treated cells with compounds 8 h after thymidine removal (T8), at which most cells had completed genome replication and accumulated in the G2 phase. We showed that the phosphorylation levels of CDK1 at tyrosine 15 (CDK1Y15) at T0 and T8 were comparably high (Fig. 5), consistent with the presence of cells in S and G2 phases, respectively. To avoid re-phosphorylation of CDK1 after cell division, the tubulin destabilizing agent, nocodazole, was added to hinder mitosis progression. We found that CDK1Y15 phosphorylation dropped from T8 to T14, indicating that cells moved to and arrested at mitosis (Fig. 5). 18A treatment resulted in a significant accumulation of CDK1Y15 phosphorylation and reduction of phosphorylation levels of mitotic markers histone H3 at Ser10 and mitotic proteins recognized by the MPM2 antibody as compared to reference compounds 6a and 6b. (Fig. 5). We also obtained similar observations from cells arrested in mitosis by Taxol and colchicine (Fig. S3A-C). These results indicate that 18A impedes CDK1 dephosphorylation, potentially through downregulating CDC25 activity, and perturbs G2/M progression. We also observed a minor reduction in Cyclin B1 phosphorylation in cells treated with 2 µM of 18A, while the higher concentration of 18A (5 µM) did not affect Cyclin B1 phosphorylation (Fig. 5). Intriguingly, the higher concentration of 18A unexpectedly increased phosphorylation levels of proteins that could also be detected by the MPM2 antibody (Fig. 5), possibly due to the activation of an unknown pathway.

We next directly tested the CDC25 inhibitory activity of 18A. Given that 18A treatment showed impaired mitotic CDK1 dephosphorylation, we examined the potency of 18A against CDC25C, the primary subtype of CDC25s for erasing CDK1 phosphorylation during mitosis [39]. This analysis revealed that 18A strongly suppressed CDC25C activity with an IC50 value of 0.23 µM, while it displayed a similar CDC25C inhibitory activity to the reference compound 6a (Fig. S3D). These results demonstrate that 18A is a potent CDC25 inhibitor.

Cell cycle analysis

After observing that 18A inhibited CDK1 dephosphorylation, we studied its impact on cell cycle progression. We administered 18A to synchronized cells at T2 and used flow cytometry to track the progression from the S phase to the subsequent cycle (Fig. 6A; T2-T16). Our findings showed that treating cells with 2 µM of 18A slowed down the progression of the S phase and delayed the exit from mitosis (Fig. 6). Treating cells with 4 µM of 18A resulted in almost complete arrest of the cell cycle in the S phase (Fig. 6). We also observed mitosis delay and arrest when cells were treated at T8 (Fig. S4).

Subsequently, we examined the effect of 18A treatment on genome replication by measuring the incorporation efficiency of the thymidine analog 5-ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU) into the replicating genome marked by chromatin-bound proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA) (Fig. 6B). Overall, these results indicate that 18A is a potent cell cycle inhibitor, possibly through the combined inactivation of CDC25s and HDACs.

Genome stability

High CDK activity has been reported to be required for efficient DNA repair processes, such as homologous recombination and checkpoint responses. CDC25 inhibition, which limits CDK activation, may impede HR and thus accumulate DNA lesions [40]. Our previous study showing DNA damage induction by 6b argued this hypothesis [31]. Moreover, HDAC inhibition alters chromatin structure, potentially compromising DNA integrity and repair mechanisms [41]. Therefore, we assessed the effect of 18A on genome stability by exploiting immunofluorescence microscopy to visualize cellular DNA damage proved by the specific antibody against H2AX phosphorylation at S139 (γH2AX). Our data revealed that the treatment of 18A significantly increased levels of γH2AX (Fig. 7A). To further characterize the DNA damage response, we performed western blot analysis to assess the effect of 18A on double-stranded DNA break (DSB) markers. We detected intensive DSB signaling, revealed by increased phosphorylation levels of H2AX at S139 (γH2AX), KRAB-associated protein-1 (KAP-1) at S824, replication protein A2 (RPA2) at S4/S8, and CHK2 at T68 (Fig. 7B). These findings suggest that 18A is a potent DNA damage-inducing agent. We also noticed that 18A treatment at 2 µM resulted in ataxia telangiectasia mutated (ATR) activation, revealed by increased RPA2 phosphorylation at S33 and CHK1 phosphorylation at S345. However, CHK1 phosphorylation was dramatically reduced when cells were treated with a higher concentration of 18A (Fig. 7), which remains to be further characterized.

Apoptosis

To explore how 18A eradicates cancer cells, we utilized flow cytometry to scrutinize the cell status following prolonged exposure to 18A. We noted a dose-dependent increase in the sub-G1 cell population after a 48-h 18A treatment (Fig. 8A). This could be attributed to DSB (Fig. 7B) or the initiation of apoptosis. Our investigation into the levels of proteins involved in apoptosis pathways revealed a surge in active caspases-3, -8, and − 9 in cells treated with 18A (Fig. 8B). These changes were less pronounced in cells treated with 6a and MS-275 (Fig. 8B). Furthermore, the 18A treatment augmented the number of early-apoptotic cells, as indicated by membrane phosphatidylserine externalization (positive for annexin V) (Fig. S5). These findings underscore the potency of 18A as a dual inhibitor of CDC25 and HDAC, which instigates cancer cell death through an apoptotic pathway.

Conclusion

In this study, a series of novel CDC25-HDAC dual inhibitors were designed, synthesized, and biologically evaluated. Our biological evaluation demonstrates the potential of 18A, a benzamide derivative linking N-benzyl aromatic to the quinoline-5, 8-dione scaffold, exhibits potent cytotoxicity against TNBC cell lines, particularly MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-436 cells. Importantly, 18A exhibited minimal cytotoxicity towards non-malignant breast cell lines, highlighting its selectivity for cancerous cells. Our evidence suggests that simultaneously targeting CDC25 and HDACs may offer a promising therapeutic avenue for TNBC.

Compound 18A displayed excellent HDAC1 inhibitory potency, surpassing the reference control MS-275. Meanwhile, the activities against HDAC2 and HDAC3 between the two compounds were comparable. In cell-based analysis, 18A treatment effectively and specifically increased the acetylation of histones H3/H4, indicating that 18A is a selective class I HDAC inhibitor. Moreover, our thorough analysis revealed that treating with 2 µM of 18A was sufficient to inhibit CDK1Y15 dephosphorylation, prevent phosphorylation of mitotic proteins, slow down S and G2/M progression, and trigger a mild DNA damage response. These findings argue that 18A is a potent inhibitor of CDC25. To our surprise, 18A displayed a similar potency as the reference compound 6a on CDC25C inhibition. While we cannot exclude the possibility that 18A may have higher potency against the other two subtypes, CDC25A and B, we favor that simultaneous inhibition of CDC25s and HDACs synergistically hinders G2/M progression, resulting in the accumulation of CDK1Y15 phosphorylation.

When used higher concentrations (4 and 5 µM), 18A led to significant activation of double-stranded DNA break (DSB) signaling, complete cell cycle arrest in S or G2/M, and increased phosphorylation of unidentified proteins recognized by the MPM2 antibody. These results suggest that the cells were experiencing severe stress and activating alternative pathways to protect themselves from cell death. Supporting this idea, the phosphorylation level of CHK1 at S345 unexpectedly decreased upon treatment with 5 µM of 18A, indicating that the ATR-CHK1 pathway was inhibited in cells with extensive DNA damage. CHK1 is a key factor for genome maintenance and acts as a negative regulator of CDC25. Restraining its activation might thus give cells an additional way to withstand the stress caused by potent CDC25 inhibition. In addition, Ser 126 is situated within the cytoplasmic retention signal (CRS) region of Cyclin B1. Its phosphorylation is associated with the entry of Cyclin B1 into the nucleus with CDK1 at the onset of mitosis [42]. Apart from CDK1, PLK1 has also been identified as an upstream kinase of Cyclin B1S126 [43]. It is therefore suggested that PLK1 might serve as one of the alternative pathways activated by high concentrations of 18A treatment to compensate for the reduced CDK1 activity. It’s important to note that 18A has both CDC25 and HDAC inhibitory activities. Simultaneous inhibition of CDC25s and HDACs by high concentrations of 18A may synergistically impede cell cycle progression, ultimately leading to cell cycle arrest before the start of mitosis. At this stage, cyclin B1 S126, but not H3S10 or proteins recognized by the MPM2 antibody, becomes phosphorylated.

Additionally, 18A impaired DNA replication and induced DNA damage in replicating cells, evidenced by elevated γH2AX levels. Moreover, 18A induced apoptotic cell death in TNBC cells, as evidenced by increased sub-G1 population, and activation of apoptosis pathways. The observed cytotoxicity, induction of cell cycle arrest, DNA damage, and apoptosis highlight the multifaceted mechanism of action of 18A in TNBC cells. These findings suggest that 18A holds promise as a targeted therapy for TNBC, providing a new approach to addressing the lack of effective treatments for this aggressive subtype of breast cancer.

Biology

Cell culture

Triple-negative breast cancer MDA-MB-231 and Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma PANC-1 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (12800-017; Gibco). Triple-negative breast cancer cells MDA-MB-436 were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium F12 (DMEM F12) medium (41300-039; Gibco). Human epidermal growth factor receptor-2 negative breast adenocarcinoma MCF-7, glioblastoma U87MG, and M10 cells were cultured in alpha-modified Eagle’s minimum essential medium (α-MEM) (11900-024; Gibco). Human leukemia cell lines KG-1, HL-60, and colorectal adenocarcinoma DLD-1 were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 (31800-022; Gibco). Human leukemia cell lines MV4-11 and K-562 were cultured in Iscove’s modified Dulbecco’s medium (IMDM) (12200-036; Gibco), containing 1.5 g/L and 3 g/L sodium bicarbonate respectively. All cell lines were cultured in a medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin-glutamine (PSG; 10378-016; Gibco) at 37 oC in a humidified atmosphere.

Chemicals and antibodies

Colchicine, Paclitaxel (Taxol), and Nocodazole were purchased from Sigma (C9754), Cytoskeleton, Inc. (TXD01), and Sigma (31430-18-9) respectively. Primary antibodies: anti-phospho-Ser/Thr-Pro-mitotic protein monoclonal 2 (MPM2) (05–368; Millipore); anti-cyclin B1 pS126 (ab55184; Abcam); anti-Cyclin-dependent kinase 1 pY15 (CDK1 pY15) (GTX1281550; GeneTex); anti-histone H3 pS10 (06–570; Millipore); anti-Histone 3 (ab1791; Abcam); anti-acetyl-Histone 3 lysine 9 (07e352; Millipore); anti-Histone 4 (ab10158; Abcam); antiacetyl-Histone 4 lysine 5/8/12/16 (06e866; Millipore); anti-SMC3 (A300-060 A; Bethyl); anti-acetyl-SMC3 lysine 105/106 (MABE1073; Millipore); anti-a-tubulin (T5168; Sigma); anti-acetyl-a-Tubulin lysine 40 (T7451; Sigma); anti- KRAB domain-associated protein 1 (KAP1) pS824 (ab70369; Abcam); anti-Checkpoint kinase 2 (CHK2) pT68 (2661; Cell Signaling Technology); anti-CHK1 pS345 (2348; Cell Signaling Technology); anti-CHK1 (G-4) (sc-8408; Santa Cruz); anti-Replication Protein A2 (RPA2) pS33 (A300-246 A; Bethyl); anti-RPA2 pS4/S8 (A300- 245 A; Bethyl); anti-γH2AX (05–636; Millipore); anti-H2AX (2595; Cell Signaling Technology); anti-actin (MAB1501; Millipore); anti-Caspase-3 (NB100-56708; Novus); anti-Caspase-8 (9746; Cell Signaling); anti-Caspase-9 (9502; Cell Signaling); anti-induced myeloid leukemia cell differentiation protein (Mcl-1)(ab32087, Abcam); anti-B-cell leukemia/lymphoma 2(Bcl-2)(3498; Cell Signaling). MTT assay

Cells were seeded in 96-well plates and treated with compounds for 48 h, followed by incubation with 1 mg/ml 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyl-2H-tetrazolium bromide (MTT; Sigma, M2128) at 37 oC for 2–3 h. Formazan crystals were then dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). The number of surviving attached cells was determined by measuring the absorbance at 562 nm (PerkinElmer VICTOR3™ multilabel plate reader).

MTS assay

Cells were seeded in 96-well plates and treated with compounds for 48 h. Surviving cells were determined by incubation with 0.2 mg/ml of 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-5-(3-carboxymethoxyphenyl)-2-(4-sulfophenyl)-2H-tetrazolium (MTS; Abcam, ab223881), and absorbance at 490 nm was measured by using PerkinElmer VICTOR3TM multilabel plate reader.

HDAC enzymes inhibition assay

Enzyme inhibition assays were performed by the Reaction Biology Corporation, Malvern, PA. (http://www.reactionbiology). The substrate for HDAC1–10 is a fluorogenic peptide derived from p53 residues 379e382 [RHKK(Ac)]. Compounds were dissolved in DMSO and tested in 10-dose IC50 mode with 3-fold serial dilution starting at 10 mM. Trichostatin A (TSA) was the reference.

Thymidine synchronization

Cells were incubated with 4 mM of thymidine (Sigma, T1895) for 20–24 h to enrich cells at the early-S phase. Cells were then washed with fresh medium twice and incubated with culture medium for recovery from thymidine block.

CDC25 enzymes inhibition assay

CDC25C enzyme inhibition assays was performed by the Eurofins discovery by using the CDC25C Human Phosphatase LeadHunter assay. Compounds were tested at 1, 0.3, 0.1, 0.03, and 0.01µM and IC50 values were calculated. NSC663284 (6a) was used as reference.

Flow cytometry

Cells were collected and fixed with 70% ice-cold ethanol. After washing with cold phosphate-buffered saline containing 1% FBS, cells were incubated with 0.05 mg/ml of propidium iodide (PI; Sigma, P4170) and 0.25 mg/ml ribonuclease A (RNase A; Sigma, R6513) at 37 ◦C for 30 min. DNA content was analyzed by using the Becton Dickinson FACSCantoTMII Flow Cytometer, and cell-cycle profiles were plotted with the FlowJo software.

Annexin V binding assay

Membrane phosphatidylserine externalization of apoptotic cells were determined using Annexin V-FITC Apoptosis Detection Kit (Strong Biotech, AVK050). Briefly, cells were collected and immediately stained with annexin V-FITC and PI without fixation. Fluorescence intensities were subsequently measured by using the Becton Dickinson FACSCantoTMII Flow Cytometer, and cell-cycle profiles were plotted with the FlowJo software.

Western blotting analysis

Cells were lysed in Laemmli sample buffer (60 mM Tris-HCl, pH 6.8, 2% sodium dodecyl sulfate, and 10% glycerol). Proteins were separated by 12% SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred to the nitrocellulose membranes. After blocking with 5% skim milk, proteins were probed with specific primary antibodies and then HRP-conjugated species-specific secondary antibodies, followed by signal detection using enhanced chemiluminescence substrates (Bio-Rad). Images were obtained by using the iBright FL-1500 system.

EdU-incorporation assay and immunofluorescence staining

The efficiency of 5-ethynyl-20-deoxyuridine (EdU) incorporation into replicating chromatin was determined using a Click-iT™ EdU Cell Proliferation Kit (C10340, Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells treated with indicated compounds were pulsed with 20 mM EdU for 30 min. Cells were then pre-extracted with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 5 min and fixed with 2% formaldehyde for 15 min. The fixed cells were incubated with the Click-iT reagent for 30 min. For the antigen retrieval of PCNA, cells were incubated with ice-cold methanol for 15 min. After blocking with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA), proteins were probed.

with primary antibodies (rabbit anti-PCNA, GTX100539, GeneTex; mouse anti-gH2AX, 05e636, Millipore), followed by incubation with species-specific secondary antibodies (goat antirabbit, 111-295-144, Jackson ImmunoResearch; goat anti-mouse, 115-545-146, Jackson ImmunoResearch). Images were collected using a Molecular Devices ImageXpress Micro XL microscope fitted with a 20_ SWD20 Plan Fluor objective lens. The intensity of EdU or gH2AX was quantified by MetaXpress® Power Core™ software.
