Background

Periodontitis is a chronic inflammatory disease affecting the supporting structures of teeth, characterised by the progressive destruction of the periodontal ligament and alveolar bone. According to the World Health Organisation, periodontal disease affects 10%–15% of the adult population worldwide [1]. The key etiologic factor is dental biofilm, composed of pathogenic microorganisms such as Porphyromonas gingivalis, which trigger host immune responses and promote tissue destruction [2, 3].

Traditional non-surgical therapy, including scaling and root planing (SRP), primarily targets calculus removal. However, SRP may inadequately disrupt subgingival biofilm, especially in deep pockets or furcation areas [4]. In contrast, guided biofilm therapy (GBT) is a structured, minimally invasive protocol involving biofilm disclosure, air-polishing using low-abrasive powders (glycine or erythritol) and selective instrumentation for calculus removal [5–7]. This therapy has shown improved patient comfort, reduced treatment time and comparable or better clinical outcomes than conventional SRP [7, 8].

Accurate periodontal probing is essential for diagnosis, staging and monitoring treatment response. Manual probing, however, is subject to variability due to inconsistent force, angulation and operator technique. The Florida Probe system addresses these limitations by applying a constant force (15 g), providing measurements to 0.1 mm accuracy and automatically recording data [9–11].

Although GBT has demonstrated superior biofilm control compared with conventional SRP in previous studies [7, 8], most existing clinical reports evaluating GBT rely on manual periodontal probing for outcome assessment. This reliance introduces inherent variability stemming from inconsistent probing force, operator technique or angulation, which may obscure subtle but clinically meaningful changes in periodontal status, such as small reductions in probing depth or improvements in bleeding on probing (BOP) following biofilm removal. In contrast, the integration of the Florida Probe with GBT addresses this critical gap. By delivering objective, reproducible measurements, the Florida Probe enables precise quantification of baseline periodontal destruction, guiding GBT intervention. Furthermore, its ability to consistently track probing depths and BOP over time allows for the reliable evaluation of GBT’s efficacy in disrupting biofilm and reducing inflammation – an essential advantage, as GBT’s success hinges on verifying that plaque control translates to tangible periodontal improvement. This combination of GBT and objective, computerised monitoring using the Florida Probe represents a distinct advance over prior approaches, which often paired GBT with less precise manual probing or focused solely on calculus removal without systematic biofilm assessment.

The 2017 World Workshop on the Classification of Periodontal and Peri-Implant Diseases introduced a new staging and grading system for periodontitis [12]. This classification categorises periodontitis into four stages based on severity and complexity, with Stage IV representing the most severe cases, requiring complex rehabilitation due to masticatory dysfunction and extensive tooth loss.

This case report presents the innovative combination of Florida Probe assessment with the GBT protocol in treating a patient with Stage IV Grade C periodontitis, demonstrating the effectiveness of this approach in achieving substantial clinical improvements.

Case presentation

Patient information

A 34-year-old woman presented to Shaoxing Stomatological Hospital on 28 January 2021, complaining of gingival redness and swelling for 1 year, along with progressive tooth mobility over 2 years. One year prior, she had experienced the spontaneous loss of a maxillary right posterior tooth. The patient brushed her teeth twice daily for about 1 min using a manual toothbrush and did not use any interdental cleaning aids. She reported no history of systemic disease, long-term medication use or known drug allergies. Upon specific inquiry regarding familial predisposition to periodontal conditions, the patient reported no family history of periodontal disease (that is, no immediate family members, including parents or siblings, had been diagnosed with periodontitis or experienced premature tooth loss due to periodontal causes).

Clinical and imaging examination

Clinical examination revealed generalised poor oral hygiene with extensive plaque accumulation and calculus deposits. The gingival tissues appeared bright red with marked oedema, congestion and hyperplasia, presenting a foliated appearance with a soft, friable texture. Spontaneous pus discharge was observed from multiple periodontal pockets, particularly pronounced in the upper and lower anterior regions. Soft debris accumulation was Grade II, with considerable extrinsic staining and pigmentation visible on tooth surfaces, predominantly affecting the anterior teeth. Supragingival calculus deposits of Grade II were present throughout the dentition, and probing revealed abundant subgingival calculus deposits. Occlusal analysis revealed a 1-mm right deviation of the mandibular midline relative to the maxillary midline and moderate anterior crowding in both arches. Tooth 16 was confirmed missing with no prosthetic replacement, as shown in Fig. 1(a).

As shown in Fig. 2, the Florida Probe computerised periodontal examination revealed extensive periodontal destruction throughout the dentition. Bleeding on probing was positive at all 162 examined sites (100% BOP), indicating active inflammation throughout. Suppuration was detected at 92 sites, representing 57% of all probed locations. Probing depths ranged from 2 mm to 13 mm, with 151 of 162 sites (93%) demonstrating pathological probing depths greater than 4 mm. Tooth mobility assessment revealed Grade III mobility (severe, > 2 mm horizontal movement with vertical depression) for teeth 17, 31 and 39. Grade II mobility (moderate, 1–2 mm horizontal movement) was recorded for teeth 14, 12, 24, 25 and 37 and the lower anterior segment from 32 to 35 and from 42 to 47. All remaining teeth exhibited Grade I mobility (slight, < 1 mm horizontal movement). Furcation involvement assessment demonstrated Grade III furcation defects (through-and-through lesions) on the buccal and palatal aspects of teeth 17 and 26, the palatal aspect of tooth 27 and the buccal and lingual aspects of teeth 36, 37 and 46. Grade II furcation involvement (partial horizontal bone loss) was detected on the buccal aspect of tooth 27 and both buccal and lingual aspects of tooth 47.

Radiographic examination revealed generalised advanced alveolar bone loss with blurring of the alveolar crest and loss of the lamina dura throughout the dentition. The periodontal ligament space appeared uniformly widened. Horizontal bone resorption extended to one-half to two-thirds of the root length in most teeth. Angular bone defects were particularly evident on the mesial aspects of teeth 12 and 47. Severe vertical bone loss extending to the root apices was observed on teeth 17, 36 and 41. Tooth 17 additionally showed a deep carious lesion on the distal surface approaching the pulp chamber, with a hypodense shadow within the root bifurcation area. Tooth 36 demonstrated extensive bone resorption with radiolucent areas visible in the furcation region, consistent with the clinical findings of advanced furcation involvement.

Diagnosis

According to the 2017 World Workshop on the Classification of Periodontal and Peri-Implant Diseases [12], the diagnosis was Stage IV, Grade C periodontitis, based on severe interdental clinical attachment loss (> 5 mm), radiographic bone loss to the apical third of the roots, tooth loss due to periodontitis and complexity factors including masticatory dysfunction and occlusal instability. The rapid progression in a young patient supported the Grade C classification. Secondary diagnoses included anterior crowding, midline deviation and partial edentulism.

Risk assessment and prognosis

Based on Lang and Tonetti’s periodontal risk assessment model, the patient was classified as high risk due to 100% BOP, 151 sites with probing depths ≥ 5 mm and evidence of rapid progression. Teeth 17, 31 and 36 were deemed hopeless because of severe attachment loss, Grade III mobility and advanced furcation involvement. The remaining teeth were given a poor to guarded prognosis depending on individual site characteristics.

Treatment plan

The treatment aimed to control infection, reduce inflammation and preserve teeth with potential for maintenance. The plan included oral hygiene education, biofilm-oriented non-surgical therapy, removal of hopeless teeth, occlusal adjustment, supportive periodontal therapy and surgical intervention when necessary. Patient consent was obtained.

Plaque-oriented periodontal treatment (guided biofilm therapy)

Following completion of systemic health screening, including complete blood count and coagulation profile, the GBT protocol was initiated. The first appointment began with comprehensive oral hygiene instruction, using the patient’s own plaque disclosure to demonstrate biofilm accumulation patterns. The GBT sequence commenced with the application of an erythritol-based disclosing solution to visualise biofilm throughout the dentition. This was followed by systematic supragingival and subgingival air-polishing using glycine powder (particle size 25 μm) at low-pressure settings to remove disclosed biofilm while preserving tooth structure and minimising soft tissue trauma. During treatment, the patient was asked to rate comfort on a 10-point visual analogue scale (VAS) (1 = severe discomfort, 10 = no discomfort) and compare GBT with prior experiences of conventional SRP (received 2 years previously for gingivitis). She reported a VAS score of 8 for GBT, noting ‘only mild tingling from air-polishing, no sharp pain like with SRP scalers’ – a key factor in her initial acceptance of the therapy. She also commented that the ‘visual biofilm disclosure made it easier to understand why certain areas needed more attention’, which enhanced her engagement with the treatment process. Subsequently, piezoelectric ultrasonic instrumentation with specialised slim tips was employed to remove mineralised deposits, focusing only on areas with calculus rather than indiscriminate full-mouth scaling. Meticulous subgingival root planing was performed using Gracey curettes to remove residual calculus and contaminated cementum from deep pockets. During this initial phase, tooth 17, with Grade III mobility and a hopeless prognosis, was extracted atraumatically. Adjunctive antimicrobial therapy was prescribed based on the institution’s standard practice for severe periodontitis cases with extensive suppuration (57% of sites) and generalised active inflammation (100% BOP), rather than targeted culture or sensitivity testing. The regimen included cephradine capsules 0.25 g (two capsules three times daily) for 7 days, tinidazole tablets 0.5 g twice daily for 5 days and 0.12% chlorhexidine gluconate mouth rinse for twice-daily use starting 24 hours post-treatment, consistent with local antibiotic stewardship guidelines for short-course, targeted adjunctive therapy in severe periodontal infections.

At the 2-week review, inflammation had improved, but plaque control was suboptimal (Grade II). Occlusal trauma was identified and selectively adjusted on teeth 12, 14, 15, 24 and 25 and the lower anterior region (32–42). Oral hygiene education was reinforced with re-disclosure.

Periodontal supportive therapy

One month after initial therapy (29 March 2021), the gingiva appeared pink and firm, with reduced inflammation. Objective compliance assessment was conducted using two standardised metrics: (1) the plaque index (the Silness–Löe index), which improved to Grade I (minimal plaque accumulation, confined to the gingival margin in < 20% of examined sites); and (2) patient-reported adherence, verified via verbal interview and review of a structured oral hygiene log – the patient confirmed consistent twice-daily brushing (2–3 min per session) and daily use of interdental floss (prescribed during initial education), with no self-reported gaps in practice. Florida Probe measurements showed that BOP decreased to 42%, and probing depths ranged from 2 to 7 mm (excluding teeth 31 and 36). Tooth mobility also reduced considerably. The patient entered a 3-month recall system.

At the 6-month visit (delayed by the COVID-19 pandemic), objective compliance assessment revealed a decline: (1) the plaque index increased to Grade II (moderate plaque accumulation, extending beyond the gingival margin in 20%–40% of sites); and (2) patient-reported adherence decreased – the patient self-reported skipping interdental floss use 2–3 times per week due to time constraints, which was corroborated by visible plaque accumulation on proximal surfaces. Bleeding on probing returned to 100%, and deep pockets were detected at teeth 12 (palatal), 24–25 (buccal) and 34–35 (lingual). Preoperative Florida Probe examination confirmed that these sites persisted with probing depths ≥ 6 mm and continuous BOP, and radiographic evaluation revealed localised angular bone defects (e.g. mesial angular defect of tooth 12, buccal bone defect of teeth 24–25). These findings met the surgical intervention criteria: failure of non-surgical therapy to resolve deep pockets (≥ 5 mm) and persistent inflammation, combined with bony defects requiring direct visualisation for thorough debridement. Thus, surgical therapy was conducted on teeth 11, 12, 24, 25, 34 and 45 using papilla preservation flaps (to maintain soft tissue architecture and support periodontal health) and thorough subgingival debridement. Wounds were closed with 4 − 0 silk sutures, and chlorhexidine rinses were continued for 1 week. The recall frequency was shortened to every 1–3 months, and additional oral hygiene counselling was provided to address adherence gaps.

Treatment outcomes

As shown in Fig. 1(b), at the 1-year follow-up (August 2021), the gingiva appeared healthy and pink, with no suppuration or spontaneous bleeding. Objective compliance assessment confirmed sustained improvement: (1) the plaque index was maintained at Grade I overall, with only localised Grade II plaque on teeth 15, 43 and 44 (attributed to difficult-to-clean proximal surfaces); and (2) patient-reported adherence stabilised – quarterly oral hygiene logs documented consistent twice-daily brushing (extended to 1.5 min per session, as advised during 6-month counselling) and daily interdental floss use, with no self-reported gaps in the 3 months before follow-up. In a post-treatment satisfaction survey, the patient rated her overall experience with the Florida Probe + GBT approach as 9/10, highlighting ‘no recurring sensitivity’ and ‘clear progress updates’ as key strengths. When asked if she would recommend the therapy over conventional SRP, she responded affirmatively, stating, ‘GBT was easier to tolerate and I can tell the results last longer’. Plaque control was generally good, with localised plaque noted on teeth 15, 43 and 44. Radiographs showed stable bone levels and slight improvement in density at sites of angular defects. Extraction sites (17, 36) had healed well. Discussion and conclusions

This case demonstrates the effectiveness of combining the Florida Probe assessment with the GBT protocol in managing severe periodontitis. The systematic approach to biofilm control, coupled with precise computerised monitoring, achieved considerable clinical improvements despite the initial severity of disease.

Plaque control represents the cornerstone of periodontal therapy. Dental biofilm acts as a complex polymicrobial community that disrupts periodontal homeostasis through synergistic bacterial interactions [2, 3]. Key periodontal pathogens, such as P. gingivalis, work synergistically with other bacteria to release inflammatory mediators, including IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF, promoting collagenase activity and accelerating tissue destruction. Therefore, effective biofilm disruption reduces pathogenic bacterial load and minimises periodontal tissue damage.

Although traditional SRP also target plaque and calculus removal, the clinical focus often shifts disproportionately towards calculus, potentially neglecting comprehensive biofilm disruption [5]. Guided biofilm therapy addresses this limitation by prioritising plaque visualisation through disclosure, followed by targeted removal using air-polishing before addressing mineralised deposits. Notably, this protocol’s rationale is supported by a randomised controlled trial (RCT) that validated plaque disclosure as a guide for improving biofilm removal efficacy [6]. This approach not only removes existing biofilm more effectively but also delays recolonisation.

Recent literature has explored various adjunctive strategies to complement non-surgical therapy and optimise host response. For instance, domiciliary probiotic protocols have been investigated as a means to modulate the oral microbiome, reduce pathogenic bacterial colonisation and support sustained periodontal health [13]. Although our case focused on integrating objective monitoring (Florida Probe) with biofilm-oriented therapy (GBT), it shares the core objective of these emerging approaches: moving beyond isolated calculus removal to address the multifactorial nature of periodontitis, whether through targeted biofilm disruption, as in GBT, or microbiome modulation, as in probiotic adjuncts. This alignment underscores that our report contributes to the growing body of research emphasising holistic management, rather than merely symptomatic treatment of severe periodontitis. Along similar lines, research on novel local therapeutic formulations with mucoadhesive properties has highlighted their potential for enhancing local treatment efficacy in oral conditions [14]. Though initially explored for aphthous ulcers, such studies resonate with our emphasis on minimally invasive and supportive approaches in periodontal therapy, as they illustrate how innovative delivery systems can bolster the effectiveness of targeted interventions.

In our case, the patient’s initial poor oral hygiene habits were successfully modified through repeated education combined with the GBT protocol. Objective assessment of compliance, via the standardised Silness–Löe plaque index (to quantify plaque accumulation objectively) and patient-reported oral hygiene logs (to track brushing and interdental cleaning frequency), was critical for guiding interventions. For instance, the decline in adherence at 6 months (evidenced by a Grade II plaque index and reduced interdental use) prompted timely reinforcement of oral hygiene counselling and more frequent recall visits, which restored and sustained compliance by the 1-year follow-up. This highlights that objective compliance metrics are essential for linking patient behaviour to treatment outcomes, as subjective assessments alone may miss subtle adherence gaps that impact disease control. The visual feedback provided by plaque disclosure enhanced patient motivation and compliance. Studies, including RCTs, have shown that GBT with glycine or erythritol powder improves patient acceptance and reduces pain perception compared with conventional treatment [7]; another RCT further confirmed that GBT yields comparable or better clinical outcomes than conventional SRP [8], reinforcing the evidence base for our therapeutic choice. Beyond clinical efficacy, patient-centred outcomes, which are key advantages of GBT, were also observed. The patient reported greater comfort during treatment, particularly with glycine-based air-polishing (noting less sensitivity than expected with traditional scaling), and expressed high acceptance of the GBT sequence due to shorter appointment times and minimal post-treatment discomfort. These factors likely contributed to her adherence to recall visits, supporting sustained plaque control. Studies have shown that GBT with glycine or erythritol powder improves patient acceptance and reduces pain perception compared with conventional treatment [7], which aligns with the patient’s experience here. The visual feedback provided by plaque disclosure further enhanced patient motivation and compliance.

The Florida Probe system contributed considerably to treatment success by providing objective, reproducible measurements throughout therapy [10, 11]. The computerised system’s constant probing force eliminated examiner variability, whereas automatic data recording prevented transcription errors. The visual representation of probing depths and bleeding sites enhanced patient understanding and engagement in their treatment.

Risk assessment played a crucial role in treatment planning and monitoring. The patient’s initial high-risk status (100% BOP, 151 sites with PD ≥ 5 mm) indicated aggressive disease requiring intensive intervention. The systematic reduction to low risk (25% BOP, only three sites with PD ≥ 5 mm) at 1-year follow-up demonstrates successful disease control.

Occlusal trauma was identified as a contributing factor, as it can exacerbate periodontal inflammation and bone loss. The bidirectional relationship between occlusal trauma and periodontitis created a cycle of destruction that was addressed through selective occlusal adjustment following initial inflammation control.

The patient’s malocclusion with anterior crowding created plaque-retentive areas that complicated oral hygiene. Despite these anatomical challenges, good plaque control was achieved through intensive education and regular maintenance. This emphasises that even in the presence of unfavourable local factors, adequate biofilm control can maintain periodontal stability – an insight further supported by research highlighting the role of personalised preventive protocols in broader dental populations [15, 16]. For example, remineralising strategies tested in paediatric patients with systemic conditions (e.g. asthma and allergic rhinitis) have shown the importance of tailoring protocols to specific patient needs, as individualised approaches better address unique risk factors and anatomical or physiological challenges [15].

The comprehensive approach combining the Florida Probe assessment with the GBT protocol represents a major advancement compared with traditional non-surgical therapies and manual probing, effectively addressing the limitations of the latter. Traditional non-surgical therapies focus more on the removal of dental calculus, whereas manual probing is subject to considerable variability. In contrast, the comprehensive approach offers distinct advantages. The GBT protocol uses a staining agent to make dental plaque visible and employs air-polishing technology to remove the biofilm. The Florida Probe features constant force, precise measurement and automatic data recording, effectively eliminating the limitations of manual probing. By adopting this comprehensive approach, examiner variability can be reduced by 40% and transcription errors avoided. This combination can also enhance patient motivation to receive treatment. The visual output of the Florida Probe allows patients to directly observe improvements in their oral condition, thereby encouraging adherence to oral care. Unlike the subjective feedback provided by manual probing, this visual display strengthens patients’ determination to maintain oral health. For example, the patient in this case continued to adhere to oral care during the 1-year follow-up, a result closely related to improvements in the probe data. The synergy between precise monitoring and targeted treatment not only achieves stable clinical outcomes but also helps overcome the obstacles faced by traditional therapies.

Additionally, lifestyle and dietary factors, which were not systematically evaluated in this case, may have implications for the treatment outcomes. Numerous studies have highlighted the impact of lifestyle factors such as smoking, alcohol consumption and stress on periodontal health [17, 18]. Smoking, in particular, is a well-established risk factor for periodontitis, as it impairs immune function, reduces blood flow to the gingiva and alters the oral microbiome, leading to increased disease severity and reduced treatment response [17]. Alcohol consumption can also disrupt the oral microbiome balance and increase inflammation, exacerbating periodontal tissue destruction [18]. Chronic stress has been associated with elevated levels of cortisol, which may suppress the immune system and promote the growth of pathogenic bacteria in the oral cavity [19].

Dietary factors also play a crucial role in periodontal health. A diet high in sugar and refined carbohydrates can promote the growth of cariogenic and periodontopathic bacteria, as these nutrients serve as energy sources for bacterial metabolism [20]. In contrast, a diet rich in fruits, vegetables, whole grains and omega-3 fatty acids has been shown to have anti-inflammatory effects and support a healthy oral microbiome [21, 22]. Omega-3 fatty acids, for example, can reduce the production of pro-inflammatory mediators such as IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF, thereby mitigating periodontal inflammation [21]. Antioxidants present in fruits and vegetables can neutralise reactive oxygen species, which contribute to tissue damage in periodontitis [22].

In this case, the patient’s lifestyle and dietary habits were not thoroughly assessed or documented. It is possible that unmeasured factors such as smoking history, alcohol intake, stress levels or dietary patterns may have influenced the initial disease severity and the response to treatment. For instance, if the patient had a diet low in sugar and high in anti-inflammatory nutrients, this may have contributed to the positive treatment outcomes observed. Conversely, if she had engaging in unhealthy lifestyle habits, the treatment results might have been less favourable. Future clinical studies evaluating the effectiveness of periodontal treatments should incorporate comprehensive assessments of lifestyle and dietary factors to better understand their impact on treatment outcomes and develop more personalised therapeutic strategies.

Limitations: The patient’s refusal of periodontal surgery and orthodontic treatment due to financial constraints prevented optimal rehabilitation. The failure to replace missing teeth resulted in occlusal instability and increased mobility of the remaining teeth. These factors highlight the importance of comprehensive multidisciplinary treatment in severe periodontitis cases. Long-term success depends on maintaining effective plaque control through patient compliance and regular professional maintenance. Studies have shown that patients receiving regular supportive periodontal therapy can maintain treatment outcomes for decades with minimal tooth loss. The lack of long-term follow-up (only 1 year) makes it impossible to confirm lasting effectiveness. Periodontitis, as a chronic disease, may recur. The improvement observed over 1 year (a BOP rate of 25%) does not rule out the possibility of future bone loss or deepening of periodontal pockets. This study did not record and quantitatively analyze the imaging indicators (such as alveolar bone height, bone defect depth, bone density value, etc.) before the initial treatment, at the 6-month follow-up and at the 1-year follow-up. It was impossible to accurately quantify the improvement degree of bone loss before and after treatment, and it was difficult to more objectively verify the actual effect of this treatment plan on bone tissue repair. In addition, this study is a single case report. Single-case reports lack the statistical power of large-scale clinical trials or cohort studies, and the results may not be generalizable to the broader population of patients with severe periodontitis. Individual patient factors, such as genetic background, immune response, and compliance, can significantly affect treatment outcomes, making it difficult to extrapolate the effectiveness of the Florida Probe + GBT combination in all patients with similar conditions. Future studies should include larger sample sizes and control groups to validate the findings of this case report. Finally, the absence of microbiological data is a crucial limitation. No tests (such as 16 S rRNA sequencing) were conducted before and after the use of GBT to evaluate changes in pathogens such as P. gingivalis, leaving the association between biofilm control and clinical improvement unvalidated.

In conclusion, this case demonstrates that combining the Florida Probe assessment with the GBT protocol provides an effective approach for managing severe periodontitis. The emphasis on biofilm control, supported by accurate computerised monitoring, achieved certain clinical improvements, including reduced inflammation, decreased probing depths and improved tooth stability. Although complete rehabilitation was limited by patient factors, the substantial improvement in periodontal health validates this treatment approach. Future studies should investigate the long-term outcomes of GBT in severe periodontitis and explore strategies to improve patient acceptance of comprehensive treatment.
