Advances in paratuberculosis research and control: a comprehensive expert review
Authors: R Arrazuria, M Alonso-Hearn, RA Juste, A Facciuolo, AP Koets, H Köhler, R Guatteo, IR Grant, K Plain, CG Mc Aloon, D Graham, C Verdugo, M Ricchi, SL Mundo, N Elguezabal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis review summarizes the 2025 update of the DISCONTOOLS gap analysis for Paratuberculosis, which was based on scientific literature review combined with expert knowledge. It summarizes recent advances across key areas including diagnostics, epidemiology, pathogenesis, vaccination, genetic resistance and alternative therapies. Progress in understanding host-pathogen interactions and immune responses is highlighted alongside developments in diagnostic tools such as molecular assays and phage-based detection. Vaccination strategies continue to evolve with efforts to improve efficacy and reduce interference with bovine Tuberculosis testing. Genetic studies reveal certain heritability of resistance traits, supporting breeding programs as complementary control measures. Alternative therapies including antibodies, mycobacteriophages, and probiotics show promise for early-life infection management. This review also addresses potential public health implications mostly related to the association with Crohn’s disease. Despite advances, significant knowledge gaps persist, emphasizing the need for advanced control strategies or therapies to effectively manage Paratuberculosis in ruminants.Keywords: Johne’s disease, Paratuberculosis, Control, Diagnosis, Ruminants

Introduction

Although described more than a century ago, Paratuberculosis (PTBC) remains a major unresolved animal health challenge, despite several initiatives for its control [1]. Significant advances have been made in understanding Mycobacterium avium subsp. paratuberculosis (MAP) host interactions, immune response, diagnostics, and epidemiology. However, the disease remains widespread and current control measures are often insufficient or slow in producing significant outcomes. This reflects not only persistent knowledge gaps and unmet research needs but also challenges in the adoption and effective implementation of existing control strategies.

The DISCONTOOLS (DISease CONtrol TOOLS) initiative funded by the European Commission, provides a key framework for identifying research priorities aimed at delivering solutions for farmers, by supporting the development of improved control tools to reduce the burden of animal diseases [2]. As an open-access database, DISCONTOOLS informs strategic research agendas for both public and private funders through systematic gap analyses conducted by expert panels that assess existing capacities and unmet needs for each disease. This review presents the 2025 update of the DISCONTOOLS gap analysis for PTBC, summarizing recent advances, highlighting remaining knowledge gaps, and identifying priority research areas to support more effective and sustainable PTBC control strategies.

Diagnostic tools

Background: what is accepted

Currently, the identification of MAP-infected animals is based on either the direct detection of the pathogen or its genetic material in faeces or tissues or on indirect methods targeting the host immune response against MAP [3]. Mycobacterial culture is still considered the standard method for the detection of viable MAP, providing information about bacterial load in organs or faecal shedding patterns. Positive cultures, which can take weeks or even months to emerge, must be confirmed by a second step, either proof of mycobactin dependence by further culture or detection of genome fragments by PCR [4]. The direct detection of genetic material from MAP using quantitative PCR (qPCR) assays has become increasingly important over the last two decades because molecular methods are rapid and allow high sample throughput. In addition to robustly validated in house qPCR methods [5], several commercial qPCR kits based predominantly on detecting the MAP-specific genome fragment IS900 are globally available [6]. Despite qPCR being able to detect a few genome equivalents, detecting both viable and dead MAP, the analytical sensitivity of the method is limited by the efficacy of DNA extraction from diagnostic samples [7]. The true sensitivity of qPCR methods relative to culture is still debated, at least for faecal specimens, since for samples with low load of MAP it has been reported that liquid culture seems to be more sensitive than qPCR [6] whereas approaches using solid media appear to be less sensitive [5]. More recently, digital PCR (dPCR) has been used to quantify MAP load in faecal and blood samples [8, 9]. Indirect diagnosis of PTBC relies mainly on detection of antibodies against MAP in serum, plasma or milk by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs), which are relatively inexpensive, rapid and high-throughput, although their sensitivity is limited, particularly in early stages of infection. While pre-adsorption of the samples with Mycobacterium phlei is a common feature to reduce non-specific responses, other test characteristics vary considerably, such as antigen preparation and conjugate reactivity. Methods for the detection of cell-mediated immunity, such as the antigen-based skin test or the IFNγ release assay (IGRA), which are the backbone of in vivo diagnosis of TB in animals, have lost importance or failed to gain acceptance in the diagnosis of PTBC.

All of the traditional methods for the in vivo diagnosis of MAP infection listed above have limitations due to the pathogenesis of the disease [3]. The sensitivity of faecal culture as well as qPCR detection is limited particularly in those stages of the disease when shedding of MAP bacteria is low or intermittent [6]. Positive ELISA results depend on the expression of a humoral immune response, which also varies by disease stage and host species. Consequently, sensitivity ranges from ~ 15–80% depending on infection stage, while specificity is typically > 90–95%. This variability, partly driven by differences in assay conditions (e.g., incubation times), highlights the need for standardized ELISA protocols. However, analytical refinements to these traditional methods will not help to close the fundamental diagnostic gap in relation to PTBC, which is the inability of currently used tests to detect MAP-infected animals at an early stage of the disease. In recent years, to overcome these limitations, other approaches have been developed for PTBC diagnosis. The new methods are based on the use of novel reagents like Mycobacterium-specific phages, or on information gained from application of omic methodologies aimed at identifying new specific and sensitive targets for early detection of MAP-infected animals.

Phage-based diagnostics

Phage-based methods for the detection of viable MAP in veterinary specimens potentially offer a rapid (one day to results) alternative to traditional culture. The two available patented phage-based tests (the Actiphage™ Rapid assay [10] and the Phagomagnetic separation (PhMS)-qPCR assay [11]; are still undergoing validation to assess their diagnostic potential. Specificity for MAP lies with the qPCR assay employed after the phage-mediated DNA extraction step, rather than with the mycobacteriophage involved. Although the endpoint of both the phage-based tests is qPCR, only DNA from viable MAP cells is detected because the D29 mycobacteriophages employed cannot multiply within dead MAP cells, and so lysis of dead MAP cells to release progeny phages and host DNA does not happen. Both tests provide an indication of the presence of viable MAP within < 24 h and the qPCR Cq result can be used to provide an estimate of numbers of viable MAP present if a standard curve is run alongside test samples. More detail of how the two tests operate is provided by Grant [11].

Despite the interest within the MAP research community to apply the phage-based tests as diagnostic tools, Martins and collaborators [12] highlighted that there was a knowledge gap in relation to test characteristics (sensitivity and specificity) when applied to faeces and blood. Recently published studies have begun to supply this missing information on performance characteristics of the phage-based assays applied to test bovine milk, faeces and blood [13–15], as well as sub-clinically infected cows and calves [16, 17].

Omics-informed diagnostic approaches

In recent years, omic approaches have gained importance in the field of PTBC, allowing the discovery, in some cases, of new features within the pathogenesis of PTBC. Transcriptomic approaches, including the evaluation of microRNAs (MiRNAs), as well as proteomic and metabolomic methodologies have been explored with the ultimate goal of identifying new biomarkers for the early detection of PTBC in animals [18].

Transcriptomic and proteomic analyses consistently highlight pathways related to lipid metabolism (notably cholesterol) and immune functions such as antigen presentation, despite variability across datasets. While several MiRNAs have been proposed as potential biomarkers [19], individual MiRNAs lack diagnostic value [18]. However, a panel of 24 MiRNAs combined with predictive modelling showed promise for diagnosing MAP-infected animals [20]. Proteomic studies have identified differentially expressed proteins, particularly those involved in iron metabolism and immune responses, but none of these candidates have yet been fully validated for PTBC diagnosis [21, 22]. These changes, including signatures of energy deficit and altered protein turnover, can appear before advanced clinical signs or strong antibody responses [23].

Metabolomic studies, building on earlier Nuclear Magnetic Resonance work [24], consistently show that MAP infection alters energy, lipid, and amino acid metabolism in both experimentally and naturally infected cattle [25–28]. Consequently, metabolomics is currently best viewed as an advanced diagnostic approach with potential to complement existing tests, for example through multi-marker panels to identify high-risk animals for confirmatory faecal PCR or culture and to better characterize host responses during infection [12]. Although metabolomic models can achieve high apparent accuracy in small or controlled studies, assays are not yet standardized, and findings require validation [12].

Epidemiology and transmission

Overview of the accepted view in terms of how MAP is transmitted between and within-herds

Between-herd transmission

Infection between herds is primarily considered to be the result of the introduction of one or more infected animals into the herd. Modelling studies have demonstrated that the probability of a herd becoming infected increases with both the absolute number of animal introductions into the herd and the number of different source herds those animals come from [29]. However, in the field, the ability to attribute the source of infection to the herd is complicated by the considerable lag between the initial introduction and the detection of a new herd infection, as well as the considerable variability in this period. Consequently, observational studies do not always detect the introduction of animals into a herd as a significant risk factor [30]. Although the role and contribution of other sources of infection including wildlife, and/or introduction via contaminated visitors’ equipment has been described, it has not been properly quantified.

Within-herd transmission

MAP is transmitted mainly by the faecal-oral route, where MAP is shed in the faeces of infected animals, contaminating soil, pasture, feed, and/or water [31–34]. While the ingestion of contaminated colostrum and milk represents a known secondary transmission route, recent epidemiological reviews have also highlighted the importance of bioaerosols (dust particles) as a viable transmission vehicle, particularly in intensive housing systems [12, 31]. Additionally, the arrangement of facilities within farm has also been identified as a risk factor [17]. Cattle susceptibility to MAP is not constant, with the greatest risk occurring during the first months of life. Although susceptibility declines with age, where animals older than 12 months are generally considered resistant [35], recent evidence regarding calf-to-calf transmission challenges the historical view of calves as “dead-end” hosts during the latent period following their infection [36]. Indeed, experimental data suggests an for calf-to-calf transmission ranging from 0.1 to 3.2, indicating significant transmission potential among young stock [37]. Even though it is accepted that primo-infection occurs during the first weeks of life, clinical disease commonly does not manifest in animals until they are at least 3–4 years old, where an average of ~ 1 clinical case for every 100 cows per year has been reported [38]. However, clinical cases have been reported in animals as young as 6 months and as old as 15 years [35, 39]. In addition to age-dependent susceptibility, variation in the genetic susceptibility to infection has also been observed primarily in dairy cattle, with heritability estimates ranging from 0.03 to 0.27 [21].

In utero/vertical transmission

Vertical (in utero) transmission of MAP has been well established [40]. In the field and in observational studies, differentiating between true in utero infection and ‘vertical’ perinatal transmission via faecal-oral transmission is difficult.

Major drawbacks

Whilst the introduction of infected animals is the primary route of the introduction of infection, understanding the role of alternative sources including the potential for wildlife, fomites and infected equipment remains unanswered. Understanding the contribution of these alternative transmission routes is important since it determines the extent to which interventions to prevent introduction should be implemented. Recent advances in pathogen genomics may aid in establishing sources of infection. However, to date, this has been undermined by the relatively low genetic diversity within the pathogen [41].

With regard to within-herd transmission, the contribution of different transmission routes, for example dust particles and in utero transmission, remains uncertain. However, understanding the relative contribution of these transmission routes to the overall burden of PTBC within the herd is important since it directly impacts culling and farm management decisions. Similarly, whilst advances in host susceptibility to disease have been well investigated, there is opportunity to include the genetic evaluation of the infectivity for infected individuals, as recent work on Mycobacterium bovis in this area has been promising [42]. In parallel, there is a need for improved methods to estimate individual infectivity from longitudinal diagnostic data (e.g. serial ELISA results analysed with hidden Markov models) [43], which, together with comorbidities, age and productivity, could support more informed prioritisation of culling decisions.

Pathogenesis of MAP infection in ruminants

Understanding of MAP pathogenesis in ruminants has shifted toward detailed host–pathogen interaction, especially innate immune modulation, host genetics, and within-host dynamics, driven by ex vivo / in vitro and in silico approaches such as organoid and mathematical modelling. These studies have clarified early MAP interactions with intestinal epithelial cells [44], macrophages during early granuloma formation [45, 46], and the influence of host genetics and MAP virulence [21, 47]. Advances in (dual) RNAseq have further highlighted metabolic and immune pathways involved in PTBC pathogenesis, affecting both pro- and anti-inflammatory responses [48–50].

Host-pathogen interaction

The host–MAP interaction is exceptionally complex, involving epithelial invasion, manipulation of innate and adaptive immunity, and extensive host regulatory remodelling at transcriptional, metabolic, epigenetic, and post-transcriptional levels. These interactions underscore that PTBC is not simply the result of immune failure but rather of immune modulation orchestrated by MAP.

The intestinal epithelium represents the primary portal of MAP entry. Advanced in vitro models, including bovine intestinal organoids, have demonstrated that MAP preferentially targets M cells, exploiting β1-integrin–mediated adhesion and providing a route for translocation into underlying lymphoid tissues [51]. Specific MAP surface molecules, such as Mce proteins and MAP1203, promote bacterial binding, invasion, and intracellular persistence within epithelial cells [52, 53]. These interactions allow MAP to traverse the epithelial barrier while minimizing overt inflammation. Epithelial-conditioned MAP antigens induce prolonged Th17 polarization while suppressing phagolysosomal maturation in immune cells, contributing to bacterial survival [54].

Macrophages are the principal intracellular niche for MAP. Multiple transcriptomic and proteomic studies have demonstrated that MAP reprograms macrophage responses to favour bacterial persistence. Early infection is characterized by altered Toll-like receptor signalling (notably TLR4), impaired antigen processing, and suppression of bactericidal pathways [55]. Proteomic analyses reveal macrophage remodelling, including cytoskeletal changes that promote bacterial trafficking and dissemination [56]. Environmental survival strategies further enhance MAP virulence. The ability of MAP to persist within free-living amoebae may pre-adapt the pathogen to intracellular survival in mammalian macrophages, reinforcing its resilience in hostile host environments [57, 58].

Neutrophils are short lived effector cells able to efficiently kill pathogens using reactive oxygen species, lytic enzymes and extracellular traps. Neutrophils have been understudied in MAP pathogenesis; however, recent data suggest that they are also able to efficiently kill MAP in vitro in contrast to macrophages [59] and they can be trained by vaccination to increase antimicrobial mechanisms such as NETosis and phagocytosis against MAP [60]. Other work indicated that MAP produces an extracellular DNase (MAP3916c) which can attack the extracellular traps produced by the neutrophils [61].

Once internalized, MAP manipulates host cellular pathways to support survival. MAP manipulates host lipid metabolism, inducing cholesterol accumulation within macrophages, a hallmark of pathogenic mycobacterial infections [62]. This lipid-rich environment supports bacterial survival and limits immune clearance. In parallel, MAP virulence determinants such as Mce and RelA proteins are critical for intracellular persistence and represent potential therapeutic targets [52, 63]. MAP3773c, a bacterial transcription factor, interacts with the host ferroportin1 coding region, potentially modulating intracellular iron availability essential for MAP persistence [64]. MAP-derived antigens such as MAP1981c and malate dehydrogenase can drive dendritic cell maturation and Th1 polarization, underscoring the pathogen’s ability to actively shape host immunity [65, 66].

Mechanism of host response

MAP establishes a persistent intestinal infection after translocating across the epithelial barrier, infecting tissue-resident macrophages in Peyer’s patches [67, 68]. Host immune responses to MAP have been studied predominantly in circulating blood immune cells, reflecting systemic immunity. In contrast, investigations of local responses at the intestinal site of infection remain limited but have revealed marked regional differences in immune responses to MAP along the gastrointestinal tract [69–72] and across distinct pathological forms of PTBC [73]. This suggests that systemic immune responses may not accurately reflect immune dynamics occurring within the intestinal microenvironment. Systemic responses, such as antigen-specific interferon gamma production and T cell lymphoproliferation, develop slowly after infection [74] despite the rapid induction of local immune responses [68, 75]. In blood, MAP-specific T cell responses can correlate strongly with disease severity rather than with effective bacterial control. As disease progresses, systemic T cell immunity wanes while humoral responses become more prominent [74]. Earlier models proposed a polarized Th1-to-Th2 shift [76], including increased local regulatory T cell activity [77], as a driver of disease progression. However, accumulating evidence cautions against these oversimplified paradigms [35]. γδT-cell subsets (WC1⁺ and WC1⁻) show differential distribution and activation in infected calves, suggesting specialized roles in mucosal surveillance and resilience [78]. Locally, granuloma formation may contribute to partial control of MAP while simultaneously facilitating bacterial persistence. Dissemination of MAP through lymphatics to peripheral tissues such as the mammary gland, results in MAP shedding in colostrum and milk. The marked variability in immune responses among animals suggests an important role for host genetics in disease susceptibility and resistance, underscoring the need for further immunogenetic studies [21, 79]. In addition, recent studies have revealed substantial perturbations in alternative splicing, microRNAs and long non-coding RNA expression in MAP-infected animals [80, 81]. Although cell-mediated immunity is thought to be central to protection, definitive immune correlates of protection remain poorly defined. Nevertheless, recent studies have provided new insights into mucosal immune responses associated with vaccine-induced protection [82–84]. Notably, not all infected animals progress to clinical disease. Experimental infection models demonstrate that some calves exhibit resilience, characterized by controlled bacterial loads and balanced immune responses [85]. Transcription factors such as Early Growth Response Factor 4 (EGR4) have emerged as potential regulators of this resilient phenotype, linking immune regulation with tissue homeostasis [22, 86]. Advanced analyses of intestinal T- and B-cell repertoires induced by MAP may hold promise for elucidating MAP-specific antigens recognized during infection. Beyond T cell-mediated immunity, further clarification is needed into the contributions of local innate immune cells and responses [73, 78, 87]. Collectively, MAP intestinal infection is a complex and dynamic interplay between pathogen and the ruminant intestinal immune system. The spatial and temporal dynamics that dictate disease trajectory remain poorly defined highlighting the need for continued investigation into pathogen-driven immune evasion and host-mediated protective responses.

Control of paratuberculosis

As PTBC is an endemic infection with a high proportion of cases with subclinical or latent forms that are very challenging to detect [88], control of its spread needs to rely on strategies that are capable to detect those latent infections. The rapid identification of these animals should be considered pivotal in the reduction of the shedding and decreasing the load of MAP in every single herd. However, the diagnostic sensitivity of commonly used tests aimed at detecting sub-clinically infected animals is extremely poor [89]. The predominance of sub-clinically infected animals with limited test options is reflected in the widespread prevalence of PTBC throughout the world, even if its official reporting is rather very low [90].

In general terms, and considering all susceptible species, PTBC control is based on three main approaches and a fourth one in development. Following the example of TB and other endemic infections, testing and culling is currently the most widely accepted strategy for management of the disease in cattle given their high individual economic value. A second approach is the use of hygiene practices to control the transmission of the disease to susceptible young livestock. These strategies are, however, unaffordable for small ruminants and therefore the predominant strategy for them is vaccination which is the third one. The fourth and more recent strategy is breeding for genetic resistance that, thanks to the popularization of genotyping for productive traits, has already been shown to hold great promise for the future and is also discussed in Sect. 7 of this review.

As mentioned earlier, currently, test-and-cull strategies are the most widely used approach to disease control and are often the only option available and pursued in most countries with a cattle PTBC control plan. The limitations of test and cull strategies have been discussed in a previous review [91] and essentially are defined by the lack of sensitivity of all current diagnostic assays, as well as the costs of changing the subsequent necessary population management at farm level.

The mainstay of PTBC diagnostics for farmers in most countries is indirect ELISA [1] of which several commercial kits are available. This method, however, as shown in a large slaughterhouse pathological and microbiological study, is unable to detect most latently infected animals [88].

Isolation of MAP is hampered by its slow growth rate, mycobactin dependence and the sequestration of bacteria within granulomas, making culture laborious and time-consuming. While ELISA and faecal PCR (FPCR) have progressively complemented or replaced culture in many settings, the choice of diagnostic approach varies across regions depending on economic and logistical constraints.

From a control perspective, FPCR-based test-and-cull strategies enable the identification of shedding animals, particularly high shedders, and can contribute to reducing environmental contamination and transmission. Recent work shows that FPCR detection method allows control of the infection in less than 10 years by annual testing [92–95]. However, such approaches are costly, and their feasibility depends on the production system and regional context. In contrast, very recent data [95] suggest that test-and-cull strategies based solely on ELISA testing cannot succeed in eradicating the disease. Consequently, increasing attention has been given to complementary approaches, including vaccination and improved hygienic management. Measures such as colostrum and waste milk treatments (e.g. copper ion-based approaches) have been explored to reduce MAP transmission [96, 97]; however, evidence remains limited and further work is needed to demonstrate their practical applicability under field conditions.

Early reports of paratuberculosis vaccination describe its use in sheep in Iceland, followed by its application in goats in Norway. Currently vaccination is the standard control measure for sheep in Australia and in Spain for both sheep and goats. It is also in use in camels and in different species in zoo collections. Vaccination combined with FPCR might be also very useful in cattle [95].

In summary, although official PTBC reporting is low, the disease is considered important and submitted to control programs in the main ruminant domestic species industry throughout the world. Test-and-cull strategies based on anti-MAP antibody detection have been widely used in cattle; however, increasing evidence supports the complementary or alternative use of FPCR, particularly for identifying shedding animals. Vaccination is the choice for control in other species and is also being proposed to complement FPCR test-and-cull in cattle in areas free or with low TB prevalence.

Vaccination

Vaccination against PTBC dates to the 1920s, starting with effective live vaccines widely used in cattle in France, although the use declined by mid-20th century due to interference with bovine TB testing but continued in small ruminants. By the late 20th century, live vaccines were largely replaced by an inactivated commercial vaccine (strain 316 F) driven by safety concerns and encouraging results observed in the field. The emergence of widespread PTBC infection in Australian sheep severely impacted the national ovine industry. The huge economic and social costs of flock stamping out disease control measures provoked the adoption of vaccination, positioning Australia as the world’s largest vaccine user [98] and generating strong field evidence of its efficacy [99]. Since then, vaccination has been extended to other domestic and wild ruminants, lowering costs associated with frequent testing and culling while preserving valuable animals.

Vaccination was widely used in European cattle before being restricted due to concerns about interference with the single intradermal test for bovine TB diagnostics and an increased reliance on management, infection detection, and culling. This shift has been less effective than expected, mainly due to the low sensitivity of the commonly used immune tests. More recently, a large field trial showed that vaccination combined with faecal PCR (FPCR) and targeted culling can control PTBC within 6 to 10 years [95], supporting the idea that even imperfect vaccines reduce transmission by decreasing environmental contamination and enhancing herd resistance. Vaccination is more cost-effective than the test-and-cull strategy for cattle (particularly in TB-free regions) and especially for exotic species.

PTBC vaccines are administered parenterally in the dewlap or in the neck. Initially administered in early life stages, vaccination was later recommended for older animals due to presumed stronger immunity. However, observed low efficacy and high cross-reactivity in older animals, along with insights from human TB vaccination, have led to renewed emphasis on early vaccination within the first three months, when survival benefits are greatest [95]. Although the currently licensed vaccine might contribute to basic control needs of the ruminant industry and its combination with FPCR allows sharp reduction of the prevalence in approximately 10 years [92–95], current constraints on its use in cattle motivate continued efforts to develop enhanced vaccines with no interference.

Vaccination in PTBC faces two challenges; cross-reactivity with PTBC detection and cross-reactivity with TB that can be reduced with the use of the comparative test. In any case, the development of a DIVA (Differentiating Infected from Vaccinated Animals) vaccine will enable the future coexistence of vaccination, PTBC monitoring and TB eradication programs. Currently, no DIVA or marker vaccine is commercially available, although several experimental approaches to circumvent interference are under investigation. These include the evaluation of alternative vaccination routes [100, 101], the development of recombinant subunit vaccines targeting MAP antigens such as Ag85A, superoxide dismutase, Ag85B and 74 F which induce strong, targeted immune responses while avoiding cross-reactivity with TB diagnostics [102]; live attenuated MAP vaccines, causing reduced cross-reactivity to M. bovis PPD [103]; and MAP specific peptides designed to minimize cross-reactivity with M. bovis antigens [104]. Progress in diagnostic differentiation has also been made through IFN-γ assays employing ESAT-6 and CFP-10 antigens, which show great potential to distinguish bovine TB infections from MAP-induced immune response [105], as well as through the development of new lateral flow assay–based diagnostic tools that detect host proteins [106], potentially providing an accessible and rapid method to differentiate M. bovis infection from vaccination or other mycobacterial exposures [107]. Efforts to improve MAP vaccines encompass a variety of approaches, including fusion protein–based formulations [108], nanoparticle-based delivery systems [109], and subunit vaccines targeting the Mce protein family [110] and the development of new recombinant live attenuated vaccines [111]. Beyond veterinary applications and although MAP has not been definitively recognized as the causative agent of Crohn’s disease (CD), a phase 1b clinical trial has been conducted to evaluate the safety, tolerability, and immunogenicity of simian adenovirus and poxvirus vectored vaccines targeting Mycobacterium avium complex subspecies in patients with active CD [112]. Moreover, recent advances in comparative genomics, proteomics, and transcriptomics have enabled the identification of novel antigenic targets that might be capable of eliciting protective immune responses while preserving diagnostic specificity.

Despite huge progress, important aspects of protective immunity against PTBC remain to be fully elucidated. Correlates of protection and the host mechanisms involved in controlling MAP persistence and shedding are not yet completely defined, which continues to challenge vaccine development. Immune markers that reliably predict vaccine efficacy are still being refined, and the influence of host genetic factors on vaccine responses is poorly defined. Although antibodies were previously considered to play a limited role, growing evidence indicates that humoral responses contribute to protection [31], underscoring the value of continued fundamental research. Additionally, correlates of protection and translation from laboratory animal to smalls ruminants needs to be defined to accurately evaluate vaccine efficacy in short-term infection models.

The development of improved vaccines requires substantial investment, involving preclinical research, safety assessments, efficacy trials, and regulatory approval processes. Field trials are especially demanding due to the long incubation period of the disease and the variability in natural infection pressure. The time required to develop a new licensed vaccine could range from five to ten years, depending on how rapidly promising candidates can be identified and validated through laboratory and field evaluations. Accelerating this process would benefit greatly from the implementation of reliable high-throughput screening methods. Although the production and manufacturing of MAP vaccines is technically achievable, significant regulatory and policy challenges persist. For instance, vaccines based on genetically modified organisms may face restrictions, and environmental regulations apply to field trials involving such organisms. Despite these obstacles, the economic and epidemiological advantages of vaccination strongly support continued investment in this field.

In summary, vaccination remains the most powerful, practical and economically efficient measure available to control PTBC. The currently available killed vaccines significantly reduce clinical disease, bacterial shedding and have other non-specific effects [113]. Although their use in cattle is limited by diagnostic interference with bovine TB, its use in small ruminants has been proven very successful. To achieve broader and safer application, next-generation DIVA vaccines capable of immunologically differentiating infected from vaccinated animals are needed to make it even more cost-efficient. Progress towards this goal will depend on advancing our understanding of MAP immunology, identifying key protective antigens, and developing efficient systems to evaluate vaccine candidates. Despite the scientific and economic challenges, investment in improved vaccination strategies offers great potential benefits for animal health, public health, and the sustainability of ruminant production worldwide.

Genetic determinants of susceptibility and resistance to paratuberculosis in ruminants

The application of animal genetics in breeding programs has become a major driver of efficient livestock production, not only by enhancing performance and productivity but also by increasing resilience and promoting health of livestock while improving their longevity.

Host genetics and candidate genes

Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in the 3′UTR microsatellite region of SLC11A1 (solute carrier family 11 member 1) gene have been associated with increased resistance to PTBC in sheep, suggesting that genetic variations can influence macrophage function and pathogen clearance [114]. Structural analysis of caprine and bovine SLC11A1 indicates that sequence variation may affect protein stability and divalent cation transport, thereby modulating host susceptibility [115]. SNPs in toll-like receptor (TLR) genes have been associated with differential susceptibility to MAP infection in cattle [116]. Functional studies using CRISPR-Cas9-mediated knockout of TLR4 in bovine epithelial cells demonstrated altered inflammatory responses to MAP, underscoring the importance of TLR-mediated innate immunity in host resistance [117]. Specific major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class II haplotypes have been linked to either susceptibility or resilience to MAP infection in cattle, with effects observed in both vaccinated and unvaccinated animals [118], suggesting that host genetics may interact with vaccination efficacy and that breeding for resistance could complement existing vaccination strategies.

Phenotyping

Most genetic association studies rely on antemortem diagnostic phenotypes from serum or milk ELISA, FPCR, or culture. Navarro-Gonzalez et al. [119] highlighted that reliable phenotypes should integrate quantitative faecal shedding and longitudinal serology to better capture disease progression. Although MAP infected cattle show lesions of varying severity, genetic associations with PTB pathology were only recently explored [120]. A major limitation for health trait evaluation is the challenge of collecting accurate largescale individual phenotypes, which must be precise, repeatable, and comparable across labs and countries. To overcome this, reductionist strategies targeting specific subsystems—especially immune cell function—are gaining interest. Ex vivo functional assays have already helped identify SNPs, Quantitative Trait Locus (QTL), and immune mechanisms underlying MAP resistance [121, 122].

Genetic architecture and heritability

Heritability estimates show that susceptibility to MAP infection is moderately heritable (0.03–0.27), similar to other health traits selected in dairy breeding programs. Medium density genotyping and imputation to whole- -genome sequence (WGS) data have enabled discovery of SNPs, QTL, and candidate genes linked to susceptibility, tolerance, and resistance in dairy cattle [21]. A WGS-based Genome-Wide Association Study (GWAS) identified QTL explaining up to 28% of genetic variance for MAP resistance, including immune- related- genes [123]. In sheep, WGS- based -GWAS revealed multiple regions associated with MAP antibody responses, confirming the polygenic nature of resistance [124]. Most associated SNPs lie in noncoding regulatory regions, implying effects via gene-expression modulation, though few causal mutations or eQTLs for PTBC susceptibility have been validated [72]. Cis-eQTLs can modify expression levels or timing and thereby influence phenotypes. Integrated genomic–transcriptomic methods such as Summary Mendelian Randomization (SMR) can identify cis-eQTLs with functional effects. Recently, SMR integrating RNASeq data from animals with PTB lesions and GWAS data from 813 culled animals detected two -cis-eQTLs regulating EGR4 and MGC134040, both involved in NF-κβ -mediated inflammation [22].

Genetic models and implications for breeding and disease control

Animals genotyped with medium density SNP chips can now be evaluated for PTBC susceptibility or resistance solely from their genomic profile. France introduced a PTBC genetic index for Holsteins in 2024 and for Normande cattle in 2025, supported by previous phenotypic and genomic studies [119, 123]. Using a single-step Genomic Best Linear Unbiased Prediction (ssGBLUP) approach combining genotypes with serology and faecal shedding data, Sanchez et al. [125] estimated Genomic Estimated Breeding Values (GEBVs) for MAP resistance, which showed moderate heritability (0.14) and identified 16 major genomic regions. GEBVs demonstrated moderate reliability (0.55), adequate for applied selection. Four categorical resistance indices (from “very sensitive” to “resistant”) have been integrated into French Holstein and Normande breeding programs [126], enabling breeders to select resistant animals and manage high risk individuals. A similar approach is planned for the Spanish Holstein breeding program using extensive phenotypes (serology, shedding, culture, histopathology, macrophage MAP load, IFNγ response) combined with genomic data [120–122, 127].

Biemans et al. [29] used a stochastic model to evaluate how genetic improvement interacts with management measures such as test and cull and calf separation. Their simulations showed that herds with higher genetic resistance maintain lower MAP prevalence even with moderately optimized management. More recently, Comper et al. [128] incorporated genetic resistance into dynamic compartmental models to assess long term effects of selective breeding, targeted culling, and infection segregation. Together, these studies show that integrating genomic selection for MAP resistance with biosecurity and diagnostic measures can sustainably reduce herd prevalence and economic losses.

Impact, knowledge gaps and future directions

Genetic improvement using disease resistance markers is a medium to long term strategy whose impact depends on selection pressure, yet its effects accumulate permanently across generations and may ultimately enable disease eradication. Selecting animals with greater PTBC resistance would particularly benefit regions with high prevalence and where vaccination is prohibited due to interference with bovine TB diagnostics. In addition to susceptibility, consideration of genetic traits influencing infectivity could further reduce the risk of transmission [42]. Once these traits become established in the population, animals could be preferentially used as replacements, providing products with lower risk of MAP transmission, improved market value, and safer stock for herd replacement.

Despite progress, key challenges persist. More precise phenotypes, broader use of immune functional assays, and crossbreed validation in dairy, beef, and small ruminants are needed to improve applicability. Functional confirmation of gene candidates remains limited, making integration of transcriptomic, proteomic, and genomic data essential for moving from association to causation. Additionally, combining genomic and epigenetic information could enhance prediction of resistance phenotypes, while in vitro CRISPR Cas9 gene editing offers insights into causal mechanisms and future intervention targets.

Alternative or additional therapies

As stated in previous sections, test-and-cull or vaccination with their restraints are the main control tools that the PTBC community depends on. Both strategies protect young stock indirectly by eliminating MAP positive infective older animals and/or by protecting these. A replacer would not give a positive result in testing, and a vaccination requires time to induce a protective immune response at the individual level, creating a susceptibility window during which no specific preventive interventions are currently available for this age group. In this context, researchers are exploring other approaches that can be applied in the initial phases of the bovine life cycle, and these are described in this subsection.

Antibodies

MAP infection may result in early elimination by innate immunity or in latent persistence within intestinal granulomas. Failure to control infection activates adaptive immunity, with macrophages and dendritic cells stimulating lymphocytes. Th1 cells produce IFNγ and TNF, promoting macrophage activation. Granulomas contain B and T cells together with innate populations, enabling MAP persistence for years. Increasing evidence indicates that humoral immunity also plays an active role against intracellular pathogens [129, 130], which is particularly relevant in calves receiving passive antibodies through colostrum.

Several models have evaluated antibody-mediated modulation of MAP responses. In vitro studies demonstrate that MAP-specific antibodies against p34 Cx or LAM enhance MAP uptake by macrophages, increase NF-κB activation, improve intracellular killing, and counteract MAP-induced inhibition of apoptosis, thereby increasing infected macrophage death and TNF secretion [131], which promotes pro-inflammatory and antimicrobial responses. As a consequence, macrophages show improved control of MAP intracellular viability.

In vivo evidence from a calf ileal loop model shows that MAP preincubated with antibodies from MAP positive cows reduces tissue invasion and bacterial load compared to antibodies from healthy donors. High levels of specific IgG1 and IgG2 were associated with this effect, along with reduced early inflammation, indicating antibodies hinder MAP entry at the intestinal portal [132].

The presence of antibodies against MAP reduced early histopathological changes, suggesting a dampened inflammatory response and lower MAP invasion at the intestinal portal, indicating a protective role for antibodies from infected cows [132]. These findings support immunoprophylactic strategies that induce early, specific antibody responses. LAM immunisation elicited predominantly IgG1, reduced MAP shedding after challenge, and, unlike whole-mycobacteria vaccines, did not interfere with IFN-based diagnostics. The observed post-challenge decline in antibody levels likely reflects their utilisation during early intestinal replication. Together with enhanced MAP uptake, improved intracellular control, induction of apoptosis, and reduced tissue invasion, these data demonstrate that antibodies actively contribute to host defence, challenging the classical Th1/Th2 paradigm and highlighting the potential of antibody-targeted immunoprophylaxis in PTBC [132]. These findings support the possibility of antibody-mediated interventions (e.g. passive immunisation), although their feasibility, cost-effectiveness and efficacy under field conditions remain to be established.

Mycobacteriophages

Phages are viruses that can effectively target and kill bacteria making them a promising strategy against bacterial infections as an alternative to antibiotics. Until recently the framework under which phage therapy was regulated was absent or vague in most countries. This is changing and the interest in phage therapy is being fuelled by the increase in antimicrobial resistance (AMR) [133]. Recent advances in dietary phages as alternatives to antibiotics in food animal production have been reviewed elsewhere [134], whereas the work that has been developed in PTBC will be reviewed in this subsection.

Many studies have reported the isolation and characterization of bacteriophages against MAP [135–137]. The use of mycobacteriophages to detect viable MAP for diagnosis has been described in a previous section in this review (see section on Phage based diagnostics). The application of mycobacteriophages as therapy or prevention is more limited. In a recent study, mycobacteriophages administered orally to calves were able to protect these from MAP replication in tissues [138]. The two month treatment with these bacteriophages was able to entirely eliminate MAP faecal shedding by week 10 after oral challenge with MAP. These results pose mycobacteriophages as a promising intervention against PTBC for newborn calves, a period that comprises the susceptibility window that cannot be covered by vaccination. The phage cocktail can be administered at birth and complement other control measures.

Probiotics

Probiotics are live non-pathogenic organisms that can benefit host health by competition with other pathogenic microbes, and they have been proposed as supplements that can increase production and improve overall health in cattle [139].

Probiotics based on Lactobacillus species have been tested against MAP in mice [140, 141]. The probiotic candidates of these studies were not able to significantly reduce viable MAP in treated as compared to non-treated animals. On the contrary, in cattle, a 90-day treatment after birth with Protexin®, a concentrate based on a cocktail of probiotic strains, mainly containing but not limited to Lactobacillus species was able to decrease ileocecal infection by MAP [142]. Dietzia subsp. C79793-74 was tested in cattle for protection [143, 144] and therapy showing promising results. Specifically, a 60-day probiotic protocol with this Dietzia strain prevented the development of PTBC after in utero and/or neonatal MAP infection [143]. In another study where Dietzia was administered to PTBC positive cattle a longitudinal decline in ELISA values in treated animals along with prolonged survival rates suggested a curative effect of this probiotic [144]. Furthermore, mechanistic studies on Dietzia subsp. C79793-74 have been performed in a rabbit model [145, 146] showing a dual role in immunomodulation. In these experiments, Dietzia subsp. C79793-74 did not protect against MAP infection [146], also displaying an inflammatory profile and a shift in microbiota [145].These differences in results between laboratory animals and cattle highlight the importance of performing probiotic experiments on the target animal species.

Future directions

Although some alternative therapies are promising further studies are needed. These may be directed at isolating mycobacteriophages from local farms to create strain-specific phage cocktails tailored to regional MAP variants. Research is also needed to determine whether calves develop immunity against phages that could cause side effects when phages are administered at multiple stages of life. Additional work should assess whether administering probiotics at birth for one month is sufficient to protect against MAP infection or whether longer treatments of 60–90 days are required if giving probiotics later in life during stressful periods such as parturition or early lactation causes benefits.

Of course, all the therapies mentioned in this subsection need further validation in field trials.

Public health

MAP has been extensively investigated for its potential role in CD. Beyond this, several immune-mediated diseases, including sarcoidosis, type 1 diabetes mellitus, Hashimoto’s thyroiditis, and multiple sclerosis, have been associated with exposure to MAP. Shared immunopathological features, increased anti-MAP immune responses, detection of MAP DNA, and proposed molecular mimicry between MAP antigens and host tissues support the hypothesis that MAP may act as an environmental trigger in genetically susceptible individuals [147–150] Although these associations remain controversial and are supported by more limited and indirect evidence than for CD, the persistence of MAP in the food chain and environment underscores the need for continued investigation into its potential role in human autoimmune and granulomatous diseases.

The association of MAP with CD has recently gained stronger support, mainly due to advances in the accurate detection of MAP in CD patients. Culture techniques and molecular diagnostics have improved the sensitivity and specificity of MAP detection in CD patients. In addition, the use of advanced culture media such as MGIT, TiKa, and Pozzato broth, along with phage assays and Hsp65 antibody detection combined with sensitive PCR methods, has improved the reliable identification of MAP in clinical samples [151, 152]. Although some limitations still exist, particularly in detecting cell wall–deficient forms [151], these advancements facilitate improved patient stratification, which is essential for elucidating MAP’s role in disease pathogenesis and for evaluating MAP-targeted therapies. Antimycobacterial therapy targeting MAP has generated increasing interest as a potential therapeutic approach for CD. Previous trials of antimycobacterial therapy did not demonstrate sustained benefit over placebo, however concerns were raised regarding aspects of trial design, antibiotic dosing, and formulation [153]. Subsequent preliminary studies showed promising effects on reducing intestinal inflammation and improving clinical outcomes.

In this context, recently published clinical trials assess the safety, tolerability, and efficacy of novel MAP-targeted treatment strategies, including immunotherapeutic approaches. Results from a Phase Ib dose-based MAP candidate vaccine in patients with active CD showed that the vaccines were safe, well tolerated, and immunogenic, inducing predominantly T cell–mediated immune responses. However, further studies are needed to establish efficacy and optimal vaccination regimens [112]. A separate clinical trial evaluated the efficacy of a triple antimicrobial therapy targeting MAP, known as RHB-104, which combines clarithromycin, rifabutin, and clofazimine, in patients with active CD. This randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study demonstrated that RHB-104 improved clinical remission rates and reduced disease activity indices compared to placebo [154]. These findings support the role of MAP in the pathogenesis of CD and highlight the potential of targeted antimycobacterial therapy. However, several key questions, discussed elsewhere [155], remain unresolved and must be clarified to fully understand the contribution of MAP to CD.

In summary, substantial progress has been made across multiple areas. However, the overall evidence remains inconclusive regarding MAP as a direct cause of CD or other related human health conditions. It is increasingly recognized that MAP may act as a causative or exacerbating factor in a subset of patients rather than as a universal driver of disease. Consequently, further well-designed clinical and translational studies are required to clarify the role of MAP in CD and to determine its relevance as a therapeutic target.

Concluding remarks

PTBC remains a persistent global challenge for ruminant livestock, as disease complexity, long incubation period and variable outcomes continue to limit effective and sustainable control. Major gaps remain in diagnostics and vaccination, with current tests lacking sufficient sensitivity for early detection and no vaccine yet available that both prevents infection and shedding and is compatible with current TB surveillance and eradication programs. Improved understanding of transmission routes, host immunity, and genetic resistance, together with validation of alternative approaches such as bacteriophages, antibodies, or probiotics, could strengthen future control efforts. Progress will depend on integrating improved diagnostics, TB-compatible vaccination, validated alternative strategies, and evidence-based management, alongside standardized protocols and consideration of economic and behavioural factors influencing farmer participation. The complexity of PTBC control and the need for integrated approaches combining diagnostics, vaccination, alternative strategies and management is summarized in Fig. 1.

Key challenges and integrated strategies for PTBC control

PTBC control is hindered by complex pathogenesis, long incubation period and environmental persistence. Current limitations include insufficiently sensitive diagnostics for early detection and the lack of vaccines that prevent both infection and shedding while remaining compatible with TB surveillance. Future control will rely on improved diagnostics, effective vaccination, better understanding of transmission and host factors (including genetics), and the evaluation of complementary approaches within integrated, farm-level strategies.
