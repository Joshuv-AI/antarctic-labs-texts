Diagnostic accuracy of intraoperative macroscopic margin evaluation in breast-conserving surgery
Authors: C Florin Pop, Claudia Sanciu-Pop, Mathilde Lecomte, Etienne El-Helou, Michel Moreau, Guzel Dzhamalova, Brigitte Verheyden, Chirine Khaled, Denis Larsimont, Isabelle Veys, Gabriel Liberale
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionIntraoperative margin assessment techniques have been developed to reduce positive margin rates after breast-conserving surgery (BCS). These techniques show limited diagnostic accuracy, particularly after neoadjuvant systemic therapy (NST). The study aim was to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of intraoperative macroscopic pathological margin evaluation (IMPME).MethodsThis was a retrospective study including patients with invasive breast cancer (BC) who underwent BCS between 2015 and 2017. A tumor-margin distance ≤1 mm was considered positive at IMPME. Diagnostic accuracy, sensitivity (Se), specificity (Sp) and negative predictive value (NPV) of IMPME were calculated according to the timing of surgery. The gold standard was margin status at final pathology.ResultsA total of 698 BC tumors, were included, 561 in the upfront BCS group (UBCS) and 137 in the delayed (after NST) BCS group (DBCS). According to IMPME, 25.1% and 27.7% of BC tumors had positive margins in the UBCS and DBCS groups, respectively. Final pathological examination showed positive margins for 5.9% in the UBCS group and 8.8% in the DBCS group. Diagnostic accuracy, Se, Sp, and NPV were, respectively, 82.5%, 83.1%, 82.5%, and 97.4%, for the UBCS group and 81.7%, 76%, 84%, and 93.9%, respectively, for the DBCS group. IMPME-guided re-excision reduced secondary surgeries rates by 74% in the UBCS group, and by 50% in the DBCS group.ConclusionIMPME can enhance correlations between the margin status of the main breast surgical specimen and final pathology. With its high NPV rates, IMPME can predict negative final margins and avoid additional intraoperative re-excisions.

Introduction

Improvements in screening techniques and evolving therapeutic strategies have made breast-conserving surgery (BCS) the surgical treatment of choice for breast cancer (BC) patients [1,2]. This conservative approach reduces surgical invasiveness and morbidity, offering survival outcomes comparable or superior to mastectomy and a better quality of life [[2], [3], [4], [5]].

Neoadjuvant systemic therapy (NST) combined with BCS has become one of the most widely used treatment options even for early-stage BC, if the tumor harbours aggressive biological characteristics or if lymph node involvement is present. This approach allows the monitoring of tumoral response, enabling the adjustment of adjuvant therapies and potentially reducing surgical aggressiveness [2,4,5].

Obtaining negative pathological margins is more challenging with BCS than with mastectomy [2,4]. Up to 20% of patients undergoing BCS have positive margins on final pathology, often requiring re-excision. Such secondary procedures may increase morbidity, compromise cosmetic outcomes, and add emotional and economic burdens [3,[6], [7], [8], [9]].

Several techniques for intraoperative margin assessment (IOMA) have been developed to guide surgery and reduce the rate of positive margins after BCS. However, none has yet been established as a standard of care [[6], [7], [8], [9], [10]].

Among IOMA techniques, intraoperative macroscopic pathological margin evaluation (IMPME) appears to be the most widely adopted approach for surgical margin evaluation globally [6,7,[10], [11], [12], [13], [14]].

Few studies have assessed the diagnostic accuracy of IMPME and most of these studies were published before 2010 [[11], [12], [13], [14]], when there was still no consensus on the definition of margins. However, no clear evidence exists regarding margin definition for patients undergoing BCS after NST. By analogy with the definition used for invasive breast cancer treated with primary surgery, a margin is considered negative when there is “no ink on tumor” [15,16]. Evaluating surgical margins after NST is particularly challenging, as tumor response may cause size reduction, fragmentation, or disappearance, while fibrosis in the peritumoral tissue further hinders intraoperative assessment [16,17]. To our knowledge, there have been no prior studies using IMPME for intraoperative surgical margin assessment in patients treated with BCS after NST.

In this study, we aimed to determine the diagnostic accuracy of the IMPME technique and its potential role in decreasing the reintervention rate in patients with invasive BC treated with primary BCS and with BCS after NST.

Methods

Study design and population

This was a single-center, retrospective, diagnostic study of our prospectively maintained data base including patients with invasive BC treated with BCS in a Cancer Center, between 2015 and 2017. The study was approved by the Institut Jules Bordet (IJB) ethics committee under the number CE 3296 and registered with ResearchRegistry.com (researchregistry11063). The requirement to obtain patient consent was waived for this anonymized, retrospective analysis by the ethics committee.

We included patients over 18 years of age, diagnosed with invasive BC treated with upfront BCS (UBCS) or delayed BCS after NST (DBCS). Patients treated with mastectomy, as well as patients with exclusively in situ disease were excluded. Clinical, pathological, and treatment management data were collected from our prospectively maintained database. For each patient, the information was collected in a database previously set up on REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture, Vanderbilt University, USA).

Surgical technique and histopathological analysis

Surgery was performed using standard BCS techniques. A preoperative carbon tumor marking is routinely used for all BCS procedures [10]. Axillary surgery was performed by sentinel lymph node biopsy and/or axillary lymph node dissection based on institutional guideline indications. IMPME was done following current institutional practice. A complete histopathological evaluation was further performed in accordance with the international guidelines of breast tumor assessment.

Standard pathological protocol for intraoperative macroscopic margin evaluation

After lumpectomy, the breast surgical specimen (BSS) is oriented by the surgeon in the operating room (Fig. 1A). It is then sent to the pathology department where is weighed, measured, and inked (Fig. 1B, C, D), before being sliced into 3-4 mm thick sections for IMPME analysis (Fig. 1E and F) of the 6 margins (upper, lower, anterior, posterior, internal, and external) (Fig. 1F, G, H). The results of IMPME analysis are communicated to the surgeon and are systematically recorded in the final pathological report. Evaluation criteria: IMPME surgical margins definitions

The IMPME margin definitions are as follows (Fig. 2).•“involved” margin, whether it is through the tumor or "ink on tumor" (0 mm);•“tangent” margin, if it's in close contact with the tumor (≤1 mm);•“close” margin, if it's located close to the tumor (>1 mm to ≤ 2 mm);•“distant” margin, if it's located at a distance from the tumour (>2 mm).Fig. 2Margin definitions for intraoperative macroscopic pathological margin evaluation (IMPME).Legend: The breast cancer (BC) tumor tissue is shown in red surrounded by healthy tissue in yellow together with the distance from the edge of the breast surgical specimen (BSS). In clinical practice, and for statistical analysis, a margin is considered positive for IMPME if it is reported as “involved” or “tangent”, in which case an intraoperative re-excision is considered indicated. In the case of "close" or "distant" responses of IMPME, margins are considered negative, and no intraoperative re-excision is indicated. The IMPME margin status of positive or negative for each BSS was compared with the final histopathology analysis (FHA) of the BSS and/or of the IMPME guided re-excision specimen. If BC tumoral tissue was seen on FHA on the edge of BSS or at the level of the IMPME guided re-excision for the cases reported as “involved” or “tangent” on IMPME, these cases were considered as true positives. The cases classed as "close" or "distant" on IMPME and with FHA negative were considered to be true negatives. For statistical analysis (and in institutional clinical practice) a margin is considered « positive » for IMPME if it is reported as “involved” or “tangent”, in which case an intraoperative re-excision is indicated. In the case of "close" or "distant" results from the IMPME, the margins are considered « negative » and no intraoperative re-excision is needed. For patients with positive final margins located posteriorly, re-excision was not performed due to the posterior contact with the muscle, which made re-excision unfeasible. The gold standard for calculating the diagnostic accuracy of IMPME is the FHA of the surgical margins on the BSS and/or intraoperative breast surgical re-excision. A clear margin on FHA was defined as “no ink on tumor” (for invasive and/or associated in situ disease), according to the international/national guideline [9,14].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was carried out using S.A.S software, version 9.4. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient clinical and tumor pathological characteristics. Nominal and/or categorical variables are reported as frequencies and proportions. Continuous variables are reported as means, medians, and interquartile ranges. The technique's diagnostic accuracy was calculated, along with its sensitivity (Se), specificity (Sp), positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), false-negative (FN) rate and false-positive (FP) rate according to the timing of BCS, as a primary surgery or after NST. The rates of intraoperative re-excision of the tumor bed and of secondary re-excision were calculated using percentage tests for independent dichotomous data such as the ratio between the number of patients with « positive » IMPME margins identified after BCS, and the number of patients with a positive margin on final pathology, respectively, in relation to the total number of patients. Stratified tests were performed to determine the interactions between the various patient, tumor, and test precision parameters. Differences were considered significant if the p-value was equal to or less than 0.05.

This work has been reported in accordance with the Standards for Reporting of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies (STARD) guidelines [18,19]. (Supplemental Data 1).

General population characteristics

The median age of the patients was 62.7 years in the UBCS group and 54.4 years in the DBCS group. The most common type of BC was invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC) in 460 cases (82%) for the UBCS group, and in 130 cases (95%) for the DBCS group. Most of the tumors were low-intermediate grade in the UBCS group (77.5%) and high-grade in the DBCS group (54.7%). DBCS patients received individual chemotherapy as NST in 117 patients (88%) and a complete response was observed at MRI in 25.1%. The median tumour size at final pathology was 12 mm (range 1.5-40 mm) for the UBCS group and 18 mm (range 1-55 mm) for the DBCS group. Pathological evaluation of margins on the breast surgical specimen

Positive margins on final pathology were found in 33 patients (5.9%) in the UBCS group and in 12 patients (8.8%) in the DBCS group. All patients underwent IMPME, with BSS preparation performed by one of the two dedicated laboratory technicians specialized in breast pathology in 77.5% of cases.

According to IMPME, 141 BC tumors (25.1%) and 38 BC tumors (27.7%) had positive margins in the UBCS and DBCS group, respectively, and an intraoperative re-excision was performed according to IMPME indications. As per study population, intraoperative re-excision was performed in 42.4% of patients in the UBCS group and in 41.6% of the DBCS group. Of these patients, 193 (81.1%) of patients in the UBCS group and 49 (86%) in the DBCS group underwent a single re-excision. The FHA led to secondary re-excision for positive margins in 15 patients (2.8%) and 5 patients (3.8%), and to mastectomy in 14 patients (2.5%) and 3 patients (2.2%), respectively, in the UBCS and DBCS groups. Diagnostic accuracy of IMPME

In this cohort, 141 BC tumors (25.1%) and 38 BC tumors (27.7%) in the UBCS and DBCS groups, respectively, had positive margins on IMPME. Among these, 87 BC tumors had a negative margin at FHA (FP 15.5%) in the UBCS group and 19 tumors had a negative margin at FHA (FP 13.9%) in the DBCS group. For the tumors with a negative IMPME margin, 11 BC tumors (FN 2%) had a positive margin at FHA in the UBCS group and 6 BC tumors (FN 4.4%) were positive in the DBCS group. According to the definitions used in this study for a positive margin (≤1 mm) on IMPME, diagnostic accuracy, Se, Sp, PPV, and NPV were 82.53%, 83.08%, 82.46%, 38.3%, and 97.38%, respectively, for the UBCS group and 81.75%, 76%, 84.04%, 50%, and 93.94%, respectively, for the DBCS group. Effect of IMPME-guided resection on secondary surgeries

Through intraoperative re-excisions based on IMPME, only 19 patients (3.4%) in the UBCS group, and 8 patients (5.8%) in the DBCS group required a second surgery. IMPME-guided re-excisions allowed avoidance of a secondary surgery in 54 (9.6%) and 8 (5.8%) patients in the UBCS and DBCS groups, respectively. The rate of final secondary procedures was consequently reduced by 74% in the UBCS group, and by 50% in the DBCS group. Discussion

According to the results of this study, we can state that if a margin after BCS is determined to be negative on IMPME, it is highly likely to also be negative on final pathology. This is reflected by the high NPV values of 97.4% in the UBCS group and 93.9% in the DBCS group. Moreover, IMPME reduced the need for a second surgery by 74% in the UBCS group and by 50% in the DBCS group.

The diagnostic accuracy of IMPME was good at 82.5% in UBCS and 81.8% in DBCS patients, with very low false-negative rates of 2% and 4.4%, respectively. The PPV was quite low, 38.3% for UBCS and 50% for DBCS, suggesting that some re-excisions performed based on intraoperative assessment may be unnecessary. The low PPV rate primarily reflects the discrepancy between the microscopic and macroscopic assessment capabilities of resection margins [17,19,20]. The lower PPV may result from China ink staining, which can obscure margins ≤1 mm, and from cautious IMPME reporting by pathologists concerned about patient risk. A shared definition of resection margins between surgeons and pathologists could help minimize this bias.

Furthermore, the stratified analyses showed no significant differences in IMPME performance, confirming consistent accuracy across patient subgroups.

However, the NPVs seem to be better in this study (97.4% vs 89%) [14]. In our institutional practice, a positive margin on IMPME was defined as ≤1 mm, as this distance represents the threshold below which macroscopic examination cannot reliably exclude tumor involvement (R1). Although we acknowledge that this cut-off does not have a universally accepted scientific basis, it has proven to be a practical and reproducible criterion in our setting, supported by emerging evidence in the literature [6,20].

Intraoperative macroscopic pathological margin evaluation and delayed BCS

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study evaluating the diagnostic accuracy of IMPME in patients who received NST before BCS. IMPME after NST is more difficult to interpret due to changes in (peri)-tumoral tissue composition related to tumor response. This can result in shrinkage of the lesion (making it more difficult to palpate), tumor fragmentation, or even disappearance of residual cancer [[15], [16], [17]].

Very few clinical studies have been reported on the effectiveness of IOMA techniques in cases of DBCS [[21], [22], [23], [24], [25]]. Most of these are exploratory studies [[23], [24], [25]]. The sole study reporting the most pertinent information for the clinic comes from the group of Schafgen, which evaluated the diagnostic accuracy of specimen radiography (SR) on a series of 362 BCS patients after NST [19]. The Se (37.3%) for SR was lower than that for IMPME (76%) observed in our study but with nearly the same NPV [21].

As is well known, the rate of final positive margins in DBCS and consequently the FN rate of IMPME (4%) is higher, most likely due to the increased difficulty of pathological evaluation after NST, upon which the accuracy of both IMPME and final margin assessment depends [26,27]. The concomitant use of additional methods for residual tumor tissue detection after NST, such as optical or fluorescence imaging, may serve as a valuable complement to IMPME, with the potential to enhance its accuracy, improve the final pathological assessment, and contribute to the standardization of the macroscopic evaluation procedure of the BSS following NST [24,25].

The results for this group of patients treated by NST should be interpreted with caution, considering their higher risk of local recurrence [26,27]. Other IOMA techniques should be further evaluated in this BC population.

Intraoperative macroscopic pathological margin evaluation and IOMA techniques

A recent meta-analysis of IOMA techniques reported superior diagnostic accuracy for histopathology-based methods, with pooled sensitivity and specificity of 0.92/0.95 for touch imprint cytology and 0.82/0.98 for frozen section (FS) [19]. The NPV values for IMPME in our study, in both UBCS and DBCS groups, are comparable to those reported for these techniques [8,19].

The major drawbacks of IMPME, as well as other IOMA techniques, are its relatively low Se and Sp, resulting in many unnecessary re-excisions [8,14,19].

In the meta-analysis by St John, intraoperative re-excision rates among IOMA techniques ranged from 4.4% to 74.2%, with a mean of 30.4% [8]. In our study, the re-excision rates were similar across cohorts, 27.7% in DBCS and 25.1% in UBCS, slightly lower than those reported in the literature (Table 4) [6,8,28]. The difference between the total intraoperative re-excision rate and that guided by IMPME results likely reflects the surgeon's clinical judgment and, occasionally, misinterpretation of the pathologist's intraoperative report.

In our study, only 2.3–2.9% of patients undergoing intraoperative re-excision for “close” or “distant” margins had residual tumor at final histopathological assessment (Fig. 4). This finding suggests that an IMPME margin of ≤1 mm may serve as a reference threshold for patients undergoing BCS, both before and after NST. Defining a positive IMPME margin as ≤1 mm could potentially reduce intraoperative re-excisions by nearly one-third.

To minimize unnecessary resections and reduce the FP rate of IMPME, a targeted intraoperative FS guided by IMPME findings could be performed only in cases with “tangent” margins (≤1 mm). This selective approach, required in about one in five patients (18% in UBCS and 24% in DBCS), would significantly shorten operative time compared with systematic FS, while improving safety and cosmetic outcomes. New IOMA techniques have been tested to improve intraoperative margin assessment, but none are yet used in routine practice [[29], [30], [31], [32], [33]].

It is important to note that, with the use of IMPME, 35 patients (6.5%) in the UBCS group and 8 patients (5.6%) in the DBCS group had their margins converted from positive to negative within the same operative session, thereby avoiding a second surgery. Consequently, the use of IMPME appears to halve the rate of secondary interventions across both patient groups.

A major limitation of IOMA techniques is their time-consuming nature, averaging about 20 min. In contrast, IMPME requires only 5–7 min (rarely more than 10) when performed by an experienced team, potentially reducing operative time and hospital costs [34].

Strengths and limitations

To the best of our knowledge, this is the largest study to assess the accuracy of IMPME after BCS and the first to evaluate its efficacy in the neoadjuvant setting. Nevertheless, several limitations must be acknowledged. The retrospective design inherently introduces potential biases; however, the validity of our findings is supported by consistent documentation of intraoperative macroscopic margin assessments and by stratified analyses showing no statistically significant differences in diagnostic performance. Another limitation is related to the rationale for intraoperative re-excisions, and the use of preoperative tumor localization techniques such as carbon marking, which may facilitate more complete resections [10,16]. The subjectivity of IMPME interpretation, dependent on the pathologist on duty, was minimized as over 75% of BSS preparations were performed under the supervision of two experienced breast pathology technicians.

Another limitation of our study relates to the IMPME method itself, which is somewhat time-consuming, although less so than other techniques currently in use, and requires experienced breast pathologists as well as highly skilled technicians for the processing of breast specimens. Its relatively high positive predictive value (PPV) indicates the need for complementary tests in cases where it identifies positive margins.

Finally, the lack of a universally accepted definition of margin status using IMPME may have influenced our results. Establishing standardized criteria for IMPME margin assessment would enhance comparability across studies.

Conclusion

IMPME represents a reliable intraoperative technique for evaluating resection margins during BCS, improving correlation with final pathology. With its high NPV (97.4% for UBCS and 93.9% for DBCS), IMPME can accurately predict negative final margins, enabling the avoidance of unnecessary intraoperative re-excisions. Despite the possibility of overestimating positive margins, its use can reduce the rate of secondary surgeries by more than half. Establishing a standardized definition of margin status on IMPME and adopting uniform terminology between surgeons and pathologists are essential steps toward optimizing oncologic safety and aesthetic outcomes in breast-conserving surgery.
