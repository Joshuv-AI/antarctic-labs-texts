The gut microbiota metabolite butyrate mitigates MPTP/MPP +‐induced Parkinson's disease by inhibiting the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway
Authors: Li‐Li Ji, Ting‐Ting Huang, Lun‐Lin Mao, Yuan‐Feng Xu, Wen‐Ya Chen, Wei‐Wei Wang, Li‐Hui Wang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractButyrate (BU), a gut microbiota‐derived metabolite, has been reported to play a neuroprotective role in Parkinson's disease (PD). However, the specific molecular mechanism of BU has not been fully interpreted. This work aimed to verify the protective effects of BU against MPTP/MPP+‐induced neurotoxicity and explore the mechanisms involved. The results showed that BU protected against MPTP‐induced motor dysfunction and decreased tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) and dopamine transporter (DAT) levels. Additionally, BU pretreatment improved PC12 cell viability and reduced MPP+‐induced PC12 cell apoptosis. BU treatment also attenuated MPP+‐stimulated oxidative stress and inflammatory response in PC12 cells. Furthermore, BU inhibited MPTP/MPP+‐induced hyperactivation of the JAK2/STAT3 signaling in mice and PC12 cells. Besides, a JAK2 agonist, Coumermycin A1 (C‐A1), substantially reversed BU‐mediated inhibition on JAK2/STAT3 phosphorylation in MPP+‐challenged PC12 cells and abated BU‐induced repression on MPP+‐triggered apoptosis, oxidative stress, and inflammatory response in PC12 cells. To sum up, BU might exert neuroprotective effects against MPP+/MPTP‐induced neurotoxicity by inactivating the JAK2/STAT3 signaling.Keywords: butyrate, gut microbiota metabolite, JAK2/STAT3, Parkinson's disease

INTRODUCTION

Parkinson's disease (PD) is a chronic neurodegenerative disease. 1 As an age‐related disorder, PD mainly affects adults aged between 40 and 70. 2 At present, PD incidence in people aged 65 and above is about 2%. 3 Clinically, major PD symptoms include motor dysfunctions (e.g., bradykinesia, rest tremor, and muscular rigidity). 4 Progressive loss of dopaminergic neurons in the midbrain, a main cause of PD, may result in concomitant dopamine depletion and α‐synuclein accumulation. 5 Besides, it is believed that neuroinflammation and oxidative stress also act key roles in PD pathogenesis, 6 suggesting anti‐oxidative and anti‐inflammatory agents might offer a promising therapeutic potential for PD. Since current drugs for PD treatment mainly target PD symptoms and cannot prevent the loss of dopaminergic neurons, 7 the discovery and development of novel neuroprotective and disease‐modifying agents for PD are still needed.

Alterations of composition and abundance of gut microbiota are known to have impacts on brain physiology. 8 Gut microbial dysbiosis can affect not only the enteric nervous system but also the central nervous system (CNS), causing CNS diseases. 9 Ample evidence indicates that gut microbial dysbiosis seems to play a role in PD pathogenesis. 10 Interventions targeting gut microbiota (e.g., fecal microbiota transplantation, antibiotics, and probiotics) can improve host health. 11 Short‐chain fatty acids (SCFAs) are one type of microbial metabolite major metabolites of gut microbiota. 12 Studies have reported that supplementation of major SCFAs can improve gut microbial dysbiosis. 13 In addition, these SCFAs also exert neuroprotective effects in PD. 14 , 15 , 16 As a prominent SCFA generated by gut microbiota, butyrate (BU) is deeply involved in the regulation of gut microbiota. 13 Recent findings have revealed that PD patients suffered from reduced BU synthesis by gut microbiota. 17 Furthermore, BU supplementation exerts neuroprotective effects against PD in vitro and in vivo. 18 , 19 However, the fundamental mechanisms involved in the neuroprotective effects of BU against PD have not been fully elucidated yet.

The JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway plays a critical role in the pathogenesis of neurodegenerative diseases, especially PD. 20 , 21 Recent studies have demonstrated that hyperactivation of the JAK2/STAT3 is closely associated with dopamine (DA) neuron degeneration and motor dysfunction in PD. 22 , 23 Furthermore, JAK2 inhibitors have been proven to be potential therapeutic agents for PD. 24 Interestingly, BU has been identified as a JAK2/STAT3 inhibitor in several human diseases, including ulcerative colitis 25 and lung ischemia–reperfusion injury. 26 Nevertheless, whether BU can mitigate MPTP/MPP+‐induced neurotoxicity by regulating the JAK2/STAT3 pathway remains unknown.

In this work, we aimed to verify the neuroprotective effects of BU in PD and explore the specific molecular mechanism involved. Herein, we established a murine PD model by administrating C57BL/6 mice with MPTP and a cellular PD model by treating PC12 cells with MPP+. The results indicated that BU protected against MPTP/MPP+‐induced neurotoxicity in vivo and in vitro through the inactivation of the JAK2/STAT3 signaling.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Establishment of murine PD model and experimental design

Male C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old; 16–25 g) were bought from Shanghai Slac Laboratory Animal Company (Shanghai, China) and maintained in a constant environment (23 ± 2°C; 60% humidity) with free access to food and drinking water.

Mice were randomly divided into Sham (n = 10), MPTP (n = 10), MPTP+BU (200 mg/kg; n = 10), and MPTP+BU (600 mg/kg; n = 10) groups. To simulate PD in vivo, the mice received an intraperitoneal injection with MPTP (30 mg/kg) for seven consecutive days. Mice in Sham group received an intraperitoneal injection with normal saline. For BU administration, mice were treated with sodium butyrate (200 or 600 mg/kg) by gavage for three consecutive weeks after MPTP induction. 19 Behavioral tests were performed 3 weeks after the last MPTP injection. After behavioral tests, all mice were sacrificed to harvest brains for further experiments. The animal experiments were permitted by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of The First People's Hospital of Changzhou (IACUC #CZFPH20210007). The schematic diagram of this study is shown in Figure S1.

Behavioral tests

Pole test and rotarod test were performed as previously described 27 to evaluate bradykinesia and motor coordination of PD mice. For pole test, mice were positioned at the top of a rough‐surfaced pole (15 mm in diameter and 50 cm in height) with head upward and allowed to practice twice before testing. The time to turn down from the top (time to turn; T‐turn) and the time to descend to the bottom (locomotion activity time; T‐LA) were measured. Each mouse was tested three times. The average value of three tests was regarded as the final result. For rotarod test, the mice were placed on a rod rotating at 5 rpm for 3 min for adaptation. Then, the rod gradually accelerated from 5 rpm to 40 rpm in 5 min. The time that the mice remained on the rod was considered latency.

Western blotting

Total proteins were extracted from brain tissue or PC12 cells with RIPA lysis buffer (50 mM Tris, 2 mM EDTA, 0.5% deoxycholate, 150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X‐100, 0.1% SDS, 1 mM Na3VO4, and 1 mM PMSF). Next, total proteins were separated into equal amounts by 10% SDS‐PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk, incubated with primary antibodies at 4°C overnight and then incubated with secondary antibody at room temperature for 2 h. Finally, protein bands were visualized by the ECL kit (Pierce).

Cell culture and treatment

Rat pheochromocytoma cells (PC12) obtained from BeNa Culture Collection (Beijing, China) were cultured in DMEM (10% FBS; 100 μg/mL streptomycin; 100 U/mL penicillin) in a humidified atmosphere (5% CO2; 37°C).

To simulate PD in vitro, PC12 cells were exposed to MPP+ (500 μM) for 24 h. For BU and/or Coumermycin A1 (C‐A1) treatment, PC12 cells were pretreated with BU and/or C‐A1 24h before MPP+ induction.

CCK‐8

PC12 cell viability was detected by CCK‐8 assay. In brief, PC12 cells were plated in 96‐well plates (2 × 104 cells/well). After certain treatment, a CCK‐8 reagent was added (10 μL/well). Next, PC12 cells were incubated with CCK‐8 reagent for another 2 h at 37°C. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm with a microplate reader (BioTek, USA).

Flow cytometry

PC12 cell apoptosis was assessed with Annexin V/Dead Cell Apoptosis Kit (Invitrogen). In brief, PC12 cells were seeded into 6‐well plates (1 × 105 cells/well). After certain treatment, PC12 cells were rinsed in ice‐cold PBS and resuspended in Annexin Binding Buffer (Invitrogen; 50 mM HEPES, 700 mM NaCl, and 12.5 mM CaCl2). Then, PC12 cells were incubated with Annexin V‐FITC (5 μL) and PI (5 μL) for 15 min at room temperature in darkness. The apoptotic PC12 cells were analyzed using a flow cytometer. Finally, the apoptotic rate of PC12 cells was quantified using FlowJo software.

ROS assessment

ROS activity was measured by a ROS Activity Assay Kit (Abnova, China) as per the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, PC12 cells were seeded to 96‐well plates (2 × 104 cells/well). After treatment, ROS assay was performed according to the manufacturer's instructions. Finally, fluorescence intensity was assessed at excitation/emission of 650/675 nm.

Determination of oxidative stress parameters

To determine oxidative damage to PC12 cells, oxidative stress parameters, including superoxide dismutase (SOD), glutathione (GSH), and malondialdehyde (MDA), were measured. Briefly, PC12 cells were seeded to 96‐well plates (5 × 105 cells/well). After treatment, SOD, GSH), and MDA levels were detected using the SOD detection kit (S0101), GSH detection kit (S0053), and MDA detection kit (S0131) as per manufacturer's instructions (Beyotime, China).

Cytokine ELISA

Briefly, cell culture media was collected and centrifuged at 2000 ×g for 10 min to remove debris. Then, cell supernatant was obtained to measure the levels of proinflammatory cytokines, TNF‐α (ab236712), IL‐1β (ab255730), and IL‐6 (ab234570), using ELISA kits (Abcam) as per the manufacturer's instructions.

Statistical analysis

Each experiment was performed three times. Data were presented as mean ± SD. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 17.0 and one‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post‐hoc test. Graphs were plotted with GraphPad Prism 6.0. p < 0.05 was deemed a statistically significant difference.

RESULTS

BU relieves MPTP‐triggered motor dysfunction and dopaminergic neuronal death in mice

To investigate the effects of BU in PD, an in vivo PD model was established by MPTP administration (30 mg/kg) via intraperitoneal injection. Mice were divided into Sham, MPTP, MPTP+BU (200 mg/kg), and MPTP+BU (600 mg/kg) groups. As illustrated by pole test results, MPTP mice exhibited a remarkable increase in T‐turn (time to turn) and T‐LA (locomotion activity time), while BU treatment reduced T‐turn and T‐LA taken by the mice in a dose‐dependent manner (Figure 1A). Rotarod test results showed that BU treatment dose‐dependently reduced MPTP‐induced reduction in latency time (Figure 1B).

BU relieves MPTP‐triggered motor dysfunction and dopaminergic neuronal death in mice. Mice were divided into Sham (n = 10), MPTP (n = 10), MPTP+BU (200 mg/kg; n = 10), and MPTP+BU (600 mg/kg; n = 10) groups. (A) Pole test performance. (B) Rotarod test performance. (C and D) Western blotting for TH and DAT protein levels in the striatum. *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001.

Tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) and dopamine transporter (DAT) are two critical regulators of dopamine neurotransmission. 28 To investigate the effects of BU on MPTP‐caused damage to dopaminergic neurons and fibers, TH and DAT levels in the striatum and substantia nigra were detected via western blotting. As shown in Figure 1C,D, the MPTP group exhibited declined TH and DAT levels in striatum and substantia nigra; however, BU treatment significantly increased TH and DAT levels, suggesting BU treatment substantially reversed the loss of dopaminergic neurons and fibers as well as the dopamine neurotransmission dysfunction in MPTP mice. Collectively, BU might protect against MPTP‐induced motor dysfunction and dopaminergic neuronal death in mice.

BU mitigates MPP +‐induced apoptosis

To confirm the neuroprotective effect of BU in vitro, PC12 cells were pre‐treated with BU (0.1, 1, and 10 μM) and then subjected to MPP+ exposure for 24 h. CCK‐8 results showed that BU pretreatment significantly and concentration‐dependently protected against MPP+‐induced reduction in PC12 cell viability (Figure 2A). In addition, the flow cytometry assay revealed that MPP+ treatment led to a remarkable increase in PC12 cell apoptosis, which was effectively diminished by BU pretreatment (Figure 2B). Similarly, BU markedly reversed MPP+‐induced increase in the Bax level and reduction in the Bcl‐2 level (Figure 2C). These results demonstrated that BU inhibited MPP+‐induced apoptosis in PC12 cells.

BU mitigates MPP+‐induced apoptosis. PC12 cells were assigned into Control, MPP+, MPP++BU (0.1 μM), MPP++BU (1 μM), and MPP++BU (10 μM) groups. (A) CCK‐8 assay for PC12 cell viability. (B) Flow cytometry for PC12 cell apoptosis. (C) Western blotting for Bax and Bcl‐2 protein levels. *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001.

BU alleviates MPP +‐stimulated oxidative stress and inflammatory responses in PC12 cells

Oxidative stress and neuroinflammation also play key roles in PD pathogenesis. 6 As shown in Figure 3A–D, MPP+ induction resulted in increased ROS and MDA levels as well as decreased SOD and GSH levels in PC12 cells, which was dose‐dependently reversed by BU pretreatment. Besides, BU pretreatment effectively abated MPP+‐induced excessive secretion of pro‐inflammatory cytokines (TNF‐α, IL‐1β, and IL‐6) in PC12 cells in a dose‐dependent manner (Figure 3E–G). The above results indicated that BU attenuated oxidative stress and inflammatory responses in MPP+‐challenged PC12 cells.

BU alleviates MPP+‐stimulated oxidative stress and inflammatory responses in PC12 cells. PC12 cells were assigned into Control, MPP+, MPP++BU (0.1 μM), MPP++BU (1 μM), and MPP++BU (10 μM) groups. (A–D) ROS, SOD, GSH, and MDA levels. (E–G) TNF‐α, IL‐1β, and IL‐6 levels. *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001.

BU inhibits MPTP/MPP +‐induced activation of JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway

Next, the effect of BU on MPTP/MPP+‐induced hyperactivation of JAK2/STAT3 signaling was explored in vivo and in vitro. As shown in Figure 4A, BU treatment markedly inhibited MPTP‐triggered upregulation of p‐JAK2 and p‐STAT3 protein levels; however, there were almost no changes observed in total JAK2 and total STAT3 protein levels. In the same way, BU pretreatment significantly abated MPP+‐induced JAK2/STAT3 phosphorylation in PC12 cells, while total JAK2 and total STAT3 protein levels remain nearly unchanged (Figure 4B). Taken together, BU abolished MPTP/MPP+‐induced activation of JAK2/STAT3 signaling.

BU inhibits MPTP/MPP+‐induced activation of JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway. (A) p‐JAK2, JAK2, p‐STAT3, and STAT3 protein levels in brain tissues from Sham, MPTP, MPTP+BU (200 mg/kg), and MPTP+BU (600 mg/kg) groups (B) p‐JAK2, JAK2, p‐STAT3, and STAT3 protein levels in PC12 cells from Control, MPP+, MPP++BU (0.1 μM), MPP++BU (1 μM), and MPP++BU (10 μM) groups. *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001.

JAK2/STAT3 activation reverses BU‐mediated neuroprotective effects on MPP +‐challenged PC12 cells

To further confirm whether BU exerted neuroprotective effects via inhibiting the JAK2/STAT3 signaling, a JAK2 agonist, Coumermycin A1 (C‐A1), was applied. It was found that BU failed to abolish MPP+‐induced JAK2/STAT3 phosphorylation in PC12 cells in the presence of C‐A1 (Figure 5A). Accordingly, C‐A1 also abated the inhibitory effects of BU on MPP+‐triggered apoptosis, oxidative stress, and inflammatory responses in PC12 cells (Figure 5B–K). Taken together, our data suggested that BU ameliorated MPP+‐induced apoptosis, oxidative stress, and inflammatory responses in PC12 cells by inactivating the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway.

JAK2/STAT3 activation reverses BU‐mediated neuroprotective effects on MPP+‐challenged PC12 cells. PC12 cells were assigned into Control, MPP+, MPP++BU (10 μM), and MPP++BU (10 μM) + C‐A1 groups. (A) p‐JAK2, JAK2, p‐STAT3, and STAT3 protein levels. (B) PC12 cell viability. (C) PC12 cell apoptosis. (D) Bax and Bcl‐2 protein levels. (E–H) ROS, SOD, GSH, and MDA levels. (I–K) TNF‐α, IL‐1β, and IL‐6 levels. *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001.

DISCUSSION

As a major cause of motor disorder globally, PD significantly impacts patients' quality of life and poses stress to their caregivers. 29 Since etiological causes of PD are complicated and have not been fully elucidated, there have been no efficient therapies. 30 BU has been shown to play anti‐apoptosis, anti‐inflammation, and anti‐oxidative roles in human diseases. 31 , 32 Many studies have indicated the neuroprotective effects of BU in PD. 18 , 19 Therefore, further investigation and exploration of molecular mechanisms involving BU in PD will provide evidence for the future application of BU in PD treatment.

To verify the effects of BU in PD, a murine PD model was first established by subjecting mice to MPTP intraperitoneal injection. MPTP‐induced mice showed poorer performance in pole test and rotarod test than mice in the Control group, while BU treatment substantially improved MPTP‐induced behavior dysfunction. In addition, BU treatment markedly reversed the MPTP‐caused decrease in TH and DAT levels in striatum and substantia nigra, suggesting BU might mitigate MPTP‐induced loss of dopaminergic neurons. Taken together, BU exerted protective effects against MPTP‐induced neurotoxicity in mice.

Neuron apoptosis is considered a major contributor to neurodegeneration in PD. 33 Interestingly, BU has been reported to reduce Aβ25‐35 or rotenone‐induced PC12 apoptosis. 18 , 34 To confirm the neuroprotective effect of BU in vitro, a cellular PD model was established by treating PC12 cells with MPP+. Herein, BU concentration‐dependently protected against MPP+‐induced reduction in PC12 cell viability and increase in PC12 cell apoptosis. Correspondingly, BU substantially reversed MPP+‐induced changes in Bax and Bcl‐2 levels. These results demonstrated that BU inhibited MPP+‐induced apoptosis in PC12 cells.

Studies have demonstrated that oxidative stress and neuroinflammation contribute to neurodegeneration and neuronal death in PD. 35 BU has been reported to ameliorate inflammation and oxidative stress in human diseases, including myocardial ischemia/reperfusion injury 31 and diabetic kidney disease. 36 In consistency with prior studies, BU dose‐dependently attenuated MPP+‐induced oxidative stress in PC12 cells, as evidenced by the decreased ROS and MDA levels and the enhanced SOD and GSH levels. Besides, BU pretreatment also attenuated MPP+‐induced overproduction of pro‐inflammatory cytokines (TNF‐α, IL‐1β, and IL‐6) in a concentration‐dependent manner. Therefore, BU mitigated MPP+‐induced oxidative stress and inflammatory reaction in PC12 cells.

BU has been demonstrated to be an inhibitor of the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway. 25 , 26 Inhibition of the JAK2/STAT3 signaling plays remarkable neuroprotective roles by inhibiting neuroinflammation in sevoflurane‐induced developmental neurotoxicity, 37 ischemic stroke injury, 38 spinal cord injury, 39 and PD. 23 In agreement with the above reports, BU treatment remarkably abrogated MPTP‐triggered excessive JAK2/STAT3 phosphorylation in mice. Similarly, the MPP+‐induced increase of p‐JAK2 and p‐STAT3 protein levels in PC12 cells was markedly eliminated by BU pretreatment. Furthermore, the JAK2 agonist, C‐A1, substantially reversed BU‐mediated inhibition on JAK2/STAT3 activation in MPP+‐challenged PC12 cells and abated BU‐mediated protection against MPP+‐induced apoptosis, oxidative stress, and inflammatory responses in PC12 cells.

CONCLUSION

Our results demonstrated that BU exerted neuroprotective effects on MPTP/MPP+‐induced murine and cellular PD models. BU protected neurons by reducing apoptosis, oxidative stress, and inflammatory responses via the inactivation of the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway. Therefore, BU may be a potent therapeutic agent for PD treatment.
