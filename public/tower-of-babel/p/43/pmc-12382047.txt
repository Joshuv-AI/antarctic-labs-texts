Integrated analysis of 16 S endophytic bacteria and non-targeted metabolomics reveals the drought response mechanisms of Portulaca Oleracea L
Authors: Xinyu Zhao, Luxiang Liang, Shi Qi, Jiahui Fang, Ping Li, Mengyun Xu, Jian Yan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDrought is currently one of the biggest challenges facing global agricultural production. Purslane (Portulaca oleracea L.) has attracted widespread attention due to its exceptional drought resistance. However, the dynamics in endophytic bacterial communities and their synergistic response mechanisms with metabolites under drought stress in purslane remain poorly understood.ResultsThe study systematically investigated the dynamic responses of endophytic bacterial communities and metabolites in purslane under drought stress and rehydration conditions through integrating 16 S rDNA sequencing and untargeted metabolomics. Results revealed that drought stress caused no significant changes in purslane’s endophytic bacterial community, whereas rehydration induced a marked restructuring of the microbiota. Co-expression network analysis identified Delftia and Stenotrophomonas were the key hub species in the drought and rehydration groups, respectively. Untargeted metabolomic analysis detected a total of 2,973 metabolites, primarily lipids, lipid-like molecules, and organic acids and their derivatives, which were significantly enriched in pathways such as phenylalanine biosynthesis, amino acid metabolism, and tyrosine metabolism. Integrated random forest modeling and Spearman correlation analysis revealed significant associations between differential bacterial genera and differential metabolites, with Stenotrophomonas showing particularly close links to multiple metabolites.ConclusionsDrought stress did not significantly change the endophytic bacterial community structure in purslane, while rehydration increased diversity and altered the dominant genera. Metabolite levels also shifted significantly under different water conditions. Strong links were found between endophytic bacteria and these metabolic changes. Together, these findings offer new insights into how microbial-metabolite interactions help purslane adapt to water stress.Keywords: Portulaca oleracea L., Endophytic bacteria, Metabolites, Drought

Introduction

Purslane (Portulaca oleracea L.), belonging to the Portulacaceae family [1], is a common field plant and widely distributed across the globe [2, 3]. This nutrient-rich plant is an excellent source of α-linolenic acid, essential ω−3 and 6 fatty acids, ascorbic acid, glutathione, tocopherols, and β-carotene [4]. Furthermore, purslane contains a variety of bioactive substances, such as organic acids, polysaccharides, alkaloids, flavonoids, and terpenoids [5–7], which have anti-inflammatory and antioxidant qualities. As a result, purslane has considerable medicinal value and is frequently used in healthcare applications [8]. Due to its remarkable stress tolerance, purslane is often referred to as the “world’s eighth weed” [1].

Drought is one of the major abiotic stresses in nature and a critical factor affecting plant growth, development, yield, and distribution [9]. Plants have developed different ways to survive water shortages, such as adjusting their internal water balance and protecting themselves from damage [10]. As a highly drought-tolerant plant, purslane holds potential as a model for studying plant adaptation mechanisms under drought conditions. Research indicates that under drought stress, purslane maintains higher levels of osmoregulators such as proline and soluble sugars, which help stabilize cellular structures and prevent oxidative damage [11]. Research has found that long periods without water cause purslane to grow shorter and develop smaller leaves. However, even after 22 days of drought stress when leaves exhibit visible wilting, rehydration can rapidly reverse the wilting symptoms [12]. Compared to other plants, purslane keeps more chlorophyll and produces more seeds during droughts [13]. Most remarkably, Moreno and colleagues discovered that purslane has a special ability to use both C4 and CAM photosynthesis methods, giving it a unique advantage in dry conditions [14].

Plant endophytes are microorganisms that live inside plant tissues, typically forming symbiotic relationships with their hosts [15]. These plant-associated microbes play many important roles in nature. They help plants grow by absorbing nitrogen, producing growth hormones, and protecting against diseases and pests. Some can even be used for biological control or to deliver genetic material [16]. Endophytic bacteria can also assist plants in resisting abiotic stresses. For example, under drought stress, the structure and function of endophytic microbial communities undergo changes. Certain bacteria and fungi enhance plant drought tolerance by producing antioxidant enzymes, modulating phytohormone levels, or improving water uptake capacity [17]. Some of these helpful bacteria can even pass from seeds to new plants, giving the next generation better stress tolerance [18]. Interestingly, research found that root endophytes from purslane show particularly strong plant growth-promoting abilities [19]. These findings highlight the crucial role of endophytic microorganisms in enhancing plant adaptability to environmental stresses.

The diverse mix of endophytes in plants significantly influences their metabolic activity [20]. Studies have found that endophytic bacteria in ginkgo leaves markedly affect the synthesis of secondary metabolites [21]. In saffron (Crocus sativus L.), Ahmad and colleagues found that these internal bacteria help plants grow better, produce more, and increase important plant chemicals [22].

In our study, we used 16 S rDNA sequencing to examine how drought and rehydration affect the endophytic bacteria in purslane. Our metabolomics analysis showed significant changes in purslane’s metabolites under water stress. Spearman correlation analysis was conducted to explore potential relationships between endophytic bacteria and metabolites in purslane. These findings are expected to establish the foundation for further explanation of drought response mechanisms in purslane.

Materials and methods

Material and experimental design

The seeds of P. oleracea used in this study were obtained from the Chemical Ecology Laboratory of South China Agricultural University. For plant cultivation, the nursery soil was pre-moistened before sowing seeds in nursery pots (5 cm × 5 cm). After sowing, a thin layer of dry soil was spread on the surface and covered with plastic wrap. The pots were then placed in an artificial climate chamber (Room 619, Science and Technology Building, South China Agricultural University) maintained at a constant temperature of 26℃ in a dark area. Post-sowing, germination was monitored regularly, and the plastic wrap was removed upon sprouting, followed by scheduled watering. Partial germination was observed after 3 days, with most samples sprouting by day 5 and complete germination achieved by day 10. All seedlings were uniformly transplanted on day 15 after sowing.

The transplantation employed pot culture methods using pots measuring 25 cm in height and 10 cm in diameter. Uniform and healthy seedlings were selected and transplanted at a density of 5 plants per pot. Following transplantation, standardized water and nutrient management protocols were implemented. Plants received daily irrigation with 100 mL per pot to maintain 70% field capacity. Fertilization was carried out once per week using a balanced nutrient solution (Huaduoduo 01, Peters®, M-77, Beijing Hetaili Agricultural Technology Co., Ltd., China) to support optimal growth. Experimental treatments were initiated when the plants reached 45 days post-transplantation.

Four potted plants were designed, namely D (samples from plants subjected to 15 days of drought), N (samples from normally grown plants), RD (samples after 8 days of rewatering), and RN (well-watered control samples collected at the same time point). The experiment was still carried out in a climate-controlled room (Room 619, Science and Technology Building, South China Agricultural University) at 26 °C and 70–80% relative humidity, with a light intensity of 300 µmol s⁻¹ m⁻² at 16 h/8 h in darkness. At the onset of the experiment (Day 0), the well-watered control group (N) maintained normal irrigation while the drought-stressed group (D) commenced water withholding. After 15 days of continuous treatment, stem samples (2 g) were collected from both groups: five plants were randomly selected from one pot per treatment group, immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C. Concurrently, the remaining pots (RN and RD groups) initiated an 8-day rehydration phase. On day 23 (8 days post-rehydration), stem samples were collected from RN and RD groups using identical procedures and preservation methods. Consistent sampling protocols were maintained throughout the study to ensure experimental reproducibility.

Extraction and analysis of 16 S endophytic bacteria from purslane

Two grams of purslane stems from each of the four treatment groups were surface sterilized, and the samples were inoculated on LB solid medium for incubation and observation. No colony growth was observed after 7 days, indicating that the surface sterilization step was thorough and effective and that epiphytic microorganisms on the surface of the samples had been successfully removed, and subsequent endophytic bacterial extractions could be carried out. We extracted DNA by using the FastDNA® Spin Kit for Soil (MP Biomedicals, Santa Ana, CA, USA). The V3-V4 hypervariable regions of 16 S rRNA genes were amplified by PCR using barcoded primers 338 F (5’-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3’) and 806R (5’-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3) [23]. The PCR reaction system contained 4 µL of 5×TransStart FastPfu buffer, 2 µL of 2.5 mM dNTPs, 0.8 µL each of forward and reverse primers (5 µM), 0.4 µL of TransStart FastPfu DNA polymerase, and 10 ng of template DNA, supplemented to 20 µL. The amplification program consisted of 95 °C pre-denaturation for 3 min, 27 cycles (95 °C denaturation for 30 s, 55 °C annealing for 30 s, 72 °C extension for 30 s), followed by 72 °C final extension for 10 min and holding at 4 °C (PCR instrument: ABI GeneAmp® 9700). PCR products were recovered using 2% agarose gel electrophoresis and purified with a DNA gel extraction kit (PCR Clean Up Kit, YuHua, China), then quantified using Qubit 4.0 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Purified PCR products were used for library construction with the NEXTFLEX Rapid DNA-Seq Kit, followed by magnetic bead purification to obtain final libraries. Sequencing was performed on the Illumina NextSeq2000 platform, with raw data deposited in the NCBI SRA database.

Raw data were processed using QIIME2 [24] to generate OTU matrices, with taxonomic classification performed using SILVA138 [25]. Functional annotation of OTUs for metabolic and ecological relevance was subsequently conducted using Tax4Fun2 based on 16 S rDNA sequences [26].

Non-targeted metabolite measurements

Purslane stems were flash-frozen, ground, and extracted overnight in 80% methanol (1 µM chrysin as an internal standard, 0.1 g/ml sample-to-solvent ratio). After centrifugation (13,400 g, 30 min, 4 °C), supernatants were analyzed by UHPLC-MS/MS (Vanquish Flex UHPLC coupled to Q Exactive Plus MS). Metabolites were separated on a Hypersil GOLD column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.9 μm, 30 °C) with mobile phases: (A) 0.1% formic acid in water and (B) acetonitrile. The gradient was 0–2 min (10% B), 2–10 min (50% B), 10.1–13 min (80% B), 14 min (95% B), and re-equilibration to 10% B by 18 min (0.3 ml/min flow rate). MS detection used full scan/ddMS2. ESI parameters: spray voltage ± 3.5/3.2 kV, capillary/aux gas temps 320/350°C, sheath/aux gas flows 35/15 (arbitrary units), S-lens RF 50. Raw mass spectrometry data files were converted to mzXML format using ProteoWizard, and data preprocessing was performed based on MSMS_Public_EXP_NEG_VS17 and MSMS_Public_EXP_Pos_VS17, with retention time calibration using the internal standard chrysin.

We analyzed the metabolomics data using R [27] with several bioinformatics tools (including DESeq2 [28], limma [29], pheatmap [30], and ggplot2 [31]) for raw data preprocessing, differential metabolite screening, and visualization. Metabolite identification was achieved by matching against the KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) database [32] and HMDB (Human Metabolome Database) database [33]. To identify important metabolite changes, we used a two-step statistical approach. First, we applied multivariate analysis (OPLS-DA model) to calculate VIP scores. Then, we combined this with standard statistical tests. We set strict selection rules: only metabolites with VIP > 1, |log2FC| >1, and p < 0.05 were considered significant. All three conditions had to be met together.

Results

Water stress alters endophytic bacterial diversity in P. oleracea

We measured the diversity of endophytic bacterial communities in purslane under different conditions using four common diversity measures (Pielou’s evenness, richness, Shannon, and Simpson indices), along with statistical testing (one-way ANOVA). From the results, the RD group exhibited markedly different α-diversity from the D, N, and RN groups (p < 0.05), whereas these three groups maintained consistent diversity measurements. These findings highlight the profound impact of rehydration on reshaping endophytic microbiota organization. (Fig. 1A-D).

Differences in endophytic bacterial communities of purslane under different water conditions. A Pielou’s evenness. B richness index. C Shannon diversity index. D Simpson index. E PLS-DA score plot showing distinct clustering of bacterial communities among four treatment groups (RD, D, N, RN). The significant separation indicates that water regimes significantly altered the endophytic microbiota structure, with the rehydration group (RD) exhibiting distinct α-diversity patterns compared to other groups (one-way ANOVA, p < 0.05)

We used PLS-DA (a multivariate analysis method) to examine differences in microbial communities across water treatments. The analysis clearly separated the bacterial communities from all four treatment groups, showing that water conditions significantly affect purslane’s endophytic bacteria. The tight clustering of samples within each group also confirmed our experimental methods were consistent and reproducible (Fig. 1E).

Structural differences in endophytic bacterial communities under varying water conditions

We examined the endophytic bacteria in purslane at both phylum and genus levels to compare differences between treatment groups. At the phylum level, Proteobacteria dominated across all groups (> 80% in N, D, and RN groups; 75% in the RD group), followed by Actinobacteria. Notably, the rehydration group (RD) exhibited an increase in Firmicutes abundance and the unique presence of phyla such as Myxococci and Bedllovibrionta, which were undetected in other groups (Fig. 2A). Genus-level analysis revealed that Delftia overwhelmingly dominated (> 90%) in the N, D, and RN groups, while its abundance sharply declined in the RD group. Concurrently, the RD group demonstrated higher genus diversity, featuring characteristic taxa including Pseudomonas, Pandoraea, and Corynebacterium that were absent in other groups (Fig. 2B).

Analysis of endophytic bacterial community structure in purslane. A Phylum-level microbial community profiling (bar plot). B Absolute abundance at the genus level (chord diagram). C Differentially abundant genera in RD vs. D group (top 20). D Differentially abundant genera in RD vs. RN group (top 20)

Since the community structures of the D, N, and RN groups were similar, no significant differences in bacterial genera were detected (Fig. 1A-D). We performed detailed differential genus screening (|log2FC|>1 and p < 0.05) using DESeq2 [28] in the RD vs. D and RD vs. RN groups. The results showed that, consistent with the phylum-level analysis, the abundance of Delftia in the rehydration group (RD) exhibited a significant downward trend compared to the drought group (D) and the control group (RN). According to existing research, Delftia, as an important Gram-negative symbiotic bacterium in plants, can colonize root surfaces by promoting biofilm formation, thereby enhancing plant resistance to drought and salt stress [34]. This finding suggests that after rehydration, the drought stress crisis in purslane was alleviated, and the drought-resistant function of Delftia may no longer be required.

Meanwhile, in the rehydration group (RD), the abundance of several bacterial genera, such as Pandoraea, Massilia, and Stenotrophomonas, was significantly upregulated compared to the D and RN groups (Fig. 2C,D).Previous studies have confirmed that Pandoraea and Stenotrophomonas can synthesize plant hormones like indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) to promote plant growth and development [35, 36]. This change in bacterial communities likely reflects purslane’s shifting needs after rehydration. When water becomes available again, the plants switch from surviving drought to focusing on growth. This change appears to favor bacteria that help plants grow.

Functional prediction analysis of endophytic bacteria

Metabolic pathway prediction via Tax4Fun2 indicated that purslane endophytes were enriched in KEGG secondary functions, particularly core biochemical processes including carbon cycling, nitrogenous base biosynthesis, transmembrane translocation, microbial population interactions, and fatty acid processing (Fig. 3A). Among the 14 most active pathways, we identified both basic energy pathways (sugar breakdown, pentose phosphate, and TCA cycle) and biofilm production pathways (like those in Pseudomonas and Vibrio bacteria). Notably, differences were observed among treatment groups: the RD group (rehydration treatment group) exhibited higher relative enrichment abundance across all detected pathways compared to other groups, while the D group (drought treatment group) showed the lowest enrichment levels. These results indicate that drought stress suppressed the overall metabolic activity of the endophytic bacterial community in purslane. Following rehydration, the bacterial community structure underwent marked changes (Fig. 2A, B), and metabolic activity was restored, indicating that rehydration may have alleviated the effects of drought stress on bacterial community function (Fig. 3B).

KEGG functional analysis of endophytic bacteria. A KEGG secondary pathway enrichment of endophytic bacteria. B Top 14 enriched KEGG tertiary pathways in endophytic bacteria

Co-occurrence network analysis of endobacterial communities

To elucidate the functional coordination mechanisms of endophytic bacterial communities in purslane under drought stress and rehydration conditions, we constructed bacterial correlation interaction networks for the drought-treated group (D group) and rehydration-treated group (RD group) using the SparCC algorithm (|r|>0.6, p < 0.05) (Fig. 4A, C). The RD group’s network showed much more complex connections between bacteria than the D group’s. This difference suggests that drought stress weakened the ecological interactions among bacterial communities, while rehydration facilitated the functional recovery and collaborative reconstruction of endophytic bacteria in purslane. By calculating network topology parameters (degree), we further identified potential “hub species” in the two groups: Delftia and Stenotrophomonas (Fig. 4B, D).

Bacterial interaction networks of D and RD groups. A, C Network visualization (|r|>0.6, p < 0.05). B, D Statistical analysis of network topology parameters

Untargeted metabolomics analysis under different water conditions

Untargeted metabolomics analysis identified a total of 2,973 metabolites, with lipids and lipid-like molecules (22.56%) and organic acids and derivatives (20.02%) representing the most abundant classes (Fig. 5A). Principal component analysis (PCA) demonstrated clear metabolic separation between treatment groups, with the control groups (N and RN) exhibiting similar metabolic profiles while other samples formed two distinct clusters (Fig. 5B).

Metabolomic profiling of purslane under different treatments. A Composition of secondary metabolite classes. B PCA score plot of different treatment groups. C, D Statistics of differentially expressed metabolites between groups

Using stringent criteria (VIP > 1, |log2FC| >1, and p < 0.05) for differential metabolite screening, we observed a significant predominance of up-regulated metabolites over down-regulated metabolites in both comparison groups (D vs. N and RD vs. RN) (Fig. 5C, D). This consistent pattern suggests that purslane primarily responds to environmental changes through activation of specific metabolic pathways rather than suppression of existing ones. The large increase in lipid-related compounds and organic acids likely helps the plant manage water stress and adjust its energy use during drought.

We analyzed the key metabolic differences between drought-treated (D) and normal (N) plants, as well as rewatered (RD) and drought-treated plants. Using the KEGG database, we found these differences were most noticeable in pathways producing important plant compounds, including tyrosine-related compounds, phenylpropanoids, and alkaloids (Fig. 6A, B).

KEGG functional annotation results of significantly differential metabolites (top 20). A D vs. N comparison. B RD vs. RN comparison

Based on these findings, we identified a metabolic pathway involving 20 differential metabolites (Fig. 7A). Our analysis demonstrated that drought stress significantly promoted the accumulation of various secondary metabolites in purslane, including tyrosine, phenylalanine, citric acid, and dopamine. Following rehydration treatment, the levels of most metabolites showed varying degrees of decrease compared to the drought-treated group (D), with the exception of dopamine. We also observed changes in fatty acid metabolism: drought reduced linoleic acid and jasmonic acid levels, but these recovered after rewatering (Fig. 7B). Together, these findings show that drought boosts production of many protective compounds in purslane, while rewatering generally reverses this effect. Fatty acid metabolism appears particularly sensitive to water availability changes.

The color of each metabolite indicates the group with its highest abundance. B Heatmap of metabolite levels across treatment groups

Correlations between endophytic bacteria and metabolites

We examined how endophytic bacteria and metabolites interact in drought-stressed purslane by analyzing the top 20 bacterial types and metabolites in both drought (D) and rehydration (RD) groups (|r| >0.6, p < 0.05). The results revealed a “many-to-many” relationship between endophytic bacteria and metabolites. Specifically, the “key genus” Stenotrophomonas in the RD group showed significant correlations with eight metabolites, confirming its central role in metabolic regulation. Additionally, genera such as Candidatus_Microthrix, Sporichthya, and Isoptericola were each significantly associated with eight metabolites, while Leifsonia exhibited no significant correlations, suggesting functional specialization among bacterial genera. The random forest analysis identified several key metabolites: lipid derivatives (Malyngamide H, FAHFA 20:1; FAHFA 2:0/18:1; [M-H]-), phenylpropanoids (Anthranoside A, L-Phenylalanine), and alkaloids (Cyclohexanamine, nor-6β-Oxycodol). These findings match our earlier discovery that phenylpropanoid pathways become more active during drought, suggesting bacteria help adjust the plant’s chemistry to cope with water stress (Fig. 8).

Discussion

Endophytic bacterial community in P. oleracea maintains stability under drought stress

As an important component of plant microecosystems, plant endophytic bacteria play a key role in promoting plant growth and development and enhancing environmental stress resistance [37, 38]. Our study revealed that purslane’s endophytic bacterial communities remained stable during drought. KEGG analyses showed that the bacteria were enriched in the biofilm synthesis-related pathway, suggesting that purslane spp. may have adopted a conservative microbial regulatory strategy to cope with drought stress. It is worth noting that the endophytic bacterial community structure was significantly altered after rehydration treatment, especially the abundance of Delftia, which decreased significantly. According to existing studies, Delftia improves plant stress tolerance by enhancing antioxidant enzyme activities and improving water uptake efficiency [34, 39], and thus is likely to be an important microbial factor in the outstanding drought tolerance of purslane; the decrease in its abundance after rewatering may reflect the reduced need for drought tolerance functions in the plant. Meanwhile, the abundance of Pandora and Stenotrophomonas (which produce phytohormones such as IAA to promote root development) [35, 36] indicates purslane shifts its focus from survival to growth when water becomes available again.

Multiple secondary metabolites play important roles in drought stress

Purslane contains many beneficial plant compounds that not only make it nutritious but also help it cope with stress [40]. Our study found these compounds play a key role in drought resistance. Specifically, we observed increased activity in pathways that produce stress-fighting compounds like phenylpropanes, tyrosine, and healthy fats. Previous research shows these pathways help plants survive drought [41–44]. For example, adding phenylalanine helped mustard plants maintain better growth during dry conditions [45]. Similarly, we found purslane accumulates more phenylalanine and related compounds when water is scarce, confirming their importance for drought protection.

Relationship of metabolites in purslane to endophytic bacteria

Endophytic bacteria in plants exhibit close relationships with metabolites [46, 47]. Our study found that two key bacteria—Delftia in drought-stressed plants and Stenotrophomonas in rewatered plants—were strongly linked to important drought-response compounds like fats, phenylpropanoids, and alkaloids. This suggests these bacteria help shape purslane’s metabolic response to drought. However, because of the complex“many-to-many”relationships between various metabolites and bacterial genera in purslane, a definitive relationship between the two needs to be determined by further studies. We also found some fungus-related compounds, such as Orbiocrellin A [48], and in future studies, we may add fungal sequencing and transcriptome sequencing to investigate the relationship between endophytes, metabolites, and differential genes in the drought-responsive process of purslane. As for the core functional bacterial genera Delftia and Stenotrophomonas found in this study, we may follow up by isolating and culturing them and conducting functional validation experiments to clarify their specific mechanism of action on metabolic regulation in purslane so as to provide an experimental basis for the theory of plant-microbe interactions.

Conclusion

Purslane’s metabolites and endophytic bacteria work together to respond to drought stress. While the bacterial community remained stable during drought, it changed significantly after rewatering. Different water conditions also affected important metabolites like fats, alkaloids, and phenylalanine compounds. Correlation analysis revealed the association between specific genera and key metabolites, indicating that the plant dynamically responded to water changes through a synergistic “colony-metabolite” network. These findings offer new insights for studying how plants and microbes work together to cope with drought.
