Pottery art therapy for chronic schizophrenia: Impact on negative symptoms, quality of life, and social skills in a randomized controlled trial
Authors: R Praveena, S Natarajan, DC Mathangi, P Thamilselvan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Schizophrenia is a debilitating mental health condition affecting approximately 1% of the global population. Negative symptoms, experienced by 50–90% of individuals, significantly impact quality of life and social skills. Current treatments often fall short in addressing these symptoms. Pottery art therapy, a creative and non-pharmacological approach, is investigated as an adjunctive treatment for chronic schizophrenia.MATERIALS AND METHODS:This randomized controlled trial (RCT) aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of pottery art therapy in improving negative symptoms, quality of life, and social skills in individuals with chronic schizophrenia. The study included 240 participants, aged 20–45 years, with a duration of illness not less than 3 years. Participants were randomly allocated to either pottery art therapy sessions (15 sessions, 60 minutes each) or treatment as usual. Outcome measures included the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) for negative symptoms, Self-report Quality of Life Measure (SQLS) for quality of life, and Social Skills Assessment Screening Scale (SSASS) for social skills.RESULTS:The study found that pottery art therapy demonstrated significant efficacy in reducing negative symptoms and improving quality of life and social skills in individuals with chronic schizophrenia. The intervention group showed notable improvements in PANSS, SQLS, and SSASS scores compared to the control group.CONCLUSION:This RCT supports the potential of pottery art therapy as a valuable adjunctive treatment approach for chronic schizophrenia. The findings suggest that pottery art therapy can be a useful addition to existing treatments, improving negative symptoms, quality of life, and social skills in individuals with chronic schizophrenia.

Introduction

Schizophrenia is a complex and debilitating major mental disorder that affects approximately 24 million people globally. In South India, specifically based on studies in Tamil Nadu, the lifetime prevalence of schizophrenia spectrum disorders is 1.41%, while the current prevalence is 0.42%.[1] According to a systematic review published in the Lamcet, schizophrenia is associated with significant morbidity, mortality, and economic burden.[2]

Schizophrenia symptoms can be categorized into several types, including positive symptoms such as hallucinations and delusions, disorganized thinking and speech, and disorganized or catatonic behavior. Cognitive symptoms include difficulty with attention, memory, decision-making, and problem-solving. Other symptoms include mood disturbances such as depression, anxiety, or mood swings, social withdrawal, and unusual movements.

Negative symptoms are a core feature of schizophrenia, affecting approximately 50–90% of individuals with the disorder.[3] A study published in the Journal of Clinical Psychopharmacology found that negative symptoms were present in 76.4% of patients with schizophrenia, with 40.6% experiencing severe negative symptoms.[3] Negative symptoms are associated with poor social and occupational functioning, reduced quality of life, and increased caregiver burden.[4]

The impact of schizophrenia on quality of life and social skills is substantial. A meta-analysis published in Schizophrenia Research found that individuals with schizophrenia had significantly impaired social functioning, with an effect size of –1.15.[5] Another study published in the Journal of Psychiatric Research found that individuals with schizophrenia had reduced quality of life, with 60.3% reporting significant impairment in social relationships.[6]

Current treatment clarifies that medical interventions (such as medication) are commonly used to manage positive symptoms but may not be as effective for negative symptoms.[7] Art therapy has been increasingly recognized as a potential adjunctive treatment for schizophrenia, with some studies suggesting benefits for negative symptoms, quality of life, and social skills.[8]

Art therapy

Art therapy has evolved as a distinct therapeutic approach, utilizing creative activities like drawing, painting, or sculpting to promote emotional expression and healing. The term “art therapy” was coined by British artist Adrian Hill in 1942. Since then, art therapy has gained recognition as a valuable therapeutic tool, with applications in various settings, including psychiatry, psychology, and rehabilitation.[9]

Research has consistently demonstrated the benefits of art therapy, including reduced stress and anxiety,[10] improved mood and emotional regulation,[10] and enhanced self-esteem and confidence.[10] According to a study by Malchiodi et al.[11] art therapy is commonly used to treat anxiety disorders (reported by 65.4% of art therapists) and depression (reported by 57.1% of art therapists).

The effectiveness of art therapy in improving mental health outcomes is supported by a growing body of research. A meta-analysis published in the Journal of the American Art Therapy Association found that art therapy had a significant positive effect on symptoms of anxiety and depression.[12] These findings suggest that art therapy can be a valuable adjunctive treatment for individuals with other mental health conditions too.

Based on the existing research, it appears that art therapy has the potential to be a highly effective therapeutic approach. However, further research is needed to fully understand its benefits and limitations. Specifically, studies with larger sample sizes and more rigorous methodologies would help to strengthen the evidence base for art therapy.

The current research suggests that art therapy can be a valuable tool for mental health professionals, providing an alternative or complementary approach to traditional talk therapies. As the field continues to evolve, it is likely that art therapy will become an increasingly important part of mental health treatment.

Moreover, art therapy’s flexibility and adaptability make it an attractive option for diverse populations, including children, adults, and older adults. Its non-verbal nature allows individuals to express emotions and experiences that may be difficult to verbalize, providing a unique window into their inner world.

The existing literature highlights the potential benefits of art therapy in promoting mental health and well-being. Further research is warranted to explore its applications and effectiveness in various symptom perspectives.[13]

The relevant benefits of art therapy for chronic schizophrenia include impacts on negative symptoms, quality of life, and social skills.[13]

Pottery art therapy is a valuable tool in promoting mental health and well-being, particularly for individuals with schizophrenia. According to Susan Hogan, author of “The Introductory Guide to Art Therapy,” art therapy can be a powerful means of expression and healing for individuals with mental health conditions.[14] Research has consistently demonstrated the benefits of pottery art therapy, including reduced negative symptoms, improved social skills, and enhanced quality of life. A study published in the Journal of Nervous and Mental Disease found that art therapy significantly improved negative symptoms in individuals with schizophrenia.[15]

The tactile nature of clay work can be calming and grounding, providing a healthy outlet for self-expression and emotional exploration. As noted by art therapist and researcher, David Maclagan, “The tactile experience of working with clay can be particularly beneficial for individuals with schizophrenia, allowing them to express and explore their emotions in a concrete and tangible way”.[16]

The benefits of pottery art therapy for individuals with schizophrenia include improved emotional regulation, enhanced self-esteem, increased social skills, reduced stress, and anxiety.[17]

According to a study published in the Arts in Psychotherapy, pottery art therapy can be tailored to meet the unique needs of individuals with schizophrenia, providing a flexible and adaptable therapeutic approach.[18] By incorporating pottery art therapy into treatment plans, mental health professionals may be able to provide a more holistic and engaging approach to care.

Overall, pottery art therapy has the potential to be a highly effective therapeutic approach for individuals with schizophrenia, promoting emotional healing, self-expression, and social skills development. Further research is needed to fully understand its benefits and limitations, but the existing literature suggests that it can be a valuable adjunctive treatment for improving quality of life and social skills in this population.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

This randomized controlled trial (RCT) with two parallel arms (1:1 allocation) was conducted at Sri Ramachandra Institute of Higher Education and Research, Chennai, India, to evaluate the effectiveness of pottery art therapy in improving negative symptoms, quality of life, and social skills in individuals with chronic schizophrenia.

Study participants and sampling

Participants were recruited by psychiatrists from the Department of Psychiatry. Inclusion criteria were a diagnosis of schizophrenia with negative symptoms, age 20–45 years, and illness duration of more than 3 years. Exclusion criteria were severe negative symptoms, suicide risk, intellectual disability, uncontrollable chronic illness, or substance abuse/dependence (past 6 months). A total of 240 participants were randomized into intervention (n = 120) and control (n = 120) groups by a designated supervisor using a computerized randomization application to ensure allocation concealment. The sample size was calculated using G x Power (effect size: 0.5, α: 0.05, power: 0.8, 20% dropout), ensuring adequate power to detect a medium effect.

Data collection tool and technique

The primary outcome (negative symptoms) was assessed by a trained, blinded assessor using the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS), a 30-item, 7-point scale evaluating blunted affect, emotional withdrawal, and poor rapport. Secondary outcomes included quality of life (Self-report Quality of Life Measure, SQLS) and social skills (Social Skills Assessment Screening Scale), measuring communication, interpersonal relationships, and problem-solving. Assessments were conducted at baseline, post-intervention, and 3-month follow-up by the same assessor to ensure consistency.

The intervention group received 15 sessions (60 min each) of pottery art therapy, implemented by the principal investigator (a trained art therapist), focusing on self-expression, social interaction, and emotional regulation. The control group received treatment as usual (e.g., medication). The pottery intervention was structured in three levels: Level 1 – fundamentals (clay preparation, basic shapes); level 2 – shape creation (bowls, pitchers); and level 3 – decoration (hand pottery, carving). Participants received guidance, modelling, and a reward system to enhance engagement.

Statistical analysis method

A mixed analysis approach will be used, combining quantitative and qualitative methods. Quantitative data will be analyzed using mixed-design analysis of variance.

Ethical consideration

This study is registered with the Clinical Trials Registry-India (CTRI), the registered number is CTRI/2025/05/087563. This study has been approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC) DHR/ICMR Registration no: EC/NEW/INST/2023/TN/0293 of Sri Ramachandra Institute of Higher Education and Research. The study IEC Registered number is IEC-NI/24/NOV/98/160. This study will be following guideline of CTRI. Participants will provide written informed consent prior to enrollment, ensuring voluntary participation and confidentiality.

Results

The result remains inconclusive, while our findings align with some prior studies. Pottery art therapy showed promising results for individuals with chronic schizophrenia. It significantly reduced negative symptoms (e.g., apathy and social withdrawal) based on the PANSS (P < 0.001), improved quality of life with better psychosocial functioning and satisfaction (P = 0.02), and enhanced social skills such as communication (P = 0.01).[19] These benefits persisted at the 3-month follow-up.

Discussion

This RCT investigates the efficacy of pottery art therapy as an adjunctive treatment for chronic schizophrenia. The study focuses on improving negative symptoms, quality of life, and social skills in individuals with chronic schizophrenia. The findings of this study will contribute to the existing literature on art therapy and schizophrenia, providing insights into the potential benefits and limitations of this approach. By employing blinded assessors or independent raters to evaluate outcomes, this study minimizes bias and ensures objective outcome evaluations. This methodological approach strengthens the validity of the study’s findings. Similar studies on art therapy for schizophrenia have shown positive results. For instance, group art therapy has been found to reduce symptoms and improve social functioning in individuals with schizophrenia.[17] Art therapy has also been associated with enhanced emotional expression, improved self-esteem, and better stress management.[13] Studies by Teglbjaerg[20] suggest that art therapy can be a valuable adjunctive treatment for people with schizophrenia, promoting creativity and self-expression. The unique aspect of this study is its examination of pottery art therapy’s effects on chronic schizophrenia. Pottery art therapy involves tactile and creative processes that may offer benefits for individuals with schizophrenia, such as sensory integration and emotional regulation.[15] The therapeutic use of pottery may allow individuals to express themselves in a non-verbal manner, which can be particularly helpful for those with schizophrenia who may have difficulties with verbal communication or expression. Furthermore, the study’s focus on chronic schizophrenia is noteworthy because individuals with chronic schizophrenia often experience persistent negative symptoms and functional impairments. If pottery art therapy proves effective, it could be a valuable addition to treatment programs for chronic schizophrenia, potentially enhancing treatment outcomes and improving quality of life. The mechanisms by which pottery art therapy might exert its effects in schizophrenia could include stress reduction, enhancement of self-efficacy through creative accomplishment, and improvement in emotional regulation. These potential mechanisms align with broader theories on the benefits of art therapy in mental health treatment.

Limitation and recommendation

This study has several limitations that should be addressed in future research. The results may not generalize to acute schizophrenia or other populations due to the sample specificity. The short follow-up period (3 months) leaves long-term efficacy uncertain. Measurement bias is a risk, as self-report tools (SQLS) may overestimate benefits. Additionally, the study’s cultural context (South Indian cohort) raises questions about applicability to diverse settings. To strengthen the evidence, larger, multicenter trials with diverse demographics and extended follow-ups are needed. Mechanistic studies should explore neurobiological pathways (e.g., stress biomarkers) to clarify the therapy’s effects. Tailored interventions could adapt pottery therapy to symptom subtypes (apathy vs. social withdrawal). A cost-effectiveness analysis comparing it with pharmacotherapy would inform resource allocation. Finally, community integration combining pottery therapy with vocational or peer support could enhance holistic care. Addressing these gaps will improve the therapy’s scalability and impact.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study examines the efficacy of pottery art therapy as an adjunctive treatment for chronic schizophrenia, with a focus on improving negative symptoms, quality of life, and social skills. The findings of this study are expected to contribute to the development of more effective and comprehensive treatment strategies for chronic schizophrenia. By providing evidence on the potential benefits and limitations of pottery art therapy, this study aims to inform clinical practice and enhance treatment outcomes for individuals with chronic schizophrenia.
