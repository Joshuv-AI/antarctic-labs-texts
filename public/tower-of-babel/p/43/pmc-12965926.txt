Interaction between transient receptor potential vanilloid 4 and glutamate NMDA receptor subunit 1 mediates endoplasmic reticulum stress and neuroinflammation in postoperative delirium
Authors: Shiqian Huang, Tianhao Zhang, Yu Wang, Hongying Du, Jingang He, Hongchun Zeng, Lulin Ma, Daling Deng, Yuxi Zhou, Shiya Liu, Wenjing Zhao, Xinxin Yang, Linlin Han, Shuai Zhao, Shaofang Shu, Shanglong Yao, Qi Zhong, Xiangdong Chen, Jie Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPostoperative delirium (POD) is a serious and prevalent neurocognitive complication that poses a major clinical challenge because its mechanism is unclear. This study identifies a pathogenic pathway centred on the direct interaction between transient receptor potential vanilloid 4 (TRPV4) and the essential N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor (NMDAR) subunit GluN1. Using a murine POD model, the neuron-centric glutamatergic dysfunction in the hippocampus was initially confirmed through ex vivo metabolic kinetic analysis. Transcriptomic analysis revealed upregulation of Trpv4, predominantly in neurons. Co-immunoprecipitation coupled with mass spectrometry revealed that TRPV4 directly interacts with GluN1. This enhanced TRPV4-GluN1 coupling promoted GluN1 phosphorylation at serine 896 and hyperactivated NMDAR signalling. We subsequently observed the concurrent induction of endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, as evidenced by a dilated ER ultrastructure and the upregulation of the expression of UPR markers (ATF6, p-PERK, p-IRE1α, and CHOP), as well as neuroinflammation, characterized by microglial activation and elevated expression of proinflammatory mediators (IL-6, IL-1β, and ICAM-1). These molecular pathologies were associated with decreased neuronal activity and the characteristic cognitive-affective deficits associated with POD. Critically, both pharmacological inhibition of TRPV4 (HC067047) and hippocampal CA3-specific Trpv4 knockdown reversed these pathologies and rescued the behaviour. Inhibiting NMDAR with MK801 recapitulated these therapeutic benefits. Furthermore, TRPV4 was significantly upregulated in early-onset Alzheimer’s disease patients. Introduction

Postoperative delirium (POD) is an acute neurocognitive disorder characterized by fluctuating disturbances in attention and awareness emerging within days following anesthesia and surgery [1]. Associated with prolonged hospitalization, accelerated cognitive decline, and increased mortality, POD imposes a substantial clinical burden [2]. While its pathogenesis remains incompletely defined, neuroinflammation and synaptic dysfunction are recognized as central drivers [3–5]. Dysregulation of glutamatergic signalling, which is crucial for synaptic plasticity, has also been reported [6], but the precise molecular pathways linking perioperative insult to neuronal dysfunction are unclear.

The transient receptor potential vanilloid 4 (TRPV4) channel, a calcium-permeable nonselective cation channel, is being increasingly recognized for its role in central nervous system (CNS) pathophysiology. TRPV4 activation contributes to neuroinflammation by modulating microglial and astrocytic activity, promoting NLRP3 inflammasome assembly, and facilitating the release of proinflammatory cytokines [7]. In addition to inflammation, TRPV4 is involved in diverse processes, including pain sensation, thermoregulation, and neurodegeneration [8–10]. Critically, TRPV4-mediated calcium influx can disrupt intracellular calcium homeostasis, a key trigger of endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress [11]. ER stress, in turn, activates the unfolded protein response (UPR), which can initiate and amplify proinflammatory cascades, creating a vicious cycle that exacerbates neuronal dysfunction [12, 13]. In models of Parkinson’s disease and glioma, TRPV4 has been shown to induce ER stress, leading to dopaminergic neuron loss or mitophagy[14]. These findings position TRPV4 at a critical intersection of calcium signalling, ER stress, and neuroinflammation—all processes relevant to POD. However, its specific role and downstream effectors in POD remain unknown.

Prior proteomic studies have identified numerous TRPV4-interacting proteins that modulate its function [15]. Notably, TRPV4 activation has been shown to increase N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor (NMDAR) currents in hippocampal neurons after ischaemic insult [16]. Furthermore, transient receptor potential melastatin 4 (TRPM4), a related cation channel, has been shown to form functional complexes with NMDAR subunits, contributing to excitotoxic neuronal injury [17]. NMDARs, particularly those containing the essential GluN1 subunit (glutamate ionotropic receptor NMDA type subunit 1), are pivotal for synaptic plasticity, and their overactivation leads to calcium overload, contributing to neuronal damage in various neurological and psychiatric disorders [18–20]. These findings suggest a potential mechanism through which TRPV4 activation could potentiate NMDAR function. However, whether direct physical and functional interactions occur between TRPV4 and GluN1 and whether these interactions serve as critical upstream events driving ER stress and neuroinflammation in POD have not been investigated.

This study aimed to elucidate the pathogenic role of the TRPV4-GluN1 axis in POD. We hypothesized that anesthesia/surgery induces the upregulation of hippocampal TRPV4 expression, which physically couples with GluN1 to promote its phosphorylation. This cascade leads to ER stress and neuroinflammation, culminating in cognitive deficits.

To test this hypothesis, we employed a multiomics approach combined with pharmacological and genetic interventions. Hippocampal transcriptomics, [1H-13C] nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) metabolic mapping, co-immunoprecipitation coupled with mass spectrometry (Co-IP/MS), and AlphaFold3 modelling were used to identify the involvement of TRPV4 and characterize its interaction with GluN1 in POD. Functional validation was performed using specific inhibitors and viral-mediated knockdown. To assess translational relevance, we also analyzed TRPV4 expression in human Alzheimer’s disease (AD) brain transcriptomes, focusing on early-onset cases. Our findings reveal that TRPV4-GluN1 coupling is a critical driver of ER stress and neuroinflammation in POD, highlighting its potential as a novel therapeutic target with broader relevance to neurocognitive impairment.

Results

Anesthesia/surgery induced cognitive deficits with metabolic disruption in the hippocampus

A murine model of POD was established by exploring the right carotid artery under sevoflurane anesthesia (Fig. 1a). Behavioural analyses (Fig. 1b) conducted one day postsurgery revealed that POD model mice exhibited anxiety-like behaviour in the open field test (OFT), as demonstrated by reduced time spent in and frequency of entries into the central zone (Fig. 1c–d), without alterations in total locomotion (Fig. 1e). This anxiogenic phenotype was confirmed in the elevated plus maze (EPM; Fig. S1a), where a significant decrease in the percentage of time spent in the open arms was observed (Fig. S1b). Impairments in both spatial and recognition memory were also evident in POD model mice, as reflected by significantly reduced exploration time and fewer entries into the novel arm in the Y-maze test (Fig. 1f-g) and a reduced discrimination ratio in the novel object recognition (NOR) test (Fig. S1c).

To investigate the underlying metabolic alterations, ex vivo [1H-13C] NMR metabolic mapping of hippocampal tissue was performed using [1-13C] glucose (Glc1) and [2-13C] acetate (Ace2) as metabolic tracers (Fig. 1h). Analysis of Glc1 metabolism, which reflects overall neuronal and astrocytic activity, revealed significant alterations in POD model mice. Specifically, the enrichment in Glu3, Glu4, Gln4, and Glx2 markedly decreased (Fig. 1i, top panel). Orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) of the Glc1 metabolic profile revealed clear separation between the CON and POD model groups (T score: 30.7%; orthogonal T score: 28%) (Fig. 1j). Variable importance in projection (VIP) analysis, which identifies the metabolites most responsible for group separation, highlighted GABA2, GABA3, Lac, Glu3, Gln4, and Glx3 (VIP ≥ 1) as the primary discriminators (Fig. 1k). In contrast, metabolic fluxes traced by the astrocyte-specific substrate Ace2 remained largely unchanged in POD model mice, with the notable exception of decreased enrichment in Glx2 (Fig. 1i, bottom panel). Consistently, while OPLS-DA of the Ace2 profile also revealed group separation (T score: 17.3%; orthogonal T score: 28%) (Fig. 1l), the major discriminating metabolites (VIP ≥ 1) included Glx3, Glx2, Gln4, NAA, and Asp (Fig. 1m). These findings indicate a preferential disruption of neuronal, rather than astrocytic, glutamate metabolism in the hippocampus following anesthesia/surgery.

Concomitant with these behavioural and metabolic deficits, neuronal activity in the hippocampus was suppressed. A significant reduction in the number of c-Fos-positive neurons was observed in the CA3 region via immunofluorescence (IF) staining (Fig. 1n). Furthermore, a marked decrease in dendritic spine density was revealed by Golgi staining (Fig. 1o), suggesting impaired synaptic connectivity. These functional and structural changes occurred in the absence of significant neuronal loss, as no increase in apoptosis was detected by TUNEL or Nissl staining (Fig. S1d–e).

Collectively, these data demonstrate that the anesthesia/surgery model recapitulates the core features of POD, including affective and cognitive deficits, a neuron-specific disturbance of hippocampal glutamate metabolism, and associated synaptic dysfunction without neuronal apoptosis.

Transcriptomic profiling revealed that Trpv4 is a key upregulated gene associated with hippocampal euroinflammation in POD and is conserved in the human early-onset AD brain

To elucidate the molecular underpinnings of POD-associated hippocampal dysfunction, transcriptomic profiling was conducted on hippocampal tissues from two independent experimental cohorts. Comparative analysis revealed 68 consistently differentially expressed genes (DEGs) across both datasets (Fig. 2a). Among these, Trpv4 emerged as a prominent candidate. Random forest machine learning analysis revealed that Trpv4 was among the top ten most important DEGs (Fig. 2b-c), and it was consistently upregulated at the transcript level in POD model mice compared with controls. Functional enrichment analysis using the Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) databases revealed that most DEGs were enriched mainly in pathways related to neuroinflammatory processes, including cytokine-mediated signalling, leukocyte migration, and immune response activation (Fig. S2-S3).

To directly evaluate the clinical relevance of Trpv4 dysregulation, we extended our analysis to human neurocognitive disorders. We analyzed a publicly available transcriptomic dataset (GSE203206) comprising brain tissue from non-demented controls (NDCs; n = 8) and AD patients stratified by age of onset. Differential expression analysis confirmed that compared with NDCs, early-onset AD (< 60 years) and late-onset AD (70–80 years) present largely non-overlapping transcriptional signatures (Fig. S4a). Notably, TRPV4 emerged as a significantly upregulated gene specifically within the early-onset AD subgroup (Fig. S4d), a change not observed in either the combined cohort (Fig. S4b) or late-onset AD (Fig. S4c). These findings from human data indicate that TRPV4 dysregulation is not limited to acute perioperative settings but is also a feature of chronic neurodegenerative conditions, thereby supporting its broader clinical and pathological relevance in the context of cognitive impairment.

The upregulation of Trpv4 expression at the transcriptome level was rigorously validated at the protein level. Western blot (WB) analysis confirmed a significant increase in TRPV4 protein abundance in the hippocampal lysates of POD model mice compared with those of control mice (Fig. 2d and f). To determine the cellular source of this upregulation, IF co-staining was performed. Quantitative colocalization analysis demonstrated that the enhanced TRPV4 immunoreactivity predominantly overlapped with that of the neuronal marker NeuN in the hippocampus (Fig. 2g-h and S5a). In contrast, minimal colocalization was observed with markers for astrocytes (GFAP; Fig. S5b) or microglia (IBA1; Fig. S5c). The specificity of the TRPV4 antibody signal was further verified by a peptide blocking assay, in which preadsorption with the immunizing peptide abolished specific staining (Fig. S5d).

Concurrent with the neuron-specific upregulation of TRPV4 expression, a robust neuroinflammatory response was evident in the hippocampus following anesthesia/surgery. This response was characterized by marked increases in the protein levels of key proinflammatory mediators, including interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM-1), as quantified by WB (Fig. 2d–e). IF analysis revealed a significant increase in the number of ionized calcium-binding adapter molecule 1 (IBA1)-positive cells, indicating microglial activation and proliferation (Fig. 2i). Furthermore, histological examination via haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining revealed clear evidence of leukocyte infiltration in the hippocampal subregions of POD model mice (Fig. 2j). Taken together, these findings establish that the specific upregulation of TRPV4 in hippocampal neurons occurs alongside a neuroinflammatory cascade in the POD model.

Pharmacological inhibition of TRPV4 expression partly rescued behavioural deficits and attenuated neuroinflammation

To establish a causal role for TRPV4 in POD pathogenesis, mice were treated with the selective TRPV4 antagonist HC067047 (10 mg/kg, i.p.) 30 min prior to anesthesia/surgery (Fig. 3a). Behavioural assessments demonstrated that pharmacological TRPV4 blockade effectively ameliorated anxiety-like and memory-related deficits in the POD model (Fig. 3b–f and Fig. S6). In the OFT, compared with their vehicle-treated counterparts, the HC067047-treated POD model mice exhibited a greater frequency of entries into the central zone, despite the lack of a significant difference in the total time spent there (Fig. 3c–d). Spatial working memory, assessed by the Y-maze test, also improved, with HC067047 treatment leading to increased time spent exploring the novel arm (Fig. 3e) and a higher frequency of novel arm entries (Fig. 3f). The therapeutic effect of HC067047 on anxiety and recognition memory was corroborated in additional tests (Fig. S6a), as it increased the percentage of time spent in the open arms of the EPM test (Fig. S6b) and enhanced the discrimination ratio in the NOR test (Fig. S6c).

At the cellular and molecular levels, HC067047 treatment effectively counteracted key pathological changes in the hippocampus. IF analysis revealed that the number of c-Fos-positive neurons, a marker of neuronal activity, was partially restored in the hippocampal CA3 region of the HC067047-treated POD model mice (Fig. 3g-h). Concurrently, the activation state of microglia was attenuated, as evidenced by a significant reduction in the density of IBA1+ cells (Fig. 3g and i). WB quantification of the hippocampal lysates confirmed the broad downregulation of proinflammatory mediator expression, as the protein levels of IL-6, IL-1β, and ICAM-1 significantly decreased following HC067047 administration (Fig. 3j–k).

Collectively, these results from pharmacological intervention demonstrate that TRPV4 channel activity is required for the manifestation of anesthesia/surgery-induced behavioural deficits, neuronal hypoactivity, and hippocampal neuroinflammation in the POD model.

TRPV4 antagonism alleviated hippocampal ER stress

Given that TRPV4 functions as a calcium-permeable channel and that intracellular calcium overload is a primary trigger of ER stress, we next examined whether TRPV4-driven neuroinflammation involves the induction of ER stress. Ultrastructural analysis by transmission electron microscopy revealed pronounced dilation of the ER lumen in the hippocampal neurons of POD model mice, a morphological hallmark of ER stress that was substantially mitigated by pretreatment with HC067047 (Fig. 4a).

Molecular profiling revealed coordinated activation of the three major UPR signalling branches initiated by ER sensors. WB analysis revealed that TRPV4 inhibition attenuated the activation of three major sensor kinases, as evidenced by reduced phosphorylation of protein kinase RNA-like ER kinase (PERK), inositol-requiring enzyme 1α (IRE1α), and activating transcription factor 6 (ATF6), (Fig. 4b–e). The induction of the integrated downstream effector CHOP (CCAAT/-enhancer-binding protein homologous protein), a common target of these pathways, was also suppressed by TRPV4 blockade (Fig. 4b and c). IF staining spatially resolved these changes, confirming the neuron-specific upregulation of ATF6 and CHOP in the CA3 region. TRPV4 blockade effectively mitigated the upregulation of ATF6, although it did not significantly reduce CHOP levels (Fig. 4f–i).

These results demonstrate that TRPV4 activation drives a multi-branched UPR and, consequently, ER stress in hippocampal neurons during POD and that pharmacological inhibition of TRPV4 activity mitigates this pathogenic cascade.

Hippocampus-specific knockdown of Trpv4 recapitulated the therapeutic effects of pharmacological inhibition

To provide genetic evidence for the necessity of TRPV4, we stereotaxically injected adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors encoding short hairpin RNA against Trpv4 (shTrpv4) or a non-targeting control (shNC) into the mouse hippocampal CA3 region (Fig. 5a–c). WB analysis confirmed the efficient knockdown of TRPV4 protein expression in the hippocampi of shTrpv4 mice (Fig. 5d–e). Remarkably, this genetic intervention phenocopied the therapeutic profile of pharmacological TRPV4 blockade. In behavioural tests, Trpv4 knockdown significantly alleviated both anxiety-like and memory-related deficits in the POD model (Fig. 5f–j and Fig. S7). In the OFT, shTrpv4-treated animals spent more time in the central zone, despite the lack of significant difference in the frequency of central zone entries (Fig. 5g and h). This anxiolytic effect was corroborated by the results of the EPM test (Fig. S7a), in which shTrpv4 mice spent a greater percentage of time exploring the open arms (Fig. S7b). Spatial and recognition memory were also rescued, as evidenced by improved performance in the Y-maze (increased novel arm time and entries; Fig. 5i and j) and a higher discrimination ratio in the NOR test (Fig. S7c).

Cellular and molecular analyses confirmed that genetic Trpv4 knockdown reversed key pathological changes. In shTrpv4 mice, the anesthesia/surgery-induced reduction in hippocampal neuronal activity (c-Fos immunoreactivity) was partially reversed (Fig. 5k). Microglial activation, as measured by IBA1+ cell density, was also attenuated (Fig. 5l). The proinflammatory state in the hippocampus was ameliorated, as shTrpv4 treatment reduced the protein levels of IL-6, IL-1β, and ICAM-1 (Fig. 5m and n). Furthermore, the ER stress response was mitigated, as evidenced by decreased ATF6 protein levels and reduced phosphorylation of the UPR sensors PERK and IRE1α (Fig. 5o–r).

Identification and validation of a functional TRPV4-GluN1 protein complex

To investigate the interaction mechanisms of TRPV4 signalling, we first performed proteomic analysis of hippocampal lysates at 6 h and 24 h post-anesthesia/surgery. Co-IP/MS revealed the NMDAR subunit GluN1 as a prominent TRPV4-interacting protein, with specific GluN1 peptides detected at both time points (Fig. S8 and S9). Functional enrichment analysis of the shared TRPV4 interactome revealed pathways related to glutamate signalling and ER processing (Fig. S8b–j), supporting a biologically relevant interaction.

We next characterized this interaction structurally and functionally. Computational modelling with AlphaFold3 predicted a high-confidence complex between the intracellular domains of TRPV4 and GluN1, revealing specific interface residues (Fig. 6a and b). IF analysis and quantitative colocalization confirmed significant spatial overlap between TRPV4 and GluN1 in the CA3 region (Fig. 6c and d). Co-IP of hippocampal lysates revealed that the physical interaction between TRPV4 and GluN1 was significantly stronger in POD model mice than in control mice (Fig. 6e). Importantly, this enhanced interaction was substantially attenuated by pretreatment with the TRPV4 antagonist HC067047, indicating that TRPV4 channel activity regulates its binding to GluN1.

Crucially, this enhanced coupling had direct functional consequences for NMDAR regulation. While total GluN1 protein levels remained unchanged in the hippocampus (Fig. 6f and g), the phosphorylation of GluN1 at serine 896 (pGluN1), a key modification linked to increased receptor activity, was significantly elevated in POD model mice. This increase in pGluN1 expression was reversed by both pharmacological TRPV4 inhibition with HC067047 (Fig. 6f) and genetic Trpv4 knockdown (Fig. 6g), indicating that TRPV4 signalling specifically modulates GluN1 through post-translational phosphorylation.

Collectively, these data reveal a novel, disease-modulated TRPV4-GluN1 complex in which TRPV4 activation promotes physical association with GluN1 and drives its phosphorylation, thereby exacerbating the pathological overactivation of NMDARs in the hippocampus in a POD model.

NMDAR inhibition phenocopied the therapeutic effects of TRPV4 blockade in POD

Building on the identification of the TRPV4-GluN1 interaction, we next investigated whether inhibiting the downstream effector NMDAR could similarly ameliorate POD pathophysiology. Administration of the NMDAR antagonist MK801 (0.5 mg/kg, i.p.) after anesthesia/surgery effectively reversed multiple pathological hallmarks of POD (Fig. 7a). MK801 directly reduced the phosphorylation of GluN1 at serine 896 (pGluN1) in the hippocampus (Fig. S10a and S10b), confirming target engagement. Behaviourally, MK801 alleviated anesthesia/surgery-induced anxiety-like behaviour and memory impairment, as evidenced by the results of multiple behavioural paradigms (OFT, Y-maze; Fig. 7b–f; EPM, NOR; Fig. S10c–e). Control experiments verified that this dose of MK801 did not alter baseline locomotion or cognitive behaviours in naïve mice (Fig. S11).

At the molecular and cellular levels, MK801 treatment normalized neuronal activity, as indicated by restored c-Fos expression (Fig. 7g and h), and attenuated neuroinflammation by reducing microglial activation (Fig. 7i and j) and lowering the hippocampal levels of proinflammatory mediators (IL-6, IL-1β, and ICAM-1; Fig. 7k and l). Most notably, MK801 significantly suppressed ER stress, as evidenced by reduced protein levels of the three major sensor kinases (PERK, IRE1α, and ATF6) and the downstream effector CHOP (Fig. 7m-p).

Thus, pharmacological inhibition of an NMDAR phenocopied the multifaceted therapeutic effects of TRPV4 blockade/Trpv4 knockdown, rescuing behavioural deficits, neuroinflammation, and ER stress.

Discussion

The specific pathological mechanisms underlying POD remain elusive. This study provides evidence that a direct protein interaction between TRPV4 and the NMDAR subunit GluN1 in hippocampal neurons is a central driver of POD pathogenesis. We delineated a signalling axis from upstream stress to downstream dysfunction: surgical/anaesthetic stress upregulates hippocampal TRPV4 expression, which directly couples with the essential NMDAR subunit GluN1. This leads to increased GluN1 phosphorylation and hyperactivation of NMDARs. The subsequent cascade results in ER stress, neuroinflammation, and ultimately decreased neuronal activity and the characteristic cognitive-affective deficits of POD.

Extensive evidence links POD to surgery and anesthesia [20–22]. We employed a sevoflurane anesthesia/carotid exposure model, which effectively mimicked clinical POD without compromising motor function, facilitating behavioural assessment. Our findings confirm that this model replicates postoperative anxiety and memory deficits, which is consistent with prior reports [5, 23]. Metabolic profiling highlighted a neuron-preferential dysfunction in the hippocampal glutamate synthesis pathway post-anesthesia/surgery. Glutamatergic dysregulation is crucial given the indispensable role of glutamate in brain health and its involvement in numerous neuropsychiatric disorders [24–26]. While differences from the findings of some prior studies may stem from variations in surgical models or assessment timepoints [6, 27], it is noteworthy that cognitive impairment in POD model mice likely arises from synaptic alterations rather than cell death, underscoring the importance of synaptic dysfunction in POD[5].

Integrated transcriptomic analyses identified Trpv4 as a significantly upregulated in the hippocampi of POD model mice. TRPV4 functions as a calcium-permeable cation channel expressed in both neurons and glial cells. Importantly, our spatial expression analysis revealed that its upregulation was predominantly localized to hippocampal neurons, suggesting a neuron-centric pathogenic contribution in this POD model. These findings are consistent with observations from a lipopolysaccharide-induced depression model [28]. While previous studies have implicated TRPV4 in glial-mediated neuroinflammation in other contexts [29], the observed neuronal enrichment here indicates a distinct cellular mechanism in POD pathogenesis. This may be attributed to the specific cellular stress responses triggered by the surgical-anaesthetic insult or differences in model systems.

On the basis of these findings, we focused on ER stress, considering that TRPV4 activation can induce calcium overload—a known trigger of ER stress—and that ER stress can directly promote neuroinflammation via pathways such as NLRP3 inflammasome activation [13, 30, 31]. Previous studies have linked ER stress to sevoflurane-induced neurotoxicity and POD [32, 33]. We thus proposed that TRPV4 activation initiates a pathogenic cascade involving ER stress and subsequent neuroinflammation. Experimental validation revealed that pharmacological or genetic inhibition of TRPV4 rescued behavioural deficits and neuroinflammation while also alleviating ER structural expansion and the upregulation of key UPR markers (ATF6, p-IRE1α, p-PERK), indicating that ER stress is a critical intermediary linking TRPV4 activation to downstream inflammation.

A central finding of this study is the discovery of a direct physical interaction between TRPV4 and the core NMDAR subunit GluN1. This finding originated from Co-IP/MS data, was modelled with high confidence by AlphaFold3, and was quantitatively confirmed. Importantly, under POD conditions, this interaction was markedly enhanced, accompanied by increased phosphorylation of GluN1 at serine 896. Notably, both the increased interaction and the increased phosphorylation could be reversed by TRPV4 inhibition or knockdown, indicating that TRPV4 signalling actively modulates NMDAR function through post‑translational modification. These findings not only extend the previous reports of TRPV4‑mediated regulation of NMDA currents in ischaemic models in ischaemia and epilepsy models [15, 34] but also establish for the first time, in the context of POD, a specific, disease‑state‑regulated TRPV4‑GluN1 protein complex.

GluN1 is essential for NMDAR function and synaptic plasticity [35]. Sustained NMDAR activation can lead to calcium overload and ER stress [36]. In our study, GluN1 phosphorylation in the hippocampus increased after anesthesia/surgery. The NMDAR antagonist MK801 alleviated ER stress and neuroinflammation in the POD model, restored neuronal activity, and improved cognition, consistent with previous research [20]. To address the potential confounding effects of MK801 on motor function, an MK801 control experiment confirmed that the administration of MK801 did not significantly affect baseline locomotor activity (Fig. S11), indicating that the behavioural improvement was due to cognitive recovery. Nonetheless, we acknowledge that subtle or context-dependent effects of NMDAR antagonists on exploration patterns cannot be entirely ruled out. These findings underscore the importance of modulating glutamatergic signalling to mitigate POD-related abnormalities.

The translational value of our findings is supported by human brain data. Analysis of transcriptomic data from AD patients revealed specific upregulation of the TRPV4 gene in early-onset cases, suggesting that TRPV4 may contribute not only as a mediator in acute animal models but also as a potential susceptibility factor in human neurocognitive disorders. Given that TRPV4 antagonists such as HC067047 are already in preclinical development for other conditions, clinical translation for POD may be accelerated [37, 38].

We acknowledge several limitations in this study. First, the experiments were conducted exclusively in male mice, and the potential influence of sex differences was not assessed. Second, although previous studies have reported that intraperitoneally administered HC067047 and MK801 can cross the blood‒brain barrier [28, 39], we did not directly measure their actual concentrations in the brains of the model animals. Furthermore, while clinical correlation analysis is suggested, further validation using samples from POD patients is needed; however, brain tissue samples from POD patients are extremely rare, making systematic analysis challenging at present. Finally, direct validation of the key residues at the TRPV4-GluN1 interaction interface through approaches such as point mutation and structural biology is needed to definitively establish the necessity of this binding in pathogenesis. Future research should focus on developing inhibitors that specifically target the TRPV4-GluN1 interaction interface, evaluating their efficacy in animal models of both sexes, and further investigating the dynamic regulation of this interaction during the perioperative period.

This study elucidates a novel pathogenesis for POD centred on the TRPV4-GluN1 protein complex. This interaction serves as a critical signal amplifier, converting initial perioperative stress into sustained neuronal GluN1 hyperactivation, ER stress, and neuroinflammation. By revealing this axis, we not only advance the mechanistic understanding of POD but also propose new therapeutic avenues: TRPV4 and the TRPV4-GluN1 interface emerge as promising novel targets.

Materials and methods

Animals

The animal experimental protocol was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (IACUC Approval No. 3720). All procedures were performed in strict compliance with institutional and national animal welfare guidelines. Thirteen-month-old male C57BL/6 J mice (25–35 g) were purchased from Biont Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). All the animals had access to food and water ad libitum and were housed in a quiet, controlled environment with a 12-h light/dark cycle prior to randomization into experimental groups.

Animal model

A murine model of POD was established via right carotid artery exploration under sevoflurane anesthesia, as previously described [20, 22]. Briefly, the right common carotid artery was isolated and surgically explored, followed by wound irrigation and closure. Postoperative analgesia was provided with lidocaine ointment. The detailed surgical protocols are provided in the supplementary materials.

Animal groups

To test our hypotheses, this study included four independent animal experimental series, as detailed below:

Experiment 1 (Fig. 1a)

Mice were randomly assigned to either the control group (CON) or the surgery group (POD) to validate the postoperative delirium (POD) model. The mice in the control group were placed in their home cages with room air for 4 h. This setup was designed to more closely emulate the clinical scenario of individuals who are not subjected to anesthesia or surgery and thus naturally breathe ambient air [40].

Experiment 2 (Fig. 3a)

Mice were randomly allocated to three groups: (1) the vehicle group (vehicle injection), (2) the POD + vehicle group (POD modelling plus vehicle injection), and (3) the POD + HC067047 group (POD modelling plus injection of the TRPV4 antagonist HC067047). The selective TRPV4 antagonist HC067047 (TargetMol, Cat. No.: T4680) was dissolved in vehicle (4% dimethyl sulfoxide [DMSO] in normal saline) and administered intraperitoneally at a dose of 10 mg/kg 30 min prior to surgery. This dose was selected on the basis of previous studies demonstrating its neuroprotective effects and favourable blood–brain barrier penetrance [28]. The mice in the vehicle group received an equal volume of the solvent alone.

Experiment 3 (Fig. 5a)

Three weeks before surgery, mice were stereotaxically injected with AAV vectors encoding short hairpin RNA (shRNA) under the control of the human Synapsin I (hSyn) promoter into the bilateral hippocampal CA3 region. The mice were subsequently randomly assigned to one of two groups: (1) the shNC + POD group, in which mice were injected with a control AAV expressing a non-targeting shRNA, or (2) the shTrpv4 + POD group, in which mice were injected with an AAV expressing a Trpv4-targeting shRNA for specific gene knockdown. The surgical procedures were identical across all groups.

Experiment 4 (Fig. 7a)

Mice were randomly assigned to one of two groups: (1) the POD + Vehicle group or (2) the POD + MK801 group. The non-competitive NMDAR antagonist MK-801 (TargetMol, Cat. No.: T6259) was dissolved in normal saline and administered intraperitoneally at a dosage of 0.5 mg/kg 30 min after surgery. This dose was chosen because of its reported neuroprotective activity without inducing significant toxicity [41]. The mice in the vehicle group received an equal volume of normal saline.

Behavioural tests

Behavioural tests were conducted as follows: OFT and Y-maze tests were conducted on postoperative Day 1 and EPM and NOR tests on Day 2. Tests were recorded and analyzed using ANY-maze software (Stoelting, USA). Specific protocols are provided in the supplementary materials.

Stereotaxic injection of recombinant AAVs

For hippocampus-specific Trpv4 knockdown, recombinant AAV vectors were stereotaxically injected into the hippocampal CA3 region. The Trpv4 knocdown vector (AAV-hSyn-mTrpv4-eGFP) and non-targeting control vector (AAV-hSyn-NC-eGFP) were purchased from Taitool Bioscience Co., Ltd. (China), with titres ranging from 5.0 to 6.0 × 1012 genome copies per mL. Under anesthesia, the mice were head-fixed in a stereotaxic frame, after which bilateral craniotomies were performed above the target coordinates. A volume of 120 nL of viral suspension was injected into each hippocampal CA3 site (coordinates relative to bregma: anterior/posterior, − 2.1 mm; medial/lateral, ± 2.5 mm; dorsal/ventral, − 2.4 mm) using a NanoFil syringe (World Precision Instruments, USA) at an injection rate of 30 nL/min. After the injection was complete, the needle was retained in place for 10 min before slow withdrawal to minimize viral reflux. The skin incision was sutured with 4–0 nylon sutures, and the mice were placed on a heating pad until they fully recovered from anesthesia.

Metabolic kinetics analysis with the 1H-[13C] NMR method

Assessment of the metabolic kinetics of the hippocampal tissue was performed using [1-13C] NMR technology one day after anesthesia/surgery. As previously described, metabolic kinetics in the hippocampus were assessed using [1-13C] Glc1 and [2-13C] Ace2 (subscript: 13C position) [24]. The details are provided in the supplementary materials.

Brain tissue collection and staining

Hippocampal tissues were collected for molecular analysis. IF staining was performed for IBA1, GFAP, NeuN, c-Fos, TRPV4, ATF6, CHOP, and GluN1. Nissl staining, H&E staining, and TUNEL staining were also conducted to assess neuronal morphology and apoptosis. The details are provided in the supplementary materials.

RNA‑sequencing and machine learning analysis

We integrated two independent RNA-seq datasets (n = 3 per group for both the CON and POD model groups) from hippocampal tissues. Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 with batch effect correction. Key candidate genes identified by the random forest algorithm were subsequently validated experimentally (see the supplementary materials for details).

WB and Co-IP-MS

WB was conducted to detect the protein expression levels of IL-1β, ICAM-1, IL-6, TRPV4, ATF6, IRE1α, p-IRE1α, PERK, p-PERK, CHOP, GluN1, and p-GluN1-S896. Co-IP–MS was performed to identify TRPV4 interactors in the hippocampus. The details are provided in the supplementary materials.

Protein complex prediction

The interaction between TRPV4 (PDB ID: 8J1D) and GluN1 (PDB ID: 7TE9) was predicted using AlphaFold3 and analyzed with UCSF Chimera. The details are provided in the supplementary materials.

Golgi staining

A Golgi staining protocol was used to analyse the details of the number of hippocampal dendritic spines (see the supplementary methods for technical details).

Human transcriptomic data analysis

Analysis of publicly available brain transcriptomic datasets was performed to compare TRPV4 expression among early-onset AD (< 60 years), late-onset AD (70–80 years), and NDC groups. Differential expression was defined by a false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted p-value < 0.05 and |log2FC|> 1. The details are provided in the supplementary materials.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using t tests and one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s HSD post hoc test. Significance was set at P < 0.05. GraphPad Prism 8.0 was used for statistical data analysis and plotting.
