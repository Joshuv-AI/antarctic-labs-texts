Association between glycemic control, mental problems and nutrition in individuals with type 1 and type 2 diabetes mellitus in primary care settings
Authors: Hala Taqatqa, Muna Ahmead, Maisa Nabulsi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundComorbid mental disorders and poor dietary habits in individuals with diabetes have been suggested as possible factors associated with inadequate glycemic control. This study aimed to assess the association between glycemic control, depression, anxiety, and dietary patterns in Palestinian patients with type 1 and type 2 diabetes.MethodA cross-sectional descriptive study design was employed, involving 531 patients with diabetes mellitus from governmental primary care centers in the Bethlehem and Hebron governorates. Data were collected using self-reported questionnaires, including the 15-item Food Frequency Questionnaire (FFQ) and the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS).ResultsThe results showed that 76.3% of participants had poor glycemic control. Modifying diet or medication sometimes based on blood sugar readings was associated with poor glycemic control compared to those who always made such modifications (AOR: 2.402, p < 0.009). Also, anxiety was significantly associated with higher odds of poor glycemic control compared to those without anxiety (AOR: 4.229, p < 0.001). Finally, adhering to a moderate dietary pattern were not associated with poor glycemic control compared to adherence to a poor dietary pattern (AOR: 0.044, p < 0.001).ConclusionThe study revealed a high prevalence of poor glycemic control. Anxiety, poor dietary patterns, and modifying diet or medication sometimes based on blood sugar readings were associated with poor glycemic control. Our research may suggest that treatments for diabetes in primary care centers located in war-torn areas are inadequate unless the underlying mental health problems are addressed and nutritional guidance is provided.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27267-7.Keywords: Type 1 diabetes, Type 2 diabetes, Depression, Anxiety, Nutrition, Health and wellbeing

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a chronic metabolic disorder characterized by elevated blood glucose levels. It affects over 536.6 million individuals worldwide and contributes to approximately 6.7 million deaths each year [1]. Diabetes includes both type 1 (T1D) and type 2 (T2D) diabetes, representing a complex condition that involves more than just the regulation of blood glucose levels. Effective management of diabetes requires adherence to medication, dietary changes, physical activity, and regular blood glucose monitoring [2–4]. Since its endorsement by the American Diabetes Association (ADA) in 1988, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) has served as the standard measure for assessing blood glucose management and glycemic control in individuals with diabetes [5]. Glycemic control refers to the typical blood sugar levels of individuals with diabetes [6]. Consequently, good glycemic control according to the American Diabetes Association (ADA) is defined as an HbA1c value of 7% or less over the past three months, while poor glycemic control is indicated by an HbA1c value exceeding 7% during the same timeframe [6, 7].

Minimizing the risk of significant long-term health complications—such as microvascular diseases like nephropathy and neuropathy, as well as macrovascular conditions including peripheral artery disease and stroke—requires maintaining blood glucose levels within specific ranges [8, 9]. Approximately one-third of individuals with type 2 diabetes experience a clinically significant fear of complications [10]. This fear heightens psychological distress and hinders effective self-management, leading to a decline in psychological health [11].

Additionally, individuals with complicated diabetes mellitus are more prone to mental health issues, including anxiety, depression, and eating disorders [12–14]. A study by Albai et al. (2024) reported that 51.7% of individuals with diabetes had anxiety, while 37.5% had depression [15]. A meta-analysis examining the association between inadequate glycemic management, depression, and anxiety revealed an inverse relationship between glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) levels and depression. Specifically, heightened initial depressive symptoms correlated with subsequent increases in HbA1c levels, while elevated baseline HbA1c levels were associated with a higher probability of developing depression [16]. Conversely, other studies have failed to establish a conclusive association between depression and poor glycemic control in individuals with type 2 diabetes mellitus [17].

Additionally, individuals with diabetes who also experience anxiety and depression may have increased cravings for calorie-dense and sugary foods, which can lead to excessive caloric intake [18, 19]. This eating pattern can contribute to dyslipidemia and impaired glucose metabolism, further worsening diabetes [20]. Research indicates that nearly 80% of cases of diabetes mellitus (DM) may be preventable through the adoption of healthier dietary habits, such as regularly consuming fruits and vegetables while reducing the intake of saturated fats, sodium, and sugar-sweetened beverages [21, 22]. Therefore, effective blood glucose regulation relies on appropriate nutritional management [23].

In Palestine, 15.5% of the population was affected by diabetes, encompassing both type 1 and type 2 cases in 2021 [24]. Additionally, there were 4,439 newly diagnosed cases of diabetes mellitus reported in the West Bank. In 2024, diabetes mellitus accounted for 14.5% of deaths in the West Bank, making it the third leading cause of mortality [24]. Furthermore, there were 1,309 deaths resulting from complications associated with diabetes, yielding a mortality rate of 43.6 per 100,000 people. Ongoing conflicts, political instability, occupation, and economic constraints hinder the Palestinian healthcare system’s ability to function effectively. Research has indicated that PTSD, anxiety, and depression are prevalent within Palestinian society, especially during and after the Gaza war in 2023 [25–27].

Additionally, war has exacerbated food insecurity by limiting food availability, undermining essential food production and distribution systems, and increasing dependence on humanitarian assistance that is sometimes inadequate and often difficult to access [28]. According to a 2017 household survey, 29.2% of the population lives in poverty, with 14.0% residing in the West Bank and 53.0% in the Gaza Strip. Furthermore, 16.8% of the population is classified as living in extreme poverty [29]. In 2018, approximately 1.7 million Palestinians experienced food insecurity, which accounts for 33% of Palestinian households [30].

There is insufficient research on the association between glycemic control, nutrition, and mental disorders among individuals with type 1 and type 2 diabetes in the Arab World and Palestine. The majority of studies examine the effects of sociodemographic factors and diabetes risk factors, ignoring mental health problems and nutrition [31–34]. Also, some research examines these relationships in isolation, neglecting to include the synergistic impact of psychological and nutritional components on glycemic outcomes [35]. Thus, our study aimed to investigate the association between mental disorders, eating pattern, and glucose control. Recent study findings indicate that integrating mental health care with diabetes management can enhance both glycemic control and mental health outcomes [36]. Furthermore, this research may help health professionals decide whether to implement psychological interventions, nutritional interventions, or a combination of both in primary care settings [35]. By developing a thorough understanding of the prevalence and determinants of glycemic control, policymakers and healthcare professionals can raise awareness and adopt effective, targeted preventive strategies in primary care. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the association between glycemic control, depression, anxiety, and dietary patterns among individuals with type 1 and type 2 diabetes living in the southern governorates of Palestine. Furthermore, it aimed to assess the factors that predict poor glycemic control in these individuals who attended governmental primary care centers.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study was a descriptive cross-sectional survey that ran from October 1, 2025, to November 30, 2025. It included people with type 1 and type 2 diabetes, both men and women aged 18 and above. Participants visited eleven government primary healthcare centers located in the Bethlehem and Hebron governorates. At these centers, they received pharmacological treatment and had their blood glucose levels monitored regularly. These centers were selected for their role as key government health facilities that provide care and treatment for individuals with both types of diabetes.

The research employed convenience sampling to recruit participants, and 531 patients agreed to participate.

Tools and measures

Participants in the study filled out a self-administered questionnaire with three sections. In the first section, participants were asked about their age, gender, monthly family income, place of residence and governorates (including Hebron and Bethlehem), marital status, degree of education, current living arrangements, and employment status. They were also asked about their medical history, as shown in Appendix1 and Table 2. The HbA1c test results were retrieved from the patients’ records, and the test should have been performed within the last two months at most. A result below 7 indicates good glycemic control, while a result of 7 or higher signifies poor glycemic control.

Anxiety and depression were assessed using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) [37]. The Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) consists of two distinct instruments: HADS-A for anxiety, which contains seven items, and HADS-D for depression, also comprising seven items. A 4-point severity scale is used to rate each item. The answers range from 0 (no impairment) to 3 (severe impairment), with a score of 3 being the highest level of anxiety or depression. A cut-off score of 11 indicates potential clinical cases of depression or anxiety [37]. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.83 for the anxiety scale and 0.85 for the depression scale.

Dietary patterns were assessed using the Food Frequency Questionnaire (FFQ) [38]. This questionnaire comprises a list of 15 food items categorized into major dietary categories. In our study, we used Persson et al.‘s (2019) scoring and classification of dietary pattern categories [39]. The responses to each question were coded as a dichotomous variable, with the alternative “meets/does not meet the recommendations.” Each guideline that was met received one point, and those that did not meet received 0, yielding a healthy eating index ranging from 0 to 15 points. This index identified three dietary patterns: good (meets nine or more guidelines), average (meets six to eight recommendations), and bad (meets five or fewer recommendations) [39]. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.70.

A panel of six mental health specialists evaluated the content of both scales to ensure cultural relevance, but they did not make any changes. After the study team completed their translation, a qualified translator translated the scales from Arabic to English. In the pilot study, we assessed linguistic clarity among 25 patients diagnosed with diabetes mellitus by using the back-translated Arabic version to verify accuracy. Once the validation process was completed, we used the Arabic version of both scales to collect data.

Statistical analysis

The data The data were analyzed using SPSS version 25 (IBM Corp., Chicago, IL, USA). Descriptive analysis for all research variables is presented as frequencies and percentages, with a chi-square test conducted. Additionally, a multivariate regression analysis was performed, and the findings are reported as adjusted odds ratios (AOR) and crude odds ratios, accompanied by a 95% confidence interval. The adjusted model accounted for all relevant study confounders and variables related to glycemic control. A p-value of less than 0.05 indicates a significant relationship. Dietary pattern was scored according to Persson et al. (2019) [39]: 1 = Meets dietary recommendations and 0 = Does not meet dietary recommendations. Dietary pattern classification (categorical variable) was defined as follows: 0 = Poor dietary pattern (0–5), 1 = Moderate dietary pattern (6–8), and 2 = Good dietary pattern (9–15). The coding for the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) was as follows: 0 indicates no clinically significant anxiety or depression (score < 11), while 1 indicates clinically significant anxiety or depression (score ≥ 11). For glycemic control, an HbA1c level of less than 7% was coded as 0 (good glycemic control), while an HbA1c level of 7% or greater was coded as 1 (poor glycemic control).

Ethics approval and consent to participate

All procedures used in the study were in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The Al-Quds University Research Ethical Committee approved the study (REF: 27/25). At the start of the survey, written information on the objective and process of data collection was provided. Individuals with diabetes mellitus gave informed consent to participate in this study by filling out the questionnaire.

Multivariable binary logistic regression analysis of factors associated with poor glycemic control among individuals with diabetes mellitus

Multivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify the factors that are associated with poor glycemic control, as presented in Table 4. The results indicated that modifying diet/medication sometimes based on blood sugar readings was also significantly associated with poor glycemic control and participants who “sometimes” did such modifications were twice as likely to experience poor glycemic control compared to those who did them “always” (AOR: 2.402, 95% CI: 1.247–4.626 p = 0.009). Additionally, anxiety had the strongest and most significant association with poor glycemic control, with participants experiencing anxiety being four times more likely to have poor control than those without anxiety (AOR: 4.229, 95% CI: 1.831–9.770, p = 0.001). Finally, following a moderate dietary pattern was significantly associated with lower odds of poor glycemic control when compared to a poor dietary pattern (AOR: 0.044, 95% CI: 0.017–0.115, p < 0.001).

Discussion

Poor glycemic control in diabetics is a significant public health issue and is a primary risk factor for disease progression and its associated complications [40]. Our research revealed that 23.4% of participants experienced anxiety, 22% exhibited depression, and 76.3% had elevated HbA1c levels (≥ 7%). These findings highlight a notably high prevalence of poor glycemic control among Palestinians with diabetes mellitus, exceeding rates reported in other studies [41, 42]. For example, one Palestinian study found that 51.4% of participants had poor glycemic control [31]. Another study conducted in Gaza reported that only 19.5% of participants maintained HbA1c levels below 7% [34]. Radwan et al. (2018) suggested that the high prevalence of poor glycemic control may stem from inadequate comprehensive diabetes management, which includes continuous glucose monitoring, dietary practices, physical activity, and diabetes awareness [34]. Individuals at risk for poor glycemic control may need targeted interventions to meet optimal glycemic targets [43]. Training primary care physicians and nurses to instruct their diabetic patients on adjusting treatment plans based on blood glucose levels is one potential approach. Moreover, further research is essential to identify the factors contributing to the prevalence of poor glycemic control in primary care settings.

Our study identified several factors associated with a high prevalence of poor glycemic control in individuals with diabetes. For instance, participants who sometimes modified their diet or medication based on blood sugar readings were significantly associated with poor glycemic control compared to those who always made adjustments. This finding aligns with prior research [44]. It suggests that “always” making adjustments reflects proactive self-monitoring, which is beneficial. However, the term “sometimes” implies an inconsistent or unpredictable level of compliance, which can be detrimental. Our findings highlight the importance of maintaining adequate glycemic control through effective diabetes self-management [45].

Conversely, a study involving inpatients with type 2 diabetes and mild cognitive impairment found that larger medication adjustment ratios were associated with poor glucose control and compromised cognitive function [46]. Research indicates that frequent changes to medication regimens may destabilize diabetes management. This underscores the importance of careful medication management and personalized treatment plans that take the patient’s cognitive abilities into account [46]. Moreover, Mendes et al. (2019) found that insulin users were more likely to experience medication non-adherence when their regimens were frequently altered [47]. In Palestine, studies have shown that health professionals often fail to adhere to the diabetes management guidelines established by the Palestinian Ministry of Health in primary care settings, which may adversely affect patients’ understanding and compliance with their treatment plans. EL Sharif et al. (2015) revealed that only half of physicians and one-third of nurses were aware of local diabetes care guidelines [32]. Thus, effective self-management involves acquiring the necessary knowledge and skills to perform various health-promoting actions, such as adjusting medications and self-monitoring while juggling daily responsibilities is important [45].

Additionally, the current study found that anxiety is associated with poor glycemic control, consistent with previous research [21, 48]. Julkarnain et al. (2025) noted that high levels of anxiety correlate with poor glycemic control and an increased risk of diabetic complications [49]. However, other studies suggest that anxiety in patients with severe mental illness and type 2 diabetes may not significantly affect glycemic control [50, 51]. Individuals experiencing anxiety often demonstrate lower adherence to self-management activities, such as medication compliance, dietary restrictions, and physical activity. These disruptions can result in increased glycemic instability, creating a detrimental cycle that can worsen overall physical health [49]. Research indicates a high prevalence of anxiety disorders among Palestinians, driven by ongoing political violence, wars, poverty, starvation, damage, economic and social decline, and pervasive feelings of grief, anxiety, despair, and depression [25–27]. Unfortunately, a comprehensive approach to diabetes management that incorporates psychosocial and behavioral health aspects is frequently neglected in Palestine’s primary healthcare systems. As a result, both patients and healthcare providers within primary care services often prioritize pharmaceutical treatments over addressing mental health issues. Effectively managing diabetes in primary care settings necessitates not only medical interventions but also psychosocial therapies.

The current study’s findings indicate that adhering to a moderate dietary pattern was significantly associated with poor glycemic control compared to a poor diet. Hamzah et al. (2025) found that individuals with healthy eating habits are more likely to maintain normal blood glucose levels, unlike those with poor dietary practices [23]. Moreover, research has documented a significant increase in fast food consumption in Palestine over the past decade, which has impacted dietary patterns [52]. One study highlighted a strong correlation between prediabetes and insufficient consumption of fruits and vegetables among Palestinians with diabetes [53]. Additionally, a diet rich in fruits and vegetables, such as the Mediterranean diet, has been linked to improved glycemic control and a lower risk of developing and progressing to prediabetes and type 2 diabetes mellitus [54]. El Bilbeisi et al. (2017) conducted a study in Palestine and identified that an “Asian-like” diet—characterized by high intake of whole grains, potatoes, beans, legumes, vegetables, tomatoes, and fruits, coupled with low intake of refined grains, sugar, sweets, and desserts—was associated with a decreased prevalence of complications related to type 2 diabetes mellitus [55]. Wang et al. (2022) found that a contemporary dietary pattern with increased red meat consumption was significantly correlated with elevated blood glucose levels in adults, while the fruit-milk dietary pattern did not show a significant association with high blood glucose [56]. In contrast, other studies did not find an association between dietary patterns and poor glycemic control [48, 57]. Thus, empowering individuals with diabetes to manage their own care through education on dietary and lifestyle factors may enhance self-efficacy, reduce feelings of helplessness, alleviate anxiety and depressive symptoms, and ultimately improve glycemic control [20, 58].

Finally, the current study found no association between poor glycemic control and depression, contradicting previous research [59, 60]. Longitudinal studies have produced conflicting results regarding the relationship between insulin resistance and depression risk; some studies identified an association between insulin resistance and an increased risk of depression [61–64], while others reported no such association [65–67]. Carter and Swardfager (2016) concluded that depression may not affect glycemic control in diabetes. However, researchers have linked anhedonia—a core symptom of depression characterized by a lack of pleasure—to poor glycemic control and increased mortality [11, 20]. Nefs et al. reported a connection between inadequate glycemic management and anhedonia, but not with depression or anxiety [68]. While these findings suggest that depression has a minimal direct effect on glycemic control, it may significantly elevate the risk of serious complications in diabetes [17, 20]. This underscores the need for holistic management strategies that address both mental and physiological aspects of diabetes care and treatment in primary care.

The present study offers significant findings as the first research in Palestine to examine both types of diabetes mellitus; however, it has notable limitations. First, the use of a cross-sectional design restricts the ability to establish causal relationships among the research variables. Consequently, it is not possible to determine a causal link between diabetes, dietary patterns, and symptoms of depression and anxiety. Second, anxiety and depression levels were assessed through a self-reported questionnaire rather than a structured interview, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Third, our study does not consider all factors that could impact glycemic control, including medication no adherence, perceived stress, PTSD, specific trauma types, patient-provider interactions, limited access to care, follow-up frequency, and political stressors. Lastly, since the research was conducted in the Hebron and Bethlehem governorates, the results may not be applicable to individuals with diabetes in other areas. Future studies could address these limitations by employing a longitudinal design to more effectively evaluate causal relationships between poor glycemic control, depression, anxiety, nutrition and other variables over time. More qualitative research is necessary to gain a deeper understanding of the underlying factors associated with high prevalence of poor glycemic control among diabetes patients in primary care settings.

Conclusion

The present study identified a high prevalence of poor glycemic control in individuals with type 1 and type 2 diabetes in primary healthcare settings. Poor glycemic control was associated with modifying diet or medication sometimes based on blood sugar readings, anxiety and poor dietary pattern. Thus, providing psychological interventions and nutrition counselling in primary healthcare settings are essential to help individuals with diabetes.
