Development and validation of a prediction model for respiratory complications and in-hospital mortality in trauma patients
Authors: Do Wan Kim, Dongjin Yeo, Juyeong Kim, Yerin Hwang, Hyunjee Kim, Seung Ha Hwang, Jaeyu Park, Jinseok Lee, Jaehyeong Cho, Selin Woo, Junepill Seok, Byungchul Yu, Youngmin Kim, Sebeom Jeon, Dong Keon Yon, Wu Seong Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Respiratory complications are major contributors to morbidity and mortality in trauma patients, yet conventional predictive models remain limited in scope and performance. We developed and validated an ensemble machine learning model that integrates both prehospital and in-hospital clinical variables to predict respiratory complications in trauma patients. We developed and internally validated machine learning models using data from the Korea Trauma Data Bank, comprising records from 19 major trauma centers in Korea between 2017 and 2022 (discovery; n = 48,376). For external validation, data from four additional trauma centers added in 2023 (external validation; n = 2,010) were used. Trauma patients were identified using S or T codes in accordance with the 7th Korean Standard Classification of Diseases. Respiratory complications were defined as a composite outcome including acute respiratory distress syndrome, pneumonia, and unplanned intubation. The models were trained using 19 pre-hospital and in-hospital variables, and the final prediction model was constructed by ensembling the top-performing models. Model interpretability was achieved through Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP). Lastly, the predicted probabilities were categorized into tertiles (T1, T2, and T3), and their association with in-hospital mortality was assessed using logistic regression analysis. Among 48,376 trauma patients in the discovery cohort, the final soft-voting ensemble model combining adaptive boosting, gradient boosting machine, and logistic regression achieved an area under receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.834 in discovery cohort and 0.839 in external validation cohort. SHAP analysis identified pre-hospital pulse, Injury Severity Score, and age as the most influential predictors of respiratory complications. Higher predicted risk of respiratory complications was significantly associated with in-hospital mortality, with adjusted odds ratios rising across predicted risk tertiles (T1: 2.59 [95% CI, 2.09–3.22]; T2: 2.77 [2.21–3.47]; and T3: 3.91 [2.30–5.11]). The proposed ensemble model exhibited high accuracy and generalizability in predicting respiratory complications and mortality risk among trauma patients. By using both pre-hospital and in-hospital clinical data, the model offers a potentially valuable tool for early triage and intervention in trauma care. Prospective validation is needed to evaluate its clinical utility in real-world trauma settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-48072-7.

Introduction

Respiratory complications, such as pneumonia and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), represent a critical transition in the clinical course of trauma patients and are strongly associated with poor outcomes, including prolonged hospitalization, mechanical ventilation, and death1–3. Early identification of patients at high risk for respiratory deterioration is essential for timely intervention and optimal resource allocation in acute trauma care. To support this need, existing risk prediction models have incorporated variables such as age, Injury Severity Score (ISS), injury mechanism, and transfusion volume to estimate the risk of respiratory compromise2. Additionally, fracture location, injury type, and inflammatory markers, including interleukin-2 receptor and interleukin-6, have been linked to post-traumatic pneumonia4.

Conventional models, however, rely on linear regression frameworks or manually weighted scoring systems, which may overlook complex, non-linear associations between clinical variables2,4–7. Theses methods often fail to capture complex, nonlinear relationships between variables and typically target isolated outcomes, limiting their generalizability and utility in dynamic clinical settings2,4 In recent years, machine learning has emerged as a powerful tool for risk prediction in trauma care, enabling the integration of multidimensional clinical data to improve predictive accuracy8–11. Despite this progress, most machine learning models have focused on mortality prediction, with relatively few addressing post-traumatic complications8–11. Furthermore, many have relied on neural networks or optimal classification trees, which may be less suitable than ensemble-based approaches for modelling structured clinical data10,11.

Given the frequency and severity of respiratory deterioration in trauma settings, there is a clear need for accurate, clinically interpretable models that can guide timely intervention. To address this gap, we developed and validated an explainable machine learning model to predict the likelihood of respiratory deterioration in trauma patients, including pneumonia, ARDS, and unexpected intubation. Using prehospital clinical metadata derived from a large-scale, nationwide trauma registry, Korean Trauma Data Bank, in South Korea, we compared models trained on data available before hospital arrival with those using information collected at emergency department presentation. We also examined the association between model-predicted risk and subsequent in-hospital mortality and employed Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) to identify the most influential variables contributing to model predictions. These findings may support the development of AI-assisted decision tools to enhance early identification and management of respiratory complications in trauma care settings.

Methods

Data source and study population

This study was conducted in accordance with the Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis and Artificial Intelligence guidelines12. We used data from the Korean Trauma Data Bank, a nationwide registry established to improve trauma care by integrating records from 19 regional trauma centers across South Korea13. The initial study sample comprised 227,567 individuals who sustained traumatic injuries between January 1, 2017, and December 31, 2022 (dataset ID: K2024-03-2-02-29). Eligible trauma cases were identified using S and T codes defined in the Korean Standard Classification of Diseases, 7th revision, which is based on the 10th revision of the International Classification of Diseases8. Respiratory complications were defined as a composite outcome that included ARDS, pneumonia, and unplanned intubation14. In the Korean Trauma Data Bank, complications are recorded by trained trauma coordinators based on comprehensive review of electronic medical records, including diagnoses, inpatient records, and consultation notes. ARDS and pneumonia are recorded when documented as inpatient diagnoses during hospitalization, and unplanned intubation is recorded when endotracheal intubation occurs unexpectedly after hospital admission. These events were considered only when they occurred during hospitalization after arrival at the emergency department. Complications documented in the Korean Trauma Data Bank, including unplanned intubation, reflect events that developed after admission and do not include airway interventions performed in the pre-hospital phase. The remaining cases after applying the exclusion criteria were used for model development and internal validation15.

For external validation, we used data from four major trauma centers, including Cheju Halla General Hospital, Gachon University Gil Medical Center, Chonnam National University Hospital, and Chungbuk National University Hospital, covering trauma cases registered from January 1 to December 31, 2023. The external validation dataset initially comprised 8,867 trauma patients identified using the same diagnostic codes and evaluated with consistent criteria as those used in the discovery cohort. Institutional Review Board approvals were obtained from all participating institutions, including Gachon University Gil Medical Center (GFIRB 2024 − 357), Chonnam National University Hospital (CNUH-2024-016), Chungbuk National University Hospital (CBNUH 2024-10-017), and Cheju Halla General Hospital (CHH-2024-L03). Informed consent was not required, as the study used administrative data for secondary research purposes in accordance with institutional and national ethics regulations. All procedures followed the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data preprocessing

Candidate variables were selected based on clinical relevance, previous literature, and availability within the trauma registry. During the variable selection process, we also evaluated the extent of missing data for each candidate variable and considered the feasibility of obtaining these variables during routine trauma assessment. Variables with substantial missingness were carefully reviewed, and clinically essential variables (e.g., vital signs measured at presentation) were retained despite missing values due to their established relevance in trauma risk assessment. Participants were excluded if they were younger than 15 years or if key variables essential for model development, such as vital signs, were missing. Given the substantial proportion of missing data, imputation was not performed. After applying these criteria, the dataset was randomly split into training and testing sets in an 80:20 ratio. The testing set was not used for model training or internal validation and used solely to evaluate the model’s performance. To address the impact of class imbalance between cases with (n = 1,744; 3.61%) and without (n = 46,632; 96.39%) respiratory complications, we applied the synthetic minority over-sampling technique during model training. This method generates additional cases with respiratory complications by interpolating existing examples, improving outcome balance and reducing bias. Prior to model development, all feature values were standardized to have a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1 using standard normalization. Normalization parameters were computed from the training dataset and consistently applied to both internal and external validation sets to preserve consistency and comparability across cohorts.

Prehospital variables included demographic and injury-related characteristics (sex and age group), pre-hospital clinical status (consciousness level, shock index, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, pulse rate, body temperature, and oxygen saturation), as well as suspected alcohol use, and presence of comorbidities (Table S1)4,16. Prehospital vital signs were recorded by emergency medical technicians at the scene and represent the first measurements obtained during the initial field assessment. For in-hospital data, we included clinical status at arrival including consciousness level, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, pulse rate, body temperature, and oxygen saturation. Additional hospital-based variables included in-hospital oxygen supplementation, Glasgow Coma Scale score (GCS), suspected alcohol use, and findings from focused assessment with sonography for trauma related to pleural effusion or pneumothorax. Variables related to clinical course and injury severity included massive transfusion and the final ISS (Table S2)16–18. Massive transfusion was defined using electronic medical records from each participating center based on either of the following criteria: (1) transfusion of ≥ 5 units of packed red blood cells within 4 h, or (2) transfusion of ≥ 10 units within 24 h19. Massive transfusion was treated as an early hospital clinical variable reflecting injury severity during initial resuscitation. The ISS was categorized into four groups: mild (< 9), moderate (9–15), severe (16–24), and very severe (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\ge\:$$\end{document}25)20. It was used as a measure of baseline injury severity, based on the initial ISS calculated by trauma coordinators from documented injuries in the trauma registry. In our database, no significant differences were observed between provisional and finalized ISS values.

Model development and training

To predict respiratory complications in trauma patients, we developed six machine learning models including adaptive boosting (AdaBoost), random forest (RF), gradient boosting machine (GBM), light GBM (LGBM), extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), and logistic regression (LR)21. Hyperparameters for each model were optimized using grid search to enhance both predictive accuracy and model stability22. The final model configurations are detailed in Table S3. To improve overall performance, we implemented ensemble modelling by evaluating all possible combinations of the three best-performing models. In addition, a voting-based ensemble using Adaboost, XGBoost, and LR was developed to improve overall prediction by combining the complementary characteristics of each model23. Internal validation was performed using five-fold stratified cross-validation24. The training data were divided into five equally sized subsets, with the original ratio of respiratory and non-respiratory complication cases preserved in each fold. For each fold, the model was trained on four subsets and evaluated on the fifth, with this process repeated five times21. This validation strategy provided stable performance estimates while addressing class imbalance in the data.

Model evaluation and external validation

To objectively assess the generalization performance of each machine learning model for predicting respiratory complications, we used an independent testing dataset that had been fully excluded from model training and internal validation phases. Model performance was evaluated using sensitivity, specificity, overall accuracy, balanced accuracy, and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), providing a comprehensive view of classification accuracy from multiple perspectives25.

Due to the highly imbalanced class distribution in the internal dataset (respiratory complication: non-complication = 1:26.74), balanced accuracy and AUROC were selected as the primary performance metrics. To further enhance classification performance, probability thresholds were optimized based on AUROC analysis26. These thresholds were applied consistently throughout all performance evaluations27,28. To interpret the contribution of individual features, we applied SHAP, which quantified the relative importance of each predictor in estimating complication risk21. This analysis identified the most influential clinical features in forecasting respiratory complications based on clinical data.

To improve the clinical applicability of predicted probabilities, the model outputs were calibrated using Platt scaling, which was trained using cross-validation on the training dataset. Model calibration was evaluated in the test set of the development cohort using both parametric and non-parametric approaches. Specifically, Cox calibration was performed by fitting a logistic regression model of the observed outcomes on the logit-transformed predicted probabilities, from which the calibration intercept (calibration-in-the-large) and calibration slope were estimated. In addition, non-parametric calibration curves were generated using quantile-based binning with locally weighted scatterplot smoothing to visualize the relationship between predicted probabilities and observed event frequencies across the risk spectrum. To further assess model behavior across the full predicted risk range, the distribution of predicted probabilities was visualized separately for patients with and without respiratory complications. Calibration performance was also evaluated across clinically relevant subgroups, including sex, age groups, and Injury Severity Score categories.

External validity was tested using data from four independent trauma centers located in different regions. Due to the continued presence of class imbalance in these datasets, balanced accuracy and AUROC were again prioritized as key evaluation metrics. Among all models tested, the one that consistently achieved the highest AUROC and balanced accuracy across both internal and external validations was designated the final proposed model29. All model development and evaluation were conducted using Python (version 3.11.11; Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE, USA; https://www.python.org) with packages including TensorFlow (2.19.0), Keras (3.10.0), NumPy (2.1.0), Pandas (2.2.3), Matplotlib (3.10.1), and Scikit learn (1.6.1).

To assess the potential impact of missing data and the possibility of non-random missingness, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using an alternative data preprocessing strategy. Specifically, missing values in vital signs were imputed using median values derived from the training dataset, and corresponding missingness indicators were included as additional covariates30. This approach allowed the model to explicitly account for potential prognostic information embedded in missing data. Model development and evaluation were then repeated using this augmented dataset, and performance metrics were compared with those from the primary complete-case analysis.

Risk stratification and in-hospital mortality analysis

Patients in the discovery cohort were stratified based on predicted probabilities generated by the final ensemble model. Because early mortality may occur before respiratory complications develop, in-hospital mortality was not included in the composite endpoint and was analyzed separately in relation to model-predicted complication risk. Those with probabilities exceeding the optimal threshold of 0.50 were classified as high-risk, while those below this threshold served as the reference group. To allow for gradient-based comparison, the high-risk group was further divided into tertiles (T1, T2, and T3) according to predicted probability ranges21. The primary outcome was in-hospital mortality following admission to a trauma center, identified from clinical records within the discovery cohort. Due to the insufficient number of deaths observed in the external validation cohort, mortality analyses were restricted to the discovery dataset. We performed multivariable logistic regression to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between predicted risk and in-hospital mortality. Two adjustment models were applied: Model 1 adjusted for age and sex, and Model 2 adjusted for all covariates listed in Table S4. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted using SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA; https://www.sas.com).

Results

Study population

The initial discovery cohort consisted of 227,567 individuals admitted to major trauma centers across South Korea. After excluding 12,793 patients younger than 15 years and 166,398 with incomplete data, 48,376 cases were included in the final analysis. Among these patients, respiratory complications occurred in 3.62% of cases, including ARDS (0.28%), pneumonia (2.89%), and unplanned intubation (0.94%). In the external validation cohort of 8,867 trauma patients, 2,010 met the criteria after excluding 394 patients under 15 and 6,463 with incomplete information (Fig. 1).

A comparison of baseline characteristics and outcomes between included and excluded patients is presented in Table S5. Overall, excluded patients tend to have slightly less severe clinical profiles and a lower incidence of respiratory complications compared with those included in the analysis.

Five-fold cross-validation and internal validation results

We assessed model performance using five-fold cross-validation, evaluating sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, balanced accuracy, and AUROC as key metrics (Table 1). Among the individual models, AdaBoost, XGBoost, and LR exhibited strong performance, with relatively high balanced accuracy and AUROC values, making them suitable candidates for ensemble modeling. To enhance overall predictive performance, we constructed multiple ensemble models using both voting and stacking strategies across all possible combinations of these top-performing algorithms. Among the evaluated ensemble models, the combination of Adaboost and XGBoost showed competitive performance during cross-validation, with a sensitivity of 0.765 (95% CI, 0.730–0.800), specificity of 0.790 (0.783–0.796), accuracy of 0.789 (0.782–0.795), balanced accuracy of 0.777 (0.760–0.794), and AUROC of 0.849 (0.839–0.860).

Notably, this model also performed well in internal validation using the test set, with a sensitivity of 0.788, specificity of 0.762, accuracy of 0.763, balanced accuracy of 0.775, and AUROC of 0.834. Performance remained consistent across validation steps, indicating no evidence of overfitting or underfitting. Given the class imbalance in our dataset, balanced accuracy and AUROC were used as the primary evaluation metrics, and the AdaBoost and XGBoost ensemble model exhibited the highest performance across these metrics. This model was therefore selected as the optimal approach for predicting the target clinical outcome.

External validation results

To evaluate the generalizability of the model, we conducted external validation using data from four major trauma centers. The proposed ensemble model combining AdaBoost and XGBoost exhibited consistent performance, with a sensitivity of 0.726, specificity of 0.789, accuracy of 0.786, balanced accuracy of 0.757, and AUROC of 0.839 (Table 1). Although the overall performance was slightly attenuated compared to internal validation, the ensemble model maintained a balanced trade-off between sensitivity and specificity. These findings support the external validity of the ensemble model and its potential applicability in trauma care settings.

Sensitivity analysis for missing data

To address potential bias due to missing data, we performed a sensitivity analysis using median imputation combined with missingness indicators for vital signs (Table S6). Model performance remained consistent across all datasets. In the external validation set, the ensemble model achieved an AUROC of 0.823 in the sensitivity analysis compared with 0.839 in the primary analysis, indicating only a modest decrease in performance and supporting the robustness of the model to different missing data handling strategies.

Model interpretation

We applied SHAP analysis to interpret the ensemble model and quantify the relative importance of each predictor in estimating the risk of respiratory complications among trauma patients (Fig. 2). The pre-hospital pulse contributed most substantially to risk prediction, followed by ISS, age, GCS, and in-hospital oxygen saturation.

In addition to discrimination metrics, model calibration was assessed using calibration-in-the-large, calibration slope, and calibration plots, while clinical utility was evaluated using decision curve analysis. As shown in Figure S1A, the model demonstrated reasonable agreement between predicted and observed risks, with some overestimation and mild overfitting (calibration intercept, -0.54; calibration slope, 0.79). Predicted probabilities were well separated between patients with and without respiratory complications (Figure S1B), and subgroup analyses showed generally consistent calibration across sex, age groups, and Injury Severity Score categories (Figure S2).

The risk of in-hospital mortality among patients after respiratory complications

Figure 3 presents the model structure of the ensemble model developed to estimate the likelihood of respiratory complications and associated mortality in trauma patients. Table 2 summarizes in-hospital mortality stratified by tertiles (T1-T3) of predicted complication probability within the discovery cohort. As predicted risk increased from T1 to T3, adjusted odds of death also increased progressively, with adjusted odds ratios of 2.59 (95% CI, 2.09–3.22) for T1, 2.77 (2.21–3.47) for T2, and 3.91 (2.30–5.11) for T3. Patients identified as high-risk for respiratory complications had significantly greater odds of in-hospital death than those not predicted to experience such complications. This pattern suggests a potential dose-response relationship between the model-predicted risk of respiratory complications and subsequent mortality among trauma patients.

Discussion

Key findings

This study utilized the Korean Trauma Data Bank, a nationwide trauma registry comprising patients treated at 19 major trauma centers in South Korea, to develop a machine learning model for predicting the likelihood of respiratory complications based on prehospital and post-hospital arrival clinical information. A soft-voting ensemble model combining Adaboost, GBM and LR, exhibited superior predictive performance compared to individual models and other ensemble combinations, achieving an AUROC of 0.831 (discovery) and 0.793 (external validation). SHAP value-based model interpretation identified pre-hospital pulse as the most influential predictor, followed by ISS and age. Notably, increasing predicted risk of respiratory complications was associated with higher odds of in-hospital mortality. These results indicate that the proposed ensemble model may offer a valuable tool for early risk stratification of respiratory complications and mortality in trauma care.

Comparison with previous studies

AI has been increasingly applied to trauma care, with most existing models primarily designed to predict mortality8–11. While some have addressed post-traumatic complications, respiratory outcomes, such as ARDS and pneumonia, have been examined individually or combined with unrelated complications such as renal failure or surgical site infection8–11. To address this limitation, our study developed a composite respiratory outcome that more directly reflects clinically significant deterioration in respiratory function following trauma. Some previous models have reported high AUROC values for specific respiratory complications, for example, an XGBoost model predicting ARDS (AUROC, 0.916), and a RF model predicting pneumonia in trauma patients (AUROC, 0.950)4,31 However, these models targeted narrower endpoints and are therefore not directly comparable to our composite outcome. Moreover, many focused on specific injury types, such as traumatic brain injury or orthopedic trauma, or were restricted to narrow subgroups, including pediatric patients9,32,33. Notably, while most prior models did not undergo external validation, our model was externally validated using data from multiple trauma centers, supporting its potential generalisability9–11. Furthermore, while most prior models relied exclusively on in-hospital data, our model was trained on a large, nationwide trauma registry incorporating both prehospital and in-hospital variables8–11. This dual-phase approach enabled the inclusion of early field indicators such as airway management, oxygen administration, and circulatory support, providing a wider spectrum of clinical decision points relevant to respiratory deterioration34.

To enhance interpretability, we applied SHAP analysis to identify features most strongly associated with respiratory complications. Pre-hospital pulse and ISS were identified as the top contributors. Although no previous study has performed SHAP analysis specifically for respiratory outcomes in trauma populations, our findings are consistent with prior work35,36. One study reported that the ISS had superior or comparable discriminatory performance for predicting ARDS relative to other trauma indices35. Another single-center retrospective study found that ISS was independently associated with ventilator-associated pneumonia in trauma patients36. This consistency with prior research suggests that our model captures clinically meaningful predictors of post-traumatic respiratory deterioration.

Clinical implications

In trauma care, respiratory deterioration represents a critical complication that can lead to prolonged mechanical ventilation, intensive care unit admission, and death, making early identification and intervention essential for improving outcomes1,37,38. Notably, delays in respiratory support, frequently due to under recognition of respiratory risk, remain a common contributor to preventable morbidity and mortality39. While existing scores such as the Trauma Induced Pulmonary Event score, Abbreviated Injury Scale, and Thoracic Trauma Severity Score, and conventional models are limited in scope or based on linear assumptions, our ensemble learning model exhibited superior predictive performance and incorporated explainable artificial intelligence to enhance interpretability40,41. From this perspective, we developed a machine learning model that predicts respiratory complications, including ARDS, pneumonia, and unplanned intubation, using both prehospital and in-hospital clinical data. The model uses structured variables routinely collected during prehospital transport and at emergency department arrival, including vital signs, oxygen saturation, injury severity measures, and transfusion status, and therefore could be implemented without additional data collection. These variables could be automatically extracted from electronic medical records to generate real-time estimates of respiratory complication risk. Predicted risk could be displayed as a probability or risk category with a brief SHAP-based summary of the main contributing variables to facilitate clinical interpretation.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, the dataset was limited to a single ethnic population which may limit the generalizability of findings to other settings. However, data were employed from the Korean Trauma Data Bank, a large, multicenter registry that includes trauma cases from various regional trauma centers, thereby enhancing internal validity. Second, a substantial number of patients were excluded due to missing data. Because these missing values were related to clinical practice rather than technical recording errors, imputation was not performed. The exclusion of patients with incomplete data may have introduced selection bias and affected the representativeness of the analyzed cohort, potentially limiting the generalizability and practical applicability of the model in real-world clinical setting where complete data may not always be available. However, sensitivity analyses incorporating missingness indicators for vital signs showed similar results, supporting the robustness of the findings. To mitigate the risk of model overfitting within the remaining dataset, we applied cross-validation and conducted external validation across multiple trauma centers. Third, as a retrospective analysis, this study is inherently susceptible to selection and analytical bias. Although we used a large, prospectively collected registry and pre-specified model development procedures, prospective validation is needed to assess the model’s clinical utility in practice. Fourth, the model was developed using structured clinical variables and did not incorporate unstructured data sources such as imaging, laboratory trajectories, or clinical notes, which may contain additional predictive signals. Future work integrating these data types may improve model performance and clinical relevance. Fifth, the Korean Trauma Data Bank does not provide precise timestamps for the occurrence of respiratory complications. Instead, the registry records only the type of medical complications and records only the type of complications during hospitalization. Therefore, the exact time interval from admission to complication development could not be determined. Nevertheless, the registry includes a large and representative trauma population from regional trauma centers nationwide, which supports the generalizability of our findings despite this limitation.

Despite these limitations, this study presents several novel strengths. This study is the first model developed and validated specifically to predict composite respiratory complications in trauma patients. By incorporating both prehospital and in-hospital clinical variables, the model captures the full scope of early trauma care and improves its relevance to real-world clinical settings. In addition to evaluating predictive performance, we examined the association between model-derived risk and in-hospital mortality, offering insight into its broader prognostic implications. The use of SHAP further supports clinical interpretability by identifying key variables contributing to individual predictions.

Conclusion

In this nationwide study, we developed and externally validated an ensemble machine learning model that predicts respiratory complications and associated in-hospital mortality in trauma patients using both prehospital and in-hospital clinical data. The model exhibited strong performance and clinical interpretability, with ISS, sex, and pre-hospital oxygen saturation identified as key contributors. By enabling early risk stratification, the model provides a clinically meaningful framework for identifying high-risk patients and informing timely interventions. Prospective studies are needed to assess its utility in real-time trauma care settings.
