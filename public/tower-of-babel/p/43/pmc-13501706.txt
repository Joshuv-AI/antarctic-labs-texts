Single-cell and spatial transcriptomics identify SPP1 + trophoblast necroptosis as a potential contributor to late-onset preeclampsia via SPP1–CD44–mediated interaction with macrophages
Authors: Qian Li, Junpeng Dong, Xiaowei Wei, Chuanmei Qin, Yunqing Zhang, Weihong Zeng, Yi Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLate-onset preeclampsia (LOPE) is a complex placental disorder characterized by inflammation and placental dysfunction, yet the underlying molecular mechanisms remain incompletely understood. Necroptosis, a regulated inflammatory form of cell death, has been implicated in various inflammation-associated diseases, but its role in LOPE has not been fully elucidated.MethodsWe performed an integrated analysis of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and spatial transcriptomics (ST) in placental tissues from LOPE and healthy pregnancies. Necroptosis activity was evaluated using gene-set scoring and validated by Western blotting and immunohistochemistry in human placentas. Pseudotime trajectory analysis, CellChat, and enrichment analysis were applied to characterize pathological cellular states and intercellular communication. Spatial deconvolution using RCTD was employed to map the localization and colocalization of specific cellular subtypes within placental niches. Functional validation was conducted in vivo using an L-NAME–induced preeclampsia-like mouse model in Mlkl knockout mice, together with multicolor immunofluorescence staining of human placental samples.ResultsSingle-cell transcriptomic profiling revealed marked cellular heterogeneity between LOPE and healthy placentas and identified activation of necroptosis-related pathways in trophoblast populations in LOPE. A distinct SPP1 + extravillous trophoblast (EVT) subtype associated with necroptosis signatures was identified. Trajectory analysis indicated that this SPP1 + EVT subset represented a pathological state enriched for cell-death and inflammatory signatures. Spatial transcriptomics demonstrated specific localization of SPP1 + EVTs within the placental microenvironment and their spatial proximity to macrophages. Cell–cell communication analysis further highlighted a prominent SPP1–CD44 signaling axis mediating interactions between SPP1 + EVTs and macrophages. In vivo, Mlkl knockout attenuated L-NAME–induced preeclampsia-like phenotypes and reduced placental SPP1 expression. Consistently, multicolor immunofluorescence confirmed increased abundance of SPP1 + EVTs and spatially adjacent macrophages in LOPE placentas.ConclusionsNecroptosis is a predominant trophoblast cell death pathway in LOPE. The SPP1–CD44 axis may mediate EVT–macrophage crosstalk, contributing to placental inflammation and dysfunction. These findings provide mechanistic insight into LOPE pathogenesis, nominate necroptosis as a potential therapeutic target, and highlight SPP1 as a promising biomarker candidate.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08402-9.

Background

Preeclampsia (PE) is a complex pregnancy disorder, clinically defined by the new-onset of hypertension after 20 weeks of gestation accompanied by at least one additional complication, such as proteinuria or maternal organ dysfunction [1]. PE affects approximately 2–8% of pregnancies worldwide and remains a leading cause of maternal and perinatal morbidity and mortality [2]. Based on gestational age at onset, PE is classified into early-onset PE (EOPE, < 34 weeks) and late-onset PE (LOPE, ≥ 34 weeks) [3]. Current management strategies mainly focus on symptomatic control of hypertension and prevention of seizures, with delivery as the only definitive treatment of the dysfunctional placenta and fetus. While significant progress has been made in the prediction and prevention of EOPE—for example, through combined early-pregnancy screening and prophylactic low-dose aspirin before 16 weeks—such advances have been far more limited for LOPE, for which mechanistic understanding and predictive approaches remain inadequate [4, 5].

The prevailing model posits that inadequate trophoblast invasion and failed spiral artery remodeling initiate placental ischemia, hypoxia, and oxidative stress, leading to the release of anti-angiogenic factors (e.g., sFlt-1), inflammatory mediators, and products of apoptotic or necrotic cell death, thereby triggering systemic maternal inflammation and widespread endothelial dysfunction [6, 7]. However, accumulating evidence suggests that PE is not driven by a single mechanism, and different subtypes may have distinct pathological bases. LOPE has been increasingly associated with multifactorial processes, including placental senescence, dysregulated inflammation, metabolic imbalance, and impaired immune adaptation [8].

Programmed cell death (PCD) and its inflammatory subtypes, such as necroptosis, pyroptosis, and ferroptosis, have recently been implicated in placental pathology [9, 10]. Necroptosis, mediated by pRIPK1, pRIPK3, and pMLKL, is characterized by plasma membrane rupture and the release of pro-inflammatory mediators, thereby exhibiting both inflammatory and tissue-damaging features [11, 12]. It is commonly observed in ischemia-reperfusion injury, viral infection, and immune-related disorders. Emerging studies suggest that necroptosis may play a crucial role in placental dysfunction. SIRT3-mediated necroptosis in EVTs, impairing their migratory and vascular remodeling capacities and leading to PE-like phenotypes [13]. In contrast, GRK2-regulated necroptosis has been shown to reduce trophoblast giant cells and disrupt maternal–fetal interface homeostasis [14].

The integration of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and spatial transcriptomics (ST) has provided powerful tools for dissecting complex diseases at cellular and spatial resolution [15]. While scRNA-seq enables the precise identification of cell types and transcriptional heterogeneity, ST preserves spatial context and microenvironmental architecture. These complementary technologies have been successfully applied to investigate invasive mechanisms and microenvironmental alterations in placenta accreta spectrum (PAS) and to characterize trophoblast and immune cell interactions in gestational diabetes [16, 17]. However, combined scRNA-seq and ST data on placentas from PE, particularly LOPE, remain scarce.

In this study, we integrated scRNA-seq and spatial transcriptomics of human LOPE placentas to define the cellular and spatial architecture of disease. We aimed to test, for the first time in vivo, whether trophoblast necroptosis plays a protective role and whether necroptosis is causally involved in LOPE pathogenesis. We further sought to identify necroptosis-associated trophoblast subsets and to characterize crosstalk between EVTs and immune cells, while nominating subtype-specific markers with potential diagnostic and therapeutic relevance for LOPE.

Methods

Data source

Single-cell data were retrieved from a public LOPE placental scRNA-seq dataset (3 LOPE, 3 NC; https://github.com/JustMoveOnnn/preeclampsia). The ST data used in this study were derived from our previously generated dataset, including one LOPE placenta and one NC placenta [18]. For ST sample collection, pregnancies with pre-existing maternal comorbidities, including hypertension, renal disease, cardiovascular disease, thyroid dysfunction, diabetes, and intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy, were excluded. Cases with chorioamnionitis or fetal chromosomal abnormalities were also excluded. Placental biopsies were obtained within 15 min after cesarean delivery, snap-frozen, sectioned at 10 μm, and processed using the 10x Genomics Visium CytAssist fresh-frozen workflow. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the International Peace Maternity & Child Health Hospital, Shanghai (Approval No. (GKLW) 2021-17), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Detailed clinical information for the scRNA-seq and ST samples is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

scRNA data preprocessing

Raw FASTQ files were processed with Cell Ranger (v7.0.1, 10x Genomics) against GRCh38 to generate UMI count matrices, which were analyzed in Seurat (v5.3.0). Cells were filtered using standard QC criteria (200–8000 detected genes, < 40,000 UMIs, < 25% mitochondrial reads, < 5% hemoglobin reads), and doublets were removed using DoubletFinder (v2.0.3). Data were log-normalized (LogNormalize; scale factor 10,000), and batch effects were corrected using mutual nearest neighbors (MNN) (batchelor v1.6.3). Highly variable genes were selected, followed by scaling, PCA, graph-based clustering, and UMAP visualization. Cluster markers were identified with FindAllMarkers (logFC > 0.25; adjusted P < 0.05), with down-sampling to 500 cells per cluster to mitigate sampling bias.

ST data preprocessing

Spatial transcriptomics (Visium CytAssist) data were processed with Space Ranger (v2.0.1, 10x Genomics) using GRCh38 to generate UMI-based spot-level expression matrices. Tissue spots were identified by aligning histology images to the capture area, and only spots within tissue boundaries were retained. Downstream preprocessing was performed in Seurat using SCTransform normalization, followed by PCA to identify major sources of variation. Samples were integrated and batch-corrected with Harmony (v1.2.3), and embeddings were visualized with UMAP. Clustering was performed on the Harmony embeddings using an SNN graph across resolutions 0.3–0.8; based on clustree (v0.5.1) and biological interpretability, a resolution of 0.5 was selected to define spatial niches.

Single-cell functional enrichment analysis and gene-set scoring

Functional pathway activity at single-cell resolution was quantified using irGSEA [19]. MSigDB Hallmark gene sets (H) were scored with six methods (AUCell, UCell, singscore, ssGSEA, JASMINE, and VIPER) and integrated by Robust Rank Aggregation (RRA); pathways with adjusted P ≤ 0.05 were visualized in clustered heatmaps. In addition, gene-set scoring was performed for all cells with the AddModuleScore function. Seven PCD gene sets—including autophagy, cuproptosis, extrinsic apoptosis, ferroptosis, intrinsic apoptosis, necroptosis, and pyroptosis—were obtained from a previously published study [20], and M1/M2 polarization signatures were similarly derived from a published reference [21]. Gene-set scores were projected onto UMAP embeddings for visualization using the FeaturePlot function.

Ro/e analysis

The ratio of observed to expected (Ro/e) was calculated to assess cell-type distribution preferences across tissues or conditions at single-cell resolution [22]. For each cluster, observed cell counts in each group were compared with expected counts calculated from the cluster’s overall proportion and the total cells in that group. Ro/e > 1 indicated enrichment and Ro/e < 1 indicated depletion. Results were discretized as: +++ (Ro/e > 1), ++ (0.8 < Ro/e ≤ 1), + (0.2 ≤ Ro/e ≤ 0.8), +/− (0 < Ro/e < 0.2), and − (Ro/e = 0).

Pseudotime trajectory analysis and functional enrichment

Pseudotime trajectories were inferred using Monocle3 [23, 24]. Raw counts and metadata were converted to a CellDataSet, preprocessed with PCA, and UMAP coordinates from Seurat were imported to ensure consistent visualization. Cells were clustered, trajectories were learned, and pseudotime was assigned. Gene expression dynamics were examined along pseudotime, and genes with significant spatial autocorrelation (Moran’s I) were grouped into co-expression modules. Module genes were annotated by GO (BP/CC/MF) and KEGG enrichment with Benjamini–Hochberg correction, and results were visualized using dot plots.

RCTD analysis

Spatial transcriptomic spots were deconvoluted using Robust cell type decomposition (RCTD) with scRNA-seq data as the reference [25]. For each sample, spot coordinates, UMI counts, and total UMIs were extracted to build a SpatialRNA object. RCTD was run in doublet mode to account for mixed-cell spots, and outputs included predicted primary cell types and estimated cell-type proportions per spot. The results were added back to the ST object as metadata for visualization and downstream analysis of spatial cell-type distributions.

PROGENy pathway activity analysis

Pathway activities were inferred with PROGENy using decoupleR [26]. SCT assays were analyzed with the top 500 human footprint genes to compute spot-level pathway activity scores. Activity scores were then integrated with spatial niche annotations, averaged per niche, and visualized as a heatmap to compare signaling programs across tissue microenvironments.

Cell–cell communication analysis

Cell–cell communication was inferred with CellChat using normalized scRNA-seq data and cell-type annotations [27]. Ligand–receptor interactions were queried against CellChatDB.human; overexpressed genes/interactions were identified and projected onto a human PPI network for smoothing. Communication probabilities were estimated using a truncated mean method with distance weighting, and interactions with insufficient cell numbers were filtered. Signaling networks were aggregated to quantify interaction number and strength between cell groups and visualized with circle and heatmap plots. Pathway-level signaling was summarized using hierarchical and bubble plots.

Animal experiments

All animal procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of the International Peace Maternity & Child Health Hospital, Shanghai (Approval No. (GKLW) 2021-17) and performed in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines. Wild-type (WT) C57BL/6 and Mlkl knockout (Mlkl-/-) mice were purchased from Wuhan Youdu Biotechnology Co., Ltd. and maintained under specific pathogen–free (SPF) conditions. Healthy 8–10-week-old females were mated with males at a 2:1 ratio; the presence of a vaginal plug the next morning was designated as embryonic day (E) 0.5. Pregnant WT dams were randomly assigned to control or PE groups. PE induction was performed using a modified L-NAME-induced preeclampsia-like mouse model, with daily subcutaneous injection of L-NAME HCl (S2877, selleck; dissolved in PBS) at 120 mg/kg/day from E9.5 to E17.5 [28, 29]. Mlkl-/- pregnant dams received the same L-NAME regimen. Maternal blood pressure was measured noninvasively at E7.5, E10.5, E14.5, and E17.5 using a CODA tail-cuff system (Kent Scientific, CODA-M1) [30]. On E18.5, dams were anesthetized and euthanized by cervical dislocation. Uteri and bilateral kidneys were collected, rinsed in PBS, and fetal number, fetal weight, and placental weight were recorded. Placentas were processed for H&E staining and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for western blotting, while kidneys were processed for H&E and Periodic Acid-Schiff staining. H&E and Periodic Acid-Schiff staining were performed on placental or kidney sections from n = 5 animals per group. For H&E staining, paraffin sections were baked at 60 °C, deparaffinized, rehydrated, stained with hematoxylin, differentiated in 0.7% hydrochloric acid ethanol, blued under running water, counterstained with eosin, dehydrated, cleared, and mounted. For Periodic Acid-Schiff staining, paraffin sections were baked at 60 °C, deparaffinized, rehydrated, oxidized in 0.5% periodic acid for 5 min, rinsed, incubated with Schiff reagent, washed with sulfite solution and running water, counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, cleared, and mounted. Blood was collected from the inner canthus, centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min at 4℃, and the supernatant plasma was collected and stored at − 80 °C until ELISA assays. Plasma ELISA assays were performed using samples from n = 6 per group.

Western blotting

Placental villous tissues were lysed in RIPA buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentration was measured by BCA, and samples were denatured (100 °C, 15 min). A total of 50–60 µg of protein was loaded for phosphorylated proteins, whereas 20–30 µg was loaded for other proteins. Proteins were separated by SDS–PAGE using 12.5% gels for low-molecular-weight proteins, including cleaved caspase-3 and BAX, and 10% gels for the remaining proteins, and then transferred to PVDF membranes (0.22–0.45 μm). Membranes were blocked (5% milk, 1 h) at room temperature (RT) and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against MLKL (HY-P80227, 1:1000, MCE), p-MLKL (Ser358) (TA7420, 1:1000, Abmart), Hif1a (610958, 1:800, BD), Spp1 (22952-1-AP, 1:1000, Proteintech), Bcl2 (T40056S, 1:1000, Abmart), Bax (T40051S, 1:1000, Abmart), Caspase 3 (TA6311S, 1:1000, Abmart), Cleaved-Caspase 3 (T40044S, 1:1000, Abmart), and β-ACTIN (20536-1-AP, 1:5000, Proteintech), followed by HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (M21001, Abmart) or goat anti-rabbit IgG (M21002, Abmart) secondary antibodies (1:10,000) for 1 h at RT. WB analysis was performed on human placental tissues from the NC and LOPE groups (n = 6 per group) and on mouse placental tissues from the WT, WT + L-NAME, and Mlkl-/- + L-NAME groups (n = 4 per group).

Quantitative real-time PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted with TRIzol (Life Technologies) and reverse-transcribed using PrimeScript RT (Takara). qRT-PCR was performed with SYBR Green (Takara) on three independent replicates, with β-ACTIN as the internal control. Relative expression was calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method. RT-qPCR analysis was performed on placental tissues from the NC group (n = 12) and the LOPE group (n = 10). Clinical characteristics of the placental samples included in the qRT-PCR analysis are provided in Supplementary Table S2. Primer amplification efficiencies were validated before relative quantification to ensure their suitability for the 2^−ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences and amplification efficiencies used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S3. Group differences were assessed by Student’s t-test (mean ± SEM), with P < 0.05 considered significant.

Multiplex immunofluorescence staining

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded placental sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval in 1 mmol/L Tris–EDTA buffer (pH 9.0). After blocking endogenous peroxidase (3% H₂O₂, 10 min) and nonspecific binding (5% BSA, 20 min), sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against SPP1 (22952-1-AP, 1:200, Proteintech), CD44 (15675-1-AP, 1:200, Proteintech), CK7 (ab181598, 1:200, Abcam), HLA-G (ab283260, 1:200, Abcam), and CD68 (ab284654, 1:100, Abcam). After incubation with each primary antibody, sections were incubated with goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L (HRP) (ab205718, Abcam; 1:2000) for 1 h at RT, followed by the corresponding TSA fluorophore reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Depending on the staining panel, two or three sequential staining cycles were performed using Try-488 (BRY-880488, Runnerbio), Try-594 (BRY-880594, Runnerbio), and/or Try-Cy3 (BRY-880530, Runnerbio). Finally, sections were mounted with antifade mounting medium containing DAPI (P0131, Beyotime). Images were acquired at 20× magnification using a Nikon Eclipse Ci-L fluorescence microscope and a Leica confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems).

Immunohistochemical staining

Paraffin-embedded placental sections were baked (65 °C, 2 h), deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0). After PBS washes, endogenous peroxidase was quenched with 3% H₂O₂ (10 min), and nonspecific binding was blocked with 5% BSA (20 min, RT). Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-MLKL (HY-P80227, 1:50, MCE) or anti-SPP1 (22952-1-AP, 1:400, Proteintech), followed by HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (166745, Jackson; 1 h, RT). Signals were developed using DAB (Servicebio, Shanghai, China) and counterstained with hematoxylin. Slides were scanned at 20× magnification using a KFBIO KF-PRO-120 digital pathology slide scanner (Jiangfeng Biological, Ningbo, China). Quantification was performed in ImageJ by converting images to 8-bit grayscale, applying a uniform threshold to define the positive staining area, and calculating the area fraction. The same analysis settings were applied to all images. IHC was performed on human placental sections from n = 5 biological replicates per group.

Statistical analysis

Data visualization and statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8 and R (v4.4.1). Quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). All statistical tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For irGSEA and gene-set scoring analyses, differential enrichment of gene sets between groups was assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for each enrichment score matrix [19]. For comparisons between two groups (e.g., control vs. LOPE), normality was first assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, as the sample size in these analyses was < 50. If the data did not follow a normal distribution, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used. If the data were normally distributed, homogeneity of variance was further evaluated. An unpaired Student’s t-test was used for data with equal variances, whereas Welch’s t-test was applied when variances were unequal. For comparisons among multiple groups (e.g., WT vs. WT + L-NAME vs. Mlkl-/-+L-NAME), the Brown–Forsythe test was first used to assess homogeneity of variance. When p > 0.05, ordinary one-way ANOVA was used; otherwise, Brown–Forsythe and Welch ANOVA tests were applied. The effects of group and time on mouse blood pressure were analyzed using two-way ANOVA. Exact statistical tests and sample sizes are indicated in the corresponding figure legends.

Results

Single-cell transcriptomic profiling identifies cellular heterogeneity in LOPE and healthy placentas

The complete workflow of the study is illustrated in Fig. 1A. Dimensionality reduction and clustering (resolution = 0.5) using the Seurat workflow initially identified 14 cell clusters (sFig. 1A), and the top 15 marker genes for each cluster are provided in Supplementary Table S4. These clusters were subsequently annotated into ten major placental cell types based on canonical marker genes (Fig. 1B,C). These included T cells (PTPRC, CD3D), extravillous trophoblasts (EVT; HLA-G, ITGA5), villous cytotrophoblasts (VCT; TEAD4), proliferating VCT (pVCT; TEAD4, MKI67), syncytiotrophoblasts (SCT; PSG1, CGB), macrophages (CD68, CD14), fibroblasts (COL1A1, ACTA2), smooth muscle cells (SMC; SMTN), erythroblasts (HBA1, HBA2), and natural killer (NK) cells (NKG7, NCAM1). UMAP visualization confirmed that the spatial expression of representative marker genes was consistent with these annotations (Fig. 1D).

The relative abundance of cell types between LOPE and NC placentas was subsequently compared. A significant reduction in the EVT proportion was observed in LOPE samples (Fig. 1E, sFig. 1B), consistent with previous reports describing a reduction in EVT numbers and impaired trophoblast invasion in PE [31–33]. Pathway activities were inferred using irGSEA. In EVTs, epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), angiogenesis, hypoxia, and coagulation programs were upregulated, consistent with an invasion/remodeling-associated milieu. In immune cells (macrophages and T cells), pathway activity was broadly decreased, suggesting an overall immunosuppressed state. Among stromal populations, SMCs showed elevated angiogenesis, hypoxia, coagulation, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) signaling, whereas fibroblasts exhibited EMT activation with reduced ROS signaling. Erythroblasts selectively enriched heme metabolism, in line with their biological role.

Overall, LOPE placentas exhibited a marked reduction in EVT abundance, the determinants of which warrant further investigation (Fig. 1F).

Necroptosis emerges as a potential pathological contributor in LOPE

Given the reduced EVT abundance, we hypothesized that programmed cell death (PCD) contributes to EVT loss. We scored seven PCD programs (autophagy, cuproptosis, extrinsic/intrinsic apoptosis, ferroptosis, necroptosis, and pyroptosis) using AddModuleScore, with the corresponding gene lists provided in Supplementary Table S5. Necroptosis, cuproptosis, and pyroptosis were significantly increased in LOPE, with necroptosis showing the strongest overall signal (Fig. 2A). Subsequent analyses were therefore focused on necroptosis activity in LOPE placentas. Phosphorylated MLKL (p-MLKL) and total MLKL were increased in LOPE placentas, consistent with enhanced necroptotic activation (Fig. 2B and C), and MLKL mRNA was significantly upregulated (Fig. 2D). Concordantly, immunohistochemistry showed increased MLKL expression (Fig. 2E,F). Together, these data support the involvement of necroptosis in LOPE pathogenesis. To define the cellular context of necroptosis, necroptosis scores were mapped onto UMAP embeddings across placental cell types in NC and LOPE (Fig. 2G). Violin plots showed significantly higher necroptosis activity in EVTs, VCTs, and SCTs (Fig. 2H, sFig. 1 C), whereas other populations displayed minimal changes or reduced scores. Given the predominance of EVTs in this dataset, subsequent analyses focused on necroptosis-associated programs and microenvironmental features within EVT subpopulations.

SPP1 + EVTs identified as a distinct subtype associated with necroptosis and LOPE

To further define EVT contributions to the necroptosis-associated microenvironment in LOPE, EVTs were subsetted and re-analyzed. Raw counts and metadata were extracted to generate per-sample Seurat objects, followed by standard preprocessing and Harmony-based batch correction. Three EVT subclusters were resolved (Fig. 3A). Cluster markers were identified using ‘FindAllMarkers’ and summarized by volcano plots (Fig. 3B). Based on representative upregulated markers, the subclusters were annotated as SPP1 + EVT, TAC3 + EVT, and CORO6 + EVT (Fig. 3C). Subtype quantification revealed a significant expansion of SPP1 + EVTs in LOPE, accompanied by a reduction in TAC3 + EVTs, whereas CORO6 + EVTs remained unchanged (Fig. 3D).

Co-expression module analysis identified three transcriptional programs (C1–C3) across EVT subtypes (Fig. 3E). C1, enriched for ER/Golgi lumen and extracellular matrix terms, was preferentially upregulated in SPP1 + EVTs, consistent with a secretory, matrix-supportive state. C2, most evident in TAC3 + EVTs, was enriched for immunoglobulin complex and Golgi network components, suggesting roles in immune modulation at the maternal–fetal interface. C3, dominant in CORO6 + EVTs, was enriched for collagen trimer assembly and granule membrane–associated processes, implicating a role in tissue remodeling. Ro/e analysis further confirmed that SPP1 + EVTs were enriched in LOPE placentas (Ro/e > 1), suggesting an association with disease-related EVT states (Fig. 3F). Core necroptosis genes (RIPK1, RIPK3, and MLKL) were then examined across EVT subtypes. SPP1 + EVTs showed the highest mean expression of MLKL and RIPK1, whereas RIPK3 was low across all subsets (Fig. 3G). Consistently, necroptosis gene-set scoring was selectively elevated in SPP1 + EVTs (Fig. 3H), indicating that this subtype is transcriptionally poised for necroptotic activation, potentially through a RIPK1–MLKL–skewed axis.

Trajectory analysis corroborates a cell-killing–enriched SPP1 + EVT state

To further elucidate developmental relationships among EVT subtypes, pseudotime trajectory analysis was performed, and cells were ordered along a continuous pseudo-temporal axis based on transcriptional similarity (Fig. 4A). A coherent progression was inferred from CORO6 + EVTs (early pseudotime) to TAC3 + EVTs (intermediate) and finally to SPP1 + EVTs (late), consistent with stepwise state transitions among these subsets. Concordantly, representative marker genes were dynamically regulated along pseudotime, supporting this ordering (Fig. 4B). PROGENy-based pathway activity profiling was conducted to compare signaling states across EVT subsets (Fig. 4C). The highest overall activity was observed in CORO6 + EVTs, with enrichment of TGF-β signaling, EMT, hypoxia, coagulation, angiogenesis, and ROS responses, consistent with a highly dynamic, remodeling-prone phenotype. Intermediate activity was detected in TAC3 + EVTs, suggestive of a transitional state. In contrast, broadly reduced pathway activity was observed in SPP1 + EVTs, consistent with a more quiescent or post-functional program and potentially a terminal state.

Trajectory-associated differential expression was used to define three gene modules (C1–C3) (Fig. 4D). GO enrichment was performed for each module to characterize functional divergence among EVT subsets (Fig. 4E). Spatial transcriptomics uncovers specific localization patterns of SPP1 + EVT in the placental microenvironment

To further investigate the spatial architecture of placental tissues, ST data from LOPE and NC samples were integrated and clustered (resolution = 0.5) after SCTransform normalization, PCA, and Harmony batch correction, yielding 10 spatial niches (Niche0–Niche9) (Fig. 5A). Spatial mapping indicated that Niche 4, 6, 7, and 9 were predominantly located in the basal plate, whereas Niche 0, 1, 2, 3, 5, and 8 were enriched in the villous region (Fig. 5B). Cell-type deconvolution was performed using RCTD to project scRNA-seq–defined cell types onto ST spots, revealing distinct spatial distributions (Fig. 5C, sFig. 2 A–B). Cell-type enrichment across niches was summarized by heatmaps of z-score–normalized average cell-type weights, shown separately for LOPE (Fig. 5D) and NC (sFig. 2 C). EVT enrichment was observed in basal plate niches (Niche 4, 7, and 9), while Niche 6 at the basal plate–villus interface showed a mixed composition including EVTs, macrophages, and fibroblasts; remaining niches were dominated by villous lineages (VCTs and SCTs) with varying stromal (fibroblasts, SMCs) and immune (macrophage) contributions. Niche proportions are shown in Fig. 5E. PROGENy analysis further indicated elevated hypoxia, PI3K, TGF-β, TNFα, and EGFR signaling in EVT-enriched niches (Niche 4, 7, and 9), consistent with a stress-adaptive and pro-inflammatory microenvironment, whereas villous-enriched niches (e.g., Niche 0, 1, and 8) displayed comparatively quiescent pathway activity (Fig. 5F).

Cell–cell communication analysis highlights SPP1–CD44 axis mediating SPP1 + EVT–macrophage crosstalk

Niche 6 showed the presence of SPP1 + EVTs, macrophages, and fibroblasts, suggesting a potential spatial context for their interaction. In scRNA-seq, CellChat analysis showed that EVT subtypes (SPP1+, CORO6+, and TAC3 + EVTs) were major signal sources, whereas macrophages and fibroblasts were primary receivers, as quantified by interaction number/strength heatmaps (sFig. 3 A,B) and the aggregated network (sFig. 3C). Given the established pathogenic relevance of macrophages in LOPE [34], ligand–receptor analysis focusing on macrophage-related communication highlighted four dominant pathways: SPP1, FN1, ANNEXIN (ANXA1), and ApoE (sFig. 3D). Within the SPP1 pathway, outgoing signals from SPP1 + EVTs were consistently the strongest regardless of whether targets were restricted to EVT subsets or extended to non-EVT populations (Fig. 6A and B), supporting SPP1 + EVTs as a highly communicative disease-associated state. FN1 primarily reflected broad ECM/adhesion interactions (sFig. 3E), whereas ANNEXIN and ApoE showed minimal signaling toward EVT targets (sFig. 3F, 3G). Expression of the SPP1–CD44 pair further supported this axis, with SPP1 enriched in SPP1 + EVTs and CD44 enriched in macrophages (Fig. 6C). To validate these findings spatially, CellChat was performed across the ten ST-defined niches, which were functionally re-annotated by the dominant communicating cell subtype (sFig. 4 A, 4B). Among SPP1-pathway ligand–receptor pairs, SPP1–CD44 emerged as the top interaction and displayed the highest communication probability across niches (Fig. 6D, sFig. 4 C).

Further subclustering of placental macrophages resolved five distinct subsets (Fig. 6E, sFig. 5 A), which were annotated by top markers as CXCR4+, LYVE1+, PTPRF+, MMP7+, and SCFD1 + macrophages (Fig. 6F, sFig. 5B–F). Mlkl knockout attenuates L-NAME–induced preeclampsia-like phenotypes and reduces placental SPP1 expression in vivo

To validate the functional involvement of necroptosis in LOPE, we established an L-NAME–induced preeclampsia-like model in wild-type (WT) and Mlkl⁻/⁻ pregnant mice (Fig. 7A). L-NAME administration induced a progressive elevation of SBP in WT mice, which was significantly attenuated in Mlkl⁻/⁻ mice (Fig. 7B). Consistently, L-NAME–treated WT mice exhibited marked fetal growth restriction, whereas MLKL deficiency partially rescued fetal weight without affecting placental weight (Fig. 7C–E). L-NAME–induced glomerular enlargement and mesangial expansion, as revealed by H&E and PAS staining, were substantially alleviated in Mlkl⁻/⁻ mice (Fig. 7F, G).

Given our transcriptomic identification of SPP1 + extravillous trophoblasts as a necroptosis-associated population, we next examined SPP1 expression in vivo. IF analysis suggested an increase in SPP1 staining within the junctional zone (JZ) following L-NAME treatment in WT pregnant mice, whereas this staining appeared weaker in Mlkl⁻/⁻ placentas (Fig. 7H). Consistently, circulating SPP1 levels were elevated in L-NAME–treated WT mice but significantly decreased in Mlkl⁻/⁻ mice (Fig. 7I). Western blot analysis further confirmed a reduction of placental SPP1 protein levels in Mlkl⁻/⁻ mice compared with WT counterparts under L-NAME treatment (Fig. 7J, K).

In addition, L-NAME–treated WT placentas exhibited increased expression of apoptosis-associated markers, including elevated cleaved caspase-3 and an altered BAX/BCL2 ratio. These changes were partially reversed by MLKL knockout, indicating an attenuation of placental cell death programs upon necroptosis blockade (Fig. 7J, M–O). Moreover, placental HIF1α expression was markedly upregulated in L-NAME–treated WT mice, whereas this hypoxia-associated response was significantly reduced in Mlkl⁻/⁻ mice (Fig. 7J-L), suggesting that hypoxic stress may act as an upstream driver of necroptosis and SPP1 induction in LOPE.

Discussion

In this study, we integrated scRNA-seq and spatial transcriptomics to characterize the placental cellular and spatial landscape in LOPE. We identified necroptosis as a prominent trophoblast cell-death program and provide, to our knowledge, the first in vivo evidence supporting a causal contribution of necroptosis to L-NAME–induced PE-like phenotypes, as genetic ablation of Mlkl ameliorated gestational hypertension, fetal growth restriction, and renal pathology. We further delineate an EVT subset, SPP1 + EVTs, as the population most strongly associated with necroptosis. Mechanistically, we suggest that SPP1 released from necroptotic trophoblasts may remodel the local immune microenvironment through SPP1–CD44 interactions with macrophages, consistent with multiplex immunofluorescence findings, thereby contributing to the inflammatory milieu of LOPE. Finally, these results highlight SPP1 as a potential biomarker candidate for LOPE.

SPP1, also known as Osteopontin (OPN), is a multifunctional glycoprotein that plays key roles in cell adhesion, migration, inflammation, immune regulation, and tumor progression [35–38]. Mechanistically, SPP1 interacts with integrins (e.g., αvβ3) and CD44 to initiate downstream signaling cascades [39, 40]. In pregnancy, SPP1 has been implicated in implantation and placental development across various species. In pigs and sheep, SPP1 serves as an important adhesion molecule during implantation by bridging conceptus attachment to the uterine epithelium through integrin binding [41]. In mice, it facilitates embryo implantation and trophoblast differentiation [42]. In human EVTs, the colocalization of OPN and CEACAM1 suggests a potential cooperative role in modulating trophoblast invasiveness at the maternal–fetal interface [43]. In vitro models have further demonstrated that OPN treatment enhances the invasive capacity of EVT cells, supporting the hypothesis that OPN and CEACAM1 may function as a regulatory complex governing placental invasion. Additional evidence shows that OPN promotes trophoblast invasion via upregulation of MMP-9 expression in vitro [44]. Notably, in infertile women with polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), elevated estrogen and pro-inflammatory cytokines were reported to increase transcriptional activation of both OPN and CD44 [45]. Consistent with our findings, SPP1 protein has previously been localized by IF to glycogen trophoblast cells in the JZ of the mouse placenta [46]. Moreover, a recent large-scale single-cell transcriptomic analysis of 101,250 placental cells identified a disease-associated IGFBP1 + SPP1+ EVT population enriched at the maternal–fetal interface in preeclamptic placentas, further supporting our finding that SPP1-linked EVT states are involved in abnormal placentation [47].

The classical “two-stage hypothesis” posits that EOPE is primarily driven by abnormal placental development, whereas LOPE is more strongly influenced by maternal factors [48]. However, this model fails to account for why certain maternal risk factors—such as high body mass index, term pregnancy, and multiple gestation—are more closely associated with LOPE [49, 50]. The recent revised model proposes that “maternal” preeclampsia does not exist, as all preeclampsia requires a placenta [51]. This perspective underscores the central role of the placenta across different preeclampsia phenotypes, including LOPE, where its contribution should not be overlooked [52–54]. Consistent with this notion, our placental scRNA-seq analysis revealed a significant reduction in EVT abundance in LOPE, suggesting structural and functional impairment of trophoblast invasion/remodeling. Similar EVT depletion in LOPE placentas relative to term controls was also reported by Chen et al. (2025) [55]. The mechanisms underlying EVT depletion in LOPE remain unclear. Given the inflammatory milieu of preeclampsia at the maternal–fetal interface, dysregulated inflammatory programmed cell death may contribute to EVT loss and placental dysfunction.

Necroptosis is a regulated form of inflammatory cell death mediated by the activation of ·As a type of programmed cell death, it has been shown to play a critical role in various inflammation-associated diseases [56, 57]. In pregnancy-related disorders, accumulating evidence links necroptosis to trophoblast dysfunction. Preeclamptic placentas have been reported to accumulate pro-death sphingolipids capable of inducing trophoblast necroptosis, which can be partially prevented by RIPK1 inhibition (e.g., Necrostatin-1) [58]. Moreover, PGAM5 upregulation in preeclamptic placentas promotes trophoblast necroptosis via Drp1 activation and downstream effectors, contributing to disease progression [59]. Other studies have demonstrated that GRK2 deficiency enhances EVT necroptosis through RIPK1/RIPK3/MLKL activation [14], while THBS1 downregulation promotes necroptosis via the NEDD4-mediated TAK1 ubiquitination pathway [60]. However, these findings are largely derived from in vitro or pathway-targeted inhibition models and therefore cannot provide direct causal evidence linking necroptosis to preeclampsia pathogenesis. In our study, we utilized Mlkl⁻/⁻ mice for the first time and achieved a significant breakthrough by directly testing this causal relationship in vivo. In addition, chronic hypoxia has been shown to be closely associated with necroptosis. Under mild or transient hypoxia, cells primarily activate the HIF-α pathway to adapt to reduced oxygen tension. However, prolonged or severe hypoxia triggers RIPK1 activation, leading to downstream necroptotic signaling and inflammatory responses [61]. This process can be mediated through mitochondrial dysfunction, ROS accumulation, or ZBP1 sensing of cytosolic DNA stress [62, 63]. Importantly, hypoxia is also tightly linked to placental dysfunction [64]. In our study, Hif1a expression was markedly increased in preeclamptic placentas compared with normal pregnancies but significantly decreased upon Mlkl deletion, suggesting that hypoxia may act as a critical upstream factor driving necroptosis during preeclampsia.

Other forms of PCD such as pyroptosis and ferroptosis have also been implicated in preeclampsia [65, 66], and were detectable in our AddModuleScore analysis. Multiple studies have reported significantly elevated levels of activated caspase-1, cleaved GSDMD, and IL-1β in preeclamptic placental tissue [67, 68]. Pyroptosis can disrupt maternal–fetal immune tolerance, drive decidual macrophages toward M2 polarization, and suppress EVT invasion and spiral artery remodeling—mechanisms that are considered central to early-onset preeclampsia [69]. Abnormal iron metabolism has also been linked to placental ferroptosis. For example, reduced ferritin light chain (FTL) expression is associated with failed spiral artery remodeling [70], while preeclamptic placentas show increased iron accumulation, decreased GPX4 activity, and lower glutathione (GSH) levels [71]. Additionally, upregulation of miR-30b-5p can induce ferroptosis by downregulating SLC7A11 and GPX4, thereby impairing trophoblast function [72]. In the L-NAME model, placentas also showed altered apoptosis-associated markers (BAX, BCL2, caspase-3 and cleaved caspase-3), and these changes were partially normalized in Mlkl^−/− dams, suggesting that necroptosis inhibition may alleviate broader trophoblast stress and apoptotic activation. The precise contribution of other forms of PCD requires further investigation.

From a clinical perspective, these findings raise several important implications. First, molecules such as SPP1, CD44, and CXCR4 could serve as potential biomarkers for LOPE, offering diagnostic or prognostic value. Second, therapeutic strategies targeting necroptosis—such as the inhibition of RIPK kinases—may hold promise for mitigating trophoblast loss and restoring immune balance. Third, our findings underscore the relevance of trophoblast death in compromising maternal–fetal tolerance, thereby advancing our understanding of how altered trophoblast–immune cell crosstalk contributes to adverse pregnancy outcomes.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the number of ST samples was relatively small, and larger cohorts will be required to validate the spatial distribution patterns observed here. Second, with respect to the resolution of spatial transcriptomics, the Visium platform used in this study has a lower resolution compared with technologies such as Xenium or HD-ST, which limits the precision of neighborhood-level analysis [73]. Alternative approaches, such as NicheNet, may provide more accurate insights into cell–cell interactions and spatial associations [74]. Third, although we confirmed elevated activation of necroptosis in LOPE tissues, the upstream mechanisms driving necroptosis in SPP1 + EVTs remain unclear. Additional sequencing approaches, such as proteomics and metabolomics, may help to identify the signals responsible for triggering necroptosis in this specific trophoblast subset [75]. Fourth, fetal sex was not taken into consideration in the mouse experiments, and this variable has increasingly been recognized as an important factor influencing placental molecular signatures. Consistent with emerging transcriptomic evidence, placental responses to late-onset preeclampsia may differ by fetal sex, with male-bearing placentas showing more pronounced alterations in immune cell composition and gene expression patterns. Therefore, future studies should incorporate fetal sex into experimental design and downstream analysis [76]. Finally, whether SPP1 secreted by EVTs can engage CD44 on macrophages to induce an M1-like phenotype remains to be determined, and this hypothesis will require in vitro experimental validation.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study supports a model in which trophoblast necroptosis contributes to LOPE by promoting macrophage-centered inflammatory remodeling at the maternal–fetal interface. The SPP1–CD44 signaling axis emerges as a plausible mechanistic link between trophoblast dysfunction and immune dysregulation, with potential implications for biomarker development and targeted therapeutic strategies in LOPE.
