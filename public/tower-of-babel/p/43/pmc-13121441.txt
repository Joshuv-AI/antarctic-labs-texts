Modulation of feedback-related negativity by objective and subjective response correctness
Authors: Yasuhisa Maruyama, Keita Aoyama, Osamu Fukayama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Monitoring actions and outcomes is essential for flexible adaptation to environmental demands. Information about errors is important regardless of whether it is internally generated or externally signaled. Following incorrect responses, an event-related potential (ERP) component known as error-related negativity (ERN) appears in electroencephalographic (EEG) signals and reflects activity of the performance monitoring system located in the posterior medial frontal cortex (pMFC). Another ERP component associated with performance monitoring, feedback-related negativity (FRN), has been shown to process feedback stimuli related to performance errors. However, unlike the ERN, which is elicited by errors that may not reach conscious awareness, it remains unclear whether the FRN is modulated by unconsciously perceived errors. To examine modulation of the FRN by objective and subjective response correctness, EEG was recorded from 28 participants performing a digit-entering task that included trials with false feedback. Single-trial EEG analysis using linear mixed-effects models revealed that FRN amplitudes were modulated by objective response correctness after controlling for subjective reports of error certainty. Response time in the subsequent trial was also influenced by objective correctness, although no behavioral measure fully aligned with ERN or FRN patterns. These findings suggest a common representation of objective correctness in response- and feedback-related pMFC activity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-43451-6.

Introduction

Monitoring actions and outcomes is essential for adapting to the environment. Errors must be detected to enable behavioral adjustment, and electrophysiological and neuroimaging studies have consistently implicated the posterior medial frontal cortex (pMFC), including the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex, as the region responsible for error processing1. Electroencephalographic (EEG) studies have identified a neurophysiological signature of error detection reflecting pMFC activity, termed error-related negativity (ERN)2,3. The ERN is a frontocentrally distributed event-related potential (ERP) component that peaks within 100 ms after the response and can sometimes emerge before it4,5. Accordingly, ERN reflects internal error detection. Several studies have shown that ERN is sensitive to objective response correctness, with larger ERN amplitudes observed for unaware errors than for correct responses6–8. Thus, ERN distinguishes erroneous from correct responses at the time of responding, even when the error is not consciously perceived. Other studies have reported modulation of the ERN by subjective response correctness, showing different ERN amplitudes between consciously perceived and unperceived errors9–13. Together, the modulation of ERN by objective and subjective response correctness has been demonstrated in different experiments, although the emergence of ERN to unperceived errors is controversial14 and the relationship between the ERN and error awareness may depend on the experimental paradigm and on how error awareness is quantified15.

In contrast to ERN, the modulation of feedback-related negativity (FRN) by objective response correctness has received less attention. The FRN is a negative deflection observed following negative feedback (e.g., external information about performance errors or monetary loss), in contrast to positive feedback reflecting correct performance or monetary gain16,17. Thus, FRN is defined as the relative negativity to negative compared to positive feedback at 200–350 ms after feedback presentation16,17, although it has also been proposed that the FRN to negative feedback reflects an absence of a positivity referred to as the reward positivity (RewP), which is elicited by positive feedback18. FRN has been suggested to reflect a prediction error or feedback-related surprise19–21; the violation of subjective expectation elicits FRN22,23. Given its similar waveform and frontocentral distribution to ERN, and given that both ERN and FRN originate in the pMFC16,24, these components have been proposed to share common neural substrates1. Thus far, several studies reported the relationship between response correctness and FRN elicited by negative feedback25–29. When a correct response is made, ERN is not elicited at the time of responding; however, negative feedback may still elicit the FRN because it violates the expectation of positive feedback. Conversely, when an error is made and internally detected by the pMFC (i.e., ERN is elicited), FRN is often not observed following subsequent negative feedback. This inverse relationship between the ERN and FRN has been consistently reported25–29. However, in these studies, participants were typically aware of their errors and confident in their correctness when receiving negative feedback on correct responses. Therefore, it remains unclear whether FRN generation is influenced by objective response correctness in addition to subjective response correctness. Critically, it remains unclear whether FRN amplitude in response to identical negative feedback is modulated by objective response correctness after controlling for the effect of subjective response correctness.

In this study, we tested the hypothesis that, when an error is internally detected at the time of responding, as reflected in ERN elicitation, the FRN is also modulated by objective response correctness in addition to subjective response correctness. Additionally, we investigated whether the interaction between these factors influences internal and external error monitoring, as reflected by the ERN and FRN. By clarifying how the brain processes feedback when error detection varies in awareness and accuracy, this study contributes to a deeper understanding of cognitive control and the neural basis of performance monitoring.

Methods

Participants

Thirty healthy right-handed participants were enrolled in the experiment. The number of participants was determined based on a previous EEG study using a task similar to the current study30 and on existing EEG studies investigating the relationship between error-related ERP components and subjective response confidence31,32. All participants were native Japanese speakers and had normal or corrected-to-normal vision, normal hearing, and no history of neurological or psychiatric disorders. Participants were monetarily rewarded for participation. Due to excessive EEG artifacts, data from one participant were excluded from the analysis. Data from another participant were excluded because 46.6% of self-generated errors were reported as a “certainly correct” response. This rate was substantially higher than that observed in the 28 participants included in the analysis (range: 0.0–16.8%; mean ± standard deviation (SD): 4.9% ± 4.5%) and likely indicated a misinterpretation of task instructions or reduced concentration or motivation. The final sample comprised 28 participants (8 women, 20 men) aged 18–43 years (mean ± SD: 24.9 ± 5.4 years). Written informed consent was obtained after the experimental procedure was explained. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee for Human Research at the National Institute of Information and Communications Technology (Approval No. N230172401) and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Experimental design

The experiments were conducted in a soundproof chamber. Participants were seated in a comfortable chair with a computer monitor and keyboard positioned in front of them. A digit-entering task was used, as previous research reported that it elicits both unaware errors and correct responses30. Figure 1 illustrates the sequence of a single trial. In each trial, participants were required to enter a five-digit number displayed on the monitor within a time limit, using a standard numeric keypad. Participants were instructed to press the keys as quickly and accurately as possible and were permitted to use only the right index finger. The target number was replaced by a fixation cross on the monitor upon the first keypress. If all five digits were entered correctly, “XXXXX” in white was displayed on a black background for 1 s, accompanied by a 600-Hz beep presented for 0.5 s immediately after the fifth keypress. An incorrect keypress triggered immediate auditory and visual feedback: a 1000-Hz beep for 0.5 s and “XXXXX” on the monitor, with the erroneous digit shown in red and the remaining four digits in white. Following such feedback, participants were prompted to report their level of error certainty by selecting one of four options: “certainly correct,” “probably/maybe correct,” “probably/maybe wrong,” or “certainly wrong.” As described, feedback was delivered both visually and auditorily immediately following each keypress, with a delay of approximately 14 ms between keypress and feedback, rendering them effectively simultaneous. If the five-digit sequence was not entered within the time limit, feedback reading “Respond faster!” (in Japanese) was presented with a 1400-Hz beep for 0.5 s. The intertrial interval varied randomly between 1.5 and 2.5 s.

Trials in which participants correctly entered all five digits and received corresponding positive feedback were classified as “correct trials.” Trials involving an incorrect keypress with corresponding negative feedback were classified as “error trials.” In addition, false feedback indicating an incorrect keypress was presented on a subset of correct responses. The probability of false feedback was 0.2 per trial; otherwise, it was set to zero. These trials were classified as “false-feedback trials.” False feedback was presented only when the number of error trials exceeded the number of false-feedback trials. Participants were informed in advance that such false feedback might occasionally occur despite correct responses.

The experiments were conducted using PsychoPy v2023.1.233. Each block comprised 100 trials, and each participant completed 10 blocks (i.e., 1000 trials in total). Each digit in the five-digit target number was randomly selected from 1 to 9 without repetition. To ensure sufficient error trials, the response time limit was dynamically adjusted based on performance. If more than half of the previous 10 trials (excluding false-feedback trials) were error or timeout trials, the time limit increased by 0.05 s. Conversely, if more than 70% of the previous 10 trials (excluding false-feedback trials) were correct, the time limit decreased by 0.05 s. The initial time limit was based on the median response time across the last 20 trials of a 30-trial practice block conducted before the main experiment.

EEG data acquisition and processing

EEG data were acquired using a 32-channel g.Nautilus RESEARCH system with g.SAHARA hybrid EEG electrodes (g.tec Medical Engineering GmbH, Schiedlberg, Austria) at a sampling rate of 500 Hz. Electrodes were positioned according to the extended international 10–20 system, and EEG was recorded from FP1/2, AFz, F7/3/z/4/8, FC7/3/z/4/8, C7/3/z/4/8, CP7/3/z/4/8, P7/3/z/4/8, PO3/z/4, and Oz. Reference and ground electrodes were placed on the right earlobe and forehead, respectively. Conductive gel (g.GAMMAgel) was applied between the skin and electrodes.

EEG data were processed using MATLAB R2022b (MathWorks, Inc., Natick, MA, USA) and EEGLAB version 2022.134. A finite impulse response (FIR) band-pass filter with cut-off frequencies of 0.5 and 30 Hz and a transition bandwidth of 1 Hz was applied. Noisy channels were removed and interpolated using spherical spline interpolation (mean ± SD: 1.3 ± 1.6 channels; FCz was retained in all datasets). Adaptive mixture independent component analysis35 (https://sccn.ucsd.edu/~jason/amica_web.html) was applied to the continuous EEG, and components associated with ocular activity were removed. The EEG signals were then re-referenced to the average of all 32 channels. For each trial, the EEG segment from 0.5 s before the first keypress to 0.5 s after feedback stimulus onset was extracted. Trials were excluded from analysis if the segment contained artifacts identified by visual inspection, data loss, or any channel with amplitudes exceeding ± 100 µV (range: 0.4–25.7%; mean ± SD: 8.9% ± 6.4%).

In this dataset, the FRN overlapped with the P300, typically observed 200–500 ms after feedback, and with the error positivity (Pe), which follows the ERN2. To attenuate the influence of these slow-wave components, EEG filtered at 3.5–30 Hz was used for the analyses of FRN and another feedback-locked negativity at 200–280 ms. Several previous studies have similarly applied band-pass filtering to isolate the FRN by removing P300-related slow waves25,36,37.

To demonstrate that the ERP obtained from EEG data high-pass filtered at 0.5 or 3.5 Hz was not attributable to filter artifacts, we additionally prepared EEG data filtered at 0.1–30 Hz. To remove ocular artifacts, independent component analysis weights derived from the EEG data filtered at 0.5–30 Hz were applied, and components associated with ocular activity were removed.

Statistical analysis

Trials in which multiple keys were registered simultaneously were excluded from the behavioral and EEG analyses (mean ± SD: 5.3 ± 4.9 trials). In these trials, participants received negative feedback indicating incorrect responses immediately after pressing two keys at once, although in most cases they had pressed the correct key in addition to one of its neighboring keys.

In the behavioral analyses, response time for the next-trial first keypress (R1), average inter-keystroke interval (IKI), and accuracy were compared between error and false-feedback trials, as well as across different levels of error certainty. Next-trial R1 and IKI were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) after logarithmic transformation to approximate a normal distribution. To examine whether next-trial response speed was modulated by objective and subjective response correctness, single-trial R1 and IKI were analyzed using LMMs with trial type (i.e., error and false-feedback) and four levels of error certainty as fixed effects, and participant-wise random intercepts as a random effect. As time pressure could also influence performance, the standardized, logarithmically transformed time limit of the next trial was included in the LMMs as a fixed effect. Only trials that preceded correct trials were included in the analyses of next-trial R1 and IKI. Next-trial accuracy was analyzed using a generalized linear mixed-effects model (GLMM), as accuracy was a binary variable (0 for error, 1 for correct). The GLMM used a binomial distribution and logit link function. Trials preceding false-feedback and timeout trials were excluded. The GLMM included trial type, error certainty, and the standardized, logarithmically transformed time limit of the next trial as fixed effects, with participant-wise random intercepts as a random effect. Participant-wise random slopes for trial type and error certainty were not included in the R1, IKI, and accuracy analyses due to convergence issues.

Similar to the next-trial behavioral analyses, single-trial ERN and FRN amplitudes were analyzed using LMMs. Based on existing studies and comparisons of grand-average ERP waveforms between error and correct trials in the current dataset, the minimum amplitude within 0–50 ms post-keypress was used for ERN analysis. Similarly, single-trial FRN amplitude was defined as the minimum value within 300–350 ms after feedback, consistent with previous literature and based on comparisons of grand-average ERP waveforms between correct and false-feedback trials in the current dataset. Although some studies define FRN as the differential waveform between positive and negative feedback, the current study defined FRN as the negative deflection in the undifferentiated original waveform, because we used the single-trial amplitude for the LMM analysis. Moreover, because another negativity was observed 200–280 ms after feedback, this ERP component was also analyzed. Single-trial amplitude of this negativity was defined as the minimum value within 200–280 ms after feedback. For the EEG data filtered at 0.1 Hz, ERP amplitudes were quantified using the peak-to-peak method. To minimize the influence of low-frequency ERP effects and artifacts, positive peaks both preceding and following the negative peak were extracted, and their mean values were subtracted from the negative peak amplitude. For the ERN, the mean value of the maximum amplitudes within 100 ms both preceding and following the negative peak was subtracted from the minimum amplitude measured within 0–50 ms post-keypress. For the FRN, the mean value of the maximum amplitudes within 50 ms both preceding and following the negative peak was subtracted from the minimum amplitude measured within 300–350 ms post-feedback. A shorter time window was used to prevent detection of secondary closest positive peaks. Similarly, for the negativity observed at 200–280 ms, the mean value of the maximum amplitudes within 100 ms preceding the negative peak and within 50 ms following the negative peak was subtracted from the minimum amplitude measured within 200–280 ms post-feedback. The amplitudes of the ERN, FRN, and the additional feedback-locked negativity at 200–280 ms were analyzed to assess how objective and subjective response correctness modulated them. LMMs included trial type, error certainty, and the standardized, logarithmically transformed time limit of the current trial as fixed effects, with participant-wise random intercepts as a random effect. To account for digit-specific variability in ERPs, digit was additionally included as a fixed effect in all ERP analyses. Participant-wise random slopes were specified for trial type and digit; however, participant-wise random slopes for error certainty were not included due to convergence issues.

LMM and GLMM analyses were conducted in R38 and RStudio39 using the lme440 and lmerTest41 packages. LMMs were fitted using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation, and GLMMs were fitted using maximum likelihood (ML) estimation. Given that the analyses included two trial types and four error certainty levels as fixed effects, models with and without interaction terms were compared using the likelihood ratio test (LRT). If the LRT did not support the interaction model (p > 0.05), statistical significance of fixed effects in the non-interaction model was assessed using Kenward–Roger’s correction of denominator degrees of freedom via the pbkrtest package42 in REML-fitted LMMs. For GLMMs, the statistical significance of each fixed effect was assessed via LRTs comparing models with and without the fixed effect. When a significant main effect was observed in fixed effects with more than two levels, or if the LRT supported the interaction model (p < 0.05), post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using the emmeans package43, and Sidak-adjusted p-values were reported. The significance threshold was set at p < 0.05. All categorical variables were sum-coded. The contrast coding for trial type, error certainty, and digit is provided in Supplementary Tables S1, S2, and S3, respectively.

Feedback-related negativity

Figure 4a displays grand-average ERP waveforms from EEG data filtered at 3.5–30 Hz, time-locked to feedback for correct, error, and false-feedback trials at FCz. To eliminate low-frequency components, such as Pe and P300, that occur 200–500 ms following erroneous responses or feedback, feedback-related ERP waveforms were quantified using EEG data after applying a high-pass filter at 3.5 Hz. All five digits and error-certainty conditions were aggregated. To eliminate any effect by feedback expectation, ERP waveforms for keys 1 to 4 are shown in Supplementary Fig. S2. Since FRN is defined as the negativity associated with negative feedback, particularly for correct responses in the current experimental context, FRN was quantified in the 300–350 ms range after feedback. Negative deflection in the 300–350 ms range was also confirmed in the comparison between negative feedback in false-feedback trials and the omission of negative feedback in correct trials (Supplementary Fig. S2). The mean topographical maps for 300–350 ms after feedback are shown in Fig. 4c. FRN topographies calculated in a peak-to-peak manner are shown in Supplementary Fig. S3. These results clearly demonstrate the emergence of the FRN in frontocentral sites at 300–350 ms after feedback in false-feedback trials, but little or no FRN in correct or error trials.

Discussion

In this study, the modulation of behavioral and electrophysiological measures including response time and accuracy in subsequent trials, ERN, FRN, and an additional feedback-locked negativity at 200–280 ms was investigated in relation to objective and subjective response correctness. A digit-entering task was employed, and participants reported their certainty regarding the correctness of their responses on each trial. Consistent with previous studies25–29, the average feedback-locked ERP waveforms showed a large FRN following false negative feedback but little or no FRN following positive feedback in correct trials or following negative feedback in error trials. Considering previous findings that ERN is modulated by objective response correctness and that common neural processes are assumed to underlie the generation of ERN and FRN1, FRN elicited by identical negative feedback is also expected to be modulated by objective response correctness. As FRN reflects feedback-related pMFC activity and represents prediction error19–21, false feedback should elicit a larger FRN than actual errors, as pMFC activity incorporates knowledge of objective response correctness. Results from LMM analysis of single-trial FRN amplitudes supported this prediction: larger FRN amplitudes were observed following false-feedback trials compared to error trials, after controlling for error certainty. To confirm these findings, LMM analyses were also performed separately for different error certainty level. The LMMs included trial type, digit, and time limit as fixed effects, with participant-wise random intercepts included as a random effect. The results revealed significantly larger FRN amplitudes for false-feedback trials than for error trials across all error certainty levels for both peak and peak-to-peak measures. These findings suggests that, in addition to the explicit processing of errors that reach subjective error awareness, implicit processing of objective response correctness also contributes to the formation of feedback expectations within the pMFC. Because expectation formation is required for FRN generation23, our results indicate that both objective and subjective response correctness allowed expectation formation even when feedback was provided immediately following the response.

To date, the modulation of FRN by subjective expectation has been repeatedly investigated22,23. However, the modulation of FRN by objective response correctness has received less attention. In a modified time-estimation task, Frömer et al. investigated the effects of subjective reward and sensory prediction error, as well as objective error magnitude, on FRN using LMM44. They found that FRN amplitude was modulated by subjective reward prediction error, whereas the modulation by objective error magnitude was not statistically significant. However, the performance monitoring system in the pMFC is not activated (i.e., ERN is not observed) during incorrect responses in time-estimation tasks because participants are not certain of the correct response time, and thus a clear representation of correct performance cannot be used for internal error detection16. In our study, utilizing a digit-entering task in which incorrect responses elicit ERN, we demonstrated modulation of FRN by objective response correctness after controlling for subjective response correctness. Consistent with previous studies22,23, FRN amplitude also varied with subjective response correctness, reflecting the unexpectedness of negative feedback. For emergence of FRN, an expectation of outcome needs to be formed before feedback presentation23. The current experimental design, in which feedback was given immediately after the response, may not have allowed sufficient time for error awareness to develop before feedback. However, the subjective feeling of error commission can arise before an erroneous response45,46. Existing studies have also reported the emergence of FRN to negative feedback presented simultaneously with the correct response26,29. Thus, even though the feedback was delivered immediately following the response, expectations proportional to subjective error certainty can be formed before the emergence of FRN, thereby modulating FRN amplitude. Of note, these results were not sensitive to the quantification method for FRN amplitude. In the current study, we quantified single-trial FRN amplitude using peak amplitude rather than mean amplitude, because peak measures are more robust to latency variation. To investigate the effect of using a different measure, we also performed the LMM analysis using mean amplitude and obtained nearly identical results. Thus far, the modulation of neural activity by objective and subjective response correctness has been reported in response-locked ERPs. Several studies have consistently demonstrated differences in ERN amplitudes between consciously perceived and unperceived errors9–13 or between unsignaled errors and correct responses6–8. In addition, both objective and subjective response correctness have been reported to modulate the Pe30. Pe typically occurs at centro-parietal sites 200–400 ms after a response and is associated with conscious error recognition, emotional processing of errors, or post-error behavioral adaptation47. The current findings extend these results to feedback-related pMFC activity, as reflected in the FRN.

In addition to the FRN at 300–350 ms after feedback, we observed another negative peak in the feedback-locked ERP waveforms at 200–280 ms after feedback presentation regardless of feedback valence. Since feedback was given both visually and auditorily in the study, this ERP component may reflect visual, auditory, or audio-visual processing of the feedback. Specifically, when contrasting ERP waveforms for audio-visual stimuli with those for visual or auditory stimuli alone, a negative deflection in frontocentral sites has been reported at around 250 ms48. Thus, the negativity preceding the FRN in our study may represent multimodal integration of visual and auditory information. The amplitude of this ERP component was modulated by subjective response correctness; a larger negativity was observed when participants believed their response was erroneous compared to other conditions. As top-down attention is suggested to facilitate the multisensory integration49, the modulation by subjective response correctness may reflect greater processing of negative feedback driven by the expectation of its delivery when participants were certain of their error. Notably, objective response correctness did not modulate this ERP component: no significant difference in amplitude was observed between error and false-feedback trials after controlling for subjective response correctness. Thus, attention-related facilitation of perceptual processing of feedback stimuli was not sensitive to objective response correctness that modulated FRN in the succeeding time range.

Around the time of the response, the ERN was observed when incorrect keys were pressed. Its waveform and topographical distribution were consistent with those reported in previous studies. In the LMM analysis, the LRT of the models estimated using ML revealed a significant trial type-by-error certainty interaction. A previous study employing a digit-entering task similarly reported modulation of the ERN by both subjective and objective response correctness, as well as their interaction30. Specifically, results from the present and previous studies indicated larger ERN amplitudes when incorrect keypresses were perceived with certainty, relative to other conditions. In contrast to feedback processing, these findings clearly demonstrate modulation of pMFC activity by the interaction between objective and subjective response correctness during response monitoring. Furthermore, ERN amplitude was enhanced in error trials compared to false-feedback trials when participants indicated certainty in their incorrect responses. This indicates that the ERN differentiates between correct and incorrect responses even when both elicit comparable levels of error awareness. Moreover, the current results also support the sensitivity of the ERN to conscious error awareness, as frequently reported15. In all ERP analyses, statistically significant main effects of time limit were not observed. In addition, LRTs provided no evidence for interactions between time limit and trial type or between time limit and error certainty. Although these results may partly not in accord with the well-known speed-accuracy tradeoff in ERN amplitude3, a recent study has questioned the relationship between the ERN amplitude and speed-accuracy manipulations50. Further research is warranted to elucidate the effects of time pressure on ERN amplitude.

Although the activation of pMFC is often assumed to lead to behavioral adaptations1, the behavioral results were not fully consistent with the pattern of ERN or FRN. Analysis of the response time for the first keypress in the succeeding trial revealed modulation by both objective and subjective response correctness. Errors are frequently proposed to induce slower responses in subsequent trials due to cognitive control processes or as an orienting response51. In the current study, post-error slowing was observed in error trials after controlling for error certainty. This result aligns with a previous EEG study using a digit-entering task30. Several other studies have also reported post-error slowing following error trials, but not following correct trials accompanied by negative feedback52–54. According to cognitive control theory, slower responses following errors enhance task performance. However, accuracy was not improved after errors compared with false-feedback trials when error certainty was controlled for. Post-error slowing does not consistently coincide with post-error improvements in accuracy55–57. The mechanisms underlying post-error slowing and post-error accuracy improvement require further elucidation. According to the orienting account51, post-error slowing is expected when error frequency is low. However, in the present experiment, error trials occurred more frequently than false-feedback trials (on average, 18.47% vs. 13.34%). Thus, the orienting response does not adequately explain the longer R1 associated with error trials. Regarding the relationship between R1 and error certainty, a longer next-trial R1 was observed when participants were uncertain about their response correctness. This pattern may reflect a behavioral tendency to avoid uncertain situations. For optimal behavioral adaptation, identifying the cause of the outcome is essential. When the outcome is attributed to one’s own error, behavioral adjustment is required to avoid future errors. Conversely, when correct behavior is followed by negative feedback, no behavioral change is necessary. To determine the cause of negative feedback in subsequent trials, participants may have slowed their responses if clear associations between responses and outcomes were not available in the current context.

In contrast to R1, accuracy in the next trial was modulated only by subjective response correctness. When participants believed they had made erroneous responses, they pressed keys more accurately in the next trial than when they believed their response was correct despite receiving negative feedback. Again, a different relationship between R1 and accuracy in terms of error certainty was observed. These findings suggest that post-error slowing and improvements in accuracy may be driven by different mechanisms, as proposed in previous literature55.

Although the present results revealed that the FRN is modulated by both objective and subjective response correctness, several issues remain for future investigation. First, participants were notified of the false feedback before the experiment. Previous studies showed modulation of FRN by reliability or informativeness of feedback58–61. Moreover, the notification of false negative feedback may have introduced a bias in participants’ certainty reports toward “certainly correct” responses. Additional experiments without advance notification are needed to examine the influence of prior knowledge on FRN by false feedback. Second, similar to previous studies26,29, feedback stimuli were presented immediately after keypresses in the current experiment. However, the simultaneous response and feedback prevented the analysis of Pe and feedback-related P300 because they were indistinguishable due to their spatiotemporal overlap. To examine whether the interval between response and feedback influences FRN modulation and to enable analyses of Pe and P300, additional experiments with a longer response–feedback interval are needed. Moreover, the certainty reports of the participants might have been biased toward “certainly wrong” judgments because they were made after preceding negative feedback in the present study. In future studies, certainty ratings should be collected before feedback is presented. Third, this study used a digit-entering task in which the ERN was sensitive to objective response correctness. Future research should employ other experimental paradigms in which the association between ERN and objective response correctness is either present or absent to determine whether FRN modulation by objective correctness depends on ERN sensitivity across different experimental paradigms. Fourth, the use of audio-visual feedback may have forced additional neural resources to integrate the multimodal input, as reflected in another feedback-locked negativity at 200–280 ms. The latency of FRN in the current study was delayed compared with the typical FRN range (250–300 ms post-feedback). In future research, unimodal sensory feedback should be used to investigate the effect of multimodal feedback on FRN latency. Fifth, we acknowledge the small number of trials in some conditions, especially error trials reported as “correct” responses and false-feedback trials reported as “incorrect” responses, similar to the previous digit-entering study30. Although some previous studies determined peak latencies based on condition-specific ERPs62,63, we determined peak latencies using single-trial waveforms because peak latency detection based on average waveforms may interact with the variability in trial sizes. Increasing both the trial size and number of participants would provide more robust evidence regarding the modulation of response- and feedback-related ERP components by objective and subjective response correctness. Sixth, based on prior studies26,27,29, we included only false negative feedback. Since differential modulation of FRN/RewP by positive and negative feedback has been reported64, future studies should incorporate false positive feedback and collect certainty reports following positive feedback to better characterize the neural mechanisms underlying FRN/RewP.
