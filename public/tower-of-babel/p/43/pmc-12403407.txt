A comparative study of flexible and navigable suction ureteral access sheath combined with single-use flexible ureteroscopes and percutaneous nephrolithotomy in the treatment of kidney stones > 2.5 cm: a single-center retrospective study
Authors: Guoqing Liu, Xin Zhang, Zhaorong Xu, Xiao Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and objectiveWhen performing retrograde intrarenal surgery (RIRS), compared with conventional ureteral access sheaths (UAS), flexible and navigable suction ureteral access sheaths (FANs) can reduce the number of lithotripsy sessions and increase the stone-free rate (SFR). They have been widely applied in flexible ureteroscopic lithotripsy (FURL). Currently, ureteroscopic flexible ureteroscopes equipped with FANs are also showing remarkable outcomes in the treatment of large kidney stones. Therefore, the objective of this study was to explore the efficacy of single-use flexible ureteroscopes (su-fURS) combined with FANs in the treatment of kidney stones larger than 2.5 cm and to compare it with percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL).MethodsData of patients who underwent treatment of 2.5–3.8 cm kidney stones in the First Affiliated Hospital of Shandong Second Medical University from January 2024 to December 2024 were included in the study. According to the different surgical methods, they were divided into PCNL group and FANs group. The general data, intraoperative and postoperative related data of the patients in the two groups were collected and compared, where P < 0.05 represents statistical significance.ResultsA total of 76 patients (41 PCNL, 35 FANs) were included in this study, and both groups achieved a very favorable SFR (85%, 93% vs. 80%, 91% P = 0.536, 0.840), and the difference was not statistically significant. The PCNL group had a shorter mean operative time (99.39 vs. 135.49 min P < 0.001) and a lower mean hospitalization cost (22, 865.60 vs. 26,031.91 ¥ P < 0.001). The FANs had a lower level of postoperative hemoglobin (Hb) drop (6.31 vs. 11.05 g/L P = 0.010) and a less severe level of postoperative pain (1.97 vs. 3.41 points P < 0.001), faster postoperative recovery (3.29 vs. 6.54d P < 0.001).ConclusionsFANs combined with su-FURS for the treatment of kidney stones larger than 2.5 cm have a high SFR, with less pain and quicker recovery for patients, making it a method worth promoting and applying in the clinic.Keywords: Urolithiasis, FANs, PCNL, Running position, SFR

Introduction

As the most prevalent disease of the urinary system, urinary stones are a widespread group of ailments globally and pose a significant economic burden on the healthcare system. Furthermore, the 10-year cumulative recurrence rate for urinary stones is approximately 50% [1]. An evaluation of global trends indicates that the prevalence of kidney stones has nearly tripled over a 30-year span, with an upward trend year after year [2].Extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy, percutaneous nephrolithotripsy, and ureteroscopic lithotripsy are the primary treatments for urinary tract stones currently [3]. With the widespread adoption of ureteral access sheaths (UAS) and single-use flexible ureteroscopes (su-fURS) featuring digital clear imaging, ureteroscopic lithotripsy has become available in a variety of modalities and has been the preferred treatment for most urinary stones for the past 20 years [3–6].Urology guidelines suggest flexible ureteroscopic lithotripsy (FURL) as the initial treatment choice for 1–2 cm stones in the upper urinary tract and as a substitute for 2–4 cm stones in the upper urinary tract [7, 8]. Historical evidence suggests that retrograde intrarenal surgery (RIRS) by itself is not suitable for the management of complex kidney stones larger than 2 cm, and in such patients, when percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL) is not an option or is not wanted, multiple RIRS procedures can be used to remove the stones. With the successful introduction of a novel UAS combined with suction, which has been reported to achieve a high stone-free rate for the treatment of kidney stones [9], urologists have continued to innovate, leading to the development of a flexible, navigable suction ureteral access sheath(FANs).It allows for the active removal of flushing fluid, stone fragments, and powders, thereby potentially reducing intrarenal pressure (IRP) and enhancing the efficiency of stone removal during the procedure [10]. Currently, an increasing number of studies indicate that in FURL, FANs result in higher stone-free rates (SFR), shorter operative times, and lower complication rates compared to conventional UAS [10–14]. However, there is a scarcity of research on FURL for the treatment of kidney stones larger than 2 cm. Consequently, we undertook this study to investigate the efficacy and safety of combining FANs with su-fURS for the treatment of 2.5–3.8 cm kidney stones.

Materials and methods

We included the data of 76 patients who underwent kidney stone treatment at the First Affiliated Hospital of Shandong Second Medical University from January 2024 to December 2024 in a retrospective study, and they were divided into two groups based on the difference in surgical methods.

Group 1

PCNL was chosen to treat kidney stones.

Group 2

FANs combined with su-fURS were chosen to treat kidney stones.

Inclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria required selection of kidney stones of 2.5–3.8 cm size for inclusion in the study. All patient data needed to fulfill several points: (1) age > 18 years; (2) maximum diameter of kidney stones or multiple stones diameter and > 2.5 cm; (3) PCNL or FURL was performed to treat the stones; and (4) basic information was complete. A review revealed that all kidney stones > 3.8 cm were treated with PCNL at our institution, so we excluded kidney stones over 3.8 cm; data from patients with abnormal kidney anatomical location; ureteral stones; and patients with ureteral stenosis detected at the time of performing a phase I FURL.(Clinical trial number: KYLL20241008-42).

Surgical approach

The surgeries were all performed by the same senior physician, and the patients who underwent FURL were in agreement about the outcome of the potentially staged procedure. Patients with routine urinalysis, urine culture, and C-reactive protein levels indicating infection were adequately treated with anti-infective therapy preoperatively to ensure that these indicators were stabilized prior to surgery.

Group 1

After successful general anesthesia, the lithotomy position was assumed. On one side of the operation, the F6 ureteral catheter was left in place, and a urinary catheter was also inserted. The patient’s position was then changed to prone, and ultrasound-guided puncture was performed. Following dilation of the puncture point, an F18 Pel-away sheath was inserted along the guidewire to establish a percutaneous renal channel. Utilizing the Pel-away sheath, an F20 Wolf nephroscope combined with EMS ultrasound, laser, and pneumatic ballistic technology, was used to fragment the stone and expel it. Upon confirmation that no stones remained in the operative field, F16 nephrostomy tubes were inserted after F6 double-J stents were placed in all patients, thus completing the procedure.

Group 2

General anesthesia was successfully administered, and the patient was initially positioned in the lithotomy posture. The patient was then turned to the healthy side, with the upper body positioned roughly perpendicular to the surgical surface and immobilized using a cushion, brace, and restraining belt. The healthy lower extremity was immobilized in hip flexion, while the affected lower extremity remained in the lithotomy posture and was abducted as far as possible to adequately expose the perineal area. A position similar to a running maneuver toward the healthy side, known as the running position, was created (Fig. 1). An F8/9.8 ureteroscope was used to enter the bladder, a zebra guidewire was left in the ureter on the operative side, and F12-14 FANs were placed along the guidewire. holmium laser lithotripsy was performed using a su-fURS with trans sheath access to the kidney. Intraoperatively, FANs were used intermittently to control IRP, while close to lithotripsy and FANs were used to aspirate residual stone and powder. An F6 double-J tube was placed after examination of each kidney calyx revealed no significant stone. A urinary catheter was left in place and the procedure was completed.

Positions similar to running to the healthy side used when performing RIRS

Index evaluation

In this study, we retrospectively collected the basic and surgical data of patients in both groups, including stone location, stone size, modified Resorlu-Unsal Scoring System (RUSS), S.T.O.N.E kidney stones score, operative time, Post-hospitalization Length of stay (PHLOS), and SFR. It was ensured that each patient underwent preoperative urologic ultrasound and Computed Tomography (CT), in which stone-related parameters including stone location, size, number, CT value, and Infundibulopelvic angle (IPA) were measured by urologic CT. In this study, RUSS was used to evaluate the difficulty of FURL in the two groups, and S.T.O.N.E kidney stones scoring system was used to evaluate the difficulty of PCNL in the two groups. The severity of hydronephrosis was measured by urologic ultrasound, with a separation of the kidney collecting system of 0.1–2.0 cm considered mild, 2.1–3.5 cm considered moderate, and ≥ 3.5 cm considered severe. For clarity, operative time was considered as the time between the insertion of the first surgical instrument and the insertion of the catheter at end of the procedure.

Surgical outcome was assessed by a combination of SFR at 1 d and 30 d postoperatively, pain level at 1 d postoperatively, postoperative complications, level of Hb decline, and days of hospitalization. Each of these patients underwent Kidney-Ureter-Bladder (KUB) and urologic ultrasound at 1 d postoperatively, and urologic CT at 30 d postoperatively to assess SFR, where SFR was defined as the absence of any stone fragments > 3 mm. The degree of pain at 1 d postoperatively was assessed by numerical rating scale (NRS), in which 0 was classified as no pain, 1–3 as mild pain that did not interfere with sleep, 4–6 as moderate pain with shallow sleep, and 7–10 as severe pain that seriously interfered with sleep. Postoperative complications were evaluated using the Clavien-Dindo classification system. Grade I: No need for medication or surgical intervention (except for antiemetics, antipyretics, analgesics, diuretics, electrolyte medications, physiotherapy, and bedside treatment for incision infections); Grade II: Requires drug treatment (beyond the medications for Grade I complications, including blood transfusions and total parenteral nutrition); Grade III: Requires surgery, endoscopy, or interventional procedures; Grade IV: Life-threatening complications necessitating either intermittent or intensive care; Grade V: Death.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 25.0 was used for data entry and analysis, first of all, to verify the normal distribution of the data and the chi-square, through the independent samples t-test, chi-square test for comparison, the measurement data were expressed by the mean ± standard deviation(‾X ± s), the count data were expressed by the frequency and percentage [X±s], and the Fisher’s exact probability method was used when a certain frequency < 5, and the P<0.05 indicated that the difference between the two groups of data had statistical Significance.

Results

A total of 76 patients were included in this study, 41 in PCNL group (group 1) and 35 in FANs group (group 2). The differences in preoperative creatinine level and the number of cases of lower pole stones (LPS) with IPA < 45° were statistically significant between the two groups, and the rest of the general data (Table 1) such as age, BMI, stone location and size, severity of hydronephrosis, RUSS and S.T.O.N.E score were not statistically significant (P > 0.05).

Table 2 shows the comparison of surgical outcomes and hospitalization costs between the two groups. The operative time was shorter in the PCNL group (99.39 ± 27.47) than in the FANs group (135.49 ± 39.78); the NFR score at 1 d postoperatively (3.41 ± 1.76) was significantly higher than that in the FANs group (1.97 ± 0.75); and the level of postoperative Hb decrease (11.05 ± 7.91) was more pronounced than that in the FANs group (6.31 ± 7.67); longer PHLOS (6.54 ± 1.91) than FANs group (3.29 ± 0.67); and lower hospitalization cost (22, 865.60 ± 3836.40) than FANs group (26,031.91 ± 2,300.48); the difference of the above comparisons was statistically significant (P < 0.05). In terms of postoperative complications, there were 2 cases of Clavien-Dindo grade II and 1 case of grade III in PCNL group. Among them, 1 patient had high fever after operation, 1 patient had obvious gross hematuria after operation, 1 patient had severe bleeding and infection, and hemostasis was performed by interventional means. The patient was discharged 17 d after operation. There was only 1 case of Clavien-Dindo grade II in the FANs group. The patient developed fever after operation, improved after symptomatic treatment, and was discharged 5 d after operation. However, there was no significant difference in postoperative complications between the two groups (P > 0.05). In the PCNL group, 35 patients (85%) achieved SFR at 1 d postoperatively and 38 patients (93%) at 30 d postoperatively, whereas in the FANs group, 28 patients (80%) achieved SFR at 1 d postoperatively and 32 patients (91%) at 30 d postoperatively. Both groups achieved very significant SFR, and the difference between the two groups was not statistically significant (P > 0.05). Interestingly, there was a statistically significant difference between the two groups in the comparison of hospitalization costs, which means that the PCNL group had lower hospitalization costs than the FANs group.

Discussion

In this study, we all completed the FURL in the running position. When the patient is in a particular position similar to running to the healthy side, the renal pelvis is the lowest position of the kidney. The residual stone falls into the ureteral sheath opening due to gravity, and the stone powder or powder is more easily suctioned out under negative pressure. We believe that compared with the lithotomy position, the new position does not require searching for fragmented stones in the renal pelvis and suctioning them out, reducing operative time.

Relevant guidelines [7, 8] suggest that PCNL is preferred for renal stones ≥ 2 cm, and FURL is only used as an alternative for 2–4 cm upper urinary tract stones. Although the complication rate of PCNL has been decreasing in recent years [15], the problems of high trauma, severe complications, and a long learning curve still exist, making the PCNL technique difficult to perform in some hospitals. Meanwhile, PCNL may cause serious complications of adjacent organ damage [16–19]. Therefore, with the continuous development and improvement of ureteroscopic techniques, the use of FURL has gradually become more widespread, and the effectiveness and safety of FURL in the treatment of larger loads of kidney stones have been evaluated, and their indications are being broadened [4]. In China, the most recent expert consensus [20] has identified FANs combined with su-fURS as a first-line option for < 3 cm upper urinary tract stones and as an alternative treatment for > 3 cm upper urinary tract stones in special circumstances. In this study, patients in the FANs group had lower postoperative pain, less Hb decline, shorter postoperative recovery time, and lower complication occurrence, which is consistent with previous studies [21, 22].

The emergence of su-FURS and UAS has greatly contributed to the advancement of urologic surgery, but there is also a headache-stone residuals. The presence of residual stones after FURL has a close correlation with upper urinary tract stone recurrence [23], so it is important to further improve the rate of immediate surgical stone clearance. Conventional UAS for FURL uses a socketed basket to grasp the stone, which is prone to stone retention, leading to recurrence, obstruction, and even stone street formation. FANs enhance SFR by suctioning out all the gravel through negative pressure. In this study, the immediate postoperative SFR of the two groups was 85% and 80%, respectively, and the SFR reached 93% and 90% at 30 d postoperatively, which indicated that both groups had significant efficacy in the treatment of complex renal calculi ranging from 2.5 to 3.7 cm, which was consistent with the findings of Bai et al. [24]and Chen et al. [25].

However, su-fURS combined with FANs is usually difficult to powder in the face of high CT values and high density stones, and the process of fragmented stone removal is similar to PCNL, with a significantly longer operative time. Therefore, PCNL is still advantageous for large-loaded and dense stones [26]. Meanwhile, patients with severe hydronephrosis are generally not preferentially recommended for FURL treatment due to the large renal pelvic space and inefficient stone clearance. The difference in preoperative creatinine between the two groups in this study was statistically significant, and we analyzed the possible reasons for this: when patients are faced with a more severe renal impairment, they prefer to remove stones with a single PCNL rather than FURL, which may carry the risk of secondary lithotripsy, despite the greater trauma.

It is worth noting that the anatomy and location of the infrarenal calyx is relatively complex, and the small IPA restricts access to the su-fURS and UAS, resulting in a situation where stones are found but the laser fiber is unable to encounter them. Meanwhile, when PCNL faces LPS parallel to the plane of puncture, additional puncture is usually required to complete lithotripsy, which adds additional trauma. In this study, we took advantage of the running position by constantly adjusting the amplitude of the running position and tapping the kidney to change the orientation. Alternatively, the sheath was placed at the neck of the lower calyx of the kidney to increase the perfusion and flush the stone out to accomplish the removal of the LPS. We found by review that most of these LPS that met IPA < 45° were a small stone in a multiple stone, and therefore we accomplished stone removal by taking advantage of the running position, which is consistent with recent findings [27]. Nevertheless, we believe that the use of su-fURS in combination with FANs for the treatment of LPS is still very problematic. More clinical evidence is needed to support the above conclusions.

Limitations

Our study was a single-center retrospective study that lacked the randomized subgroups characteristic of prospective studies and had a relatively small sample size, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Therefore, larger and more detailed studies are needed to confirm these findings.

Conclusions

The results of this study showed that the use of su-fURS combined with FANs in the running position for the treatment of kidney stones > 2.5 cm have a high SFR. compared with PCNL, the latter is less invasive, simpler to operate, and faster to recover. In conclusion, this new suction ureteral access sheath is a promising tool in urologic stone surgery. It makes stone surgery safer and more efficient. It is worth promoting in clinical practice.
