Ketamine Dosing Across Clinical Indications: A Narrative Review Organized by Proposed NMDA‐Related Mechanisms
Authors: Paul Glue, Ben Beaglehole
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractKetamine is a non‐competitive antagonist at the N‐methyl‐D‐aspartate (NMDA) glutamate receptor. It was approved by the US Food and Drug Administration for anesthesia in 1970. The only other related US regulatory approval since then has been for esketamine nasal spray for depression in 2019. Over this time ketamine has been repurposed by mainly academic researchers, reporting clinical activity in diverse disorders such as status epilepticus, acute asthma, hyperactive delirium, and treatment resistant depression and anxiety. Importantly, dosing and length of treatment varies considerably by indication. Clinical indications responsive to ketamine fall into three categories: those where glutamatergic tone is normal and NMDA antagonism produces a desired effect (e.g., single bolus dosing for anesthesia, procedural sedation, and acute pain treatment); disorders where there is overactivity in glutamatergic tone and NMDA antagonism is therapeutic (e.g., prolonged infusion dosing for status epilepticus or status asthmaticus); and disorders where NMDA antagonism can enhance neuroplasticity (e.g., intermittent bolus treatment for depression/anxiety). Ketamine doses and duration of dosing differ for each of these three categories. This categorization may help generate hypotheses regarding dosing strategies for other potential novel indications such as eclampsia, anorexia nervosa, or functional neurological disorders.

Introduction

Ketamine was first synthesized in 1962 as an anesthetic with less prominent emergence delirium than its precursor, phencyclidine. 1 Compared with other anesthetics, it had several unique clinical characteristics, including a lack of effect on respiration, a modest pressor effect, and dissociative qualities. It was approved as an anesthetic by US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 1970. In parallel with its approval, several research groups were reporting that it also had analgesic properties, but at lower doses/plasma concentrations than those associated with anesthesia. 2 This was the first of many publications on its off‐label benefits for a broad range of medical conditions.

For a 56‐year‐old drug, there is still considerable research activity for ketamine and its enantiomer esketamine. A review of clinical trial activity in research studies listed on clinicaltrials.gov between 2014 and 2024 showed 363 studies. 3 Approximately 22% were for anesthesia indications, 28% were for pain management (both for ketamine), and 29% were for treatment‐resistant depression (TRD; mainly for esketamine). The way that ketamine is used in these disorders is extremely heterogeneous, in terms of doses administered, frequency of dosing, and duration of dosing. We hypothesized that some of this heterogeneity could be due to differences in glutamatergic tone. Additionally, if there are obvious clusters of disorders, dosing information from these clusters could be used to predict ketamine dosing for novel indications.

Ketamine has complex pharmacology which may partially explain the broad range of medical conditions in which it shows activity. Ketamine produces its immediate effects through non‐competitive antagonism of a subtype of glutamate receptor, the N‐methyl‐D‐aspartate (NMDA) receptor. Ketamine exists as two enantiomers, esketamine and arketamine. Esketamine has four times greater affinity for the NMDA receptor than arketamine 4 and is approximately three times more potent as an analgesic and 1.5 times more potent as an anesthetic compared with arketamine. In addition, both enantiomers interact with multiple other pharmacological targets, including mu‐opioid, dopamine, serotonin and sigma receptors, dopamine and serotonin transporters, and ion channels such as voltage‐gated sodium channels. 5 Ketamine also has potent immunosuppressive activity, reducing production of TNF‐α, interleukins, and nitric oxide. 6 Both ketamine enantiomers have active metabolites, norketamine and hydroxynorketamine, that produce rapid synaptic and antidepressant behavioral activity due to activity‐dependent release of brain derived neurotropic factor (BDNF). 7 , 8

The purpose of this narrative review is to categorize ketamine‐responsive medical indications based on glutamatergic tone, acknowledging that other pharmacological mechanisms may also be important for its therapeutic effects.

Methods

This narrative review aims to synthesize knowledge on the range of ketamine‐responsive disorders and draw links with glutamatergic tone. A search strategy was performed to identify all relevant English language articles in the Ovid and EMBASE databases (non‐English articles were excluded because of language limitations). The search dates were from 1970 (the year that ketamine received FDA approval for anesthesia) to January 2026 for non‐psychiatric medical disorders, and for psychiatric disorders, the search dates were from 2000 (the year ketamine's activity in treatment resistant depression was first reported) to January 2026. The authors used the Scale for the Assessment of Narrative Review Articles (SANRA) for reporting articles included in this review. 9 Preference was given to systematic reviews, however where these were not available, narrative reviews or clinical trial reports were also included. Articles were included if they involved adult participants (≥18 years), and reported on acute, subacute, and/or chronic changes in clinical endpoints. The search did not include gray literature, conference abstracts, dissertations, or books. The search strategy used a combination of keywords including “ketamine” AND “anesthesia” OR “acute pain” OR “procedural sedation” OR “chronic pain” OR “status epilepticus” OR “traumatic brain injury” OR “status asthmaticus” OR “depression” OR “generalized anxiety disorder” OR “social anxiety disorder” OR “post‐traumatic stress disorder” OR “obsessive compulsive disorder.” Reference lists of selected articles were also hand‐searched for other relevant citations. The following variables were extracted from each included study: (1) medical indication; (2) ketamine dose, (3) dose frequency, and (4) duration of dosing. Ketamine can be administered via multiple routes, so we selected only IV dose ranges (dose ranges appear to differ by route of administration). Given the heterogeneity of study designs and large number of medical indications identified, a narrative review was felt to be most appropriate for this article. Risk of bias was not formally assessed, because the purpose of this review was an exploration of ketamine's activity across multiple medical disorders, rather than a formal evaluation of the quality of studies.

Results

Initial searches identified 2749 studies after de‐duplication of 158 studies from the two databases. After review of the titles and abstracts of identified articles, 2651 article were excluded, resulting in 198 articles that were selected for a detailed full‐text examination (Figure 1). Medical Indications with Normal Glutamatergic Tone

Anesthesia

Ketamine is approved for use as a general anesthetic either on its own or in combination with other medications. It can also be used as a pre‐anesthetic for the induction of general anesthesia when combined with other general anesthetic agents. It is highly effective for brief medical procedures that do not require skeletal muscle relaxation. 10 , 11 Ketamine is an alternative to the simultaneous use of an induction agent and paralytic for endotracheal intubation. 12 Its use has diminished due to side effects such as delirium, dysphoria, and emergence hallucinations. 13 Ketamine is FDA approved for enhancing the effects of low‐potency agents such as nitrous oxide. Ketamine induction is particularly useful for patients with bronchospasm because of its bronchodilatory properties. It maintains normal pharyngeal and laryngeal reflexes and permits spontaneous respiration. It enhances normal skeletal muscle tone and is associated with cardiovascular and respiratory stimulation. The initial IV ketamine dosage is within the range of 0.5–2 mg/kg, and this can be maintained by repeat dosing, or by the addition of benzodiazepines or opioids. 11 , 14

Acute Pain/Procedural Sedation

A consensus statement from US pain specialists and anesthetists recommended ketamine as the drug of choice to treat acute pain refractory to standard analgesic regimens or in patients with opioid dependence. 16 Meta‐analyses of studies comparing ketamine with morphine for acute pain in the Emergency Department reported generally comparable analgesic effects. 17 , 18 , 19 Ketamine 0.2–2 mg/kg IV can rapidly produce sedation and analgesia in children and adults for brief painful procedures in the Emergency Department 20 (Table 2), with excellent tolerability. 21 Combining ketamine with propofol 22 or benzodiazepines 23 can reduce the number or severity of emergence symptoms. Many ketamine protocols describe IV dosing however ketamine can be dosed via multiple routes of administration 24 including via nebulizer. 25 , 26

Medical Indications Associated with NMDA Overactivity

The pathophysiology of this group of indications is diverse, however a common factor is increased NMDA activity in the nervous system or peripheral tissues. In status epilepticus, NMDA receptor activation promotes internalization of GABA‐A receptors in brain tissue, particularly those containing benzodiazepine‐sensitive γ2 subunits, whereas benzodiazepine‐insensitive δ subunit‐containing receptors are preserved. 27 , 28 , 29 In contrast, altered sensitivity does not appear to occur to NMDA antagonists. 29 In status asthmaticus, there is hyperreactivity of NMDA receptors in various airway tissues, contributing to airway constriction, which may be in response to inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor. 30 , 31 In chronic neuropathic pain, augmented pre‐ and post‐synaptic NMDA activity in the spinal cord dorsal horn amplifies nociceptive input from primary sensory neurons to produce central sensitization. 32 , 33 In traumatic brain injury (TBI), there is unregulated release of glutamate from neurons, which can produce neurotoxicity and cytotoxic edema, 34 and elevated glutamate levels can persist in cerebrospinal fluid for up to 1 week. 35

Chronic Pain

Ketamine has been used for decades to treat chronic pain, including cancer pain, 36 neuropathic pain, 37 and complex regional pain syndrome 38 (CRPS). Ketamine infusions appear to be effective for patients with CRPS, however there are mixed results for cancer pain, chronic post‐surgical pain, 39 and chronic neuropathic pain. 40 For patients with acute aggravation of chronic neuropathic pain, who may have hyperalgesia from chronic use of opioids, ketamine may reduce hyperalgesia and may also reduce opioid tolerance. 41 Ketamine dosing in CPRS often involves IV infusions, with doses between 0.35 and 7 mg/kg/h, given for up to 10 days 42 (Table 2).

Neurological Disorders

Status epilepticus (SE) is a neurological emergency characterized by prolonged seizures that are unresponsive to antiepileptic drug treatment. Extreme nonresponse to antiepileptic drugs (refractory SE or RSE) is associated with mortality rates of 20%–40%. 43 Treatment algorithms for RSE typically include ketamine as a later rather than an earlier treatment option. 44 , 45 The first report of ketamine being used to treat status epilepticus which was nonresponsive to benzodiazepines was in 1998. 46 The use of ketamine has subsequently become much more common. 47 A retrospective case series analysis from US and EU centers reported that ketamine infusions achieved permanent control of RSE in 57% of cases. Worse outcomes occurred if ketamine was started 8 or more days after RSE onset, if there was failure to respond to seven or more prior medications, or it was administered at infusion rates below 0.9 mg/kg/h. 48 Another case series reported that 81% of patients (55/68) had a 50% or greater reduction in seizure burden, with complete cessation of seizures in 43 patients (63%). 49 The mean ketamine infusion dose in the latter case series was 2.2 mg/kg, although reported dose ranges can be as high as 10 mg/kg/h. 48 Although the published literature appears promising, it only includes case reports and case series, and no randomized controlled studies have been published.

Traumatic Brain Injury and Intracranial Hypertension

Severe TBI is highly prevalent, with 5.48 million cases annually, 50 and these account for over half of all trauma‐related deaths. Episodes of hypotension along with raised intracranial pressure (ICP) are linked to increased mortality and worse outcomes in patients with severe TBI. In the acute phase of TBI, ketamine may have an important role in providing sedation and analgesia. A systematic review reported that use of ketamine in patients with acute brain injury did not worsen ICP or increase mortality. 51 Doses used in published clinical trials range between 1 and 5 mg/kg/h. 52

Respiratory Disorders

Status Asthmaticus

The use of ketamine in asthma is limited to severe cases where there has been no response to beta‐agonist therapy, or subcutaneous epinephrine. Ketamine's mechanisms of action in this disorder are complex: as well as antagonism of NMDA receptors in lungs and airways, it has anti‐inflammatory effects (e.g., reducing IL‐4 production and reducing nitric oxide synthetase activity), and promotes smooth muscle relaxation via reducing norepinephrine reuptake into presynaptic sympathetic neurons and decreasing L‐type calcium channel activity. 53 Ketamine appears to improve peak expiratory flow rate 54 , 55 and may lead to lower rates of mechanical ventilation. 54 Most published studies have dosed ketamine as an IV bolus of up to 2 mg/kg, with a continuous infusion of up to 2 mg/kg/h subsequently, however there are some reports where ketamine has been administered via nebulizer. 54

Indications with Impaired Neuroplasticity

Psychiatric Disorders

Overview

Probably the greatest expansion of recent new indications for ketamine has been in psychiatry. Ketamine responsiveness is one component of a glutamatergic theory of depression 56 and in other internalizing disorders. 57 In human and preclinical models of depression, there are patterns of functional dysconnectivity associated with altered brain levels of glutamate and GABA dysregulation across a number of brain areas, rather than a simple increase or decrease in glutamate levels. 58

Ketamine shows broad activity across a range of mental disorders, including major depressive disorder, generalized and social anxiety disorders, post‐traumatic stress disorder, and obsessive compulsive disorder. Patients respond to similar parenteral doses (0.5–1 mg/kg), they experience similar durations of symptom relief (3–7 days), and there are similar proportions of treatment responders across all diagnostic groups. These diagnoses fall within a cluster of internalizing disorders 59 and are associated with high trait neuroticism. McNaughton and Glue 60 proposed that the similar effects of ketamine on these disorders might be due to switching off a brain network linked to trait neuroticism.

Depression

The initial report 25 years ago that IV infusions of ketamine 0.5 mg/kg produced a rapid onset antidepressant effect in patients with TRD 61 has led to an enormous increase in research activity in this indication. Most published studies in this area have been academic and used racemic ketamine. 62 Janssen Pharmaceuticals completed a development program and gained FDA and EMA approval for an esketamine nasal spray formulation (Spravato) in 2019. 63 The initial esketamine approval was to also start a new monoaminergic antidepressant at the same time as Spravato, however a recent study where Spravato was started without an antidepressant showed more robust antidepressant activity compared with a control group. 64

A network meta‐analysis of 25 treatments for TRD identified 6 treatments, including ketamine and ECT, which showed higher response rates than placebo. 65 ECT's odds ratio and associated 95% confidence intervals for treatment response (12.86 (4.07, 40.63)) were greater than those reported for ketamine (3.4 (2.14, 5.41)). However a recent large randomized controlled trial reported that ketamine was non‐inferior to ECT in terms of efficacy and had a more favorable safety and tolerability profile due to ECT's cognitive side effects. 66 The ECT data from Saelens’ meta‐analysis 65 were dated and might exaggerate its efficacy relative to other treatments. Additional clinical trial data are needed to clarify this point.

A recent systematic review and meta‐analysis of 49 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) of racemic ketamine and esketamine in patients with TRD included an analysis of the effect of dose on ratings of depression severity. 62 Dose was categorized as a binary factor of “high” if given at an intravenous equivalent of ≥0.5 mg/kg (or option of titrating up to this level) or “low” if <0.5 mg/kg. Greater effect sizes were noted for high dose levels compared with low doses in single dose data analyses; there were insufficient data for an analysis of repeat dosing treatment arms.

Most studies in TRD administered ketamine via IV infusion, injection, and for esketamine, by nasal spray. Patients experience strong side effects, including dissociation, increased blood pressure and sedation, which necessitate dosing in clinic under medical/nursing supervision. A meta‐analysis of 41 treatment arms from studies which reported the pharmacokinetics, safety, and tolerability of different ketamine formulations identified that formulations that maximized first pass metabolism and delayed time of maximum concentration had minimal dissociation or blood pressure changes. 67 A subsequent Phase 2 trial of an extended‐release ketamine tablet that had these pharmacokinetic characteristics confirmed the improved tolerability of this formulation, allowing most doses to be taken at home. 68

Generalized Anxiety Disorder/Social Anxiety Disorder

There have been two single ascending dose studies in patients with comorbid GAD/SAD, one single dose placebo‐controlled crossover study in patients with SAD, and a 3‐month maintenance treatment trial in patients with comorbid GAD/SAD. 69 , 70 , 71 , 72 Single ketamine doses of 0.5–1 mg/kg produced greater reductions in anxiety ratings compared with a 0.25 mg/kg dose. 69 , 70 Improvements in anxiety ratings could be sustained with once or twice weekly maintenance dosing, and after 3 months of treatment, 25% of patients remained in remission post‐study. 71

Post‐Traumatic Stress Disorder

There have been six studies examining the effects of single and repeat dose racemic ketamine on PTSD symptoms. Five of these were positive 73 , 74 , 75 , 76 , 77 ; the one exception was a large (n = 158) repeat dose comparison of infusions of ketamine 0.2 or 0.5 mg/kg, or saline placebo, which did not show any difference in PTSD endpoints between any of the dose groups. 78

Obsessive Compulsive Disorder

There have been two placebo‐controlled randomized crossover trials in patients with OCD: a two‐arm study 79 and a three‐arm study. 80 The two‐arm study had a strong carry‐over effect, in that the OCD symptoms of patients who received ketamine in the first dosing phase remained improved by the time they received their second (placebo) dose. A comparison of treatment responders during the first dosing phase showed a statistically significant difference in favor of ketamine treatment. 79 The three‐arm study showed that both ketamine doses (0.5 and 1 mg/kg) produced greater improvement in OCD symptom ratings, however there were no dose‐related differences. 80

Predictions for Ketamine Treatment of Novel Indications

The categorization of ketamine‐responsive medical indications based on underlying NMDA activity may help predict how to dose ketamine for a range of novel disorders. Two examples that appear to reflect increased glutamatergic activity are eclampsia and severe alcohol withdrawal. Eclampsia is a pregnancy‐related disorder characterized by hypertension, proteinuria, and seizures. Excessive activity of the pro‐inflammatory cytokine TNF‐α potentiates NMDA activity and may be one factor in the development of seizures. 81 An early small case series reported resolution of seizures in two women with eclampsia after IV infusions of 150—250 mg of ketamine. 82

One component of the pathophysiology of alcohol withdrawal is due to reduced central nervous system inhibitory tone, along with increased excitatory tone, including NMDA. 83 Mild to moderate alcohol withdrawal can be managed with benzodiazepines 84 however for patients with symptoms of severe alcohol withdrawal, where benzodiazepines may be ineffective, adding an IV infusion of ketamine appears to be highly effective. Three case series reported that after starting a ketamine infusion, the frequency of seizures and hallucinations, and the severity of alcohol withdrawal symptoms, improved. 85 , 86 , 87 After use of ketamine, use of benzodiazepines decreased, and patients had shorter stays in intensive care and in hospital. Based on our observations of other disorders with increased NMDA activity, we would predict both of these disorders would require prolonged infusions of relatively high dose ketamine.

Other examples that are likely due to impaired neuroplasticity include anorexia nervosa 88 and functional neurological disorders 89 , 90 , 91 which are linked to high levels of trait neuroticism, 92 , 93 and which have few effective pharmacotherapies. Based on our understanding of ketamine dosing for other disorders with impaired neuroplasticity, we predict that these disorders would need repeat dosing with relatively low dose ketamine over several months.

Discussion

Over the past 55 years, ketamine has been extensively repurposed, mainly in small studies designed and funded by academic researchers. Interest in ketamine has not diminished over time: in the last decade, there were over several hundred research studies involving ketamine or esketamine reported on clinicaltrial.gov. 3 Many of the indications include those where NMDA antagonism will produce a desired therapeutic effect (e.g., anesthesia and acute pain), or for disorders where there is NMDA overactivity or loss of GABAergic inhibitory control (e.g., status asthmaticus, status epilepticus, and severe alcohol withdrawal). In other disorders associated with impaired neuroplasticity and regional NMDA changes, such as depression and anxiety, ketamine metabolites such as norketamine and hydronorketamine can increase transcription of BDNF, 56 , 57 an important mediator of neuroplasticity. In addition, ketamine's interactions with non‐glutamatergic targets, including immunological targets, are also important.

As shown in Table 2, ketamine dosing and duration of dosing varies considerably for the above indications. IV bolus dosing for a relatively brief duration is required for acute pain, procedural sedation, and anesthesia, respectively. For status asthmaticus, status epilepticus, TBI, and some chronic pain syndromes, moderate to high dose IV infusions are required over several days up to several weeks. Treatment of depression and anxiety require low IV doses given several times a week over several months to promote neuroplasticity, although other routes of administration (intramuscular/intranasal/oral) are also likely to be effective. This categorization provides guidance on how to dose ketamine for novel indications, based in glutamatergic tone.

The limitations in the datasets reviewed in this article must be acknowledged. It is clear that ketamine's clinical activity cannot be attributed solely to NMDA antagonism, as it affects other pharmacological targets and the immune system, as mentioned in the Introduction. There is robust published evidence for anesthesia, acute pain, procedural sedation, and depression. For most of the other indications, evidence is more limited, study sizes are small, there is a predominance of case series rather than RCTs, and design differences between studies often make it difficult to combine studies via formal meta‐analysis. Given that research into most of the off‐label indications is from academic groups, who may have limited funding, the predominance of smaller studies is not surprising.

In conclusion, since its initial regulatory approval, ketamine has been extensively repurposed for a wide range of indications. As an older drug that has no patent coverage, there is minimal commercial rationale to seek regulatory approval for any of the currently off‐label indications. Despite this, it is likely that the range of off‐label indications for ketamine will expand in the future, in the search for novel treatments for different disorders. We may now be able to predict how to dose ketamine, and for how long, in such novel indications.
