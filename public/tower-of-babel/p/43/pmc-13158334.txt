Barriers and facilitators to, and knowledge of, cord blood banking among pregnant Hispanic women at a university obstetric clinic
Authors: Martha Rojo, Laura Mayfield, Jing Jin, Soumya Pandey, Anh Pham, Michele Cottler‐Fox
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGenetic diversity in cord blood (CB) banks is essential for minorities to find a matching graft, yet Hispanics, the largest growing United States (U.S.) minority, remain underrepresented in registries. This study aimed to describe knowledge and awareness of CB banking among Spanish‐only speaking women in their third trimester, to identify barriers and facilitators, and to compare these findings to a similar study of English‐speaking women at the same university obstetric clinic.Study Design and MethodsWe recruited women between August 2023 and April 2024 from the Obstetrics clinic. Participants completed a 29‐item Spanish‐language survey assessing demographics, cord blood knowledge, awareness, and perceived barriers/facilitators identical to that used in our study of English‐speaking women. Descriptive and inferential statistics were used to analyze responses using SAS software.ResultsOnly 31.4% had heard of CB banking, and 70% did not understand the purpose. Reported barriers included lack of knowledge (91.3%), distrust of the process (63%), and fear about donating (20%). Participants wanted information on CB collection during prenatal classes such as the types of banking, accessible website resources, and family or friend support as facilitators. Awareness was higher among those with greater education, income, or a family history of cancer (p < .05).DiscussionSpanish‐only speaking women had less knowledge than the English‐speaking women at the same clinic (92% vs. 66%). They differed in preferred information sources, favoring written or online materials while English‐speaking women preferred discussions with a trusted MD or RN, reflecting communication barriers since most clinic staff did not speak Spanish.

INTRODUCTION

Cord blood (CB) banking is the process of collecting and storing blood from the umbilical cord after a child is delivered 1 and has been available in the United States since 1992. CB is rich in hematopoietic progenitor cells which serve as a graft for blood and marrow transplant. Despite its clinical value, CB banking rates remain low at 3% nationally, and less than 1% in Arkansas. 2 , 3 Additionally, CB donation rates vary by ethnicity, with African Americans (AA) and Hispanics showing significantly lower levels of participation. 4 , 5 , 6 For successful transplantation, a close genetic match is best, and such matches are more likely to be found within the same racial or ethnic group. The U.S. Health Resources and Services Administration 7 has emphasized the urgent need to increase CB donations from racially and ethnically diverse groups.

The question of race and ethnicity is complex, and self‐reported race, ethnicity, and geographic ancestry do not always align with genetic ancestry. 8 Hispanic individuals are the largest minority population in the United States, 9 and often have admixed ancestry, including Indigenous, European, and African heritage, which can make it more challenging to find compatible donors within current CB inventories. CB banking is especially important in Hispanic communities because of this unique genetic diversity within the population. 10 , 11 Expanding CB donations from Hispanic families would enhance the genetic diversity of CB banks and improve the likelihood of finding suitable transplant matches for these patients.

In a previous study conducted at the same university obstetrics clinic, we examined the barriers and facilitators to CB banking among English‐speaking women. 3 The purpose of the current study was to compare those earlier findings with the experiences of pregnant, Spanish‐only speaking Hispanic women, focusing on their knowledge of CB banking and likely unique barriers and facilitators that may influence their participation. We selected Spanish‐only speaking patients because they represent a rapidly growing population in Arkansas and face persistent barriers to accessing health information. Individuals with limited English proficiency in general may have lower awareness of cord blood banking due to lack of access to health information in other languages, with a recent study 12 suggesting that the issue of inaccessibility to medical portals and information for patients with limited English proficiency is a nationwide issue. Spanish‐only speaking patients are vulnerable because they rely exclusively on Spanish‐language medical materials, which are limited, inconsistently available, or not culturally tailored. Focusing on this group addresses a critical gap and may allow us to develop linguistically appropriate strategies to support informed decision‐making.

STUDY DESIGN AND METHODS

This study aimed to explore Hispanic, Spanish‐only speaking pregnant women's knowledge and awareness of CB collection and banking, and to identify barriers and facilitators associated with CB banking. All study materials and procedures were approved by the University of Arkansas for Medical Sciences Institutional Review Board (IRB# 261549) and supported by funding from the Windgate Foundation.

Participants were recruited between August 2023 and April 2024 at the University of Arkansas for Medical Sciences (UAMS) Obstetrics (OB) clinic. The clinic sees approximately 400 obstetric patients per day, of whom 40% identify as Hispanic/Latino. Eligible participants were women at least 18 years‐of‐age, in their third trimester of pregnancy (28–40 weeks gestation), who self‐identified as Hispanic (from Mexico, Central America, South America, or Puerto Rico), and exclusively spoke and read Spanish. Women were excluded if they had visual impairments affecting their ability to read or were unable to make independent decisions.

All participants were recruited from the UAMS OB clinic waiting room while waiting for their scheduled appointment. The clinic front desk staff signed patients in through EPIC Electronic Health Record (EHR). If the EHR indicated that a patient identified as Hispanic and was in her third trimester, bilingual research staff approached potential participants, explained the purpose of the study, assessed eligibility, and answered questions. Informed consent was obtained, and those who agreed to participate could access the survey via a QR code on their own device or on an iPad provided by the research team. Most participants opted not to access the survey through a QR code but preferred a paper version. Consent and survey responses were collected using Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap), a widely used, secure, web‐based application designed to manage online surveys. 13 The survey used branching logic to adapt questions to each participant and took approximately 10 min to complete.

Survey instrument

The investigators developed the 29‐item survey to assess sociodemographic characteristics, knowledge and awareness of CB banking, and perceived barriers and facilitators to participation in CB banking. See Table 1 for sample questions of the survey. The original English‐language survey was translated into Spanish by a certified translator and subsequently reviewed by the institution's health literacy department, which confirmed the survey at an eighth grade reading level. Survey questions were created after a comprehensive literature review of CB banking studies published in the past decade.

The survey included 11 demographic items, such as age, race, ethnicity, household income, educational attainment, marital status, health insurance status, family history of cancer, and number of children in the household. In addition, it also included two historical obstetric items: number of pregnancies and mode of delivery (such as C‐section or normal delivery). No personal identifying information was collected. The remaining survey items explored the participants' knowledge and awareness of CB banking, as well as barriers and facilitators that could influence their decision to donate.

Statistics

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize and analyze the demographic characteristics of the participants. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. The chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test were used to assess the associations between demographics, and whether the participants had heard of cord blood banking. A two‐tailed p‐value of <.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 software. 14

Knowledge and awareness of CB banking

Three survey questions were used to evaluate women's knowledge and awareness of CB banking. The participants were asked, “Have you ever heard of CB before this survey?,” “How well do you feel you understand the purpose of cord blood collection?,” and “Has anyone discussed CB banking with you during this pregnancy?” If a participant reported prior awareness of CB banking, then branching logic asked, “Where did you first hear about cord blood collection and storage?” The response options included: “Internet,” “OB/GYN provider,” “OB/GYN staff,” “TV,” “This survey,” and “Other.” The participants who reported they had discussed CB banking during this pregnancy were also asked which trimester this had occurred. Overall, 68.6% of the participants had not heard of CB banking before this survey, while 31.4% reported prior awareness. Among the women who had heard of CB banking, the most common source of information was from a friend or family member (54%), followed by the internet (28.6%) and had heard during their OB/GYN appointment (17%). The next question asked whether anyone had talked to them about collecting their baby's cord blood during their current pregnancy; 94.8% reported “no.” The final question asked how well the participant understood the purpose of collecting their baby's CB; 70% did not understand the purpose of CB banking, and 25.9% knew only a little about this concept.

Tables 1 and 2 present demographic characteristics and comparisons of CB awareness across subgroups. CB awareness was significantly associated with higher educational attainment, higher household income, and a family history of cancer (p < .05).

Barriers and facilitators

To explore barriers and facilitators associated with CB banking, participants were asked two questions allowing for multiple response selections and free‐text responses. The questions were, “What things might keep you from collecting your baby's CB?” and “What would help you decide to collect your baby's CB?”

As shown in Figure 1, the most frequently reported barrier was lack of knowledge regarding CB banking (92.3%), followed by not knowing anyone who has donated CB (52.6%), and concerns about completing the paperwork or receiving help to complete the paperwork (42.3%). Among the 120 participants who did not want to donate CB, 63.3% expressed distrust in the process and procedures, while 20.8% were concerned about the risks of donating. Verbally, most of the women stated that language was a major barrier to participating in CB banking. They further stated that their mode of communication with their health provider or clinic staff was through digital devices or clinic interpreters.

The factors which would help the participant to make a decision about CB banking were (Figure 2) providing information about the process (88%) and its benefits (80%), information on different CB banking options (69.8%), the ability to learn about CB banking in prenatal classes (27.6%), improved accessibility to the CB bank website (25%), and support from partner, family members, and friends (19.9%).

How participants would like to receive information

Figure 3 illustrates participants' preferences for receiving information about CB banking. Over half of the participants (51.9%) requested written material information (such as a brochure or a fact sheet), 36.5% wanted a short informational video (approximately five minutes in length), while 25% wanted information from their healthcare provider. When asked who they would prefer to provide them with information about collecting CB, 67.3% preferred to receive the information from the healthcare provider or OB clinic staff, and 31.4% preferred cord blood bank staff. These findings underscore the importance of offering multiple educational modalities tailored to participants' language and literacy needs and highlight the central role of prenatal healthcare providers in delivering trusted health information.

DISCUSSION

Over the last two decades, despite the clinical importance of CB banking, few studies have focused on minority group participation. An integrative review by Peberdy et al. 15 revealed significant gaps in general parental knowledge and awareness of CB banking, but of the five U.S.‐based studies, 4 , 6 , 16 , 17 , 18 only one explored knowledge, attitudes, beliefs, and practices of Hispanic and non‐Hispanic black women. 6 No similar studies have been reported since.

In this study we use the term Hispanic to describe the population served by our clinic. We recognize the important cultural and semantic distinctions between Hispanic and Latino identities. However, our terminology reflects the clinic's existing EHR categories and the predominant self‐identification of the population, which is largely Spanish‐speaking and of heritage from Spanish speaking countries, consistent with the definition of Hispanic used in U.S. public health and clinical settings. 19 Nevertheless, we understand that these labels do not capture the full complexity of identity within Latin American communities.

Hispanic populations exhibit unique challenges in CB banking due to their significant genetic diversity, which stems from their complex history of admixture of Amerindigenous, European, and African ancestries. 11 The diverse ancestral backgrounds of today's Hispanic populations result in unique Human Leukocyte Antigen (HLA) haplotypes that are less represented in donor registries, reducing match probabilities compared to individuals of more genetically homogenous backgrounds. 20 These factors reiterate the importance of increasing Hispanic representation in donor registries but based on National Marrow Donor Program (NMDP) data, 21 only 13% of registry members identify as Hispanic or Latino.

Our clinic provides CB banking educational materials in both Spanish and English, which are readily available in the waiting area for patients to review independently. In addition, we offer a QR code that patients can scan to access reliable, easy‐to‐understand information about cord blood banking on their mobile devices. While medical translators are available in the clinic, their support is primarily reserved for OB‐related clinical discussions, and they are not consistently available for supplemental educational topics such as CB banking. Most study participants were unaware of CB banking, its purpose, or the available donation options. Only 31.4% had heard of CB banking prior to the survey. These findings align with previous research showing that women of childbearing age generally lack awareness about CB banking. 6 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 22 , 23 A previous study conducted in the same clinic with English‐speaking women 3 found significantly higher awareness (58.9%). A key difference is that the Mayfield et al. 3 sample was more diverse, as it included AA, Caucasians, and a small number of English‐speaking Hispanic participants. Notably, the last study to specifically examine CB banking awareness among Hispanics and AA women was published in 2010, and it reported similar findings. This consistency across studies highlights a persistent gap in knowledge and awareness within these populations. Outreach efforts in culturally relevant community settings such as churches, nail salons, and local events 24 have been used successfully to raise awareness of other health topics and may offer a promising approach for CB education and recruitment.

Like other studies, 3 , 6 , 15 , 17 , 22 , 23 our research found the primary barrier to CB banking among Spanish‐only speaking Hispanic women was a lack of information. In our study, 92.3% of participants identified limited knowledge as a major barrier, compared to 66.4% in the study by Mayfield et al. 3 whose sample was predominately Caucasian and English speaking. In this study and the Mayfield study, women identified “I don't know enough about it” as a primary barrier to CB banking. This shared finding suggests that educational needs extend across the entire clinic population, not only among Spanish‐only speaking women. Strengthening universal clinic wide education efforts may benefit all patients. Most participants in our study expressed uncertainty about the purpose, benefits, and process of CB banking, which prevented them from seriously considering it as an option. However, many expressed a desire to learn more about CB collection and storage. This is also consistent with Sugarman et al., 18 where participants similarly cited insufficient information as the primary barrier to CB banking. In addition to informational gaps, some participants in our study reported other deterrents, such as not knowing anyone who had previously donated CB, and concerns about their ability to complete the required forms for collection and storage. Notably, 12.8% of participants in our sample reported that the forms were too difficult to complete, compared to 1.7% in the Mayfield et al. 3 study. In our sample, 23.7% of participants had less than a high school education and 50% had completed high school, for a combined total of 73.7%. In contrast, Mayfield et al. reported that 34.6% of their sample fell into the combined categories of less than high school or high school education. This indicates that participants in our study had a substantially lower overall level of educational attainment. Although there is a clear difference in education levels between the two samples, it seems unlikely that education alone explains the observed difference. Another difference between this study and the Mayfield et al. study 3 is the selection of the barrier “There are too many forms to fill out” by 10.26% of Hispanic Spanish‐only speaking women, while none of the English‐speaking women identified this as a barrier. This suggest that educational strategies across different cultures are needed in order to reach all populations living in the United States.

Participants in this study identified several potential facilitators to CB banking, including receiving more information about the process of collecting CB, particularly through written materials or short videos (see Figure 2). Mayfield et al. 3 also showed the need for providing women with additional information, but in that sample the participants selected information about why it is good to collect CB, the process, and the different CB banking options. The primary facilitator identified in our study, information on the process of collecting cord blood, differs from the top facilitator in Mayfield et al., where participants prioritized information about why it is good to collect your baby's cord blood. This distinction suggests that the Spanish‐only speaking population may require more practical, step‐by‐step guidance, whereas Mayfield's sample emphasized wanting to understand the rationale and benefits. These differences highlight the importance of tailoring educational material to address both procedural clarity and underlying purpose. Process‐focused information may include infographics and videos, while rationale‐focused materials should emphasize benefits, uses, and potential outcomes of cord blood banking. Further, another difference is that the participants in Mayfield et al. 3 preferred to receive CB banking information by talking with their healthcare provider rather than written materials or short videos. It is possible that the women in our study were unable to communicate effectively with their healthcare providers due to their lack of English proficiency, making Spanish‐language written materials or videos preferable to verbal communication with the non‐Spanish speaking healthcare provider. 25 , 26 Resources in Spanish allow for at‐home review and make them more accessible. Another notable difference between the two studies is that 27.6% of participants in our sample expressed a desire to learn about CB banking during prenatal classes, compared to only 9.7% in the Mayfield et al. 3 study. Consistent with other studies, healthcare professionals were viewed as credible and influential sources of health information. 6 , 15 , 22 , 23 This highlights an important opportunity to train providers and clinic staff on how to effectively communicate the purpose and benefits of CB banking during routine prenatal care visits and prenatal classes. Additionally, developing accessible Spanish educational videos about CB banking should be considered as a strategy for this population.

One limitation of this study is that our dataset did not include information on the country in which participants completed their education. All the individuals in our sample were born outside of the United States, so it is possible that their educational experiences were shaped by systems outside the United States. This lack of specificity limits our ability to account for potential differences in the structure, quality, and equivalency of educational backgrounds. Another limitation is the small data set. However, although this study was relatively small and limited to Spanish‐only speaking women in a rural Southern state, the findings are similar to what was seen in a previous study of English‐speaking women from the same clinic, and to studies in other populations both within and outside of the United States. Essentially, our data show there are two problems facing a cord blood bank. First, no matter their primary language, women need to have clear information about both how cord blood collection is done and why banking is important. They also need to understand the difference in donation for public use and private banking. To effectively deliver this information successfully, each cord blood bank and obstetric clinic needs to understand the cultural, linguistic, and regional characteristics of the population it serves. While our study looks at Spanish‐only speaking women, other populations will also need culturally and linquistically appropriate information.

Second, many women prefer to receive information about cord blood banking from a trusted person in their obstetrical clinic, either a physician or nurse. As physicians and nurses in clinics are often tasked with seeing many patients in a day, and consequently lack time for extended discussion of CB banking, we suggest that patients might easily be asked if and how they would like to receive information about cord blood banking while in clinic. Written information in the appropriate language or a link to a short video might then be given, or they could easily be referred to a cord blood bank coordinator for discussion.

CONCLUSIONS

This study is the first to examine knowledge and awareness of CB banking among Spanish‐only speaking pregnant Hispanic women. As we did not include a validated knowledge scale in the survey, awareness and perceived knowledge were assessed through two items: (1) the barrier question, “I do not know enough about it,” and (2) the question, “have you ever heard of cord blood before this survey?” These indicators provide insight into participants' perceived familiarity and awareness of CB banking. Our conclusion that the Spanish‐only population demonstrated lower awareness of cord blood banking is based on two self‐reported indicators rather than a formal knowledge scale. First, 92% of our participants endorsed the barrier “I do not know enough about it,” compared to 66% in the English‐fluent Mayfield sample. Second, only 31.4% of our respondents had heard of cord blood before the survey, compared with 58.9% in Mayfield et al. Taken together, these measures reflect lower perceived knowledge and awareness among our Spanish‐only speaking participants.

By focusing on this underserved population, this study addresses a critical gap in understanding awareness, barriers and facilitators influencing CB banking decisions. Without comprehensive insights into this or any other community's views, we risk failing to meet their specific needs and concerns. By addressing these barriers and focusing on the facilitators we can increase representation of a significant minority population in CB banking.
