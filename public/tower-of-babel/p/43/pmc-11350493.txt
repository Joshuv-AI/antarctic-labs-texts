Hospital-acquired respiratory viral infections while applying droplet precautions on-site (DroPS) - prospective observation during the 2019/20 influenza season, Bern, Switzerland
Authors: Michèle Birrer, Katrien Draps, Fabienne Hobi, Marianne Laguardia, Eveline Hofmann, Martin Luginbühl, Martin Perrig, Drahomir Aujesky, Jonas Marschall, Rami Sommerstein
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummarySingle room isolation for respiratory viral infections (RVI), like influenza, puts hospitals under pressure. During the influenza season 2019/20, we implemented Droplet precautions on-site (DroPS) for RVI in two acute care hospitals and prospectively assessed the rate of hospital-acquired RVI (HARVI). 318 patients were admitted with RVI, 85 had Influenza or RSV, 75 stayed in multi-bed rooms with DroPS. From 764 patients at risk, hospitalised ≥ three days in a multi-bed room, 11 (1.4%) developed a clinical HARVI, and three tested positive (3/764, 0.4%; 2x RSV, 1x influenza). DroPS may represent an alternative strategy to deal with respiratory viral infections.

Introduction

Respiratory virus infections (RVI) are a challenge for acute care hospitals. Incidence estimates for hospitalisations due to Influenza are approximately 9,459,000 cases worldwide per year, with up to 8000 cases in Switzerland [1]. A Swiss prospective multicenter study reported that about 15% of hospitalised influenza cases are nosocomial and carry a significant morbidity (complications in 10.3%, intensive care unit admission in 5–8%) and mortality (6.1%) [2]. Hospital-acquired respiratory viral infections (HARVI) other than influenza may have an underestimated impact on patient morbidity and mortality [3,4]. Moreover, HARVIs are also associated with prolonged hospital stay [4] and outbreaks in healthcare settings [5] and thus can represent a significant challenge and economic burden for health-care institutions.

The currently recommended pathogen-based isolation strategy for patients with RVI that advises single rooms for influenza or respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) [6] is problematic: 1) shortage of single hospital rooms in times of increased demand (i.e. seasonal influenza epidemic), 2) variable isolation regimens for different respiratory viruses may lead to confusion in health-care workers (HCW) (i.e. contact precautions for RSV may result in omitting a mask), 3) potential for undermining the role of standard precautions and neglect of nosocomial transmission risk from other respiratory viruses not associated with particular isolation precautions, and 4) dependence on costly diagnostic tools for any pathogen-based isolation strategy.

A pragmatic preventive approach that focuses on coping with shortage of single rooms and reducing patient risk from all respiratory viruses is Droplet precautions on-site (DroPs), a symptom-based initiation of respiratory precautions at the patient bedside, independent of the causative pathogen. DroPS measures can be started by any HCW for a patient with suspected respiratory infection and include the following: curtain drawn next to patient bed; signage of the patient bed; distribution of surgical masks and bedside disinfectant to the patient; surgical mask use for HCW and visitors when close to patient/bed site; enforcement of standard hygiene precautions. A detailed description of all DroPS measures can be found in Supplementary Table I.

Materials and methods

Setting

Prospective observational study conducted at two affiliated hospitals in Bern, Switzerland, consisting of a surveillance of selected wards where DroPS had been introduced: 1) 950-bed tertiary care university hospital including all wards of the Department of General Internal Medicine (totaling 97 beds, including nine single rooms, 26 twin rooms, six four-bed and two six-bed rooms); 2) 150-bed secondary care city hospital including all wards, except those of the Department of Geriatrics and the Intensive Care Unit (totaling 103 beds, including 23 single rooms, 31 twin rooms, two three-bed and three four-bed rooms).

The observation and data collection started 16.01.2020, when influenza cases exceeded the national epidemic threshold (69/100,000 inhabitants/week), and was prematurely stopped 17.03.2020 at the beginning of the confinement measures due to the COVID-19 pandemic in Switzerland.

Data collection

During the period of observation (16.01.2020–17.03.2020), for each patient hospitalised on a DroPS ward the following data were collected: admission date, patient identifier, case identifier, date of birth, gender, ward, room number and respiratory isolation status once daily (at 12:00 p.m.). Swab results and hospitalisation duration was retrieved from the digital patient management system.

Definitions, outcomes and diagnostics

Patients were defined as being at risk for the acquisition of HARVI if they did not require respiratory precautions (due to absence of acute respiratory symptoms) during the first two days of hospitalisation (i.e. day 0, 1 or 2) and who were hospitalised on a DroPS ward for ≥ 3 days.

A clinical HARVI was defined as acute onset of respiratory symptoms (cough, bronchitis, rhinitis, sore throat, a diagnosis of pneumonia or exacerbated COPD) ≥ 3 days after a patient's admission to a DroPS ward, and was considered as secondary outcome. A microbiologically confirmed HARVI was a clinical HARVI with a positive test result for influenza A/B or RSV from a nasopharyngeal swab (Cobas® LIAT®, Roche, Switzerland), considered as the primary outcome. For details see Supplementary Figure 1.

The data analysis was descriptive. Categorical variables were presented as number (percentages) and continuous variables as median (interquartile range; IQR). The main result was the unadjusted rate of the outcomes in the patient population at risk.

Results

1867 patients were hospitalised on DroPS wards during the observation period (16.01.2020–17.03.2020).

Exclusions

On admission, 17% (318/1867) of patients were placed on precautions (DroPS) due to respiratory symptoms. 78% (247/318) of them were tested for influenza/RSV, one third with a positive result (Influenza A n=61, Influenza B n=8, RSV n=16). Eighty-eight percent (75/85) of them were hospitalised in a multi-bed room, half of them in a double room (39 (52%); influenza n=28, RSV n=11), one RSV in a three-bed room, 25 (34%) in four-bed rooms (influenza n=25, RSV n=2) and 10 (14%, only influenza) in six-bed rooms (Table I). Outcomes

There were 888 patients hospitalised on DroPS wards for ≥ 3 days without respiratory precautions, considered at risk for HARVI (Figure 1). Most of them were hospitalised in multi-bed rooms (86% (764/888) vs 14% (124/888) in single rooms), corresponding to a total of 6145 patient days at risk in multi-bed rooms. The median age was 75 years [IQR 62–84]. The proportion of females was 46% (404/888). The median length of hospital stay was 6 days [IQR 4–10]. Baseline and characteristics of all the hospitalised patients at risk are summarized in Table I.

For the main outcomes, only the at-risk patients who stayed in multi-bed rooms were considered (n=764): Overall, there were 11/764 (1.4%) clinically diagnosed HARVI. All were tested for influenza/RSV and three could be microbiologically confirmed (1x influenza B, 2x RSV). The rate of hospital-acquired influenza/RSV infections on all DroPS wards was 3/764 (0.4%). No HARVI occurred in patients exposed to infected neighbours (n= 241) (Table I). For a detailed analysis see Supplementary Table 2.

Discussion

We implemented Droplet precautions on-site (DroPS) strategy across two hospitals and assessed subsequent hospital-acquired respiratory viral infections (HARVI) during the pre-pandemic influenza season 2019/20. We found a low rate of 0.4% PCR-confirmed HARVI (1x influenza B, 2x RSV) and only a few cases of clinical HARVI (11x; 1.4%). This observation is in line with our experience and the local data from a pilot study that revealed a HARVI rate of 0.3–0.7% [7].

A strength of our study was the prospective and systematic daily screening for respiratory symptoms in hospitalised patients by a trained study nurse, and, if present, an immediate microbiological testing carried out by the HCW in charge. This enabled a low threshold to detection and a detailed analysis of every possible HARVI.

DroPS offers a straightforward approach for managing patients with suspected RVI, where measures can be started by any HCW without consulting infection control experts, which can lead to faster decision making, a critical factor in stopping potential outbreaks. Time-consuming patient transfers (to single rooms/cohorting) and related cleaning measures are reduced. Especially in settings with limited resources, or at times of increased demand (such as during outbreaks), DroPS may reduce the pressure on single rooms and increase bed capacity. As reported previously, DroPS strategy seems to enjoy a high level of appreciation among HCW [7,8].

Safety considerations

There were two HARVIs with RSV. This could question the safety of DroPS: RSV is traditionally associated with contact precautions and DroPS strategy promotes standard precautions without requiring contact isolation [6]. Therefore, one could argue that the risk for hospital-acquired RSV transmissions may increase due to omission of contact precautions. Of note, in an in-depth analysis, in neither of the two patients with a nosocomial RSV infection, could a source for transmission be found - neither on the same ward, in the same room nor from a neighbouring patient or a HCW. A detection bias (especially in the latter group) cannot be excluded: only absence of absenteeism and respiratory illness was investigated, without asymptomatic screening for RSV. We also cannot exclude visitors as a potential source. We believe that physical barriers are well ensured with DroPS, due to the reinforcement of standard hygiene precautions (i.e. hand hygiene, wearing gloves and gown if contact with body fluids/substances), and that a symptom-based approach for the initiation of respiratory precautions may provide additional protection against those respiratory viruses which otherwise would not result in specific precaution measures due to a probably underestimated HARVI risk [3,9,10]. It is important to note that immunosuppressed patients are not allowed in the same room as a patient with DroPS (Supplementary Table I).

Limitations

This was a single-arm, pragmatic, prospective observation study of nosocomial influenza and RSV occurring with the DroPS strategy as a novel infection control and prevention concept, and – of note – without a direct comparison to the standard single room isolation strategy. Our previous pilot study, which compared DroPS to the single room strategy, didn't show a difference in HARVI rate but was underpowered to draw meaningful conclusions [7]. Comparisons with other published Swiss HARVI data are difficult due to a lack of a national benchmark and significant differences in methodology and reporting.

There was no follow-up of the patients after discharge. Therefore, we cannot exclude having missed some HARVI acquired on a DroPS ward. A potential exclusion bias lies in the focus on the study population at risk for HARVI while excluding all patients with respiratory precautions on admission (17% of all patients hospitalised), which were not screened for “new” respiratory symptoms. HARVI occurring in this population would have caused substantial methodological difficulties (e.g. acquisition of detailed clinical data and its interpretation). Further, the use of a qualitative test method (here: PCR without measurement of cycle thresholds) lacks information about viral load as an indirect marker for infectivity, meaning, we were neither able to estimate relative transmission risk from infected patients. As the real transmission risk derived from a newly hospitalised patient with influenza/RSV infection could be low (i.e. hospitalisation >5 days after symptom onset due to an infection with influenza will lead to a positive test result despite a probably low infectivity), the effectiveness of DroPS may be overestimated.

We did not evaluate how often patients had to be transferred to a single room after initiation of DroPS because of poor adherence to the measures. Also, routine screenings to assess asymptomatic infected patients were not done. Patient immunisation status (influenza vaccination or recent RVI) was not systematically assessed at admission and could therefore not be considered. Furthermore, there was non-systematic assessment of RVI in HCW or visitors, a potential source for HARVI.

Further considerations

DroPS as a symptom-driven strategy would ideally reduce the burden of diagnostic tests. Nevertheless, from a clinical perspective, diagnostic testing for viral pathogens (other than SARS-CoV-2, i.e. influenza) is likely to continue due to therapeutic considerations in high-risk patients. Further, given that the sole trigger for DroPS is the presence of respiratory symptoms, this may lead to a low threshold to initiate respiratory precautions which includes non-infectious causes (i.e. cough because of congestive heart failure, chronic pulmonary disease etc.) and an increase of respiratory precautions compared to the pathogen-based standard strategy. DroPS requires cooperation from the patient and adherence to the measures is sometimes difficult to anticipate. Dementia should therefore be considered a contraindication for DroPS. A relevant aspect seems to be the need for information and education about DroPS to the non-isolated neighbouring patients (and their relatives) in a room with patients on DroPS.

Conclusions

This prospective observational study of hospital-acquired influenza/RSV infections after introducing the Droplet precautions on-site (DroPS) strategy yielded a low HARVI rate. DroPS could be a straightforward and safe alternative strategy to standard single room isolation in hospitalised patients with non-pandemic respiratory viral infections.

As next step, a multi-center, cluster-randomized study with complete post-discharge follow-up should be pursued to determine the clinical utility and safety of DroPS as a novel approach to HARVI prevention.

Ethics

Not required. According to Swiss Federal law and hospital internal rules, quality assurance projects are exempt from ethical committee evaluation. Infection prevention projects are considered quality improvement with direct benefits to the patients and the hospital, and the tasks are usually commissioned by the hospital (hospital management, clinic management, department). The implementation of DroPS and data outcome evaluation was conducted in agreement with the hospital's legal service and the infection prevention committee.
