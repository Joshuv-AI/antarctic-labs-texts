The Immunosuppression Paradox in Multiple Sclerosis: Are We Fueling the Fire by Suppressing Immunity in an Epstein–Barr Virus–Linked Disease?
Authors: Anna Onisiforou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecent studies strongly implicate Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) as a necessary trigger for multiple sclerosis (MS). Longitudinal data show that EBV seroconversion precedes MS onset, while mechanistic studies identify molecular mimicry between EBV antigens and central nervous system proteins. Yet, MS treatments remain largely immunosuppressive, potentially impairing antiviral surveillance required to control EBV latency. Therapies that deplete T cells or impair lymphocyte trafficking have been linked to EBV-driven lymphoproliferative complications, potentially leading to EBV reactivation and exacerbation of MS. In contrast, B-cell–depleting therapies may reduce EBV reservoirs by targeting infected memory B cells and thus be more suited to treating EBV-associated MS. In this Review, we examine the paradox of treating an EBV-associated disease with immunosuppression, highlight data suggesting EBV reactivation under certain MS therapies, and propose that future MS management may require integration of EBV-targeted strategies, including vaccines and virus-directed immunotherapies, to address the underlying viral etiology.Keywords: multiple sclerosis, Epstein–Barr virus, immunosuppressive drugs, neurovirology, autoimmunity, molecular mimicry

BACKGROUND

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic, immune-mediated demyelinating disease of the central nervous system (CNS), affecting over 2.8 million people worldwide [1]. It is marked by demyelination, neuroinflammation, and progressive decline and is the leading cause of neurological disability in adults aged 18–40 [2]. Pathologically, MS is marked by inflammatory lesions in both white and gray matter, accompanied by infiltration of autoreactive T and B lymphocyte [3, 4]. Early in the disease course, immune-mediated inflammation drives cycles of demyelination followed by partial remyelination, resulting in transient clinical recovery. Over time, repeated inflammatory insults lead to axonal injury, glial scarring, and irreversible neurodegeneration.

Multiple sclerosis is clinically heterogeneous and is classified into 4 clinical types. Clinically isolated syndrome (CIS) represents a first episode of inflammatory demyelination and may evolve into MS. Relapsing-remitting MS (RRMS), accounting for ∼85% of cases, is characterized by episodic inflammatory relapses followed by partial recovery [5]. Many patients with RRMS transition to secondary progressive MS (SPMS), typically after 10–20 years, marked by sustained disability accumulation. In contrast, primary progressive MS (PPMS) is defined by continuous neurological deterioration from disease onset and reflects a more neurodegenerative disease trajectory [6].

MS exhibits a strong female predominance, with a female-to-male ratio of approximately of 3:1 [7], and is widely regarded as a multifactorial disease arising from complex interactions between genetic susceptibility and environmental or lifestyle factors [8, 9]. Genetic risk is strongly associated with immune-related loci, most notably the human leukocyte antigen (HLA)-DRB1*15:01 allele, which influences antigen presentation and immune responsiveness and represents the strongest known inherited risk for MS [10]. Environmental factors implicated in MS susceptibility and disease course include vitamin D deficiency, obesity, psychological stress, smoking, air pollution, and viral infections [9].

Among these environmental factors, Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) has emerged as the most compelling infectious candidate linked to MS. A growing body of epidemiological and immunological evidence indicates that EBV infection is a necessary, though not sufficient, factor in MS pathogenesis. A major longitudinal study found that EBV seroconversion increases MS risk over 30-fold, indicating a strong temporal and likely causal link [11]. These findings have shifted prevailing views of MS from a purely autoimmune disorder toward a model in which chronic viral infection contributes to immune dysregulation and autoimmunity in genetically susceptible individuals.

Despite this evolving understanding, current disease-modifying therapies (DMTs) for MS remain largely focused on broad immunosuppression or immunomodulation. This raises a fundamental paradox: if EBV contributes to MS pathogenesis, could suppressing the immune system impair the host's ability to control EBV, inadvertently facilitating EBV reactivation and further immune dysregulation? In essence, are we unintentionally fueling the fire by undermining antiviral surveillance against EBV while trying to suppress autoimmune-mediated inflammation? Indeed, several highly effective DMTs are associated with increased susceptibility to opportunistic infections. Natalizumab (Tysabri) has been linked to progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (PML), a fatal demyelinating disease caused by John Cunningham (JC) virus reactivation [12]. Similarly, DMTs like fingolimod, ocrelizumab, and natalizumab increase susceptibility to opportunistic infections, including EBV reactivation [13–15]. These observations underscore the delicate balance between suppressing autoimmune inflammation and preserving antiviral immune surveillance.

This review examines the emerging EBV-centric model of MS pathogenesis and the immunosuppression paradox it presents. We argue that future therapeutic strategies should integrate antiviral or EBV-targeted approaches and that existing MS therapies differ substantially in their impact on EBV control. In particular, while therapies such as fingolimod, natalizumab, and alemtuzumab impair T-cell function or trafficking and may potentially compromise EBV control, B-cell–depleting therapies such as ocrelizumab may be more suited to EBV-driven MS by reducing both autoreactive immune responses and the pool of EBV-infected memory B cells.

EBV AND MS PATHOGENESIS

EBV, also known as human herpesvirus 4, is a ubiquitous gammaherpesvirus that infects over 95% of the global adult population [16]. Following primary infection, EBV enters a dormant state and establishes lifelong latency predominantly within memory B cells, which serve as its primary reservoir [17]. In most individuals, the virus remains in the latent phase without causing any clinically significant health issues. During latency, EBV persists with minimal viral gene expression, evading immune detection, while periodic lytic reactivation may stimulate immune responses and promote viral dissemination. However, EBV can undergo episodic reactivation in both immunocompromised and immunocompetent individuals, transitioning to lytic replication in permissive cells, a process that may contribute to EBV-associated diseases through both latent and lytic infection phases. While most individuals remain asymptomatic carriers of EBV, a subset develops EBV-associated diseases, including autoimmune conditions such as MS [11], as well as several malignancies, including Burkitt lymphoma, Hodgkin's lymphoma, nasopharyngeal carcinoma, cervical cancer, and gastric carcinoma [18–22].

Host genetic factors not only influence the risk of autoimmunity but may also shape susceptibility to EBV infection, viral control, and downstream immune dysregulation. Genome-wide association studies have identified numerous MS risk loci, with the strongest association linked to the HLA-DRB1*15:01 allele, which plays a critical role in antigen presentation and T-cell activation [23]. Individuals carrying HLA-DRB1*15:01 exhibit a 24-fold increased risk of developing MS when they mount antibody responses against the EBV nuclear antigen 1 (EBNA-1), specifically the amino acid region 385–420 [24, 25]. Intriguingly, HLA-DRB1*15:01 has also been shown to act as a coreceptor for EBV entry into B-cell lines, providing a mechanistic link between genetic predisposition and viral susceptibility [26].

Nearly all individuals with MS are EBV-seropositive, with longitudinal data demonstrating that EBV seroconversion precedes MS onset by several years, supporting a temporal and potentially causal relationship [11]. In one notable exception, a single individual who remained EBV-seronegative developed MS; however, the possibility of a misdiagnosis was considered by the study authors [11]. Given the high prevalence of EBV in the general population, EBV infection alone is insufficient to cause MS in most individuals, highlighting the need for additional genetic, environmental, and immunological cofactors in disease pathogenesis.

The timing of EBV infection appears to influence MS risk. While most individuals acquire EBV asymptomatically in early childhood, delayed infection in adolescence or adulthood, often presenting as infectious mononucleosis, is associated with a significantly increased risk of MS [27]. This suggests that both the age at infection and the host immune response during primary EBV exposure shape autoimmune susceptibility.

Sex differences further influence MS risk and progression. Women have a higher incidence of MS, earlier onset, and more frequent relapses, while men generally exhibit faster disease progression and tend to have more severe outcomes [1, 28–32]. While EBV infects nearly everyone, sex-based differences in immune responses, including humoral and cellular immunity, may contribute to these disparities [33, 34]. Thus, it is not merely EBV exposure that determines MS risk, but rather the host's immunological response, shaped by genetics, sex hormones, and age at infection, that drives disease susceptibility and severity.

Several mechanisms have been proposed by which EBV may drive autoreactivity in MS [35]. Rather than acting through a single pathway, EBV is thought to promote CNS autoimmunity through multiple, nonmutually exclusive immunopathogenic mechanisms that converge to drive both T- and B-cell autoreactivity and trigger virus-induced immunopathology associated with autoimmune disorders such as MS [36]. The most prominent mechanism by which viral infections are proposed to induce autoreactivity is molecular mimicry, that occurs between EBV antigens, particularly EBNA-1, and CNS proteins such as GlialCAM and α-crystallin may trigger cross-reactive CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell responses that escape peripheral tolerance and initiate CNS-directed autoimmunity [37, 38]. A second, closely related mechanism involves EBV-infected B cells. EBV–infected B cells may serve as a reservoir of autoreactive B cells, act as aberrant antigen-presenting cells that sustain pathogenic T-cell activation, and promote autoantibody production through epitope spreading and bystander activation [39]. Additional amplification mechanisms further exacerbate EBV-driven immune dysregulation. These include bystander tissue injury mediated by EBV-specific CD8+ T cells, innate immune activation through EBV-encoded small RNAs within the CNS, and aberrant expression of αB-crystallin by EBV-infected B cells, further amplifying autoreactive T-cell responses against oligodendrocytes [40]. EBV can also modulate host cellular processes, including immune pathways such as Th17 cell differentiation, through virus–host protein–protein interactions [41]. Th17 cell differentiation plays a critical role in MS pathogenesis, as Th17 cells promote CNS inflammation and demyelination [42]. Together, these mechanistic insights support both the outside-in hypothesis, in which peripheral EBV-driven immune dysregulation triggers CNS autoimmunity, and the inside-out hypothesis, in which primary CNS injury may expose novel antigens that fuel EBV-driven autoreactivity [41, 43]. Although EBV's centrality in MS is strongly supported, its role as a universal cause remains debated. Even if not solely causal, persistent EBV likely amplifies autoimmunity and sustains disease, justifying EBV-targeted therapies in seropositive MS patients. Alternative hypotheses, such as nonviral environmental factors and gut microbiota, should also be considered for a comprehensive understanding of MS heterogeneity [44].

EBV's role appears most pronounced in RRMS, characterized by autoimmune relapses and remissions resembling EBV's episodic reactivation. Environmental triggers like infections and heat promote both viral reactivation and MS relapses [45], with relapses often following infections [46]. In contrast, progressive forms of MS (SPMS and PPMS) feature CNS-compartmentalized immune responses and neurodegeneration, driven by mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and microglial activation, explaining reduced efficacy of immunosuppressive therapies [47]. Yet, EBV may continue contributing via sustained B-cell activity in meningeal follicles, suggesting its pathological relevance even in progressive stages.

Recent systems-level analyses indicate a clinical type–dependent contribution of EBV to MS. EBV engagement is most pronounced during early inflammatory stages (CIS and RRMS), where it preferentially targets dysregulated immune pathways, including B-cell–associated processes, Toll-like receptor signaling, and infection-like inflammatory responses, while suppressing antiviral and nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB)–mediated signalling pathways [48]. In contrast, progressive MS shows fewer EBV–host interactions but distinct mechanistic shifts, with SPMS associated with vascular and tissue repair pathways and PPMS enriched for vacuolar and lysosomal remodeling [48].

THERAPIES, IMMUNE SURVEILLANCE, AND EBV REACTIVATION IN MS

Current DMTs for MS primarily aim to suppress or modulate immune responses to prevent autoreactive damage. However, because EBV persists within memory B cells and is controlled largely by cytotoxic T cells, suppressing either arm of the immune system may compromise this balance and enable viral reactivation, while B-cell–depleting therapies may reduce EBV reservoirs. Alemtuzumab, a CD52-targeting monoclonal antibody, depletes both T and B cells and is effective in reducing RRMS relapses [49]. However, this broad lymphocyte depletion also eliminates tolerance-promoting and virus-controlling CD8+ T cells, increasing the risk of secondary autoimmunity [50] and impairing surveillance of latent viruses like EBV. Prolonged lymphodepletion following alemtuzumab has been linked to EBV-related complications, including lymphoproliferative disease, in both MS and other autoimmune conditions such as polymyositis [51]. Recovery of lymphocyte populations after alemtuzumab is heterogeneous, B cells typically repopulate within 7–8 months, whereas CD4+ and CD8+ T cells may take 20–35 months, while some patients demonstrate incomplete T-cell reconstitution [52]. Although opportunistic infections are relatively rare, sustained T-cell lymphopenia may compromise EBV-specific immunity in susceptible individuals. Notably, alemtuzumab has been used in systemic chronic active EBV to target infected CD4+ T cells and reduce viral load [53]. However, in MS, where EBV persists primarily in memory B cells, rapid B-cell repopulation in the setting of prolonged T-cell deficiency may limit its long-term impact on the EBV reservoir.

Fingolimod, a sphingosine-1-phosphate receptor modulator, reduces CNS inflammation by sequestering lymphocytes in lymphoid tissues, restricting their trafficking into the circulation. While this mechanism is effective in controlling inflammatory relapses in highly active MS or rapidly progressing disease [54], it also simultaneously restricts systemic and CNS immune surveillance including antiviral responses against latent viruses. Impaired antiviral immunity under fingolimod is reflected in reduced antibody responses and diminished seroconversion following severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) vaccination, especially with prolonged treatment [55]. In rare cases, EBV-positive primary CNS lymphoma, as well as B- and T-cell non-Hodgkin lymphomas, including fatal EBV-positive B-cell lymphoma, have been reported in fingolimod-treated patients, highlighting a key limitation of therapies that suppress immune cell circulation rather than selectively targeting pathogenic pathways [56].

Natalizumab alters immune surveillance through a distinct mechanism by blocking immune cell entry into the CNS. In RRMS, natalizumab treatment leads to an expansion of circulating memory B cells, the primary EBV reservoir, alongside reductions in plasmablasts and serum IgG levels [57], potentially compromising humoral antiviral immunity and allowing peripheral EBV load to increase. Studies examining EBV serologic profiles during natalizumab therapy have reported increases in anti-viral capsid antigen (VCA) immunoglobulin G (IgG) levels at month 15, suggestive of transient EBV reactivation, while anti-EBNA-1 IgG levels remained stable throughout treatment [13]. However, limitations such as small sample size and infrequent sampling (every 3 months) may have underestimated the frequency and clinical relevance of short-lived EBV reactivation events. In progressive MS, similar expansion of memory B cells occurs; however, continued immune exclusion from CNS compartments and reduced plasmablast levels in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) [57] may permit localized EBV persistence, particularly within meningeal ectopic lymphoid structures. Together, these findings raise concerns that natalizumab may alter EBV dynamics in ways not fully captured by routine clinical monitoring.

Interferon-beta (IFN-β), one of the earliest MS therapies, exerts immunomodulatory effects by limiting proinflammatory cytokine production, reducing T-cell activation, and stabilizing the blood–brain barrier. Interferon-beta dampens T-cell responses to latent EBV antigen EBNA-1, which may reduce autoimmune cross-reactivity but could also weaken antiviral surveillance [58]. Although EBV-specific immune responses are not completely abolished, IFN-β appears to have limited efficacy in controlling latent EBV infection. Its favorable safety and low risk of severe immunosuppression have made it a long-standing therapy, but its modest clinical efficacy has shifted therapeutic focus toward agents with greater impact on disease activity. In contrast, interferon-γ (IFN-γ) plays a central role in antiviral immunity by enhancing cytotoxic T-cell function and antigen presentation, processes critical for EBV control. However, IFN-γ can exacerbate MS pathology by promoting CNS inflammation [59]. EBV has evolved mechanisms to counteract IFN-γ signaling, including suppression of IFN-γ receptor expression through the viral BZLF1 protein [60], illustrating the complex interplay between host antiviral defenses and viral immune evasion in MS.

Not all MS therapies carry equal risk of EBV reactivation. B-cell–depleting therapies such as ocrelizumab and rituximab target CD20+ B cells, including memory B cells that serve as the principal EBV reservoir. Ocrelizumab has been shown to decrease proliferation of autologous EBV-infected lymphoblastoid cell lines and to selectively diminish IFN-γ responses to EBV antigens over 6–12 months, without broadly impairing responses to other viruses [61]. This pattern is consistent with a selective modulation of EBV-specific immune activation rather than global antiviral suppression. In parallel, depletion of EBV-infected memory B cells may reduce antigen presentation and inflammatory signaling within the CNS, potentially contributing to the marked clinical efficacy of ocrelizumab in MS [61]. Collectively, these observations suggest that anti-CD20 therapies may exert therapeutic benefit not only through immunomodulation but also by reducing EBV-infected B-cell populations that drive CNS inflammation, supporting a partially antiviral mechanism of action [62].

Rituximab, which also depletes CD20+ B cells, is a frontline therapy for EBV-driven lymphoproliferative disorders and has demonstrated efficacy in reducing disease activity in MS, particularly in off-label use [63]. Its antiviral potential stems from the targeted depletion of memory B cells, the primary reservoir for latent EBV. However, in cases where EBV-associated posttransplant lymphoproliferative disease (EBV-PTLD) becomes refractory to rituximab, adoptive immunotherapy using third-party EBV-specific cytotoxic T lymphocytes (EBV-CTLs) has been effective in inducing remission [64]. This highlights the limitations of B-cell depletion alone in controlling EBV under conditions of profound immune dysregulation and underscores the need for more comprehensive antiviral strategies in MS, especially in EBV-seropositive individuals.

Taken together, these observations highlight that while current MS therapies differ in their effects on EBV immune surveillance, none are specifically designed to eradicate latent EBV or restore durable antiviral immunity, underscoring a major unmet therapeutic need in the field.

EBV-TARGETED THERAPEUTIC STRATEGIES: FUTURE DIRECTIONS

EBV infection leads to outcomes ranging from asymptomatic persistence to malignancies and autoimmune diseases such as MS, underscoring the need for tailored prevention, treatment, and biomarker strategies. These outcomes arise from interactions among host genetics, immune responses, and environmental factors, with sex-specific differences influencing disease risk and progression. The absence of approved EBV-specific antivirals, vaccines, or immune-based therapies highlights a major unmet therapeutic need and underscores the importance of developing EBV-directed interventions for MS. While developing a vaccine against EBV represents a promising strategy for addressing EBV-associated diseases, like MS, several challenges remain. A central unresolved question is whether a single vaccine can provide protection across distinct EBV-associated disease outcomes or whether disease-specific strategies will be required. Given the differing mechanisms driving EBV-associated malignancies and autoimmune diseases, including MS, an effective vaccine strategy may need to target both latent and lytic phases of EBV infection. In respect to MS, such approaches may need to focus on modulating immune dysregulation and molecular mimicry rather than solely preventing primary infection.

Recent advances in EBV vaccine development illustrate growing momentum toward both prophylactic and therapeutic strategies. An investigational nanoparticle-based EBV vaccine, MDX2201, is currently being evaluated in a Phase I clinical trial (NCT06655324). This vaccine employs a ferritin nanoparticle platform displaying multiple EBV entry glycoproteins, including gH, gL, gp42, and gp350, representing an evolution beyond earlier gp350-only vaccine approaches. In parallel, therapeutic EBV vaccines have been explored in EBV-associated malignancies such as nasopharyngeal carcinoma, including viral vector-based vaccines (NCT01094405 and NCT01147991) and a peptide-based vaccine (NCT00078494). In addition, a novel mRNA-based EBV therapeutic vaccine is currently undergoing Phase I evaluation in patients with refractory EBV-associated malignancies (NCT05714748). Although these efforts have primarily focused on EBV-mediated oncogenic pathogenesis, they provide a clear rationale for the possible future testing of such vaccines in MS.

Sex-based differences are likely to influence the efficacy and safety of EBV-directed vaccines and immunotherapies in MS. Women generally exhibit heightened immune reactivity, which may result in stronger antiviral responses but also an increased risk of autoimmune activation, whereas men may require alternative dosing or adjuvant strategies to achieve sufficient immune activation. Sex-based differences in vaccine responses have been reported for influenza vaccines [65] and the adjuvanted recombinant zoster vaccine [66], underscoring the need to evaluate sex effects in a platform-specific manner.

Host genetic variability, particularly within immune-related loci, is also likely to shape responses to EBV-targeted interventions in MS. HLA alleles associated with MS risk, most notably HLA-DRB1*15:01, may influence immune responses to EBV antigens and vaccine-induced immunity. Individuals carrying this allele may exhibit heightened or dysregulated responses to EBV-derived epitopes, necessitating vaccine or immunotherapy designs that minimize autoimmune risk while preserving antiviral efficacy.

In addition to vaccine-based strategies, several nonvaccine EBV-directed therapeutic approaches are under active investigation and may be relevant to MS. However, it is notable that there is currently no approved antiviral drug for the treatment of EBV infection. Gershburg and Pagano proposed 3 principal reasons for this gap [67]. First, EBV infection is difficult to diagnose at a stage amenable to antiviral intervention, as primary infection is often asymptomatic, has a prolonged incubation period, and typically presents late, unlike other herpesvirus infections [67]. Second, effective antiviral therapy would need to achieve sustained high concentrations at sites of EBV shedding, particularly the oropharynx [67]. Although acyclovir can transiently reduce EBV shedding when administered intravenously or orally, viral release rapidly returns to baseline after treatment cessation [68, 69]. Most importantly, the clinical manifestations of EBV-associated disease are largely driven by host immune responses against EBV-infected B cells rather than by active viral replication [67]. Consistent with this, combined antiviral and immunomodulatory treatment (acyclovir plus prednisolone) suppressed oropharyngeal EBV replication but failed to improve clinical symptoms or alter EBV-specific cellular immunity in controlled trials [69]. These limitations underscore why conventional antivirals targeting lytic replication alone are unlikely to be effective in EBV-associated immune-mediated diseases such as MS, reinforcing the need for strategies that restore durable EBV-specific immune surveillance or target latent EBV reservoirs.

More promising are immune-based approaches aimed at restoring EBV-specific antiviral surveillance. Adoptive transfer of EBV-CTLs has demonstrated strong efficacy and safety in EBV-driven diseases without inducing global immunosuppression. Both patient-tailored and third-party, off-the-shelf EBV-CTL products, derived from stem cell donors, related donors, or unrelated HLA-matched donors, have induced complete or sustained partial remission in a majority of patients with refractory EBV infections or EBV-associated malignancies, including rituximab-refractory EBV-PTLD, with minimal toxicity and durable clinical benefit [64, 70]. Notably, “switch” EBV-CTL therapy, using T cells restricted by an alternative HLA allele, has rescued responses in patients failing initial treatment, highlighting the flexibility and robustness of this strategy [64]. Persistence of EBV-specific T cells in vivo correlates with clinical response [70], providing strong proof-of-principle that selective reinforcement of EBV-specific immunity, rather than broad immunosuppression, is feasible and effective, supporting future exploration of EBV-CTL–based immunotherapy for EBV-associated immune dysregulation in MS.

Additional insights arise from studies of EBV latency in cancer. EBV–associated lymphomas exhibit distinct latency patterns characterized by differential expression of viral antigens, which determines susceptibility to EBV-specific T-cell–mediated clearance [71]. Tumors expressing highly restricted latency patterns can evade immune recognition, prompting the development of latency-disrupting strategies designed to increase viral antigen expression and sensitize infected cells to immune elimination [71]. Although these approaches have been developed specifically for EBV-associated cancers, they provide a conceptual framework for understanding how modulation of EBV latency might enhance immune recognition of EBV-infected cells. Such principles may be relevant to MS, where EBV-infected memory B cells persist despite ongoing immune responses.

Together, these observations support a future therapeutic paradigm in which EBV-directed strategies, such as virus-specific immunotherapies, latency-modulating approaches, or carefully designed vaccines, could be integrated with selective immunomodulation to address EBV-driven immune dysregulation in MS. While substantial translational work remains, particularly to ensure safety in autoimmune disease, these advances illustrate a growing EBV-targeted therapeutic landscape that may ultimately move MS treatment beyond broad immunosuppression toward more precise, mechanism-based interventions.

CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE PERSPECTIVES

The precise role of EBV persistence in MS and its interaction with genetics and environmental cofactors remains incompletely understood. While most people harbor EBV asymptomatically, others develop MS, highlighting the need to clarify host immune responses that govern these outcomes.

Breaking the self-sustaining cycle of EBV persistence and autoreactivity may require dual targeting of both the virus and the immune dysfunction it sustains. Promising strategies include EBV-specific adoptive T cell therapies, therapeutic vaccines, and latency-reversing agents. The success will depend on advances in precision immunomonitoring, such as quantifying latent EBV burden, identifying EBV-infected B-cell subsets, and evaluating virus-specific T-cell function. Recent virus-informed, systems-level approaches, including our VirTarget framework, illustrate how integrating EBV biology with host genetics and drug-response signatures may support more precise, mechanism-based therapeutic selection in MS [72]. Genetic and immunologic profiling can enable personalized therapy, while computational digital twin models may improve treatment prediction. For clinical use, biomarkers must support rapid, cost-effective multiplex testing suitable for routine care. These innovations could shift MS therapy away from broad immunosuppression toward precision, multitargeted approaches that address both EBV and autoimmune pathology.
