Inference of Cytochrome P450 Evolutionary History Using Structural and Physicochemical Metrics
Authors: Jamie D Dixson, Abhijay Azad, Pamela A Padilla, Rajeev K Azad, Jordan zz Douglas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCytochrome P450s are a superfamily of heme-binding monooxygenases involved with the detoxification of intrinsic and extrinsic toxins. They are near ubiquitous within biological domains and are found in all domains. Members of families within the superfamily are defined based on amino acid identity thresholds, with thresholds as low as 40% in some families. Relationships among Cytochrome P450 families have proven elusive due to sub-Twilight Zone interfamily identities (<30%) that result in poor multiple sequence alignment quality and thus low levels of support for downstream phylogenetic reconstructions. Despite the low identities, Cytochrome P450 structures are remarkably well conserved both within and among families. In such cases, structural phylogenetics has the potential to unveil elusive relationships because the selectively favored physicochemical properties giving rise to the structure and function of the proteins persist despite sequence-level divergence. Recently, in two separate publications, we demonstrated that by utilizing physicochemical vectors, dynamic time warping, and hierarchical clustering (PCDTW), large swaths of protein domain families and betacoronavirus receptor-binding domain clades were congruent with validated functional/structural relationships. These were important findings because anomalous sequence alignment-based maximum likelihood phylogenetic findings, which were not congruent with the known functional relationships, were resolved. That also validated the use of physicochemical vectors in making inferences about structural/functional homology. Additionally, it illuminated that the same methods might be applied to other protein families with relationships that are difficult to resolve from sequence data alone. Herein, we used Molecular Weight and Hydrophobicity Physicochemical Dynamic Time Warping (MWHP PCDTW) along with structural and sequence alignment-based phylogenetic methodologies to analyze all of the Cytochrome P450s found both in the high-fidelity Structural Classificaction of Proteins (SCOP) database and the reviewed sequences with both experimentally resolved and de novo predicted structures in the Protein Data Bank and the AlphaFold (AF) Protein Structure Database, respectively. We compared the resulting phylogenetic topologies and found that in some cases, structure-based methods may be less able to resolve random/convergent similarity than physicochemical and sequence-based methodologies. This finding agrees with previous findings that demonstrate the usefulness of physicochemical properties in resolving both random structural similarity and potentially convergent relationships.Keywords: PCDTW, structural phylogenetics, Twilight Zone, Foldtree, physicochemical, remote homology

Remote homology inference and subsequent phylogenetic mapping are difficult when using alignment-based techniques because alignment accuracy diminishes when identities drop below 30%. Physicochemical property-based and structure-based methods allow for protein relatedness to be assessed from the specific characteristics of a protein that are subject to selection. This allows for circumvention of potentially erroneous alignments. Herein, we demonstrate that physicochemical and structural methods can be used together to minimize issues that may arise when using either alone for the mapping of remotely homologous relationships.

Introduction

Cytochrome P450 proteins (CYPs) comprise a nearly ubiquitous protein superfamily that spans all domains of life. These proteins are involved in the metabolism of a wide range of intrinsic and extrinsic toxins. They also function in the degradation of many byproducts of cellular metabolism and the biosynthesis of some steroid hormones (Werck-Reichhart and Feyereisen 2000; Rendic and Peter Guengerich 2018). The name Cytochrome P450 is derived from the heme-binding activity of CYPs and their characteristic ability to absorb UV light of wavelength 450 nm (Werck-Reichhart and Feyereisen 2000). CYPs are almost exclusively identified by the concurrent presence of two signature motifs (Syed and Mashele 2014). In addition to those two motifs found among all CYPs, there are other signature motifs that are characteristic of individual families and/or taxa (Nelson 2006).

Putative CYPs are largely assigned to families and subfamilies according to amino acid level identity. Clusters of CYPs showing no less than 40% amino acid identity are generally assigned to a family, and clusters showing no less than 55% amino acid identity are generally assigned to a subfamily (Nelson et al. 1996; Syed and Mashele 2014 ). These are not strict rules for family/subfamily assignment and are sometimes violated because more weight is given to cladal placement than to identity when the CYP nomenclature committee assigns a name to a novel CYP (Nelson 2006). Early in the classification of CYPs, phylogenetic placement was determined using standard multiple sequence alignment of <160 CYP sequences along with the putative CYP, followed by either unweighted pair group method with arithmetic mean (UPGMA) or neighbor-joining tree construction (Nelson 1998 ). Now that other tools and greater computing power are available, phylogenetic placement by the committee is determined by aligning the sequences to known CYPs using Clustal Omega (CO) (Sievers and Higgins 2018). Those alignments are then used for neighbor-joining dendrogram construction as implemented directly within CO and without any preprocessing of the multiple sequence alignment (Nelson 2018).

While there is far from strict compliance with the preferred nomenclature outlined by the CYP Nomenclature Committee (Nelson 2006 ), CYPs are preferably named according to a system with each protein receiving a name of CYPXYZ, where CYP indicates that the protein belongs to the Cytochrome P450 protein superfamily, X indicates the gene family, Y indicates the subfamily, and Z indicates the individual protein (Nelson 1998, 2006). The gene family and individual protein indicators are numeric, while the protein subfamily indicator is a capital alpha character. This system of nomenclature is widely applied and was originally proposed as a means to standardize the naming of proteins within this superfamily (Nelson et al. 1996; Nelson 1998). In addition to the standard nomenclature for the CYPs, some CYPs are given names according to phenotypic characteristics, introducing another level for potential ambiguity. The uncertainty presented by multiple parallel naming conventions is not a new struggle, and similar difficulties have been noted with other families of proteins for many years (Dixson et al. 2003). This underscores the need for either strict naming rules or very robust cross-referencing capabilities that are based on a unifying principle of each protein superfamily, family, or subfamily. This is also necessary for the maximum utility of the sequences in the various databases. Key, in our opinion, is a naming scheme that is not subject to the complicating aspects of homoplasy and generally allows for the classification of proteins, according first to their evolutionary history, with only secondary consideration of functional/structural characteristics. Stated differently, and considering the vast amounts of biological data available, it is strongly advisable that the nomenclature of CYPs and all other protein families begin the transformation toward a system that is strictly reflective of ancestry. We make this suggestion since higher levels of structural/functional similarity generally indicate closer shared ancestry as compared with lower levels of structural/functional conservation (Dixson and Azad 2025). Pivotal in the re-evaluation of CYPs is accurate phylogenetic placement despite low sequence identity among families and evidence that convergence has had a strong effect on the evolution of this superfamily (Rezen et al. 2004; Christ et al. 2019; Wybouw et al. 2019; Ortiz-Álvarez et al. 2024).

Structural similarity can be leveraged for phylogenetic placement when low sequence identity obscures similarity. In such instances, the structure of a protein may persist despite sequence divergence and because of evolutionary pressure for the maintenance of function. Currently, the Protein Data Bank (PDB) contains ∼240,177 experimentally solved structures, and approximately 15,000 structures were added in 2024 (https://www.rcsb.org/stats/growth/growth-released-structures) (accessed August 7, 2025). The AlphaFold (AF) database of de novo predicted structures, on the other hand, currently contains ∼215 million structures (https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/faq) (accessed August 7, 2025). The exact number of AF predicted protein structures produced each year is not published, but it is likely in the millions. This rapid increase in the availability of structures has increased both interest in and the ability to perform structural phylogenetic analyses (Moi et al. 2023) deep into the Twilight and Midnight Zones of 20% to 30% amino acid identity and 0% to 20% amino acid identity, respectively (Rost 1999). For brevity, the 0% to 30% range of amino acid identities will be referred to as the Twilight Zone for the remainder of this communication.

One potential source of error when using structures as indicators of evolutionary descent is convergent evolution. Where convergent evolution has occurred, high structural similarity may erroneously indicate a close evolutionary relationship. Convergent evolution in the morphological sense is widely acknowledged. The independent evolution of wings in birds and mammals is perhaps the most prolific example. Convergent evolution at the molecular level is not as easily conceptualized and therefore has not been explored as heavily. Despite this, many examples of convergent molecular evolution do exist (Baldwin 1996; Davies and Sykes 1997; Castoe et al. 2009, 2010; Christ et al. 2019; Wybouw et al. 2019; van Dorp et al. 2020; Wang et al. 2021; Jian et al. 2023; Ortiz-Álvarez et al. 2024). In addition to convergent evolution, horizontal gene transfer (HGT) may also be proposed to explain anomalous taxa placement (Azad and Lawrence 2012). It is common to consider convergent evolution and HGT as distinct concepts in discussions of anomalous phylogenetic topologies. However, it should be noted that HGT is the physical transfer of genetic material from one organism to another that is not its progeny (Zhaxybayeva and Doolittle 2011), while convergent evolution is the evolution of the same trait in two divergent lineages (Castoe et al. 2009, 2010). In other words, HGT does not preclude convergent evolution. On the contrary, it may in some cases be part of the process resulting in convergent evolution (Dorrell et al. 2023).

In a general sense, convergent molecular evolution has been described as the independent evolution of similar traits in unrelated lineages (Castoe et al. 2009, 2010). That definition is sufficient for general purposes, but it does not account for the fact that lineages can diverge and then once again converge upon a similar function. This is known as a molecular reversal and has been partially reproduced in directed evolution experiments in bacteria (Kaltenbach et al. 2015). It has also been documented with regard to hemoglobin oxygen affinity in birds that live at low versus high altitudes (Storz 2016), within the genomes of echolocating mammals (Parker et al. 2013), and also within the CYPs (Christ et al. 2019; Wybouw et al. 2019; Ortiz-Álvarez et al. 2024). Considering those works, a more widely applicable definition of convergent molecular evolution is the evolution of similar structure/function in divergent lineages. Whether such lineages are completely unrelated or recently diverged from a common ancestor is only relevant when considering the extent of convergence, not whether it has occurred. Therefore, this conceptual definition accounts for the fact that convergent proteins can diverge and then once again converge, or they can evolve similar traits in unrelated lineages. The former is more likely in protein families where there is strong selective pressure for overall structure and function accompanied by specialization of individual proteins for very specific, but pivotal, functions (Kaltenbach et al. 2015). The CYP protein family is one such family (Werck-Reichhart and Feyereisen 2000).

There is a hypothetical extreme with regard to convergent molecular evolution of proteins. At that extreme, two structures that are similar could concurrently exhibit no amino acid identity, indicating the absence of common descent. Protein structures are generally quite resistant to perturbations resulting from amino acid changes (Lolkema and Slotboom 1998). Therefore, quite different amino acid sequences can result in very similar solved or predicted structures (Dixson and Azad 2025), implying that with high levels of selective pressure, convergent evolution may be quite likely but also difficult to distinguish from remote homology, especially among large numbers of remote homologs in the Twilight Zone of <30% amino acid identity (Rost 1999).

Structural analyses can expose homology below 30% identity, but it has been demonstrated that even structural comparisons are subject to error as the result of convergence and chance similarity in that identity range (Dixson and Azad 2025). A recently proposed physicochemical technique, known as Molecular Weight and Hydrophobicity Physicochemical Dynamic Time Warping (MWHP PCDTW), has been shown to effectively discriminate between convergent proteins and those similar by descent using simulated data. Those results were also supported by smaller-scale analyses of actual protein family data (Dixson and Azad 2025). Another application of MWHP PCDTW is in reconstructing phylogenies based on the physicochemical similarity of amino acids. Most notably, MWHP PCDTW has been used to properly cluster betacoronavirus receptor binding domains based on function when standard phylogenetic techniques could not (Dixson and Azad 2021). For these reasons, MWHP PCDTW may be beneficial in the analysis of large protein superfamilies like the CYPs that are likely to have experienced convergent evolution and have low interfamily amino acid identity (Christ et al. 2019; Wybouw et al. 2019; Ortiz-Álvarez et al. 2024).

Utilization of structural similarity in phylogenetic reconstruction of the CYP superfamily of proteins might also be useful in determining relatedness among families due to the aforementioned low sequence identity among families. Until recently, large-scale structural phylogenetic analyses have not been plausible due to a limited number of experimental protein structures and the lack of efficient algorithms to predict and analyze structures either through homology modeling (Xiang 2006; Vyas et al. 2012) or de novo protein modeling (Du et al. 2021; Jumper et al. 2021a). The PDB has ∼160 highly verified experimental structures for CYPs, and the AF database has ∼1,600 de novo predicted CYP structures. This presents the unique opportunity to compare phylogenetic topologies obtained using structural, physicochemical, and standard sequence-based phylogenetic methods.

Herein, we use Foldtree (FT) (Moi et al. 2023) to reconstruct the CYP phylogeny using structural data. We use MWHP PCDTW to reconstruct the CYP phylogeny using physicochemical data (Dixson and Azad 2021, 2025; Dixson et al. 2024), and we use CO to reconstruct the CYP phylogeny using amino acid sequence data (Sievers and Higgins 2018). We hypothesize that because of its lack of reliance on the symbolic amino acid sequence, MWHP PCDTW will outperform CO, where homoplasy can be reasonably expected to be a factor, such as between CYP families. FT will likely perform well in structural phylogenetic placement but may also suffer from error in the cases of convergent evolution. We identify cases where convergent evolution appears to have occurred in the FT phylogenetic tree using manual comparison of individual placements and a retrospective evaluation of the FT topologies using an MWHP PCDTW-derived metric and standard alignment-based approaches. In summary, we hypothesize that the evolutionary history of CYPs will be clearer when sequence, structural, and physicochemical phylogenies are considered together rather than individually.

Results

Analysis of Highly Verified Experimental Structural/Sequence Data

Four parallel analyses of 158 Cytochrome P450 records from the Structural Classification Of Proteins (SCOP) database were conducted. These included two structure-based analyses, a physicochemical property-based analysis and a sequence-based analysis. The structural analyses involved using both experimentally determined structures and AF de novo predicted structures as input for FT. The physicochemical analysis was conducted using MWHP PCDTW of the amino acid sequences with UPGMA clustering. Finally, the sequence-based analysis was conducted using a CO multiple sequence alignment of the amino acid sequences with neighbor-joining clustering. Collectively, these analyses resulted in four very similar dendrograms. Those dendrograms are illustrated in Fig. 1 and can be accessed interactively at https://itol.embl.de/shared/realtorjamied. The FT-derived dendrograms from either experimental structures or de novo predicted structures were the most similar. Their cladal membership and branching patterns were nearly fully consistent between them. All four dendrograms had an overall star pattern, likely indicative of a lack of resolution between major clades. The MWHP PCDTW dendrogram is unique in that the CYP51 clade is split by a clade containing bacterial, archaea, and fungal CYPs that have not been fully annotated. The four topologies are nearly identical for the vertebrate CYP clades except for slightly different points of divergence for the CYP1 and CYP21 clades. Human CYP51 clustered with a single fungal sequence, and the remaining fungal CYP51 sequences clustered separately from the other CYP51 sequences in the MWHP PCDTW analysis (Fig. 1c). This contrasted with the clustering found from FT and CO analyses (Fig. 1a, b, and d), where all of the fungal sequences clustered together. It is also potentially indicative of convergent similarity. To explore that possibility further, we closely examined the human and fungal CYP51s. The human CYP51 (UniProt: Q16580) uniformly showed ∼37% identity to each of the yeast CYP51s (UniProt: E9QY26, A6ZSR0, P10614, and P50859) when aligned using CO. That level of identity holds true regardless of whether the yeast CYP51 is from Aspergillus, Candida, or Saccharomyces yeast. While the Aspergillus CYP51s share ∼37% identity with the human CYP51s, they share only ∼47% identity with the other yeast CYP51 proteins. Two broad phylogenetic groups of yeast are represented by these three species. Those are the subphylum Saccharomycotina (Saccharomyces cerevisiae and Candida glabrata) and the class Eurotiomycetes (Aspergillus fumigatus). Additionally, a rooted representation of the generally accepted evolution of yeasts indicates that Saccharomycotina are likely more derived than Eurotiomycetes (Naranjo-Ortiz and Gabaldón 2019). One potential explanation for the Eurotiomycetes clustering with the human, rather than the Saccharomycotina, is that the Eurotiomycetes converged upon a specialized variant of the CYP51 structure/function to address a selectively favored function common to both humans and Eurotiomycetes, while the other three fungal CYP51s evolved a slightly different function common to other yeasts. This is supported by the fact that the Saccharomycotina are known to have only a single CYP51 while the Eurotiomycetes are known to have multiple paralogs, allowing for neofunctionalization and thus sampling of a greater adaptive landscape (Zhang et al. 2019; Celia-Sanchez et al. 2022).

Another anomalous finding is that in the CO dendrogram, the human and rat TOMM22 sequences occur on distant clades from each other, inconsistent with their common name. However, with both MWHP PCDTW and FT dendrograms, both sequences are found within the same monophyletic clade. This potentially indicates functional/structural orthology that was apparent when function was attributed to these proteins but would not have been recognized using CO trees, as with the current method used to genotypically name CYPs. In this case, both MWHP PCDTW and FT provide unification of what appears to be a functional/structural relationship and orthologous descent. A similar observation was made where both FT and MWHP PCDTW place all mammalian TMEM97s into the same monophyletic clade, and yet CO places the human and cow TMEM97s in sister clades and the mouse TMEM97 in a distant clade. A third example where the FT and MWHP PCDTW topologies seem to detect remote homology when CO does not is the case where both FT and MWHP PCDTW place mouse and human NDOR1 proteins in the same monophyletic clade, and yet CO places them on distant clades from each other. Despite thorough evaluation, cases where CO clustered sequences based on their common name and FT/MWHP PCDTW did not, were not identified. One potentially anomalous finding was that the CYPs that mediate the biosynthesis of brassicicene C (BSC CYPs) were uniformly and consistently distributed across three separate clades in all analyses. However, the clade containing BSC1 and the clade containing BSC7 are exclusively fungal clades in both the CO and MWHP PCDTW dendrograms but contained mixed genera in the FT dendrogram. This potentially indicates molecular convergence undetectable by FT but identified by both CO and MWHP PCDTW.

The previous examples highlight how FT and MWHP PCDTW can expose potential remotely homologous relationships that are sometimes missed by sequence-based methods such as CO. The inverse can also be true, where a physicochemical method like MWHP PCDTW can agree with a sequence-based method but not a purely structural method like FT. There were 14 CYP2As considered in building the trees in Fig. 2. CYP2A7 from a baboon (UniProt Acc. Num.: P80055) is placed distantly from all other CYP2As in the MWHP PCDTW tree and in the CO tree. The remaining 13 CYP2As occur in monophyletic clades in the MWHP PCDTW and CO trees, and yet all 14 CYP2As occur in a single monophyletic clade in the FT dendrogram. One of the other homologs present in the dataset is the human CYP2A7 (UniProt Acc. Num.: P20853), which one would expect to cluster close to the corresponding baboon sequence regardless of whether structure or sequence is considered, and yet does not. This potentially indicates another case of structural convergence.

While large trees such as those in Fig. 2 are difficult to subject to exhaustive comparison, the aforementioned examples illuminate that a metric such as that used in MWHP PCDTW can serve as an intermediate method between a structure-based method and a sequence-based method, thus allowing for the discrimination of homologous versus convergent relationships on the structural end of the spectrum and the minimization of homoplastic noise on the sequence-based end of the spectrum. The examples presented herein are meant to demonstrate the complex and potentially synergistic use of these three methods in the analysis of proteins spanning from high to low amino acid identity, both within and among families and subfamilies. Additional examples are included in S1 of the Supplementary Materials.

Determination of Ability to Discriminate Against Random/Convergent Similarity

Dixson and Azad previously produced a dataset containing in silico evolved and random homologs of template sequences from 943 SCOP families (Dixson and Azad 2025). Their randomly evolved sequences were used to represent a simulated convergent dataset. This was possible because, following the production of random sequences, each sequence was homology modeled using templates of equal length. This resulted in many structures that, when evaluated structurally, were well within accepted distances for remote homologs despite having very low identities. This is consistent with what would be expected for structurally convergent proteins. We used that dataset to assess the ability of All-Atom Root Mean Square Deviation (RMSD), Fident, MWHP PCDTW, and template modeling score (TM-Score) (Zhang and Skolnick 2004) to discriminate between random/convergent and divergent similarity. Both Fident and TM-Score can be used in the calculation of the distance matrices used by FT for structure-based phylogenetic reconstruction. The ability of MWHP PCDTW versus All-Atom RMSD to discriminate between random and divergent similarity was previously assessed, showing that MWHP PCDTW distance is a better discriminant (Dixson and Azad 2025). MWHP PCDTW and All-Atom RMSD assessment was duplicated here for the purpose of comparison to Fident and TM-Score. The results of this assessment (Fig. 3) show that MWHP PCDTW has greater discriminatory power with regard to random/convergent versus divergent evolution of protein sequences with limited overlap in the distributions of MWHP PCDTW distances in the 0% to 10% identity range and no overlap in the 10% to 20% identity range. On the other hand, All-Atom RMSD, Fident, and TM-Score show greater regions of overlap in the random versus divergent distributions for both 0% to 10% and 10% to 20% identity bins. Despite the very apparent benefit of using MWHP PCDTW versus the other metrics in comparing random/convergent sequences, all methods have some discriminatory power, as evidenced by the low P-values for both identity bins. Of general note is that Fident distributions were unusually broad across all identity bins as compared with the other three metrics.

Probabilistic Assessment of Convergent Evolution

One of the largest obstacles to the use of phenotypic data in the accurate determination of phylogenetic topology is the error introduced by phenotypic convergence. Structure, whether molecular or macro-morphological, is more susceptible to inaccurate phylogenetic inference as a result of convergence than are inferences based on or derived from the amino acid sequence (e.g. MWHP PCDTW) (Wiens et al. 2003; Zou and Zhang 2016). For this reason, it was prudent to address the probability of convergence in the structural dendrograms created using FT. To that end, we used a simulated random/convergent dataset to train a Support Vector Classifier (SVC) to discriminate between random/convergent and divergent proteins using both MWHP PCDTW distance and amino acid identity as features. We then classified each CYP using that model and recorded the probability of each belonging to the random/convergent data. Additionally, we calculated the absolute normalized amino acid identity difference between each leaf and its nearest five neighbors in the FT tree. In cases where the probability of a sequence coming from the random/convergent dataset is high and the absolute difference in terms of amino acid identity between a sequence and its five closest neighbors in the FT dendrogram is also high, it can be assumed that there is a high likelihood that the sequence has experienced some level of random/convergent evolution.

Using the SVC, 126 of the 1,630 leaves in the FT dendrogram were identified as having a greater than 80% chance of coming from a random/convergent dataset. In three cases, both the SVC probability and absolute differences in the standard alignment indicated that the FT placement of a leaf may be incorrect due to convergence or random similarity. Furthermore, the biological process and molecular function data are not fully consistent with the surrounding proteins, thus supporting the proposal of incorrect placement. The predicted local distance difference test (pLDDT) values also indicate that the AF models for the three proteins are relatively high-quality models, thus minimizing the possibility that low model quality resulted in incorrect placement. This validates the use of these structural data. A small number of structural models showed low pLDDT values. Only 10 of the 1,630 leaves showed both a high probability of being random/convergent placements (≥0.80) and also low model quality (pLDDT < 70). Therefore, it is unlikely that model quality or overall dataset quality contributed heavily to potentially erroneous FT placements. The full dendrogram can be viewed in an interactive form at https://itol.embl.de/shared/realtorjamied, and a highly collapsed version of that tree, showing the three top candidates for random/convergent evolution, is shown in Fig. 4.

Code Execution Time Assessment

The time to construct a dendrogram from 1,630 CYP proteins using FT, as deployed in the previously described Google Colab, was 320 min, the time to construct a dendrogram from the same 1,630 proteins using MWHP PCDTW was 19 min, and the time to produce a multiple sequence alignment and dendrogram using CO was 31 min. Note that this was not meant to be an exhaustive comparison of the raw efficiency of these algorithms, rather it was meant only to be a comparison of the most readily accessible versions of these algorithms.

Discussion

Sequence-Based Homology Determination

Determining the nature and extent of remotely homologous relationships among diverse protein family members is a, challenging but necessary, task that can provide functional insight for drug target discovery (Lepesheva et al. 2008), synthetic biology, conservation, and many other applications. Some protein families are quite diverse with many remote homologs. Analyses using pairwise or multiple sequence alignment methods could be difficult in the Twilight Zone because poor alignments can lead to unreliable homology inferences and increased false positives (Rost 1999; Jaroszewski et al. 2000, 2002).

Owing to the low expense of sequencing and well-developed sequence alignment methods, pairwise or multiple sequence alignment-based analyses are likely the most widely used approach for homology inference (Altschul et al. 1990; Sievers and Higgins 2018; Johnson et al. 2024). Despite widespread use, the unreliability of Twilight Zone homology inferences using sequence alignment methods poses a formidable challenge (Rost 1997, 1999). At those levels of identity, protein structures or the physicochemical properties of the amino acids may serve as better evolutionary landmarks since they are less sensitive to the effects of substitution saturation than the sequence data (Lolkema and Slotboom 1998; Moi et al. 2023; Dixson and Azad 2025). Herein, we have used sequence-based, structure-based, and physicochemical property-based analyses synergistically to draw inferences regarding the CYP protein superfamily. This included both manual comparisons of the phylogenetic trees produced with each method and algorithmic means to highlight locations of disagreement between the methods.

Structure-Based Homology Determination

Homology inference from protein structures is possible because shared ancestry often leads to similar structure and function (Dixson and Azad 2025; Moi et al. 2023; Orengo and Bateman 2014, 21). The connection between amino acid sequence and structure was proposed half a century ago (Anfinsen 1973). However, it was not until recently that protein structure could be reliably inferred from just the amino acid sequence (Jumper et al. 2021a; Varadi et al. 2022). This has produced a flood of protein structures and necessitated the development of methods to analyze those structures phylogenetically (Moi et al. 2023). Despite the previous lack of protein structures, a few studies dating as far back as the 1960s attempted the use of structural phylogenetic methods (Goodman 1962; Eventoff and Rossmann 1975). In recent decades or years, there have been several limited scope structural phylogenetic inquiries (Johnson et al. 1990; Bujnicki 2000; Garau et al. 2005; Agarwal et al. 2009; Finn et al. 2017). Other than those few studies, structural phylogenetic methodology was not widely explored until recently because of the limited availability of high-quality structures.

Owing to the development of methods to reliably infer large numbers of protein structures from just the amino acid sequence, large-scale structural phylogenetic techniques are now under development and use (Moi et al. 2023). While structural phylogenetics offers the ability to reach much further back into the evolutionary record than sequence alignment methods, it has one apparent weakness. Structure is a phenotypic trait, and like any phenotypic trait, it is potentially subject to convergent evolution. While some might argue that molecular convergence is rare, some authors suggest that it is quite prevalent (Castoe et al. 2010; Parker et al. 2013). In fact, the body of work regarding molecular convergence in fungi and echolocating mammals suggests that not only is molecular convergence much more pervasive than once thought, but that it also occurs over far greater evolutionary timespans and with greater penetrance with respect to genomes (Parker et al. 2013; Merényi et al. 2020). This makes it incumbent on the scientific community to consider the possibility of not only the state of convergence but also the extent of convergence when performing protein structure-based phylogenetic analyses.

Physicochemical Property-Based Homology Determination

The physicochemical properties of amino acids have long been recognized as having potential applications in the development of structural phylogenies without actually having a protein structure to analyze (Lolkema and Slotboom 1998; Liu and Wang 2006; Dixson and Azad 2021, 2025; Dixson et al. 2024). This is possible because it is the physicochemical properties of amino acids in proteins that are subject to selection. The reason for this is that the physicochemical properties of the amino acids give rise to the structures of proteins (Anfinsen 1973). Physicochemical approaches potentially have the benefit of combining some of the aspects of sequence alignment-based methods with some of the benefits of structure-based methods. These are most notably processing time and the ability to reach into the Twilight Zone, where sequence alignment-based techniques could be more error-prone (Rost 1999).

Physicochemical methods may also provide increased resolution of convergence. A recent benchmarking study, using simulated data, demonstrated that MWHP PCDTW could be used to reliably infer relatedness between large swaths of protein structures using only the amino acid sequence. MWHP PCDTW, when compared to structural metrics, was also able to better discriminate between random/convergent sequences and sequences synthetically evolved to maintain structure, while at the same time experiencing Twilight Zone level divergence (Dixson and Azad 2025). One potential weakness of this technique is that it only considers the physicochemical properties of molecular weight and hydrophobicity. Those two properties provide a good approximation of the physicochemical relatedness among proteins (Dixson and Azad 2025). However, to provide optimal resolution across a broad spectrum of phylogenies, it will be necessary to determine the actual physicochemical properties that selection has most heavily acted upon for each residue. Research is ongoing in our lab to incorporate that information.

Synergistic Phylogenetic Reconstruction

We hypothesized that structural comparisons, such as those used in FT, would be susceptible to the confounding effects of random/convergent evolution. Those effects result in situations where two structures, which do not have a shared ancestry, exhibit higher than expected structural similarity and thus an unexpected topological placement in a phylogenetic tree. This was confirmed in our analysis of random/convergent homologs and derived structural homologs (Fig. 3), where none of the structure-based metrics were able to distinguish between random/convergent and evolved homologs better than MWHP PCDTW. This is important because, although FT has proven highly accurate in recovering known phylogenetic relationships, both with sub- and super-Twilight Zone identities (Moi et al. 2023), in certain instances, convergent relationships could likely be relatively rare and, therefore, could easily be overlooked in the evaluation of broad phylogenies such as the CYP protein superfamily. Furthermore, since MWHP PCDTW is a hybrid technique, combining physicochemical vectors and dynamic time warping (Anderson and Gaby 1983; Permanasari et al. 2019; Dixson and Azad 2021, 2025), it may also impart the ability to discern true divergence and molecular-level convergence.

A simple conceptual test for convergence is the presence of structural and/or functional conservation with an unexpectedly elevated level of amino acid sequence divergence. To evaluate this, we calculated the normalized alignment-based distance between each CYP and its five closest neighbors in the FT dendrogram. We then calculated the absolute difference between that distance and the FT distance. Those absolute distances were then mapped as a data band on the FT dendrogram containing 1,630 CYPs (Fig. 4). This band illuminates three leaves whose placement seems to be out of place. Upon closer examination of those three proteins, we found that two of them (UniProt Acc. Num.: Q5Y750 and Q6ZSU1) are from suspected pseudogenes, and the third protein (UniProt Acc. Num.: P9WKH3) is from a bacterium. This bacterial protein clusters with fungal proteins in the FT dendrogram.

We also applied an SVC trained to discriminate between random/convergent similarity and similarity expected due to common descent using a simulated dataset. This classifier indicated a high likelihood that each of the three proteins represents randomly or convergently similar proteins. Another indication that FT may have misplaced these three proteins is the fact that the biological process and molecular function data for each of them are not in full agreement with the surrounding proteins in the FT dendrogram. Additionally, their placement using physicochemical and sequence-based phylogenetic reconstruction methodology (Fig. 2 and https://itol.embl.de/shared/realtorjamied) places each of these three proteins quite distant from their FT placement. These are only the most extreme examples we identified where multiple lines of evidence point to incorrect topological placement by FT. These may therefore be indicative of random similarity, convergent similarity, or just artifacts arising due to unidentified limitations inherent to FT.

There are 123 more examples in the full tree at https://itol.embl.de/shared/realtorjamied where machine learning classification indicated potential random/convergent evolution. In many of those cases, differences between the amino acid identity-based distances and the FT distances, as well as biological process and molecular function data, also indicate potential misplacement. This is supportive of our previous assertion that structure-based metrics may improperly place certain proteins due to structural convergence. Regarding some placements, this could alternatively be a consequence of methodological artifacts. In some cases, where the SVC indicates potential convergence or random similarity, model quality may have contributed to misplacement in the FT dendrogram. This is supported by the observation that some of the protein structures for the leaves flagged with the SVC also had pLDDT values indicating low model quality. This highlights that while FT may be a good structural phylogenetic reconstruction technique, it depends on the quality of models used. The models herein were produced using AF. Owing to the reliance of AF on multiple sequence alignment, the extent of amino acid divergence may have contributed to AlphaFold producing lower quality models in certain cases (Jumper et al. 2021a, 2021b). This underscores the need for complementary techniques for remote homology inference and mapping of that homology that are independent of multiple sequence alignment.

Convergent Evolution

One might argue that convergence is rare overall and should therefore be rare in CYPs. That argument is largely based on conjecture since the study of convergence and especially, molecular convergence, is in its infancy. When traits are molecular in nature, the study of convergence is complicated by incomplete annotation of protein records in the molecular databases, homoplasy, and an incomplete understanding of the selection process with regard to physicochemical properties (Dixson and Azad 2025). The three extreme examples we gave of potential convergence are only emphasized because both physicochemical classification and pairwise amino acid alignment indicate misplacement of the structures in the FT dendrogram. Less extreme examples certainly do exist. The use of a machine learning-based classifier is important here because it allows us to assign a probability that each of these proteins represents a convergent or randomly similar protein. This is akin to bootstrap values, on a multiple sequence alignment-based phylogenetic tree, in that this metric can be used to highlight locations of minimal topological confidence. In such cases, it is imperative that additional data be incorporated to fully decipher the extent of misplacement.

Another argument that might arise is that the misplacements shown in our dendrograms are indicative of horizontal transfer events rather than convergence. We do not posit that HGT could not have contributed to such misplacements. However, HGT is a transfer of genetic material, and convergence is the evolution of a similar phenotype despite a different evolutionary origin and path. In other words, a horizontal transfer may be considered a source of variation that can result in unexpected placements in a phylogenetic tree but may also contribute to convergent evolution. The two concepts are related and should be interpreted in that light. Another topic that is often misunderstood concerns the extent of convergence. Convergence can occur even when two proteins only recently diverged. In other words, any two or more divergent protein sequences can converge upon a common trait, and as we presented in the introduction, even molecular reversals can be considered convergence (Kaltenbach et al. 2015). The key to understanding the extent of convergent evolution of proteins is the development of metrics that can measure the probability of convergence having occurred. We have shown both herein and previously that a simulated dataset can serve as an acceptable surrogate for randomly and convergently similar proteins, when assigning the probability of a protein being randomly or convergently evolved (Dixson and Azad 2025).

Metadata-Based Identification of Topological Misplacement

In addition to the probabilistic and alignment-based inferences of convergence, we presented multiple metadata-based inferences that suggest misplacement and/or convergence. This was apparent because similar structures clustered together when their sequences, functions, names, and/or physicochemical profiles suggested a different evolutionary trajectory. As structures become increasingly complex, and the likelihood of extreme convergence decreases, the likelihood of convergence among closely related base structures with specialization of function may increase (Kaltenbach et al. 2015). There are many reasons to suspect that convergence could have taken part in the evolution of the extant CYPs, including that the basal CYP51s are an ancient CYP family with conservation across eukaryotes and prokaryotes and that the observed conservation may, in some cases, have resulted from convergence following divergence. This type of conservation is often indicative of an essential function shared among kingdoms. In the case of CYP51s, the essential function is in the production of the sterols needed for cell membranes (Rezen et al. 2004). The maintenance of that function likely requires a high level of selective pressure. High levels of selective pressure are often implicated in convergent evolution (Chikina et al. 2016).

It has also been posited that HGT and convergent evolution may be essential to the maintenance of function with regard to CYP51s (Celia-Sanchez et al. 2022). This is especially apparent in fungal CYPs, where convergence for sterol production may have allowed fungi to evolve alternate proteins with slight functional variations, providing resistance to widely used antifungal drugs that target CYP51s (Celia-Sanchez et al. 2022). This provides additional circumstantial evidence for convergent evolution in the CYP51 family and more broadly in the CYPs.

Reassessment of CYPs

The MWHP PCDTW dendrogram in Fig. 1 differs slightly from the other dendrograms in that it shows a bifurcation of the ancestral CYPs. This difference is easily explained between the FT with AlphaFold predicted structure (FTAFDB) dendrogram and the MWHP PCDTW dendrogram as potential convergence, but a similar difference exists between the MWHP PCDTW dendrogram and the CO dendrogram. While the use of default CO neighbor joining trees by the CYP nomenclature naming committee has been standard practice (Nelson 2006), it should not be considered a robust solution for the placement of CYPs with regard to the overall evolution of the superfamily. Most of the comparisons among CYP families represent sub-Twilight Zone identities, which are notoriously hard to properly align in multiple sequence alignments (Jaroszewski et al. 2000, 2002; Chen et al. 2018; Al-Fatlawi et al. 2023). Furthermore, it has been shown that pairwise alignment is much more reliable in making distance-based remote homology inferences (Chen et al. 2018). The CO multiple sequence alignment of the 1,630 CYPs is highly gapped with an alignment length of 5,895, a mean of 1,485 gaps at each position of the alignment, and a mean of 5,373 gaps in each sequence of the alignment. The mean length of CYPs in the alignment is 521 amino acids, meaning that there are ∼10× as many gaps in the alignment as there are actual residues. Such highly gapped multiple sequence alignments can lead to erroneous trees using likelihood and distance-based techniques for phylogenetic reconstructions. Furthermore, distance estimates from highly gapped alignments are likely to negatively impact tree reconstruction, using neighbor-joining, since it is difficult to determine the exact nature and extent of multiple consecutive insertions and/or deletions. Erroneous distance estimates from faulty alignments can have confounding effects on final topology from neighbor-joining analyses (Saitou and Nei 1987), and thus the general consensus is that highly gapped alignments should be avoided in neighbor-joining analyses (Talavera and Castresana 2007). Furthermore, trees produced from such highly gapped multiple alignments could be unreliable, and if alignment-based approaches are to be used, pairwise alignment may provide more meaningful distances when remote homology is involved (Chen et al. 2018). With that being said, current phylogenetic placement and thus naming of CYPs should be further investigated using structure and physicochemical techniques, to unveil what may be many misnamed/misplaced CYPs from an evolutionary perspective. Herein, this has been conducted for the reviewed sequences that also have inferred structures in the AF Protein Structure Database. Further investigation should be made into the ∼100× more unreviewed CYP structures in the AF Protein Structure Database. Key to that reassessment is consideration of the FT dendrograms along with the MWHP PCDTW and identity-based metrics used, in the full and collapsed versions of Fig. 4, to identify potentially problematic placements.

Final Thoughts

While there may be some difficulties with the generalized implementation of FT as a definitive method for building evolutionary trees, it has been demonstrated to be an effective tool for inferring relationships among Twilight Zone remote homologs. There may also be a benefit to combining FT, MWHP PCDTW, and pairwise sequence identity to annotate FT topologies, highlighting potentially erroneous placements. Our hypothesis that FT would present potentially convergent topological placements and that MWHP PCDTW, along with sequence identity, would allow for identification of those placements was confirmed both through comparisons made using simulated data, amino acid identities, and through manual examination of the topological placement of actual CYPs using FT, MWHP PCDTW, and CO. In summary, both FT and MWHP PCDTW are valuable tools in the quest to quantify and map homologous relationships using methods that are less sensitive to homoplasy than standard alignment-based techniques. As the scientific community digs deeper into the realm of previously elusive homologous relationships, it is prudent to combine methods, such as these, to not only map structural relationships, as FT does, but also to identify structural relationships that may not be strictly evolutionary, as MWHP PCDTW does. In cases where FT and MWHP PCDTW disagree and divergent taxa cluster together in FT topologies, convergent evolution might be considered as an explanation. This was the case for several examples presented herein.

When attempting to identify remote homologs, it is tempting to assume that FT or any similar structure-based technique is the optimal solution. However, we have demonstrated herein that FT topologies could be subject to errors conferred by convergent and/or random similarity. To date, no phylogenetic reconstruction technique has proven to be the penultimate solution in inferring evolutionary relationships. In a broad sense, this is the reason why researchers often use multiple techniques and then compare the results of each (Dixson et al. 2003). The reconstruction of phylogenetic trees, including remote homologs, is no exception, and we have shown how FT, CO, and MWHP PCDTW can be used in conjunction to infer evolutionary relationships.

Having the most accurate evolutionary map possible for the CYPs is necessary in properly naming the CYPs and for the maximum utility of the available data. Most topological placements in the FT dendrogram show no indication of misplacement. However, the problematic placements that we identified, by combining structure, physicochemical and sequence-based analyses, raise the possibility of incorrect placement. That assumption is also supported by biological process, molecular function, and naming discrepancies. CYP analysis from a functional standpoint is highly specialized. It is advisable that, as each CYP family is reanalyzed and newly sequenced proteins are named, the structural phylogenetic placement and potential for random/convergent similarity are also considered. Furthermore, the methods used herein could be used for future evaluation of other protein families suspected to contain convergent proteins. These include, but are not limited to, the serine proteases (Brodie 2010), the antifreeze proteins (Chen et al. 1997; Sackton and Clark 2019), and the alcohol dehydrogenases (McQuillan et al. 2022).

Materials and Methods

Comparative Analysis of Highly Verified SCOP Structural/Sequence Data

The SCOP database available at https://www.ebi.ac.uk/pdbe/scop (July 6, 2022 release) is a manually curated database of protein domains and their experimentally-determined structures (Li et al. 2016). There are 163 members of the CYP superfamily in the SCOP database. Five of those records were not retrievable from the AF database (https://alphafold.com/) (accessed October 5, 2024) (Jumper et al. 2021a; Varadi et al. 2022) using the FT (Moi et al. 2023) Colab at https://colab.research.google.com/github/DessimozLab/fold_tree/ (accessed October 5, 2024). Those records were excluded from our dataset. For each of 158/163 records that were used, the corresponding sequence, UniProt accession number, and PDB identifier/chain were mined from SCOP for further analyses. The PDB identifier/chain was used to download experimentally determined structures for each of the records from the PDB. Those structures were then used to construct structural distance dendrograms using FT (Moi et al. 2023) at https://neurosnap.ai/service/Foldtree (accessed October 3, 2024). The accession numbers for those records were used as input for the FT Colab (https://colab.research.google.com/github/DessimozLab/fold_tree/ (accessed October 5, 2024)) to construct a structure-based distance dendrogram for de novo predicted structures. The sequences were used for MWHP PCDTW analysis (Dixson and Azad 2021, 2025). The authors of FT reported that TM-Score and LDDT were unreliable in recovering benchmark phylogenetic topologies (Moi et al. 2023). For that reason, only Fident (FT measure) was used in constructing our dendrograms. In addition to structural and physicochemical analyses, CO-based neighbor-joining analysis of the sequences was conducted to construct distance-based dendrograms (Sievers and Higgins 2018). This analysis was conducted using the CO web server available at https://www.ebi.ac.uk/jdispatcher/msa/clustalo (accessed October 5, 2024).

Comparative Analysis of Large Numbers of Reviewed Protein Sequences

To assess the ability to infer the overall evolutionary landscape for CYPs, 1,630 CYP sequences that were available both as reviewed records in the UniProt database and also as predicted structures in the AF database were analyzed using FT, MWHP PCDTW, and CO in the same manner as for the SCOP structural/sequence data as described above. An analysis of experimental structures was not performed here due to a lack of availability and because the resulting phylogenetic topology using the 158 CYPs was essentially identical between the use of experimental and de novo predicted structures. Gene ontology terms for each record were retrieved from UniProt via API. Molecular function and biological process terms were then segregated into 20 and 35 clusters, respectively, using the GOATOOLS Python package (Klopfenstein et al. 2018) and K-means clustering as implemented in the SciKitLearn Python package (Pedregosa et al. 2011). The appropriate number of clusters for biological process and molecular function was determined by hierarchical clustering and manual examination of the UPGMA dendrogram produced using pairwise Resnik similarity with the basic ontology matrix (go-basic.obo) available at https://geneontology.org/docs/download-ontology/ (accessed October 10, 2024). The clusters and also taxa classifications were then used to annotate dendrograms produced using FT, MWHP PCDTW, and CO with the Interactive Tree of Life tree editing software available at https://itol.embl.de (accessed October 24, 2024) (Letunic and Bork 2021). The dendrograms were exported in both circular and unrooted form.

Topological Comparison of Trees

All trees were manually assessed and compared to identify interesting patterns/placements. In addition to manual assessment of the topologies, all trees within the 2 analysis scopes (158 CYPs and 1,630 CYPs) were subjected to pairwise CN metric and inverse normalized RF metric comparisons using the phangorn (RF) and dendextend (CN) R packages (Schliep 2011; Galili 2015).

The RF metric measures divergence between phylogenetic trees by calculating the proportion of unshared bifurcations to the maximum possible. In this way, it is a measure of dissimilarity, whereby a value of 0 indicates that the trees are identical (in terms of their bifurcations or splits) and a value of 1 indicates that they are very different (Galili 2015). While the RF metric accounts for overall tree structure divergence, the CN metric accounts for cladal membership similarity. The CN metric represents the proportion of clades that have the exact same leaves in both trees being compared. In this way, a value of 0 indicates that the trees are topologically as dissimilar as possible, and a value of 1 indicates that they are as similar as possible (Schliep 2011).

Determination of Ability to Discriminate Against Random Similarity

To assess the relative ability of FT and MWHP PCDTW to detect random structural similarity, we used a simulated dataset containing both evolved and randomly created homologous sequences to members of SCOP protein domain families. The synthetic evolution of this simulated dataset was described previously (Dixson and Azad 2025). Briefly, the algorithm has an objective function that minimizes the amount of divergence, with respect to molecular weight and hydrophobicity, while concurrently maximizing the amount of sequence-level divergence. The dataset that was used in this study is publicly available at https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.15555455. This dataset is comprised of 11,530 sequences/structures (Dixson and Azad 2025). Those structures were inferred from 1,684 randomly produced sequences and their homology models and 9,846 evolved sequences and their homology models, collectively representing 943 SCOP families.

Each of the randomly evolved sequences was subjected to homology modeling using a protein domain of equal length as the template. This resulted in many structures that are well within commonly applied thresholds for structural metrics when determining structural similarity of homologs. Additionally, all of the sequences showed very low amino acid identity (<15%). For this reason, the randomly produced sequences and their associated structures are appropriate as a synthetic surrogate dataset for both truly convergent sequences and sequences with random similarity to their template (Dixson and Azad 2025). The use of a synthetic dataset was necessary and appropriate because there is no large-scale convergent evolution dataset consisting of proteins with validated convergent relationships. The use of simulated data in such cases is both common and appropriate (Ravenhall et al. 2015; Kohnert and Kreutz 2025). Additionally, simulated data is used in many of the common bioinformatics competitions, including the widely known Kaggle competitions (https://www.kaggle.com/).

FT uses Foldseek (FS) to develop a distance matrix between taxa. FS uses one of several distance measures, of which Fident has proven most reliable in terms of taxonomic congruence (Moi et al. 2023). We calculated the mean forward/reverse pairwise TM-Score, as calculated using the tmtools Python library (Zhang and Skolnick 2005), and the Fident score, using the command line version of FS (van Kempen et al. 2024), as metrics to evaluate the ability of FT to detect random similarity. For All-Atom RMSD, Fident, TM-Score, and MWHP PCDTW, the full dataset described above was used. Raw distances were normalized to a 0 to 1 scale if they were not already on that scale, with 0 indicating the greatest level of similarity. Pairwise alignments of random sequences in the simulated dataset indicated that they lie within the 0% to 15% identity range. Therefore, hypothesis testing was performed on the 0% to 10% identity bins and the 10% to 20% identity bins. The Jarque–Bera test (Jarque and Bera 1987) was utilized to determine normality. Normality was not confirmed for the majority of the two bins across all methods. Therefore, comparison of random/convergent versus evolved divergence was evaluated using the nonparametric Mann–Whitney test (Mann and Whitney 1947).

Probabilistic Assessment of Convergent Evolution

To assess the probability of a topological placement being representative of convergent evolution, we used a machine learning model trained on the aforementioned simulated dataset containing random/convergent and simulated remote homologs. Since the distributions representing random/convergent and evolved sequences had minimal overlap in terms of MWHP PCDTW and amino acid identity, we used those data along with data from our FT dendrogram to build Logistic Regression and SVC models using the ScikitLearn Python package (Pedregosa et al. 2011). SVC and LR classifiers were 10-fold cross-validated. The SVC showed a mean F1 value of 0.867(±0.023), and the LR classifier showed a mean F1 value of 0.789 (±0.026) for discriminating between random/convergent and evolved data. Owing to it having a greater F1 value, the SVC was used to classify each leaf in the FT dendrogram, of 1,630 CYPs, by calculating the MWHP PCDTW between each leaf and its nearest neighbor in the dendrogram. Standard alignment was also conducted for each pair using the PairwiseAligner module of biopython with a Blosum45 matrix, a −10 gap opening penalty, and a −0.5 gap extension penalty (Cock et al. 2009).

In addition to calculating the probabilities using an SVC, the standard alignments of each leaf in the FT tree to its five nearest neighbors were calculated and compared to the mean FT branch distance for the same five comparisons. Both FT distance and alignment distances were normalized to a 0 to 1 scale, and the absolute difference between them was calculated. This measure was used to represent the degree of difference between FT distances for each leaf and distances based on standard alignments. The probabilities and the absolute differences were used to identify sequences that potentially experienced convergent evolution.

Code Execution Time Assessment

To assess the time required to complete the algorithmic creation of dendrograms using FT, MWHP PCDTW, and CO, we recorded clock time while processing the aforementioned 1,630 CYP sequences. FT was executed on the previously mentioned Google Colab with a paid subscription, MWHP PCDTW was executed on a Windows 11 laptop computer with an I5 2.7 GHz processor and 8.0GB RAM, and CO analysis was conducted on the previously described CO web server. Although this is not a direct comparison due to platform differences, algorithmic methods should be evaluated in their most accessible forms, specifically those that most researchers are likely to use. It is clear that each of these methods could likely be optimized for various platforms. FT in particular is optimized to evaluate large datasets using a Snakemake pipeline (Köster and Rahmann 2012). That creates additional overhead, which reduces its efficiency on smaller datasets. Here, we intend to give researchers an idea of the time that each method will require in its most commonly used form.
