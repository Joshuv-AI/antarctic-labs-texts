Correlation between human composition analysis and risk of dyslipidemia in health examination
Authors: Li Han, Yanping Feng, Yuexia Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aims to explore the correlation between body composition and dyslipidemia in a health examination population.MethodsThe study subjects were 376 individuals undergoing health examinations at community clinics (185 males and 191 females). Body composition parameters including fat mass, fat-free mass, body fat percentage, waist-to-hip ratio, visceral fat level, muscle mass, skeletal muscle mass, body mass index (BMI), and basal metabolic rate were measured using bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA). Dyslipidemia was diagnosed according to the “Chinese Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of Adult Dyslipidemia (2016 Revision)”. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to examine the association between body composition parameters and dyslipidemia.ResultsThe dyslipidemia group showed significantly higher values than the normal lipid group in fat mass (23.12 ± 4.24 vs. 18.29 ± 4.35 kg), body fat percentage (27.28 ± 4.16 vs. 23.26 ± 4.28%), and visceral fat level (4.16 ± 1.13 vs. 2.15 ± 1.05) (all P < 0.05). Multivariate logistic regression analysis, adjusted for age and sex, revealed that each 1-level increase in visceral fat level (adjusted OR = 1.22, 95%CI 1.15–1.30) and each 1% increase in body fat percentage (adjusted OR = 1.18, 95%CI 1.10–1.27) were positively associated with dyslipidemia risk, whereas muscle mass showed an independent inverse association (adjusted OR = 0.94, 95%CI 0.90–0.99). Stratified analysis indicated that visceral fat level remained a significant risk factor in both males (adjusted OR = 1.25, 95%CI 1.16–1.35) and females (adjusted OR = 1.18, 95%CI 1.09–1.28), and exhibited superior predictive strength for elevated non-HDL-C in individuals with abdominal obesity (adjusted OR = 1.38, 95%CI 1.25–1.52).ConclusionVisceral fat level and body fat percentage are independent risk factors for dyslipidemia, while muscle mass exhibits a protective effect. Bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) of body composition can provide important evidence for dyslipidemia risk assessment in health examinations.Keywords: physical examination, human composition, dyslipidemia, risk, correlation

Introduction

Dyslipidemia is a core modifiable risk factor for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), requiring early identification for primary prevention. While current practice focuses on low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (non-HDL-C)—encompassing all atherogenic lipoproteins—has emerged as a more comprehensive risk indicator [1–2]. Large prospective cohorts, such as the Framingham Offspring Study, established non-HDL-C as an independent predictor of cardiovascular events [3], with particular superiority in metabolic syndrome populations [4]. Elevated non-HDL-C in youth can predict cardiovascular risk decades later [5], supporting its designation as a secondary lipid target in guidelines [6].

Dyslipidemia pathogenesis is closely linked to metabolic status, where body composition plays a key regulatory role. Traditional measures like body mass index (BMI) cannot differentiate fat-muscle distribution or identify ectopic visceral fat. Notably, 15%–30% of normal-BMI individuals have excess visceral fat and metabolic abnormalities [7]. Visceral fat accumulation promotes lipid disorders by releasing free fatty acids into the portal circulation, enhancing hepatic VLDL synthesis and atherogenic lipoprotein production [8]. Each standard unit increase in visceral fat area raises triglycerides by 37% [9]. Conversely, low muscle mass reduces basal metabolism and impairs lipoprotein lipase activity, worsening triglyceride clearance; each 1 kg muscle loss increases hypertriglyceridemia risk 1.3-fold [10]. Body composition parameters such as visceral fat level and body fat percentage show stronger associations with insulin resistance and dyslipidemia than BMI [11], underscoring their clinical relevance.

Body composition analysis via bioelectrical impedance (BIA) offers non-invasive, rapid assessment suitable for health examinations. It enables early detection of high-risk “metabolically obese normal weight” (MONW) individuals, overcoming a key limitation of conventional screening [12]. Nevertheless, evidence on body composition-dyslipidemia relationships in large health examination cohorts remains limited. This study therefore enrolled participants aged 20–80 years to represent the typical adult screening population and capture age-related variations in body composition and lipids. Using standardized BIA, we investigated associations between parameters—body fat percentage, visceral fat level, muscle mass—and dyslipidemia, aiming to inform improved early screening strategies.

Objects and methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted to examine the associations between body composition parameters and dyslipidemia risk. The study enrolled 376 participants from individuals undergoing routine health examinations at community medicine clinics between January 2023 and January 2024. Participants were recruited consecutively from the health examination centers of collaborating community clinics. The age range of 20–80 years was selected to represent the typical adult population undergoing health examinations and to capture age-related variations in body composition and lipid profiles.

Inclusion criteria

(1) Age 20–80 years; (2) Underwent health examinations at community medicine clinics between January 2023 and January 2024; (3) Voluntarily participated and provided written informed consent.

Exclusion criteria

(1) Abnormal liver/kidney function (ALT/AST > 2× upper limit of normal; eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m²); (2) Use of corticosteroids/sex hormones within the past 6 months; (3) Comorbid diabetes, cardiovascular disease, thyroid dysfunction, or malignancy; (4) Pregnancy or lactation; (5) History of or current psychiatric disorders; (6) Allergy to any equipment/reagents used in the study.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Jishuitan Hospital, Capital Medical University (Approval No.: [K2024] No. [279]-01), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

General information

Comprehensive clinical data were collected for all participants. Basic characteristics included age, sex, and blood pressure measurements. Anthropometric measurements included height, weight, and body composition parameters. Laboratory assessments included lipid profiles and liver/kidney function tests. Blood pressure was measured using standardized protocols with participants in a seated position after 5 min of rest. Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg, or current use of antihypertensive medications. Information regarding current medication use was systematically collected, including the use of lipid-lowering drugs (statins, fibrates, ezetimibe) and anti-obesity medications. Participants were specifically asked about medication use during the health examination interview.

Research methods

Body composition was assessed using bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) with the InBody 770 device (Biospace Co., Ltd., Seoul, Korea). All measurements were conducted in a temperature-controlled, clean, and quiet room. Participants underwent BIA under standardized conditions: fasting state, after urination, and wearing lightweight clothing. The measurement procedure followed manufacturer specifications: participants stood naturally in a relaxed posture while electrodes were placed at specific hand and foot positions. The device automatically calculated fat mass, fat-free mass, body fat percentage, waist-to-hip ratio, visceral fat level, muscle mass, skeletal muscle mass, body mass index (BMI), and basal metabolic rate. Triplicate measurements were performed for each participant, with mean values used for statistical analysis.

Measurement Procedure: (1) Participant Registration: Recorded name, unique hospital ID number, height, date of birth, and gender. Documented measurement date and time. (2) Weight Measurement: Precisely measured body weight using a calibrated electronic scale. (3) BIA Setup: Performed device calibration and settings according to manufacturer specifications. 4)BIA Testing: Participants stood naturally in a relaxed posture. Electrodes were placed at specific hand and foot positions. A mild electrical current measured segmental impedance values. The device automatically calculated: Fat mass, Fat-free mass, Body fat percentage, Waist-to-hip ratio, Visceral fat level, Muscle mass, Skeletal muscle mass, Body mass index (BMI), Basal metabolic rate. 5) Data Analysis: Compared results against normative ranges. Generated comprehensive reports including: Measured values, Reference ranges, Clinical interpretations. 6) Quality Control: Maintained consistent measurement conditions across participants. Performed triplicate measurements for each participant. Used mean values for statistical analysis.

Blood sample processing and laboratory methods

Venous blood samples were collected after an overnight fast of at least 8 h. Serum samples were obtained by allowing blood to clot at room temperature for 30 min, followed by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 15 min. The resulting serum was aliquoted and stored at -80 °C until analysis. Lipid parameters including total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) were measured using enzymatic methods on an automated biochemistry analyzer. Non-HDL-C was calculated as TC minus HDL-C. Liver function (ALT, AST) and kidney function (estimated glomerular filtration rate, eGFR) were assessed using standard automated biochemical methods.

Diagnostic criteria​

Dyslipidemia was diagnosed according to the Chinese Guidelines on Prevention and Treatment of Dyslipidemia in Adults (2016 Revision) [11], with the following clinical classification criteria: Hypercholesterolemia: TC ≥ 6.22 mmol/L; Hypertriglyceridemia:​TG ≥ 2.26 mmol/L; Low HDL-C: HDL-C < 1.04 mmol/L; Hyper-LDL-C: LDL-C ≥ 4.14 mmol/L; High non-HDL-C: Non-HDL-C ≥ 4.9 mmol/L. Participants were classified as having dyslipidemia if they met any of the above criteria or had a prior dyslipidemia diagnosis from a district/county-level (or higher) hospital with current use of lipid-lowering medications.

The primary rationale for adopting the Chinese guidelines in this study is that they are specifically validated and recommended for the risk stratification and management of the adult Chinese population, which constitutes our study cohort. These criteria are tailored to the specific epidemiologic and metabolic characteristics of this population and are the standard used in clinical practice and health examinations within China, ensuring the direct applicability of our findings to the local healthcare context.

Data analysis​

All analyses were performed using SPSS 20.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables with normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x̄ ± SD) and compared using independent samples t-tests. Non-normally distributed data were presented as median (interquartile range) and analyzed with Mann-Whitney U tests. Categorical variables were reported as counts (percentages) and compared via chi-square tests. Multivariate logistic regression models were employed to examine associations between body composition parameters and dyslipidemia, adjusting for age and sex. Body composition parameters with P < 0.1 in univariate analyses and known confounders were included in initial models. Backward stepwise elimination was applied to remove non-significant variables (P > 0.05). Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF < 5). A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline clinical characteristics

The dyslipidemia group (n = 185) was comparable to the normal lipid group (n = 191) in terms of age and sex distribution. However, the dyslipidemia group had a significantly higher proportion of individuals with hypertension (28.1% vs. 15.7%, P = 0.003). As expected, all measured lipid parameters—total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (non-HDL-C)—were significantly elevated in the dyslipidemia group, while high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) was significantly lower (all P < 0.001). The use of lipid-lowering medications was significantly more frequent in the dyslipidemia group (18.9% vs. 2.1%, P < 0.001) (Table 1).

Comparison of body composition parameters between participants with dyslipidemia and those with normal lipid profiles

The dyslipidemia group exhibited significantly higher body composition values compared to the normal lipid group across multiple parameters (Table 2). Specifically, the dyslipidemia group had greater fat mass (23.12 ± 4.24 vs. 18.29 ± 4.35 kg, t = 5.029, P < 0.001), body fat percentage (27.28 ± 4.16% vs. 23.26 ± 4.28%, t = 4.260, P < 0.001), waist-to-hip ratio (1.09 ± 0.13 vs. 0.89 ± 0.21, t = 5.121, P < 0.001), visceral fat level (4.16 ± 1.13 vs. 2.15 ± 1.05, t = 8.241, P < 0.001), body mass index (26.19 ± 3.13 vs. 24.29 ± 2.19 kg/m², t = 3.146, P = 0.002), and basal metabolic rate (1559.21 ± 219.32 vs. 1465.21 ± 219.11 kcal/day, t = 3.708, P < 0.001). Conversely, the dyslipidemia group showed significantly lower fat-free mass (48.35 ± 9.24 vs. 52.19 ± 1.09 kg, t = 2.610, P = 0.011), muscle mass (29.09 ± 5.24 vs. 31.36 ± 5.14 kg, t = -2.312, P = 0.021), and skeletal muscle mass (24.38 ± 4.24 vs. 26.16 ± 4.29 kg, t = -2.104, P = 0.035).

Multivariate logistic regression analysis

Dyslipidemia was defined as the dependent variable (“yes” = 1, “no” = 0). Independent variables, including fat mass, fat-free mass, body fat percentage, waist-to-hip ratio, visceral fat level, muscle mass, skeletal muscle mass, body mass index (BMI), and basal metabolic rate, were treated as continuous variables and entered into the logistic regression model using their actual measured values. In the multivariate logistic regression model for the total population, after adjusting for age and sex, visceral fat level (per 1-level increase) (β = 0.201, adjusted OR = 1.22, 95% CI: 1.15–1.30, P < 0.001) and body fat percentage (β = 0.165, adjusted OR = 1.18, 95% CI: 1.10–1.27, P < 0.001) were independently and positively associated with dyslipidemia risk, while muscle mass showed an independent inverse association (β = − 0.062, adjusted OR = 0.94, 95% CI: 0.90–0.99, P = 0.018). Waist-to-hip ratio and basal metabolic rate were excluded from the final model due to multicollinearity (variance inflation factor > 5 and P > 0.05) (Table 5).

Stratified multivariate logistic regression analysis

Building upon the total population model, we conducted further stratified analyses by sex and abdominal obesity status, with adjustment for age (Table 6). Key findings include: Males: Visceral fat level (adjusted OR = 1.25, 95% CI: 1.16–1.35) and body fat percentage (adjusted OR = 1.15, 95% CI: 1.05–1.26) were independent risk factors for dyslipidemia (both P < 0.05). Muscle mass exhibited a protective effect (adjusted OR = 0.93, 95% CI: 0.87–0.99, P < 0.05). Females: Visceral fat level (adjusted OR = 1.18, 95% CI: 1.09–1.28) and body fat percentage (adjusted OR = 1.21, 95% CI: 1.10–1.33) remained significant risk factors (both P < 0.05). The protective effect of muscle mass was not statistically significant (adjusted OR = 0.95, 95% CI: 0.89–1.01, P > 0.05).

Non-abdominal obesity group: Body fat percentage (adjusted OR = 1.16, 95% CI: 1.06–1.27) and visceral fat level (adjusted OR = 1.20, 95% CI: 1.10–1.31) remained significant risk factors (both P < 0.05), with muscle mass demonstrating a protective effect (adjusted OR = 0.92, 95% CI: 0.86–0.99, P < 0.05).

In the abdominal obesity group, visceral fat level had superior predictive value for elevated non-HDL-C (defined as ≥ 4.9 mmol/L), with an adjusted OR of 1.38 (95% CI: 1.25–1.52, P < 0.05), compared to other metrics (Table 6).

Discussion​

This study systematically evaluated the association between body composition and dyslipidemia in a health examination population using BI). The results demonstrated significantly elevated fat mass, body fat percentage, waist-to-hip ratio, visceral fat level, and basal metabolic rate in the dyslipidemia group, while fat-free mass, muscle mass, and skeletal muscle mass showed inverse correlations. Notably, sex-stratified analyses revealed more pronounced effects of visceral fat and body fat percentage on dyslipidemia in males, whereas the protective role of fat-free mass in females did not reach statistical significance, suggesting sex-specific heterogeneity in metabolic risk profiles.

The core role and mechanisms of visceral fat​

This study revealed that each 1-unit increase in visceral fat level was associated with a 22% elevated risk of dyslipidemia (OR = 1.22), which aligns closely with CT imaging studies—Pacquelet et al. [9] demonstrated that per 1-SD increase in visceral fat area conferred 37% higher hypertriglyceridemia risk. This dose-response relationship may originate from the unique metabolic activity of visceral adipocytes: their released free fatty acids (FFA) directly enter the liver via the portal vein, stimulating hepatic synthesis of very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) and apolipoprotein B while inhibiting reverse cholesterol transport [13, 14]. More notably, this study is the first to demonstrate in a health screening population that visceral fat’s predictive strength for non-HDL-C (encompassing all atherogenic lipoproteins) significantly outperforms conventional metrics like LDL-C. These findings complement the 2025 JACC mega-study (n = 2.9 million) which confirmed lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] > 50 mg/dL correlates with increased subclinical atherosclerosis risk [15], suggesting visceral adiposity may serve as a metabolic amplifier of Lp(a)’s atherogenic effects [16].

Sex-specific heterogeneity in the metabolic protection of muscle mass​

This study revealed that increased muscle mass confers protective effects against dyslipidemia risk, with particularly pronounced benefits in non-abdominally obese individuals. The underlying mechanisms may involve dual regulation of triglyceride clearance by skeletal muscle: (1) Muscle contraction activates lipoprotein lipase (LPL), accelerating the hydrolysis of very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) and chylomicrons [17]; (2) Muscle tissue enhances fatty acid oxidation capacity through the AMPK/SIRT1 pathway, reducing circulating free fatty acids [18]. Supporting evidence comes from a 2022 JAHA cohort study demonstrating that after 15 weeks of high-intensity training, young males with developed musculature showed a 13.5% increase in ABCA1-mediated cholesterol efflux capacity alongside an 8.2% elevation in HDL-C [19].

However, this protective effect did not reach statistical significance in women. This sexual dimorphism may be attributed to hormonal regulation: androgens promote muscle anabolism, whereas estrogen attenuates lipolytic efficiency by suppressing muscle AMPK signaling [20]. These findings carry important clinical implications, suggesting that dyslipidemia management in women may require greater emphasis on visceral fat control rather than muscle-building alone, with particular attention to postmenopausal women experiencing estrogen withdrawal-induced adipose tissue redistribution.

Comparative advantages of body composition indicators over traditional obesity assessments​

Although BMI remains the most widely used clinical indicator of obesity, this study confirmed its exclusion from the final multivariate regression model, aligning with previous research findings: when BMI and body fat percentage classifications conflict (e.g., normal BMI with high body fat percentage), cardiovascular risk factors (CVRFs) primarily correlate with body fat percentage rather than BMI [21]. This phenomenon highlights the unique value of bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) in health screening settings—it can identify metabolically obese normal-weight (MONW) individuals who constitute 15%-30% of the normal-BMI population, whose visceral fat excess and metabolic abnormalities are often masked by BMI [22]. From a public health perspective, body composition parameters demonstrate significant cost-effectiveness advantages in dyslipidemia prediction. Compared to expensive MRI/CT examinations, BIA enables non-invasive measurement within 5 min, making it particularly suitable for large-scale screening programs.

Study limitations and future directions​

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding the relationship between body composition and dyslipidemia. Second, the health examination cohort may exhibit a “healthy worker effect” (i.e., participants predominantly being health-conscious individuals), potentially underestimating risks in the general population. Additionally, residual risk markers such as lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] and apolipoprotein B were not measured. Finally, the absence of dietary patterns and physical activity records precluded adjustment for lifestyle confounding factors.

Future research should focus on three key aspects: (1) Conducting prospective cohort studies to validate the predictive value of body composition indicators for dyslipidemia, with particular attention to ​​dynamic changes in visceral fat and muscle mass; (2) Developing integrated assessment models that incorporate ​​residual risk markers​​ (e.g., Lp(a), apoB); (3) Designing ​​lifestyle intervention trials​​ to evaluate body composition optimization strategies.

Conclusions​

In summary, dyslipidemia is a prevalent issue identified during health examinations and is associated with an elevated risk of cardiovascular disease. Body composition analysis provides a comprehensive assessment of health status and disease risk, with visceral fat accumulation showing a particularly strong correlation with dyslipidemia. Notably, males exhibit higher values than females across multiple lipid parameters, potentially indicating a greater cardiovascular risk profile.
