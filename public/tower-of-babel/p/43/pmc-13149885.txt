Effects of Korean Red ginseng on the regulation of inflammation, cell death, and fibrosis in a mouse model of rheumatoid arthritis featuring spike protein overexpression
Authors: JooYeon Jhun, Young Joon Lee, Hyun-Sik Na, Seung Yoon Lee, Yeon Su Lee, Se Gyeong Han, JeongWon Choi, Mi-La Cho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCOVID-19 causes inflammation, autoantibody production, and thrombosis-features commonly observed in autoimmune diseases like rheumatoid arthritis (RA). In RA patients infected with COVID-19, immunosuppressive treatments can weaken viral defenses and worsen inflammation. The effects of red ginseng on these patients remain unexplored. We investigated the therapeutic potential of red ginseng extract (RGE) in an RA model with SARS-CoV-2 spike protein overexpression.Materials and methodsPlasmids expressing the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein and ACE2 were delivered into mice with collagen-induced arthritis (CIA). They were orally administered RGE, and effects were assessed via immunohistochemistry, confocal analysis, and ELISA. We analyzed the regulation of Th17 and Treg cells and related cytokines, as well as markers of inflammation, cell death, and fibrosis in splenocytes and fibroblasts. We also examined the combined effects of RGE and methotrexate (MTX).ResultsOral RGE supplementation improved joint inflammation and damage, increasing Treg cells in the spleen. RGE reduced the expression of inflammatory cytokines (IL-17, IL-6, MCP-1, IL-1β, TNF-α), cell death markers (pMLKL, Caspase1), and fibrosis markers (α-SMA, Col1A1) in synovial tissue. In vitro, RGE decreased the expression of these markers in fibroblasts and splenocytes. RGE and MTX had inhibitory effects in the CIA model, with regulatory effects on immune cells, suppressing Th17 cells and enhancing Treg cells.ConclusionRGE inhibits inflammatory cell death, fibrosis, and modulates immune cells. Its inhibitory effect with MTX suggests therapeutic benefits for RA patients co-infected with COVID-19.Keywords: Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), Rheumatoid arthritis (RA), Red ginseng extract (RGE), Inflammation cell death, Fibrosis

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic autoimmune disease, accompanied by synovial inflammation, cartilage damage, and bone erosion. If left untreated, chronic RA can progress to systemic inflammation leading to abnormalities in solid organs including the heart, liver, intestine, and muscles [1]. Methotrexate (MTX) is the most widely used DMARD for both early and established rheumatoid arthritis (RA) due to its long-term safety and efficacy. However, monotherapy often yields only partial responses and is frequently discontinued because of adverse effects or insufficient efficacy. Consequently, combination therapies with agents including hydroxychloroquine and adalimumab are commonly employed to improve outcomes [2].

Severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) infection triggers coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19); it initiates infection via an interaction between its spike protein and human angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) of host cells. It usually triggers a mild to moderate respiratory disease. Emerging evidence suggests that COVID-19 may contribute to the development of RA [3,4]. Derksen et al. [5] showed that 3 of 61 patients infected with SARS-CoV-2 developed polyarthritis after infection, although their levels of anti-citrullinated peptide antibody (ACPA) were not elevated. Parrot et al. [6] reported the first case of elevated ACPA levels after SARS-CoV-2 infection leading to RA. Another report documented a case of seropositive RA after SARS-CoV-2 infection [7]. Marin et al. [8] reported a significant association between SARS-CoV-2 infection and an increased incidence of RA. SARS-CoV-2 infection and RA are both associated with abnormal immune responses associated with inflammation. SARS-CoV-2 infection induces a hyperinflammatory state referred to as a cytokine storm, namely, the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6. In the lungs, it can induce the differentiation of Th0 cells into Th17 cells that produce IL-17, which promotes neutrophilic inflammation. In RA, Th1 hyperactivation leads to the secretion of pro-inflammatory mediators such as IFN-γ, IL-2, and TNF-α. Additionally, the IL-17 from Th17 promotes the production of IL-6, IL-8, VEGF-A, and matrix metalloproteinases (MMP-1 and MMP-3) in synovial fibroblasts [3].

Ginseng, belonging to the genus Panax, has been used as an herbal medicine for thousands of years. Korean ginseng (Panax ginseng Meyer, Araliaceae), long used as a traditional medicine in East Asia, undergoes steaming and drying to produce red ginseng, a process that helps enhance preservation. This process enhances its bioactivity and reduces adverse effects compared to fresh ginseng [9].

Ginseng has been reported to ameliorate RA symptoms. Kim et al. [10] identified the inhibitory effects of ginsenoside Rb1 (G-Rb1) on arthritis in mice with CIA. Chang et al. [11] showed that BT-201, a butanol extract of Panax notoginseng, inhibited the onset and progression of CIA in mice as well as the secretion of inflammatory cytokines including IL-1β by THP-1 cells. In another study, Red ginseng extract (RGE) ameliorates arthritis in a CIA mouse model through the suppression of pathogenic Th17 cells and osteoclast differentiation [12]. However, there are currently no reports on the effects of RGE on RA exacerbated by COVID-19.

Therefore, in this study, we investigated this issue. Specifically, we identified the effects of RGE on the expression of genes related to inflammation, cell death, and fibrosis in human fibroblast-like synoviocytes (FLSs) and mouse splenocytes. We also investigated the inhibitory effects of RGE on CIA using a mouse model of CIA featuring spike protein and ACE2 overexpression. We also explored the synergistic inhibitory effects of the combination of RGE with MTX on this condition as well as the effects on the expression of genes related to inflammation, cell death, and fibrosis. The effects of RGE on immune cells including Th17 and Treg cells and their cytokine production were also investigated using mouse splenocytes.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Care Committee of The Catholic University of Korea (Approval No. 2023-0135-03), and conducted according to relevant institutional and national guidelines, including the Laboratory Animals Welfare Act, the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, and the institutional policies on rodent research established by the IACUC of the university's School of Medicine. The studies involving human participants were reviewed and approved by the institutional review board of Seoul St. Mary's Hospital (IRB approval number: KC24TNSI0358). The participants provided their written informed consent to participate in this study.

Cell culture

Mouse splenocytes and human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were cultured in complete RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. Human FLSs were maintained under the same conditions using complete DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. Depending on the experimental conditions, cells were exposed to anti-CD3, anti-CD28, IL-6, lipopolysaccharide (LPS), or TNF-α, with or without the addition of RGE or MTX. The RGE used in this study was obtained from the Korea Ginseng Corporation.

qRT-PCR

Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed using the LightCycler FastStart DNA Master SYBR Green I kit (Takara) on a StepOne Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems/Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 40 cycles. The following primers were used: IL-10, 5′- GGCCCAGAAATCAAGGAGCA-3′(sense), 5′-AGAAATCGATGACAGCGCCT-3′(antisense); Foxp3, 5′-GGCCCTTCTCCAGGACAGA-3’ (sense), 5′-GCTGATCATGGCTGGGTTGT-3’ (antisense); IL-17, 5′- CCTCAAAGCTCAGCGTGTCC-3′(sense), 5′-GAGCTCACTTTTGCGCCAAG-3’ (antisense); RORrt, 5′-TGTCCTGGGCTACCCTACTG-3’ (sense), 5′-GTGCAGGAGTAGGCCACATT-3′(antisense); α-SMA, 5′-GTCCCAGACATCAGGGAGTAA-3′(sense), 5′-TCGGATACTTCAGCGTCAGGA-3′(antisense); COL1A1, 5′-GCCAAGAAGACATCCCTGAAG-3′(sense), 5′-TGTGGCAGATACAGATCAAGC-3′(antisense); RIPK1, 5′-CTGTTCCCTGTGCCCAATAA-3′(sense), 5′-ATGACTCTGAAGCTGTCCTTTC-3′(antisense); RIPK3, 5′-GCACTCCTCAGATTCCACATAC-3′(sense), 5′-GTGTCTTCCATCTCCCTGATTC-3′(antisense); CASP1, 5′-CTTGGAGACATCCTGTCAGGG-3′(sense), 5′-AGTCACAAGACCAGGCATATTCT-3′(antisense); β-actin, 5′-GAAATCGTGCGTGACATCAAAG-3′(sense), 5′-TGTAGTTTCATGGATGCCACAG-3′(antisense). The levels of mRNA expression were normalized relative to that of β-actin mRNA.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The levels of IFN-γ, IL-10, and IL-17 in the cell culture supernatants were measured using ELISA kit (R&D Systems). Absorbance was measured at 405 nm using an ELISA microplate reader (Molecular Devices).

CIA model with spike/ACE2 overexpression, and treatment with MTX and RGE

Collagen-induced arthritis was induced in male DBA1/J mice (Orient Bio) as described by Lee et al. [13]. On day 7 following the second immunization, mice received an intravenous injection of 200 μg pLEX307-ACE2 puro in 1 mL saline, which was subsequently repeated every other day. One week after the initial ACE2 vector injection, 200 μg of pcDNA3.1-SARS2-spike in 1 mL saline was administered, and the two plasmids were thereafter alternated at 7-day intervals. The severity of arthritis was evaluated using arthritis scoring system [14].

Histopathological analysis

Mice were euthanized, and their knee joints and lungs were collected for analysis. Joints were fixed in 10% formalin and decalcified using Decalcifying Solution-Lite (Calci-Clear Rapid; National Diagnostics), while lungs were fixed in 10% formalin without decalcification. Both tissues were paraffin-embedded, sectioned at 5 μm, deparaffinized with xylene, rehydrated through graded ethanol. Histopathological evaluation of joint tissues was performed using hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) and safranin O staining, and synovial inflammation, bone erosion, and cartilage damage were assessed according to the evaluation criteria [14]. Lung tissues were scored according to the evaluation criteria for the degree of inflammatory cell infiltration, hyaline membrane, and Ashcroft scale [[15], [16], [17]].

Confocal microscopy

Spleen tissues were cryosectioned (6 μm), fixed with acetone, and incubated for 2 h at room temperature with fluorescent primary antibodies: anti-CD4 Alexa Fluor 488, anti-CD25 APC, anti-Foxp3 PE (BioLegend/eBioscience), anti-CD19 FITC (BD Biosciences), and anti-IL-10 PE (BioLegend). The paraffin joint sections were deparaffinized with xylene and rehydrated through graded ethanol. Some were further incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-IL-17 (Abcam), anti-CD19 (Invitrogen), or anti-CD138 (R&D Systems), followed by 2 h incubation at room temperature with fluorescent secondary antibodies: goat anti-rabbit PE (Southern Biotech) for IL-17 and CD138, and goat anti-rat Alexa Fluor 488 (Invitrogen) for CD19. Double- or triple-positive cells were counted in three high-power fields (400 × ) per section. Imaging was performed using an LSM 700 confocal microscope (Zeiss).

Immunohistochemistry

Paraffin-embedded sections were incubated at 4 °C with the following primary monoclonal antibodies: anti-IL-17 (Novus Biologicals), anti-IL-6 (Novus Biologicals), anti-MCP-1 (Abcam), anti-IL-1β (Novus Biologicals), anti-TNF-α (Abcam), anti-pMLKL (Abcam), anti-CASP1 (Novus Biologicals), anti-α-SMA (Invitrogen), anti-COL1A1 (Invitrogen), anti-STAT3 (Abcam), or anti-pSTAT3 (Abcam). Then they were incubated with secondary biotinylated antibodies, followed by a 30 min incubation with streptavidin–peroxidase complex. They were developed using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine chromogen (Dako).

Western blot

Mouse splenocytes were lysed using lysis buffer (Applied Science), and protein concentrations were measured with the Micro BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Equal amounts of protein were separated on 10% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes (Invitrogen). The membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against pSTAT3, STAT3, MLKL, pMLKL and GAPDH (Abcam), and RIP3 (Invitrogen), followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Santa Cruz Biotechnology). Detection was performed using the SuperSignal West Pico PLUS substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.1 (GraphPad Software). Data are shown as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Mann-Whitney U test was used for two-group comparisons; ANOVA for multiple groups. p < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

RGE decreases the expression of genes related to inflammation, cell death, and fibrosis, while increasing the expression of genes associated with anti-inflammatory processes

RGE decreased IL-17 production in splenocytes in a concentration-dependent manner while having no effect on IFN-γ expression (Fig. 1A). Meanwhile, it increased the mRNA levels of IL-10 and Foxp3 when splenocytes were stimulated with anti-CD3 to activate T cells. Similar effects were observed when they were stimulated with LPS to activate other immune cells, including B cells and macrophages [18,19] (Fig. 1B). It also decreased the mRNA levels of IL-17 and RORγt under the same stimulatory conditions (Fig. 1C). Furthermore, it decreased pSTAT3 levels in a concentration-dependent manner, when inflammatory responses are induced, and reduced STAT3 expression at a concentration of 1000 μg/mL (Fig. 1D). STAT3 mediates Th17 differentiation [20]. Our results suggest that RGE has anti-inflammatory effects. We also found that it reduced the expression of fibrosis-related genes including α-SMA and COL1A1 in FLSs (Fig. 1E). In addition, both the protein expression levels of RIP3, MLKL, and phosphorylated MLKL (p-MLKL) and the mRNA expression levels of RIPK1, RIPK3, and CASP1, all of which are cell death–related molecules, were reduced (Fig. 1F and G). These findings indicate inhibitory effects on fibrosis and cell death pathways.

The secretion of IL-17 and IFN-γ was analyzed by ELISA. (B) For 72-h stimulations, splenocytes were treated with either anti-CD3 (2 μg/mL) or LPS (100 ng/mL), in the presence or absence of RGE. mRNA expression levels of IL-10 and Foxp3 were quantified using qRT-PCR. RGE attenuates CIA featuring spike/ACE2 overexpression, while increasing the levels of tregs cells and decreasing those of Th17 cells

After inducing arthritis in mice, spike/ACE2 overexpression vectors were intravenously injected to mimic COVID-19. In addition, RGE was orally administered on a weekly basis. MTX was used as a positive control (Fig. 2A). We investigated whether RGE could attenuate arthritis. Arthritis scores in mice treated with RGE were lower than those in controls (Fig. 2B). We also assessed indicators of arthritis severity according to inflammation, bone erosion, cartilage damage, and total histological scores. All indicators were decreased by treatment with RGE and MTX but not significantly (Fig. 2C). RGE also increased the levels of CD25+FOXP3+ T (Treg) cells in splenocytes and decreased those of CD4+IL-17+ T (Th17) cells (Fig. 2D and E). These results suggest that RGE inhibits CIA aggravated by COVID-19 by increasing Treg cell levels and decreasing Th17 cell levels.

RGE reduces the expression of genes related to inflammation, cell death, and fibrosis in the joints of CIA mice featuring spike/ACE2 overexpression

RGE reduced the frequency of cells producing inflammatory cytokines such as IL-17, IL-6, MCP-1, IL-1β, and TNF-α (Fig. 3A). It also reduced the levels of cells containing pMLKL and CASP1, both of which are related to inflammatory cell death (Fig. 3B). Moreover, it decreased the frequency of cells showing increased expression of fibrosis-related genes such as α-SMA and COL1A1 (Fig. 3C).

Combined treatment with RGE and MTX has synergistic inhibitory effects on CIA featuring spike/ACE2 overexpression

After creating the model, MTX was orally administered, alone or in combination with RGE on a weekly basis (Fig. 4A). MTX attenuated the severity of arthritis and RGE enhanced its effects. The combination reduced inflammation, bone erosion, cartilage damage, and total histological score, compared to MTX alone. In addition, the indicators of arthritis in mice treated with both were lower than those in controls (Fig. 4B). These findings demonstrate the inhibitory effects of the combination of MTX and RGE on CIA. Furthermore, the combination significantly increased the number of CD4+CD25+FOXP3+ T cells and CD19+IL-10+ B cells in spleen, compared to MTX alone and controls (Fig. 4C–E). This suggests that the synergistic effects extend to the levels of Treg cells and Breg cells. It also decreased the levels of Th17 cells in synovium, compared to controls (Fig. 4D). Moreover, it significantly decreased the frequency of cells expressing inflammation-related genes such as IL-17, IL-6, and MCP-1 in the joints, compared to MTX alone and controls (Fig. 4F).

Combined use of RGE and MTX synergistically reduces the expression of genes related to inflammation, cell death, and fibrosis in CIA mice featuring spike protein and ACE2 overexpression

A significant reduction in IL-1β- and TNF-α-producing cells was observed with the combination treatment compared to MTX alone and controls (Fig. 5A). This again highlights the synergistic effects of the combination. In addition, it reduced cells expressing cell death–related markers (pMLKL and Caspase1), STAT3/pSTAT3 involved in Th17 differentiation, and fibrosis markers such as α-SMA and COL1A1 (Fig. 5B, C, D).

RGE with MTX attenuates inflammation and fibrosis in the lungs and reduces the expression of genes related to inflammation, cell death, and fibrosis in FLSs and PBMCs

The combination significantly decreased inflammation cell infiltration of the hyaline membrane in the lungs of CIA mice featuring spike/ACE2 overexpression, compared to MTX alone and controls. It also reduced the Ashcroft score but not significantly (Fig. 6A). This demonstrates the inhibitory effect of RGE and MTX on lung inflammation and fibrosis. Furthermore, the combination decreased the mRNA levels of genes related to cell death (CASP1, RIPK1, RIPK3) in splenocytes, compared to controls, and significantly reduced CASP1 expression, compared to MTX alone. Additionally, it suppressed genes related to fibrosis (α-SMA and COL1A1) in FLSs, compared to controls, and significantly decreased COL1A1 expression, compared to MTX alone (Fig. 6B). This suggests that the combination inhibits cell death and fibrosis. Furthermore, in mouse splenocytes and human peripheral blood mononuclear cells stimulated with anti-CD3 and LPS, co-stimulation with RGE and MTX significantly increased IL-10 expression in mouse splenocytes compared with MTX stimulation alone, while similarly reducing IL-17 and IFN-γ cytokine expression. In human peripheral blood mononuclear cells, co-stimulation with RGE and MTX also significantly increased IL-10 and IFN-γ expression and significantly decreased IL-17 levels compared with MTX stimulation alone (Fig. 6C and D).

Discussion

RA patients are at a higher risk for infection compared to the general population, attributable to factors such as immunosuppression, chronic inflammation, and comorbidities [21]. Risk factors for death from COVID-19 involve comorbidities including RA along with diabetes, asthma, respiratory disease, chronic heart disease, liver disease, stroke/dementia, lupus, and psoriasis [22]. In addition, emerging evidence suggests that SARS-CoV-2 infection may promote the development of RA [[5], [6], [7], [8]], while ginseng has inhibitory effects on RA [[10], [11], [12],23]. Korean Red Ginseng (KRG) has been reported not to be associated with rheumatoid arthritis disease flare or an increased rate of adverse event development in RA patients [24]. In contrast, both short- and long-term use of glucocorticoids (GCs) has been linked to a wide range of adverse effects, including cardiovascular events, increased risk of bone fracture, and elevated mortality rates [25]. Clinical use of Tripterygium wilfordii tgpolyglycoside has been reported to induce multiple toxicities, including gastrointestinal, reproductive, hepatic, renal, hematological, and cutaneous adverse effects, along with other systemic damages [26]. Thus, research exploring the therapeutic effects of RGE on RA in the context of COVID-19 is relevant and worth exploring.

In this study, we investigated this topic. We found that RGE decreased the expression of IL-17 and RORγt in the splenocytes of mice. RORγt is a member of the ROR family of transcription factors that induces Il17a transcription in human and murine Th17 cells [27]. Ginseng-derived panaxadiol inhibits the transcriptional activity of RORγ, leading to the inhibition of IL-17A production and the alleviation of STZ-induced Type 1 diabetes [28]. RGE increased the expression of IL-10 and Foxp3. Ginsenoside Rd increases the production of immunosuppressive cytokines including IL-10, TGF-β1, IL-10, and IL-35, and promotes Treg differentiation by upregulating Foxp3 expression [29]. It decreases pSTAT3 levels in CD4+ T cells from mice under Th17-differentiation conditions [12]. Phosphorylation of STAT3 is a critical process in the differentiation of Th17 cells in the presence of transforming growth factor (TGF)-β1 [30]. STAT3 mediates Th17 differentiation by promoting RORγt expression [20]. Therefore, the decreases in the levels of STAT3 and pSTAT3 by RGE are in line with the decrease in the level of Th17 by RGE. RGE also reduced α-SMA and COL1A1 expression in FLSs, which suggests that it may prevent fibrosis induced by COVID-19. Pulmonary fibrosis is a sequela of COVID-19 pneumonia and acute respiratory distress syndrome [31]. RGE inhibits liver or renal fibrosis in mice [32,33].We also observed that RGE reduced the expression of genes related to cell death (RIPK1, RIPK3, MLKL, RIPK1, and CASP1) in splenocytes. This suggests that RGE may inhibit necroptosis and pyroptosis. Necroptosis is a type of programmed cell death with characteristics of both necrosis and apoptosis. It is mediated by RIPK1, RIPK3, and MLKL and can elicit significant inflammation [34]. Pyroptosis is an inflammatory programmed cell death controlled via the enzymatic activity of inflammatory proteases in the caspase family. CASP1 is involved in pyroptosis, which occurs via the formation of CASP1-dependent pores in the plasma membrane allowing ion flux, osmotic lysis, and cytoplasmic swelling [35].

We investigated whether RGE could attenuate arthritis in a mouse model of CIA featuring the spike protein of SARS-CoV-2. The spike-treated group exhibited markedly increased bone and cartilage damage, along with enhanced inflammatory infiltration. In addition, this model exhibited features associated with COVID-19 infection, including autoantibodies against CXCL4 (also known as PF4) and antiphospholipid antibodies (APLA), as well as increased type I interferon expression in splenocytes [13]. Arthritis scores and indicators of arthritis severity were decreased by RGE. Several reports have demonstrated that ginseng inhibits CIA in the CIA mouse model [[10], [11], [12]]. Furthermore, in our study, it increased the levels of Treg cells while decreasing those of Th17 cells in the joints of mice treated with it. These effects are consistent with the aforementioned in vitro findings. We also found that RGE reduced the frequency of cells producing inflammatory cytokines, including IL-17, IL-6, MCP-1, IL-1β, and TNF-α, in joints of the RGE-treated group, compared to controls. SARS-CoV-2 infection and RA both promote inflammatory responses [3]. Thus, RGE has the potential to treat RA in the context of COVID-19. In line with this, emerging data demonstrate the inhibitory effects of ginseng on the production of inflammatory cytokines including TNF- α, IL-6, IL-17 and IL-1β [10,11,36]. RGE reduced the levels of cells containing pMLKL and CASP1, both of which are related to cell death [35,37]. Moreover, it decreased the expression of fibrosis-related genes, such as α-SMA and COL1A1. These findings suggest that RGE inhibits pyroptosis, necroptosis, and fibrosis.

We identified the effects of RGE on levels of different types of splenocytes. The percentage of Th17 cells among splenocytes, and IL-17 expression, significantly decreased following treatment. Ginseng-derived panaxadiol has been found to inhibit IL-17A production by inhibiting IL-17 transcription factor RORγ and alleviating STZ-induced Type 1 diabetes [28]. Interestingly, in our study, RGE increased the expression of the inflammatory cytokine IFN-γ, which inhibits Th17 cell development and whose knockout promotes CIA [38]. In addition, RGE increased the levels of Treg cells. RGE has been known to promote Treg differentiation by upregulating Foxp3 expression [29].

The ultimate goal of RA treatment is to achieve remission or at least low disease activity. However, MTX monotherapy does not always reach these goals, suggesting the need to combine it with other drugs including DMARDs [39]. In our study, RGE enhanced the therapeutic effects of MTX. The combination of RGE and MTX reduced indicators of arthritis severity, compared to MTX alone. Additionally, they significantly increased the levels of Treg, compared to MTX alone. As mentioned above, RGE promotes Treg differentiation [29]. Similarly, MTX is also known to restore defective Treg cell function by increasing Foxp3 expression [40]. Moreover, the combination of RGE with MTX synergistically decreased the expression of inflammation-related genes such as IL-17, IL-6, MCP-1, IL-1β, and TNF-α in the joints. RGE and MTX both have anti-inflammatory effects [36,41], suggesting potential synergistic effects, which our findings support.

We also investigated the effects of RGE on pulmonary damage, a key characteristic of severe cases of COVID-19 [42]. The combination of RGE with MTX synergistically decreased inflammation and cell infiltration into the hyaline membrane of the lungs. In addition, it reduced the Ashcroft score compared to MTX alone, although the change was not statistically significant.

In in vitro studies, the combination significantly reduced CASP1 expression in splenocytes and COL1A1 expression in FLSs, compared to MTX alone. The CAPS1 gene is related to pyroptosis and the COL1A1 gene is related to fibrosis [28,35]. The combination significantly increased IL-10 expression in mouse splenocytes, compared to MTX alone, while it significantly increased IL-10 expression and significantly decreased IL-17 in human PBMCs, compared to MTX alone. These findings suggest the potential of RGE to be used with MTX to enhance the therapeutic effects.

In conclusion, we examined the effects of RGE on RA in the context of COVID-19. RGE decreased the expression of genes related to inflammation, necroptosis, and pyroptosis in mouse splenocytes. Fibrosis is associated with COVID-19 and RGE reduced fibrosis-related genes in FLSs. It also reduced the levels of Th17 cells among mouse splenocytes and IL-17 expression in splenocytes, while increasing the levels of Treg cells. In in vivo studies, RGE attenuated CIA in a mouse model of CIA featuring spike protein and ACE2 overexpression. It reduced the expression of genes related to inflammation, cell death, and fibrosis in joints. The combination of RGE and MTX exhibited inhibitory effects against CIA. Moreover, it attenuated inflammation and fibrosis in lungs, conditions commonly observed in COVID-19. These findings suggest the therapeutic effects of RGE on RA aggravated by COVID-19, especially in combination with MTX.
