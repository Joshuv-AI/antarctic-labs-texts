Application of next-generation sequencing in the treatment of myelodysplastic syndrome: from mechanisms to clinical practice
Authors: Ying-Ying Ma, Cheng Zhang, Ying Chen, Lingyu Zeng, Xi Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) is a malignant clonal disorder originating from hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells and is characterized by ineffective hematopoiesis and a high propensity for transformation into acute leukemia. Research has indicated that the pathogenesis of MDS is closely linked to genetic mutations and that its progression may encompass multiple stages, including cytogenetic and molecular alterations, leading to the acquisition of oncogenic mutations. The widespread application of next-generation sequencing (NGS) technologies, including whole-genome sequencing, whole-exome sequencing, and RNA sequencing, has significantly improved our understanding of the genetic alterations and transcriptomic modifications underlying MDS. These technologies not only deepen our understanding of molecular mechanisms but also facilitate the identification of potential therapeutic targets and prognostic biomarkers. Consequently, the 2022 World Health Organization Classification and the International Consensus Classification have incorporated molecular features into the MDS classification system, and the Molecular International Prognostic Scoring System (IPSS-M) was introduced. This review aims to summarize the role of NGS in the precise diagnosis, classification, risk assessment, treatment selection, and evaluation of therapeutic effectiveness in MDS, with implications for advancing precision medicine in hematological malignancies.

1. INTRODUCTION

Myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) is a heterogeneous group of myeloid neoplastic diseases that originate from hematopoietic stem cells, characterized by abnormal differentiation and maturation of myeloid cells, pathological or ineffective hematopoiesis, refractory cytopenia, and a high risk of transformation to acute myeloid leukemia (AML).1 Gene mutations and abnormalities commonly involved in MDS include epigenetic regulators (eg, TET2 and DNMT3A), splicing complexes (eg, SF3B1), signaling pathways (eg, NRAS), transcription factors (eg, RUNX1), and tumor suppressor such as the TP53, which contribute to disease onset and progression.2

The widespread application of molecular genetic testing technologies, including whole-genome sequencing (WGS), whole-exome sequencing (WES), and RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), has deepened our understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying MDS and facilitated the identification of potential therapeutic targets and prognostic biomarkers,3–5 as summarized in Figure 1. Consequently, the 2022 World Health Organization (WHO) classification system and International Consensus Classification (ICC) have incorporated molecular features into the MDS classification system.6 The NGS-based diagnostic workflow for MDS is shown in Figure 2. Moreover, the roles of WGS and RNA-seq in the precise diagnosis, classification, risk assessment, treatment selection, and efficacy evaluation of myeloid tumors have become increasingly important.7

Next-generation sequencing (NGS) technologies have emerged as pivotal advancements in genomics and transcriptomics, enabling detailed characterization of genetic and transcriptomic modifications, primarily through DNA and RNA sequencing. DNA sequencing detects somatic and genomic alterations. The primary technologies include WGS, WES, and targeted sequencing. WGS provides a comprehensive analysis of all types of genomic variants, but is costly, whereas shallow whole-genome sequencing (sWGS) serves as a cost-effective method primarily for detecting chromosome-level copy number alterations.8,9 WES focuses on identifying variants within protein-coding regions.10 Targeted sequencing panels enable highly efficient and deep sequencing of specific gene sets associated with MDS pathogenesis, prognosis, treatment, and measurable residual disease monitoring.11–13 Variants detectable by DNA sequencing include single-nucleotide variants (SNVs), insertions and deletions (INDELs), copy number alterations (CNAs), and structural variants (SVs). Currently, a prognostic stratification system for MDS, known as IPSS-M,12 which relies on gene mutations, has been introduced. In terms of treatment, detection of gene mutations plays a dual role: it identifies molecular targets for targeted therapies and reveals sensitivity or resistance to specific drugs, enabling timely adjustments to treatment plans. Furthermore, NGS can assess and monitor minimal residual disease (MRD), thereby predicting relapse in MDS patients.

RNA-seq, also known as transcriptome sequencing, employs high-throughput technology to analyze intracellular transcripts by sequencing the transcriptome, thereby enabling investigation of gene expression, transcript diversity, splicing variations, mutations, and other post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms.4 Its main application in daily MDS practice is the detection of fusion genes.14 RNA-seq can detect alterations in gene expression and splicing aberrations in hematological disorders, facilitating the identification of novel pathogenic genes and disease biomarkers. Additionally, RNA-seq can detect fusion genes in hematological malignancies, theoretically identifying all fusion transcripts and improving detection accuracy and positivity rates. Moreover, RNA-seq can be used to investigate the mechanisms underlying the development and progression of hematological diseases. By analyzing changes in gene expression profiles, this method can elucidate disease-related signaling pathways and molecular mechanisms, providing a basis for the development of new therapeutic approaches. However, clinical applications of RNA-seq remain relatively limited.

Despite these advancements, challenges remain in fully elucidating the molecular landscape of MDS and translating NGS findings into clinical practice. This review aims to provide a comprehensive overview of the applications of NGS technologies in MDS.

2. APPLICATION IN CLINICAL PRACTICE

Currently, the application of WGS and RNA-seq in hematological diseases is becoming increasingly widespread, encompassing disease diagnosis and classification, prognostic stratification, targeted therapy, MRD monitoring, and investigation of disease mechanisms. This review elaborates on the application of NGS in each of the aforementioned aspects.

2.1. Application in diagnosis and classification

In up to 90% of MDS patients, recent advancements in NGS have resulted in the identification of recurrent somatic mutations within genes that play pivotal roles in RNA splicing, epigenetic regulation, gene transcription, and cell signaling. These mutations are crucial for diagnosing MDS.15–19 With the widespread application of NGS, diagnostic criteria for MDS are constantly being updated and refined. Considering the 2016 WHO classification of MDS as an example, this classification introduced NGS technology to analyze the mutation status of the SF3B1 gene, thereby further enhancing the diagnostic accuracy of MDS-RS (refractory anemia with ring sideroblasts) and MDS-MLD (MDS with multilineage dysplasia). Historically, the original requirement for diagnosis involved the identification of more than 15% ring sideroblasts; however, owing to the implementation of NGS, only SF3B1 mutations with more than 5% ring sideroblasts are currently required to diagnose MDS-RS. Additionally, the diagnostic criteria for MDS-MLD have been revised from the initial requirement of RS <15% to either RS <15% or RS <5% in the presence of an SF3B1 mutation.20 This change may have resulted in a higher number of patients receiving luspatercept.

In the 2022 classification systems of the WHO and ICC, 2 additional subtypes of MDS defined by genetic alterations were incorporated into the MDS classification: MDS with a low blast count and SF3B1 mutation and MDS with biallelic TP53 mutation (MDS-biTP53).21,22 Recently, Komrokji et al23 used the WHO 2022 and ICC 2022 classification standards and reclassified 7017 patients with MDS from an international cohort from the European repository of the GenoMed4All Consortium and Moffitt Cancer Center, whose diagnoses were previously defined according to the WHO 2016 classification criteria. They observed discordant classification in 3202 (45.6%) participants, including 382 (19.1%) of the 2003 participants in the genetically defined subgroups. Discordant classifications were observed in 233 (35.5%) of 657 participants with MDS with TP53 mutations, 149 (14.7%) of 1017 participants with MDS with SF3B1 mutations, no participants with MDS with del(5q), and 2820 (56.2%) of 5014 participants in morphologically defined subgroups, thus providing evidence of the need for NGS detection in the diagnosis and classification of MDS.23

Huber et al24 from the MLL Leukemia Laboratory in Munich also compared the classification of 717 newly diagnosed patients with MDS based on the WHO 2016, WHO 2022, and ICC criteria. The results demonstrated that, compared to the WHO 2016 classification, the most significant changes in the WHO 2022 and ICC classifications were observed in MDS-biTP53, a subgroup predominantly composed of MDS with excess blasts (MDS-EB) (30/40 patients; 75%).24 Zhang et al25 re-evaluated the classification of patients with MDS who were previously diagnosed using the WHO 2022 criteria; 53 patients were classified as MDS-biTP53, 34 of whom were previously diagnosed with MDS-EB.25

According to the WHO 2022 classification, individuals with KMT2A, MECOM, and NUP98 rearrangements as well as NPM1 mutations are classified as having AML, regardless of the percentage of detected blasts. Additionally, 12 cases of MDS, including 8 cases of MDS-EB2, were reclassified as AML in the study by Huber et al24 (MECOM-r, n = 5; KMT2A-r, n = 1; NPM1, n = 6). When the WHO 2016 criteria were compared with the ICC criteria, 8 previous MDS-EB2 cases were categorized as AML according to the ICC criteria (NPM1: n = 4; CEBPA: n = 4), 137 patients were classified as MDS/AML (including AML with TP53 mutations, AML-MR with gene mutations, AML-MR with cytotoxic abnormalities, and MDS/AML, NOS), and 572 patients were still classified as MDS.26 Similarly, Zhang et al25 re-evaluated the classification of patients with MDS who were previously diagnosed using the WHO 2022 criteria, and the results revealed that 30 patients who were previously classified as having MDS with NPM1 mutations were reclassified as having AML.25 A summary of studies comparing the different classifications of WHO 2016, WHO 2022, and ICC is presented in Table 1.24–32

Furthermore, NGS can facilitate the identification of precursor diseases of MDS, such as clonal cytopenia of undetermined significance (CCUS) or clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP), which may evolve into MDS. Zhang et al of the Hematology Hospital of the Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences utilized the WHO 2022 classification criteria to re-evaluate previously diagnosed MDS cases and reported that 9 cases previously classified as MDS-unclassifiable (MDS-U) were re-diagnosed as CCUS.31 Mutations in the following genes should be investigated at diagnosis to define CCUS: TET2, DNMT3A, ASXL1, SRSF2, ZRSR2, SF3B1, U2AF1, IDH1/2, RUNX1, EZH2, JAK2, CBL, KRAS, CUX1, and TP53.33,34 The risk of developing MDS can be assessed.5 Furthermore, NGS technology can be used to distinguish MDS from various types of cytopenia caused by other factors, such as infections, autoimmune diseases, prior cancer chemotherapy or radiation treatment, or exposure to toxins. The effects of these factors are often transient, whereas mutations in the genes associated with MDS indicate persistent clonal disorders.

RNA-seq is a potent technological tool for detecting gene fusion events in MDS, which are sometimes associated with specific tumor subtypes. Hence, it can be utilized for diagnostic purposes. Biancon et al35 developed eCLIP-seq to map protein RNA-binding sites. This technique involves enhanced UV cross-linking and immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing. By integrating these high-resolution binding data with bulk RNA-seq, single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq), and RNA turnover data, researchers reported that aberrant splicing resulting from the Q157R mutation was predominantly associated with loss of binding, whereas aberrant splicing arising from the S34F mutation exhibited a gain-of-binding pattern. They subsequently validated the upregulation of stress granules in cancer at the RNA level via scRNA-seq, and at the functional level via immunofluorescence (IF) imaging in primary samples derived from patients with U2AF1-mutant MDS/AML. The increased formation of stress granules in U2AF1 mutant cells was associated with improved cell fitness under stress, which was reversed by the application of a stress granule inhibitor. These findings indicate that research conducted at the RNA-seq level can further elucidate the primary cellular mechanisms underlying tumor development, thereby facilitating diagnosis and treatment.35 Despite this, the application of RNA-seq in the diagnosis of MDS is relatively underreported in the literature, suggesting the need for further research to explore the potential and applications of RNA-seq in this domain.36 With technological advancements and in-depth research, RNA-Seq may become indispensable for the diagnosis and treatment of MDS.

2.2. Application in prognostic stratification

In terms of specific clinical and molecular risk factors, MDS is associated with varying degrees of progression to AML.37,38 Therefore, various prognostic systems have been proposed to improve the ability to predict the survival and progression of patients with MDS to AML, of which the Revised International Prognostic Scoring System (IPSS-R) has been widely adopted.39

With the clinical implementation of molecular detection technologies, such as NGS, an increasing number of clinical studies have focused on the relationship between gene mutations and the prognosis of MDS. Haferlach et al40 of the MLL Leukemia Laboratory in Munich, Germany, attempted to comprehensively classify MDS patients from a genetic perspective via WGS. Multivariate Cox analysis revealed that biallelic TP53 inactivation, complex karyotype, RUNX1, ASXL1, and SF3B1 mutations were independent factors affecting overall survival (OS).40 This study demonstrates that classification based on genetic abnormalities is superior to morphological classification and provides a basis for the precise treatment of MDS in the future.

Fang et al41 retrospectively analyzed the clinical and laboratory variables of 233 patients classified as having LR-MDS according to the FAB criteria, and used NGS data to evaluate the relative contribution of mutation status upon the addition of IPSS-R to estimate the OS and progression-free survival (PFS) of LR-MDS patients. Multivariate survival analysis revealed that the incorporation of JAK2 and RUNX1 mutations and bone marrow blasts into the IPSS-R can improve risk stratification in patients with LR-MDS.41 Liang et al42 developed a new MDS prognostic scoring model using a cohort of 201 patients with MDS from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database, which included 6 risk factors (age, bone marrow blast percentage, ETV6, TP53, EZH2, and ASXL1). A single-center cohort consisting of 115 MDS patients from Northwest China was used for external validation. Compared to IPSS and IPSS-R, this prediction model improved the prognostic stratification of the entire analysis cohort, thus demonstrating the predictive value of NGS for MDS patient stratification.42

With the increasing number of clinical applications of NGS, we have gained a deeper understanding of the mechanisms underlying the molecular pathways of MDS. Consequently, the Molecular IPSS (IPSS-M) classification system was developed in 2022.12 IPSS-M not only includes the presence of specific clinical and chromosomal abnormalities but also encompasses a set of prognostic somatic genetic alterations that can more accurately predict the prognoses of patients with MDS, including SF3B1, TET2, RUNX1, ASXL1, DNMT3A, TP53, and EZH2, which are associated with disease prognosis and can help to more accurately stratify the risk of MDS patients.15,42,43

To validate the IPSS-M scoring system extensively, Sauta et al44 retrospectively analyzed the clinical data of 2876 patients with primary MDS using the GenoMed4All Alliance. The results demonstrated that IPSS-M could improve prognostic discrimination across all clinical endpoints with respect to the IPSS-R (with a concordance of 0.81 vs 0.74, respectively, and 0.89 vs 0.76, respectively, for leukemia-free survival). A five-to-five comparison between the IPSS-R and IPSS-M risk groups resulted in the restratification of 1324 patients (46%), of whom 679 (23.6%) were upstaged and 645 (22.4%) were downstaged. In patients treated with hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT), IPSS-M significantly improved the prediction of disease relapse and post-transplantation survival compared to IPSS-R (with a concordance of 0.76 vs 0.60, respectively, and 0.89 vs 0.70, respectively, for the probability of relapse).

Lee et al45 validated the IPSS-M (based on the 2022 ICC) in 649 patients with primary MDS and compared its prognostic ability with IPSS and IPSS-R.43 The results revealed that 42.5% of the patients were reclassified, and 29.3% were upgraded from IPSS-R. After reclassification, 16.9% of patients received different treatment strategies. This study also confirmed that IPSS-M can better stratify the risk of patients with MDS to optimize treatment decisions. Additionally, specific molecular markers, such as variant allele frequency (VAF) ≥20%; non-DNMT3A, TET2, and ASXL1 (DTA) mutations; and the presence of at least 2 mutations, can also serve as independent high-risk progression markers for MDS. These markers can predict disease progression in patients who have not yet met the diagnostic criteria for MDS.7,51–53

Compared to WGS, there is currently limited research on the use of RNA-seq in the prognostic stratification of MDS. Recently, Zeng et al54 reported transcriptome alterations in hematopoietic stem cells of myelodysplastic tumors in the Journal of Translational Medicine.52 Single-cell transcriptomic results indicated that MDS patients with high expression of MAML3 and PLCB1 exhibited shorter OS, and the expression levels of MAML3 and PLCB1 in patients with MDS-EB were significantly higher than in patients without MDS-EB. Additionally, the white blood cell count and neutrophil number at diagnosis were lower, and the proportion of patients at “very high” risk was greater in the PLCB1-high group compared to the PLCB1-low group. Moreover, the proportion of patients who developed AML was higher in the PLCB1-high group.

In a study published by Todisco et al55, genomic and transcriptome analyses were integrated to evaluate whether transcriptome profiling could improve the current risk stratification methods. They concluded that SF3B1, SRSF2, or TP53 multihit mutations are distinct disease-modifying entities in 89% of MDS patients with RS, and that outcomes significantly differ between categories, highlighting the importance of identifying the TP53 multihit status in MDS patients with RS. Transcriptome-defined hematopoietic progenitor signatures can predict survival independent of mutational categories and IPSS-M in multivariate analyses, suggesting that hematopoietic progenitor quantification is a valuable prognostic marker in myeloid neoplasms. Therefore, in the future, risk stratification of MDS may be evaluated from a transcriptomic perspective, although further research is required.

2.3. Application in precision therapeutics

MDS is a highly heterogeneous myeloid malignancy; therefore, patient management and treatment should be individualized. In the past, treatment options for MDS were primarily based on patients’ prognostic stratifications, and individualized treatment plans were selected in combination with factors such as age, performance status, comorbidities, and treatment compliance. In the current era of molecular analysis, abundant information is available to guide treatment decisions and advance of precision medicine for patients with MDS. NGS can provide physicians with a comprehensive understanding of the disease spectrum of patients with MDS and identify those with favorable or unfavorable prognoses. These data can enhance the targeting of specific mutations previously established in AML and now extended to MDS.

For low-risk MDS, the erythropoiesis-promoting activity of the activin receptor ligand trap, luspatercept, in MDS with SF3B1 mutation is a prime example of precision therapy. A 2023 European Hematology Association (EHA) report revealed that for common MDS mutations (such as SF3B1, ASXL1, TET2, DNMT3A, EZH2, IDH2, and U2AF1), luspatercept is superior to erythropoietin (EPO).56,57 Hypomethylating agents (HMAs), such as azacitidine, decitabine, and decitabine/cedazuridine, can improve the long-term prognosis of patients with methylation-related mutations, such as mutations in TET2 and DNMT3A.58–60 Roxadustat (FG-4592), an oral hypoxia-inducible factor prolyl hydroxylase inhibitor (HIF-PHI), modulates the oxygen-sensing pathway and augments EPO production and erythropoiesis in patients with chronic kidney disease.61 The MATTERHORN study, a randomized, double-blind, phase 3 clinical trial, demonstrated that roxadustat-treated patients achieved a higher rate of transfusion independence compared with the placebo group.62,63

Higher telomerase activity and telomerase reverse transcription (TERT) expression in mononuclear cells as poor prognostic features in MDS.64 Imetelstat, a competitive telomerase inhibitor, has demonstrated durable transfusion independence (approximately 1 year) and disease-modifying activity for heavily transfused LR-MDS patients who are unresponsive to or ineligible for erythropoiesis-stimulating agents (ESAs).65,66 In Jun 2024, the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) officially approved imetelstat for adult patients with MDS, ranging from low risk to intermediate-1 risk, who are transfusion-dependent and unresponsive to or unsuitable for ESA treatment.67

For high-risk MDS patients (especially those with a high proportion of primitive cells), HMA, anticancer drugs, and allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT) are the most common treatment options. However, allo-HSCT remains the only curative therapy, and recurrence after allo-HSCT is the main factor affecting long-term survival.68 NGS-guided precision therapy provides new approaches for treating these patients. For example, ASXL1, TET2, and DNMT3A mutations are often associated with poor prognosis, but azacitidine treatment can prolong long-term survival in such patients.20,56 U2AF1 mutations are associated with poor prognosis,69–71 and several studies have demonstrated that U2AF1 and SF3B1 mutations in MDS bone marrow cells can induce oncogenic isoforms of the target gene IRAK4, leading to hyperactivation of NF-κB signaling and increased fitness of leukemic stem and progenitor cells (LSPCs). The IRAK4 inhibitor CA-4948 has demonstrated efficacy in both preclinical studies and MDS clinical trials, with patients harboring splicing factor mutations exhibiting higher response rates.72,73

Approximately 5% of MDS patients harbor IDH1/IDH2 mutations.57,60 Ivosidenib (AG-122) and enasidenib (AG-LH) are oral small-molecule inhibitors of IDH1 and IDH2, respectively, which exhibit good tolerability and can induce responses in patients with MDS, including those previously treated with HMAs.74–76 TP53 abnormalities, observed in approximately 5% to 10% of de novo AML and MDS patients, are associated with poor outcomes.77,78 Eprenetapopt (APR-246), a prodrug that restores WT p53 conformation and function, induces programmed cell death in cancer cells. Combination therapy with eprenetapopt and azacitidine has demonstrated a high response rate and good tolerability in TP53-mutated AML/MDS patients in phase II clinical trials.79,80 Additionally, the clinical trial of magrolimab (Hu5F9-G4), a monoclonal antibody targeting CD47, combined with azacitidine, in newly diagnosed high-risk MDS showed better efficacy in the poor prognosis TP53 subgroup.81

Recent studies have reported KMT2A rearrangements (KMT2Ar) in MDS. Patients with KMT2Ar have worse prognosis, especially in treatment-related cases.82,83 The KMT2A inhibitor JNJ-75276617 (bleximenib), an orally bioavailable, potent, and selective protein–protein interaction inhibitor of menin-KMT2A binding, exhibits preclinical activity in leukemia with KMT2A or NPM1 alterations and synergizes with gilteritinib, venetoclax, and azacitidine.84 Venetoclax in combination with azacitidine is currently being tested for high-risk MDS treatment.85 KRAS mutations are common in MDS and typically associated with poor prognosis. Emerging KRAS inhibitors offer potential therapeutic options.86–89 Common gene mutations and targeted therapies for MDS are summarized in Table 3.

RNA-seq has only recently been applied in clinical practice; thus, research on its clinical applications is relatively limited. Berastegui et al91 detected upregulation of DNA damage inducible transcript 3 (DDIT3) in MDS-specific lesions by analyzing HSC transcriptomes. Overexpression of DDIT3 in healthy HSCs induced an MDS-like transcriptional state and dyserythropoiesis, whereas DDIT3 knockdown in CD34+ cells from MDS patients restored erythropoiesis. Zeng et al54 reported transcriptomic changes in HSPCs in MDS according to pseudotime and highlighted PLCB1 as key in MDS progression to leukemia. The PLCB1 inhibitor U73122 suppressed proliferation, induced cell cycle arrest, and activated apoptosis of leukemic cells in vitro. Targeting PLCB1 pharmacologically may provide a strategy for patients with high PLCB1 expression.

2.4. Response evaluation applications

Traditionally, the monitoring of MRD in patients with MDS has primarily focused on MDS-EB, and the clinical significance of MRD monitoring in these patients is not well defined. Recent multicenter clinical studies have demonstrated that MDS-EB patients with pre-transplantation flow cytometry (FCM-MRD) negativity exhibit better long-term survival rates.68 The International Working Group (IWG) 2023 recently proposed that the response criteria for higher-risk MDS (HR-MDS) would be the first criteria to incorporate the status of MRD as an exploratory endpoint and recommend this status as a “CR equivalent” for patients with <5% blasts who suffer from HR-MDS due to adverse cytogenetics and/or severe cytopenias. Under these criteria, patients who achieve complete cytogenetic response and blood counts that meet the CR criteria are considered to be “CR equivalent” but should be separately reported.1 However, the IWG 2023 criteria do not provide detailed information on the specific application of MRD testing or a formal definition of MRD response.

Advanced technologies, such as NGS, have enabled the continuous monitoring of gene mutations and tracking of the dynamic trajectory of clonal evolution in patients with HR-MDS. NGS-based MRD detection methods can achieve high sensitivity and specificity via deep sequencing of amplicons or enriched molecules, which can detect leukemia cell levels as low as 0.001%, and provide additional information regarding tumor clone structure and evolution.92–97

TP53-mutated cells tend to exhibit resistance to various therapies such as HMAs and HSCT; however, TP53 mutation clearance (which is usually incomplete) is associated with a longer response duration and longer OS.97,98 Using ongoing molecular analyses, Hunter et al98 revealed that the clearance of TP53 mutations during HMA therapy is associated with superior OS and improved prognosis in patients undergoing allo-HSCT.90 In phase II studies assessing the effects of azacitidine plus eprenetapopt treatment, a significant reduction in TP53 VAF was associated with better clinical responses.80 Yun et al99 employed univariate and multivariate Cox regression models to comprehensively analyze the prognostic impact of the NGS trajectory on OS based on the results of the mutation clearance rate (NGS) in a sequence assessment of 157 patients. Multivariate analysis revealed that patients who achieved NGS-based mutation clearance exhibited significantly improved OS compared to patients with persistent mutations. Serial TP53 VAF evaluation can predict outcomes, with TP53 clearance representing an independent covariate of superior OS. Additionally, Hou et al100 suggested that monitoring MRD via FCM along with the clearance of gene mutations on day 30 could help predict disease progression in patients with MDS after transplantation.

In patients with MDS, the FLT3 mutation clearance rate is associated with prognosis. Analysis of the 1012 BRIGHT AML and MDS studies revealed that in patients in the AML and MDS cohorts who demonstrated FLT3 mutations at baseline and achieved CR, FLT3 mutations were cleared (VAF <0.05) in all patients at CR. When FLT3 mutations were monitored in patients who experienced relapse, the VAF of the FLT3 mutation increased, suggesting that continuous molecular monitoring is essential.90

As expected, the detection or reemergence of MRD markers after HSCT was associated with a higher incidence of relapse and poorer outcomes. Duncavage et al101 performed error-corrected sequencing of bone marrow samples from patients with MDS who underwent HSCT. Their findings revealed that patients who exhibited persistent disease-associated mutations with a VAF ≥0.5% in their bone marrow (either on day 30 or day 100 post-allogeneic HSCT) demonstrated a notably elevated risk of disease progression and exhibited a reduced rate of PFS compared with those who were mutation-free.101 In another study, Tobiasson et al11 used droplet digital polymerase chain reaction (ddPCR) to monitor multiple mutations (that were previously identified via NGS at diagnosis) after HSCT and demonstrated that MRD positivity at any time point was strongly associated with shorter OS and relapse-free survival. Moreover, an association was observed between the cutoff value for positivity and prognosis, with estimated cumulative incidence rates of relapse after the first positive MRD of 41%, 55%, and 63% when cutoff values of 0.1%, 0.3%, and 0.5% VAF were used, respectively.11

Collectively, the abovementioned studies highlight the clinical value of serial NGS during treatment and provide a prospective validation of NGS negativity as a biomarker for assessing treatment outcomes. In MDS (wherein a large number of genes may be implicated), evaluating the feasibility of establishing a minimal consensus gene panel for both diagnostic and follow-up purposes is essential because the size of the gene panel directly impacts the capacity of laboratories to deliver results within clinically meaningful timeframes and influences the necessary sequencing depth and sample throughput. Furthermore, an optimal monitoring strategy for MDS may necessitate the integration of multiple technologies such as chimerism analysis, flow cytometry, and molecular biology techniques to ensure comprehensive and accurate disease management.102 The emergence of new technologies (including single-cell sequencing, which provides both genomic and proteomic data) may represent a new advancement in the development of disease surveillance, both for measuring MRD and accurately characterizing this condition.103

3. LIMITATIONS AND SOLUTIONS

Although NGS technology offers advantages such as high throughput and sensitivity, several challenges and limitations remain in its technical and operational aspects, clinical application, data interpretation, and other potential concerns.104,105 First, NGS platforms typically generate short read lengths, usually only a few hundred base pairs, complicating the analysis of recombination events, repetitive sequences, and structural genomic variations. Second, NGS generates vast amounts of data, requiring substantial computing resources for storage, management, and analysis, as well as professional bioinformatics support, which increases the complexity and cost of technical applications. Third, certain NGS platforms may exhibit high error rates, particularly at the read end, potentially resulting in false positives or negatives and affecting mutation detection accuracy. Furthermore, many NGS methods involve PCR amplification during the sample preparation stage, which may introduce amplification bias, especially in regions with high or low GC content. These biases can potentially affect data consistency and reproducibility. Finally, although some gene mutations are prognostically relevant, the clinical efficacy of corresponding targeted agents remains suboptimal. Effective translation of NGS findings into clinical applications to improve patient outcomes is a critical challenge that must be addressed.

To overcome these limitations, novel research methodologies have emerged in the fields of gene and transcriptome sequencing. For genome sequencing, third-generation sequencing technology enables single-cell long-read genome sequencing.106 Single-molecule sequencing technology can sequence DNA at the individual molecular level, avoiding errors introduced during PCR amplification.107 Recent studies have demonstrated the capacity of single-cell sequencing to discriminate between heterogeneous subclones of cells in MDS by comparing their transcriptomes and epigenomes with those of the nearest normal cell or group.108 Emerging single-cell profiling techniques enable precise characterization of individual cells and have already transformed our understanding of normal and malignant hematopoiesis. Nanopore sequencing technology uses nanopores to sequence DNA molecules and is easy to perform, offers faster sequencing speeds, and reduces sequencing costs.109,110

In transcriptomics, advancements in novel research techniques such as single-cell transcriptomics, spatial transcriptomics, and programmable full-length isoform transcriptome sequencing have provided a deeper understanding of transcriptomic alterations in MDS. Spatial transcriptomics examines the spatial distribution of cell types within tissues by monitoring gene expression.111,112 This approach elucidates cell–cell interactions, spatial arrangements, and functions, particularly within the tumor microenvironment and during development and tissue regeneration. Single-cell transcriptome sequencing enables transcriptomic profiling of individual cells, providing a comprehensive and rapid method for acquiring sequence and expression data for nearly all transcripts in a given state, including protein-coding mRNAs and diverse noncoding RNAs.113 This technology also captures intercellular heterogeneity and enhances understanding of gene expression patterns, regulatory mechanisms, and biological processes such as disease initiation and progression.

Programmable full-length isoform transcriptome sequencing (PROFIT-seq) employs combinatorial reverse transcription to capture polyadenylated, non-polyadenylated, and circular RNAs, coupled with a programmable control system that selectively enriches target transcripts during sequencing. This approach offers a new method for the accurate and efficient sequencing of target transcripts while preserving overall transcriptome quantification, with potential applications in clinical diagnostics and targeted enrichment in the future.113

Furthermore, as technology evolves, multiomics sequencing has emerged as a crucial research methodology.114 Through joint analysis of multiple omics datasets, including genome, transcriptome, and proteome data, researchers can gain a more holistic understanding of genetic information and regulatory mechanisms.

4. SUMMARY AND OUTLOOK

NGS technology is rapidly evolving and has been extensively integrated into scientific research, clinical diagnostics, and therapeutic management. WGS and WES technologies enable comprehensive acquisition of genetic information, encompassing both structural and expression landscapes of tumor cell genomes. RNA-seq facilitates precise analysis of gene structure and function in MDS at the transcriptomic level, elucidating the biological characteristics and molecular mechanisms underlying disease pathogenesis and progression. NGS-based MRD monitoring provides robust evidence for selecting optimal treatment strategies. Collectively, these findings support clinical precision diagnosis and treatment.

Moreover, emerging technologies, including spatiotemporal transcriptomics, multiomics integration, and machine learning-assisted variant interpretation, will further elucidate the molecular mechanisms governing MDS initiation and progression. With the widespread application of NGS and the development of these emerging technologies, the molecular mechanisms underlying MDS will be further clarified, providing new strategies for accurate diagnosis, prognostic stratification, personalized therapy, and disease monitoring to improve therapeutic efficacy and patient prognosis.
