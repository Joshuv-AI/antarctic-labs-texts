Dilemma of self-management among patients with diabetes in primary care settings in China: a qualitative study
Authors: Feifei Jin, Wenjing Fang, Chen Li, Yuanyuan Zhang, Jian Zheng, Tianbing Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis qualitative study aimed to explore the self-management dilemmas faced by patients with diabetes in Chinese primary care and collect suggestions for improvement.DesignQualitative methods are used in this study. Thematic analysis was used to analyse the transcripts.SettingFour primary care communities in Beijing. The interviews were conducted between April and August 2025.ParticipantsThis qualitative study used face-to-face, semi-structured interviews with 32 patients with type 2 diabetes. Data collection continued until information saturation was reached.ResultsFour core themes and multiple subthemes were identified. The first theme, ‘Inadequate Disease Cognition and Health Literacy’, showed that patients had a limited understanding of diabetes, often delaying diagnosis and only learning about complications after they appeared. Misconceptions about diet and a lack of medication management knowledge were also common. The second theme, ‘Suboptimal Daily Management’, highlighted that physical activity was unstructured, glucose monitoring was irregular and emergency response capabilities were poor. The third theme, ‘Fragmented Healthcare Resources and Inadequate Family Support’, revealed systemic barriers such as limited primary care competencies, homogeneous health education formats that failed to meet patients’ needs and insufficient family support. The fourth theme, ‘Limitations in Self-Management Decision-Making’, demonstrated that patients’ decision-making processes were predominantly experience-driven, relying on personal or communal anecdotes rather than scientific medical evidence.ConclusionsThe self-management challenges among Chinese patients with diabetes in primary care are a complex interplay of inadequate individual cognition, suboptimal daily practices and fragmented support systems. The study suggests that future interventions should focus on enhancing general practitioner training, developing culturally sensitive health education and rebuilding family and community support networks to sustainably resolve these management dilemmas.

Introduction

Diabetes and its complications constitute a severe public health challenge in primary care settings across China, presenting distinctive epidemiological characteristics within the rural–urban dichotomy.13 Lifestyle changes accompanying economic development in rural areas have directly driven a rapid surge in diabetes incidence—currently reaching 320 per 100 000 person-years in rural regions, comparable to urban baselines yet exhibiting significantly higher growth rates over the past decade.4 More critically, a triple burden pattern of high prevalence, low control and high complications continues to intensify in rural areas: an undiagnosed rate as high as 70% perpetuates low awareness (36.7%), subsequently causing dual collapses in treatment (32.9%) and standardised management rates (10.3%).5 6 With a control rate of merely 50.1%, disabling complications such as diabetic foot ulcers have surged to 61.7%.7 This vicious cycle is intrinsically linked to structural imbalances in healthcare resource allocation. Although China has achieved 3.28 general practitioners (GPs) per 10 000 population nationally, significant disparities persist in rural areas regarding diagnostic equipment availability, healthcare professional qualifications and continuing training systems, resulting in systemic weaknesses in primary care diabetes prevention and control capabilities.8 9 At the level of disease management practice, patient self-management behaviours reveal profound cognitive-behavioural dissociation.10 Self-management, as a core strategy in chronic disease management, has a conceptual evolution that can be traced back to the 1980s. This theoretical framework comprises six core skills: problem-solving, decision-making, resource utilisation, patient-provider collaboration, action planning and self-tailoring.11 12 While most patients demonstrate superficial awareness of basic principles like ‘dietary control’, knowledge of core treatment targets such as HbA1c<7% remains critically inadequate.13 Patients commonly simplify disease risks into tangible representations like ‘non-healing wounds’.6 Such cognitive biases further trigger non-normative coping behaviours including self-medication and blood glucose monitoring avoidance, reflecting fundamental barriers in health knowledge translation.14 Although the three prevailing management models (hospital-centred, community-platform-based and integrated hospital-community collaboration) have demonstrated efficacy in certain regions, they encounter specific challenges in rural contexts: standardised management protocols face limitations when meeting patients’ constrained health literacy, rendering mere improvements in case detection rates insufficient for overcoming management efficacy bottlenecks.15 16 Deconstructing patients’ behavioural logic to establish culturally congruent participatory pathways is thus urgently required. Current research on diabetes primarily emphasises the quantitative analysis of risk factors, while neglecting to explore the behavioural decision-making mechanisms among patients in primary care settings. There is a particular lack of institutional attribution analysis regarding non-normative management strategies.1719 These methodological limitations prevent the current prevention and control systems from adequately addressing the core pathological mechanisms of the disease.

Therefore, the current study aimed to explore the dilemma of self-management among patients with diabetes in primary care settings and to collect suggestions through qualitative interviews.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a qualitative research approach to comprehensively investigate the practical challenges and potential improvement strategies in self-management behaviours among patients with diabetes.20 The research design strictly adhered to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guidelines for standardised reporting.21 A systematic analytical framework based on thematic analysis techniques provided a structured process for qualitative data interpretation. The interview protocol focused on core dimensions of self-management practices among primary care-based patients with diabetes, thereby establishing a thematic analysis-guided qualitative research strategy. Data collection was conducted between May and June 2025 through in-depth interviews with diagnosed type 2 patients with diabetes at four community-based primary care institutions in Beijing.

Participants

This study employed purposive sampling22 to select representative patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) from four rural primary care communities, inviting them to participate in qualitative interviews23 24 aimed at comprehensively investigating self-management challenges and potential improvement strategies. To address the broadness of the initial criteria, we intentionally selected participants with diverse disease durations (1–5 years, 6–10 years and >10 years) and different socioeconomic backgrounds (employed, retired and unemployed). This sampling approach ensured that the study captured a wide range of perspectives on daily disease management, enhancing the transferability of the findings. Eligible patients were first identified by primary care physicians during routine clinical visits; then, research staff provided detailed study information to the potential participants and obtained written informed consent from those who agreed to participate. Attending physicians screened patients against the basic inclusion/exclusion criteria and assisted with patient interviews, but they were not involved in participant enrolment decisions or data analysis. Subject recruitment continued until reaching information saturation—defined as the point when consecutive interviews ceased to generate new analytical unit codes—at which point enrolment was terminated.25

Data collection

The data for this study were collected by four GPs, who had received uniform standardised training organised by the research team prior to data collection. This study conducted face-to-face, semi-structured interviews,21 26 27 organised by primary care physicians who recruited patients with diabetes from community settings.28 Each interview documented session duration and participants’ basic demographic information, with all subjects providing written informed consent. The interview protocol (detailed in online supplemental 1) was codeveloped by the research team through literature synthesis and clinical expertise refinement, with feasibility validated via pilot interviews involving both GPs and two patients with diabetes. Interviews were conducted in meeting rooms at primary care clinics, during which moderators encouraged comprehensive elaboration of perspectives.

Four trained primary care physicians from participating communities served as interviewers, all possessing qualitative research methodology training and professional interviewing experience.

This study invited 36 potential participants, four of whom declined the interview due to scheduling conflicts. Ultimately, 32 respondents were included (table 1), completing 32 in-depth interviews. The mean age of respondents was 62.75±8.26 years (ranging from 35 to 76 years), with a mean diabetes duration of 10.38±9.12 years (ranging from 1 to 30 years). Demographic characteristics showed that females accounted for 81.25% (26/32), agricultural workers accounted for 87.50% (28/32) and 50.00% (16/32) reported a family history of diabetes. Among diabetic complications, diabetic retinopathy was the most common, occurring in 21.88% (7/32) of participants.

Data analysis

All interview recordings underwent verbatim transcription by two coders (FFJ and JZ), with strict avoidance of respondent privacy details. Coding and memoing commenced concurrently with the first interview, progressing through iterative cycles of data collection, coding and analysis until achieving information saturation. Saturation was characterised by analytical conclusions in the final interviews aligning closely with established coding patterns without generating new conceptual elements. Two trained qualitative researchers (FFJ and JZ) employed thematic analysis2931 using QSR NVivo V.14 software to conduct the coding. Both researchers independently reviewed the transcripts and performed inductive open coding to develop an initial structured coding framework. Through repeated and close reading of the data—annotating, comparing and synthesising initial codes—the core themes were allowed to emerge naturally from the data itself, without being constrained by a predefined theoretical framework. Following this inductive phase and the identification of preliminary core themes, a subsequent phase of deductive selective coding was implemented. This stage was guided by established concepts from clinical self-management literature and guidelines. These existing theoretical constructs were selectively applied to re-examine, refine and formally structure the identified themes, thereby integrating the data-driven findings with relevant academic knowledge. To ensure analytical validity, an independent analyst (WJF and MD in clinical research methodology with extensive qualitative data analysis experience) conducted verification analysis of the research data.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Inadequate disease cognition and health literacy

Patients demonstrated insufficient knowledge of diabetes and limited health literacy, encompassing poor disease awareness, dietary misconceptions and deficient medication management understanding.

Deficient diabetes awareness

Diagnosis was frequently delayed, with many cases incidentally identified during physical examinations. Patients often failed to recognise early symptoms (eg, weight loss, fatigue) as diabetes-related, attributing them to non-specific causes like physical exhaustion. Postdiagnosis, systemic understanding of chronic hyperglycaemia’s impact on organs and diabetes complications remained limited, with knowledge typically acquired only after complication onset or witnessing peers’ experiences. A female patient reported:

I was losing weight day by day but thought it was just work fatigue. Only after a checkup did I learn it was diabetes.

Patients rarely proactively sought structured diabetes education, citing unclear learning channels and insufficient physician consultation time. A male patient stated:

I take whatever medications the doctor prescribes. Asking too many questions delays other patients. I mainly learn from TV health programs and mobile phone content.

Knowledge gaps regarding long-term hyperglycaemic damage persisted even after symptom manifestation. Two female patients in their 60s commented:

My vision became blurred like oil paper was covering my eyes—the doctor said diabetes caused this! I never knew high blood sugar could damage eyesight.

Foamy urine? I thought it was 'internal heat'. Only when my daughter took me for tests did we find kidney damage from hyperglycemia.

Dietary control misconceptions and implementation gaps

Dietary management was erroneously equated with ‘avoiding sweet foods’, neglecting core principles of caloric restriction and meal regularity. Suboptimal hypoglycaemia management further exacerbated glycaemic fluctuations.

Most patients restricted obvious sweets (eg, candies and pastries) but consumed uncontrolled portions of staple carbohydrates (rice and noodles) and high-glycaemic-index fruits (bananas lychees). A female patient noted:

The doctor said ‘eat less sweets’, so I avoided apples—but I ate two bowls of rice per meal since rice isn’t sweet.

High-fat/high-carbohydrate foods (eg, fried sugar pastries) were commonly used for hypoglycaemia rescue. An elderly female patient described:

When I feel palpitations or tremors, I immediately eat fried dumplings or sugary oil cakes.

Inadequate medication management knowledge

Patients lacked understanding of pharmacodynamics and risk mitigation, misinterpreting transient glucose reductions as cure signals. This led to dangerous self-adjustment of medications without monitoring.

Over half of interviewees were unaware of drug-specific risks (eg, sulfonylurea-induced hypoglycaemia). One patient shared a critical incident:

After sweeping the yard, I suddenly had blurred vision and drenching sweats—the doctor later said my medication caused this.

Premature self-discontinuation of drugs was prevalent when symptoms improved. A male patient in his 50s admitted:

I adjust medications based on glucose readings: stop drugs for two days when levels drop to 5 mmol/L, then restart when they reach 8 mmol/L.

Suboptimal daily management

Patients exhibited unscientific self-management practices, including unplanned physical activity, irregular glucose monitoring and poor emergency response.

Unstructured exercise management

Physical activity primarily involved uncontrolled daily tasks (eg, housework and walking) rather than structured regimens aligned with medications or diet. A female patient with retinopathy explained:

The doctor advised exercise, but brisk walking makes dark spots float in my vision. I can only sweep the yard slowly.

Irregular glucose monitoring

Testing was symptom-driven rather than protocol-based, with fasting glucose prioritised over postprandial measurements. A male patient reported:

Pricking fingers 2–3 times monthly suffices since medications suppress glucose anyway.

Deficient emergency response capability

Patients used inappropriate hypoglycaemia rescue methods (eg, high-fat foods) and lacked early-warning awareness. A male patient in his 60s described a severe episode:

I mistook chills for a cold, drank congee, and slept. Fortunately, my family rushed me to the hospital.

Fragmented healthcare resources and inadequate family support

Systemic barriers included limited primary care capacity, standardised health education and insufficient familial support.

Community healthcare providers had inadequate diabetes management competencies due to educational and experiential gaps, resulting in non-individualised guidance. A male patient in his 40s stated:

The community doctor advised dietary control and weight loss but gave no specifics. My condition worsened before I sought hospital care.

Limitations in patients’ self-management decision-making

Experience-driven tendency in decision-making rationale

In key domains (disease cognition, lifestyle management and healthcare resource utilisation), patients’ decisions were not anchored in clinical guidelines but prioritised personal lived experience and convenience. This experience-centric paradigm inherently caused discrepancies between their health behaviours and evidence-based medical recommendations. For example, early diabetes symptoms were misattributed to work exhaustion, delaying diagnosis; during hypoglycaemia, patients used prestocked fried foods instead of clinically recommended fast-acting carbohydrates. As one participant recounted:

I lost several kilograms, dismissing it as work tiredness. Diabetes was only diagnosed incidentally during a routine physical exam.

Passive disposition in decision-making mindset

In clinical consultations and health education, patients predominantly exhibited a passive decision-making mindset, lacking initiative to acquire professional information or articulate needs. This passivity exacerbated decision biases: they refrained from inquiring about medication details or dietary rules to avoid occupying clinical time; when given generalised advice by community providers, they did not solicit personalised plans, defaulting to ‘no supplementary guidance indicates no need for further intervention’. As one patient stated:

Clinicians are busy in clinics; I hesitate to ask excessive questions to avoid delaying others. A general recollection of the doctor’s advice suffices.

Discussion

This study employed qualitative methods to investigate the self-management dilemmas of patients with diabetes in Chinese communities, revealing three core challenges. The findings both validate the ‘high prevalence, low control rate’ phenomenon observed in prior quantitative studies and also elucidate the unique behavioural logic and barriers faced by rural patients through rich qualitative data. This provides theoretical foundations and practical pathways for optimising chronic disease control systems.

Limitations in disease cognition and health literacy

Patients exhibited fragmented disease understanding, characterised by oversimplified perceptions of aetiology, unilateral treatment goals and neglect of medication risks. While consistent with global findings on health literacy deficits, 32rural patients demonstrated pronounced ‘experiential cognition’—relying on personal or communal anecdotes rather than medical evidence for decision-making.33 34 Notably, complication awareness often lagged behind clinical manifestations (eg, understanding retinopathy only after its onset), resulting in chronically passive disease management.

Dietary control revealed critical practice-policy gaps. Patients equated ‘sugar-free’ with ‘carbohydrate-free’—a misconception contrasting sharply with urban patients’ scientific grasp of low-carb diets.35 This cognitive divide may stem from culturally insensitive health education materials, where terms like ‘HbA1c’ lacked localised explanations, driving patients towards a binary ‘sweet versus non-sweet’ classification. During hypoglycaemia, non-scientific interventions like consuming sugary oil cakes or fried dumplings highlighted conflicts between traditional dietary culture and medical guidance, underscoring the need to integrate local wisdom into health education.

Medication safety risks emerged prominently: >50% of patients could not explain their drugs’ mechanisms or monitoring requirements. This ‘know-how without know-why’ usage pattern correlated directly with insufficient medication counselling time in primary care. 36A qualitative study conducted in Stockholm on patients with T2DM found that patients had difficulty tailoring lifestyle advice and information to fit their daily lives.37 More alarmingly, ‘glucose-driven drug discontinuation’ reflected patients’ autonomous dose adjustments beyond their competency, exposing deficiencies in shared decision-making.

Daily management barriers

Physical activity manifested as ‘quantity over quality’. Equating housework with structured exercise deviated significantly from guideline-recommended regimens.38 This misalignment partly arose from limited rural sports facilities and professional guidance but deeper from misconceived exercise efficacy—most patients expected immediate glucose-lowering effects rather than long-term metabolic benefits. Unmet expectations naturally reduced adherence.

Symptom-driven glucose monitoring violated the continuity principle of chronic disease management, heavily influenced by healthcare accessibility.39 Individualised ‘normal glucose’ thresholds (ranging subjectively from 5 to 8 mmol/L) indicated lack of personalised targets, contradicting the International Diabetes Federation’s ‘patient-centred’ philosophy.40

Inadequate emergency response capabilities revealed structural flaws in self-management education. The classic definition of self-management proposed by Lorig and Holman refers to the process in which patients actively participate in disease control through three key tasks: ‘medical management, role management and emotional management’, emphasising its focus on developing problem-solving skills beyond traditional health education.41 Its effectiveness has been validated in numerous randomised controlled trials (RCTs)—for example, the Arthritis Self-Management Programme demonstrated a 30% reduction in pain scores among participants, with improved functional status lasting over 2 years. In recent years, the international academic community has further developed contextualised theories. For instance, Riegel’s ‘Situation-Specific Theory of Heart Failure Self-Care’ highlights that patients’ self-management behaviours are influenced by the triadic interaction of ‘individual-family-healthcare system’. Meanwhile, Corbin and Strauss’s ‘Three-Dimensional Model of Chronic Illness Management’ emphasises the balance among ‘illness work, everyday life work and biographical work’, providing key theoretical support for this study’s analysis of the conflict between ‘medication management and farm work arrangements’ among rural older adults.42 Existing programmes emphasised knowledge transfer over practical skills. During hypoglycaemia, high-fat/high-sugar foods exacerbated glycaemic fluctuations, creating a ‘hypo-hyperglycaemia cycle’. This contrasted sharply with urban patients’ standardised use of glucose tablets,43 urging a shift from ‘knowledge dissemination’ to ‘capacity building’ in rural health education.

Systemic constraints: healthcare resources and social support

Primary care capacity gaps constituted the foremost bottleneck. Despite China’s 3.28 GPs per 10 000 people, rural areas suffered ‘quantity-quality mismatch’. Patients reported community doctors ‘only prescribe without explanations’, eroding trust. When unsatisfied, patients turned to TV health programmes and social media—a ‘non-formal knowledge substitution’ phenomenon linked to urban–rural resource disparities.44

Homogeneous health education formats hindered knowledge translation. Lecture-heavy sessions saturated with terms like ‘HbA1c’ and ‘insulin resistance’ clashed with patients’ limited health literacy. When medical advice (eg, ‘control staples’) was misinterpreted as ‘avoid sweets’, education became performative.45 A study conducted in Singapore demonstrated that providing continuous glucose monitor support to patients with T2DM led to improvements in both healthier dietary choices and more frequent blood glucose monitoring.46

The weakening of family support reflects the challenges of chronic disease management in an ageing society.47 The statement that ‘medication is only supervised when the daughter is at home’ reveals the care gap dilemma against the backdrop of rural hollowing-out. While traditional family support functions weaken and community support systems remain underdeveloped, patients naturally fall into an unsupervised state in self-management.48 This support vacuum stands in stark contrast to mature peer support programmes in urban communities,19 highlighting the urgency of establishing primary care community support networks.

Limitations in self-management decision-making

Studies have found that the self-management decision-making of diabetic patients is mostly based on experience rather than scientific medical evidence, which constitutes a core obstacle to the effective control of the disease. Specifically, it is manifested in two aspects: first, guiding behaviours based on personal experience or the illness experiences of relatives and friends. For example, ignoring the necessity of regular blood glucose monitoring because others do not monitor their blood glucose and still remain healthy; second, adjusting medication or diet based solely on subjective feelings. For instance, assuming hypoglycaemia and reducing insulin dosage without authorisation when feeling fatigued, lacking the awareness of making decisions based on objective test results.49

In addition to experiential decision-making, the passive attitude of patients in medical interactions further exacerbates decision-making biases. Many patients are unwilling to take the initiative to inquire about personalised management plans during clinical visits, citing reasons such as ‘not understanding medical terminology’. This passivity stems both from low health literacy and also from the asymmetric power structure in doctor–patient communication at the primary care level.50 Domestic studies have also confirmed that patients’ passive communication is significantly associated with insufficient health education and low trust in primary care providers, which is consistent with the results of this study.

Practically, diabetes management interventions should both focus on knowledge dissemination and also prioritise fostering patients’ active decision-making capabilities. For example, scenario-based training can help patients clarify their health needs and conduct effective doctor–patient communication.

This study fills the paradigm gap in diabetes self-management research through qualitative methods.51 Existing studies have predominantly focused on quantitative risk factor analysis, whereas this research reveals a ‘cognitive-behavioural-systemic’ three-dimensional dilemma model: individual cognitive biases and systemic constraints mutually reinforce to form a vicious cycle of self-management. This multidimensional explanatory framework transcends the traditional ‘patient compliance’ perspective, providing a novel lens for understanding primary chronic disease management. The study identifies a unique ‘crisis-driven cognitive development’ pattern among primary care patients, wherein disease cognition is established only after complication onset. This delayed cognitive formation mechanism challenges the traditional assumption of ‘knowledge first, action later’ in health education.52 Digital health technologies have emerged as promising tools for enhancing self-management and patient education in chronic conditions, including T2DM.53 54 In this context, a meta-analysis of 25 RCTs involving 3360 participants with diabetes or prediabetes revealed that mobile health app interventions significantly improved glycaemic control, with studies on T2DM reporting an average HbA1c reduction of 0.90%.55

Concurrently, systemic reforms are imperative to build capacities and networks enabling the implementation of Integrated Health and Social Care. First, enhance training for GPs/family physicians. Specially trained GPs should assume responsibility for continuous follow-up and personalised guidance, ensuring sufficient communication time to explain test results and treatment plans.56 As core coordinators and providers of Integrated Health and Social Care services in primary care settings, they must possess the capability to integrate medical needs with daily care requirements.57 Second, integrate family support functions by developing ‘Family Health Literacy’ training programmes and reconstructing family care networks.58 59 Empowering family members as informal caregivers with enhanced health management capabilities constitutes an indispensable foundational pillar within the Integrated Health and Social Care system. Third, promote the integration of primary medical and elderly care resources. Encourage the establishment of tight collaboration mechanisms between community health centres, township hospitals, daycare centres and nursing homes. This includes resource sharing and streamlined referral pathways, thereby achieving seamless integration of medical-nursing and daily living care services.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several methodological limitations. First, the samples were collected from four primary communities in Beijing, and the geographical diversity of rural areas may not be fully covered, so the generalisability of the research results requires cautious interpretation. Second, the proportion of female patients was as high as 81.25%, which is related to the gender ratio in rural populations; future studies should further pay attention to the perspectives of male populations. Third, regarding the inherent interpretive bias in qualitative research,25 this study minimised such biases to the greatest extent through researcher triangulation.

With in-depth knowledge of patients’ clinical history, disease progression and treatment adherence, physicians could ask targeted follow-up questions and interpret responses clinically. Their established rapport also built trust, prompting patients to openly share daily disease management experiences and challenges. However, even with standardised training, this interview setup carried response bias risks—patients might avoid expressing negative views on treatment or care due to concerns about their physician relationship. To address this ethical issue, the team adopted four safeguards: (1) prohibiting physicians from guiding patient responses; (2) having researchers present throughout interviews to prevent inappropriate leading; (3) informing participants that interviews would be anonymised, unlinked to medical records and unrelated to future treatment and (4) assigning independent third parties to transcribe recordings verbatim, with the research team reviewing transcripts to ensure data objectivity and consistency.

Conclusion

Self-management challenges among rural Chinese patients with diabetes concentrate in three dimensions: inadequate disease cognition, suboptimal daily practices and fragmented support systems. These reflect systemic misalignments between chronic disease control frameworks. Future interventions should target these dimensions to resolve management dilemmas sustainably, improving glycaemic control and quality of life while advancing rural chronic disease solutions.
