Peer review of cardiothoracic surgical manuscripts: Does timing of review impact the outcome?
Authors: Sara Sakowitz, Ravi Rajaram, Mara B Antonoff
Published: 2025

Peer reviews completed at night or on weekends were rated as lower quality.

Central Message

We found that cardiothoracic surgical peer reviews submitted overnight or during weekends were associated with lower editor-assigned quality ratings relative to those completed during weekdays.

The peer-review process plays a pivotal role in upholding the integrity, rigor, and credibility of scientific research.1 The quality of a surgical journal relies not only on the content of submitted work but also the peer reviewership and timeliness of manuscript evaluation.2 Unfortunately, peer review faces several notable escalating obstacles in the modern era, including challenges with recruiting reviewers, delayed or incomplete reports, and low-quality reviews.3,4 Such setbacks are further exacerbated by the exponential increase in manuscript submissions seen in recent years, concurrently accompanied by reduced time and value placed on academic, nonrevenue-generating activities by physicians.5 Altogether, a limited pool of volunteer peer reviewers must balance journal reviews with numerous other clinical, research, and educational obligations. The impact of these competing priorities and contemporary challenges on the quality of submitted review remains unclear.

Methods

We analyzed deidentified peer review data of manuscripts undergoing primary review in 2023 for The Journal of Thoracic and Cardiovascular Surgery (JTCVS). Reviews were individually rated by Associate Editors on a scale of 1 (lowest quality) to 100 (highest quality). Ratings for reviews were normalized to each reviewer's personal mean score. Subsequently, multivariable models were developed to ascertain the independent association of the time and day of review completion with the overall editor-rated quality of peer review. Models adjusted for conference affiliated submission, submission topic, and total number of previous reviews completed, representing cumulative reviewer experience.

Results

In total, 4341 articles proceeded to primary review and met study inclusion criteria. Manuscripts were reviewed by 943 independent assessors, with a mean peer review quality rating of 78.6 ± 9.0. The mean rating for studies recommended reject was 79.0 ± 8.9, compared with 76.4 ± 10.4 for those recommended accept, 79.7 ± 8.1 revise and re-review for the JTCVS, 76.8 ± 10.2 transfer to JTCVS Techniques, and 78.8 ± 8.6 transfer to Seminars or JTCVS Open.

Just more than one half of reviewers submitted evaluations ≥1 day before the due date (2357, 54%). Another 353 reviews (8%) were submitted within 12 hours of the deadline, 1162 (27%) on time, and 822 (19%) late. Further, 1300 (30%) reviews were completed over the weekend. With regard to time of day, 29% were submitted in the evening, 9% from 12:00 am to 6:00 am, and 63% during the morning or afternoon (Table 1).

Upon multivariable analysis, review completion between 12:00 am and 6:00 am was associated with a significant rating decrement relative to review submission from 6:00am to 11:59pm (β −1.02 points; confidence interval [CI], −2.03 to −0.001; Figure 1). Similarly, performing peer review over the weekend was also linked with lower review quality compared with completion during the week (β −1.23 points; CI, −2.26 to −0.20). The association between late review submission and inferior review quality approached, but did not reach, statistical significance (β −0.90 points; CI, −1.84 to 0.04 points; reference: on-time).

Comment

Academic cardiothoracic surgeons must balance a growing number of competing priorities: their clinical practice, research pursuits, educational experiences, and home as well as family life. It is perhaps unsurprising, therefore, that engagement with the peer-review process is squeezed into already-full personal and professional schedules and takes place between scheduled cases or during nights, early mornings, or weekends. Notably, we found the timing of peer-review submission significantly impacted peer-review quality. In fact, performing peer reviews overnight or during the weekend was associated with a modest, but statistically significant, reduction in editor-assigned quality ratings relative to reviews submitted during the weekday.

The incremental impact of such a ratings difference on ultimate manuscript decisions is unclear. However, awareness of potential time biases by both peer reviewers and journal editors may permit more objective journal decision-making when considering whether submitted articles are worthy of publication. This may be of particular importance in the case of manuscripts assigned borderline scores or given mixed evaluations by peer reviewers.

Furthermore, our findings have implications for the consideration of peer reviewers themselves. We found ∼30% of reviews were submitted over the weekend, and nearly ∼40% between the hours of 6:00 pm and 6:00 am. Although we applaud these reviewers’ clear commitment to the scientific research process, these data underscore the challenges faced by contemporary academic surgeons, both in terms of balance and in potential burnout.5

High-quality peer review takes significant time and effort yet remains uncompensated and underincentivized. To encourage reviewers, some have suggested offering journal acknowledgements, publisher discounts, Continuing Medical Education credits, reviewer-specific networking events, or even direct payments for dedicated reviewership.2 Although journal efforts are important, buy-in is also warranted from academic surgical departments, promotional committees, and compensation structures. Peer review should be valued as an essential component of surgical research by all institutions within the broader academic surgical domain.

Our study was limited to peer review at a single, high-impact cardiothoracic surgical journal, and reviewer or editor demographic information was not reported. We could not account for editor bias in rating reviews or selecting reviewers, editorial board participation, or trends over time outside of the 2023 calendar year. Nonetheless, our findings are of relevance and interest to editors and academic surgeons more broadly, who engage with and experience the ongoing challenges of contemporary peer review.

In conclusion, we found 25% to 30% of reviews were submitted over nights or weekends, yet timing of cardiothoracic peer review impacted quality. Future work should evaluate peer-review patterns in other specialties and develop publisher and institutional-level strategies to improve rating quality and facilitate engagement with the peer-review process more broadly.
