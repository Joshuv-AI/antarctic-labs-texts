Metabolic plasticity underlies ferroptosis resistance driven by mutant p53 in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma
Authors: Adriana Celesia, Filippo Piccoli, Tian Wang, Yushu Hu, Federica Danzi, Antonino Aparo, Barbara Cisterna, Raffaella Pacchiana, Maria Poles, Maria Teresa Scupoli, Claudio Luchini, Stefano Ugel, Massimo Donadelli, Alessandra Fiore
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is an aggressive malignancy characterized by late diagnosis, rapid progression, and resistance to conventional therapies. Mutations in the tumor suppressor gene TP53 are prevalent in this cancer and have been associated with gain-of-function activities that promote tumor survival. Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death driven by lipid peroxidation, represents a potential therapeutic vulnerability. However, the influence of mutant p53 on ferroptosis susceptibility remains poorly understood.MethodsWe employed isogenic pancreatic cancer cell models, including CRISPR-Cas9-mediated TP53 Knockout (TP53-KO) and transient overexpression of common mutant TP53 variants, to examine ferroptosis sensitivity. Cell viability, reactive oxygen species accumulation, lipid peroxidation, mitochondrial integrity, and metabolic profiling were assessed following treatment with ferroptosis inducers. Transcriptional changes were analysed by RNA sequencing, and functional contributions of glycolysis and mitochondrial respiration were evaluated using metabolic flux assays. Pharmacological reactivation of wild-type p53 was tested in combination with ferroptosis inducers in vitro and in orthotopic murine models.ResultsDeletion of mutant TP53 sensitized pancreatic cancer cells to ferroptosis, accompanied by increased oxidative stress, lipid peroxidation and mitochondrial dysfunction. Mutant TP53 expression preserved mitochondrial integrity and sustained bioenergetic flexibility under ferroptotic stress. Transcriptomic analyses revealed a multi-layered adaptive program, including upregulation of antioxidant and metabolic genes, and activation of PI3K–AKT signaling, which is associated to a selective glycolytic shift to maintain ATP levels. Functional modulation confirmed that glucose supplementation enhanced, whereas glycolytic inhibition impaired, survival under ferroptotic conditions in mutant TP53-expressing cells, but not in TP53-KO cells. Pharmacological reactivation of wild-type p53 disrupted this adaptive network, abrogating glycolytic reprogramming and significantly increasing ferroptotic cell death both in vitro and in vivo.ConclusionsMutant p53 orchestrates transcriptional and metabolic plasticity that confers ferroptosis resistance in pancreatic cancer. Reactivating wild-type p53 in combination with ferroptosis induction overcomes this resistance, highlighting a therapeutically actionable vulnerability and supporting the development of combinatorial strategies for this aggressive malignancy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12943-026-02746-y.

Background

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) remains one of the deadliest malignancies worldwide, with a dismal five-year survival rate of just 13% [1]. Its poor prognosis is largely driven by late diagnosis, rapid metastatic progression, profound metabolic plasticity, and intrinsic resistance to conventional therapies [2]. Despite advances in targeted and immunotherapeutic strategies, effective treatment options for advanced PDAC remain extremely limited, underscoring the urgent need to identify novel therapeutic vulnerabilities.

Among the key genetic alterations driving PDAC progression, mutations in the tumor suppressor gene TP53 are particularly prevalent, occurring in about 50–70% of cases [3, 4]. Mutant p53 promotes tumorigenesis through multiple mechanisms, including loss of its tumor-suppressive function [5], dominant-negative inhibition of wild-type p53 [6], and the acquisition of gain-of-function (GOF) activities that actively drive cancer progression, metastasis, and therapy resistance [7–9]. Importantly, different TP53 missense mutations exhibit substantial functional heterogeneity. Common hotspot mutants such as TP53R175H, TP53R248W, and TP53R273H display distinct structural and transcriptional properties, leading to divergent biological outcomes depending on cellular and tissue context [10, 11]. However, the contribution of specific PDAC-associated mutant p53 variants to therapy resistance mechanisms remains incompletely understood. Recent advances have highlighted ferroptosis, a regulated iron-dependent form of cell death characterized by lipid peroxidation, as a promising therapeutic vulnerability in PDAC [12, 13]. PDAC cells exhibit profound alterations in iron metabolism, redox homeostasis, and lipid remodeling, rendering them particularly susceptible to ferroptotic stress under specific metabolic conditions [14, 15]. Importantly, modulation of ferroptosis-related pathways, such as GPX4, iron metabolism, and lipid peroxidation, has been shown to overcome chemotherapy resistance by enhancing ferroptotic cell death in tumor cells [16]. Nevertheless, the molecular determinants governing ferroptosis sensitivity in PDAC remain poorly defined. Wild-type p53 has been implicated in ferroptosis regulation through context-dependent mechanisms involving SLC7A11 repression, reactive oxygen species accumulation, and modulation of lipid peroxidation pathways, generally promoting ferroptotic sensitivity [17, 18]. In contrast, the role of mutant p53 in ferroptosis remains far less clear and appears highly context-specific. While some studies suggest that certain p53 mutants may enhance oxidative stress tolerance and metabolic adaptation, thereby suppressing ferroptotic cell death [19], the mechanistic relationship between PDAC-associated mutant p53 variants and ferroptosis susceptibility has not been systematically investigated. In particular, whether ferroptosis resistance represents a shared feature across different GOF mutants or a mutation-specific phenotype remains an unresolved question. In this study, we investigate how mutant p53 modulates ferroptosis sensitivity in PDAC cells, focusing on both functional outcomes and underlying molecular mechanisms. Using CRISPR-Cas9 knockout models, overexpression systems, and multi-level metabolic and transcriptional analyses, we reveal that mutant p53 confers resistance to ferroptotic cell death by preserving mitochondrial integrity and orchestrating a metabolic adaptation towards glycolysis that supports survival under oxidative stress. Importantly, our work provides mechanistic insight into how mutant p53 rewires ferroptosis-related metabolic pathways in PDAC and identifies a combinatorial therapeutic strategy capable of increasing ferroptosis sensitivity in mutant p53-expressing cells. These findings not only clarify a previously underexplored link between mutant p53 and ferroptosis regulation in PDAC but also highlight mutant p53-driven metabolic plasticity as a clinically actionable barrier to ferroptosis-based therapies.

Results

Mutant p53 deletion sensitizes PDAC cells to ferroptosis

To investigate the role of mutant p53 in regulating ferroptosis in PDAC, we used CRISPR-Cas9 knockout (KO) clones generated from the human PDAC cell line PANC-1 [20, 21], harbouring the hotspot TP53R273H mutation commonly found in PDAC patients. Three independent TP53-KO clones were selected following confirmation of p53 loss by western blotting (Fig. 1A). To assess their sensitivity to ferroptosis, we treated both parental and TP53-KO cells with three distinct ferroptosis inducers: Erastin (a system Xc⁻ inhibitor [22]), RAS-Selective Lethal 3 (RSL3; a GPX4 inhibitor [23]), and Ferroptocide (a ferroptosis inducer targeting the thioredoxin antioxidant system [24]), across a range of concentrations. Cell viability was evaluated using three complementary approaches: MTT assay (for metabolic activity evaluation) (Fig. 1B), CellTox Green cytotoxicity assay (for membrane integrity assessment) (Fig. 1C), and Annexin V/PI staining followed by flow cytometry (for cell death profiling) (Fig. 1D and S1A). All three assays consistently demonstrated that TP53-KO clones are significantly more sensitive to ferroptosis-inducing agents compared to parental PANC-1 cells. The effect was robust and reproducible across all three TP53-KO clones and all three ferroptosis inducers. To confirm that the observed cell death was specifically attributable to ferroptosis, TP53-KO cells were co-treated with established ferroptosis inhibitors Ferrostatin-1 [25] and Liproxstatin-1 [26]. Both inhibitors fully rescued cell viability in TP53-KO clones treated with the aforementioned ferroptosis inducers (Fig. 1E). In contrast, pharmacological blockade of alternative cell death pathways, including apoptosis (z-VAD-fmk), necroptosis (Necrostatin-1), and autophagy (Bafilomycin A1), failed to prevent cell death, confirming the specificity of the ferroptotic response (Fig. S1B-C). Mechanistically, the increased susceptibility to ferroptosis of TP53-KO cells was associated with significantly elevated levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) (Fig. 1F), and enhanced lipid peroxidation, measured by 4-Hydroxynonenal (4-HNE) western blot (Fig. 1G and Fig. S1D). Ultrastructural analysis by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) further revealed early and profound organelle damage in TP53-KO PANC-1 cells exposed to RSL3, including dilated endoplasmic reticulum, loosened Golgi apparatus, and a pronounced dilatation of the perinuclear space, all evident within 6 h of treatment. On the contrary, parental cells remained morphologically intact at this time point (Fig. 1H). Altogether, these findings suggest that TP53R273H mutation in PANC-1 cells confers resistance to ferroptosis and that its genetic ablation re-sensitizes cells to ferroptotic cell death by amplifying oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation. Importantly, the differential sensitivity to ferroptosis was not associated with altered proliferative capacity, as Ki67 staining performed following treatment with ferroptosis-inducing agents revealed comparable proliferation rates (Fig. S1E). To further validate these observations under a more physiological ferroptosis-inducing condition, cells were subjected to cyst(e)ine (CYS) deprivation. Consistent with the results obtained using pharmacological ferroptosis inducers, CYS starvation [27, 28] triggered ferroptotic cell death with similar kinetics, with parental PANC-1 cells displaying significantly greater resistance than TP53-KO clones (Fig. S1F). Furthermore, in line with previous findings demonstrating that ferroptosis evasion could be related to chemotherapy resistance [29], we found that prolonged exposure to low-dose gemcitabine for 5 days resulted in a significantly greater loss of viability in TP53-KO cells compared with parental PANC-1 cells (Fig. S1G). Finally, the increased ferroptosis sensitivity associated with mutant p53 loss was confirmed in previously established TP53-KO clones derived from the independent PDAC cell line MIA PaCa-2 (Fig. S1H) [20], harboring the hotspot TP53R248W mutation, suggesting that this phenotype is not restricted to a single cellular model (Fig. S1I).

Over-expression of PDAC-associated mutant p53 variants limits ferroptotic cell death in p53-null pancreatic cancer cells

To further dissect the contribution of GOF mutant p53 isoforms to ferroptosis resistance, we employed a complementary approach based on transient expression of mutant p53 in p53-null PDAC cells AsPC-1. As a preliminary validation, we compared the response of PANC-1 and p53-null AsPC-1 cells to ferroptosis induction and we found that AsPC-1 cells were significantly more sensitive to RSL3 and Ferroptocide treatment than PANC-1 cells (Fig. S2A), consistent with a ferroptosis-protective role of mutant p53. Then, six of the most prevalent TP53 hotspot mutations identified in PDAC patients (TP53R273H, TP53R175H, TP53R282W, TP53R248H, TP53R248Q, and TP53Y220C) were individually expressed and protein expression was confirmed by western blotting (Fig. 2A). Following transfection, cells were exposed to Erastin, RSL3, or Ferroptocide for 24 h and ferroptotic cell death was quantified by Annexin V/PI staining and flow cytometry. Notably, expression of each mutant p53 isoform significantly attenuated ferroptosis-induced cell death compared with mock-transfected controls, resulting in increased cell viability across all three ferroptosis-inducing conditions (Fig. 2B and Fig. S2B). These findings are consistent with the increased ferroptosis sensitivity observed following TP53 deletion in PANC-1 and MIA PaCa-2 cells and indicate that ferroptosis resistance represents a shared functional property of multiple PDAC-associated mutant p53 proteins. As a biological comparison, we next examined the effect of wild-type p53 expression in the same experimental setting. Given the rapid turnover and tight physiological regulation of wild-type p53, lower amounts of wild type TP53 expression plasmid were used compared with those employed for mutant p53 isoforms. In contrast to the protective effect exerted by mutant p53 protein, wild-type p53 expression increased the susceptibility of AsPC-1 cells to RSL3-induced ferroptosis in a dose-dependent manner, resulting in enhanced cell death relative to mock-transfected cells (Fig. S2C-D). These findings are consistent with the established tumor suppressor function of wild-type p53 and with previous studies demonstrating that wild type p53 promotes ferroptosis [17]. Mechanistically, mutant p53 expression was associated with reduced ROS accumulation following RSL3 treatment compared with mock-transfected controls (Fig. 2C). Furthermore, all mutant p53 variants significantly attenuated lipid peroxidation, as evidenced by decreased accumulation of 4-HNE protein adducts detected by western blot analysis (Fig. 2D). Consistent results were obtained using C11-BODIPY staining, which revealed reduced lipid ROS levels in cells expressing TP53R273H following ferroptosis induction (Fig. S2E). Together, these data indicate that distinct GOF mutant p53 isoforms converge on a common ferroptosis-resistant phenotype characterized by suppression of oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation.

To investigate whether this phenotype is reflected in human disease, we analysed transcriptomic and mutational data from the TCGA pancreatic adenocarcinoma (TCGA-PAAD) cohort. Patients were stratified according to TP53 mutational status and expression of genes involved in ferroptosis regulation and redox homeostasis was examined. TP53-mutated tumors displayed significantly higher anti-ferroptotic and redox-related transcriptional signatures than TP53 wild-type tumors. Specifically, a composite GPX4/SLC7A11 anti-ferroptotic score was significantly increased in TP53-mutated samples (p = 0.043), and an extended GPX4/SLC7A11/TXN redox-ferroptosis score showed an even stronger association with TP53 mutational status (p = 6.5 × 10⁻⁶) (Fig. 2E-F). These findings support the existence of an enhanced anti-ferroptotic transcriptional program in TP53-mutated PDAC. Given our observation that TP53-deficient cells display increased sensitivity to prolonged gemcitabine treatment, we next investigated whether anti-ferroptotic programs are associated with adaptation to chemotherapy. To this end, we interrogated publicly available RNA-seq datasets from mutant p53-PDAC cell lines (PANC-1, BxPC-3, CFPAC-1) exposed to gemcitabine or exhibiting acquired gemcitabine resistance (GEM-R). Analysis of GSE249302 and GSE140077 revealed a consistent increase in a composite anti-ferroptotic signature comprising GPX4, SLC7A11, and TXN following gemcitabine exposure and in gemcitabine-resistant derivatives. This trend was observed across all PDAC models analysed (Fig. 2G-J). These findings suggest that activation of ferroptosis-protective pathways may represent an adaptive response associated with chemotherapy tolerance and resistance. Notably, we previously demonstrated that chemotherapeutic treatments, including gemcitabine, induce Ser15 phosphorylation of mutant p53, a modification associated with its activation [21, 30]. Therefore, the induction of anti-ferroptotic programs upon gemcitabine exposure may reflect the engagement of mutant p53-dependent adaptive responses, raising the possibility that the acquisition of gemcitabine resistance is mediated, at least in part, through the ferroptosis-protective mechanisms described here.

Mutant p53 status is associated with preserved mitochondrial integrity during ferroptosis in pancreatic cancer cells

Mitochondria play a crucial role in ferroptosis, as their morphology and functionality can influence cellular redox status and susceptibility to lipid peroxidation [31]. Building on our recent publication demonstrating distinct mitochondrial morphology and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) profiles in PDAC cells harboring mutant p53 compared to p53 wild-type or null cells [20], we conducted a detailed ultrastructural and functional analysis of mitochondria in our PANC-1 models upon ferroptosis induction. TEM analysis performed after only 6 h of RSL3 treatment revealed that TP53-KO cells exhibited severely compromised mitochondrial integrity, characterized by disrupted cristae architecture, damaged mitochondrial membranes, and marked alterations in overall shape (Fig. 3A). In these cells, mitochondria displayed a reduced aspect ratio, reflecting a shift toward a more rounded morphology (Fig. 3B), together with an overall increase in mitochondrial area (Fig. 3C). This enlargement is consistent with mitochondrial swelling, a feature typically found in cells affected by ferroptosis and not observed in RSL3-treated parental cells. Indeed, PANC-1 mitochondria largely preserved their structural integrity under RSL3 treatment, showing only minor morphological changes and no evidence of swelling (Fig. 3A-C). Interestingly, flow cytometry assessment of mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm) revealed opposite trends between mutant p53-expressing and TP53-KO cells in response to RSL3-induced ferroptosis. Indeed, while parental PANC-1 cells exhibited rapid mitochondrial hyperpolarization upon RSL3 treatment, TP53-KO cells displayed significant depolarization (Fig. 3D). This divergence is particularly relevant given that mutant p53 PANC-1 cells are generally more sensitive to the mitochondrial uncoupler agent CCCP, a feature typically associated with cells exhibiting a high reliance on OXPHOS [32]. In addition, immunoblot analysis of the mitochondrial respiratory complexes (CI–CV) revealed that, at 8 h post-ferroptosis induction with RSL3, mutant p53 PANC-1 cells upregulate all five complexes (Fig. 3E), further suggesting an active compensatory response to maintain electron transport chain function and mitochondrial bioenergetics under ferroptotic stress. We therefore performed the Seahorse Mitostress assay to evaluate OXPHOS capacity. Confirming our previous observation [20], PANC-1 cells harboring mutant p53 display significantly higher baseline of OXPHOS levels compared to TP53-KO PANC-1 (Fig. 3F). Taking this intrinsic difference into account, we found that under ferroptotic conditions, both cell types exhibited a reduction in key respiratory parameters including basal respiration, maximal respiration, and ATP production but the decline was significantly more pronounced in TP53-KO cells (Fig. 3F). Collectively, these results indicate that mutant p53 is associated with mitochondrial integrity during early ferroptotic stress, contributing to ferroptosis resistance in PDAC cells.

Mutant p53 orchestrates a complex gene expression pattern preventing ferroptosis in PDAC

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying mutant p53-mediated resistance to ferroptosis, we performed comprehensive RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) profiling on TP53R273H PANC-1 cells and three independent TP53-KO PANC-1 clones following 6 h of treatment with the three ferroptosis inducers (Erastin, RSL3, and Ferroptocide; Sup. Tables 1–2). This early time point was selected to capture transcriptional changes in TP53-KO viable cells, as later time points would compromise cell integrity and alter the following analysis. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) revealed a clear distinct clustering of parental and TP53-KO PANC-1 cells, as well as between the different treatment groups (Fig. 4A and S3A). Notably, parental PANC-1 cells harbouring mutant p53 displayed a roughly five-fold greater number of differentially expressed transcripts (both upregulated and downregulated) in response to ferroptosis inducers compared to TP53-KO clones, indicating a more robust transcriptional program (Fig. 4B). Among the analysed transcripts, we identified several genes that were consistently upregulated in TP53R273H-cells (log2FC > 0.8, p < 0.05) by at least two different ferroptosis inducers and are known to functionally contribute to cellular protection against ferroptosis through complementary mechanisms (Fig. 4C). These genes act through diverse strategies: Ten-Eleven Translocation methylcytosine dioxygenase 1 (TET1) regulates gene expression and control inflammation, helping cells to manage oxidative stress and survive harmful conditions [33, 34]; Nitric Oxide Synthase 3 (NOS3) and Superoxide Dismutase 3 (SOD3) directly modulate oxidative stress by balancing reactive oxygen species and nitric oxide levels, thereby preventing lipid damage that drives ferroptosis [35–37]. Increased levels of Aldehyde Oxidase 1 (AOX1) counteract oxidative stress by detoxifying lipid-derived aldehydes [38, 39], while metabolic regulators such as Pyruvate Dehydrogenase Kinase 4 (PDK4) fine-tune energy pathways and produce antioxidant metabolites that further shield cells from oxidative injury [28, 40, 41]. Alpha-Tocopherol Transfer Protein (TTPA) provides targeted antioxidant defence and modulate inflammatory signaling to reduce susceptibility to ferroptosis [42, 43]. Transcriptional and mitochondrial regulators such as SRY-Box Transcription Factor 13 (SOX13) and Forkhead Box Q1 (FOXQ1) enhance resistance to ferroptosis by sustaining bioenergetics and activating antioxidant pathways that limit lipid peroxidation and intracellular iron accumulation [44, 45]. We confirmed the upregulation of these transcripts in PANC-1 parental cells by real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) on independent samples, validating the RNA-seq findings and highlighting their coordinated role in anti-ferroptotic defence (Fig. 4D). To investigate the biological pathways associated with the mutant p53-dependent transcriptional response to ferroptotic stress, we performed Over-Representation Analysis (ORA) on up-regulated genes using KEGG pathway annotations (Fig. 4E and S3B-D). Notably, among all enriched pathways, the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway is consistently maintained as the most significantly represented across all three ferroptosis-inducing treatments. Consistent with these transcriptomic findings, protein-level analyses demonstrated that RSL3 treatment activates a compensatory PI3K-AKT signaling response in mutant p53-expressing PANC-1 cells, characterized by increased AKT Ser473 phosphorylation and preservation of downstream mTOR signaling. This adaptive response was lost in p53-deficient cells, which displayed reduced AKT Thr308 and phospho-S6 levels following ferroptotic stress (Fig. 4F and Supplementary Fig. S4A). In addition, flow cytometry analyses demonstrated that pharmacological inhibition of the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway significantly enhanced ferroptosis sensitivity in PANC-1 cells. This effect was consistently observed using four independent inhibitors targeting different nodes of the pathway, including the PI3K inhibitors LY294002, PI-103, and Copanlisib, as well as the AKT inhibitor MK-2206 (Fig. 4G-H and Fig. S4B-C). Notably, MK-2206 potentiated cell death induced not only by RSL3 but also by Erastin and Ferroptocide (Fig. S4D-E), indicating that PI3K–AKT signaling broadly contributes to ferroptosis resistance irrespective of the ferroptosis-inducing stimulus. Together, these findings indicate that mutant p53 promotes a broad, multi-component protective program, encompassing coordinated transcriptional adaptations and activation of PI3K-AKT signaling, thereby contributing to ferroptosis resistance.

Mutant p53 promotes glycolysis to sustain ATP production under ferroptotic stress

PI3K-AKT signaling is a central regulator of glycolytic metabolism in cancer, promoting glucose uptake and ATP production to support cell survival [46]. The persistent activation of this pathway observed in mutant p53-expressing PANC-1 cells under ferroptotic stress suggests its potential contribution in maintaining ATP levels. To directly examine how cellular metabolism responds to ferroptotic stress, we focused on the metabolic profile of TP53R273H and TP53-KO PANC-1 cells by measuring glycolytic and mitochondrial ATP production rates under treatment with the ferroptosis inducers RSL3, Erastin, and Ferroptocide. These analyses confirmed the expected metabolic differences between the two genotypes, with TP53R273H PANC-1 cells displaying a more metabolically active phenotype at baseline, characterized by higher ATP production rates from both glycolytic and mitochondrial pathways, compared to their TP53-KO counterpart. Upon ferroptosis induction, compared to parental cells, TP53-KO cells exhibited a marked reduction in ATP production rate, consistent with an impaired metabolic adaptation to ferroptotic stress. In contrast, mutant p53-expressing cells largely maintained ATP production and showed a trend toward increased glycolytic ATP generation following treatment (Fig. 5A and Fig. S5A-B). To further investigate this phenotype, we performed a glycolysis stress test. In line with the ATP rate assay, RSL3-treated TP53R273H-PANC-1 cells exhibited higher glycolytic activity, glycolytic capacity, and glycolytic reserve compared to TP53-KO cells (Fig. 5B), corroborating the notion that mutant p53 supports and promotes a glycolytic response under ferroptotic stress. Importantly, this phenotype was recapitulated in MIA PaCa-2 cells (TP53R248W) (Fig. 5C), but not in PDAC cells with TP53wt (Hs766T) (Fig. 5D) or in non-tumorigenic human pancreatic ductal epithelial cells (TP53wt-HPDE) (Fig. 5E), which in contrast displayed a reduction in glycolysis after ferroptosis induction. These data indicate that glycolytic adaptation is a mutant p53-specific feature. To assess the functional impact of metabolic plasticity, parental and TP53-KO PANC-1 cells were treated with ferroptosis inducers in presence of either increasing concentrations of glucose or the glycolytic inhibitor 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG) (0.5-5 mM). In TP53R273HPANC-1 cells, supplementation with glucose significantly increased survival under ferroptotic stress, whereas inhibition of glycolysis with 2-DG markedly increased cell death (Fig. 5F). In contrast, TP53-KO cells showed no appreciable change in survival under both condition (Fig. 5G). These results demonstrate that glucose metabolism specifically supports ferroptosis resistance in mutant p53-expressing cells, highlighting glycolytic reprogramming as a key adaptive mechanism.

Restoring wild type p53 functions to enhance ferroptosis-based therapies in PDAC

Our findings provide evidence that mutant p53 acts as a central coordinator of a robust, multi-layered transcriptional and metabolic network that enables PDAC cells to withstand ferroptotic stress. By simultaneously preserving mitochondrial integrity, sustaining bioenergetic flexibility, and activating complementary antioxidant and metabolic defence programs, mutant p53 establishes a highly redundant ferroptosis resistance architecture. This functional redundancy suggests that targeting individual downstream effectors is unlikely to effectively dismantle ferroptosis resistance. To translate these mechanistic insights into a therapeutic strategy for advanced PDAC, we therefore shifted our focus upstream to mutant p53 itself. Specifically, we evaluated the effect of the pharmacological reactivation of mutant-p53 using APR-246 (Eprenetapopt), a first-in-class compound capable of restoring wild-type p53 conformation and neutralizing mutant p53 oncogenic functions [47]. We found that co-treatment with ferroptosis inducers, Erastin, RSL3 or IKE, and APR-246 strongly sensitized PANC-1 and MIA PaCa-2 cells to ferroptosis, suggesting that targeting mutant p53 can mitigate its anti-ferroptotic effects (Fig. 6A and S6A-D). Notably, this combinatorial approach significantly reduced the effective doses of both APR-246 and the ferroptosis inducers, enhancing the translational potential of this strategy and making it more clinically relevant. Mechanistically, this sensitization was associated with a failure to activate the glycolytic response previously observed, highlighting the critical role of this compensatory metabolic adaptation in supporting cell survival under ferroptotic stress (Fig. 6B and S6E-F). To validate these findings and translate them in vivo, we first introduced a new murine PDAC model, the 7940b KPC cell line harbouring TP53R172H, derived from a highly aggressive pancreatic tumor and characterized by rapid growth, typically forming big masses in less than one month [48, 49]. Functionally, 7940b cells reproduced the strong synergistic response to Erastin in combination with APR-246 previously observed in the human system, further supporting the conservation of the mutant p53-driven anti-ferroptotic program across species (Fig. S6G). For in vivo experiments, we selected IKE as ferroptosis inducer, given its superior metabolic stability relative to Erastin and its widespread use in the literature, for eliciting ferroptosis in animal models [50–52]. In vitro, 7940b cells exhibited ferroptotic cell death in response to IKE, which was effectively blocked by the ferroptosis inhibitors Ferrostatin-1 and Liproxstatin-1 (Fig. 6C), but was markedly potentiated when combined with APR-246 (Fig. 6D). The potentiating effect of APR-246 was also observed with Erastin in 7940b cells (S6G). Synergism between APR-246 and IKE was quantitatively assessed by CompuSyn software analysis, which identified strong synergistic interactions at effect levels below 50% cell viability as compared to untreated control (Fig. 6E-F). Consistent with these observations, a second murine PDAC line derived from KPC tumors, MT3, exhibited the same pattern of sensitivity and combinatorial potentiation with both Erastin (Fig. S6H) and IKE (Fig. S6I-K), thus reinforcing the reproducibility of our mechanistic model. On the basis of these results, we proceeded with in vivo validation using an orthotopic model in immunocompetent C57BL/6 mice (Fig. 6G). 7940b cells were orthotopically implanted and, 14 days post-implantation, the presence of localized tumors smaller than 100 mm³ was confirmed via ultrasounds, indicating early yet established disease. At this stage, animals were randomized into 4 treatment groups to evaluate the effect of single agents and the APR-246 + IKE combination within a physiologically relevant tumor microenvironment. Mice received intraperitoneal (ip) administration of APR-246 (40 mg/kg), IKE (40 mg/kg), or their combination over four treatment cycles. On day 20 post-implantation, ultrasound imaging was performed to monitor tumor growth. Notably, only tumors in mice treated with the combination therapy showed a significant reduction in growth compared to those receiving either single agent or vehicle, highlighting the enhanced therapeutic efficacy of co-targeting mutant p53 and inducing ferroptosis in vivo (Fig. 6H).

Importantly, the antitumor activity of the combination treatment was not associated with overt signs of systemic toxicity. Body weight measurements collected at the experimental endpoint revealed no significant differences among treatment groups, and treated animals did not exhibit clinical signs of cachexia (Fig. S7A). Moreover, serum analyses performed at sacrifice showed no significant alterations in alanine aminotransferase (ALT) activity or creatinine levels, indicating the absence of detectable hepatic or renal toxicity following treatment with APR-246 and IKE (Fig. S7B-C). Together, these findings suggest that the therapeutic benefit of the combination regimen is achieved within a favorable preliminary tolerability window. Following two additional treatment cycles, control mice began to exhibit signs of distress. We therefore sacrificed the animals, harvested tumors, and prepared tumor lysates for metabolic profiling to investigate how combination therapy affects tumor bioenergetics. Both ATP production rate assays and glycolysis stress tests on these tumor lysates revealed a ferroptosis-induced glycolytic shift in tumors treated with IKE alone, which was completely reversed by co-treatment with APR-246, effectively abolishing the mutant-p53 metabolic reprogramming underlying ferroptosis resistance (Fig. 6I-J). To further validate ferroptosis induction in vivo, we analysed 4-HNE accumulation in tumor lysates collected at sacrifice. Tumors treated with the combination of IKE and APR-246 exhibited significantly higher levels of 4-HNE compared with vehicle- or single-agent-treated tumors (Fig. S7D), indicating enhanced lipid peroxidation in vivo. These findings further support the ability of APR-246 to potentiate ferroptosis induction in mutant p53-driven PDAC tumors.

Together, our data identify mutant p53-driven metabolic plasticity as a key determinant of ferroptosis resistance in PDAC. Reactivating wild-type p53 functions collapses this adaptive state and restores ferroptotic vulnerability in vitro and in vivo, uncovering a therapeutically actionable dependency in pancreatic cancer (Fig. 7).

Discussion

PDAC exemplifies the challenges of modern oncology, with mortality exceeding that of many other solid tumors due to late diagnosis, aggressive local invasion, and multifactorial therapy resistance. Despite advances in immunotherapy and targeted therapies, median survival in patients with metastatic disease remains below 12 months, underscoring the urgent need to identify novel therapeutic vulnerabilities [53]. In this context, ferroptosis emerges as a promising paradigm to overcome these barriers bypassing apoptosis, which is frequently impaired in PDAC. Recent studies have shown that ferroptosis inducers, such as Erastin and RSL3, exert selective cytotoxic effects on PDAC cells, both in vitro and in xenograft models, suggesting immediate clinical potential [14]. However, the translational success of these strategies is hindered by robust intrinsic resistance mediated by prevalent genetic alterations, including TP53 mutations. TP53 mutations not only abrogate wild-type tumor-suppressive functions, but also confer GOF pro-tumorigenic activities that remodel cellular metabolism, promote survival, and drive chemoresistance. In non-PDAC contexts, mutant p53 has been implicated in regulating ferroptosis via mechanisms such as NRF2 and SLC7A11 upregulation, which detoxify lipid peroxides [54, 55]. Yet, the specific role of PDAC-associated hotspot TP53 variants in modulating ferroptosis sensitivity remains poorly defined. Indeed, to date the only prior evidence for an anti-ferroptotic function of mutant p53 comes from triple-negative breast cancer, where genetic ablation of hotspot mutation induced tumor ferroptosis through loss of NRF2-mediated transcriptional control of lipid peroxide detoxifying enzymes [19]. In addition to TP53 alterations, PDAC progression is driven by recurrent mutations in KRAS, CDKN2A, and SMAD4, which may also influence cellular stress responses and therapeutic adaptation [56]. Among these, KRAS has been implicated in ferroptosis regulation through metabolic rewiring and modulation of redox-associated pathways [57, 58], whereas the contribution of CDKN2A and SMAD4 to ferroptosis remains less clearly defined. Although additional PDAC drivers may participate in shaping ferroptotic susceptibility, our findings support a direct role for mutant p53 in promoting ferroptosis resistance in PDAC cells.

Our study fills this gap, demonstrating for the first time that mutant p53 establishes a multifactorial barrier to ferroptosis in PDAC cells, integrating mitochondrial preservation, glycolytic metabolic adaptation, and antioxidant/stress-response pathway activation. Using isogenic CRISPR-Cas9 knockout models, mutant p53 overexpression systems, and integrated transcriptomic and metabolic analyses, we demonstrate that mutant p53 coordinates a multilayered protective network that maintains mitochondrial integrity, preserves bioenergetic flexibility, and engages complementary antioxidant and stress-response pathways. These mechanisms collectively buffer oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation, enabling survival under ferroptotic insults. A key component of this adaptive network is the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway. Consistent with its established role in regulating glycolysis and survival in cancer, PI3K-AKT is selectively hyperactivated in mutant p53-expressing PDAC cells under ferroptotic stress. This activation preferentially increases glycolytic ATP production, thereby maintaining energy homeostasis when mitochondrial respiration is compromised. In contrast, TP53-KO cells experience a collapse of both glycolytic and mitochondrial ATP production, underscoring the essential role of metabolic plasticity in ferroptosis resistance. Functional manipulation of glucose availability further highlights this dependency: glucose supplementation enhances survival in mutant p53 cells, whereas glycolysis inhibition with 2-deoxy-D-glucose accelerates ferroptotic cell death. These results define the glycolytic shift as a critical adaptive mechanism downstream of mutant p53, directly linking metabolic reprogramming to ferroptosis resistance.

Our data extend prior observations focused on wild-type p53, demonstrating its role in promoting ferroptotic sensitivity via SLC7A11 downregulation and context-dependent enhancement of lipid peroxidation, positioning mutant p53 as pro-survival driver [17]. From a translational perspective, our findings highlight the challenges of targeting ferroptosis resistance in PDAC: the redundancy and robustness of mutant p53-mediated protective programs suggest that single-target interventions may prove insufficient. In contrast, pharmacological reactivation of mutant p53 with APR-246, which restores a wild-type-like conformation and transcriptional activity, not only blocks mutant p53’s anti-ferroptotic barrier, but also exploits restored wild-type p53 pro-ferroptotic functions, abrogating glycolytic adaptation, and potently sensitizing cells to ferroptosis both in vitro and in vivo. This dual-action combinatorial strategy (suppressing anti-ferroptotic GOF while unleashing wild-type pro-ferroptotic activity) demonstrates the feasibility of co-targeting mutant p53 and ferroptosis to overcome intrinsic resistance, potentially enabling lower doses of ferroptosis inducers and broadening clinical applicability.

Conclusions

In summary, our study identifies mutant p53-driven metabolic and transcriptional plasticity as a central barrier to ferroptosis in PDAC. By linking mitochondrial preservation, transcriptional control, and glycolytic reprogramming through PI3K-AKT signaling, we provide a mechanistic framework for understanding ferroptosis resistance in this aggressive cancer. Importantly, pharmacological reactivation of mutant p53 with APR-246, which restores wild-type-like p53 activity, disrupts this adaptive network, thereby restoring ferroptotic vulnerability and offering a therapeutically actionable strategy. These findings underscore the potential of co-targeting mutant p53 and ferroptosis as a precision-based approach to overcome intrinsic resistance and improve treatment outcomes in PDAC.

Materials and methods

Cell cultures

The PDAC human cell lines PANC-1 (TP53R273H), AsPC-1 (p53-null), MIAPaca-2 (TP53R248W), and Hs766T (TP53wt) were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). The Human Pancreatic Duct Epithelial Cell Line (HPDE) was kindly provided by Prof. Aldo Scarpa (University of Verona, Italy). The mouse KPC-derived cell lines 7940b and MT3 were kindly provided by D. Tuveson (Cold Spring Harbor Laboratory) and G.L. Beatty (University of Pennsylvania) via Material Transfer Agreement (MTA). For routine propagation, unless otherwise indicated, all cell lines were cultured in RPMI 1640 (Gibco, 52400025) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco, A5256701) and 50 µg/mL Gentamicin (Gibco, 15750037). Cells were kept in a humified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Phosphate-Buffered Saline (PBS) (Gibco, 14190094) and trypsin-EDTA (Gibco, 25200056) were used for cell washing and cell detachment during maintenance.

TP53 CRISPR/Cas9 knock-out (KO) cell lines

TP53-KO PANC-1 and TP53-KO MIA PaCa-2 cells were generated in our laboratory using the CRISPR/Cas-9 technology (CRISPR KN 2.0 Gene Knockout Kit, Origene Technologies, Rockville, MD, USA) as previously described [20]. Unless otherwise indicated, three independent TP53 KO clones were used in all experiments. The absence of TP53 expression in these KO cells was periodically verified by Western blot analysis.

Overexpression of mutant TP53 via transient plasmid transfection

MTT cell viability assay

Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)

PANC-1 and TP53 KO-PANC-1 were seeded in 6-wells plates (3.5 × 105 cells/well). The following day, cells were treated with 0.5 µM of RSL3 for 6 hours. Then, 1 × 106 cells were counted and collected for further processing. Cell pellets were fixed in a solution of 2.5% glutaraldehyde and 2% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer, pH 7.4, at 4 °C for 2 h. Cell pellets were then rinsed in PBS, postfixed in 1% OsO4 and 1,5% potassium ferrocyanide at 4 °C for 1 h, dehydrated with acetone, and embedded in Epon 812 resin. Seventy-thick ultrathin sections were collected on a Formvar-carbon-coated cooper grids, stained with lead citrate for 1 min and observed with a Philips Morgagni transmission electron microscope operating at 80 kV and equipped with a Megaview III camera for digital image acquisition. Micrographs (×18,000) of 25 randomly selected mitochondria from 20 different cells were taken for each sample. The mitochondrial sectional area and the lengths of the major and the minor mitochondrial axes were measured. The aspect ratio parameter (as an index of mitochondrial elongation) was calculated as the ratio of the two lengths. The measurements were performed by using ImageJ software (NIH Image, Bethesda, MD, USA).

RNA samples preparation

PANC-1 and TP53-KO PANC-1 cells were seeded in 6-well plates (3.5 × 105 cells/well). After overnight adhesion, cells were treated for 6 h with the ferroptosis inducers. Then, total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Venlo, Netherland, Cat. No. 74104) according to the manufactures’ instructions. The RNA was eluted in RNAse-free water and stored at – 80° C until quantification. The concentration and purity of the RNA were measured using a Nano Drop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) by evaluating the absorbance at 260 nm and the 260/280 ratio. Only high-quality RNA samples (260/280 ratio between 1.8 and 2.1) were used for downstream applications, including RNA-seq analysis and further RT-qPCR validation analyses.

RNA-sequencing analysis

Total RNA was extracted from 24 samples as described above, and RNA integrity was evaluated using the Fragment Analyzer (Agilent Technologies). RNA-seq library preparation was performed starting from 1,000 ng high-quality total RNA using the “TruSeq Stranded mRNA library prep kit” (Illumina, San Diego, USA). Briefly, the mRNA fraction was purified from total RNA by polyA capture, fragmented and subjected to cDNA synthesis. Barcoded DNA adapters were ligated to both ends of the double-stranded cDNA and subjected to PCR amplification. The library products were evaluated using Fragment Analyzer (Agilent Technologies), then sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq2000 sequencer using 75 bp single-end reads, generating 17 million reads per sample. After quality filtering according to DRAGEN RNA pipeline v3.10.12, reads were aligned to human reference genome GRCh38 (hg38) using an alt-aware reference bundle, and a raw, unnormalized gene-level read count matrix was generated and aggregated across all samples. For each treatment (DMSO/CTRL, Erastin, RSL3, Ferroptocide), differential gene expression analysis was performed by comparing TP53-KO PANC-1 cells with the corresponding PANC-1 control condition, using three biological replicates per group. Differential expression analysis was carried out in R using DESeq2 [59]. Genes with low expression were filtered by retaining those with at least 10 reads in 50% of the samples. Count data were normalized using the median-of-ratios method, dispersion estimates were obtained according to the DESeq2 pipeline, and p values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. For data exploration and visualization, normalized counts were transformed using Variance Stabilizing Transformation (VST). Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed on the 1,000 most variable genes.

Over-representation analysis (ORA)

Over-Representation Analysis (ORA) was performed using clusterProfiler (KEGG) [60]. Differentially expressed genes were selected using adjusted p value ≤ 0.05 and |log2 fold change| ≥ 1, and were separated into upregulated and downregulated gene sets. ORA was performed for KEGG pathways analysis, using Benjamini–Hochberg correction, with a significance threshold of adjusted p value ≤ 0.05.

TCGA-PAAD analysis

To evaluate the clinical translatability of the ferroptosis/redox-related phenotype, we analysed publicly available pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma data from The Cancer Genome Atlas pancreatic adenocarcinoma cohort (TCGA-PAAD). RNA-seq gene expression data, somatic mutation data, and clinical annotations were retrieved from the Genomic Data Commons using the TCGAbiolinks R package. Patients were classified according to TP53 mutational status using somatic mutation data. Patients carrying non-synonymous TP53 alterations, including missense, nonsense, frameshift, in-frame, splice-site, nonstop, or translation-start-site mutations, were classified as TP53-mutated. All remaining patients were classified as TP53 wild-type. Expression values for GPX4, SLC7A11, TXN, and TP53 were extracted from the RNA-seq dataset and analysed as log2(TPM + 1) values. To summarize the anti-ferroptotic/redox-related signal, two exploratory composite scores were generated. An anti-ferroptotic score was calculated as the average of the z-scored log2(TPM + 1) expression values of GPX4 and SLC7A11. An extended redox/ferroptosis score was calculated as the average of the z-scored log2(TPM + 1) expression values of GPX4, SLC7A11, and TXN. Associations between TP53 mutation status and marker expression or score values were evaluated using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. All analyses were performed with R software.

Gemcitabine treatment in PANC-1 cells

PANC-1 and TP53-KO PANC-1 cells were seeded in 24-wells plates (5 × 104 cells/well). After overnight adhesion, cells were treated with Gemcitabine at 0.25, 0.5, 0.75 and 1 µM. After 5 days of treatment, cells were collected and analysed with Annexin V/PI staining. Flow cytometry analysis was performed using a BD LSR-Fortessa (BD Bioscience) flow cytometer. The population of Annexin V-/PI- (double negative) cells was considered viable. The results are presented as mean ± SD from three independent replicates.

Gemcitabine treatment and ferroptosis in PDAC

Publicly available processed RNA-seq data from GSE249302 and GSE140077 were downloaded from GEO. GSE249302 was used to evaluate the transcriptional effect of gemcitabine treatment in PDAC cell lines, comparing gemcitabine-treated versus control BxPC3 and PANC-1 cells. GSE140077 was used to evaluate transcriptional features associated with acquired gemcitabine resistance, comparing gemcitabine-resistant derivatives with their corresponding parental PDAC cell lines, namely BXPC-3-GR versus BXPC-3 and CFPAC-1-GR versus CFPAC-1. Gene-level FPKM values were transformed as log2(FPKM + 1). Expression of GPX4, SLC7A11 and TXN was analysed separately within each cell line/model. To summarize ferroptosis/redox-related features, an exploratory composite redox/ferroptosis score was calculated at the sample level as the mean of z-scored GPX4, SLC7A11 and TXN expression. Z-score normalization was performed within each cell-line comparison. Differences between treated/resistant and control/parental cells were assessed using two-sided t-tests on log2-transformed expression values or score values. Nominal p-values were reported for these predefined marker-based analyses, together with Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted p-values as a more conservative reference.

Seahorse metabolic assays and DNA normalization

PANC-1, TP53-KO PANC-1 (2 × 104 cells/well), Hs766T (1.5 × 104 cells/well), HPDE (2.5 × 104 cells/well), and MIAPaca-2 cells (3 × 104 cells/well) were seeded in Seahorse XF 24-well cell culture microplates previously coated with poly-D Lysine (100 µg/mL; Thermo Fisher Scientific, A3890401), depending on the cell line. The following day, cells were treated with the ferroptosis inducers, and metabolic analyses were performed using the Seahorse XFe24 Analyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocols.

For the Mito Stress Test, PANC-1 and TP53-KO PANC-1, cells were treated with RSL3 for 4 h prior to the assay performed using the Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress Test kit (Agilent Technologies, 103015-100). For the ATP Rate Assay (PANC-1, TP53-KO PANC-1, Hs766T, HPDE, and MIA PaCa-2), cells were analysed with the Seahorse XF ATP Rate Assay kit (Agilent Technologies, 103582-100) after treatment. For the Glycolysis Stress Test (PANC-1 and TP53-KO PANC-1), cells were treated with RSL3 for 4 h prior to analysis with the Seahorse XF Glycolysis Stress Test kit (Agilent Technologies, 103020-100). For all assays, Seahorse XF RPMI media (Agilent Technologies, 103576-100) supplemented as appropriate was used: for Mito Stress and ATP Rate assays, 10 mM glucose (103577-100), 2 mM glutamine (103579-100), and 1 mM sodium pyruvate (103578-100); for the Glycolysis Stress Test, 2 mM glutamine only. At the end of the treatment, culture media was replaced with fresh XF RPMI, and plates were incubated for 1 h at 37 °C in a non-CO₂ incubator before running the assay. All cartridges were incubated overnight at 37 °C in a non-CO₂ incubator with calibrant solution (Agilent Technologies, 100840-100) before the day of the assay. For the Mito Stress Test, oligomycin (1 µM), FCCP (2 µM), and rotenone/antimycin A (0.5 µM) were loaded; for the ATP Rate Assay, oligomycin (1.5 µM) and rotenone/antimycin A (0.5 µM) were loaded; for the Glycolysis Stress Test, glucose (10 mM), oligomycin (1 µM), and 2-deoxyglucose (50 mM) were loaded. All conditions were performed in at least technical triplicate.

At the end of each assay, the 24-well plates were collected, the medium discarded, and the dried cell plates stored at − 80 °C overnight. Total DNA was quantified the following day using the CyQUANT™ Cell Proliferation Assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, C7026) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The CyQUANT™ working solution was prepared by diluting the dye 1:400 and the lysis buffer 1:20 in sterile, autoclaved H₂O. A total of 200 µL of this solution was added to each well and, after cell lysis, the contents were transferred to a 96-wells half-area Greiner plate (675096) for quantification. Fluorescence intensity was measured on a Tecan Infinite® M Nano Plus microplate reader (Tecan Group Ltd., Zürich, Switzerland) with excitation at 480 nm and emission at 520 nm. DNA quantity (µg) was calculated using the standard curve provided in the kit and uploaded to Wave software for Seahorse data normalization. Considering the intrinsic metabolic differences observed at baseline between the cell lines, data obtained after normalization were referred to the correspondent control for each condition. Results are shown as fold change to control (CTRL).

Synergism evaluation and Compusyn analysis

For these evaluations, 7940b and MT3 cells were seeded in 96-wells plate as previously described. The following day, cells were treated with IKE and APR-246 as indicated in the figure, using a fixed ratio of 1:5. After 24 h treatment, the percentage of viable cells were analysed by MTT assay. Then, the percentage of dead cells were calculated for each condition, and used to evaluate synergism. In detail, data were analysed with CompuSyn (1.0) software, and the Combination Index (CI) was used to calculate the degree of the synergism in the combination (where CI < 1 = synergism), while the Fa (Fraction affected) indicates the percentage of the cells affected and represents the response level of the combinatory treatment.

Mouse models and orthotopic tumor implantation

Seven-week-old female C57BL/6 N (H-2b) mice were obtained from Charles River Laboratories (Calco, Italy). Mice were orthotopically implanted with mouse-derived KPC pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) 7940b cells (carrying the mutant TP53 allele R172H, equivalent of the human TP53 R175H hotspot mutation). Briefly, mice were anesthetized, and a small incision was made in the left abdominal flank to expose the pancreas. A total of 5 × 104 cells, resuspended in cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), were injected into the pancreatic tail using a 25-gauge insulin syringe (BD Micro-Fine). Successful engraftment was confirmed by the appearance of a localized bleb without leakage. The peritoneum was closed with absorbable sutures (Vetsuture), and the skin was secured with wound clips. Following surgery, mice were monitored daily for recovery, signs of pain or distress, and proper wound healing.

Tumor growth and progression were monitored by high-resolution ultrasound imaging (Vevo 2100, FUJIFILM VisualSonics). Tumors were identified using established anatomical landmarks, including the spleen, kidneys, liver, portal vein, and inferior vena cava. Imaging was performed in a blinded fashion, and data analysis was independently supervised by two investigators (Y.H. and T.W.). Tumor volume was determined by measuring the maximal longitudinal (length) and transverse (width) diameters and calculated using a modified ellipsoid formula: volume = ½ × (length × width²).

Tumor-bearing mice with comparable mean tumor volumes were randomized into treatment groups (n = 14/group). The experimental groups included vehicle control (DMSO), IKE (40 mg/kg), APR-246 (40 mg/kg), and the combination of IKE and APR-246. All compounds were administered via intraperitoneal injection (i.p.) once every other day. Tumor volumes were reassessed by ultrasound following four treatments. After two additional treatments, mice were euthanized, and tumors were collected for downstream analyses. Tumor burden did not exceed the maximum size permitted by institutional ethical approval (OPBA) and European regulations.

Mice were maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions at the University of Verona animal facility, with ad libitum access to food and water. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with national and European regulations (protocols C46F4.26 and C46F4.30; Principal Investigator: Stefano Ugel) and approved by the University of Verona Ethical Committee. All procedures complied with FELASA guidelines and the Amsterdam Protocol for animal welfare. Animals were monitored daily and euthanized upon signs of excessive distress.

Isolation of tumor cells

Collected tumors from the in vivo experiment were then cut into small pieces with scissors under sterile conditions and incubated in freshly prepared dissociation buffer (collagenase 1 mg/mL, hyaluronidase 1 mg/mL, DNase I 0.025 mg/mL in RPMI) for 45 min at 37 °C. Tumor cell culture medium was added to terminate the dissociation and then pass through the 70 μm strainer to obtain single-cell suspensions. Cells from five different mice were kept in RPMI + FBS 10% and seeded in 24-wells plates at 3 × 104 cells/well. The following day, Seahorse ATP Rate Assay and Glycolysis Stress Test were performed as previously described.

Alanine transaminase (ALT) activity evaluation

Alanine Transaminase (ALT) activity was measured in sera from mice (n = 8) from the in vivo experiment as index of liver damage. In detail, on the day of the sacrifice, blood samples were collected and sera were extracted and stored at -80 °C. ALT activity was evaluated by using the Alanine Transaminase Activity assay kit (ab105134) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For this assay, sera were diluted 1:2. After 10 min incubation, outputs were measured at OD570 nm using a kinetic mode and considering an incubation time of 20 min. Results were obtained using a Tecan Infinite® M Nano Plus microplate reader (Tecan Group Ltd., Zürich, Switzerland) and concentrations were obtained using the standard curve provided by the kit.

Creatinine assay

Creatinine was measured in sera from mice (n = 8) from the in vivo experiment as index of renal damage. In detail, sera were collected as previously described, and centrifuged for 10 min at 4 °C (10,000 x g) using the 10 kDa spin columns (ab93349) and the filtrate was used for the following analysis. For this assay, sera were diluted 1:2. Then, creatinine levels were measured using the Creatinine Assay Kit (ab65340) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After 1 h incubation at 37 °C, outputs were read at OD570 nm using a Tecan Infinite® M Nano Plus microplate reader (Tecan Group Ltd., Zürich, Switzerland). Concentrations were quantified using the standard curve provided by the kit.

Statistical analysis and software

Results are presented as mean ± SD, except for the in vivo experiments, which are presented as mean + SEM. For experiments with n ≤ 15, individual data points are shown. Statistical significance between two groups was determined using a two-tailed unpaired t-test, while comparisons among three or more groups were assessed using 2 wayANOVA. All the data were plotted using GraphPad Prism 10.3.1, which was also used for statistical analyses. Statistical significance is described in the figure legends as: ∗ p < 0.05, ∗∗ p < 0.01, ∗∗∗ p < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗ p < 0.0001.

For western blot analysis, Image Lab software 6.1 was used, while flow cytometry data were analysed using the FlowJo software v10.8.1. For Seahorse data analysis, the Wave v2.6 software was used. For synergism evaluation, the CompuSyn (1.0) software was used. For RNA-seq data processing, R 4.5.2 software was used. In vivo scheme represented in Fig. 6G and graphical abstract were created using Biorender.com.
