To Manipulate or Not? Management of Pediatric Knee Arthrofibrosis Following Operative Fixation of Tibial Spine Fractures
Authors: Matthew J Folkman, Neeraj M Patel, Alexandra C Stevens, Aristides I Cruz, R Jay Lee, Indranil Kushare, Theodore J Ganley, Henry Ellis, Peter Fabricant, Daniel Green, Benjamin Johnson, Scott Mckay, Gregory Schmale, Yi-Meng Yen, R Justin Mistovich
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundKnee arthrofibrosis is a common complication after surgical fixation of tibial spine fractures. However, there is no standardized treatment modality for resultant arthrofibrosis, with some surgeons electing for nonoperative management, while others prefer manipulation under anesthesia with a possible arthroscopic lysis of adhesions, if indicated. To better understand indications and outcomes from these treatment modalities for arthrofibrosis, we examined patients treated by both approaches.MethodsWe performed a multicenter retrospective case series of patients with arthrofibrosis after tibial spine fracture surgery. Arthrofibrosis was defined as loss of knee extension ≥10.0° and/or knee flexion ≥25.0° compared to the contralateral, uninjured knee at three months following index surgery. Patients were organized into 2 cohorts: nonoperative and operative treatment. Data were collected for demographics, fracture classification, concomitant injuries, treatment timeline, and knee range of motion.ResultsThere were 16 patients in the operative group and 10 patients in the nonoperative group. At the time of diagnosis, the operative group had a mean 10° larger flexion deficit compared to the nonoperative group. Both treatment modalities resulted in similar ranges of motion at terminal evaluation. Final flexion was recorded as 130° in the operative group and 127° in the nonoperative group. Final extension deficits were 3° in both cohorts.ConclusionsOperative and nonoperative treatment modalities can be effective in management of knee arthrofibrosis after fixation of tibial spine fractures. Nonoperative treatment may be more suitable for milder range of motion deficits, but further research is necessary to guide clinical practice.Key Concepts(1)Cohorts were similar in demographics, fracture classification, and concomitant injuries.(2)Patients who received a second operation presented with a greater flexion deficit.(3)The mean time between initial surgery and reoperation was 85 days.(4)Operative and nonoperative treatment of arthrofibrosis resulted in similar outcomes.Level of EvidenceLevel IV, Case SeriesKeywords: Arthrofibrosis, Tibial spine fracture, Manipulation under anesthesia, Trauma

Introduction

Tibial spine fractures (TSF) are uncommon injuries that are most prevalent in young patients between 8 and 14 years old [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]]. This injury is functionally an avulsion fracture of the anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) [1,3]. Tibial spine fractures typically occur in the setting of sports related injuries, bicycle falls, and ski or snowboard injuries [1,[3], [4], [5]]. Biomechanically, the tensile force of the ACL is stronger than the developing proximal tibial epiphysis, resulting in an avulsion fracture when a large, acute force is applied [1,2,5,6].

The most common complication after surgical fixation of TSFs is arthrofibrosis, a fibrotic joint disorder that results in decreased range of motion (ROM) and pain [[6], [7], [8], [9], [10]]. Knee arthrofibrosis has an incidence of 3–18.5 ​% in patients who undergo surgical treatment of the tibial spine fractures [7,11,12]. Recently determined risk factors for developing knee arthrofibrosis following operative management of tibial spine fractures include age less than 10 years, traumatic injury, concomitant ACL tears, and casting postoperatively [6]. Additionally, delayed surgery and operative times longer than 2.5 ​h for TSFs are associated with an increased risk of knee arthrofibrosis [13].

Currently, no literature exists to guide optimal management of arthrofibrosis that develops after TSF surgical fixation [10]. Treatment options include manipulation under anesthesia (MUA) with or without lysis of adhesions (LOA) or nonoperative treatment with continued physical therapy and home exercises. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to describe outcomes of operative and nonoperative treatment of knee arthrofibrosis after fixation of TSFs in children and adolescents utilizing retrospective data from a large, multicenter study group.

Materials and methods

We collected a multicenter case series composed of patients with operative tibial spine fractures who subsequently developed postoperative arthrofibrosis across 9 different United States children's hospitals between January 1, 2000, and January 31, 2019. This study was institutional review board approved. We included patients 18 years of age and younger. Based on previous studies, knee arthrofibrosis was defined as loss of knee extension ≥10° and/or loss of knee flexion ≥25° compared to the contralateral, uninjured knee at three months following index surgery [14,15]. We excluded patients with polytraumatic lower extremity fractures, concomitant ACL reconstructions, age greater than 18 years, and those who were lost to follow-up prior to 6 months after the diagnosis of arthrofibrosis.

We collected patient demographics, fracture classification, concomitant injuries, treatment modality, surgery characteristics, return to operating room, ROM throughout treatment, and follow-up duration. Patients were categorized into 2 cohorts: those who received nonoperative knee arthrofibrosis treatment and those who returned to the operating room for MUA and possible LOA. We recorded knee range of motion for both cohorts 3 months after index surgery (the time at which arthrofibrosis was diagnosed) and 6 months after index surgery. For the surgical group, we also recorded ROM at the time of return to the operating room (ROR). We calculated descriptive statistics using SPSS for Macintosh (Version 27.0; IBM).

Results

This multicenter database included 663 total patients with tibial spine fractures among nine institutions. Of these, there were 34 patients provisionally eligible for the study that developed arthrofibrosis after surgery. Two patients were excluded for undergoing an ACL reconstruction surgery, 5 patients were excluded for incomplete follow-up, and 1 patient was excluded because they required 2 manipulations. This left a final cohort of 26 patients who met the inclusion criteria. There were 16 patients in the operative group and 10 in the nonoperative group. Within the operative group, three patients underwent MUA alone, while 13 had a LOA and MUA. 15 of 16 patients had their knee immobilized after the index surgery. Nine received a brace, 4 received a cast, and 2 received a knee immobilizer. There were 11 patients formally treated by physical therapy for a mean duration of 23 ​± ​6 weeks after surgery.

Concomitant injuries were present in both cohorts. The operative group had a concomitant injury incidence of 63% (10/16) while the nonoperative group had an incidence of 40% (4/10). Discussion

We evaluated pediatric patients with operatively treated tibial spine fractures who subsequently developed arthrofibrosis. Both operative and nonoperative modalities ultimately resulted in a similar, acceptable final ROM, though the operative group had larger baseline flexion deficits and a higher frequency of concomitant injury. To our knowledge, this is the first multicenter study to compare outcomes after nonoperative and operative treatment of arthrofibrosis in tibial spine fractures.

Both patient cohorts were seen at similar 3-month and 6-month time points after index surgery. Patients in the operative group returned for MUA and/or LOA a mean of 126 ​± ​52 days postoperatively. The decision to proceed with MUA occurred following the ROM findings during the 3-month postoperative visit. This decision-making timeframe of 12 weeks was similar to other reported reoperation timelines of 8–12 weeks in the reported literature [16].

At the time of diagnosis of arthrofibrosis, the operative cohort had 10° less flexion than the nonoperative group (99° vs 109°) and similar extension deficits (8° vs 7°). The treatment modality selection is potentially associated with the extent of limited ROM at the time of arthrofibrosis diagnosis and surgeon preference, though other factors may contribute as well. Nonetheless, patients in both cohorts achieved satisfactory flexion and extension 6 months after index surgery (3 months after the diagnosis of arthrofibrosis), irrespective of treatment modality. While this finding should be tempered due to the nonrandomized, retrospective, and smaller sample size of the data, this is the first study to describe that equipoise exists between the 2 treatment modalities for arthrofibrosis, with both cohorts attaining satisfactory final ROM. The authors advocate for further study to determine which patient and injury risk factors and characteristics would benefit from operative treatment, and which are amenable to avoiding subsequent surgery while still reaching excellent final ROM.

The literature remains lacking on evidence-based guidance on how to best treat arthrofibrosis, especially in the postoperative setting after fixation of a tibial spine fracture. While a previous study reported that MUA and LOA for knee arthrofibrosis restored ROM to similar native anatomic values, we are not aware of any prior studies investigating nonoperative management [17].

While arthrofibrosis is the most common postoperative complication in TSFs, it is also often associated with ACL reconstruction, knee fractures, and various other pediatric knee surgeries [5,10,[18], [19], [20]]. Typical prevention measures often involve mobilization of the joint early and often. In some instances, continuous passive motion (CPM) has been reported as an effective method to mitigate the risk of arthrofibrosis postoperatively and increase ROM [21,22]. Published literature regarding CPM following TSF surgery is limited, and there remains equipoise regarding its clinical utility [23]. Findings from other studies have shown improved ROM outcomes when patients begin early active ROM after index surgery [14,16,24]. Conversely, the odds of developing knee arthrofibrosis are 12 times higher in patients who undergo prolonged immobilization [14,16]. As such, early motion and physical therapy after index surgery may be critical for preventing knee arthrofibrosis and mitigating the risk of either reoperation or delays in a full return to prior activities. Early data on flexion and extension deficits may have later implications with respect to ROR. For example, one study found that ROM deficits after ACL reconstruction 6-weeks postoperatively predicted a ROR 3 months postoperatively [20]. Likewise, future research should evaluate if a similar predictive ROM cutoff for TSF arthrofibrosis exists.

Our study found that concomitant injuries were relatively common in both groups. The concomitant injury rates were similar to other reported incident ranges of 32%–68% in the literature [5,16,[25], [26], [27]]. Interestingly, there is a possibility that concomitant injuries may have affected the early rehabilitation protocol and subsequently limited ROM. As such, there may be a theoretical risk of developing arthrofibrosis due to postoperative ROM restrictions as a function of concomitant injuries. Further research should explore the risks of concomitant injuries sustained with TSFs and resultant knee arthrofibrosis.

Overall, we found the majority (62%) of TSF arthrofibrosis patients required MUA with possible LOA. This contrasts with other previously reported reoperation rates of 33% for arthrofibrosis patients [6]. We also found both groups had similarly distributed patient demographics and fracture classifications with a slight increase in male patients compared to the reported literature [10]. These differences may be attributable to our exclusion criteria of patients with limited follow-up data and/or our limited sample size of this population.

While this study provides unique insight into the treatment of arthrofibrosis after TSF, it is not without limitations. This is a retrospective study with a limited sample size, reducing our ability to make statistical comparisons. The range of motion was estimated by independent physicians, typically without a goniometer, which limited the fidelity of data collection. Surgical and postoperative protocols varied by nine institutions and 16 different surgeons, which may have created heterogeneity in treatment and outcomes. Additionally, we were unable to account for factors such as pain, access to reliable and structured physical therapy, compliance with home ROM exercises, cost of care, and surgeon biases, all of which can influence clinical decision-making. This study also did not evaluate the use of CPM or other different devices, which may limit its generalizability to various treatment modalities. These limitations may create difficulty providing generalizability of our conclusions to the general population. While the data in this study were drawn from one of the largest cohorts of tibial spine fractures, our arthrofibrosis sample size was nonetheless limited. Despite this small sample size, it is relatively consistent with the sample sizes of similar published papers evaluating pediatric knee arthrofibrosis [[6], [7], [8],17,[28], [29], [30]]. Ultimately, multicenter prospective data is needed to holistically evaluate the long-term outcomes of TSF arthrofibrosis treatment. This study will be potentially important in the development of these future large studies, as it is the first to, in general, demonstrate a level of equipoise between operative and nonoperative treatment modalities.

Despite these limitations, this study presents new information on the treatment of arthrofibrosis after fixation of TSFs. To our knowledge, this is the first study to report the outcomes of both surgical and nonsurgical management of arthrofibrosis in this patient population. These preliminary findings can provide context for surgeon decision-making when considering manipulation based on ROM at early time intervals. Large prospective randomized controlled multicenter studies are needed to best compare treatment modalities for arthrofibrosis after TSF fixation and to provide robust evidence-based clinical guidance.
