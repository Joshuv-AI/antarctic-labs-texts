Deep learning-based neonatal outcome prediction: an LSTM autoencoder framework for pattern analysis and risk assessment
Authors: Safiya Parvin A, Saleena B
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe phenomenon of neonatal mortality is a significant issue in health care, which necessitates the use of advanced analytical tools for early risk prediction. This work utilized the Medical Information Mart (MIMIC) Pediatric Intensive Care (PIC) database to develop a framework for identifying key biomarkers associated with mortality risk. The combination of many clinical data sources comprising more than two thousand Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU) Neonatal patient admissions (both alive and dead) were extracted. Biomarkers with an abnormal value were taken into consideration for further analysis. The patterns were analyzed using Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) autoencoders that converted the biomarker data of both deceased and live neonates into meaningful representations. Similar biomarker patterns were clustered to gain insights into the mortality risk factors. The suggested framework is found to be superior to the existing neonatal scoring systems with a sensitivity of 88.1%, specificity of 89.5%, and Area Under Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUCROC) of 0.92. These results indicate the possibility of employing the biomarker patterns as predictors of neonatal mortality and the basis of a decision-support model.Keywords: LSTM autoencoders, Clustering, Mortality patterns, Neonatal sepsis, Neonatal sequential organ failure assessment (nSOFA), Pediatric RISk of mortality (PRISM)

Introduction

Innovations in clinical care have significantly enhanced neonatal survival rates. According to the UNICEF statistical analysis, the neonatal mortality rate per 1000 live births decreased from 37 in 1990 to 17 in 2023. Even though the death rate has shown a decline over the past couple of years, it still remains one of the biggest problems in the under-five age group. In Fig. 1, 79% of neonatal mortality can be attributed to three separate and distinct causes: preterm birth complications, birth asphyxia, and infections. Statistics of UNICEF’s neonatal morality rate.

Preterm birth complications include deaths resulting from prematurity-related conditions such as respiratory distress syndrome (RDS), intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH), necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC), and other complications of immature organ systems. This category excludes infection-related deaths. Infections specifically include neonatal sepsis, pneumonia, meningitis, and other infection-related mortality. Birth asphyxia includes deaths due to perinatal asphyxia and hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy. This classification follows the UNICEF framework for categorizing causes of neonatal deaths.

Figure 1 presents the UNICEF neonatal mortality statistics, highlighting the critical role of early sepsis detection in guiding appropriate disease management strategies. Timely diagnosis and intervention in neonatal sepsis cases can prevent approximately 30% of newborn deaths through effective treatment practices. In low weight and preterm infants, mortality related risks are often associated with neonatal sepsis. Mortality is a consequence of multiple risk factors such as late detection of the disease, resistance to antibiotics, insufficient sepsis screening, and limited access to sophisticated care. This explains the impact of predictive methods required to decrease the deaths associated with sepsis in newborns.

Role of biomarkers in mortality prediction

Neonatal sepsis presents diagnostic challenges due to variable clinical features and biomarker characteristics. Studies by Gosai et al.1 and Stocker et al.2 emphasize the critical role of biomarkers in predicting mortality. Based on these research works, Zhang et al.3 came up with a machine learning [ML] model to track mortality in sepsis patients. The group applied seven ML algorithms to determine the most influential features that led to death. The team also checked the validity of the model by means of SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP). In the same manner, Yoon et al.4 developed an ML model based on four biomarkers that was able to outperform conventional scoring systems. The ML model was quicker and provided more precise diagnosis.

While machine learning shows promise improving diagnostic accuracy and enabling early detection, several barriers hinder effective neonatal care, particularly in resource-constrained settings. This has been worsened by limited access to maternal and neonatal healthcare in developing countries. The lack of proper antenatal care, practices of improper births, and late management cases pose a higher risk of neonatal infection and associated deaths5. Nevertheless, recent evidence points at reducing risk by means of interventions that are specific. For instance, after normalization of Heart Rate Characteristics (HRC) in infants with very-low-birthweight, the all-cause mortality decreased by 22%, while the all-cause mortality post-infection reduced by 40%. If solutions to these mortality-associated components in the diagnosis and management of sepsis are implemented, the ML techniques can assist in the early observation and origination of decisions.

Due to the rapid pace of technological advancement and the subsequent creation of clinical calculators, including the neonatal early onset sepsis calculator, it is now possible to predict the risk of sepsis in an infant based on the maternal history and the perinatal risk factors. This quantitative approach enables proper utilization of antibiotics that lowers their misuse while also targeting high-risk infants6.

While existing ML models have demonstrated improved prediction accuracy, they often rely on static features and do not fully capture the temporal dynamics of biomarker patterns that characterize disease progression in neonates. This gap motivates the development of deep learning approaches capable of modeling time-series biomarker data.

Gaps in biomarker-guided sequential models

Most of the clinical models follow the statistical approaches where as the temporal models analyses the normal and abnormal biomarker levels to identify the mortality progression.

Although biomarkers have been recognized as critical predictors, current models do not fully leverage biomarker-guided sequential learning architectures such as LSTM autoencoders that can both compress complex temporal biomarker data and reconstruct patterns to identify mortality risks through reconstruction error analysis. Pattern interpretation of live and deceased newborns were lacking in the literature. Differentiating normal and anomaly biomarkers identification are missing in the existing literature.

Novelty of the proposed approach

The proposed biomarker-guided LSTM autoencoder framework introduces several novel contributions that distinguish it from the existing ML/DL approaches for neonatal mortality prediction. Unlike existing supervised models that require labeled outcomes for training3,4, the proposed approach employs unsupervised learning through LSTM autoencoders to discover inherent biomarker patterns without predefined mortality labels. This enables the model to learn the natural temporal evolution of biomarkers in neonates. It also identifies subtle pattern deviations that may not be apparent in supervised classification. Clusters are generated based purely on biomarker similarity rather than outcome labels.

While traditional models use direct classification predicting mortality as a binary outcome, this framework employs a novel reconstruction-based anomaly detection mechanism with the following advantages. The model learns to reconstruct “normal” biomarker sequences from surviving neonates. High reconstruction errors indicate deviations from normal patterns, serving as mortality risk indicators. This approach is more sensitive to early warning signs and gradual deteriorations that might be missed by threshold-based classifiers.

Research objectives and contribution

To address the identified gaps, this study aims to develop a biomarker-guided deep learning framework using Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) autoencoders that can predict neonatal mortality risk based on the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care—Pediatric Intensive Care (MIMIC-PIC) database. Utilizing a dataset of over 2,000 NICU admissions (both survivors and deceased neonates), critical biomarkers including lactate, bilirubin, and creatinine were extracted and analyzed. The main contributions of this research are twofold: (1) Pattern extraction and risk clustering—LSTM autoencoders were employed to transform biomarker data into lower-dimensional representations, identifying distinct mortality-associated patterns in deceased neonates and clustering them into risk-specific groups for comparison with surviving neonates and (2) Performance validation—The proposed model was evaluated against established neonatal risk scoring systems (nSOFA and PRISM) to demonstrate enhanced mortality prediction accuracy.

Related work

Extensive research on the prediction of mortality in patients with neonatal sepsis has led to a variety of models designed using both conventional and modern methods. This section elaborates the modelling methods for neonatal mortality prediction from the existing literature.

ML related models

The most frequent type of model used to predict the risk of neonatal mortality in the population is logistic regression (LR) owing to its ease of use and interpretability9. Out of the five predictive models evaluated by Patterson et al.10, the LR model emerged as the best model with the ability to identify clinical factors related to low birth weight deaths. Another ML model namely, the K-Nearest Neighbor (KNN) algorithm was employed to determine the risk factors of low birth weight in infants within the two maternal groups11.

DL related models

Recent developments in the field of DL have improved further with the incorporation of both static and dynamic clinical data into DL-based mortality prediction models. In order to represent the multifaceted time-dependent interaction between individual series of data, a gated recurrent unit-based recurrent neural network (RNN) model was designed8. Mortality prediction in preterm infants in the NICU was performed using a DL model7. Analysis of records from 285 neonates proved that the incorporation of real-time physiological signals significantly improved the predictive performance of the model that achieved an Area Under the Curve (AUC) of 0.897 and outperformed traditional ML techniques.

Scoring related models

The traditional scoring systems are still important in neonatal mortality prediction to formulate clinically appropriate interventions. Ganguly et al.12 determined the predictive ability of the Score towards Neonatal Acute Physiology II (SNAP-II) and the extended variant SNAPPE-II of prognosis. The scores were positively and significantly greater in non-survivors than in survivors. Thus, increased SNAP-II and SNAPPE-II scores are indicative of high chances of death.

Mortality risk-related models were mentioned by Dorling et al.13 and Kaur et al.14. Tesfie et al.15 presented a model of mortality prediction using a nomogram. Stoll et al.16 and team created a model involving mortality. Using a broad clinical setting, W. W. Zhang et al.17 designed and tested a large-scale mortality prediction model using the Sino-Northern Neonatal Network (46 NICUs across China).

Hybrid models (combining ML, DL, and clinical scores)

To enhance the accuracy of mortality risk assessments, hybrid models combining clinical scoring systems with ML and DL techniques have been developed. The power of ML models over scoring models was tested by Li et al.18. Subsequently, another team19 combined Systemic Inflammatory Response Syndrome (SIRS) and quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) to arrive at a new qSIRS score that identified biomarkers earlier than qSOFA. Thus, the integration of a scoring system with an alert-based system improves the mortality risk assessment ability20.

Initially, the MIMIC PIC Database was used to generate patterns for both deceased and live infants. The next step involved comparing the patterns of live infants to those of deceased infants. If a live infant’s pattern did not resemble the pattern of any of the deceased infants, the system moved on to check the next live pattern. Finally, the decision was sent to a panel of doctors for further evaluation and medical intervention.

Dataset description and initial analysis

MIMIC PIC database is an extension of the well-known MIMIC database, which focuses on pediatric (age 0–18 years) patients who were hospitalized in the intensive care unit of the Children Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine. The dataset was developed with the aim to enhance the quality of intensive care given to children by providing an abundant source of data to be used in research. The dataset is arranged in tables as spreadsheets with defined information stored in columns (patient identifiers) and rows (the patient identifiers with a particular value). This data set can be found on the web at the PIC data base at physionet.org/content/picdb/1.1.0/. In this research, some of the most critical tables of the MIMIC PIC database were considered, which contained all the necessary data including patient demographics, health history, and clinical events.

It is essential to consider NICU patients as these patients in the neonatal category are highly exposed to life-threatening illnesses, including but not limited to infection, respiratory distress, viral sepsis, and multi-organ failure. This research work explored and examined clinical and laboratory data of neonates who perished in the first year to determine the biomarkers and trends pertaining to death. This research independently identified patterns that are linked to poor outcomes by conducting a deeper study of the laboratory testing and other clinical measurements of the patients. The results can be utilized to evaluate and predict the risks in surviving neonates, thus enabling healthcare professionals to offer timely interventions and make more accurate decisions regarding their treatment.

MIMIC PIC has various departments which include cardiac surgery, general Intensive Care Unit (ICU), infectious disease department, Pediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU), Surgical Intensive Care Unit (SICU), and Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU). Pattern generation using autoencoders

LSTM autoencoder model works on the concept of unsupervised representation learning. In this model, the network also learns to reduce the high-dimensional sequences of temporal biomarkers into a lower-dimensional latent state and then recreate the original input. This encoder-decoder model is especially intelligent in detecting neonatal sepsis because it has both the temporal relationship and complex non-linear relationship between the consecutive laboratory test results.

The encoder has 2 stacked LSTM layers that have 64 and 32 units. The input sequence is initially fed through the first LSTM layer that takes 10 time-steps, with each time-step consisting of a number of biomarker features (Admission_ID, Item_ID, Value, Unit, Flag). This level is trained to recognize the short-time variations and instant changes in the values of the biomarkers. The result of the first LSTM layer is inputted into the second LSTM layer with 32 units that further reduces the data with the help of higher order abstraction and longterm relationships. The last latent of the encoder is the compressed latent representation, which consists of necessary patterns of the input sequence that are stored in a small form.

The Repeat Vector layer copies the condensed representation of the latents into all timestamps which means that the decoder gets repeated context to reconstruct. Such a bottleneck design compels this model to adapt only those salient features required to achieve an accurate reconstruction and essentially filters the noise as well as learns the underlying patterns that define normal physiological states.

The decoder consisting of two LSTMS layers with 32 and 64 units resembles the encoder model. These layers gradually increase the latent representation all the way to the original sequence dimensions. Time Distributed Dense layer takes the identical fully connected operation on each time step separately and produces the reconstructed output sequence with the same dimensions as the input.

During training, the LSTM autoencoder learns to model normal biomarker patterns predominantly from surviving neonates. The network minimizes the Mean Squared Error (MSE) between the original input sequence and its reconstruction which serves as the loss function:

where X represents the original normalized sequence, X' denotes the reconstructed sequence, and n is the number of time points in the sequence. Thus, the autoencoder learns to capture the characteristic patterns present in normal biomarker sequences.

The Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.0001 ensures stable convergence, while the gradient clipping prevents exploding gradients that are common in recurrent architectures. Through ten epochs of training with mini-batch processing, the autoencoder learns the characteristic temporal patterns of normal laboratory test progressions, including expected values for critical parameters such as white blood cell counts, C-reactive protein levels, and platelet counts. It also maintains the temporal patterns of biomarkers over time. Figure 5 shows the anomalies detected for a sample input sequence using the LSTM autoencoder.

Cluster formation and risk assessment

As a first step in assessing patient risk using anomaly detection, the trained LSTM autoencoder generated reconstructed outputs for the input dataset. The reconstruction error is then calculated as the mean absolute difference between the original input and reconstructed output across all time steps and features. These reconstruction errors serve as anomaly scores, indicating how well the model reconstructs each patient’s data, with higher errors suggesting deviations from normal patterns. Results and discussion

This section discusses the evaluation of a predictive model for neonatal sepsis prediction through biomarker-based profiling and trend analysis. The findings demonstrate that lactate, bilirubin, and creatinine are clinically meaningful indicators of sepsis severity and mortality risk in neonates. The following discussion interprets the model’s outputs in a structured manner, progressing from individual case analysis to cluster-level patterns, risk stratification, and longitudinal biomarker trends.

Cluster based risk stratification

To extend beyond individual cases, the predictive model stratified the full cohort of 1,000 neonates into three risk clusters based on biomarker concentrations such as low-risk, medium-risk, and high-risk. The neonates with low-risk included around 200 patients with average lactate level of 5.3 mmol/L, bilirubin 0.4 mg/L, and creatinine 1.2 ng/mL indicating signs of preserved organs with a corresponding low mortality of 3%. The medium risk group (600 neonates) had intermediate biomarker levels (e.g. lactic acid 9.8 mmol/L, bilirubin 1.2 mg/L, and creatinine 2.5 ng/mL) and thus higher mortality rate (15%). On the other hand, high-risk group (200 neonates) had highly increased values of organ dysfunction profile with mean values of 20.1 mmol/L, 5.8 mg/L, and 4.7 ng/mL for lactate, bilirubin, and creatinine levels, respectively, and a mortality rate of 72% , which explains the substantial contribution of critical organ dysfunction to mortality. These observations made from Table 6 support the possible significance of these biomarkers in risk stratification and early interventions of critically ill neonates.

Comparative analysis with existing neonatal scoring and ML models

The LSTM autoencoder was compared with the traditional clinical scoring systems and two specific ML models (LR and KNN). The neonatal Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (nSOFA) and Pediatric RISk of Mortality (PRISM) were assessed in terms of performance measures such as sensitivity, specificity, accuracy and AUC-ROC.

From Table 9, it is evident that the LSTM autoencoder showed better results, with a sensitivity of 88.1%, specificity of 89.5%, accuracy of 89.9% and AUC-ROC of 0.92, which indicates the proposed models’ success in identifying cases while simultaneously reducing false positives.

The conventional ML models like LR showed varying degrees of effectiveness. LR achieved competitive sensitivity (85.7%) and a reasonable AUC-ROC (0.86), but its lower specificity (82.4%) and accuracy (80.2%) suggest it may not fully capture the complex temporal dynamics of neonatal deterioration. KNN performed least favourably across all metrics (78.3% sensitivity, 80.4% specificity, 81.5% accuracy, 0.84 AUC-ROC,), which is expected given its instance-based approach lacks mechanisms for modeling temporal relationships in time-series data.

Conclusion

The main aim of this research study was to enhance the survival rate of the neonates. In this study, the MIMIC PIC database was analyzed and details of the NICU neonates were extracted. The status of individual neonates was determined based on their biomarker readings. Biomarker-based clusters were formed using LSTM autoencoders, which effectively classified mortality risk into low, medium, and high-risk categories. The risk status of a live patient was determined by comparing it with a deceased cluster. A trend analysis based on a specific biomarker, such as creatinine, was carried out by comparing the creatinine levels of a live patient with those of a deceased patient to precisely predict the category of mortality risk. A comparative analysis of the LSTM autoencoder with traditional scoring systems (nSOFA and PRISM) highlights the ability of the LSTM autoencoder to predict patient risk with improved accuracy. The improved sensitivity (88.1%), specificity (89.5%), and AUC-ROC (0.92) scores of the LSTM autoencoder demonstrated effective capture and utilization of temporal dependencies in the neonate’s records for outcome prediction. Thus, the proposed framework improves the identification of risk patterns in live neonates, which in turn enhances their survival rates.
