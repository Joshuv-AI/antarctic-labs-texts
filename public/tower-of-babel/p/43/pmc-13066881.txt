Left ventricular parameters and mass indexing methods in cardiac magnetic resonance imaging in prediction of major adverse cardiovascular events and mortality
Authors: Patrycja S Matusik, Jarosław D Jarczewski, Mateusz Nawrocki, Tomasz Górecki, Aleksandra Jarczewska, Janina Schwarz, Katarzyna Mikrut, Agnieszka Pac, Tadeusz J Popiela, Paweł T Matusik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIncreased left ventricular mass (LVM) is a predictor of adverse outcomes. We investigated which cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging parameters and LVM indexing methods are associated with major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) and mortality.MethodsIn this exploratory analysis, we included patients with known cardiovascular diseases who underwent CMR. Left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) was defined as LVM >148 g for men or >96 g for women. Moreover, various LVM indexing methods were studied including indexing to body surface area (BSA) with cut-off values determined by the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA). The data on the MACE and mortality occurrence in the 6-year follow-up were collected.ResultsThe study group included 84 patients with a median age of 45.0 (32.0–60.0) years (26% were female). The majority of patients had established cardiovascular disease (77%), and 51% had preserved left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF). MACE occurred in 14 (16.7%) patients, while 5 patients (6.0%) died. Heart failure, increased LVM/BSA (MESA) and low left ventricular stroke volume (LVSV) were associated with MACE [79% vs. 47%, P=0.03; 50% vs. 21%, P=0.04 and 56.9 (49.1–74.7) vs. 79.6 (62.0–96.1) mL, P=0.004]. In multivariable regression analysis low LVSV independently predicted MACE [hazard ratio (HR): 0.97; 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.94–1.00, P=0.02], while increased LVM/BSA (MESA) was an independent predictor of all-cause mortality (HR: 11.03; 95% CI: 1.22–99.73, P=0.03). However, after correction for multiple testing across eight LVH definitions in univariable analyses, none was statistically significant.ConclusionsSelected CMR-derived parameters, particularly LVSV and LVM indexed using MESA-derived cut-off values may be associated with adverse clinical outcomes. Further research on predictive meaning of CMR-derived parameters, utilizing larger, adequately powered studies is needed.Keywords: Cardiovascular risk, left ventricular mass (LVM), indexation methods, stroke volume, major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs)

Introduction

Increased left ventricular mass (LVM) is a marker of adverse cardiovascular events (1-3). The Framingham Heart Study showed that the relative risk of cardiovascular mortality increased by 1.73 in men and 2.12 in women for each 50 g increase of LVM (1). On the other hand, it has been shown that reduction of LVM as a result of medical therapy is related to improvement in cardiovascular outcomes (4,5). Despite that, the importance of left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) in clinical practice is not widely appreciated. For example, in patients with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM), risk stratification commonly relies on maximal left ventricular (LV) wall thickness ≥30 mm assessed by echocardiography (6). However, some studies suggest that LVM may provide incremental prognostic information in this setting (7). It has been demonstrated that indexed LVM determined by cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) correlates poorly with maximal LV wall thickness yet demonstrates greater sensitivity in predicting HCM-related mortality (7).

On the other hand, there are some disadvantages linked to the LVM evaluation. Assignation and inclusion or exclusion of the basal short axis slice or papillary muscles impacts the LVM measurements (8). Current guidelines for CMR studies assessment indicate that either approach with respect to papillary muscle inclusion (lead to increase in values of LVM) or exclusion is acceptable (9). However, the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA) study has demonstrated better reproducibility of the calculation of LVM when papillary muscles were excluded (10). LVM shows substantial inter-individual variability and is influenced by multiple biological and anthropometric factors, including body size and composition, obesity, blood pressure, age, sex, ethnicity, physical training, and genetic background (11-14). These determinants complicate the distinction between physiological adaptation and pathological hypertrophy and limit the interpretability of non-indexed LVM values. Consequently, appropriate indexation of LVM is required to account for these confounding influences and to enable meaningful clinical interpretation.

Thus far, measurement and indexation of LVM during CMR studies has not been clearly established and depending on the algorithms used for LVM measurement and indexation, there are various cut-off values for LVH diagnosis each with different levels of accuracy (11,12,15,16). Only a small amount of data on the role of LVM indexing method in long-term cardiovascular risk prediction is available (17). In this study we investigated which CMR parameters and most importantly which of the LVM indexing methods are associated with major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) and all-cause mortality in patients with prevalent cardiovascular diseases. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2025-1973/rc).

Methods

Studied group

We involved participants who were referred for CMR imaging at the Department of Diagnostic Imaging at the University Hospital in Krakow due to a variety of cardiovascular diseases between 2011 and 2017 (N=175). Referrals were made for a variety of cardiovascular diseases, including heart failure (HF). The data were obtained by performing a structured medical records review or telephone contact with the patients. Patients were eligible for inclusion if CMR imaging quality allowed reliable assessment of LVM and its indexed measures and if follow-up data were available, enabling evaluation of predefined clinical outcomes. Data on the MACE occurrence in the 6-year follow-up were obtained and analyzed. Only patients with known 6-year follow-up or with an event that occurred during these 6 years were included in the analysis (n=84). The MACE was defined as coronary artery disease (CAD) requiring revascularization, acute myocardial infarction, HF requiring hospitalization, stroke, or death (cardiovascular mortality or all-cause death). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Bioethics Committee at the Regional Medical Council in Krakow (No. 39/KBL/OIL/2018). The requirement for written informed consent was waived due to the design of the study.

CMR

CMR data were gathered using a 1.5 Tesla GE Signa HDxt scanner (General Electric, Milwaukee, WI, USA). The fast imaging employing steady-state acquisition (FIESTA) cine sequences; other sequences where used when appropriate (18-20). Left ventricular parameters were evaluated using QMass® MR analysis software, version 7.6 (Medis Medical Imaging Systems bv, Leiden, the Netherlands). LVM was measured excluding the papillary muscles from the endocardial contouring to reduce observer variability. This approach aligns with established recommendations for ensuring reproducibility and standardization, as reference ranges employing the same methodology should be utilized (2,9,15,16,21,22). We compared multiple LVM indexation approaches to quantify the extent to which prognostic associations depend on the choice of body-size normalization and published LVH thresholds. Eight different criteria to diagnose LVH by CMR (CMR-LVH) were used (Table 1). Specifically, we included non-indexed LVM and body surface area (BSA)-indexed LVM, which are routinely reported in CMR practice (16,23); height-based allometric indices (LVM/height1.7 and LVM/height2.7) to mitigate potential underestimation of LVH in overweight/obese individuals; and percentage of predicted LVM (%pLVM) with cut-off values proposed in the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA) which incorporates additional anthropometric determinants (2,15,21,22). For the primary comparison, each indexation method was operationalized as a binary LVH definition based on published cut-off values; continuous indices were evaluated separately as sensitivity analyses. Preserved LVEF was defined as LVEF ≥50%.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were compared using Student t-test or U Mann-Whitney test and are depicted as mean ± standard deviation or median [interquartile range (IQR)]. Categorical variables were assessed using the Pearson χ2 test or Fisher exact test and are given as count and percentage. Normality was tested with the Shapiro-Wilk test. Associations between CMR parameters and outcomes were evaluated using univariable Cox regression analysis. Given the limited number of events, multivariable Cox models were restricted to parsimonious, pre-specified covariate sets to reduce overfitting. Kaplan-Meier curves with log-rank tests were used for descriptive time-to-event comparisons. Logistic regression was used for selected sensitivity and interaction analyses to avoid instability of time-to-event models. For the primary comparison across eight correlated LVH definitions, each definition was evaluated in a separate univariable Cox model and P values were adjusted using Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate correction; Bonferroni correction was used as a conservative sensitivity analysis. Analyses were performed in IBM SPSS Statistics v26, Statistica v13.3, and Stata (StataNow/BE 19.5, StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

Study population

The study group included 84 patients (26% female) with a median age of 45.0 (32.0–60.0) years. In 65 (77.0%) patients different cardiovascular diseases, such as HF, HA or CAD were noted. Within this group, HF was observed in 44 (52.4%) patients, HA in 35 (41.7%), dyslipidemia in 29 (34.5%), CAD in 26 (31.0%), while the history of smoking was noted in 19 (22.6%) patients. These conditions were not mutually exclusive and frequently coexisted. Median left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was 50.7% (29.6–59.5%), median left ventricular end-diastolic volume (LVEDV) was 172.9 (137.1–226.7) mL, and median left ventricular end-systolic volume (LVESV) was 85.0 (59.0–152.0) mL. Preserved LVEF was observed in 43 (51.2%) participants, while LVEF between 41 and 49% was noted in 9 (10.7%) patients. No missing data were present for variables used in the primary analyses.

Clinical outcomes and event rates during follow-up

During 6-year follow-up MACE occurred in 14 (16.7%) patients. Four patients (4.8%) required revascularization due to CAD, 1 patient (1.2%) had myocardial infarction, and 11 patients (13.1%) needed hospitalization related to exacerbation of HF. No one has had a stroke or TIA during this time. Three patients (3.6%) died due to cardiovascular causes, and 2 (2.4%) due to other causes. Impact of LVM indexation on LVH classification

There was a difference in diagnosis of LVH depending on the LVM indexing method used. LVH was observed in 56 (66.7%) patients when used non-indexed LVM, in 17 (20.2%) patients when used %pLVM, in 56 (66.7%) when used LVM/BSA (Petersen et al.), in 22 (26.2%) with used LVM/BSA (MESA), in 20 (23.8%) with used LVM/height1.7, in 20 (23.8%) when used LVM/height2.7, in 43 (51.2%) when used LVM/BSA (Luu et al.), and in 34 (40.5%) when used LVM/BSA by age (Luu et al.). The prevalence of LVH varied considerably depending on the applied definition, underscoring the influence of the chosen indexing method. Descriptive analyses by comorbidities

Consistent with disease-specific pathophysiology, LVM differed significantly across major cardiovascular disease categories. Patients with HF exhibited significantly higher LVM compared with those without HF [167.5 (134.7–228.4) vs. 133.6 (112.3–157.8) g, P<0.001]. Similarly, LVM was higher in patients with HA [161.0 (133.4–234.2) vs. 135.0 (112.4–177.2) g, P=0.012], in diabetics [173.6 (156.1–247.2) vs. 143.6 (117.4–178.0) g, P=0.02] and in those with CAD [170.8 (149.5–227.9) vs. 136.0 (115.0–177.2) g, P=0.002], when compared with the remainder patients. In contrast, no significant difference in LVM was observed between patients with and without dyslipidemia (P=0.18). The incidence of MACE during follow-up was higher among patients with HF (25.0% vs. 7.5%; Fisher exact P=0.041). No significant differences in MACE incidence were observed in groups of patients with HA, dyslipidemia, diabetes, or CAD.

Patients with and without preserved LVEF

To assess whether the association between LVM and clinical outcomes differed across LVEF categories, an interaction analysis between continuous LVM and LVEF category (≥50% vs. <50%) was performed. No significant interaction between LVM and LVEF category was observed (P=0.44). Continuous LVM was not independently associated with MACE in patients with and without preserved LVEF after multivariable adjustment [odds ratio (OR): 1.00, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.98–1.01; P=0.65].

Univariable Cox regression analysis

In univariable Cox regression, HF was associated with a trend towards higher risk of MACE [hazard ratio (HR): 3.48, 95% CI: 0.97–12.46; P=0.056]. As expected, HA (HR: 1.48, 95% CI: 0.52–4.21; P=0.47), dyslipidemia (HR 2.09, 95% CI 0.73–5.97; P=0.17), and CAD (HR: 0.89, 95% CI: 0.28–2.83; P=0.84) were not associated with MACE in univariable analyses in our cohort.

From CMR parameters only LVSV was a predictor of MACE (HR: 0.97; 95% CI: 0.94–0.99, P=0.01; Table 3). In prediction of all-cause mortality MRV ratio, LVSV and age reached statistical significance in the univariable Cox analysis (HR: 10.04; 95% CI: 1.66–60.79, P=0.01; HR: 0.95; 95% CI: 0.9–1.0, P=0.04; HR: 1.07; 95% CI: 1.01–1.14, P=0.04; Table 3). In prediction of cardiovascular mortality only MRV ratio reached statistical significance in the univariable Cox analysis (HR: 11.59; 95% CI: 1.23–109.49, P=0.03; Table 3). The univariable Cox analysis showed that only LVM/BSA (MESA) was significant predictor of MACE (HR: 3.18; 95% CI: 1.11–9.07, P=0.03; Table 4). However, after correction for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate procedure, none of the LVH definitions remained statistically significant. Similar results were obtained using the conservative Bonferroni correction. All LVM indexing method with cut-off values determined by the MESA (LVM/BSA, LVM/height1.7, LVM/height2.7, and %pLVM) were associated with prediction of all-cause mortality (HR: 12.2; 95% CI: 1.36–109.23, P=0.03; HR: 13.97; 95% CI: 1.56–125.13, P=0.02; HR: 13.97; 95% CI: 1.56–125.13, P=0.02, HR: 6.34; 95% CI: 1.06–37.95, P=0.04, respectively; Table 4).

Increasing age and CAD also remained independently associated with higher event risk.

Kaplan-Meier survival analyses by LVH definition

There was a significant difference in survival curves for MACE between patients with and without LVH diagnosed with LVM/BSA (MESA); log-rank test P=0.02. The Kaplan-Meier survival probability estimates at 6 years after CMR were 0.68 for patients with LVH and 0.89 for patients without LVH (Figure 3). There was also a significant difference in survival curves for the all-cause mortality between patients with and without LVH diagnosed with LVM/BSA (MESA); LVM/height1.7, LVM/height2.7, and %pLVM (log-rank test P<0.01, P<0.01, P<0.01, P=0.02, respectively; Figure 4).

Prognostic importance of LVM indexation methods

Non-indexed LVM showed strong associations with BSA-indexed, %pLVM, and height-indexed LVM, while BSA-indexed and %pLVM measures were nearly identical. Agreement between binary LVH definitions ranged from moderate to excellent (κ≈0.30–0.87), with higher concordance among threshold-based definitions and lower agreement with the MESA-derived cut-off. Despite these methodological differences, prognostic performance was comparable across models. Discrimination of models including continuous LVM/BSA vs. a MESA-derived LVH definition did not differ significantly (AUC: 0.789 vs. 0.807; P=0.327).

Interaction and sensitivity analyses

To evaluate whether the association between LVM and clinical outcomes differed across diseases, interaction terms between continuous LVM and HF or CAD were tested in multivariable logistic regression models. No statistically significant interactions were observed (all interaction P values >0.40), indicating that the relationship between LVM and MACE was not meaningfully modified by underlying disease subtypes.

Sensitivity analyses using multiple categorical definitions of LVH based on alternative indexation schemes—including MESA-derived LVM/BSA thresholds, Armstrong-based criteria, height-indexed definitions (height1.7 and height2.7), sex-specific BSA cut-offs, and age-adjusted definitions—yielded consistent results. None of the LVH definitions was independently associated with MACE after adjustment for clinical variables. For example, there was only a trend in terms of MESA-based LVH and increased event risk (OR: 2.50, 95% CI: 0.60–10.43; P=0.21). Sensitivity analyses yielded qualitatively similar results across alternative LVH definitions.

Discussion

Risk assessment, based on various biomarkers and clinical factors, in patients with different cardiovascular diseases is one of the key challenges in clinical practice (24-28). Currently, CMR is used for differential diagnosis in various cardiovascular diseases and brings great value to the conducted research (29). Importantly, CMR is considered the gold standard for evaluation of LVM (9,30). The use of different post processing methods can influence the estimation of LVM, although there is no clear consensus as to which method is the best (9). Generally, LVM is assessed at resting lung volume from end-diastolic phase of short axis cine steady-state free precession (SSFP) images (12) that cover the LV from base to apex. Then the myocardial volume is assessed and multiplied by the myocardial density (1.05 g/mL), and the observed LVM values are obtained (12). Reference ranges for these values permit differentiation between normal cardiac mass and LVH. So far, only several large studies, mostly using linear measurements, have reported normal values of LVM. Majority of studies assessing LVM during CMR that were performed so far were done with the use of the gradient-echo (GRE) technique (2,15,22). Nowadays, GRE has been replaced by SSFP, and LVM measured by the SSFP sequence is lower (31). However, existing studies using SSFP sequences have been based mainly on ratiometric scaling or non-indexed LVM (16,32). Therefore, further clinical investigations should be performed to define the new standard cut-off values for normality, especially for allometric indexed LVM.

The extensive study on reference values for LVM assessed by CMR, conducted on a large multi-ethnic cohort and employing SSFP sequences, underscores the importance of establishing standardized reference ranges and provides valuable context for interpreting our results (33). At the time this study was designed and the primary analyses were conducted, large SSFP-based reference datasets such as those from the Healthy Hearts Consortium were not yet available. Consequently, these data could not be prospectively incorporated into the study methodology. However, we discuss them to provide contemporary context. At that time, the largest available data were based on a study that provided CMR-specific sex- and age-based normal reference ranges for LVM/BSA using SSFP sequences derived from a multi-ethnic population of 3206 participants free of cardiovascular disease (23). However, there is a lack of association of this method of indexation with prediction of MACE in literature. Our study demonstrated that there is no statistically significant association between indexation methods with cut-off values established with SSFP sequences and prediction of MACE or all-cause mortality. Although the MESA-based LVM/BSA definition showed the strongest unadjusted association with MACE, this association did not withstand correction for multiple testing and should be interpreted with caution given the strong collinearity among LVM indexation methods and the limited number of outcome events. The application of MESA-derived cut-off values represents a transfer of thresholds established using GRE imaging in a demographically distinct population, which limits their direct clinical applicability in an SSFP-based cohort. Systematic differences between GRE and SSFP sequences, demographic and risk-profile differences between the MESA cohort and the present study population, and the risk of model instability or overfitting due to small sample size and wide confidence intervals could contribute to observed associations. Accordingly, the present findings should not be interpreted as evidence of superiority of MESA-derived indexing methods, but rather as exploratory and hypothesis-generating observations requiring confirmation in larger, adequately powered studies using contemporary SSFP-based imaging standards.

Current guidelines on image interpretation and post processing in CMR advise to report non-indexed and BSA-indexed values of LVM (9). However, indexing LVM to BSA underestimates the prevalence of LVH in obese and overweight hypertensive patients (34). It has been demonstrated that when LVM is measured by CMR, the prevalence of hypertrophy is higher for indices without weight (19,22). What is more, the association between body size and LVM is nonlinear and use of ratiometric scaling approaches may be not appropriate (35). Therefore, an allometric approach, which allows comparisons amongst different individuals and adjustment of cut-off values according to ethnicity and sex may be better (35). Compared with LVM/BSA and LVM/height, indexation of LVM by height to some allometric power provides a more sensitive cut-off for LVH (12). It was found that LVH defined by LVM/height1.7 was more sensitive than LVM/BSA in identification of obesity-related LVH. What is more, examples of longitudinal studies using CMR to estimate LVM as predictor of clinical outcomes have also demonstrated that the method of indexing LVM may influence the ability to predict future cardiovascular events (15). LVH defined by LVM/height1.7 had the strongest association with cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality in the healthy population (15). However, our study performed on patients with prevalent cardiovascular diseases showed that indexation of LVM/BSA is not inferior to indexation by height1.7 and height2.7.

The consistent association between lower LVSV and incident MACE observed in this study is physiologically justified. LVSV reflects the ability of the LV to deliver effective forward blood flow and therefore captures not only contractile performance but also the combined effects of ventricular remodeling, chamber size, preload, afterload, and valvular regurgitation (36). LVEF may remain preserved despite reduction in LVSV, such as in concentric remodeling with a small LV cavity, increased afterload, restrictive filling, or significant valvular regurgitations. In contrast, LVSV directly quantifies effective output and may serve as a pragmatic marker of impaired global hemodynamic performance and limited functional reserve (37). The persistence of LVSV as a predictor after adjustment for LVEF in our analyses supports the concept that volumetric flow measures may capture clinically meaningful aspects of cardiac function not fully reflected by LVEF alone.

The interpretation of our findings is substantially limited by small sample size and low number of events, particularly all-cause deaths. Under these conditions, prognostic modelling using multivariable Cox regression is likely to yield unstable estimates and be prone to overfitting, as evidenced by the extremely wide confidence intervals observed for several hazard ratios. Due to methodological constraints, including differences in imaging techniques and population characteristics compared with MESA, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Therefore, the results should be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Validation in larger, adequately powered studies is required before clinical implications can be inferred. Moreover, although the 6-year follow-up period is a strength, the inclusion of only patients with complete follow-up data could introduce selection bias. Of the 175 patients who underwent CMR during the study period, only 84 had complete 6-year follow-up data and were therefore included in the final analysis. This may have introduced selection bias and limits the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, the diversity of cardiovascular diseases within the study group adds heterogeneity and potentially influences the outcomes. Although age and HF are known predictors of MACE, their inclusion in the multivariable models did not yield statistically significant results, likely due to the limited sample size. This limitation underscores the need for larger studies to confirm these findings and further investigate additional contributing factors. Moreover, our study population consisted only of Caucasian individuals, which differs from the diverse ethnic composition of the MESA cohort. This disparity, along with the use of different imaging sequence (GRE in MESA vs. SSFP in our study), may limit the comparability of our findings with those of the MESA study.

A promising direction for future research is the application of artificial intelligence (AI) in CMR image analysis and automated assessment of LVM. Machine learning algorithms could substantially reduce subjectivity and variability of measurements while enabling faster and more consistent evaluation of large clinical datasets. Moreover, integrating AI-driven predictive models with traditional indexation methods may allow for more precise risk stratification and personalized therapeutic strategies. In the future, the use of AI could become a key tool supporting clinical decision-making in the diagnosis and prognosis of cardiovascular events.

Conclusions

The observed associations suggest that LVSV and certain LVM indexing approaches, including those based on MESA-derived cut-off values, show exploratory associations with adverse cardiovascular outcomes. Further studies in larger, independent populations using standardized imaging techniques are needed to clarify the prognostic value of LVM indexing methods and to determine their potential role in clinical risk stratification.
