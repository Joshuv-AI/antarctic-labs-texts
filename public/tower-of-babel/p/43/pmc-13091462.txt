Influenza vaccine response is predicted by chronotype and differentially associated with pre- and post-vaccination sleep
Authors: Elizabeth M. Harrison, Rachel U. Lee, Chad K. Porter, Sandra D. Isidean, Alexandra P. Easterling, Lindsey White, David A. Boulifard, Katherine DeTizio, Zhaodong Liang, Martha Sedegah, Janine Danko, Gena L. Glickman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Identifying strategies to enhance vaccine response supports public health efforts, particularly in settings where individuals face elevated exposure risks, such as during military deployment or in other high-demand contexts. Accumulating evidence suggests that longer sleep duration may enhance vaccine response, but the role of sleep quality and timing remain less well understood. Additionally, although the circadian system interacts with the immune system, it is unclear whether improved vaccination response with morning administration is driven by internal biological timing. In this study, we examined both objective and subjective measures of sleep quantity, quality, and timing before, immediately after, and in the weeks following influenza vaccination to determine how sleep and vaccine response interact over time in individuals with and without 2 weeks of randomly assigned, exogenous melatonin post-vaccination.Sleep patterns were minimally different between treatment groups; however, the melatonin group reported worse sleep quality pre-vaccination and shorter sleep latency on the first night post-vaccination as compared to controls. After adjusting for sex, age, group, and baseline levels, better pre-vaccination sleep quality was associated with stronger immune responses, including higher hemagglutination inhibition (HAI) titers for the Victoria lineage and elevated fluorospot interferon gamma (IFN-γ), granzyme B (GzB), and IFN-γ+GzB responses (all p < 0.05). In contrast, associations between post-vaccination sleep and immune outcomes were more variable and marker-dependent. Chronotype was also associated with immune function, with earlier chronotypes exhibiting more robust responses across multiple measures. Mediation analysis also confirmed that our previously-reported increase in fold-rise IFN-γ+GzB in the melatonin group was not attributable to sleep, either directly or indirectly.These findings suggest that sleep exerts complex, time-dependent effects on vaccine response, independent of melatonin supplementation. Moreover, chronotype and time of vaccination should be considered in future immunization studies. Ultimately, improving sleep quality prior to vaccination and administering melatonin post-vaccination may improve vaccine immune response.

Introduction

Sleep and the immune system are intricately linked, with even short-term sleep disturbances altering immune function. This is particularly relevant to the identification of strategies for augmenting vaccine efficacy in populations with both elevated exposure risks and sleep disturbance, such as service members on deployment and in other operational contexts. Accumulating evidence suggests that sleep surrounding vaccination can impact both the strength of the immune response and the degree of protection conferred from vaccination (Benedict et al., 2012; Besedovsky et al., 2012; Lange et al., 2011; Prather et al., 2012; Prather et al., 2021; Schmitz et al., 2022, Spiegel et al., 2002, 2023; Tauman et al., 2024; Taylor et al., 2017). Although it remains unclear whether sleep before or after vaccination is more influential, limited findings suggest that pre-vaccination sleep is more predictive of longer-term immune outcomes. Sleep just prior to influenza vaccination, as compared to just after, was more strongly associated with immune responses several weeks later (Prather et al., 2021), and sleep at the time of vaccination was able to predict responses up to seven months later (Tauman et al., 2024). To date, most research on sleep and vaccination has focused on sleep duration, with fewer studies examining the role of sleep quality, and results are mixed (Prather et al., 2021; Quach et al., 2023; Taylor et al., 2017). Furthermore, very few studies have used objective sleep measures to examine these relationships (Livieratos et al., 2025; Schmitz et al., 2022).

Sleep is only one of many outputs of the circadian timing system. The circadian system, in general, interacts with immune function in complex ways. Immune cell counts and reactivity vary by time of day (Besedovsky et al., 2012; Haspel et al., 2020), and circadian disruption (e.g. that caused by shiftwork) can impair vaccine responses (Ruiz et al., 2020; Tauman et al., 2024; but see Brouwers et al., 2025). Thus, it is not surprising that the time of day an individual gets vaccinated may impact the immune response generated. Specifically, recently-published literature suggests vaccination in the morning may enhance immunogenicity (Kurupati et al., 2017; Long et al., 2016; Otasowie et al., 2022; Vink et al., 2024), although findings are mixed (Faustini et al., 2025; Wyse et al., 2024).

One potential explanation for these inconsistencies is a failure to account for internal circadian timing. Factors such as chronotype (an individual's preferred sleep/wake timing relative to the solar clock), or circadian phase at the time of vaccination (e.g. relative to the timing of individual circadian clock outputs, such as body temperature or sleep) may influence vaccine efficacy but are rarely measured. For example, it could be the case that vaccination in the morning is better for all, irrespective of internal timing, or it could be that “early” (morning) chronotypes have more robust vaccine responses and self-select earlier vaccination times. Limited evidence suggests that later chronotypes are more susceptible to infections such as COVID-19, potentially due to reduced innate antiviral immunity or weaker vaccine response (Han et al., 2023). These distinct yet interrelated mechanisms (clock time, chronotype, and circadian timing) warrant further investigation in the context of vaccination.

Exogenous melatonin, a regulator of sleep and circadian rhythms, has also been proposed as a potential vaccine adjuvant (Lee and Glickman, 2021; Lee et al., 2024; Maestroni, 2020). However, it remains unclear from existing data whether its effects on immunogenicity are direct, or mediated indirectly via improvements in sleep and/or circadian alignment (Lee and Glickman, 2021). In human in vitro and animal model systems, melatonin enhances vaccine efficacy (Carrillo-Vico et al., 2004; Moreno et al., 2018; Regodón et al., 2005), and in-vitro, it promotes both humoral and cellular immune response (Cardinali et al., 2008; Carrillo-Vico et al., 2004, 2013), including markers relevant to influenza vaccination, such as interferon gamma (IFN-γ) and granzyme B (GzB) (Otani et al., 2021; Janssens et al., 2022).

In recently-published results from our randomized control clinical trial, two weeks of post-vaccination melatonin supplementation increased fold-rise in dual IFN-γ + GzB-secreting peripheral blood mononuclear cells, although no effects were found on HAI titers or single-marker responses (Lee et al., 2024). Melatonin may act directly on immune pathways or indirectly by enhancing sleep, particularly through reductions in sleep onset latency (Posadzki et al., 2018). Light-induced suppression of endogenous nocturnal melatonin further complicates the picture, particularly in the context of circadian disruption (Vetter et al., 2022). While the optimal timing of exogenous melatonin for improving circadian alignment and sleep is known (∼1-2 h before a consistent bedtime) (Arendt and Rajaratnam, 2008), its ideal timing relative to vaccination remains unclear.

Unlike fixed individual-level determinants of vaccine response (e.g. sex, age, genetics), sleep, circadian timing, and melatonin administration are modifiable behaviors and could represent promising vaccine adjuvant strategies; however, their individual and combined effects on immunogenicity remain poorly understood (Anderson et al., 2023; Benedict and Cedernaes, 2021; Lee and Glickman, 2021). To address these gaps, we conducted a pilot study to evaluate the effects of melatonin and sleep on immune responses to influenza vaccination. While prior reported analyses from the Melatonin and Vaccine Response Immunity and Chronobiology Study (MAVRICS) focused on melatonin's effects alone (Lee et al., 2024), this paper examines how sleep duration, quality and timing, as well as chronotype, contribute to vaccine-induced immunity.

Methods

Study design

This randomized, open-label pilot trial was conducted between October, 2022 and January, 2023 at Walter Reed National Military Medical Center (WRNMMC) and the Naval Medical Research Command (NMRC) Clinical Trials Center in Bethesda, Maryland. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of NMRC (NMRC.2021.0006) and registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04953754). All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment.

Participants were recruited from the immunization clinic at WRNMMC, through the NMRC Clinical Trials registry, and via flyers and word-of-mouth. Volunteers were screened for health status and eligibility. Exclusion criteria included: age <18 or >64 years; known allergy or other contraindication(s) to influenza vaccination; receipt of influenza vaccine within the past 6 months; pregnancy; diagnosed sleep disorder requiring medication; use of immunosuppressive or immune-modifying treatments in the past 3 months; immune-compromising conditions (e.g. HIV, active cancer); and/or use of any sleep-related medications or supplements within the past month. Eligibility was confirmed by a study physician. Eight volunteers were excluded (1 recent flu shot, 1 sleep disorder, 4 medical conditions, 1 prohibited medication, 1 unknown reason), and four participants withdrew post-randomization (1 time constraint, 1 COVID-19 infection, 1 difficulty providing blood sample, 1 lost to followup). Sample size was calculated based on seroconversion rates of ≥20% for two groups (melatonin and control); see Lee et al. (2024) for the sample size calculation and participant flow diagram details.

Fig. 1 shows the overall design of the study. At visit 1 (V1), following informed consent and prior to influenza vaccination, participants (N = 108) were randomized to melatonin treatment (n = 53) or control (n = 55) groups. Randomization was performed using R (Vienna, Austria), with block sizes of 4 and 6 and a 1:1 allocation ratio. At V1, participants provided a blood sample and completed demographic information forms, the Munich Chronotype Questionnaire (MCTQ), and the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI). Participants were then instructed to receive the influenza vaccine (IIVA; Flulaval Quadrivalent, GlaskoSmithKline, Quebec, Canada) at the WRNMMC immunization clinic within 24-48 h (VAX), and to return for Visit 2 (V2) in 2-3 weeks to capture the expected peak humoral response while allowing for scheduling flexibility (Halliley et al., 2010). Staff recorded vaccination date and time. Vaccine strains included A/Victoria/2570/2019 (H1N1), A/Darwin/9/2021 (H3N2), B/Austria/1359417/2021 (B/Victoria lineage), and B/Phuket/3073/2013 (B/Yamagata lineage).

Participants in the melatonin group were provided with melatonin caplets (REMfresh 5 mg, Nestle Health Science) at V1 and instructed to take them 1 h before bedtime for 14 consecutive nights, beginning the night of vaccination, recording the timing of intake on the daily log (see below for details). Dosage and formulation choices were guided by established use in sleep research and clinical practice; a 5-mg extended-release formulation was selected to provide sustained nocturnal exposure, supporting both sleep onset and maintenance (Andersen et al., 2016; Arendt, 2019; Brouwers et al., 2025; Bueno et al., 2017). Independent testing via high-performance liquid chromatography verified melatonin content (5.5-6.1 mg) (NSF Laboratories, Ann Arbor, Michigan).

All participants were instructed to wear an actigraphy device and complete a daily sleep log. At V2, a second blood sample was collected; actigraphs and sleep logs were returned; and the PSQI was completed again.

Sleep & timing measures

Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI)

The PSQI is a validated self-report measure of sleep over the prior month (Buysse, 1989). A total, Global Sleep Quality Score (range 0-21) is generated from the scoring of 7 different components. Because the melatonin treatment condition affects responses to the item about sleep medication, it was not included in the Global score (resultant range of 0-18). To reduce the number of statistical comparisons, and because components such as total sleep time [TST], sleep onset latency [SOL], and sleep efficiency were measured objectively with actigraphy, we focused on two PSQI scores: the modified Global score and the single-item Sleep Quality component (During the past month, how would you rate your sleep quality overall? Very good, fairly good, fairly bad, very bad). Lower scores indicate better sleep in both cases. At V1, participants completed the PSQI for the month prior to vaccination. At V2, they were instructed to respond based on the time since V1.

Actigraphy & sleep log

Participants wore actigraphs (Actiwatch Spectrum Plus, Phillips, Murrysville, PA, USA) continuously from VAX to V2 (mean ± SD, 14.40 ± 2.16 days; range 6-21 days), and were instructed to use the event marker to indicate intent to initiate and end sleep. Data were scored using ActiWare software (Version 6.3), with standard parameters (medium sensitivity, 5 min on- and off-set thresholds), by two independent scorers blind to condition (EH & AP), yielding 99.2% inter-rater reliability. Event markers and sleep logs distinguished time in bed from sleep attempts and were used to confirm rest intervals, which were adjusted when necessary based on concordance with automated scoring, light (<1 lux, indicating “lights off”) and activity thresholds (0 counts), as in Patel et al. (2015).

Key actigraph metrics included: TST (per 24 h); sleep efficiency; SOL; wake after sleep onset (WASO); and midsleep time. Metrics were extracted for two time frames: 1) acute (the night of vaccination) and 2) extended (the full post-vaccination period between VAX and V2). For extended sleep metrics, averages were computed across all valid intervals, yielding per-24 h estimates. Sleep efficiency was calculated as TST/time in bed (TST + SOL + WASO). One participant had no actigraphy data, and two others lacked data for the night of vaccination but were included in extended analyses.

Timing measures

Six timing measures were considered: 1) chronotype, 2) social jetlag, 3) acute and 4) extended actigraphy-based midsleep, 5) the clock time of vaccination (ClockTV), and 6) the estimated circadian timing of vaccination (CircTV). As part of the MCTQ completed at V1, participants reported their bed and wake times on “free” days, from which midsleep on free days (MSF) was then calculated (Zavada et al., 2005). Thus, higher values represent relatively later chronotypes. A measure of sleep regularity and circadian disruption, “social jetlag”, was also derived from the MCTQ, calculated as the difference in hh:mm between mid-sleep on free days versus work days. Actigraphy-based midsleep was calculated for sleep intervals >180 min (1530/1658 sleep intervals, 92.3%) for sleep on the night following vaccination (acute) as well as for the period between VAX and V2 (extended). Estimated circadian timing of vaccination (CircTV) was calculated as the difference in hh:mm between MSF and vaccination clock time.

Immunological outcomes

Vaccine response was assessed via serologic and cell-mediated immunity measures from blood collected at V1 and V2. Hemagglutination inhibition titers (HAI) were calculated from sample duplicates to assess serologic response. For cellular response, peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PMBCs) were stimulated with 1ug/ml of the immunizing vaccine. Cytokine-secreting cells were quantified for IFN-gamma (IFN-γ), granzyme B (GzB), and dual IFN-γ+GzB markers. See Lee et al. (2024) for full laboratory procedures.

Analysis

Sleep was examined separately across three time periods: 1) Pre-Vaccination (PSQI Global and PSQI Sleep Quality), covering the month before vaccination, 2) Acute Post-Vaccination (actigraphy TST, SOL, WASO, and SE), covering the first night after vaccination, and 3) Extended Post-Vaccination (PSQI Global and PSQI Sleep Quality, as well as actigraphy TST, SOL, WASO and SE), across the VAX-V2 interval.

Group differences by treatment condition were examined via independent t-tests. Based on prior findings of melatonin treatment effects on IFN-γ+GzB fold-rise (Lee et al., 2024), mediation analyses were planned for sleep parameters that differed between groups to evaluate whether sleep mediated those effects.

Descriptive Pearson correlations examined relationships among the four timing variables and between subjective and objective sleep measures.

To assess the direct effects of sleep on vaccine response, linear regressions were conducted with the sleep and timing variables as predictors (per Prather et al., 2021). Covariates included sex (male/female), age in years, and V1 baseline immune levels, as they have been shown to influence sleep and immune response to influenza vaccination (Benedict et al., 2012; Faustini et al., 2025; Kurupati et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2022; Phillips et al., 2008; Prather et al., 2021; Quach et al., 2023). Treatment group was also included as a covariate due to the sleep and circadian effects of exogenous melatonin (Auld et al., 2017; Lockley et al., 2000). Outcomes included V2 HAI titers for four strains and V2 cytokine responses (IFN-γ, GzB, and IFN-γ+GzB). Results are reported with both unadjusted (p < 0.05) and Benjamini-Hochberg-corrected p values.

Analyses were conducted in SPSS v29.0.2.0 (IBM; Armonk, NY) and R (using the ‘circular' package). Statistical assumptions for linear regressions were verified and evaluated via actual versus predicted plots, residual plots, correlation matrices, and examination of outliers. Outliers were defined as values >3x IQR from the median. One participant had extreme WASO and sleep efficiency values for the acute sleep period; however, exclusion did not alter results.

Melatonin, sleep, and timing

Actigraphy indicated mean nightly sleep durations of 7 h 04 min in the melatonin treatment group and 6 h 55 min in controls during the post-vaccination period. The average time of vaccination was centered around noon for both groups, ranging from 08:22 to 18:30; see Table 1 for ClockTV group means. At V1, the melatonin group reported significantly poorer sleep quality on the PSQI compared to controls (t(105) = −2.45, p = 0.016), but no difference was observed at V2 (t(105) = 0.66, p = 0.510) (Fig. 2). On the night following vaccination, the melatonin group exhibited significantly shorter sleep onset latency (SOL) than controls (t(103) = 2.02, p = 0.046). Given the group differences in pre-vaccination sleep quality and acute SOL, these variables were tested as mediators of melatonin's previously reported effects on IFN-γ+GzB fold-rise (Lee et al., 2024). Mediation analyses showed no significant direct nor indirect effects (all p > 0.57; See Fig. S1 and 2 for coefficients and p values), indicating that melatonin's immune effects were not mediated by improvements in sleep. Additionally, when IFN-γ+GzB fold-rise was analyzed using linear regression (rather than t-tests), melatonin did not significantly predict immune outcomes (Fig. S1 and S2, direct and indirect effects of melatonin on sleep quality and acute sleep onset latency; p = 0.07 and p = 0.11).

Pre-vaccination sleep

Better subjective sleep quality prior to vaccination predicted enhanced immune responses at V2. Specifically, lower PSQI sleep quality scores (indicating better sleep) were associated with increased HAI titers to the Victoria strain (p = 0.014) and elevated fluorospot responses for IFN-γ (p = 0.015), GzB (p = 0.020), and IFN-γ+GzB (p = 0.030; see Fig. S3 for associated scatterplots). Similar trends were observed for PSQI Global scores and responses to Victoria and GzB (p = 0.06 and 0.08, respectively), though they were not statistically significant. No associations were observed between pre-vaccination sleep and other immunologic responses (all p > 0.23).

Timing

Earlier chronotype was significantly associated with more robust IFN-γ (p = 0.021) and IFN-γ+GzB (p = 0.034) responses at V2 (see Fig. S3 for associated scatterplots). Other timing variables, including ClockTV and CircTV, were not significantly associated with any immune outcomes (all p > 0.13). Dichotomizing vaccination time into morning versus afternoon, as has been done in prior studies, also did not yield significant associations (all p > 0.28).

Acute post-vaccination sleep

Contrary to pre-vaccination findings, poorer sleep on the night following vaccination was associated with enhanced immune responses. Specifically, shorter TST and lower sleep efficiency were associated with higher HAI titers to the Darwin strain (p = 0.006 and p = 0.025, respectively), and longer SOL was associated with higher Austria strain titres (p = 0.010). Other associations between acute sleep variables and immune markers were not statistically significant (all p > 0.15).

Extended post-vaccination sleep

Consistent with findings in acute post-vaccination sleep, poorer objectively-measured sleep across the VAX-V2 interval was also associated with stronger immune responses. Notably, longer SOL and lower sleep efficiency were associated with higher Austria strain HAI titers (p = 0.010 and 0.028). There was also a trend for higher WASO to predict stronger responses (p = 0.063). Worse PSQI sleep quality scores for this same period were associated with stronger responses to the Darwin strain (p < 0.007). In contrast, better PSQI Global scores were positively associated with responses to the Victoria strain (p = 0.034) and with GzB fluorospot values (p = 0.039). All other extended sleep variables were not significantly associated with immune outcomes (all p > 0.16).

Covariates

The statistical significance levels of the covariates in each model are shown alongside primary outcomes in Table 3. With the exception of the Victoria strain, baseline levels of immune markers were significant in most models at the p < 0.001 level. Age was significant at the p < 0.05 level in the majority of the models that included the Victoria and Austria strains, with higher age associated with lower response. Sex (male/female) was associated with IFN-γ response only, typically at the p < 0.05 level, with a higher response in female participants. Sex-stratified estimates are reported in Table S4 and S5 for descriptive purposes, but should be interpreted with caution. Finally, treatment group was significant in models of IFN-γ+GzB and both Chronotype and pre-vaccination Sleep Quality (p < 0.05), with higher IFN-γ+GzB in individuals taking melatonin. Post hoc sub-analyses of sleep and immune markers in the melatonin group, based on whether they took melatonin the night before V2 blood collection (n = 29) or not (n = 24), yielded no statistically significant differences (all p > 0.18).

Discussion

To our knowledge this is the first study to simultaneously examine multiple facets of sleep and circadian timing, encompassing both subjective and objective sleep measures across several timepoints, clock time of vaccination, estimated circadian timing of vaccination and chronotype, in relation to influenza vaccine response. This trial demonstrated that earlier chronotype was associated with a more robust cellular immune response; better self-reported sleep quality before vaccination predicted enhanced humoral and cellular immunity; and post-vaccination sleep showed mixed associations with immune outcomes, which varied by strain. These results add complexity to the growing literature linking sleep and circadian factors with vaccine efficacy.

Earlier chronotype, assessed via the Munich Chronotype Questionnaire, was associated with a more robust cellular response, consistent with literature suggesting that ‘morning types’ exhibit more favorable immune profiles (Butler et al., 2023; Han et al., 2023). Interestingly, this effect was not mirrored in other timing measures (e.g. actigraphy-based midsleep, social jetlag, or CircTV), despite their intercorrelations (see Table 2). This discrepancy may reflect differences in measurement sensitivity, biological relevance of timing metrics, or light exposure patterns (Münch et al., 2022). The fact that chronotype, but not clock time of vaccination, predicted immune response underscores the potential importance of internal biological timing over external timing alone.

Unlike prior studies that dichotomized vaccination time (e.g. morning vs afternoon) (Faustini et al., 2025; Kow and Hasan, 2021; Kurupati et al., 2017; Langlois et al., 1995; Liu et al., 2022; Long et al., 2016; Phillips et al., 2008), we initially analyzed timing of vaccination as a continuous variable to maximize detection of more nuanced trends and to avoid arbitrary cutoffs that may not align with biologically meaningful divisions. However, our follow-up analyses using dichotomized time did not alter the null findings, suggesting that clock time alone may not be a reliable predictor of vaccine efficacy, at least in our relatively younger cohort.

We found a trend towards older individuals getting vaccinated at a relatively later circadian phase than younger participants. Many studies that have examined the effects of timing of vaccination on immune response only included, or only found effects in, older populations (Christensen et al., 2024; Kurupati et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2022; Long et al., 2016). Thus, apparent clock time differences in the literature to date may not fully generalize across ages and/or ideal scheduling of vaccination may be slightly later (at similar circadian phase) for younger adults.

Better subjective sleep quality before vaccination predicted enhanced immune responses across both humoral and cellular markers, particularly for the Victoria strain. These findings are consistent with prior work showing sleep duration preceding influenza vaccination led to increased antibody levels, though in that study, no significant effects of sleep quality were found (Prather et al., 2021). This discrepancy highlights the value of considering multiple dimensions of sleep longitudinally.

In contrast, findings related to post-vaccination sleep were more complex. Consistent with our pre-vaccination findings, better post-vaccination PSQI Global scores were positively associated with responses to two markers (Victoria and GzB fluorospot values); however, paradoxically, lower sleep quality for the same period was associated with a more robust response for Darwin. Poorer objectively-measured post-vaccination sleep was also associated with stronger immune responses, particularly to the A/Darwin/9/2021 (H3N2) and B/Austria/1359417 strains, both newly introduced in the 2022-2023 vaccine. Strain-specific differences in sleep and circadian-related immune response have been reported in previous studies (Langlois et al., 1995; Long et al., 2016; Quach et al., 2023), and effects of vaccination timing on response did not hold with revaccination (Langlois et al., 1995), suggesting the effects of sleep and circadian rhythms on immune response may depend on antigenic novelty.

Our results of poorer post-vaccination sleep and enhanced immune response also diverge from earlier work that involved experimental sleep restriction immediately following vaccination under more highly controlled conditions, where increased sleep resulted in greater antibody response (Benedict et al., 2012; Lange et al., 2011). This is quite different from the naturally-occurring variations in sleep captured in our study. One possible explanation for these apparent paradoxical associations is that they reflect immune-driven sleep disruption rather than sleep influencing immunity. Prior studies have shown that immune activation can impair sleep (Han et al., 2023; Xiao et al., 2023), supporting a reverse causal or reactive interpretation. Alternatively, both sleep and immune responses may be influenced by unmeasured factors or by the timing of immune activation earlier in the response trajectory. Additionally, we did not control for or monitor work schedule during the study period; thus, it is unknown whether individuals were vaccinated on work or free days, and extended sleep measures are averaged across both day types. It is possible this schedule variation influenced results, though no effects of social jetlag were observed. Circadian stability both preceding and following vaccination is another important direction for future work.

These findings warrant further longitudinal investigation, ideally incorporating daily symptom tracking, side effect profiles, and repeated immune assessments to clarify temporal directionality. Additionally, methodological differences between subjective and objective sleep assessments may have contributed to variability in our results. Unfortunately, we were unable to collect actigraphy before vaccination and although both types of sleep measures covered the post-vaccination period, duration of the post-vaccination period of actigraphy varied (6-21 days) and PSQI at V2 was modified to reflect the entire interval between visits. Temporal mismatches across measures and small differences in sample size for the sleep data (n = 105-108) may have introduced additional noise, underscoring the need for synchronized, multi-modal sleep measurement in future work. Finally, actigraphy may overestimate total sleep time, and does not permit assessment of sleep stages. Future studies incorporating polysomnography and/or dim light melatonin onset would allow direct assessment of sleep architecture and stage-specific effects of melatonin, as well as more precise separation of circadian and homeostatic influences on sleep outcomes.

Melatonin treatment modestly reduced sleep onset latency on the night of vaccination but did not alter midsleep or total sleep times, nor did it improve sleep across the full post-vaccination period. This may indicate short-lived effects, insufficient dosage or duration, or the limited sensitivity of actigraphy to detect nuanced changes. Our exploratory mediation analyses further suggested that melatonin may exert immunological effects independent of its impact on sleep, though further mechanistic studies are needed. Preclinical models point to direct immunomodulatory effects of melatonin (Carrillo-Vico et al., 2004; Moreno et al., 2018); however, our data do not clarify the mechanism in humans.

While variability in the number of days between vaccination and V2 resulted in some (but not all) participants receiving melatonin the night immediately preceding the second blood collection, there is limited concern for potential acute spill-over effects of melatonin. Within our dataset, post hoc analyses revealed no differences in sleep or immune markers when analyzed as a function of the number of days between vaccination and V2, and characterization data from extended-release melatonin indicate that melatonin was likely not circulating at meaningful levels the next morning (Mun et al., 2024). In addition, it is worth noting that the primary objective of this study was to assess peak post-vaccination humoral response, and the data collected here do not allow for examination of longer-term effects of melatonin or assessment of the durability of immune response. Future trials should use pharmaceutical-grade melatonin, include placebo controls, and systematically study or administer different doses, formulations, and timing of melatonin administration to better evaluate its potential as a vaccine adjuvant.

This study reinforces that pre-vaccination sleep quality is a modifiable factor with the potential to improve vaccine response. In addition, chronotype emerges as a novel predictor of immune response, distinct from sleep duration or external timing. Together, these findings suggest that individualized behavioral interventions, such as improving sleep hygiene or optimizing vaccination timing based on chronotype, could enhance vaccine efficacy. By contrast, the associations between post-vaccination sleep and immunity were less consistent and may reflect immune reactivity or symptom-driven sleep changes, rather than sleep influencing immunity. These bidirectional relationships complicate interpretation and highlight the need for longitudinal, mechanistic studies to disentangle directionality. Future research should track both symptoms and immune responses over time; include larger, more diverse cohorts; test placebo-controlled melatonin protocols with varied timing, formulation, and dosage; incorporate more granular circadian phase markers (e.g. dim light melatonin onset); and explore interactions between strain novelty, prior immunity, and behavioral factors.

In sum, our findings contribute to the growing body of evidence linking behavioral and circadian factors to vaccine efficacy. While melatonin did not enhance immunity via sleep pathways, subjective pre-vaccination sleep quality and earlier chronotype emerged as promising, low-cost behavioral predictors of vaccine response.
