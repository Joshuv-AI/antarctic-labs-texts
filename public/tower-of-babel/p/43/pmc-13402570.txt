Multiparametric cardiovascular magnetic resonance for differentiating cardiac masses: clinical impact from a case series

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Cardiac masses represent a diagnostically challenging entity that includes primary tumours, metastatic disease, tumour-related thrombi, and bland thrombi. Accurate differentiation is essential because misclassification may alter staging and management. Cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) enables comprehensive multiparametric tissue characterization and plays a central role in diagnostic evaluation. Case summary

Summary figure

Case 1

A 31-year-old man with a known history of non-seminomatous testicular germ cell tumour, previously treated with left radical orchiectomy followed by four cycles of BEP chemotherapy (bleomycin, etoposide, and cisplatin), presented with progressively worsening mild dyspnoea. On admission to the emergency department, he was haemodynamically stable.

Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a mobile intracardiac mass involving the right-sided cardiac chambers. A contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) scan of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis showed widespread metastatic disease, including pulmonary, mediastinal, and bulky retroperitoneal involvement, along with a large intracardiac mass extending through the right heart and great vessels. Cardiac findings included situs solitus with levocardia and a large, mobile mass at the level of the tricuspid valve with regular borders, extending into the right atrium, the superior cavoatrial junction, and superior vena cava and projecting into the right ventricle, infundibulum, and pulmonary artery trunk, with evidence of contrast mixing. Delayed post-contrast imaging demonstrated heterogeneous partial enhancement of the mass, suggestive of viable tumour tissue.

Further evaluation with CMR confirmed intracardiac metastasis with heterogeneous signal intensity, first-pass perfusion, and heterogeneous late gadolinium enhancement (LGE), consistent with metastatic involvement with superimposed tumour-related thrombus (Figure 1). Histopathological confirmation had previously been obtained from the primary germ cell tumour. The intracardiac lesion was considered metastatic based on concordant multimodality imaging findings and disseminated systemic disease; therefore, direct biopsy was not pursued. Serum markers, including alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), beta-human chorionic gonadotropin, and lactate dehydrogenase, were relevant for prognostic stratification and disease monitoring. Given the extent of disease, the patient was started on systemic chemotherapy with bleomycin and etoposide.

(Case 1) (A) Native T1 mapping demonstrating heterogeneous relaxation times within the right-sided intracardiac mass. (B) T1 mapping in the right ventricular two-chamber view demonstrating heterogeneous relaxation times. (C) First-pass perfusion imaging with contrast uptake within the cranial and caudal portions of the lesion with central hypoenhancement, suggesting mixed malignant and thrombotic tissue. (D) Late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) imaging demonstrating heterogeneous peripheral enhancement with central non-enhancing regions, consistent with intracardiac metastasis complicated by thrombus.

Case 2

A 51-year-old woman with a history of Hodgkin lymphoma, currently receiving R-CHOP chemotherapy, was referred for cardiology follow-up in the context of anthracycline therapy.

Transthoracic echocardiography performed during outpatient evaluation revealed a mass involving the right atrium and right ventricle, suspicious for secondary cardiac involvement. A contrast-enhanced CT scan demonstrated mediastinal soft tissue infiltration associated with pericardial thickening and severe pericardial effusion, as well as an intracardiac mass involving the right-sided chambers, with poorly defined interfaces towards adjacent cardiac structures, including the left ventricle. For further tissue characterization, CMR was requested, confirming an intracardiac lesion with features consistent with metastasis and associated thrombotic components, in keeping with cardiac involvement from haematologic malignancy (see Supplementary material online, Video S1). Histological confirmation of lymphoma had previously been established from the primary haematologic disease. Cardiac involvement was diagnosed based on characteristic imaging findings and concordance with systemic disease; therefore, endomyocardial biopsy was not performed. Management was focused on continuation of systemic chemotherapy as the primary treatment strategy, along with close cardiologic monitoring.

Case 3

A 16-year-old male with a history of acute promyelocytic leukaemia presented with fever during routine follow-up. Emergency evaluation revealed severe neutropenia and septic shock, requiring admission to the intensive care unit for haemodynamic support. As part of haemodynamic monitoring, transthoracic echocardiography was performed and identified two masses within the right ventricle. A contrast-enhanced CT scan was obtained as complementary imaging and demonstrated multiple bilateral pulmonary consolidations, predominantly in the left lower lobe, suggestive of an infectious process, as well as findings compatible with tumour-related thrombus in the right ventricle. Further evaluation with CMR revealed non-infiltrative right ventricular masses: (i) a posterior–inferior sessile mass adjacent to the inferoseptal region and (ii) an anterior–cranial partially pedunculated mass attached to the apical interventricular septum. Overall findings were consistent with chronic right ventricular thrombi (Figure 2). The diagnosis was established based on characteristic CMR tissue characterization findings, non-infiltrative behaviour, and clinical context. The patient was managed in the intensive care unit and treated with anticoagulation using apixaban 5 mg every 12 h.

Case 4

A 59-year-old man presented to the emergency department with progressive dyspnoea on moderate exertion. His history was notable for left lower extremity osteosarcoma treated with surgical resection and systemic chemotherapy 6 years earlier, without evidence of recurrence during follow-up.

Chest radiography demonstrated an enlarged cardiac silhouette with a ‘water bottle’ configuration, suggestive of significant pericardial effusion. A CT scan was performed for further characterization, confirming the presence of pericardial effusion. Cardiology consultation was requested, and TTE demonstrated a large pericardial effusion with early haemodynamic compromise, as well as a mass adherent to the pericardium adjacent to the inferolateral wall. Given the oncologic history, CMR imaging was performed, revealing pericardial and bilateral pleural effusions, along with a sessile, infiltrative, heterogeneous mass adherent to the pericardium, consistent with pericardial metastasis from primary sarcoma (see Supplementary material online, Video S2). Histological confirmation was obtained, consistent with metastatic sarcoma. Management was focused on systemic chemotherapy.

Case 5

A 47-year-old woman with a history of breast cancer was referred for cardiology evaluation prior to initiation of anthracycline-based chemotherapy (planned doxorubicin, four cycles at 60 mg/m2 each). During baseline cardiovascular assessment, transthoracic echocardiography revealed a right atrial mass, prompting further characterization with CMR. Cardiovascular magnetic resonance findings, including T1 scout behaviour and absence of contrast uptake on first-pass perfusion and LGE sequences, were consistent with a subacute thrombus (Figure 3). The diagnosis was established based on characteristic CMR findings and the absence of infiltrative or vascularized features suggestive of neoplastic tissue. The patient was treated with enoxaparin 1 mg/kg every 12 h for 1 week, followed by apixaban 5 mg every 12 h.

Case 6

A 2-year-old female with a known diagnosis of hepatoblastoma was referred for cardiology evaluation as part of routine preoperative assessment prior to planned surgical resection.

Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a mass within the right atrium, extending from the inferior vena cava, raising suspicion for tumour-related thrombus. Contrast-enhanced CT revealed a heterogeneous solid mass involving all hepatic segments with abdominal extension. Multiple pulmonary nodules were also identified. Additionally, a filling defect suggestive of tumour-related thrombus was observed extending into the right atrium.

Cardiovascular magnetic resonance complemented the diagnosis, demonstrating a well-defined mass extending along the inferior vena cava into the right atrium, consistent with a tumour-related thrombus secondary to intracardiac extension of hepatoblastoma (see Supplementary material online, Video S3). Histopathological confirmation had previously been obtained from the primary hepatic tumour. The intracardiac lesion was diagnosed as tumoural extension based on imaging continuity with the primary neoplasm and characteristic tissue features. The patient underwent surgical resection of the intracardiac tumour-related thrombus, completed systemic chemotherapy, and subsequently underwent surgical resection of the primary tumour after significant reduction in tumour burden. Serum AFP levels were used for disease monitoring and therapeutic response assessment.

Case 7

A 34-year-old man with a known history of primary sarcoma of the right lower extremity, treated 1 year earlier, was referred for cardiovascular evaluation. During routine oncologic follow-up, contrast-enhanced thoracic CT demonstrated pulmonary nodules along with a new filling defect in the right ventricle, suggestive of a ventricular thrombus. Given the patient’s oncologic history and the need for accurate characterization, CMR was requested. Cardiovascular magnetic resonance demonstrated an intracardiac mass within the right ventricle with imaging features consistent with metastatic involvement. Additional findings included pulmonary lesions with similar signal characteristics, supporting the diagnosis of intracardiac and pulmonary metastases from primary sarcoma (Figure 4). Histopathological confirmation had previously been established from the primary sarcoma. The cardiac lesion was considered metastatic based on concordant imaging findings and disseminated disease. The patient was managed with systemic oncologic therapy.

Case 8

A 17-year-old female presented with a history of syncope and was referred for cardiologic evaluation. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a well-defined mass within the left atrium, adjacent to the interatrial septum, prompting further imaging assessment.

Cardiovascular magnetic resonance was performed for tissue characterization, demonstrating a left atrial mass with imaging features consistent with cardiac rhabdomyoma. Based on the CMR findings, a benign cardiac tumour was considered the most likely diagnosis (Figure 5). Histological confirmation was obtained. The patient underwent surgical management with favourable clinical evolution.

Case 9

A 41-year-old man was admitted to the emergency department with inferolateral ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction. Coronary angiography revealed no obstructive coronary artery disease, and the event was classified as myocardial infarction with non-obstructive coronary arteries (MINOCA). During subsequent transthoracic echocardiographic evaluation, a mass was identified within the left atrium, prompting further assessment. Cardiovascular magnetic resonance demonstrated a left atrial mass with irregular borders and heterogeneous tissue characteristics, consistent with cardiac myxoma (Figure 6; Supplementary material online, Video S4). Associated imaging findings suggested embolic coronary involvement, explaining the clinical presentation. The myxoma was surgically resected, and histopathological analysis confirmed the diagnosis. The patient was subsequently anticoagulated with apixaban every 12 h.

Discussion

Cardiac masses constitute a rare but clinically consequential entity. Autopsy series report an incidence of primary cardiac tumours between 0.001% and 0.03% with benign lesions accounting for ∼75% of cases.1 Despite their rarity, intracardiac masses detected in contemporary clinical practice pose a major diagnostic challenge.

Echocardiography remains the first-line imaging modality due to its availability and ability to assess haemodynamic consequences,2 but its capacity for tissue characterization is limited.1 In our series, transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) identified all masses but was insufficient for definitive etiologic classification in most cases.

The European Society of Cardiology guidance in cardio-oncology emphasizes the early use of advanced cardiovascular imaging to refine diagnosis and guide management when cardiac involvement is suspected in patients with cancer. In this context, when TTE is non-diagnostic or tissue characterization is required, CMR provides decisive information.3

Cardiovascular magnetic resonance is considered the reference imaging modality for cardiac mass characterization because it combines multiplanar imaging, tissue characterization, perfusion assessment, and evaluation of extracardiac extension.4 Prior studies have demonstrated diagnostic accuracies exceeding 95% for differentiating tumours from thrombi using multiparametric assessment.5,6 In our series, CMR was essential for differentiating metastatic lesions, bland thrombi, tumour-related thrombus, and benign tumours, such as myxoma and rhabdomyoma.

Tissue characterization techniques, particularly first-pass perfusion and LGE, together with clinical context were critical for diagnosis. Malignant masses typically demonstrate perfusion, heterogeneous enhancement, and infiltration of adjacent structures and are frequently associated with pericardial effusion, whereas bland thrombi characteristically lacked perfusion and enhancement.

The presence of mixed features, as observed in tumour-related thrombosis, further underscores the importance of multiparametric evaluation rather than reliance on a single imaging characteristic. Prior descriptions emphasize that vascularized tumour components may coexist with non-enhancing thrombotic material, reinforcing the necessity of comprehensive tissue assessment rather than reliance on morphology alone. Our findings are consistent with these observations.

The incremental value of multiparametric evaluation lies not in any isolated sequence but in the integration of four imaging domains: infiltration, perfusion, enhancement pattern, and clinical context.

Beyond diagnostic clarification, the prognostic implications of accurate CMR classification are substantial. Multicentre outcome data have demonstrated that patients classified by CMR as having malignant tumours or thrombus exhibit significantly higher mortality compared with those with benign tumours or pseudomasses.5 Notably, CMR classification provides incremental prognostic value beyond the presence of extracardiac malignancy or coronary artery disease. In this context, misclassification may lead to inappropriate anticoagulation, delayed chemotherapy, or unnecessary surgery, all of which carry significant clinical consequences.

In primary malignant tumours, heterogeneous peripheral enhancement with central necrosis is frequently observed, whereas highly specific patterns, such as the so-called ‘sunray appearance’, are rare and should not be considered typical.6 Recognition of enhancement patterns, therefore, requires careful interpretation within clinical context to avoid over-reliance on isolated imaging signs.

The present series underscores the practical clinical impact of early CMR integration. Imaging findings modified oncologic staging, guided systemic chemotherapy, initiated anticoagulation therapy, and optimized surgical planning. These observations support the concept that CMR plays a central role in decision-making when intracardiac masses are detected. Histological confirmation was available either from the cardiac lesion itself or from the corresponding primary malignancy in conjunction with concordant multimodality imaging findings.

Despite its high diagnostic accuracy, CMR has limitations, including reduced availability, dependence on acquisition quality and reader expertise, and challenges differentiating highly vascular benign lesions from malignant tumours.7,8,9 Therefore, imaging findings should always be interpreted within a multimodal framework, integrating clinical history, echocardiography, CT, and, when feasible, histopathology.

While previous studies have demonstrated the diagnostic utility of CMR, our series expands this knowledge by integrating multiparametric imaging features into a structured and clinically applicable framework. Importantly, we illustrate how this approach performs across a broad spectrum of cardiac masses, including benign tumours, malignant lesions, tumour-related thrombus, and bland thrombi, highlighting its impact on real-world clinical decision-making.

Conclusion

This case series highlights the broad and complex spectrum of cardiac masses and the limitations of morphology-based assessment alone. Multiparametric CMR enables reliable differentiation between malignant tumours, benign lesions, tumour-related thrombi, and bland thrombi, with important implications for staging and treatment. Early integration of CMR should be considered whenever intracardiac masses are detected, particularly in oncologic patients.
