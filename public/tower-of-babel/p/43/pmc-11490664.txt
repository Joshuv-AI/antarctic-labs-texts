Racial/ethnic disparities, artificial intelligence, and cutting-edge research: Proceedings from the 2023 Florida cardio-oncology symposium
Authors: Katelyn A Bruno, Michael G Fradley, Sherry-Ann Brown, Avirup Guha, Lakeshia Cousin, Yi Guo, Walter G O'Dell, Ashely J Smuder, Shuang Yang, Dejana Braithwaite, Carl J Pepine, Yan Gong
Published: 2024

Introduction

In March 2023, the University of Florida Health Cancer Center (UFHCC) Cardio-Oncology Working Group hosted the inaugural UF Health Cardio-Oncology Symposium, “Emerging Topics in Cardio-Oncology”. The goal of this one-day symposium was to bring together faculty, staff, and students interested in the cardio-oncology field at the University of Florida (UF) to foster a collaborative environment that will lead to new projects and grant applications in this important multidisciplinary area of study. As a result of this symposium, new collaborations would form leading to at least five grant applications (NIH R21, NIH R01, Department of Defense, or Foundation Grants) in the next three years. Another goal was to form and grow the Cardio-Oncology Working Group membership as part of the UFHCC. There were three external speakers with expertise in the cardio-oncology field and four internal UF speakers who currently focus on cardio-oncology in their respective research. Also, thirteen poster presentations were displayed during the symposium. This symposium was jointly planned by a Cardiovascular Medicine faculty member and a Pharmacy faculty member. The symposium was sponsored by the UFHCC, the Department of Pharmacotherapy & Translational Research, and the Center for Pharmacogenomics & Precision Medicine. This manuscript outlines the themes discussed at the Symposium along with some cutting-edge research being conducted at UF and by other leaders in the field.

The symposium opened with remarks from the Associate Director of the UFHCC, Dr. Dejana Braithwaite, followed by an opening presentation by Dr. Carl Pepine, a pioneer of cardio-oncology at UF. He briefly reviewed some pioneering work done over the past two decades in cardio-oncology at UF. This included the first description of aortic valve stenosis resulting from radiation therapy for Hodgkin's Disease [1] and the first cell-based therapy for anthracycline-related cardiomyopathy [2].

Dr. Michael Fradley from the University of Pennsylvania gave the first keynote presentation and set the stage for the symposium by providing an overview of the evolution of cardio-oncology. During its initial stages, the field of cardio-oncology focused primarily on heart failure associated with anthracyclines [3,4] and anti-human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) targeted therapies [5]. While these treatments are widely utilized to treat various cancers, there remains a significant gap in understanding the mechanism of cardiotoxicity, and how to prevent cardiac dysfunction. Nevertheless, a paradigm shift has occurred in the treatment of cancer in the last two decades, moving away from cytotoxic chemotherapy to treatments that harness the immune system, target aberrant cellular signaling pathways, and better deliver radiation to avoid organs at risk [6]. These novel treatments have substantially improved overall survival and progression-free survival for many different malignancies. As these therapeutics are used more widely, there is increasing recognition of various adverse cardiovascular events ranging from myocarditis to vascular toxicities to arrhythmias [6]. Looking toward the future, the increased understanding of the shared mechanisms of cardiovascular disease and cancer will likely lead to even more treatment options and potentially novel cardiotoxicities. Finally, while there is a great deal of focus on the cardiac and vascular toxicities of cancer treatments, there may be an opportunity to repurpose some therapies to treat cardiovascular disease [6]. For example, stereotactic body radiation therapy which has been used to treat various tumors has shown promise in the management of ventricular arrhythmias [7]. Imatinib which revolutionized the approach to chronic myeloid leukemia may be beneficial for pulmonary hypertension [8]. Research should be done on the use of new cardiovascular disease therapies, such as SGLT2 inhibitors, as an example, in the treatment of cancer treatment-related toxicity. Additionally, understanding mechanisms of cardio-toxicity utilizing pre-clinical studies can lead to novel drug development specifically targeting toxicity without interfering with cancer medication effectiveness. This perspective of cardio-oncology's past, present, and future served as the foundation upon which the UFHCC Cardio-Oncology symposium commenced.

The second keynote speaker, Dr. Arirup Guha from the Medical College of Georgia at Augusta University, presented his keynote address on the role of chronic toxic stress, allostatic load, and racial/ethnic disparities in prostate cancer and cardiovascular disease. Dr. Guha and his team discuss the second highest cause of death in prostate cancer is cardiovascular disease. The article discusses that chronic toxic stress increases cardiovascular disease risk and the burden is higher in black men [9]. Dr. Sherry-Ann Brown from the University of Wisconsin Madison presented the third keynote address on artificial intelligence (AI) and racial disparities in Cardio-Oncology (manuscript to appear in the same special issue). Dr. Brown discussed the integration of AI into cardiovascular medicine and a cardio-oncology practice will aid in providing personalized and precise patient care. Additionally, she showed that technology and AI integration were possible in diverse populations.

Four researchers from UF also presented their research. Dr. Walter O'Dell from the Department of Radiation Oncology described the imaging tools his lab is developing and refining to precisely quantify treatment-related changes in heart function in breast cancer patients [10]. He discussed the tools he utilizes to quantify sub-clinical changes in cardiovascular health early on in cancer treatment to predict future disease and monitor potential cardiovascular disease complications. Dr. Ashley Smuder of the UF Department of Applied Physiology and Kinesiology presented her research on doxorubicin-related effects on cardiorespiratory function and body composition using a rodent model [11]. She discussed the development of an exercise mouse model (both moderate and high-intensity training) to study mechanisms of exercise therapy for cardio-protection in breast cancer patients. Dr. Lakeshia Cousin, a faculty member in the UF College of Nursing, discussed her research focusing on the interplay of psychosocial stress, inflammation, and cardiometabolic health among black breast cancer survivors [12]. She reviewed the study design of her clinical trial titled “The Grateful Strides Toward Physical Activity and Well-Being for Black Breast Cancer Survivors, “ a culturally relevant goal-setting and exercise readiness intervention. Shuang Yang, a Ph.D. student, and Dr. Yi Guo in the UF Department of Health Outcomes and Biomedical Informatics presented their team's cardio-oncology research on the impact of frailty on the rate of adverse cardiovascular events in breast cancer patients receiving chemotherapy [13]. They found that frailty was predictive of worse cardiovascular outcomes in breast cancer patients and could be a tool for cancer patients in the future.

The symposium ended with poster presentations. A total of thirteen posters were presented by graduate students, postdoctoral fellows and/or faculty members from six different colleges. The topics ranges from basic science research focusing on the mechanisms of cardiotoxicity, to translational research focusing on identifying biomarkers for risk stratification, to using exercise for cardioprotection from cardiotoxicity [14].

The three primary themes for this year's symposium were: 1) racial/ethnic disparities, 2) artificial intelligence, and 3) innovative research. In each of these areas, speakers considered leaders in the field presented overviews of the topic and their specific research or clinical work. This special issue contains a summary of each of the speaker's presentations including a final paper containing the abstracts from the poster presentations and key takeaways from the symposium.

This inaugural symposium was a great success. A total of 65 faculty and trainees attended the symposium either in person or virtually. There was great interest and enthusiasm in the field of cardio-oncology among all who attended. A bi-annual event is anticipated.

Key points of the symposium were:

1.These conference proceedings demonstrate the highly interdisciplinary field of cardio-oncology research and highlight the need for continued focus on cardiovascular health in cancer patients. Primary directions addressed by the 2023 Cardio-Oncology Symposium and by the UFHCC Cardio-Oncology Working Group emphasized the need for continued work to address health disparities both in patient populations as well as in the development of AI algorithms to facilitate patient care. Additional attention is also needed in furthering the mechanistic understanding of cancer therapy-related adverse cardiovascular outcomes for the development of behavioral and/or pharmacological therapeutic countermeasures.
