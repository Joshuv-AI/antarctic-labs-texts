Complete chloroplast genome of Cupressus funebris and comparative analyses within Cupressaceae
Authors: Sen Cao, Zhen Zhang, Zhichun Zhou, Haizhen Zhang, Xiuli Chu, Ting Wang, Junzhe Gu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cupressus funebris Endl., a widely cultivated evergreen species in China, plays important ecological and economic roles in afforestation, ecological restoration, and the production of essential oils and medicinal compounds. However, its phylogenetic position and taxonomic status within Cupressaceae has long been debated. In this study, we sequenced and annotated the complete chloroplast genome of C. funebris and performed comparative analyses using nine additional Cupressaceae species as references to place C. funebris in a broader evolutionary context. The genome of C. funebris is 127,816 bp in length and encodes 117 genes. Comparative analyses indicated that the chloroplast genome structure of C. funebris is highly conserved relative to other Cupressaceae species, while sequence divergence was mainly concentrated in several genes, including accD, ycf1, ycf2, and rpoC2. Codon usage analysis of C. funebris revealed a clear bias toward codons ending in A or U. In addition, a total of 709 simple sequence repeats (SSRs) identified from comparative analyses provided potential molecular markers relevant to genetic studies of C. funebris. Phylogenetic analysis based on 65 complete chloroplast genomes further clarified the evolutionary placement of C. funebris within Cupressaceae and yield relationships largely consistent with traditional taxonomy. Notably, SNP-rich regions within the rpoA and rpoC2 genes were identified as informative loci for distinguishing C. funebris from closely related taxa. Overall, this study provides a comprehensive chloroplast genomic resource for C. funebris and contributes to a better understanding of its evolutionary position and taxonomic relationships within Cupressaceae.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-12660-8.Keywords: Cupressus funebris, Chloroplast, Genome, Cupressaceae, SNP

Introduction

As one of the most essential organelles, the chloroplast possesses an independent genome that encodes chloroplast-specific components [1]. In contrast to those encoded by nuclear DNA, a small proportion of chloroplast proteins are encoded by the chloroplast genome and synthesized through its own transcription and translation processes [2]. Comparative analyses of chloroplast genome structure have revealed that most chloroplast genomes are highly conserved in both gene order and gene function [3, 4], and generally exhibit a relatively stable genomic architecture [5, 6]. These stable genetic features provide valuable resources for studies in phylogenetics, DNA barcoding, taxonomy and species evolution [7–9]. Currently, chloroplast genomic data have increasingly served as valuable resources for assessing plant genetic variation, advancing conservation genetics, and facilitating the development of molecular markers [10–13].

The broad Cupressaceae family contains approximately 162 species across 32 genera with a worldwide distribution [14–16]. However, due to similarities in phenotypic traits, coupled with relictual characteristics in biogeography and paleobotany [17, 18], the classification system of the broadly defined Cupressaceae has long been subject to debate. The current broad circumscription of the family was initially revised by Gadek et al. based on a phylogenetic tree constructed using two chloroplast DNA regions (matK and rbcL) [19], and was later further supported by Li et al. through the analysis of the evolutionary rate stability of matk and rbcL using a relative rate test [20]. Between these two studies, t several additional revisions to the broad concept of Cupressaceae were proposed by Little and other researchers [21–23]. Together, these successive revisions underscore the taxonomic complexity of the family.

Cupressus funebris Endl. (syn. Callitropsis funebris), a species of the genus Cupressus L. within the family Cupressaceae, comprises evergreen tree species with a long history of cultivation in China. It was primarily distributed in southern China and was characterized by its strong ecological adaptability [24, 25]. As an important afforestation species on barren and degraded mountainous lands, C. funebris plays a significant role in ecological restoration and environmental protection. In addition, it is also regarded as a resource species due to its production of cypress essential oils, fragrances, and medicinal compounds [26]. However, the taxonomic status and species diversity of Cupressus L. have long been subjects of debate in studies of conifer systematics and evolution [27]. With advances in molecular systematics and population genetics, the circumscription of Cupressus and the number of species recognized within the genus remain contentious. Adams et al. [21] and Little [22] reclassified Cupressus L., Juniperus L., Callitropsis Oerst. and Xanthocyparis Farjon & T. H. Nguyen using nuclear gene sequences and chloroplast DNA sequences, respectively. More recently, the application of high-throughput sequencing, particularly chloroplast genome and transcriptome sequencing, have provided new insights into the phylogeny of Cupressus L [27–29]., providing improved possibilities for the identification and classification of C. funebris and related species.

In this study, the chloroplast genomes of C. funebris and nine other Cupressaceae species were sequenced. Sequence and structural variations were analyzed, and phylogenetic analyses were conducted using the chloroplast genomes of 53 previously published Cupressaceae species to clarify the phylogenetic position of C. funebris within the family. Furthermore, a set of highly polymorphic SSR and SNP molecular markers were developed for species identification and germplasm screening.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and DNA sequencing

Fresh leaf samples from 10 Cupressaceae species were collected from Hangzhou and Shanghai, China, and immediately preserved in liquid nitrogen. Species identification was conducted by Ting Wang from Hangzhou Botanical Garden and Junzhe Gu from Shanghai Botanical Garden (Table S1). In addition, chloroplast genome sequences of 53 Cupressaceae species and 2 outgroups were retrieved from NCBI for phylogenetic tree construction. Detailed information is provided in Table S2.

Total DNA was isolated from leaves (100 mg) using the modified CTAB method (Aidlab, China). DNA concentration and purity were assessed with a microspectrophotometer (NanoDrop-2000, Thermo, USA). All samples were sequenced at Tianjin Smart Genomics Corporation (Tianjin, China). In this study, the chloroplast genome was sequenced using the Illumina platform.

Chloroplast genome assembly and annotation

The chloroplast genomes were assembled from the sequencing data using the GetOrganelle software (v1.7.5) with default parameters [30], and circular chloroplast genome sequences were obtained. Assembled chloroplast genome sequences of 10 Cupressaceae species had been deposited in the NCBI Genbank database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) with the primary accession code PX355009-PX355018. Gene annotation of the chloroplast genomes was performed using CPGAVAS2 [31], and genome visualization was carried out using OGDRAW [32]. Annotation of tRNA genes was conducted with tRNAscan-SE [33], and rRNA genes were annotated using BLASTN [32]. The complete chloroplast genome sequences and annotation information for all ten species have been submitted to the GenBank public database and the accession number are shown in Table S3.

Comparative chloroplast genome and synteny analysis

Suquence similarity analysis and visualization of the chloroplast genomes of 10 Cupressaceae species were performed using the mVISTA program (https://genome.lbl.gov/vista/mvista/ submit.shtml) under the ShuffleLaGAN mode [34]. Synteny analysis of chloroplast genome sequences and genes among 10 Cupressaceae species was conducted using the Mauve software [35], and the results were visualized accordingly. Sequence alignments were performed for all commonly annotated genes present in all ten Cupressaceae species using the MUSCLE algorithm implemented in SnapGene (Ver. 6.0.2) [36]. The start and stop codons of predicted genes are manually edited using the Apollo program (v1.11.8) [37] and calculated using the EMBOSS program (v6.3.1) [38].

Repeat sequences and SSR analysis

Tandem, palindromic, forward, reverse and complementary repeats were identified in the ten Cupressaceae species chloroplast genomes by the REPuter (https://bibiserv.cebitec.uni-bielefeld.de/reputer) [39]. The Simple sequence repeats (SSRs) were detected by MISA (https://webblast.ipk-gatersleben.de/misa/) [40], with the threshold set at 10 repeat units for mononucleotides, 5 for dinucleotides, 4 for trinucleotides, and 3 for tetra-, penta-, and hexanucleotides. Dispersed and palindromic repeat sequences were also identified with REPuter, applying a maximum Hamming distance of 3, a minimum repeat length of 30 bp, and a sequence identity of no less than 90%.

Phylogenetic relationship analysis

A total of 65 chloroplast genomes, including 63 from Cupressaceae, were used for the phylogenetic analysis, including Taxus baccata and Torreya grandis as outgroups. Multiple sequence alignments were conducted using MAFFT software [41], followed by phylogenetic tree reconstruction based on shared genes using IQ-TREE [42]. The best-fitting model was GTR + F+I+G4, and bootstrap analysis was conducted with 1000 replicates. The resulting phylogenetic relationships were visualized using iTOL platform (https://itol.embl.de/) [43].

Results

Features of chloroplast organization and structure

The size of ten complete chloroplast genomes of Cupressaceae ranged from 127,074 bp (Platycladus orientalis ‘Sieboldii’) to 129,175 bp (Thujopsis dolabrata)(Fig. 1; Tables 1 and 2). Among the ten Cupressaceae species, all ten chloroplast genomes were extremely conserved in GC contents and rRNA gene number, which ranged from 34.67% ~ 35.01% and presented 4 rRNA-coding genes called rrn4.5 S, rrn5S, rrn16S and rrn23S. Besides, six species including C. funebris were extremely conserved in gene number, which presented 117 genes, including protein-coding gene, rRNA gene and tRNA gene (Table 1). Particularly, C. funebris and C. sempervirens both have 82 protein-coding gene while Chamaecyparis pisifera ‘Squarrosa’, Chamaecyparis pisifera ‘Filifera’, Chamaecyparis obtusa and Thujopsis dolabrata have an additional gene called rps16. In the tRNA-coding gene set, compared to C. funebris, ‘Squarrosa’, ‘Filifera’ and C. obtusa lack trnT-GGU, T. dolabrata lack trnP-GGG, Juniperus formosana lack trnP-GGG and trnL-CAA, while Hesperocyparis arizonica and Hesperocyparis lusitanica lack trnfM-CAU.

Comparative structural analysis of ten Cupressaceae chloroplast genomes

The chloroplast genomes of 10 Cupressaceae were compared by using the MAUVE program (Fig. 2). The collinearity analysis divided the 10 species into three groups. The structure of chloroplast genomes in C. funebris, C. sempervirens, H. arizonica and H. lusitanica are extremely similar and have good collinearity. J. formosana and P. orientalis ‘Sieboldii’ clustered together into another group, while the remaining species, including C. pisifera ‘Squarrosa’, C. pisifera ‘Filifera’, C. obtusa and T. dolabrata, also displayed good collinearity within their group.

The name corresponding to each sequence was shown in the lower left corner. The sequence with the same color represented similar regions across different species, differing only in their relative positions within the genome

In order to evaluate the sequence divergence among the ten Cupressaceae chloroplast genomes, A sequence similarity comparison using mVISTA was conducted with the plastome of C. funebris designated as the reference genome in Fig. 3. The results showed varying degrees of sequence similarity among different species, with most of the sequence divergence was mainly concentrated in the protein-coding regions, particularly in the genes such as accD, ycf1, ycf2 and rpoC2 (Fig. 3). Overall, the analyzed sequences exhibited a relatively high level of sequence divergence across the Cupressaceae family.

Codon usage analysis

The relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) values were estimated for 10 Cupressaceae species (Fig. 4). The RSCU value of this codon is equal to one if codon use is not preferred, whereas a value greater than 1 suggests that the codon is used more frequently than expected, and vice versa. In these chloroplast genomes, a total of 64 codons (including the stop codons UAA, UGA and UAG) were identified, encoding 21 amino acids involved in protein biosynthesis. The corresponding RSCU values for each codon are presented in Table S4. Statistical analysis of the codon usage revealed that among all codons, UUA (encoding leucine, Leu) was the most frequently used, with an average RSCU value of 2.051, whereas CUG (encoding Leu) was the least used, with an average RSCU value of only 0.315.

Codon composition corresponding to 21 amino acids across all protein-coding genes in the chloroplast genomes of 10 Cupressaceae species. The histogram of each amino acid indicated codon usage within Cupressaceae (from left to right: C. funebris, C. sempervirens, C. pisifera ‘Squarrosa’, C. pisifera ‘Filifera’, C. obtusa, T. dolabrata, J. formosana, P. orientalis ‘Sieboldii’, H. arizonica and H. lusitanica)

Overall, 32 codons and one stop codon (UAA) exhibited RSCU values greater than 1, among which 30 codons ended with either A or U, accounting for 93.75% of the total (Table S4). However, the codon AUA (encoding isoleucine, Ile) showed an RSCU value less than 1 only in C. obtusa, resulting in only 29 codons ending with A or U. These findings indicate that the chloroplast genomes of Cupressaceae species exhibit a clear codon usage bias, with a preference for codons ending in A or U.

Long repeats and simple sequence repeat (SSR) analysis

Five types of long repeats-tandem, palindromic, forward, reverse and complementary-were illustrated in Fig. 5. The C. obtusa possessed the highest number of long repeats, totaling 775, whereas P. orientalis ‘Sieboldii’ had the fewest, with only 247 repeats identified. On average, each species harbored approximately 417 long repeats, although substantial deviations from this mean were observed among different types. Analysis of the variation in long repeats revealed that forward repeats accounted for the highest proportion at 53.48%,while complementary repeats exhibited the lowest proportion at 5.50% (Table S5-S7).

Repeated sequences in 10 Cupressaceae chloroplast genomes

Simple Sequence Repeats (SSR) analysis revealed the presence of six major types of SSRs across the ten Cupressaceae species (Table 3). A total of 709 SSRs were identified, among which C. pisifera ‘Filifera’ exhibited the highest number, with 86 markers developed. Despite these quantitative differences, the overall composition of SSR motif types was broadly conserved across species. The motif compositions of SSRs varied among species (Table S8). Monomer repeats were the most abundant in all ten species, accounting for 36.51% to 62.67% of the total SSRs, followed by dimer repeat (11.48% ~ 38.10%), trimer repeat (5.81% ~ 19.23%) and tetramer repeat (9.26% ~ 16.18%). Moreover, Pentamer and Hexamer repeats were concurrently identified in only three species-C. pisifera ‘Squarrosa’, C. pisifera ‘Filifera’ and J. formosana-constituting merely 2.12% of all SSRs.

Phylogenetic analysis

Figure 6 shows a phylogenetic tree constructed based on shared genes from chloroplast genomes of 63 Cupressaceae species. The overall clustering results based on the chloroplast genome were largely consistent with traditional taxonomy. However, C. sempervirens from the genus Cupressus and Juniperus chinensis from the genus Juniperus were not clustered within their respective genera. 22 species in the genus Juniperus were clustered together, representing the largest cluster. C. funebris showed close genetic affinity to other Cupressus species but did not cluster within the same branch. These findings suggested that chloroplast genomes could reflect interspecific phylogenetic relationships to a certain extent and might serve as a preliminary substitute for nuclear genome data in evolutionary studies.

SNP development and species identification based on the chloroplast genomes

This study developed species-specific molecular markers based on the highly variable regions of the rpoA and rpoC2 genes of the chloroplast genomes of 10 Cupressaceae species, enabling precise taxonomic identification. In rpoA, the sequences of the 10 species exhibited 10 SNP sites in 139 bp, which could be used as molecular markers to distinguish any pair of species (Fig. 7a, a ~ j). Marker a, b, c and g were capable of differentiating the four species: C. pisifera ‘Squarrosa’, C. pisifera ‘Filifera’, H. arizonica and H. lusitanica, marker e and j could distinguish between C. sempervirens and J. formosana. While each of the marker d, f, h and i was uniquely present in C. obtusa, C. funebris, T. dolabrata and P. orientalis ‘Sieboldii’, respectively. In rpoC2, ten species could be distinguished by 8 SNPs located within a 106 bp region (Fig. 7b, a ~ h). Marker a, b and g could be jointly analyzed to distinguish among T. dolabrata, H. arizonica and H. lusitanica, while marker c and h could distinguish among C. pisifera ‘Squarrosa’, C. pisifera ‘Filifera’ and P. orientalis ‘Sieboldii’. Furthermore, marker d, e and f were uniquely present in J. formosana, C. funebris and C. obtusa, respectively.

Discussion

Chloroplast genome structure

Advances in next-generation sequencing (NGS) technologies have enabled the comprehensive assembly of complete chloroplast genomes and facilitated the development of molecular markers. In this study, the chloroplast genomes of 10 Cupressaceae species were sequenced and comparatively analyzed for structural variation, and a total of 82 shared protein-coding genes were identified. Most of these protein-coding genes have been well characterized; whereas genes with currently unknown functions(such as ycf1, ycf2, and ycf4) are also widely present in the chloroplast genomes of various Cupressaceae species. Sequence divergence analyses revealed that variations among these species were primarily associated with these genes. Although these genes are widely detected across most gymnosperms, their biological functions remains unclear.

Codon usage analysis

The codon usage frequency and bias in the chloroplast genomes of 10 Cupressaceae species were systematically analyzed using relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU). The results revealed notable differences in codon usage both among genes within the same species and across different species, although these patterns appear to be driven by distinct factors. Intraspecific variation in codon preference has been reported to correlate with gene expression levels, amino acid composition, and constraints related to protein-level noise [44]. By contrast, the interspecific codon usage differences observed in this study are likely shaped by broader influences, including overall genomic GC content [45] and environmental factors such as optimal growth temperature [46]. Notably, codon usage convergence was evident among phylogenetically related taxa or those sharing similar tRNA repertoires [44], which may underlie the pronounced bias toward A- or U- ending codons consistently observed across all 10 Cupressaceae species.

Analysis of long repeats and SSR markers

In the chloroplast genome of C. funebris, 252 long repeat pairs were detected, consisting of 53 tandem repeats, 28 palindromic repeats, 151 forward repeats, 12 reverse repeats and 8 complementary repeats (Table S5). Both the number and size range of these long repeats were similar to the other nine Cupressaceae species. SSRs are considered ideal molecular markers due to their widespread genomic distribution, high interspecific polymorphism, locus-specific co-dominance, and broad transferability [47]. Recently, the availability of chloroplast genome data generated through next-generation sequencing (NGS) has provided a robust foundation for the high-throughput screening of SSR loci, thereby facilitating the efficient development of SSR markers across a wide range of plant taxa [48, 49]. In this study, a total of 709 SSRs were identified in the chloroplast genomes of 10 Cupressaceae species (Table 3), and these loci exhibited detectable variation among species. The abundant SSR loci and potential species-specific polymorphic SSRs provide valuable genetic information and sequence resources for subsequent studies on species identification, genetic diversity assessment, and phylogenetic research in Cupressaceae [50].

Phylogenetic analysis

With the advancement of NGS technologies, chloroplast genomes have been extensively utilized to resolve plant phylogenetic relationships and elucidate evolutionary patterns [51, 52]. The phylogenetic tree revealed the evolutionary positions of the 10 Cupressaceae species and their relationships with other members of the family. The results demonstrated that the genetic relationships among most species were relatively stable, thereby providing valuable insights for refining the taxonomic framework of the Cupressaceae. It should be noted that the phylogenetic reconstruction in this study was based on genes shared among chloroplast genomes of different species, rather than on whole chloroplast genome sequences. This strategy was adopted in light of collinearity analyses performed after standardization of sequence starting positions, which demonstrated pronounced structural variation among species and consequently reduced the number of homologous regions available for reliable whole-genome alignment. Furthermore, The number of species within the genus Cupressus remains unresolved. Farjon [17] recognized a total of 15 species in Cupressus, while Little [22] considered the narrowly defined Cupressus to include only 12 species. Terry et al. [53] reconstructed the phylogenetic relationships of Cupressus based on Little’s [23] 12 species and three species proposed by Silba [54], resulting in a total of 15 taxa. For the sake of taxonomic stability, the classification system of 12 species proposed by Little [22] is still commonly adopted.

Except for C. sempervirens and J. chinensis, the clustering results based on chloroplast genome sequences were largely congruent with traditional taxonomic classifications. Recent phylogenomic studies focusing on the narrowly defined Cupressus s.s., Hesperocyparis, Callitropsis, and Xanthocyparis, as well as broader analyses across the family Cupressaceae and even gymnosperms as a whole, consistently support a sister-group relationship between Cupressus s.s. and Juniperus [15, 27, 29, 55]. This well-supported phylogenetic pattern provides a plausible explanation for the unexpected placement of C. sempervirens—the type species of Cupressus s.s.—within the Juniperus clade in our phylogenetic tree. Furthermore, the phenotype of C. sempervirens from the Shanghai Botanical Garden was re-examined to ensure accurate species identification (Fig. 8). The failure of J. chinensis to cluster within its expected genus may have another explanation. In Flora Reipublicae Popularis Sinicae, the genus Sabina Mill, with J. chinensis as its type species, was treated as a genus distinct from Juniperus L., and the taxonomic treatment proposed by Yang et al. [56] supported this view. But in Flora of China, Sabina was incorporated into the genus Juniperus, which is consistent with the taxonomic treatment proposed by Christenhusz et al. [57] and is also the classification system adopted in the construction of the phylogenetic tree. J. chinensis exhibits typical morphological characteristics of the genus Sabina, and the continuous revision of taxonomic systems may be an important reason for its unexpected clustering pattern in this study.

The rpoA and rpoC2 as a DNA marker used for 10 Cupressaceae species identification

SNP markers are short, relatively conserved fragments in the chloroplast genome that distinguish species by stable mutation sites. SNP markers are now widely utilized in various fields, including assessments of genetic diversity and population structure, species identification and classification, and population genetics studies [58–61]. Among the coding regions, the most variable genes include matK, rpoC1, rpoC2, ycf1, ycf2, ndhD and ndhF, which have also been reported as highly divergent regions in previous studies [62]. These regions represent conserved segments with clustered variations rich in SNPs and indels, commonly referred to as “mutation hotspots”. To date, the rpoC1, rpoC2 and ycf1 genes have been confirmed as mutation hotspot regions in multiple species [63–65].

Based on a preliminary comparison of 82 shared genes across the 10 species, in addition to rpoC2, rpoA was also identified as a mutation hotspot. rpoA is known to function primarily in DNA replication and transcription [66]. In Cupressaceae, the high mutability and evidence of positive selection in rpoA may have contributed to adaptive evolution during genomic replication under diverse environmental conditions [67, 68]. Further sequence alignment revealed SNP markers within rpoA and rpoC2 that can be effectively used for accurate identification of these species. This approach could also be extended to develop specific markers for other Cupressaceae species and their closely related taxa, enabling rapid and reliable identification.

Conclusion

Through comprehensive chloroplast genome analyses of ten Cupressaceae species, this study substantially enriches the chloroplast genomic resources of the family. It elucidates interspecific variations in sequence composition, structural organization, and codon usage bias. Notably, highly variable regions within the rpoA and rpoC2 genes were identified, from which multiple diagnostic SNP markers were developed, enabling accurate species discrimination. Given the important ecological role of C. funebris and its considerable economic value in the development of cypress essential oils and medicinal products, these findings provide both theoretical and practical foundations for future taxonomic, phylogenetic, and applied research in Cupressaceae.
