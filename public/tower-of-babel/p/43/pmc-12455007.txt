ALAD as a prognostic biomarker regulates metabolism and immune responses in renal cell carcinoma through multi-omics analysis
Authors: Wencheng Gong, Minghui Ge, Xiaotong Xi, Zhongyu Lu, Xing Zhang, Dongsheng Chen, Lijuan Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•ALAD expression is significantly downregulated in RCC tissues, associated with advanced tumor stages and poor prognosis, emerging as a potential prognostic biomarker.•Multi - omics analysis uncovers ALAD's connections with metabolic pathways, immune cell infiltration, and sensitivity to anti - cancer drugs, influencing the tumor immune microenvironment.•Single - cell analysis reveals specific expression patterns of ALAD across cell types, and its potential roles in metabolic and immune processes, offering novel insights for RCC treatment.Keywords: Renal cell carcinoma, ALAD, Prognosis, Tumor immune microenvironment, Multi-omics analysis

Introduction

Renal cell carcinoma (RCC), a common malignant tumor of the urinary and reproductive system arising from renal tubular epithelial cells[1], is exemplified by clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC), which is distinguished by its unique transparent cell morphology and characterized by metabolic reprogramming[2]. Previous studies have found that about one-third of patients with RCC tumor recurrence or metastasis after surgery, which is the main cause of death in patients with RCC[3]. The advent of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) has revolutionized the treatment landscape of RCC[4]. The combination of anti-angiogenic agents and immunotherapeutics has emerged as the international standard first-line treatment for advanced renal cancer[5]. Numerous clinical studies have demonstrated that, compared to monotherapy with targeted agents, this combined targeted and immunotherapy approach significantly improves the prognosis and enhances the survival of patients with advanced renal cancer[[6], [7], [8], [9], [10]]. While the synergistic effect of anti-angiogenic and immunotherapeutic agents has shown remarkable clinical benefits, challenges remain in terms of efficacy and the development of drug resistance.

The metabolic reprogramming of ccRCC is driven by a variety of gene mutations that alter metabolic pathways such as glucose metabolism, aerobic respiration, lipid metabolism, and amino acid metabolism[11]. This results in the formation of a highly adaptive metabolic network that impacts the tumor microenvironment[12]. The metabolic disturbances in ccRCC may be associated with increased immune evasion by tumor cells. During tumor cell proliferation, metabolic pathways such as glycolysis, one-carbon metabolism, the mitochondrial electron transport chain (ETC), and the tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA) provide essential energy for tumor cell growth[[13], [14]]. Heme synthesis is closely linked to these metabolic processes, and the metabolic reprogramming of tumor cells can influence heme synthesis pathways. These metabolic changes can alter the tumor microenvironment, thereby affecting immune cell infiltration and function, and may either enhance or interfere with immunotherapy based on immune checkpoint inhibitors[15]. δ-Aminolevulinic acid dehydratase (ALAD) catalyzes the second step of heme biosynthesis and functions as an endogenous inhibitor of the 26S proteasome[16]. Research has indicated that the heme synthesis pathway related to the ALAD gene influences the immune microenvironment and treatment outcomes in cancers. For example, ALAD inhibits the progression of breast cancer by regulating epithelial-mesenchymal transition mediated by transforming growth factor-β, and may be a potential biomarker for breast cancer[17]. Low expression of ALAD is related to poor prognosis with hepatocellular carcinoma[18]. However, the role of this gene in renal cancer, particularly its potential influence on the tumor microenvironment and the efficacy of combined anti-angiogenic and immunotherapy, remains to be fully elucidated.

In this study, we utilized multi-omics datasets from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), the Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC), and the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) to conduct a comprehensive analysis of the ALAD gene. We aimed to clarify its function and mechanism in the existing anti-angiogenic and immunotherapy regimens for renal cell carcinoma. This work may potentially identify novel therapeutic targets to improve the prognosis of renal cell carcinoma patients.

Methods

Data acquisition and preprocessing

Comprehensive multi-omics data for kidney renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC) were obtained from public databases for subsequent analyses. All data were preprocessed and standardized for downstream analyses to ensure consistency across datasets. Additionally, clinical information for all patients corresponding to the datasets was also downloaded for further analysis.

Bulk transcriptomic data

The TCGA-KIRC dataset (N = 607) was accessed from The Cancer Genome Atlas (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/), and the CPTAC-KIRC dataset (N = 521) was retrieved from the Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (https://pdc.cancer.gov/pdc/browse). Additionally, two GEO datasets, GSE53757[19] (N = 144) and GSE53000 [20](N = 62), were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). All datasets included tumor and paired normal samples. For TCGA and CPTAC transcriptomic data, raw read counts and FPKM values were downloaded, and FPKM values were normalized to TPM (Transcripts Per Million) for downstream analyses. GEO datasets were processed from microarray platforms (GSE53757 from GPL570 and GSE53000 from GPL6244), where probe annotations were matched to gene symbols. For genes with multiple probes, expression values were averaged.

Proteomic data

Proteomic data were retrieved from the CPTAC platform, specifically the CPTAC2 Confirmatory and CPTAC3 Discovery and Confirmatory projects. Data were obtained from TMT10 and TMT11 mass spectrometry platforms, comprising 1916 samples (683 normal and 1233 tumor), with preprocessed and normalized protein expression values provided.

Methylomic data

Methylation data from TCGA-KIRC (N = 483; 160 normal and 323 tumor samples) were generated using the Infinium Human Methylation 450 BeadChip platform. Normalized β-values were downloaded via the UCSC Xena platform[21] (https://xenabrowser.net/datapages/).

Genomic data

Genomic alterations, including somatic mutations (N = 336) and gene-level copy number variations (N = 589), were obtained from TCGA-KIRC via the UCSC Xena platform[22].

Single-cell transcriptome data

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data were obtained from the GEO dataset GSE139555[16,23], which included tumor and adjacent normal tissues from three renal cancer patients. Raw scRNA-seq data were preprocessed and transformed using the Seurat[24] R package(version 5.0.1). The NormalizeData and ScaleData functions were applied to normalize and scale the RNA TPM values. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using the FindMarkers function to compare transcriptomic differences between cells expressing ALAD and those not expressing ALAD within each cell type. Genes with an adjusted p-value 〈 0.05 and an absolute log2 fold change〉 1 were considered significantly differentially expressed. Cell types were annotated into the following 11 categories: B Cell, CD4Tconv Cell, CD8T Cell, CD8Tex Cell, DC Cell, Endothelial Cell, Mast Cell, Mono/Macro Cell, NK Cell, Plasma Cell, and Tprolif Cell. These data were preprocessed and normalized for downstream cell-type-specific analyses.

Functional enrichment analysis

To identify the biological pathways associated with the target gene list, functional enrichment analysis was performed using the enrichKEGG function from the clusterProfiler[25] R package(version 4.10.0). This method utilizes the KEGG[26](Kyoto Encyclopedia of genes and genomes) pathway database to conduct pathway enrichment based on hypergeometric testing. Pathways with a p-value < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched. Results from the enrichment analysis were visualized using dot plots to facilitate interpretation. Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network was established and visualized by the STRING[27] website (https://string-db. org/)

Metabolic signatures and tumor immune microenvironment analysis

To evaluate the relationship between ALAD expression and tumor metabolism, the “ssGSEA”[28] algorithm from the R package IOBR[29](version 0.99.0) was used to calculate enrichment scores for 114 tumor metabolism-related signatures as proposed by Smiraglia et al.[30]. For assessing immune cell infiltration, the CIBERSORT algorithm was employed to estimate the proportions of 22 common immune cell types based on transcriptomic data. Only results with a p-value < 0.05 were considered reliable. Additionally, the expression differences of eight critical effector genes associated with CD8+ T cells[31] (CD8A, GZMA, GZMB, IFNG, CXCL9, CXCL10, PRF1, TBX21) were compared between high and low ALAD expression groups to evaluate the potential impact of ALAD on CD8+ T cell activity and function.

Drug sensitivity analysis

To investigate the potential association between ALAD expression and drug sensitivity, we focused on a dataset comprising 31 renal cancer cell lines and their responses to 251 tested drugs. Drug sensitivity data were obtained from The Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer[32] (GDSC) project (https://www.cancerrxgene.org/), which provides comprehensive drug response information for over 700 cancer cell lines across approximately 75,000 experiments. Drug response metrics, including the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) and area under the dose-response curve (AUC), were calculated using the R package pRRophetic[33] (version 0.5). These metrics were used to evaluate the sensitivity of renal cancer cell lines to each drug. Correlation analyses were conducted to explore the relationship between ALAD expression and drug response, potentially identifying drugs that may be effective in targeting tumors with altered ALAD expression levels.

The summary data-based mendelian randomization

The Summary data-based Mendelian Randomization[34] (SMR) method was applied to explore the causal relationship between ALAD expression and the development of KIRC by SMR tools(version 1.03, https://cnsgenomics.com/software/smr/#Overview). Blood eQTL data were obtained from the GTEx V8[35] dataset(https://www.gtexportal.org/home/), and the KIRC genome-wide association study (GWAS) data were retrieved from the NHGRI-EBI GWAS Catalog(https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas/studies/) using the GCST90320059 cohort[36] (N = 738,319). The SMR tool was used with the following parameters:ld-upper-limit 0.9, including SNPs with high linkage disequilibrium (LD); –ld-lower-limit 0.05, excluding SNPs with insufficient LD; –peqtl-smr 5e-8, setting a stringent threshold for significant eQTLs; and –maf 0.01, filtering out rare variants. Sensitivity analysis was conducted using the HEIDI test to determine whether the observed associations were due to a single causal variant. Significant results were identified with P_SMR < 0.05 and P_HEIDI > 0.05, indicating robust associations without heterogeneity. The analysis results were visualized using an effect plot and a locus plot to facilitate interpretation.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using the R programming language(version 4.3.2). Correlation analyses were conducted using the Spearman correlation coefficient to evaluate relationships between variables. Group comparisons were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test to assess statistical differences. The diagnostic value of ALAD in RCC was evaluated by calculating the area under the curve (AUC) values across multiple cohorts using the pROC R package(version 1.18.5). Survival analyses based on overall survival data were carried out using both univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression models. Multivariate models incorporated patient clinical features, and associations were considered significant if p-value < 0.05, identifying ALAD as an independent prognostic risk factor. A nomogram model was constructed using the R package rms(version 6.7.1) to integrate ALAD expression with clinical features for prognostic prediction. Patients were stratified into high and low ALAD expression groups based on the median expression level. Survival differences between the two groups were assessed using Kaplan-Meier (K-M) survival analysis by the survival(version 3.5.7) and survminer R package(version 0.4.9) with the log-rank test, and p-values < 0.05 were considered indicative of statistically significant differences.

Result

Differential expression and genetic stability of ALAD in RCC across multi-omics data

In the TCGA and CPTAC public databases, we found that the ALAD gene is significantly downregulated at both the transcriptomic and proteomic levels in various cancer tissues. Specifically, in the TCGA-KIRC and CPTAC-KIRC datasets, the expression of the ALAD gene in tumor tissues was significantly lower than that in normal tissues (Fig. 1A–B). Similarly, in the GSE53757 and GSE53000 RCC cohorts, the expression of the ALAD gene was also significantly reduced in tumor tissues (Fig. 1C–D). To assess the potential of the ALAD gene in diagnosing RCC, we used receiver operating characteristic curves to determine its diagnostic capability (Fig. 1E). The results showed that the AUC values for the ALAD gene were very high (>0.94), indicating that ALAD has a good diagnostic potential for RCC. At the protein level, the expression of the ALAD gene in tumor tissues was significantly downregulated compared to normal tissues (Fig. 1F). Further analysis of the correlation between the transcriptomic and proteomic expression of the ALAD gene in normal and RCC tumor tissues (Fig.1G–H) revealed a high correlation in normal tissues, but a poor correlation in RCC tumor tissues, suggesting that the translational regulation of this gene may be affected. Additionally, we analyzed the methylation expression differences in different TSS regions between normal and RCC tumor tissues and found that the methylation levels in tumor tissues were significantly higher than those in normal tissues (Fig. I-J), indicating that the gene regulatory mechanisms in tumor cells have been altered.

Prognostic significance of ALAD expression in RCC across multi-omics data

To investigate the association between ALAD gene expression levels and the clinical parameters and patient prognosis of RCC, we used three cohorts: GSE53757, TCGA-KIRC, and CPTAC-KIRC. The Kruskal-Wallis test revealed a significant correlation between ALAD gene expression and pathological stage, with lower expression levels observed in advanced stages (Fig. 2A–C). In the proteomic analysis of the CPTAC-KIRC cohort, ALAD protein expression was notably reduced in M1 stage and high-grade T3&T4 tumors (Fig. 2D–E), indicating that decreased ALAD expression is associated with invasive characteristics of renal cancer. Furthermore, by dividing patients into high-expression and low-expression groups based on the median ALAD gene expression across TCGA transcriptome, CPTAC transcriptome, and CPTAC proteome cohorts, Kaplan-Meier survival analysis demonstrated that RCC patients with low ALAD expression had significantly shorter survival times compared to those with high ALAD expression (Fig. 2F–H). This suggests that ALAD gene expression levels are closely linked to patient prognosis. Additionally, we developed Nomogram models based on both transcriptional (Fig. 2I) and protein expression (Fig. 2L) of the ALAD gene to predict RCC patient survival status. Calibration curves indicated that these models exhibit good predictive accuracy (Fig. 2J–K& 2M-N), further supporting the potential of the ALAD gene as a prognostic marker.

Expression patterns and cell type specificity of ALAD in RCC

To reveal the expression patterns of ALAD in different cell types and its potential functions in tumor tissues, we analyzed the single-cell RNA-seq data of the GSE139555 cohort. Fig. 3A presents a UMAP plot illustrating distinct clustering of cell types, with normal and tumor tissues showing differential distributions. With assessment, 16 different major cell types were identified. Next, we compared the expression levels of ALAD in various cell types between normal tissues and tumor tissues (Fig. 3B). Unexpectedly, ALAD expression levels were largely non-significant (ns) across most cell types. Subsequently, we analyzed the proportion of cells expressing ALAD in normal tissues and tumor tissues. Notably, certain cell types, such as endothelial cells and tprolif cells, had a higher proportion of ALAD expression in both normal and tumor tissues(Fig. 3C–D). However, there was no statistically significant difference. Next, we selected four types of cells with the highest expression of the ALAD gene, DC, endothelial, mast, and plasma cells, to analyze their differentially expressed genes (DEPs)(Fig. 3E). We found that cells expressing ALAD have higher ANKFY1 expression (Fig. 3F), suggesting a possible relationship or co-regulation between these two genes in the cellular context. To further explore the biological functions of the ALAD gene in three cell populations (Fig. 3G–I), we found that the ALAD gene is involved in multiple metabolic pathways, including tyrosine metabolism and carbon metabolism, indicating that ALAD has potential functions in various metabolic processes. Subsequently, we analyzed genes related to antigen presentation (ap_sigs), co-stimulation, and effector molecules in four cell types (DCs, endothelial cells, mast cells, and plasma cells) comparing ALAD-positive and ALAD-negative cells. The results revealed significant differences in effector molecules such as OXT and SEMA3D in mast cells, as well as in antigen presentation-related genes including PDCD1LG2 in plasma cells. These molecules were almost exclusively expressed in ALAD-positive cells (Fig. 3J). Overall, these data reveal the expression patterns of ALAD across various cell types and its potential functions in normal and tumor tissues, particularly in metabolic pathways and immune cell functions.

Next, we performed KEGG pathway enrichment analyses to present the significantly upregulated and downregulated genes, respectively. Notably, several metabolic pathways, including Carbon metabolism was significantly enriched among the upregulated genes (Fig. 4C). Conversely, downregulated genes are enriched in pathways such as Parathyroid hormone synthesis, secretion and action, Insulin resistance and Axon guidance (Fig. 4D), highlighting the intricate metabolic rewiring associated with ALAD expression in RCC. Subsequently, we displayed a heatmap illustrating the Spearman rank correlation coefficients between ALAD expression and a set of highly correlated genes across multiple datasets. This analysis reveals a network of genes that are consistently co-expressed with ALAD, suggesting shared regulatory mechanisms or functional interdependencies (Fig. 4E). Proceeding from the initial findings, we explored the protein-protein interaction network of genes highly correlated with ALAD expression. This network analysis underscores ALAD's potential involvement in complex molecular pathways through its interactions with various proteins (Fig. 4F), potentially influencing cellular processes such as metabolism and signal transduction. Building upon the correlation analysis, we further examine the KEGG pathway enrichment among genes highly correlated with ALAD expression (Fig. 4G). Next, we analyzed a comprehensive heatmap of 43 metabolic signatures that are significantly altered between ALAD high-expression and low-expression groups in both TCGA-KIRC and CPTAC-KIRC cohorts (Fig. 4H), and revealed distinct metabolic profiles associated with ALAD expression levels. Thereafter, we delved into the relationship between ALAD expression and immune cell infiltration within tumor tissues (Fig. 4I). The results showed significant positive correlations with several immune-related genes. Finally, we compared the expression levels of eight CD8+ T cell effector genes—CD8A, GZMA, GZMB, IFNG, CXCL9, CXCL10, PRF1, and TBX21—between ALAD high-expression and low-expression groups (Fig. 4J). These data indicate that ALAD expression is positively correlated with the expression of these effector genes, suggesting a link between ALAD and CD8+ T cell-mediated immune responses in the tumor context.

SMR effects of ALAD expression in blood

We have conducted a multidimensional in-depth analysis of the pivotal role of the ALAD gene in RCC. To elucidate the causal relationship between the ALAD gene and RCC, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of RCC eQTL data, providing mechanistic insights into renal cancer development. Our findings revealed a significant correlation between the eQTL effect of ALAD and its GWAS effect, with ALAD expression being negatively correlated with RCC (Fig. 5A). Furthermore, we generated a locus plot that clearly delineates the genomic region surrounding ALAD and its associated signals, highlighting its association with RCC (Fig. 5B), suggesting a potential causal link that warrants further investigation.

ALAD expression association with drug sensitivity in RCC

To provide new targets and strategies for precision therapy in renal cancer, we investigated the correlation between ALAD gene expression and drug sensitivity in RCC cell lines, aiming to explore the potential value of the ALAD gene in the diagnosis and treatment of renal cancer. We analyzed the expression levels of ALAD in 31 RCC cell lines and their sensitivity to 251 drugs. Based on the drug sensitivity indicators IC50 and ROC, we observed 19 drugs that showed strong sensitivity in RCC cell lines (Fig. 6A). Among these, BMS-509,744, BX-795, and PD-0332,991 exhibited a strong positive correlation with ALAD expression (Fig. 6B–D). These associations suggested exploratory links that functional validation is required.

Discussion

Recent advancements in targeted therapy and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have significantly improved outcomes for advanced RCC[[37], [38]]. Combination strategies, including ICI-tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) combinations are now first-line standards, with large phase III trials demonstrating superiority over TKI monotherapy[[39], [40], [41], [42]]. However, response rates to current regimens (including ICI + anti-angiogenic agents or bispecific antibodies) remain limited, with only a subset of patients deriving significant benefit[[43], [44]]. Consequently, identifying prognostic biomarkers and tumor microenvironment (TME) features is critical for optimizing therapeutic strategies. In this study, we demonstrate that ALAD may serve as a prognostic biomarker and potentially a therapeutic target.

ALAD participates in metabolic processes[45], and its low expression significantly enhances tumor cell proliferation and invasion[[17], [18]]. Utilizing transcriptomic, proteomic, and single-cell data from TCGA, CPTAC, and GEO databases, we propose δ-aminolevulinate dehydratase (ALAD) as a novel potential prognostic biomarker in RCC. We observed marked downregulation of ALAD at both mRNA and protein levels in RCC versus normal tissue, intensifying with advanced stage. Concurrently, ALAD methylation levels were elevated in RCC. Hypermethylation-induced epigenetic silencing has been linked to tumor progression, enhanced ccRCC invasiveness, ICI resistance, and poor outcomes[46]. Consistent with this, RCC patients with low ALAD expression exhibited worse prognosis in our study, supporting its value as a prognostic predictor. Thus, ALAD serves as an independent prognostic biomarker, with low expression significantly correlating with high invasiveness and unfavorable prognosis. Our findings align with prior studies in breast [17] and liver cancer [18], where ALAD downregulation similarly correlated with advanced disease stage, metabolic dysregulation, and immunosuppression.

Single-cell RNA-seq analysis showed variable ALAD expression patterns across cell types. While statistically non-significant in most populations, endothelial cells and proliferating T cells (tprolif) exhibited relatively higher ALAD expression in both tissue types, suggesting a potential role in their core functions such as metabolism or proliferation[16]. This preliminary observation identifies cell subtypes for further investigation; however, functional roles require experimental validation. We further demonstrated ALAD's involvement in RCC metabolic reprogramming[47]. “Metabolic reprogramming” is one of the key molecular pathological features of ccRCC. It is currently believed that some key enzymes and transporters involved in glycolytic reprogramming may potentially serve as therapeutic targets for the future treatment of ccRCC[48]. A series of drugs targeting these novel targets is currently under development. It’s association with key metabolic pathways. Its co-expression network, including interaction with the inflammation/immunity-related gene ANXA1[[49], [50]], and immune infiltration analysis indicated that ALAD expression correlates with specific metabolic signatures and immune cell infiltration patterns, suggesting it may influence the immune microenvironment via metabolic modulation – processes known to be interconnected[47,51]. In the majority of cell types, the proportion of ALAD+ cells was lower in tumor samples than in normal samples, with the most pronounced decreases observed in endothelial, plasma, mast, and dendritic (DC) cells, covering both immune and non-immune cell populations. The expression of three genes (OXT, SEMA3D, and PDCD1LG2) were observed in ALAD-positive cells, all of which are associated with immunosuppression[[52], [53], [54], [55]]. These findings suggest that ALAD may exert functions in both immune and non-immune cells, and its association with immune infiltration (e.g., CD8+ T cells) likely reflects a combined tumor microenvironment effect arising from ALAD downregulation across multiple cell types, including endothelial, plasma, DC, and mast cells, which indicates ALAD+ immune cells display distinct activation and functional states in the tumor context.

Importantly, ALAD expression positively correlated with CD8+ T cell effector genes like IFNG and GZMB[[56], [57]], implying a role in modulating CD8+ T cell-mediated anti-tumor immunity. ALAD is a key enzyme downstream of 5-aminolevulinic acid (5-ALA) in the heme biosynthesis pathway, and its activity directly dictates the flux of heme precursors as well as the profile of downstream metabolites. Recent study[58] further indicates that showed that 5-ALA enhances the activation and function of CD8+ T cells in a mouse colitis model. This raised ALAD may influence T cell function not only indirectly via tumor metabolism but also through metabolic intermediates like 5-ALA that can directly regulate immune cells. This provides new insights into differential responses to immunotherapy such as immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) and suggests that low ALAD expression may be associated with ICI resistance, potentially serving as a biomarker for selecting more appropriate therapies including immunotherapy combinations. Stratifying patients based on their ALAD expression levels may identify those with low ALAD expression as potential candidates for intensified therapeutic regimens or dual-immunotherapy approaches.

The limitations of the study include the comprehensiveness of information sourced from databases, with a lack of more detailed clinical data. The results were derived through computational analysis, which can only establish correlations, while causality still needs to be verified through experiments. The regulated mechanisms of ALAD, such as whether the mediation of immune effects is due to the direct effect of ALAD within immune cells or an indirect effect following tumor cell metabolic remodeling, as well as the drug sensitivity results, will validate through in vitro/in vivo functional experiments. In addition, as a biomarker with potential for clinical translation, the prognostic value of ALAD in RCC and its role in risk stratification need to be rigorously assessed and validated in further large-scale prospective clinical trials.

Conclusion

This study identified ALAD as a significant prognostic biomarker associated with RCC progression. Its expression level is intricately associated with tumor progression, metabolic reprogramming, and modulation of the immune microenvironment. Further investigation into the functional mechanisms and therapeutic potential of ALAD is anticipated to provide a robust theoretical foundation for developing more effective treatment strategies, ultimately enhancing the prognosis of RCC patients.
