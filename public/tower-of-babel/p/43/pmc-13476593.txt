Lateral Ulnar Collateral Ligament Reconstruction With Positive Pivot Shift Test
Authors: Matthew J. Schultz, William DiCiurcio, Hunter Smith, Tyler Radack, Luke S. Austin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Posterolateral rotatory instability of the elbow (PLRI) results from incompetence of the lateral ulnar collateral ligament (LUCL), part of the lateral elbow ligamentous complex. A pivot shift test is a sensitive and specific intraoperative maneuver to assess for PLRI. While several LUCL repair and reconstruction techniques have been described, ligament reconstruction remains the gold standard treatment.Indications:LUCL reconstruction is indicated for active patients with symptomatic PLRI whose conservative treatment has failed. Patients classically present with pain and mechanical symptoms, often during active elbow extension, supination, and valgus loading, or with maneuvers such as pushing up from a chair. While the pivot shift test has poor sensitivity in awake patients, it is useful as an examination under anesthesia. This case involves a patient with PLRI and a positive pivot shift test under anesthesia.Technique:We present a video-based technique guide of LUCL allograft reconstruction utilizing a docking technique at the humerus with transosseous ulnar tunnels. The patient was positioned supine with the arm on a hand table. The lateral elbow was approached through a Kocher interval. A semi-tendinosis allograft was passed through transosseous ulnar tunnels at the supinator crest, docked into the humerus at the isometric point, and secured over a bone bridge. A postreconstruction pivot shift test confirmed stability of the elbow.Results:LUCL reconstruction with ulnar tunnels reliably improves pain and restores elbow stability. In this case, the pivot-shift test was eliminated after graft fixation, and the patient's symptoms resolved postoperatively. This technique restores native LUCL biomechanics and durable elbow stability.Discussion/Conclusion:LUCL reconstruction with ulnar transosseous tunnels is a reproducible technique for addressing posterolateral rotatory instability. The pivot-shift test is a valuable intraoperative tool for assessing PLRI. This case and video may serve as a useful tool for orthopaedic surgeons implementing biomechanically sound allograft reconstruction of the LUCL via transosseous tunnels.Patient Consent Disclosure Statement:The author(s) attests that consent has been obtained from any patient(s) appearing in this publication. Background and Indications

Posterolateral rotary instability, also known as posterolateral rotatory instability of the elbow (PLRI), results from incompetence of the lateral ulnar collateral ligament (LUCL).1,4,5 It classically presents with pain and mechanical symptoms and feelings of instability with maneuvers like pushing up from a chair and loading the joint. The best test to evaluate it is the pivot-shift test. It is sensitive and specific, but usually requires the patient to be sedated as they can guard against pain. 6 I will present a video showing the technique for LUCL reconstruction with semi-tendinosis allograft, tunnels through the ulna, and docking in the humerus.

This is a case of a 34-year-old female bartender with right elbow pain and instability after a fall at work. She was treated with 2 months of physical therapy, but continued to have symptoms, mostly of instability. She was actually able to demonstrate a pivot-shift test on her own. She had a full range of motion, a positive tabletop test, and a positive pivot-shift test. Indications for surgery were continued instability and failure of conservative treatments.

Radiographs reveal normal bony architecture. Magnetic resonance imaging showed an LUCL rupture with bruising of the radiocapitellar joint.

Technique Description

To perform a pivot shift test, you lay the patient supine, you extend the elbow, you place an axial load and valgus force, and you put the forearm in supination. In doing so, as you flex the elbow, the radial head can sublux posteriorly, which is a positive pivot shift test, as you see in this demonstration.

You can then peel the musculature off the top of the lateral ulna collateral ligament, which will expose what is left of the ligament below it. This will take you down to the ulna. Results and Discussion

It is important to understand that there are different approaches and techniques for this repair. There are repairs and reconstructions, and there are different grafts. It is very important that you put your ulnar attachment distally at its anatomic attachment point, as too proximally can lead to instability, and also that you place your humeral tunnel at the isometric point.

I will splint the patients for 2 weeks and then bring them back to the office. At that point, they will start range-of-motion exercises, but no strengthening with a 1 pound weight restriction. I do use a hinged elbow brace that opens fully for 6 weeks. We remove the brace at that point and can start strengthening with a 10 pound weight restriction. At 3 months, we allow them to use the arm as tolerated with no weight restrictions. However, I will not start participating in contact sports until 4 months after the procedure.

There are good to excellent outcomes with this procedure, with 85% achieving elbow stability and 93% achieving functional range of motion. 1 The complication rate is variable, but fairly low, and there is really no good evidence on what graft type to use.2,3
