m⁶A-Modified hsa_circ_0002694 facilitates OSCC progression via the METTL14/YTHDC1/miR-616-3p/c-Myc axis
Authors: Lina Liu, Bo Xu, Xin Leng, Yixuan Li, Jun Zhao, Jie Wu, Jiayin Deng, Wensheng Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) is an aggressive malignancy with poor prognosis and limited therapeutic options. N6-methyladenosine (m⁶A) modification has emerged as a key regulator of RNA fate, including circular RNAs (circRNAs), yet the functional contribution of m⁶A-modified circRNAs to OSCC remains incompletely defined. Here, we characterized hsa_circ_0002694, a previously unannotated circRNA, and investigated its m⁶A-associated regulatory mechanism in OSCC. We found that hsa_circ_0002694 was markedly upregulated in OSCC tissues and cell lines. Loss-of-function assays showed that hsa_circ_0002694 silencing inhibited OSCC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion in vitro and suppressed xenograft tumor growth in vivo. Mechanistically, METTL14-dependent m⁶A modification supported hsa_circ_0002694 abundance and enabled recognition/association by the nuclear m⁶A reader YTHDC1, contributing to its stability and nuclear retention/distribution. In the cytoplasm, hsa_circ_0002694 functioned as a competing endogenous RNA by sequestering miR-616-3p, thereby de-repressing METTL14 and reinforcing a putative positive feedback loop. In parallel, METTL14 increased m⁶A enrichment on MYC transcripts and, together with the m⁶A reader YTHDF1, promoted c-Myc protein expression, linking the circRNA circuit to an established oncogenic driver in OSCC. RNA immunoprecipitation, MeRIP-PCR, and RNA pull-down assays supported the m⁶A-associated regulatory relationships among hsa_circ_0002694, METTL14, YTHDC1, and miR-616-3p. Collectively, our findings define an m⁶A-regulated hsa_circ_0002694-centered network that connects METTL14/YTHDC1 control of circRNA fate with miR-616-3p-mediated de-repression and METTL14/YTHDF1-dependent c-Myc upregulation, providing mechanistic insight into epitranscriptomic circRNA regulation in OSCC and suggesting hsa_circ_0002694 as a potential biomarker and therapeutic target.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13005-026-00598-x.

Introduction

Oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) is one of the most common malignancies of the head and neck and is characterized by aggressive local invasion, frequent lymph node metastasis, and a high rate of recurrence, leading to poor clinical outcomes [1–3]. Despite advances in surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy, the 5-year survival rate for OSCC remains unsatisfactory, largely due to recurrence and metastasis [4, 5]. Therefore, identifying robust biomarkers and actionable therapeutic targets is essential for improving early detection, prognostic stratification, and treatment decisions.

Circular RNAs (circRNAs) are a distinct class of noncoding RNAs with covalently closed-loop structures lacking 5′ caps and 3′ poly(A) tails, which confers strong resistance to exonuclease-mediated degradation and contributes to their high stability [6, 7]. Growing evidence indicates that circRNAs participate in tumor initiation and progression, in part by acting as competitive endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs) that modulate microRNA (miRNA) availability and downstream gene expression [8, 9]. In OSCC, circRNA_100290 promotes cell proliferation and invasion by sequestering miR-29 family members and increasing CDK6 expression [10]. Similarly, circUHRF1 contributes to OSCC progression and epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) through ceRNA-mediated regulation of multiple miRNAs [11]. However, despite expanding functional evidence, the upstream mechanisms that govern circRNA stability, subcellular distribution, and functional output in OSCC remain incompletely understood.

Epitranscriptomic regulation, particularly N6-methyladenosine (m⁶A), has emerged as a major layer of post-transcriptional control influencing RNA splicing, nuclear export, stability, translation, and localization [12, 13]. m⁶A marks are installed by the METTL3/METTL14 methyltransferase complex (writers) [14, 15], removed by demethylases such as FTO and ALKBH5 (erasers) [16], and interpreted by reader proteins including YTHDC1 and the YTHDF family [17, 18]. Importantly, circRNAs can also undergo m⁶A modification, which has been linked to altered circRNA fate and function, including stability, localization, and regulatory capacity [19]. For instance, m⁶A-modified circNSUN2 exhibits enhanced stability and facilitates colorectal cancer metastasis by promoting HMGA2 mRNA stabilization [20]. Recent work further supports a broader paradigm in which m⁶A contributes to circRNA “fate decisions,” while ceRNA-based regulatory wiring represents a common functional framework in cancer biology [21, 22]. Nevertheless, how these regulatory layers converge on specific circRNAs to shape OSCC progression remains poorly defined.

In the present study, we experimentally identified hsa_circ_0002694, derived from exons 3–5 of the UHRF1 gene, as being upregulated in OSCC tissues and cell lines. Bioinformatic analyses suggested the presence of putative m⁶A consensus motifs within hsa_circ_0002694, raising the possibility that its stability and subcellular distribution may be influenced by m⁶A-associated regulators. Given the established oncogenic role of c-Myc in OSCC progression [23, 24] and emerging evidence supporting a tumor-suppressive role for miR-616-3p [25], we hypothesized that hsa_circ_0002694 may participate in OSCC tumorigenesis through an m⁶A-associated ceRNA mechanism. Specifically, m⁶A modification and reader-mediated recognition could shape the localization and availability of hsa_circ_0002694, enabling it to modulate the miR-616-3p/c-Myc axis.

In this study, we aimed to elucidate the biological functions of hsa_circ_0002694 in OSCC and to dissect the underlying molecular framework linking m⁶A regulation to ceRNA-mediated oncogenic signaling. We focused on evaluating how METTL14-mediated m⁶A modification and YTHDC1 recognition may influence the stability, nuclear–cytoplasmic distribution, and downstream regulatory capacity of hsa_circ_0002694 in the context of the miR-616-3p/c-Myc axis. Our findings provide insights into the m⁶A–circRNA regulatory network in OSCC and suggest that hsa_circ_0002694 may represent a potential biomarker and therapeutic target for this malignancy.

Materials and methods

Detailed information regarding all reagents and instruments used in this study is provided in Table S1 (Reagents and instruments used).

Cell culture and maintenance

The human oral keratinocyte cell line (HOK), oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) cell lines (SCC15, SCC25, and CAL27), and the head and neck squamous cell carcinoma cell line FaDu were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). All cell lines were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 IU/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin, and maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂.

Cells were passaged at approximately 90% confluence using 0.25% trypsin and reseeded at a dilution ratio of 1:4. For cryopreservation, cells were resuspended in freezing medium consisting of 90% fetal bovine serum and 10% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), followed by stepwise cooling at 4 °C, − 20 °C, and − 80 °C prior to long-term storage in liquid nitrogen.

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from cultured cells using TRIzol reagent, followed by column-based purification involving chloroform extraction and ethanol precipitation. RNA concentration and purity were assessed spectrophotometrically. Genomic DNA contamination was removed using DNase I, and reverse transcription was subsequently performed using MuLV reverse transcriptase with random hexamer primers.

The resulting complementary DNA (cDNA) was used as the template for quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) using SYBR Fast qPCR Master Mix. The amplification program consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 2 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 20 s. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH serving as the internal reference gene.

To specifically detect circular RNA, divergent primers spanning the back-splice junction were used. All primer sequences are provided in Table S2 (qPCR primer sequences).

Stable ShRNA cell line construction

To establish oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) cell lines with stable knockdown of hsa_circ_0002694, three short hairpin RNA (shRNA) oligonucleotides targeting the mature sequence of hsa_circ_0002694 were designed and cloned into the pLKO.1-TRC vector using AgeI and EcoRI restriction sites. Recombinant plasmids were verified by restriction enzyme digestion and Sanger sequencing.

Lentiviral particles were generated by co-transfecting HEK-293T cells with the shRNA expression plasmids and packaging plasmids. Viral supernatants were harvested at 48 and 72 h post-transfection and subsequently filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane filter. SCC15 and CAL27 cells were infected with the lentiviral supernatants in the presence of 8 µg/mL polybrene, followed by selection with 1 µg/mL puromycin for 7 days to establish stable cell lines.

Knockdown efficiency of hsa_circ_0002694 was confirmed by RT-qPCR using divergent primers specific for the back-splice junction, while infection efficiency was evaluated by fluorescence microscopy. Detailed reaction components and conditions used during cell line construction are provided in Table S3 (shRNA oligonucleotide sequences) through Table S9 (double enzyme digestion system for plasmid identification).

Sanger sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from SCC15 cells and reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA). The resulting cDNA was submitted to Synbio Technologies (Suzhou, China) for Sanger sequencing to verify the back-splice junction of hsa_circ_0002694.

RNA fluorescence in situ hybridization (RNA-FISH)

RNA fluorescence in situ hybridization (RNA-FISH) was performed to examine the subcellular localization of hsa_circ_0002694, its colocalization with miR-616-3p, and its spatial association with RNA methyltransferase components in OSCC cells. Cells were seeded onto coverslips and cultured to approximately 30–50% confluence. After fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature, cells were permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS at 4 °C for 5 min. Pre-hybridization was then conducted at 37 °C for 30 min.

Hybridization was carried out overnight at 37 °C in hybridization buffer containing 5 µL of fluorescently labeled probes, including a Cy3-labeled probe targeting hsa_circ_0002694, a FITC-labeled probe specific for miR-616-3p, and additional probes targeting METTL3 and METTL14, where indicated. Following hybridization, cells were washed using graded saline–sodium citrate (SSC) buffers at 42 °C and subsequently counterstained with DAPI to visualize nuclei. Fluorescent signals were captured using a fluorescence microscope.

RNase R digestion assay

Total RNA was extracted from cultured cells and quantified using a NanoDrop 3000 spectrophotometer. For RNase R digestion, 2 µg of total RNA was incubated with RNase R, RNase inhibitor, and 10× RNase R reaction buffer in a final volume of 20 µL at 37 °C for 30 min. A parallel control reaction was performed under identical conditions in the absence of RNase R. Following digestion, RNA was purified using the miRNeasy Mini Kit. Reverse transcription and subsequent RT-qPCR were performed to evaluate the resistance of transcripts to RNase R digestion.

Actinomycin D chase assay

To assess RNA stability, OSCC cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 3 × 10⁵ cells per well and cultured for 24 h. Cells in the first well were harvested at the 0 h time point without actinomycin D treatment and immediately lysed in TRIzol reagent. The remaining wells were treated with actinomycin D at a final concentration of 10 µg/mL. Total RNA was extracted at 4, 8, 12, and 24 h after treatment using TRIzol reagent.

The relative abundance of hsa_circ_0002694 and its corresponding linear transcripts was quantified by RT-qPCR and normalized to GAPDH. RNA decay kinetics were analyzed over time to evaluate transcript stability.

Nuclear–cytoplasmic fractionation

To examine the subcellular distribution of hsa_circ_0002694, nuclear–cytoplasmic RNA fractionation was performed using the Cytoplasmic & Nuclear RNA Purification Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. OSCC cells were harvested and lysed on ice with cytoplasmic lysis buffer, followed by centrifugation at 4 °C to separate the cytoplasmic supernatant from the nuclear pellet. RNA from each fraction was subsequently purified using column-based extraction.

RT-qPCR analysis was conducted to determine the distribution of hsa_circ_0002694 between nuclear and cytoplasmic compartments, with GAPDH and U6 serving as cytoplasmic and nuclear reference markers, respectively. To evaluate the potential involvement of YTHDC1 in regulating the subcellular localization of hsa_circ_0002694, SCC15 and CAL27 cells were transfected with siRNAs targeting YTHDC1 or a non-targeting control for 48 h prior to fractionation. The relative abundance of hsa_circ_0002694 in nuclear and cytoplasmic fractions was then quantified.

The composition of the nuclear fractionation buffer is provided in Table S10 (Nuclear fractionation buffer composition).

Cell proliferation assay (CCK-8)

Cell proliferation was evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay. OSCC cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 10³ cells per well in 100 µL of complete medium and maintained under standard culture conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂). At the indicated time points (0, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h), 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well and incubated for 3 h. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Each time point was assessed in triplicate, and all experiments were independently repeated three times.

Cell migration and invasion assays

The effects of hsa_circ_0002694 on OSCC cell motility were assessed using wound-healing and Transwell assays. For the wound-healing assay, SCC15 and CAL27 cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 6 × 10⁵ cells per well and cultured to full confluence. A linear wound was created across the cell monolayer using a sterile 10 µL pipette tip, and detached cells were removed by washing with PBS. Cells were then cultured in serum-free medium, and images were captured at 0, 12, 24, and 48 h using an inverted microscope. Wound closure was quantified using ImageJ software.

For Transwell migration assays, 5 × 10⁴ cells suspended in 200 µL of serum-free medium were seeded into the upper chamber of Transwell inserts (8 μm pore size), while 600 µL of medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum was added to the lower chamber. For invasion assays, the upper chamber membrane was pre-coated with Matrigel diluted 1:8 in serum-free medium and incubated at 37 °C for 3 h prior to cell seeding. After 24 h of incubation, non-migrated or non-invaded cells were removed from the upper surface of the membrane. Cells that had migrated or invaded to the lower surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 1% crystal violet. Five random fields per well were imaged and counted under a light microscope at 200× magnification.

Bioinformatic prediction and target analysis

Putative microRNAs (miRNAs) potentially interacting with hsa_circ_0002694 were predicted using multiple online databases, including TargetScan, miRanda, and miRDB. Annotation information for hsa_circ_0002694 was obtained from circBase. Predicted circRNA–miRNA interactions were further evaluated using CircInteractome and starBase, while miRNA–mRNA target relationships were predicted using TargetScan.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

A dual-luciferase reporter assay was performed to functionally evaluate the predicted regulatory relationship between miR-616-3p and its target sequences. Wild-type (WT) and mutant (MUT) fragments of the METTL14 3′ untranslated region (3′UTR), as well as fragments of hsa_circ_0002694 containing the putative miR-616-3p binding site, were cloned into luciferase reporter plasmids. These constructs, together with a Renilla luciferase plasmid as an internal control, were co-transfected into SCC15 and CAL27 cells.

Cells were harvested 24–48 h after transfection, and luciferase activity was measured using a dual-luciferase assay system according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase activity, and relative luciferase activity was expressed as the ratio of firefly to Renilla signals. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

RNA pull-down assay

An RNA pull-down assay was performed to examine the association of hsa_circ_0002694 with candidate microRNAs and proteins. A biotin-labeled RNA probe spanning the back-splice junction of hsa_circ_0002694 was synthesized by in vitro transcription using T7 RNA polymerase in the presence of a biotin RNA labeling mix. The RNA probe was denatured at 90 °C for 2 min, immediately chilled on ice, and subsequently refolded in RNA structure buffer at room temperature.

The refolded RNA probe was incubated with pre-cleared OSCC cell lysates supplemented with RNase and protease inhibitors at 4 °C for 1 h. Streptavidin-coated magnetic beads were then added and incubated for an additional 1 h to capture RNA-associated complexes. After extensive washing, bound RNAs and proteins were eluted separately. The enrichment of miR-616-3p was analyzed by RT-qPCR, while associated proteins, including METTL3, METTL14, and YTHDC1, were examined by Western blotting. A scrambled RNA probe was used in parallel as a negative control.

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP)

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assays were conducted to assess the association between RNA-binding proteins and target RNAs in OSCC cells. Cells were lysed in RIP buffer supplemented with RNase and protease inhibitors, and the resulting supernatants were incubated overnight at 4 °C with magnetic beads conjugated to antibodies against METTL14 or YTHDC1. Rabbit IgG was used as a negative control.

Following extensive washing, co-immunoprecipitated RNAs were extracted using phenol–chloroform followed by ethanol precipitation. The enrichment of hsa_circ_0002694, miR-616-3p, and c-Myc in the immunoprecipitates was quantified by RT-qPCR, with input samples used for normalization. The composition of the RIP buffer is provided in Table S11 (RIP buffer composition).

Methylated RNA immunoprecipitation (MeRIP-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent, and RNA integrity was verified by agarose gel electrophoresis. RNA fragmentation was performed at 70 °C using the fragmentation buffer provided in the MeRIP kit. Fragmented RNA was incubated with an anti-m⁶A antibody in 1× immunoprecipitation (IP) buffer, followed by immunoprecipitation using magnetic beads pre-conjugated with the antibody.

After incubation and extensive washing, the m⁶A-enriched RNA was eluted and purified using magnetic separation columns. The enrichment of specific transcripts was analyzed by RT-qPCR. Input RNA was used for normalization, and relative enrichment was calculated using the ΔCt method.

Western blot analysis

Total protein was extracted from cultured cells using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay. Equal amounts of protein (10 µg per lane) were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes.

Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature and subsequently incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against METTL14, YTHDF1, c-Myc, or GAPDH. After washing, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagents and captured with a chemiluminescence imaging system. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software, and protein expression levels were normalized to GAPDH.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays were performed to examine the association between METTL3 and METTL14. CAL27 and SCC15 cells were lysed in pre-chilled immunoprecipitation (IP) buffer and incubated on ice for 30 min. After centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C, the supernatants were incubated with 4 µg of anti-METTL3 or anti-METTL14 antibodies for 2 h at 4 °C, followed by incubation with Protein A/G agarose beads under gentle rotation overnight at 4 °C.

After three washes with IP buffer, the immunoprecipitated complexes were eluted by boiling in SDS loading buffer. Eluted proteins were subjected to SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes for subsequent Western blot analysis to detect co-precipitated METTL3 and METTL14.

Xenograft tumor model in nude mice

To evaluate the in vivo tumorigenic potential of hsa_circ_0002694, CAL27 and SCC15 cells stably expressing control shRNA or shRNA targeting hsa_circ_0002694 were harvested during the logarithmic growth phase, washed, and resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Approximately 1–5 × 10⁶ cells were subcutaneously injected into the dorsal axillary region of 4–6-week-old BALB/c nude mice.

Tumor growth was monitored every three days by measuring tumor length and width with calipers, and tumor volume was calculated using the formula: (length × width²)/2. After three weeks, mice were euthanized, and tumors were excised and weighed. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Tianjin Medical University and were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines and regulations.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were independently repeated at least three times. Quantitative data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance between two groups was assessed using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test, while comparisons among three or more groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. All statistical analyses and graphical representations were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Hsa_circ_0002694 is upregulated, cytoplasmically enriched, and highly stable in OSCC cells

Quantitative RT-PCR analysis demonstrated that hsa_circ_0002694 expression was significantly increased in all examined oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) cell lines (SCC15, SCC25, and CAL27) as well as in the hypopharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma cell line FaDu, compared with normal human oral keratinocytes (HOK). Among these cell lines, SCC15 and CAL27 exhibited the highest expression levels (p < 0.0001; Fig. 1A).

Based on genomic annotation (chr19:4929233–4932968) and NCBI reference data, hsa_circ_0002694 is derived from exons 3–5 of the UHRF1 gene and generated through canonical back-splicing, resulting in a circular transcript of 632 nucleotides (Fig. 1B). Sanger sequencing further confirmed the presence of a head-to-tail back-splice junction, consistent with the characteristic structure of circular RNAs (Fig. 1C).

The subcellular localization of hsa_circ_0002694 was examined by RNA fluorescence in situ hybridization (RNA-FISH) in SCC15 and CAL27 cells. hsa_circ_0002694 signals were predominantly detected in the cytoplasm, with limited nuclear distribution, indicating a primarily cytoplasmic localization pattern (Fig. 1D).

To further characterize its circular nature and stability, RNase R digestion assays were performed. hsa_circ_0002694 exhibited marked resistance to exonuclease-mediated degradation, whereas the corresponding linear UHRF1 mRNA was efficiently degraded (Fig. 1E). In addition, actinomycin D chase assays revealed that hsa_circ_0002694 displayed greater transcript stability than its linear counterpart, maintaining relatively higher expression levels over a 24-hour period following transcriptional inhibition (Fig. 1F).

Collectively, these results indicate that hsa_circ_0002694 is a stable, cytoplasmically enriched circRNA that is upregulated in OSCC cells. Based on its expression pattern, SCC15 and CAL27 cells were selected for subsequent mechanistic analyses.

Silencing hsa_circ_0002694 suppresses OSCC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion in vitro

To investigate the functional role of hsa_circ_0002694 in oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC), three independent short hairpin RNAs (shRNA-1, shRNA-2, and shRNA-3) targeting the back-splice junction of hsa_circ_0002694 were constructed and introduced into SCC15 and CAL27 cells. Quantitative RT-PCR analysis confirmed a significant reduction in hsa_circ_0002694 expression in all shRNA-transduced groups compared with the negative control (sh-NC), with shRNA-1 exhibiting the highest knockdown efficiency (p < 0.0001; Fig. 2A). Notably, the expression level of the corresponding linear UHRF1 transcript was not significantly altered across groups, indicating the specificity of circRNA silencing (Fig. 2B).

Functional assays were subsequently performed to assess the impact of hsa_circ_0002694 depletion on OSCC cell behavior. CCK-8 assays demonstrated that knockdown of hsa_circ_0002694 significantly inhibited cell proliferation in both SCC15 and CAL27 cells in a time-dependent manner (Fig. 2C). Consistently, wound-healing assays revealed a marked reduction in migratory capacity following hsa_circ_0002694 silencing (Fig. 2D–E).

In agreement with these observations, Transwell migration and invasion assays showed that suppression of hsa_circ_0002694 substantially impaired the migratory and invasive abilities of OSCC cells, with shRNA-1 again producing the most pronounced inhibitory effects (Fig. 2F–G).

Collectively, these findings indicate that hsa_circ_0002694 contributes to the proliferative, migratory, and invasive phenotypes of OSCC cells in vitro.

Knockdown of hsa_circ_0002694 attenuates OSCC tumor growth in vivo

To further evaluate the functional significance of hsa_circ_0002694 in vivo, a xenograft tumor model was established by subcutaneously injecting SCC15 cells stably expressing shRNA targeting hsa_circ_0002694 (sh-hsa_circ_0002694) or a negative control (sh-NC) into BALB/c nude mice. During the 21-day observation period, tumors derived from hsa_circ_0002694-depleted cells exhibited significantly reduced growth compared with those in the control group (Fig. 3A–B).

Quantitative analysis of tumor volume revealed a marked suppression of tumor expansion in the sh-hsa_circ_0002694 group, with a significant difference emerging from day 9 post-injection and becoming more pronounced over time (Fig. 3C). At the experimental endpoint, tumors excised from mice injected with sh-hsa_circ_0002694 cells displayed a significantly lower average weight than those from the sh-NC group (p < 0.01; Fig. 3D).

Collectively, these in vivo findings are consistent with the in vitro observations and indicate that depletion of hsa_circ_0002694 restricts OSCC tumor growth in a xenograft model.

hsa_circ_0002694 functions as a sponge for miR-616-3p and modulates METTL14 expression

To investigate the potential molecular mechanism of hsa_circ_0002694 in OSCC, integrated bioinformatic analyses were first performed to predict miRNAs potentially associated with hsa_circ_0002694. A total of 22 candidate miRNAs were identified by Circular RNA Interactome, each harboring one predicted binding site on hsa_circ_0002694 (Fig. 4A; Supplementary Table S12). Among these candidates, miR-616-3p was prioritized based on RNA expression profiling following hsa_circ_0002694 knockdown, which revealed a significant increase in miR-616-3p abundance upon circRNA silencing (Fig. 4B).

To functionally evaluate the association between hsa_circ_0002694 and miR-616-3p, dual-luciferase reporter assays were performed. Transfection of miR-616-3p mimics significantly reduced luciferase activity driven by reporters containing the wild-type hsa_circ_0002694 sequence, whereas mutation of the predicted seed region abolished this effect (Fig. 4C). In parallel, RNA fluorescence in situ hybridization (RNA-FISH) analysis demonstrated predominant cytoplasmic colocalization of hsa_circ_0002694 and miR-616-3p in SCC15 and CAL27 cells (Fig. 4D), supporting their spatial proximity and the feasibility of post-transcriptional regulation.

Consistent with these observations, qRT-PCR analysis showed that miR-616-3p expression was significantly downregulated in OSCC cell lines compared with normal human oral keratinocytes (HOK) (Fig. 4E). Moreover, knockdown of hsa_circ_0002694 resulted in a marked upregulation of miR-616-3p in both SCC15 and CAL27 cells (Fig. 4F), in line with a competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) regulatory model.

Next, potential downstream targets of miR-616-3p were explored with a particular focus on m⁶A-related genes. TargetScan 8.0 analysis identified several m⁶A-associated factors as putative targets of miR-616-3p, among which METTL14 harbored two predicted binding sites with higher-confidence seed matches (Supplementary Table S13). Dual-luciferase assays confirmed that miR-616-3p mimics significantly suppressed the activity of a reporter containing the wild-type METTL14 3′UTR, whereas no significant effect was observed with the mutant construct (Fig. 4G). Baseline expression analysis further demonstrated that METTL14 mRNA levels were elevated in OSCC cell lines relative to HOK cells (Fig. 4H). In accordance with these results, overexpression of miR-616-3p led to a pronounced reduction in METTL14 mRNA and protein expression, while inhibition of miR-616-3p resulted in increased METTL14 levels in OSCC cells (Fig. 4I–K).

To further determine whether hsa_circ_0002694 regulates METTL14 expression through a miR-616-3p–dependent mechanism, rescue experiments were performed. Silencing of hsa_circ_0002694 significantly reduced METTL14 expression, whereas co-transfection with miR-616-3p inhibitors partially restored METTL14 mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 4L–N).

Collectively, these findings indicate that hsa_circ_0002694 may function as a miRNA sponge for miR-616-3p, thereby modulating METTL14 expression in OSCC cells.

METTL14 and YTHDF1 mediate m⁶A-dependent regulation of c-Myc protein expression in OSCC cells

To determine whether c-Myc transcripts are associated with METTL14-mediated m⁶A modification, RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assays were performed using anti-METTL14 antibodies in SCC15 and CAL27 cells. c-Myc mRNA was significantly enriched in METTL14 immunoprecipitates compared with IgG controls (Fig. 5A), indicating an association between METTL14 and c-Myc transcripts. Consistently, Western blot analysis revealed that knockdown of METTL14 markedly reduced c-Myc protein levels in both SCC15 and CAL27 cells (Fig. 5B–C). In parallel, MeRIP-qPCR assays demonstrated a significant decrease in m⁶A enrichment on c-Myc transcripts following METTL14 depletion (Fig. 5D), supporting a role for METTL14 in modulating the m⁶A status of c-Myc mRNA.

We next examined whether YTHDF1, an m⁶A reader protein implicated in translational regulation, participates in the regulation of c-Myc expression. RIP assays using anti-YTHDF1 antibodies showed significant enrichment of c-Myc transcripts relative to IgG controls in both SCC15 and CAL27 cells (Fig. 5E). Consistent with this association, knockdown of YTHDF1 led to a pronounced reduction in c-Myc protein expression, as determined by Western blot analysis (Fig. 5F), with quantitative densitometry confirming significant decreases in both cell lines (Fig. 5G).

To further substantiate the involvement of YTHDF1, gain- and loss-of-function experiments were performed. Western blot analysis showed that depletion of YTHDF1 suppressed c-Myc protein expression, whereas overexpression of YTHDF1 partially restored or enhanced c-Myc levels (Fig. 5H). Densitometric analyses demonstrated consistent changes following YTHDF1 knockdown in SCC15 cells (Fig. 5I) and YTHDF1 overexpression in CAL27 cells (Fig. 5J).

Taken together, these results indicate that METTL14-mediated m⁶A modification and YTHDF1-dependent recognition are associated with the regulation of c-Myc protein expression in OSCC cells.

hsa_circ_0002694 is associated with the METTL3–METTL14 complex and is recognized by the m⁶A reader YTHDC1

To explore the potential association between hsa_circ_0002694 and the m⁶A methyltransferase complex, RNA pull-down assays followed by Western blot analysis were performed. Both METTL3 and METTL14 proteins were enriched in complexes pulled down by the hsa_circ_0002694-specific probe compared with the scrambled control probe in SCC15 and CAL27 cells (Fig. 6A), indicating an association between hsa_circ_0002694 and components of the methyltransferase complex. Consistently, RNA-FISH combined with immunofluorescence staining revealed cytoplasmic colocalization of hsa_circ_0002694 with METTL3 and METTL14 proteins in OSCC cells (Fig. 6B), providing spatial support for their association.

Western blot analysis showed that overexpression of hsa_circ_0002694 was accompanied by an increase in METTL14 protein levels in both SCC15 and CAL27 cells, whereas METTL3 expression remained largely unchanged (Fig. 6C–D). To further assess whether hsa_circ_0002694 influences the association between METTL3 and METTL14, co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays were performed using anti-METTL3 antibodies under hsa_circ_0002694 overexpression conditions. An increased level of METTL14 was detected in METTL3 immunoprecipitates compared with control conditions (Fig. 6E), suggesting enhanced METTL14 co-precipitation with METTL3 under hsa_circ_0002694 overexpression.

To evaluate the m⁶A status of hsa_circ_0002694, MeRIP-PCR assays were conducted. Knockdown of METTL14 resulted in a significant reduction in m⁶A enrichment on hsa_circ_0002694 (Fig. 6F), indicating that METTL14 contributes to the m⁶A modification of hsa_circ_0002694.

To identify RNA-binding proteins potentially associated with hsa_circ_0002694, additional RNA pull-down assays were performed using biotin-labeled hsa_circ_0002694 probes. Western blot analysis revealed enrichment of the m⁶A reader protein YTHDC1 in the pull-down complexes (Fig. 6G). This association was further supported by RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assays, which demonstrated significant enrichment of hsa_circ_0002694 in YTHDC1 immunoprecipitates compared with IgG controls (Fig. 6H).

Finally, subcellular fractionation analyses demonstrated that depletion of YTHDC1 resulted in a marked reduction of hsa_circ_0002694 in the nuclear fraction. Importantly, forced overexpression of METTL14 failed to restore the nuclear retention of hsa_circ_0002694 under YTHDC1-depleted conditions (Fig. 6I). This rescue experiment indicates that the nuclear–cytoplasmic transport of hsa_circ_0002694 is subject to the control of YTHDC1, which functions as a regulatory factor independent of METTL14.

Collectively, these results indicate that hsa_circ_0002694 is associated with components of the METTL3–METTL14 methyltransferase complex and is recognized by the m⁶A reader YTHDC1, providing mechanistic insights into the regulation of hsa_circ_0002694 in OSCC cells.

Discussion

In this study, we identified hsa_circ_0002694, a previously uncharacterized circular RNA, as significantly upregulated in OSCC tissues and cell lines. Functionally, silencing hsa_circ_0002694 suppressed OSCC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion in vitro and in vivo, supporting its involvement in OSCC progression. Mechanistically, our data support an m⁶A-associated regulatory framework in which hsa_circ_0002694 is modulated by the METTL14/YTHDC1 machinery and participates in post-transcriptional regulation of c-Myc through miR-616-3p. These findings integrate circRNA methylation, ceRNA regulation, and oncogene control, providing additional insight into OSCC tumorigenesis.

Our data showed that hsa_circ_0002694 is highly stable, resistant to RNase R digestion, and maintains elevated stability following transcriptional inhibition, aligning with known circRNA features [26]. Furthermore, RNA-FISH assays revealed predominant cytoplasmic distribution with partial nuclear localization. Nuclear circRNAs commonly associate with nuclear proteins or undergo epigenetic modifications, influencing gene expression or alternative splicing processes [27–29]. In this context, accumulating evidence indicates that m⁶A can shape circRNA fate decisions—particularly stability and subcellular distribution—across multiple cancer settings [21, 30, 31]. Consistent with this concept, our MeRIP-PCR and RNA pull-down assays supported the presence of m⁶A marks on hsa_circ_0002694 and its association with METTL14-containing methyltransferase components. Knockdown of METTL14 significantly reduced m⁶A enrichment on hsa_circ_0002694, supporting a predominant role of METTL14 in mediating this modification, consistent with previous reports on tumor-associated circRNAs [32–34].

We further observed that METTL14 and METTL3 form a co-associated m⁶A writer complex in OSCC cells based on Co-IP analyses. Notably, the METTL3–METTL14 heterodimer has been well characterized as the functional core m⁶A writer complex in previous studies, in which METTL14 serves primarily as a structural and RNA-binding component rather than a determinant of METTL3 protein stability [15]. In our study, Co-IP assays performed under hsa_circ_0002694 overexpression conditions showed an increased METTL14 signal in METTL3 immunoprecipitates, indicating enhanced METTL14 co-precipitation with METTL3 under these conditions. However, given that METTL14 expression itself is influenced by the hsa_circ_0002694/miR-616-3p ceRNA module, these Co-IP findings should be interpreted as changes in co-precipitation readouts rather than direct evidence of altered complex assembly or intrinsic stability.

RIP assays also demonstrated enrichment of hsa_circ_0002694 in YTHDC1 immunoprecipitates, supporting recognition by this nuclear m⁶A reader. Subsequent fractionation analyses showed that YTHDC1 depletion reduced the nuclear fraction of hsa_circ_0002694, and METTL14 overexpression did not restore this phenotype, suggesting that YTHDC1 contributes to circRNA nuclear localization in a manner not rescued by increased writer abundance. Notably, methodologically similar RNA stability/localization assays have been applied in OSCC and related epithelial tumor contexts to support reader-dependent RNA fate regulation [35]. Together, these observations support a coordinated METTL14–YTHDC1 axis that regulates the m⁶A status and nuclear localization of hsa_circ_0002694, consistent with reader-dependent regulation reported for other circRNAs [36–38].

Beyond epitranscriptomic regulation, hsa_circ_0002694 participates in miRNA-mediated gene control. Emerging studies suggest that the nuclear retention and stability of circRNAs can critically influence their downstream functions, including miRNA regulation and associated signaling pathways [39]. In our study, multiple lines of evidence—including dual-luciferase reporter assays, RNA pull-down, and RNA-FISH colocalization—support a ceRNA-like mechanism in which hsa_circ_0002694 functionally associates with miR-616-3p, consistent with a broader circRNA–ceRNA regulatory paradigm in cancer [22]. miR-616-3p has been reported as a tumor-suppressive miRNA in several cancers, where it modulates oncogenic processes such as proliferation and metastasis [40]. Rescue analyses further supported the functional relevance of the hsa_circ_0002694/miR-616-3p module, as inhibition of miR-616-3p partially restored METTL14 expression in hsa_circ_0002694-silenced cells. This ceRNA-mediated regulation provides a mechanistic bridge between circRNA-associated miRNA sequestration and downstream m⁶A-dependent oncogenic signaling. Importantly, c-Myc was selected as a downstream effector in this study based on mechanistic prioritization rather than bioinformatic convenience. c-Myc is a well-established oncogenic transcription factor in OSCC, critically involved in tumor proliferation, invasion, and malignant progression, and is consistently associated with poor clinical outcomes [41, 42]. Moreover, c-Myc has been reported as an important functional readout of METTL14-mediated m⁶A regulation in multiple contexts, providing a mechanistically coherent readout within our METTL14/YTHDC1-associated framework [43, 44]. While additional downstream targets and pathways may also contribute to hsa_circ_0002694-driven phenotypes, we focused on c-Myc as a biologically high-impact and mechanism-consistent representative effector to establish a coherent regulatory axis.

Taken together, we propose a mechanistic model (Fig. 7) in which METTL14-mediated m⁶A modification enhances the stability of hsa_circ_0002694 and facilitates its recognition by YTHDC1. This modification-dependent process may influence the nuclear–cytoplasmic distribution of hsa_circ_0002694, thereby enabling its subsequent association with cytoplasmic miRNAs. Specifically, cytoplasmic hsa_circ_0002694 acts as a competing endogenous RNA by modulating miR-616-3p availability, thereby relieving miRNA-mediated repression of c-Myc expression [25]. Importantly, these observations support a putative positive regulatory loop: hsa_circ_0002694 increases METTL14 expression via ceRNA regulation, while METTL14 in turn promotes the m⁶A status and functional availability of hsa_circ_0002694, collectively sustaining oncogenic signaling. This multilayered model aligns with growing evidence highlighting the functional complexity and regulatory versatility of m⁶A-modified circRNAs in tumorigenesis [45, 46], while the temporal dynamics and relative contributions of each layer warrant further investigation.

Our findings may have potential translational implications. Due to their intrinsic stability, sequence conservation, and tumor-associated expression patterns, circRNAs such as hsa_circ_0002694 hold potential as diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers [47–49]. Moreover, the m⁶A-associated regulatory architecture involving METTL14/YTHDC1 recognition and ceRNA-like control suggests multiple therapeutic entry points, including suppressing circRNA expression, disrupting circRNA–miRNA regulatory relationships, or modulating m⁶A machinery to restrain OSCC progression [50, 51].

This study has several limitations. First, although the functional relevance of hsa_circ_0002694 was validated in cell lines and xenograft models, its clinical significance requires confirmation in larger, well-annotated OSCC cohorts. Second, the precise m⁶A modification sites on hsa_circ_0002694 were not mapped in this study; high-resolution approaches such as m⁶A-seq or miCLIP will be necessary to define site-specific modifications and clarify their functional consequences [52, 53]. In addition, although our data support an m⁶A-associated regulatory framework linking hsa_circ_0002694 to METTL14/YTHDC1 and downstream c-Myc regulation, the present study does not establish strict m⁶A dependency for all functional effects of hsa_circ_0002694. Further studies employing targeted epitranscriptomic perturbation strategies will be required to dissect the relative contributions of m⁶A-dependent and m⁶A-independent mechanisms.

In summary, our study identifies hsa_circ_0002694 as a novel m⁶A-modified circRNA involved in OSCC progression. Through coordinated regulation by METTL14 and YTHDC1, hsa_circ_0002694 participates in a ceRNA network that modulates miR-616-3p availability and c-Myc expression, and may form a positive regulatory loop sustaining oncogenic signaling. These findings broaden the current understanding of circRNA-mediated post-transcriptional regulation and underscore the functional complexity of m⁶A-modified circRNAs in OSCC.
