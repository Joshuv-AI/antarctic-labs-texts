Metabolic and functional pathways of gut microbiota in patients with gastric cancer
Authors: Ryo Matoba, Hiroshi Iijima, Yasuhiro Sakamoto, Ryohei Kawabata, Atsushi Ishiguro, Yusuke Akamaru, Yosuke Kito, Masaki Aizawa, Jin Matsuyama, Masazumi Takahashi, Akitaka Makiyama, Takahisa Suzuki, Masahiro Tsuda, Hisateru Yasui, Jun Hihara, Hiroyuki Okuda, Junji Kawada, Takashi Yoshioka, Hisato Kawakami, Takako Eguchi Nakajima, Kei Muro, Wataru Ichikawa, Masashi Fujii, Yu Sunakawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We analysed the differences in bacterial composition between 475 Japanese patients with advanced gastric cancer (median age, 70 years; median BMI 20.0) and 106 healthy individuals using a comprehensive metagenome shotgun analysis. Among the patients with advanced gastric cancer, 71% were male, 37% had relapsed, and 55.5% previously underwent gastrectomy. Bifidobacterium, Anaerostipes, and Parabacteroides were predominant in healthy individuals, whereas Streptococcus, Lactobacillus, and Odoribacter were predominant in patients with advanced gastric cancer. Additionally, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathway analysis showed that butanoate and pyruvate metabolism was enriched in healthy individuals, whereas factors, such as ABC transporters and ribosomes, were enriched in patients with advanced gastric cancer. Cluster analysis broadly classified patients with advanced gastric cancer and healthy individuals into two clusters; however, clustering using pathway data more clearly classified patients with advanced gastric cancer and healthy individuals than clustering using flora analysis. Moreover, healthy individuals showed higher bacterial flora diversity than those with advanced gastric cancer. Although the dataset we used was limited and may be difficult to generalise, we identified some molecular characteristics and functional pathways of the microbial genera within the intestines of patients with advanced gastric cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47830-x.

Introduction

Recently, microbiome research and development has been gaining momentum worldwide, and is expected to be a promising tool for understanding “personalized medicine”, as represented by the Precision Medicine Initiative. Additionally, microbiome analysis has become an important tool for monitoring the state of health before illness occurs1,2.

Analysis of gut microbiota is closely related to autoimmunity. Proteins, metabolites, and other substances produced by gut bacteria affect immune cells in the gut, which strongly influence autoimmunity3–7. Therefore, it may be possible to predict individual health conditions by examining gut microbiota. Moreover, specific intestinal microbiota and their metabolites are correlated with immunotherapy efficacy8–11.

Furthermore, recent advances in NGS technology have resulted in large amounts of sequencing data that can be obtained simultaneously. Microbiome sequencing can be performed in two ways: 16S ribosomal RNA analysis for the identification of bacterial species and metagenome shotgun analysis to obtain a large amount of random sequencing data. Although the metagenome shotgun method requires a large amount of sequence data, it provides information on bacterial species, diversity, non-bacterial (e.g. viruses and eukaryotic microorganisms) information, and functional data, such as metabolic enzymes from the sequence information12,13.

This study aimed to identify patient-specific microbiome markers in patients with gastric cancer by performing a comprehensive metagenome shotgun analysis of the intestinal microbiota of patients with gastric cancer and comparing to faecal microbiome data from healthy individuals.

Results

Patient with cancer and health characteristics

Overall, 501 patients were enrolled in the DELIVER trial (Japan Clinical Cancer Research Organization [JACCRO] GC-08) from 67 institutes between March 2018 and August 2019. Of these, samples from 487 patients were available for clinical data and biomarker analysis, and 475 faecal samples were available for genome shotgun sequencing. Metagenomic data were obtained for each sample with a minimum of 5 Gbp and an average mapping rate of 98.2%. The same metagenomic data for 106 healthy individuals were analysed from clinical data and FASTQ files, using the database from a previously published paper14. Profiling of differences between patient with cancers and healthy individuals

We performed a comparative analysis between patients with gastric cancer and healthy individuals using genus data, for whom more than 80% of the data were available. Although unclear, the results showed a tendency for healthy individuals and patients with GC to be divided into several clusters (Fig. 2a, Supplemental Table 3, and Supplemental Fig. 1). Subsequently, we compared the genera between patients with gastric cancer and healthy controls. Bifidobacterium, Anaerostipes, and Parabacteroides were significantly more abundant in healthy individuals (p < 0.005) that those in patients with cancer. Specific bacteria and functional pathway of patients with cancer

Next, a similar analysis was performed, focusing on functional pathways, with 234 pathways for which expressed data were available for more than 80% of the samples (Supplemental Table 4). Cluster analysis was performed using the expression values of these pathways. The expression patterns of the pathways clearly distinguished healthy individuals from patients with gastric cancer (Fig. 3a, Supplemental Table 5, and Supplemental Fig. 2), compared to the analysis using the genus. This implies that the functional pathways in healthy individuals differ from those in patients with gastric cancer. Additionally, clinical information on patients with gastric cancer, such as ECOG performance score, diagnosis status, gastrectomy status, and Helicobacter pylori infection status, was analysed for its association with the gut microbiota. Although Eubacterium and Streptococcus were associated with ECOG status, Lactobacillus and Veillonella with diagnosis status, and Veillonella, Alistipes, and ribosome of the pathway with gastrectomy, there were no definite differences in the expression levels (Supplemental Fig. 3). Notably, Streptococcus, Lactobacillus, Alistipes, and Bifidobacterium were more abundant in patients infected with H. pylori; however, the difference was not statistically significant.

ROC (Receiver Operating Characteristic) analysis was performed for 10 candidate markers. The results provided AUC values ranging from 1 to 0.611 (Figs. 2c and 3c), and the sensitivity for distinguishing patient with cancers from healthy individuals ranged from 40.8% to 100%, whereas the specificity ranged from 64.2 to 100% (Supplemental Table 6).

To confirm the confounding clinical factors, we examined whether these markers reproduced differences based on diagnosis, gastrectomy status, PS status, and the presence or absence of H. pylori infection. Most markers showed similar significant differences between healthy individuals and patients with gastric cancer, regardless of these clinical factors, with the exception of the Parabacteroides marker, which varied with H. pylori infection status (Supplemental Fig. 4).

Analysis of age-sex matched pair samples

Although differences were identified in the faecal samples from patients with gastric cancer and healthy controls, our analyses showed that the age distributions of patients with gastric cancer and healthy controls were different. Therefore, we examined whether the markers obtained would remain significant when the 73 patients with gastric cancer and 24 healthy individuals were matched for age and sex (Table 2). All six genera and four pathway markers were statistically significant and showed different expression profiles in the faeces of patients with gastric cancer and healthy individuals (Fig. 4a, b). Discussion

This study aimed to identify candidates for patient-specific microbiome markers in patients with gastric cancer and found that genes related to the butanoate and pyruvate metabolic pathways were downregulated in patients with gastric cancer. Butyrate is a typical short-chain fatty acid implicated in the immunity and inflammatory response for gastric cancer15–17. Pyruvic acid is a key component of the metabolic pathway and has been implicated in cancer growth18,19. Based on the current data, we identified pathways as surrogate markers. However, the association between the butyrate/pyruvate pathway and gastric cancer has not been elucidated. Furthermore, ribosome synthesis activation indicates changes in the ribosomal protein gene copy number and abnormal expression, suggesting that translational activity is in an abnormal state.

Our analysis revealed that Anaerostipes and Bifidobacterium were significantly more abundant in healthy individuals, whereas Odoribacter, Lactobacillus, and Streptococcus were significantly more abundant in patients with cancer. Anaerostipes and Bifidobacterium were more common in healthy individuals than in patients with colorectal cancer20.Odoribacter, of the Bacteroidales order, is a common short-chain fatty acid-producing member of the human intestinal microbiota21. Decreased abundance of Odoribacter is related to microbiota-associated diseases, such as non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, cystic fibrosis, and inflammatory bowel disease22. Although these results are contradictory, recent studies reported that Odoribacter has positive effects, indicating that both positive and negative effects may be present9.

We analysed whether there was any correlation between the gut microbiome and clinicopathological characteristics. Several genera and pathways were significantly related. Lactobacillus was more abundant in patients with ‘advanced’ than in those with ‘relapsed’ cancer, and Veillonella was more abundant in patients with ‘relapsed’ than in those with ‘advanced’ in cancer. Veillonella was also abundant in patients with a previous gastrectomy, which may be related to the salivary microbiome23.

Currently, 16S rRNA analysis, a method for classifying and identifying bacteria at the species level, is the mainstream method for microbiome analysis. However, metagenomic analysis, which analyses all genomic DNA-encoding genes, is expected to become crucial in the coming years. Although metagenomic analysis is time-consuming and costly, it can provide information on both phylogenetic and functional composition. Thus, it is possible to analyse the functional genetic information of the microbiome using metagenomic analysis.

The healthy control data used in this analysis lacked information on gastrectomy status, H. pylori infection, and antibiotic use history. Therefore, the results of this analysis cannot be generalised. However, by grouping patients with gastric cancer based on their clinical information regarding gastrectomy and H. pylori infection status, and comparing them with healthy controls, we observed similar reproducibility for the nine genera and pathways.

The association between the efficacy of cancer immunotherapy and intestinal microbiota has been established, suggesting that microbiota analysis could play an important role in the selection of specific treatment modalities. Although data acquisition has just begun, microbiome testing should be incorporated into health check-ups in the near future.

Conclusion

We identified the molecular characteristics of microbial genera and functional pathways in the gut of patients with advanced gastric cancer. Bifidobacterium and Anaerostipes were predominant in healthy individuals, whereas Streptococcus, Lactobacillus, and Odoribacter were predominant in patients with advanced gastric cancer. Furthermore, pathway analysis showed that butanoate and pyruvate metabolism was enriched in healthy individuals, whereas factors, such as ABC transporters and ribosomes, were enriched in patients with advanced gastric cancer. Cluster analysis broadly classified patients with advanced gastric cancer and healthy individuals. Clustering using pathway data enabled a clearer classification of patients with advanced gastric cancer and healthy individuals than clustering using flora analysis. This analysis represents the characteristics of a specific group of patients with gastric cancer, and therefore provides limited results. To generalise these findings, data from a larger number of patients with gastric cancer is necessary.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

Patients with advanced or recurrent unresectable gastric or gastroesophageal junction cancer that was histologically confirmed to be adenocarcinoma, were enrolled in this study. Faecal samples were collected by patients before treatment with nivolumab (in the DELIVER trial) at home, immediately after evacuation, using scoop collection tubes containing 3 mL of GuSCN solution from the TechnoSuruga Laboratory24.

DNA extraction and metagenome shotgun sequencing

Genomic information in faecal samples was measured using genome shotgun sequencing.

We extracted DNA from the faeces of patients with advanced gastric cancer enrolled in an observational/translational study, the DELIVER trial (JACCRO GC-08: UMIN000030850), which evaluated the clinical outcomes of nivolumab and developed host-related biomarkers for nivolumab in 500 patients with advanced gastric cancer.

The metagenomic DNA was extracted using zirconia beads added to the specimens. The bacteria were crushed (5 m/s for 2 min), purified, and eluted using magnetic beads24. DNA quality was assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis, and DNA concentration was confirmed using a NanoPhotometer spectrophotometre (IMPLEN, CA, USA) and Qubit 2.0 Flurometer (Life Technologies, CA, USA). Sequence libraries were prepared using the NEBNext Ultra DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, MA, USA) using 1 µg of genomic DNA. The AMPure XP system was used for library purification, and library quality was evaluated using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Sequencing was performed on a NovaSeq6000 (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) with 150-bp paired ends.

Data analysis

Genome annotation, bacterial strain assignment, function assignment, and metabolic pathway investigations were performed using custom pipelines implemented in Bash and Python. This pipeline includes steps for the quality control, trimming, assembly, and annotation of metagenomic reads. The data of healthy individuals and patients with cancer were analysed using an identical pipeline with FASTQ files as the starting point.

The quality control step involved assessing the quality of raw reads before trimming using FastQC (version 0.11.8; www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc). The adapter sequences and low-quality bases were trimmed from the reads using TrimGalore (version 0.6.3; www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/trim_galore/).

Contaminant removal included the removal of human reads using the BMTagger tool (version 3.101, ftp://ftp.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pub/agarwala/bmtagger/) to ensure the analysis focused on microbial content. Thereafter, the cleaned reads were assembled de novo using SPAdes (version 3.11.1)25 and contigs shorter than 500 bp were discarded. Gene prediction and annotation were performed during the gene annotation step using Prokka software (version 1.14.5)26. The annotation process generated files containing predicted genes in GFF format, which were subsequently converted to GTF format for further analysis. A gene catalogue was created using DIAMOND (version 0.9.25)27 by aligning the predicted genes against the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database. This produced a table of gene counts for each sample, which was used to generate functional profiles. In the data analysis, the input amount in the sequencing FASTQ data was adjusted to the same data volume. The reads were mapped to the gene catalogue using Bowtie2 (version 2.2.1)28 and results were processed using SAM tools (version 1.3.1)29 to obtain sorted BAM files. Gene expression levels were quantified using HTSeq count (version 0.11.2)30, followed by conversion to transcripts per million using a custom Python script. The gene abundance tables were constructed for each sample. Functional annotation and pathway analysis were performed by mapping the predicted genes to KEGG orthology terms using DIAMOND. Cluster analyses were performed using MeV tool (webmev.tm4.org). For the Genus and Pathway data, the total amount of data obtained was normalised to the ratio of each component, with the total set at 100%.
