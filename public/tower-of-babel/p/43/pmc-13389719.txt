Selective phosphodiesterase 4B inhibition in fibrotic lung disease: a scoping review of vascular–immune mechanisms and extrapulmonary implications
Authors: Fabio Perrotta, Domenica Francesca Mariniello, Raffaella Pagliaro, Andrea Bianco
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPreferential phosphodiesterase 4B (PDE4B) inhibition with nerandomilast has demonstrated attenuation of forced vital capacity decline in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis and progressive pulmonary fibrosis. Because PDE4 enzymes regulate cAMP-dependent inflammatory and endothelial signalling, class-level PDE4 inhibition has been consistently linked to vascular–immune pathways. Whether preferential PDE4B inhibition reproduces these effects remains uncertain.ObjectivesTo map the available mechanistic and translational literature on extrapulmonary or systemic biological effects of PDE4/PDE4B inhibition and to place nerandomilast trial findings within that framework without inferring clinical efficacy outside pre-specified trial outcomes.MethodsWe performed a the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews informed scoping review of PubMed/MEDLINE from database inception to 1 March 2026. Eligible studies included pre-clinical, translational and clinical reports addressing endothelial, immune, microvascular or organ-level effects of PDE4/PDE4B modulation. Evidence was charted and narratively synthesised rather than quantitatively pooled.Results71 studies were included. Experimental data (primarily derived from class-level PDE4 inhibition) consistently link these pathways to modulation of endothelial permeability, leukocyte–endothelial adhesion, thromboinflammatory signalling, and macrophage resolution programmes across pulmonary and extrapulmonary models. However, most extrapulmonary evidence derives from class-level PDE4 biology rather than nerandomilast-specific investigations, and clinical evidence for extrapulmonary benefit in fibrotic lung disease remains indirect.ConclusionsBecause PDE4 enzymes regulate cAMP-dependent inflammatory and endothelial signalling, class-level PDE4 inhibition has been consistently linked to modulation of vascular–immune pathways. Whether preferential PDE4B inhibition reproduces these effects in humans remains to be established. While at present, extrapulmonary clinical benefit in fibrotic lung disease remains unproven, future studies should prospectively evaluate systemic biomarkers and organ-level outcomes rather than relying on post hoc interpretation of numerical signals.

Introduction

Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is a chronic, progressive and ultimately fatal interstitial lung disease characterised by irreversible architectural distortion and declining lung function. Median survival remains ∼3–5 years after diagnosis [1], and contemporary epidemiological estimates suggest that up to 311 000 individuals may be living with IPF in the United States of America [2]. Despite antifibrotic therapies that slow functional decline, IPF continues to confer substantial morbidity and mortality. However, IPF represents only one phenotype within the broader spectrum of fibrosing interstitial lung diseases (ILDs). A substantial proportion of patients with non-IPF fibrosing ILDs develop a progressive phenotype characterised by worsening respiratory symptoms, functional decline and radiological progression despite standard management. This entity, now formally defined as progressive pulmonary fibrosis (PPF), encompasses heterogeneous underlying diagnoses that converge toward a relentlessly progressive course resembling IPF [3]. The prevalence of progression among fibrosing ILDs has been variably estimated between approximately 13% and 53%, depending on underlying disease and applied criteria [4]. Importantly, once progression is established, outcomes in PPF approach those observed in IPF, suggesting partial convergence of downstream pathogenic mechanisms including epithelial stress, telomere-related vulnerability, and impaired regenerative capacity lower the threshold for irreversible remodelling [5]. Comorbidity burden is high and prognostically relevant in both IPF and PPF. In a cohort of 272 patients, survival declined stepwise according to the number of comorbidities, from 66 months in those without comorbidities to 35 months in those with four or more (p=0.004), and particularly cardiovascular disease and lung cancer independently predicted mortality risk [6]. Registry data further confirm the high prevalence of cardiovascular disease in IPF populations [7] and underscore that a meaningful proportion of deaths are attributable to nonrespiratory causes [8].

Phosphodiesterase 4 (PDE4) enzymes regulate intracellular cAMP degradation and thereby modulate inflammatory, immune and endothelial signalling pathways. Among PDE4 isoforms, PDE4B has been implicated in leukocyte activation, cytokine amplification and vascular dysfunction. Nerandomilast (BI 1015550), an oral preferential PDE4B inhibitor, demonstrated attenuation of forced vital capacity decline in both IPF and PPF clinical trials [9, 10]. The parallel efficacy of PDE4B inhibition across IPF and PPF is consistent with modulation of shared downstream pathways, although this interpretation remains inferential rather than directly demonstrated. Although not powered for mortality, directional numerical separation in all-cause mortality across datasets, including extended follow-up analyses [11]. In the FIBRONEER-IPF phase III trial, deaths up to the first database lock occurred in 21 (5.4%) out of 392 patients in the nerandomilast 18 mg group, 26 (6.6%) out of 392 in the 9 mg group, and 28 (7.1%) out of 393 in the placebo group (hazard ratio 0.81, 95% CI 0.46–1.43 for 18 mg versus placebo) [9]. At final database lock, the hazard ratio for death with nerandomilast 18 mg was 0.66 (95% CI 0.41–1.08), indicating a persistent numerical separation despite the absence of statistical power for this end-point. In the FIBRONEER-ILD phase III trial (PPF other than IPF), deaths occurred in 24 (6.1%) out of 391 patients in the nerandomilast 18 mg group and 33 (8.4%) out of 393 patients in the nerandomilast 9 mg group and in 50 (12.8%) out of 392 patients in the placebo group over the 52-week period, again showing a directional reduction without statistical confirmation [10]. At this time, no clinical trial to date has prospectively evaluated systemic vascular or inflammatory biomarkers in PDE4B-treated fibrotic lung disease populations. Accordingly, the present Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA)-guided scoping review aims to systematically map extrapulmonary biological effects of PDE4/PDE4B inhibition and to integrate these findings within a unified IPF–PPF framework. We specifically explore whether systemic vascular–immune modulation may represent a mechanistic axis relevant to clinical outcomes across progressive fibrotic lung diseases. Given the established role of PDE4B in vascular and immune regulation, and the high burden of systemic comorbidity in fibrotic lung disease, it is therefore reasonable to hypothesise that modulation of cAMP-dependent pathways, including those regulated by PDE4B, may extend beyond the pulmonary interstitium.

Methods

Protocol and reporting framework

This scoping review was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA extension for scoping reviews (PRISMA-ScR). The aim was to map, rather than quantitatively estimate, the extrapulmonary or systemic biological effects of PDE4/PDE4B modulation and to contextualise these observations relative to fibrotic lung disease trials. The review question, eligibility framework, and charting fields were defined before screening. Searches were performed in PubMed/MEDLINE from database inception to 1 March 2026, and reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews were manually screened to identify additional primary reports (supplementary table S1).

Search terms combined controlled vocabulary and free-text concepts related to PDE4/PDE4B, endothelial and vascular biology, leukocyte trafficking, thromboinflammation, fibrosis, organ injury and nerandomilast. A representative PubMed strategy was: ((“phosphodiesterase 4B” OR PDE4B OR “PDE4 inhibitor” OR nerandomilast OR roflumilast OR apremilast) AND (endothelial OR vascular OR leukocyte OR macrophage OR permeability OR adhesion OR fibrosis) AND (microvascular OR inflammatory OR organ OR systemic)). Targeted supplementary searches were used to identify studies on endothelial–leukocyte interactions, cAMP signalling, extrapulmonary injury models, and clinical studies reporting biomarker or organ-level outcomes.

Eligibility criteria and operational definitions

Eligible records included 1) pre-clinical in vitro, ex vivo or animal studies evaluating PDE4/PDE4B-related biological effects beyond isolated lung fibroblast proliferation; 2) translational studies using human cells or tissues reporting endothelial, immune, microvascular or organ-level readouts; and 3) clinical studies of PDE4 inhibitors that reported systemic biomarkers, extrapulmonary outcomes or trial outcomes relevant to fibrotic lung disease. Randomised phase II/III trials of nerandomilast in IPF or PPF were included for clinical context. Interventions comprised preferential PDE4B inhibition, nonselective or broader PDE4 inhibition (including nerandomilast, roflumilast, apremilast and cilomilast), or genetic modulation of PDE4 isoforms. Outcomes of interest included endothelial permeability, leukocyte adhesion and activation, cytokine profiles, thromboinflammatory signalling, resolution biology and organ-level injury or repair indices. For the purposes of this review, “extrapulmonary” refers to biological effects measured outside the pulmonary interstitium, whereas “systemic relevance” refers to pathways shared across pulmonary and extrapulmonary vascular–immune compartments. Original research articles were included in the evidence map. Narrative reviews, editorials and commentaries were excluded from formal charting, although selected reviews were consulted only to contextualise enzyme classification and signalling biology. Studies limited to dermatological efficacy without mechanistic vascular, immune, endothelial or organ-level outcomes were excluded.

Study selection, evidence charting and synthesis

Titles and abstracts and full-text were screened independently by two reviewers. Only articles published in English were included. As this was a scoping review designed to map available evidence, no formal risk-of-bias assessment was undertaken; accordingly, conclusions are limited to qualitative interpretation rather than causal inference. Disagreements were resolved by discussion and, when required, adjudication by a third reviewer. For each included study, we charted study design, model system, intervention/exposure, comparator when available, organ or cell compartment assessed, principal mechanistic endpoints, direction of effect, and relevance to the predefined vascular–immune framework (table 1). For nerandomilast clinical studies, we additionally extracted trial population, treatment duration, effects on forced vital capacity decline, adverse events, and any reported mortality or hospitalisation data. No formal risk-of-bias tool was applied because the objective was evidence mapping rather than pooled effect estimation. The PubMed/MEDLINE search retrieved 1160 records from database inception to 1 March 2026. After predefined publication-type filters and removal of clearly non-eligible records, 136 reports underwent title/abstract screening. 43 were excluded as out of scope and 22 additional dermatology-focused studies were excluded because they did not report mechanistically relevant vascular, immune, endothelial or organ-level outcomes (supplementary table S2).

Phosphodiesterases: tissue distribution, subcellular localisation and functional specialisation

Phosphodiesterases comprise a superfamily of 11 gene families (PDE1–PDE11) encoded by 21 genes, generating >100 isoforms through alternative splicing and promoter usage [12]. These isoforms differ not only in substrate specificity (cAMP-specific: PDE4, PDE7, PDE8; cGMP-specific: PDE5, PDE6, PDE9; or dual-substrate: PDE1, PDE2, PDE3, PDE10, PDE11), but also in regulatory domains and subcellular targeting sequences that determine compartmentalised signalling [13]. PDE4, the principal cAMP-specific family in inflammatory and structural lung cells, is encoded by four genes (PDE4A–D), each producing multiple long, short and supershort isoforms characterised by distinct upstream conserved regions (UCR1/UCR2) that regulate catalytic activity and protein–protein interactions [14]. PDE4 isoforms are highly expressed in leukocytes (neutrophils, monocytes/macrophages, T-cells), airway smooth muscle, lung fibroblasts and vascular endothelium [15]. Within these cells, PDE4 does not distribute uniformly, but is recruited into multiprotein signalling complexes (signalosomes) through interaction with A-kinase anchoring proteins, β-arrestins and receptor-associated scaffolds, thereby shaping discrete cAMP nanodomains [16]. G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR)–cAMP signalling is spatially encoded rather than globally diffusive. Upon activation of Gs-coupled receptors (e.g. β2-adrenergic receptors, prostaglandin receptors), adenylyl cyclases generate cAMP locally at the plasma membrane or endosomal compartments. PDEs positioned within the same nanodomain limit cAMP spread and define receptor-specific signalling outputs [17]. Subcellular targeting of PDE isoforms is governed by N-terminal regulatory domains that mediate membrane association, cytosolic retention or anchoring to specific protein complexes [18]. PDE4D isoforms can associate with β-arrestin complexes following receptor activation, dynamically reshaping local cAMP degradation [16]. Functionally, PDE localisation enables integration of cyclic nucleotide signalling with GPCR activation state, calcium/calmodulin signalling (PDE1), cGMP–cAMP cross-talk (PDE2 activation by cGMP), and feedback phosphorylation by protein kinase A (PDE3, PDE4) [19]. Thus, PDEs act as dynamic regulators of signal amplitude, duration and spatial confinement. Importantly, because PDE4 isoforms are enriched in inflammatory cells and vascular endothelium, their inhibition affects leukocyte activation thresholds, endothelial barrier stability, and cytokine production rather than only smooth muscle tone [20].

From a systems perspective, PDEs should therefore be viewed not merely as degradative enzymes, but as architectural determinants of cyclic nucleotide nanodomains. Their tissue distribution and compartmentalisation help explain how preferential targeting of PDE4B may modulate immune, endothelial and fibroinflammatory programmes across organs without uniformly altering global cAMP levels. This framework is mechanistically informative, but it does not by itself establish extrapulmonary clinical efficacy for any specific PDE4B-directed therapy.

PDE4/PDE4B signalling within a vascular–immune amplification framework

Progressive fibrotic lung diseases are sustained not only by fibroblast activation, but also by persistent vascular–immune amplification loops that perpetuate tissue injury and systemic vulnerability. Within this framework, PDE4B regulates compartmentalised degradation of cAMP in leukocytes and vascular cells, thereby modulating leukocyte activation thresholds, endothelial adhesiveness and microvascular inflammatory tone (figure 1).

Endothelial–leukocyte cross-talk and thromboinflammatory signalling

cAMP is an important negative regulator of leukocyte adhesion and activation. By hydrolysing cAMP within compartmentalised signalling domains, PDE4 enzymes can facilitate integrin-dependent adhesive responses and downstream pro-inflammatory signalling; given its prominent role in inflammatory cells, PDE4B is likely to contribute to this regulatory axis [21].

Human ex vivo mechanistic evidence

In primary human neutrophils and monocytes, roflumilast N-oxide inhibited PI3K-dependent Akt phosphorylation and Src-mediated Pyk2 activation, resulting in reduced Mac-1 (CD11b/CD18)-dependent adhesion to activated platelets and endothelial substrates [22]. Importantly, the same study demonstrated suppression of neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation and reduced tissue factor expression during platelet–leukocyte interactions, directly implicating PDE4 inhibition in modulation of thromboinflammatory effector functions rather than cytokine production alone [22]. These findings are relevant because integrin-mediated adhesion, NETosis, and monocyte tissue factor induction represent central components of microvascular inflammatory propagation and immunothrombosis.

In vivo microvascular injury models

In a murine myocardial ischaemia–reperfusion model, targeted delivery of roflumilast reduced neutrophil accumulation within the injured microcirculation and attenuated inflammatory tissue damage [23]. Although conducted in an acute cardiac injury model, this work confirms that PDE4 modulation can influence leukocyte–endothelial dynamics in vivo under conditions of vascular stress. Across these systems, studies using roflumilast consistently show that PDE4-dependent cAMP compartmentalisation regulates integrin activation, cytoskeletal signalling, and leukocyte retention within inflamed microvascular beds.

Resolution biology and monocyte reprogramming

In a mouse model of rapidly resolving lung injury, PDE4b was identified as the dominant inducible cAMP-regulating gene in alveolar macrophages during peak inflammation [24]. PDE4B expression increased at maximal injury and declined during resolution, suggesting dynamic regulation across inflammatory phases. Pharmacological PDE4 inhibition administered at peak injury increased intracellular cAMP in macrophages, expanded the population of reparative alveolar macrophages, and accelerated oedema resolution [24]. Importantly, depletion of recruited monocytes abolished this reparative effect, demonstrating that PDE4-sensitive monocyte-derived populations are mechanistically required for resolution programming [24]. Although this represents an acute injury model rather than chronic fibrosis, this provides mechanistic evidence that PDE4 inhibition can alter immune cell fate decisions. However, these findings arise from broader PDE4 modulation and highlight the capacity of cAMP-dependent signalling to reshape immune cell fate and resolution dynamics. In progressive fibrotic lung diseases, where recurrent epithelial injury and impaired resolution contribute to trajectory acceleration, such immune reprogramming may be biologically relevant.

Microvascular and fibroinflammatory modulation in extrapulmonary injury models

In adenine-induced chronic kidney disease, roflumilast reduced serum creatinine, suppressed transforming growth factor (TGF)-β expression, and downregulated inflammatory mediators including interleukin (IL)-1β, tumour necrosis factor (TNF)-α, monocyte chemoattractant protein-1, intercellular adhesion molecule (ICAM)-1 and fibronectin, with parallel histopathological improvement [25]. This model demonstrates attenuation of both inflammatory and pro-fibrotic pathways in a nonpulmonary organ system. In murine renal ischaemia–reperfusion injury, roflumilast reduced oxidative stress markers, suppressed IL-6 and TNF-α, modulated BAX/Bcl-2 signalling, and improved renal functional indices [26]. The intervention thus influenced inflammatory, apoptotic, and redox pathways in the context of acute vascular injury. In a lipopolysaccharide-induced sepsis model, PDE4 inhibition reduced TNF-α, IL-6 and IL-1β expression and ameliorated histopathological kidney injury [27], linking cAMP modulation to organ protection during systemic inflammatory stress. Across these pre-clinical systems, recurring biological themes emerge: suppression of leukocyte recruitment, attenuation of cytokine amplification, modulation of apoptosis, and reduction of TGF-β-driven fibrotic signalling. While extrapolation to chronic fibrotic lung disease requires caution, the consistency of microvascular and inflammatory modulation across organ systems supports the presence of a shared cAMP-dependent signalling axis across organ systems. Importantly, most observations derive from studies using roflumilast in renal, ischaemic and septic injury models, consistently showing attenuation of inflammatory and fibroinflammatory pathways.

Nerandomilast-specific endothelial and barrier effects

To bridge class biology to preferential PDE4B inhibition, Reininger et al. [28] evaluated nerandomilast in cytokine-stimulated human IPF lung fibroblasts and human lung microvascular endothelial cells, including a three-dimensional microfluidic permeability system. Nerandomilast reduced endothelial permeability, decreased vascular cell adhesion molecule (VCAM)-1 expression, and limited inflammatory cell adhesion readouts. In an adeno-associated virus–human diphtheria toxin receptor mouse model of acute lung injury, nerandomilast reduced bronchoalveolar lavage protein content and inflammatory cell influx [28]. These findings provide direct experimental evidence, across human cellular systems and murine lung injury models that preferential PDE4B inhibition can stabilise endothelial barrier function and attenuate inflammatory recruitment within the pulmonary microvasculature.

Clinical evidence and limits of translational inference

Clinical evidence supporting extrapulmonary effects is limited, but most directly available for PDE4 inhibition. In pooled placebo-controlled COPD trials, roflumilast was associated with a lower rate of major adverse cardiovascular events than placebo (hazard ratio 0.65, 95% CI 0.45–0.93), suggesting that systemic PDE4 inhibition may be associated with modulation of vascular risk trajectories, although causal relationships remain questionable [29]. By contrast, clinical data from other PDE4 inhibitors remain less conclusive. In psoriasis, apremilast has shown neutral effects on vascular inflammation with mixed, but generally favourable, biomarker changes, without clear evidence of benefit on hard cardiovascular end-points [30]. Finally, in chronic kidney disease, clinical modulation of cyclic nucleotide pathways with nonselective phosphodiesterase inhibition has been associated with reduced proteinuria and slower decline in estimated glomerular filtration rate in randomised trials [31, 32]. Although these studies involve phosphodiesterase classes distinct from PDE4, they illustrate an important translational principle: pharmacological modulation of cyclic nucleotide signalling pathways can influence clinical outcomes beyond the primary therapeutic indication. These findings support the concept that systemic modulation of cAMP-dependent pathways may influence vascular and inflammatory biology beyond the primary disease target. However, these clinical observations are largely derived from studies using roflumilast and, to a lesser extent, apremilast, and therefore provide indirect support for systemic vascular and inflammatory effects.

Integrating mechanistic and clinical observations

Current nerandomilast trials were not designed or powered to test extrapulmonary efficacy or survival. Accordingly, any interpretation of numerical separation in all-cause mortality must remain cautious and explicitly hypothesis-generating. The mechanistic literature reviewed here supports a coherent biological framework in which PDE4/PDE4B modulation can influence endothelial barrier function, leukocyte–endothelial interactions, thromboinflammatory signalling, and resolution pathways across organ systems (figure 2). These pathways are relevant to systemic vulnerability in progressive fibrotic lung disease, but the present evidence does not permit a causal link between those mechanisms and clinical outcome differences observed in phase III datasets. Importantly, the mechanistic framework described here is largely informed by studies using nonselective PDE4 inhibitors, whereas direct evidence from preferential PDE4B inhibition (nerandomilast) remains comparatively limited.

Study limitations

This work has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the present review was designed as a PRISMA-guided scoping review aimed at mapping mechanistic and translational evidence rather than performing quantitative synthesis. Consequently, formal risk-of-bias assessment and meta-analytic evaluation of included studies were not undertaken. Second, a substantial proportion of the evidence reviewed derives from pre-clinical models or human cellular systems. While these studies provide mechanistic insights into the vascular–immune regulatory roles of PDE4 signalling, extrapolation to clinical disease must be interpreted with caution. Third, mechanistic experimental data specifically evaluating nerandomilast remain limited, and several biological observations discussed in this review derive from other PDE4 inhibitors, such as roflumilast, that share similar pharmacological targets. Fourth, although numerical reductions in mortality were observed in phase III trials of nerandomilast in both IPF and PPF populations, these studies were not powered for survival end-points and causal relationships between systemic biological modulation and clinical outcomes cannot be established. Finally, the literature search was conducted in PubMed/MEDLINE, which provides comprehensive coverage of biomedical and pharmacological literature, but may not capture all potentially relevant publications indexed exclusively in other databases. In addition, the absence of formal risk-of-bias assessment limits the ability to evaluate the internal validity of included studies.

Conclusions

Preferential PDE4B inhibition engages biological pathways that extend beyond fibroblast proliferation within the pulmonary interstitium. The reviewed experimental literature supports effects on endothelial permeability, leukocyte adhesion dynamics, thromboinflammatory signalling, and macrophage resolution programming, but the strength of inference varies substantially across evidence tiers. Nerandomilast phase III trials in IPF and PPF demonstrated attenuation of lung function decline; however, they were not designed to establish survival benefit or extrapulmonary clinical efficacy. The most defensible conclusion is therefore that PDE4B may represent a vascular–immune regulatory node based on convergent mechanistic evidence with potential systemic relevance, not that extrapulmonary benefit has been demonstrated. Prospective studies incorporating predefined systemic biomarkers of endothelial activation, thromboinflammation, and organ dysfunction, together with organ-level outcomes, will be required to determine whether this mechanistic framework translates into clinically meaningful extrapulmonary effects.
