Global Trends and Forecast of Cardiovascular Diseases, Cancer, and Shared Risk Factors: Insights From the GBD 2021
Authors: Chieh Y Koo, Bryan Chong, Jayanth Jayabaskaran, Srinithy Nagarajan, Silingga M Jauhari, Yiming Chen, Li L Tan, Anurag Mehta, Muhammad S Khan, Mark Muthiah, Derek Hausenloy, A M Richards, Dennis T Ko, Christian D Mallen, Eric A Secemsky, Mark Y Chan, Paaladinesh Thavendiranathan, Nicholas W S Chew, Mamas A Mamas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiovascular disease (CVD) and cancer share multiple risk factors and are the leading causes of morbidity and death worldwide. This study aims to examine historical and projected trends of the combined CVD–cancer global estimates and their shared risk factors.MethodsDeath and disability‐adjusted life years (DALYs) associated with CVDs, cancers, and their risk factors were obtained from the GBD (Global Burden of Disease) 2021 study. Historical estimates from 1990 to 2021 and forecast data from 2025 to 2050 were examined across age groups, regions, sociodemographic index, and sex.ResultsIn 2021, CVD–cancer resulted in age‐standardized DALY rates of 8009 per 100 000 population (63% contributed by CVD) and age‐standardized mortality rates of 352 per 100 000 population (67% by CVD) globally. While age‐standardized DALYs of the CVD–cancer burden declined by 30% (33% in CVD and 26% in cancer), crude DALYs increased by 46% from 1990 to 2021. CVD–cancer resulted in higher age‐standardized DALY rates in men (9654 per 100 000 population) than women (6556 per 100 000 population). While low‐middle sociodemographic index regions had the highest CVD‐related age‐standardized DALY rates (6744 per 100 000 population), high‐middle sociodemographic index regions had the highest cancer‐related rates (3388 per 100 000) in 2021. The top risk factors driving CVD–cancer morbidity burden in 2021 were high systolic blood pressure (largely CVD contribution), dietary risks (89% by CVD), and tobacco (60% by CVD), persisting to 2050.ConclusionsGlobal improvements in CVD–cancer age‐standardized morbidity will be offset by the ever‐rising crude CVD–cancer morbidity burden. Preventive efforts should prioritize key shared risk factors of hypertension, dietary risks, and tobacco.Keywords: cardiovascular diseases, death, disability‐adjusted life years, neoplasms, risk factorsSubject Categories: Cardiovascular Disease, Epidemiology, Risk Factors, Primary Prevention, Secondary Prevention

What Are the Clinical Implications?

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) and cancer are the leading causes of morbidity and death worldwide, accounting for more than two‐thirds of all premature deaths attributable to noncommunicable diseases (NCDs). 1 The United Nations Sustainable Development Goals have set a target for 2030 to reduce premature death related to NCDs by one third through prevention and treatment. 2 Given that CVD and cancer share common risk factors, stakeholders should ideally address these common pathomechanistic pathways, rather than treating CVD or cancer in silos. 3

Existing studies have examined the global trends in CVD, cancer, and their risk factors in isolation. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 However, there remains the lack of integrated analyses assessing the shared morbidity, forecasted trajectories, and common modifiable risk factors of the 2 leading NCDs. Understanding the extent to which the shared risk factors of CVD and cancer contribute to the respective disease burden both individually and collectively, and how this contribution is expected to vary with time, is imperative to inform more unified prevention strategies, particularly in the context of population aging, multimorbidity, and rising health care demands.

As such, the study uses data from the GBD (Global Burden of Disease) 2021 to address 4 key objectives: (1) to estimate global trends in the combined burden of CVD and cancer from 1990 to 2021; (2) to examine shared metabolic, behavioral, and environmental risk factors; (3) to explore subgroup differences by sex, sociodemographic index (SDI), and region; and (4) to forecast the future burden of combined CVD–cancer outcomes from 2025 to 2050. Despite current NCD preventative strategies, we hypothesize that the rising trends of combined CVD and cancer burden will continue to persist into the future, with a more pronounced rise in cancer‐related burden owing to better survivorship and the aging global populace. Underpinned by shared modifiable metabolic and behavioral risk factors, the disproportionate burden of risk factors borne by vulnerable population groups will likely parallel the disparate distribution of CVD and cancer burden across sex, region, and socioeconomic status. With multimorbidity becoming an increasingly prevalent global health issue, exacerbated by health care inequities across regions, 9 there is a growing impetus to structure targeted and effective interventions of shared risk factors between CVD and cancer worldwide.

Methods

Overview and Definition

All data and code used in this article are available on reasonable request to the corresponding author. This study complies with the Guidelines for Accurate and Transparent Health Estimates Reporting statement. 10 Data estimates from 1990 to 2021 were collected from the GBD 2021 study, a collaboration by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation across 204 countries and territories. 11 Annual causes, risk factors, death, and disability‐adjusted life years (DALYs) estimates by locations are provided, allowing for geospatial and temporal burden comparisons. 12 Mortality and DALY data retrieved from the Global Health Exchange 13 website were modeled from vital registration systems, verbal autopsies, and alternative surveillance systems.

With the GBD database, an ecological analysis of world and regional disease burdens can be inferred. This study includes the established 7 GBD super‐regions, 14 namely, (1) Central Europe, Eastern Europe, and Central Asia; (2) Latin America and Caribbean; (3) Southeast Asia, East Asia, and Oceania; (4) South Asia, (5) North Africa and the Middle East, (6) Sub‐Saharan Africa; and (7) High‐Income regions (Table S1). Subgroup analysis on sociodemographic index (SDI), health systems, and sex were performed. SDI comprised 5 groups (1) low; (2) low‐middle; (3) middle; (4) middle‐high; and (5) high SDI. Health systems were classified into (1) minimal, (2) limited, (3) basic, and (4) advanced categories. The definitions and criteria for SDI and health systems can be found in the Supplementary Materials.

CVD and Cancer‐Related Death and DALYs

As described in previous studies, 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 crude and age‐standardized estimates of death and DALYs with their 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs) for CVD and cancer were obtained. CVDs were classified into 12 subtypes, while cancers were arranged into 34 subtypes (Supplementary Methods 1). The 1990 to 2021 estimates, as well as 2025 to 2050 forecast estimates of overall CVD and cancer, were analyzed.

Risk Factors

This study evaluates differences in deaths and DALYs attributable to risk factors for CVD and cancer using crude and age‐standardized estimates from 1990 to 2021. Risk factors of CVD such as high systolic blood pressure (SBP), dietary risks, high low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, tobacco, high body mass index (BMI), kidney dysfunction, high fasting plasma glucose, nonoptimal temperature, air pollution, other environmental risks, low physical activity, and high alcohol use were examined. Cancer‐related risk factors, including tobacco, dietary risks, occupational risks, high BMI, high alcohol use, high fasting plasma glucose, low physical activity, other environmental risks, air pollution, unsafe sex, and drug use were evaluated (Table S2).

Statistical Analysis

This study reports both crude measures and age‐standardized measures, which serve distinct epidemiological and policy purposes. Crude measures (crude numbers and rates) represent the actual absolute disease burden experienced by populations and have direct health policy value for resource allocation, health care planning, and understanding the real‐world impact on health care systems. Age‐standardized measures (age‐standardized rates) remove the influence of population age structure differences and show changes in underlying disease‐driving forces, enabling assessment of intervention effectiveness and valid comparisons across populations and time periods. 19

The age‐standardized rates and DALYs were calculated using the direct standardization method, consistent with the GBD methodology. 19 , 20 Age standardization was performed against the GBD world standard population, as provided by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation. This ensures comparability across time and populations by removing the influence of differing age structures. DALYs were estimated as the sum of years of life lost and years lived with disability, following standard GBD definitions, and then age standardized using the same direct method. GBD 2021 mortality estimates by location, age, sex, year, and cause were attained from the Cause of Death Ensemble model, which used covariate selection algorithm and assessed using out‐of‐sample predictive validity, as discussed in previous publications. 12 GBD 2021 DALY estimates were attained from years of life lost combined with years lived with disability across location, sex, and year. 19 For projected estimates, historical age‐standardized rates were fitted into the forecasting model. By working with age‐standardized rates rather than raw counts, the effects of changes in underlying age structure were implicitly captured in the historical trend of the time series. As such, the forecast model assumes the influence of demographic transitions on disease burden which is embedded within the historical patterns of the standardized rates, thereby reflecting both epidemiological changes and demographic effects on disease estimates. The forecast model has been described in detail previously. 20

Attributable burden of CVD and cancer risk factors from GBD 2021 was quantified by the GBD Comparative Risk Assessment framework, which builds upon the concept of attributable risk. The burden represents the decrease in the current health impact that would have occurred if past population exposure had been at the theoretical minimum risk exposure level (TMREL). All TMREL values were directly obtained from the GBD 2021 comparative risk assessment framework developed by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation. 21 In the GBD framework, each risk factor has its TMREL defined individually on the basis of large‐scale cohort studies, meta‐analyses, and trial evidence. The rules for TMREL determination differ according to the shape of the exposure–response relationship. 21 For harmful risks with monotonically increasing functions (eg, tobacco, alcohol use, unsafe sex, lead exposure, occupational risks), the TMREL is set to 0 exposure. For J‐ or U‐shaped risks (eg, high BMI, high blood pressure, high fasting glucose, sodium intake), the TMREL is defined at the nadir of the risk curve, with uncertainty intervals when the lowest‐risk range is broad or poorly defined. For protective risks, TMRELs are set at the highest exposure supported by robust data to avoid extrapolation beyond evidence. Importantly, most TMRELs are globally fixed (eg, tobacco, alcohol, air pollution, blood pressure, glucose, cholesterol). Some are defined with age‐ or population‐specific considerations, such as BMI (adult versus child reference) and dietary risks (age‐ and sex‐stratified intake distributions). Only nonoptimal temperature uses a location‐specific TMREL, defined as the local temperature associated with the lowest mortality risk. This ensures appropriate comparability across exposures, populations, and time. 21 The present analysis therefore follows the standardized Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation/GBD 2021 definitions without modification, ensuring consistency with published burden‐of‐disease estimates.

Forecasting Model

Forecast data for death and DALYs were obtained from GBD Foresight Visualization. The forecasting framework used by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation in generating these projections have been described extensively. 22 As an overview, estimates and drivers of disease burden, together with demographic indicators, were used in a multistaged modeling process. Future trends of risk factors were estimated using ensemble modeling of GBD summary exposure values for 68 risk factors. This included a combination of 6 annualized rate‐of‐change models and 6 Meta‐Regression—Bayesian, Regularized, Trimmed spline models, all parameterized by the SDI. These models incorporated different recency‐weighting schemes to better capture evolving exposure patterns. For behavioral and metabolic risk factors such as tobacco use, high BMI, and dietary risks, additional mediation modeling accounted for secondary effects through systolic blood pressure, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, and fasting glucose. Independent health drivers, including summary exposure values of risk factors, and sociodemographic determinants of health (including met need for contraception, education, lag‐distributed income, and fertility) were first forecasted. The SDI, a country‐ and year‐specific composite index of fertility in age <25 years, educational attainment, and gross domestic product per capita was used together with the independent drivers above to generate cause‐specific mortality forecasts. These cause‐specific deaths were used to generate forecasts of all‐cause mortality rates and years of life lost. 22

Incidence and prevalence forecasts were obtained using a linear mixed‐effects regression model predicting mortality‐to‐incidence or mortality‐to‐prevalence ratios, which were applied to cause‐specific mortality forecasts. Prevalence forecasts of nonfatal diseases were generated using a linear mixed‐effects model on prevalence directly. Disability weights were used to forecast years lived with disability, which, summed with years of life lost, generated DALY forecasts. Population forecasts were generated through death, fertility, migration, and sex ratio at birth projections applied to GBD 2021 starting populations. 22

Forecasts of alternative health scenarios were obtained to demonstrate trends in disease reduction potentially attainable with strengthened public health policy. These were generated by replacing the projected reference trajectories for risk factors with alternative trajectories modeling the gradual, linear elimination of relevant risk factors from current levels to 2050. In particular, scenarios for improved behavioral and metabolic risks as well as safer environments were obtained, illustrating disease burden under the scenario where exposure to relevant risk factors are eliminated, as described in detail in previous GBD papers. 22 More information is available in Supplementary Methods 2.

Ethics

This study was exempted from Institutional Review Board review as the publicly available data did not contain any confidential or identifiable patient information.

Results

Overview

Collectively, the global burden of CVD and cancer was estimated to be 681.6 million (95% UI, 650.9–712.3) DALYs in 2021, with CVD and cancer contributing to 428.3 million (95% UI, 403.7–453.7) and 253.3 million (95% UI, 237.5–270.6) DALYs, respectively (Table S3). Combined CVD–cancer age‐standardized DALYs amounted to 8009.5 (95% UI, 7653.2–8365.8) per 100 000 population (with 63.1% CVD contribution) in 2021 (Graphic Abstract; Table S4). Ischemic heart disease, stroke, and hypertensive heart disease emerged as the top 3 drivers of CVD‐related DALYs in 2021 (Figure S1; Table S4). Meanwhile, tracheal, bronchus, and lung cancer, followed by colon and rectum cancer, and stomach cancer ranked the top 3 causes of cancer‐related DALYs (Figure S1; Table S4). Mortality data can be found in Tables S5 and S6.

Historical and Forecast Trends

An increasing trend in the global crude DALYs attributable to CVD and cancers was observed from 467.4 million (95% UI, 453.5–481.2) in 1990 to 681.6 million (95% UI, 650.9–712.3) in 2021, and anticipated to rise further to 896.2 million (95% UI, 749.1–1043.4) by 2050 (Table S7). Global age‐standardized DALY rates attributable to CVD and cancer decrease from 11 519.4 (95% UI, 11 138.3–11 900.4) in 1990 to 8009.5 (95% UI, 7653.2–8365.8) per 100 000 population in 2021, and are expected to decline further to 5895.3 (4897.6–6893.1) per 100 000 population by 2050 (Table S8). In terms of cause‐specific trends, there were increases in crude DALYs for CVD (44.0% [95% UI, 35.8–52.2]) and cancer (49.1% [95% UI, 38.6–61.2]) from 1990 to 2021 (Figures 1 and 2; Table S9). In contrast, age‐standardized DALY rates decreased for CVD (−33.0% [95% UI, −36.5 to −29.4]) and cancer (−25.6% [95% UI, −30.6 to −19.6]), with more pronounced decline recorded for CVD (Figures 1 and 2; Table S10). Similar trends were observed for crude and age‐standardized mortality rates for CVD and cancer (Figure S2A; Tables S11 and S12).

Between 2025 and 2050, crude DALYs are anticipated to increase for CVD (434.3 million [95% UI, 402.5–467.6] to 508.1 million [95% UI, 404.8–640.4]) and cancer (269.5 million [95% UI, 251.2–286.8] to 388.1 million [95% UI, 334.8–452.6]) (Figure 1; Figure S3; Tables S13 and S14). Within the same time frame, age‐standardized DALY rates are set to decline for CVD from 4675.2 [95% UI, 4345.1–5029.2] to 3215.2 [95% UI, 2412.3–4304.8] per 100 000 population, and for cancer from 2891.8 [95% UI, 2704.1–3070.5] to 2680.2 [95% UI, 2401.0–3034.1] per 100 000 population. The anticipated reduction in age‐standardized DALY rates will be more pronounced for CVD compared with cancer (Figure 1 and Figure S3; Tables S15 and S16). Improving behavioral and metabolic risks, as well as achieving a safer environment, will likely result in greater reductions in age‐standardized DALY rates attributed to CVD compared with cancer (Figure S4). Similar trends will be observed for crude and age‐standardized mortality rates for CVD and cancer (Figure S2B; Tables S17 through S20). In 2050, ischemic heart disease, stroke, and hypertensive heart disease will contribute to the largest proportion of age‐standardized DALYs; while the leading cancer subtypes driving age‐standardized DALY rates will be lung cancer, followed by colorectal cancer and breast cancer (Figure S1).

Shared Risk Factors

The top 3 risk factors contributing to the shared burden on age‐standardized DALYs associated with CVD and cancers in 2021 were high SBP (2513.4 [95% UI, 2115.3–2902.8] per 100 000 population), dietary risks (1753.5 [95% UI, 1055.2–2451.8] per 100 000 population), and tobacco (1428.0 [95% UI, 1251.3–1604.7] per 100 000 population) (Tables S21 through S30; Figures S5 and S6). In terms of CVD‐specific risk factors, the leading drivers of age‐standardized DALYs in 2021 were high SBP followed by dietary risks and air pollution (Figures 3 and 4; Tables S21 through S30). On the other hand, the leading cancer‐specific drivers of age‐standardized DALYs were tobacco, followed by dietary risks and unsafe sex. In terms of dietary risks, the largest contributor of age‐standardized DALYs is a diet high in sodium (CVD, 437.7 [95% UI, 103.0–940.2] per 100 ,000 population; cancer, 20.8 [95% UI, 0–102.4] per 100 000 population), followed by a diet low in fruits (CVD, 411.9 [95% UI, 155.7–613.7] per 100 000 population; cancer, 18.5 [95% UI, 9.5–26.9] per 100 000 population), and a diet low in whole grains (CVD, 352.7 [95% UI, 161.6–519.6] per 100 000 population; cancer, 50.2 [95% UI, 20.4–76.3] per 100 000 population).

Sex Differences

The combined CVD and cancer‐associated age‐standardized DALYs in 2021 were higher in men (9653.7 [95% UI, 9136.1–10 171.3] per 100 000 population) compared with women (6555.8 [95% UI, 6203.6–6908.0] per 100 000 population). In 2021, men had higher age‐standardized DALYs associated with CVD (6169.0 [95% UI, 5774.3–6613.8] per 100 000 population) than women (4048.2 [95% UI, 3721.4–4327.0] per 100 000 population). Similarly, men experienced higher age‐standardized DALYs associated with cancer (3484.7 [95% UI, 3209.0–3814.8] per 100 000 population) compared with women (2507.6 [95% UI, 2327.1–2686.8] per 100 000 population).

In men, high SBP was the leading risk factor contributing to CVD and cancer in 2021, with a combined age‐standardized DALYs of 3018.4 (95% UI, 2530.1–3506.7) per 100 000 population, contributed mainly by CVD. The other key drivers of the combined CVD and cancer‐related age‐standardized DALYs were tobacco use (2442.3 [95% UI, 2145.8–2738.8] per 100 000 population, with 59.0% CVD contribution), and dietary risks (2223.8 [95% UI, 1339.2–3108.4] per 100 000 population, with 90.8% CVD contribution). For female subjects, while high SBP was the key risk factor (2051.0 [95% UI, 1709.6–2392.4] per 100 000 population), dietary risks (1321.9 [95% UI, 757.7–1886.1] per 100 000 population, with 86.5% CVD contribution) and air pollution (962.6 [95% UI, 790.2–1135.0] per 100 000 population, with 93.6% CVD contribution) were the next important risk factors contributing to CVD and cancer‐related morbidity.

Notably, sex‐specific attributable DALY burdens reveal distinct patterns of exposure to cancer and CVD risk factors. Male subjects had higher age‐standardized DALY rates attributable to behavioral risk factors compared with female subjects, especially for tobacco use (men, 2442.3 [95% UI, 2145.8–2738.8]; women, 511.7 [95% UI, 417.7–605.7]), alcohol use (men, 379.1 [95% UI, 222.2–536.0]; women, 67.2 [95% UI, 41.7–92.7]), and drug use (men, 26.2 [95% UI, 20.5–33.2]; women, 11.4 [95% UI, 8.1–15.2]). Occupational risk factors were also substantially higher in male subjects (138.3 [95% UI, 106.6–170.2]) compared with female counterparts (34.5 [95% UI, 25.7–43.9]). Conversely, certain metabolic risk factors such as high fasting plasma glucose and impaired kidney function demonstrated smaller gaps between the sexes (Figure S7; Table S31).

Age Differences

The 65‐ to 69‐year age group experienced the highest DALYs associated with both cancers and CVD in 2021 (Figure S8). The majority of risk factors associated with CVD peaked at the 65‐ to 74‐year age group, with the exception of high low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (peaking earlier at 60–64 years) and tobacco (peaking at 55–59 years). Similarly, most cancer‐related risk factors peaked at the 65‐ to 74‐year age group, except for alcohol use (peaking earlier at 55–59 years), high BMI (peaking at 60–64 years), and unsafe sex (peaking at 50–54 years). Importantly, the DALY burden associated with tobacco peaked earlier for CVD‐related morbidity (55–59 years) compared with cancer (65–69 years). Despite DALYs for cancers and CVDs peaking at the same age group, cancer‐related death peaked at 70 to 74 years, a decade earlier than CVD‐related death (peaking at 80–84 years).

Sociodemographic Index

In 2021, low‐middle SDI regions had the highest CVD‐related age‐standardized DALY rates, followed by low and middle SDI regions (Table S32), while high SDI regions observed the lowest. On the contrary, cancer‐related age‐standardized DALY rates were highest in high‐middle SDI regions, followed by high and middle SDI regions, while low‐middle SDI regions observed the lowest (Tables S32 and S33).

Overall CVD–cancer related age‐standardized DALYs in 1990 were highest in high‐middle SDI regions (13 261.4 [95% UI, 12 778.0–13 744.8] per 100 000 population), followed by middle (11 622.4 [95% UI, 11 044.7–12 200.0] per 100 000 population) and low SDI (11 100.8 [95% UI, 10 354.1–11 847.5] per 100 000 population) regions. In 2021, however, low‐middle SDI regions had the highest combined disease burden (9121.1 [95% UI, 8635.6–9606.6] per 100 000 population), followed by low (8962.1 [95% UI, 8258.4–9665.8] per 100 000 population) and middle SDI regions (8357.3 [95% UI, 7867.5–8847.1] per 100 000 population). By 2050, low SDI regions (6355.5 [95% UI, 5028.1–7682.8] per 100 000 population) are expected to overtake low‐middle SDI regions (6195.4 [95% UI, 4697.4–7693.4] per 100 000 population) (Tables S34 through S41).

In all regions, high SBP was the main risk factor contributing to combined CVD–cancer‐related age‐standardized DALY. In low and low‐middle SDI regions, the other 2 leading risk factors were dietary risks and air pollution. In middle SDI regions, dietary risks were followed by tobacco instead. In high‐middle and high SDI regions, tobacco was the second‐leading risk factor contributing to CVD–cancer burden (Tables S42 through S49). Trends across the quality of health systems can be found in Tables S50 through S67.

Regional Differences

In 2021, regions that observed the largest age‐standardized DALYs for CVD include Central Europe, Eastern Europe, and Central Asia, followed by North Africa and Middle East and South Asia, while high‐income regions had the lowest (Table S4). The regions with the highest age‐standardized DALYs for cancer were Central Europe, Eastern Europe, and Central Asia, followed by Southeast Asia, East Asia, and Oceania, and high‐income countries, while South Asia had the lowest (Figure 5; Table S4).

Discussion

Fundamental to the advancements of the field of cardio‐oncology, 23 the study has several principal findings: First, with advancements in medical therapy following the diagnosis of CVD and cancer, the age‐standardized DALYs of CVD and cancer have been, and will continue, declining with larger success on the CVD front. This will, however, be dampened by the rising crude DALYs for CVD and cancer, reflecting both better survivorship and an enlarging and aging global populace, with a more pronounced rise in the cancer‐related burden. Second, the study has identified 3 key modifiable risk factors underpinning the total burden of patients with CVD and patients with cancer: high SBP, dietary risk, and tobacco. Variations in the risk factor profile were observed on the basis of sex (eg, a larger CVD–cancer burden borne by men that was primarily driven by male‐dominant behavioral risk factors such as tobacco and dietary factors). 24 , 25 Third, the divergent CVD and cancer trends were reflective of the epidemiological transition of specific regions, with the highest CVD burden recorded in low SDI regions, while the largest cancer burden was observed in high and high‐middle SDI regions.

The study portrays the decline in age‐standardized DALYs of both CVD and cancer, a trend that is suggestive of the progress in treatment advancements. Our analysis reveals the complexities of CVD and cancer burden, with larger improvements observed with age‐standardized CVD morbidity compared with that of cancer, which likely stems from the proficiencies in primary and secondary CVD prevention. 26 This is expectedly dampened by the rise in overall crude burden of CVD and cancer, a consequence of the enlarging and aging global populace of cancer survivors at increased risk of multimorbid CVD and vice versa. The contrasting trends between crude and age‐standardized measures observed in our study exemplify their distinct epidemiological significance and health policy value. The declining age‐standardized CVD–cancer rates demonstrate that public health interventions targeting disease‐driving forces, including prevention programs, improved treatments, and risk factor modification, are effectively reducing the underlying disease risk in populations. However, the rising crude CVD–cancer burden highlights the growing absolute health care challenge that health systems must address and adapt, driven primarily by demographic transitions including population aging and growth. This distinction is fundamental for health policy development: While age‐standardized measures reflect the moderate successes of CVD–cancer prevention and treatment strategies in clinical practice, the crude measures forewarn of the increasing direct health system impact of resource allocation, health care workforce planning, infrastructure development, and health care system capacity planning to address the rising NCD burden. 27 , 28 , 29

The slower reduction in age‐standardized cancer burden can be explained by the extended latency associated with disease development and risk factor exposure, compared with CVD. Many cancers have long latency periods, with risk exposures such as tobacco potentially taking years to decades to culminate in malignancy and death, making it less responsive to primary and secondary prevention strategies. This temporal disconnect offers an explanation to the more rapid decline in CVD burden observed in our findings, as the cumulative effects of risk factor modification on cancer may be realized only over substantial time lags. This is further compounded by delays in the benefits of cancer treatment and greater heterogeneity of risk factors relative to CVD management. Therefore, while both diseases share modifiable upstream determinants, policymakers must adapt the timing, intensity, and duration of national cancer prevention strategies to the local cancer profile. 26 , 30 , 31 , 32

Our findings have identified several “best buys” of shared risk factor targets that could be prioritized for NCD control, namely high SBP, air pollution, dietary risk, and tobacco, across the socioeconomic–geospatial spectrum. 23 , 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 , 39 , 40 , 41 The forecast analysis emphasizes that the largest scenario difference between the reference and alternative forecast models was observed with improvements in behavioral and metabolic risks. 4 , 16 The best buys in reducing the global CVD–cancer burdens will be public health interventions targeted at modifying behavioral and metabolic risks, 4 , 16 , 42 , 43 , 44 with potential disruptors likely including the emergence of new and scalable metabolic pharmacotherapies 45 , 46 , 47 such as glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists and sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors. 48 , 49 , 50 , 51 , 52

The study found that the key dietary contributor to the combined CVD–cancer burden was the diet high in sodium. A multifaceted and regulatory approach is necessary to incentivize the public in engaging with healthy dietary habits in schools, workplaces, and public spaces, coupled with implementation of legislation for nationwide salt reduction initiatives that include food reformulation, behavior change education, media campaigns and front‐of‐pack labeling. 53 Moreover, population‐based tobacco control measures should be prioritized, especially with lung and colorectal cancer identified as the top 2 leading causes of cancer by 2050. These tobacco interventions have been proven to be cost effective in low‐ and middle‐income countries, with vast potential in achieving economic gains that can be reinvested. 54 Practical recommendations such as the World Health Organization’s MPOWER package offer a practical and scalable framework for achieving cost‐effective reductions in tobacco‐related burden, from tobacco use monitoring, advertising bans, and optimizing taxation on tobacco, particularly in resource‐limited settings. 55 , 56 With the largest CVD–cancer burden borne by vulnerable populations in low‐ to middle‐income regions, the key tenet in CVD–cancer prevention will be to ensure that the delivery of these cost‐effective preventive interventions are equitable and accessible in resource‐limited regions, where the “treatment‐risk paradox” is most prevalent. 20 , 37 , 40 , 57 , 58 , 59 , 60

The sex‐specific differences in CVD and cancer burden reveals the deleterious effects of divergent male‐dominant behavioral risk profiles with increased tobacco and alcohol exposure. 26 The elevated occupational risks in men also reflect disproportionate employment of men in manual labor and heavy industry, associated with increased exposure to physical strain, air pollutants, chemical toxins, and shift work. 61 These common factors elevate the risk of CVD and cancers through mechanisms including chronic inflammation and oxidative stress. 62 , 63 In terms of biological differences, premenopausal women generally have lower cardiometabolic risks than men; however, menopause attenuates this advantage through a decline in estrogen levels, which may lead to increased visceral adiposity, insulin resistance, and dyslipidemia. As such, cardiometabolic risks in postmenopausal women tend to be elevated, often exceeding that of men. 64 Furthermore, women in low‐ and middle‐income regions continue to face substantial barriers to health care access, with these disparities shaped by factors including education, marital status, wealth, and literacy rates. 65 , 66 These findings reinforce the need for tailored sex and age‐specific public health strategies in addressing behavioral, occupational, structural, and environmental determinants of health.

Limitations

The study has several limitations. The GBD 2021 framework handles cause attribution and potential overlaps between conditions by establishing a mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive cause hierarchy with sophisticated comorbidity corrections and sequelae mapping. 67 This methodological approach ensures that diseases and injuries are organized into hierarchical nested categories where causes are mutually exclusive at each level of aggregation, while sequelae (health outcomes) are mapped to specific diseases using disability weights that prevent overlap in burden calculations. The framework employs comorbidity simulation methods that model the co‐occurrence of nonfatal causes within populations, using multiplicative functions to assign cumulative disability weights and adjust for the simultaneous presence of multiple conditions. 19 However, the main limitation of the GBD framework’s cause attribution methodology is that it is unable to ascertain individuals with coprevalence of cancer and CVD, and address treatment‐related cardiotoxicity or disease‐related CVD complications in patients with cancer. Moreover, individuals with concomitant CVD and cancer are more likely to undergo screening and surveillance, thus increasing the chances of detection of comorbidities and ascertainment bias. Hence, the results must be interpreted with caution owing to the potential for confounding, reverse causality, and selection bias. 23 For instance, individuals with concomitant CVD and cancer are more likely to undergo screening and surveillance, thus increasing the chances of detection of comorbidities and ascertainment bias. Nevertheless, the present methodological approach offers new insights into the combined burden of CVD and cancer, as well as the parallel trends of both NCDs that often coexists, across regions. The focus on shared risk factors, and the proportion of attributable risk of CVD and cancer, extends the current knowledge in identifying important best buys that should be prioritized in resource allocation and preventive strategy implementations. Second, while the study extends the current knowledge by examining the attributable risk of each risk factor on CVD and cancer, the attributable risk estimates in this paper require careful interpretation due to inherent modeling assumptions in the GBD methodology. The key limitation of the GBD 2021 study is that it does not allow for the analysis of the interactions and synergistic effects of combined risk factors on CVD and cancer. For example, the combined effect of hypertension and smoking on the burden of CVD has been shown to be greater than their individual effects. 68 Hence, it is important that stakeholders shift their focus from the traditional preventative approach of tackling risk factor epidemics in silos, toward the concerted efforts in galvanizing shared solutions for the combined biological and societal drivers of the multiple synergistic risk factors associated with CVD and cancer. Third, the forecast models are unable to fully represent transformative changes in health care policies, technological and medical care advancements, socioeconomic transitions, or geopolitical developments, that may impact disease trajectories in a way that may not be accurately forecasted. 4 , 17 , 18

Conclusions

The reduction in age‐standardized CVD and cancer DALY burden globally, with greater success evident in CVD control, is a reflection of the advancements of medical care following diagnosis. However, this is dampened by the rising crude CVD and cancer morbidity trajectory, suggesting that the net effect of summative preventive efforts may be overwhelmed by the enlarging and aging global populace living with CVD and cancer. Primordial preventive efforts should prioritize the key shared risk factors of CVD and cancer, namely, hypertension, dietary risks, and tobacco. One of the key areas of focus that can deliver the largest improvements in global CVD–cancer prevention will be targeting behavioral and metabolic risks, especially in vulnerable populations in the low SDI regions. This study offers a road map for policymakers to design interventions tailored to the region’s socioeconomic status and its phase within the epidemiological transition, to effectively curb the premature morbidity and death associated with CVD and cancer, and to accelerate efforts in achieving the Sustainable Development Goals 2030.
