Pancreatic SBRT on four modern platforms: dosimetric and radiobiological gains with the Ethos ring-gantry linac versus TrueBeam, Halcyon, and helical tomotherapy
Authors: Bin Zhang, Xiangyue Kong, Gongsen Zhang, Zejun Jiang, Tianyuan Dai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo perform the first comprehensive dosimetric and radiobiological comparison of four modern delivery platforms for stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT) in pancreatic cancer: a conventional C-arm linac (TrueBeam), two established ring-gantry systems (Halcyon and helical tomotherapy [HT]), and the novel online-adaptive ring-gantry platform (Ethos).Materials and methodsEighteen patients with pancreatic cancer were retrospectively planned to 35 Gy in 5 fractions using identical contours and dose constraints on all four platforms. Three-arc VMAT plans were generated for TrueBeam, Halcyon, and Ethos (Acuros XB dose calculation for Ethos; AAA for TrueBeam/Halcyon). For HT plans, a dynamic field width of 2.5 cm was used, and the collapsed cone convolution algorithm was utilized. Target coverage, conformity index (CI), homogeneity index (HI), gradient metrics (R50, D2cm), organs-at-risk (OARs) doses, and equivalent uniform dose (EUD) with tissue-specific parameters were compared using statistical tests.ResultsAll plans met clinical constraints. Coplanar platforms (TrueBeam, Halcyon, Ethos) achieved significantly better CI vs. HT (0.90 ± 0.02 vs. 0.84 ± 0.10) and lower R50 (3.48–3.53 vs. 4.71 ± 0.58) and, and steeper dose fall-off compared with HT. Ethos yielded the lowest mean doses to bilateral kidneys (2.80 ± 0.57 Gy vs. 3.98–4.93 Gy on other platforms) and the most favourable high-dose sparing for bowel, stomach, and duodenum. EUD analysis confirmed Ethos achieved the lowest values in seven of eight OARs, with the largest gains in kidneys (6.4 ± 1.4 Gy vs. 9.1–11.8 Gy) and bowel.ConclusionAmong contemporary platforms, Ethos provided a more favorable planning performance under these standardized conditions. These findings are limited to standard-dose (35 Gy/5F) SBRT planning and do not imply clinical superiority at escalated dose levels.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13014-026-02812-1.Keywords: Pancreatic cancer, SBRT, Ethos, Ring-gantry linac, Dosimetric comparison, Radiobiological

Introduction

Pancreatic cancer is one of the most lethal cancers, often diagnosed at an advanced stage due to the difficulty of early detection. By the time symptoms such as jaundice, abdominal pain, or weight loss appear, the tumor is frequently inoperable. Surgical resection, the only potentially curative option, is feasible in only a small percentage of patients. Consequently, chemotherapy and radiotherapy are commonly used for palliative treatment, but the prognosis remains poor [1–3]. Stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT) has emerged as a promising approach for locally advanced or unresectable pancreatic cancer, delivering high-dose radiation with improved precision and reduced side effects [4, 5].

SBRT delivers high-dose radiation precisely to the tumor while sparing surrounding healthy tissues, making it an ideal treatment for cancers near critical organs, such as the pancreas. The technique enables accurate radiation delivery, minimizing damage to adjacent structures, including the liver, stomach, and small intestine [6, 7]. In pancreatic cancer, where conventional radiation is often hindered by complex abdominal anatomy and tumor motion, SBRT provides a more effective solution. By delivering high-dose in fewer treatment sessions, it enables improved local tumor control and prolonged survival in select patients. Moreover, SBRT is generally better tolerated than traditional radiation therapies, with fewer side effects, making it a preferred option for patients unable to tolerate more aggressive treatments [8, 9].

Several radiation therapy devices are used for pancreatic SBRT, each with distinct design features. C-arm-based systems, such as Varian TrueBeam, utilize a rotating gantry for flexible beam angles and robust image guidance, ensuring accurate tumor localization, especially for tumors near critical structures like the pancreas [10]. Ring-mounted systems, including Halcyon, Ethos, and Helical Tomotherapy (HT), offer unique advantages: HT delivery improves dose homogeneity and organs-at-risk (OARs) sparing, such as reducing doses to the stomach and duodenum [11], while Halcyon, a rotational linear accelerator, is known for faster treatment times, reducing session durations and enhancing patient comfort [12]. The newer ring-mounted system, Ethos, supports online adaptive radiotherapy, enabling real-time plan adjustments for anatomical variations and tumor motion influenced by surrounding organs, making it a promising option for pancreatic SBRT [13, 14]. These systems differ in beam delivery, image guidance, and adaptive capabilities, influencing their suitability for pancreatic SBRT.

Existing studies have explored the dosimetric performance of different radiation therapy platforms in pancreatic SBRT, focusing on C-arm linear accelerators (e.g., Trilogy), Halcyon, and HT. For instance, Song et al. compared HT and Halcyon, revealing that HT delivery provided better dose homogeneity (HI) and OARs sparing, while Halcyon’s O-ring design reduced treatment times by approximately 30% [15]. Similarly, Sarkar B et al. showed that Halcyon outperformed TrueBeam in plan quality and gamma passing rate, though its coplanar beam arrangement may limit conformity for complex target volumes [16]. However, data integrating the Ethos platform into a direct comparison with established C-arm and helical modalities are limited. Specifically, the dosimetric impact of Ethos’s intelligent optimization workflow, relative to conventional manual planning or binary collimation, has not been fully characterized in the context of pancreatic SBRT.

This study addresses this gap by performing a simultaneous dosimetric and radiobiological evaluation of TrueBeam, Halcyon, Ethos, and HT. While ablative dose escalation is increasingly pursued, selecting a consistent prescription is essential for objective platform comparison. In this study, 35 Gy in 5 fractions was selected as a standardized baseline to enable a direct evaluation of intrinsic planning capabilities. This regimen strictly adheres to the consensus guidelines established by the American Society for Radiation Oncology (ASTRO) (recommending 33–40 Gy) [17]. The findings will help optimize SBRT protocols, and guide clinical decision-making.

Materials and methods

Patient cohort

This retrospective study was conducted to collect data from 18 pancreatic cancer patients who received SBRT at the Shandong Cancer Prevention and Treatment Research Institute between January 2023 and March 2025. This study was approved was granted by the Institutional Review Board of the Shandong Cancer Hospital (SDTHEC2023010027), and written informed consent was waived. The patient inclusion criteria were as follows: (I) histopathological confirmation of locally advanced pancreatic cancer, (II) inoperable pancreatic cancer, (III) underwent SBRT in our institution. CT simulation and contouring

For CT simulation, all patients were performed with head-first supine position on the computed tomography (CT) scaner (Siemens Healthcare, Forchheim, Germany) with a vacuum-formed cushion to ensure consistent and accurate positioning throughout both the imaging and treatment processes. Four-dimensional with a slice thickness of 3 mm was performed. All CT scan images were exported to the Eclipse Treatment Planning System (TPS, Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, CA, USA; V16.01.10) for contouring and treatment plan design.

An experienced radiation oncologist delineated the gross target volume (GTV) and the OARs for patients based on CT images, following the Radiation Therapy Oncology Group (RTOG) guidelines [18, 19]. To account for patient uncertainty in positioning, the planning target volume (PTV) was generated by expanding the GTV with a uniform 3–5 mm margin. A senior radiation oncologist reviewed the contours of critical structures, including the spinal cord, bowel, liver, stomach, duodenum and bilateral kidneys, to ensure that these structures were properly considered in the treatment plan.

SBRT treatment planning

For each patient, four SBRT treatment plans were generated, utilizing the TrueBeam, Halcyon, helical tomotherapy (HT), and Ethos delivery platforms. The treatment was prescribed to a total dose of 35 Gy in 5 fractions. This dose level was specifically selected to provide a consistent dosimetric benchmark, as it falls within the 33–40 Gy range recommended by the ASTRO for pancreatic SBRT [17]. This dose level selected as a standardized baseline to enable cross-platform comparison rather than to reflect contemporary ablative SBRT practice. We recognize that this conservative prescription may underestimate inter-platform differences that would likely become more pronounced in the OAR-limited environment of ablative dose escalation. All plans were designed with the dual objectives of ensuring at least 95% of the PTV was covered by the prescription dose and maintaining the doses to all OARs as low as reasonably achievable (ALARA). Planning objectives and dose constraints for target volume and organs at risk

The treatment plans for the TrueBeam (Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, CA, USA) and Halcyon (Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, CA, USA) platforms were generated within the Eclipse TPS. A volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT) technique was employed for plan design, utilizing three full coplanar arcs for both platforms. Plan optimization was performed using the progressive resolution optimization (PRO) algorithm, and dose calculation was conducted with the analytic anisotropic algorithm (AAA) at a calculation grid size of 2.5 mm. Both plans were configured to utilize 6 MV photon beams, with the dose rates set to 600 MU/min for TrueBeam and 800 MU/min for Halcyon.

For the Ethos platform, plan generation was performed using the dedicated Ethos TPS (version 02.01.00; Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, CA, USA). The plan design similarly employed a VMAT technique with three full coplanar arcs. Dose calculation was managed by the Acuros XB algorithm, configured with a 2.5 mm dose calculation grid. The plan utilized a 6 MV photon beam, delivered at a dose rate of 800 MU/min.

For HT platform, all plans were generated on the dedicated TomoTherapy TPS (Precision version 3.5.0.0, Accuray Incorporated, Sunnyvale, CA, USA). The plan design was characterized by a continuous helical delivery pattern, which incorporates dynamic jaw technology with a primary 2.5 cm field width and cranial-caudal dynamic narrowing to 1 cm, further enhancing longitudinal dose conformity and sharp dose fall-off at the superior and inferior target borders. Dose calculation was performed using a fine calculation grid setting. Initial key machine parameters were configured as follows: a field width of 2.5 cm, a pitch of 0.287, and a modulation factor of 2.8. Dose calculation was performed using the collapsed cone convolution (CCC) algorithm. A 6 MV flattening-filter-free (FFF) photon beam was utilized for all deliveries.

Evaluation of physical dosimetric and biological metrics

For dosimetric evaluation of the PTV, parameters including maximum dose (Dmax), minimum dose (Dmin), dose received by 98% (D98%), 50% (D50%), and 2% (D2%) of the target volume, volume receiving 35 Gy (V35Gy), and mean dose (Dmean) were analyzed. These parameters were used to evaluate the dose coverage and overall dose distribution within the target volume. D2cm (the maximum dose at any point located 2 cm beyond the PTV boundary) and R50 (defined as the ratio of the 50% prescription isodose volume to the PTV volume) was used to evaluate the dose gradient outside the target region [21]. It should be emphasized that EUD is employed here as a comparative biological descriptor for plan evaluation rather than a clinical predictor of normal tissue complication probability (NTCP) or clinical toxicity. Given the BED of the prescribed regimen (35 Gy in 5 fractions), differences in EUD should be interpreted as indicators of the platform’s optimization capability to spare healthy tissue, rather than implying definite reductions in late toxicity rates.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess normality of continuous variables. Paired comparisons between the four platforms were conducted using Student’s paired t-test for normally distributed data or the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for non-normally distributed data. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation unless otherwise stated.

Discussion

This study represents the first direct dosimetric and radiobiological comparison of four contemporary radiotherapy platforms - TrueBeam (C-arm linac), Halcyon (O-ring gantry systems), Ethos (adaptive-capable O-ring gantry system), and helical tomotherapy (HT) - for SBRT in pancreatic cancer using a uniform 35 Gy in 5 fractions prescription and identical planning constraints. All platforms achieved clinically acceptable plans, but systematic differences emerged that consistently favoured Ethos, particularly in parallel organ sparing (kidneys), bowel/stomach sparing, and intermediate-dose spillage (R50, D2cm) control. These advantages persisted despite the inherent anatomical constraints in pancreatic tumors, where the target is often inseparable from the duodenal wall. In pancreatic SBRT - where target and duodenum are frequently inseparable - such advantages are clinically consequential because the dominant barrier to BED advantage is almost never PTV coverage but rather OARs proximity and day-to-day anatomical volatility. In this sense, the comparisons here should be interpreted less as a contest of “plan acceptability” and more as a test of each platform’s capacity for OAR-limited ablative treatment.

Across all platforms, target coverage and homogeneity were essentially equivalent, underscoring that modern inverse planning systems reliably meet basic ICRU-based objectives even in complex abdominal sites. The real differentiators emerged in conformity and gradient behaviour. The coplanar or near-coplanar linac-based systems (TrueBeam, Halcyon, Ethos) achieved tighter conformity and lower intermediate-dose spillage than HT. The larger R50 and lower CI for HT align with prior abdominal SBRT work [25] and reflect a structural trade-off inherent to continuous helical delivery and binary MLCs: superior longitudinal uniformity is obtained by distributing intermediate dose more widely, reducing gradient sharpness. This contrasts with the recent work of Watcharawipha et al. [26], who reported comparable plan quality across different SBRT techniques for liver tumors. This divergence is likely attributable to anatomical differences: unlike the relatively large liver parenchyma, the pancreas is tightly bounded by the duodenum, imposing stricter gradient constraints (R50 and D2cm) that magnify the mechanical advantages of coplanar platform over HT.

Importantly, this should not be interpreted as a universal inadequacy of HT; rather, it highlights that HT’s performance is highly field-width dependent. The inferior gradient metrics of HT in the present work must be interpreted in the context of field width selection. We employed the commonly used 2.5 cm field width setting, which balances modulation capability with delivery efficiency. Multiple studies have shown that reducing field width to 1.0 cm significantly steepens dose fall-off and lowers OARs mean doses in pancreatic and liver SBRT, albeit at the expense of 2–3 fold prolongation of treatment time and increased sensitivity to intrafraction motion [27, 28]. This trade-off is well-documented across various disease sites. For instance, Zhang et al. [29] demonstrated in nasopharyngeal carcinoma that narrower jaw widths (1.0 cm) significantly improve OAR sparing and dose conformity compared to 2.5 cm. Similarly, Watcharawipha et al. [30] reported superior conformity indices with smaller field widths in intracranial stereotactic radiosurgery. However, both studies noted that these dosimetric gains come at the cost of substantially prolonged treatment times. Consequently, our specific use of the 2.5 cm setting explains the comparatively weaker gradient metrics observed for HT in this study, reflecting a clinical prioritization of delivery efficiency over maximal theoretical conformity.In institutions where beam-on time > 15 min is clinically prohibitive, 2.5 cm remains the pragmatic standard; our results therefore reflect real-world HT performance rather than its theoretical optimum. Future HT comparisons should explicitly report field width, as 1 cm configurations may narrow or even reverse the gradient disadvantage observed here.

Ethos and Halcyon share the same O-ring hardware and beam model, yet Ethos systematically outperformed Halcyon across nearly all OARs endpoints. This divergence is best explained by two interacting factors. First, algorithmic fidelity: Ethos plans were calculated with a rigorously validated Acuros XB configuration, whereas Halcyon planning in much of the literature (and historically in many clinics) relies on AAA, which is less robust in heterogeneous abdominal paths containing gastric air, bowel gas, and vertebral bone [31, 32]. AXB’s linear Boltzmann transport equation solution yields 2–8% lower doses in low-density lung and air cavities and 3–5% higher bone doses compared to AAA in hypofractionated regimes - differences that compound in abdominal SBRT where beam paths traverse variable gastric/stomach air and vertebral bone. In studies directly comparing AXB vs. AAA on identical Halcyon plans, AXB reduced PTV D2% hot spots by 3–5% and OAR near-maximum doses, mirroring the Ethos-Halcyon gap observed here [33].

Second, and likely more decisive, is the optimization paradigm. Manual planning on Halcyon/TrueBeam depends on iterative human steering and inevitably carries planner-dependent variability. Ethos employs GPU-accelerated, knowledge-based Intelligence Optimization Engine (IOE) workflows that jointly optimise fluence and deliverable segmentation using predictive DVH priors [13]. In anatomically constrained sites like locally advanced pancreatic cancer (LAPC) SBRT, where small weighting differences can shift duodenal hotspots by several Gy, automation is not merely a convenience but a mechanism for consistently reaching the Pareto-efficient frontier. The near equivalence of TrueBeam and Halcyon (both manual) contrasted with Ethos superiority supports the interpretation that KBP-driven optimisation confers a genuine dosimetric advantage under identical constraints. Multiple multi-institutional validations of automated/knowledge-based planning (KBP) in pancreatic and liver SBRT have demonstrated 15–30% reduction in planner-dependent variability, 1–3 Gy lower OAR doses, and equivalent or superior plan quality compared to expert manual plans, with planning time reduced from hours to minutes [34, 35]. The near-identical performance of TrueBeam and Halcyon in our dataset versus the systematic Ethos superiority provides indirect but compelling evidence that automated IOE optimization confers a genuine dosimetric edge in this anatomically complex site [36].

The most transformative attribute of Ethos - daily online adaptation - was not exploited in the present planning study, as all platforms used baseline anatomy simulations. This represents both a limitation and the most exciting implication of our findings. Pancreatic tumors exhibit median interfraction duodenal displacement of 8–12 mm and gastric volume variations > 200% between fractions, rendering 30–50% of non-adaptive plans constraint - violating on random treatment days [36]. Prospective series of CBCT-based online-adaptive SBRT (directly analogous to Ethos workflow) report re-optimisation in 70–90% of fractions, yielding cumulative duodenal D0.1cc reductions of 5–10 Gy EQD₂, grade ≥ 3 toxicity rates < 8%, and 2-year local control 85–95% even at ablative doses (50 Gy/5 fx) [36]. MR-guided adaptive platforms have achieved similar outcomes with even lower toxicity (< 5% grade ≥ 3 GI), but at substantially higher cost and infrastructure burden [37]. The dosimetric superiority of Ethos observed here on baseline anatomy therefore likely underestimates its true clinical advantage when daily adaptation is activated.

Radiobiological modelling corroborated the physical dose findings, demonstrating that Ethos achieved the lowest EUD in seven of eight structures - most notably in the kidneys (Δ3–5 Gy EQD₂ vs. HT) and bowel. These reductions in Equivalent Uniform Dose (EUD) suggest meaningful biological sparing and are of particular relevance in the context of improving systemic therapy, which continues to extend median survival beyond 24 months in LAPC [38]. Consequently, the clinical value of a platform like Ethos is better framed by its ability to compress normal-tissue risks - addressing the two dominant survivorship-limiting toxicities, late renal impairment and GI injury - while preserving a pathway for future BED escalation, rather than merely satisfying current conservative prescription goals.

A comparison with other emerging platforms helps contextualize these findings. Systems such as CyberKnife - excluded from the present analysis due to limited clinical availability - can achieve dose gradient quality comparable to Ethos in pancreatic SBRT, leveraging non-isocentric and non-coplanar delivery; however, this comes at the cost of significantly prolonged beam-on time [24]. In contrast, MR-Linac platforms offer superior soft-tissue visualization and real-time gating, consistently maintaining duodenal sparing in over 90% of adaptive fractions, though at the expense of substantially reduced patient throughput and considerable infrastructure requirements [39]. Ethos thus occupies a pragmatic middle ground, delivering much of the dosimetric advantage of online adaptation without MR-level complexity or cost. From a systems perspective, this suggests that workflow-integrated adaptation combined with knowledge-based planning optimization may yield broader population-level benefits than incremental hardware advances alone.

This study has several limitations that should be considered. Its retrospective, single-institution design and modest cohort size (N = 18) may affect the generalizability of the findings. The use of fixed-dose regimens rather than biologically escalated dosing limits assessment of the technology in its most relevant clinical context. Additionally, the absence of intrafraction motion simulation beyond gating and lack of independent, blinded plan quality review represent notable methodological constraints. While planner experience bias was partly mitigated by protocolized objectives, the comparison between automated Ethos planning and manual Halcyon/TrueBeam optimization may inherently favour Ethos - though this arguably reflects a genuine clinical advantage of automated planning rather than an artificial mismatch. Conversely, excluding online adaptation likely underestimates Ethos’s full potential. Importantly, dosimetric superiority does not guarantee improved clinical outcomes; prospective multi‑institutional validation with hard endpoints (PFS, OS, late toxicity, quality of life) and blinded plan review remains essential before drawing definitive conclusions regarding outcome superiority.

Finally, we acknowledge that the prescription utilized in this study (35 Gy in 5 fractions) is conservative compared to emerging ablative regimens, such as the 50 Gy protocol validated in the prospective MRI-guided SMART trial [40]. However, the inter-platform differences observed in our analysis would likely be more pronounced under such dose-escalated conditions. While the gradient requirements for a 35 Gy plan are stringent but achievable, ablative planning (e.g., delivering 50 Gy to the target while maintaining duodenal constraints < 33 Gy) imposes significantly stricter physical fall-off demands. Given that Ethos achieved the lowest Dmax and D5cc for the duodenum and bowel among all platforms in this study, its optimization capabilities appear well-suited to meet these heightened challenges.

In conclusion, the Ethos platform demonstrated the most favorable dosimetric and radiobiological profile for pancreatic SBRT among contemporary platforms, achieving superior kidney and bowel sparing. This advantage stems from a synergistic combination of accurate Acuros XB dose calculation, knowledge-based planning driven by GPU-accelerated optimization, and - most critically - the inherent capacity for daily online adaptation, even when not explicitly utilized in this study. These findings provide robust mechanistic justification for prioritizing adaptive O-ring platforms in future pancreatic SBRT protocols, particularly those pursuing ablative BED escalation or renal-preserving strategies in the era of prolonged systemic therapy. Looking ahead, randomized trials integrating adaptive capability with functional imaging (e.g., perfusion CT for hypoxia targeting) [38] are warranted to translate these dosimetric advantages into measurable improvements in survival and quality of life.

Conclusion

In conclusion, under a standardized prescription (35 Gy in 5 fractions), Ethos demonstrated the most favorable performance for critical OARs sparing - particularly for kidneys and bowel - among the four platforms, despite yielding slightly higher gradient indices (D2cm) compared to TrueBeam and Halcyon. While these findings are specific to non-adaptive standard dose planning and may not directly extrapolate to ablative regimens or MRI-guided strategies, the superior OARs sparing capabilities of Ethos suggest it possesses a strong dosimetric foundation to support future safe dose escalation in high-throughput clinics.
