Macrophage INSIG1 deficiency drives psoriasiform dermatitis via the SREBP2-STAT1 axis
Authors: Xiangzheng Li, Yu Zang, Xi Li, Tiancheng Sheng, Shaowen Liu, Leyi Chen, Xiaohe Xu, Lin Wang, Rongzhuo Long, Mengfan Zhang, Xu Chen, Cheng Tan, Lei Qiang, Yuan He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPsoriasis is an autoimmune skin disease, and its pathogenesis remains incompletely understood. As central drivers of psoriasis pathogenesis that bridge innate and adaptive immunity, macrophages are known to be modulated in their function by insulin-induced gene 1 (INSIG1), a regulator of cholesterol synthesis. Bioinformatic analysis of human psoriatic lesions reveals a significant negative correlation between INSIG1 expression and pro-inflammatory cytokine levels (IL-23, IL-17A). Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and immunofluorescence confirm significantly reduced INSIG1 expression specifically in macrophages from psoriatic skin. In vivo, myeloid-specific Insig1 deficiency exacerbates imiquimod (IMQ)-induced psoriasiform dermatitis, characterized by increased epidermal thickening and immune infiltration. By activating sterol regulatory element-binding protein 2 (SREBP2), INSIG1 deficiency leads to the transcriptional upregulation of STAT1, which drives M1 macrophage polarization and ultimately amplifies psoriatic inflammation. This study reveals an important role of macrophage INSIG1 in regulating inflammatory responses and lipid metabolism, providing insight into the pathogenesis of psoriasis and potential therapeutic targets.Subject terms: Psoriasis, Monocytes and macrophages

Introduction

Psoriasis is a systemic immune-mediated disorder driven by a self-sustaining cycle of pathogenic crosstalk between innate and adaptive immunity1–3. Although biologic therapies targeting the IL-23/IL-17 axis represent a therapeutic breakthrough, persistent disease recurrence in a subset of patients and the well-established link to metabolic comorbidities highlight the existence of uncharted and unexplored pathogenic drivers4–10.

Several metabolic disorders associated with psoriasis include obesity, hyperlipidemia, and insulin resistance, which are underpinned by systemic dysregulation of lipid metabolism and cholesterol homeostasis11–13. This dysmetabolic state is reflected locally within psoriatic lesions, where spatial proteomic and biochemical analyses demonstrate significant upregulation of cholesterol synthesis pathways, elevated free cholesterol levels, and disrupted cellular cholesterol homeostasis14–17. These disruptions in cholesterol homeostasis are key drivers of the local immune dysregulation and chronic inflammation characteristic of psoriatic lesions. Macrophages are regarded as a key immune cell population affected by this dysmetabolic microenvironment. In fact, psoriasis is characterized by increased frequencies of M1-like monocytes in the peripheral blood and enhanced infiltration of M1 macrophages within lesional skin18–21. While altered cholesterol levels have been implicated in macrophage polarization, where elevated cholesterol promotes a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype22–25, growing evidence indicates that key proteins regulating cholesterol homeostasis act as central mediators of this immune-metabolic crosstalk. Several critical regulators, including liver X receptor α (LXRα), ATP-binding cassette transporters A1 and G1 (ABCA1 and ABCG1), and insulin-induced gene 1 (INSIG1), are themselves modulated by cholesterol levels and may exert non-canonical, lipid-independent effects on macrophage function26. For instance, LXRα antagonizes nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) signaling to suppress M1 polarization27, while ABCA1 and ABCG1 modulate toll-like receptor (TLR) signaling and efferocytosis, thereby indirectly shaping macrophage activation and inflammatory responses28,29.

Of these regulators, INSIG1 appears to be a promising candidate in the intersection of cholesterol metabolism and macrophage immune responses. INSIG1 is an endoplasmic reticulum-resident protein that serves as a central regulator of cellular cholesterol and lipid homeostasis by anchoring and inhibiting the activation of sterol regulatory element-binding protein (SREBP) transcription factors30. Beyond its canonical metabolic function, emerging evidence indicates that INSIG1 also modulates inflammatory responses in macrophages. For example, INSIG1 is essential for the stimulator of interferon genes (STING) pathway-mediated antiviral immunity and suppresses the NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain-containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome activation and IL-1β production31,32. Moreover, reduced INSIG1 expression activates SREBP2, leading to upregulated expression of inflammatory cytokines in macrophages, such as IL-1β, C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 10 (CXCL10), and CXCL11, independent of the cholesterol synthesis pathway33,34. Besides, a study reported a negative association between INSIG1 and the pathogenesis of psoriasis35. Still, its cell-specific function, particularly within macrophages, and its precise role in psoriasis pathogenesis remain poorly defined.

Here, we investigate the role of macrophage INSIG1, an important linker between cholesterol synthesis and inflammation, which plays a pivotal role in psoriasis pathogenesis. It was demonstrated that Insig1 is negatively correlated to psoriasis-associated key cytokines (IL-23A and IL-17), and macrophage-specific Insig1 deficiency exacerbates IMQ-induced psoriasiform dermatitis in mice. The mechanism underlying this phenomenon was found to involve the activation of SREBP2 by INSIG1 deficiency in macrophages, which directly promotes STAT1 transcription and subsequently drives both innate and adaptive immune responses, independent of cholesterol-directed inflammation. Our study provides insights into the distinctive role of INSIG1 in immune regulation within psoriasis and suggests a potential therapeutic target, not only for this disease but also perhaps for other autoimmune skin diseases.

Results

Reduced INSIG1 expression was observed in antigen-presenting cells from psoriatic lesions

Although cholesterol homeostasis is disrupted and immune responses are dysregulated in the psoriatic microenvironment, the role of INSIG1 in this context remains largely unexplored. To initially assess the potential involvement of INSIG1 in disease pathogenesis, we first analyzed the correlation between INSIG1 expression and psoriasis signature genes using bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data (GSE54456). The results revealed negative correlations of INSIG1 with IL23A (r = −0.5055), IL17A (r = −0.5231), and IL12B (r = −0.4861) expression levels (Fig. 1a, Supplementary Fig. 1a), suggesting INSIG1 might negatively correlate with psoriasis pathogenesis. To identify the key cellular target for subsequent functional studies, single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) data were utilized to compare INSIG1 expression level change across cell types in psoriatic versus healthy skin. The cells were categorized into 9 clusters: M1 and M2 macrophages, melanocytes, endothelial cells, NK cells, dendritic cells (DCs), Treg cells, T cells, and keratinocytes (KC) (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Fig. 1b). As a functional paralog of INSIG1 in cholesterol regulation, INSIG2 expression was also profiled across cell types. Among these, macrophages and DCs from psoriatic skin exhibited pronounced downregulation of INSIG1, and INSIG2 showed a low expression level with no significant change in M1 and M2 macrophages compared to the HD group (Fig. 1c). This observation was also verified by another scRNA-seq dataset (GSE162183) (Supplementary Fig. 1c). Some lipid metabolism-related genes (e.g., ABCA1, APOE, and ABCC1) were upregulated, whereas other genes (e.g., SCAP, HMGCR, LDLR) remained unaltered in macrophages (Supplementary Fig. 1d). Other studies have reported that during inflammatory skin diseases such as psoriasis, circulating monocytes act as plastic sensors of inflammation36,37. After recruitment into inflamed skin, these cells infiltrate the dermis and differentiate into macrophages, thereby contributing to chronic inflammation and psoriatic lesions36,37. Therefore, INSIG1 alterations observed in lesional macrophages may already be reflected at circulating precursors. We next evaluated its expression in patient-derived clinical samples. Consistent with skin macrophages’ scRNA-seq data, peripheral blood CD14+ monocytes from psoriasis patients exhibited significantly decreased mRNA levels of INSIG1 compared to the HD group (Fig. 1d). Notably, INSIG1 expression showed a significant negative correlation with CD86, a marker of pro-inflammatory monocyte activation (Supplementary Fig. 1e). Immunofluorescence (IF) analysis further demonstrated reduced mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of INSIG1 in CD68⁺ macrophages from psoriatic lesions (Fig. 1e, f). To validate this finding, Imiquimod (IMQ)- and IL-23-induced mouse models of psoriasis were established (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and Ki67 immunohistochemistry (IHC) of skin tissues revealed that both IMQ and IL-23 significantly induced epidermal hyperproliferation and increased skin thickness (Supplementary Fig. 2b–i). Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis further showed elevated expression of inflammatory cytokines (Il12b, Il17a, Il23a, Tnf, Il6, and Il1b) in the skin of these psoriasiform mouse models (Supplementary Fig. 2j, k). All these data suggest IMQ- and IL-23-induced mouse models of psoriasis were successfully established. Next, results from IF staining and flow cytometry (FCM) demonstrated that the MFI of INSIG1 in skin macrophages was significantly reduced in IMQ-treated mice compared with control mice (Fig. 1g, h, Supplementary Fig. 3a, b). Similarly, decreased INSIG1 expression was observed in F4/80⁺ macrophages from IL-23-induced psoriasiform dermatitis mouse models (Supplementary Fig. 3c, d). To further validate scRNA-seq findings, we performed IF staining on skin samples. In healthy skin, melanocytes (Melan A+) were barely detectable, and only a few appeared in the basal layer of psoriatic skin, yet their INSIG1 expression remained extremely weak (Supplementary Fig. 3e). CD11c+ DCs showed low INSIG1 expression in healthy skin but variable expression in psoriatic skin, which is inconsistent with the results from scRNA-seq (Supplementary Fig. 3f). Collectively, these results suggest that INSIG1 is downregulated in macrophages from psoriatic dermatitis.

Macrophage INSIG1 deficiency exacerbates the IMQ-induced psoriasiform dermatitis

To investigate the role of macrophage INSIG1 in psoriasis pathogenesis, we first used the Lyz2-Cre system to generate mice with myeloid-specific Insig1 deletion (Insig1flox/flox; Lyz2-Cre, Insig1 cKO) (Fig. 2a, b, Supplementary Fig. 4a) and applied IMQ in Insig1 cKO and control mice (Insig1flox/flox, Insig1 WT) to establish a psoriasiform dermatitis mouse model (Fig. 2c). Compared with Insig1 WT controls, Insig1 cKO mice exhibited increased skin scaling, higher psoriasis area and severity index (PASI) scores, and increased epidermal thickness as assessed by H&E and Ki67 IHC staining (Fig. 2d–h, Supplementary Fig. 4b, c). These findings prompted us to investigate whether INSIG1 deficiency alone triggers an inflammatory response without IMQ induction. It has been reported that Aldara vehicle cream contains isostearic acid, which has been reported to trigger low-grade innate immune activation, including IL-1 release38,39. Hence, additional histological analyses were performed on skin tissues from Insig1 WT and Insig1 cKO mice that received no topical treatment (neither control cream nor IMQ). The results revealed no significant differences in overall skin structure or epidermal thickness between genotypes only (Supplementary Fig. 4d–g), indicating that Insig1 deficiency in macrophages can amplify KC proliferation via the secretion of various cytokines after a minimal trigger. Additionally, the spleen-to-body weight ratio, an indirect indicator of systemic inflammation, was significantly increased in Insig1 cKO mice (Supplementary Fig. 4h).

It is known that DCs serve as a major source of IL-23, which consequently maintains Th17 cell function. Meanwhile, γδ T cells are the predominant source of IL-17 in IMQ-induced model40, while Th17 and Th1 cells are considered as the major effector cells in human psoriasis41–43. Hence, we investigated the effects of myeloid-specific Insig1 deficiency on immune effector cells, including DCs, γδ T cells, Th17 cells, and Th1 cells. FCM analysis of mouse skin tissue showed no significant difference in DC infiltration between Insig1 cKO and Insig1 WT mice (Supplementary Fig. 5a, b, Supplementary Data 1a). A significant increase in γδ T cells was observed in the skin of Insig1 cKO mice following IMQ treatment (Fig. 2i, j, Supplementary Data 1b). Furthermore, both IF and FCM analysis demonstrated that Th17 and Th1 cells were also significantly increased in the skin of Insig1 cKO mice under IMQ-induced conditions (Supplementary Fig. 5c–j, Supplementary Data 1c, d). Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELIZA) results revealed correspondingly higher IL-17A levels in skin homogenates from Insig1 cKO mice (Fig. 2k). FCM analysis also showed significantly higher Th17 cell proportions in lymph nodes of Insig1 cKO mice (Fig. 2l, m, Supplementary Data 1e). Together, these data demonstrate that myeloid-specific Insig1 deficiency promotes the accumulation of lymphatic effector cells in lesional skin. Given that the epidermis thickness of Insig1 cKO mice was significantly increased compared to that of Insig1 WT mice, we next examined the expression of KC proliferation-related cytokines (S100a9, Krt17, Lcn2) and inflammatory factors (Il36g, Il23a) by RT-qPCR. These cytokines were significantly upregulated in Insig1 cKO mice following IMQ induction (Supplementary Fig. 5k). Cytokine profiling of vehicle groups between genotypes revealed detectable but low levels of various cytokines associated with KC proliferation and inflammation (e.g., Tnf, Il1b, Il6, Il23a, and Cxcl10), and these levels were markedly elevated in the IMQ-treated groups (Supplementary Fig. 5l).

Although the knockout efficiency of myeloid-specific Insig1 deletion with the LysM-Cre system is highest in mature macrophage44, the potential “off-target” effect on neutrophils and DCs cannot be overlooked. To exclude gene recombination by the LysM-Cre system in DCs, bone marrow cells from Insig1 cKO mice were induced to differentiate into DCs in vitro (Supplementary Fig. 6a). The FCM analysis results showed Insig1 deficiency inhibited DC differentiation and maturation (Supplementary Fig. 6b, c, Supplementary Data 1f), which is contrary to Insig1 deficiency-worsened inflammatory phenotype. Given the high specificity of Mrp8 for neutrophils45, to specifically delete Insig1 in neutrophils, Insig1flox/flox, Mrp8-Cre (Insig1 cKOneu) transgenic mice were established to employed as an IMQ-induced psoriasiform dermatitis mouse model (designated Insig1 WT and Insig1 cKOneu, respectively) (Supplementary Fig. 6d). The results suggested that no differences in psoriatic symptoms, epidermal thickness, IL-17A levels, or immune cell infiltration (Th17 cells and neutrophils) were observed between the IMQ-induced Insig1 cKOneu and Insig1 WT psoriasiform dermatitis mouse models (Supplementary Fig. 6e–l). Together, these findings demonstrate that macrophage-specific Insig1 deficiency aggravates IMQ-induced psoriasiform dermatitis, with enhanced KC hyperproliferation and infiltration of γ/δ T, Th1, and Th17 cells.

INSIG1 deficiency promotes M1 polarization to facilitate T cell-mediated function

To investigate how macrophage Insig1 deficiency impacts T cell function, we assessed the intrinsic impact of INSIG1 deficiency on macrophage polarization. The infiltration of macrophage subtypes (M1 and M2) in mouse skin tissues was evaluated. FCM and IF analysis revealed that a significant increase was observed in M1 macrophage infiltration in the skin lesions of Insig1 cKO mice compared with the Insig1 WT group (Fig. 3a, b, Supplementary Fig. 7a, b and Supplementary Data 1g), whereas no significant differences were seen in M2 macrophage infiltration (Supplementary Fig. 7c–f, Supplementary Data 1c). Consistent with the observation of an increase in CD86+ M1-like monocytes in the peripheral blood of psoriasis patients18,19, peripheral blood M1-like monocytes were elevated in Insig1 cKO mice after IMQ treatment (Fig. 3c, d, Supplementary Data 1h). Similarly, Insig1 cKO bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) exhibited an increasing trend in CD86 expression with or without LPS + IFN-γ stimulation (Fig. 3e, f, Supplementary Data 1i). Expression of M1-associated genes, including Nos2, Il12b, Il1b, Il6, and Cxcl10, was significantly upregulated in Insig1 cKO BMDMs compared to Insig1 WT group, especially after LPS + IFN-γ stimulation (Fig. 3g, Supplementary Fig. 7g). To validate these findings further, we established INSIG1 knockdown THP-1 (THP-1.shINSIG1) (Supplementary Fig. 7h). INSIG1 knockdown in THP-1 cells increased pro-inflammatory gene expression (NOS2, IL12B, IL1B, IL6, CXCL10) and surface marker CD86 levels compared to control macrophages (THP-1.shCON) (Fig. 3h, Supplementary Fig. 7i, j). Conversely, INSIG1 overexpression suppressed the expression of the above relative genes (Supplementary Fig. 7k). Notably, CXCL10 can recruit lymphocytes, including Th1 and Th17 cells, to inflammatory sites, thereby amplifying local immune responses46,47. The cytokines (e.g., IL-23, IL-6) secreted by macrophages, along with their antigen-presenting functions, can promote the differentiation and activation of T cells (e.g., Th1 and Th17 cells)48–50. To directly evaluate the functional impact of INSIG1-deficient macrophages on T cell activation, a mixed lymphocyte reaction (MLR) assay was performed by co-culturing CD4⁺ T cells with Peritoneal macrophages (pMs) from Insig1 WT or Insig1 cKO mice (Fig. 3i). Insig1 cKO pMs induced higher CD4+ IL-17A+ T cell proportions and elevated IL-23/IL-17A levels compared to Insig1 WT macrophages (Fig. 3j, k). Together, these data collectively suggest that INSIG1 deficiency promotes M1 macrophage polarization and T cell–mediated function in psoriasis.

Decreased INSIG1 upregulates STAT1 expression to drive M1 macrophage polarization

Given that INSIG1 is a key regulator of cellular cholesterol homeostasis, we speculated that its reduction could potentially alter the activity of transcription factors governing inflammatory responses (e.g., STAT1, NF-κB) or cellular metabolic pathways such as glycolysis or oxidative phosphorylation known to influence macrophage polarization51,52. To identify the downstream mediators affected by INSIG1, RNA-seq was performed on THP-1.shINSIG1-derived macrophages. The data analysis revealed significant upregulation of multiple genes in INSIG1-knockdown cells, including chemokines (CXCL10, CXCL11, CCL8, and CCL2), the M1 surface costimulatory marker (CD86), and signaling molecules (STAT1, TLR4) critical for inflammatory responses (Fig. 4a). Gene ontology (GO) analysis revealed enrichment for IFN-γ-related pathways (Fig. 4b), which was supported by gene set enrichment analysis (Fig. 4c). Given that IFN-γ primarily activates the transcription factor STAT1, which plays a critical role in M1 macrophage polarization, STAT1 was therefore selected for further investigation. To elucidate the potential correlation between INSIG1 and STAT1, the expression profiles of STAT1 target genes were examined in macrophages with INSIG1 knockdown. Our results revealed that a broad range of STAT1-regulated genes were potentially significantly upregulated in INSIG1-deficient macrophages (Fig. 4d). Based on these findings, further STAT1 mRNA expression levels were assessed using RT-qPCR in both primary Insig1-deficient macrophages and THP-1.shINSIG1 cells. The results confirmed that STAT1 mRNA was increased in these cells (Fig. 4e, f). while overexpression of INSIG1 suppressed STAT1 mRNA expression in HEK293T cells (Fig. 4g). Importantly, NF-κB p65, another key transcription factor in M1 macrophage polarization, showed no significant change in protein levels in Insig1-deficient pMs, THP-1.shINSIG1, or HEK293T.GFP-INSIG1 compared to their control cells (Supplementary Fig. 8a–c). Even under LPS + IFN-γ stimulation conditions, the phosphorylation status of NF-κB p65 remained comparable between THP-1.shINSIG1 and THP-1.shCON (Supplementary Fig. 8d, e). However, under LPS + IFN-γ stimulation, STAT1 mRNA levels were further elevated in INSIG1-deficient macrophages compared to controls (Supplementary Fig. 8f). To determine whether the increased mRNA expression translated to changes at the protein level, we examined STAT1 protein abundance and activation. INSIG1 deficiency increased the abundance of both total and phosphorylated STAT1 (p-STAT1), but did not alter the p-STAT1/STAT1 ratio regardless of LPS + IFN-γ treatment (Fig. 4h, i). This points out that the increase in p-STAT1 levels is a result of the rise in total protein levels. A similar effect was observed in INSIG1-knockdown THP-1 cells, with no significant difference in the p-STAT1/STAT1 ratio (Fig. 4j, k). To confirm that INSIG1 negatively regulates STAT1, we overexpressed INSIG1 in HEK293T cells. STAT1 and p-STAT1 levels were reduced in HEK293T.GFP-INSIG1 and THP-1.GFP-INISG1 cells compared to control cells, while no notable difference was observed in the p-STAT1/STAT1 ratio (Fig. 4l, m, Supplementary Fig. 8g, h). Enhanced STAT1 nuclear translocation was observed in INSIG1-knockdown cells after LPS + IFN-γ stimulation (Fig. 4n). These findings demonstrate that INSIG1 drives M1 polarization via STAT1, not NF-κB signaling. To further verify INSIG1-mediated STAT1 expression in macrophages in a psoriatic environment, FCM and IF analyses of mouse skin tissues showed that the MFI of p-STAT1 and STAT1 in F4/80+ cells were significantly higher in psoriatic skin lesions of Insig1 cKO mice compared to Insig1 WT mice (Supplementary Fig. 9a–i, Supplementary Data 1j, k). The ratio of p-STAT1 to STAT1 in F4/80+ cells did not differ significantly between genotypes (Supplementary Fig. 9d, i). These results indicate that Insig1 deficiency leads to increased STAT1 expression in psoriatic lesional macrophages, thereby enhancing their activation. Since our previous sequencing data identified INSIG1 as a potential regulator of M1 macrophage polarization through the IFN-γ signaling pathway (Fig. 4b), similar effects were observed with IFN-γ alone (Supplementary Fig. 9j–l), indicating that INSIG1 primarily regulates STAT1 expression rather than activation. Together, these data support that INSIG1 regulates macrophage polarization primarily by controlling STAT1 expression, rather than affecting STAT1 activation status.

INSIG1-mediated modulation of STAT1 is independent of its canonical function in cholesterol

Following the above findings, we next sought to determine whether this regulation occurs solely through a direct transcriptional mechanism or additionally through altered cholesterol levels. Based on reports that INSIG1 loss activates SREBP2 and upregulates cholesterol biosynthetic genes (e.g., HMGCR, HMGCS1) and increases cholesterol production53,54, the changes in cellular cholesterol levels were assessed to influence STAT1 expression and activation in INSIG1-deficient macrophages (Fig. 5a). Filipin III staining as a cholesterol indicator showed elevated free cholesterol levels in THP-1.shINSIG1 cells compared to THP-1.shCON controls (Fig. 5b–e). The results showed that intracellular free cholesterol was elevated 1.3-fold in INSIG1-deficient macrophages (Fig. 5c, e). Consistently, Insig1 cKO pMs exhibited similar cholesterol accumulation (Supplementary Fig. 10a, b). These findings are consistent with previous studies demonstrating that INSIG1 deficiency or activation of its downstream signaling promotes increased cellular cholesterol levels55,56. To assess whether INSIG1 deficiency induces free cholesterol and cholesterol metabolic alterations, we performed untargeted lipidomic profiling in THP-1.shCON and THP-1.shINSIG1 cells, with or without phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA)-induced macrophage differentiation. Principal component analysis (PCA) showed that INSIG1 also had a modest effect on lipid changes (3.48%), while the most significant change in lipid was in PMA-induced THP-1 cell differentiation (85.9%) (Supplementary Fig. 10c). A clustering heatmap of differentially abundant lipids across the four groups revealed which lipid classes changed the most. From most to least changed, these were glycerophospholipids (GP), sphingolipids (SP), glycerolipids (GL), fatty acids (FA) and sterols (ST) (Fig. 5f, Supplementary Fig. 10d). With or without PMA treatment, cholesterol levels in INSIG1-knockdown THP-1 cells remained consistently 1.3-fold higher than in control cells (Fig. 5g, h, Supplementary Fig. 10e, f). To determine whether a 1.3-fold increase in cholesterol is sufficient to induce STAT1 expression, THP-1 cells were treated with water-soluble cholesterol (β-cyclodextrin-cholesterol, β-CD-chol) at varying concentrations for different durations. The condition of 0.5 μg/mL β-CD-chol stimulation for 3 h was identified as inducing a 1.3-fold increase in intracellular cholesterol levels in THP-1 cells (Supplementary Fig. 10g–j). Under this condition, no significant changes were observed in either the mRNA or protein levels of STAT1 (Fig. 5i–l, Supplementary Fig. 10k). Therefore, INSIG1 regulates STAT1 independently of changes in cellular cholesterol.

INSIG1 downregulates STAT1 transcription by suppressing SREBP2-mediated activation of the STAT1 promoter

To delineate the mechanism of STAT1 regulation by INSIG1, the stability of STAT1 mRNA was assessed in actinomycin D (Act D)-treated HEK293T cells. No significant differences in STAT1 mRNA decay kinetics were observed between HEK293T.shINSIG1, HEK293T.GFP-INSIG1, or THP-1.shINSIG1 cell pair (Fig. 6a–d, Supplementary Fig. 11a–d), suggesting that the regulation of STAT1 by INSIG1 likely occurs at the transcriptional level. Building on these findings, transcriptional regulatory elements responsible for the upregulation of STAT1 were mapped in INSIG1-deficient cells. Luciferase reporter assays revealed increased activity associated with the region (−1000 to −1500 bp) in the STAT1 promoter (Fig. 6e, Supplementary Fig. 11e). As an ER-resident protein lacking a nuclear localization signal (NLS), INSIG1 cannot undergo active translocation into the nucleus57. Consequently, we postulated that INSIG1 might regulate STAT1 expression through intermediary transcription factors. A systematic interrogation of candidate regulators is performed via integrated bioinformatics analysis using the UCSC Genome Browser, Human Transcription Factor Database (TFDB), and PROMO databases. Among predicted factors (SP1, ATF3, JUN, STAT1, MYB, SREBF2), SREBP2 (encoded by SREBF2) is prioritized (Fig. 6f, g). Knockdown of INSIG1 led to elevated N-SREBP2 levels and reduced ATF3 expression, whereas the activation of SP-1 and c-Jun remained unaffected. (Supplementary Fig. 11f). In addition, the translocation of N-SREBP2 was significantly enhanced upon INSIG1 knockdown (Supplementary Fig. 11g). Upon knockdown or overexpression of SREBF2 (Supplementary Fig. 11h, i), the mRNA expression level of STAT1 correspondingly exhibits a parallel trend (Fig. 6h, i). Luciferase assays then confirmed that SREBP2 overexpression increased STAT1 promoter activity, particularly in the −1000 to −1500 bp region (Fig. 6j). Mutation of predicted SREBP2 binding sites, identified through the JASPAR database, markedly reduced STAT1 promoter activity, and this activity was further attenuated upon SREBP2 knockdown (Fig. 6k, l). Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) sequencing data (GSE129229) from macrophages show a pronounced binding peak for SREBP2 at the STAT1 promoter upon SREBP2 overexpression (Fig. 6m). Electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) further validated direct binding of purified N-SREBP2 to the predicted STAT1 promoter sequences (Fig. 6n, Supplementary Fig. 11j). Thus, INSIG1 deficiency enhances the proteolytic cleavage of SREBP2 in the ER, generating the transcriptionally active fragment N-SREBP2. Subsequently, N-SREBP2 translocates to the nucleus, where it binds to the STAT1 promoter and directly promotes STAT1 transcription. The subsequent increase in STAT1 protein levels drives the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including CXCL10, IL-23, and IL-6 (Fig. 6o).

INSIG1 curbs M1 polarization by downregulating the SREBP2-STAT1 axis

Our above findings identify that INSIG1 and SREBP2 are the critical factors in the regulation of STAT1. To validate STAT1 regulation by the INSIG1/SREBP2 axis, THP-1 cells with double knockdown of INSIG1 and SREBF2 (THP-1.shINSIG1 shSREBF2) were constructed. SREBF2 knockdown significantly reduced both STAT1 mRNA and protein levels. Notably, this reduction was not rescued in double-knockdown macrophages (Fig. 7a–c), indicating that SREBP2 is the primary transcriptional factor mediating the transcription of STAT1 downstream of INSIG1. To further confirm INSIG1 indeed regulated the SREBP2-STAT1 axis, THP-1.shINSIG1-derived macrophages were treated with or without Fatostatin, an inhibitor of SREBP2 translocation. INSIG1 deficiency-mediated STAT1 activation was suppressed by SREBP2 inhibition with Fatostatin, as was CD86 expression (Fig. 7d–f, Supplementary Fig. 12a). Given the reported influence of macrophage-derived cytokines (e.g., TNF, IL-6, IL-1β) mediated by STAT1 signaling on KC proliferation in psoriasis58–60, and the observation of epidermal thickening in Insig1 cKO mice (Fig. 2g), the role of macrophage INSIG1 deficiency in promoting KC proliferation via remodeling of the cutaneous microenvironment was investigated. Hence, TNF, IL-6, and IL-1β levels were quantified by ELISA in the culture supernatant of THP-1.shINSIG1 cells after PMA-induced adherence, in the presence or absence of LPS + IFN-γ stimulation. INSIG1 knockdown significantly increased the secretion of all three cytokines compared with control cells, while LPS + IFN-γ stimulation further enhanced cytokine production (Supplementary Fig. 12b). To model this interaction in vitro, we utilized HaCaT cells, an immortalized human keratinocyte cell line widely used to study epidermal biology and psoriatic pathophysiology. HaCaT cells were treated with THP-1-derived macrophage conditioned medium (CM), and proliferation was assessed by crystal violet staining (Fig. 7g). The results suggested that CM from THP-1.shSREBF2 cells inhibited KC proliferation (Fig. 7h, i). In line with this, the proliferation rate in KC was increased in the CM from THP-1.shINSIG1 cells compared to that from THP-1.shCON cells, which was attenuated by Fatostatin CM (Fig. 7j, k). Similar effects were observed in complementary gain-of-function experiments. CM from INSIG1-overexpressing THP-1 cells suppressed, whereas CM from SREBF2-overexpressing THP-1 cells enhanced HaCaT cell proliferation (Supplementary Fig. 12c–e). In summary, these findings demonstrate that INSIG1 deficiency exacerbates M1 polarization and KC hyperproliferation by enhancing SREBP2-dependent STAT1 transcription.

Discussion

Despite emerging evidence supporting a role for INSIG1 in inflammatory signaling31,32, its influence on macrophage biology in psoriasis is poorly understood. In this study, the INSIG1–SREBP2–STAT1 axis was uncovered as a pivotal signaling pathway that promotes macrophage M1 polarization and fuels inflammation in psoriasis, thereby highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target. Bioinformatic analyses initially revealed a negative correlation between INSIG1 expression and signature cytokines IL-23 and IL-17A in skin tissues. Using several psoriasiform dermatitis mouse models, myeloid-specific INSIG1 deficiency was found to exacerbate disease severity and drive M1 macrophage activation. INSIG1 deficiency promotes M1 polarization by upregulating STAT1 transcription and boosting its protein expression. Notably, SREBP2 activation downstream of INSIG1 is identified as the key driver of STAT1 upregulation.

scRNA-seq data revealed that INSIG1 is highly expressed in macrophages and demonstrated significant alterations in expression between lesional and non-lesional states (Fig. 1), which led us to focus subsequent investigations on myeloid-derived immune effector cell function. Although neutrophils and DCs are known to contribute to psoriasis pathogenesis through their infiltration and correlation with disease severity61,62, neutrophil-specific INSIG1-mediated effects were ruled out in preliminary experiments (Supplementary Fig. 6). It is important to note that the efficiency of loxP-mediated gene recombination driven by LysM-Cre varies markedly among myeloid cell types63–65. with the highest efficiency observed in mature macrophages (approximately 83–98%) and much lower efficiency in splenic DCs (about 16%)44. Consequently, the LysM-Cre line has been widely applied for macrophage-specific gene deletion.

Intriguingly, the discrepancy between the scRNA-seq and IF data for CD11c⁺ DCs is evident. This discrepancy may be explained by two factors. First, the gene sets used to identify DC subsets differ fundamentally between scRNA-seq and IF. Second, there is an inherent discordance between transcriptional and protein expression levels of certain genes. To further exclude the potential DC involvement, we performed preliminary in vitro experiments using bone marrow-derived dendritic cells generated from Insig1flox/flox, LysM-Cre mice (Supplementary Fig. 6a). Interestingly, Insig1 deficiency impaired DC differentiation in this setting (Supplementary Fig. 6b, c), a finding that is not consistent with the exacerbated inflammatory phenotype observed in vivo. This suggests that altered DC function is unlikely to be the primary driver of disease aggravation in Insig1 cKO mice. Nevertheless, future studies employing dendritic cell-specific Cre driver lines (Zbtb46-Cre) to investigate and specifically interrogate dendritic cell function in vivo66.

Interestingly, INSIG1 exhibited a similar expression pattern in peripheral blood monocytes from psoriasis patients, showing lower levels compared to healthy controls (Fig. 1d). This reduction may reflect their pro-inflammatory susceptibility, as circulating monocytes are known to act as plastic sensors of inflammation during psoriatic disease progression37. Upon recruitment into inflamed skin, monocytes infiltrate the dermis and differentiate into macrophages, thereby contributing to chronic inflammation and lesion development36,37. Consistent with prior reports indicating an increase in CD86⁺ monocytes in the peripheral blood of psoriasis patients and elevated M1 macrophage infiltration in psoriatic skin18,19, a significant negative correlation was observed between INSIG1 expression and CD86 levels in circulating monocytes (Supplementary Fig. 1e), suggesting that lower INSIG1 expression in blood monocytes may be associated with a pathogenic macrophage fate following skin infiltration. Of note, whether INSIG1 downregulation in circulating monocytes makes these cells susceptible to pathogenic macrophages after recruitment into inflamed skin remains an important question for future investigation.

Both in vivo and in vitro experiments confirmed that macrophage INSIG1 plays an important role in regulating PsO pathogenesis. Intriguingly, the factors that modulate INSIG1 expression within the psoriatic skin microenvironment and how alterations in INSIG1 modulate downstream signaling in psoriasis remain an important question. In the psoriatic microenvironment, macrophages are exposed to multiple stimuli that exert opposing effects on INSIG1 expression. TNF, a key pathogenic cytokine, markedly suppresses INSIG1 protein levels in THP-1-derived macrophages (Supplementary Fig. 13), consistent with its high abundance in lesions and its known autocrine and paracrine actions. The clinical efficacy of TNF blockade further indicates the potential association between TNF and INSIG167,68. Nevertheless, other stimulators, such as IFN-γ, hydrogen peroxide, and arachidonic acid, significantly upregulate INSIG1 expression, whereas IL-17A alone had no effect. Together, these observations suggest that additional, as-yet-unexplored factors may also contribute to the regulation of INSIG1 expression in the psoriatic microenvironment. On the other hand, while multiple cytokines likely contribute to the enhanced KC proliferation observed in the context of macrophage INSIG1 deficiency (Supplementary Fig. 12b), IL-6 emerges as a particularly strong candidate based on the provided data and existing literature. IL-6 is known to directly promote KC proliferation by activating STAT3 signaling69, and, together with TNF and IL-1β, can form a feedback loop sustaining the IL-23/Th17 axis, indirectly driving proliferation via IL-17 and IL-2270–73. TNF, as we cited previously, is also a master regulator that can induce further IL-6 production and directly enhance KC proliferation74. Our findings confirmed that macrophage INSIG1 plays a functional role in psoriasis pathogenesis, and highlight the complexity of its regulation within the psoriatic microenvironment, with multiple stimuli exerting opposing effects, while also identifying IL-6 as a key downstream mediator linking INSIG1 deficiency to enhanced KC proliferation (Supplementary Fig. 12b, Fig. 7), underscoring the need to further elucidate the regulatory networks and signaling pathways involved.

M1 macrophage polarization is primarily mediated by the activation of STAT1 and NF-κB, which promotes a pro-inflammatory phenotype and the expression of cytokines, including TNF, IL-23, CXCL10, and IL-1β. To delineate the relative contribution of INSIG1 to these key pathways, its effect on both STAT1 and NF-κB was assessed. We find that in resting macrophages, INSIG1 deficiency did not affect NF-κB protein levels but markedly enhanced both expression and activation of STAT1 (Fig. 4h–n, Supplementary Fig. 8). The specificity of INSIG1 in regulating inflammation is highlighted by its role within the INSIG1-gp78 complex, which promotes the degradation of NLRP3 while leaving NF-κB signaling unaffected32. These findings demonstrate that INSIG1 functions through an NF-κB-independent mechanism. While STAT1 phosphorylation activation by interferon signaling is well-characterized, its transcriptional regulation, particularly under non-interferon conditions, is less defined. Existing studies suggested that under type I or II interferon stimulation, STAT1 transcription is primarily governed by transcription factors such as IRF1 or through its own auto-activation75–77. Although these data indicated that regulatory factors at the 5.5 kb upstream regulatory regions (5.5URRs) can bind the STAT1 promoter, it remains unclear which factors are functionally critical, particularly under non-interferon conditions75. INSIG1 is identified as a regulator of STAT1 transcription, which acts by activating SREBP2, a transcription factor validated to bind directly to the STAT1 promoter within a region pinpointed between −1000 and −1500 bp upstream of the transcription start site via ChIP-seq and EMSA (Fig. 6). Importantly, this SREBP2-mediated transcriptional upregulation was observable even under LPS + IFN-γ stimulation (Supplementary Fig. 8f), indicating that this metabolic regulatory pathway operates in parallel to canonical cytokine-driven STAT1 regulation.

Given that SREBP2 activation can promote cholesterol synthesis56, we assessed whether changes in cellular cholesterol mediate the effect on STAT1. While INSIG1-deficient monocytes/macrophages showed a consistent 1.3-fold increase in cholesterol (Fig. 5b–h, Supplementary Fig. 10e, f), exogenous supplementation to mimic this increase did not alter STAT1 transcription or activation (Fig. 5i–l). These findings warrant cautious interpretation. First, INSIG1 deficiency in macrophages does not lead to major alterations in intracellular cholesterol accumulation beyond a consistent 1.3-fold increase. Second, the conclusion that INSIG1-STAT1 signaling is “cholesterol-independent” is valid under conditions where cellular cholesterol levels remain at or below this 1.3-fold threshold. However, under stimulation by agents that further elevate cholesterol, the possibility of cholesterol-modulated INSIG1–STAT1 interactions cannot be excluded. Third, INSIG1 may also be involved in regulating other lipid classes in macrophages, potentially affecting physiological functions. This limitation here is yet interesting to explore further. Other studies have demonstrated that the INSIG1-SREBP2 axis plays a pivotal role in systemic lipid metabolism and certain infectious diseases. However, our study uncovers a mechanism that uniquely matters in psoriasis pathogenesis. Past studies on INSIG1 in macrophages did not look closely at how INSIG1 controls macrophage changes with or without cholesterol uptake. For example, Wang et al. reported that Tim-4 disrupts the interaction between INSIG1 and SCAP, thereby promoting SREBP2 activation and increasing cholesterol biosynthesis in macrophages, which in turn restricts type I interferon responses56. This study did not examine whether INSIG1-SREBP2 signaling influences macrophage polarization. Another study showed that TNF can activate SREBP2, leading to M1 macrophage polarization33. However, the downstream transcriptional mechanisms by which SREBP2 drives the polarization process were not thoroughly explored. Our study innovatively established the macrophage-specific INSIG1-SREBP2-STAT1 axis in psoriasis and elucidated its transcriptional regulation. We demonstrate that STAT1 is a direct transcriptional target of SREBP2, showing its binding to and activation of the STAT1 promoter (Fig. 6j–n). While prior studies have linked INSIG1-SREBP2 signaling mainly to inflammatory cytokines like CXCL10, CXCL11, and IL-1β, our findings reveal a distinct regulatory mechanism31–34. Besides, INSIG1 deficiency promotes STAT1 expression and activation, while NF-κB protein levels, phosphorylation status, and nuclear localization remain unchanged. Although NF-κB is a major inflammatory driver in psoriatic macrophages, INSIG1 does not directly modulate NF-κB signaling. Together, these findings define a distinct INSIG1-SREBP2-STAT1 signaling axis, rather than a reiteration of previously described SREBP2-mediated inflammatory cytokine regulation. These complete the INSIG1-SREBP2 lipid-sensing pathway to STAT1-driven macrophage polarization in PsO pathogenesis.

Despite the rigorous methodology employed, several limitations in this study should be acknowledged. First, the scRNA-seq datasets used have inherent constraints. GSE151177 provides high-quality immune cell transcriptional profiles but cannot fully exclude culture-induced alterations due to its sample preparation with ex vivo emigrating cell culture, while GSE162183 offers deep cellular resolution but is limited by its small sample size, potentially constraining statistical power despite high-quality cell capture. Hence, findings derived from scRNA-seq analyses must be verified through thorough validation of the underlying mechanisms using multiple complementary in vivo and in vitro experimental approaches. Second, it remains unclear whether the modulation of macrophage INSIG1 is specific to psoriasis or also occurs in other inflammatory skin disorders, such as atopic dermatitis. A thorough verification using both in vivo and in vitro experiments will be required to further elucidate its role in other inflammatory skin diseases and to complete the overall understanding of the mechanisms underlying the pathogenesis of these conditions.

Taken together, this study establishes macrophage INSIG1 as a pivotal regulator of psoriasis pathogenesis by identifying a INSIG1–SREBP2–STAT1 signaling axis. Our findings demonstrate that INSIG1 deficiency in macrophages exacerbates psoriasiform dermatitis by promoting M1 polarization through SREBP2-mediated transcriptional upregulation of STAT1 (Supplementary Fig. 14). These insights refine the role of INSIG1-mediated pathways in PsO, and also position INSIG1 as a potential node for therapeutic intervention in psoriasis and possibly other macrophage-driven inflammatory diseases.

Methods

Mice

Insig1 wild-type (WT) mice (Insig1flox/flox) were originally purchased from Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc. (Cat. No. NM-CKO-231965), which were kindly provided by Prof. Lirui Wang’s laboratory at Nanjing University. Lyz2-Cre mice (#004781) and Mrp8-Cre mice (#021614) were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory. Insig1flox/flox, Lyz2-Cre mice were generated by crossing Insig1flox/flox mice with Lyz2-Cre mice to achieve myeloid cell-specific Insig1 deletion. Insig1flox/flox, Mrp8-Cre mice were generated by crossing Insig1flox/flox mice with Mrp8-Cre mice to achieve granulocyte-specific deletion of Insig1. All experimental mice were female, aged 6–8 weeks. C57BL/6 (female, aged 6–8 weeks, body weight 18–22 g) were purchased from Nanjing Ziyuan Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Certificate of Conformity No. 20220704Abzz0105000989. The mice were housed and maintained under specific-pathogen-free conditions in the animal house of China Pharmaceutical University (room temperature, 21 ± 1 °C; relative humidity, 40–70%; a 12-h light-dark cycle). All animal experiments were performed in accordance with protocols approved by the Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of China Pharmaceutical University. All experimental and control mice were housed under identical husbandry conditions in separate cages but within the same specific pathogen‑free (SPF) room. Before tissue collection, the animals were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane (Cat. #R510-22, RWD Life Science Co., Ltd, Shenzhen, China), then euthanized by cervical dislocation, and tissues were immediately harvested.

Psoriasiform dermatitis mouse models

For the IMQ-induced psoriasiform dermatitis mouse models, after being anesthetized with isoflurane and shaved on the dorsal skin, female C57BL/6 mice (n = 7 /group) received daily topical applications of 62.5 mg Aldara cream (5% imiquimod) on shaved dorsal skin for 6 days. The female Insig1flox/flox, Lyz2-Cre (Insig1 cKO), Insig1flox/flox, Mrp-Cre (Insig1 cKOneu), and Insig1flox/flox (Insig1 WT) mice were subjected to identical treatment. All animal procedures were performed in compliance with the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines and the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of China Pharmaceutical University (Approval No. YSL-202505047). To establish an IL-23-induced psoriasiform mouse model, vehicle (phosphate-buffered saline) or recombinant mouse IL-23 (500 ng/10 μL) (Cat. #14-8231-63, Thermo Fisher Scientific Ltd. USA) was intradermally injected into the ears of mice (n = 7 /group) every day for 11 days. And the ears were collected and stored at −80 °C for further experiments. All animal experiments were performed in compliance with the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines and the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and approved by the Ethical Committee of the Institute of Dermatology, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences, Peking Union Medical College (Approval No. 2022-DW-018). Humane endpoints were pre‑established according to the committee’s guidelines, as shown here. In brief, mice exhibiting >20% body weight loss relative to baseline, skin ulceration >1 cm², or severe signs of distress (e.g., hunched posture, ruffled fur, lethargy) were immediately euthanized by cervical dislocation. Throughout the experiments, none of the mice reached these endpoints. To evaluate the severity of psoriasiform dermatitis, the Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI) was assessed daily. Erythema and scaling were each scored independently on a scale of 0 to 4 (0 = none, 1 = slight, 2 = moderate, 3 = marked, 4 = very marked)78. For each mouse on each day, three blinded investigators performed the scoring independently. The percentage of occurrences of each score (0–4) relative to the total number of scores (across investigators, mice, and days) was then calculated.

Clinical samples collection

Skin paraffin sections from 12 psoriatic patients and 12 healthy controls were provided by the Department of Dermatology at the Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine. The Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine approved this clinical study (Approval No. 2022NL-061-02). Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Primary human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from patients with psoriasis (n = 13) and healthy donors (n = 11) following written informed consent and approval from the Ethical Committee of the Institute of Dermatology, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences, Peking Union Medical College (Approval No. 2017-KY-022). All experiments were conducted with the following relevant guidelines and regulations. Human PBMCs were prepared by following the instructions of Lymophprep solution (Cat. #07811, Stemcell, USA). After isolation of PBMCs, CD14+ monocytes were isolated using the CD14+ microBeads (Cat. #130-050-201, Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and cultured under the indicated treatment for further experiments.

Cell lines

THP-1 (human leukemia monocytic cell line, Cat. #SCSP-567, RRID: CVCL_0006) cells, HEK293T (human embryonic kidney cells, Cat. #SCSP-502, RRID: CVCL_0063), and HaCaT (human keratinocytes, Cat. #SCSP-5091, RRID: CVCL_0038) were purchased from the Shanghai Biochemistry and Cell Biology Cell Bank, Chinese Academy of Sciences. HEK293T and HaCaT cells were cultured in DMEM medium (Cat. #11965092, Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific Ltd., USA) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Cat. #C04001, Bioind, Shanghai XP Biomed Ltd., Shanghai, China) at 37 °C, 5% CO2, and 95% humidity. THP-1 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Cat. #11875119, Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific Ltd., USA) supplemented with 10% FBS. To generate THP-1-derived M1-like macrophages, THP-1 cells were treated with phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA, 100 nM) (Cat. #524400, Sigma-Aldrich, Germany) for 24 h, followed by Lipopolysaccharides (LPS) (100 ng/mL) (Cat. #L2630, Sigma-Aldrich, Germany) + IFN-γ (20 ng/mL) (Cat. #300-02, Thermo Fisher Scientific Ltd., USA) for another 24 h.

Isolation and culture of primary cells

Primary murine peritoneal macrophages (pMs) were obtained 72 h after intraperitoneal injection of starch broth and cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS. Bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were generated by culturing bone marrow cells flushed from mouse femurs and tibias in RPMI-1640 medium with 10% FBS and M-CSF (10 ng/mL) (Cat. #315-02, Thermo Fisher Scientific Ltd. USA) for 5 days. M1-like macrophages were induced by treating BMDMs on day 5 with LPS (100 ng/mL) and IFN-γ (20 ng/mL) (Cat. #315-05, Thermo Fisher Scientific Ltd., USA) for 24 h. Splenocytes were isolated by mechanical dissociation of mouse spleens, followed by lysis of red blood cells. CD4+ T cells were purified using magnetic microbeads (Cat. #130-094-131, Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) and cultured in RPMI-1640 medium with 10% FBS. Bone marrow cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% FBS, GM-CSF (20 ng/mL) (Cat. #315-03, Thermo Fisher Scientific Ltd. USA), and IL-4 (10 ng/mL) (Cat. #214-14, Thermo Fisher Scientific Ltd. USA). After 7 days, harvest BMDCs for flow cytometry analysis.

Plasmid construction and lentivirus production

The pLKO.1 plasmids harboring shCON (small hairpin RNA targeting a negative control, product codes: SHC001), shINSIG1 (product codes: TRCN0000323002) and shSREBF2 (product codes: TRCN0000020667 and TRCN0000020664) sequences were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. The pLenti CMV GFP-CON lentiviral vector, pcDNA3.1 Flag vector and pLenti CMV Puro DEST vector were purchased from Addgene (Cat. #17448, #47388, and #17452). The pLenti CMV GFP-INSIG1 was constructed by inserting the INSIG1 mRNA sequence (NM_001346592.2), and the pLenti CMV Flag-SREBF2 was generated by inserting the SREBF2 mRNA sequence (NM_004599.3) into the pLenti CMV Flag vector. Lentiviruses were produced by co-transfecting HEK293T cells with pLKO.1 sh-targeting gene of interest or plenti-targeting gene vector, in combination with the psPAX2 packaging plasmid (Cat. #12260, Addgene) and pMD2.G (Cat. #12259, Addgene) envelope vector. Transfections were performed using Lipofectamine 2000 Transfection Reagent (Cat. #11668019, Thermo Fisher Scientific Ltd. USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Primer sequences used for plasmid construction are listed in Supplementary Table 2. All oligonucleotide primers used in this study were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd.

Establishment of transfection cell lines

Viral supernatants were collected, filtered (0.45 μm), and used to infect THP-1 or HEK293T cells in the presence of 8 μg/mL polybrene (Cat. #TR-1003, Sigma-Aldrich, Merck, Germany). For selection, cells were cultured in complete medium supplemented with 2 μg/mL puromycin (Cat. #A610593, Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) for 3 days. Surviving cells were expanded and validated by RT-qPCR and Western blotting to confirm knockdown efficiency or overexpression levels. Control cell lines were generated using control virus produced by empty vectors (shCON or GFP-CON) and processed in parallel.

Mixed lymphocyte reaction (MLR)

Peritoneal macrophages (pMs) were plated in 96-well plates 3 h before co-culture to allow adherence. Allogeneic CD4⁺ T cells were then added at a responder-to-stimulator ratio of 10:1 (CD4⁺ T cells: pMs) and cultured as indicated79. The cells were co-cultured for 5 days. On day 5, supernatants and mixed cells were collected for the flow cytometry assay and cytokine detection.

Flow cytometry

Collected or cultured cells were harvested and washed twice with cold PBS (Cat. #10010023, Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific Ltd., USA). Cells were then stained with LIVE/DEAD™ Fixable Yellow Dead Cell Stain Kit (Cat. #L34968, Thermo Fisher Scientific) to exclude dead cells. After washing, cells were incubated with human or mouse Fc receptor blocking reagent (Cat. #422302 or #101320, BioLegend, Inc.) to minimize non‑specific binding. Subsequently, cells were stained with fluorochrome‑conjugated antibodies against surface markers (e.g., CD3, CD4, F4/80, CD86) or corresponding IgG isotype controls for 30–60 min at 4 °C in the dark. Following staining, cells were washed with PBS containing 1% FBS and then acquired on a flow cytometer (NovoCyte, Agilent, USA). Data analysis was performed using the NovoExpress software (v1.5.0, Agilent Technologies). For assessing cytoplasmic or intracellular proteins, such as CD206 and IL-17A, cells were first stained with antibodies targeting surface proteins. Afterward, the cells were fixed and permeabilized (Cat. #00-8333-56, Cat. #00-8222-49, Thermo Fisher Scientific Ltd., USA) and then stained with specific antibodies targeting the cytoplasmic protein. The cells were incubated on ice for 1 h before washing and proceeding with FACS analysis.

To quantify cellular cholesterol levels, THP-1 or pM cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) (Cat. #J19943.K2, Thermo Fisher Scientific Ltd. USA) and then stained with 50 mg/mL Filipin III (Cat. #GC12048, GlpBio) for 45 min at room temperature. The MFI of Filipin was analyzed by flow cytometry.

For flow cytometry of skin tissue, dorsal skin was harvested from mice, defatted with forceps, and minced with surgical scissors. Tissue fragments were digested in 2 mg/mL collagenase IV (Cat. #17104019, Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific Ltd., USA) and 100 μg/mL DNase I (Cat. #B002138, Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) in DMEM (10 mL total in a 50 mL tube) at 37 °C with shaking (220 rpm) for 45 min. Digestion was quenched with complete DMEM (10% FBS), filtered through a 70 μm strainer. The collected cells were then resuspended in PBS prior to flow cytometric analysis. All flow cytometry gating strategies are provided in Supplementary Data 1.

RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis

RNA-seq data derived from THP-1.shCON and THP-1.shINSIG1-derived macrophages have been deposited in the Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database under accession number PRJNA1344602. THP-1.shCON and THP-1.shINSIG1 cells were primed with 100 nM PMA for 24 h, followed by a 12-h resting period before harvesting. The experiment was independently replicated three times. Total RNA was extracted, and KAITAI-BIO Co. performed RNA-seq following standard protocols. For comparison to in vitro data, upregulated differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined as having a log2 fold change (FC) > 0.585 and an adjusted P value < 0.05, whereas downregulated genes were defined as log2 (FC) < −0.585 and an adjusted P value < 0.05.

Lipidomics analysis

Prior to lipidomic analysis, THP-1.shCON and THP-1.shINSIG1 cell lines were passaged continuously for three generations to ensure stable cellular status and to minimize variability associated with prior culture conditions. After the third passage, cells were harvested and seeded at a density of 1 × 107 cells per experimental condition. For each cell line, cells were split into two experimental groups. One group was cultured in normal medium, and another group was treated with PMA (100 nM) to induce differentiation. After 24 h of incubation, cells were collected, washed with PBS, and rapidly processed for downstream analysis. All samples were snap-frozen and transported on dry ice to Kindstar Sequenon Biotechnology (Wuhan) Co., Ltd. for untargeted lipidomics analysis.

H&E staining

Skin tissues were fixed in 4% PFA, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 5 μm slices. The sections were then deparaffinized and stained with H&E (Cat. #G1001, #G1004, Servicebio, Wuhan, China). H&E-stained areas from 10 fields were quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA). Epidermal thickness was re-quantified following standard protocols, measuring only the interfollicular epidermis80,81. For each mouse, epidermal thickness was measured in 10 randomly selected microscopic fields, and the mean value was calculated for subsequent statistical analysis.

Immunohistochemistry staining (IHC)

Skin tissues were fixed in 4% PFA, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 5 μm slices. Sections were then incubated with the primary anti-Ki67 antibody (Cat. #ab15580,Abcam) overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with a secondary antibody. Indicated samples were then rinsed and incubated with biotin-labeled horseradish peroxidase before visualization with the diaminobenzidine (DAB) method (Cat. #PK6101, #SK4105, Vector Laboratories (Burlingame, CA, USA), resulting in a brown color. The background was stained with haematoxylin. Images were obtained using an optical microscope (BX53, Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). IHC-stained areas from 10 fields were quantified using Image J software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA). Ki67-positive cells were counted in defined visual fields (893 μm × 502 μm), and the number of positive cells per mm² was calculated. The mean value was calculated across multiple fields from different skin sections in each mouse.

Immunofluorescence staining (IF)

Cells or tissue slices were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min at room temperature, washed three times with PBS, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 (Cat. #G3068, Servicebio, Wuhan, China) in PBS for 15 min, and blocked with PBS containing 0.5% BSA (Cat. #A600332, Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) and 5% goat serum (Cat. #16210064, Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific Ltd. USA) for 1 h. They were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, washed three times with PBS, and counterstained with secondary antibodies in BSA-PBS for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. After additional washes, the samples were stained with DAPI (Cat. #D1306, Thermo Fisher Scientific Ltd. USA) for 5 min and mounted on slides.

INSIG1 fluorescence in macrophages was quantified in CD68+ or F4/80+ cells. The MFI of INSIG1 in either CD68+ or F4/80+ cells was measured using ImageJ software. For both human samples and mouse tissue samples, 3 randomly-selected fields per sample were analyzed. The mean INSIG1 fluorescence intensity per field was calculated from all CD68⁺ or F4/80⁺ cells, with each field as one data point. For the quantification of cell number by IF, five to ten randomly selected fields per mouse were examined, and the average value was used for statistical analysis.

Western blot (WB) analysis

WB analysis was performed as described previously82. Briefly, cells or ground skin tissue were lysed in RIPA lysis buffer (Cat. #89900, Thermo Fisher Scientific Ltd., USA) supplemented with Protease and Phosphatase Inhibitor Cocktail (Cat. #C0001, #C0003, TargetMol Chemicals Inc., USA). The total cell lysates were separated by SDS-PAGE along with protein ladder (Cat. #26617, Thermo Fisher Scientific Ltd. USA), transferred to nitrocellulose membranes (Cat. #Z358657, Millipore, Merck Chemicals (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. Shanghai, China), and then subjected to immunoblot assay using primary antibodies, followed by incubation with appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Cat #31460 and #31430, Thermo Fisher Scientific Ltd., USA). Blots were detected by chemiluminescence (Cat. #34580, Thermo Fisher Scientific Ltd., USA). WB analyses were performed using 3 independent biological replicates. Band intensities were quantified by densitometry using ImageJ software and normalized to the corresponding loading control (e.g., GAPDH or β-actin). The normalized value obtained from each biological replicate was used as a single data point for statistical analysis.

Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) assay

Total RNA was extracted using the RNA Isolater Total RNA Extraction Reagent (Cat. #R401-01, Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China). Subsequently, 1 μg of the extracted RNA was used to synthesize first-strand cDNA with the HiScript II Q RT SuperMix (Cat. #R222-01, Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed using the LightCycler 480 Instrument II (Roche, Basel, Switzerland), with ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Cat. #Q311-02/03, Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China). RT-qPCR was performed with three technical replicates per sample in each independent experiment, and the mean value was used as a single data point. Results from three independent biological replicates were combined for statistical analysis. The sequences of all primers used are provided in Supplementary Table 3. All oligonucleotide primers used in this study were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay ( ELISA)

The protein levels in mouse serum and cell culture medium were determined using ELISA kits, following the manufacturer’s protocols: Mouse IL-17A, Mouse IL12/23, Human IL-6 (Cat. #1211702, #1211232, #1110602, DAKEWE) and Human TNF, Human IL-1β (Cat. #EK182, #EK101B, MultiSciences, LiankeBio).

Luciferase reporter assays

HEK293T shCON and HEK293T shINSIG1 cells were transfected with pGL3 STAT1-Luc, pGL3 STAT1-Luc mutant, and pRL-TK (used as a transfection efficiency control; Promega, E2241) using Lipofectamine 3000 (Cat. #L3000075, Thermo Fisher Scientific Ltd. USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Luciferase activity was measured using the Dual Luciferase Assay System (Cat. #FR201, TransGen Biotech Co., Ltd.), and the levels of firefly luciferase activity were normalized to Renilla luciferase activity. For luciferase reporter assays, THP-1.shCON and THP-1.shINSIG1 cells were transfected with pGL3 STAT1-Luc and pRL-TK plasmids by electroporation. Briefly, cells were transfected with pGL3-based STAT1 promoter reporters (pGL3 STAT1-2000-Luc, −1500-Luc, −1000-Luc, or −500-Luc) and pRL-TK control plasmid via electroporation. Briefly, cells were collected and resuspended in electroporation buffer at a density of 1 × 10⁷ cells/mL. Plasmid DNA (1 µg/µL for each pGL3 construct and 0.1 µg/µL for pRL-TK) was mixed with 4 × 10⁶ cells in a 4 mm cuvette and electroporated using the Gene Pulser Xcell System (Bio-Rad) set to 200 V and 960 µF. Following electroporation, cells were promptly transferred to pre-warmed complete medium and cultured for 48 h. Luciferase activity was measured using the Dual Luciferase Assay System, and firefly luciferase signals were normalized to Renilla luciferase activity.

Electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA)

Electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) was performed using a commercial EMSA Kit (Cat. #GS009, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. GST-tagged proteins were purified using Glutathione Agarose (Cat. #16101, Thermo Fisher Scientific Ltd. USA) according to standard procedures, dialyzed against a buffer (20 mM HEPES [pH 7.9], 100 mM KCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM DTT, and 20% glycerol), and stored at −80 °C. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay. The DNA probes used included a biotin-labeled double-stranded oligonucleotide (Biotin-5′-GATTTGAGGATCAGGTGTTGCGAGGTG AGG-3′), an unlabeled wild-type sequence (5′-GATTTGAGGATCAGGTGTTGCGAGG TGAGG-3′), and an unlabeled mutant sequence (5′-TGAGGCCGAGAAATTGCGAG-3′). Protein-DNA complexes were resolved on a 6% non-denaturing polyacrylamide gel in 0.5× TBE buffer at 100 V for 90 min at 4 °C, transferred to a positively charged nylon membrane, and probed with HRP-conjugated streptavidin (Cat. #ab7403, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) for 45 min at room temperature. Detection was performed using enhanced chemiluminescence.

Crystal violet staining

HaCaT cells were seeded at 5000 cells/well in 96-well plates and cultured with the indicated conditional medium for 24–48 h. Following treatment, cells were fixed with 4% PFA for 15 min, stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Cat. #C0121, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) in 20% methanol (Cat. #A506806, Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) for 30 min, and washed extensively with water. Bound dye was solubilized in 10% acetic acid (Cat. #A501931, Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China), and absorbance was measured at 570 nm. Cell numbers were quantified using a standard curve generated by plating defined numbers of cells (1000–40,000 cells per well) and measuring absorbance at 570 nm under identical conditions. A linear regression model (R² > 0.99) was used to calculate cell numbers from absorbance values.

Single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) data analysis

The gene expression profiling datasets for psoriasis were downloaded from the GEO database under the accession numbers GSE151177 and GSE162183. Single-cell RNA sequencing data were processed and analyzed using the Seurat R package (v4.4.0) with standard quality control, normalization, and Harmony batch correction. Dimensionality reduction, clustering, and cell type annotation were performed using PCA, UMAP, and marker-based identification with SingleR. Differential expression analysis of INSIG1, INSIG2, and lipid metabolism genes was conducted using the Wilcoxon test with Benjamini-Hochberg multiple testing correction.

Statistics

All quantitative data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism (version 8.4.3). The RNA-seq expression matrix of 174 human skin samples from the GSE54456 dataset was downloaded from the GEO database. Associations between two genes (e.g., INSIG1 with IL23A, IL17A, or IL12B) were assessed using Spearman’s rank-order correlation, which yields a correlation coefficient (r) that ranges from −1 to +1, indicating the strength and direction of a monotonic relationship. Two-tailed P-values were computed, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. P > 0.05 denotes not significant (ns). For multiple group comparisons, one-way ANOVA (for single-factor analysis) or two-way ANOVA (for two-factor analysis) was performed. Data are presented as mean ± SD.
