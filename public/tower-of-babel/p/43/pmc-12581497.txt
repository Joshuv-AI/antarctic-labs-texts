Parental knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding strabismus in children
Authors: Ting Song, Yinhua Chen, Ning Li, Yilian Jiang, Weiling Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to investigate the knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) regarding strabismus among parents whose children have strabismus.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted among parents of children with strabismus at the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University between January and June 2024. A total of 459 parents were recruited using convenience sampling. Demographic information and KAP scores were collected and assessed using a self-designed questionnaire. Data were analyzed using group comparisons, and correlation analyses to assess distributions, differences between groups, and relationships among KAP scores.ResultsThe study included 459 participants, of whom 247 (53.81%) were male. The mean knowledge, attitude, and practice scores were 11.80 ± 2.08 (possible range: 0–20), 37.73 ± 3.80 (possible range: 10–50), and 17.34 ± 2.94 (possible range: 5–25), respectively. The attitude scores were significantly influenced by the presence of hyperopia (P = 0.018), astigmatism (P = 0.032), surgical intervention (P = 0.048), and eye patch use (P = 0.024) in their children. In addition, practice scores were notably impacted by the parent’s education level (P = 0.015) and whether the child had myopia (P = 0.039). Pearson correlation analyses showed insignificant correlations between knowledge and attitudes (r = 0.071, P = 0.131), attitudes and practices (r = 0.043, P = 0.354), and knowledge and practices (r = -0.091, P = 0.052).ConclusionsIn our study, parents of children with strabismus exhibited low levels of knowledge and practices, despite high levels of attitudes towards managing the condition. To address these gaps, targeted educational interventions are essential, to enhance parental understanding of strabismus, including its causes, treatment options, and the importance of follow-up care.Clinical trial numbernot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24940-1.Keywords: Knowledge, attitudes, practices; Parent; Strabismus; Cross-sectional study; Children

Background

Strabismus is an eye disorder characterized by the misalignment of the eyes, where one deviates either vertically, horizontally, or rotationally relative to the other, often resulting in significant binocular visual impairment and amblyopia. It is particularly prevalent among children, affecting approximately 3.55% of Asian children [1, 2]. A specific study conducted in Eastern China found a prevalence of 5.65% among preschool students aged 36–72 months [3]. Beyond visual impairment, strabismus also impacts psychosocial well-being, leading to reduced self-esteem and increased anxiety, and can negatively affect the quality of life for both patients and their families [4, 5].

The Knowledge, Attitudes, and Practices (KAP) survey is an essential tool in health literacy, functioning on the premise that improved knowledge enhances attitudes, which, in turn, shape behaviors [6]. It operates on the premise that enhanced knowledge positively influences attitudes, which in turn shape behaviors. This model is crucial in understanding the dynamics within specific populations, such as parents of children with pediatric conditions, whose role is pivotal in managing treatment adherence and outcomes [7]. In the context of strabismus, parental involvement is vital. A deep understanding of how parents are informed about strabismus, their perceptions of the condition, and their behaviors towards its management can reveal knowledge gaps and barriers to effective treatment.

Moreover, in China, strabismus surgery is often considered “cosmetic,” excluding it from health insurance coverage and perpetuating misconceptions [8]. This mislabeling hampers early treatment for strabismus patients, highlighting a critical area for intervention. To our knowledge, there is a lack of KAP studies directed at this specific issue within the Chinese context. Therefore, this study aims to explore the KAP of Chinese parents whose children have strabismus.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted at the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University between January, and June 2024, involving parents of children diagnosed with strabismus. A total of 459 parents participated in the study, recruited through convenience sampling in outpatient clinics and inpatient wards. Ethical approval was granted by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University, and informed consent was obtained from all participants. The inclusion criteria of parents were: (1) Child has been diagnosed with strabismus [9]; (2) Parents volunteered to participate in the study and were able to understand the questionnaire. Exclusion criteria involved parents of children with severe ocular conditions like glaucoma or cataracts that might interfere with the study.

Questionnaire

The initial design of the questionnaire was informed by “2017 AAO Esotropia and Exotropia Preferred Practice Pattern® [10]”, and published literature [11, 12]. Contributions from seven specialists in the field led to the elimination of redundant or similar questions and the clarification of ambiguous statements. A pilot test involving 15 respondents yielded a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.7774, indicating acceptable internal consistency.

The final version of the questionnaire, presented in Chinese, encompassed four dimensions of data collection (Additional file 1. Questionnaire). The demographic information included 12 items: parent’s gender, age, educational level, residence, monthly per capita family income, child’s gender, age, age at initial strabismus diagnosis, type of strabismus, severity of strabismus, associated comorbidities, and treatment methods. The knowledge dimension comprised 10 items, with scores assigned as follows: 2 points for “well aware,” 1 point for “heard of it,” and 0 points for “uncertain,” resulting in a possible score range of 0 to 20. The attitude dimension included 10 items, assessed using a five-point Likert scale ranging from “strongly agree” (5 points) to “strongly disagree” (1 point), yielding a total score range of 10 to 50. The practice dimension consisted of 7 items; while items P6 and P7 were analyzed using descriptive statistics only, the remaining items were scored on a five-point Likert scale from “always” (5 points) to “never” (1 point), with a total possible score range of 5 to 25. Adequate knowledge, positive attitudes, and proactive practices were defined as scores exceeding 70% of the maximum possible in each respective dimension [13].

Questionnaire distribution and quality control

This study utilized both electronic and paper-based questionnaires, which were distributed in outpatient clinics and inpatient wards. The researchers and trained assistants explained the study objectives to all participants in detail and addressed questions without influencing responses. The electronic questionnaire was created using the Sojump platform (https://www.wjx.cn/), with a WeChat QR code generated to facilitate data collection. Participants could complete the electronic version by scanning the QR code. For those unfamiliar with electronic devices or without internet access, paper questionnaires were provided. To ensure consistency in data analysis, one member of the research team entered all paper questionnaire responses into the Sojump platform, and a second team member verified the accuracy of the data entry. Subsequently, all questionnaires were thoroughly reviewed by the research team for completeness, consistency, and validity.

Statistical methods

Data analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and AMOS 24.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of continuous variables was tested using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Continuous data that were normally distributed are presented as means ± standard deviations (SD) and were analyzed with Student’s t-test or ANOVA for comparisons between groups. For continuous variables with skewed distribution, data are reported as medians (ranges) and were analyzed using the Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney U-test or Kruskal-Wallis test. Categorical variables are expressed as n (%). Pearson correlation was used to assess the relationships among knowledge (K), attitude (A), and practice (P) scores. A two-sided P-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

A total of 459 parents participated in this study, with 247 (53.81%) being male, and a mean age of 31.42 ± 2.37 years. Of the participants, 244 (53.16%) resided in urban areas, and 159 (34.64%) had a college degree. Regarding their children, 233 (50.76%) were boys, with a mean age of 5.59 ± 1.42 years; 228 (49.67%) had moderate strabismus, 345 (75.16%) wore spectacles, 336 (73.20%) underwent surgery, and 315 (68.63%) received vision training (Table 1).

Basic information of participants

The mean scores for knowledge, attitude, and practice were 11.80 ± 2.08 (possible range: 0–20), 37.73 ± 3.80 (possible range: 10–50), and 17.34 ± 2.94 (possible range: 5–25), respectively. Demographic analysis revealed no significant impact on knowledge scores, while attitude scores were significantly affected by factors such as the presence of hyperopia (P = 0.018), astigmatism (P = 0.032), surgery (P = 0.048), and eye patch usage (P = 0.024). Parental education (P = 0.015) and child myopia (P = 0.039) significantly impacted practice scores (Table 1).

The responses to the KAP sections

The distribution of knowledge dimensions showed that the three questions with the highest number of participants choosing the “Uncertain” option were “Have you heard of the torchlight corneal reflex test?” (K5) with 80.16%, “Common treatments for strabismus include vision training, medication, and surgery.” (K8) with 38.56%, and “The causes of strabismus are complex, influenced by both congenital and acquired factors.” (K2) with 37.69% (Table 2).

Response of practice dimension

When it comes to the sources of information on strabismus and its treatment, the most commonly reported were recommendations from friends and relatives (72.98%) and news/television (70.15%) (Figure S1 A). When it comes to primary concerns regarding strabismus surgery, the most commonly reported were the safety of the surgery (79.96%) and the long-term follow-up and monitoring of the postoperative recovery process (72.98%) (Figure S1 B).

Discussion

In our study, parents of children with strabismus exhibited low levels of knowledge and practices, despite high levels of attitudes towards managing the condition. This observation aligns with prior studies indicating that while positive attitudes toward a disease are crucial, they do not necessarily translate into effective management practices or enhanced knowledge [14]. Possible reasons for these gaps may include limited access to reliable information sources and a lack of professional guidance. As depicted in Figure P6, only 43.14% of parents utilized the internet as a primary source of information, while more than 70% relied on TV and personal contacts. This highlights the possibility that readily accessible sources may not always provide sufficient or accurate medical guidance. Additionally, Figure P7 shows that concerns about surgery were prevalent (79.96% expressed anxiety), suggesting that insufficient professional reassurance may hinder parents from fully understanding treatment options and their implications. These challenges underscore the ongoing need for improved educational outreach and better communication between healthcare providers and parents, to ensure optimal treatment adherence and outcomes.

The Pearson correlation analyses from our study revealed insignificant correlations between knowledge and attitudes, attitudes and practices, and knowledge and practices. This lack of significant relationships suggests that having knowledge of a condition does not ensure positive attitudes or proactive practices. Several studies have highlighted similar patterns, especially in chronic disease management. For example, research on chronic illness indicates that while individuals may possess knowledge about their condition, external factors such as insufficient access to healthcare resources, lack of personalized support, and socio-economic barriers can prevent this knowledge from translating into better attitudes or practices [15]. Furthermore, cultural norms and personal beliefs may also play a role in moderating the impact of knowledge on behavior, as seen in studies on other health conditions, which suggest that knowledge alone is often insufficient in bringing about behavioral change [16, 17]. This finding aligns with previous reports, reinforcing the complexity of behavior change in health management contexts.

Regarding significant differences, education level notably impacted practice scores, indicating that parents with higher education levels were more proactive in seeking and adhering to treatment recommendations. This correlation supports the findings that higher educational attainment generally correlates with better health management behaviors due to increased access to resources and a greater awareness of health issues [18, 19].

Regarding the treatment measure of children with strabismus wearing spectacles, the analysis results show no significant differences in parents’ knowledge (P = 0.382) and practices (P = 0.905). However, a non-significant trend towards more positive attitudes was observed among parents whose children wore spectacles (P = 0.654). This difference may be related to the expectations for treatment outcomes and parents’ understanding of disease management. Although wearing spectacles is a common intervention to correct vision in strabismus, its direct impact on parents’ knowledge and practices appears limited, potentially reflecting a lack of adequate professional guidance and information dissemination. The slight trend towards more positive attitudes may stem from increased confidence in treatment efficacy, but this attitude shift has not translated into higher levels of knowledge or practice. This finding suggests that relying solely on treatment measures may be insufficient to improve parents’ overall knowledge and practices, and further interventions may be necessary to strengthen education and guidance for parents to achieve more comprehensive disease management [20, 21].

Significant gaps were particularly noted in certain knowledge and practice areas. Specifically, gaps were observed in parents’ understanding of screening methods and treatment options (knowledge), as well as their limited engagement in learning, follow-up visits, and exploring alternative treatments (practices). For instance, a large proportion of parents were not familiar with the torchlight corneal reflex test and the various treatments available for strabismus, reflecting a crucial knowledge deficit that could impact treatment outcomes. As the torchlight corneal reflex test is a simple and widely used screening method to detect ocular misalignment, limited parental awareness may delay timely medical consultation and consequently postpone appropriate treatment. Literature suggests that enhanced parental knowledge correlates strongly with better management practices and improved patient outcomes in pediatric conditions [22].

Given these findings, specific recommendations can be made to improve the current situation. First, developing targeted educational programs that detail the nature of strabismus, its causes, potential complications, and the spectrum of treatment options available could empower parents [23, 24]. Such programs should be accessible not only at ophthalmologists’ offices but also through public health campaigns and online platforms to reach a broader audience [25, 26]. Moreover, considering the low engagement in proactive learning and alternate treatment options observed in practice scores, healthcare providers should consider regular follow-up sessions that not only assess the child’s progress but also include educational segments for the parents [27, 28]. Healthcare policies should also reflect the need for comprehensive care approaches that integrate education, treatment, and support for families dealing with strabismus. Implementing training programs for healthcare providers to enhance their communication skills could further ensure that parents receive clear, empathetic, and thorough guidance throughout their child’s treatment process [29, 30].

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the cross-sectional design restricts the ability to establish causality between the knowledge, attitudes, and practices of the parents and the management outcomes of strabismus in their children. Second, the sample was limited to parents visiting a single hospital, which may not accurately represent the broader population of parents dealing with strabismus in different regions or healthcare settings. Lastly, self-reported data may introduce response bias, as participants might have provided answers they perceived as socially desirable, rather than their true thoughts and practices.

Conclusions

In our study, parents of children with strabismus exhibited low levels of knowledge and practices, despite high levels of attitudes towards managing the condition. To address these gaps, targeted educational interventions are essential, to enhance parental understanding of strabismus, including its causes, treatment options, and the importance of follow-up care.
