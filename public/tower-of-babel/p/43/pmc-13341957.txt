Adjunctive Mediterranean Diet in a Middle‐Aged Male With Autoimmune Encephalitis and Generalized Tonic–Clonic Seizures: A Longitudinal Case Report
Authors: Hamid Abbasi, Faezeh Mousavinia, Mahdi Shadnoush, Farzad Ashrafi, Mojtaba Arabshahi, Mohammadjavad Pasand, Hoda Zahedi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWe describe a 45‐year‐old male with autoimmune encephalitis and recurrent tonic–clonic seizures. In addition to receiving standard immunotherapy, he adopted a Mediterranean diet as adjunctive therapy. Six months later, brain MRI showed resolution of the hippocampal hyperintensities and a reduction in periventricular white matter changes; EEG demonstrated normal background activity without epileptiform discharges.Keywords: autoimmune encephalitis, Case report, diet, electroencephalogram, magnetic resonance imaging, Mediterranean

Introduction

Autoimmune encephalitis (AE) is a heterogeneous group of inflammatory brain disorders marked by the involvement of the immune system, leading to rapidly progressive neuronal injury, and is recognized as a scarce but increasingly acknowledged impairment that affects individuals of all ages, including both children and adults [1]. The initial documentation of this disorder dates back to 1968, when it was identified as a paraneoplastic AE linked to small‐cell lung carcinoma [2]. Over the last 20 years, the incidence of AE has increased from 0.4 to 1.2 cases per 100,000 person‐years [3]. Its prevalence, currently estimated at 13.7 cases per 100,000, surpasses that of infectious encephalitis, which stands at 11.6 cases per 100,000 [3]. This upward trend can be partially attributed to the growing identification of various autoantibodies and the associated inflammatory mediators that accompany these conditions [4]. A multicenter study executed in the United States found that 22.8% of patients diagnosed with AE tested positive for N‐methyl D‐aspartate (NMDA) receptor antibodies [5]. Furthermore, data from the California Encephalitis Project indicate that anti‐NMDA receptor encephalitis occurs more frequently than viral forms of encephalitis [3].

The treatment of AE primarily relies on prompt immunotherapy, which encompasses both first‐line and second‐line medications [6, 7]. First‐line therapies include intravenous (IV) corticosteroids [8, 9], IV immunoglobulin [10], and plasma exchange [11], which can be utilized either sequentially or in combination, especially when patients present with severe or rapidly progressing symptoms [12]. If there is no clinical improvement after 2–4 weeks of first‐line treatment, second‐line therapies such as Rituximab and Cyclophosphamide may be considered [13]. Cyclophosphamide is often favored in cases of paraneoplastic disorders due to its ability to deplete T cells and its penetration through the blood–brain barrier [14]. The management of antibody‐mediated autoimmune encephalitis has evolved to include targeted immunotherapies. While consensus guidelines provide a framework for first‐line treatments and general algorithms [13], the management of refractory cases, particularly those involving LGI1 antibodies, has been increasingly informed by evidence for B‐cell depletion therapies. Multiple case reports and series have demonstrated the efficacy of Rituximab in achieving seizure control and cognitive improvement in patients with refractory LGI1 encephalitis [15, 16, 17, 18]. Specifically, these studies highlight its role in cases where conventional immunotherapy fails [15, 16] and its potential impact on electroclinical patterns [17]. Supporting these observations, data from the GENERATE registry provides broader real‐world evidence, confirming that Rituximab treatment is associated with favorable long‐term outcomes in a larger cohort of autoimmune encephalitis patients [19]. Currently, there is no precise cure for AE, but timely and appropriate treatment, along with anti‐inflammatory dietary therapies, can lead to improvement in many patients [20].

The Mediterranean diet (MedDiet) is recognized for its anti‐inflammatory properties, characterized by elevated consumption of polyunsaturated and monounsaturated fatty acids (PUFA and MUFA), substantial dietary fiber intake, and reduced levels of saturated fatty acids [21]. This dietary pattern is predominantly observed in Mediterranean nations such as Greece, Spain, Italy, France, and regions of northern Africa [22]. Originally, the MedDiet was delineated as a traditional eating pattern emphasizing low saturated fat intake alongside a high consumption of vegetable oils [23]. However, it is fundamentally a modest diet that prioritizes abundant plant‐based foods, moderate amounts of poultry and fish, dairy products, red wine, and minimal consumption of red meats, processed foods, and sugary desserts [21]. The MedDiet has been shown to mitigate the expression of inflammatory markers while simultaneously augmenting the body's overall antioxidant capacity [24]. Despite the absence of published articles examining the influence of dietary interventions, particularly the MedDiet, on individuals with AE, this case report seeks to explore the impacts of the MedDiet on biochemical parameters, electroencephalogram (EEG) findings, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) outcomes in a middle‐aged male diagnosed with AE.

Case Presentation

A 45‐year‐old male with a history of generalized tonic–clonic (GTC) seizures following methadone cessation 8 months earlier was admitted to our facility (Shohadaye Tajrish Educational Hospital, affiliated with Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences in Tehran, Iran) on November 2, 2024, with recurrent seizure activity. Prior to this incident, the patient had been in good health with no considerable history of significant medical or surgical conditions, allergies, or habitual behaviors. He reported no regular medication use before admission. The patient experienced four additional GTC seizures, each lasting approximately 3 min, with postictal phases of 30 min. During interictal periods, he remained conscious, though psychomotor slowing was observed, and his Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) score was 28. The patient's chief complaint upon admission was related to these recurrent GTC seizures. Vital signs on admission were within normal limits: body temperature, 36.8°C; pulse rate, 86 beats per minute; blood pressure, 110/70 mmHg; respiratory rate, 18 breaths per minute. The serum levels of complete blood count differentiation, liver function tests, renal function parameters, and C‐reactive protein were within normal ranges (Table 1). The diagnosis of AE was established in congruence with the 2016 Graus et al. criteria [4], with the patient meeting criteria for probable autoimmune encephalitis based on subacute onset of seizures and memory deficits, bilateral medial temporal MRI abnormalities (Figures 1A,B), EEG findings (Figure 2A), and exclusion of other etiologies. Cerebrospinal fluid analysis was declined, and comprehensive neuronal surface antibody testing was unavailable; however, the immunological panel indicated a positive antinuclear antibody and elevated cardiolipin antibodies, which supported an underlying autoimmune process. MedDiet Intervention

Following effective management of the patient's condition, he was referred to a clinical dietitian, Hamid Abbasi, M.Sc., under the supervision of Assistant Professor of Nutritional Science, Dr. Hoda Zahedi, Ph.D., prior to discharge to modify his dietary pattern, which was shifted to the MedDiet pattern. This was intended to enhance his well‐being and facilitate disease management until the next scheduled appointment. Height and weight were recorded using a Seca scale and a wall‐mounted stadiometer (Seca GmbH & Co. KG, Hamburg, Germany). Following anthropometric measurement, the patient's height was 175 cm, weight was 90 kg, and body mass index (BMI) was 29.38 kg/m2, which exhibits an overweight status. Energy requirements were estimated using the Mifflin‐St Jeor equation and individualized according to the patient's nutritional status. A calorie‐controlled MedDiet providing 2176 kcal/day was prescribed, with a macronutrient distribution of 50% carbohydrate (299 g), 18% protein (98 g), and 32% fat (77 g). Daily salt intake was restricted to approximately 1600 mg. The composition of the individualized MedDiet tailored for this patient are detailed in Table 4.

The patient received individualized nutritional counseling on adherence to the MedDiet, encompassing increased consumption of fruits, vegetables, legumes, whole grains, fish, and olive oil, while restricting processed foods and saturated fats. Dietary compliance was assessed during follow‐up clinic visits and via online Iranian social media discussions regarding adherence to the prescribed meal plan. However, dietary records and objective biomarkers of dietary adherence were not collected.

Neuroimaging Findings

The initial brain MRI performed on November 2, 2024 (Figures 1A–1B), revealed bilateral hyperintensities in the hippocampal regions on T2‐weighted coronal imaging, along with periventricular and deep white matter hyperintensities on axial FLAIR sequences, findings consistent with AE and supportive of seizure activity. After initiating a calorie‐controlled MedDiet, the follow‐up MRI on May 25, 2025 (Figures 1C,D) demonstrated marked resolution of hippocampal hyperintensities and significant reduction in FLAIR signal abnormalities in the periventricular white matter.

The initial 1‐h EEG performed on November 2, 2024 (Figure 2A), revealed intermittent generalized epileptiform discharges predominantly in the frontotemporal regions, accompanied by diffuse background slowing. These findings were consistent with cortical hyperexcitability and subclinical seizure activity, supporting the diagnosis of AE in the context of recurrent GTC seizures. The follow‐up 1‐h EEG on May 25, 2025, indicated substantial electrophysiological improvement (Figure 2B). The background rhythm normalized to a well‐organized alpha pattern with restoration of the posterior dominant rhythm, and no interictal epileptiform discharges were observed during the recording session. In addition, radiologic improvement was noted, suggesting that the MedDiet may have been associated with stabilizing neuronal function, reducing neuroinflammation, and promoting cortical recovery in the AE patient.

Discussion

Main Findings

In this longitudinal case report, we presented a 45‐year‐old male diagnosed with AE who experienced recurrent GTC seizures. Diagnostic evaluations, encompassing brain MRI, 1‐h EEG, biochemical markers, and immunological panel, corroborated AE, and the patient was started on standard pharmacological treatment. Due to his overweight status and the potential benefits of dietary modulation in neuroinflammatory conditions, he was placed on a calorie‐controlled MedDiet prior to discharge. The diet was designed to support neurological recovery through an anti‐inflammatory macronutrient profile. Six months later, follow‐up MRI and EEG revealed significant improvements in brain structure and function, comprising resolution of hippocampal and white matter abnormalities and normalization of cortical activity, with no further seizures reported. These findings suggest a viable adjunctive role for the MedDiet in managing AE.

The MedDiet, traditionally rich in polyphenols, omega‐3 fatty acids, and antioxidants, has been broadly documented for its anti‐inflammatory and neuroprotective properties. Its benefits have been extensively reported in chronic diseases including cardiovascular disorders [25], metabolic syndrome [26], and several autoimmune diseases [27, 28, 29]. For instance, a cross‐sectional study by Pocovi‐Geradino et al. demonstrated that adherence to the MedDiet was linked to reduced disease activity and cardiovascular risk in patients with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), suggesting a role in immunomodulation [30]. Similarly, a population‐based case–control study by Sundstrom et al. found that higher adherence to the MedDiet correlated with a reduced risk of developing rheumatoid arthritis (RA), particularly in men [31]. These findings reinforce the hypothesis that the MedDiet's bioactive components may modulate immune responses and chronic inflammation.

While direct studies on the MedDiet's effects on AE are lacking, research on other dietary interventions provides indirect insights. The ketogenic diet (KD), a high‐fat, low‐carbohydrate diet, has been studied for its efficacy in managing refractory epilepsy, including cases associated with AE. A case series by Cervenka et al. reported that KD led to seizure reduction in patients with super‐refractory status epilepticus (SRSE), some of whom had underlying AE [32]. Uchida et al. described a patient with SRSE and autoimmune encephalitis in whom a combination of KD and stiripentol was associated with seizure cessation, which suggests a synergistic effect between dietary and pharmacological metabolic modulation [33]. Furthermore, Prasoppokakorn et al. reported a 19‐year‐old female with SRSE secondary to AE in whom a medium‐chain triglyceride (MCT) KD led to rapid seizure control within 6 days, even without achieving positive ketosis, which suggests that medium‐chain fatty acids (e.g., octanoic and decanoic acid) may exert direct anti‐seizure effects via α‐amino‐3‐hydroxy‐5‐methyl‐4‐isoxazolepropionic acid receptor inhibition and Peroxisome Proliferator‐Activated Receptor Gamma activation [34]. Kaul et al. provided practical considerations for implementing KD therapy in adult SRSE [35]. In that review, seven patients with AE were included who responded to KD, which supported the potential of metabolic dietary interventions in autoimmune neurological diseases. However, KD's highly restrictive nature, reliance on saturated fats, and associated metabolic side effects pose challenges for long‐term adherence.

Strengths and Limitations

This longitudinal case report had several strengths. A key strength lies in the integration of multimodal assessments, including MRI, 1‐h EEG, biochemical and immunological evaluations, which objectively confirmed neurological recovery following the dietary intervention. The structured nutritional plan, calculated meticulously using the Mifflin‐St Jeor equation and tailored to energy and macronutrient needs, demonstrated methodological rigor and reproducibility. Furthermore, the interdisciplinary collaboration between neurology and clinical nutrition teams reflects a comprehensive, patient‐centered approach that aligns with best practices in integrative care. The 6‐month follow‐up period also allows for an insightful temporal association between the MedDiet improvements in seizure activity, imaging, and cortical function.

Despite its innovative approach, the report is inherently limited by its single‐patient design, which restricts generalizability to broader AE populations. Confounding by standard immunotherapy is the central limitation, as the patient received rituximab, methylprednisolone, prednisolone, levetiracetam, topiramate, and other medications, which makes it arduous to isolate the specific contribution of the dietary intervention. The absence of a control group and lack of adjustment for potential confounders make it arduous to attribute the observed improvements solely to the dietary intervention. Additionally, adherence to the prescribed MedDiet was not objectively monitored through tools like dietary records or biomarkers, raising uncertainty about the consistency of implementation.

Conclusion

In summary, this is the first case report documenting the use of the MedDiet as an adjunctive therapy in a patient with AE. The observed radiological and electrophysiological improvements, along with seizure resolution, were temporally associated with the dietary intervention. However, given the concurrent use of multiple standard immunotherapies, encompassing corticosteroids, Rituximab, and antiseizure medications, causality cannot be inferred. The precise mechanisms by which the MedDiet may influence seizure frequency, neuroinflammatory processes, and disease trajectory in AE remain speculative and warrant further investigation through randomized controlled trials. At present, the MedDiet should be considered a potentially beneficial adjunctive nutritional strategy rather than an independent treatment for AE.
