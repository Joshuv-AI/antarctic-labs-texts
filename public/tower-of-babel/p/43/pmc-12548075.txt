Workplace sexual harassment and suicide ideation in female nurses: multiplying effects of social stigma and job insecurity
Authors: Long Cheng, Rongxia Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesTo examine the link between workplace sexual harassment and suicidal ideation among female nurses in private hospitals, testing social stigma as a mediator and job insecurity as a moderator, and distinguishing sources of harassment (colleagues vs patients).MethodsCross-sectional explanatory survey of 312 female nurses employed ≥1 year in private hospitals. Standardized measures assessed sexual harassment, perceived social stigma, job insecurity, and suicidal ideation. Structural equation modeling (SmartPLS) evaluated direct, mediated, and moderated effects, with robustness checks for common-method bias and demographic controls.ResultsHarassment showed a significant positive association with suicidal ideation. Social stigma partially mediated this pathway, indicating that victimization increases perceived stigma, which in turn elevates suicidal thoughts. Job insecurity amplified the harassment–ideation link: nurses reporting higher insecurity experienced stronger adverse effects and reported greater difficulty resisting harassment. Effects were observed for harassment by both colleagues and patients.ConclusionFemale nurses in private hospitals face substantial risk of sexual harassment and consequential psychological harm, including suicidal ideation. Interventions should integrate rigorous anti-harassment enforcement, protected and confidential reporting, stigma-reduction initiatives, accessible mental-health services, and job-security safeguards. Addressing stigma and precarious employment is essential to mitigating harm and protecting nurses’ well-being.KEYWORDS: Suicidal ideation, sexual harassment, social stigma, job insecurity, female nurses

Introduction

The problem of workplace sexual harassment is still widespread in the healthcare sector, with female nurses prone to such harassment more than their peers. Pushing through demanding work environments, complex patient interactions, and the hierarchical structure of healthcare settings all contribute to nurses being particularly susceptible to mistreatment at work (Subedi et al., 2013). The healthcare industry’s pronounced power dynamics, often characterised by male-dominated leadership and authority structures, create significant challenges for female nurses, particularly in private hospitals (Kahsay et al., 2020; Spector et al., 2014). Private hospitals, unlike their public counterparts, frequently employ nurses on contractual or temporary bases, which heightens job insecurity and limits access to robust support systems or reporting mechanisms (Boya et al., 2008; Darvishmotevali et al., 2024). These conditions amplify the vulnerability of female nurses to sexual harassment, as they may fear job loss or retaliation when confronting inappropriate behaviour (Cortina & Areguin, 2021). Gendered power imbalances, where male supervisors or colleagues hold positions of authority, further exacerbate this vulnerability, creating an environment where harassment is more likely to occur and less likely to be reported due to fear of stigma or professional repercussions (Fitzgerald et al., 1995).Sexual harassment is one of these challenges that constitute a significant public health issue impacting on nurses’ psychological well-being, job satisfaction and mental health (Kahsay et al., 2020). Despite increasing recognition of this, the psychiatric repercussions of workplace sexual harassment on nurses, particularly with regards to suicidal ideation, are still insufficiently researched.

Suicidal ideation can be defined as chronic thoughts of looking to end one’s life and is a serious mental health problem with serious implications if not treated. The occupational pressure, emotional exhaustion, and traumatic patient experiences render healthcare professionals such as nurses at higher risk of developing suicidal ideation. Previous studies have shown that workplace sexual harassment negatively affects the psychological symptoms, depression, and burnout of female nurses (Cortina & Areguin, 2021; Hunt et al., 2010). The interplay of gender and power dynamics in private hospitals intensifies these effects, as female nurses often face a culture of silence driven by fear of victim-blaming or job loss (Glomb et al., 1997).

Nevertheless, there is little research that has addressed direct workplace sexual harassment and suicidal ideation specifically in the context of a hospital and clinical or health-sensitive setting, particularly in private hospitals with a significant female workforce, where job security may be tenuous and support systems weak.

One of the factors that worsen the effects of sexual harassment in the workplace is social stigma. In many societies, victims of sexual harassment find themselves blamed, dismissed, and subject to reputational damage, preventing them from disclosing incidents or seeking redress (Frost, 2011). Social stigma, often amplified by gendered societal norms that unfairly scrutinise female victims, may cause emotional distress, social rejection, and reduced self-esteem, which could increase suicidality (Krahé & Berger, 2020). Furthermore, this study seeks to understand the societal stigmatisation resulting from sexual harassment and how this stigma may drive suicidal thoughts in affected female nurses. It is hypothesised that the pervasive issue of sexual harassment could lead to suicidal ideation. Tragically, in the given context, those subjected to sexual assault often face societal stigmatisation, resulting in diminished esteem within their community. As such, we posit that sexual harassment causes social stigma, which in turn may increase the risk of suicidal ideation.

Job insecurity is another critical factor influencing suicidal ideation among nurses. Many female nurses in private hospitals are employed on a contractual basis, making them particularly vulnerable to job loss if they report harassment or reject inappropriate advances. The fear of job termination or retaliation often compels nurses to endure workplace harassment, contributing to chronic stress and negatively impacting their psychological well-being (M. Kim & Beehr, 2023; S. M. Kim & Jo, 2024). This fear not only discourages victims from speaking out but also reinforces the power imbalance between harassers and their targets, worsening the harmful effects of harassment (Darvishmotevali et al., 2024). The gendered nature of these power dynamics, where male authority figures may exploit their position, further entrenches this imbalance, making it harder for nurses to resist or report harassment without risking their livelihoods (Cortina & Areguin, 2021). Despite its critical implications for mental health in nursing, there is limited research on how job insecurity moderates the relationship between workplace sexual harassment and suicidal ideation.

The current academic research about workplace sexual harassment and psychological effects does not encompass how the combination of social stigmatisation and job insecurity, and gendered power dynamics worsens its impact particularly for nurses at work. Numerous research studies examine either general workplace sexual harassment or broader occupational stress aspects affecting nurses’ mental well-being but do not report direct suicidal thinking that stems from harassment experiences. To address this gap, this study aims to:

Investigate the direct relationship between workplace sexual harassment and suicidal ideation among female nurses in private hospitals.

Examine the mediating role of social stigma in the association between workplace sexual harassment and suicidal ideation.

These objectives seek to elucidate the mechanisms through which sexual harassment, compounded by social stigma and job insecurity, contributes to suicidal ideation, providing insights for targeted interventions to improve nurses’ mental health in private hospital settings (Cortina & Areguin, 2021; Kahsay et al., 2020).

Literature review

Workplace violence causes nurses to develop suicidal thoughts and self-harming behaviours which result mostly from emotional distress and feelings of isolation (Ullah, Shah, et al., 2021). Workplace sexual harassment prevails as a serious issue in the healthcare sector, predominantly striking female nurses. Workplace sexual harassment includes unwanted sexual advances and demands as well as all sexual behaviours that result in a threatening environment (Alam et al., 2021; Hersch, 2024). People who experience sexual harassment at work experience severe psychological and professional consequences that often lead to increased stress, depression, anxiety and job satisfaction decline (McDonald, 2012). Workplace harassment produces detrimental effects that weaken both nursing employee well-being and disrupts the quality of patient care.

Literature shows consistent documentation of high suicide thinking rates which affect female nursing professionals (Celik & Çelik, 2007). A study conducted on Bangladeshi nurses found that extensive suicidal thoughts affected 13.26% of the population while workplace bullying together with burnout acted as prominent causative factors (Kabir et al., 2023). The nursing profession shows signs of higher suicidal ideation than the general workforce but many nurses refrain from seeking professional help because they fear possible occupational repercussions and social judgements (Ali et al., 2015).

Nurses who experience sexual harassment endure additional mental health consequences because of social stigma (Zeighami et al., 2022). The fear of reputation damage along with scepticism and false blame against victims deters healthcare workers from coming forward to report harassment incidences (Ghaffar et al., 2024). The avoidance to report incidents prolongs psychological suffering and social seclusion while harming self-esteem which builds risk towards suicidal thoughts (Ullah et al., 2023). The connection between sexual harassment and societal stigma forms a self-perpetuating cycle which intensifies the psychological decline of assaulted nurses.

Job insecurity is of the influential factors the profoundly influence nurses’ mental well-being and the possibility of suicidal ideation (Boya et al., 2008). The nurses working in private hospitals or healthcare centres, are employed under contract-based employment, making them prone to job loss if they resist inappropriate advances or report harassment (Boya et al., 2008; Idrees et al., 2018). The fear of termination or retaliation often compels the victims to endure harassment in silence, provoking chronic stress and psychological distress (Prado-Gascó et al., 2021). This imbalance of power between the mighty and the weak reinforces a poisonous workplace culture, further threatening nurses’ mental health (Ullah, Sulaiman, et al., 2021).

Research shows that nursing professionals experience depression more frequently due to the additive stress of bullying alongside burnout as well as workplace violence and job dissatisfaction (Iqbal et al., 2022; Zeighami et al., 2023). Multiple research studies confirm that workplace violence links directly to emotional disorders which reinforce the negative impact of poor working environments (McDonald, 2012; Ullah, Shah, et al., 2021). Research shows workplace violence causes nurses to develop suicidal thoughts and self-harming behaviours which result mostly from emotional distress and feelings of isolation (Groves et al., 2023).

The growing demands for the healthcare professionals, particularly during and after pandemic outbreak, have further increased the mental health burden on nurses (Kelsey et al., 2021). Unrealistic workloads, work-family imbalance, lack of institutional support, and intensified stress levels contribute to more psychological pressure, making female nurses more exposed to anxiety, burnout, and suicidal ideation (Ahmad et al., 2021; Vandewalle et al., 2019). These issues demand for the urgent need for inclusive workplace policies, mental health support systems, and preventive measures to ensure a conducive and more supportive environment for female nurses (Kabir et al., 2023).

Theoretical framework

Stress Process Model (Pearlin et al., 1981) is used to provide theoretical base to this study. Stress Process Model argues that mental conditions are influenced by relationships and social environment specifically when individuals feel that they lack the resources needed to cope with difficult social situations. This stressful condition can lead to emotional, behavioural, and physiological degradation, increasing the risk of mental diseases and physical ailments (Hemmati et al., 2021). In the context of nursing, workplace sexual harassment is the major social stressor, disturbing professional relationships and creating a negative work environment. This harassment not only affects the nurses but also creates a culture of fear and mistrust among staff. The resulting mental conditions can lead to psychological distress, including depression, anxiety, and suicidal ideation.

Definitions of variables

Workplace sexual harassment

Any undesired sexual approach, request for sexual lenience, spoken or tangible action or sign with a sexual intent, or any other sexually-oriented behaviour that could justifiably be seen or felt as offensive or demeaning to another person. Sexual harassment has been taken as the predictive variable in the study (Powell, 1983; Siuta & Bergman, 2019).

Suicidal ideation

It happens when an individual carries a desire for death, encompassing thoughts and contemplations regarding the prospect of terminating one’s own life (Beck et al., 1979). Such thoughts typically surface when individuals undergo intense pain and despair, perceiving life as an overwhelming burden (Klonsky & May, 2015). In the research model, suicidal ideation is utilised as the criterion variable

Social stigma

The stigma represents a negative label or tarnish attributed to an individual because of a suicide attempt or other societal factors (Room, 2005). Such stigma leads to societal condemnation or the community casting blame on someone due to specific social traits (Dardas & Simmons, 2015). Consequently, those who are stigmatised encounter adverse perceptions and prejudice stemming from socially formed beliefs

Job insecurity

Job insecurity is defined as the perceived threat of losing one’s job or employment stability (Greenhalgh & Rosenblatt, 1984). In private hospitals, nurses often work on contractual terms, making them highly vulnerable to employment termination and remain worried about becoming unemployed. Job insecurity has been taken as the moderating variable between social sexual harassment and suicidal ideation.

Hypotheses development

Facing workplace sexual harassment is a critical psychosocial stressor that deteriorates victims’ mental well-being. Nurses facing harassment often internalise their distress, leading to psychological disorders such as depression, anxiety, and other mental illnesses (Seun Ross et al., 2019). Thus nurses facing frequent sexual harassment at workplace can produce suicidal thoughts in them.

Workplace sexual harassment is positively associated with suicidal ideation among female nurses.

Sexual harassment victims commonly experience victim-blaming, stigmatisation, or social exclusion, leading to a heightened sense of shame and isolation (Krahé & Berger, 2020). Stigma worsens emotional distress and self-worth degradation, making victims more prone to suicidal thoughts (Hildembrand, 2019). Given that social stigma impedes reporting mechanisms and isolates victims, it likely plays a mediating role between sexual harassment and suicidal ideation.

Social stigma is likely to mediate the relationship between workplace sexual harassment and suicidal ideation among female nurses.

Job insecurity compounds the psychological concerns of sexual harassment by restricting victims from seeking justice or reporting (Shoss, 2017). Nurses fearing job loss may experience increased stress, compelling them into learned helplessness and emotional distress, ultimately leading to suicidal ideation. When job security is weak, harassment’s psychological distress becomes more severe, reinforcing suicidal tendencies.

Job insecurity moderates the relationship between workplace sexual harassment and suicidal ideation, such that the relationship is stronger for nurses experiencing high job insecurity.

Methodology

Population and sampling

This research study is fundamentally characterised as explanatory, quantitative, and cross-sectional in nature. The data collection method employed for this study was a survey. The research population comprised female nurses employed in four prominent private hospitals within the metropolitan city of Hangzhou, China. Data collection occurred from September to November 2024, following approval from hospital ethical review boards. These hospitals were selected based on their willingness to provide lists of female nursing staff, from which participants were randomly sampled. Random sampling was conducted by assigning each nurse on the provided lists a unique number and using a random number generator to select 360 participants, ensuring an unbiased and representative sample (see Figure 1 for the sampling flowchart). Inclusion criteria required participants to be female nurses with at least one year of experience in the private hospital, verified by hospital HR departments during list compilation. Exclusion criteria eliminated student nurses and those with less than one year of experience, screened prior to questionnaire distribution. Nurses with less than one year of experience were excluded to ensure participants had sufficient exposure to workplace dynamics, enabling reliable assessment of chronic stressors like sexual harassment, social stigma, and job insecurity, which are linked to suicidal ideation. Exposure to workplace sexual harassment or violence was not a prerequisite for participation; the survey included questions to assess harassment exposure, enabling analysis of both exposed and non-exposed nurses. The selection of these particular hospitals was based on their willingness to provide lists of their female nursing staff, from which we randomly sampled participants for the study. We initiated contact with the administrators of each hospital to secure permission for data collection, and we submitted a written declaration to the ethical review boards of each hospital to ensure the adherence to ethical standards throughout the data collection process. To address any potential ethical concerns, the authors adhered to the ethical norms outlined in the Helsinki Declaration (Association, 1964). As part of this commitment to ethics, every participant was guaranteed by the authors that any information shared by an employee would remain confidential and would only be used for the purposes of the current research. We excluded student nurses and the nurses having less the one-year experience. We administered 360 questionnaires to the randomly selected sample and received 334 responses back. We selected 312 questionnaires complete from all respects were included for analysis.

Measures

We utilised established scales to evaluate our variables. We assessed sexual harassment using Nurses’ Sexual Harassment Scale (NSHS) developed by Zeighami et al. (2024). This 18-item scale, designed for nursing contexts, measures experiences of unwanted sexual behaviours on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Never, 5 = Very Often). The Scale of Social Stigma (SOSS) by Batterham et al. (2013) was used to gauge stigma. This 10-item scale assesses perceived societal judgement and victim-blaming on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly Disagree, 5 = Strongly Agree). SOSS measures social stigma broadly, without distinguishing public stigma (societal judgement) from self-stigma (internalised shame), which may limit precision in assessing its mediating role in suicidal ideation, as self-stigma could exert a stronger influence. Job Insecurity was measured with Job Insecurity Scale (JIS) that captures employees’ perceptions of the threat to their job continuity (De Witte, 2000). This 7-item scale evaluates concerns about job loss on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly Disagree, 5 = Strongly Agree). Suicidal Ideation was assessed with the Suicidal Ideation Attributes Scale (SIDAS) of Van Spijker et al. (2014), a 5-item scale designed to measure the frequency and severity of suicidal thoughts. This scale uses a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Never, 5 = Always) to assess suicidal ideation. As NSHS and SIDAS were developed in Western contexts, they were adapted for Chinese nurses through translation and back-translation by bilingual experts to ensure linguistic and cultural equivalence. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) confirmed their validity, with NSHS showing a single-factor structure (CFI = 0.92, RMSEA = 0.06) and SIDAS a unidimensional structure (CFI = 0.95, RMSEA = 0.05), indicating good fit for the Chinese context (see Table 2). Additionally, the survey included demographic questions on age, occupation (e.g., staff nurse, head nurse), and marital status, providing context for the findings, as presented in Table 1. These demographics were collected to characterise the sample and explore potential associations with workplace sexual harassment and suicidal ideation, such as the influence of age or occupational role on vulnerability to harassment, though not included in the primary analysis. All scales demonstrated high reliability (Cronbach’s alpha > 0.80) in prior studies and were adapted to the study’s context.

Data collection

The survey was delivered through a structured collaboration with the HR departments of the participating hospitals. Initially, researchers sought formal approval from hospital administrators to conduct the study on site. Following approval, hospital HR departments provided randomised lists of eligible female nurses who met inclusion criteria. After random selection using a number generator, researchers prepared sealed survey packets containing an information sheet, consent form, and questionnaire. These packets were delivered in person to the hospital HR offices by the research team. HR staff then distributed these packets to the selected nurses during their scheduled shifts to avoid disrupting patient care.

Participation was entirely voluntary, and nurses were explicitly informed in writing that they could decline participation or withdraw at any time without consequences. Nurses were not required to complete the survey immediately on site; instead, they were encouraged to take the survey home or complete it privately during break times to minimise any perceived coercion from face-to-face delivery.

Completed questionnaires were returned via sealed envelopes to secure collection boxes placed in designated HR offices at each hospital. This procedure preserved confidentiality and allowed participants to return surveys anonymously without interacting further with the research team. The research team later collected these sealed boxes.

This approach was designed to limit social desirability bias and reduce the possibility that personal delivery or face-to-face interaction would influence participation decisions. The study team emphasised privacy, autonomy, and voluntary participation at all stages.

Data analysis

The process of analysing the data consisted of two primary stages. In the first stage, quality assurance tests were conducted to validate the data and ensure its reliability. These tests also examined potential issues like multicollinearity and Common Method Bias. In the second stage, the researchers applied inferential statistics to evaluate the hypotheses. The study made use of the Partial Least Square (PLS) technique for Structural Equation Modelling (SEM). This approach facilitates simultaneous examination of both the structural (path model) and measurement (factor model) models within one overarching framework. By using SEM in this way, the study provided a holistic view of reliability, validity, and causal relationships

Ethical consideration

The research protocol for this study was reviewed and approved by the Zhejiang University Ethics Committee (ZUEC). All procedures involving human participants were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant national guidelines. Prior to participation, all respondents provided informed consent, were assured of the confidentiality of their responses, and were free to withdraw at any time without penalty. No personally identifiable information was collected, and data were analysed in aggregate form only. Participants received post-survey debriefing sessions to address potential distress, conducted by trained research staff. Access to free counselling services was provided through hospital-based mental health programmes and local community resources. Contact information for counselling was included in consent forms and reiterated during debriefing. These measures aimed to minimise psychological risks and support participants’ well-being.

The sample (N = 312) was entirely female, reflecting the focus on women nurses in private hospitals. Age was skewed towards younger staff: 38% were 18–25 years old and another 31% were 26–32, with only 10% aged 41 or above. Professionally, most respondents (73%) were charge nurses, while head nurses and floor nurses made up 13% and 14%, respectively. Table 1 shows the demographic information.

Reliability

Even though the scales had undergone reliability and validity tests, we conducted additional tests to account for potential discrepancies due to shifts in context and respondents. We assessed reliability using both Cronbach’s alpha and composite reliability tests (see Table 2). All variables showed Cronbach’s alpha values exceeding the threshold of 0.70, confirming inter-rater reliability. Similarly, the composite reliability values for all five variables surpassed the benchmark of 0.70. Both of these tests affirm the scale’s reliability (Wong, 2013).

Validity

The Average Variance Extracted (AVE) values, as seen in Table 2, were employed to assess convergent validity. All variables exhibited AVE values surpassing the threshold of 0.50, confirming the presence of convergent validity for each variable. We applied Fornell and Larcker (1981) criterion and Heterotrait-Monotrait (HTMT) to confirm discriminant validity as recommended by Henseler et al. (2015). Based on the Fornell-Larcker criterion, a variable should more effectively account for the loadings of its own indicator than those of a different latent variable (Hair et al., 2014). Thus, the value of each construct is greater than the correlations of other constructs endorsing high Discriminant Validity (Table 2). According to the Heterotrait-Monotrait ratio the values above 0.9 show a lack of Discriminant validity (Gold et al., 2001). In the provided scenario, every value is below 0.90, underscoring the presence of discriminant validity (refer to Table 2).

Collinearity statistics

Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) assesses multicollinearity among predictor variables in regression models, indicating how much the variance of a regression coefficient is inflated due to linear relationships with other predictors. Typically, a VIF exceeding 10 signals significant multicollinearity, though some researchers use more conservative thresholds, such as 5 (Hoyle, 1995). These values are well below the standard threshold of 10, suggesting that multicollinearity is not a significant concern in this model (Table 2). Even when applying a more stringent threshold of 3.3, the VIF values remain within acceptable limits (Hoyle, 1995). In the same vein the value below 3.3 rule out the possibility of common method bias issues (Kock & Lynn, 2012).

For Social Stigma, an R2 of 0.435 means that 43.5% of its variance is explained by the model’s predictors. The adjusted R2 of 0.433, slightly lower, suggests minimal overfitting, indicating that the model’s predictors are relevant. Likewise, R2 value for Suicide Ideation remain 0.648, meaning 64.8% of its variance is explained by the predictors. The adjusted R2 of 0.644, close to the R2, further confirms the model’s robustness. These values validate that the models have sufficient explanatory power and predictor variable have effectively accounted for the significant portion of both criterion constructs (Table 3).

This significant positive relationship suggests that sexual harassment directly increases the likelihood of suicidal thoughts among nurses. Similarly, the path from Sexual Harassment to Social Stigma shows a coefficient of 0.660 with a STDEV of 0.029. The t-statistic is notably high at 22.604, and the p-value is 0.001. This strong positive relationship indicates that sexual harassment significantly contributes to the perception of social stigma among nurses. The high t-statistic underscores the robustness of this finding. In the same way, the path from Social Stigma to Suicide Ideation has a coefficient of 0.332 and a STDEV of 0.063. The t-statistic is 5.307, with a p-value of 0.002. This indicates that higher levels of perceived social stigma are associated with increased suicidal ideation, and this relationship is statistically significant.

Discussion

The findings of this study provide critical insights into the psychological consequences of workplace sexual harassment among female nurses, particularly its association with suicidal ideation. This study extends the existing literature by demonstrating how social stigma and job insecurity interact to exacerbate the mental health toll of workplace harassment in private hospital settings. The results confirm the direct effect of workplace sexual harassment on suicidal ideation (H1), the mediating role of social stigma (H2), and the moderating effect of job insecurity (H3). These findings hold significant implications for both theoretical advancements and practical interventions in occupational mental health.

The findings validate workplace sexual harassment as a robust predictor of female nurses’ suicidal ideation (H1). Path analysis demonstrated a significant positive relationship between nurses who experience sexual harassment either from colleagues, supervisor or patients and suicidal thoughts (β = 0.618, p < 0.001) indicating that the nurses who were sexually harassed are at risk of suicidal thoughts. According to this research, it lines up with previous research that has shown how workplace harassment has damaging psychological effects such as depression, burnout, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) (Spector et al., 2019). These findings validate the argument of the Social Stress Theory, which states that due to the chronic stressors of job harassment, one is prone to psychological distress that may eventually be expressed in the form of suicide ideation (Pearlin, 1989). Due to the high stakes involved in the profession of nursing, the persistent exposure to harassment creates even greater levels of emotional exhaustion and emotional distress, which cause heightening of suicidal thoughts. This finding underscores the urgent need for targeted mental health interventions and organisational policies to mitigate the psychological consequences of workplace harassment in the nursing profession.

The study provides empirical evidence for the mediating role of social stigma in the relationship between workplace sexual harassment and suicidal ideation, confirming H2. The mediation analysis indicates that sexual harassment leads to heightened perceptions of social stigma (β = 0.660, p < 0.001), which, in turn, increases suicidal ideation (β = 0.332, p = 0.002). The indirect effect of sexual harassment on suicidal ideation through social stigma (β = 0.219, p = 0.002) suggests that stigma significantly amplifies the psychological distress of harassment victims.

This finding is consistent with prior research indicating that victims of workplace harassment often experience victim-blaming, scepticism, and reputational harm, discouraging them from reporting their experiences or seeking support (Krahé & Berger, 2020). In the healthcare sector, particularly in conservative societies, nurses who experience sexual harassment may fear being perceived as unprofessional or complicit, leading to social withdrawal, emotional distress, and self-blame (Buchanan & Fitzgerald, 2008). This aligns with Goffman (1963)stigma theory, which explains how social labelling and discrimination can erode self-esteem and psychological well-being.

The distress is further aggravated by social stigma which discourages victims to seek remedial support. Studies in occupational psychology affirm that social stigma related stress leads to depressive symptoms, self-isolation and enhanced suicidality (McKenzie et al., 2022). The existence of partial mediation in this study also suggests that though sexual harassment directly leads to suicidal ideation, stigma is one of other mechanisms through which sexual harassment will amplify the degree of distress. Investing this amount of time and energy presents an important moment to recognise the importance of workplace awareness programmes and institutional reforms as ways to fight the stigma surrounding workplace harassment.

The moderating effect of job insecurity (H3, β = 0.106, p = 0.006) was statistically significant but low in magnitude, indicating limited clinical significance. While job insecurity amplifies the harassment – suicidal ideation relationship, its small effect size suggests a modest practical impact in clinical or workplace settings. This finding implies that interventions targeting job security may have limited efficacy in mitigating the psychological consequences of harassment compared to addressing harassment directly or reducing stigma. Future studies should explore the practical impact of job insecurity’s moderating role in diverse settings, including longitudinal designs to assess whether sustained job insecurity exacerbates outcomes over time. These insights underscore the need for comprehensive workplace policies that address harassment, stigma, and job security to support nurses’ mental health. However, this adds further support to the existing body of research that implies that contract based employment and the fear of job loss prevents nurses from fostering resistance or reporting any kind of misconduct as it causes them to undergo prolonged psychological suffering (Rubaca & Munir, 2025). Theoretically, this study advances understanding of social stigma’s role as a partial mediator (H2) in the harassment – suicidal ideation relationship, refining models of workplace stress for example, Lazarus and Folkman (1984). The moderating role of job insecurity (H3) highlights how resource loss exacerbates vulnerability, aligning with Hobfoll’s Conservation of Resources (COR) theory (Hobfoll, 1989). According to COR, job insecurity represents a loss of critical resources (e.g., economic stability, workplace agency), amplifying stress from harassment and increasing suicidal ideation. This resource depletion compounds power imbalances, as nurses in precarious employment lack the bargaining power to resist or report harassment, heightening psychological distress. These findings suggest that occupational health models should integrate resource loss and power dynamics to explain harassment’s impacts. Practically, hospitals should foster supportive cultures to reduce stigma and enhance mental health resources. Promoting open communication about harassment and providing psychological support can mitigate its adverse effects, particularly for nurses facing job insecurity.

In line with work (Shoss, 2017) that establishes an association between unstable work conditions and increased mental health problems associated with workplace stress, job insecurity moderates the results. The psychological burden of nurses in precarious employment situation is further increased due to their vulnerability and susceptibility to exploitation and workplace mistreatment in the context of this study. Individuals are likely to perceive fewer exit strategies or coping mechanisms when they believe that their job security is lacking (Jiang & Probst, 2019). In fact, the findings are also in line with other studies within occupational health psychology, where job insecurity has been associated with burnout, anxiety and depressive symptoms, such as in the nursing sector, which is mostly emotionally demanding (Tito et al., 2017). Many private hospitals make use of contract based employment models for instance, and thus, this sets up a situation where employees accept mistreatment so as to keep their jobs, perpetuating power imbalances between the perpetrators and victims. This finding confirms that stronger labour rights and policies against retaliation need to be put in place so nurses feel secure when reporting on harassment concerns that they will not lose their job.

Our findings align with studies on workplace sexual harassment among doctors and women in other industries, yet reveal distinct differences. Similar to research on doctors e.g., Yang et al. (2018), our study found harassment linked to adverse mental health outcomes, with social stigma mediating the impact. However, nurses face unique occupational stressors, such as lower professional autonomy compared to doctors, which may amplify stigma’s role. Compared to women in non-healthcare industries (Jones et al., 2017), the prevalence of harassment among nurses was higher, possibly due to patient – nurse interactions and hierarchical workplace dynamics in Chinese hospitals. Unlike studies in Western contexts, cultural barriers in China, such as reluctance to report harassment, likely intensify stigma and suicidal ideation. These comparisons highlight the need for context-specific interventions, particularly in nursing, to address harassment and its psychological consequences.

Theoretical and practical implications

These findings contribute significantly to workplace psychology and healthcare literature by extending Social Stress Theory to the context of nursing professionals who experience harassment, stigma, and job insecurity. By introducing job insecurity as a boundary condition, the study highlights how precarious employment situations exacerbate mental health risks among nurses. Additionally, it sheds light on the psychological mechanisms – particularly social stigma – through which workplace harassment influences suicidal ideation, offering valuable insights for developing targeted intervention strategies.

From a practical standpoint, the study underscores the urgent need for organisational reforms in healthcare institutions, particularly in private hospitals where employment conditions tend to be less stable. To foster a safer and more supportive work environment, hospitals must implement zero-tolerance policies against workplace harassment and strengthen reporting mechanisms to encourage accountability. Establishing mental health support systems is also crucial in providing nurses with the necessary resources to cope with workplace distress. Furthermore, addressing social stigma through workplace sensitisation programmes can help promote open dialogue about harassment, reducing its harmful effects. Lastly, improving job security measures, particularly for contract-based nurses, can mitigate their vulnerability to workplace exploitation and enhance overall well-being in the healthcare sector.

Limitations and future research prospects

Despite its significant contributions, this study has certain limitations that should be acknowledged.

First, the cross-sectional research design limits the ability to establish causality between workplace sexual harassment, social stigma, job insecurity, and suicidal ideation.

This study has several limitations. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences, as associations between workplace sexual harassment, social stigma, job insecurity, and suicidal ideation do not imply causation, necessitating longitudinal studies for confirmation. Specifically, the correlation between sexual harassment and suicidal ideation cannot rule out reverse causality, where pre-existing psychological distress may heighten perceptions of harassment. Future research should employ longitudinal designs to establish temporal relationships and qualitative interviews to explore underlying causal mechanisms. The Scale of Social Stigma (SOSS) does not distinguish between public and self-stigma, potentially limiting insights into self-stigma’s direct influence on suicidal ideation via internalised shame. Future studies should use scales differentiating these stigma types to clarify their distinct effects. The verification of partial mediation by social stigma (H2) did not explore other potential mediators, such as depression or helplessness, which may also explain the harassment – suicidal ideation link. Future research should investigate these mediators to enhance understanding of underlying mechanisms. Second, the study relied on self-reported data, which may introduce common method bias and social desirability effects. The sample, drawn exclusively from four private hospitals in Hangzhou, China (N = 312), may reflect regional and cultural biases, such as higher barriers to reporting sexual harassment in Chinese contexts, limiting generalisability to other settings. Future studies should include public hospitals and cross-cultural comparisons to explore variations in harassment reporting and enhance the applicability of findings. Although statistical controls were applied to minimise bias, future research could incorporate multi-source data collection methods, such as supervisor or peer assessments, to enhance objectivity.

Third, the study was conducted in private hospitals, where employment conditions and organisational policies may differ from public healthcare institutions. Future research should explore whether these findings generalise to nurses in public hospitals or other healthcare settings with different employment structures.

Additionally, this study focused on female nurses, acknowledging that gendered power dynamics often contribute to harassment. However, future studies should investigate whether male or non-binary nurses experience similar workplace mistreatment and its psychological consequences, allowing for a more comprehensive understanding of the issue.

Finally, while this study examined social stigma and job insecurity as key mechanisms, future research could explore additional psychosocial moderators and mediators, such as workplace support systems, coping strategies, or organisational culture, to identify protective factors that may buffer against the negative effects of harassment.

By addressing these limitations, future studies can further advance the understanding of workplace mental health and contribute to the development of more effective policies and interventions to protect healthcare professionals from psychological distress.

Policy recommendations

To address workplace sexual harassment (WSH) among nurses, we propose specific interventions, informed by our findings and prior research (Caruso et al., 2022). First, implement mandatory WSH training for hospital administrators and staff to enhance recognition, prevention, and response to harassment (Xiao et al., 2024). Second, establish anonymous reporting systems to encourage disclosure without fear of retaliation, addressing cultural barriers in China. Third, conduct regular audits of workplace safety policies with external oversight to ensure compliance and effectiveness. Fourth, provide hospital-based mental health programmes, including free counselling, to support nurses experiencing harassment-related distress and reduce suicidal ideation. These targeted policies aim to create safer, supportive work environments for nurses.

Conclusion

This manuscript offers a novel contribution by empirically examining the complex relationship between workplace sexual harassment and suicidal ideation among female nurses in private hospitals. It uniquely integrates social stress theory to reveal how social stigma mediates, and job insecurity intensifies, the detrimental effects of sexual harassment on mental health. By employing a robust cross-sectional design and Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) on data from a substantial sample of 312 nurses, the study provides compelling evidence that fills a critical gap in existing literature. Its findings not only enhance our theoretical understanding of occupational psychological distress in healthcare settings but also have significant practical implications. The insights derived can directly inform the development of targeted anti-harassment policies, mental health support systems, and job security measures, ultimately aiming to safeguard and improve the well-being of nursing professionals in vulnerable work environments.
