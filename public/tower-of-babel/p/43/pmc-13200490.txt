Safety of hepatitis E vaccine in pregnancy
Authors: Fan Zhang, Guang Yang, Yuting Chen, Dongjuan Chen, Zuliang Shi, Jianbo Xia, Ling Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis comment discusses the critical public health challenge of hepatitis E in pregnancy and highlights the significant safety evidence gap for the HEV239 vaccine in this vulnerable group. Focusing on a landmark study from South Sudan by Nesbitt RC et al., we provide a critical appraisal of its findings, integrate them with prior evidence from clinical trials and animal studies, and discuss implications for vaccination policy in outbreak settings, particularly in fragile and conflict-affected contexts. The comment concludes that vaccination during pregnancy shows no detected increased risk of fetal loss, providing crucial real-world data to inform life-saving vaccination policies, while acknowledging remaining uncertainties.

Hepatitis E virus (HEV), particularly genotype 1, poses a significant and disproportionate threat to pregnant women, with infection during pregnancy being associated with high rates of fulminant hepatic failure, miscarriage, and maternal mortality, which can exceed 30% in them [1, 2]. While a licensed and efficacious vaccine, HEV239(Hecolin; Xiamen Innovax Biotech, Xiamen, China) [3], has demonstrated high efficacy and a good safety profile in the general adult population, a critical evidence gap remains regarding its use during pregnancy. Pregnant women are routinely excluded from initial clinical trials of new vaccines due to justifiable ethical and safety concerns. Consequently, data on the safety of HEV239 in this vulnerable demography is inherently limited. This comment adopts a policy-oriented, translational perspective, bridging human and animal evidence to inform decision-making for hepatitis E vaccination in pregnancy, especially in fragile and conflict-affected settings.

Research on hepatitis E vaccine in women of childbearing age or pregnant animals

Establishing the safety profile of HEV239 during pregnancy is a paramount public health priority, especially in endemic regions. Such evidence is crucial for informing potential vaccination strategies that could protect this high-risk group from severe outcomes. Therefore, the recent publication in The Lancet Global Health by Nesbitt et al. [4] detailing the safety of the HEV239 in pregnancy represents a significant milestone in tropical medicine and public health. Conducted in the challenging environment of the Bentiu internally displaced persons (IDP) camp in South Sudan, this study provides the first robust, real-world evidence that administering the HEV239 during pregnancy does not increase the risk of fetal loss. This finding is not merely an academic conclusion; it is a powerful tool that can directly influence life-saving public health decisions during future hepatitis E outbreaks. Beyond restating these results, our comment aims to (i) contextualize them within the broader body of evidence from clinical trials, post-hoc analyses, and animal studies; (ii) critically appraise the methodological strengths and limitations of the Bentiu study; and (iii) derive practical, context-specific recommendations for policymakers.

The main findings are clear within the study’s limitations: the cumulative risk of fetal loss was 7.2% among pregnant women vaccinated during pregnancy, compared to 6.1% in the unvaccinated group, with a risk ratio of 1.2 and a 95% confidence interval of 0.7–1.9. Since the confidence interval includes 1 (the null value), this result did not detect a statistically significant increase in the risk of fetal loss. This conclusion was consistently confirmed in subgroup analyses focusing on vaccination during the first trimester (the most sensitive period for fetal development) and in analyses of pre-pregnancy vaccination. Multiple sensitivity analyses also failed to alter this fundamental conclusion, demonstrating the robustness of the findings. Supporting translational evidence comes from rabbit model studies, which have shown that HEV239 vaccination before or during pregnancy can protect against adverse pregnancy outcomes. These findings complement the human data but cannot substitute for clinical evidence [5, 6].

This discovery has profound public health implications. First, it directly addresses the concerns of policymakers and clinicians, providing much-needed safety evidence for including pregnant women in the target population for vaccination during hepatitis E outbreaks. Second, it strengthens confidence in the existing The World Health Organization (WHO) recommendation, which was already based on a balance between the “extremely high risk of HEV infection to pregnant women” and the “expected very low risk from the recombinant vaccine.” Now, with this real-world safety data from a sample of over 2000 women vaccinated during pregnancy, the balance of this trade-off is further supported by these findings. The WHO has already recommended that in fragile, conflict-affected and vulnerable settings with documented hepatitis E virus circulation, the benefits of vaccinating women of childbearing age (≥ 16 years and over) outweigh the potential harms, including during pregnancy where the risk of severe disease in particularly high during the second and third trimester [7]. Despite these advancements, implementation decisions remain context-dependent and should consider outbreak severity, healthcare access, and individual risk assessment. And that safety data alone does not resolve all implementation questions; outbreak use in pregnancy also depends on the expected degree of protection under real-world campaign conditions, including vaccine effectiveness, logistical feasibility, and community acceptance.

The study has several inherent limitations. First, reliance on self‑reported pregnancy status and vaccination dates—with documentary confirmation for only 57–65% of participants—risks misclassification. This is especially concerning for very early pregnancies: women who miscarried before recognizing or reporting their pregnancy would be systematically missed. Such under‑ascertainment would bias results toward the null if vaccination truly increased early fetal loss. Conversely, differential recall by outcome status could bias in either direction. However, the team cross‑checked reports against campaign dates and vaccination card photos, and sensitivity analyses restricted to documented cases yielded similar results, mitigating this concern. Second, unmeasured confounding cannot be fully excluded despite propensity score matching. The bias indicator analysis—comparing pregnancies that ended before vaccination—showed no baseline difference in fetal loss between future vaccinated and unvaccinated groups, reducing the likelihood that confounding explains the null result. Third, the study likely missed early miscarriages, biasing toward null. Yet the consistency of findings across subgroup analyses (first‑trimester exposure, miscarriage alone) and sensitivity analyses (different matching criteria, card‑confirmed vaccination) supports robustness. The cumulative risk estimates were nearly identical (7.2% vs. 6.1%), with a confidence interval (0.7–1.9) excluding clinically meaningful harm. Thus, while absolute safety cannot be proven, the available evidence suggests that any undetected risk is probably small. However, given the observational nature of the data and the specific context of the Bentiu camp, some residual uncertainty remains. The findings are reassuring but not definitive, and further studies in diverse settings are needed to confirm safety across pregnant population. Finally, the prolonged duration of the three doses (0, 1 and 6 months) routine vaccine immunization schedule is also an impact. Animal or population studies have shown that two doses (0, and 30 days) or accelerated vaccination schedule (0, 7 and 21 days/ 0 and 7 days) may could be used to reduce this effect [6, 8, 9]. An ongoing phase 4 randomized trial in Pakistan (NCT05808166) is prospectively evaluating the safety and immunogenicity of HEV239 in pregnant women during the second and third trimesters, which will provide complementary data to the South Sudan findings.

In conclusion, the Nesbitt et al. study provides critical real-world evidence that HEV239 vaccination during pregnancy does not increase the risk of fetal loss, may reducing uncertainty in this area. These findings support the inclusion of pregnant women in outbreak vaccination strategies, particularly in high-risk settings. However, ongoing and future studies are needed to confirm safety across pregnant populations and to assess long-term outcomes.
