Science and sensitivity in the study of not having sex
Authors: Felix C. Tropf
Published: 2025

Sexual activity—a fundamental human function—is declining in the United States. A recently published report by the Institute for Family Studies (IFS) documents what they call: a sex recession (1). Between 1990 and 2024, the frequency of at least weekly sexual intercourse of American adults in the ages between 18 and 64 y fell from 55 to 37% (1), and between 2013-15 and 2022-23 virginity among young adults (aged 22 to 34) increased from 4 to 10% for males and from 5 to 7% for females (2). In PNAS, Abdellaoui et al. (3) study social and genetic correlations with having had or never having had sex. This is not only of interest for researchers from various fields, but also relevant for public health and relationships in society. Having no sex can—but does not have to—lead to disconnection and in very extreme cases even to radicalization and violence.

Correlates with Never Having Had Sex

Studying residents from the United Kingdom with European ancestry and aged 39 to 73, Abdellaoui et al. (3) successfully link having had sex or not to 148 out of 251 selected measures from across the social and life sciences—and to DNA. This topic is hard to research because only approximately 1% of the study population remained having had no sex implying uncertainty for statistical statements. However, the UK BioBank balances this with its large sample size of around 400,000 individuals included in the study.

This is clearly a landmark study confirming and expanding previous research (4–6) on a very large scale. Especially the sample size and number of tested associations are by far the largest studied until today. Furthermore, the UK BioBank measures having had no sex at later ages than previous studies. Finally, Abdellaoui et al. (3) included same sex sexual behavior in their study which preceding work had not.

A snapshot of the main findings in this explorative effort is that having had no sex is statistically associated with psychological/psychiatric traits (e.g., more introversion, higher cognitive abilities, higher on the autistic spectrum), socioeconomic traits (e.g., more education), physical traits (e.g., lower grip strength, lower upper body strength for males), social circumstances (e.g., social disconnection, loneliness), substance use (less engagement with drugs and alcohol), and lower well-being on average. Studying the context of individuals who have not had sex, they live in regions with higher income inequality and males live in regions with fewer women. While associations cover a broad spectrum of measures, each of the estimated effect sizes is small.

A special feature is that the authors investigate the molecular genetic basis of a life without sex. They find that 12% of the observed differences between individuals with and without having had sex are associated with genetic differences between those groups. This might be surprising, given that natural selection should have reduced genetic variation in reproductive traits toward zero in the past since people who did not have sex have not passed on their genes across generations (7). Ancestral analyses show that indeed the strongest genetic signal they find got trimmed throughout the past 10,000 y—most likely by the process of natural selection. However, genetic variation may be maintained to some extent since genes associated with having no sex are not entirely the same for males and females and therefore can be transmitted via the other sex. Other possible reasons are, for example, changes in society and culture (e.g., sexual revolution) which may lead to associations of new regions of the genome with sexual behavior, as well as novel mutations entering the population, migration, and others (8).

Understanding the Correlations

Correlation is not causation, and the study highlights the need for a better understanding of the observed patterns. The correlations do not tell us whether, for example, a lack of sex lowers people’s well-being or whether individuals with lower well-being have no sex—or whether a third factor such as a set of genes influence both simultaneously. This applies to all observed correlations, and complex causal relations are possible.

Determining causality is challenging though especially since having sex or not as an experimental treatment would be both infeasible and unethical. Observational studies ideally utilize so called quasi-experimental approaches which mimic experiments on a statistical basis. The authors suggest the use of Mendelian Randomization, exploiting effects of randomly assorted genes as experiment (9)—a strategy that has also been criticized (10). Nongenetic quasi-experiments are possible too, such as using the regional sex-ratio as randomizer for having sex assuming, among others, it is not causal for a tested outcome (see also ref. 11).

Importantly, having sex and being in a partnership are intertwined and can have effects in the same direction. Both can have positive effects on well-being, via stress release (12) or social support (3), for example. While having sex is an indicator of being in a relationship, it is not exclusive to partnerships—and a partnership does not imply having sex. Nonetheless, correlations might be a challenge to tease apart. In this example, evidence also suggests that the effect of stress release from having sex depends on the relationship quality (12), which starts to make things more complicated, and shows that we required a deeper understanding.

The Need for Education and Intervention

Causality and a better understanding of associations will be of great importance if we think about impact and interventions based on this type of research too. Next to general educational programs and psychological support to help people better understand their sexuality and sexual identity, there are severe threats to the safety in society, especially for women.

One example is the group of male involuntary celibates (INCELs) who are under the belief—among others—that a small group of men have sex with most of the women, which however is not the case (13). Consequences can be dramatic. Such groups have previously gathered in internet forums and radicalized, advocating for and manifesting societal disruption, including violence toward women. Only between 2014 and 2018, reactionary INCELs killed 50 people across North America and Europe and the thorough analysis of factors that amplify young men’s difficulties in partnering is needed (14). The female group of female involuntary celibates (FEMCELs), in contrast, mainly spreads irony and nihilism online (15). They suffer from dissociation and disillusionment based on a logic of pain and fatalism, experience sadness, depression, and (social) hopelessness, which does not lead to physically aggressive attacks on society, but presents an outlet reinforcing their views (15).

A More Differentiated View

However, it is very important to keep in mind that INCELS/FEMCELs are a special case among individuals who remain without having sex. Not all individuals who would like to but do not have sex become INCELs/FEMCELs. This extreme trajectory has been linked to mental health issues, ideology, and younger ages (16). Having no sex, also can be voluntary, and this can likewise have different reasons. It can be based on a lack of sexual attraction [asexuality (5)] or, for example, have cultural roots (17).

Also, the experience of having sex—positive or negative—requires differentiation. An undifferentiated comparison of groups of individuals who had sex and who hadn’t can hide substantial insights. As an example, having sex is associated with a more meaningful life for males in this study, but also voluntary sexlessness can as well be regarded as meaningful by individuals depending on spirituality or context (17). Differentiating by reasons for having had no sex, group comparisons could therefore show up in statistics with larger effects than the presented ubiquitously small effect sizes.

At the same time, interventions need to be tailor-made too. In the case of INCELs/FEMCELs selection mechanisms need to be understood and early detection and intervention is required. The asexual visibility & education network (https://www.asexuality.org/), in contrast, helps individuals, families, friends, and the public to better understand reasons for asexuality and to accept that it is intrinsic, and that lifestyles, romantic feelings, and levels of happiness are as diverse—and high, if people are empowered to live the life they genuinely wish—within their network as it is in the general population. Understanding, acceptance, and empowerment are key here, not intervention.

Science and Sensitivity

This is a very timely study given the trends initially reported, and cautious remarks are necessary for interpretation. First, as mentioned before, all associations are small and potentially only detectable due to the large data source utilized. Second, while large, the UK BioBank is not representative of the UK population which limits generalizability of results—future research will benefit from work on these issues (e. g. 18). Third, genetic results have been replicated in Australia which gives confidence that they apply across Western contexts, but investigations beyond European ancestry groups are needed. Finally, results have been placed in the context of having no sex in general. Unique features of a lifetime without sex need to be understood separately and it is likely asexuality plays an important role here as the prevalence of 1% in the UK BioBank approximates the population level (5).

Two important mentions of the genetic analysis are first that genes predict with probability—not deterministically. Second, the study shows that genes associated with having had no sex are predictive for age at having sex the first time, suggesting that sexlessness is on a genetic spectrum rather than binary. However, further considerations are required for the interpretation of the presented genetic analyses - see also Box 1 in Abdellaoui et al. (3) - in particular since sexlessness is a sensitive phenotype (19, 20).

A central concern is that we are talking about combinations of very small minority populations with potentially great, while different sensitivities and vulnerabilities. Having sex is connected to intimacy and can relate to identity also based on religious feelings (17). The interpretation as well as the utilization of such findings therefore need to be treated with consideration. Practically, that means, for example, scientists working together with ethics specialists and/or with the respective communities (20).

Finally, above suggestions for future research all ask for even larger and more detailed data. Practically, this is possible. Projects such as the Future Health Biobank UK plan to release up to five million individuals with detailed data. Here, however, we do not only keep in mind the very low prevalence of individuals who never had sex in the population and the resulting, higher chances for identification of groups and individuals. Moreover, while in depth scientific rigor is what we work for and what can empower counselors, educational programs, and decision makers, it cannot come on the costs of vulnerable populations, and it is not what we need to mutually accept and appreciate our sexual identities in partnerships, families, and societies (see also https://www.asexuality.org/ for further information).
