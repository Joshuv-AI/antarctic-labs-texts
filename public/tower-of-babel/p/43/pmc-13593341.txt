Effect of selective and nonselective fusion on cervical sagittal alignment in Lenke 5 scoliosis
Authors: Muhammed Fatih Serttas, Alauddin Kochai, Buğra Han Eryilmaz, Uğur Özdemir, Abdülhalim Akar, Ahmet Çağri Uyar, Mustafa Erkan İnanmaz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:This study compares the change in cervical sagittal alignment (CSA) after surgery in Lenke 5 patients who underwent selective and nonselective fusion and to evaluate the effect of this situation on functional results.Methods:A total of 50 Lenke 5 AIS patients who underwent posterior pedicle screw fusion between January 2015 and March 2021 were retrospectively reviewed. The patients were divided into 2 groups: selective and nonselective fusion groups. Preoperative and postoperative radiological measurements of the patients were made using the SURGIMAP (Nemaric Inc., New York) (https://www.surgimap.com/) measurement system. Clinical outcomes were evaluated with the Scoliosis Research Society-22 (SRS-22) and Neck Disability Index (NDI) questionnaires.Results:While cervical lordosis increases significantly in the selective group, it does not change in the nonselective group. While TK decreased significantly in the nonselective fusion group, it did not change in the selective group. C2 slope measurements show a significant decrease in the selective group in the postoperative period, and T1 slope shows a significant increase in the postoperative nonselective group. A positive, statistically significant relationship was found between the rate of change in CL measurements and the rate of change in cSVA measurements in the selective group. There is no significant difference between the SRS-22 and NDI scores of the preoperative and postoperative groups.Conclusion:While postoperative C2 to C7 lordosis increased significantly in patients who underwent selective fusion, the straight of CSA did not change in the nonselective group. Since this may be a risk factor for cervical pathologies in older ages in the nonselective group, care should be taken not to reduce TK in patients with nonselective fusion.

1. Introduction

The main aim of surgical treatment of adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS) is to obtain a balanced spine by correcting the 3-dimensional deformity.[1] Surgical treatment has been mainly focused on correction in the coronal plane, but sagittal spinal alignment (SSA) has gained importance in recent years.[2–5] Different results regarding SSA have been reported following surgical treatment of AIS.[6] When evaluating SSA, cervical lordosis (CL), thoracic kyphosis (TK), lumbar lordosis (LL), and the relationships between the pelvis and sacrum are taken into account.[7]

Although different treatment methods have been described, posterior spinal fusion (PSF) is still the most commonly used technique in the surgical treatment of AIS.[8] According to Lenke classification, Type 5 curves consist of major TL/L curve and compensatory thoracic curve, and selective fusion is recommended for major TL/L curves in these patients if the thoracic curve is flexible.[9] Selective fusion refers to the surgical correction of only the major curve while allowing the compensatory curve to correct spontaneously. The main goal is to preserve the motion of as many segments as possible. Sanders et al reinforced this by recommending selective fusion when the TL/L:T Cobb ratio is > 1.25 and the thoracic curve corrects to < 20°.[10] In contrast, nonselective fusion includes both the major and compensatory curves in the surgical correction. This is considered when the compensatory curve is structural or rigid, or when correcting only the major curve risks postoperative imbalance, such as shoulder asymmetry or residual deformity. A few studies examine the relationship between cervical spinal alignment (CSA) and other spinal sagittal profiles in Lenke 5 AIS patients who underwent surgical correction.[11–13] Although all of these studies evaluate the results of patients who underwent selective fusion, no study evaluating the CSA of Lenke 5 patients who underwent nonselective fusion.

This study aims to compare postoperative CSA in Lenke 5 patients who underwent selective versus nonselective fusion and to evaluate its effect on functional results.

2. Methods

2.1. Patient selection and clinical investigation

Following ethics committee approval, 87 Lenke 5 AIS patients who underwent single-stage posterior pedicle screw fusion between January 2015 and March 2021 were evaluated for the study. In all patients, the indication for surgery was a major TL/L curve >40°. Patients with TL/L:T Cobb ratio < 1.25, thoracic curve correction > 20°, and rib hump underwent nonselective fusion, while other patients underwent selective fusion. The inclusion criteria were patients operated on for AIS; visibility of the pelvis, femoral heads, and C2 vertebral body on standing whole spine AP and lateral radiographs in the preoperative and postoperative periods; follow-up period of at least 2 years; patients with preoperative functional scores.

Patients who underwent revision spine surgery, patients over 19 years of age, and those without coronal balance at the final postoperative follow-up (defined as a distance >20 mm between the vertical lines drawn from the middle of the S1 vertebra and the center of the C7 vertebra) were excluded from the study. Additionally, patients with a long main TL/L curve extending above T10 at the thoracolumbar junction were excluded from the study to maintain similar curve distributions between the groups. According to the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 50 AIS patients were included in the study.

The patients were divided into 2 groups. Group 1: patients with an upper instrumented vertebra at T10 or below, including only the main TL/L curvature, were classified into the selective PSF group. Group 2: patients with upper instrumented vertebra at T5 or above, including the compensatory curvature, were classified into the nonselective PSF group. The radiological and functional results of the groups were compared.

2.2. Radiological evaluation

Preoperative and final follow-up radiographs of the patients were taken on long cassettes in the posteroanterior and lateral planes while standing, using standard procedures. Radiological measurements were evaluated using the SURGIMAP (Nemaric Inc.) (https://www.surgimap.com/) measurement system. The flexibility of the main and compensatory curvatures was determined from bending and traction radiographs. Cobb angles of structural curves were measured using standing whole spine radiographs, and the main TL/L curve was measured as described by Lenke et al.[9] The measured sagittal spinal and spinopelvic parameters were as follows: TK (T5–12); thoracolumbar kyphosis (TLK, T10–L2); CL (C2–7 Cobb angle); T1 slope; LL (L1–S1); pelvic incidence; sacral slope; pelvic tilt; C7 sagittal vertical axis (SVA); C2 to C7 sagittal vertical axis (cSVA); C2 slope.

All data were reported as the mean ± standard deviation of 3 independent measurements, and categorical variables were presented as percentages. An experienced spine surgeon also reviewed the medical records and clear radiographs for all cases.

2.3. Postoperative clinical assessment

The clinical influences were assessed using the Scoliosis Research Society-22 (SRS-22) and Neck Disability Index (NDI) questionnaires. The questionnaire was completed before surgical treatment and at the last follow-up.

3. Statistical analysis

NCSS (Number Cruncher Statistical System) 2007 (Kaysville) program was used for statistical analysis. Descriptive statistical methods (mean, standard deviation, median, frequency, percentage, minimum, maximum) were used when evaluating the study data. The suitability of quantitative data for normal distribution was tested with the Shapiro–Wilk test and graphical analysis. Student t test was used to compare normally distributed quantitative variables between 2 groups; Mann–Whitney U test was used for comparisons between 2 groups of quantitative variables that did not show normal distribution. The choice between Student t test and Mann–Whitney U test was based on the normality of data as determined by the Shapiro–Wilk test and graphical distribution analysis. Paired Samples t-test was used in the preoperative and postoperative evaluation of normally distributed variables. Fisher Exact test was used to compare qualitative data. Spearman correlation analysis was used to evaluate the relationships between quantitative variables. Statistical significance was defined as P < .05.

The number of fused vertebrae ranged from 4 to 14 and was statistically higher in the nonselective fusion group. 5. Discussion

Studies in the literature have focused on preserving the number of mobile segments by minimizing the fusion level in patients with major TL/L curvature. Sanders et al reported that selective fusion was successful in cases where the TL/L: T Cobb ratio was >1.25, the thoracic curve was corrected <20°, and the triradiate cartilage was closed.[10] Conversely, surgeons argue that nonselective fusion may be required due to concerns about postoperative shoulder imbalance, thoracic hump remaining and coronal balance not being achieved. Although much emphasis is placed on how postoperative coronal balance is affected in AIS, there are very few studies in the literature about SSA. Additionally, the mechanism and factors affecting cervical sagittal imbalance in patients with AIS remain unclear.[3–5] While some studies evaluate the effect of selective fusion on CSA and SSA, no studies have addressed how CSA is affected in patients undergoing nonselective fusion.[14] In this study, we observed that cervical straightening continued in patients who underwent nonselective fusion, while natural CL developed over time in those who underwent selective fusion. Because the fusion applied to the thoracic region caused a decrease in kyphosis, we observed cervical straightening as the spine adjusted to maintain sagittal harmony.

AIS patients often have a kyphotic or straight cervical spine and a hypokyphotic thoracic spine in the preoperative period.[15–17] Yagi et al examined 89 AIS patients and reported that the preoperative cervical alignment was kyphotic, and even if the TK was restored postoperatively, the CSA remained kyphotic or slightly lordotic.[18] It has also been reported that hypokyphotic thoracic alignment causes sagittal malalignment in the cervical spine after surgery.[15,19,20] In their study examining CSA changes, Luo et al showed a strong correlation between CL and TK and noted that CL was preserved postoperatively in AIS patients with preoperative thoracic hypokyphosis. They also reported that preoperative thoracic normokyphotic patients were postoperatively hypokyphotic and experienced increased cervical kyphosis.[21] However, while the majority of patients in these studies were AIS patients with main thoracic curves, such as Lenke 1, very few were Lenke 5 patients, and the relationship between postoperative CSA and other sagittal spinal parameters has not been fully examined. Our study evaluated the radiological and functional parameters of Lenke 5 AIS patients in detail, both preoperatively and postoperatively (Figs. 1 and 2). We examined the correlation of CSA with changes in other sagittal spinal parameters over an average follow-up of 5 years.

Okubo et al reported that in Lenke 5 AIS, postoperative TK and CL increased in patients with an UEV at T9 and above, whereas no significant change in sagittal alignment was observed in patients with a UEV at T10 and below.[11] In our study, we excluded patients whose TL/L curvature ended above the thoracolumbar junction to maintain a similar distribution between the groups. Wang et al reported a strong relationship between C2 and C7 lordosis and the T1 slope in Lenke 5 AIS, noting that the restoration of global thoracic sagittal alignment directly affects CSA.[12] However, the adjustment of CSA was limited due to the inherent stiffness of the cervical spine. In our study, the cervical spine remained straight in the nonselective fusion group, while CL significantly increased in selective fusion patients. This may be because the CSA remains straight to maintain global sagittal alignment due to the reduction of thoracolumbar junction kyphosis and TK in nonselective fusion. In selective fusion, over time, the CSA developed the mobile segment necessary for lordotic alignment. We observed a more ergonomic alignment characterized by an increase in the T1 slope and a decrease in the C2 slope.

The effect of postoperative CSA on clinical symptoms in AIS patients is unclear; neck pain is reported in up to 57% of patients following surgical correction.[22,23] Additionally, cervical malalignment may pose a risk factor for disc herniation in older age. Patients with cervical imbalance reportedly have significantly higher VAS values than the control group.[24] However, the impact of cervical alignment on functional scores in Lenke 5 AIS is unknown. In this study, we evaluated the preoperative and postoperative functional outcomes of patients undergoing selective and nonselective fusion using the SRS-22 and NDI, observing no significant difference between the groups. Youn et al reported significant relationships between postoperative cervical sagittal parameters and health-related quality of life (HRQOL) scores, with particularly high T1 slope negatively impacting HRQOL scores.[25] In our study, we observed a significant postoperative increase in the T1 slope in the nonselective group, which may be a risk factor in older age. Evaluating long-term follow-up results would be useful to assess the effect of CSA on HRQOL.

Our study has inherent methodological limitations due to its retrospective natüre, resulting in an inevitably low level of evidence.ü. The small sample size and relatively short follow-up period are notable disadvantages. In addition, standing AP and lateral radiographs may not have fully demonstrated the 3-dimensional deformity of scoliosis. Moreover, although patients were instructed to look straight ahead, slight changes in head position could have affected various cervical sagittal parameters. Additionally, due to the relatively small sample size, multivariate analysis could not be performed on the effect of follow-up duration on the results. Despite these limitations, our study is important for evaluating in detail the effect of cervical alignment on radiological and functional outcomes in Lenke 5 AIS patients. Its greatest value lies in being the first study to focus on the effect of nonselective fusion on CSA in Lenke 5 AIS.

6. Conclusion

While postoperative C2 to C7 lordosis increased significantly in patients who underwent selective fusion, the straight CSA did not change in the nonselective group. Although this situation does not change functional results, care should be taken not to reduce TK in patients undergoing nonselective fusion, as it may pose a risk factor for cervical pathologies in older age. Given the relatively small number of patients and short follow-up period in this study, studies with larger sample sizes and longer follow-up periods may provide more definitive results.
