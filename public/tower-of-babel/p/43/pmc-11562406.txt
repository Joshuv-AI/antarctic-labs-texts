Multiple disruptions of superior suspensory shoulder complex: A case report
Authors: Manh Khanh Nguyen, Van Hoc Nguyen, Hoang Gia Khoa Le, Dinh Bang Cao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe superior shoulder suspensory complex (SSSC) is a ligamentous anatomical structure that maintains shoulder girdle stability. It comprises a ring of bones and tissues, including the glenoid fossa, coracoid process (CP), coracoclavicular ligaments, distal clavicle, acromioclavicular (AC) joint, and acromion. Goss first described the structure in 1993. Disruption in two or more structures results in an unstable lesion. This report presents a rare case of triple disruption of the acromion, CP, and AC joint. Triple disruption of the SSSC is uncommon; therefore, the treatment is debatable. In our case, surgical intervention was performed to fix the instability of the shoulder girdle, which improved the patient's shoulder function.Case presentationA 43-year-old woman presented with an acromial fracture, a CP fracture, and dislocation of the AC joint after a traffic accident. The patient underwent surgery and postoperative rehabilitation.DiscussionSeveral treatment options exist for multiple disruptions of the shoulder stabilizing SSSC, including K-wire fixation, plate fixation, or arthroscopic-assisted devices. As the patient's acromion size was smaller than the average female acromion, K-wire fixation was deemed the most suitable method.ConclusionMultiple disruptions of the SSSC are rare and typically result from high-velocity injuries. Surgical treatment should be tailored to the specific injury patterns and the individual patient's condition.

Introduction

The superior shoulder suspensory complex (SSSC) was first described by Goss in 1993 [1]. SSSC injuries are relatively rare and account for 3–5 % of all shoulder girdle fractures and 1 % of all fractures [2]. The SSSC contains several bone and tissue structures that maintain the biomechanics of the shoulder, including the glenoid fossa, coracoid process (CP), coracoclavicular ligament, distal clavicle, acromioclavicular (AC) joint, and acromion. The ligamentous complex connects the superior and inferior arches of the shoulder girdle (Fig. 1) [3]. The superior arch includes the AC joint, sternoclavicular joint, and costoclavicular junction, while the inferior arch consists of the glenoid fossa, scapulothoracic space, and scapula.Fig. 1The superior suspensory shoulder complex. a: anteroposterior view; b: lateral view [3].Fig. 1

The superior suspensory shoulder complex. a: anteroposterior view; b: lateral view [3].

Disruption in two locations in the SSSC complex results in an unstable lesion [1], most commonly after high-velocity trauma. The injury patterns described in this case report involve multiple disruptions affecting the AC joint, coracoclavicular ligaments, and acromion, collectively referred to as “triple disruptions” of the SSSC.

The choice of treatment for multiple SSSC disruptions remains controversial. Studies on these injuries are rare, and management guidelines are absent. Kose et al. [4] reported 48 cases of shoulder injuries with various treatment options, ranging from conservative therapy to surgical intervention. Surgical therapy involves open reduction and internal fixation using K-wires, plates, cancellous screws, endobuttons, or tightropes in arthroscopic-assisted surgery. Proponents of operative management contend that stabilizing the unstable components of the shoulder enhances its integrity, leading to improved rehabilitation and function. In the present case, we examined whether surgical intervention is indicated to restore all violated components, promote early movement, and improve shoulder function outcomes.

Respecting the SCARE 2023 statement we present the following case [5].

Case presentation

Clinical presentations and radiological findings

A 43-year-old female patient was admitted to our department after a high-energy motorcycle accident 2 weeks ago. The patient presented with shoulder pain, bruising, and loss of normal function without any other associated trauma. Clinical examinations revealed the wellness of the neurovascular system. Shoulder radiographs showed acromion and coracoid fractures, as well as AC joint dislocation. Three-dimensional (3D) computed tomography (CT) reconstruction revealed a Kuhn type II acromion fracture extending to the scapular line, a CP fracture classified as type II according to the Ogawa classification, and an AC joint separation graded as grade III (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Preoperative. a: Radiographic images; b, c: computed tomography images.Fig. 2

Surgical procedure

Surgery was performed 2 weeks after the accident. The patient was placed in a beach chair position under general anesthesia. First, we made a 14-cm skin incision originating from the scapular spine and extended it to the deltopectoral line. The coracoid was exposed after deep dissection of the deltopectoral line. The coracoid fracture was type II according to the Ogawa classification and was displaced inferiorly due to contraction of the short head of the biceps. Intraoperative evaluation confirmed the AC joint dislocation and associated damage to the coracoclavicular ligament. The CP fracture was later fixed with a 3.5-mm cancellous screw. Second, we retracted the posterior part of the deltoid muscle laterally and exposed the fracture of the acromion extending to the scapular line, which was a Kuhn type II fracture. The patient's acromion was minor in length, width, and thickness compared to the average values reported by Saha and Vasudeva [6] and Piyawinijwong et al. [7]. After fracture reduction, two 2.0-mm Kirschner wires were used to fix the fracture site. The AC joint and distal clavicle were then exposed. Because the acromion was small, there was only sufficient space to fix the AC joint using a 1.8 mm K-wire with the tension-band technique. Finally, the AC ligament injury was repaired using synthetic absorbable sutures (Fig. 3).Fig. 3a: Operative approach; b: postoperative radiographic images.Fig. 3

a: Operative approach; b: postoperative radiographic images.

Postoperative management

The patient was instructed to wear a shoulder brace for 4 weeks. Passive gentle motion of the shoulder and active motion of the elbow were encouraged from the 2nd day to reduce local swelling and maintain shoulder function. After 4 weeks, the patient started more vigorous passive exercises and progressed to active, non-weight-bearing physiotherapy after seven weeks. At the end of 4 months, the patient experienced no pain and achieved full range of motion (Fig. 4).Fig. 4Patient's shoulder function at the end of 4 months.Fig. 4

Discussion

Fractures involving two or more SSSC components are rare. To our knowledge, only a few cases have been reported in the literature. Therefore, treatment of these complex injuries remains controversial. Careful preoperative planning and surgical intervention are fundamental to ensure positive outcomes and maintain shoulder function. Preoperative MRI could have been utilized to confirm coracoclavicular ligament damage and potential rotator cuff injuries. However, intraoperative assessment confirmed the coracoclavicular ligament injury, necessitating AC joint fixation.

Goss's case report [1] comprised two SSSC injury cases. In one case, the acromial fracture was fixed using a plate, the distal clavicle fracture was fixed with a guidewire, and the coracoid fracture was fixed with a K-wire. In the other case, Goss decided not to operate on the distal clavicle and CP fractures and stabilized the acromial fracture with a guidewire. However, Goss recommended using cannulated screws instead of K-wires for the stabilization of coracoid fractures.

Jaen et al. [8] reported an SSSC fracture involving the distal clavicle, CP, and acromion. In this case, three plates were used to completely stabilize the distal clavicular fractures. The acromion was fixed with two plates, whereas the CP fracture was fixed with cannulated screws. Finally, an endobutton was used to repair the coracoclavicular ligament injury. Both the clavicular and acromial fractures healed, whereas pseudarthrosis developed in the CP fracture. The second procedure was performed to ensure healing of the CP fracture.

Kim et al. [9] presented a complicated case of a fracture involving the SSSC and humeral shaft. The acromion fracture was fixed using a tension-band wire system, the AC joint separation was stabilized with three pins, and a cannulated screw was used to fix the CP fracture. The patient also received an intramedullary nail for the fixation of the humeral shaft fracture.

Tuor et al. [10] reported five cases of SSSC injuries that were treated operatively. In the first case, the CP was reduced and fixed with a 3.5-mm cannulated screw and the AC joint with a hook plate. In the second case, two cannulated screws were arthroscopically inserted into the CP, and the AC joint was reduced and transfixed with two K-wires. In the third case, the patient's CP fracture was reduced and fixed using a 4.5 mm cannulated screw combined with osteosynthesis of the clavicle fracture with a plate. Similar to the first case, the patient in the fourth case had the CP fixed with a cannulated screw and an AC joint with a hook plate. However, an additional anchor was used to repair the AC ligament injury. In the fifth case, the AC joint hook plate and CP-cannulated screw were the chosen option. In addition, the patients had no complicated combined injuries that affected the subsequent rehabilitation process.

In our case, the CP was reduced and fixed using a 3.5-mm screw. The acromion fracture was stabilized using a tension-band wire system with two 2.0-mm K-wires. The AC joint separation was later transfixed with only one 1.8-mm K-wire. The patient's acromion size was below that of the average female acromion, according to Piyawinijwong et al. [7] (width, 26.2 mm; length, 47.3; thickness, 8.1 mm) and Saha and Vasudeva [6] (width, 21.82 mm; length, 41 mm; thickness, 6.58 mm). Preoperative CT 3D reconstruction showed that the patient's acromion size was 17.27 mm in width, 33.95 mm in length, and 5.14 mm in thickness, which was insufficient for the insertion of the second K-wire (Fig. 5). However, all three K-wires were inserted bicortically; therefore, the fracture sites were successfully stabilized. The application of a hook plate in this case may have induced the impingement syndrome and increased the difficulty of the rehabilitation process [11]. In the presence of CP fractures, modern AC joint-stabilization techniques, such as arthroscopic fixation with Endobutton or Tightrope, are not advisable [10].Fig. 5The patient's acromion size. a: wide; b: length, c: thickness.Fig. 5

Similar to our case, triple disruptions of SSSC are rare and have been reported in only a few studies. There are currently no specific guidelines for the treatment of SSSC injuries. Therefore, the treatment of choice for multiple SSSC disruptions remains controversial. Goss introduced the concept of SSSC injuries as a ring fracture model, asserting that the disruption of two or more components results in an unstable shoulder girdle that necessitates surgical stabilization [1]. Treatment should be individualized based on factors, such as the patient's age, related injuries, and biomechanics [12].

Conclusion

SSSC injuries are relatively rare and are often unstable. High-energy-directed trauma is the most common cause of such injuries. SSSC have a ring structure, similar to the pelvis, and if the ring is damaged in one area, there should be other injuries in the ring. In the present case, triple disruption of the SSSC caused shoulder girdle instability in a young patient. Therefore, a surgical procedure is indicated. The selected surgical option should be personalized according to the patient's size, age, sex, requirements, and correlated injuries. In our case, the patient's acromion was smaller than the mean size of the female acromion; therefore, K-wire fixation was congruent.
