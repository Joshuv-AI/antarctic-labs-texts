A STAT1/ETC/GBP1 axis represents a potential therapeutic target for noncommunicable granulomatous skin disease

Stanczak, Luisa Bopp, Karla Witschurke, Robert Seitz, Maria Lopéz Martinez, Jan Zamek, Sinika Henschke, Nisha Rana, David O’Sullivan, Joy Steinkamp, Jan-Wilm Lackmann, William Damsky, Esther von Stebut, David E. Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Granuloma annulare (GA) and cutaneous sarcoidosis (cSAR) have an overlapping immunopathology, in which the aberrant activation of macrophages by IFN-γ constitutes a central driver. Nevertheless, the molecular understanding in GA and cSAR macrophages remains limited. We reanalyzed single-cell RNA sequencing data of GA and cSAR and performed in vitro experiments with primary human cells showing that oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) is a dominant metabolic pathway in IFN-γ–activated macrophages. Furthermore, we identify an IFN-γ–induced response network in GA and cSAR macrophages, sensitive to electron transport chain (ETC) inhibitors. Guanylate-binding protein 1 (GBP1) was central in controlling IFN-γ–mediated macrophage activation. Meanwhile, inhibition of IFN-γ signaling, ETC complexes, and GBP1 reduced granuloma formation in a human in vitro model. Metformin, a clinically used ETC complex I inhibitor, suppressed IFN-γ activation of macrophages and in vitro granulomas. Together, we suggest that OXPHOS and GBP1 represent druggable targets in granulomatous diseases and that drug repurposing of metformin is a possible strategy.

INTRODUCTION

Granuloma annulare (GA) and cutaneous sarcoidosis (cSAR) represent idiopathic, noncommunicable skin diseases characterized by dermal infiltration of macrophages. In both conditions, skin lesions present as local or disseminated papules, plaques, or nodules. Given varying and overlapping clinical appearances, histology is often needed for a definitive diagnosis. Histologically, GA shows interstitial or palisading granulomas with paucicellular areas of altered collagen and elastic fibers (necrobiosis) and nonsulfated hyaluronic acid (mucin) (1–3). On the contrary, cSAR commonly exhibits distinct granulomas with relatively sparse lymphocytic infiltrates (naked granulomas) (4–7).

GA and cSAR substantially affect the quality of life of affected persons (8, 9). Moreover, population-based cohort and case-control studies document an association with metabolic comorbidities and autoimmunity in GA (1, 10, 11). In sarcoidosis, extra-cutaneous organ involvement can cause significant morbidity and mortality (12–14). In clinical practice, immunosuppressive drugs, e.g., glucocorticoids and, more recently, Janus kinase inhibitors (JAKi), are used to treat GA and cSAR. Nevertheless, clinical responses are variable, and all treatment approaches regularly fail to control disease activity sufficiently (1, 15, 16). Therefore, effective therapies for GA and cSAR still represent an important unmet medical need.

Early in the 2000s, interferon-γ (IFN-γ) was defined as a central cytokine involved in the pathologic activation of macrophages in GA and cSAR (17–20). This finding was substantiated by the emergence of mRNA microarray analyses and bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) in the past decade (21–24). Recently, single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) and spatial transcriptomics confirmed the pivotal role of type 1 immunity and especially IFN-γ in the pathophysiology of GA and cSAR (11, 17–21, 23–28). Such technology offered, on a transcriptional level, a much deeper characterization of GA and cSAR macrophages in regard to their polarization state, immune functions, and metabolic phenotypes. Nevertheless, causal relationships that interconnect aberrant function and metabolic pathways of IFN-γ–activated granuloma macrophages remained poorly defined. Here, we sought to unravel common molecular immune and metabolic mechanisms that drive pathological responses in granuloma macrophages. Therefore, we performed experiments with primary human cells, in vitro human granuloma models, and studied human GA and cSAR skin samples as ex vivo models. We found that a functional electron transport chain (ETC), together with guanylate-binding protein 1 (GBP1), orchestrates the pathological immune response of IFN-γ–activated macrophages in human cutaneous granulomas. Along with JAK, our study thereby defines previously unidentified therapeutic targets for cutaneous granulomatous diseases.

RESULTS

Single-cell transcriptomes identify OXPHOS as a central metabolic pathway in GA and cSAR macrophages

GA and cSAR provide a unique opportunity for studying the biology of human macrophages. We reanalyzed published scRNA-seq datasets of GA and cSAR in which macrophages were present either in diseased skin (GA dataset) or in affected dermis (cSAR dataset) and compared them to macrophages and other myeloid cells in the skin of healthy donors or to macrophages in nonaffected skin of the same donors, respectively (25, 28). For our analysis, we selected differentially expressed genes (DEG) in macrophages with at least 1.2-fold change in regulation and a P ≤ 0.05 from each of the datasets (DEG-Mφall-GA and DEG-Mφall-cSAR). Under these criteria, our analyses identified a total of 667 genes in the DEG-Mφall-GA and 1448 in the DEG-Mφall-cSAR datasets (Fig. 1A). Spearman’s rank correlation showed a moderate to strong positive correlation (ρ = 0.63, P = 2.2 × 10−16) of macrophages transcriptomes in GA and cSAR, and a total of 331 genes were shared (DEG-Mφall-common). We further identified 316 down-regulated DEGs in macrophages from GA (DEG-Mφdown-GA) and 257 down-regulated DEGs in macrophages from cSAR (DEG-Mφdown-cSAR), of which 74 were shared (DEG-Mφdown-common), as well as 351 up-regulated DEGs in macrophages from GA (DEG-Mφup-GA) and 1191 from cSAR (DEG-Mφup-cSAR), of which 257 were shared (DEG-Mφup-common) (Fig. 1A and tables S1 to S4). To identify commonly enriched pathways in macrophages, we next performed gene set enrichment analyses (GSEAs) of the DEG-Mφup-common, DEG-Mφup-GA, and DEG-Mφup-cSAR sets using Hallmark gene sets (Fig. 1B and tables S5 to S8). “Interferon gamma response” (M5913) was the most significantly enriched pathway in the DEG-Mφup-common set (31 of 200 items, P = 6.53 × 10−30) and the second most significantly enriched pathway in both the DEG-Mφup-GA (35 of 200 items, P = 7.66 × 10−31) and DEG-Mφup-cSAR (67 of 200 items, P = 1.07 × 10−43) sets. Furthermore, “oxidative phosphorylation” (OXPHOS; M5936) was the second most significantly enriched pathway in the DEG-Mφup-common set (30 of 200 items, P = 1.57 × 10−28) and among the top five enriched pathways in both DEG-Mφup-GA (31 of 200 items, P = 9.48 × 10−26) and DEG-Mφup-cSAR (120 of 200 items, P = 1.00 × 10−100). Together, these data suggest that OXPHOS represents a common metabolic pathway of GA and cSAR macrophages.

IFN-γ promotes OXPHOS in human macrophages

IFN-γ has been established as a key upstream regulator of macrophage responses in GA and cSAR (21–24). Consistently, “interferon gamma” was a strongly enriched transcriptional program in the DEG-Mφup-GA, DEG-Mφup-cSAR, and DEG-Mφup-common sets (Fig. 1C), P = 5.7 × 10−20, P = 5.1 × 10−17, and P = 2.6 × 10−15, respectively, further confirming collective evidence of the pivotal role of IFN-γ in GA and cSAR (11, 18–28). To test whether IFN-γ promotes OXPHOS in human macrophages, we generated primary human monocyte–derived macrophages (MDMs) from healthy individuals. After overnight incubation with IFN-γ, we conducted a Mito stress test with Seahorse extracellular flux analysis (Fig. 1D). IFN-γ–activated MDMs were characterized by a significantly increased basal oxygen consumption rate (OCR) (Fig. 1, D and E), indicating enhanced basal OXPHOS. Noteworthy, the OCR in IFN-γ–treated MDMs after carbonyl cyanide p-trifluoromethoxyphenylhydrazone (FCCP), which disrupts the mitochondrial membrane potential, was identical to basal OCR levels, suggesting maximal OXPHOS commitment at baseline (Fig. 1D). Meanwhile, the extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) did not exhibit significant changes in response to IFN-γ, which is indicative for stable/reduced glycolysis (Fig. 1E). Consequently, the OCR/ECAR ratio was significantly increased in IFN-γ–activated MDMs (Fig. 1E), reflecting an increase in mitochondrial substrate oxidation without an increase in glucose metabolism. In addition to the Seahorse analyses, we tested metabolic dependencies and capacities of IFN-γ–treated MDMs using SCENITH, a method that measures cellular protein translation as a proxy for adenosine triphosphate (ATP) consumption (29, 30). Inhibition of glycolysis with 2-deoxy-d-glucose (2-DG) had no significant effect on the rate of protein translation (Fig. 1F). However, when OXPHOS was inhibited with oligomycin (ETC complex V inhibitor), the protein translation was profoundly inhibited (Fig. 1F). Consequently, the SCENITH data revealed a high dependency on mitochondrial metabolism of nonglucose substrates (potentially via fatty acid and amino acid oxidation) to fuel OXPHOS for ATP production in IFN-γ–activated MDMs (Fig. 1F). Of note, we detected neither enhanced production of mitochondrial superoxide nor increased mitochondrial mass, determined by MitoSox and MitoTracker staining, respectively, in IFN-γ–activated MDMs (fig. S1, A and B). In accordance with the Seahorse and SCENITH data, intracellular ATP measurement showed an increased content in IFN-γ–activated MDMs, which was completely blocked by oligomycin (fig. S1C). Furthermore, RNA-seq analyses of MDMs showed that IFN-γ activation induced the expression of nuclear-encoded genes for the ETC complexes I, II, IV, and V in MDMs (fig. S2A); however, the individual ETC genes induced were different from those observed in the DEG-Mφup-common dataset (table S9), reflecting a central challenge in our study. To evaluate possibly different in vitro and

in vivo metabolic and immunologic macrophage profiles, we compared the identified transcriptional responses between the DEG-Mφall-common and the MDMs exposed to IFN-γ. Specifically, we performed GSEA of all DEGs in the IFN-γ–activated MDMs dataset based on the top 10 enriched hallmark terms of the in vivo DEG-Mφall-common dataset (tables S10 and S11). In both the in vitro and in vivo data, the immune hallmarks “interferon gamma response” (M5913) and “Tnfa signaling via nfkb” (M5890) were strongly enriched. Likewise, “mtorc 1 signaling” (M5924) was an enriched pathway in both datasets (fig. S2B). Meanwhile, 50 of 200 genes belonging to “oxidative phosphorylation” (M5936), strongly enriched in the in vivo situation, were significantly regulated in vitro, yet this enrichment failed to reach statistical significance given the overall large number of genes regulated by IFN-γ in MDMs (fig. S2B). In sum, these analyses define MDMs as a meaningful model for studying the IFN-γ activation of macrophages in the context of granulomatous skin disease, with more limitations in mimicking the metabolic response than in reflecting immune pathways of in vivo granuloma macrophages on a transcriptional level.

Clinically, the prominent role of IFN-γ–induced activation of the signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 (STAT1) signaling cascade provides one key rationale for treating GA and SAR with JAKi. In this regard, the JAKi tofacitinib blocked IFN-γ signaling and T helper cell 1 immunity in patients with GA and cSAR (26, 31–34). Consistently, tofacitinib blocked STAT1 Tyr701 phosphorylation (pSTAT1) induced by IFN-γ in MDMs (Fig. 1G and fig. S3A) and abolished both the basal increase in OCR (Fig. 1H) and the OCR/ECAR ratio in IFN-γ–activated MDMs, while there was no significant effect on ECAR (fig. S1D). Together, our experiments demonstrate that IFN-γ signaling via STAT1 promotes OXPHOS in human macrophages (35). Consistent with their transcriptional profiling, we found that IFN-γ regulated transcription of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) salvage pathway genes, with significant induction of the rate-limiting enzyme nicotinamide phosphoribosyltransferase (NAMPT) (Fig. 1I). Furthermore, we found that the NAMPT inhibitor FK866 suppressed OCR in IFN-γ–activated MDMs (Fig. 1, J and K), which is consistent with findings for human monocytes reported by McCann et al. (35). Last, we find both increased NAD+ and increased NADH (reduced form of NAD+) levels in IFN-γ–activated MDMs with an overall unchanged NAD+/NADH ratio (fig. S1E). In sum, this suggests that NAD metabolism is efficiently and tightly regulated in human IFN-γ–activated MDMs to support their increase in OXPHOS.

Oligomycin inhibits IFN-γ activation of human macrophages

We next examined the effects of blocking OXPHOS by chemically inhibiting the ETC, specifically complex V, and glycolysis on the response induced by IFN-γ in human macrophages. To this end, we used the expression of CD86, a commonly used marker of macrophage activation (36–38), to corroborate IFN-γ–induced activation. MDMs were stimulated with IFN-γ or media control under the following three conditions: media supplemented with glucose (Glc) with or without the addition of oligomycin or media supplemented with galactose (Gal). The replacement of Glc with Gal prevents energy production via glycolysis in cells (39). IFN-γ induced the expression of CD86 in MDMs cultured in medium supplemented with Glc or Gal to the same extent (Fig. 2A). In contrast, oligomycin significantly inhibited the IFN-γ induction of CD86 in the Glc-containing conditions. Next, we performed proteomic analyses of IFN-γ–activated MDMs cultured in media supplemented with Glc with or without the addition of oligomycin or media supplemented with Gal instead of Glc. Replacing Glc with Gal had almost no effect on the MDM proteome (four proteins were significantly up-regulated and zero down-regulated) (fig. S4A). Likewise, replacing Glc with Gal did not affect the IFN-γ response of MDMs on a transcriptional level as measured by RNA-seq (fig. S4B). In contrast, oligomycin significantly altered the proteome of IFN-γ–activated MDMs (Fig. 2, B and C, and fig. S4C). Of note, one of five human biological replicates was not sensitive to oligomycin treatment. We detected significant down-regulation of 297 proteins and up-regulation of 89 proteins in oligomycin-treated samples (Fig. 2C). Consistent to previous findings in mice, programmed cell death 1 ligand 1 (PD-L1), intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM-1), CD44, CD40, S100A9, and STAT1 were among the repressed proteins (fig. S4D) (40). GSEA revealed “interferon gamma response” (M5913) as the most significantly down-regulated pathway in the 297 oligomycin–down-regulated proteins (Fig. 2D). Meanwhile, immunoblot analyses showed that oligomycin does not reduce pSTAT1 in MDMs activated with IFN-γ (Fig. 2E and fig. S3B), indicating that oligomycin inhibits the IFN-γ response in macrophages downstream of pSTAT1 signaling. Furthermore, enzymes of the NAD+ salvage pathway were regulated by oligomycin in IFN-γ–activated MDMs, with a significant repression of NAMPT (Fig. 2F and fig. S4E). In sum, these data demonstrate that oligomycin inhibits IFN-γ–induced activation and the complex metabolic rewiring of human macrophages.

GBP1 is part of a common ETC-sensitive pathway in granuloma macrophages

We next sought to uncover potentially drug-targetable macrophage proteins in human skin granuloma. Given the abovementioned limitations in the in vitro models precisely reflecting the in vivo situation, we decided to focus on those genes/proteins that are shared in the two situations. We performed an overlay analysis of the 257 DEG-Mφup-common set list (Fig. 1A) alongside the induced DEG in IFN-γ–activated MDMs. The overlapping DEGs were further overlaid with the set of proteins repressed by oligomycin in IFN-γ–activated MDMs (Fig. 2C). This analysis identified 18 overlapping genes/proteins (Fig. 3A). In addition, a previous bulk RNA-seq analysis of pre- and posttreatment skin samples from patients with GA who responded to tofacitinib treatment revealed the down-regulation of 72 DEGs (25). Overlaying these DEGs with the identified 18 genes/proteins uncovered 7 shared genes/proteins: GBP1, GBP4, GBP5, STAT1, CXCL10, LAP3, and SOD2 (Fig. 3, A and B). Among the seven identified genes/proteins, all except SOD2 have multiple known functional and physical protein associations (Fig. 3B) (41). Analyses of our proteomic dataset on the individual blood donor level showed that oligomycin repressed these seven proteins in all individuals, except the above-mentioned outlier (Fig. 3C). RNA-seq of MDMs confirmed IFN-γ induction of the identified six-protein network and SOD2 in all six biological replicates (Fig. 3D). Previously, CXCL10, one of the seven identified proteins, has been reported as both a central mediator and a biomarker in granulomatous inflammation (42–44). The analysis of the scRNA-seq data showed an increase of CXCL10 by 2.91-fold in GA and 1.64-fold in cSAR macrophages (25, 28). In an independent experiment, we measured CXCL10 concentrations in MDMs supernatants by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) and found that IFN-γ triggered its secretion, which was attenuated by oligomycin (Fig. 3E). Flow cytometry analyses confirmed not only that oligomycin inhibits IFN-γ–induced production of CXCL10 (Fig. 3F) but also that this effect on CXCL10 production was not restricted to complex V inhibition, as the complex III inhibitor antimycin A and the complex I inhibitor rotenone likewise inhibited IFN-γ–mediated induction of CXCL10 in MDMs (Fig. 3F).

On the basis of fold change, GBP1 was the most strongly repressed protein by oligomycin in our proteomic experiment (Fig. 2C). At the same time, the scRNA-seq analysis showed that GBP1 expression was increased 1.66-fold in GA and 1.92-fold in cSAR macrophages compared to control macrophages (25, 28). Moreover, GBP1 has recently been proposed as a transcriptional biomarker for granulomatous diseases (45–47). We analyzed whether GBP1 is expressed at the protein level in GA and cSAR macrophages using immunofluorescence (IF) microscopy. Macrophages, identified by CD68 costaining, strongly expressed GBP1 in both GA and cSAR (Fig. 3G), reaching significant differences when compared to CD68-positive cells in control skin (Fig. 3H). Consistently, the Pearson correlation coefficient for spatial overlap of CD68 and GBP1 in GA and cSAR was 0.77 and 0.83, respectively (fig. S5A). In addition, we performed flow cytometry analyses of freshly isolated single-cell suspension from GA skin lesions costained with anti-CD68, anti-CD40, and anti-GBP1 antibodies. CD68+ cells stained positive with GBP1 and its expression correlated with the expression of CD40, a commonly used macrophage activation marker (Fig. 3I) (48, 49). Together, these data showed that GBP1 is expressed by macrophages in GA and cSAR. Meanwhile, we used flow cytometry and quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) analysis to confirm that oligomycin reduces IFN-γ–up-regulated GBP1 protein and mRNA expression in human MDMs in vitro (Fig. 3, J and K). As seen for CXCL10, the inhibitory effect on GBP1 was not restricted to complex V inhibition, as antimycin A and rotenone likewise repressed IFN-γ–mediated induction of GBP1 in MDMs (Fig. 3K and fig. S5B). In sum, our data identify GBP1 as a central component of a pathway that is sensitive to ETC inhibitors in granuloma macrophages.

IFN-γ–mediated CXCL10 induction is dependent on GBP1

GBP1 has been suggested as a possible regulator of CXCL10 (50, 51). To investigate whether GBP1 regulates IFN-γ induction of CXCL10 and other immune-response genes in human macrophages, we performed knockdown of GBP1 (GBP1kd) in MDMs using a GBP1-specific small interfering RNA (siRNA). A scrambled siRNA (scr) served as control. GBP1kd MDMs and scr MDMs were activated with IFN-γ and mRNA expression levels of GBP1 and CXCL10, as well as STAT1 and CD40 were determined by qPCR. Among the four genes investigated, mRNA expression of GBP1 as well as CXCL10 was significantly reduced in IFN-γ–activated GBP1kd MDMs in comparison to scr MDMs counterparts (Fig. 4A). Furthermore, GBP1 and CXCL10 protein levels, as analyzed by intracellular flow cytometry staining, were significantly reduced in IFN-γ–activated GBP1kd MDMs (Fig. 4B). Last, CXCL10 secretion into the culture supernatant in response to IFN-γ was repressed in GBP1kd MDMs (Fig. 4C).

The small-molecule NSC756093 represents a specific inhibitor of the interaction between GBP1 and PIM1 kinase (52), which is necessary for GBP1 phosphorylation at Ser156 (53), a modification that can limit GBP1 effector functions in the absence of infection (53). We observed that mRNA expression of CXCL10 was significantly repressed by NSC756093 in IFN-γ–activated MDMs (Fig. 4D). Furthermore, intracellular flow cytometry staining demonstrated reduced CXCL10 protein expression in IFN-γ–activated MDMs upon NSC756093 treatment (Fig. 4E). On the contrary, NSC756093 did not affect the induction of STAT1 and its phosphorylation at Tyr701 (Fig. 4F and fig. S3C). NSC756093 did not alter the increase in OCR in response to IFN-γ (Fig. 4G). These findings suggest that GBP1 regulates CXCL10 induction downstream of pSTAT1-mediated OXPHOS induction in IFN-γ–activated human macrophages.

In vitro granuloma formation depends on an IFN-γ/STAT1/ETC/GBP1 axis

Thus far, our integrative approach that combines insights from both in vitro systems and ex vivo data derived from patient samples suggests a targetable IFN-γ/STAT1/ETC/GBP1/CXCL10 pathological activation program in GA and cSAR macrophages. Giving a lack of animal models that accurately replicate human granuloma biology, we decided to exploit a recently developed in vitro human granuloma model to test for pharmacological interference of this axis (54–61). We found that, as described in the original publications (55, 57, 58, 61), peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) activated with purified protein derivative (PPD)–coated beads (Fig. 5, A to F) secreted significantly more IFN-γ (Fig. 5D) and exhibited multiple granuloma-like structures after 4 days (Fig. 5, B and C) when compared to PBMCs treated with media alone or uncoated beads. Furthermore, CXCL10 was increased in the supernatant of the PPD-treated cultures as determined by ELISA (Fig. 5E). To measure gene expression of macrophages in the two-dimensional (2D) granuloma model, we performed qPCR on adherent macrophages. Expression levels of STAT1, GBP1, as well as CXCL10 mRNA were significantly increased in adherence-enriched cells of the cultures treated with PPD-coated beads (Fig. 5F). Furthermore, we characterized the adherence-enriched cells for representative ETC genes by qPCR (NDUFA9, NDUFS1, SDHA, SDHB, UQCRC2, UQCRFS1, COX5B, COX17, ATP5F1B, and ATP6V0A2), finding significant induction for the complex V genes ATP5F1B and ATP6V0A2 (fig. S6A).

Using the in vitro granuloma model, we then tested the relevance of IFN-γ and its inherent JAK-STAT1 signaling in granuloma formation. To this end, anti–IFN-γ antibodies or tofacitinib was added from day 0 to 3, and cultures were evaluated microscopically on day 4 (Fig. 5, G to J). Our analyses showed that anti–IFN-γ antibodies and tofacitinib reduced the number and diameters of granuloma-like structures compared to the control condition (Fig. 5, G to J). Next, we tested the potential of the ETC complex V and GBP1:PIM1 inhibition to prevent in vitro granuloma formation. Therefore, we added oligomycin or NSC756093 from day 0 to 3 and evaluated the cultures microscopically on day 4 (Fig. 5, K to N). We found that oligomycin and NSC756093 both significantly reduced both the number and size of granuloma-like structures (Fig. 5, K to N), as well as a trend toward reduced CXCL10 secretion (fig. S6B). Together, these data suggest that the IFN-γ/STAT1/ETC/GBP1 represents a potentially targetable pathway for treatment of pathological macrophage activation in the context of human granulomatous diseases.

Given that drug repurposing strategies of established, well-tolerated drugs represent a straightforward method to explore previously unconsidered treatment options, we tested metformin, a standard medication for type 2 diabetes mellitus and a known ETC complex I inhibitor, in our system. As expected for an ETC complex I inhibitor, metformin repressed OCR/ECAR induction by IFN-γ in MDMs (fig. S7A). Moreover, analyses of a previously published proteomic dataset by our group (62) showed that metformin repressed CXCL10, GBP1, GBP4, and GBP5 protein expression, with unchanged expression of LAP3, SOD2, and STAT1 proteins in IFN-γ–activated MDMs (fig. S7B). Last, metformin significantly inhibited granuloma diameters and numbers (Fig. 5, O to P), as well as CXCL10 secretion (fig. S7C), in the 2D granuloma model, suggesting a potential of metformin as an adjuvant therapy in human skin granulomatous diseases.

DISCUSSION

Our study identifies an IFN-γ–induced immunometabolic macrophage program that is shared by GA and cSAR. Within this pathway, GBP1 represents a central molecular link between metabolic remodeling toward OXPHOS and inflammatory immune activation. We propose a model (Fig. 6), in which IFN-γ via STAT1 signaling rewires metabolism resulting in enhanced OXPHOS in macrophages. Meanwhile, inhibiting ETC complexes dampened the IFN-γ–mediated induction of GBP1. In turn, GBP1 promotes expression of pathology-driving molecules, such as CXCL10. Together, our model proposes three consecutive levels for macrophage-targeted therapies in GA and cSAR: JAK-STAT signaling, ETC signals/function, and GBP1:PIM1 interaction.

GBP1 has been defined as a transcriptional biomarker in peripheral blood of humans suffering from systemic sarcoidosis (63). Moreover, independently generated transcriptomic datasets of GA (25) and cSAR (26, 28) define GBP1 as a cellular signature gene in diseased macrophages. We here demonstrate GBP1 expression in macrophages at the site of pathology in both GA and cSAR on a protein level, not only substantiating its potential as a biomarker but also suggesting GBP1 as a possible therapeutic target. GBP1 represents one of the most strongly induced proteins in the cellular response to IFN-γ (64, 65) and reportedly is involved in mediating its cellular effects (64, 66). We showed that, in human macrophages, the induction of CXCL10 expression, among other IFN-γ–induced immune-response molecules, is partially GBP1 dependent. With emerging complex roles of GBP1 in human macrophage biology (51, 67–72), the underlying mechanisms by which GBP1 interacts with IFN-γ signaling to support CXCL10 expression warrants further investigation. Interactions with IFN-γ signaling pathways, transcriptional regulators, and metabolic processes that may increase chromatin accessibility and promote a more permissive transcriptional state could play important roles. In parallel, it may be worthwhile to explore additional other mechanisms. For example, GBP1 has been implicated in core cellular processes, such as membrane biology (71, 73–79), cell death (53), and mitochondrial integrity (68, 80).

Our study confirms that IFN-γ triggers OXPHOS in human macrophages, a fact that has been noted previously in studies with human monocytes and macrophages (35, 81). It is important to emphasize that the in vitro evidence showing OXPHOS induction by IFN-γ is based on a simplified model, because in vivo macrophages are subject to many different stimuli. Nevertheless, the fact that scRNA-seq data consistently found increased OXPHOS in IFN-γ–associated granuloma macrophages argues for a role of IFN-γ in, at least partially, enhancing OXPHOS in human skin macrophages. From a pathophysiological point of view, this finding is highly conceivable, as both, GA and cSAR, represent chronic conditions, characterized by slowly developing, long-term stationary macrophage infiltrates and not acute inflammatory states with rapidly dividing cells, which should require glycolysis for meeting metabolic demands. Thus, we speculate that OXPHOS is needed for the long-term persistence of dermal macrophages in human GA and cSAR. Albeit this fact awaits definitive confirmation in vivo, it should be noted that a lack of animal models that accurately replicate the complex genetics and immunology characterizing the human conditions (57, 82) makes such investigation difficult. In our study, we decided to use a human in vitro model to demonstrate that oligomycin resolves granuloma formation. Human granulomas are complicated biological structures, with overlapping macrophage phenotypes in complex spatial organization and multifaceted evolution over time. For long, it has been appreciated that human granuloma simultaneously displays not only different macrophage immune but also metabolic phenotypes (83), a notion that has been substantiated by scRNA-seq and spatial transcriptomics in more recent years (11, 25, 28). In this instance, Nakamizo et al. (84) reported about the activation of the pentose phosphate pathway in triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 (TREM2)-positive macrophages and its important involvement in disease pathogenesis of cSAR. It will be informative to decipher the complex interplay of metabolic pathways, in particular OXPHOS, tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA), NAD metabolism, and the pentose phosphate pathway activated in different macrophages in granulomatous inflammation in future investigations.

An important finding was that ETC complex activities, but not glycolysis, regulated the human macrophages response to IFN-γ. The fact that inhibition of the MDM IFN-γ response was reproducible with complex I, III, and V inhibitors suggests a general dependence on ETC function in our system. In part, OXPHOS and IFN-γ–induced proteins, such as GBP1, may have a permissive relationship, meaning that OXPHOS-derived ATP is a limiting factor for macrophages in mounting a robust response to IFN-γ. Nevertheless, direct signaling effects of the ETC or other metabolic changes occurring under ETC inhibition, for instance, changes in TCA flux, likely also play important roles and require future investigations. Identifying the specific mitochondrial signal that regulates GBP1 expression may provide additional low-toxic therapeutic targets to treat skin granulomatous disease.

In systemic treatments, OXPHOS inhibition harbors a risk for toxic side effects. In skin, however, targeting metabolism by topical formulations, e.g., of OXPHOS inhibitors, at efficient concentrations without causing systemic effects is feasible. Meanwhile, metformin demonstrated effectiveness in counteracting granuloma formation in our current investigation. In addition, we recently reported its efficacy in a proof-of-concept study involving a patient with necrobiotic xanthogranuloma, a rare and distinct granulomatous skin disease (62). In this context, investigations studying which substrates fuel OXPHOS in macrophages, for instance, neutral lipids, which are reportedly increased in sarcoidosis macrophages (85), hold additional promise for identifying sophisticated macrophage-targeting therapies.

In summary, our study proposes a mechanism driving aberrant macrophage activation in cSAR and GA, which is triggered by IFN-γ through STAT1 signaling, confirming previous data pointing toward IFN-γ as a key pathology-driving cytokine in these entities (11, 17–21, 23–28). The similarities of macrophage transcriptomes in GA and cSAR allowed us to identify OXPHOS as a common metabolic pathway overrepresented in IFN-γ–exposed macrophages in humans in vivo. Using primary human cells in vitro, we demonstrate that IFN-γ directly drives OXPHOS in human macrophages, and, in turn, a functional ETC is required for the activation of immune-related molecules by IFN-γ in these cells. Thereby, we identify key elements of an immune-metabolic interplay in IFN-γ–driven macrophage activation. Integrating our in vitro data with analyses of the in vivo data further resulted in the identification of a drug-targetable protein network, in which GBP1 and CXCL10 play major roles. Further in vivo evidence that IFN-γ–activated STAT1 signaling drives GBP1 and CXCL10 expression in macrophage comes from human studies on monogenetic diseases. Macrophages derived from patients with deficiencies in either STAT1 or interferon regulatory factor-1, a key IFN-γ–induced transcription factor, failed to up-regulate GBP1 expression in response to IFN-γ stimulation (86). In addition, studies in patients with dominant-negative IFN-γ receptor 1 variants showed decreased CXCL10 secretion in response to IFN-γ stimulation compared with controls (87). While CXCL10 has been extensively linked to immune-pathogenesis in GA and cSAR, we established that the IFN-γ–mediated induction of CXCL10 and other immune-effector molecules is supported by GBP1. The inhibition of IFN-γ signaling at the level of the JAK-STAT1 interaction has entered clinical practice in the past years. Yet, varying therapeutic responses and treatment failures emphasize the need for more effective therapies. In this regard, our study defines GBP1 as well as ETC complexes as additional targets for previously unconsidered, potentially combined, treatment approaches. Given that the ETC inhibitor metformin showed effectiveness in our in vitro system in this regard, a drug repurposing approach could be rapidly translated to the clinic.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design

This study included skin samples of patients diagnosed with GA or cSAR as well as blood samples from healthy donors (buffy coats/leucopacks). Skin biopsies were obtained for diagnostic purposes at the Department of Dermatology at the University Hospital of Cologne. Trained dermatologists histologically and clinically confirmed GA or cSAR. Biopsies were immediately fixed with formalin or in part digested to generate single-cell suspensions. Blood samples from healthy donors were obtained from Institute of Transfusion of the University Hospital of Cologne, Institute for Clinical Transfusion Medicine, of the University Hospital of Jena and from the Blood Donation Center of the University Hospital Freiburg. Samples were provided in a fully deidentified manner, and no information on donor identity, sex, or age was made available to us. This study was conducted according to the principles expressed in the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the local Ethic Committee (Ethikkommission) of the University of Cologne (votes #08-144, #20-1082, and #19-1146) and University of Jena (2025-3776). All donors participated voluntarily and provided written informed consent for collecting samples and subsequent analyses.

Enrichment analysis

Enrichment analysis of sequencing and proteomic data was performed using GSEA and the freely available metascape web tool (88). Hallmark terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathway databases were used for enrichment analysis. The selected terms associated to the IFN-γ transcriptional program (Fig. 1C) were as follows: GO:0060332, GO:0060335, GO:0060333, GO:0063030, GO:006334, GO:0140888, GO:0032729, GO:0071346, GO:0034341, GO:0032649, and R-HSA-877300.

Monocyte-derived macrophages

PBMCs were obtained from buffy coats by density gradient centrifugation using Ficoll-Paque Plus (Cytiva, 17-1440-03) or Lymphoprep (STEMCELL Technologies, 07851), and monocytes were separated with CD14+ Magnetic Cell Separation (MACS) technology (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-050-201), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. To induce differentiation into macrophages, eluted CD14+ cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Life Technologies, 21875091 or 21875158) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS)/fetal calf serum (Pan-Biotech, P30-3302 or Thermo Fisher Scientific, 10099141), 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Sigma-Aldrich, P0781 or Gibco, 15140-122) and macrophage colony-stimulating factor (50 ng/ml; Miltenyi Biotec, 130-096-492 or R&D Systems, 216-MC-500) for 5 to 7 days. Activation of MDM was achieved by a treatment with IFN-γ (BD Biosciences, 554617) at a concentration of 10 ng/ml for 21 hours, unless otherwise stated. As indicated, 2 μM oligomycin (Selleck Biotechnology, S1478), 1 μM rotenone (Sigma-Aldrich, 557368), 1 μM antimycin A (Sigma-Aldrich, 557368), 10 μM tofacitinib (AdipoGen Life Sciences, AG-CR1-3732), 10 μM NSC756093 (Sigma-Aldrich, SML1310), 5 nM FK688 (Selleck Chemicals, S2799), or 1 mM metformin (Sigma-Aldrich, 317240) was added 2 hours before IFN-γ addition. Chemical inhibitors were tested in dose titration assays for their effect on cell viability as determined by Fixable Viability Dye eFluor 780 (Invitrogen, 65-0865-14) staining (fig. S8).

RNA sequencing

MDMs were lysed and total RNA was extracted using the RNAeasy mini kit (QIAGEN, 74106) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA was quantified using Qubit 2.0 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Libraries were prepared using the TruSeq stranded mRNA kit (Illumina) and sequenced in a HISeq 3000 (Illumina) by the Deep-sequencing Facility at the Max-Planck-Institute for Immunobiology and Epigenetics. Sequenced libraries were processed with deepTools (89), using Spliced Transcripts Alignment to a Reference (STAR) for trimming and mapping (90) and featureCounts to quantify mapped reads (91). Raw mapped reads were processed in R (Lucent Technologies) with DESeq2 (92) to generate normalized read counts to visualize as heatmaps using Morpheus (Broad Institute) or Prism 10.1.2 software (GraphPad) and determine DEGs with greater than twofold change and lower than 0.05 adjusted P value.

Extracellular flux analysis

OCR and ECAR were measured in a Seahorse XFe 96 extracellular flux analyzer (Seahorse Bioscience) on MDM cells 21 hours postactivation. MDMs (7 × 104 cells per well; at least three to five injections per condition) were plated in 96-well XF Pro M cell culture microplates (Agilent, 103792-100) and suspended in XF RPMI medium (pH 7.4) or unbuffered RPMI (Agilent, 103576-100 or Gibco, 21875034), supplemented with 10 mM glucose (Agilent, 103577-100 or Roche, 0634373200), 1 mM pyruvate (Agilent, 103578-100 or Invitrogen, 11360070), and 2 mM glutamine (Agilent, 103579-100 or Gibco, 25030149). Cells were equilibrated for 1 hour at 37°C without CO2. OCR and ECAR were assessed in basal conditions and in response to 1 μM oligomycin, 1.5 μM FCCP, and 0.5 μM rotenone/antimycin A (all from Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress Test Kit, 103015-100 or Sigma-Aldrich, 75351, C2920, R8875, and A8674, respectively). Basal mitochondrial respiration was determined from baseline measurements before injections.

SCENITH

A total of 2 × 105 MDMs per well were plated in 96-well plates. Cells were treated for 80 to 120 min with medium, 2-DG 3 mM (Agilent, 103020-100), oligomycin 2 μM (Selleck Biotechnology, S1478), a sequential combination of the drugs at the mentioned concentrations, or cycloheximide (20 μg/ml; Merck, 01810-1G) as negative control. Puromycin (10 μg/ml; Merck, P7255) was added for the final 80 min of the metabolic inhibitor treatment. Afterward, the cells were washed, stained against surface markers, fixed, and permeabilized using FOXP3 fixation and permeabilization buffer (Thermo Fisher eBioscience, 00-5523-00). Next, cells were stained intracellularly with an Alexa Fluor 488–conjugated anti-puromycin antibody (Sigma-Aldrich, 333814) and analyzed by flow cytometry.

Flow cytometry analysis

Cell surface and intracellular antigens were analyzed using live cell suspension derived from human blood or skin samples. Antibodies used for staining are found in table S12. Unspecific binding was blocked with FcR blocking (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-059-901), and dead cells were excluded by staining them with Fixable Viability Dye eFluor 780 (Invitrogen, 65-0865-14). Extracellular staining (CD86 and CD40) was performed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) with 2 % FBS (Pan-Biotech, P30-3302) and 0.1% sodium azide [fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) buffer] for 20 min at 4°C. Intracellular staining (CD68, CXCL10, puromycin, and GBP1) was done after cell permeabilization with eBioscience Foxp3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer (Invitrogen, 00-5523-00) or the Cytofix/Cytoperm Permeabilization Kit (BD Biosciences, 554714). To analyze mitochondrial mass and superoxide levels, cells were incubated with 100 nM MitoTracker Green FM (Thermo Fisher Scientific, M7514) and 2.5 μM MitoSox Red (Thermo Fisher Scientific, M36008), respectively, at 37°C. The MitoSox Red acquisition was performed using the absorption peak at 396 nm. For flow cytometry acquisition, cells were suspended in FACS buffer. Data were acquired on an Attune NxT Flow Cytometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) or a LSRFortessa (BD Biosciences). As analysis software, we used FlowJo (Tree Star, Ashland, OR). In all analyses, cells were gated for single and live events.

ATP measurement

MDMs were activated with IFN-γ ± oligomycin added 2 hours before IFN-γ or media for 21 hours followed by intracellular ATP measurement with the Luminescent ATP Detection Assay Kit (Abcam, 113849), according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Immunoblots

MDMs were lysate in radio immunoprecipitation assay buffer with added protease inhibitors (cOmplete EDTA-free Protease-Inhibitor-Cocktail, Roche, 11873580001) and phosphatase activity inhibitors (PhosStop, Roche, 04906845001) after 30-min or 2-hour incubation with IFN-γ. Protein samples were then processed by sonication for 30 s at 60% amplitude using the BransonSonifier 450 (Marschall Scientific). Lysates were centrifuged at 1300 rpm, 10 min, 4°C, the supernatant was transferred to a new tube, and 4× reducing Laemmli buffer was added to the cell lysate. Subsequently, lysates were subjected to denaturation by boiling for 5 min at 95°C. Afterward, samples were separated via SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis using a 5% stacking gel and a 12% separating gel. A molecular weight standard, Page Ruler Plus Prestained Protein Ladder (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 26620), was used to estimate the molecular weights of the proteins. Gel electrophoresis was carried out using the Mini-PROTEAN Systems (Bio-Rad) at a current of 25 mA per gel. Proteins were transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (Whatman, Sigma-Aldrich, WHA10400206) through wet transfer. To ensure successful transfer, membranes were then stained with a 0.2% Ponceau S Solution for Electrophoresis (Serva) for 20 min. For further analysis, membranes were cut into smaller pieces, enabling detection of multiple proteins on the same membrane. Ponceau S was removed using tris-buffered saline + Tween (TBS-T) buffer. Next, membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk in TBS-T buffer. The primary antibodies anti–phospho-Stat1 (Tyr701) (Cell Signaling, 9167), anti-Stat1 (Cell Signaling, 9172), and anti-actin (Sigma-Aldrich, mab1501R) were diluted 1:1000 in 5% bovine serum albumin in TBS-T added to the membranes and incubated overnight at 4°C. After three washes of 5 min each with TBS-T buffer, secondary antibodies labeled with horseradish peroxidase were added for 45 min at room temperature. Following another five washes 5 min each with TBS-T, membranes were exposed to Amersham ECL Prime Western Blotting detection reagent (Cytiva, RPN2236) for 5 min at room temperature. Signals were captured on x-ray film developed using the Curix 60 film processor (AGFA). For quantification of the protein bands, ImageJ1 software (version 1.54 g) was used.

NAD+/NADH levels

NAD+/NADH levels were quantified using the colorimetric NAD+/NADH Assay Kit (Abcam, ab65348) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, samples were extracted using extraction buffer II, clarified by centrifugation, and deproteinized using a 10-kDa spin column (Cytiva, 28-9322-47). For total NAD (NADt) measurements, samples were loaded directly; for NADH-specific measurements, NAD+ was decomposed by heating extracted samples to 60°C for 30 min before plating. Reaction mix containing cycling buffer I and NAD cycling enzyme mix was added to each well and incubated for 5 min at room temperature, followed by addition of developer solution II. Absorbance was measured at optical density at 450 nm after 1 hour, and NADt and NADH concentrations were calculated from the standard curve; NAD+ was obtained by subtracting NADH from NADt, and the NAD+/NADH ratio was calculated accordingly.

Proteomic analysis

For sample preparation, 2 × 106 MDMs per condition were lysed in an 8 M urea buffer with a proteinase and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 1861284), sonicated on ice (3 cycles/30 s) and centrifuged for 15 min at 20,000g. Next, 150 μg of protein was incubated in 5 mM dithiothreitol at 25°C for 1 hour. Afterward, chloroacetamide was added to a final concentration of 40 mM and incubated in the dark for 30 min. Successively, the sample was diluted with 50 mM triethylammoniumbicarbonate to achieve a concentration of <2 M urea. Protein digestion with trypsin was followed by acidification with formic acid and stage tip purification of peptides. For data acquisition, samples were analyzed by the CECAD Proteomics Facility on an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer equipped with a high-field asymmetric waveform ion mobility spectrometry (FAIMS) that was coupled to a Vanquish neo high-performance liquid chromatography system running in trap and elute mode (all from Thermo Fisher Scientific). Samples were loaded onto a precolumn (Acclaim 5 mm PepMap 300 m Cartridge) for 1 min at 60 μl of flow with eluent A (0.1% formic acid) before reverse-flushed onto an in-house packed analytical column (30-cm length, 75-mm inner diameter, filled with 2.7-mm Poroshell EC120 C18, Agilent). Peptides were chromatographically separated at a constant flow rate of 300 nl/min on a linear gradient from 4% eluent B (80% acetonitrile, 0.1% formic acid) to 35 % B over 77 min, followed by column wash with 95% solvent B and reequilibration to initial condition. The FAIMS pro was operated at −50-V compensation voltage and electrode temperatures of 99.5°C for the inner electrode and 85°C for the outer electrode. MS1 scans were acquired from 399 mass/charge ratio (m/z) to 1001 m/z at 15,000 resolution. Maximum injection time was set to 22 ms and the automatic gain control (AGC) target to 100%. MS2 scans ranged from 400 m/z to 1000 m/z and were acquired at 15,000 resolution with a maximum injection time of 22 ms and an AGC target of 100%. Data-independent acquisition (DIA) scans covering the precursor range from 400 to 1000 m/z and were acquired in 60 windows of 10 m/z each. All scans were stored as centroid. In addition, a gas-phase fractionated library (93) was generated by acquiring the range of 400 to 1000 m/z in six runs of 100 m/z each using a pool generated from all samples. Chromatographic settings for these runs were identical to the samples, but MS1 scans were acquired in 60,000 resolution and MS2 scans in 30,000 resolution with staggered 4 m/z windows, resulting in nominal 2 m/z windows after deconvolution using ProteoWizard (94).

For processing, samples were analyzed in DIA-NN 1.8.1 (95). A human Swissprot canonical database (UP5640, downloaded 04 January 2023) was used for gas-phase fractionation (GPF) building with settings matching acquisition parameters. Samples were then processed using the GPF-based library, and DIA-NN was run with the additional command line prompts “—report-libinfo” and “—relaxed-prot-inf.” Further output settings were as follows: filtered at 0.01 false discovery rate, N-terminal methionine excision enabled, maximum number of missed cleavages set to 1, minimum peptide length set to 7, maximum peptide length set to 30, min precursor m/z set to 400, max precursor m/z set to 1000, and cysteine carbamidomethylation enabled as a fixed modification. Afterward, DIA-NN output was further filtered on library q value and global q value ≤ 0.01 and at least two identified peptides per protein and at least four fragments per precursor using R (4.1.3). Last, label-free qualification values were calculated using the DIA-NN R package. Afterward, analysis of results was performed in Perseus 1.6.15 (96). A total of 21 samples, representing seven biological replicates under three different experimental conditions, were analyzed. However, two samples from different biological replicates were excluded from normalization due to lower data quality. Subsequently, to conduct a paired statistical analysis (paired t test), all experimental conditions associated with the excluded biological replicates were also removed from the analysis.

STRING interaction analyses

Protein-protein interaction network analysis was performed with the search tool for retrieval of interacting genes/proteins database (Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins, version V12.0, available online: https://string-db.org/, accessed on 13th January 2025). Medium confidence was applied for the analysis.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

Cell culture supernatants from MDM were analyzed using the human CXCL10/IP-10 Quantikine ELISA kit (R&D Systems, DIP100) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Real-time qPCR

Cells were lysed and total RNA was purified using the RNAeasy mini kit (QIAGEN, 74106) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Reverse transcription to cDNA was performed using the iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad, 1708890) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. cDNA, primers (table S13), and the GoTaq qPCR Master Mix (Promega, A6002) were loaded into an MicroAmp Fast Optical 96-Well Reaction Plate (Applied Biosystems, 4346907), and reverse transcription qPCR was performed using StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR Systems (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 4376600). H36B4 gene was used as the internal reference gene, and the ΔΔCt method was used to calculate the results as previously described (97, 98).

Histology and IF protein staining

Histology and IF protein staining were performed on formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) human skin sections. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) stainings were obtained from the clinical dermatopathology unit (Dermatology, University of Cologne, Germany). For IF, FFPE samples were cut at 4-μm thickness, then dewaxed by immersing the slides into xylene, and hydrated by sequential immersion in descending ethanol concentrations (100, 95, 80, 70, and 50%). Antigen retrieval was performed using citrate buffer (pH 6.0) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 005000). The samples were then left to reach room temperature while immersed in the antigen retrieval buffer. A hydrophobic barrier pen (Dako, F2002) was used to draw a hydrophobic barrier around the tissues, and normal goat serum [Dako, X0907; 10% in PBS + tween (PBST)] blocking buffer solution was used to avoid nonspecific binding for the antibodies. The antibody mix or respective isotype controls (table S14) were prepared in 1% normal goat serum/PBST and were left to incubate with the samples overnight at 4°C. The next day, the samples were washed with PBST with gentle agitation. The secondary antibody cocktail mix, including 300 nM 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (Invitrogen, D1306), was applied to the sections for 30 min at room temperature. After thorough washing with PBST, sections were mounted using Immu-Mount (Epredia, ref9990402) and stored at 4°C.

Microscope image acquisition

Microscopy images of histology (H&E) and IF protein staining were acquired at room temperature using the BZ-9000E inverted fluorescence phase-contrast microscope (Keyence) at magnification of ×20 [Nikon, flat-field correction, apochromatic correction, numerical aperture (NA) 0.75] or ×40 (Nikon, flat-field correction, apochromatic correction, NA 0.95) with a 2/3-inch (1.693 cm) charge-coupled device camera, 1.5 mega pixel monochrome, colorized by a liquid crystal filter for color imaging. Output signals were 8-bit each (RGB). Image acquisition was done using the BZ-II Viewer. Further image processing (merge of images and channels, scale bar annotation) was done using the BZ-II Analyser.

Spatial colocalization analysis

Quantification of CD68-GBP1 colocalization in IF microscopy was performed using the Fiji version of ImageJ (version 14.0/1.54f). Five representative photomicrographs were obtained of GA and cSAR at 40×. The raw data images of GA and cSAR were cropped to include >75% area of granulomatous infiltrate excluding papillary dermis, epidermis, vessels, and adnexal structures. Each photo from each section was scored independently. To quantify the IF data, images were imported into Fiji, and colocalization analysis was performed using the JaCoP Plugin. Threshold was set by the Costes’ automatic threshold, and the Pearson’s correlation coefficient was calculated. Spatial colocalization analysis was performed for each pair of channels of photomicrographs and compared to its randomized control by 90° rotation of one channel.

Immunofluorescence quantification of GBP1 in human skin

Quantification of IF was performed using QuPath (v0.5.0). Representative photomicrographs were obtained for each region of interest at 20×. The raw data images were cropped to include interstitial cell infiltrates excluding papillary dermis, epidermis, vessels, and adnexal structures. For CD68-positive cell detection, an automated threshold was applied to classify cells as positive or negative based on the CD68 cell fluorescence intensity measurement. GBP1 expression was quantified on the basis of mean fluorescence intensity of the respective channel. The performance of the detection algorithm was manually corrected, if needed. Each photomicrograph was scored independently.

Generation of single-cell suspension from skin for flow cytometry

Human skin samples were processed with the Whole Skin Dissociation Kit, human (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-101-540) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Samples were incubated for 3 hours at 37°C while gently shaking. Mechanical dissociation was performed using the h_skin_01 program of the gentle MACS Dissociator. Cell suspensions were consecutively passed through sterile 70- and 40-μm filters before further proceedings.

siRNA transfection

GBP1kd was performed using the P3 Primary Cell 4D-Nucleofector X Kit S (Lonza, V4XP-3032) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. We used 200 nM GBP1 siRNA (Thermo Fisher Scientific, s5620 and s5621) or a Silencer Select Negative Control #1 siRNA (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 4390843) as a control (scr). The siRNA transfection was performed in 20 μl 16-well Nucleocuvette Strips. For one well, 1 × 105 MDMs were suspended in a 20-μl mix containing 2 μl of siGBP1 (200 nM) or scramble siRNA (200 nM), 14.76 μl of Nucleofector Solution, and 3.24 μl of Supplement solution. The nucleocuvette was placed into the 4D-Nucleofactor X unit, and the nucleofection was conducted using the program CM137. Each 20-μl cell suspension was added to wells of a 96-well plate containing 180 μl of prewarmed RPMI, supplemented with 10% FBS (Pan-Biotech, P30-3302), and incubated at 37°C. Two hours posterior to transfection, cells were activated with IFN-γ or left untreated for 21 hours.

In vitro granuloma model

Carboxylate microspheres (1.0 μm) (Polysciences, 17458-10) were either suspended in PBS or covalently coupled to PPD (17458-10) following a previously published protocol for a human granuloma model (57). These prepared beads were diluted in RPMI (Life Technologies, 21875091) and added to isolated PBMCs (2 × 106) suspended in RPMI supplemented with 10% FBS (Pan-Biotech, P30-3302) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Sigma-Aldrich, P0781) at a ratio of 1:50 relative to the number of monocytes present (~10% of all PBMCs). The mixture was incubated at 37°C for 4 days. As indicated, PBMCs were treated with either anti–human-IFN-γ monoclonal antibodies (0.1 μg/ml; Invivogen, H7WM120), 10 μM tofacitinib (AdipoGen Life Sciences, AG-CR1-3732), 2 μM oligomycin (Selleck Biotechnology, S1478), 10 μM NSC756093 (Sigma-Aldrich, SML1310), or metformin (1 mM) daily from day 0 to 3. Cells were imaged by light microscopy on day 4. For qPCR analysis, we took advantage of the fact that human macrophages adhere to plastic. Thus, at day 3 of the granuloma model, we removed the nonadherent cells and subjected the adherent cells to qPCR analyses as described in the “Real-time qPCR” section.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Except for RNA-seq and proteomic data, statistical analyses were performed using Prism 10.1.2 software (GraphPad), and the results are represented as mean ± SEM unless noted otherwise. In the case of strong donor interindividual differences in raw values, values were normalized for analysis. Given the continuous and independent nature of the data, P values were calculated using two-tailed Student’s t tests. *P ≤ 0.05, **P ≤ 0.01, ***P ≤ 0.001, and ****P ≤ 0.0001. All data shown are representative of at least two independent experiments. The sample size (n) refers to the number of biological replicates tested.
