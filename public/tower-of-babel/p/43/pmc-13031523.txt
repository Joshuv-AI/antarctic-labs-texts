Identifying GFAP-expressing cell susceptibility to SARS-CoV-2 infection using human iPSC-derived neural cells
Authors: Nithi Asavapanumas, Phaewa Chaiwijit, Ampa Suksatu, Saroj Kasemsinsup, Nongluk Saikachain, Juthamard Chantaraamporn, Suchanan Chieowisaman, Sarintip Nguantad, Damita Jevapatarakul, Narita Thungsatianpun, Tanapat Pornsukjantra, Tanida Chokpanuwat, Suwimon Manopwisedjaroen, Kanit Bhukhai, Patompon Wongtrakoongate, Suradej Hongeng, Ponpan Matangkasombut, Varodom Charoensawan, Arunee Thitithanyanont
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Neurological manifestations are common in COVID-19. However, a key limitation to understanding the mechanisms and developing new therapeutic approaches is the lack of a suitable testing model. In this study, we developed 2D iPSC-derived neural cells to investigate the mechanism underlying the neurotropism of SARS-CoV-2. After two weeks of differentiation, these cells exhibited several cortical brain cell subtypes, as determined by the transcription levels of key marker genes. Interestingly, this heterogeneity of cellular subtypes is comparable to 10-month-old brain organoids developed in an earlier study. Our 2D neural model was predominantly susceptible to SARS-CoV-2 infection in the astrocyte subpopulation. The expression of SARS-CoV-2 entry factors in the astrocyte subpopulation was further explored. Notably, the cells with high expression of BSG were predominately found in the astrocytes. Taken together, our findings demonstrate the benefits of our 2D iPSC-derived neural cell model in elucidating the neurotropism of SARS-CoV-2 and its underlying mechanisms. This model may also be useful for studying the neurotropism of other viruses.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-41177-z.

Introduction

Neuroinvasive infections that result in encephalitis, meningitis, and myelitis often lead to serious and enduring neurological conditions. Despite advancements in knowledge and technology that have improved detection and diagnosis, the underlying mechanisms and novel therapeutic approaches for each neurotropic infection remain limited, especially in the context of endemic diseases, such as Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID-19). A key challenge in studying emerging neuroinvasive infections is the lack of physiologically relevant in vitro models1.

COVID-19, caused by SARS-CoV-2, was declared a global pandemic by the WHO in May 2020. Although the disease is primarily characterized by respiratory symptoms, accumulating evidence indicates that SARS-CoV-2 can affect the nervous system. Neurological manifestations range from acute symptoms, such as anosmia, confusion, stroke, and seizures, to persistent neurological sequelae that continue beyond the acute phase of infection, commonly referred to as long COVID or post-acute sequelae of SARS-CoV-2 infection (PASC)2–5.

It has been shown that coronaviruses can infect not only the respiratory system, but also in the central nervous system (CNS), as demonstrated in the detection of coronavirus RNA in human brain tissue6. Desforges et al. suggested a similar direct entry of the virus into the CNS, as viral RNA has been observed in various routes, including the olfactory and trigeminal nerves, cerebrospinal fluid, and lymphatic system7. However, the exact mechanism by which SARS-CoV-2 enters the CNS has not yet been clearly identified. Furthermore, viral infections can trigger a severe systemic inflammatory response, leading to the release of pro-inflammatory factors and oxidative stress in multiple organs8–10.

Several in vitro studies have demonstrated the different infectivity of human SARS-CoVs in various human neural cell lines, such as U-87MG, U-373 MG and SK-N-SN cell lines11–13. However, the limitation of using these tumor cell lines is that the human brain contains heterogeneous cells and tissues, leading to a complexity level that cannot be captured by cell lines. Human induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived models have emerged as one of the most promising in vivo systems, representing both a microphysiological and microenvironmental model1. These models can be directly generated from primary human cells, forming the connecting neuronal network, which can be recorded by neuronal activities that can represent specific regions of the human brain14,15. Several reports have demonstrated SARS-CoV-2 neurotropism in various models, predominantly focusing on the 3D organoid system16–21. While the 3D human iPSC-derived model or 3D human organoids deliver a more complex recapitulation structure and function than the 2D model. However, these 3D organoid models require developmental time of at least months or longer depending on the different protocols22–25, which might not be suitable for being a model for studying emerging diseases.

Therefore, the objective of this study is to assess the possibility of using a 2D model to study neuroinvasive infection by SARS-CoV-2 and identify potential underlying mechanisms. In this study, we have utilized the 2D iPSC-derived neural cell model, which required only two weeks of maturation, to evaluate their susceptibility to SARS-CoV-2. Using single-cell transcriptomic analysis to assess the cellular component of the human cortex and 3D organoid model, we have elucidated the insights into a model for studying the mechanisms involved in the neurological involvement of COVID-19.

Materials and methods

Ethics approval for experiments involving human samples

The iPSC line used in the study was derived from a patient who provided an informed consent form and was previously established26. The handling of samples, methods, and experiments conducted in this study were approved by the Ethics Committee on Human Rights Related to Research Involving Human Subjects, Faculty of Medicine Ramathibodi Hospital, Mahidol University, Thailand (Approval number: MURA2021/41, Date of Initial Approval 19 January 2021).

Human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) derived neural cells generation

Human iPSC-derived neural cells were generated using a protocol adapted from ref27. Initially, iPSCs were cultured and induced into neuroprogenitor cells using neural induction medium (NIM) composed of a 1:1 ratio of DMEM F/12 and Advanced neurobasal medium (Gibco, USA), 1X GlutaMAX (Gibco, USA), 1X B27 supplement without vitamin A (Gibco, USA), 1X N2 supplement (Gibco, USA), 10 µM SB431542 (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), and 0.1 µM LDN193189, along with penicillin/streptomycin. After 10 days, the cells were switched to neural expansion medium (NEM) consisting of a 1:1 ratio of DMEM F/12 and Advanced neurobasal medium, GlutaMAX, B27 supplement without vitamin A, N2 supplement, 20 ng/µl FGF-2, 20 ng/ml EGF, and penicillin/streptomycin. Subsequently, neural progenitor cells were incubated with neural differentiation medium (NDM), which included a 1:1 ratio of DMEM F/12 and Advanced neurobasal medium, GlutaMAX, B27 supplement without vitamin A, N2 supplement, 50 µM DB-cAMP, 200 µM Ascorbic acid, 20 ng/ml BDNF (Peprotech, USA), and 10 ng/ml GDNF (Peprotech, USA). The NDM was refreshed every other day for a period of 14 days.

SARS-CoV-2 infection

The different variants of SARS-CoV-2, including LJ07 (Wuhan wild-type), CV127 (Wuhan wild-type with D614G mutation), and NH88 (Alpha), were cultured in Vero E6 cells using previously established methods28. All experiments involving live SARS-CoV-2 viruses were conducted within a certified biosafety level 3 facility at the Department of Microbiology, Faculty of Science, Mahidol University. Prior to infection, cells were plated one day in advance and rinsed with serum-free media. Cells were then exposed to the virus at various multiplicities of infection (MOIs) for 2 h, followed by three washes with serum-free media.

The culture supernatants from SARS-CoV-2-infected cells were collected to evaluate the viral output. The plaque assay method was utilized to measure the viral content in the supernatants. Initially, Vero cell monolayers were seeded in 96-well plates and allowed to grow for 24 h before infection. The cells were then exposed to different dilutions of the supernatants containing the virus for 1 h at 37 °C. Following that, the cells were covered with an overlay medium consisting of MEM supplemented with 5% FBS and 1% agarose (3 mL per well). The cultures were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 3 days to enable plaque formation. Subsequently, the plaque phenotypes were visualized by staining with a 1.25% crystal violet solution. The number of plaques was determined and reported as focus-forming units per milliliter (FFU/mL).

Immunofluorescence and image analysis

Cells were washed once with PBS and fixed for 10 min with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA, Wako, USA) at RT. After fixation, cells were washed 3 times with PBS. Then, the cells were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton-X 100 in PBS for 15 min at 37 °C and blocked with 2% bovine serum albumin in PBS (blocking solution) for 1 h. Primary antibodies, including SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid (1:200, Sino Biological (40143-R001)), OCT4 (1:200, Santa Cruz Biotechnology (SC5279)), SSEA4 (1:200, Santa Cruz Biotechnology (SC59368)), Nanog (1:200, Santa Cruz Biotechnology (SC33759)), Tra-1-60 (1:200, Santa Cruz Biotechnology (SC21705)), Nestin (1:200, Cell Signaling (33475)), Tuj1 (1:200, Santa Cruz Biotechnology (SC51670)), GFAP (1:100, Abcam (ab190288)), S100β (1:100, Abcam (ab52642)), NF-kB (1:200, Cell Signaling (8242)) and ACE2 (1:200, Sino Biological (10108-T24)) were diluted in blocking solution and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Cells were then washed three times with PBS containing 0.05% Tween-20. Appropriate Secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor Anti-mouse 488 (1:400, 4408S, Cell Signaling Technology), Alexa Fluor Anti-mouse 555 (1:400, 4409S, Cell Signaling Technology), Alexa Fluor Anti-mouse 647 (1:400, 4410S, Cell Signaling Technology), Alexa Fluor Anti-rabbit 488 (1:400, 4412S, Cell Signaling Technology), Alexa Fluor Anti-rabbit 555 (1:400, 4413S, Cell Signaling Technology) and Alexa Fluor Anti-rabbit 647 (1:400, 4414S, Cell Signaling Technology)) were diluted in blocking solution and incubated with the cells for 1 h at RT. The cells were then washed 3 times and nuclear counter-stained with Hoechst33342 (Invitrogen (H3570)) in PBS at 1:2500 for 5 min, RT. Fluorescence images were captured using a Zeiss LSM 900 Airyscan 2 confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss, Germany).

Quantitative real time PCR

Total RNA was isolated from cells using RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen, USA) with DNAse I treatment according to the manufacturer’s protocol. First-strand cDNA synthesis was performed from 1 µg of total RNA using RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The cDNAs were further diluted at a 1:20 ratio, and 2 µl were used for each Luminaris Higreen qPCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) reaction with 5 µM of each primer. The qPCRs were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions using Biorad CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System. Relative expressions were calculated using the ΔCt method; GAPDH was used as a reference gene.

Single-cell preparation and library construction

The iPSC-derived neural cells were trypsinized and dissociated using 0.5% trypsin in PBS (Gibco, USA) and then resuspended in 0.04% BSA in PBS. A 40-µm strainer was used to remove cell debris and clumps, to obtain single-cell suspension. Single-cell transcriptomic libraries were generated following the Chromium Single-cell 5’ Reagent Kits V2 User Guide (10x Genomics, USA), with a target recovery of 10,000 cells per sample. The quality of the single-cell libraries was assessed using the Agilent High Sensitivity D1000 Tapestation (Agilent, USA). These libraries were subsequently subjected to sequencing using the HiSeq system (Illumina, USA) at Macrogen Inc., Korea.

Single-cell transcriptomic analysis

The initial processing of raw sc-RNAseq data (FASTQ files) was conducted using the Cell Ranger pipeline (10x Genomics, version 6.1.2), following a slightly modified version of the methodology described in our previous work29. This involved mapping the sequences to the GRCh38 human reference genome and filtering to retain only cell-associated 10x barcodes. The SoupX package (version 1.6.2) was employed to eliminate ambient RNA contamination exclusively from the 2D droplet-based scRNA-seq datasets. Subsequent processing of gene expression matrices was carried out using the Seurat R package (http://satijalab.org/seurat/, version 4.3.0). Seurat objects were created for each timepoint (e.g., day 10 or day 14), and doublets were identified and excluded using DoubletFinder (version 2.0.3). Cells were kept for further downstream analyses if the number of detected genes in each cell was between 200 and 3,000 genes per cell, to exclude false and doublet cells. Cells with more than 5% mitochondrial genes were also removed. An exception was made for the day 10 dataset, where the maximum gene threshold was adjusted to 6,000 genes per cell due to depth of sequencing. Mitochondrial gene content and cell cycle effects were regressed out using Seurat’s scaling functions to focus on biologically relevant variations in the data. For the 2D iPSC-derived neural cell analysis, the top 2000 variable genes from both samples were chosen as anchor genes for integration using reciprocal PCA30. Clusters were identified using the Louvain method with the top 30 principal components. Cell types were identified using a set of established marker genes from the literature, as summarized in Table S1. To compare the 2D iPSC-derived neural cells with 3D organoids, scRNA-seq data of the 6- and 10-month human brain organoid23 retrieved from the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (accession numbers: GSM3734296 and GSM3734297). The Seurat pipeline, consistent with the 2D iPSC-derived neural cell data, was applied for quality control during preprocessing. Following integration, clustering and cell type annotation were performed using the same consistent pipeline and marker genes as used with the 2D iPSC-derived neural cell data described earlier (Table S2).

Statistical analysis in infection studies

Statistical analysis was calculated using GraphPad Prism version 8.0.0 (GraphPad Software, USA). A sample size was estimated based on previous neuroblastoma cell lines for neurodegenerative studies to obtain biometrical sample size estimation and the assumptions of a power of 0.8 and a type I error rate of 0.05. Data was expressed as mean ± SEM. Mann-Whitney test was performed for comparisons between two independent variables. A one-way ANOVA test was performed for comparisons among several groups. 2-way ANOVA test was performed for comparisons 2 factors among several groups.

Results

2D human iPSC-derived neural cells represent cortical neurons

We first generated and characterized a 2D model of human iPSC-derived neural cells to represent cortical neurons. We used a two-step differentiation protocol (modified from27 with iPSC lines. We then utilized an iPSC cell line from a healthy volunteer (MUi-01926), which expresses high levels of pluripotency markers, including NANOG, OCT4, SSEA4, and TRA-1-60. Following the neural induction process, neuroprogenitor cells (NPCs) expressed PAX6 markers and were expanded for 3–5 passages. By day 14 of neuronal maturation, the 2D human iPSC-derived neural cells expressed high levels of the neuronal marker TUJ1, indicating successful development of the iPSC cell line (Fig. 1A).

Considering the heterogeneity of cell types in cortical neurons, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing of two neural cell populations at days 10 and 14 after differentiation. After the quality control process, we obtained ~ 15,000–18,000 cells in each sample, 8,000–19,000 reads, and 1,500-2,500 genes per cell (from 2 independent experiments and datasets). We then applied the reciprocal PCA method to integrate the two libraries and clustered cells into eight major clusters. Each cluster was annotated based on their specific markers (Fig. 1B and C) and the top 20 differential expressing genes in each cluster, as shown in table S1. The major cell types in these iPSC-derived neural cells are radial glia (26.8%), glutamatergic neuron (14.9%) and immature neuron (3.4%). For the other type of cells which include the astrocyte (3.3%), intermediate progenitor cell (4.8%), interneuron (30.4%), and sub cortical neuron (3.6%). In summary, these results demonstrate the presence of diverse neuronal cell types in our 2D iPSC-derived neural model.

2D Human iPSC-derived neural cells share diversity with 3D organoids

We then compared these models to a 3D organoid previously presented by Trujillo et al.23, by first integrating our dataset with their 3D organoid models at 6 and 10 months. After quality control using the same criteria as described above, the 6-month-old dataset contains 4,540 cells, and the 10-month-old dataset contains 3,001 cells, which were then used for further downstream analysis. The analysis assessed the similarity in transcriptomic profiles by clustering cells into a total of 9 major cellular subtypes based on their transcriptomic profiles (see Fig. 2A and C). Figure 2B illustrates that cells from our 2D and this 3D models were present in most clusters, indicating a shared similarity in transcriptomic profile distribution overall. We then investigated in more detail by further comparing the cluster abundances between the two datasets. The majority of cell types in the 3D organoid model were radial glial cells and glutamatergic neurons, while the 3D model exhibited a limited number of interneurons. Next, we assessed the similarity of the transcriptomic profile of the 3D organoid models at 6 and 10 months of age to the 2D model. Interestingly, the 2D model, after 2 weeks of maturation, exhibited a similar complexity of cell clusters to the 10-month-old 3D model, sharing major cellular contributions including interneurons, glutamatergic neurons, immature neurons, and radial glial cells. However, the 10-month-old 3D model showed a limited contribution from astrocytes (refer to Fig. S1).

Human iPSC-derived neural cells are susceptible to SARS-CoV-2

Having demonstrated the advantages of the 2D model, particularly its short differentiation time for studying emerging diseases, we further investigated the potential susceptibility of SARS-CoV-2 infection in the central nervous system using this model. First, we compared the susceptibility of SARS-CoV-2 infection between the 2D model and the widely used neuronal cell line SHSY-5Y, which is commonly employed in neurodegenerative research31,32. After incubating both cell types with the Wuhan wild-type strain of SARS-CoV-2 for 48 h, 2D model showed positive staining for the nucleocapsid protein (NP) of SARS-CoV-2, starting at multiplicities of infection (MOI) 0.1 and increasing at MOI 1. In contrast, no NP staining was observed in SHSY-5Y (Fig. S2). To further evaluate viral production after infection, we collected the culture media of SHSY-5Y and the 2D model following incubation with SARS-CoV-2 for 48 h. No viral particle was found in the culture media from SHSY-5Y. However, the culture media from the 2D model showed a dose-dependent presence of SARS-CoV-2 particles. Therefore, an MOI of 1 was used in the following experiments. Next, we evaluated the kinetics of SARS-CoV-2 infection at 24, 48, and 72 h post-infection in 2D iPSC-derived neural cells, with Vero E6 cells used as a positive control (Fig. 3A and B). Infected cells, indicated by green fluorescence, were observed as early as 24 h post-infection in both Vero E6 (Fig. 3A) and 2D iPSC-derived neural cells (Fig. 3B). Green fluorescence intensity increased from 24 to 48-hour post-infection and was highest at 48 h post-infection, followed by a reduction at 72 h. We next investigated whether different variants of SARS-CoV-2 affect the susceptibility of central nervous system infection. To address this, 2D iPSC-derived neural cells were incubated with three viral strains: LJ07 (Wuhan wild-type), CV127 (Wuhan wild-type with D614G mutation), and NH88 (alpha). Figure 3C and D shows no differences in infectivity among the three SARS-CoV-2 viral variants in both cell models, as assessed by the number of viral particles released in the supernatant after 48 h of infection.

Altogether, these results support the susceptibility of 2D iPSC-derived neural cells to SARS-CoV-2 infection, which could not be observed in the neuronal cell line SHSY-5Y. Notably, although the proportion of infected cells was low (less than 2% of the total cell population), this level of infection was sufficient to produce detectable viral output as shown in Fig. 3F.

Predominant susceptibility of GFAP-expressing cells to SARS-CoV-2 infection

Due to the heterogeneity of cell types in the 2D model as shown in Fig. 1B, we further tested whether there is a preference for a specific cell type that is susceptible to SARS-CoV-2 infection. Previous study has shown a preferential infection in glial cells33. To investigate this, we compared the susceptibility of SARS-CoV-2 infection between neuronal and glial cells using an immunofluorescent technique. Therefore, we counterstained NP with two markers of neuronal and glial cells: TUJ1, which stains for the neuronal marker (Fig. 3G), and GFAP, which stains for the glial marker (Fig. 3H). Interestingly, the immunofluorescent stain of NP revealed more colocalization with GFAP than with TUJ1. We further quantified the preferential colocalization of NP between 2 markers using colocalization correlation analysis. As expected, the Pearson’s R score of GFAP and NP was significantly higher than the score calculated from TUJ1 and NP (Fig. 3I). In addition to Pearson’s r value, Mander’s overlap coefficient was also evaluated. This analysis showed a similar trend with greater overlap intensity between NP and GFAP compared with TUJ1, although the difference did not reach statistical significance. To further assess whether astrocyte-like cells are predominantly infected in this model—given that GFAP is also expressed in radial glial and neural progenitor cells—we performed additional immunostaining using the astrocyte marker S100β. Notably, NP immunofluorescence showed colocalization with S100β (Fig. 3J). Altogether, these results indicate that NP predominantly colocalized with GFAP-expressing, astrocyte-like cells in the 2D iPSC-derived neural cell model.

No global neuroinflammation after SARS-CoV-2 infection

It has been demonstrated that SARS-CoV-2 infection triggers various cellular responses, including inflammatory processes34. Therefore, in the next set of experiments, we examined the cellular response following SARS-CoV-2 infection. We measured the activation of nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB), a transcription factor that regulates the expression of genes involved in immune and inflammatory responses, by assessing the translocation of NF-κB at 48 h after infection. Unfortunately, there was no increase in NF-κB translocation observed at 48 h after infection with different variants compared to the control condition (Fig. 4A-D). Next, we collected the supernatant of iPSC-derived neural cells that were incubated with LJ07, CV127, and NH88 at MOI 0 (control) and 1 for 48 h to measure the release of the inflammatory cytokines IL-6. The U87-MG cell line was used as a positive control for detecting IL-6. However, we found no significant differences in the levels of IL-6 in the supernatant of iPSC-derived neural cells incubated with LJ07, CV127, and NH88 at MOI 0 (control) and 1 for 48 h, as assessed by the ELISA technique (Fig. 4E). To assess the inflammatory response in 2D iPSC-derived neural cell model under high-MOI conditions, cells were incubated with wild-type SARS-CoV-2 at MOI 1 and 10 and the supernatant were collected to measure the release of the inflammatory cytokines IL-6 (Fig. 4F) and IL-1β (Fig. 4G). Consistent with the previous experiments, no significant differences in IL-6 and IL-1β levels were observed at either MOI compared with the control group. Next, we assessed the cellular consequences of infection by examining the apoptotic pathway. The mRNA level of pro-apoptotic gene (BAX) and the anti-apoptotic gene (Bcl-2) were evaluated. No significant differences in the expression in both gene were observed between the infected and control group.

Gene expression of interest associated with cellular susceptibility in SARS-CoV-2 infection

The differential susceptibility of cells to SARS-CoV-2 infection has been associated with variations in the expression of factors involved in viral entry into target cells. Therefore, our first step was to measure the mRNA expression from the total cell lysate of well-characterized factors, including ACE2, TMPRSS2, and NRP-1, known to be involved in SARS-CoV-2 infection35,36. Figure 5A presents the relative mRNA expression levels of ACE2, TMPRSS2, and NRP-1 in our 2D iPSC-derived neural cells and SHSY-5Y cells, with Calu-3 cells used as a positive control. The relative mRNA expression of ACE2, TMPRSS2, and NRP-1 in iPSC-derived neural cells showed a higher trend compared to SHSY-5Y cells; however, the differences did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 5A). Interestingly, the ACE2 protein expression in iPSC-derived neural cells exhibits a heterogeneous pattern when compared to SHSY-5Y and A549 cells, which show a more homogenous expression of ACE2. Therefore, when calculating the total cell expression of ACE2 protein, iPSC-derived neural cells exhibit the lowest expression of ACE2 when normalized to the nuclear staining (Fig. 5B-C). To further confirm heterogenous pattern of ACE2 expression in iPSC-derived neural cells, we perform flow cytometry analysis (Fig. 5D). As shown in Fig. 5D and E, approximately 2% of the total cell population was positive for ACE2 expression. In addition, double staining for ACE2 and GFAP revealed no detectable double (ACE2/GFAP) positive cells by flow cytometry analysis (Fig. 5F). This finding suggests that SARS-CoV-2 infection of GFAP-positve cells might involve additional or alternative factors contributing to viral entry.

Discussion

The neurological involvement of SARS-CoV-2 is evident through various symptoms such as headache, loss of smell and taste, dizziness, and fatigue. Severe complications including encephalopathy, stroke, seizures, and encephalitis can also occur2,3. These effects are observed in both mild and severe cases, suggesting possible involvement of the central nervous system. However, understanding of SARS-CoV-2 infection in brain cells remains limited due to the lack of suitable human models for studying infection of the central nervous system. In this study, we established and utilized a 2D iPSC-derived neural model to assess CNS susceptibility to SARS-CoV-2 infection, as well as to identify factors contributing to infection.

Our findings revealed a diversity of cellular subtypes comparable to those in the 3D organoid model. Despite the short preparation time, the 2D iPSC-derived neural model was used to investigate SARS-CoV-2 neuroinvasion. Our study further revealed that: (1) 2D iPSC-derived neural cells were susceptible to SARS-CoV-2 infection; (2) there was no difference in infectivity among different SARS-CoV-2 variants tested; (3) GFAP-expressing cells exhibited a higher susceptibility to SARS-CoV-2 infection.

The iPSC-derived neural cell models were recognized as representative models for the study of pathogenesis in the central nervous system due to their ability to encompass multiple cell types, similar to what is observed in the living brain38–40. Currently, 3D iPSC-derived brain organoid models have been developed and are of general interest due to their capability to represent neural maturation and network formation23. However, one of the main challenges in 3D iPSC-derived brain organoid models is the requirement for a long-term culture21,23, which might not be suitable for studying emerging diseases that require a prompt response, such as COVID-19. Therefore, in this study, we aimed to characterize 2D iPSC-derived neural cells. After 2 weeks of culture, 2D iPSC-derived neural cells generated mixed neuronal and glial populations (Fig. 1). The single-cell transcriptomic analysis showed that the heterogeneity of cellular subtypes in the 2D model is comparable to that of 3D human brain organoids. However, distinct differences in cell type composition are observed between the two models, including a lower proportion of glutamatergic neurons in the 2D model compared to the 3D models (Fig. 2). Conversely, interneuron populations, which are present in the 2D cultures, were largely absent in the 3D model. These compositional differences highlight inherent trade-offs between the two systems. Accordingly, the 2D model provides a complementary platform that is particularly suitable for studying cell-type–specific neuroinvasive infection in cortical neural cells in the context of emerging infectious diseases.

Our findings also demonstrated that 2D iPSC-derived neural cells are susceptible to SARS-CoV-2 infection, in contrast to SH-SY5Y cells. These results align with previous studies indicating limited neuronal infection in various neuronal cell lines, 3D organoid models, and postmortem brain tissue41–47. While SH-SY5Y is commonly utilized as an in vitro model for several neuronal diseases31,32, it is a neuroblastoma cell line with a specific catecholamine machinery, potentially limiting its representation of other neuronal subtypes that may exhibit distinct susceptibility to SARS-CoV-2 infection. These observations emphasize the advantages of employing iPSC-derived neural cells for evaluating SARS-CoV-2 infection in the CNS. It has been demonstrated that different strains of SARS-CoV-2 exhibit varying degrees of clinical severity48. However, within the central nervous system (CNS), there is no difference in infectivity observed among iPSC-derived neural cells when exposed to the Wuhan wild-type, Wuhan wild-type with D614G mutation, and alpha viral strains. These findings suggest that, in this model, the tested viral strains do not differ in their infectivity in neural cells. Furthermore, our results indicate that the GFAP-positive cells, which include astrocytes and radial glial cells, are predominantly involved in SARS-CoV-2 infection within the CNS. Hence, these cell types hold potential significance for SARS-CoV-2 infection in iPSC-derived neural cells. This finding aligns with previous research demonstrating SARS-CoV-2 infection in GFAP-positive cells in post-mortem brain samples and other in vitro models33,49,50. Notably, other studies have reported variable astrocyte susceptibility to SARS-CoV-2 depending on the experimental model, viral strain, and culture conditions, including differences among culture systems46,47,51,52. These discrepancies highlight the context-dependent nature of astrocyte vulnerability, which may be influenced by differential expression of host factors related to viral tropism.

To investigate the susceptibility of iPSC-derived neural cells compared to SH-SY5Y and the underlying mechanisms, we initially evaluated the gene expression of factors involved in viral entry, including ACE2, TMPRSS2, and NRP-1. These factors were expressed at low levels. However, there were no significant differences between iPSC-derived neural cells and SH-SY5Y. These findings are consistent with previous studies11,53,54 and suggest that the bulk gene expression analysis may not provide a clear understanding of infectivity mechanisms, given that SARS-CoV-2 predominantly infects GFAP-positive cell types. In addition, we did not detect ACE2 protein expression in GFAP-positive cells in the 2D model, which is consistent with previous findings in the 3D cortical brain model showing limited ACE2 expression in astrocytes46. Therefore, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing analysis to identify specific cell types and investigate factors involved in SARS-CoV-2 infection. We compared known factors involved in SARS-CoV-2 infection from previous studies, including ACE2, NRP-1, BSG, TMPRSS, FURIN, and HSPA537,55. Interestingly, astrocytic clusters exhibited higher expression of the BSG gene compared to other cellular clusters in the iPSC-derived neural cell model, which is consistent with a previous study46. These findings suggest that BSG may play a role in CNS infection by SARS-CoV-2 and are consistent with reports of BSG expression in human brain tissue56. BSG encodes the transmembrane glycoprotein CD147, which has been proposed as a host factor in SARS-CoV-2 infection. Wang et al. demonstrated that the viral spike protein interacts with CD147, and that blocking or knocking down CD147 reduces viral replication, whereas its overexpression enhances infection57. Additionally, the BSG gene has been identified as a core receptor for SARS-CoV-2 in microglia derived from iPSCs58. Although our data suggest that CD147 may play an important role in infection in our model, future studies using CD147-neutralizing approaches will be necessary to further validate its functional involvement.

This study demonstrates neurotropism in 2D iPSC-derived neural cells; however, several limitations should be considered. These include: (i) the lack of a blood–brain barrier (BBB) and neurovascular unit, which limit the ability to model pathogen entry into the central nervous system (CNS); (ii) the absence of microglia, which may restrict the ability to capture the full inflammatory response; (iii) the iPSC-derived neural cells resemble early developmental stages, which may respond differently compared to the mature or aging brain; and (iv) the single-cell transcriptomic analysis requires dissociation of the cultures, resulting in loss of spatial information and limiting interpretation of cell–cell interactions present in intact neural tissue. Notably, this model did not show a global inflammatory response or significant cell death following infection, which has been reported in other systems46,47. This may be due to the relatively low proportion of infected cells, which could reduce the sensitivity to detect downstream cellular responses due to signal dilution from the larger uninfected population. Single-cell transcriptomic analysis following infection, with selective analysis of infected cells, would be a more appropriate approach to characterize cell-intrinsic inflammatory and stress responses in future studies. Although our findings suggest a potential association between astrocyte susceptibility and BSG expression, further functional studies are needed to confirm its direct role in viral entry. Therefore, this model provides a useful and complementary platform for investigating cell-type–specific susceptibility and host factors associated with SARS-CoV-2 neurotropism.

In summary, our study provides evidence supporting CNS infection by SARS-CoV-2 and highlights that infection is largely restricted to the astrocytic population within this model. Furthermore, the infection of SARS-CoV-2 in iPSC-derived neural cells does not elicit a global inflammatory response. Our single-cell analysis identified the BSG gene, highly expressed in the astrocytic population, as a potential candidate factor involved in SARS-CoV-2 infectivity in the CNS. However, further studies are required to confirm the involvement of BSG expression in SARS-CoV-2 infection. Additionally, several important questions remain unanswered, such as analyzing individual cellular responses to SARS-CoV-2 infection and investigating the mechanisms underlying the neurological manifestations of long COVID.
