Humicola ovoideum sp. nov. and Pseudohumicola obscura sp. nov., Two Novel Chaetomiaceae Species Reported in Korea
Authors: Hae-Dam Kim, Mohammad Hamizan Azmi, Seong-Keun Lim, Gang-Won Jeong, Seung-Yeol Lee, Hee-Young Jung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDuring an investigation of soil micro-fungi in Korea, two fungal strains designated as strains KNU-1816T and KNUF-20-124T were isolated from soil samples collected from Gyeongbuk province. The morphological features of strain KNU-1816T included obovate greenish-yellow ascomata with bristle-like dark-brown hairs and clavate asci with 8 biseriate bilaterally flattened limoniform olivaceous brown ascospores containing an apical germ pore and subhyaline or pale brown conidia. While strain KNUF-20-124T produced aleurioconidia-like conidia and hyaline, Acremonium-like conidiophores. Phylogenetic analyses based on the concatenated nucleotide sequences of the internal transcribed spacer (ITS) regions, partial sequences of the large subunit (LSU) 28S rRNA, RNA polymerase II largest subunit (RPB2), and beta-tubulin (TUB2) placed strains KNU-1816T and KNUF-20-124T within the family Chaetomiaceae, specifically in the genera Humicola and Pseudohumicola, respectively. Each occupied a distinct phylogenetic position separate from known species, thereby warranting their designation as novel species. Here, we describe and illustrate two new species, Humicola ovoideum sp. nov. KNU-1816T and Pseudohumicola obscura sp. nov. KNUF-20-124T, providing detailed morphological descriptions and phylogenetic insights.Keywords: Humicola ovoideum sp. nov, morphological analyses, phylogenetic analyses, Pseudohumicola obscura sp. nov

Introduction

Fungi are directly or indirectly related to the soil environment and play a variety of roles in maintaining ecosystems [1]. Beyond their ecological importance, fungi also exhibit antagonistic properties by parasitizing plant pathogens, thereby reducing the impact of the latter [1]. Furthermore, fungal metabolites are industrially significant, with diverse applications in agriculture, pharmaceuticals, and biotechnology. Among the diverse fungal families, Chaetomiaceae is particularly relevant due to its ecological versatility and predominance in various environments [2]. Chaetomiaceae is a family of fungi in the phylum Ascomycota, class Sordariomycetes, order Sordariales that are mostly saprophytic and found in a variety of environments, including feces, air, seeds, compost, decaying plants, and indoor environments [2]. To date, 275 species across 50 genera have been identified within Chaetomiaceae, among which Chaetomium, Humicola, and Pseudohumicola are prominent [3]. Among them, the genus Humicola was first reported in 1914, and a total of 24 species were reported following a reclassification [4]. Traditionally, Humicola species have been recognized by their production of thick-walled unicellular spores located at the sides or terminus of the hyphae, with no known sexual morphs [4]. However, advancements in molecular phylogenetics have revealed species from other genera within Chaetomiaceae that share characteristics with Humicola, leading to their reclassification into this genus [4]. This redefinition has expanded our understanding of Humicola, which now includes species exhibiting both sexual and asexual morphs. Humicola conidia can be produced terminally on the short branches of hyphae, which could be interpreted as minimally differentiated (i.e., micronematous) conidiophores. In addition, the asexual generation has been reported to form aleurioconidia-like conidia or Acremonium-like conidiophores [4]. Type species H. fuscoatra exemplifies the genus, while H. olivacea and H. alopallonella are the only two species that have been previously recorded in Korea [5–7].

Conversely, the genus Pseudohumicola has emerged from molecular phylogenetic studies that have reclassified certain species previously placed in Humicola [2]. Although Pseudohumicola species also form aleurioconidia-like conidia and Acremonium-like phialides, this genus is distinguished from Humicola by unique morphological features such as twisted apical hairs on the conidia [2]. Only twelve Pseudohumicola species have been reported worldwide (thus illustrating the scarcity of species in this genus), type species P. subspiralis is the only one to have been reported in Korea [2,5, 8–10].

In this study, we investigated the cultural, morphological, and molecular phylogenetic characteristics of two fungal strains isolated from Korean soil, both of which belong to the family Chaetomiaceae. The findings enhance our understanding of fungal species diversity, distribution, and ecological roles in Korea while contributing to the global catalog of fungal biodiversity.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and fungal strain isolation

The fungal strains involved in this investigation were isolated from soil samples collected from Yasan, Gimcheon-si, Gyeongbuk province (36°11’00.6ʺN, 128°07’05.8ʺE) and Dokdo-ri, Ulleung-eup, Ulleung-gun, Gyeongbuk province (37°14’28.9ʺN, 131°51’54.5ʺE), Korea. Isolation was performed using the plate dilution method, as previously described in Sastoque et al. [9]. Single colonies were then transferred to new potato dextrose agar (PDA; Difco, Detroit, MI) plates at a temperature of 25 °C. Strains KNU-1816T and KNUF-20-124T were chosen for additional molecular analyses, as well as cultural and morphological evaluations. The strains used in this study were deposited at the National Institute of Biological Resources under the accession numbers NIBRFGC000502238 for KNU-1816T and NIBRFGC000507842 for KNUF-20-124T.

Cultural and morphological characterization

To investigate the cultural and morphological characteristics of strains KNU-1816T and KNUF-20-124T, they were cultured on oatmeal agar (OA; Difco, Detroit, MI), corn meal agar (CMA; Difco, Detroit, MI), malt extract agar (MEA; Difco, Detroit, MI) and potato carrot agar (PCA: Himedia, Thane, Maharashtra, India) and incubated in an incubator at 25 °C for 7 d [4]. Their cultural characteristics (color, shape, and size) were noted, while their morphological characteristics were determined using a light microscope (BX-50, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) and a stereoscopic microscope (Digital MicroScope-M5, Siwon, Anyang, Korea).

Genomic DNA extraction, polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification, and sequencing

Total genomic DNA was extracted from the fungal mycelium of strains KNU-1816T and KNUF-20-124T using a HiGene Genomic DNA Prep Kit (BIOFACT, Daejeon, Korea) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The internal transcribed spacer (ITS) regions, partial sequences of the large subunit (LSU) 28S rRNA, RNA polymerase II largest subunit 2 (RPB2), and beta-tubulin (TUB2) were amplified using primer pairs of ITS1F/ITS4, LR0R/LR5, RPB2-5F2/RPB2-7cR, and T1/TUB4Rd, respectively [10–19]. Subsequently, the PCR products were purified using ExoSAP-IT PCR Product Cleanup Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) and sequenced by SolGent (Daejeon, Korea). The resulting sequences from the two representative strains have been deposited to DNA Data Bank of Japan (DDBJ).

Molecular phylogenetic analyses

Strains KNU-1816T and KNUF-20-124T were subjected to similarity analyses using the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) against the datasets available in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database. Subsequently, several related sequences were retrieved from the database for the purpose of conducting phylogenetic analyses (Table 1). Reference taxa were selected based on BLAST similarity searches and previous taxonomic and phylogenetic studies of Humicola, Pseudohumicola, and related genera in Chaetomiaceae [2,4, 8–10]. In particular, reference strains used in previous phylogenetic studies, including the study by Wang et al. [4], were considered, and type or ex-type strains were preferentially included when sequence data for ITS, LSU, RPB2, and TUB2 were available. Phylogenetic trees were constructed from the concatenated sequences of the ITS regions, LSU, RPB2, and TUB2 by employing the maximum-likelihood (ML) method in MEGA11 and Bayesian inference (BI) with MrBayes version 3.2.7 (Swedish Museum of Natural History, Stockholm, Sweden) [20, 21]. The evolutionary distance matrices for the ML analyses were generated using the general time reversible model in MEGA11, with bootstrap values based on 1000 replications [20]. BI analysis was conducted with four Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) using the GTR + I + G model for 10 million generations, sampling every 1000 generations. The first 25% of samples were discarded as burn-in before calculating posterior probabilities, and the consensus tree was viewed in FigTree version 1.4.4 (Institute of Evolutionary Biology, University of Edinburgh, Edinburgh, UK) [22].

Cultural characteristics of strain KNU-1816T

Colonies on all media were incubated at 25 °C for 7 d. On OA, the colonies appeared white with the edges entire, growing to a diameter of 23–25 mm. The aerial hyphae were floccose, with no observable pigmentation. The reverse side of the colonies was not distinct (Figure 1(A)). On CMA, colonies with entire edges grew to 32–34 mm in diameter. The center of the colony was characterized by a dense cluster of white aerial hyphae, while the rest of the colony had sparse aerial hyphae and appeared whitish-beige. The reverse side of the colony was also whitish-beige (Figure 1(B)). On MEA, white-brownish aerial hyphae were observed. The colonies measured 30–31 mm in diameter, and the reverse side was white to orange toward the center, with no pigmentation on the obverse side (Figure 1(C)). On PCA, the colonies measuring 26–29 mm in diameter appeared filamentous with aerial hyphae. The colonies were white and floccose, and the reverse side appeared light yellow to orange. No pigmentation was observed on the obverse side of the colonies (Figure 1(D)).

Morphological characteristics of strain KNU-1816T

Ascomata measuring 100–150 × 80–130 μm (n = 20) were greenish-yellow and obovate, with dark-brown ascomatal hairs observed on the top and sides of ascomata (Figure 1(E,F)). Conidia subhyaline or pale brown in color and measuring 5.0–5.5 × 6.0–6.5 μm (n = 20) were observed to be globose to subglobose at the sides or apex of the hyphae (Figure 1(G)). The ascomatal wall observed on the surface of the ascomata was composed of angular or irregularly shaped brown cells (Figure 1(H)). The ascomatal hairs were bristle-like (straight or gently wavy), dark-brown in color, and thinning toward the distal end and becoming fainter (Figure 1(I)). The ascomatal hairs were unbranched, septate, dark brown in color, clustered at the top, and scattered laterally. The asci were clavate, comprising the spore-bearing part 20.1–33.3 × 6.5–10.8 μm and stalks 4.2–5.8 μm (n = 10) long, and contained 8 biseriate ascospores (Figure 1(J)). When mature, the latter were olivaceous brown, limoniform, bilaterally flattened, and 5.7–9.1 × 4.5–6.7 μm (n = 60) in size with an apical germ pore (Figure 1(K)).

Note: On OA, strain KNU-1816T formed whitish floccose colonies 23–25 mm in diameter that were smaller than those of H. homopilata CBS 157.55T (42–48 mm) [4]. On CMA, colonies appeared whitish-beige with interspersed aerial hyphae, which is unlike those of H. christensenii CBS 127760T [4]. On MEA, it formed white powdery aerial hyphae that were distinct from those of the cottony hyphae in H. homopilata CBS 157.55T colonies [4]. On PCA, its hyphal length measured 26–29 mm, which is smaller than that of H. homopilata CBS 157.55T (41–47 mm) [4]. The ascomata of KNU-1816T were greenish-yellow and ovoid in shape, with dark-brown hairs measuring 100–150 × 80–130 μm being observed on the top and side of the ascomata. In contrast, colonies of H. homopilata CBS 157.55T (150–210 × 110–160 μm) and H. christensenii CBS 127760T (160–240 × 105–150 µm) are pale luteous to amber, obpyriform and buff to pale luteous, and ellipsoidal, while their ascomata are larger than those of KNU-1816T. Ascospores of KNU-1816T were 5.7–9.1 × 4.5–6.7 µm, which is a similar size to those of Humicola species but larger than those of H. sphaeralis CBS 985.87 (7–7.5 × 5.5–6.5 × 4–4.5 µm) [4]. Conidia of KNU-1816T were 5.0–5.5 × 6.0–6.5 μm, a similar size to those of Humicola species but smaller than those of H. homopilata (7.0–8.5 μm) and H. christensenii (5.5–9.0 μm) [4].

Detailed comparisons show that KNU-1816T exhibited unique cultural and morphological traits (Table 2). Although, its shared similarities with Humicola species (albeit with significant differences), thus confirming that KNU-1816T is a novel species in the genus Humicola.

Phylogenetic analyses of strain KNU-1816T

The ITS regions, LSU, RPB2, and TUB2 of strain KNU-1816T were amplified, and sequence fragments were obtained with lengths of 472, 800, 816, and 659 bp, respectively. The sequence of the ITS regions showed the highest similarity (98.3%) with that of H. christensenii CBS 127760T, followed by a 98.1% similarity with those of H. wallefii CBS 147.67T and H. pinnata CBS 467.66T. Similarity in the partial sequences of the LSU was 100% with those of H. christensenii CBS 127760T, H. fuscoatra var. fuscoatra CBS 118.14T, and H. homopilata CBS 157.55TT. The RPB2 sequence was most similar to that of H. fuscoatra var. fuscoatra CBS 118.14T (95.4%), followed by a 95.3% similarity with that of H. christensenii CBS 127760T and a 95.2% similarity with that of H. homopilata CBS 157.55T. The TUB2 sequence showed a 93.1% similarity with that of H. homopilata CBS 157.55T, a 92.88% similarity with that of H. pinnata CBS 467.66T, and a 92.4% similarity with that of H. christensenii CGMCC 3.08497. After determining the taxonomic position of KNU-1816T based on the concatenated sequences of the ITS regions, LSU, RPB2, and TUB2 and assessing the ML and BI phylogenies, strain KNU-1816T formed a distinct cluster independent from other members of the genus Humicola (Figure 2), although it was closely related to H. christensenii, H. pinnata, H. homopilata, H. sphaeralis, and H. wallefii. The ML and BI analyses showed congruent topologies, with no major conflicts in the phylogenetic placement of KNU-1816T. In combination with distinct morphological and cultural characteristics, these findings support its classification as a novel species within the genus Humicola, for which we propose the name Humicola ovoideum sp. nov.

Cultural characteristics of strain KNUF-20-124T

On OA, the colonies growing to a diameter of 23–24 mm appeared white with entire edges. The aerial hyphae were floccose, with no observable soluble pigmentation. The reverse side of the colonies was not distinct (Figure 3(A)). On CMA, the colonies were measured to be 40–44 mm in diameter with filiform edges. The aerial hyphae were whitish-beige and beige on the reverse side (Figure 3(B)). On MEA, the 38–39 mm-sized colonies were downy with entire edges, with white aerial hyphae being observed, and the reverse side was white to isabelline (Figure 3(C)). On PCA, the colonies grew to 42–44 mm and appeared white with entire edges, and the reverse side was dark brown to fuscous black (Figure 3(D)).

Morphological characteristics of strain KNUF-20-124T

Conidia were produced on the sides of or between the hyphae, either directly or on short branches. The aleurioconidia-like conidia were unicellular, olive to dark brown in color, and had thick dark-brown walls (Figure 3(E,F)). The morphology of the spores was subglobose to spherical, and the size was 8.5–9.5 μm (n = 20) (Figure 3(E,F)). Hyaline Acremonium-like conidiophores of length 1.0–2.5 × 15.0–20.0 μm (n = 5) with no septa and branching were observed on the side of the hyphae (Figure 3(G)). The hyaline conidia were chain-like, obovate, lacked septa, had a smooth surface, and measured 2.5–3.2 × 1.3–2.0 μm (n = 20) (Figure 3(H)).

Note: On OA, strain KNUF-20-124T formed white colonies 23–24 mm in diameter, which is smaller than those of Pseudohumicola alba COAD 3126T (31–37 mm) and P. semispiralis CBS 723.97T (36–42 mm) [2,4,8]. On CMA, colonies of P. semispiralis CBS 723.97T developed filamentous edges with sparse aerial hyphae, while those of P. alba COAD 3126T have rounded edges and those of P. semispiralis CBS 723.97T comprise white cottony mycelium [2,4,8]. On MEA, KNUF-20-124T grew to 38–39 mm with downy aerial hyphae, which is different from those of P. alba COAD 3126T (27–33 mm, cottony, wooly hyphae) and P. semispiralis CBS 723.97T (39–45 mm, thick yellowish-brown hyphae) [2,4,8]. On PCA, colonies of KNUF-20-124T grew to 42–44 mm with white hyphae, and the reverse side was dark brown to fuscous black, which is distinct from those of P. alba COAD 3126T (35–40 mm, buff to white, floccose, reverse side: olivaceous gray) and P. semispiralis CBS 723.97T (34–40 mm, aerial hyphae sparse, reverse side: uncolored), P. pulvericola CBS 144165T (39–45 mm, sparse, white-to-pale luteous, reverse side: slightly pale luteous). Its dark-brown aleurioconidia-like conidia were 8.5–9.0 μm, which is smaller than those of P. alba COAD 3126T (8.8–10.4 μm) and P. semispiralis CBS 723.97T (9.0–11.0 μm). The Acremonium-like conidiophore of KNUF-20-124T was 1.0–2.5 × 15.0–20.0 μm, which is different from those of P. alba COAD 3126T (2.2–2.6 × 12.5–15.5 μm), while none have been observed for P. semispiralis CBS 723.97T [2,4,8]. KNUF-20-124T produced conidia 2.5–3.2 × 1.3–2.0 μm in size, which is smaller than P. alba (3.1–3.3 × 1.5–1.7 μm) [8]. Detailed comparisons reveal that KNUF-20-124T exhibits traits comparable to those of other Pseudohumicola species, albeit with distinct cultural and morphological characteristics (Table 3). Thus, strain KNUF-20-124T was identified as a novel species in the genus Pseudohumicola.

Phylogenetic analyses of strain KNUF-20-124T

The ITS regions, LSU, RPB2, and TUB2 of strain KNUF-20-124T were amplified, and sequence fragments were obtained with lengths of 572, 797, 788, and 700 bp, respectively. The sequence of the ITS regions showed a maximum of 100% similarity with P. alba COAD 3126T, a 99.8% similarity with P. semispiralis CBS 723.97T, and a 99.6% similarity with P. pulvericola CBS 144165T. For the LSU sequence, a 100% similarity was observed with P. alba COAD 3126T, P. semispiralis CBS 723.97T, and P. pulvericola CBS 144165T, and a 99.8% similarity with P. lutea COAD 3127T. The RPB2 sequence showed the highest similarity with P. pulvericola CBS 144165T at 95.6%. For the TUB2 sequence, P. alba COAD 3126T had the highest similarity of 91.1%, followed by P. pulvericola CBS 144165‍T (90.8%) and P. semispiralis CBS 723.97‍T (88.2%). Although the ITS and LSU sequences of KNUF-20-124T showed very high similarity to those of previously described Pseudohumicola species, the RPB2 and TUB2 sequences showed relatively lower similarity values, with 95.6% for RPB2 and 88.2–91.1% for TUB2. Therefore, the recognition of KNUF-20-124T as a distinct species is supported by the relatively lower similarity observed in the RPB2 and TUB2 sequences, together with its independent placement in the multilocus phylogenetic analyses.

The taxonomic position of KNUF-20-124T was determined based on ML and BI phylogenetic analyses of the concatenated sequences of the ITS regions, LSU, RPB2, and TUB2, as shown in Figures 4 and 5. In both analyses, KNUF-20-124T did not form a cluster with previously known members of the genus Pseudohumicola, but formed its own distinct cluster instead. The ML and BI analyses produced congruent topologies, with no major conflicts in the placement of KNUF-20-124T. In the phylogenetic tree, strain KNUF-20-124T was found to be closely related to P. alba COAD 3126T, P. semispiralis CBS 723.97T, and P. pulvericola CBS 144165T. Combined with distinct morphological and cultural characteristics, these findings support its classification as a novel species in the genus Pseudohumicola, for which we have proposed the name Pseudohumicola obscura sp. nov.

Discussion

Despite the ecological and industrial significance of Korean soil fungi, their exploration, specifically within the Chaetomiaceae family, has been relatively limited compared to other regions. This study was initiated to address this gap and contribute to the growing body of knowledge about soil fungi in Korea. While the global diversity of Humicola species is relatively well-documented (with 25 species reported worldwide), research within Korea remains sparse, with only two species (H. olivacea and H. alopallonella) having been described domestically [5–7]. Similarly, only one species of Pseudohumicola (a genus reclassified from Humicola in 2022), P. subspiralis, has been documented in Korea [2,5,8]. These limited records highlight a significant gap in the understanding of Korean fungal biodiversity, especially given the ecological importance and potential applications of these species. This study addresses this gap by identifying two novel species from Korean soil, Humicola ovoideum sp. nov. KNU-1816T and Pseudohumicola obscura sp. nov. KNUF-20-124T. This discovery demonstrates the untapped fungal biodiversity in this ecosystem and emphasizes the importance of further exploration of soil fungi in Korea.

Humicola species have attracted attention for their potential industrial applications, including their use as biocontrol agents against plant diseases [22]. For example, fuscoatrol A (a secondary metabolite from H. fuscoatra) has demonstrated antibiotic activity against Staphylococcus aureus (a pathogen responsible for respiratory infections in humans), while also exhibiting parasitism toward of aflatoxin-producing Aspergillus flavus, thereby mitigating the pathogenicity of the latter [23,24]. The potential of Humicola species as biocontrol agents also extends to agricultural settings. For example, soils enriched with Humicola species have shown a reduced incidence of cotton Verticillium wilt disease [25]. Another species, H. phialophoroides, produces a compound that inhibits the germination of zoospores of Phytophthora capsici (a causative agent of pepper blight), thereby suggesting its potential for biological control of this plant pathogen [26]. Collectively, these findings highlight the genus as a valuable resource for industrial and agricultural applications. Based on its taxonomic and functional profile, Humicola ovoideum sp. nov. KNU-1816T is a promising candidate for further investigation in plant pathogen management.

In contrast, Pseudohumicola species are relatively rare, with only 12 (including the one described in this study) having been documented globally [2,5, 8–10]. While the secondary metabolites and ecological functions of Humicola species have been extensively characterized, those of Pseudohumicola remain largely unexplored. However, one significant discovery is that P. pulvericola was found to be a pathogen that causes keratitis in humans [27]. Furthermore, it has also been shown to degrade enrofloxacin, a type of fluoroquinolone antibiotic widely used as a feed additive for disease prevention in aquaculture [28]. Given the close taxonomic relationship between these genera, it is plausible that Pseudohumicola species could produce biocontrolling-related metabolites. Therefore, further exploration of the metabolites produced by Pseudohumicola obscura sp. nov. KNUF-20-124T is essential to assess its utility in industrial or agricultural applications.

The discovery of these two novel species contributes significantly to the documentation of fungal biodiversity in Korea. With the increasing importance of safeguarding biological resources, as emphasized by the Convention on Biological Diversity (1992) and the Nagoya Protocol (2010), these findings underscore the value of cataloging native species [29,30]. Novel and unreported fungal species continue to be discovered from soil samples collected in Korea [31–33]. The identification of H. ovoideum sp. nov. and P. obscura sp. nov. isolated from soil samples in this study expands our understanding of fungal distribution in Korea.
