The prognostic value of the PALBI score for mortality prediction in stroke: an internal and external validation study
Authors: Ying Jiao, Chuanyuan Zhang, Hua Bai, Riletemuer Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Recent studies show that there’s a link between liver problems and how well someone does after having a stroke. The platelet—albumin—bilirubin (palbi) score is a new liver function evaluation method which has been proved useful in predicting hepatic condition. But it is still unknown whether it can predict the prognosis of stroke people. This study aimed to investigate the association between the PALBI score and clinical outcomes in stroke patients. We screened out severely ill stroke patients in MIMIC-IV by ICD-9/10 criteria, then divided them into PALBI score quartiles. The primary outcome was 1-year mortality, and the secondary outcome was in-hospital mortality. Cox proportional hazards regression and logistic regression analyzed associations with 1-year and in-hospital mortality, respectively. RCS curves showed the relationship between dose and response, and K-M survival curves were drawn. Subgroup analysis and interaction tests were performed for robustness. Random Forest and SVM machine learning models were built to rank feature importance and compare discrimination ability with the standard logistic regression model. Also, an external validation was done with the eICU Collaborative Research Database (eICU-CRD) to verify the results. A total of 2,265 patients were included (57.75% male). 1 year mortality rate was 35.01%, and mortality rate during hospital stay was 15.94%. Multivariate Cox regression revealed that a higher PALBI score had a significant impact on an increased risk of 1 year overall mortality (aHR: 1.42, 95%CI: 1.23–1.65, p < 0.001). Multivariate logistic regression showed that higher PALBI scores correlated with a greater chance of dying during a hospital stay (aOR: 1.35, 95% CI: 1.03–1.77, p = 0.01). RCS analysis shows that the risk of death increases gradually as the level of PALBI rises. Machine learning models consistently ranked the PALBI score as the second most important predictor, following the Charlson Comorbidity Index, and its inclusion improved the AUC of the Random Forest model from 0.707 to 0.724 in the validation set. Using eICU-CRD for external validation found a strong connection between high PALBI scores and higher in-hospital mortality rates (continuous variable, aOR: 1.35; 95% CI: 1.107–1.663; P = 0.003). Our findings indicate that there is a significant relationship between the PALBI score and 1-year overall mortality as well as in-hospital mortality for people with stroke. The same results in MIMIC-IV and eICU database show that the PALBI score may have some use clinically to find out which stroke patient has worse results.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-39342-5.

Introduction

Stroke is a common type of acute neurological disease that is widespread around the world and poses a great danger to human health as well as causing permanent brain injury1,2. Globally about 5.5 million people die every year due to stroke3, nearly 1/5th of all stroke patients die within a year of having a stroke4.

Stroke is a systemic disease that induces widespread inflammatory and metabolic reactions beyond local brain injury. Consequently, the liver, central to metabolism and immunity, plays a crucial role in patient prognosis. Previous studies have linked liver pathologies—such as non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, chronic hepatitis B, and cirrhosis—to poor stroke outcomes5–9. Therefore, evaluating liver function is essential in stroke management.

An ideal prognostic biomarker for stroke would be one that is both clinically useful and easy to apply. The Platelet-Albumin-Bilirubin (PALBI), as an all-in-one evaluation method, has proven itself with better prediction power than individual indicators10–13. Compared with ALBI Score, PALBI innovatively adds up platelets which can more fully show hepatic synthetic and reserve function, it’s also close to post-stroke microcirculation perfusion and the risk of hemorrhagic transformation at the same time, so as to grasp both liver condition and stroke pathological characteristics. Unlike the FIB-4 index which mainly assesses chronic liver fibrosis, Studies have demonstrated that the PALBI score exhibits strong stability during the acute phase of various critical illnesses14,15. In contrast, the ALT and AST values incorporated into the FIB-4 index are easily affected by general inflammation and other confounding factors16, whereas the components of PALBI better represent liver function in the acute phase.

Therefore, because of the complete PALBI score, stability in the critical care environment, and inherent link to the pathophysiology of stroke, it has potential to be an effective prognostic biomarker. By means of the MIMIC-IV database, this paper intends to assess the connection between PALBI and 12-month all-cause mortality and also in-hospital mortality for stroke patients so as to fill in the evidence gap concerning this indicator’s prognostic worth in cerebrovascular illness. In order to strengthen our results and make them more applicable, we used an outside group from the eICU Collaborative Research Database (eICU-CRD) to validate the connection between the PALBI score and death during hospitalization.

Materials and methods

Data source

In the investigation, de-identified clinical records were obtained from MIMIC-IV (v3. 0), which is a large critical care database with anonymized patient information from Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center’s ICU admissions between 2008 and 2022. This public repo has all kinds of details about it, such as (1) who the people were, (2) what sicknesses they had (using special doctor numbers), (3) how their body worked, (4) what blood tests showed, (5) what medicine doctors gave them, and (6) how they felt better or worse. The dataset contains information on vitals, medications administered, microbiology, fluids, and ancillary care documentation. IRB approval was not required for secondary analysis of already existing anonymous records. The research team finished the mandatory human subject protection instruction (CITI program certification) before getting the database. All particular ICD-9 and ICD-10 diagnostic codes that were used to identify stroke patients are described in detail in Table S1. The stroke subtypes shown in this table will be used in later subgroups analysis to find out if there is a link between PALBI scores and how well different groups of patients do.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Patients diagnosed with stroke via ICD-9 or ICD-10 codes were included. Only the first ICU admission for each patient was considered. We excluded patients aged < 18 or > 90 years, those with an ICU length of stay (LOS) < 4 h, and those missing platelet, bilirubin, or albumin data. Data extraction

Structured Query Language (SQL) in PostgreSQL was used to extract patient data, including: (1) demographics (sex, age); (2) hospitalization details (prognosis, complications [hypertension (HTN), diabetes mellitus (DM), pneumonia (PNA), ischemic heart disease (IHD), sepsis], comorbidities); (3) vital signs on ICU admission (heart rate [HR], temperature, diastolic blood pressure [NBPd], systolic blood pressure [NBPs], oxygen saturation [SpO2], respiratory rate [RR]); (4) laboratory parameters (first measurement upon ICU admission: white blood cells [WBC], hemoglobin [HGB], platelet count [PLT], red blood cells [RBC], albumin [ALB], glucose [Glu], potassium [K], sodium [Na], lactate [LAC], INR, prothrombin time [PT], alanine aminotransferase [ALT], aspartate aminotransferase [AST], total bilirubin [TBIL], creatinine [Cr], blood urea nitrogen [BUN], total cholesterol [TC], triglycerides [TG], brain natriuretic peptide [BNP]); (5) severity scores (SOFA, SIRS, APS III, SAPS II, OASIS, GCS, Charlson Comorbidity Index, PALBI score). To address missing data: (1) Variables with missing rates of less than 20% were imputed using the Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) method with 5 iterations ($m = 5$). These variables included liver function markers (alanine transaminase, aspartate transaminase), coagulation profiles (international normalized ratio, prothrombin time), kidney function indices (blood urea nitrogen, serum creatinine), vital signs (body weight, Glasgow Coma Scale), and routine blood parameters (hemoglobin, red blood cells, white blood cells, glucose, potassium, sodium). The results were pooled using Rubin’s rules; (2) variables with ≥ 20% missing data (e.g., HR, temperature, TG, TC) were discarded (Fig. S1, Table S2). Outcomes included: (1) primary outcome (360-day post-stroke mortality); (2) secondary outcome (in-hospital mortality, with survival time calculated as days from ICU admission to death).

The PALBI score was calculated using the first PLT, TBIL, and ALB values upon ICU admission, This approach was chosen to accurately capture the patient’s baseline hepatic function status at the time of critical care entry, thereby minimizing the potential influence of subsequent therapeutic interventions or dynamic changes in clinical condition on the PALBI score. PALBI = 2.02 × log10[TBil (μmol/L)] − 0.37 × {log10[TBil (μmol/L)]}2 − 0.04 × Alb (g/L) − 3.48 × log10[PLT (109/L)] + 1.01 × {log10[PLT (109/L)]}2 (Note: The platelet unit 109/L is equivalent to 103/μL.)

Statistical analysis

(1) Baseline analysis: Continuous variables (mean ± standard deviation, Welch’s t-test); categorical variables (counts and percentages,χ2test; significance: P < 0.05). (2) Regression models: (a) Multivariable Cox regression (360-day mortality); (b) multivariable logistic regression (in-hospital mortality); three models (unadjusted, partially adjusted, fully adjusted). For the multivariable logistic and Cox regression models (Model 3), covariate selection was based on clinical relevance and established risk factors identified in previous literature, ensuring rigorous control for potential confounders regardless of their statistical significance in preliminary screening17,18. In order to make sure our multivariable models stay stable and don’t get too fit, we did something called an “events per variable” calculation for every single regression model. The EPV was calculated as the number of events divided by the number of independent variables in the model; the fully adjusted Model 3 included 32 independent variables (covering demographics, comorbidities, laboratory parameters, severity scores, and clinical interventions). Based on standard criteria19 (32 predictors, 35.0% prevalence, Nagelkerke R2 = 0.19), the minimum sample size required to ensure a global shrinkage factor ≥ 0.9 was calculated to be 2156. Our cohort (N = 2265) exceeds this threshold, confirming adequate statistical power to minimize overfitting.

For the fully adjusted model (Model 3) which has the most covariates, the EPV values were 24.78 for 360-day mortality (with 793 events) and 11.28 for in-hospital mortality (with 361 events). Both exceeded the commonly accepted threshold of EPV = 10, indicating that our models were strong. (3) Other analysis: (a) Restricted cubic spline (RCS, PALBI—mortality dose—response relationship); (b) K-M curves (cumulative mortality visualization); (c) subgroup analysis (age, gender, race, marital status, hypertension, PNA, diabetes, IHD, vasopressors, CRRT). (4) Predictive Modeling: we built Random Forest (RF) and Support Vector Machine (SVM) models, and ranked the importance of each feature. To facilitate the visualization of performance metrics (e.g., ROC curves and calibration plots), the dataset was initially divided into a training set (80%) and a hold-out testing set (20%). To prevent overfitting, we performed Grid Search with fivefold cross-validation on the training set. The optimal hyperparameters were identified as max_depth = 4, n_estimators = 100 and criterion = ‘gini’ for Random Forest and C = 0.9 (RBF kernel) for SVM. However, to rigorously assess model stability and correct for optimism in accordance with TRIPOD guidelines, we further employed internal validation using bootstrapping with 1000 iterations on the entire cohort. In each iteration, the model was refitted on a bootstrap sample, and the optimism was calculated to derive the optimism-corrected AUC and Brier Score. Detailed results of this rigorous bootstrapping validation are presented in Supplementary Table S3, while the visualization figures are based on the hold-out testing set. Lastly, after comparing the AUCs between models with and without the PALBI score, we quantified how much better the PALBI score was at predicting 1-year all cause mortality and in hospital mortality compared to the other models. (5) Mediation analysis: Investigate the links between PALBI and 360 day mortality, as well as in hospital mortality. Finally, Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) was employed to evaluate the clinical utility and calculate the net benefit of the predictive models across a range of threshold probabilities.

External validation

To externally validate the association between the PALBI score and in-hospital mortality in stroke patients, we utilized data from the eICU Collaborative Research Database (eICU-CRD), a multi-center ICU database containing over 200,000 patient admissions across the United States. We applied identical inclusion and exclusion criteria as used for the MIMIC-IV cohort. Stroke patients were identified using ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes. The PALBI score was calculated using the same formula based on the first recorded platelet, albumin, and total bilirubin measurements during the ICU stay. The primary outcome for the external validation was in-hospital mortality. We employed multivariable logistic regression models with a comparable covariate adjustment strategy to our primary analysis (Model 3) to assess the independent association of the PALBI score with in-hospital mortality in the eICU cohort. Due to inherent differences in available variables between the MIMIC-IV and eICU databases (as detailed in previous comparisons), the specific set of adjusted covariates in the eICU external validation model was not entirely identical to that in MIMIC-IV. Specifically, certain variables available in MIMIC-IV—including severity scores (SOFA, APSIII, SIRS, SAPS II), the Charlson Comorbidity Index, CRRT use, and marital status—were not uniformly available in the eICU dataset and were therefore excluded from the validation model. Conversely, the APACHE IV score was available in eICU but not in MIMIC-IV. Despite these differences, the validation model included all consistently available and clinically relevant factors (e.g., age, sex, race, weight, comorbidities, laboratory parameters, severity scores, and interventions). The baseline characteristics of the eICU cohort are presented in Table S4.

Laboratory assessments demonstrated that non-survivors exhibited significantly elevated levels of WBC, potassium, glucose, INR, PT, total bilirubin, creatinine and BUN compared to survivors, while showing lower ALB and HGB levels. No statistically significant differences were observed in PLT or RBC counts between groups.

Demographic analysis indicated that non-survivors were older, had lower body weight, and included a higher proportion of male and married patients compared to survivors. No significant mortality differences were observed across racial groups. Severity scores including SOFA, APSIII, SIRS, SAPSII, OASIS, GCS, PALBI and Charlson comorbidity index were all significantly higher in non-survivors, who also had higher rates of vasopressor use and CRRT administration during hospitalization.

Association between PALBI and 360-day prognosis/in-hospital mortality in stroke patients

This study employed multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis to evaluate the relationship between PALBI and 360-day prognosis in stroke patients, while logistic regression was used to assess its association with in-hospital mortality. Three progressively adjusted models were constructed: Model 1 included only PALBI; Model 2 added demographic variables (age, sex, race, weight, marital status); and Model 3 further incorporated clinical comorbidities (HTN, PNA, diabetes, IHD, sepsis), laboratory parameters (HGB, RBC, WBC, GLU, electrolytes, coagulation markers, liver/renal function tests), severity scores (SOFA, APSIII, SIRS, SAPSII, OASIS, GCS, Charlson), and interventions (VP use, CRRT, thrombolysis, mechanical thrombectomy).

The Cox regression analysis demonstrated that PALBI significantly predicted 360-day mortality across all models. In Model 1, the continuous PALBI variable showed a hazard ratio (HR) of 1.93 (95% CI 1.689–2.205), with quartile comparisons revealing progressively increasing risk (Q2: HR 1.701, 95% CI 1.359–2.129; Q3: 1.795, 1.437–2.244; Q4: 2.656, 2.148–3.283). These associations persisted in Model 2 (continuous PALBI HR 1.961, 95% CI 1.712–2.247; Q2: 1.672, 1.335–2.095; Q3: 1.74, 1.392–2.176; Q4: 2.616, 2.115–3.237) and Model 3 after full adjustment (continuous PALBI HR 1.423, 95% CI 1.228–1.65; Q2: 1.37, 1.09–1.724; Q3: 1.381, 1.098–1.737; Q4: 1.72, 1.371–2.158), with all P-values < 0.05. A significant dose–response trend was observed (P for trend < 0.001).

Similarly, logistic regression analysis revealed significant associations between elevated PALBI scores and in-hospital mortality. Model 1 showed an odds ratio (OR) of 2.32 (95% CI 1.866–2.908) for the continuous PALBI variable, with quartile comparisons demonstrating escalating risk (Q2: OR 1.88, 95% CI 1.297–2.754; Q3: 1.98, 1.374–2.903; Q4: 3.57, 2.530–5.120). These relationships were maintained in Model 2 (continuous PALBI OR 2.39, 95% CI 1.912–3.008; Q2: 1.88, 1.295–2.769; Q3: 1.95, 1.347–2.866; Q4: 3.62, 2.560–5.222) and Model 3 after comprehensive adjustment (continuous PALBI OR 1.35, 95% CI 1.034–1.770; Q4 vs Q1: OR 1.70, 95% CI 1.140–2.567), all with P < 0.05. The consistent dose–response pattern (P for trend < 0.001) was observed. To further explore the robustness of our findings across different stroke etiologies, we conducted subgroup analyses based on stroke subtypes. The results are presented in Supplementary Tables S5, S6, and S7. In patients with ischemic stroke (n = 1708), higher PALBI scores were significantly associated with increased risks of both 360-day all-cause mortality and in-hospital mortality, consistent with the findings in the overall cohort (Table S5). Similarly, for hemorrhagic stroke patients (n = 400), PALBI score also demonstrated a significant association with both 360-day all-cause mortality and in-hospital mortality (Table S6). However, in the unclassified stroke subgroup (n = 147), the associations between PALBI score and both mortality outcomes did not reach statistical significance (Table S7).

Predictive performance of PALBI score and machine learning models for mortality risk assessment

Mediation analysis

To quantify the pathways linking the PALBI score to clinical outcomes, we calculated the Average Causal Mediation Effect (ACME) and the Proportion Mediated for potential mediators. For 1-year all-cause mortality, the analysis revealed that: (1) Systemic Inflammation (Sepsis) mediated approximately 12.8% of the total association (95% CI: 7.8 – 19.0%, P < 0.001); (2) Infection (Pneumonia) mediated 9.71% of the total effect (95% CI: 5.75 – 15.43%, P < 0.001); and (3) Coagulation (INR) mediated 6.16% (95% CI: − 2.03 to 10.94%, P = 0.002). Regarding in-hospital mortality, the mediation patterns were similar but with a notably stronger role for sepsis: (1) Sepsis accounted for 17.9% of the total effect (95% CI: 11.6 – 26.85%, P < 0.001); (2) Pneumonia mediated 7.90% (95% CI: 3.87 – 13.55%, P < 0.001); and (3) INR mediated 9.26% (95% CI: −3.22 to 16.13%, P = 0.002). Although these indirect pathways were statistically significant, the Proportion Mediated for each generally remained below 20%, indicating that the majority of the PALBI score’s prognostic value corresponds to the direct effect (representing the residual association not mediated by these acute complications). While we hypothesize that this residual effect may reflect baseline physiological reserve and unmeasured systemic frailty, it may also encompass unmeasured confounders or other biological pathways. Rather than being fully explained by these specific complications. Detailed results are shown in Supplementary Table S8 and Fig. S3.

External validation in the eICU cohort

A total of 4729 stroke patients from the eICU database were included in the external validation analysis. The baseline characteristics of the eICU cohort are summarized in Table S4. Discussion

This study using information from the MIMIC-IV database about people who had strokes shows that there is something special about how the platelets, albumin, and bilirubin work together–called the PALBI score–when it comes to understanding if someone might die because of their illness or during their time in the hospital. And this important discovery confirms just how vital liver function status is for stroke prognosis and also lines up with past studies that connected liver problems to bad results after having a stroke12. As a new liver function assessment indicator, the PALBI score is constantly obtaining more proof.

PALBI score which consists of platelet count, serum albumin, and bilirubin levels provides a complete evaluation of liver function due to its multi-parameter structure. It has proven to have better prognosis than the conventional Child–Pugh Scoring in HCC patients13, and it’s been applied clinically in different diseases; for example, there is a strong link between it and 30-day mortality in acute respiratory distress syndrome patients20. Although we utilized quartile-based categorization to capture the distribution within our cohort, it is worth noting that the original PALBI grading system defines scores $ > − 2.09$ as Grade 3, indicating severe liver dysfunction. In our study, patients in the highest quartiles generally overlapped with this high-risk category.

Our subgroup analyses according to stroke subtypes give us additional information about how well the PALBI score can predict outcomes. The persistent significant connection between PALBI and mortality among individuals with either ischemic stroke or hemorrhagic stroke shows that it is broadly applicable to the main causes of stroke. It seems that no matter what caused your stroke, the hidden problems with your liver, as shown by the PALBI score, could still make things worse. Regarding unclassified stroke, we observed no significant association. While the relatively small sample size (n = 147) may limit statistical power, it is also possible that the PALBI score has limited prognostic value in this subtype due to its heterogeneous etiology. Future studies with larger groups of particular unclassified stroke kinds would help to see if this holds true. Subtype-specific results make PALBI better as a way to predict how sick someone will get from a stroke. Doctors can think about how well their patient’s liver is working when they look at different kinds of strokes.

Consistent with previous observations, stroke patients with concomitant hepatic indications exhibited significantly higher mortality rates21. Rather than implying a direct causal pathway, this association likely reflects the integral role of the liver in maintaining systemic homeostasis. The PALBI score serves as a composite surrogate marker for broader physiological derangements, capturing the aggregate burden of metabolic disturbances frequent in critical illness22,23 and the intensified systemic stress response often linked to adverse acute stroke trajectories24,25. Additionally, given the liver’s function in hemostasis, the PALBI score effectively captures underlying coagulopathies that coexist with cerebrovascular events26,27, while indirectly reflecting the systemic inflammatory burden, a known prognostic factor in stroke28. These findings align with recent epidemiological evidence linking chronic liver conditions, such as hepatitis B infection9 and metabolic dysfunction, to stroke prognosis29,30. Notably, our subgroup analyses provide novel context to these mechanisms. The observed interaction between pneumonia and the PALBI score may be explained by cirrhosis-associated immune dysfunction (CAID), suggesting that patients with high PALBI scores possess impaired immune surveillance, rendering them more vulnerable to severe outcomes when pneumonia is present. Similarly, this interaction likely reflects a ‘Stress-Reserve Mismatch’. Vasopressor use signals high metabolic demand where hepatic function (e.g., lactate clearance, coagulation) is critical. In this high-stress context, patients with limited reserve (high PALBI) succumb to ‘metabolic crash,’ driving the mortality correlation. Conversely, without vasopressors, physiological demand remains within a compensated range, allowing even frail livers to maintain homeostasis. Thus, PALBI unmasks the ‘vulnerable host’ specifically under hemodynamic stress.

For stroke people who have liver problems too, doctors need to take extra care about heart dangers, how sugar levels work, fat amounts, and blood pressure numbers. As an easy-to-use evaluation method, PALBI score can detect high-risk patients and provide personalized treatment plans. There is a concern that PALBI would just be a stand-in for unadjusted severity (i.e., sepsis, liver disease). We wanted to make it clear that our multivariable Model 3 had taken into account a large number of confounding variables, such as different kinds of severity scores (SOFA, APSIII, SIRS, SAPSII, OASIS, GCS), the Charlson comorbidity index, the existence of sepsis, etc. The ongoing substantial link between PALBI and death even after all these adjustments means that PALBI shows certain liver-related problems that affect the body differently from other sickness, so it tells us about special ways that having a stroke makes someone worse, separate from just how sick they already were. Therefore, our findings have tangible implications for risk stratification rather than direct therapeutic intervention. Given the results of our mediation analysis, the PALBI score should be interpreted primarily as a robust marker of the residual risk unmediated by acute complications. This likely reflects’ systemic physiological collapse’ and baseline frailty, rather than a modifiable causal target. Clinically, an elevated PALBI score identifies a high-risk phenotype characterized by diminished physiological reserve. For these patients, management should focus on ‘preservative supportive care’: (1) enhanced surveillance for non-neurological systemic deterioration; (2) strict avoidance of hepatotoxic medications to prevent further depletion of reserve; and (3) early nutritional optimization. The goal is to identify and protect this vulnerable population from secondary hits, rather than attempting to treat liver dysfunction in isolation.

And also the external validation done with the multi-center eICU database strongly supported what we found before, showing that when people who had strokes got higher PALBI scores, there was a big difference in how many died during their stay at the hospital. This success at an external site further bolsters the generalizability and clinical applicability of the PALBI score for prediction purposes and reduces concerns about single-center biases which improves confidence in our results.

In future studies, we need to focus on some aspects. Firstly, explaining the causal relationship between hepatic dysfunction and stroke outcome, especially the molecular mechanism related to inflammation pathway, oxidative stress response, and coagulation abnormality. Secondly, if we were to develop specific treatments for these mechanisms, such as therapies that target liver inflammation or oxidative stress, it could provide new ways to improve stroke outcomes. This kind of inquiry would result in something that is both theoretically interesting and useful in terms of actual patient care.

Limitations

There are some limits to this study: First, it is a retrospective study based on the MIMIC-IV database, which may be subject to selection bias and unmeasured confounding, even after adjusting for all covariates. Second, though multiple imputation was used on variables with less than 20% missing data, there is still concern for non-random missingness in important lab parameters. Third, due to database limitations, specific causes of death (neurological vs. medical) were not available for post-discharge mortality, preventing specific competing-risk analyses. Fourth, since both MIMIC-IV and eICU-CRD are US-based databases, the generalizability of our findings to other ethnic or geographic populations remains to be established. Fifth, differences in available covariates between the two databases precluded a completely identical adjustment strategy, which may introduce potential bias in the comparative analysis. Finally, a notable limitation is the absence of stroke-specific severity scales (NIHSS, mRS) in the MIMIC-IV database, which precluded direct comparison with PALBI. Although we adjusted for the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) and general severity scores (SOFA, APSIII) to control for disease severity, future prospective validation against these specific neurological metrics is required.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study shows that the PALBI score is independently correlated with 1 year all cause mortality and in hospital mortality in stroke patients. The fact that results from the MIMIC-IV and eICU databases were consistent and that there was good performance in the machine learning models suggests that the PALBI score could be a useful prognostic tool in the form of a practical risk stratification system for use in stroke care. Finally, although our study confirmed the PALBI score’s validity across US institutions, its global generalizability remains unverified. Future studies should use Internal–External Cross-Validation (IECV) or Leave-One-Site-Out (LOSO) in diverse international cohorts (e.g., Europe, Asia) to test the score’s broader applicability.
