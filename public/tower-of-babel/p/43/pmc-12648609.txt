The changing landscape of HIV epidemiology and management

Introduction

Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) is a retrovirus that causes severe immune dysfunction through depletion of CD4+ T-lymphocytes. Left untreated, death is inevitable from opportunistic infections and malignancy. In 2023, 39.9 million people were living with HIV globally and 630,000 people died of HIV-related disease.1 Although HIV cannot be cured, there are highly effective treatments that have saved millions of lives. The first antiretroviral (ARV) approved by the US Food and Drug Administration for the treatment of HIV was zidovudine (AZT) in 1987.2 Since then, there has been enormous progress in HIV treatment. Antiretroviral therapy (ART) uses multiple highly active antiretroviral drugs, transforming HIV from a fatal disease into a controllable chronic condition. In the pre-ART era, few people lived beyond about 10 years after seroconversion.3 Now, many populations living with HIV have life expectancies only a few years below that of the general population.4

HIV can be transmitted sexually, via blood (eg transfusion or sharing of injecting equipment), or vertically from mother to child. HIV binds to CD4 via the gp120 glycopeptide, and to a co-receptor (either CXCR4 or CCR5). After entering the cell, reverse transcriptase converts the HIV RNA genome into a double-stranded DNA copy, which is integrated into the host chromosome by viral integrase. Host transcription and translation machinery then produces a viral polypeptide chain from the integrated provirus template, which is cleaved into component proteins by viral protease. Reverse transcriptase, integrase and protease are key targets of ARVs (Fig. 1).

In this narrative review, we describe the changing landscape of HIV epidemiology and treatment, from an international and UK perspective. While research into HIV vaccines and gene editing therapies are ongoing, here we focus on ART and the latest developments in long-acting antiretrovirals.

Changing epidemiology

In 2023, 65% of the 39.9 million people living with HIV were in sub-Saharan Africa; the greatest burden was in eastern and southern Africa.1 A total of 30.7 million people were receiving ART, and accordingly the number of HIV-related deaths had reduced by 69% since its peak in 2004,1 from over 2 million to 630,000 (Fig. 2). This was associated with a rebound in life expectancy in sub-Saharan Africa from 56.3 years in 2010 to 61.1 years in 2023.

The epidemiology of HIV in the UK is changing (Fig. 3). People living with HIV are living longer, reflecting an ageing population and the success of ART; heterosexual sex has overtaken sex between men as the most common mechanism of HIV transmission; and there are changing patterns of migration. In 2023, there were 100,063 people accessing HIV care in England, a year-on-year increase from 2020.5 51% of people accessing HIV care in England were aged 50 years or older, compared to 27% in 2013. Those aged 50 years or older were more likely to present late (defined as CD4 count below 350 cells/mm3 of blood within 91 days of diagnosis and no evidence of a recent infection), which was associated with poorer outcomes: people diagnosed late in England in 2022 were 10 times more likely to die within a year of their diagnosis, compared to those diagnosed promptly.5 The rise in older people living with HIV emphasises the importance of addressing age-related chronic conditions in these patients, particularly as HIV increases the risk of cardiovascular disease.6

There were 6,008 new HIV diagnoses in England in 2023, a 51% increase from 2022, with over half (53%) previously diagnosed abroad. The number of new HIV diagnoses first made in England increased by 15% from 2022 to 2023. Of the new HIV diagnoses made in England in 2023, nearly half (49%) were exposed through sex between men and women; 29% were among men exposed through sex between men; just 2% were from injecting drug use, 1% by vertical transmission, and 1% through exposed blood products. Just under two-thirds (61%) of new diagnoses in England in 2023 were individuals born outside the UK – an increase of 52% from 2022.

Antiretrovirals

The goal of ART is to fully suppress viral load to clinically undetectable levels and achieve restoration of physiological CD4 T-cell counts. Combining multiple ARVs of different classes forms the cornerstone of ART, driving down HIV RNA copy number and reducing the risk of resistance.7 A minimum of two drugs from two ARV classes are required for an effective regimen. ART regimens typically comprise a nucleoside/nucleotide reverse transcriptase inhibitor (NRTI) backbone, plus either an integrase strand transfer inhibitor (INSTI), a non-nucleoside/nucleotide reverse transcriptase inhibitor (NNRTI) or a protease inhibitor (PI).7 Conventionally, three ARVs have been used: an NRTI backbone comprising two NRTIs, plus a third agent – either NNRTI, INSTI or PI. For example, the combination of two NRTIs – tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF) and lamivudine (3TC) – and dolutegravir (DTG, an INSTI), is now widely used as first-line therapy in sub-Saharan Africa. Clinical trials have demonstrated the safety of two-drug ART regimens for maintaining viral suppression in selected patients (eg8, 9, 10).

ARVs have become better tolerated, with reduced side effect profile and reduced intensity of monitoring required. Single-tablet combinations have been developed to reduce pill burden, including two- and three-drug fixed-dose ART combinations, although their cost can be prohibitive. ART is now usually initiated at the time of HIV diagnosis or soon after, as clinical trials demonstrated superior outcomes for early ART initiation compared to delaying ART until CD4 T-cell count was reduced.11 To maintain viral control, ART is continued without interruption even after T-cell count recovery.12

As well as treating the individual, ART also greatly reduces the risk of infecting others. The discovery of ‘treatment as prevention’ by the HPTN 052 study group, analysing serodiscordant couples,13 was the Science journal ‘Breakthrough of the Year’ in 2011. If HIV viral load is undetectable, the risk of sexual transmission of HIV is effectively zero.14,15 This fact is the basis of the public health message that ‘undetectable = untransmittable’, or ‘U=U’. ART also dramatically reduces the risk of vertical transmission of HIV. In a large French cohort (ANRS-EPF), vertical transmission decreased from 1.1% in 2000–2005 to 0.2% in 2011–2017 as ART coverage expanded.16,17 For each 10% increment in ART coverage, the rate of new HIV diagnoses in Canada decreased by 8%.18 The centrality of ART to the global response to HIV is highlighted by the UNAIDS ‘95-95-95’ targets: for 95% of people living with HIV to know their status, for 95% of those testing positive to be on ART, and for 95% of those on ART to achieve viral suppression. In 2023, the global status was 86%, 89% and 93%, respectively.1

Drug resistance is a potential threat to HIV control and can be transmitted or acquired during therapy. HIV has a high mutation rate, due to a combination of reverse transcriptase, the action of host APOBEC innate antiretroviral restriction factors that drive cytidine deamination, and host DNA-dependent RNA polymerase activity.19 Over the course of infection, HIV can evolve resistance to the host immune response20 and to ARVs.21 Genetic recombination, arising when reverse transcriptase switches between genomic templates during replication, can potentially bring different drug resistance mutations together to generate multidrug-resistant viruses.22 Access to molecular diagnostics for HIV viral load monitoring and viral genetic sequencing for drug resistance mutation detection is therefore an important component of HIV care.23

Drug interactions with ARVs can be challenging. Special caution should be exercised when co-administering ARVs with anticoagulation, anti-TB drugs, antimalarials, proton pump inhibitors, methadone and certain cardiac drugs, among others. The Liverpool HIV drug interaction website24 is an excellent resource for healthcare providers to check for potential drug–drug interactions. Drug interactions can also be exploited for therapeutic benefit. ‘Boosted’ ARVs combine an active agent with a cytochrome p450 enzyme inhibitor, to increase the levels of active agent and prolong its half-life. Examples include atazanavir/ ritonavir and darunavir/cobicistat.

New horizons: long-acting injectables

Long-acting injectables and infusions are designed to produce a sustained release of the active compound over weeks or months. There are three potential advantages: first, patients are not required to take tablets every day; second, some of the newer long-acting drugs can be used in the setting of treatment-experienced patients with challenging drug resistance; third, long-acting ARVs are well-suited to HIV prevention as pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP). Four long-acting injectables/infusions currently available are cabotegravir, rilpivirine, lenacapavir and ibalizumab; these are discussed further below.

Cabotegravir plus rilpivirine was the first long-acting injectable combination available in the UK in 2020. Cabotegravir is an INSTI and rilpivirine is an NNRTI. Therapy comprises 2-monthly intramuscular (IM) injections and is suitable for people living with HIV with a fully suppressed HIV-1 viral load and no evidence of viral resistance or failure to INSTIs or NNRTIs. The half-life of injectable cabotegravir is 5.6–11.5 weeks, and that of rilpivirine is 13–28 weeks. The ATLAS study was a phase three, open-label, multi-centre, non-inferiority trial comparing standard oral ARV regimens vs injectable cabotegravir/ rilpivirine for maintaining virological suppression.25 The trial recruited ART-experienced persons who were virally suppressed for at least 6 months. Participants were randomised to either continue daily oral ART or to switch to injectable cabotegravir/rilpivirine (308 participants in each group). At week 48, there was detectable HIV viral load (>50 copies/mL) in 1.6% of the injectable group vs 1% of the daily oral ART group, meeting the trial’s criteria for non-inferiority. Adverse events were more common in the injectable group, such as injection-site pain (75% of participants). However, these events were generally mild, with serious adverse events reported in less than 5% of both groups.

Lenacapavir is a novel, first-in-class inhibitor of HIV-1 capsid function. The HIV capsid core is a conical structure comprised of viral capsid proteins and housing the viral genome within mature virions. Lenacapavir binds directly to the interface between capsid protein subunits and interferes with multiple stages of the viral life cycle.26,27 There is no known cross-resistance with other ARV classes, and the pharmacokinetic profile enables 6-monthly dosing. In 2024, Science named lenacapavir the 2024 ‘Breakthrough of the Year’.

The CAPELLA study was a phase three, multi-centre, two-cohort study investigating the efficacy of lenacapavir for reducing viral load in patients with multidrug-resistant HIV in whom their current regimen was failing.28 Patients with multidrug resistance involving at least three of the four established ARV classes and an HIV viral load ≥400 copies/mL for at least 8 weeks were eligible. 72 patients were enrolled and divided equally into two cohorts, based on the change in viral load between screening and cohort selection: cohort one had stable viraemia despite ART; cohort two demonstrated a reduction in viral load. The background ART regimen was optimised and continued in both groups. In cohort one, 36 participants were randomised 2:1 to receive either oral lenacapavir loading followed by 6-monthly SC lenacapavir; or placebo followed by oral lenacapavir loading then 6-monthly SC lenacapavir. 88% (21/24) of participants receiving the initial loading with oral lenacapavir achieved a decrease of at least 0.5 log HIV RNA copies/mL by day 15, compared with 17% (2/12) of those receiving placebo for the initial period. All 36 participants in cohort two received oral lenacapavir loading followed by 6-monthly SC lenacapavir. At week 26, HIV was undetectable (<50 copies/mL) in 81% of cohort one and 83% of cohort two. No serious adverse events were reported. Again, injection site reactions were common (63%). Capsid mutations associated with decreased lenacapavir susceptibility were detected in eight patients. These data suggest that lenacapavir could be considered for adults in whom their current ART regimen is failing who are heavily treatment-experienced and have multidrug-resistant HIV-1.

Ibalizumab is a humanised IgG4 monoclonal antibody with a novel mechanism of action, binding to human CD4 and inhibiting viral entry into the host cell.29,30 Ibalizumab binds to domain two of the extracellular portion of CD4, inhibiting post-attachment steps required for viral entry but without disrupting human immune function. Ibalizumab is administered intravenously (IV) every 2 weeks and possesses potent and broad anti-HIV-1 activity.29 In a single-group, open-label, phase three trial, adults were recruited with multidrug-resistant HIV-1 in whom multiple previous ARV therapies had failed and who had a viral load >1,000 copies/mL.31 Participants received ibalizumab plus an individualised optimised ART regimen. 40 patients were enrolled and 31 completed the study. At week 25, patients who received ibalizumab plus an optimised ART regimen had a mean decrease of 1.6 log copies/mL, and 43% achieved an undetectable viral load (<50 copies/mL). Of the 10 patients with virological failure or rebound, nine were found to have reduced susceptibility to ibalizumab compared to baseline. In eight of nine of these patients, loss of predicted N-linked glycosylation sites in gp120 was the primary genetic change associated with reduced susceptibility. There was a favourable safety profile with no drug interaction issues and no risk of cross-resistance. The indication is similar to lenacapavir; however, ibalizumab is not currently available in the UK. For both lenacapavir and ibalizumab, larger studies would be helpful to confirm their clinical utility and to further explore the potential signal of reduced susceptibility.

Combining long-acting antiretrovirals for HIV treatment?

Recent small-scale studies have investigated whether twice-yearly administration could be viable for HIV treatment. The combination of lenacapavir plus teropavimab and zinlirvimab (LTZ) has shown promise. Teropavimab and zinlirvimab are broadly neutralising monoclonal antibodies that synergistically target different conserved sites of the HIV envelope, blocking viral attachment and fusion. Teropavimab targets the CD4-binding site of gp120, while zinlirvimab targets the HIV-1 envelope V3 glycan supersite. A randomised, blind, phase 1b, proof-of-concept study has evaluated the safety of LTZ32 in patients established on oral ART with well-controlled HIV and confirmed susceptibility to teropavimab and zinlirvimab. Participants stopped oral ART and received one dose of SC lenacapavir plus an oral loading dose, with intravenous teropavimab and zinlirvimab given on day 1, randomised 1:1 to different doses of zinlirvimab. 21 participants were randomised and one person withdrew from the study. No serious adverse events occurred. 85% (17/20) reported injection-site reactions. One individual (on the lower zinlirvimab dose) had viral rebound, which was suppressed with resumed oral ART.

A phase two study of LTZ in virally suppressed adults is evaluating 6-monthly LTZ infusions vs. stable oral ART; findings were presented at the 2025 Conference on Retroviruses and Opportunistic Infections (CROI).33 The primary endpoint was the proportion of participants with HIV-1 RNA ≥50 copies/mL at week 26, with 96% (51/53) of the LTZ cohort remaining virally suppressed, vs 96% (26/27) of the comparator oral ART arm. No serious adverse events were noted. LTZ was granted FDA breakthrough therapy designation in January 2025.

Next-generation HIV prophylaxis

PrEP refers to people who are HIV negative taking ARVs pre-emptively to reduce their risk of acquiring HIV, and is most beneficial for people at higher risk of HIV exposure. PrEP using oral ARVs such as TDF with emtricitabine (TDF/FTC) or tenofovir alafenamide fumarate (TAF) with emtricitabine (TAF/FTC) has been shown to be effective at preventing HIV transmission.34, 35, 36, 37, 38 The PURPOSE studies evaluated the efficacy of lenacapavir for preventing HIV acquisition through sex. PURPOSE-1 evaluated lenacapavir for PrEP in 5,388 young women and adolescent girls.39 Participants were randomised 2:2:1 to one of three groups: (1) SC lenacapavir twice yearly plus placebo TAF/FTC tablets; (2) SC lenacapavir twice yearly plus placebo TDF/FTC tablets; (3) SC lenacapavir twice yearly placebo injections plus active TAF/FTC or TDF/FTC tablets. The PURPOSE-two study evaluated lenacapavir for PrEP in 3,265 men and gender-diverse persons randomised 2:1 to: (1) SC lenacapavir twice yearly, or (2) daily oral TDF/FTC tablets.40 Both trials demonstrated that lenacapavir was superior to oral TDF/FTC or TAF/FTC for preventing HIV.

Novel formulations of lenacapavir could potentially push PrEP dosing out even further, to once yearly. A phase one, open-label, pharmacokinetic study randomised 40 participants without HIV to two formulations of IM lenacapavir given yearly: 5 g with 5% ethanol or with 10% ethanol.41 Lenacapavir concentrations at week 52 following the IM administration exceeded the median trough concentrations of twice-yearly SC lenacapavir at week 26. Clinical trial data are required, but this finding holds promise that a once-yearly IM injection could provide effective protection against HIV infection.

Potential risks of long-acting HIV medications include logistical issues for delivery, especially in the developing world; access and infrastructure requirements; injection-site reactions; other adverse reactions to drugs with long half-lives; concern whether patients may miss doses; and the potential for developing drug resistance and/or viral rebound. Further research and mitigation strategies are needed to address these for clinical implementation.

Concluding remarks: HIV past, present and future

There has been tremendous progress in HIV management over the last 40 years. HIV epidemic control is on the horizon in many countries. These trends are illustrated by Botswana: in 2003, there were an estimated 18,000 HIV-related deaths in Botswana; in 2023, this had reduced by almost 80%, to 3,900.42 Treatments are becoming easier and better tolerated with combined tablet formulations. Long-acting injectables and infusions are already available, with increasing data suggesting that they could be viable options, at least for certain patient groups, including for multidrug-resistant HIV. Larger clinical studies are needed to further evaluate these emerging treatments and their implementation. Ensuring affordability for low- and middle- income countries, where the burden of HIV is greatest, is essential.

Continuing the progress made against HIV relies on sustained funding. As of 2023, five major donors provided over 90% of international HIV funding: the USA (72.6%), UK (9.1%), France (4.1%), Germany (2.9%) and the Netherlands (2.4%).43 As of February 2025, each of these countries has announced substantial cuts. The US president’s Emergency Plan for AIDS Relief (PEPFAR) has been a key funder since its launch in 2003. According to HIV.gov, the PEPFAR programme has invested over US$100 billion in the global HIV response and saved over 25 million lives.44 PEPFAR can be considered one of the most effective and successful global health intervention programmes in the last 20 years.

HIV funding at the global level remains highly vulnerable following the recent temporary suspension of PEPFAR funding. Although partial waivers were granted, the situation remains uncertain and the stakes are extremely high. Modelling has suggested that complete withdrawal of PEPFAR funding without any mitigations would, by 2030, result in an additional 10.75 million new HIV infections and 2.93 million additional HIV-related deaths.43 The cuts risk jeopardising decades of progress, disrupting essential health services and further exacerbating health inequality among vulnerable and marginalised communities.

In summary, ART can be considered one of the greatest success stories in the history of medicine, and innovations are ongoing in this dynamic field. For the majority of people living with HIV, severe HIV-related illness is entirely preventable using ART. As the UNAIDS 2024 report states, ‘the global AIDS response is at a crossroads: success or failure will be determined by which path leaders take today’. Hopefully, humanity will find the means and the commitment to continue, and indeed accelerate, the progress made over the last 20 years, to save more people from preventable harm and death caused by HIV.
