KRT80 promotes colon adenocarcinoma progression via stat3 pathway activation and immune microenvironment remodeling
Authors: Lu Nie, Yu Nie, Ruiyang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundColorectal adenocarcinoma (COAD) is a leading cause of cancer-related death globally, necessitating the identification of new molecular drivers and therapeutic targets. Keratin 80 (KRT80) is an intermediate filament protein whose dysregulation has been linked to malignancy in various cancers, yet its role in COAD pathogenesis is poorly understood. Given the critical need to understand COAD progression, this study was designed to determine the expression and prognostic significance of KRT80 in COAD, its impact on the tumor immune microenvironment, and its functional effects on cancer cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, along with the potential signaling pathways involved.MethodsKRT80 expression and its prognostic value in COAD were analyzed using TIMER database, UALCAN database, GEPIA database, and Kaplan-Meier plotter. Immune cell infiltration was evaluated via CIBERSORT and single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA). Somatic mutations were analyzed from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) data. Functional enrichment analysis [Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)] was performed on KRT80-associated genes. In vitro, KRT80 protein levels were examined in COAD cell lines by Western blot (WB). Stable knockdown and overexpression models were created. Functional assays [Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8), Transwell, wound healing] assessed proliferation, migration, and invasion. WB measured STAT3 pathway activity.ResultsKRT80 was significantly overexpressed in COAD tissues and high expression correlated with poorer overall, first progression, and post-progression survival (PPS). KRT80 expression positively correlated with M0 macrophage and activated mast cell infiltration, and negatively with resting memory CD4+ T cells and naive B cells. Although KRT80 itself was not mutated, associated genes were enriched in pathways like Wnt signaling. In vitro, KRT80 knockdown inhibited cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, while its overexpression promoted these effects. KRT80 was found to modulate STAT3 phosphorylation.ConclusionsKRT80 is overexpressed in COAD and predicts poor prognosis. It promotes tumor cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, likely through activating the STAT3 signaling pathway, and correlates with an altered immune microenvironment. KRT80 represents a potential prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target for COAD.Keywords: Colon adenocarcinoma (COAD), tumor immune microenvironment, Keratin 80 (KRT80), cell experiments, Western blotting

Key findings

• Keratin 80 (KRT80) is overexpressed in colorectal adenocarcinoma (COAD) and correlates with poor survival. It promotes proliferation, migration, and invasion via STAT3 activation and alters the immune microenvironment.

What is known and what is new?

• KRT80 has been known as an oncogene in other cancers.

• This study newly defines its prognostic role, pro-tumor functions, and STAT3 mechanism specifically in COAD.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• KRT80 is a potential prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target for COAD.

• Research should now focus on developing strategies to inhibit KRT80/STAT3 signaling.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) poses a significant health challenge globally (1), being the third most prevalent cancer diagnosis and the second foremost cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide. Among the various CRC variants, colon adenocarcinoma (COAD) is recognized as the predominant histological subtype (2), arising from the glandular epithelium of the colon. Despite substantial progress in screening methodologies, surgical techniques, and therapeutic options over recent decades, the outlook for individuals diagnosed with advanced or metastatic COAD remains bleak, with a mere 5-year survival rate of approximately 14% (3). This concerning statistic highlights the imperative for a more profound comprehension of the molecular mechanisms underpinning the disease, which is critical for enhancing early diagnostic capabilities and formulating more effective, personalized treatment approaches (4). The advancement of COAD is a multifaceted, stepwise phenomenon characterized by the incremental accumulation of both genetic and epigenetic modifications (5). The classical adenoma-carcinoma sequence model, initially articulated by Fearon and Vogelstein, delineates the transition from normal colonic epithelium to invasive adenocarcinoma via a succession of distinct histological and molecular phases (6). Significant mutations in pivotal genes such as APC, KRAS, TP53, and SMAD4 are often associated with the disruption of essential cellular pathways that regulate processes including cell proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, and genomic integrity (7,8). In conjunction with these genetic alterations, epigenetic changes, notably the CpG island methylator phenotype (CIMP) (9) and microsatellite instability (MSI) (10) resulting from deficient DNA mismatch repair (dMMR) (11), are instrumental in contributing to tumor heterogeneity and influencing clinical outcomes. The classification of colorectal adenocarcinoma (COAD) using bulk genomic and epigenetic features has yielded important insights into prognosis and treatment predictions (12-14); however, significant variability in patient outcomes persists within these broad categories. This inconsistency underscores the limitations of depending solely on group-level characteristics and highlights the urgent need for more precise and detailed biomarkers (15). In this regard, the study of individual gene alterations has become increasingly significant (16). Single-gene biomarkers are particularly promising because they have clear roles in the mechanisms of cancer development (17), are relatively easy to detect in clinical environments, and can be directly targeted for treatment. Therefore, identifying and validating strong single-gene biomarkers is essential for progressing towards truly personalized medicine in COAD (18), with the ultimate goal of enhancing survival rates and improving the quality of life for patients battling this challenging disease.

Keratin 80 (KRT80) belongs to the type II keratin gene family and encodes intermediate filament proteins that are essential structural components of the epithelial cytoskeleton (19). These proteins are vital for maintaining cellular integrity, providing mechanical strength, and regulating important processes such as cell signaling, proliferation, and apoptosis. In the context of cancer, KRT80 has been identified as an oncogene that is upregulated in various malignancies, including colorectal, breast, ovarian, and lung cancers (20-24). Mechanistically, KRT80 enhances tumor aggressiveness by promoting cell proliferation, migration, and invasion (25). It primarily activates the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway (26,27), which inhibits apoptosis and allows for uncontrolled cell growth. Additionally, KRT80 acts as a strong inducer of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) by downregulating epithelial markers such as E-cadherin and upregulating mesenchymal markers like N-cadherin and vimentin (28,29), enabling cancer cells to gain migratory and invasive capabilities. Beyond these direct effects on cells, KRT80 also plays a role in creating an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (25,30,31). It is associated with reduced infiltration of CD8+ T cells and increased presence of immunosuppressive cells (32,33), such as myeloid-derived suppressor cells and regulatory T cells (34). This is achieved through the regulation of chemokine secretion (35), which creates a barrier against cytotoxic lymphocytes and promotes a pro-tumor environment. Furthermore, KRT80 expression has been linked to resistance against chemotherapy and targeted therapies in certain cancer types (24,30), underscoring its significant impact on treatment outcomes.

Nevertheless, our current knowledge of KRT80 expression, regulation, and function in COAD is still limited. In our study, we aimed to investigate the expression, prognostic significance, function, and mechanisms of KRT80 in COAD. Multi-database analysis revealed that KRT80 is significantly overexpressed in COAD and is strongly associated with poor patient overall survival (OS), first progression, and post-progression survival (PPS). KRT80 expression correlates with tumor immune cell infiltration but not with tumor mutation burden. Functional enrichment analysis suggested that KRT80 is involved in multiple key biological processes and signaling pathways. In vitro experiments demonstrated that KRT80 promotes the proliferation, migration, and invasion of colon cancer cells. Mechanistically, KRT80 may exert its oncogenic effects through activation of the STAT3 signaling pathway. We present this article in accordance with the MDAR reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2025-aw-2355/rc).

Methods

Data sources

The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) (36) database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) offers comprehensive molecular and clinical data for patients with COAD, such as downloadable mRNA expression profiles, summaries of genetic mutations, and anonymized patient histories. In this study, we utilized a dataset containing transcriptomic information from 505 samples, which included 41 normal tissue specimens and 464 tumor tissue specimens. To identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between these normal and cancerous tissues, we performed an analysis using the Limma package (version 3.64.3) in R. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Analysis of gene expression data via the UALCAN database

A comprehensive online analysis investigating differential gene expression between cancerous and non-cancerous tissues was conducted utilizing the UALCAN database (http://ualcan.path.uab.edu/) (37), leveraging data sourced from TCGA. This database facilitated the categorization of patients according to their gene expression profiles, enabling a comparative assessment of the survival outcomes among various expression cohorts.

Analysis of the TIMER database

To investigate the differential expression of genes between tumor and precancerous tissues, we utilized TIMER (http://timer.cistrome.org/) (38), a user-friendly web application designed for the analysis of large-scale cancer genomics data from TCGA. This tool enabled a systematic comparison of gene expression profiles between tumor samples and matched precancerous samples from the same patients.

Analysis of the Kaplan-Meier plotter database

Based on the survival data of COAD patients, we conducted survival analysis using the Kaplan-Meier survival curve in the online database (https://www.kmplot.com/analysis/). Patients with KRT80 expression levels above the median were defined as the high expression group, while those below the median were defined as the low expression group. Survival curves were plotted, hazard ratios were calculated, and confidence intervals were estimated to evaluate the impact of KRT80 on the prognosis of COAD patients.

Profiling of the immune landscape via bioinformatics analysis

The composition of 22 distinct human immune cell types was determined through the LM22 gene signature in conjunction with the CIBERSORTx algorithm (39). By employing single-sample gene enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) (40) within the R package GSVA (version 2.2.0), we assessed the degree of infiltration of 28 immune cell types (41).

Landscape of somatic mutations in COAD

Tumor mutational burden (TMB) is defined as the quantity of somatic mutations per megabase of DNA (Mut/Mb), encompassing various genetic alterations such as insertions, deletions, single-base substitutions, and chromosomal translocations (42).

Functional enrichment analysis

Based on KRT80 expression levels, TCGA patients were categorized into high-expression (top 50%) and low-expression (bottom 50%) groups. DEGs between these two groups were identified using the “DESeq2” (43) R package (v1.48.1), with a significance threshold set at adjusted P<0.05 and |log2 fold change (FC)| >1. To further explore the functional implications of KRT80 in COAD, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed using the “ClusterProfiler” (44) R package (v4.16.0), with terms considered significantly enriched at P<0.05.

The TCGA patients were divided into low (0–50%) and high (51–100%) groups based on KRT80 expression levels. The R package DESeq2 (version 1.48.1) was used to identify DEGs between the high-KRT80 and low-KRT80 expression groups. The threshold for significance was set to an adjusted P value <0.05 and |log2 FC| >1. To further investigate the role of KRT80 in COAD, we used the ClusterProfiler R package (version 4.16.0) to identify enriched GO terms and KEGG pathways, and GSEA with P<0.05.

Cell culture and transfection

Human CRC cell lines (HT-29, HCT116, SW620), mouse colon cancer cell line CT26, and normal colon epithelial cell line NCM460 were cultured in high-glucose DMEM medium. All cell lines were purchased from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China), and cell identity was authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling to ensure no cross-contamination. The culture medium was supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Bioind, Israel) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. All cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 ℃ with 5% CO2.

Small interfering RNAs targeting KRT80 (si-KRT80 #1, si-KRT80 #2) and negative control siRNA (si-NC), as well as KRT80 overexpression plasmids (OE-KRT80 #1, OE-KRT80 #2) and empty vector control (pcDNA3.1), were constructed and synthesized by Shanghai GenePharma Co., Ltd. Transfection was performed using Lipofectamine 2000 (YEASEN, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were harvested 48 hours post-transfection for subsequent functional studies.

Polymerase chain reaction (PCR)

Using a high-fidelity DNA polymerase (such as Phusion High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase), cDNA from colon cancer cells was used as the template, and PCR amplification was performed with specific primers. Primer sequences were as follows: STAT3 forward primer (5'-ACTGCCAACAGATGGCCC-3') and STAT3 reverse primer (5'-CACCATGTCCTTGGGTCCAG-3'), KRT80 forward primer (5'-AAGCTCACCTGAGCCTAACAG-3') and KRT80 reverse primer (5'-GGGAGCCAGCATTTCCAAGT-3'), GAPDH forward primer (5'-TGCAACCGGGAAGGAAATGA-3') and GAPDH reverse primer (5'-GCCCAATACGACCAAATCAGA-3'). The PCR reaction system included template DNA, primers, deoxynucleotide triphosphates (dNTPs), buffer, and DNA polymerase. The reaction conditions were set as follows: initial denaturation at 95 ℃ for 3 minutes, followed by 30–35 cycles (denaturation at 95 ℃ for 30 seconds, annealing at a temperature set based on the primer Tm, and extension at 72 ℃ for a duration determined by the product length), and a final extension at 72 ℃ for 5–10 minutes. The PCR products were verified for size by agarose gel electrophoresis, purified, and recovered for subsequent experiments.

Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

Transfected HT-29 and HCT116 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1×104 cells per well, with 100 µL of culture medium added to each well. Three replicate wells were set up for each group, along with blank control wells. Detection was performed at 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4 days of culture, respectively. At each time point, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent (Cat. C0037, Beyotime) was added to each well, followed by incubation at 37 ℃ with 5% CO2 for 2 hours. The absorbance [optical density (OD) value] at a wavelength of 450 nm was measured using a BioTek Synergy H1 microplate reader (BioTek). A cell proliferation curve was plotted with culture time as the x-axis and OD value as the y-axis to analyze the effect of KRT80 on the proliferation of colon cells.

Transwell migration and invasion experiment

Transwell inserts (pore size: 8 µm, Cat.3422, Corning) were placed in a 24-well plate, and 600 µL of Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) medium containing 10% FBS was added to the lower chamber for equilibration over 30 minutes. Transfected HT-29 and HCT116 cells were collected, washed twice with serum-free DMEM, and resuspended in serum-free DMEM to adjust the cell concentration to 5×104 cells/mL. Then, 200 µL of the cell suspension was added to the upper chamber, with three replicate wells set for each group. After 24 hours of incubation at 37 ℃ with 5% CO2, the Transwell inserts were removed. Non-migrated cells in the upper chamber were gently wiped off with a cotton swab. The migrated cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 minutes, stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 20 minutes, and washed three times with PBS before air-drying. The number of migrated cells was counted in five randomly selected fields per insert under an optical microscope (200× magnification), and the average value was calculated to compare differences in cell migration ability among groups. The experimental procedure for the invasion assay was essentially the same as that for the migration assay.

Cell scratch test

Transfected HT-29 and HCT116 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 5×105 cells per well and cultured until reaching 90–100% confluency. A uniform wound was created by scratching the monolayer vertically with a sterile 200 µL pipette tip, followed by three PBS washes to remove detached cells. Serum-free DMEM medium was then added. Images of the same field were captured at 0, 24, and 48 h post-scratching using an optical microscope (100× objective). The wound width was measured with ImageJ software, and the wound healing rate was calculated as: Healing rate = (0 h wound width − wound width at each time point)/0 h wound width ×100%. This methodology was used to analyze the effect of KRT80 on the migratory ability of colon cancer cells.

Western blot (WB)

WB was performed to detect the expression level of KRT80 protein in colon cancer cells. Colon cancer cells in the logarithmic growth phase were collected, and total protein was extracted by lysis followed by quantification using the BCA method. Equal amounts of protein samples were separated by SDS-PAGE and subsequently transferred to a PVDF membrane. After blocking, the membranes were incubated with anti-KRT80 primary antibody (Rabbit, #16835-1-AP, Proteintech, CHN; 1:1,000 dilution) and the internal reference antibody anti-β-actin (or anti-GAPDH, Rabbit, #2118T, Cell Signaling Technology, USA; 1:2,000 dilution) at 4 ℃ overnight. The next day, after washing, the membranes were incubated with HRP-labeled Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG secondary antibody (#7074, Cell Signaling Technology, USA; 1:3,000 dilution) for 1 hour at room temperature. The antibodies used are listed in Table S1. Protein bands were then visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) chemiluminescence and recorded with an image acquisition system.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.5.0), GraphPad Prism 9, and ImageJ. Experimental data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD). Statistical comparisons were performed with GraphPad Prism 9, using independent samples t-test for comparisons between two groups and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for multi-group comparisons. A P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Elevated KRT80 expression in COAD

Analysis of the TIMER database revealed markedly increased KRT80 mRNA expression across multiple cancer types, with COAD showing particularly high levels (Figure 1A). This finding was further validated using the UALCAN database, which also indicated significant upregulation of KRT80 in COAD samples (Figure 1B). Consistent with these results, elevated expression of KRT80 in COAD was corroborated through the GEPIA database (Figure 1C).

High expression of KRT80 is associated with poor prognosis in COAD

Through analysis of the Kaplan-Meier plotter database, it was observed that patients exhibiting high expression levels of the KRT80 gene demonstrated significantly inferior OS [hazard ratio (HR) =1.44, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.01–2.06, P<0.05; Figure 2A], first-progression survival (FPS) (HR =1.62, 95% CI: 1.27–2.07, P<0.001; Figure 2B), and PPS (HR =1.72, 95% CI: 1.37–2.17, P<0.001; Figure 2C) when compared to patients with low expression levels.

Kaplan-Meier survival curve of COAD patients with KRT80 expression. (A-C) High KRT80 expression showed a significantly lower (A) OS rate. (B) FPS. (C) PPS. CI, confidence interval; COAD, colon adenocarcinoma; FPS, first progression survival; HR, hazard ratio; KRT80, Keratin 80; OS, overall survival; PPS, post-progression survival.

KRT80 expression and the immune microenvironment of COAD

To evaluate the relationship between KRT80 expression and immune cell infiltration, correlation analyses were performed. A matrix representing the relative abundance of 22 distinct tumor-infiltrating cell types was generated for 465 samples using CIBERSORT software locally (Figure 3A). Patients with COAD were then stratified into high and low KRT80 expression groups based on the median expression level. Using CIBERSORTx analysis, we investigated the tumor immune microenvironment characteristics across these groups. The results indicated a statistically significant positive correlation (P<0.05) between KRT80 expression and the frequencies of activated memory Macrophages M0, M0 macrophages, and Mast cells activated. In contrast, KRT80 expression showed a statistically significant negative correlation (P<0.05) with the proportions of resting memory CD4+ T cells, naive B cells, CD8+ T cells, and resting mast cells (Figure 3B). Additionally, based on single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA), KRT80 expression demonstrated positive correlations (P<0.05) with several immune cell types, including Gamma delta T cell, Plasmacytoid dendritic cell, gamma delta T cells, memory B cells, and type 2 T helper cells (Figure 3C).

(B) Comparative abundances of 22 TIIC subtypes between COAD patients with high vs. low KRT80 expression. (C) ssGSEA scores for immune-related pathways stratified by KRT80 expression. ns, not significant; *, P<0.05; **, P<0.01; ***, P<0.001. COAD, colon adenocarcinoma; KRT80, Keratin 80; NK, natural killer; ssGSEA, single-sample gene set enrichment analysis; TIICs, tumor-infiltrating immune cells.

The genomic landscape of somatic mutations in colorectal adenocarcinoma

Somatic mutation data from 437 COAD patients were acquired from the TCGA database and visualized using the “maftools” R package (v2.24.0). The resulting mutation landscape illustrates distinct mutation types using color codes, with a corresponding annotation provided below. The ten most frequently mutated genes were identified as follows: APC (71%), TP53 (54%), TTN (49%), KRAS (42%), SYNE1 (30%), MUC16 (30%), PIK3CA (30%), FAT4 (25%), RYR2 (22%), and CSMD3 (21%) (Figure 4A).

Somatic mutation landscape in COAD. (A) Mutation spectrum showing the top mutated genes ranked by mutation frequency (right) and the distribution of mutation types (bottom). (B) Profiles of variant classifications, SNV categories, and mutation types in the COAD cohort. COAD, colon adenocarcinoma; SNV, single nucleotide variant; TMB, tumor mutational burden.

Further analysis revealed that missense mutations represent the predominant variant category, and single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were more abundant than insertions or deletions. Among single nucleotide variants (SNVs), C-to-T transitions were the most prevalent type observed in COAD samples (Figure 4B). Additionally, the total number of mutations per sample was quantified, and the distribution of mutation types is summarized in a box plot, where each category is color-coded for clarity (Figure 4B).

Notably, no somatic mutations were detected in KRT80 (mutation frequency: 0%), suggesting that its role in COAD prognosis is unlikely to be mediated through TMB.

Functional enrichment analysis of DEGs related to KRT80

In this study, we evaluated the potential role of KRT80 in COAD through differential expression analysis. Based on the median expression value of KRT80, COAD patients were divided into high and low expression groups, with 232 patients in each group. The R package “DESeq2” was then used to screen for DEGs associated with KRT80. A total of 154 DEGs were identified, including 99 up-regulated and 55 down-regulated genes. The results are shown in the volcano plot (Figure 5A), with the threshold set at |logFC| >1.0 and P<0.05.

Functional enrichment analysis of KRT80-associated DEGs. (A) Volcano plot visualizing DEGs between high and low KRT80 expression groups. (B) GO terms enriched among the DEGs. (C) Significantly enriched KEGG pathways. (D-F) GSEA of the DEG sets. BP, biological process; CC, cellular component; DEGs, differentially expressed genes; GO, Gene Ontology; GSEA, gene set enrichment analysis; KEGG, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes; KRT80, Keratin 80; MF, molecular function.

GO enrichment analysis indicated that DEGs associated with KRT80 are involved in several biological processes (BPs), including intermediate filament-based process, humoral immune response, antimicrobial humoral response. In addition, it is also implicated in cellular component (CC) and molecular function (MF). The CCs included the protein-DNA complex, nucleosome, CENP-A containing chromatin, keratin filament, and epidermal lamellar body. The MFs included serine-type endopeptidase activity, serine-type peptidase activity, serine hydrolase activity, growth factor activity, and calcitonin receptor binding (Figure 5B).

In addition, KEGG analysis (Figure 5C) revealed the involvement of KRT80 in various pathways, such as Estrogen signaling pathway, Wnt signaling pathway, Staphylococcus aureus infection, Systemic lupus erythematosus, and Vascular smooth muscle contraction.

The GSEA results indicate that KRT80 is involved in the following functions: epidermal cell differentiation, structural molecule activity, epithelium development (Figure 5D-5F).

KRT80 is significantly highly expressed in CRC cells

WB analysis was performed to detect the protein expression level of KRT80 in the normal colon epithelial cell line NCM460 and COAD cell lines (HCT116, SW620, HT-29), as well as the mouse colon cancer cell line CT26. The results showed that compared with the normal NCM460 cells, the protein expression of KRT80 was significantly increased in HCT116, SW620, HT-29, and CT26 cells (Figure 6), suggesting that KRT80 may play a cancer-promoting role in the occurrence and development of COAD.

Verification of KRT80 knockdown and overexpression efficiency

To investigate the function of KRT80, HT-29 and HCT116 cells were transfected with si-KRT80 (interference sequences #1 and #2) or OE-KRT80 (overexpression sequences #1 and #2), respectively, and the interference and overexpression efficiencies were validated by WB and PCR. Compared with the control group transfected with si-NC, the protein expression of KRT80 was significantly decreased (P<0.001) in HT-29 and HCT116 cells after transfection with si-KRT80 #1 and si-KRT80 #2, and both interference sequences were effective (Figure 7A,7B). Compared with the control group transfected with the empty vector NC, the protein expression of KRT80 was significantly increased (P<0.001) in HT-29 and HCT116 cells after transfection with OE-KRT80 #1 and OE-KRT80 #2, and both overexpression sequences were effective (Figure 7C,7D). For subsequent experiments, si-KRT80 #2 and OE-KRT80 #2 were selected for functional validation.

(B,D) Validation of KRT80 knockdown and overexpression in HCT116 cells. KRT80 expression was significantly downregulated by si-KRT80 and upregulated by OE-KRT80 compared to controls (P<0.05). ***, P<0.001. KRT80, Keratin 80; NC, negative control; OE, overexpression; PCR, polymerase chain reaction.

KRT80 effect on CRC cell proliferation in CCK-8 assay

The effect of KRT80 on the proliferative ability of HT-29 and HCT116 cells was detected by CCK-8 assay. The results showed that compared with the Ctrl group, the proliferative ability of cells in the si-KRT80 group was significantly decreased (P<0.001), while that in the OE-KRT80 group was significantly increased (P<0.001) (Figure 8A,8B). Compared with the Ctrl group, the proliferative ability of cells in the si-KRT80 group was significantly decreased (P<0.001), and that in the OE-KRT80 group was significantly increased (P<0.001) (Figure 8C,8D). The above results indicate that KRT80 significantly promotes the proliferation of colon cancer cells.

CCK-8 assay was performed to evaluate cell viability. (A,B) CCK-8 assay showing the viability of HT-29 cells with modulated KRT80 expression. (C,D) Proliferation of HCT116 cells after KRT80 knockdown or overexpression. KRT80 knockdown inhibited, while overexpression promoted, cell viability (vs. si-NC or NC, P<0.05). ***, P<0.001. CCK-8, Cell Counting Kit-8; KRT80, Keratin 80; NC, negative control.

KRT80 effect on CRC cell migration in transwell assay

We investigated the role of KRT80 in cell motility using Transwell assays. For the migration assay, KRT80 knockdown (si-KRT80) led to a significant decrease in migrated cells compared to the negative control (si-NC), whereas KRT80 overexpression (OE-KRT80) resulted in a significant increase compared to the vector control (NC) (P<0.001; Figure 9A,9B). For the invasion assay, where chambers were coated with Matrigel, the si-KRT80 group similarly showed reduced invasion, and the OE-KRT80 group showed enhanced invasion (P<0.001; Figure 9C,9D). These results, consistent across both cell lines, indicate that KRT80 facilitates colon cancer cell migration and invasion.

KRT80 promotes invasion and migration of colon cancer cells. (A,C) Transwell assays showing the impaired invasion and migration abilities after KRT80 knockdown in HT-29 and HCT116 cells. (B,D) Enhanced invasion and migration abilities upon KRT80 overexpression (vs. si-NC or NC, **P<0.01). *, P<0.05; ***, P<0.001. Staining: crystal violet; magnification: ×200. KRT80, Keratin 80; NC, negative control; OE, overexpression.

Investigating the role of KRT80 in the motility of CRC cells using a wound healing assay

Wound healing assays were further performed to validate the effect of KRT80 on the migration of colon cancer cells. The results demonstrated that in HT-29 cells, compared with the 0 h time point, the wound closure rate in the si-KRT80 group was significantly lower than that in the si-NC group at both 24 and 48 h (P<0.001), while the OE-KRT80 group exhibited a significantly higher wound closure rate compared to the NC group (P<0.001) (Figure 10A,10B). Similarly, in HCT116 cells, the wound closure rate in the si-KRT80 group was significantly reduced compared to the si-NC group at 24 and 48 h relative to 0 h (P<0.001), whereas the OE-KRT80 group showed a significant increase in wound closure compared to the NC group (P<0.001) (Figure 10C,10D). These findings indicate that KRT80 significantly promotes the migration of colon cancer cells.

KRT80 knockdown impeded, while its overexpression accelerated, wound closure (vs. si-NC or NC at the same time point, *P<0.05). **, P<0.01; ***, P<0.001. KRT80 affects the functions of CRC cells by modulating the STAT3 signaling pathway

We investigated whether KRT80 regulates cancer cell functions through the STAT3 pathway. The results showed that in HT-29 cells, compared with the NC group, the protein expression of p-STAT3 (phosphorylated STAT3) was significantly decreased in the si-KRT80 group and significantly increased after KRT80 overexpression, while the protein expression of total STAT3 (T-STAT3) showed no significant change (Figure 11A). In HCT116 cells, compared with the NC group, the protein expression of p-STAT3 was significantly decreased in the si-KRT80 group and significantly increased in the OE-KRT80 group, while the protein expression of T-STAT3 remained unchanged (Figure 11B).

The total STAT3 level remained unchanged in both groups. (B) An identical regulatory pattern was confirmed in HCT116 cells. KRT80, Keratin 80; NC, negative control; OE, overexpression.

Discussion

COAD represents the most prevalent pathological variant of CRC (45). This molecular heterogeneity is intricately linked to the metabolic reprogramming observed in tumor cells (46), exemplified by the Warburg effect and the dysregulation of glutamine metabolism, which collectively establish a bioenergetic environment conducive to the unrestrained proliferation of cancer cells (47). Within this intricate context, KRT80—an intermediate filament protein of type II—exhibits various oncogenic activities (25,48,49). Beyond facilitating invasion and metastasis through the activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway and the induction of EMT (28,50), KRT80 also modulates mitochondrial function to promote glycolysis and disrupts mitotic precision, resulting in chromosomal instability (CIN) that propels tumor advancement. Therefore, a comprehensive investigation into the mechanistic role of KRT80 in COAD not only elucidates the multifaceted oncogenic characteristics of this structural protein but also presents novel therapeutic targets aimed at enhancing survival outcomes for patients with advanced disease.

Our analysis of multiple public databases, including TIMER, UALCAN, and GEPIA, consistently revealed significant upregulation of KRT80 mRNA expression in COAD tissues compared to normal colon samples. This elevated expression was further confirmed at the protein level in a panel of COAD cell lines (HT29, HCT116, CT26, SW620), with the most pronounced overexpression observed in HT29 and HCT116 cells. Clinically, high KRT80 expression was strongly associated with unfavorable prognosis, demonstrating significant correlations with reduced OS (HR =1.39, P<0.001), FPS (HR =1.52, P<0.001), and PPS (HR =1.27, P=0.034). These findings position KRT80 as a promising prognostic indicator in COAD patients.

Beyond its prognostic value, our study unveiled a potential role for KRT80 in shaping the tumor immune microenvironment. Using CIBERSORT and ssGSEA algorithms, we identified significant correlations between KRT80 expression levels and specific immune cell populations. Notably, KRT80 expression positively correlated with pro-tumorigenic immune cells such as M0 macrophages and activated mast cells, while showing negative correlations with anti-tumor lymphocytes including resting CD4+ T cells and naive B cells. These observations suggest that KRT80 may contribute to an immunosuppressive microenvironment that facilitates tumor immune evasion and progression, adding a new dimension to our understanding of its oncogenic functions.

To elucidate the functional significance of KRT80 in COAD pathogenesis, we performed gain-of-function and loss-of-function experiments in vitro. Efficient knockdown and overexpression of KRT80 were achieved in HT29 and HCT116 cells, enabling rigorous functional characterization. Our results demonstrated that KRT80 silencing significantly impaired cellular proliferation, migration, and invasion capabilities, whereas KRT80 overexpression enhanced these malignant phenotypes. The consistent results across multiple functional assays (CCK-8, Transwell, wound healing) strongly support the conclusion that KRT80 plays a causal role in promoting COAD progression.

Mechanistically, we provided evidence that KRT80 may exert its oncogenic effects through activation of the STAT3 signaling pathway. WB analysis revealed that KRT80 modulation significantly affected STAT3 phosphorylation levels without altering total STAT3 expression. Specifically, KRT80 knockdown reduced phosphorylated STAT3 levels, while its overexpression enhanced STAT3 activation. Given the well-established role of STAT3 signaling in promoting cell proliferation, survival, migration, and immune modulation in various cancers, our findings suggest that KRT80 may facilitate COAD progression through STAT3 pathway activation.

Furthermore, genomic analysis revealed that while KRT80 itself is not frequently mutated in COAD (mutation frequency 0%), it may influence tumor behavior through expression-mediated mechanisms rather than genetic alterations. Functional enrichment analysis of KRT80-associated DEGs indicated involvement in several critical biological processes and pathways, including intermediate filament-based processes, humoral immune response, estrogen signaling, and Wnt signaling pathways. These findings suggest that KRT80 may participate in a complex regulatory network that influences multiple aspects of COAD biology.

Our study possesses several notable strengths. First, we employed a comprehensive approach that integrated multi-database bioinformatic analyses with experimental validation, thereby enhancing the robustness and reliability of our findings. The consistent overexpression of KRT80 across multiple independent cohorts (TIMER, UALCAN, GEPIA) and its correlation with adverse clinical outcomes strongly support its prognostic relevance in COAD. Furthermore, the combination of differential gene expression, functional enrichment, and immune microenvironment analyses provided a systematic understanding of the potential roles and mechanisms of KRT80 in tumor progression. Finally, the in vitro functional experiments confirmed the oncogenic role of KRT80 in promoting proliferation, migration, and invasion, and preliminary mechanistic insights suggested the involvement of the STAT3 signaling pathway.

However, there are certain limitations in this study. Firstly, the bioinformatic analyses were based largely on public databases, and further validation using independent prospective clinical cohorts is warranted to confirm the prognostic value of KRT80. Secondly, although we identified a potential link between KRT80 and STAT3 activation, the precise molecular mechanisms through which KRT80 regulates STAT3 phosphorylation remain unclear. Additional investigations, such as co-immunoprecipitation or kinase assays, are needed to elucidate whether this interaction is direct or indirect. Moreover, all functional experiments were conducted in cell lines; thus, in vivo studies using animal models would strengthen the biological and translational relevance of our findings. Lastly, while KRT80 expression was associated with immune cell infiltration, its functional impact on the tumor immune microenvironment requires further experimental exploration.

Conclusions

In summary, our study comprehensively demonstrates that KRT80 is significantly overexpressed in COAD and is associated with poor patient prognosis. Through multi-database bioinformatics analysis and experimental validation, we established that high KRT80 expression promotes the proliferation, migration, and invasion of COAD cells. Mechanistically, KRT80 likely exerts its oncogenic functions by activating the STAT3 signaling pathway, as evidenced by correlated phosphorylation levels of STAT3 upon modulation of KRT80 expression. Furthermore, KRT80 expression is linked to specific immune cell infiltration patterns in the tumor microenvironment, although it does not contribute to tumor mutation burden .These findings highlight KRT80 as a potential prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target in COAD, providing new insights into the molecular mechanisms driving COAD progression.
