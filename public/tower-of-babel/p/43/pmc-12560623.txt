Introduction

Myxomas represent the most common primary cardiac tumour, arising from the left atrium in over 75% of cases. Manifestations other than intra-atrial are rarely seen. In this report, we describe the case of a giant right atrial myxoma with transmural breakthrough and epicardial growth, as well as its successful surgical resection.

Case presentation

A 65 year-old male patient presented for cardiological evaluation with increasing pectanginous symptoms (CCS 3) and dyspnoea (NYHA III). He reported no other complaints such as fever, weight loss, peripheral oedema, or night sweats. The patient had received coronary artery bypass grafting for two-vessel disease (left internal mammary artery to left anterior descending; SVG to D1 and D2, SVG to RCA) at the age of 49. Moreover, his medical history included sinus bradycardia, arterial hypertension, type II diabetes, obesity (body mass index 34 kg/m2), and sleep apnoea. Vital signs and physical evaluation on admission were unremarkable.

Coronary angiography demonstrated disease progression, with a new left main ostial stenosis as well as total occlusion of the saphenous vein graft to RCA; the left ventricular function was moderately impaired. In order to assess myocardial vitality, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging was performed. This unexpectedly revealed a round tumour of over 6 cm in diameter in the lower right mediastinum. The tumour was T1w-hypointense and T2w-STIR hyperintense with multiple nodular hyperintense foci. It showed neither early nor late gadolinium enhancement, and there was no contrast intake in the additional computed tomography (CT) scan (Figure 1, Supplementary material online, Video).

Echocardiography confirmed a hypoechogenic epicardial mass with compression of the right atrium as well as the superior and inferior vena cava (Figure 2). No pulsatile signal was present above the centre of the mass, and there was no communication with the right atrial cavity.

Transthoracic echocardiography. Hypoechogenic epicardial mass compressing the right atrium in the apical four-chamber view.

Thoracoabdominal CT excluded the presence of lymphadenopathy. Biopsy of the unclear mass was not considered due to the previous cardiac surgery and the proximity of the tumour to the right atrium. Even though there was no increase in size in the follow-up, the patient complained of persistent dyspnoea and opted for surgical removal of the tumour.

Surgery was performed under general anaesthesia and total cardiopulmonary bypass. As the tumour was inseparable from the right atrial wall, both were resected and the right atrial wall was reconstructed using a bovine pericardial patch (Figure 3).

Surgical removal. (A and B) Careful dissection of the epicardial tumour growing into the right atrial wall. (C) Resected spherical mass with a firm, smooth capsule and individual muscle strands in the right atrial region.

The tumour was resected entirely in healthy tissue. The resected mass was sharply demarcated, with individual muscle strands next to an otherwise smooth capsule. The interior consisted predominantly of necrotic parts, which made up over 80% of the tumour mass. Histological examination detected Gamna–Gandy bodies, classifying the tumour as a myxoma.

Post-operatively, the patient was admitted to ICU, where he was extubated shortly afterwards. Inotropes were reduced until the fifth post-operative day, and intermittent atrial fibrillation was adjusted using beta-blockers and amiodarone. Post-operative echocardiography and CT excluded any remaining tumour tissue. The patient was discharged home in good clinical condition 13 days post-operatively.

Discussion

Cardiac myxoma is the most common cardiac tumour, predominantly affecting middle-aged female patients.1,2 Depending on location, size, mobility, and invasiveness, symptoms may range from dyspnoea, palpitations, and embolism to heart failure and sudden death due to intracardiac obstruction.1,2 Myxomas are most frequently found in the left atrium, typically appearing as a pedunculated mass arising from the area of the fossa ovalis.3 There are very few case reports describing extracardiac myxomas. Spirito et al.4 reported an extracardiac right ventricular myxoma as an asymptomatic incidental finding. In another case, an epicardial right atrial myxoma was discovered in the post-mortem autopsy of a 43 year-old women.3 Given the atypical broad-based extracardiac growth in the case described here, alternative diagnoses beyond myxoma were initially considered. Differential diagnoses in the presence of an unclear epicardial mass may broadly be grouped into three categories: non-tumour masses, benign tumours, and malignancies.5,6 Non-tumour masses include thrombi and haematomas as well as aneurysms of saphenous vein coronary artery bypass grafts, which were considered in the differential diagnoses given the patient’s medical history.5-8 In the absence of lymphadenopathy and B-symptoms, benign tumours such as lipomas appeared more likely than cardiac malignancies such as sarcoma, lymphoma, or metastasis.5-8

Although the presence of extensive central necrosis would be considered atypical in a myxoma, the histological diagnosis in our case was unequivocal, supported by residual calretinin-positive spindle cells and characteristic Gamna–Gandy bodies.3 Gamna–Gandy bodies are granulomatous lesions, consisting of degenerated collagen fibres, iron, and calcium, which are typically seen in cardiac myxomas.9

Conclusion

This case emphasizes that an extracardiac myxoma should be considered in the presence of an unclear epicardial mass. Surgical resection can confirm the diagnosis and help relieve associated symptoms.
