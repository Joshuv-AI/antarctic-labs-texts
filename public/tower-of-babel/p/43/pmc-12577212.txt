The morphological association between nasal septal deviation and craniofacial asymmetry
Authors: Xiao-ming Wang, Wei-ning Wang, Yu Zhang, Hui Wei, Yi-fei Huo, Hui Xue
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe nasal septum plays a pivotal role in craniofacial growth and development, and nasal septum deviation (NSD) is a common finding in clinical practice. This study aims to investigate the morphological relationship between the NSD and craniofacial transversal asymmetry.Materials and methodsCone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) scans from 114 patients were collected and analyzed to examine the association between NSD parameters and transverse metrics of the maxilla, dentoalveolus, and cranium. Additionally, craniofacial transverse parameters were compared across subgroups stratified by NSD variables, including Septal Deviation Angle (SDA), Deviated Septal Width (DSW), and Deviated Septal Curve Angle (DSCA), to evaluate their efficacy in predicting transverse asymmetry.ResultsNSD variables (SDA, DSW, and DSCA) were significantly associated with transverse asymmetry of the maxilla, dentoalveolus, and cranial base. Specifically, maxillary parameters reflecting transverse asymmetry of the maxillary sinus lateral wall around the palatal plane level and dentoalveolar parameters reflecting transverse asymmetry of the alveolar bone and dental arch exhibited significant differences across subgroups with varying NSD severity, as classified by SDA, DSW, and DSCA.ConclusionsOur findings confirm a robust relationship between NSD and transverse asymmetry of multiple craniofacial structures. SDA and DSW demonstrate higher sensitivity, while DSCA shows greater specificity in identifying craniofacial asymmetry. Collectively, these parameters play key roles in the development of transverse asymmetry within the nasomaxillary skeleton.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-07101-z.Keywords: Nasal septum deviation, Maxilla, Cranial base, Dentoalveolus, Craniofacial asymmetry

Introduction

The nasal septum, located in the median sagittal plane of the facial region, is composed of the perpendicular plate of the ethmoid bone, the vomer, septal cartilage, and the nasal crests of both the maxillary and palatine bones. Its development commences during the 4th to 5th weeks of embryogenesis, originating from the medial nasal processes, and undergoes rapid progression between the 6th and 9th weeks. Through interactions with the adjacent nasal, frontal, maxillary, palatine, and sphenoid bones, the nasal septum plays a crucial role in shaping the formation of facial contours [1, 2].

Accumulating evidence indicates that the nasal septal cartilage functions as a growth center, exerting regulatory effects on facial growth and development. Its expansion contributes to the disarticulation of facial sutures, thereby facilitating facial development [3]. Studies have confirmed that removal or damage to this cartilage impairs anteroposterior facial growth [4]. A longitudinal twin study revealed that early nasal septal injury can lead to saddle nose deformity, retarded midfacial growth, and an altered occlusal plane, underscoring its significance in craniofacial development [5]. While the aforementioned studies demonstrate that the nasal septum influences craniofacial growth in both sagittal and vertical dimensions—particularly in the maxilla—understanding of its impact on the transverse dimension remains limited.

In terms of transversal craniofacial morphology, symmetry stands as a key indicator critical for assessing plasticity, though minor asymmetry is often overlooked due to measurement constraints [6]. Clinical observations have noted that infants with marked nasal septum deviation (NSD) face an elevated risk of developing posterior crossbite and palatal asymmetry by two years of age [7]. Additionally, Akbay and Jongkhum identified associations between NSD and abnormal palatal vault morphology [8, 9]. Some perspectives propose that the nasal septum absorbs growth-related pressures from the skull and oral cavity, with excessive pressure potentially inducing NSD—a common transversal deformity [9, 10]. This causal relationship suggests a potential link between NSD and transversal asymmetry of craniofacial structures. However, the specific nature of their association remains unclear, limiting our understanding of how the nose influences transversal craniofacial growth and development.

In the present study, we aim to systematically investigate the association between nasal septum deviation (NSD) and transverse craniofacial asymmetry through the measurement of three-dimensional (3D) CBCT images. By identifying NSD indicators that are closely correlated with craniofacial asymmetry, we seek to provide a reference for preoperative transverse assessment and planning in plastic surgery and orthodontics, while also shedding light on the role of NSD in the interplay of craniofacial deformities.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

This is a retrospective cross-sectional study conducted in compliance with the ethical principles for medical research involving human subjects as outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was granted by the Ethics Committee of XXX Hospital of Stomatology, XXX University. Prior to undergoing CBCT scanning for oral examinations, all patients provided written informed consent, explicitly authorizing the use of their scan data for educational and research purposes.

We retrieved and screened CBCT data from the archives of XXX Hospital of Stomatology, XXX University, cross-referencing corresponding medical records for the period 2020–2023. All CBCT images included in this study were acquired by the same experienced technician using the identical equipment (Smart3D-X). The scanning parameters were strictly standardized as follows: 100 kV, 6 mA, exposure time of 26.9 s, field of view of 23 × 18 cm, and voxel size of 0.25 mm. CBCT data were included in the study based on the following criteria:

Including criteria: (1) age > 18 years; (2) Chinese population; (3) CBCT images from patients without history of facial plastic surgery and dental treatment; (4) complete maxillary dental arch; (5) no obvious facial deformities; (6) no unilateral chewing habits. Excluding criteria: (1) history of orthodontic or functional orthopedic therapy, orthognathic surgery, or plastic surgery; (2) cleft lip and palate, other craniofacial anomalies, or a history of craniofacial trauma; (3) history of septoplasty or rhinoplasty; (4) temporomandibular joint disorders; (5) functional mandibular protrusion or retrusion; (6) permanent tooth loss; (7) unilateral chewing habits.

Sample size calculation: In line with the American Board of Orthodontics Objective Grading System (ABO OGS), a measurement difference of less than 0.5 mm is deemed to be clinically insignificant. From a clinical standpoint, DSCA emerged as the most sensitive and representative indicator among the NSD parameters. Therefore, we utilized DSCA to estimate the required sample size using the G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7). For a two-tailed test, we set the alpha (α) level at 0.05 and the statistical power at 80.0%. A minimum sample size of 16 participants per subgroup was determined to be necessary to detect a significant difference in the DSCA values across the mild, moderate, and severe groups. A total of 114 subjects were enrolled in this study, with the smallest subgroup containing 19 participants.

Measurement items and procedures

CBCT data saved in DICOM format were imported into Dolphin software (version 11.8; Dolphin Imaging and Management Solutions, Chatsworth, CA, USA) for measurement. Prior to measurement, each subject’s CBCT image was subjected to three-dimensional adjustment, following protocols adapted from previous studies with minor modifications [8, 11–14]. Specifically: In the coronal view, the red reference line was positioned to coincide with the median sagittal plane. In the sagittal view, the skull was rotated to align the blue reference line with the palatal plane. In the axial view, the skull was rotated to ensure the red reference line equally separated the cervical vertebrae. A recheck in the coronal view confirmed that the blue reference line ran parallel to the horizontal part of the palatine bone (Fig. 1).

Transversal measurement items for the nasal septum, maxilla, dentoalveolar structures, and cranium were established using slices of varying dimensions, as defined in Table 1 and illustrated in Fig. 2. For the measurement of nasal septum deviation (NSD) on coronal slices, the view displaying the most significant deviation was selected, and NSD parameters (SDA, DSW, DSCA, SVL) were quantified (Fig. 2A). Parameters of the zygomatic bone (ZF-MA, ZM-MA), orbital bone (IC-MP, IO-MA), maxillary sinus (MSN-MP, MSO-MP, MS-MA), and palatal bone (HPT, MP-MP, PAD, PA-MA, HP-MA) were measured on the coronal slice (Fig. 2B) that, in the sagittal view, equally divides the palatine plane into anterior and posterior segments (green reference line in Fig. 1B). For molar parameter measurements (BMA-MP, LMA-MP, PA-MP, MM-MP, MMA-HA, LA-MA), the coronal slice was specifically positioned at the buccal groove of the maxillary first permanent molars (Fig. 2B). Parameters of the temporomandibular joint (MSF-MP, LSF-MP) were assessed on the coronal slice passing through the maximum cross-sectional area of the condyles (Fig. 2I). Other parameters, including those of the canine (MC-MP, Fig. 2C), pterygoid plate (LPP-MP, Fig. 2D), frontozygomatic suture (FS-MP, Fig. 2E), temporozygomatic suture (TS-MP, Fig. 2F), and orbit (LO-MP, Fig. 2G; MO-MP, Fig. 2H), were measured at different levels of axial slices. The definitions of the aforementioned measurements were referenced from previous studies [13], and XXX performed the whole measurements.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS software (version 27.0; IBM Corp., Chicago, IL, USA). Due to violations of the Shapiro-Wilk normality test, non-parametric tests were employed for enhanced robustness. To assess measurement reliability, XXX conducted the intra-observer measurement on 20 randomly selected CBCT subjects, and XXX performed the inter-observer measurement after a two-month interval. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used to evaluate intra- and inter-examiner reproducibility, respectively. The craniofacial parameters were the side-to-side differences in transverse measurements, and Spearman correlation analysis was applied to explore relationships between NSD and craniofacial indicators. Subgroup differences stratified by the severity of NSD variables were analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis H test, followed by Conover-Iman post-hoc tests and Bonferroni adjustment to get the adjusted P values. Correlation coefficients were denoted as ‘γ’, with significance levels set as follows: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ns (non-significant) for P > 0.05.

Results

Among the subjects, 64 were female, accounting for 56.14% of the total. The mean age of male subjects was 23.8 years, and that of female subjects was 25.1 years, with no statistically significant difference observed between the two groups.

The inter-examiner intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were 0.735 and 0.977, while the intra-examiner ICCs ranged from 0.810 to 0.980 (all with 95% confidence intervals [CIs]). Wilcoxon tests further confirmed no significant differences (P > 0.05), indicating that the data reliability in this study was acceptable, as detailed in Table 2. The correlations between NSD and craniofacial transversal asymmetry parameters are presented in Table 3. In the maxillary region, MSN-MP and MSO-MP showed significantly positive correlations with SDA and DSW (P < 0.01) and significantly negative correlations with DSCA (P < 0.01). In the dentoalveolar region, LMA-MP and PA-MP exhibited similar correlation patterns to MSN-MP and MSO-MP (P < 0.05), with the additional finding of significantly positive correlations with SVL (P < 0.05). In the cranial region, the significant correlations were more varied: LPP-MP and ZF-MA were significantly positively correlated with DSW (P < 0.05); TS-MP was significantly positively correlated with SVL (P < 0.05); and LSF-MP was significantly positively correlated with SDA (P < 0.05). These correlation patterns are summarized in the heat map (Fig. 3), which visually demonstrates that maxillary MSN-MP and MSO-MP, as well as dentoalveolar LMA-MP and PA-MP, were significantly correlated with NSD variables (SDA, DSW, DSCA). Additional Spearman correlation analyses revealed no significant correlations between NSD variables and the direction of craniofacial asymmetry, as indicated by the calculated differences between the deviated and non-deviated sides. Positive values denote larger measurements on the deviated side (P > 0.05, Supplementary Table 1).

To further validate the effectiveness of NSD variables in identifying craniofacial transversal asymmetry, the subjects were categorized into three subgroups (mild, moderate, and severe) based on the value of SDA, DSW and DSCA. For subgroups classified by SDA, the maxillary parameters MSN-MP and MSO-MP showed significant differences across mild/moderate, mild/severe, and moderate/severe subgroups. In contrast, PA-MP exhibited significant differences only between the moderate and severe subgroups. Discussion

For the first time, this study comprehensively examined the relationship between nasal septal deviation (NSD) and craniofacial bony asymmetry, extending the analysis beyond the nasal cavity to encompass the maxilla, dentoalveolar, and cranial base—regions previously overlooked [15–17].

While researchers have long recognized that NSD can influence the transverse growth of craniofacial structures, prior studies have predominantly concentrated on two specific aspects. One is the relationship between NSD and palatal vault asymmetry in orthodontic patients [8]. The other is the correlation between asymmetric facial soft tissue contours and nasal cavity structure in patients with NSD who require facial aesthetic surgery [18]. Unlike aforementioned studies, this study investigated the impact of NSD on overall craniofacial symmetry within the orthodontic population with a broader perspective and a larger sample size. Consistent with earlier paranasal investigations utilizing posteroanterior cephalograms [18, 19], we observed that NSD markedly disrupts the transverse development of multiple craniofacial components. These findings underscore NSD as a pivotal, yet under-recognized, determinant of craniofacial asymmetry that warrants heightened vigilance during pediatric growth and development.

We observed that the magnitude of nasal septal deviation (NSD) correlated strongly with the absolute left–right discrepancies in the dentoalveolar, maxillary, and cranial-base regions. Yet, when the directional component of NSD was considered, no significant association emerged for any craniofacial territory, indicating that NSD predominantly dictates the extent, rather than the orientation, of asymmetry. This finding diverges from Kim et al., who reported that the directional bias of NSD influences differential growth of the facial halves [18]. Kang et al. asserted that rightward nasal septal deviation (NSD) coincides with progressive widening of the right facial half [20]. Yet their cohort comprised individuals seeking rhinoplasty and facial cosmetic correction for symptomatic NSD—patients whose altered respiratory physiology represents a distinctly pathological state. Moreover, their analyses relied on inconsistent 2D photographs or 3D surface reconstructions, restricting assessment to external soft-tissue morphology and precluding the in-depth evaluation of internal osseous structures accomplished in the present study.

In the present study, left- and right-sided deviations of the nasal septum occurred with similar frequency. Although earlier studies reported a predominance of leftward deviations [7, 21], they provided no convincing mechanistic rationale. Furthermore, linear septal height (SVL) proved less sensitive than the angular indices (SDA, DSW, and DSCA) for capturing NSD characteristics. This observation accords with the hypothesis that, during craniofacial development, vertically directed compressive forces from the cranial base and oral cavity are concentrated on the cartilaginous septum [10, 22]. Consistent with this model, patients with severe NSD exhibited markedly reduced SVL values, implying that such vertical loading ultimately constrains septal height.

Among the 114 subjects in this study, the mean SDA was 11.5° ± 3.6° (mild: 7.7° ± 0.9°, moderate: 11.7° ± 1.3°, severe: 17.1° ± 2.8°), the mean DSW was 5.33 mm ± 1.71 mm (mild: 3.97 mm ± 0.75 mm, moderate: 5.82 mm ± 0.57 mm, severe: 7.98 mm ± 1.06 mm), and the mean DSCA was 154.9° ± 7.5° (mild: 160.5° ± 3.6°, moderate: 152.1° ± 1.9°, severe: 142.8° ± 5.6°). Subgroup analyses based on any of the three NSD parameters revealed that subjects with severe NSD deviation consistently exhibited greater craniofacial transverse asymmetry. This pronounced asymmetry may be attributed to dysfunction in the oral and maxillofacial regions [23–25]. However, we observed that SDA and DSW were more sensitive indicators of craniofacial transverse asymmetry compared to DSCA, as they incorporated a broader range of craniofacial variables. This finding is further supported by Jongkhum et al., who concluded that DSW is a more rational classification technique [8]. Nevertheless, when compared to SDA and DSW, the highest specificity for identifying craniofacial asymmetry was achieved using the DSCA classification. This may be attributed to the constraining effect of the craniofacial bones on the nasal septum, which leads to a reduction in the vertical height of the nasal septum and the subsequent development of deviation. DSCA is particularly effective in reflecting these two characteristics.

In the maxillary region, the transverse asymmetry differences of MSN-MP and MSO-MP exhibited a strong association with the degree of nasal septal deviation (NSD), while other palatal measurements showed no significant correlation. In contrast, although the correlation analysis did not yield statistically significant results for the PA-MA measurement, significant differences were observed among subgroups. This suggests that the relationship between NSD and palatal arch asymmetry is more complex and intricate, rather than a simple linear correlation [8, 26]. In the dentoalveolar region, the asymmetry differences of LMA-MP and PA-MP were significantly associated with NSD, suggesting a potential interaction between NSD and oral function. As reported by Ascanio et al., children with obligate mouth-breathing due to nasal septum deviations exhibit facial and dental anomalies compared to nose-breathing controls [27]. Both LMA-MP and BMA-MP are indicators of alveolar width. However, a significant correlation was detected between LMA-MP and NSD, whereas no such correlation was found for BMA-MP. This discrepancy can be attributed to the differences in the structural properties of the alveolar bone. The buccal alveolar bone is thinner and more porous compared to the lingual alveolar bone, making it more susceptible to external mechanical stimulation and subsequent remodeling. As a result, the influence of growth and development appears to be less pronounced in this context [28]. In the cranial base region, MO-MP and IC-MP exhibited no correlation with NSD but showed significant differences among subgroups categorized by the severity of NSD. Conversely, ZF-MA, TS-MP, and LPP-MP demonstrated correlations with NSD but lacked significant differences among subgroups. Notably, LSF-MP, which represents the width of the temporomandibular joint fossa, showed both significant correlations and differences among subgroups. This finding is particularly intriguing. Yalçınkaya et al. previously suspected an association between NSD and temporomandibular joint disorder [29]. Our observation of the correlation between SDA and LSF-MP asymmetry provides novel support for this hypothesis, suggesting a potential link between NSD and temporomandibular joint asymmetry. Additionally, the relatively weak association between NSD and other cranial base features may stem from the fact that cranial development typically reaches completion earlier than that of the nasomaxillary skeleton.

Systematic examination is essential for effective treatment planning and achieving optimal outcomes in orthodontic and plastic surgery. Our study underscores the critical relationship between nasal septal deviation (NSD) and craniofacial asymmetry, highlighting the need for clinicians to consider the impact of NSD on overall craniofacial development. However, it is important to acknowledge the limitations of this study. For instance, our study sample consisted exclusively of adults; including adolescents in future research would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the association between NSD and craniofacial asymmetry. Additionally, the cross-sectional nature of our study design precluded us from exploring the causal relationship between NSD and craniofacial asymmetry from a developmental standpoint. It was demonstrated that NSD occurred progressively over a long period and during the developmental process. According to epidemiological studies, the incidence of nasal septal deviation in children aged 2.5 to 6 years was 12.45%; whereas the diagnosis rate of nasal septal deviation in children aged 6 to 9 years was 13.6%. On the other hand, this rate was as high as 90% in adults [9, 30]. The worsening of nasal septal deviation with age might imply that during early childhood (ages 1 to 10), when the nasal septum was exposed to pressure, an obtuse-angled deviation, including the perpendicular plate, may occur. However, due to the same pathological process during late childhood (ages 10 to 17), a sharp and crest-shaped deviation might develop [9]. Thus, these perspectives and limitations highlighted in the current study underscore the imperative need for more in-depth research on NSD in the future.

Conclusions

Nasal septal deviation (NSD) is significantly associated with the transverse asymmetry of the maxilla (MSN-MP, MSO-MP) and dentoalveolar region (LMA-MP, PA-MP), as well as with the morphological asymmetry of the palatal vault (PA-MA).

Nasal septal deviation (NSD) is significantly associated with the transverse asymmetry of the maxilla (MSN-MP, MSO-MP) and dentoalveolar region (LMA-MP, PA-MP), as well as with the morphological asymmetry of the palatal vault (PA-MA).

NSD may also be related to cranial base asymmetry (LSF-MP, LPP-MP, ZF-MA, TS-MP, MO-MP, IC-MP); however, this association is relatively weak and requires further validation.

Among the NSD parameters, SDA and DSW appear to be more sensitive for quantitatively assessing NSD and predicting craniofacial asymmetry. In contrast, DSCA demonstrates optimal specificity. All three parameters are critical for analyzing craniofacial asymmetry.
