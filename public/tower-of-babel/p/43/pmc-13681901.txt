Causal relationship between cardiac arrhythmias and sepsis: a two-sample bidirectional mendelian randomization study
Authors: Xinglei Li, Yadong Yang, Xunqi Zhang, Tianyi Lu, Lingling Liu, Zhengjun Kong, Tao Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study used a bidirectional two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) framework to investigate whether genetic liability to cardiac arrhythmias increases the risk of sepsis and whether genetic liability to sepsis increases the risk of cardiac arrhythmias.MethodsSummary statistics were obtained from publicly available genome-wide association studies in individuals of European ancestry. Cardiac arrhythmia data were derived from FinnGen (32,416 cases and 139,739 controls; IEU ID: finn-b-CARDIAC_ARRHYTM), and sepsis data were derived from UK Biobank (11,643 cases and 474,841 controls; IEU ID: ieu-b-4980). Independent single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with each exposure at P < 5 × 10^-6 were selected, clumped at r^2 < 0.001 within a 10,000-kb window, harmonized, and filtered to retain instruments with F-statistics > 10. The primary analysis used random-effects inverse-variance weighting (IVW), with weighted median, MR-Egger, simple mode, and weighted mode analyses as complementary methods. Horizontal pleiotropy and outliers were assessed using MR-PRESSO and the MR-Egger intercept. Heterogeneity was evaluated with Cochran’s Q test. Because the two causal directions were prespecified, false-positive control was strengthened by combining concordant directional evidence across methods with Benjamini-Hochberg false-discovery-rate assessment for the two primary IVW tests.ResultsGenetic liability to cardiac arrhythmias was associated with a higher risk of sepsis (IVW OR = 1.066, 95% CI: 1.007–1.128, P = 0.0268). Genetic liability to sepsis was also associated with a higher risk of cardiac arrhythmias (IVW OR = 1.078, 95% CI: 1.005–1.156, P = 0.0347), although the reverse-direction analysis was based on only three SNPs and should be interpreted cautiously. Effect directions were concordant across complementary MR models, Cochran’s Q test did not indicate substantial heterogeneity, MR-Egger intercepts did not support directional pleiotropy, and the two primary IVW tests showed an FDR-adjusted q value of 0.0536.ConclusionThe present bidirectional MR analysis supports a robust association from cardiac arrhythmia liability to sepsis risk and suggests a possible reverse association from sepsis liability to cardiac arrhythmias. These findings support closer rhythm and infection surveillance in high-risk patients while highlighting the need for larger and biologically resolved validation studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13019-026-04539-2.Keywords: Sepsis, Causality, Mendelian randomization, Cardiac arrhythmias, Genome-wide association study

Introduction

Sepsis is a major contributor to global mortality and morbidity, accounting for a substantial proportion of deaths worldwide, whereas cardiac arrhythmias are common complications in critically ill patients and are associated with adverse short-term outcomes [1–4]. New-onset atrial fibrillation and other rhythm disorders frequently occur during sepsis, but whether underlying arrhythmia susceptibility itself predisposes to sepsis has not been clearly established.

Clarifying the direction of this relationship is clinically important. If cardiac arrhythmias increase susceptibility to sepsis, patients with pre-existing rhythm disorders may represent a subgroup requiring closer infection surveillance. Conversely, if sepsis liability increases arrhythmia risk, this would further support intensive rhythm monitoring during acute infection and recovery.

Traditional observational studies are vulnerable to residual confounding and reverse causation, especially in critically ill populations in whom inflammation, electrolyte imbalance, organ dysfunction, vasoactive drugs, and corticosteroid exposure coexist [5]. Mendelian randomization studies on sepsis-related traits have begun to address these limitations [6], but bidirectional evaluation of sepsis and cardiac arrhythmias has remained insufficiently explored.

Mendelian randomization uses germline genetic variants as instrumental variables and can strengthen causal inference when the core instrumental-variable assumptions are met [7–12]. Building on this framework and following STROBE-MR recommendations [13], we performed a bidirectional two-sample MR study to evaluate both arrhythmia-to-sepsis and sepsis-to-arrhythmia pathways using large-scale GWAS summary statistics.

Materials and methods

Study design overview

We conducted a bidirectional two-sample Mendelian randomization analysis using summary-level GWAS data to evaluate the causal relationship between cardiac arrhythmias and sepsis. Only publicly available, de-identified summary statistics were used; therefore, additional institutional review board approval was not required. The analytical framework followed the STROBE-MR statement [13]. Three MR assumptions underpinned the design: the selected SNPs were associated with the exposure, were independent of major confounders, and influenced the outcome only through the exposure of interest. Data sources

Genetic association estimates for cardiac arrhythmias were obtained from FinnGen (IEU GWAS ID: finn-b-CARDIAC_ARRHYTM), including 172,155 participants of European ancestry, of whom 32,416 were cases and 139,739 were controls. The phenotype captured clinically coded cardiac arrhythmias, with ventricular arrhythmias constituting an important component of the case definition. Sepsis summary statistics were obtained from the UK Biobank-derived IEU dataset (IEU GWAS ID: ieu-b-4980), which included 486,484 participants of European ancestry, comprising 11,643 cases and 474,841 controls. Sepsis cases were defined using ICD-based diagnostic coding as previously described [14]. Restriction to European ancestry reduced population stratification bias but also limited ethnic generalizability.

Selection of genetic instruments

For each exposure, SNPs associated at P < 5 × 10^-6 were selected as candidate instruments because the number of genome-wide significant variants at P < 5 × 10^-8 was too limited for stable bidirectional MR, particularly in the reverse-direction sepsis analysis. Variants were clumped using a European reference panel with r^2 < 0.001 and a 10,000-kb window. We excluded palindromic SNPs with ambiguous strand orientation when allele frequencies precluded reliable harmonization, and we used LD proxies with r^2 > 0.80 when a lead SNP was unavailable in the corresponding outcome dataset. Instrument strength was quantified using the variance explained (R^2) and the F-statistic, and only variants with F > 10 were retained to reduce weak-instrument bias. The final analysis included 50 SNPs for cardiac arrhythmias and 3 SNPs for sepsis. Detailed SNP-level information is provided in Supplementary Tables S2 and S3.

Potential horizontal pleiotropy was evaluated using MR-PRESSO global testing and the MR-Egger intercept [15]. MR-PRESSO was used as a diagnostic procedure to screen for outlying variants before the primary MR estimation, and no outlier requiring removal was identified in either direction.

Univariable MR analyses

The primary causal estimate was obtained using the random-effects IVW model, which provides the highest efficiency when horizontal pleiotropy is balanced [16]. To evaluate robustness, we additionally performed weighted median, MR-Egger, weighted mode, and simple mode analyses. The weighted median estimator provides consistent inference when at least half of the total instrument weight is derived from valid variants [17, 18], whereas MR-Egger regression can detect and partially account for directional pleiotropy at the cost of lower precision [19]. Effect sizes are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) per genetically predicted increase in liability to the exposure.

Sensitivity analyses

Between-SNP heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q statistic [20, 21]. Horizontal pleiotropy was evaluated using the MR-Egger intercept test and MR-PRESSO [15, 22]. Leave-one-out analysis, funnel plots, and scatter plots were also generated to examine whether the observed associations were disproportionately driven by a single variant. Bidirectionality was not inferred from one pathway alone; instead, each causal direction was estimated independently using direction-specific instruments, and the combined interpretation relied on whether both pathways showed internally coherent effect estimates and no major evidence of pleiotropy or instability.

Statistical analyses

Analyses were performed in R using the TwoSampleMR and MRPRESSO packages in accordance with STROBE-MR guidance [13]. For the two prespecified primary IVW tests, false-positive control was evaluated using the Benjamini-Hochberg false-discovery-rate procedure. Complementary MR methods and sensitivity analyses were interpreted as robustness checks rather than independent primary hypotheses. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered nominally significant, and FDR-adjusted q values are reported for the two primary bidirectional IVW analyses.

Results

Identification and validation of genetic instruments

Using the predefined threshold of P < 5 × 10^-6, 61 candidate SNPs were initially associated with cardiac arrhythmias. After harmonization, exclusion of unavailable variants without suitable proxies, and instrument-quality filtering, 50 independent SNPs remained for the arrhythmia-to-sepsis analysis. For sepsis, 8 candidate SNPs were initially identified, and 3 independent SNPs remained after harmonization and availability checks for the reverse-direction analysis. All retained variants had F-statistics greater than 10, supporting adequate instrument strength. MR-PRESSO did not identify any outlying SNPs requiring correction.

MR analyses

Arrhythmia → Sepsis: Genetic liability to cardiac arrhythmias was associated with a higher risk of sepsis in the primary IVW analysis (OR = 1.066, 95% CI: 1.007–1.128, P = 0.0268). The effect direction was consistent in the weighted median (OR = 1.112, 95% CI: 1.016–1.216, P = 0.020) and weighted mode analyses (OR = 1.094, 95% CI: 0.975–1.228, P = 0.132), whereas the MR-Egger and simple mode estimates were directionally similar but less precise (Table 1; Fig. 2).

Sensitivity and pleiotropy analyses

Cochran’s Q test showed no meaningful heterogeneity in either direction (arrhythmia → sepsis: Q = 46.80, P = 0.562; sepsis → arrhythmia: Q = 0.10, P = 0.756). The MR-Egger intercept test did not support directional pleiotropy (arrhythmia → sepsis intercept = -0.004, SE = 0.006, P = 0.501; sepsis → arrhythmia intercept = 0.004, SE = 0.022, P = 0.871) Table 3). Funnel plots and leave-one-out analyses did not indicate that the results were driven by a single SNP. For the two primary IVW analyses considered jointly, the Benjamini-Hochberg FDR-adjusted q value was 0.0536.

Taken together, these analyses support a stable arrhythmia-to-sepsis signal and a concordant but less certain reverse-direction sepsis-to-arrhythmia signal, rather than an apparent bidirectional relationship created by one isolated association.

Discussion

This study provides genetic epidemiological evidence that cardiac arrhythmia liability is associated with a higher risk of sepsis and that sepsis liability may also contribute to arrhythmia risk. The forward-direction estimate was more robust because it was supported by a larger number of instruments, whereas the reverse-direction estimate was less precise and should be interpreted cautiously.

The present findings are directionally consistent with observational literature showing that arrhythmias, especially new-onset atrial fibrillation, are common in sepsis and are associated with adverse in-hospital outcomes [2–4, 23]. Our bidirectional design extends this literature by testing whether inherited susceptibility on either side of the clinical relationship contributes to risk, thereby reducing the likelihood that the observed association is explained only by acute illness severity or reverse causation.

Several biological pathways could explain these findings. Sepsis can trigger systemic inflammation, cytokine surges, autonomic dysfunction, myocardial injury, and coagulation abnormalities, all of which may increase electrical instability and promote arrhythmogenesis [24–26]. Conversely, pre-existing arrhythmia liability may reflect shared inflammatory, metabolic, or cardiovascular vulnerability that also increases susceptibility to severe infection, although this pathway remains less well understood and requires mechanistic follow-up.

A major strength of this study is the bidirectional MR design using large public GWAS resources, which reduces residual confounding compared with conventional observational analyses and allows evaluation of both causal directions within the same analytical framework [7–11, 27–29]. The use of complementary MR methods, pleiotropy testing, heterogeneity testing, and leave-one-out analysis further strengthened result interpretation.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, all GWAS data were derived from European-ancestry populations, so the results may not generalize to other ancestries. Second, only three SNPs were available for the sepsis-to-arrhythmia analysis, limiting statistical power and precision in the reverse direction. Third, although MR reduces confounding, it cannot completely exclude bias from residual horizontal pleiotropy, phenotype misclassification, or sample characteristics not captured by the available summary statistics. Fourth, the arrhythmia phenotype was broad and did not allow stable subtype-specific analyses in this dataset.

From a clinical perspective, the results support careful infection surveillance in patients with substantial arrhythmia burden and continued rhythm monitoring in patients with sepsis, especially in intensive care and emergency settings. However, these data do not justify assuming that both directions are equally strong; rather, they support a more confident inference for arrhythmia liability increasing sepsis risk and a more cautious interpretation for the reverse pathway.

Overall, the present MR analysis indicates that the observed clinical interplay between sepsis and cardiac arrhythmias is unlikely to be explained solely by one-direction observational bias. Future multi-ancestry studies, better-powered sepsis GWAS datasets, and mechanistic investigations are needed to determine whether the reverse-direction signal is biologically causal and to identify the pathways linking infection and arrhythmogenesis.

Conclusion

This bidirectional two-sample MR study supports a causal contribution of genetic liability to cardiac arrhythmias to sepsis risk and suggests a possible reverse contribution of sepsis liability to cardiac arrhythmias. The findings reinforce the importance of integrated infection and rhythm surveillance while highlighting the need for larger and more detailed validation studies.
