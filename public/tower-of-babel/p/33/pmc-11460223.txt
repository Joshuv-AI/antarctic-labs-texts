Equity and trends in the allocation of health human resources in China from 2012 to 2021
Authors: Yulin Chai, Guowei Xian, Ruxin Kou, Mengxue Wang, Yanxu Liu, Guoqi Fu, Sheng Luo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAs the second most populous country in the world, China has a huge demand for health human resources. Over the past decade, the Chinese government has continuously increased investment in the health sector and implemented relevant policies and systems in an attempt to enhance the overall quantity and equity of health human resources. This study aims to analyze the equity and developmental trends in the allocation of health human resources in China from 2012 to 2021, providing reference and theoretical support for the government’s further optimization of health human resources allocation.MethodsThis study collected health human resources data from 31 provinces, municipalities, and autonomous regions in China from 2012 to 2021. The methods employed included Lorenz curves, Gini coefficients, and agglomeration degree analysis, which examined the developmental trends and equity of health human resources from the perspectives of population and geographical.ResultsThis study found that over the past decade, there has been a significant increase in the total quantity of health human resources in China, accompanied by an improvement in equity, although disparities still exist. There are notable variations in the distribution of health human resources based on geographical area, with disparities between different regions. Relative shortages of health human resources are observed in western regions, while some areas in the eastern and central regions exhibit an abundance or surplus of health human resources.ConclusionOverall, health human resources in China have experienced stable development, with an incremental improvement in allocation equity, yet there remains considerable room for progress. It is imperative to continue strengthening policy guidance and regulation to facilitate the rational flow of health human resources, narrow regional disparities, and achieve the goal of universal health coverage.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13690-024-01407-0.Keywords: Health human resources, Equity, Gini coefficient, Agglomeration degree

Contributions to the literature• This study provides a comprehensive analysis of the equity and trends in health human resource (HHR) allocation in China from 2012 to 2021, a period of significant health sector reforms and growth.• Utilizing Lorenz curves, Gini coefficients, and agglomeration degree analysis, the research offers a nuanced understanding of the spatial and demographic distribution of HHR, revealing areas of surplus and shortage.• It adds to the methodological literature by employing a combination of established and novel analytical tools to assess HHR allocation, offering a replicable approach for other countries facing similar distributional challenges.

Introduction

Health services is an area where market failure is commonly observed. Reasonable health policies and organized allocation of health resources are crucial for health services [1]. Health resources form the foundation of health service development, and the rational allocation of these resources not only affects the health level of the population but also plays an essential role in the sustainable development of health services. Therefore, the fair distribution of health resources has always been a concern [2]. The equitable distribution of health resources is a core goal pursued by the government’s health service system [3]. The fairness of health resource allocation directly impacts the fairness of health services and is one of the conditions for achieving equal access to health services [4].

As a developing country with the second-largest population and the largest aging population, China’s demand for healthcare services continues to grow, and the healthcare system is at risk in the long term [5]. China initiated healthcare system reforms as early as 2009 to increase health investment and allocate medical resources reasonably. Despite its vast territory and complex geographical conditions, China has made significant achievements in health equity in the past few decades [6]. However, there are still deficiencies in the accessibility, structural distribution, and fair distribution of health resources [7]. Although the total amount of health resources has increased significantly, there is spatial aggregation in the distribution of resources, which tends to be concentrated in wealthier areas [8]. In addition, equity in the distribution of health resources by population is better than by geography [9]. As patients from resource-poor areas move to resource-concentrated areas, resources in resource-concentrated areas will become more and more concentrated, ultimately leading to a vicious cycle of resource allocation [10]. The development of socio-economy and changes in population structure have posed new challenges to health services and exacerbated the contradictions in health resource allocation [11]. In 2016, the Chinese government proposed the “Health China 2030” initiative, which stipulates four basic principles of health services: fairness and justice. This initiative requires promoting equal access to essential public health services, maintaining the public welfare nature of essential medical and health services, gradually reducing disparities in essential health services and health levels between urban and rural areas, regions, and population groups, achieving universal health coverage, and promoting social equity. It also proposes that essential health resources should be reasonably distributed based on population and distance while strengthening the construction of health human resources (HHR) [12].

It is widely recognized that HHRs are the most critical part of healthcare resources and are essential to achieving better health. They typically account for a significant portion of healthcare expenditures. They are the foundation of the healthcare system, which can only deliver healthcare if it has adequate human resources [13, 14]. At the same time, HHR is also the core and foundation for a country to achieve universal health coverage [15]. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that by 2030, there will be a global shortage of 10 million healthcare workers, with the majority in low-income and middle-income countries. Different countries face varying degrees of difficulties in healthcare workers’ education, employment, deployment, retention, and performance. Therefore, the WHO has specifically formulated the “Global Strategy on Human Resources for Health: Workforce 2030,” further emphasizing the need to ensure fairness in HHR [16]. The global health workforce is expected to reach 84 million by 2030, but there is still a shortage of 10 million. While the workforce’s size is increasing, this masks considerable inequalities, especially in less developed regions [17]. The most significant factor constraining most countries from achieving equal access to health services is the shortage of HHR. This depends on whether there are sufficient qualified and motivated HHRs and, more importantly, the equitable distribution of HHRs [18].

Currently, there are many studies in academia regarding HHR allocation. Most researchers use methods such as Lorenz curves, Gini coefficients, and Theil indices to reflect the fairness of health resource allocation [19–22]. However, these methods have certain limitations. For example, Lorenz curves and Gini coefficients can reflect the overall fairness level. However, they cannot capture regional differences, while the Theil index can distinguish regional disparities but lacks analysis of geographical factors [23]. In order to comprehensively analyze the impact of geographical factors and population distribution on the fairness of HHR allocation in different regions of China, this study also employs an agglomeration degree analysis to investigate the fairness of HHR allocation in China [24]. Agglomeration degree analysis considers both the fairness of resource allocation based on population and geographical distribution and analyzes regional equity differences.

Therefore, this study analyzes the current status, trends, and fairness of HHR allocation in China from 2012 to 2021. Firstly, Lorenz curves and Gini coefficients are used to discuss the fairness of HHR distribution in terms of geographical and population allocation in China. Based on this, Agglomeration degree analysis is conducted to analyze the regional differences in HHR fairness. This study provides reference and theoretical support for the Chinese government to further optimize HHR allocation.

Methods

Data sources

The relevant data for this study mainly comes from the “China Health Statistics Yearbook” [25–34] and the “China Statistical Yearbook” [35–44] from 2013 to 2022. Referring to existing literature [45]. All persons involved in activities whose primary purpose is to promote health can be characterized as health workers, but this is too broad a scope. Some studies have paid particular attention to the level of education and practical work experience of health workers in order to quantify the professional competence of health workers [46]. Therefore, three populations of health technicians (HT), licensed (assistant) physicians (L(A)P), and registered nurses (RN) with specialized medical education and clinical experience were selected to represent HHR in this study. Among them, HT includes health professionals such as licensed physicians, licensed (assistant) physicians, registered nurses, pharmacists, laboratory technicians, imaging technicians, health supervisors, and trainee physicians (pharmacists, nurses, technicians) engaged in clinical work but excludes health technicians involved in management. L(A)P refers to persons whose “level” of the Physician’s License is “assistant practitioner” and who are engaged in medical treatment and preventive health care, excluding assistant practitioners who are engaged in management. The categories of practicing physician assistants are clinical, traditional Chinese medicine, dental, and public health. RN refers to those who have completed more than 3 years of professional nursing and midwifery courses, including more than 8 months of nursing clinical practice, who have passed the state-organized nursing licensing examination after obtaining the corresponding academic certificates, and who are engaged in nursing, excluding nurses engaged in management.

Evaluation methods

Lorenz curve and Gini coefficient

In health resource allocation, the greater the curvature of the Lorenz curve, the more unequal the resource distribution; conversely, a flatter curve indicates more equality [47]. This study plots the cumulative percentage of HHR on the y-axis. In contrast, the cumulative percentage of the population is plotted on the x-axis to draw the Lorenz curve. The Gini coefficient (1912) measures the degree of income distribution inequality. It is often used in combination with the Lorenz curve. When G < 0.2, it indicates an even distribution; when 0.2 < G < 0.3, it suggests a relatively balanced distribution; when 0.3 < G < 0.4, it signifies a relatively reasonable distribution; when 0.4 < G < 0.5, it indicates a relatively unfair distribution; and when G > 0.5, it suggests a highly unfair distribution [49].

Agglomeration degree analysis

The agglomeration degree represents the degree of concentration of resources within a particular region compared to the superior region, specifically the Population Agglomeration Degree (PAD) and Health Resources Agglomeration Degree (HRAD) [50]. PAD is an essential indicator for evaluating population density, representing the proportion of the population clustered on 1% of the national land area in a particular region. HRAD, on the other hand, is an indicator for evaluating the geographical fairness of health resource allocation, representing the proportion of health resources clustered on 1% of the total land area in a particular region. When PAD ≥ 2, the region is densely populated; when 0.5 ≤ PAD < 2, the region is an area with an average population; when PAD < 0.5, the region is sparsely populated. The closer HRAD is to 1, the better the geographical fairness of HHR allocation in the region. HRAD > 1 indicates sufficient resources, and a value significantly greater than 1 represents resource surplus. Conversely, it indicates unfair distribution or insufficient resources. When HRAD/PAD is closer to 1, it signifies better fairness of health human resources allocation based on regional population distribution. HRAD/PAD > 1 indicates sufficient resources, and a value significantly greater than 1 represents resource surplus. Conversely, it indicates unfair distribution or insufficient resources [51].

Data analysis

We collected and descriptively analyzed data on HHR in China using Stata 18.0. Lorenz curves were plotted and calculated using the Distributive Analysis Stata Package (DASP) developed by Abdelkrim and Duclos of Laval University [52]. In addition, Gini coefficients, PAD, and HRAD were calculated using Microsoft Excel 2016.

Results

Basic status of HHR in China

The total HT in China increased from 6.6755 million in 2012 to 11.2442 million in 2021, while the total L(A)P increased from 2.6161 million in 2012 to 4.2876 million in 2021. Additionally, the total RN increased from 2.4966 million in 2012 to 5.0194 million in 2021. The average annual growth rates from 2012 to 2021 for HT, L(A)P, and RN were 5.96%, 5.64%, and 8.07%, respectively (Fig. 1).

Trends of HHR in China (10,000 population)

In 2021, China had 79.64 HT per 10,000 population, 30.40 L(A)P per 10,000 population, and 35.58 RN per 10,000 population. Among them, 15 regions, including Hebei and Liaoning, had lower than average HT per 10,000 population. 16 regions, including Anhui and Fujian, had lower than average L(A)P per 10,000 population. 11 regions, including Tianjin and Hebei, had lower than average numbers of RN per 10,000 population.

In addition, China had 11,688.92 HT per 10,000 square kilometers, 4,461.14 L(A)P per 10,000 square kilometers, and 5,222.58 RN per 10,000 square kilometers in 2021. 9 regions, including Inner Mongolia and Jilin, had lower than average HT per 10,000 square kilometers. 8 regions, including Inner Mongolia and Heilongjiang, had lower than average L(A)P per 10,000 square kilometers. 9 regions, including Inner Mongolia and Jilin, had lower than average numbers of RN per 10,000 square kilometers. Meanwhile, 7 regions, including Beijing and Shanxi, had significantly higher values for all indicators than the national average (Table 1).

Gini coefficient of HHR in China from 2012–2021

By plotting the trend of Gini coefficients for HHR from 2012 to 2021 (Fig. 2), it can be observed that both dimensions show a decreasing trend, with a more significant decrease in the population dimension. This indicates that the fairness level of HHR distribution in China is improving, especially regarding the population dimension. The Gini coefficient for the population dimension remains below 0.2 (except in 2013, when RN was higher by 0.001). It continues to decrease, indicating a fair and optimizing distribution of HHR regarding the population dimension. However, the Gini coefficient for the geographical dimension consistently remains above 0.6, indicating significant inequality in HHR allocation at the geographical dimension.

Gini coefficient trend of HHR in China from 2012 to 2021

The Lorenz curves for HHR distribution from 2012 to 2021 demonstrate that the curves for both population and geographical dimensions have become less curved, resulting in a smaller residual area between the curves and the 45° line (representing absolute fairness). This suggests a trend towards fairer resource distribution. However, in comparison, the curve for the geographical dimension has a more significant curvature and shows noticeable differences from the curve for the population dimension. It is further away from the line of absolute fairness, indicating poorer fairness in HHR distribution in China’s geographical dimension. For detailed results, please refer to Additional File 1.

Agglomeration degree

Based on the agglomeration degree results of HHR configurations in various regions (Additional file 2), in 2021, the HRAD values of HT in 14 regions such as Beijing (15.077) and Tianjin (8.750) were significantly greater than 1, indicating an excessive allocation of HT based on geographical in these regions, while the HRAD values of HT in 9 regions such as Qinghai (0.061) and Xinjiang (0.103) were less than 1, indicating an insufficient allocation of HT based on geographical in these regions. There is a significant disparity in the fairness of China’s HT allocation based on geography. However, there is a gradual improvement in the fairness of HT allocation based on geography in some regions. For example, Yunnan’s HRAD value for HT increased from 0.610 to 0.827, while Shanxi’s HRAD value decreased from 1.836 to 1.537. The HRAD/PAD values of HT in 16 regions, such as Beijing (1.658) and Tianjin (1.113), were more significant than 1, indicating a relatively sufficient allocation of HT based on population in these regions.

Conversely, the HRAD/PAD values of HT in 15 regions, such as Guangdong (0.864) and Jiangxi (0.850), were less than 1, indicating an insufficient allocation of HT based on population in these regions. Overall, the HRAD/PAD values of HT in most regions are close to 1, indicating a relatively reasonable fairness in China’s allocation of HT based on population. At the same time, the fairness of HT allocation based on population in some regions continues to improve. For example, Hebei’s HRAD/PAD value for HT increased from 0.873 to 0.943, while Beijing’s HRAD/PAD value decreased from 1.917 to 1.658 (Additional file 2: Table S1.2).

The HRAD values of L(A)P in 15 regions, such as Beijing (15.379) and Tianjin (9.753), were significantly greater than 1, indicating an excessive allocation of L(A)P based on geography in these regions. Conversely, the HRAD values of L(A)P in 8 regions, such as Qinghai (0.058) and Xinjiang (0.095), were less than 1, indicating an insufficient allocation of L(A)P based on geographical in these regions. There is a significant disparity in the fairness of China’s L(A)P allocation based on geography. However, there is a trend of gradual improvement in the fairness of L(A)P allocation based on geography in some regions. For example, Ningxia’s HRAD value for L(A)P increased from 0.720 to 0.760, while Jilin’s HRAD value for L(A)P decreased from 1.204 to 1.044. The HRAD/PAD values of L(A)P in 15 regions, such as Beijing (1.691) and Tianjin (1.241), were more significant than 1, indicating a relatively sufficient allocation of L(A)P based on population in these regions. Conversely, the HRAD/PAD values of L(A)P in 16 regions, such as Jiangxi (0.811) and Guangdong (0.829), were less than 1, indicating an insufficient allocation of L(A)P based on the population in these regions. Overall, the HRAD/PAD values of L(A)P in most regions are close to 1, indicating relatively good fairness in China’s allocation of L(A)P based on population. At the same time, the fairness of L(A)P allocation based on population in some regions continues to improve. For example, Gansu’s HRAD/PAD value for L(A)P increased from 0.859 to 0.933, while Beijing’s HRAD/PAD value for L(A)P decreased from 1.852 to 1.691 (Additional file 2: Table S1.3).

The HRAD values of RN in 15 regions, such as Beijing (14.488) and Tianjin (7.532), were significantly greater than 1, indicating an excessive allocation of RN based on geography in these regions. Conversely, the HRAD values of RN in 9 regions, such as Tibet (0.012) and Qinghai (0.057), were less than 1, indicating an insufficient allocation of RN based on geography in these regions. There is a significant disparity in the fairness of China’s RN allocation based on geography. However, there is a gradual improvement in the fairness of RN allocation based on geography in some regions. For example, Yunnan’s HRAD value for RN increased from 0.594 to 0.887, while Beijing’s HRAD value for RN decreased from 18.669 to 14.488. The HRAD/PAD values of RN in 20 regions, such as Beijing (1.593) and Shanghai (1.173), were more significant than 1, indicating a relatively sufficient allocation of RN based on population in these regions.

Conversely, the HRAD/PAD values of RN in 11 regions, such as Tianjin (0.958) and Hebei (0.849), were less than 1, indicating an insufficient allocation of RN based on population in these regions. Overall, the HRAD/PAD values of RN in most regions are close to 1, indicating relatively good fairness in China’s allocation of RN based on population. At the same time, the fairness of RN allocation based on population in some regions continues to improve. For example, Guizhou’s HRAD/PAD value for RN increased from 0.754 to 1.033, while Shanghai’s HRAD/PAD value for RN decreased from 1.435 to 1.173 (Additional file 2: Table S1.4).

Overall, there is a significant disparity in the fairness of China’s HHR allocation based on geography, while the allocation based on population is relatively reasonable. Furthermore, there is a trend of gradual improvement in fairness in both dimensions, consistent with the results of the Lorenz curve and the Gini coefficient.

Discussion

This study evaluated the changes in China’s HHR over a decade from 2012 to 2021. Overall, the number of HHRs in China has steadily grown, with continuous increases in HT, L(A)P, and RN. The HHR per 10,000 population and 10,000 square kilometers have also continuously increased. In 2021, China had 30.4 L(A)P per 10,000 population, surpassing the primary target of 3 L(A)P per 1,000 population set in the “Health China 2030” initiative. However, China’s total number and growth rate of RN still significantly lag behind developed countries. According to the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) report, 2021 the average RN per 1,000 population in OECD member countries was 9.2 [53], far higher than China’s 3.6. Additionally, in 2021, China’s physician-nurse ratio was only 1:1.17, far from the government’s latest proposed ratio of 1:2.

Based on the results of Lorenz curves, Gini coefficients, and Agglomeration degree analysis, China’s HHR distribution is relatively equitable based on population (Gini coefficient is consistently less than 0.2 most of the time, and most areas’ HRAD/PAD is close to 1). In contrast, India, another populous country, still faces significant challenges in the equitable distribution of HHR [54]. This indicates that China has made significant efforts in this aspect and has reaped rewards. However, as one of the world’s largest countries with a land area of approximately 9.6 million square kilometers, accounting for 1/15 of the Earth’s land area [55], uneven geographical distribution is the primary issue in China’s healthcare human resource allocation. There are significant disparities in the geographical distribution of HHR in China, with the quantity of HHR in economically developed regions far exceeding that in underdeveloped regions. This uneven distribution is also more severe in developing countries such as Cameroon and Rwanda [56, 57]. It is encouraging that, although, to a small extent, both the Gini coefficient and HRAD based on the geographical distribution in China tend towards equity (the Gini coefficient is continuously decreasing, and HRAD in each region is approaching 1). However, there is still considerable room for improvement. Furthermore, we can observe that while the numbers of HT, L(A)P, and RN are all increasing and becoming more equitable, there are discrepancies in the growth rates of their total numbers and the level of equity improvement. Among them, RN’s growth rate and equity improvement are the highest, while those of L(A)P rank lowest.

On the one hand, this may be attributed to the government’s policy bias and increased health financial investment, which has not only led to increased investment in HHR in various regions of China to enhance the total HHR but has also taken fairness into more significant consideration in resource allocation, further optimizing resource allocation. However, since the new health reform, more attention has been focused on the population-based allocation of health resources, significantly promoting the rational flow of HHR but paying attention to the geographical distribution constraints of HHR. Combined with geographical differences, economic disparities, and varying levels of attention between regions, the fairness of China’s HHR allocation based on geographical distribution has always been weaker than that based on population distribution [58]. Therefore, in formulating policies in the future, the government should strengthen its leading role, fully consider regional differences, and reduce the unfairness caused by factors such as population, geography, and economy. While promoting the overall development of HHR, it should focus on reasonable layout, balanced structure, and equitable allocation, encourage regions with sufficient resources to improve the quality and efficiency of HHR further and assist areas with insufficient resources, achieving resource sharing. Meanwhile, regions with adequate resources should strive to narrow the gap, adapt measures to local conditions, seize opportunities, and increase the number of HHR [59].

On the other hand, although China’s overall development of HHR tends to be equitable, whether based on population or geographical distribution, Gini coefficients and Agglomeration degree analysis indicate that the equity of L(A)P’ allocation is currently the poorest among the three. This shows that China is still in the exploratory stage regarding reasonably allocating L(A)P and requires further reforms and improvements in policies and systems. Therefore, the government should strengthen investigations into the allocation of L(A)P, promptly and accurately identify the reasons for unfair distribution, and effectively formulate targeted and operational policies and systems, such as allowing physicians to practice at multiple institutions, developing general practitioners, and implementing free targeted training for medical students [60].

Furthermore, according to HRAD, China’s HHR suffers from inefficiency and resource surplus in certain regions. This may be due to local governments blindly responding to central policy requirements, overlooking the actual needs of regions and residents, and indiscriminately increasing healthcare resource input without considering resource allocation. This meets residents’ growing health service needs and leads to a waste of limited healthcare human resources, increasing the burden on the country, collectives, and individuals and exacerbating unfair HHR distribution. Therefore, with the advancement of science, technology, economy, and social development, as the distribution and utilization of health resources continue to expand, the government should keep pace with the times, enhance the construction of the health resource network, break through the limitations of geographical conditions, promote spatial sharing of health resources, and reduce excessive input and waste of health resources [61].

Conclusion

Over the past decade, China has placed a significant emphasis on the development of its health system, allocating substantial financial resources towards the expansion of its health infrastructure and implementing a series of policies designed to ensure fairness and sustainability in alignment with the World Health Organization’s objective of achieving universal health coverage. Our analysis indicates that challenges persist despite China’s notable advancement in total HHR and attaining specific policy objectives. In particular, there are ongoing concerns regarding geographical disparities, with western regions needing more HHR compared to more adequately or surplus-provisioned central and eastern areas.

Continuous policy reforms and institutional adjustments must be implemented to address these challenges effectively. It is recommended that the government implement targeted interventions to rectify geographic imbalances and ensure effective policy implementation to foster equitable and sustainable development of health resources. The findings highlight the necessity for targeted policy measures to address the geographical imbalances in the distribution of health resources (HHR). Policies should aim to incentivize the deployment of health professionals in underserved western regions and develop strategies to balance HHR across different areas.

This study acknowledges several limitations. First, our analysis was constrained to three representative HHR groups, potentially overlooking broader distributional trends. Second, our focus on geographic and population-based equity is separate from other factors, such as economic status and living conditions, which could further influence HHR distribution. Finally, data constraints prevented the inclusion of the most recent figures from 2022, which could enhance the relevance and accuracy of future analyses.
