Cities at Sea: Coastal Urbanization Generates Local Biodiversity Hotspots but Homogenizes Marine Fish Communities Regionally
Authors: Bastien Macé, Erwan Delrieu‐Trottin, David Mouillot, Alice Valentini, Morgane Bruno, Laure Velez, Amandine Avouac, Julie Deter, Thomas Bockel, Marie Orblin, Pierre Boissery, Gwénolé Le Roux, Stéphanie Manel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTUrbanization is rapidly expanding along the world's coasts, transforming natural ecosystems into increasingly uniform artificial environments. Yet, the patterns and processes shaping marine biodiversity under urban pressure remain poorly understood. We investigated whether coastal urbanization drives marine communities toward greater similarity, a process well documented in terrestrial and freshwater systems as urban biotic homogenization but rarely examined in the sea. We analyzed fish communities using 222 seawater environmental DNA samples collected across Mediterranean seaports and less urbanized coastal sites. Seaports exhibited higher local species richness than less urbanized sites, yet their communities were markedly more similar to one another, revealing a local‐regional decoupling paradox in which gains in local diversity coincided with biotic homogenization at the regional scale. This pattern emerged in the absence of non‐indigenous species, which are commonly invoked as main drivers of homogenization. Instead, homogenization was primarily driven by increased occupancy (i.e., the proportion of sites occupied) of disturbance‐tolerant native taxa, half of which showed occupancy levels approximately 2.6 times higher in seaports than elsewhere. Biotic homogenization was evident at both taxonomic and phylogenetic levels, indicating that disturbance‐tolerant taxa are phylogenetically clustered. Overall, urban pressure emerged as the dominant driver of community similarity, exceeding spatial and environmental predictors. Together, these results show that coastal urbanization emerges as a fundamental and underappreciated driver of marine biodiversity reorganization.

Introduction

Human activities are profoundly altering biodiversity, but accumulated evidence reveals a more nuanced picture than a simple narrative of a pervasive biodiversity loss (Dornelas et al. 2023). While biomass and abundances have declined for several taxonomic groups and more species are now at risk of extinction (Díaz et al. 2019), community datasets show little change in local species richness across ecosystems but instead, major shifts in the taxonomic composition of communities through species turnover across space and time (Dornelas et al. 2014, 2023; Keck et al. 2025), particularly in the marine realm (Blowes et al. 2019). Such community reorganization renews attention on biotic homogenization, revealing that its mechanisms and outcomes remain more complex than previously assumed. The biotic homogenization process, known to reduce ecological distinctiveness among ecosystems and undermine their resilience (Lockwood and McKinney 2001; Olden et al. 2004), was originally defined as the replacement of local biotas by non‐indigenous species (McKinney and Lockwood 1999). The process was later broadened to encompass any mechanism leading to increased species similarity among communities over time (Olden and Rooney 2006). Recent studies (Blowes et al. 2024; Rolls et al. 2023) have further clarified its underlying mechanisms: biotic homogenization arises when the change in local diversity (α‐diversity, the number of species in a site) exceeds the change in regional diversity (γ‐diversity, the overall number of species). Following Whittaker's definition of β‐diversity (β = γ/α; (Whittaker 1960)), this situation leads to a reduction in β‐diversity as the proportion of sites occupied by a species (i.e., occupancy) increases on average (Arita et al. 2008).

Although no consistent global trend toward community homogenization has emerged and patterns remain highly context‐dependent (Blowes et al. 2024; Keck et al. 2025), including in the marine realm (Kitchel et al. 2025) urban ecosystems emerge as a notable exception. In these environments, biological communities tend to become increasingly similar with rising urban intensity (Chien 2025; Lokatis and Jeschke 2022), leading to the early adoption of the term “urban biotic homogenization” (McKinney 2006). This phenomenon is well documented in terrestrial and freshwater systems (Groffman et al. 2014; Knop 2016; Piano et al. 2020; Rahel 2002; Sidemo‐Holm et al. 2022), but the marine realm has received comparatively little attention (Lokatis and Jeschke 2022). Yet, shoreline ecosystems are increasingly impacted by urbanization worldwide (Bugnot et al. 2021) under the ever‐increasing human population growth on the coast (Cosby et al. 2024; Small and Nicholls 2003). Consequently, nearly all coastlines are now heavily artificialized (Williams et al. 2022) by infrastructures such as seaports, breakwaters, and seawalls which have replaced naturally diverse habitats with simplified, engineered substrates (Firth et al. 2016). Beyond physical transformation, coastal urbanization also encompasses multiple anthropogenic stressors such as noise, light, and chemical pollution, as well as increased tourism and maritime traffic (Dafforn et al. 2015; Duarte et al. 2021; Todd et al. 2019). These alterations are expected to disrupt ecological connectivity at both population and community levels (Bishop et al. 2017) and to transform biodiversity patterns with changes in the composition of biological communities (Firth et al. 2016; Todd et al. 2019). Yet, despite these extensive impacts, the consequences of coastal urbanization on marine biodiversity remain largely overlooked (Graells et al. 2021; Madon et al. 2023), and relatively few studies have explicitly examined whether such community shifts translate into biotic homogenization in marine ecosystems.

Here, we assess how coastal urbanization influences marine biodiversity by investigating differences in fish community composition across sites under contrasting levels of urban pressure along the highly human‐transformed French Mediterranean coast (Coll et al. 2012), where the spatial extent of artificial coastal land has remained stable over the past four decades (Bottin et al. 2022). We aimed to test whether urban biotic homogenization occurs at taxonomic, phylogenetic, and functional levels, comparing communities in seaports (highly urbanized sites) with other coastal areas located outside seaports (less urbanized sites). Based on recent theoretical frameworks linking biotic homogenization to increased occupancy (Blowes et al. 2024; Rolls et al. 2023), we expect homogenization in our system to result from either (i) the extirpation of low‐occupancy species (i.e., low proportion of sites occupied) in seaports, or (ii) increased occupancy of species outweighing the occupancy loss of other species. A third mechanism, homogenization through gains in seaport regional diversity driven by non‐indigenous species—often considered a major cause of biotic homogenization—is less likely in our study region, where few such species have been reported to date among fishes (Azzurro et al. 2022). Finally, we evaluated the relative influence of urbanization, environmental, and spatial factors on community dissimilarity to identify the main drivers shaping marine fish communities. Urban pressure is expected to be a major driver of community change, although substrate cover and geographic distances are also likely to contribute substantially. This study provides a unique opportunity to assess whether human‐induced homogenization occurs in coastal marine ecosystems and investigate the underlying mechanisms, a dimension of global change still largely overlooked.

Material and Methods

Experimental Design

This study is part of the BioDivMed project, which surveyed marine fish biodiversity along the entire French Mediterranean coast using environmental DNA (eDNA) metabarcoding and was conducted during summer 2023. To capture the gradient of marine urbanization along the coast, we restricted our analyses to the coastal sampling sites located in waters shallower than 50 m. This subset includes 111 sites: 87 along the mainland coastline and 24 around Corsica, each sampled in duplicate, yielding a total of 222 eDNA samples, including 20 recreational seaports (i.e., marinas) and 91 coastal sites located outside seaports (Figure 1A; Data S1). This sampling design provided both an urbanization contrast between seaports and outside areas and an overall continuous gradient of urban pressure to test our hypotheses (Figure 1B–D).

This shift is hypothesized to be associated with lower community dissimilarity in seaports compared to other coastal sites (Δβ < 0), reflecting biotic homogenization (the opposite pattern would indicate differentiation). This process would result from (i) the extirpation of low‐occupancy species from seaports, or (ii) species‐specific responses to urbanization with occupancy gains (increases) outweighing losses (decreases) overall (adapted from Blowes' diagram (Blowes et al. 2024) where space substitutes for time). (D) Community dissimilarity is expected to be primarily driven by differences in urban pressure, which may exert a stronger influence than substrate cover or geographic distance.

All sampling was conducted from a boat. For each sample, 30 L of seawater were filtered along a transect 1 m below the sea surface through a VigiDNA 0.2 μm crossflow filtration capsule (SPYGEN, France) connected to sterile tubing using an Athena peristaltic pump (Proactive Environmental Products, USA) at a 1.0 L/min nominal flow. Single‐use equipment (gloves, tubing, and capsule) was employed for each sampling event and contact between the pump and any other components was avoided. All surfaces involved in kit handling were decontaminated with bleach prior to sampling. After filtration, capsules were emptied of remaining seawater, filled with 80 mL of CL1 conservation buffer (SPYGEN), and stored at room temperature until DNA extraction. Transects were carried out from the boat to capture representative areas: within seaports, the largest area possible was covered, while outside seaports, 2 km‐long transects parallel to the coastline were performed.

Environmental DNA Extraction, Amplification, and Sequencing

DNA extraction, amplification, and sequencing were conducted following a previously described protocol (Pichot et al. 2025). Briefly, two independent extractions per sample were carried out alongside negative extraction controls to monitor potential contamination using the NucleoSpin Soil kit (Macherey‐Nagel, Germany), and the two extracts per sample were pooled. PCR amplifications were performed using the teleo primer pair (Valentini et al. 2016), targeting a ~60 bp fragment of the 12S mitochondrial DNA region in fish (teleosts and elasmobranchs). Twelve PCR replicates per sample were run, each with a unique eight nucleotide tag for downstream identification (identical tag for both the forward and the reverse primer). Negative PCR controls were analyzed in parallel. Amplified products were quantified, purified, and pooled to reach a theoretical depth of 1,000,000 reads per sample before library preparation. High‐throughput sequencing libraries were prepared using the TruSeq DNA PCR‐Free kit (Illumina, USA) and sequenced on a NextSeq 1000 (Illumina) using a P1 or P2 kit (Illumina).

Bioinformatic Pipeline and Data Processing

Sequencing outputs were processed through a bioinformatic pipeline (Guerin et al. 2023) detailed in Table S1 that clusters similar sequences into molecular operational taxonomic units (MOTUs), used as proxies for species (Marques et al. 2020). Prior to clustering, paired‐end reads were assembled into amplicons, demultiplexed, primer‐trimmed, filtered by length (20–120 bp), sequences containing IUPAC ambiguities discarded, and dereplicated. MOTU clustering was performed using swarm (Mahé et al. 2021). After chimera detection, the most abundant sequence from each MOTU was compared against a reference database for taxonomic assignment. This database was built using in silico PCR with CRABS v1.0.3 (Jeunen et al. 2023) allowing up to three nucleotide mismatches per primer. It combined two sources: GenBank sequences (release 264) downloaded via MIDORI2 (Leray et al. 2022) and a custom regional 12S dataset of fish sequences. Post‐processing steps, including filtering of errors arising from tag‐jump and index‐hopping, removal of MOTUs with fewer than 10 reads, PCR control filtering, chimera removal, and retention of sequences assigned to Actinopterygii or Chondrichthyes, as well as curation with the post‐clustering algorithm lulu (Frøslev et al. 2017) were performed.

Both samples from each of the 111 study sites were aggregated to produce a single representative dataset per site. This aggregation resulted in a final MOTU table summarizing the presence and abundance of each MOTU across all sites. All sites exhibited adequate diversity saturation with sufficient read depths (Figure S1) and were retained for further analyses. We filtered the initial dataset to retain coastal Mediterranean marine MOTUs and minimize potential biases by removing low‐confidence detections, taxa requiring higher sampling effort for reliable detection, and highly exploited commercial species associated with seaports (see Supporting Information Methods). This stringent filtering preserved overall biodiversity patterns while reducing potential artefacts (Table S2). Final MOTU presence‐absence table is given in Data S2 and full assignments are detailed in Data S3.

Statistical Analyses

All statistical analyses were conducted in R v4.3.2. Biodiversity patterns were assessed at three complementary levels: taxonomic, phylogenetic, and functional. Analyses requiring species‐level identity, including hierarchical modeling of species communities (HMSC), phylogenetic and functional analyses (see below) were restricted to MOTUs assigned to species or species complexes. For these analyses, occurrences of MOTUs sharing the same assignment were merged across sites, resulting in a subset of 120 species‐level MOTUs. For phylogenetic diversity analyses, a tree was built from the species obtained in our dataset using the Rabosky phylogeny (Rabosky et al. 2018), implemented in the fishtree package v0.3.4 (Chang et al. 2019). This phylogeny is restricted to teleosts, therefore seven MOTUs assigned to elasmobranchs were excluded, and because Spicara flexuosa is not distinguished from Spicara maena in this phylogeny, their corresponding MOTUs were combined, resulting in a final set of 112 MOTUs. One species was randomly selected within each complex to represent the MOTU. For functional analyses, functional traits were compiled for the 120 species‐level MOTUs using data from FishMed (Albouy et al. 2015) and FishBase, accessed via the rfishbase package v5.0.1 (Boettiger et al. 2012). The resulting trait matrix included vertical distribution in the water column, preferred substrate, adult diet type, trophic level, maximum adult length, tolerance to brackish water, and diadromy (see Table S3 for details). For species complexes, the mean trait value across all species in each complex was used. In all other analyses, the full MOTU dataset (160 MOTUs) was retained.

A synthetic urban pressure index (UPI) was derived for each site from fine‐scale anthropogenic pressure layers using a principal component analyses, with scores along the first axis (53.5% variance explained, Figure S2) scaled between 0 and 1 to represent a gradient of urbanization (see Supporting Information Methods).

Comparing Diversity Patterns Between Seaports and Other Coastal Sites

We used MOTU richness to compare local diversity (taxonomic α‐diversity) between seaports and other coastal sites. We fitted a Poisson generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) implemented with the fitme function from the spaMM package v4.5.0 (Rousset and Ferdy 2014), and tested the difference in richness with a likelihood ratio test. A Matérn spatial covariance structure based on site coordinates was included to account for potential spatial autocorrelation. Residual spatial autocorrelation was assessed with Moran's I, using exponential distance‐decay weight matrices at a 5 km scale, constructed separately for mainland and Corsican sites from marine distances, and calculated with the marmap package v1.0.10 (Pante et al. 2023). No significant spatial autocorrelation was detected after model fitting (Moran's I = −0.122, p = 0.937). To compare regional richness (γ‐diversity) between seaports and other coastal sites, MOTU accumulation curves were estimated separately for seaports and coastal sites with the iNEXT package v3.0.1 (Chao et al. 2014; Hsieh et al. 2024) with Hill numbers at q = 0. The asymptotic values of these curves, representing the expected number of MOTUs under exhaustive sampling, were used as proxies for γ‐diversity.

Phylogenetic α‐diversity was computed by calculating Faith's phylogenetic diversity (PD) using the Rabosky phylogeny with the pd function from the picante package v1.8.2 (Kembel et al. 2010). Because PD is strongly correlated with species richness (Figure S3), standardized effect size (SES.PD) was computed with the ses.pd. function (picante package) using the trial‐swap algorithm, which maintains species occurrence frequencies and site richness constant in the null model. Difference between seaports and other coastal sites was evaluated using a linear model with significance assessed by a t‐test. Functional α‐diversity was computed from a trait‐based Gower distance matrix derived from the species trait matrix. An UPGMA clustering was performed on this Gower distance matrix, resulting in a functional dendrogram. Functional analogue of PD, i.e., functional diversity (FD), was then calculated similarly with the picante package. SES.FD was generated with the trial‐swap null model to control for richness (Figure S4). Difference between seaports and other coastal sites was evaluated using a linear model with significance assessed by a t‐test.

To identify urbanophile and urbanophobic species, we fitted a hierarchical joint species distribution model using the Hmsc package v3.0.13 (Tikhonov et al. 2022). Seaports were included as a fixed effect in the model, and taxonomic family as a species trait, assuming a probit distribution. Four MCMC chains were run, each with 5000 samples, a thinning interval of 1, and a burn‐in of 10,000. We then assessed MCMC convergence by checking the effective sample sizes and Gelman‐Rubin diagnostics for the species‐level (β) and trait‐level (γ) parameters. To identify species traits associated with seaports, community‐weighted mean (CWM) of each trait or modality was computed from the trait matrix using the functcomp function from the FD package v1.0.12.3 (Laliberté et al. 2014; Laliberté and Legendre 2010), with all species weighted equally. Differences in CWMs between seaports and coastal sites were tested with Wilcoxon rank‐sum tests.

A linear model restricted to seaports was also fitted to assess whether intrinsic seaport characteristics influenced MOTU richness. Specifically, we tested the effects of (i) the water surface area within the seaport, (ii) the presence of hydrological connections to freshwater streams or Mediterranean lagoons, as some seaports are located at river mouths or directly connected to lagoons, and (iii) the dominant substrate type outside the seaport, which can consist of Posidonia oceanica meadows, rocky bottoms, or sandy areas. Statistical significance of model coefficients was evaluated using t‐tests.

Assessing Homogenization of Communities in Seaports

Differences in community composition between sites (taxonomic β‐diversity) were quantified using pairwise Jaccard dissimilarities to the presence‐absence MOTU matrix using the beta.pair function from the betapart package v1.6 (Baselga et al. 2023). Sites with fewer than three MOTUs were excluded to avoid inflation of dissimilarity due to very small communities, resulting in 99 retained sites (20 seaports and 79 other coastal sites). To control for potential effects of richness differences, pairwise Raup‐Crick dissimilarities were also computed using the raupcrick function from the vegan package v2.6.10 (Oksanen et al. 2025). Jaccard and Raup‐Crick dissimilarity matrices were compared through a Mantel test, and a Procrustes analysis was applied on their respective principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) ordinations. Both tests indicated strong concordance between the two approaches (r = 0.807 and 0.859, respectively), confirming that observed patterns primarily reflect compositional differences rather than richness variation. Pairwise Jaccard derived phylogenetic dissimilarities (phylogenetic β‐diversity) were similarly calculated using the phylo.beta.pair function (betapart package), based on the species‐level MOTUs presence‐absence matrix (without elasmobranchs and with Spicara flexuosa and Spicara maena combined) and the Rabosky phylogeny. Functional β‐diversity was computed by first performing a PCoA on the Gower distance matrix of species traits, retaining the first four axes (74.34% of variance). These trait axes were then combined with the presence‐absence species‐level MOTUs matrix to compute pairwise Jaccard derived functional dissimilarities using the functional.beta.pair function (betapart package). Sites with fewer than five species were excluded to ensure adequate trait representation (87 sites: 20 seaports and 67 other coastal sites). For all three diversity dimensions (taxonomic, phylogenetic, and functional), PCoA was performed on the respective pairwise Jaccard dissimilarity matrices to visualize compositional patterns among sites.

To assess urban biotic homogenization, i.e., reduced β‐diversity among seaports compared to other coastal sites, differences in community composition were tested for each β‐diversity facet using PERMANOVA implemented in adonis2 (vegan package), with 999 permutations restricted to resampling among coastal sites to account for unequal sample sizes. Differences in dispersion around group centroids were assessed with PERMDISP using betadisper and permutest (vegan package), applying the same permutation constraints. Additionally, pairwise distances in the PCoA spaces were compared between groups using Gamma generalized linear mixed models (site ID as a random effect; lme4 package v1.1.37 (Bates et al. 2015)), with 999 restricted permutations. Bootstrap p‐values were computed for all these analyses.

A distance‐based redundancy analysis (dbRDA) was performed on pairwise Jaccard dissimilarities using the capscale function (vegan package) to test whether intrinsic seaport characteristics influenced community composition. Explanatory variables (Data S1) included the water surface area within the seaport, the presence of hydrological connections to freshwater streams or Mediterranean lagoons, and the dominant substrate type outside the seaport ( Posidonia oceanica meadows, rocky bottoms, or sandy areas). The significance of these variables was assessed using permutation tests with the anova.cca function (vegan package), and patterns were visualized using PCoA based on the Jaccard dissimilarity matrix.

Investigating Mechanisms Behind the Homogenization Process

We explored potential mechanisms underlying the urban biotic homogenization obtained between seaports and other coastal sites following the Blowes framework (Blowes et al. 2024). Changes in local (Δα) and regional (Δγ) diversity were quantified as log ratios: Δα¯=logα¯Seaportα¯Coastal, Δγ=logγSeaportγCoastal; where ᾱ is site‐level mean MOTU richness and γ is the asymptote of the accumulation curve. Confidence intervals (from GLMM estimates and accumulation curve asymptotes) were used to account for uncertainty and to calculate minimum and maximum values reported in the Blowes' diagram. Because these changes are directly linked to species occupancy through Whittaker's definition of diversity (Arita et al. 2008; Whittaker 1960), we also quantified occupancy differences between seaports and other coastal sites for each species as the log ratio of mean occupancy (ΔOccupancy¯=logOccupancy¯SeaportOccupancy¯Coastal). To do this, we fitted a Bayesian multi‐species occupancy model including seaport presence as a site‐level covariate using the NeMO package v1.0.0 (Macé et al. 2026).

Evaluating Anthropogenic, Environmental, and Spatial Drivers of Community Dissimilarity

To explore whether urban pressure at non‐seaport sites is associated with their compositional similarity to seaports, we computed the distance of each coastal site to the seaport centroid in the taxonomic, phylogenetic, and functional PCoA spaces. We then fitted linear models including three predictors: (i) the UPI, (ii) the marine geographic distance to the nearest seaport (calculated using the marmap package and given in Data S1), and (iii) their interaction. Collinearity between the two continuous predictors was checked and found to be relatively strong (Spearman's ρ = −0.65). The significance of each predictor was evaluated using t‐tests on the estimated model coefficients.

We applied generalized dissimilarity models (GDMs) to matrices of pairwise community dissimilarities (based on Jaccard distances) at the taxonomic, phylogenetic, and functional levels using the gdm package v1.6.0.7 (Fitzpatrick et al. 2025). GDM is a flexible nonlinear extension of matrix regression that models how biological dissimilarity between sites varies as a function of differences in environmental or spatial predictors. It explicitly relates pairwise community dissimilarities to pairwise site distances, allowing the relative contribution of multiple predictors to be assessed. Three predictors were included: difference in UPI, marine geographic distance (calculated using the marmap package), and difference in substrate cover (derived from fine‐scale habitat maps, see Supporting Information Methods). Before fitting the GDMs, we conducted Mantel tests to assess the overall correlations between community dissimilarities and each predictor. To evaluate the functional form of the relationships between predictors and community dissimilarities, we plotted the fitted GDM I‐splines and quantified their uncertainty using non‐parametric bootstrapping with the plotUncertainty function, performing 999 iterations with 70% resampling of site pairs at each iteration. Analyses were conducted separately for mainland and Corsican sites, as including both would have introduced bias due to the much larger geographic distances.

Results

Characterization of Urbanized Fish Communities

Comparison of local diversity between seaports and other coastal areas revealed significantly higher MOTU richness (taxonomic α‐diversity) in seaports, with nearly twice as many species on average (χ 2 = 21.26, p < 0.001; Figure 2A). In contrast, comparison of estimated asymptotes from accumulation curves indicated higher regional richness (γ‐diversity) outside seaports (174 MOTUs; 95% confidence interval: [156–217]) than inside (130 MOTUs; 95% confidence interval: [114–173]), but confidence intervals slightly overlap (Figure 2B). The accumulation curves also suggested that the sampled habitats did not completely capture the full diversity of the region, although approximately 80% of the asymptote was reached in both seaport and other coastal sites. Considering species‐level MOTUs, the patterns observed for PD and FD metrics were consistent with taxonomic α‐diversity patterns (Figures S3 and S4). However, because both PD and FD were strongly correlated with species richness (Spearman's ρ = 0.967 and 0.983 respectively), we computed standardized effect sizes (SES) to account for this dependency. SES.PD was lower in seaports than in other coastal sites (F = 17.33, p < 0.001; Figure 2C), indicating reduced phylogenetic diversity after controlling for richness. In contrast, SES.FD did not differ significantly between seaports and other coastal sites (F = 1.07, p = 0.303; Figure 2D).

Hierarchical modeling of species communities (HMSC) on species‐level MOTUs identified taxa that were positively or negatively associated with seaports. Following the terminology of Wittig (Wittig et al. 1985), these taxa are hereafter referred to as urbanophile (mostly associated with seaports) and urbanophobic (least associated). For 35 species and four families, the 95% credible interval of the seaport effect did not overlap zero, indicating significant associations (Figure 3A; Figure S5). Among these species, 23 were urbanophile (19% of the total) and 12 were urbanophobic (10% of the total). Urbanophiles included Sparidae (e.g., Diplodus spp., Pagellus spp.), Gobiidae (e.g., Gobius spp., Pomatoschistus spp., Zosterisessor ophiocephalus ), and Mugilidae (e.g., Chelon spp.), along with species such as Atherina boyeri , Dicentrarchus labrax , and Salaria pavo . Conversely, urbanophobics were Labridae (e.g., Coris julis , Symphodus ocellatus ), together with Muraena helena , Sardinella aurita , and Tripterygion tripteronotum. Functional trait analyses based on CWMs (traits listed in Table S3) revealed clear differences between seaports and other coastal sites (Figure 3B). Seaport communities were dominated by benthic species (W = 1335, p = 0.001) with preference for sandy substrates (W = 1632.5, p < 0.001). They also included proportionally more omnivorous than carnivorous species (W = 1223, p = 0.016; W = 617, p = 0.025), which translated into a lower overall trophic level (W = 612, p = 0.022). In addition, species associated with seaports tended to be tolerant to brackish conditions (W = 1267.5, p = 0.006) and to include diadromous migrants (W = 1560, p < 0.001).

Biotic Homogenization Associated to Urbanization

Clear differences in community composition between seaports and other coastal sites were observed at the taxonomic, phylogenetic, and functional levels in PcoA ordinations, and were confirmed by PERMANOVAs (Figure 4A, Figure S6). Comparison of intra‐group mean pairwise distances in the PCoA space revealed significantly lower β‐diversity between seaports (i.e., greater similarity among their communities) than between other coastal sites at the taxonomic and phylogenetic levels (Figure 4B, Figure S7). Consistently, the mean distance of sites to their group centroid in the PCoA space was smaller for seaports than for other coastal sites, except at the functional level (Figure 4C; Figure S8), indicating reduced within‐group variability. Together, these results demonstrated taxonomic and phylogenetic homogenization of fish communities within seaports compared to outside areas.

Within seaports, biodiversity patterns were not influenced by inherent seaport characteristics (Data S1) such as water surface area, the presence of hydrological connections to freshwater streams or Mediterranean lagoons (10 seaports concerned), and the dominant substrate type outside the seaport ( Posidonia oceanica meadows, rocky bottoms, or sandy areas). None of these potential confounding variables showed a significant effect on MOTU richness (Table S4) or community composition (Table S5; Figure S9).

Ecological Explanation of the Urban Biotic Homogenization Process

The mechanism underlying the homogenization process (Blowes et al. 2024) was investigated by calculating the changes in local (Δᾱ) and regional diversity (Δγ). Considering the confidence intervals around these estimates, we obtained Δᾱ > 0 (Δᾱ min = 0.27; Δᾱ max = 0.93) and Δγ that was mostly negative, with a small portion above zero (Δγ min = −0.65; Δγ max = 0.1), confirming communities' homogenization as illustrated in the Blowes' diagram (Figure 5A, range below the Δγ = Δα line). Most values fell within the Δᾱ > 0 and Δγ < 0 region, indicating that this homogenization was driven by an overall increase in probabilities of species to occupy sites (i.e., occupancy gain) in seaports which outweighed occupancy losses and local extirpations. This mechanism therefore resulted in gains in α‐diversity (Δᾱ > 0) but losses in γ‐diversity (Δγ < 0). A small portion of the resulting range in the Blowes' diagram fell within the Δγ > 0 region, due to overlap in the γ‐diversity confidence interval (Figure 2B), although an actual gain in fish species within seaports remains unlikely given their dispersal abilities. This increase in average occupancy associated with homogenization (Blowes et al. 2024) was supported by a positive effect of seaports on occupancy probability estimates (posterior mean = 0.392, 95% highest density interval: [0.005–0.776]). When analyzing occupancy changes for each MOTU individually between seaport and other coastal sites (Figure 5B), we observed a similar proportion of MOTUs with occupancy gains (n = 84) and losses (n = 76). The MOTUs with gain (Figure 5B, green distribution) have occupancy changes higher than MOTUs with loss (Figure 5B, red distribution). Indeed, MOTUs showing occupancy loss decreased from a mean occupancy of 0.10 in sites outside seaports to only 0.04 in seaports (mean change = −0.06, Figure 5C). In other words, they occupy 10% of sites outside seaports but only 4% of the seaports surveyed, corresponding to a 60% reduction. In contrast, MOTUs showing occupancy gains increased from a mean occupancy of 0.07 outside seaports to 0.25 within seaports (mean change = +0.18), representing a 257% increase. Some MOTUs were detected exclusively within seaports (n = 18), whereas others were restricted to sites outside seaports outside (n = 58). However, these patterns should be interpreted with caution, as accumulation curves indicated that our sampling did not capture the full MOTU diversity (Figure 2B). With a more exhaustive sampling effort, some species currently detected only outside might also be found within seaports, and vice versa. Most of these MOTUs have low overall occupancy values (Figure S10), suggesting that they are generally rare across both seaports and other coastal areas. Nevertheless, notable exceptions were observed. For instance, Sardinella aurita (proportion of sites occupied outside seaports = 0.429) and Coris julis (0.161) were relatively common outside seaports but were not detected inside, suggesting potential local extirpation. Conversely, some species likely rare outside seaports appeared to be widespread within seaports, such as Salaria pavo (proportion of seaports occupied = 0.677), or relatively common, like Pomatoschistus microps (0.198), and Zosterisessor ophiocephalus (0.186).

Community Dissimilarity Decay With Urbanization

For non‐seaport sites, the distance of each point to the seaport centroid in the PCoA space, reflecting how far its community is from the mean seaport community, was compared with both the urban pressure index (UPI) and the geographic distance to the nearest seaport. A significant negative linear relationship was observed with UPI at the taxonomic (slope = −0.201, p < 0.001), phylogenetic (slope = −0.217, p < 0.001), and functional (slope = −0.231, p < 0.001) levels, indicating that higher urban pressure was associated with community composition being closer to the mean seaport community (Figure 4D). Neither the distance to the nearest seaport nor the interaction term was significant (Tables S6–S8). It suggests that the similarity of non‐seaport communities to the mean seaport community was driven primarily by local urban pressure rather than by geographic proximity to a seaport.

We assessed relationships between community dissimilarity and urban (difference in UPI), environmental (difference in substrate cover) as well as spatial (geographic distance) predictors. For mainland sites, Mantel tests revealed significant relationships between community dissimilarity (based on pairwise Jaccard distance) and absolute differences in UPI at the taxonomic (r = 0.251, p < 0.001), phylogenetic (r = 0.240, p < 0.001), and functional (r = 0.221, p < 0.001) levels. At the taxonomic and phylogenetic levels, geographic distance and differences in substrate cover were also significant but showed weaker correlations than UPI (Table S9). In contrast, at the functional level, only UPI showed a significant association with community dissimilarity. Overall, these results indicated that community similarity decayed most strongly with increasing differences in urban pressure. GDMs further supported this pattern, showing that community dissimilarity was primarily structured by absolute differences in UPI among sites (Figure 6). Although overall explanatory power was moderate, urban pressure consistently emerged as the strongest driver of taxonomic and phylogenetic dissimilarity, outweighing geographic distance and substrate cover. Functional dissimilarity exhibited only a weak relationship with the predictors but was largely driven by urban pressure. Mantel tests performed on Corsican sites did not detect a significant effect of urbanization (Table S10), likely due to the limited number of sites with high UPI values. GDMs were therefore not computed for this subset.

Discussion

Marine urbanization emerges here as a powerful driver of biodiversity, restructuring coastal fish communities at the taxonomic, phylogenetic, and functional levels. Our results identify coastal urbanization as an underappreciated architect of marine biodiversity reorganization through biotic homogenization, extending the Homogenocene concept (Dudgeon and Liew 2026) to marine systems. We further uncover a paradoxical pattern in urbanized seascapes: while local species richness increases, regional diversity declines. This counterintuitive result stems from a net increase in the proportion of sites occupied by fish species across seaports. Nearly half of all species exhibited reduced occupancy—some potentially facing local extirpation—but gains among disturbance‐tolerant taxa outweighed these losses, leading to a convergence toward increasingly similar communities in highly urbanized areas. Consistent with global patterns showing a roughly balanced number of winners and losers in human‐impacted communities (Dornelas et al. 2019), we observe a similar balance. However, the magnitude of occupancy changes in seaports is notably higher, highlighting the strong impact of urbanization at local scales. Our findings further reveal that seaports act as “hotspots” for a subset of opportunistic species, while simultaneously driving regional biotic homogenization.

Previous work on marine urbanization has focused mainly on sessile organisms colonizing artificial structures, consistently reporting altered community composition between natural and artificial habitats based on percent cover metrics (Aguilera et al. 2014; Bulleri et al. 2005; Connell 2001; Lam et al. 2009; Scherner et al. 2013). Like these benthic assemblages, we detected strong compositional shifts along the urbanization gradient, supporting the role of habitat artificialization as a pervasive ecological filter across functional groups. However, while some benthic studies also reported reduced species richness in urbanized habitats (Aguilera et al. 2014; Scherner et al. 2013), our fish communities showed elevated local richness in seaports. Extending this framework to mobile fish communities detected through eDNA metabarcoding, our study captures compositional shifts along an urbanization gradient at a fine spatial scale rarely achieved by traditional surveys. Our results are consistent with recent eDNA‐based studies on fish communities in the same region based on more limited sampling (Macé et al. 2024; Manel et al. 2024). Those studies similarly reported marked differences between sites located inside and outside seaports, but did not detect significant differences in species richness between seaports and adjacent natural areas. The observed restructuring suggests that marine urbanization acts as a strong ecological filter, favoring species whose traits confer resilience to disturbance. Echoing patterns described in terrestrial ecosystems (Alberti 2015; McKinney 2006), many “urbanophile” species identified here are known to tolerate degraded environments (Louisy 2022). These urbanophile taxa share a consistent suite of traits that confer ecological advantages in seaports, where anthropogenic inputs, including organic enrichment, chemical pollution, and altered hydrodynamics, reshape resource availability and food web structure (Malerba et al. 2019). Most are omnivorous, exploiting a broad range of prey from invertebrates to detritus, which buffers them against resource fluctuations and results in lower trophic levels than those of communities outside seaports. Their predominance coincides with a decline in strict carnivores, reflecting limited opportunities for specialized predation within seaport basins. Species with benthic and sandy preferences dominate, consistent with the substrates typical of seaports, while demersal and pelagic taxa are less abundant due to the scarcity of open water and complex habitats. Contrary to expectations, artificial concrete structures, which are often vertical and lacking microhabitats such as crevices, fail to provide suitable refuges for reef‐associated species (Bulleri and Chapman 2010; Momota and Hosokawa 2021). Traits such as tolerance to brackish conditions and diadromous behavior may facilitate persistence in seaports, where salinity can fluctuate and locally depart from open‐coast conditions. Indeed, restricted water circulation reduces the buffering effect of adjacent coastal waters within seaports, leading to distinct and more variable physico‐chemical conditions compared to surrounding water masses, a pattern further amplified by the low tidal amplitude in the region (Bouchoucha et al. 2025). Collectively, these characteristics promote the emergence of a guild of opportunistic, generalist species thriving in urbanized marine environments.

Urban biotic homogenization in our study extended beyond taxonomic similarity to the phylogenetic dimension. Seaport communities comprised more closely related species, indicating urban‐driven phylogenetic clustering. This pattern reveals that urbanization not only alters species composition but also narrows the evolutionary breadth of marine communities. Although seaport and non‐seaport communities also differed at the functional level, functional homogenization remained limited. Both functional α‐diversity and pairwise functional β‐diversity tended to be lower within seaports, but not significantly so, suggesting that taxa persisting in urban environments retain diverse functional traits that buffer ecosystem functioning against phylogenetic convergence. Variation among seaport communities was low, and structural attributes (total area, hydrological connectivity to freshwater or brackish systems, and surrounding substrate composition) did not significantly influence biodiversity patterns. This indicates that similar ecological filters operate across seaports, generating relatively stable and predictable communities. Beyond seaports, the urban pressure index emerged as a strong predictor of compositional similarity. Non‐seaport sites exposed to higher urban pressure hosted fish communities increasingly resembling those in seaports, while geographic distance to the nearest seaport had no detectable effect. Across all sites, differences in urban pressure explained community dissimilarity more effectively than either geographic distance or difference in substrate cover. Together, these results highlight urban pressure as a dominant driver of coastal fish community structure, demonstrating that the principles of urban ecology, initially developed for terrestrial systems, also extend to coastal marine environments (Todd et al. 2019; Touchard et al. 2023). In this context, seaports act as true extensions of cities into the sea.

A main limitation of our study lies in the use of presence‐absence data from eDNA, which precludes estimating species abundances. Although semi‐quantitative approaches have been developed, the relationship between read counts and actual abundance remains mostly validated under controlled conditions (Blackman et al. 2024; Hansen et al. 2018). Additionally, the “ecology of DNA”, defined as the biotic and abiotic influences on eDNA production, persistence, and degradation (Barnes and Turner 2016; Stewart 2019), may differ between ports and non‐port areas due to variation in physicochemical conditions (Bouchoucha et al. 2025) and organism life stages. PCR amplification biases further complicate the quantitative interpretation of read counts in complex communities (Elbrecht and Leese 2015). Consequently, the range of diversity metrics that can be applied is limited, including abundance‐weighted measures of dissimilarity, and prevents testing whether the patterns of biotic homogenization observed here persist when abundance is considered (Fletcher Jr. et al. 2025). Determining whether species frequently present in seaports are also numerically dominant or occur at low densities would be particularly informative for future work. The lack of abundance information also affects functional analyses since traits cannot be weighted by species dominance. As a result, the functional metrics reported here describe only the composition of traits, not their relative contributions to community functioning. Functional interpretations are further constrained by the trait dataset itself, which depends on the availability and completeness of trait information (Májeková et al. 2016; Villéger et al. 2017). Missing or coarse trait data may bias estimates of functional diversity and obscure links between traits and environmental filters. In addition, eDNA provides no information on the ecological status of detected organisms (Beng and Corlett 2020), preventing assessment of whether seaports host adults, juveniles, or larvae, or whether reproduction and healthy populations are maintained in these disturbed habitats. Finally, while our spatial approach provides insights into current patterns, it does not allow us to infer temporal dynamics directly. Although spatial gradients represent the primary method employed to quantify biotic homogenization (Lokatis and Jeschke 2022), long‐term monitoring will be essential in the future to determine whether the observed patterns of homogenization and trait composition persist or evolve through time (Olden and Rooney 2006).

Indeed, the homogenization process identified here is driven entirely by shifts in native species distributions, rather than non‐indigenous species (NIS), even though urban biotic homogenization was originally defined as a process driven by biological invasions (McKinney and Lockwood 1999). We did not detect any NIS, although some MOTUs remained unassigned and could potentially correspond to such taxa. The Mediterranean Sea is nevertheless recognized as an invasion hotspot (Edelist et al. 2013), due to the influx of Indo‐Pacific biota through the Suez Canal (Katsanevakis et al. 2014; Lejeusne et al. 2010). While relatively few fish species have so far reached the Western Mediterranean to date (Azzurro et al. 2022), recent studies indicate an acceleration of the invasion process (Zenetos et al. 2022). This dynamic is likely to be further amplified by ongoing ocean warming and the increasing frequency and intensity of marine heatwaves, which have been particularly pronounced in the Western Mediterranean in recent years (Tejedor et al. 2024). Warming seas are expected to drive local extinctions or severe population declines among thermally sensitive native species (Penn and Deutsch 2022), while simultaneously favoring warm‐affiliated NIS originating from the Red Sea (Azzurro and D'Amen 2022). Many species already established in the Eastern Mediterranean are likely to spread westward, reflecting an “invasion debt” (Galil et al. 2021) that has not yet fully materialized. In this context, the absence of detected NIS in the present study should not be interpreted as evidence of long‐term resistance to invasions, but rather highlights the need for sustained temporal monitoring to capture delayed invasion dynamics and their interaction with climate‐driven disturbances. Such time‐series data will be essential to disentangle transient phases of community differentiation from longer‐term trajectories toward biotic homogenization (Socolar et al. 2016; J. Wang et al. 2025), particularly in urbanized seascapes where ecological filters may amplify these processes. The interplay between biological invasions and climate change suggests that a global homogenization of fish communities is likely in the future (Occhipinti‐Ambrogi 2007). While the response of native species to these threats is unpredictable—with today's winners potentially becoming tomorrow's losers—the question remains whether this process will be particularly pronounced in urbanized seascapes, or occur more broadly across regions.

Urban biotic homogenization has important ecological consequences. By reducing β‐diversity, it erodes spatial variation in community composition even in the absence of species extinctions, thereby contributing to overall biodiversity loss. In such systems, opportunistic species that tolerate human‐altered conditions tend to expand at the expense of more specialized taxa, potentially leading to local extirpations over time. Reduced β‐diversity can also result in synchronized ecosystem dynamics, with increasingly similar communities responding in parallel to environmental change, including climate‐driven perturbations (Larsen et al. 2025; S. Wang et al. 2021). Mitigating homogenization in marine environments remains challenging. Limiting further coastal urbanization is a first essential step, but restoration strategies must also be carefully designed. Recreating “natural‐like” habitats through eco‐engineering within urbanized seascapes may seem beneficial (Strain et al. 2018), yet it should be performed with caution as it would be counterproductive if it mainly favors urbanophile species or facilitates the establishment of NIS (Gauff et al. 2023). In contrast, maintaining preserved, connected areas, whether as well‐managed marine protected areas or other forms of low‐impact habitats, may act as sources of ecological differentiation, sustaining distinct community compositions and buffering against large‐scale homogenization, much like no‐take protected areas that maintain distinct community structures, particularly for exploited species (Blowes et al. 2020). Protecting such zones of heterogeneity appears crucial to conserving biodiversity patterns in increasingly urbanized seas.
