Tele-rehabilitation for individuals with long COVID: a randomised clinical trial
Authors: Tania Janaudis-Ferreira, Marla K. Beauchamp, Amanda K. Rizk, Catherine M. Tansey, Bryan A. Ross, Maria Sedeno, Jean Bourbeau, Andrea Benedetti, Laura Barreto, Pei Zhi Li, Kriti Agarwal, Rebecca Zucco, Julie Lopez, Emily Crowley, Julie Cloutier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMost trials in long COVID rehabilitation included only previously hospitalised patients and omitted outcome measures such as mobility, an important outcome for assessing life impact. The primary objective was to investigate whether an 8-week tele-rehabilitation programme for individuals with long COVID improves functional mobility compared with usual care.MethodsThis was a randomised controlled trial involving individuals with long COVID and persistent symptoms of reduced mobility, muscle weakness, dyspnoea or fatigue. Participants were randomised to either an individualised 8-week tele-rehabilitation programme plus usual care or usual care. In both groups, usual care was supplemented with generic guidance on physical activity and symptom management. The primary outcome was activity measure for post-acute care (AM-PAC) mobility. The secondary outcomes were dyspnoea, fatigue, quality of life, physical function, mental health, acceptability and adverse events.Results132 participants were randomised (65 intervention; 67 control; 48±11.8 years; 75% female), with 78% not previously hospitalised. Adherence was high (96%), although 39% of intervention participants were unable to progress their exercises. No significant between-group differences were found in AM-PAC mobility (mean difference (MD) 0.61; 95% CI −0.91–2.13). The intervention group showed greater improvements in EQ-5D-5L pain (MD −0.36; 95% CI −0.70– −0.03), health status (MD 7.74; 95% CI 1.05–14.43), fatigue (MD −0.90; 95% CI −1.78– −0.02) and dyspnoea (MD 1.24; 95% CI 0.10–2.37). No serious adverse events occurred.ConclusionsIn the context of limited evidence around exercise in non-hospitalised individuals with long COVID, our study offers important insights into feasibility, safety and clinical relevance.

Introduction

COVID-19 can result in long-term health consequences, with symptoms persisting for months or even years [1]. Defined by the World Health Organization (WHO) as long COVID or post-COVID-19 condition, symptoms emerge or persist 3 months after initial infection, lasting for at least 2 months without alternative explanation [1]. Globally, millions of people are diagnosed with long COVID [2, 3], with thousands unable to resume work or schooling [4].

While fatigue, shortness of breath and memory problems are commonly reported, long COVID encompasses a spectrum of over 200 manifestations, impacting daily functioning [5] In a US nationwide survey involving 7926 individuals with long COVID, 41% had mobility disability [6]. In a Canadian prospective cohort study, 55.3% of 254 previously hospitalised COVID-19 patients demonstrated clinically important mobility and physical function deficits at 12-months follow-up [7]. The large population-based Canadian Longitudinal Study on Aging similarly demonstrated new-onset mobility problems, even in the absence of hospitalisation [8]. This highlights the potential need for rehabilitation interventions in this population to address symptoms and functional limitations. The WHO recommends rehabilitation [1], albeit with relevant caveats. Given the high percentage of post-exertional malaise (PEM) in individuals with long COVID, [9] it is recommended that physical rehabilitation provide a personalised approach for those ready to increase their physical activity [1, 10]. PEM is an increase in the severity of symptoms and/or the appearance of new symptoms after physical or cognitive exertion [11].

Although emerging evidence suggests that rehabilitation for long COVID improves functional exercise capacity, dyspnoea and quality of life, [12] several shortcomings exist in available trials [12]. Most trials to date included only previously hospitalised patients, not representative of the broader long COVID population; few monitored adverse events or addressed strategies to avoid PEM; [12] and many omitted outcome measures such as mobility, recommended as part of a core outcome set for assessing life impact [13]. Measures of exercise capacity, such as the 6-min walk test or incremental shuttle walk test used in previous trials, are not appropriate for individuals with long COVID who experience PEM [1, 13].

This study aimed to evaluate the effect of an 8-week tele-rehabilitation programme added to usual care on functional mobility (as measured by the activity measure for post-acute care (AM-PAC) basic mobility) in individuals with long COVID, compared with usual care that was supplemented by generic guidance on physical activity and symptom management. Secondary objectives included assessing the impact of the intervention on lower body strength, fatigue, dyspnoea, PEM, health-related-quality-of-life, anxiety, depression, distress, cognitive function, health service use, adverse events and participant satisfaction with the intervention. We hypothesised that an 8-week tele-rehabilitation programme would yield greater improvements in functional mobility than the supplemented usual care alone.

Methods

This study used a prospective, multicentre, assessor-blind, randomised controlled trial (RCT) design. Eligible subjects were randomly allocated in a 1:1 ratio to receive either an 8-week home-based tele-rehabilitation programme in addition to usual outpatient care or usual outpatient care. Usual care in both groups was supplemented with generic physical activity and symptom management guidance. The reporting of the study followed the CONSORT guidelines [14]. The study was approved by the Research Ethics Board of the Research Institute of McGill University Health Centre (no. 2022-8559) and the Hamilton Integrated Research Ethics Board (no. 14858). The study was registered in ClinicalTrials.gov (identifier NCT05298878).

Randomisation and masking

A biostatistician generated the randomisation sequence using computer-generated permuted (Blockrand package in R), balanced blocks of varying sizes (2–8), stratified by recruitment location (Quebec or Ontario). The sequence was entered into REDCap by a research team member who was not involved in recruitment, intervention or assessments. The principal investigator who was also not involved in recruitment, assessment or intervention, randomised participants after the baseline assessment to ensure allocation concealment and notified them of their group allocation. Assessors involved in recruitment and data collection, as well as the data analyst, remained blinded to group allocation.

Participants

Participants were recruited from the McGill University Health Centre (MUHC) COVID-19 clinic and Biobank, Quebec Action for Post-COVID Project, [15] McMaster Functional Recovery of COVID-19 patients Prospective Cohort Study [7] and social media (individuals with a clinical diagnosis of long COVID made by a healthcare professional).

Inclusion criteria were 1) adults (aged ≥18 years) with long COVID, defined according to the WHO as having “a history of probable or confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection, usually 3 months from the onset of COVID-19, with symptoms that last for at least 2 months and cannot be explained by an alternative diagnosis”; [1] 2) self-reported persisting symptoms of either reduced mobility, muscle weakness, dyspnoea or fatigue; 3) technologically capable of connecting with an online video conferencing platform; and 4) able to collaborate with the research assistant to complete the online assessment sessions or have a family member available to help. Exclusion criteria were 1) pre-existing or newly identified severe cognitive impairment; 2) inability to speak or comprehend English or French; or 3) known or self-reported acute and/or uncontrolled cardiac, musculoskeletal or neurological condition that might render rehabilitation participation unsafe.

Procedures

Participants underwent evaluations at baseline and after the intervention period (at 8 weeks) and provided self-reported healthcare service usage at 30 days after the evaluation. They also completed a questionnaire about demographic information (age, self-reported sex and gender [16], height, weight, education level, household income, work status, smoking history, self-reported comorbidities, hospital and intensive care unit length of stay, number of COVID-19 infections, medication, supplemental oxygen use and level of physical activity). The exercise training and assessments were 100% remote using the Zoom for Healthcare Platform. Questionnaires were collected using a REDCap link sent to participants.

Usual care

The usual care group received usual outpatient care which consisted of any outpatient visits with a physician or other healthcare professional. It was supplemented by a PDF prepared by the research team (supplementary methods) containing generic instructions on how to manage symptoms and safely engage in physical activity while avoiding the “push-and-crash” cycle that may occur with individuals with PEM [10].

Tele-rehabilitation programme

The intervention consisted of online one-on-one sessions with kinesiologists, held three times per week at times convenient for the participants, with a phase-out design changing to one supervised session/week and two independent sessions during weeks 5–8. Supervised sessions lasted 40 min, combining education, breathing exercises, aerobic (seated or standing marching or swing arm across chest), functional lower and upper body strengthening and flexibility exercises (supplementary table S1). The target intensity for aerobic and strengthening exercises was moderate (12–14 points on Borg 6–20 scale). However, initial intensities varied by participant; for example, we targeted Borg 9–11/20 (light intensity) for severely deconditioned participants or those who reported PEM at study entry. Training progression was tailored to each subject and, when deemed safe by the kinesiologists, included modifications to frequency, intensity, type of exercise or a combination of these factors. These progressive modifications were guided by participant feedback (e.g. improvements on the Borg 6–20 with no symptoms of PEM) at the beginning and end of each session (supplementary table S2). During the independent sessions, participants repeated the exercises from that week's supervised sessions. Educational sessions were delivered once per week. Participants in the intervention group received the same PDF as the control group containing generic instructions on how to manage symptoms and safely engage in physical activity. The same kinesiologists delivered online training at both sites. Further details on the intervention (monitoring, target intensity, training progression/regression and education topics) are available in the supplementary material (supplementary methods and supplementary table S2).

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was a change in functional mobility. In this study, functional mobility was operationalised using the AM-PAC basic mobility measure [17] which is a patient-reported activity limitation instrument based on the International Classification of Functioning, Disability, and Health (ICF) [18]. The AM-PAC assesses difficulty in performing housework, bed mobility, transfers, ambulation and stair use. It includes 18 questions and each item is scored from 1 (unable to perform) to 4 (none or no difficulty) with lower scores indicating lower levels of function [2].

AM-PAC mobility aligns with the core set outcome recommendations for the life impact domain in long COVID, [13] and is a widely used, validated tool in North American inpatient and outpatient acute and rehabilitation settings for assessing functional mobility across diverse populations [19]. AM-PAC has been used in a Canadian multicentre cohort study of long COVID [7], is well validated and responsive to change [17, 20]. The minimal detectable change (MDC) for AM-PAC basic mobility and the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) is 3.3 [21].

The following secondary outcome measures were assessed at baseline and after the 8-week intervention period: 1-min sit-to-stand (STS) test [22], fast timed-up-and-go (TUG) test [23], baseline and transition dyspnoea index (BDI/TDI) [24], fatigue visual analogue scale [25], 12-item short-form (SF-12), EuroQol five-dimensions, five-level (EQ-5D-5L) questionnaire [26], DePaul symptom questionnaire – PEM (DSQ-PEM) [27], AM-PAC cognition subscale [28], hospital anxiety and depression scale (HADS) [29] and impact of event scale – revised (IES-R) [30]. Adverse events were monitored throughout the intervention period [31], whereas adherence rates and satisfaction with the intervention were assessed at the end of the intervention. Self-reported healthcare utilisation at 30 days after the intervention period. An adverse event was defined as any unfavourable medical occurrence in the trial participant, such as a new illness, worsening symptom or sign that happens during treatment or an intervention but is not necessarily caused by the treatment/intervention itself [31]. Further details on the definition and classification of adverse events, as well as how adherence rates, satisfaction with the intervention and healthcare utilisation were assessed, are available in the supplementary methods.

Statistical analyses

The sample size calculation was performed using Proc power in SAS for a two-group simple t-test with a randomisation of 1:1. We assumed that the average difference in the change of AM-PAC from baseline to 8 weeks would be 3.3 points (the MDC), and the sd for each group to be equal to 5.0 [21]. Therefore, we estimated that we would need a total of 132 subjects (66 in the intervention group and 66 in the control group) to enable adjustment for four potential covariates for a two-tailed test with a power of 90%, an α error of 0.05 and an estimated attrition rate of 10% [32].

Analyses followed the intention-to-treat principle, using multiple imputation via chained equations with baseline age, sex, body mass index (BMI) and pre-test values, generating 10 imputed datasets. Results from the imputed datasets were very similar to those from the complete-case analysis, suggesting that missing data had minimal impact on the overall findings. Generalised estimating equation (GEE) models were used to estimate within-group changes, with normal distribution via an identity link for continuous variables, and binary distribution with identity link function for categorical variables to estimate proportion difference. Between-group differences were tested for the pre–post-by-arms interactions to estimate whether average change in the measures from pre to post differed in the two arms. A repeated statement was included in all models to account for possible correlation within subjects. In adjusted analyses, we fitted GEE models and adjusted for age and number of comorbidities, both strongly correlated with mobility. We used a binomial GEE model with an identity link and robust variance estimation to estimate the proportion difference for correlated binary data. If the GEE model failed to converge for the correlated binary outcomes, we used logistic regression as an alternative to estimate the proportion difference [33]. We applied the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method to control for type I errors. Among the 25 outcomes presented in table 2 comparing changes between the two arms, none remained statistically significant after applying the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure using an FDR threshold of 0.25.

We also conducted a per-protocol analysis including only intervention participants who progressed exercise training by intensity, duration, time or type, adjusting for important covariates. The per-protocol analysis excludes non-progressors but still compares the remaining participants with all controls, which may introduce bias. However, since there were no significant baseline differences between progressors and non-progressors, comparing the progressed group to the full control group may help minimise this bias. Sex-stratified analyses (separate models for males and females) were performed, and the effect modification of sex was tested using the interaction term pre–post-by-sex. This was an exploratory analysis as we may have been underpowered. Statistical significance was defined as two-sided p-value<0.05. Statistical analyses were conducted using SAS v.9.4.

Results

Recruitment occurred between August 2022 and July 2023. Of the 264 individuals assessed for eligibility, 132 individuals were randomised to either the intervention group (n=65) or control group (n=67) (figure 1). 127 individuals completed the post-assessment and were included in analyses, leading to a retention rate of 96% (five dropouts) (figure 1).

Participants in the intervention group attended a mean of 13.4 out of 14 supervised exercise sessions (96% adherence) and a mean of 7.4 out of 10 independent sessions (74% adherence). 25 participants (39%) in the intervention group could only perform basic range of motion and flexibility exercises or were unable to progress their exercises through one of the FITT (frequency, intensity, time or type) principles due to symptoms (e.g. dyspnoea, fatigue, pain or headache).

No between-group differences were found for change in AM-PAC mobility following the intervention (difference in change: 0.61; 95% CI −0.91–2.13) (table 2). Results from analyses adjusted for age, sex, BMI, number of comorbidities and smoking status were similar to results in the unadjusted analyses for AM-PAC mobility. The proportion of participants achieving the MDC and MCDI of 3.3 in the AM-PAC mobility total score at the end of the intervention period; however, was higher in the intervention group (35.8% (se 6.0%) versus 17.0% (se 4.7%); 95% CI 3.9–33.8).

Compared with changes observed in the control group, in the unadjusted analyses, the magnitude of improvement in the intervention group was greater for EQ-5D-5L pain/discomfort (difference in change, −0.36; 95% CI −0.70– −0.03), EQ-5D-5L VAS (difference in change, 7.74; 95% CI 1.05–14.43), VAS fatigue (difference in change, −0.90; 95% CI −1.78– −0.02), as well as for the TDI functional (mean difference (MD), 0.39; 95% CI 0.07–0.72), effort (MD 0.61; 95% CI 0.10–1.12) and total scores (MD 1.24; 95% CI 0.10–2.37) (table 2). No adverse events related to the intervention were classified as grade 3 (severe), 4 (life-threatening) or 5 (death) (table 3). Adverse events classified as “definitely” or “possibly” related to the intervention (45 adverse events reported by 30 participants) were either grade 1 (mild symptoms, medication intervention not indicated; 13 adverse events) or grade 2 (moderate symptoms, minimal local intervention required; limiting age-appropriate instrumental activities of daily living; 32 adverse events) (table 3). The most common types of adverse events were musculoskeletal and multisystem (table 3). 28 adverse events were classified as PEM-related, with 11 of these “definitely” and 17 “possibly” related to the intervention. All PEM-related adverse events were classified as mild or moderate. 36 adverse events were categorised as “unrelated” to the intervention (supplementary table S3).

In ad hoc analyses, when comparing the demographics of participants in the intervention group who did not experience adverse events (n=35) with those who had adverse events classified as “definitely” or “possibly” related to the intervention (n=30), those who experienced adverse events had a significantly higher proportion of comorbid cardiac disease (MD 13%; 95% CI 11.70–25.50) and higher PEM scores (MD 1.01; 95% CI 0.26–1.76) (supplementary table S4). Furthermore, when comparing participants who did not experience adverse events (n=35) with those who had PEM-related adverse events classified as “definitely” or “possibly” related (n=22), the latter group had a significantly higher proportion of comorbid cardiac disease (MD 18.18%; 95% CI 2.06–34.30), a shorter length of stay during their first hospitalisation (MD −5.73 days; 95% CI −11.23– −0.23), higher PEM scores at baseline (MD 0.83; 95% CI 0.11–1.55), and a greater proportion of participants with PEM triggered by mental effort (MD 22.86%; 95% CI 8.96–36.77) (supplementary table S5).

There were no significant baseline differences between the control group and those who progressed through the exercise programme (n=40) (supplementary table S6). Per-protocol analysis comparing the control group (n=66) to participants who progressed in the intervention group (n=36) showed improvements in SF-12 physical score (difference in change, 4.04; 95% CI 0.61–7.46), EQ-5D-5L pain/discomfort (difference in change, 0.45; 95% CI −0.84– −0.06), EQ-5D-5L VAS (difference in change, 11.48; 95% CI 3.24–19.73), VAS fatigue (difference in change, −1.40; 95% CI −2.42– −0.39), HADS anxiety score (difference in change, −1.38; 95% CI −2.68– −0.08) as well as in the TDI functional (MD 0.67; 95% CI 0.32–1.02), effort (MD 1.05; 95% CI 0.51–1.59), and total scores (MD 2.11; 95% CI 0.92–3.31) in favor of the intervention (supplementary table S7).

In our exploratory analysis, the changes in outcomes were similar between male and female participants in both groups, except for the EQ-5D-5L VAS, where females showed greater improvement (MD 8.15; 95% CI 0.50–15.79). (supplementary table S8).

57 of 61 participants (93% response rate; 70% female) who completed the intervention filled out the satisfaction survey. Most respondents (83%) would recommend the programme, with 91% agreeing it was easy to learn and 82% finding it relevant to their needs (table 4). Positive comments highlighted life-changing experiences like “this programme has changed my life” and “the programme made me alive again”. However, 14% commented negatively on how the programme affected their symptoms, for example, “after most sessions, I was unable to be productive later in the day”, and 7% found the programme not suitable. Suggestions included a longer programme with more recovery time between sessions and a multidisciplinary approach.

Discussion

Our study showed no statistically significant between-group differences in AM-PAC mobility. A greater proportion of participants in the intervention group achieved improvements exceeding established MDC and MCID thresholds. The intervention group demonstrated greater improvements in key secondary outcomes, including fatigue, dyspnoea, pain, discomfort and perceived health. The individualised tele-rehabilitation programme showed high adherence, no severe adverse events and strong participant satisfaction, highlighting its feasibility and acceptability.

The observed improvements in symptoms and health status, contrasted with the lack of statistically significant changes in AM-PAC mobility and performance-based physical function measures such as the TUG and 1-min STS, may reflect the specific focus of the rehabilitation programme. Participants were taught pacing and energy conservation strategies aimed at reducing symptom burden and preventing PEM exacerbation during exercising. While these approaches likely contributed to improvements in perceived health, they may have also limited participants’ performance during daily mobility and exertion during physical performance tests, potentially masking gains in functional capacity. Additionally, symptom and health status measures may be more sensitive to the types of changes experienced by individuals with long COVID, especially over a relatively short intervention period. Furthermore, within-group improvements in certain outcomes such as mobility, cognition and fatigue, were observed in the control group. These changes may be attributable to natural recovery of the condition or to the supplementary educational material they received from our research team (also received by the intervention group), which may have influenced the magnitude of between-group differences.

Finally, the average BMI of our participants was high (31.3±17 kg·m−2), which is understandable given that obesity is a known risk factor for developing long COVID symptoms [34]. However, this may have attenuated the benefits of the exercise programme.

Our findings contrast with those of a recent rehabilitation trial involving individuals recovering from post-hospitalisation COVID-19 (n=181), which reported improvements in exercise capacity following face-to-face and tele-rehabilitation compared with usual care but no significant changes in symptoms or quality of life [35]. Several factors may explain this discrepancy. First, our study population mainly consisted of non-hospitalised individuals with long COVID, who present with a variety of lingering symptoms, despite not requiring acute care during their initial illness. Notably, long COVID prevalence among non-hospitalised individuals has been reported to be as high as 69%, underscoring the substantial burden in this population [36]. Second, the comparator trial excluded individuals with PEM [35], whereas our trial included participants with PEM and tailored the intervention accordingly. As a result, our rehabilitation programme was deliberately designed around functional exercises, while considering pacing and symptom stabilisation, rather than relying on moderate-to-high-intensity training. This approach may have contributed to improvements in symptoms and health status, even in the absence of significant gains in performance-based mobility measures. These differences underscore the need to adapt rehabilitation strategies to the characteristics and needs of specific long COVID subgroups. Another recent trial (n=42) evaluated a hybrid pulmonary rehabilitation programme in formerly hospitalised individuals with long COVID and fatigue symptoms, reporting significant improvements in exercise capacity, peak V′O2 and quality of life [37]. However, both the intervention and study population differed substantially from ours, as the programme incorporated high-intensity interval training in individuals who did not appear to experience PEM. In contrast, 93% of participants in our study reported PEM at baseline, a symptom profile for which high-intensity exercise would be inappropriate. A large multicentre trial (n=233) evaluated a resistance exercise programme in individuals with long COVID and reported improvements in exercise capacity and quality of life [38]. The intervention and study population were more similar to ours, as the programme was tailored to participants’ functional status (i.e. bed-bound, able to sit or ambulatory) and 62% of participants had not been hospitalised for acute COVID-19. However, it was not reported whether participants experienced PEM, and adverse events were not described. A common finding across these studies, including ours, is that tele-rehabilitation seems feasible in individuals with long COVID and can therefore be an accessible intervention.

An unexpected finding was that 39% of participants in the intervention group could only perform basic range of motion and flexibility exercises or were unable to progress their exercises due to symptoms. This reflects both the level of disability of the participants and their ability to communicate their symptoms and, importantly, the kinesiologists’ careful judgment in recognising when to regress or pause the exercise training.

While 52% of participants in the intervention group reported at least one mild or moderate adverse event related to the intervention, these events involved minor symptoms or minimal disruptions to daily activities, requiring only rest or as-needed medication. The lack of adverse event monitoring in the control group may have led to overreporting in the intervention group due to more careful monitoring. Many adverse events involved exacerbation of symptoms present at baseline linked to other activities during the intervention period, such as social events, pet care and computer use. A 2023 systematic review on rehabilitation interventions in long COVID, which included 14 trials (eight of which included an exercise component with 429 patients) found that only two out of eight trials assessed adverse events, highlighting significant uncertainty in this area [12]. An RCT by McGregor et al. [24] involving 585 previously hospitalised individuals with long COVID who participated in online exercises sessions, reported 44 adverse events (16 in usual care and 28 in intervention group), with four classified as related to the intervention. They also reported 21 serious adverse events with one “possibly” related to the intervention [24]. In their trial, no instances of PEM were identified [24]. A recent RCT by Daynes et al. [35] reported two serious adverse events (one being death) which were adjudged to be unrelated to the intervention. The discrepancy between findings may be explained by differences in study populations such as prior hospitalisation status and presence of PEM at baseline, as well as variations in exercise protocols and methods used to assess adverse events.

Our ad hoc analyses provide insights into the characteristics of individuals with long COVID who may be more susceptible to adverse events, including those PEM-related. Specifically, our findings suggest that those with comorbid cardiac disease and higher PEM scores at baseline may be at greater risk for experiencing all-cause adverse events during exercise. In contrast, those with comorbid cardiac disease, higher PEM scores at baseline, PEM triggered by mental effort and a shorter hospital stay may be at greater risk for experiencing PEM-related adverse events.

Our findings offer unique contributions to the literature. First, although long COVID prevalence among non-hospitalised patients can be as high as 69%, [36] this cohort had not been included in previous rehabilitation trials comparing exercise training with usual care [12, 35]. Second, to our knowledge, this is the first RCT to include individuals with PEM. Participants received an individualised, symptom-titrated exercise programme, alongside educational support and usual care, with careful monitoring of PEM and adverse events throughout. This approach has yielded valuable insights into the characteristics of people with long COVID who may be more susceptible to adverse events, particularly those associated with PEM. Third, all outcome measures align with the core outcome set for long COVID [13]. The robust RCT methodology, high adherence and low dropout rate are additional strengths.

Our study has limitations. It is possible that the study was underpowered to detect a difference in the primary outcome, AM-PAC mobility, as the sample size calculation was based on an MDC of 3.3, whereas the observed difference was 0.61 (table 2). Another limitation, common to studies in this population, is that physical function prior to SARS-CoV-2 infection was unknown. This lack of baseline data made it difficult to determine the extent of recovery or decline attributable to long COVID versus pre-existing conditions. Adverse events were not monitored in the control group, and cautious monitoring of adverse events in the intervention group may have led to overreporting. The 8-week programme, with sessions three times per week, may not have allowed some participants to fully recover from fatigue between sessions; indeed, some participants suggested a longer programme with more recovery time between sessions in the satisfaction survey. Our programme focused on physical rehabilitation and education. A multidisciplinary team could further enhance outcomes by incorporating expertise in areas such as mental health and sleep. Although our kinesiologists were trained in behaviour change strategies and symptom management, including anxiety-related issues, additional psychological support might help address concerns about symptom exacerbation and improve participants’ confidence in interpreting normal physiological responses to exercise. The sample's high education and socioeconomic status as well as its predominately white demographic may limit generalizability of the findings. In addition, the majority of participants were middle-aged; therefore, it is unclear whether the study findings are generalisable to older patients who have a higher number or greater severity of comorbidities and/or require supplemental oxygen. Most participants in our study had not been hospitalised during their acute illness and therefore did not have laboratory-confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection. However, our inclusion criteria were aligned with the WHO's clinical case definition of long COVID, which includes individuals with a history of probable or confirmed infection, typically 3 months from onset, with symptoms lasting at least 2 months and not explained by an alternative diagnosis [1]. In keeping with this definition, all our participants had a clinical diagnosis of long COVID made by a healthcare professional. While the absence of test-confirmation may introduce heterogeneity, it reflects the reality of how long COVID presents in community settings and enhances the generalisability of our findings to this large and often underserved subgroup.

Nevertheless, the findings suggest that personalised, symptom-based rehabilitation interventions may play a meaningful role in improving symptoms and health status in individuals with long COVID, supporting the offer of these interventions to individuals who continue to experience persistent long COVID symptoms. Participant acceptability of the programme was high, reflected in the strong adherence rate and satisfaction survey responses. The tele-rehabilitation format's convenience and flexibility allowed participants to integrate sessions into their schedules, promoting consistent engagement. Further research is needed to understand the mechanisms by which exercise impacts long COVID symptoms, which could inform more targeted interventions.

In conclusion, between-group differences in the primary outcome, AM-PAC mobility, did not reach statistical significance. Secondary outcomes demonstrated benefits of the individualised tele-rehabilitation programme, including improvements in overall health status, reductions in persistent symptoms, high adherence and participant satisfaction. While 52% of participants in the intervention group reported mild or moderate adverse events, these events involved minor symptoms or minimal disruptions to daily activities. In the context of ongoing debate and limited evidence around exercise in this population, especially among non-hospitalised individuals, our study offers important insights into feasibility, safety and clinical relevance, areas that remain critically underexplored.
