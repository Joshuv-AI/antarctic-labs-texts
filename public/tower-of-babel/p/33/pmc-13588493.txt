Crowd Evaluations of Entrepreneurs with Physical Disabilities: Stereotype Subtyping through the Lens of Benevolent Ableism
Authors: Silvia Stroe, Charlotta Sirén, Tom Vanacker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We investigate how crowds evaluate pitches by entrepreneurs with versus without physical disabilities. Leveraging stereotype subtyping, we argue that entrepreneurs with physical disabilities are evaluated as a distinct subtype, rather than as people with disabilities or as conventional entrepreneurs. We hypothesize that benevolent ableism shapes how this subtype is evaluated, inflating both warmth and competence perceptions. Three experiments support our hypotheses, and key informant conversations with professional investors and entrepreneurs with disabilities corroborate the proposed mechanisms. Our findings contribute to unconventional entrepreneurship research by examining how bias can manifest as patronizing positivity rather than overt negative discrimination.

Introduction

Whether and why certain groups of entrepreneurs are evaluated differently by prospective resource providers remain central questions in entrepreneurship (Anglin et al., 2025; Becker-Blease & Sohl, 2007; Brush et al., 2014; Carter et al., 2007; Johnson et al., 2018). Prior research suggests that “conventional entrepreneurs,” or those whose traits (e.g., male, white, able-bodied) align with the stereotypical entrepreneurial prototype, enjoy the “greatest access to the resources required for entrepreneurship” (Bakker & McMullen, 2023, p. 2). Conversely, historically disadvantaged groups (e.g., females, racial minorities, disabled), also labeled “unconventional entrepreneurs” (Bakker & McMullen, 2023), are expected to find it more difficult to access resources (Brooks et al., 2014; Renko et al., 2016; Younkin & Kuppuswamy, 2018).

A prevailing perspective is that new venture evaluators, such as prospective investors, are biased against unconventional entrepreneurs (Brush et al., 2004). More specifically, stereotypes—the set of beliefs regarding the attributes of a social group (Ashmore & Del Boca, 1981)—can distort evaluations and restrict access to capital (Balachandra et al., 2019; Butticè et al., 2023). This bias is especially acute during the initial screening phase, a rapid gatekeeping process where the majority of entrepreneurs are excluded before ever reaching formal due diligence (Butticè et al., 2022; Maxwell et al., 2011).

A common assumption in the unconventional entrepreneurship literature is the implicit assumption of direct stereotype transfer (Bakker & McMullen, 2023). Most research assumes that the stereotype content of a parent minority category, such as women or people with a disability, is simply inherited by the entrepreneurs, regardless of the role context (Balachandra et al., 2019; Malmström et al., 2017). This inheritance logic overlooks a core tenet of social psychology: stereotype subtyping (Fiske & Neuberg, 1990). When individuals occupy high-status or agentic roles that contradict their minority group’s broader stereotype, evaluators often section them off into a distinct, role-specific category (Richards & Hewstone, 2001). While this process is well-documented in social psychology for gender and race (e.g., “professional women,”Heilman, 2012; “Black professionals,”Devine & Baker, 1991), the entrepreneurship literature has not attended to a stereotype subtyping perspective when studying unconventional entrepreneurs. For disability, the gap is more fundamental: social psychology itself treats disability as a largely undifferentiated paternalized category (Fiske et al., 2002; Rohmer & Louvet, 2018), with no established professional subtypes for evaluators to draw on. We, therefore, do not yet know how entry into the entrepreneurial role reshapes the disability stereotype, or if this underdetermined status makes the group uniquely susceptible to specific evaluative distortions.

This reliance on undifferentiated categories is mirrored in how the entrepreneurship literature on unconventional entrepreneurs commonly assumes that historically disadvantaged groups are necessarily evaluated negatively, treating them as “unilaterally stereotyped on a single evaluative dimension: good–bad” (Clausell & Fiske, 2005, p. 164). While the Stereotype Content Model (SCM) from social psychology offers a more nuanced view, suggesting that social evaluations are made on two dimensions, warmth and competence, with a trade-off between them, its compensatory logic still assumes that bias always manifests as a relative penalty on at least one dimension, with marginalized groups perceived as warmer but less competent (Fiske et al., 2002; Lee & Huang, 2018). Minority entrepreneurship scholars have typically focused on the penalized dimension, neglecting the other. This approach, however, fails to account for evaluative patterns that deviate from the trade-off logic entirely, namely cases where both evaluative dimensions shift in the same positive direction, representing a form of bias that frameworks grounded in hostile or compensatory discrimination are ill-equipped to explain.

To address the above complexities, we examine how entrepreneurs with physical disabilities are evaluated when they pitch new ventures to crowds. We ask: Do crowds evaluate pitches by entrepreneurs with versus without physical disabilities differently, and if so, through what mechanisms? We focus on physical disabilities for several reasons. First, physical disabilities are often quickly observable and thus serve as highly salient cues for social categorization, even more so than other minority cues such as gender and ethnicity (Rohmer & Louvet, 2009; Wiener & Willborn, 2011). Second, physical disability is the most common type of disability (US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2023). Finally, while prior research on disability stereotypes has largely focused on low-skill contexts (Dwertmann et al., 2025; Kulkarni & Gopakumar, 2014), open questions remain about how disability cues are interpreted in high-skill entrepreneurial settings.

Theoretically, we build on the SCM (Fiske et al., 2002) and the benevolent ableism perspective (Campbell, 2001; Nario-Redmond et al., 2019). The SCM identifies warmth and competence as the key dimensions on which any social target is judged (Fiske et al., 2007; Svetek, 2023). Within the SCM, we draw on stereotype subtyping (Fiske & Neuberg, 1990; Ford et al., 1994) to argue that entrepreneurs with physical disabilities who pitch an investable business will be evaluated as a distinct subtype, neither the stereotypical “person with a disability” nor the conventional entrepreneur. We further argue that evaluators interpret this new subtype through the lens of benevolent ableism, a form of bias rooted in assumptions of vulnerability and need for protection (Campbell, 2001; Nario-Redmond et al., 2019). Through this lens, disability cues can inflate both warmth perceptions (via paternalistic assumptions of vulnerability; Nario-Redmond et al., 2019) and competence perceptions (via heroic construal that frames ordinary achievement as extraordinary; Biernat, 2003; Zhang & Haller, 2013), inflating early-stage evaluations through both pathways.

We test these theoretical ideas using three experiments in which participants with self-reported investment experience (i.e., a crowd) evaluate entrepreneurial pitches from entrepreneurs with and without physical disabilities. The experiments are based on real venture pitches that successfully raised funding, with disability cues experimentally manipulated. To contextualize the proposed mechanisms and illustrate how these dynamics are articulated in practice, we also conducted key informant conversations with 19 professional investors and 6 entrepreneurs with disabilities (Wiklund et al., 2019; see Supplemental Appendix A for more details).

Our work makes three important contributions to the unconventional entrepreneurship and entrepreneurial finance literatures. First, we challenge the prevailing assumption that entrepreneurs with disabilities necessarily inherit the stereotypes of their parent minority category (Bakker & McMullen, 2023). We demonstrate that they are instead evaluated as a distinct social subtype and that the entrepreneurial role may function as a mechanism for cognitive differentiation, decoupling these founders from the broader disability stereotype and allowing for a context-dependent categorization. By showing how entry into a high-skill, agentic role triggers contextualized stereotype activation (Fiske et al., 2007), our study shifts the scholarly conversation from asking whether minority founders are penalized to asking how specific role-contexts reshape the very content of the stereotypes they encounter.

Second, we move beyond the SCM trade-off assumption (Fiske et al., 2007) by identifying patronizing positivity as a distinct evaluative phenomenon. Specifically, through benevolent ableism, bias can inflate both warmth and competence simultaneously: warmth rises via paternalistic assumptions of vulnerability, while competence rises through heroic construal that frames ordinary achievement as extraordinary. This contribution reveals that bias in entrepreneurial finance and entrepreneurship more broadly can manifest as a subjective evaluative inflation that, while seemingly positive on the surface, is grounded in patronizingly lowered expectations rather than genuine recognition of merit (Campbell, 2001).

Third, our findings of inflated initial evaluations of pitches by entrepreneurs with disabilities may be viewed as creating a paradox: entrepreneurs with disabilities face documented funding gaps (Renko et al., 2016) despite our evidence of favorable initial evaluations or investment intentions. However, our findings suggest that key barriers do not lie in the initial pitch evaluation stage (McSweeney et al., 2025), but in subsequent phases of the investment decision-making process or structural hurdles beyond evaluator impressions. By demonstrating that exclusion does not necessarily occur at the initial screen, we redirect the scholarly search for bias toward later stages, such as due diligence or capital allocation.

Entrepreneurs with Disabilities: A Distinct Category of Unconventional Entrepreneurs

Not all groups of unconventional entrepreneurs are evaluated equally (Fiske et al., 2002; Towler & Schneider, 2005), nor do they experience the same obstacles, challenges, and opportunities (Bakker & McMullen, 2023). Entrepreneurs with disabilities are a subgroup of unconventional entrepreneurs, which Bakker and McMullen (2023) define as those with traits that do not fit perceived beliefs of who entrepreneurs are (Caldwell et al., 2020; Renko et al., 2016). While there is no single definition of a “disabled” person, a disability can be defined as having “a physical or mental impairment that has a ‘substantial’ and ‘long-term’ negative effect on [a person’s] ability to do normal daily activities” (Equality Act, 2010). While this legal definition centers on individual impairment, our theoretical approach aligns with the social model of disability (Oliver, 1990), which locates disadvantage not in the impairment itself but in societal responses to it (see also Omansky Gordon & Rosenblum, 2001).

Although entrepreneurs with disabilities can share sociocultural similarities with other, more deeply investigated groups of unconventional entrepreneurs (e.g., women or racial minorities), several features make disability a theoretically distinct category in the context of stereotype-based evaluation. First, physical disability is a more salient cue than gender or ethnicity. Experimental evidence by Rohmer and Louvet (2009), for example, shows that people with a physical disability are more often described by their disability than their gender or ethnicity, suggesting that disability cues dominate initial impression formation more powerfully than other minority cues. Second, unlike gender and race, disability is always a potential status: any person can become disabled (Omansky Gordon & Rosenblum, 2001). This “it could happen to me” quality may amplify empathic and protective responses from evaluators. Third, evidence suggests that societal attitudes toward disability change more slowly than attitudes toward other stigmatized groups. Charlesworth and Banaji (2019) show that implicit attitudes toward people with disabilities have remained largely stable over the past decades, while implicit biases related to race, gender, and sexual orientation have measurably declined. This suggests that disability stereotypes may be entrenched, making it risky to generalize findings from gender or race contexts to disability.

A few studies on entrepreneurs with disabilities have focused on those in a social entrepreneurship context to point to finance and funding development as one of the key barriers for social entrepreneurs with disabilities (Caldwell et al., 2020; Parker Harris et al., 2013, 2014). More generally, research has examined the legitimacy-building strategies (Kašperová, 2021) and identity work (Jammaers & Zanoni, 2020) of entrepreneurs with disabilities. Renko et al. (2016) drew on the Panel Study of Entrepreneurial Dynamics II, which captures nascent entrepreneurship broadly, including self-employment and various types of new venture creation, and tracks the startup process over time. They showed that, for entrepreneurs with disabilities, startup efforts are less likely to result in a viable venture, and entrepreneurs with disabilities attract smaller start-up investments than entrepreneurs without disabilities.

Thus, while we know that funding is an important issue for entrepreneurs with disabilities, we have a limited theoretical and empirical understanding of whether and how they are evaluated differently during an initial screening and what drives these evaluations. This gap is particularly consequential in contexts where entrepreneurs pitch scalable, high-growth ventures, because these settings activate the strongest tension between disability stereotypes and entrepreneurial role expectations.

Theory and Hypotheses

Before developing the specific mechanisms, we state our overarching prediction. Drawing on the stereotype subtyping literature and benevolent ableism, we expect that entrepreneurs with disabilities (vs. non-disabled entrepreneurs) pitching an investment-ready venture will receive more favorable initial evaluations from crowds. This prediction rests on two premises developed in detail below: (a) entrepreneurs with disabilities will be cognitively subtyped into a new category, that is, evaluated as neither “disabled” nor “conventional entrepreneurs,” and (b) because this subtype lacks a stable, role-specific schema, benevolent ableism will shape the warmth and competence content ascribed to that subtype, thereby elevating initial investment intentions through both warmth and competence pathways.

Subtyping as a Resolution to Competing Stereotypes

The SCM argues that perceived warmth and competence are two universal dimensions of social cognition that largely determine social evaluations (Fiske, 2018; Fiske et al., 2002, 2007). Warmth captures whether a target’s intentions are good or ill, encompassing traits such as trustworthiness, honesty, and cooperativeness. Competence captures whether the target can enact those intentions, including elements such as capability, agency, intelligence, and efficiency (Clausell & Fiske, 2005; Fiske, 2018; Svetek, 2023). The SCM maps social groups into quadrants based on combinations of high and low warmth and competence. Two quadrants are directly relevant here. People with disabilities are consistently placed in the high-warmth, low-competence quadrant (Fiske, 2018, p. 68). By contrast, businesspeople and professionals, categories often used to characterize the conventional entrepreneur (Gupta et al., 2009, 2019), occupy the low-warmth, high-competence quadrant: respected for their skills but perceived as cold or calculating (Cuddy et al., 2009; Fiske et al., 2002). An entrepreneur with a disability, therefore, activates two diametrically opposed stereotype contents at once, namely the “warm-but-incompetent” disability stereotype and the “competent-but-cold” entrepreneur stereotype. This tension is unlikely to be resolved through simple averaging, because multiple social identities interact to create perceptions not predictable from their constituent parts (Parent et al., 2013). How, then, do evaluators resolve this conflict? We argue that evaluators resolve this tension by forming a new stereotype subtype.

Stereotypes are not fixed, stable responses (Blair, 2002; Fiske & Neuberg, 1990). Instead, they are highly sensitive to the situational context in which a person from a social group is encountered, reflecting contextualized stereotype activation (Blair, 2002; Wittenbrink et al., 2001). Indeed, marginalized groups versus professionals from marginalized groups can be confronted with different stereotypes (e.g., a Black professional is evaluated differently from Black people generally; Fiske et al., 2002, 2009). As Clausell and Fiske (2005, p. 164) clarified in the SCM, stereotypes are not monolithic, and subtyping can occur, where “subtypes focus entirely on counter-stereotypic attributes, often described as a splintering process. Subtyping occurs when a target is perceived as atypical of the superordinate group.”

In subtyping, individuals belonging to a marginalized group but operating in an unexpected context and conforming to context-specific expectations that violate the broader, more general stereotype get sectioned off from the overall stereotype and seen as exceptions to the rule (Fiske, 2018; for a review, see Richards & Hewstone, 2001). Thus, the context provides the very foundation for the creation of a subtype, a process that allows for nuanced, situation-dependent judgments while insulating the superordinate stereotype from fundamental change (Fiske & Neuberg, 1990; Wittenbrink et al., 2001).

Subtyping is not the only way evaluators may resolve the cognitive conflict triggered by an entrepreneur with disability, namely the simultaneous activation of the “warm-but-incompetent” disability stereotype and the “competent-but-cold” entrepreneur stereotype. Social cognition research identifies several alternative processes, including individuation and stereotype updating. However, subtyping is the most likely outcome in the initial pitch evaluation context. Initial screenings of entrepreneurial pitches amplify dependence on categorical processing because evaluators have limited time and cognitive resources (Johnson et al., 2018; Kalvapalle et al., 2024) and rely on cognitively frugal means of evaluation (Gigerenzer, 2008; Khurana & Lee, 2023). Under these conditions, individuation, in which evaluators set aside category membership and judge the person on their unique attributes (Fiske & Neuberg, 1990), is unlikely because a pitch primarily provides diagnostic evidence about the venture rather than the entrepreneur’s personal history or idiosyncratic traits. Evaluators form stereotype-based impressions of the entrepreneur’s warmth and competence that are inferred from category membership (e.g., disability status, entrepreneurial role) rather than from individuating information about the specific person. Moreover, at the initial screening stage, crowd evaluators make one-directional judgments that are not yet motivated by interdependence or accountability toward the person being evaluated, further discouraging effortful individuation. Stereotype updating, that is revising group-level beliefs in light of the disconfirming case, is equally implausible here because it requires that the disconfirming case be perceived as representative of the group rather than as an exception (Richards & Hewstone, 2001; Weber & Crocker, 1983). An entrepreneur with disability presenting an investment-ready venture is, by definition, atypical of the broader disability category, making it precisely the kind of case that triggers subtyping rather than generalized revision of group-level beliefs.

This subtyping process may be especially pronounced for disability relative to other minority categories. For gender and race, the SCM literature has documented rich pre-existing subtype taxonomies, such as professional women versus homemakers, Black professionals versus Black people generally (Clausell & Fiske, 2005; Fiske et al., 2002, 2009). When a woman or a racial minority enters the entrepreneur role, evaluators can draw on these established subtypes to guide their judgment. For people with disabilities, however, the SCM literature treats disability as a relatively undifferentiated category (Fiske, 2018). There is no well-established, role-specific subtype such as “entrepreneur with disability” or “disabled professional” for evaluators to readily access, making the evaluative meaning of this new subtype especially dependent on the interpretive lens brought to the encounter. Because benevolent ableism is the culturally dominant framework through which non-disabled people process disability cues (Nario-Redmond et al., 2019), it is likely to shape how this underdetermined subtype is interpreted.

Benevolent ableism is a form of prejudice that appears positive on the surface but is rooted in patronizing assumptions about vulnerability, dependency, and need for protection (Campbell, 2001, 2009; Nario-Redmond et al., 2019). As a paternalistic mode of stereotyping, it shapes perceptions along both fundamental evaluative dimensions of warmth and competence (Cuddy et al., 2008; Nario-Redmond et al., 2019). Unlike hostile ableism, which manifests as contempt, dehumanization, and the perception that people with disabilities are threatening or burdensome, benevolent ableism presents as kindness and support (Hein & Ansari, 2022; Nario-Redmond, 2019).

Benevolent Ableism and the Evaluation of the Entrepreneur with Disability Subtype

Having established that evaluators will likely form a new subtype for the entrepreneur with disabilities, we now theorize about its evaluative content. In our theoretical framework, subtyping is the cognitive process through which evaluators construe a more specific category for the entrepreneur with disabilities, while benevolent ableism is the evaluative lens that shapes the specific stereotype content (i.e., the warmth and competence attributions) attached to that underdetermined subtype. We elaborate on each dimension below.

A first evaluative dimension is perceived warmth. Warmth judgments are made quickly, automatically, and are driven by the most affectively salient identity cue available (Eisenbruch & Krasnow, 2022; Fiske et al., 2002). A visible physical disability is precisely such a cue. Indeed, Rohmer and Louvet (2009) showed that people with a disability are immediately described by disability, independent of other social categorizations. We thus argue that the disability cue is likely to activate benevolent ableism associations.

Several features of benevolent ableism contribute to elevated warmth perceptions of entrepreneurs with disabilities. First, the visible disability may be linked to vulnerability, which activates caregiving and protective responses from perceivers (Cuddy et al., 2007). Second, entrepreneurs with disabilities may be perceived as non-competitive, that is, they do not threaten evaluators’ status, resources, or goals. In the SCM framework, perceived non-competitiveness is a key antecedent of warmth attributions (Fiske et al., 2002). Third, cultural narratives surrounding disability often emphasize moral virtues, further reinforcing perceptions of good character and good intentions (Nario-Redmond, 2019). Consistent with this fast warmth inference, one business angel from our key informant conversations 1 explicitly described an immediate trust reaction:Right off the stomach, I’d say they inspire more integrity and trust. Because they experienced bad things and maybe were treated badly. So they probably don’t want to treat other people badly. (I8)

These reactions illustrate the insidious nature of benevolent ableism: it feels positive, and evaluators whose perceptions are shaped by benevolent ableism may genuinely believe they are being kind and supportive. Yet the warmth they attribute reflects not the entrepreneur’s actual character but the perceiver’s paternalistic schema.

Importantly, the entrepreneurial role is not expected to exert meaningful downward pressure on warmth perceptions for this subtype. The “coldness” attributed to conventional entrepreneurs stems from perceptions that they are competitive and calculating (Fiske et al., 2002; Gupta et al., 2009). However, benevolent ableism effectively neutralizes these attributions: because the disability cue activates vulnerability and non-competitiveness perceptions, it removes the very basis of the coldness stereotype. The entrepreneur with a disability is seen as someone who has courageously entered a domain despite significant personal challenges. In this way, benevolent ableism does not merely preserve the high warmth of the disability stereotype; it actively defuses the coldness otherwise associated with the entrepreneurial role. One business angel illustrates this dynamic, explicitly contrasting entrepreneurs with disabilities against negative perceptions of conventional founders:So some negatively associated behaviors that you sometimes see also with entrepreneurs, they [entrepreneurs with disabilities] would be less likely (I15).

An entrepreneur with disability noted that investors perceive founders with disabilities as “softer” (E2). This paternalistic warmth response, grounded not in the entrepreneur’s actual character but in the perceiver’s stereotype-driven assumptions of vulnerability and gentleness, should lead people to rate entrepreneurs with disabilities as warmer, more trustworthy, and more likable than their non-disabled counterparts. Thus, we put forward:

H1a: Entrepreneurs with (vs. without) physical disabilities will be rated higher on perceived warmth by crowds.

Warmth perceptions also carry direct evaluative consequences. Perceived warmth can be paramount in the evaluation of entrepreneurial ventures, as it fundamentally influences the assessment of the risk posed by misbehavior and expectation of venture success (Maxwell & Lévesque, 2014). In early-stage investment contexts where formal assurances are lacking, elements of warmth such as trust and cooperativeness compensate by reducing perceived risk and signaling alignment of interests (Ciuchta et al., 2018; Huang & Knight, 2017). This is especially true in crowd evaluation settings, where evaluators have even less access to formal due diligence and must rely more heavily on impression-based cues (Drover et al., 2017; Mollick, 2014). Indeed, Johnson et al. (2018) showed that trustworthiness perceptions, a core element of warmth, directly increase the crowds’ willingness to invest in early-stage ventures. Overall, perceptions of warmth can mitigate uncertainty and enhance the crowd’s confidence in their initial screening. The following hypothesis captures this relationship:

H1b: Perceptions of warmth are positively related to investment intentions.

H1b: Perceptions of warmth are positively related to investment intentions.

Combined, we then expect that:

H1c: Greater perceptions of warmth will mediate the positive effect of physical disability on investment intentions.

H1c: Greater perceptions of warmth will mediate the positive effect of physical disability on investment intentions.

A second evaluative dimension is perceived competence. While people with disabilities are stereotyped as low in competence (Fiske et al., 2002), we focus on how competence judgments are recalibrated when an entrepreneur with disability is cognitively segregated into a new subtype when pitching for external funding. Unlike general societal encounters with people with disabilities or low-skill contexts from prior research, where there is often no diagnostic competence information to counteract the low-competence stereotype, an entrepreneurial pitch can provide diagnostic evidence of capability: the entrepreneur has developed a business and is presenting an investment-ready venture. This evidence prevents the simple transfer of the low-competence stereotype, but it is available equally to disabled and non-disabled entrepreneurs. What differs is the interpretive lens through which evaluators process it. Because evaluators lack an established, role-specific schema for an entrepreneur with disability, this diagnostic evidence is interpreted through the same culturally prevalent lens, benevolent ableism (Campbell, 2001; Nario-Redmond et al., 2019).

A central feature of benevolent ableism is heroic construal, a cultural-narrative phenomenon (Zhang & Haller, 2013) that frames disability as so debilitating that functioning at a normal level constitutes an extraordinary achievement (Nario-Redmond, 2019). Disability scholars term this the “supercrip” narrative (Clogston, 1990; Silva & Howe, 2012): people with disabilities who achieve success are framed as extraordinary heroes who have overcome their disability, rather than as equally competent individuals who happen to have a disability. This narrative shapes the reference point against which performance is judged, setting evaluators’ baseline expectations lower for people with disabilities. Because heroic construal lowers this reference point, the same entrepreneurial achievement of presenting an investment-ready venture exceeds expectations by a wider margin for an entrepreneur with disability than for a non-disabled one. Evaluators translate admiration for “overcoming” (Nario-Redmond et al., 2019, p. 729) into higher perceived capability, even when objective performance is held constant. The result is an outsized positive competence impression, a pattern Biernat (2003) terms shifting standards: evaluators judge members of different groups against different reference points, and meeting or exceeding a lowered standard produces inflated subjective ratings.

Entrepreneurs with disabilities from our key informant conversations recognized this heroic construal narrative from their daily experience. As one wheelchair-using founder recounts:The first initial reaction is always the same. They are fascinated. […] You have people telling you, Oh my God, it’s so inspiring. (E1)

The result is that an entrepreneur with disability is expected to receive higher perceived competence ratings than a non-disabled entrepreneur presenting an identical pitch, not because the entrepreneur with disability has demonstrated greater competence, but because their performance is interpreted through a heroic construal fueled by patronizing admiration. Thus:

H2a: Entrepreneurs with (vs. without) physical disabilities will be rated higher on perceived competence by crowds.

H2a: Entrepreneurs with (vs. without) physical disabilities will be rated higher on perceived competence by crowds.

While warmth determines the valence of the judgment (positive vs. negative), competence defines the magnitude of that valence (Fiske et al., 2007) and therefore also plays an important role in social evaluations. Perceptions of the entrepreneur’s capability and preparedness are important criteria used during screening (Chen et al., 2009; Maxwell et al., 2011). Entrepreneurs who are perceived as more competent are seen as more likely to achieve milestones, attract customers and talent, and successfully exit (Svetek, 2023), leading to more favorable evaluations of their pitches. In crowd evaluation contexts specifically, signals of entrepreneurial competence facilitate fundraising success (Ahlers et al., 2015; Oo et al., 2022). Overall, elevated competence perceptions, regardless of whether they reflect accurate assessments, should positively influence investment evaluations because they signal that the entrepreneur can deliver. Thus:

H2b: Perceptions of competence are positively related to investment intentions.

H2b: Perceptions of competence are positively related to investment intentions.

Taken together, we further propose that:

H2c: Greater perceptions of competence will mediate the positive effect of physical disability on investment intentions.

H2c: Greater perceptions of competence will mediate the positive effect of physical disability on investment intentions.

Having derived the specific warmth and competence pathways (H1a–c and H2a–c), we note that they jointly support the overarching prediction stated at the outset of this section: entrepreneurs with a physical disability (vs. non-disabled entrepreneurs) will receive higher investment intentions. We examine this overarching prediction first (Study 1), before testing the constituent pathway hypotheses that unpack how the effect operates through warmth and competence perceptions (Studies 2 and 3).

Overview of Studies

We conducted three experimental studies in which we randomly assigned participants to experimental conditions. Experiments enable us to control the presence of a physical disability and to assess, with high internal validity, whether the existence of a disability causes different evaluations among participants. It also enabled us to eliminate effects related to the characteristics of the individual entrepreneur (e.g., age, gender, etc.) and verbal or nonverbal parameters (e.g., language and gesturing, and attractiveness), which were all held constant.

We conducted our experiments with samples of individuals with self-reported investment experience (i.e., “the crowd”) recruited from Prolific Academic, a UK-based online platform that specializes in research recruitment (Palan & Schitter, 2018). Prolific is widely used in entrepreneurship and entrepreneurial finance research (Fellnhofer & Deng, 2024; Nguyen et al., 2024; Zunino et al., 2022) and has been found to produce data of higher quality than comparable behavioral research crowdsourcing platforms (Peer et al., 2017, 2021). Prolific achieves this by incorporating multiple data quality mechanisms, including CAPTCHA verifications to screen out bots, attention checks, manipulation checks, and time-based controls on task completion to ensure engaged and attentive participation (Chandler et al., 2024; Heupel et al., 2024).

For our experiments, we used multiple screening criteria and only recruited participants who were 18 years of age or older, resided in the United States, United Kingdom, and Western Europe, were fluent in English, and had a 95% prolific approval rate. Prolific’s preset screening ensured that all participants had self-reported experience with at least one form of investment (i.e., experience with stocks, crowdfunding, angel [syndicate] investing, private equity funds, venture capital funds—as in Butticè et al., 2022; Van Balen et al., 2019). In our post-experiment survey, we further assessed whether the participants had self-reported professional investment experience (business angel or venture capital), which we controlled for in the analyses.

In Study 1, we test our baseline that a pitch from an entrepreneur with a physical disability (in a wheelchair) is evaluated more favorably than the same pitch from a non-disabled entrepreneur. In Study 2, we replicate the baseline hypothesis in the context of an entrepreneur with a physical disability that might be viewed by the crowd as less severe (i.e., prosthetic leg), and we test our hypotheses on the mediating roles of warmth and competence (H1a–c and H2a–c). In Study 3, we again replicate our baseline hypothesis and the findings for both mediators (H1a–c and H2a–c), this time using the case of an entrepreneur with a physical disability that might be viewed as more severe by the crowd (being in a wheelchair, as in Study 1).

In addition to the hypothesized relations, we also test alternative explanations in the experiments. In Study 1, we test whether the positive main relation between an entrepreneur’s disability and investment intention can be ascribed to whether that new venture’s offering is disability-related (or not), accounting for potential effects of user entrepreneurship and venture-person fit (Shah & Tripsas, 2007). In Study 2, we test alternative mediating mechanisms between entrepreneurs’ disability and investment intentions related to attention and memory. Specifically, a visible disability may capture disproportionate visual attention due to its atypicality in an entrepreneurial context (Rinck & Becker, 2006), and this attentional shift could affect recall of pitch content (Baddeley & Hitch, 1974). To exclude attention and memory as alternative explanations for the relationship between disability and investment intentions, we use eye-tracking technology and a memory recall task.

Study 1

Study 1: Method

Using the abovementioned screening criteria, we constructed a sample of 341 participants with self-reported investment experience. We invited participants to watch a video of an entrepreneur pitching a new business by following a hyperlink to an online study website. Participants were instructed to imagine they were considering investing in a new venture and were about to view the entrepreneur’s funding pitch. After watching the presentation, participants completed various measures using an inbuilt survey tool.

We employed a one-factor, between-subjects 2 × 2 design and randomly assigned participants to one of the four treatment groups. We manipulated the presence of disability and the type of product pitched by the entrepreneur. We employed a professional actor and video-recorded four versions of the pitch. We chose the actor to match a prototypical entrepreneur, who is normally portrayed as a Caucasian middle-aged male (Cech, 2022). To ensure the presentations possessed psychological realism (Colquitt, 2008), we chose two products of real new ventures that have proven to attract investment and changed the identifying details: a disability-related product (a sit-on ski sleigh for paraplegic users) and a disability-unrelated product (foldable skis, convenient to carry). We selected these cases to fit our research design and because they belong to the same industry, allowing us to use almost the same precise pitch wording in both cases. Moreover, while novel and unfamiliar to the crowd, the products and associated technology do not require advanced knowledge to be assessed.

The first experimental factor we manipulated was the presence of a physical disability of the entrepreneur. In the disability condition, the entrepreneur delivered the pitch in a wheelchair, an obvious sign of a severe physical disability (see Figure 1, left). In the no disability condition, the entrepreneur delivered the pitch standing, without any signs of a physical disability (see Figure 1, right). The second experimental factor we manipulated was the disability-related offering. In the disability-related offering condition, the entrepreneur pitched the ski sleigh device (Figure 2, left). In the disability-unrelated offering condition, the entrepreneur pitched the foldable skis (Figure 2, right).

Manipulation of the disability condition: Entrepreneur with disability versus non-disabled entrepreneur (Studies 1 and 3).

Manipulation of the disability-related offering condition: Disability-related product and disability-unrelated product (Study 1).

Except for our manipulations, the pitches adhered to a strict script to ensure identical content across conditions. Specifically, each presentation contained information typically included in a pitch, including details on the product’s features (its evolution, development, technological competencies, and benefits), the characteristics of the target market (industry size and market growth), development of the business and strategy (sales to date, marketing, and distribution), details on the management team, and information on the funding amount sought. All four videos were approximately 2 min long, as would be typical for a new venture pitch, and showed the entrepreneur (i.e., actor) for the whole duration, as well as a picture of the product in a side window. Participants were required to watch the entire video and then report their investment intentions as well as various demographic control variables.

Before conducting the analysis, we excluded 35 responses because of non-completion of the questionnaire or failed attention tests (Meade & Craig, 2012). The sample that entered the final dataset included 306 participants’ answers, of which 156 were in the disability condition and 150 were in the non-disability condition.

Study 1: Measures

Disability

The non-disability condition was coded 0, and the disability condition was coded 1.

Disability-Related Offering

The disability-related offering condition (sit-on ski sleigh device) was coded 1, and the non-disability-related offering condition (foldable skis) was coded 0.

Investment Intentions

Following Clarke et al. (2019), we asked participants to indicate whether, based on the pitch, they would take the opportunity further with a view to investing in the business, by “finding out more information about the business,”“doing further research into the industry and/or market of the business,”“meeting with the entrepreneur again,” and “finding out more information about the entrepreneur/management team,” using a Likert-type scale (1 = “strongly disagree” to 5 = “strongly agree”). Investment intentions are an important dependent variable that captures the crowd’s willingness to further engage with a venture opportunity (Clarke et al., 2019). Our measure captures this evaluative judgment in a simulated investment context among participants with self-reported investment experience, rather than actual investment behavior. We averaged responses to these items to form a single score of investment intentions (α = .93).

Control Variables

We controlled for a range of variables identified in prior studies on new venture investment: participant gender (dummy coded 0 = man, 1 = woman) and age (in years) to account for homophily and risk preferences (Harrison & Mason, 2007); general human capital variables including education (coded 1–6 from no formal qualifications to PhD), work experience (in years), and founding experience (dummy coded 1 = founded a new venture); and investment-specific human capital including pitch viewing experience (dummy coded 1 = has pitch experience) and self-reported professional investment experience (dummy coded 1 = business angel or venture capital experience). We also controlled for participants having a disability or a close association with someone with a disability (Stone & Colella, 1996), skiing experience (both dummy coded), and attention to content (in milliseconds) as an attentional control.

Manipulation Checks

We conducted manipulation checks to assess the efficacy of our experimental manipulations of disability and disability-related offering. For the disability manipulation check, we asked participants, “Did the entrepreneur have a visible physical disability?” (coded as “1” for yes and “0” for no answer). An ANOVA showed a strong effect of assigned condition on the manipulation check, F(1, 304) = 2,700, p ≤ .001, ηp2 = .90. Overall, 97.4% of participants (298 of 306) correctly identified the assigned condition; see Supplemental Appendix B for a detailed breakdown of failure rates and robustness checks. 2

For the disability-related offering manipulation check, we asked participants, “Was the product that the entrepreneur was describing in the presentation disability-related (product aimed at disabled people as customers)?” (coded as “1” for yes or “0” for no answer). An ANOVA showed a strong effect of assigned condition on the manipulation check, F(1, 304) = 183, p ≤ .001, ηp2 = .38. In total, 80.7% of participants (247 of 306) correctly identified the product type; see Supplemental Appendix B for a detailed breakdown of failure rates and robustness checks (See Note 2).

Study 2

Study 2: Method

We conducted a second experiment with the main objective of testing the mediating effect of perceptions of warmth and competence in the relationship between disability and investment intentions. Furthermore, we wanted to investigate whether we can exclude alternative explanations, such as attentional and memory processes for the relationship between disability and investment intentions. We used eye-tracking to capture people’s attentional processes and a memory task to capture information processing.

We follow the same sample selection and procedure as in Study 1 with a non-overlapping sample. Of the 302 initial participants who passed the screening and started the eye-tracking session, we excluded 27 due to initial calibration errors of the eye-tracking or technical issues regarding head pose or lighting conditions. The remaining 275 participants’ answers, of which 129 were in the disability condition and 146 were in the non-disability condition, entered the final dataset.

We employ a one-factor between-subjects design and randomly assigned participants to one of the two treatment groups. We employed the same professional actor as in Study 1 and created two videos corresponding to the treatment groups. We based the script for the pitch on a real-life case of a new venture that had attracted external funding, and changed the identifying details (Colquitt, 2008). The case involves an entrepreneurial team that has developed an AI-based mobile application designed to help small business owners manage and grow their online sales.

For the experiment, we manipulated the entrepreneur’s physical disability by showing him with and without a prosthetic leg. The prosthetic, which was professionally photoshopped into the video after recording, was chosen as a visible but less severe disability than a wheelchair. The use of a photoshopped prosthetic also allowed the entrepreneur (actor) to maintain an identical standing posture in both conditions. In the disability condition, the entrepreneur appeared with a prosthetic leg (Figure 4, left); in the no disability condition, he appeared without one (Figure 4, right).

Manipulation of the disability condition: Entrepreneur with a disability versus non-disabled entrepreneur (Study 2).

Both videos were approximately 3:30 min long and showed the entrepreneur for the whole duration of the video. Participants were required to watch the entire video and then to report their investment intentions, their perception of the entrepreneur’s warmth and competence, as well as various demographic control variables.

Study 2: Measures

Investment Intentions

We use the same measure as in Study 1 from Clarke et al. (2019) and averaged responses to the items to form a single score of investment intentions (α = .86).

Warmth and Competence Perceptions

We follow Lee and Huang (2018) and included traits associated with warmth and competence, adapted from Fiske et al. (2002) and validated across a large body of studies in social psychology (Aaker, 1997; Judd et al., 2005; Yzerbyt et al., 2005). We asked participants to indicate on a scale from 1 = “strongly disagree” to 5 = “strongly agree” the extent to which they would describe the entrepreneur as compassionate, kind, and warm (for warmth) and competent, proficient, and adept (for competence). We averaged responses to the three items to form a single score of warmth (α = .83) and competence (α = .85).

Control Variables

We used the same controls as in Study 1, except for skiing experience.

Manipulation Check

As in Study 1, we asked participants, “Did the entrepreneur have a visible physical disability?” (coded as “1” for yes or “0” for no answer). An ANOVA showed a strong effect of assigned condition on the manipulation check, F(1, 273) = 1,686, p < .001, ηp2 = .86. Overall, 265 out of 275 participants (96.4%) correctly identified the assigned condition; see Supplemental Appendix B for a detailed breakdown of failure rates and robustness checks. 3

Results for Testing Alternative Explanations of Memory and Attention

To account for attentional processes as an alternative explanation, we utilized eye-tracking. We used remote webcam-based eye-tracking, a method shown to produce reliable gaze data (Semmelmann & Weigelt, 2018; Van der Cruyssen et al., 2024; Yang & Krajbich, 2021) to track participants’ eye movements during the pitch videos. As a measure of visual attention, we used the total dwell time on the leg-region area of interest (the area where the disability cue was presented, see Figure 4; in milliseconds). We then used the natural log of this measure (Butticè et al., 2022). Dwell time is a typical eye-tracking measure indicating attention (Duchowski, 2017; Holmqvist et al., 2011). Following Madera and Hebl (2012), participants’ memory was tested using a 20-item multiple-choice test (with four answer options) on facts about the entrepreneurs and their new venture, based on what was said during the pitch. The number of correct items was summed up and used as the measure of memory.

We tested the mediation model (PROCESS custom model) with three parallel mediators (warmth, competence, and attention) and a fourth sequential mediator from attention to investment intentions (recall) by including all the estimated relationships in one model. The results show that while warmth (albeit marginally) and competence still show evidence of mediation (β = .04, p = .084 for warmth and β = .12, p ≤ .01 for competence), attention (β = .005, p = .62) and recall (β = .003, p = .77) were not significant mediators. Also, the path from attention to recall was not a significant mediator of the baseline relation between disability and investment intentions (β = .004, p = .78). Interestingly, despite the marginally positive effect of disability on attention (β = .01, p = .10) and the negative relation between attention and recall (β = −10.6, p = .052), the relationship between disability and investment intention remains positive and is not mediated by attention and recall. These additional findings add strength to our main mediation explanations of competence and warmth grounded in the SCM.

Study 3

Study 3 aims to further establish the robustness of the results of the previous studies and assess whether the mediating roles of warmth and competence identified in Study 2 are generalizable across physical disabilities of varying severity.

Discussion

Understanding whether entrepreneurial finance markets are inclusive and why some groups of entrepreneurs are evaluated differently are central issues in entrepreneurship research (Anglin et al., 2025). This study investigates how and why crowds respond differently to pitches by entrepreneurs with physical disabilities compared to their non-disabled peers. While the prevailing narrative suggests that unconventional entrepreneurs naturally face negative evaluations, our findings reveal a more complex pattern where bias can also manifest as patronizing positivity. Across three experimental studies, we document this counterintuitive dynamic: crowds evaluate physically disabled entrepreneurs as a distinct subtype and report higher initial investment intentions. We argue that benevolent ableism inflates evaluations through two pathways: higher warmth ratings via paternalistic stereotype activation, and higher competence ratings via heroic construal. These effects hold across different physical disability presentations (i.e., wheelchair use vs. a prosthetic leg), supporting the robustness of the findings. Key informant conversations with investors and entrepreneurs provide contextual material that is broadly consistent with this interpretation and illustrate how these dynamics may be expressed in practice.

Contributions

Our research makes three main contributions to the minority entrepreneurship literature, the scarce literature on entrepreneurs with disabilities, and entrepreneurial finance research.

Subtyping in Minority Entrepreneurship Research

In the unconventional entrepreneurship literature (Bakker & McMullen, 2023), minority entrepreneurs are largely assumed to be evaluated through direct stereotype transfer from the “parent” minority category to the entrepreneurial role. In other words, female entrepreneurs are evaluated as “women,” entrepreneurs with disabilities as “people with disabilities,” and so forth. We challenge this implicit inheritance logic. Consistent with stereotype subtyping (Fiske & Neuberg, 1990; Fiske et al., 2009) and contextualized stereotype activation (Blair, 2002; Wittenbrink et al., 2001), we demonstrate that entrepreneurs with physical disabilities who pitch investment-ready ventures are decoupled from the minority parent category and evaluated as a distinct social subtype, neither as the average person with disabilities nor as the conventional entrepreneur.

While the social psychology literature has documented pre-existing subtypes for gender and race such as professional women versus homemakers, Black professionals versus Black people generally (Fiske et al., 2002, 2009), the entrepreneurship literature has not yet engaged with these findings. For disability, the gap is even more fundamental, since the social psychology literature itself treats disability as a relatively undifferentiated category with no established “disabled professional” subtype for evaluators to draw on (Fiske, 2018; Fiske et al., 2002, 2009; Rohmer & Louvet, 2018). We argue and show that evaluators will, therefore, have to form a new subtype for an entrepreneur with disability, making the evaluative content of this subtype especially dependent on the dominant cultural lens evaluators bring to the encounter. Because benevolent ableism is the culturally dominant framework through which people process disability cues (Campbell, 2001; Nario-Redmond et al., 2019), it becomes the lens that strongly shapes the new subtype’s content.

Our contribution to the unconventional entrepreneurship literature is that we show that the implicit parent category inheritance logic does not hold for disability, and we explain why: the absence of an established subtype makes disability evaluations uniquely susceptible to the specific distortions of benevolent ableism. More broadly, our findings call for a shift in the conversation in unconventional entrepreneurship research from asking whether minority entrepreneurs are evaluated negatively to asking how contextual role entry restructures stereotype content itself.

The Multi-Dimensional Evaluation of Entrepreneurs with Disabilities Subtype and Patronizing Positivity

Prior work has largely invoked the SCM trade-off logic that unconventional entrepreneurs are seen as warmer but less competent (Lee & Huang, 2018) to predict and interpret evaluative differences from conventional entrepreneurs, or focused on the negative (i.e., less competent) evaluative dimension for unconventional entrepreneurs only (Balachandra et al., 2019). We provide evidence that directly challenges the trade-off and negative evaluation assumption by identifying patronizing positivity as a distinct evaluative pattern where bias inflates both warmth and competence simultaneously. Crucially, these effects are not necessarily driven by neutral evaluative coherence, as sometimes suggested (Fiske et al., 2007), but by subtle bias.

Building on benevolent ableism (Nario-Redmond et al., 2019), we show why both warmth and competence evaluations can be simultaneously high: for entrepreneurs with disabilities, warmth rises via paternalistic assumptions of vulnerability and non-competitiveness, while competence rises via heroic construal, that is, the supercrip narrative that frames ordinary achievement as extraordinary when accomplished by someone with a disability. The result is a form of subjective evaluative inflation that maintains underlying inequality. Importantly, this is not a positive evaluation rooted in genuine admiration of demonstrated merit; rather, it is a positive evaluation rooted in patronizing assumptions of helplessness and lowered expectations. By introducing patronizing positivity as a distinct evaluative phenomenon, we extend the SCM by identifying a mechanism through which warmth and competence dimensions co-elevate under bias rather than trade-off, and we demonstrate that in high-skill, high-stakes contexts, positivity itself can serve as a form of discrimination.

We also extend the benevolent ableism literature (Nario-Redmond, 2019) itself: prior research on benevolent ableism has predominantly examined low-skill, non-agentic social encounters (Dwertmann et al., 2025; Kulkarni & Gopakumar, 2014), leaving open whether and how it shapes evaluations in high-skill, high-stakes contexts. Our findings suggest that it does, and in such contexts its warmth and competence pathways activate simultaneously and compound one another in ways not previously theorized.

Structural Barriers in the Entrepreneurial (Finance) Process

Prior studies acknowledge that people with disabilities face structural barriers to entrepreneurship, including funding constraints (Renko et al., 2016). However, the field lacks insight into the specific mechanisms through which these barriers operate (Bakker & McMullen, 2023). Entrepreneurial finance and crowdfunding literature have made significant strides in understanding how evaluator impressions shape early-stage funding (Ahlers et al., 2015; Johnson et al., 2018; Oo et al., 2022), yet disability remains a blind spot in this work. More broadly, the implicit assumption running through both the entrepreneurial finance literature and the broader unconventional entrepreneurship literature is that the pitch evaluation stage is a likely site of exclusion—that entrepreneurs with disabilities are screened out early because evaluators respond to disability cues with lower assessments.

Our evidence of inflated initial evaluations might be viewed as a paradox. Indeed, entrepreneurs with disabilities face documented funding gaps (Renko et al., 2016), while our evidence highlights that they receive favorable initial impressions. However, combined, this evidence suggests that the pitch stage is not the primary site of exclusion (McSweeney et al., 2025). If entrepreneurs with disabilities receive favorable initial impressions yet still face substantial funding gaps, the barriers must lie elsewhere. For example, they emerge in later stages of due diligence where patronizing positivity may give way to conventional discrimination, in structural mechanisms that operate independently of evaluator impressions, or in an intention-behavior disconnect, where the superficial desire to be supportive fails to materialize into actual capital allocation. Our findings thus do not merely add evidence to an existing debate; they redirect the scholarly search for bias and exclusion toward later stages, calling for greater scrutiny of exclusion mechanisms that operate beyond the initial pitch evaluation, such as subsequent due diligence or capital allocation.

Limitations and Avenues for Further Research

Despite being one of the first studies to quantitatively investigate how entrepreneurs with disabilities are evaluated, and to show repeated, causal evidence of our claims through three experiments, the study is not without its limitations. First, as highlighted before, the study utilized a prototypical entrepreneur (i.e., a Caucasian man in his early 40s) in the experimental design to isolate the impact of disability. This choice may limit the findings’ applicability across entrepreneurs of different races and genders. Future research should examine how varying these characteristics may influence crowd perceptions, particularly in intersectional contexts where race, gender, and disability overlap.

Second, disability is not a single, uniform concept; rather, it encompasses a wide range of conditions that differ in type and severity (Dwertmann, 2016). Our research focused exclusively on physical disabilities, limiting the generalizability of the findings to other types of disabilities, such as cognitive, sensory, or mental disabilities. While our experiments used two different physical disability presentations (wheelchair use vs. prosthetic leg) as a robustness check, we did not theorize or test severity as a moderator. In both conditions, the disability was clearly visible, and the entrepreneur remained relatively high-functioning. Future research should examine whether severity moderates the strength of the benevolent ableism mechanisms we build on—for example, whether more severe disabilities produce stronger heroic construal effects or whether extremely severe disabilities might instead trigger hostile rather than benevolent ableism.

Third, our experiments are designed to test the downstream evaluative consequences of benevolent ableism based on theories of subtyping and stereotype content. Consequently, we do not directly measure benevolent ableism as an explicit, individual-level attitude. Inferring ambivalent bias from structural patterns in evaluations—rather than relying on direct self-reports that often fail to capture paternalistic or implicit attitudes—is a robust methodological approach in social psychology (Cuddy et al., 2007; Dovidio & Gaertner, 2000). Nonetheless, we acknowledge the possibility that alternative mechanisms could produce similar inflation in warmth and competence ratings (e.g., guilt-driven overcorrection). Moreover, our framing of benevolent ableism as the most culturally available narrative assumes a Western cultural context (Nario-Redmond, 2019), and different cultural scripts around disability may produce different evaluative dynamics elsewhere (Ingstad & Whyte, 2007). We hasten to stress that our findings may not translate to all potential investors. One angel investor, for example, explained (why) he had a negative bias:It would be difficult for me to invest honestly [in entrepreneurs with disabilities]. Because being a founder is a tough journey where you have to be a leader for lots of people and this person [entrepreneurs with disabilities] will struggle. I’m afraid this is my honest answer. (I17)

Lastly, our findings are limited to the initial screening stage of the investment process and may not generalize to later stages such as business due diligence, evaluation of the management team, and term sheet negotiation. While benevolent ableism may produce inflated evaluations at the initial screening, those apparently favorable surface-level assessments may become less prominent in later stages of the fundraising process, while other underlying biases can persist or adapt, for example “prove it again” bias, where initial credibility must be repeatedly re-established (Bertrand & Mullainathan, 2004). Relatedly, as evaluators gain information about or repeated exposure to entrepreneurs with disabilities, reference points may converge, reducing the heroic-construal “extra credit” and making competence judgments less dependent on disability-linked baselines. Such convergence is desirable when it reflects equalized standards, meaning the same pitch evidence produces similar competence inferences regardless of disability, but problematic if it reflects a transition from patronizing positivity to more conventional discrimination or exclusion in later-stage decisions. Future research should examine the possibility of such dynamics across time.

More broadly, future research should employ observational, field-experimental, or ethnographic approaches to examine whether the patterns we identify in crowd-based experimental settings also appear in actual funding decisions and investor–founder interactions.

Practical Implications

Our findings offer several implications for early-stage venture evaluators, ecosystem builders, and entrepreneurs seeking to build inclusive entrepreneurial ecosystems.

Potential crowd investors and early-stage venture evaluators more broadly should be aware that apparently favorable evaluations can still be fundamentally biased. When evaluating early-stage pitches, feeling inspired by an entrepreneur’s perceived resilience or assuming they have high integrity due to their disability are signals that benevolent ableism is actively influencing the evaluation. To mitigate this, investment committees and accelerator screening panels should implement standardized evaluation rubrics to consciously anchor their judgments to objective industry baselines (e.g., market size, customer acquisition cost, and technical viability) and ensure that evaluations reflect the merits of the venture rather than the cultural scripts evaluators bring to the encounter.

For policymakers and ecosystem builders, our qualitative conversations raise the possibility that this initial positivity may not translate into actual funding decisions, a pattern our informants described vividly as receiving admiration but not capital. As founders recount:I get a lot of very positive reactions from everybody… but in the end that doesn’t help from the business side, you know you need the money to develop products… I need money, not recognition. Because I can’t pay with recognition. (E1)So there was definitely a sort of wanting to be supportive and helpful. But fundamentally weren’t going to invest. Then I was there for the money, not for someone to be polite or caring for me. (E5)

If future observational work confirms that this disconnect holds more broadly, then the most consequential barriers may lie not at the screening stage but at later decision points such as due diligence, term sheet negotiations, and ongoing investor-founder relationships. Interventions focused solely on helping entrepreneurs with disabilities “get in the door” through pitch training, while valuable, would then be insufficient. If the entrepreneurial ecosystem is to move past heroic construal, future efforts must focus on ensuring that initial positivity translates into equitable financial backing, rather than stopping at well-intentioned praise.

Finally, while it is unfair that the burden of managing evaluator bias should fall on the entrepreneur, our findings may offer some practical guidance for entrepreneurs with disabilities themselves. As one founder described:Somebody’s always going to be over here going, ‘they can’t do that, they’re not capable,’ then somebody else is going to be on this side going, ‘oh my God, they’re incredible’… what we have to do is shut that out and be like, we’re just going to do our thing. (E6)

Because evaluators may be prone to subtyping and heroic construal, entrepreneurs with disabilities might wish to consider, where feasible, steering pitch interactions away from personal resilience narratives and toward objective venture evidence (e.g., traction, unit economics, and customer validation). Redirecting attention toward venture fundamentals may encourage evaluators to base judgments on commercial merit, rather than on a heroic construal of the founder’s competence. However, deeper responsibility lies with evaluators and ecosystems to recognize and counteract patronizing positivity at its source.

Conclusion

This study set out to understand how crowds evaluate pitches by entrepreneurs with physical disabilities. Our central finding is that bias in this context does not take the form the field has come to expect. Rather than depressing evaluations, disability cues inflate them, producing patronizing positivity, in which both warmth and competence perceptions are elevated through paternalistic assumptions rather than genuine recognition of merit. Across three experiments, this pattern proved robust across different physical disability presentations, and key informant conversations with investors and entrepreneurs with disabilities revealed how positive surface-level evaluations can coexist with deeper patterns of exclusion. Together, these findings provide initial evidence that entrepreneurs with physical disabilities are evaluated as a distinct subtype, and benevolent ableism shapes the warmth and competence content ascribed to this subtype.
