Forging forward: SG60 and Changi General Hospital at 90
Authors: Kee Chong Ng, Chuin Siau, Chien Earn Lee, Kwong Ming Fock, Gek Kheng Png, Jessica Xia Yu
Published: 2025

Changi General Hospital (CGH) stands today as a cornerstone of healthcare in Singapore’s eastern region, with a proud legacy spanning nine decades. In this article, we trace CGH’s remarkable evolution — from its early beginnings to its transformation into a modern, integrated care institution. Along the way, CGH has been home to pioneering medical professionals who played key roles in shaping Singapore’s healthcare landscape. We also highlight CGH’s unwavering commitment to patient-centred care, its leadership within the Eastern Health Alliance (EHA) and its ongoing efforts to build a healthier community through strong partnerships and innovative care models.

CHANGI HOSPITAL

Ninety years ago, in 1935, a 150-bed Royal Air Force (RAF) Hospital was set up by the British as part of Changi Naval Base on the small Barrack Hill along Netheravon Road. As the first healthcare institution in eastern Singapore, the RAF Hospital delivered more than 1000 babies during its time.[12] It was renamed the United Kingdom Military Hospital under ANZUK (Australia, New Zealand and the United Kingdom) in 1971 and became the Singapore Armed Forces (SAF) Hospital in December 1975. That same year, a 36-bed Changi Chalet Hospital, ‘located at a former British officer’s club on Halton Road about 100 metres away from the UK Military Hospital’, was opened, providing ‘emergency services to holidaymakers who had minor accidents or to people with other minor medical complaints’, as well as health services to Changi Prison, Pulau Tekong and Pulau Ubin polyclinics and SAF.[34] In July 1976, Changi Chalet Hospital merged with SAF Hospital to form the 180-bed Changi Hospital [Figure 1].[5]

Changi Hospital and its medical specialists in 1988.[5]

Etymologically, ‘Changi’ — written and pronounced as ‘Chengai’ — first appeared in our colloquial lingo in the 1800s, with a southeast area in Singapore called ‘Tanjong Changi’. The distinctive ‘Changi Tree’, standing at 76 m, was featured in sea navigation charts in 1888. This tree was felled in February 1942 ‘to prevent the Japanese from using it as a ranging point’.[678]

THOMSON ROAD AND TOA PAYOH HOSPITALS

Located at Toa Payoh Rise, the 396-bed Thomson Road Hospital was initially set up to care for ‘the acute sick on 20 May 1959 with a single ward, two doctors, and seven nursing staff’ [Figure 2].[9101112131415] It was renamed Thomson Road General Hospital in 1968 and later in 1975 as Toa Payoh Hospital (TPH). Thomson Road–Toa Payoh Hospital, together with Singapore General Hospital and Tan Tock Seng Hospital, was one of the three public general hospitals during Singapore’s early years.[9101112131415161718] ‘Prof Seah Cheng Siang [Figure 3] was the first Medical Superintendent and his … doctors included Dr Andrew Chew Guan Khuan (later … Permanent Secretary for Finance and Head of the Civil Service, and Chairman of the Public Service Commission). Prof Lim Pin (later Vice-Chancellor of the National University of Singapore from 1981 to 2000) and Dr FJ Jayaratnam (later Head of Department of Medicine I of TTSH and Emeritus Consultant in Geriatric Medicine of Changi General Hospital) received their postgraduate clinical training in the hospital’ [Figure 4].[9]

CHANGI GENERAL HOSPITAL

Birthed from the merger of Changi Hospital and TPH in 1997, CGH is the first purpose-built regional general hospital serving eastern Singapore and the first acute hospital that integrates care with a community hospital [Figure 8]. A groundbreaking ceremony was held on 28 November 1992 and officiated by Mr Yeo Cheow Tong, then Minister for Health and Community Development. Toa Payoh Hospital closed on 15 February 1997 and moved to its new premises at ‘New Changi Hospital’ from 18 December 1996, and it was subsequently renamed ‘Changi General Hospital’ [Figure 9].[2425]

Changi General Hospital — merger of Changi Hospital with Toa Payoh Hospital.

Singapore’s then Deputy Prime Minister, BG (NS) Lee Hsien Loong officially opened CGH on 28 March 1998 [Figure 10], declaring ;… the new name reflects (CGH’s) role as a regional hospital offering a broad range of services, and the main provider of hospital services for the 750,000 Singaporeans living east of the Kallang Basin’.[27]

Initial years (1997–2011)

From 1997, CGH introduced new medical specialties, namely dermatology, psychiatric medicine, geriatric medicine, respiratory medicine, renal medicine and sports medicine. New services, including the Integrated Sleep Centre, Breast Centre@Changi, Endoscopy Centre and Changi Sports Medicine Centre (CSMC) were also established. As Singapore’s largest multidisciplinary sports facility, CSMC is the leading provider of sports medicine services and programmes. CGH became part of the SingHealth cluster from 2000 to 2011.

As early as 2001, CGH pioneered an online pharmacy to enable patients and the public to purchase homecare and retail medical supplies online. It also started online healthcare assessment tools for corporations and individuals. It was the second hospital in Singapore to receive the Joint Commission International (JCI) Accreditation (11 June 2005) and the first hospital to have a JCI accreditation for its Heart Failure Programme and Acute Myocardial Infarction Programme.

Eastern Health Alliance (2011–2018)

In 2011, with the national direction to transform healthcare as a continuum of services with the patient at the centre rather than as episodic transactional events (especially for individuals with long-term medical conditions), CGH became a founding partner of EHA, a regional health system for communities in eastern Singapore. Together with EHA’s other foundation partners [Figure 11] — Saint Andrew’s Community Hospital (SACH), SingHealth Polyclinics, Salvation Army Peacehaven Nursing Home and Health Promotion Board — the group collaborated to create and deliver innovative community health initiatives that scaled to become population-level programmes.[282930]

Eastern Health Alliance founding partners—Changi General Hospital, Saint Andrew’s Community Hospital, SingHealth Polyclinics, Salvation Army Peacehaven Nursing Home and Health Promotion Board.

As the acute care partner within EHA, CGH focused on four strategic thrusts: saving lives, restoring optimal functionality, providing complex outpatient care and serving as a strong resource for the alliance. In collaboration with EHA, CGH played a key role in developing and delivering numerous healthcare delivery innovations, including the Health Management Unit, which provides telecare support for discharged patients with chronic conditions; community health centres, which support general practitioners (GPs) with screening and ancillary services for their patients; transitional care programmes, which help bridge care between hospital and home; GPFirst, a programme that aims to reduce the number of less serious cases presenting to the accident and emergency (A&E) department and encourage patients to visit their GPs instead; the Heart Failure Telehealth programme; and Eastern Community Health Outreach, a community-based, chronic disease prevention programme that was awarded the People’s Association Community Spirit Merit Award in 2017.

In 2011, CGH embarked on the redevelopment of its Simei Campus to create new spaces, as well as expand its facilities and capabilities. The Integrated Building (IB), a new 280-bed facility developed in partnership with SACH, began operations in December 2014. It offers seamless integration of acute, subacute and rehabilitation services, all anchored in a patient-centred model of care. Designed to facilitate independent living, the IB empowers patients on their journey back to wellness. It houses patients in clusters of 10 beds with a common dining room, family area and rehabilitation facilities to encourage interactivity and peer support among patients. It also makes use of alcoves along the corridors as rehabilitation, family meeting and consultation spaces. The comprehensive integrated rehabilitation centre provides needs-driven care with predischarge rehabilitation in mock-up public flats, a geriatric day hospital for elderly patients with complex issues, as well as the SACH Day Rehab Centre and therapy gardens.

The CGH Medical Centre, completed in 2018, has transformed CGH’s specialist outpatient care model and capabilities, with the introduction of new models such as integrated multidisciplinary clinics (e.g., Diabetes Obesity Metabolism and Endocrine clinic). In addition, remodelling and expansion of the main CGH building, including wards, operating theatres, A&E and public spaces within the building, were carried out [Figures 12–14].

Changi General Hospital nursing

Geriatric nursing development

The development of geriatric nursing at CGH began in 1998 with the establishment of two dedicated geriatric wards. This marked the beginning of a concerted effort to provide specialised care to older adults. Initially, the focus was on building competency in holistic approaches to geriatric care and fundamental nursing skills such as intermittent catheterisation. Targeted training programmes, such as geriatric and continence workshops, were conducted to enhance nurses’ knowledge and skills.

In 2014, Nurses Improving Care for Healthsystem Elders (NICHE) was introduced in CGH. The programme was designed with the aim of providing high-quality, patient-centred care to older adults. To identify gaps in care practices, a needs assessment involving literature review, patient outcome analysis, and staff and patient feedback was conducted. Areas of assessment included skin health, delirium management, inpatient fall prevention, dementia care and medication management. A comprehensive plan, including development of care protocols, staff education, policy revisions and environmental modifications, was established to address these gaps.

Geriatric Nursing implemented the NICHE model with key components, including Geriatric Care Champion (GCC) training and development, geriatric care protocols, and an interdisciplinary team approach and services. A cohort of nursing GCC was recruited and trained to serve as resources and champions for geriatric care. The unit also developed and implemented geriatric care protocols, such as guidelines and workflow for pressure injury management, fall prevention, delirium detection, postural hypotension protocol and frailty. Staff education and engagement were critical to the success of the NICHE programme. Ongoing education and training were provided to all staff, including nurses, physicians and allied health professionals, along with other quality improvement initiatives and feedback. A quality improvement team was formed to monitor and evaluate the effectiveness of the initiatives. Key performance indicators, including patient outcomes, staff satisfaction and family satisfaction, were tracked, and regular audits were conducted to ensure compliance with geriatric care protocols.

In 2024, CGH NICHE received the ‘Exemplar’ site status in recognition of its commitment to excellence in geriatric care [Figure 15]. Throughout the NICHE journey, we found many opportunities for growth and development. These include building consensus and engaging stakeholders to drive culture change for geriatric care excellence, leveraging limited resources to drive innovation and improvement, and sustaining momentum and energy over time to ensure ongoing success for better geriatric care.

Advanced Practice Nurse development

The CGH Advanced Practice Nurse (APN) journey began in 2011 when three pioneering APNs established a structured Collaborative Practice Agreement with their collaborating physicians. This initiative introduced new care services by defining clinical roles and responsibilities, developing clinical guidelines for pharmacological management and outlining medication furnishing processes for the collaborative prescriber. APNs were granted the right to furnish medication and conduct patient reviews in the outpatient clinics, with prescriptions cosigned by the collaborating physicians. This new model of advanced nursing practice expanded the scope of nursing in our hospital.

When the National Collaborative Prescribing Programme (NCPP) was launched in the National University of Singapore (NUS) in 2018, the pioneering APNs completed the programme and were certified as collaborative prescribing practitioners. In 2019, the Credentialing Committee and Service Review Committee — composed of physicians, pharmacists, nursing leaders and senior APNs — were established to govern the safe practice of APNs, ensuring quality patient care.

By 2024, APN services had expanded to 20 areas with 27 APNs, and 26 APNs had completed NCPP. The services provided by APNs span from nursing home and community care to inpatient adult and adult acute care. APNs work collaboratively with other healthcare professionals to provide quality care to patients. Some APNs have become important members of medical emergency, palliative care, trauma and vascular teams. The CGH Wound Healing Centre, the only one of its kind among public hospitals, was established in 2021 to provide one-stop care for patients with chronic wounds through a multidisciplinary approach.

In addition, our APNs conduct regular training sessions for healthcare professionals, both within CGH and across the wider healthcare community. Several APNs have been appointed as adjunct faculty in various institutes of higher learning. They also contribute to various internal and external workgroups and committees, supporting improvements in clinical practice, nursing professional development and formulation of national healthcare policies. Our APNs strive to achieve excellence by integrating and promoting nursing research, evidence-based practices, continuous learning, innovation and quality improvement initiatives. To date, they have contributed to more than 20 publications, both as main and co-authors. Moving forward, CGH APNs will continue to lead clinical nursing practice, as well as contribute to the professional development of the nursing community and the body of evidence for nursing practice.

Nursing research development

Evidence-based practice improves quality patient care and outcomes and optimises workforce capability. A research culture that is rich and productive does not happen by chance, but by deliberate cultivation over time.[313233]

To establish an active research culture, the CGH Nursing Research Department has developed structured nursing research programmes. In 2022, a three-tiered strategy, targeting three groups of nurses, was established to cultivate nursing research culture. Tier 1 consisted of nurses with an innate passion for research, Tier 2 comprised nurses with a strong interest to conduct research projects and Tier 3 consisted of nurses with less research interest.

A research training roadmap was introduced to provide guidance and ensure systematic development of the nurses in research. A nursing research residency programme was implemented to ensure that interested and experienced nurses are given protected time, guidance, resources and recognition for their research projects. Various types of training are conducted to enhance research capabilities, and different platforms are identified for nurses to showcase their work. This systematic way of developing nurses in research ensures that more nurses will become competent in research and more evidence will be generated to support changes in clinical nursing practice.

Nursing quality

Monitoring and data mining of indicators is the foundation of Nursing Quality (NQ), driving future improvement initiatives. NQ has enhanced data accessibility through the development of various indicator dashboards using Tableau. This advancement allows users to perform real-time data drilldowns, which are no longer restricted by static charts.

The team from NQ has maintained strong engagement with nurses through workshops and active participation in incident reviews. A notable example is the Fall Safety Champions (FSCs) workshops initiated in 2010. At these quarterly workshops, the NQ team engages FSCs to provide updates on fall safety and encourages sharing of clinical challenges faced by them. These engagements have facilitated the implementation of crucial fall safety interventions, including standardisation of fall risk identifiers and the introduction of the ‘DUAL device’, a fall safety device that triggers an alarm when a magnetic pull-string attached to the patient is pulled away from the device.

Over the years, the NQ team’s presence and influence have grown substantially, with representation in numerous workgroups from cluster to departmental levels. In 2024, NQ participated as an expert panel member in the quarterly hospital patient safety event, addressing patient safety issues and advocating the importance of improving handover communication. Moving forward, NQ will continue to identify and evaluate risk factors related to nursing products and processes, utilising research methodologies and evidence-based, data-driven approaches to implement quality initiatives and innovation projects.

Nursing transformation

The CGH Nursing Transformation (NT) structures innovation for nurses. From 2013 to 2017, NT led numerous projects that integrated technology to address on-the-ground issues, enhancing innovation capabilities [Figure 16]. In 2016, time–motion studies identified five key nursing task areas for improvement, namely preparation and administration of intravenous medications, documentation, monitoring of patients, managing hygiene of immobile patients, and ordering and delivery of meals to patients. Since then, various innovation projects have been conducted to aid nurses in these manual tasks.

Overview of Changi General Hospital Nursing Innovation projects. CGH: Changi General Hospital.

A workflow was developed to guide innovation projects, with a Nursing Review Panel evaluating the ideas. In 2023, a Nursing Innovation Training Roadmap was designed to guide the training of nurses in innovation. To date, NT has hosted fellows from multiple institutions, promoting cross-institutional learning. Separately, the SparkTank Nursing Innovation Interest Group was formed in 2024 to better foster the culture of innovation. In addition, two of our nurses have completed the Singapore Biodesign Faculty Fellowship. CGH NT continues to evolve through successes and challenges, ensuring nursing remains at the forefront of healthcare innovation.

Niche areas and partnerships

Changi General Hospital rejoined SingHealth in 2018 [Figure 17]. With the establishment of the CGH-led SingHealth Duke-NUS Disease Centre for Sport and Exercise Medicine, we continue to lead in this field nationally and regionally, working closely with Sport Singapore to optimise care for our national athletes. With the recent announcement of the ‘Kallang Alive Masterplan’, the team will work with our national stakeholders to enhance sport and exercise medicine for better synergies with key sports and the Singapore Sports School.

The Changi General Hospital Simei Campus today.

The CGH Occupational and Aviation Medicine Service (OAMS) has been accredited as a training department for Aviation Medicine by the Joint Committee for Specialist Training, Ministry of Health, Singapore (MOH). The OAMS runs the Civil Aviation Medical Examination Centre, along with Civil Aviation Authority of Singapore and Singapore Changi Aeromedical Centre for the Republic of Singapore Airforce at Paya Lebar. This offers great opportunities to initiate impactful research and innovation collaborations to advance aviation medicine and health for air traffic controllers and pilots. The OAMS has also started training the Changi Airport Emergency Services in ‘First Responders Medical Skills’ through a modified prehospital trauma life support course and simulation training on clinical scenarios related to air crash incidents.

In the area of correctional health, CGH, along with SingHealth Polyclinics, serves the healthcare needs of Changi Prisons. We are expanding the use of telemedicine in the clinical care of prison inmates and systematically integrating onsite primary and psychiatric services. Together with Nanyang Polytechnic, we developed a specialist diploma in Correctional Health, with the inaugural batch in 2024 comprising CGH correctional health nurses working in Changi Prisons.

We are actively exploring the practice of complementary therapies, including the role of Traditional Chinese Medicine, as part of an integrative medicine approach. This aims to address not only the healthcare needs but also the physical, mental and psychosocial needs of our community, in line with our overall HealthierSG initiatives and with the support of MOH.

In 2023, CGH partnered with Shimadzu (Asia Pacific) to set up the Shimadzu–CGH Clinomics Centre (SC3), based in Science Park, both for the provision of laboratory tests and for research. Notably, on 22 February 2023, SC3 launched laboratory test services for plasma aldosterone and renin activity for investigation of primary aldosteronism.

As part of Singapore’s largest academic healthcare cluster, CGH has strong academic collaborations with key institutions of higher learning, particularly Institute of Technical Education, Nanyang and Temasek polytechnics, Singapore University of Technology and Design (SUTD), Singapore Institute of Technology and Singapore University of Social Sciences, in addition to partnerships with NUS, Duke-NUS Medical School and Nanyang Technological University.

Internationally, CGH has close collaborations with the Chang Gung Health Cluster and Cheng Kung University in Taiwan, not only in clinical medicine, but also in the fields of artificial intelligence (AI), and clinical innovation and care transformation.

Through CGH’s many community partners and collaborators, we have advanced our efforts to deliver seamless care and improve the health and well-being of the eastern Singapore community. Under HealthierSG and within SingHealth, CGH is responsible for the eastern region, which serves a population of 748,000 across six precincts [Figure 18].

Innovation, automation, digital medicine and a SMART hospital

Since the mid-2010s, CGH has led the way in innovation, robotics and automation. We strive to leverage technology to meet the evolving needs of patients, caregivers and staff, particularly in response to an ageing population and workforce. The CGH Centre for Innovation (CFI) is an innovation platform funded by the Economic Development Board of Singapore under its Living Lab Fund. Officially opened by President Tony Tan on 22 July 2012, CFI aims to develop an innovative culture in CGH through experimenting with new care models or prototypes through partnership with companies and to serve as a reference site for healthcare innovation. In 2016, CGH established the Office of Innovation (OOI) to better support our innovators, and CFI was integrated with OOI and renamed the Alice Lee Innovation Centre of Excellence at CGH Campus (A.L.I.C.E @CGH Campus) in 2024.

The Centre for Healthcare Assistive and Robotics Technology (CHART) was officially launched by President Tony Tan on 23 July 2015 [Figure 19]. The centre supports our national clinical/medical robotics programme by serving as a clinical anchor to provide perspectives on use cases for robotics and automation in healthcare facilities. It aims to inspire and enlarge the pool of future-ready clinicians who are able to translate between clinical and robotics engineering to address healthcare challenge statements through robotics and technologies. During the coronavirus disease 2019 pandemic, CHART deployed robots at the various treatment care facilities across Singapore to ensure smooth operational flow and patient care.

Official launch of CHART (Centre for Healthcare Assistive and Robotics Technology) in 2015.

In 2024, CGH set up the AI and Digital committee in partnership with AI Singapore and AI Verify Foundation (a non-profit foundation wholly owned by Infocomm Media Development Authority) to ensure that CGH staff are AI-literate in this age of generative and agentic AI. We also actively collaborate with various agencies, including SUTD, to ensure that CGH will have a sustainable pipeline of healthcare data analysts in the future.

Recognising the importance of nurturing our human capital, we are actively investing in developing and attracting the next generation of younger clinical innovators and researchers from among doctors, nurses, allied health and pharmacists. We strive to develop a green and ‘phygital’ health and healthcare system within CGH and set up a ‘SMART’ healthcare ecosystem — S: secure, standardised and sustainable (both economically and environmentally); M: measurable and meaningful (for the population and staff); A: adaptable and agile; R: responsive, reliable and resilient; and T: time-sensitive and timely.

TOMORROW’S MEDICINE

Today, CGH remains ‘the main provider of hospital services for the 750,000 Singaporeans living east of the Kallang Basin’, as so presciently articulated by Mr Lee Hsien Loong in 1998. Throughout its journey, CGH has strived to be a leader in healthcare innovation and a strong partner in integrating care services across eastern Singapore. It aims to be a caring hospital — trusted by both patients and staff — renowned for clinical excellence and innovation. CGH is committed to making healthcare better and more accessible, driving care models that optimise recovery and rehabilitation and enabling seamless delivery of care from hospital to home.

In 1984, during the celebration of TPH’s silver jubilee, the then Minister of Health, Mr Howe Yoon Chong, cited key principles to cope with changes as a public hospital in Singapore: (1) a hospital can only work well if it is properly planned; (2) plans should always take into account the inevitable changes a neighbourhood will face over one to two decades; and (3) there must be flexibility in planning the layout and administration of the hospital.[34] These principles still resonate and remain relevant today. Proper forward planning is crucial, so that the system can take into account the changes that will inevitably emerge. Plans and strategies must have agility and flexibility wired into their basic architecture and DNA because change remains the invariable constant, especially in today’s fourth Industrial Revolution. Just as Singapore advanced rapidly from third to first world over a period of 60 years, CGH has been integral in developing and advancing medicine in Singapore over the last 90 years.

CONCLUSION

As CGH celebrates its 90th anniversary [Figure 20], it is timely to reflect on how a military hospital and a modest chronic hospital — built on resilience and a steadfast commitment to serving the community — has evolved into a leading healthcare institution. Guided by this enduring mission, CGH will continue to forge forward to meet the needs of the 21st century.
