Performance and safety of the PYTHEAS Your Guided Trajectory® for intra-pedicular screw placement in spine surgery
Authors: Vincent Haignère, Aymeric Faure, Hadrien Giorgi, David Afonso, Guillaume Luquiens, Sarah Kabani, Thierry Chevallier, Pascal Kouyoumdjian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeSafe and accurate pedicular screw placement in spine posterior approach may be a challenging procedure. Navigation systems help reduce pedicular screw mal-positioning. This study aimed to assess the safety and usability of the PYTHEAS Your Guided Trajectory® angular assistance navigation device for pedicular screw placement.MethodsThis prospective, two-centre, single-arm, open-label, first-in-human study recruited 13 adult patients with indication for posterior arthrodesis of at least two lumbar or thoracic vertebrae with placement of pedicle screws at vertebral level. The primary outcome was the performance of the PYTHEAS medical device on the correct direction of pedicle aiming, allowing intrapedicular perforation, without false path. Calibration mode and surgery time were recorded. Surgeon satisfaction of the device was measured using custom questionnaires and usability was assessed according to the System Usability Scale (SUS) questionnaire. The operator defines levels of vertebrae, then per level, entry point and direction of screw, and calibration landmarks using a pre-operative standard CT scan. During surgery, the system is calibrated via specific or global mode according to surgeon preference. Pedicle is canulated using angular guided probe with a real-time display of its current versus planned orientation. Then screw is inserted using angular guided screwdriver.ResultsAll 83 cannulations were classed as having “good” placement according to the palpation technique and confirmed on screenshots of the trajectory taken versus that indicated by the device, and by CT scan. All screw placements were satisfactory on post-operative CT scan. No adverse events were recorded. Surgery time ranged from 50 min to 259 min, corresponding to 23.1 ± 14.7 min per pedicle operated in global calibration mode. Usability score was 74.2 ± 10.9, exceeding the threshold for satisfactory usability. Surgeons were very satisfied with the transfer time and ease of data transfer between the tablet and computer (84.6% and 76.9%, respectively).ConclusionsThe PYTHEAS device allowed accurate pedicular screws in thoracic, lumbar and sacral levels, with an excellent safety profile and user satisfaction.Trial registration NCT05565131, registered 30 September 2022.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-026-06749-9.Keywords: Accuracy, First-in-human, Navigation technology, Pedicle screw, Posterior arthrodesis, Spine surgery

Introduction

Spinal injury, such as a herniated disc, spinal deviation, or spinal instability due to disease, may require spinal fusion (or fusion of vertebrae or arthrodesis). Spondylodesis surgery often uses screws fixed in the vertebral bodies, either directly for anterior intervention, or through the bony structure (pedicle) uniting the posterior articular facets to the vertebral body for posterior intervention.

Pedicle screw placement starts with identifying the pedicle entry point, followed by perforation of the two pedicles, with verification of the correct pedicle path by palpation of the pedicle walls. Finally, the intrapedicular screw is placed following the planned route. Pedicle screw malposition is common, linked to poor positioning of the entry point and/or poor direction of the perforation path. The rate of implant malposition in freehand spine surgery varies between 6.4 and 65% in scoliosis [1–8], and around 9 to 10% of freehand perforations are poorly angled, resulting in poorly positioned screws [1–9]. Access to good quality images during surgery through pre-operative planning facilitates pedicle screw placement [10–12].

Electromyography has reduced the rate of reoperations in scoliosis surgery [13]. Surgical navigation allows the surgeon to visualise the procedure on a screen during surgery. Various surgical navigation technologies have increased pedicle screw placement accuracy, including opto-electronic technology, electromagnetic navigation, and rotating and motorised fluoroscopy devices technology [14, 15]. Cadaveric studies have previously demonstrated the feasibility and precision of inertial measurement unit-based navigation for pedicle screw placement [16].

The PYTHEAS Your Guided Trajectory® (PYGT) device assists pedicle screw insertion during posterior spine procedures at the sacral, lumbar and thoracic levels in patients with skeletal maturity. The device provides intraoperative trajectory guidance on the direction of the pedicle to facilitate perforation, tapping and insertion of the screw, based on the reference axis defined prior to surgery, with minimal radiation exposure.

In this first-in-human study, we measured the performance, usability and safety of this angular assistance medical device. We hypothesised that the PYGT device used during pedicular screw positioning would optimise intra-pedicular perforation, thereby reducing the risk of pedicle screw malposition.

Methods

Study design

This two-centre, single-arm, open-label, first-in-human study was approved by the Comité de Protection des Personnes Sud Est 1 and registered on clinicaltrials.gov (NCT05565131). All patients provided signed consent.

Participants and settings

Patients were consecutively recruited from CHU de Nîmes and Institut Méditerranéen du Dos from 23 September 2022 to 2 February 2023. Patients were eligible if they were aged > 18 with indication for spine posterior instrumentation of at least two lumbar/thoracic vertebrae with pedicular screw placement.

Indications included: traumatology: fracture with significant vertebral compression or major kyphosis deformity, ligament and/or disc injury resulting in instability, requiring freeing the marrow or nerve roots; deformity: idiopathic scoliosis with surgical indication for increased curvature, thoracic curvature > 45° or lumbar curvature > 35°; revision surgery for extension of the assembly with possible adjacent syndrome responsible for instability and/or central or foraminal stenosis; or degenerative: symptomatic spondylolisthesis, degenerative scoliotic deformity, narrow lumbar canal with canal release leading to instability, syndrome adjacent to a previously performed montage. Exclusion criteria were contraindications to screw placement or CT scan, silicon allergy, and pregnancy, parturiency or breastfeeding. There was no follow-up.

Data sources/measurements

Baseline patient data included sociodemographic characteristics and number of levels of vertebrae for surgery.

Patient disability was recorded via Oswestry functional score, where a score 0–20% represented minimal disability, 21–40% indicated moderate disability, 41–60% indicated severe disability, 61–80% denoted house-bound patients, and 81–100% represented complete disability [17].

Surgeon experience in spine surgery, navigation and angular assistance systems was noted.

Intervention

Surgery was performed as usual with the addition of the PYGT device in the pre-operative and intra-operative stages (Fig. 1).

Pre-operative stage

Pre-operative surgical planning was based on a 3D spine model generated from a spinal CT scan. The reference vertebra was selected among the vertebrae to be instrumented and served as the basis for the CT-based 3D reconstruction; levels of instrumentation, screw entry points, and orientations were then defined. The calibration points to position the palpation tool for specific calibration were defined for each vertebra. Information was stored in a planning file.

Intra-operative stage

The planning file was accessible on a touch screen. Hand tool orientation was measured via integrated inertial sensor. A calibration procedure enabled the PYGT system to record the current vertebra orientation. After vertebral posterior exposure, the surgeon located the entry point based on a visual analogy with the planned entry point displayed on the screen. Using PYGT probe linked to the orientation sensor, the operator canulated the pedicle with visual angular guidance in addition to standard haptic feedback, with real-time display of the current orientation compared to planned orientation. Screws (SPINEART®) were then and driven into the pedicle hole using angular guidance. Subsequent surgical tasks followed standard techniques without PYGT device assistance.

Calibration modes

The system was calibrated using tools linked to the inertial sensor to establish the angular alignment between the vertebra and 3D model. The device continuously indicated the possible and/or necessary calibration procedures.

In the specific calibration mode, the palpation tool indicated the current orientation of the vertebra, allowing direct angular alignment of the orientation of the 3D model versus reality. This calibration relies on palpation of three previously defined characteristic points for each vertebral level. System inaccuracy is related to the device’s intrinsic angular measurement performance and the imprecision of manual palpation. However, palpation is not always possible (calcification, fusion, trauma, etc.). The surgeon must calibrate at each level, increasing operating time.

For global calibration mode, a calibration tool indicated the cranio-caudal orientation of the patient’s spine and horizontal plan. This enables the angular alignment of the vertebra to be instrumented in relation to the 3D model. The true orientation of the vertebra is not directly measured, therefore inaccuracy is linked to the device’s intrinsic angular measurement performance, inaccuracy linked to the angular evolution of the vertebra between the supine position of the patient during pre-operative imaging (CT scan), and the prone intraoperative position [18].

Calibration mode was at the surgeon’s discretion, where global calibration mode was used only if specific calibration was impossible. If required, calibration could be repeated intraoperatively for a given vertebral level, particularly after significant manipulation or exposure modification, helping ensure intraoperative reproducibility of angular alignment.

Surgeon expertise and training

Three surgeons and their teams (surgical assistants, instrument technicians, etc.) were trained by a PYGT representative. Surgeons were instructed in the use of the software over a two-hour practical training in a simulated environment with a mannequin. The PYGT software was used in the planning phase, followed by “Sawbone” to simulate the surgery and view the system live. For the first 2–5 surgeries, a member of the PYGT team was available to guide the surgeon during the planning phase using the software and present in the operating room as required. A user guide was provided. Surgeons had 8, 9 and 11 years of experience.

Outcome measurements

The primary outcome was PYGT device performance on the correct direction of pedicle aiming (maintaining planned axial and sagittal angles within 5°) allowing intrapedicular canulation, without false path. The correct canulation rate was calculated as the number of trajectories in the correct direction/total number of canulations. The correct direction was verified by palpatory technique using a palpation guide prior to a screw installation and classed as satisfactory if transpedicular perforation was successful for all four walls: internal, external, superior and inferior.

A screenshot of the angle data at the time of canulation confirmed the alignment between the planned and actual orientation. We applied a threshold of 95% of accurate pedicular screw placement. The calibration method used was noted.

Secondary outcomes included the correct screw positioning rate according to post-operative CT scan, whereby the position was considered correct if the direction deviated by less than 5° versus the planned route. Other secondary outcomes were the operating time (from opening to closure) according to the calibration method (specific/global), and surgeon satisfaction with calibration, tablet interaction and usability. Adverse events were recorded according to the Clavien-Dindo classification.

Surgeon satisfaction was measured after each surgery on a 5-point Likert scale from very unsatisfied to very satisfied for calibration mode, transfer time and ease of transfer to the tablet, tablet responsiveness and interactivity, and image quality. A textbox was provided to describe preferred calibration mode and for which cases, and to suggest improvements. Surgeons rated tablet usability through the System Usability Scale [19], a 10-item questionnaire on a 5-point Likert scale with a score between 0 and 100, where a score > 75 is considered “good”, “fair” between 50 and 75 and < 50 indicates major problems. A threshold of > 70 was chosen for this study.

Sample size

For this first-in-human trial, we limited the sample size to a maximum of 20 patients. As the unit of analysis was the correct direction of the perforation, a minimum of 80 measurements was considered sufficient to measure performance and safety.

Statistical methods

Results are presented as means ± standard deviations, and as medians and inter-quartile ranges for quantitative variables. For the qualitative variables, associated numbers and percentages are presented. All analyses were performed using Rstudio software.

The unit of analysis was the vertebral pedicle, taking into account the fact that only one screw insertion was possible per pedicle.

Results

Participants

Fourteen patients were recruited and included. One patient did not receive any screws, and 13 patients received screws for 42 vertebrae in total. All levels received two screws except one patient with one screw for T12 level (left only), for a total of 83 screws. Patients had an average age of 67 years, and 52% were female (Table 1).

Screw positioning

According to the palpation technique, the placement of all 83 (100%) cannulations was classed as “good”. Comparing screenshots, the trajectory was also classed as correct compared to that indicated by the device (Fig. 3), including for an in-out-in approach (Fig. 4). Postoperative CT scan confirmed correct positioning for all 83 screws. Operating time

Operating time ranged from 50 to 259 min, irrespective of the number of pedicles or calibration modes. The average operating time per pedicle ranged from 8.8 to 65.0 min. The longest insertion was a revision surgery, where four screws were placed following the previous route. Operating time according to calibration mode could only be calculated for single mode cases, i.e., in 9 cases performed in global mode only. This resulted in an average operating time per pedicle of 23.2 min ± 14.7 min in global mode.

Discussion

PYGT device performance was considered acceptable since the 100% rate of pedicle perforation without pedicular breach exceeded the pre-defined threshold of 95%. The postoperative CT spine scan confirmed 100% correct screw placement, regardless of the calibration method. The 100% rate of properly placed screws indicated a concordance with the pre-operative. This did not necessarily mean an intra-pedicular trajectory, which is sometimes impossible due to pedicle anatomy, where narrowness can dictate an “in-out-in” trajectory [20] (Fig. 4). Screws never penetrated the medial cortex of the pedicle. Surgeon satisfaction was very encouraging, particularly for tablet interactivity. They also reported a good level of device usability. While we did not explicitly test learning curve, question 7 on the SUS scale “I would imagine that most people would learn to use this system very quickly” was answered either as “agree” or “strongly agree” by all responders.

Recently developed assistance technologies in pedicular screw placement offer improved safety compared to fluoroscopic freehand technique [21]. The MEDTRONIC StealthStation® Navigation with O-arm® Imaging System allowed 97.5% correct screw placement for 1922 screws in thoracic, lumbar and sacral section [22]. Unlike these systems, PYGT provides angular trajectory assistance only and should be considered an angular assistance system rather than a volumetric navigation system. Indeed, it does not provide real-time 3D positional tracking, pedicle wall visualisation, or depth guidance, and is not intended to replace O-arm-based navigation or robotic guidance.

With the robotic MAZOR®, a case study reported screw placement accuracy of 98.3%, with a decreasing trend in total screw placement time and fluoroscopy time [23]. With MySpine® patient-matched drill guides, a randomised trial reported a 96.1% rate of screws in the “safe area” in the experimental group versus 82.9% using freehand technique [24]. Therefore, with 100% of safe screw placement, the PYGT device demonstrated good accuracy compared to existing technologies.

Although radiation exposure was not measured here, PYGT operation does not require X-ray for calibration. Indeed, surgery planning is via pre-operative CT scan, which can be low-dose. In a study of patient-matched drill guides [24], radiation dose was reduced despite low-dose pre-operative CT necessary for surgical planning. Similar results could therefore be expected with the PYGT technique. Low-dose CT acquisition protocols can reduce radiation exposure by approximately 50–80% compared with standard CT protocols, while maintaining sufficient accuracy for pedicle screw planning, as previously demonstrated in spine surgery planning studies [24]. As preoperative CT scan is mandatory, radiation could be reduced with PYGT in the intraoperative phase by avoiding intraoperative 3D imaging acquisition or fluoroscopic guidance.

The operating time per pedicle was highly variable (8.8 to 65 min), with an average of 23.1 ± 14.7 min in global mode. Instrumentation time per pedicle could not be calculated because operating times were collected per patient and not per pedicle implantation. The average time was increased by one lengthy revision surgery. Nevertheless, global calibration mode appeared to be beneficial on surgery time due to fast registration operation, only required once at the beginning of the procedure. Specific calibration mode requires preparation of bone contact at the posterior area (spinous process and posterior summits of transverse processes) to enable palpation, thus inducing a limited time increment per level. During use, interactions with the user interface and periodic recalibration operations as advised by the system further increase surgical time. These tasks could be performed by assistants or surgeons. Surgeons considered that overall surgery time using PYGT compared to freehand technique remained low and acceptable due to fast setup and calibration operations, with good integration into standard workflow. Compared to navigation or robotic guidance, PYGT saves time by avoiding imaging acquisition and transfer time. Surgical time with PYGT is likely similar to patient-specific drill guides as both techniques require posterior region preparation, whereas preparation time would be better for PYGT because preoperative planning is not required multiple days in advance.

The PYGT system provides angular guidance only and does not deliver feedback on screw depth or surrounding anatomy, restricting its use to open thoracic, lumbar, and sacral procedures. It is not intended for percutaneous or cervical applications and cannot replace full-scale optical or electromagnetic navigation. However, by simplifying intraoperative guidance reducing radiation exposure and complex imaging acquisition, the system offers a practical alternative for open spinal instrumentation. The foundational cadaveric work by Jost et al. [16] confirmed that inertial measurement unit-assisted navigation could achieve high accuracy in pedicle screw placement. Our study advances this concept by translating it into a clinical environment, providing the first prospective evidence of safety, performance, and user experience in human surgery. Based on this first-in-human experience, the PYGT system appears most suitable for open posterior thoracic, lumbar, and sacral instrumentation in adult patients, where preservation of tactile feedback and workflow simplicity are desirable. The system may be particularly relevant in multi-level degenerative, traumatic, deformity, and revision cases in which posterior exposure is already planned.

The main practical advantages of PYGT include real-time angular trajectory assistance, portability, rapid integration into standard surgical workflow, and the absence of intraoperative 3D imaging acquisition or optical tracking requirements. These features are particularly beneficial where access to full navigation or robotic systems is limited or when seeking to minimise intraoperative imaging steps. However, PYGT provides angular guidance only and does not offer real-time volumetric navigation, pedicle wall visualisation, or depth control. Its use therefore remains dependent on adequate exposure and anatomical conditions and it is not intended for percutaneous workflows or cervical instrumentation. PYGT should thus be considered a complementary tool rather than a replacement for established navigation or robotic systems.

This study, however, had several limitations. First, the number of instrumented pedicles was restricted to reduce exposure to risk in this first-in-human trial, with a total sample size of only 13 patients. The small number of operators (n = 3), with similar levels of expertise (8–11 years) prevented interpretation according to surgeon seniority. Furthermore, T1, T2, and T3 vertebrae could not be tested because no patients presented operative indication for these levels. However, pedicle instrumentation in the T1–T10 range is homogeneous over this range due to the fairly constant pedicle anatomy [25]. Furthermore, we did not consider the Gertzbein-Robbins classification for screw placement [26]. This first-in-human design did not include a control group. All participating surgeons were highly experienced to ensure patient safety, which may limit the generalisability of the accuracy results. Future comparative studies including freehand and navigation-assisted controls are planned.

Finally, radiation exposure was not measured and no follow-up was performed.

In conclusion, the clinical performance of the PYGT device during manual perforation of the pedicle and during pedicular screw insertion for sacral, lumbar and thoracic levels is satisfactory, exceeding the pre-specified beneficial threshold. No adverse events were noted in this small cohort, and all screws were placed as planned, including one extra-pedicular route. Future studies should be conducted on a larger range of patients and operators with evaluation of screw placement using standard classification, implantation time and radiation exposure, with a long-term follow-up to examine delayed complications and revision rates.
