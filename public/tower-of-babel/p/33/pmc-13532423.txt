Consecutive Emergency Carotid Artery Stenting and Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement in a Patient With Cardiogenic Shock: A Case Report
Authors: Max Groche, Claudio Silwanis, Clemens Steinwender, Thomas Lambert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSevere aortic stenosis (AS) carries a poor prognosis when symptomatic, particularly in cardiogenic shock (CS). Emergency transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) is rarely performed but may represent a rescue therapy. Coexistent carotid artery stenosis (CAS) further increases procedural risk. We report the case of an 83‐year‐old female patient with severe AS and beginning CS. Imaging revealed severe right internal CAS and occlusion of the left internal carotid artery. Despite the use of norepinephrine, the patient progressed to deteriorating CS with acute kidney injury and impaired mental status. Given high surgical risk, a combined strategy was performed: right carotid stenting followed by transfemoral TAVR. Post‐procedure, renal replacement therapy was initiated with rapid recovery of renal function. The patient was hemodynamically stabilized and discharged 14 days after the procedure with normalized ventricular function and a well‐functioning prosthesis. This case illustrates the feasibility of consecutive transfemoral TAVR and interventional revascularization of CAS as life‐saving rescue therapy in severe AS complicated by CS and cerebrovascular disease (CVD). A combined interventional approach addressing both CVD and valve obstruction enabled procedural safety and favorable recovery, supporting TAVR as a viable option in selected high‐risk patients.Keywords: cardiogenic shock, carotid stenosis, TAVR

Background

Aortic stenosis (AS) is the most common primary valve disease in Europe and northern America, with severe symptomatic AS having an unfavorable prognosis if left untreated [1, 2, 3]. Degenerative valve calcification is the leading cause of AS in developed countries, with increasing prevalence due to population ageing and accelerated progression at higher haemodynamic severity [4].

So far, drug therapy has not proven to modify the natural course of AS, and valve intervention is recommended in all eligible symptomatic patients with high‐gradient severe AS. Most patients with AS undergoing valve intervention receive a bioprosthetic heart valve, either through surgical or transcatheter replacement [4]. Transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) has become a safe procedure with low complication rates and a widely accessible treatment option [5, 6]. TAVR is recommended for patients > 70 years of age with tricuspid aortic valve and suitable anatomy [4]. Balloon valvuloplasty is a potential treatment option with similar perioperative risks as TAVR but with the downsides of inferior acute hemodynamic improvement, high rates of inducing aortic insufficiency or of relapsing AS [6, 7].

Severe AS can lead to acute cardiac decompensation and ultimately result in cardiogenic shock (CS) with high mortality [4, 8]. Relief of aortic valve obstruction remains the only effective treatment for acute heart failure and progressive organ dysfunction [4, 9]. TAVR can disrupt the shock spiral, but postprocedural outcomes are often futile [6]. The optimal treatment strategy for patients with CS due to severe AS remains unknown, and emergency TAVR remains a rare event [4, 6, 10]. Thirty‐day mortality in patients with CS due to AS undergoing interventional emergency valve replacement has been reported to be up to 46.2%, and although emergency TAVR is associated with increased 90‐day mortality, outcomes thereafter are comparable to elective interventions [6, 11, 12].

The presence of cerebrovascular disease (CVD), often present as carotid artery stenosis (CAS), is common among TAVR patients and is associated with a higher occurrence of periprocedural cerebrovascular events [13]. Interventional treatment of CAS can induce asystole or hypotension and is considered a high‐risk procedure in patients with AS [14]. Bilateral CAS is a relative contraindication for surgery and carotid endarterectomy and poses a high risk for the occurrence of ischemic stroke [15].

Case Presentation

An 83‐year‐old female patient was admitted to the emergency intervention room due to an acute onset of dyspnea and hypertensive crisis. Prehospital assessment revealed hypertensive systolic blood pressure (> 200 mmHg), tachycardia (140 bpm), tachypnoea (25/min), and low peripheral oxygen saturation. Emergency medical services administered treatment for suspected hypertensive pulmonary edema with diuretics, sublingual nitroglycerine, and oxygen supplementation. At the time of admission, the patient showed signs of beginning CS with a serum lactate level of 2.3 mmol/L. Noninvasive ventilation therapy was established. The initial ECG demonstrated sinus tachycardia with no signs for ischemia. Auscultation revealed coarse bilateral crackles and a systolic murmur with a point of maximal intensity at the right second intercostal space. The patient had a mild impairment of consciousness (GCS 12). Her only medication was low‐dose aspirin.

The patient was transferred to the medical intensive care unit for further evaluation and treatment. Bedside echocardiography revealed a heavily calcified aortic valve with combined disease of severe stenosis (Vmax 4.3 m/s, maximum/mean pressure gradient [PG] 76/48 mmHg, calculated valve opening area 0.3 cm2) and mild‐to‐moderate regurgitation (Figure 1A). The left ventricle was concentrically hypertrophied (interventricular septum thickness 14 mm), with a reduced ejection fraction of 35%. Bilateral extensive B‐lines indicated severe pulmonary congestion. Doppler sonography of the supplying neck vessels suspected of multiple stenoses. MR angiography confirmed critical CVD and revealed severe right internal CAS, long‐segment narrowing of the left common carotid artery, long segment occlusion of the left internal carotid artery, and long‐segment occlusion of the right vertebral artery (Figure 2). CT imaging of the thorax and aorta was performed to rule out aortic dissection. Cerebral CT excluded macro‐ischemia, acute intracerebral hemorrhage, or tumor. Calculated EURO‐SCORE II and STS‐Score at time of admission showed high risk for perioperative mortality (16.4% and 40%, respectively).

After initial stabilization, the patient developed progressive hemodynamic deterioration, complicated by acute kidney injury KDIGO III and anuria. Further dialogue with the patient and her relatives expressed the wish to undergo acute valve replacement. Uremic symptoms became increasingly apparent with rising blood urea nitrogen, confusion, and somnolence. The hemodynamic situation worsened due to valvular‐induced cardiogenic shock (SCAI stage D) with progressive end‐organ dysfunction despite continuous catecholamine therapy with norepinephrine.

Due to acute decompensation, a combined interventional procedure was performed, consisting of left heart catheterization, revascularization of the right internal carotid artery, and transfemoral TAVR. Left heart catheterization revealed a peak‐to‐peak PG of 87 mmHg and a mean PG of 55 mmHg across the aortic valve. Coronary angiography demonstrated no relevant stenosis or total occlusion. Acute coronary intervention was deferred. Carotid intervention was carried out under cerebral protection (Medtronic Spider FX) using a self‐expanding stent (6−8/30 Accu‐Link) followed by balloon angioplasty (Abbott Viatrac 14 Plus 20 mm) (Figure 3). Subsequently, balloon valvuloplasty of the aortic valve was performed (Numed Nucleus 22 mm), followed by implantation of a 25 mm Abbott Navitor prosthesis and consecutive ballon‐dilatation (24/4 Atlas Gold) (Figure 4). Post‐implantation, the transvalvular gradient was reduced to 3/2 mmHg.

Following the intervention, renal replacement therapy (RRT) with continuous venovenous haemodiafiltration (CVVHDF) was initiated. Under ongoing RRT, renal function improved rapidly, and CVVHDF was discontinued after a 72 h cycle. Vasoactive support was discontinued within 24 h after the procedure, and a stable haemodynamic situation was achieved. Control echocardiography 11 days post‐procedure demonstrated good function of the aortic valve prosthesis (Vmax 1.7 m/s, mean PG 6 mmHg) (Figure 1B) and recovered left ventricular function (ejection fraction 60%). The patient continued to improve clinically and was discharged 14 days after the intervention. The patient was contacted 4 weeks after the intervention and was alive and doing well.

Discussion

Severe symptomatic AS requires timely intervention due to its high mortality and poor survival rates if left untreated [1, 2, 3]. Evidence regarding the treatment of patients presenting with CS and CAS remains limited, and outcomes of emergency TAVR are rather poor [6, 11, 12].

Our patient initially presented with signs of beginning CS, including tachycardia and elevated lactate levels, and subsequently developed progressive hemodynamic deterioration. Despite the use of norepinephrine as the preferred drug choice in CS [16], the patient developed multiorgan dysfunction with acute kidney injury and impaired mental status. Possible mechanisms for end‐organ failure include tissue hypoxia and impaired systemic perfusion due to severely reduced cardiac output, with the combination of CS and severe AS being the underlying cause. The situation was complicated by the extent of CVD as a critical comorbidity that further impaired cerebral perfusion.

In our case, a combined strategy to address AS and CAS was essential. The transfemoral route facilitated a combined strategy that included coronary angiography, carotid revascularization, and valve implantation within a single session. In this critically ill patient, the intention was to minimize physical stress and address all relevant pathologies in one procedure. Coronary angiography was performed to exclude significant coronary artery lesions as a frequent reason for CS [17]. TAVR was selected as rescue therapy regarding the high surgical perioperative mortality risk. Balloon aortic valvuloplasty was not considered an adequate option as either definitive or bridging therapy because of poor long‐term outcomes and the need for a reliable hemodynamic improvement [6, 7]. The presence of extensive CVD posed a high risk for ischemic stroke, especially in the setting of reduced cardiac output [15]. With the left internal carotid artery being occluded and the right internal carotid artery showing high‐grade stenosis, cerebral perfusion relied almost entirely on one critically narrowed vessel. The patient presented signs of cerebral hypoperfusion, and the need for revascularization was urgent to restore adequate cerebral blood flow. For this reason and due to anticipated rapid pacing for valve deployment, carotid artery revascularization was performed prior to TAVR. Use of iodinated contrast was unavoidable. Because of acute kidney injury, RRT had been planned post‐procedure, with CVVHDF being immediately available. Following successful valve implantation, the hemodynamic situation improved, vasoactive support was discontinued, and renal function recovered rapidly after RRT.

Conclusion

Consecutive interventional carotid artery revascularization and TAVR may represent a viable rescue therapy for patients with severe symptomatic AS complicated by CS and extensive CVD. The frequent coexistence of CVD in this population may necessitate a combined interventional approach, including cerebral revascularization and valve replacement, to optimize procedural safety and clinical outcomes.
