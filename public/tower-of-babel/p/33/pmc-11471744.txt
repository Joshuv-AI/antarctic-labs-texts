Glacier Retreat Induces Contrasting Shifts in Bacterial Biodiversity Patterns in Glacial Lake Water and Sediment
Authors: Keshao Liu, Qi Yan, Xuezi Guo, Wenqiang Wang, Zhihao Zhang, Mukan Ji, Feng Wang, Yongqin Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Glacial lake ecosystems are experiencing rapid changes due to accelerated glacier retreat. As glaciers recede, their influence on downstream habitats diminishes, potentially affecting the biodiversity of glacial lake microbial communities. However, there remains a knowledge gap regarding how bacterial biodiversity patterns in glacial lakes are altered by diminishing glacial influence. Here, we investigated shifts in bacterial communities in paired water and sediment samples collected from seven glacial lakes on the Tibetan Plateau, using a space-for-time substitution approach to understand the consequences of glacier retreat. Our findings reveal that bacterial diversity in lake water increases significantly with a higher glacier index (GI), whereas sediment bacterial diversity exhibits a negative correlation with GI. Both the water and sediment bacterial communities display significant structural shifts along the GI gradient. Notably, reduced glacial influence decreases the complexity of bacterial co-occurrence networks in lake water but enhances the network complexity in sediment. This divergence in diversity and co-occurrence patterns highlights that water and sediment bacterial communities respond differently to changes in glacial influence in these lake ecosystems. This study provides insights into how diminishing glacial influence impacts the bacterial biodiversity in glacial lake water and sediments, revealing contrasting patterns between the two habitats. These findings emphasize the need for comprehensive monitoring to understand the implications of glacier retreat on these fragile ecosystems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00248-024-02447-3.

Introduction

Glacial lakes are directly affected by glacier retreat [1], and their development is influenced by long-term climate change [2, 3]. In recent decades, rapid global warming has led to substantial mass loss in glaciers worldwide [4–7]. These changes can prompt shifts in glacier-fed lakes and streams from their initial states to alternative ones [8–11]. On the one hand, new glacial lakes are formed in previously ice-covered areas as a result of glacier retreat, which offers suitable habitats for various biota [12]. On the other hand, as glaciers continue to melt, the distance between glacial lakes and glaciers increases, reducing the glacial influence on lake ecosystems. Consequently, when glacial lakes lose their hydrological connection to the glacier, their conditions change from cold and turbid to warmer and clearer [13]. Additionally, when this hydrological connection is lost, specific resources (e.g., nutrients, sediment, minerals) provided by the glaciers are no longer available to the lake ecosystems. It is expected that these significant changes in the glacial lakes will have a profound impact on the microbial diversity of the glacial lakes. Previous observations have highlighted the importance of the distance between glacial lakes and the ice cap as a driver of microbial diversity in West Greenland lakes [14]. Recent evidence suggests that bacteria originating from glaciers can contribute significantly (40–96%) to downstream glacial lake microbial communities, but their contribution decreases with increasing distance from the glacier terminus [15]. In addition, previous studies have indicated that glacier retreat can affect the microbial diversity of downstream glacial lakes by altering their physicochemical conditions [16, 17]. For example, glacier meltwater can transport a large amount of fine inorganic particles into glacier-fed ecosystems, leading to high turbidity in these ecosystems [18]. The high turbidity limits the penetration of light available for primary production [15, 19], further affecting microbial communities and reducing microbial diversity.

Within lake ecosystems, water and sediment represent two distinct lacustrine habitats, each characterized by unique environmental properties (e.g., mobility, redox conditions, and organic matter content). Bacteria in lake water originate from sources such as glacier meltwater, rainfall, groundwater, and surrounding soils [20, 21], and lake sediment receives bacteria through processes of accumulation, deposition, and lakeshore erosion [22]. Glacial meltwater is also an important source of bacteria in lake sediment, particularly important for glacial lakes [23]. Previous studies have pointed out that bacterial communities in water or sediments respond differently to environmental change [24–28]. For example, planktonic bacterial communities in freshwater lakes are more susceptible to eutrophication than sediment bacterial communities [25]. In addition, a study conducted in glacier-fed rivers demonstrated that water bacterial diversity was more sensitive to mean annual precipitation, while sediment bacterial diversity was more influenced by nitrogen concentration [29]. However, the extent to which bacterial communities in water and sediment change during the ontogeny of glacial lakes, and whether there are differences in response to environmental changes induced by glacier retreat, remains unclear.

To address this knowledge gap, we explored the biodiversity and co-occurrence patterns of bacterial communities in water and surface sediment samples from seven lakes formed by the retreat of the Kuoqionggangri glacier I. Although the formation times of these lakes are unknown, the distance of the lakes from the glacier also reflects, to some extent, a gradient of the ontogenetic age of the glacial lakes, with the nearest lakes being most recently formed. Therefore, these glacial lakes offer a unique opportunity to examine changes in bacterial communities during lake ontogeny as a consequence of the glacier retreat. Given the significant role of glaciers in the ontogeny of glacial lakes, we hypothesized that the biodiversity and co-occurrence patterns of bacterial communities in the water and sediment of downstream glacial lakes are strongly influenced by glacial influence. Furthermore, we hypothesized that downstream glacial lake water and sediment bacterial communities would exhibit differential responses to glacial influence.

Materials and Methods

Study Area and Sample Collection

Kuoqionggangri glaciers (KQGR, 29°51′48″N, 90°12′39″E) are located in the southern regions of the Tibetan Plateau (Fig. 1). As one of the largest glaciers in Nyainqen Tangglha range, KQGR are mainly controlled by the South Asian monsoon [30]. The annual mean air temperature in this region is − 1.28 °C and the annual mean precipitation is ~ 550 mm, with approximately 90% of the precipitation occurring in the summer season from June to September [31]. KQGR consist of seven small glaciers, and at least nine glacial lakes are expanding as glacier melting. Among them, seven lakes originated from a rapidly retreating KQGR I glacier (Fig. 1). The area of KQGR I glacier was about 1.072 km2. Of the seven lakes, three lakes (Lakes 1, 2, and 3) are in close proximity to the glacier but are located in different sides of the glacier. Lake 4 is connected to Lake 3, and Lake 5 is connected to the glacier by a stream. Lakes 6 and 7 lie behind a ridge and have lost connection to the glacier terminus due to the retreat of KQGR I glacier [32].Fig. 1Overview of the studying glacial lakes and their geographic locations in this study

Overview of the studying glacial lakes and their geographic locations in this study

We collected surface snow (0–15 cm) on KQGR I glacier, paired water, and surface sediment samples synchronously from seven nearby (pro)glacial lakes on 11 July 2020. At least three replicate water and sediment samples were collected for each lake. In total, 16 snow samples and 24 water and 24 sediment samples. For snow sampling, we used a sterilized Teflon shovel to transfer freshly fallen surface snow into sterile 700 ml Whirl–Pak® bags. The samples were stored in cooling containers and transported to the laboratory and slowly thawed over 24–48 h. After melting, approximately 500 mL snowmelt water was filtered onto a 0.22 µm polycarbonate membrane (47 mm, Millipore, USA) for DNA extraction. For water sampling, 1 L of water was collected at a depth of 10–20 cm into a sterile plastic bottle using a 1000 mL Ruttner water sampler (Hydro-Bios, Altenholz, Germany). About 300 g surface sediment samples were collected with stainless-steel shovel and placed into the sterile Whirl–Pak® bags. The stainless-steel shovel was cleaned with 75% ethanol prior to each sediment sample collection to avoid contamination. All water and sediment samples were stored in cooling containers and quickly transported to the laboratory. In the laboratory, approximately 500 mL water was pre-filtered through 20 µm mesh (Millipore, USA) to remove large particles and debris, and then filtered similarly to snowmelt samples. The rest of the sample was used for physicochemical analysis. The filters and sediment samples were stored at − 80 °C until further analysis.

Sampling site coordinates were collected using a Global Positioning System (GPS) and straight-line distances from the sampling site to the glacier terminus were calculated from remote sensing image based on GPS coordinates. GI value of 1 indicates that the distance between the sampling site and the glacier terminus is zero, where the glacier has the greatest influence, while a GI value of zero means that the glacier has the least influence.

Physicochemical Analysis

The water temperature (Temp), pH, conductivity (Con), and total dissolved solids (TDS) were measured in the field using a multiprobe Water Quality Sonde (Hydrolab DS5, Hach, USA). In the laboratory, water samples were filtered through 0.45 µm hydrophilic polyethersulfone syringe filter (25 mm, Anpel) to determine the dissolved organic carbon (DOC), total nitrogen (TN), nitrate (NO3−), and sulfate (SO42−). DOC and TN were measured by a TOC-Lcph (Shimadzu Corp., Japan). The NO3− and SO42− concentration was determined with Dionex ion chromatography (IC) system 600 (Thermo Fisher Scientific Instrument Co., LTD). Sediment pH and conductivity were determined in a 1:5 sediment/water suspension using a PHS-3C pH and DDS-307A conductivity meter, respectively. The sediment total carbon (TC) was determined using a TOC-Lcph analyzer. The sediment NH4+-N and NO3− -N were extracted using a ratio of 5 g sediment to 25 mL 2 mol/L KCl. After shaking for 1 h, the extracts were filtered using a filter paper, and the concentrations were measured using a continuous flow Injection analyzer (Smartchem200). Total nitrogen (TN) and total phosphorus (TP) contents were determined by digestion H2SO4 and H2SO4-HClO4, respectively, and ultimately determined via flow analyzer (Smartchem200). Details about the samples and physicochemical measurements are in Table S1.

DNA Extraction and Sequencing

The total bacterial DNA was extracted from the filter membrane or 0.5 g sediment samples using the MoBio Power Soil DNA isolation kit (MoBio Laboratories, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The extracted DNA was resuspended into 100 µL of nuclease-free water and quantified using a Qubit 4.0 fluorometer with the dsDNA Broad Range assay kit (Invitrogen, Singapore).

The V4-V5 region of the bacterial 16S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) gene was amplified using primer set 515F (5′-GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA-3′) and 907R (5′-CCGTCAATTCMTTTRAGTTT-3′) [34]. The 50 µL polymerase chain reaction (PCR) reaction systems were prepared in triplicate and each contained 10 ng DNA template, 5 µL 2 × Premix Taq DNA polymerase (Takara Biotechnology, Dalian, China), 1 µL of each primer (10 µM), and 20 µL of nuclease-free water. The PCR reaction was performed under the following conditions: 94 °C for 3 min, 30 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 60 °C for 30 s, 72 °C for 1 min, and a final cycle of 5 min at 72 °C. Afterward, PCR products were purified with Agencourt AMpure XP beads, pooled at equal volumes, and sequenced on the Illumina MiSeq platform at Magigene (Guangzhou, China) using a paired-end strategy (2 × 250 bp).

Sequence Analysis

A total of 4,011,585 raw 16S rRNA amplicon reads were quality-controlled and analyzed using QIIME2 pipeline (2020.04). Basic criteria were respected following the official suggestions. DADA2 was used for the detection and correction of amplicon sequence data, as well as the generation of the phylogenetic tree. Taxonomic assignment of sequences was assigned using the Naive Bayes classifier trained by the Silva 138.1 16S rRNA database [35]. With the pipeline provided by QIIME2, Amplicon Sequence Variants (ASVs) were produced in place of Operational Taxonomic Units (OTUs) for a higher resolution of taxonomic annotations [36]. ASVs that were annotated as chloroplasts, archaeal, and unassigned sequences (not affiliated with Bacteria) were excluded from subsequent analysis. Moreover, the ASV table was normalized to the minimum sequencing depth (25,087 sequences) associated with 100 bootstrap resampling runs for the following analysis. After rarefying the ASV table to the lowest number of sequences per sample, we obtained a total of 1,605,568 high-quality sequences from 64 samples, which were clustered into 10,820 ASVs.

Estimation of the Underlying Ecological Processes

The community assembly processes of lake water and sediment bacterial communities were evaluated by using neutral and null models. By combining the following analyses, we aim to investigate whether there are differences in the ecological processes that shape lake water and sediment bacterial communities. The neutral community model (NCM) was applied to evaluate the potential importance of stochastic processes on community assembly [37]. The null model-based stochastic ratio was calculated using the modified method with the Bray–Curtis distance to determine the relative importance of stochastic and deterministic processes in shaping community assembly [38, 39]. The normalized stochasticity ratio (NST) is performed in R using the “NST” package to reflect the contribution of stochastic processes based on relative differences between the observed situation and the null expectation [40]. The NST index defines 0.5 as the boundary point to determine whether the community assembly is more deterministic (< 0.5) or more stochastic (> 0.5). Levins’ niche breadth index was calculated for both water and sediment bacterial communities using the “niche.width” function within R package “spaa” [41]. In general, a bacterial community with a wide niche breadth is expected to be more metabolically flexible at the community level than one with a narrow niche breadth.

Network Analysis

To estimate how species coexistence changes in different habitats as glaciers retreat, we first divided the lakes into two groups based on GI values: lakes close to the glacier terminus with high GI values (GI > 0.8, HGI; Lake 1 to 3), and lakes away from the glacier terminus with low GI values (GI < 0.8, LGI; Lake 4 to 7). Then, constructed separate correlation networks for 24 water and 24 sediment samples. Finally, subnetworks of HGI and LGI were extracted from the water and sediment network using the induced_subgraph function in “igraph” package in R [42] and analyzed individually. All robust correlations with Spearman’s correlation coefficients (ρ) > 0.8 and false discovery rate [FDR]-adjusted P-value < 0.01 were used to construct networks. Network visualization was generated with Gephi version 0.9.2 [43]. Each node represents one ASV, and each edge represents a strong and significant correlation between two nodes. For each network, we calculated a set of topological features: average path length, network diameter, average degree, clustering coefficient, and graph density. Higher average degree, clustering coefficient, and graph density suggest a more connected network. In addition, lower average path lengths and diameters indicate closer associations in the network [44, 45]. Furthermore, the 1000 Erdös-Réyni random networks, which exhibit the same number of nodes and edges as the real networks, were calculated in the “igraph” R package.

Community cohesion was calculated to quantify the degree of connectivity of the water and sediment bacterial communities [46]. In this analysis, positive and negative cohesions were calculated for each sample as the sum of the significant positive or negative correlations between taxa weighted by ASV abundances. We use the absolute value of negative:positive cohesion to test whether changes in network stability are caused by changes in GI values.

Statistical Analyses

To quantify the extent of contribution of potential source to the sink, the Bayesian-based SourceTracker method was performed [47]. In the present study, SourceTracker analysis was performed twice: (i) lake water samples were defined as sink, and the snow samples as potential source, and (ii) both lake water and snow samples were considered potential sources and sediment samples as sinks. Spearman correlation analysis was used to determine the bacterial genera in water and sediment samples that were significantly correlated with the distance from the glacier. Bacterial alpha diversity indices, including the Chao1 richness and Shannon index were calculated for each sample in the R package “vegan.” The Bray–Curtis dissimilarities of bacterial communities between water and sediment were examined using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, performed using the wilcox.test function in the “stats” package in R [48]. Water and sediment bacterial community compositions were visualized using non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarities. Vegan’s functions metaMDS was used for ordinations, envfit was used to fit environmental vectors and ordisurf, which uses generalized additive models, was used to visualize the sampling site distance from the glacier in the ordinations [49]. Analysis of similarity (ANOSIM) was used to evaluate differences in bacterial communities between habitats. All statistical analyses were performed in R3.6.1 (http://www.r-project.org). The distance-based multivariate linear model (DistLM) based on the community Bray–Curtis dissimilarities was performed in the DISTLM_forward3 program to determine the combination of environmental variables that significantly explain the observed dissimilarity among the samples [50].

Results

Bacterial Taxonomy and Alpha Diversity Patterns

Water samples from lakes (Lake 1 to 3) close to the glacier terminus had higher GI values but lower water temperature, conductivity, TDS, DOC, and TN concentrations than those from lakes (Lake 4 to 7) away from the glacier terminus (Table S1). Sediment samples from Lakes 1, 2, and 3 had higher pH and conductivity but lower TN and TC than those from Lakes 4, 5, 6, and 7. For water samples, GI was significantly positively correlated with pH but negatively correlated with water temperature (Fig. S1a). No significant relationships were observed between GI and TN or DOC. For sediment samples, TN and TC were significantly and negatively correlated with GI (Fig. S1b), reflecting the high nutrient concentrations in the sediment of early formed glacial lakes.

The dominant bacterial phyla (Pseudomonadota in Class) present in the water samples, ranked in decreasing order by mean relative abundance, were Alphaproteobacteria (51.5% ± 18.8%), Gammaproteobacteria (22.5% ± 14.5%), and Bacteroidota (13.7% ± 7.1%) (Fig. 2a). In sediment samples, Gammaproteobacteria, Bacteroidota, Acidobacteriota, and Alphaproteobacteria were the dominant bacterial linkage, accounting for 28.4%, 20.9%, 13.5%, and 11.3% of the total sequence, respectively (Fig. 2b).Fig. 2Relative abundance of the dominant bacterial phyla/classes (relative abundance > 1% in at least one sample) in water (a) and sediment (b) samples of studying glacial lakes

At the genus level, we found a total of 18 bacterial genera in lake water and sediments to be significantly correlated with GI (all P < 0.05) (Table S2). Among them, the genera Longivirga (affiliated to Actinobacteriota), Tetrasphaera (Actinobacteriota), Edaphobaculum (Bacteroidota), RBG-13–54-9 (Chloroflexota), Syntrophorhabdus (Desulfobacterota), Schlesneria (Planctomycetota), Roseococcus (Proteobacteria), UKL13-1 (Pseudomonadota), Pseudolabrys (Pseudomonadota), and Terrimicrobium (Verrucomicrobiota) both in water and sediment were negative correlated with GI. However, the relative abundance of the genera Arcicella (Bacteroidota) and Roseomonas (Pseudomonadota) showed a decreasing distance trend. In addition, Gemmatimonas (Gemmatimonadota), Phenylobacterium (Pseudomonadota), Rhizobacter (Pseudomonadota), Ellin6067 (Pseudomonadota), and Undibacterium (Pseudomonadota) genera in lake water exhibited a positive correlation with GI, but a negative correlation with GI in lake sediment.

Alpha diversity indices of bacterial communities in both lake water and sediment samples are presented in Table S3. The Shannon index ranged from 3.67 to 6.00, and the highest value occurred in Lake 1, which was close to the glacier terminus. Sediment samples exhibited higher Chao1 and Shannon diversity index compared with the corresponding water samples. The Chao1 and Shannon index in sediment samples ranged from 575 to 1144, and 6.88 to 9.06, respectively. Both indices increased from Lake 1 to Lake 5 and decreased towards Lake 7, which was far away from the glacier terminus. The Assembly of Water and Sediment Communities

The neutral community model successfully estimated a large fraction of the relationship between the frequency of occurrence of ASVs and their relative abundance variation in the water communities, explaining 73.1% of the community variance for the water bacterial communities (Fig. S2a). However, the dispersal-related stochasticity explained only 48.0% of the sediment bacterial community variance (Fig. S2b). The m value of the water bacterial communities (0.016) was higher than that of the sediment bacterial communities (0.008), indicating that species in water habitats were more dispersed than species in sediment habitats.

The assembly of the water and sediment communities was also explored using a null model approach (Fig. S3a). The average normalized stochasticity ratio (NST) of the water communities was 65.1%, which was significantly higher (P < 0.001) than that in the sediment communities (24.3%), suggesting that stochastic processes had more contribution on the water community assemblage, while deterministic processes had more influence on the sediment community assemblage. The water communities exhibited significantly wider (P < 0.001) niche breadths than the sediment communities, further indicating that the sediment communities were more influenced by deterministic processes (Fig. S3b).

Co-occurrence Network of Water and Sediment Communities

To determine the co-occurrence of bacterial communities in waters and sediments of KQGR glacial lakes, samples were grouped according to the degree of glacial influence on the lakes and four sub-networks were constructed (Fig. S4). We found that all network degrees exhibited scale-free characteristics for the water (Power law: R2 > 0.87) and sediment (Power law: R2 > 0.90) networks, indicating that the network structure in both waters and sediments was non-random. The topological properties in the empirical networks were higher than those in the respective Erdös–Réyni random networks, which suggested that the networks had “small-world” properties and modular structure (Table S4). There were 166 nodes and 1052 edges in the water HGI subnetwork (Lakes 1, 2, and 3), and the number of nodes and edges in the water LGI subnetwork (Lakes 4, 5, 6, and 7) was 84 and 180, respectively. Additionally, the water HGI subnetwork had a higher average degree, graph density, and centrality, but lower modularity than the water LGI subnetwork (Table S4). This suggests that the greater the glacial influence, the more frequent the associations between bacterial taxa in water habitats. However, in sediment habitats, the LGI subnetwork showed a more complex network structure compared to the HGI network, characterized by higher average degree and centrality, shorter average path length, and lower modularity (Table S4).

The water HGI subnetwork had a larger proportion of positive edges (99.5%) than that of the water LGI subnetwork (97.8%), indicating that the water LGI subnetwork was more stable (Fig. S4b). The sediment subnetwork showed the opposite trend, with a smaller proportion of positive edges in the sediment HGI subnetwork (98.0%) and a larger proportion of positive edges in the sediment LGI subnetwork (99.2%). The ratio of negative:positive cohesion was higher in the water LGI and the sediment HGI bacterial communities than in the water HGI and the sediment LGI communities, respectively (Fig. S4c). This further suggests that the stability of the water bacterial co-occurrence network increases with less glacial influence, and the stability of the sediment bacterial co-occurrence network decreases with less glacial influence.

Discussion

Glacial lakes are essential for understanding biodiversity responses to glacier retreat. Substituting space for time, the present study provides empirical evidence that glacier retreat exerted significant effects on bacterial communities in glacial lakes, with higher diversity and network complexity in the lake water of newly formed lakes near the glacier terminus, and lower diversity and network complexity in the sediment of these newly formed lakes. Understanding these contrasting biodiversity patterns is crucial for predicting how glacial lake ecosystems might respond to ongoing glacier retreat driven by climate change.

Distinct Patterns of Bacterial α-diversity in Lake Water and Sediment Under Glacial Influence

We observed that bacterial alpha diversity in lake water increased with increasing glacial index (GI), suggesting that newly formed lakes closer to the glacier terminus harbor more diverse bacterial communities than early formed glacial lakes (Fig. 3a). This result aligns with findings that the lake nearest to a retreating alpine glacier had higher diversity than other lakes [51]. It is widely recognized that the biodiversity of glacier-fed streams is under threat as rising ambient temperatures accelerate the ongoing retreat of glaciers worldwide [9, 52, 53]. Our result suggests that as glaciers retreat, the bacterial diversity in glacial lake water decreases with the loss of hydrological connectivity between the glacial lakes and the glacier. The higher bacterial alpha diversity observed in the water of newly formed, glacier-proximal lakes in this study could be attributed to massive inputs of bacteria originating from glacier surface snow (Fig. 5a). Previous work showed that bacterial communities in the glacier-fed connected hydrological continuum are mainly regulated by stochastic dispersal processes [15]. Indeed, we observed that dispersal-related stochasticity played a key role in structuring the lake water communities. As glacier melting, those glacier-derived microbes can flow into downstream aquatic ecosystems, leading to higher bacterial alpha diversity in lakes closer to the glacier terminus.

Contrarily, we found that the alpha diversity of sediment bacterial communities increased with decreasing GI (Fig. 3b), indicating higher diversity in the sediment of early formed glacial lakes further from the glacier terminus. Similar results were observed in glacial lakes within the forefield catchments of three glaciers in the Eastern Swiss Alps [23]. Despite sediments also receiving glacier snow-derived bacteria in a manner similar to lake water, the average proportion was much lower (2.5% compared to 49.3% in water) (Fig. 5b). This implies that sediment bacterial diversity appears less influenced by inputs of glacial snow-derived species. One possible explanation is that, in addition to receiving glacial sources from snow, sediments can act as ecological reservoirs, recruiting bacteria from supraglacial cryoconite and subglacial sediment [54, 55], or soils, sands, and plant debris from surrounding environments [56], potentially leading to higher diversity in early formed lakes. Another possibility is that the distinct environmental conditions observed along the GI gradient likely exert a sorting effect on sediment bacterial communities. Local environmental conditions may favor or inhibit specific bacterial populations, influencing the sediment communities through deterministic ecological processes, such as selection and competitive exclusion [56–58], resulting in lower diversity in newly formed glacial lakes. This is supported by our neutral and null model results, which indicate a strong influence of deterministic processes on community assembly in lake sediment.

Contrasting Beta Diversity Trends in Water and Sediment of Glacial Lakes

Insights into how glacier retreat shapes microbial communities can inform conservation strategies aimed at preserving biodiversity and maintaining ecological balance in these sensitive environments. We found that both lake water and sediment bacterial community compositions were significantly structured along the GI gradient (Fig. 4b and c). This observation suggests a direct link between changes in bacterial communities during glacial lake ontogeny and glacier retreat. Interestingly, bacterial β-diversity shows contrasting trends in lake water and sediment as the GI increases. In lake water, bacterial β-diversity increases with higher GI values, while in lake sediment, it decreases (Fig. 4e). The contrasting trends in bacterial β-diversity between glacial lake water and sediment may reflect fundamental differences in these habitats and their temporal dynamics. For water communities, newly formed glacial lakes experience more variable conditions, such as fluctuations in temperature and turbidity, due to recent deglaciation and ongoing glacial influence. This variability creates diverse niches across glacial lakes, supporting distinct bacterial communities in newly formed glacial lake water [51]. In contrast, sediment communities in early formed glacial lakes have had more time to develop distinct, specialized communities in increasingly isolated environments [9, 59]. This longer development period results in higher β-diversity among these more established glacial lake sediments.

Glacier retreat changes meltwater input, sediment load, and nutrient flux, reshaping bacterial communities in downstream glacial lakes [60]. We found that local environmental conditions, such as conductivity and temperature, also played important roles in shaping the distribution of bacterial communities in the water. This finding is consistent with earlier studies highlighting the sensitivity of microbial communities to water temperature and conductivity in freshwater lakes in northern Europe [61] and Tibetan glacial lakes [17]. Nutrients like ammonium and total phosphorus are also significant factors influencing the shift in sediment bacterial community composition. This points to the critical role of nutrient dynamics in driving the composition of sediment bacterial communities. Studies have shown that the enrichment of C, N, and P from hydrological changes with glacier retreat can reshuffle microbial communities and impact ecosystem functions [62].

In the KQGR glacial lakes, we found 18 genera in water and sediment with relative abundances significantly related to GI (P < 0.05, Table S2). For example, the relative abundances of the genera Gemmatimonas, Phenylobacterium, Rhizobacter, Ellin6067, and Undibacterium were positively correlated with GI in lake water, but negatively correlated with GI in lake sediment. Members of the genus Gemmatimonas are known for their abilities to involve in phosphonate and phosphinate metabolisms, and can also participate in the denitrification process [63, 64]. Phenylobacterium, Rhizobacter, Ellin6067, and Undibacterium species can participate in denitrification processes and have been found in a variety of freshwater environments [65, 66]. This suggests that in newly formed glacial lakes, more water bacteria mediate nitrogen and phosphorus cycling, whereas in early formed glacial lakes, more sediment bacteria are involved in nitrogen cycling. However, we should keep in mind that glacier retreat not only affects individual taxonomic groups (e.g., bacteria) in glacial lakes but can also affect the structure and function of entire ecosystems by altering the composition and dynamics of food webs [67]. Future research should investigate microbial diversity changes not in isolation, but as part of a complex web of ecological interactions that span multiple trophic levels.

Linkages Between Bacterial Diversity and Co-occurrence Network Complexity

Network analysis can improve our understanding of the complex interactions between microorganisms [68]. Our results revealed that lake water bacterial networks were more complex in high glacial influence (HGI) lakes near the glacier than in low glacial influence (LGI) lakes further away from the glacier. In contrast, sediment networks showed the opposite pattern, with greater complexity in LGI lakes (Fig. S4a and Table S4). Network complexity is often positively associated with community stability, as highly connected networks can promote stability [69]. However, we found that less complex networks (water LGI and sediment HGI) exhibited stronger stability compared to more complex networks (water HGI and sediment LGI), as evidenced by our analyses of the proportion of negative edges and cohesion (Fig. S4b and S4c). This finding is in line with previous studies concluding that higher interaction complexity might weaken network stability [70, 71]. This may be because complex species interactions can lead to unstable coupling in microbial interactions and weakening species interactions usually promotes community stability [72]. It is also possible that an increase in the strength of interactions between some key taxa could destabilize trophic level interactions [73].

Microbial co-occurrence patterns provide a better understanding of the maintenance of bacterial diversity in the water and sediment of glacial lakes during lake ontogeny. The relationship between species interactions and microbial diversity can be described by two models: the Ecological Control model (EC), where high diversity limits further diversification due to niche occupancy, and the Diversity Begets Diversity model (DBD), where interactions create new niches promoting diversification [28, 74, 75]. Our results supported the latter model, with higher diversity in the water of HGI lakes where species associations were more clustered and complex than in LGI lake water. Similarly, the relationship between sediment bacterial community diversity and species interactions followed this model as well. This suggests that in glacial lakes, microbial interactions in water and sediment could serve as diversifying selective forces via niche differentiation, influencing diversification and community stability [28, 75].

Conclusion

In summary, by substituting space for time, our results suggest that bacterial diversity, network complexity, and stability in lake water and sediment showed opposite trends during glacial lake ontogeny. In lake water, bacterial diversity increased with increasing glacial influence, with higher diversity in newly formed glacial lakes, which may be related to the increased contribution of glacial snow-derived bacteria. However, the deposition of exogenous microorganisms and the enhancement of species interactions may be the reasons for the increase in bacterial diversity in sediments in early formed glacial lakes. As glacier retreat progresses with continued anthropogenic climate change, the ontogeny of glacial lakes could lead to the extinction of highly adapted species and the homogenization of aquatic microbial communities. Moving forward, it would be valuable to explore how these contrasting patterns of microbial diversity impact broader ecosystem functions and processes and contribute to the resilience or vulnerability of glacial lake ecosystems in the face of climate change. Such insights could further inform conservation strategies and management practices aimed at preserving these unique and rapidly changing habitats.
