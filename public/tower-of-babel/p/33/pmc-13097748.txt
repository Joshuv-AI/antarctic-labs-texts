Assessment of treatment outcomes among bipolar patients at Mathari National Teaching and Referral Hospital, Kenya
Authors: Esther Wangari Wahome, Amsalu Degu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBipolar disorder (BD) is a chronic psychiatric illness associated with significant morbidity and functional impairment. Although symptom control is a key goal of pharmacological treatment, functional recovery often remains elusive. In sub-Saharan Africa, and particularly Kenya, limited data exist on treatment outcomes for BD. This study assessed remission, functional recovery, and suicidality among bipolar patients at Mathari National Teaching and Referral Hospital, Kenya’s primary mental health institution.MethodsA cross-sectional descriptive design was carried out among 51 adult patients diagnosed with BD (Type I or II) and treated for at least 6 months. Manic and depressive symptoms were assessed at the time of evaluation by using the Young Mania Rating Scale (YMRS) and Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAM-D); functional outcomes were measured using the Functioning Assessment Short Test (FAST). Suicidality was assessed through structured clinical interview questions.ResultsApproximately 92.2% scored below the threshold for clinically significant manic symptoms at the time of assessment, and 74.5% for depression. However, only 45.1% achieved functional recovery (FAST score < 11). Occupational and interpersonal functioning were the most impaired domains. Suicidal thoughts or behaviors were reported by a minority of participants. Specifically, 15.7% (n = 8) reported suicidal ideation and 5.9% (n = 3) reported a history of suicide attempt.ConclusionsEven though a high proportion of participants scored below symptomatic thresholds, indicating low current symptom burden, substantial functional impairment persisted, underscoring the need for integrative treatment strategies targeting psychosocial and occupational domains in BD management.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Bipolar disorder, Remission, Functionality, Suicide, Treatment outcome, Suicidal risk, Mathari National Teaching and Referral Hospital

Background

Bipolar disorder is a chronic mental health disorder that is characterized by one or more episodes of mania or hypomania with a history of one or more major depressive episodes not caused by a medical or psychiatric condition or substance abuse. It comprises a clinical spectrum including bipolar I, bipolar II and cyclothymia that is diagnosed using the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders- Fifth Edition (DSM-5). Effective treatment involves both psychoeducation and pharmacological treatment, which includes mood stabilizers, anticonvulsants and antipsychotics [1].

Bipolar disorder affects an estimated 46 million individuals globally and ranks among the leading causes of disability-adjusted life years. As few as 10% of people with BD receive care in low-and middle-income countries [2]. In Kenya, research on BD remains sparse, with treatment primarily focused on symptom suppression rather than comprehensive recovery.

The natural history of BD involves periods of remission and relapse, especially if there is poor treatment adherence. The nature of the initial episode can predict the polarity of subsequent episodes. Patients with a predominantly depressed polarity are more likely to attempt suicide and end up being diagnosed with bipolar II. Patients with a predominantly manic polarity present at a younger age and have a higher incidence of substance abuse and impulsivity. Subsyndromal states have been noted to be more common than full syndromal episodes [3, 4].

Many patients suffering from BD are unable to recover function socially and at work. They suffer from impairment in health-related quality of life, frequent relapses, functional impairment, long-term unemployment, suicide and substance or alcohol abuse [5]. There is also a high rate of marital disputes due to hypersexuality, impulsivity, and misuse of assets, resulting in a high rate of divorce among couples [6]. Achieving functional recovery has proven to be more difficult than symptomatic improvement. Even patients who are seen to respond well to treatment and who remain well syndromally for some time period, have been seen to demonstrate subthreshold syndromes, psychosocial morbidity and cognitive impairment [7].

Previous studies to assess treatment outcomes in BD have found that: poor functioning is a key driver of disability-adjusted life years and such patients have been seen to show poor cognitive performance such as an inability to remember long-term information which subsequently affects their occupational functioning and interpersonal relationships. Overall quality of life is also significantly impacted with lower scores in physical, emotional and social well-being. Another study reported that about three-quarters of the patients with BD had functional recoveries, but 22.4% of this number suffered a new manic, hypomanic, or mixed episode [8–10]. Evidently, there is a huge gap between clinical and functional outcomes.

The data on treatment outcomes in Africa is very limited. In sub-Saharan Africa, the quality of life of BD patients is concerning due to the challenges experienced in supporting individuals with mental illness [11]. The data on treatment outcomes in Kenya is basically non-existent. Kenya lacks sufficient data on treatment outcomes for mental health due to a critical shortage of mental health professionals, widespread stigma that hinders help-seeking, poor follow-up care leading to high relapse and suicide risk, limited healthcare resources, and the exclusion of mental health from key health indicators [12, 13]. Hence, this study aimed to evaluate symptom burden, functional recovery, and suicidality among patients receiving care at Mathari National Teaching and Referral Hospital. Specifically, the study sought to identify the proportion of patients achieving symptomatic remission, assess levels of functional recovery across key domains and determine the prevalence of suicidal thoughts and behaviors. By addressing these objectives, the study provides a comprehensive overview of treatment outcomes and highlights potential gaps between clinical remission and everyday functioning in a sub-Saharan African context.

Methods

Study design, setting and period

A descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted to assess treatment outcomes among bipolar patients by analyzing medical records to confirm DSM-V diagnosis, duration of follow-up, history of hospital admissions, general course of illness, documented clinical stability during follow-up visits and the current and past treatment regimens. Clinical interviews and filling out questionnaires were also carried out. However, because symptom severity was not assessed using standardized rating scales at each visit, the chart data were not used to quantify symptom change over time or determine longitudinal remission. The present study, therefore, focused on symptom status and functioning at the time of assessment. The study was conducted at Mathari National Teaching and Referral Hospital, located west of Nairobi, along the Nairobi-Thika Road highway. The institution admitted patients with severe psychiatric disorders who could not afford private services and were difficult to manage in other public facilities within the community. It is Kenya’s premier psychiatric hospital. The data collection was carried out from October 2024 to January 2025.

Study population

The study population consisted of both inpatients and outpatients with bipolar disorders in Mathari National Teaching and Referral Hospital who had been treated for 6 months and above.

Sample size

The sampling size was a census, which included all 51 BD patients who met the inclusion criteria and had been on treatment for 6 months or longer.

Data collection instruments

This involved using structured interviews with patients to gather information on their symptoms to assess remission, functioning and suicidal ideation. In this study, previously published standard scales such as the Young Mania Rating Scale (YMRS), Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAM-D) and Functioning Assessment Short Test (FAST) were used on all the participants [14–16]. Functioning was assessed using the FAST test. The patients were ranked based on level of difficulty from 0 (no difficulty) to 3(severe difficulty). Domains were based on grouping of the 24 individual items: autonomy (4 items), occupational functioning (5 items), cognitive functioning (5 items), financial issues (2 items), interpersonal relationships (6 items) and leisure time (2 items). The FAST total score (range 0–72) was calculated as the sum of each of the 24 item scores, with higher scores representing worse functions. Each FAST domain has a different maximum possible score (e.g., autonomy = 12, occupation = 15, cognition = 15, finance = 6, interpersonal relationships = 18, leisure = 6). The score indicated < 11 for functional recovery, 12–20 for mild impairment, 21–40 for moderate impairment, and 41–72 for severe impairment [14]. A low symptom burden was assessed using HAM-D for depressive symptoms and indicated a total score of < 7, signifying the absence of symptoms for at least 2 months with a concurrent total score of < 4 on YMRS for manic symptoms to define full remission from acute episodes in BD and < 7 for milder symptoms. HAM-D consists of 17 items rated on a scale from 0 to 4, or 2 depending on severity, with the total score ranging from 0 to 53. A reduction in HAM-D scores from baseline to endpoint was interpreted as an improvement in depressive symptoms, indicating a low level of current depressive symptom burden at the time of assessment [15]. In YMRS, four core items were scored on a scale from 0 to 8 (irritability, speech, bizarre content, and disruptive–aggressive behavior), and seven items were graded on a severity scale from 0 to 4 with a total score of 60 [16]. The medical records of the patients were also reviewed to obtain information on their diagnosis, treatment history, hospitalization, relapse and overall improvement from the time of admission.

The data collection instruments had good psychometric properties in previous studies, including internal consistency and validity [17–20]. Nevertheless, to the best of our knowledge, these instruments did not undergo validation in the context of the Kenyan population, although the tool has been widely used in similar settings.

Suicidality was assessed through structured interviewer-administered questions during the clinical evaluation. Participants were asked about their lifetime history of suicidal ideation and suicide attempts, as well as any suicidal thoughts within the past month. Suicide attempts were based primarily on participant self-report, with confirmation from medical records when documentation was available. Participants reporting any ideation, plan, or attempt were categorized as having suicidality. No standardized suicide risk rating scale was used; therefore, findings are presented as the prevalence of reported ideation and attempts. The tool was piloted before data collection to ensure clarity.

Data collection technique

Data collection was conducted by trained research assistants and clinicians at the hospital. It involved a combination of interviews and a review of medical records. Participants were recruited from Mathari Hospital’s outpatient and inpatient units. Informed written consent was obtained from all participants. The HAM-D, YMRS, and FAST scales were administered by trained clinician raters during face-to-face interviews with participants. The patients were required to have been on treatment for at least 6 months since previous studies reported that there is a reduction in symptom burden after six months of treatment [21–24].

Data from questionnaires and clinical assessments were entered into a secure database using double entry to minimize errors. The data was checked for missing values, outliers and inconsistencies. Missing values were minimal and did not warrant imputation or exclusion; analyses were therefore conducted on the available data. All data was stored securely in encrypted files and backed up regularly to prevent loss.

To collect data for this study, firstly, a research permit from the National Commission for Science, Technology & Innovation (NACOSTI) was acquired. Ethical clearance was then obtained from the USIU-Africa Institutional Review Board (Ref No: USIU-A/IERC/US264-2024). The participants for the study were sourced from hospital medical records, and their eligibility was assured by meeting the inclusion criteria set for the study. Participants were recruited consecutively during their scheduled clinic visits. A full explanation of the study was provided, and written consent was obtained. Structured interviews with patients to gather information on their symptoms were done to assess remission, functioning and suicidal ideation. Standard rating scales such as the Young Mania Rating Scale, Hamilton Depression Rating Scale and Functioning Assessment Short Test were used.

To assess consistency in the assessment of suicidality among research assistants and clinicians, a randomly selected 10% of the total sample size (n = 5) was independently evaluated by two assessors. Agreement between raters on the classification of suicidality was examined using Cohen’s kappa statistic. The results indicated substantial inter-rater agreement (κ = 0.82; 95% CI: 0.71–0.93), suggesting good consistency.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) software, version 29.0.01. A low symptom burden was defined as YMRS and HAM-D scores < 7. Functionality was assessed using the FAST scale, which evaluates six domains: autonomy, occupational functioning, cognitive functioning, financial issues, interpersonal relationships, and leisure time, with recovery indicated by scores < 11. Descriptive statistics such as means, standard deviations, frequencies, and percentages were used to summarize socio-demographic and clinical variables. No inferential statistical tests were applied due to the descriptive nature of the study.

Operational definition of terms

Suicidal ideation

Any self-reported thoughts of ending one’s life, including passive thoughts (e.g., wishing to be dead) and active thoughts of self-harm with the intent of dying.

Suicide attempt

A self-reported or documented act of self-injurious behavior carried out with at least some intent to die.

Non-suicidal self-injury

A deliberate self-harm without intent to die, and was not classified as a suicide attempt.

Results

During the study period, 51 bipolar disorder patients were enrolled in the study based on the inclusion criteria. Outpatient participants comprised the majority at 70.6% (n = 36), while 29.4% (n = 15) were inpatients. The study population was predominantly males (56.9%, n = 29) than females (43.1%, n = 22). The mean (± SD) age of diagnosis of the study population was 31.6 ± 10.1 years. The mean (± SD) duration of illness was 10.4 ± 7.8 years.

In terms of marital status, 56.9% of the participants were single, while 33.3% were married. Employment status revealed that nearly half of the participants (49%) were unemployed, 47.1% were employed, and 3.9% were retired. Regarding education, 39.2% of the patients completed secondary school and pursued a college education (Table 1).

Clinical characteristics and treatment modalities

In this study, over half of the patients had co-morbidities, with hypertension (25.5%) and substance use disorder (21.6%) being the most common. A significant proportion of the participants, 52.9% (n = 27), were actively receiving psychotherapy. Regarding medications prescribed for bipolar disorder, olanzapine (47.1%, n = 24) and carbamazepine (43.1%, n = 22) were the most frequently used, while 58.8% (n = 30) had a history of antidepressant use (Table 2).

Treatment outcomes of bipolar patients

The mean YMRS and HAM-D scores were 0.9 ± 0.3 and 0.8 ± 0.4, respectively. As per the YMRS score, most of the patients (92.2%, n = 47) had scored below the threshold for clinically significant manic symptoms, while 74.5%(n = 38) of the patients had scored below the threshold for clinically significant depressive symptoms, as per the HAM-D score (Table 3). However, more than half the patients were functionally impaired (54.1%, 29), with only 45.1%(n = 22) achieving functional recovery (FAST score of < 11). The highest mean domain score was observed in occupation (5.0 ± 4.28), followed by interpersonal relationships (3.9 ± 4.28). Leisure had the lowest mean score (1.3 ± 1.43). At assessment, 15.7% (n = 8) of participants reported suicidal ideation and 5.9% (n = 3) reported a past suicide attempt, while 78.4% (n = 40) reported no suicidality (Table 4).

Discussion

The objective of this study was to assess treatment outcomes among BD patients in Mathari National Teaching and Referral Hospital, with remission status, functionality and suicidality being the main focus. Key findings revealed that while the majority of patients had below symptom threshold scores for manic symptoms (92.2%) and depressive symptoms (74.5%), functional recovery remained suboptimal, with only 45.1% of patients achieving full functional recovery as indicated by a FAST score of < 11, while the rest were functionally impaired.

The disparity between symptomatic remission and functional recovery represents a critical finding. The high proportion of participants scoring below symptom thresholds indicates low current symptom burden. Interpretation of these findings is limited to the current clinical status at assessment. The lower rate of functional recovery (45.1%) indicates that symptom resolution alone may not translate to improved daily functioning. This disconnect is particularly evident in occupational functioning, which showed the highest impaired domain (5.0 ± 0.6) while leisure activities were the least impaired(1.3 ± 0.2).

The findings regarding the gap between symptomatic and functional recovery align with previous research in bipolar disorder. Previous studies demonstrated that functional recovery often lags behind symptomatic improvement, and there is persistent functional impairment in BD even during symptom remission [21, 25–28]. The high rates of low symptom burden and low rate of functional recovery were comparable to studies done in Ethiopia, where 40% of the study patients were functionally impaired. The impairment level was better due to the high adherence level of 77.9% and the euthymic status of the participants [29, 30]. The findings emphasize the need for a holistic approach that addresses both functional improvement and clinical symptoms.

Occupational functioning is a critical element of functional outcome, and patients with bipolar disorder generally exhibit impairments in this area. It was the most impaired domain in the study, with nearly half of the participants being unemployed at the time of assessment. This study did not examine employment history or reasons for unemployment; therefore, no causal relationship with bipolar disorder can be established. Because the study was designed as a descriptive cross-sectional assessment, inferential analyses examining associations between employment and functioning were not performed. However, previous literature has documented substantial employment challenges among individuals with BD, showing that occupation is usually the most severely affected due to the disruptive effect of BD on work, regardless of educational attainment [30–32]. These findings highlight the role of employment in improving mental health outcomes by providing structure, purpose and social interaction. These individuals would benefit from targeted vocational rehabilitation.

Inter-personal relationships were also another significantly affected domain. Previous studies showed that the course of illness may interfere with the ability to sustain friendships, and the lack of social support is a vulnerability for the recurrence and severity of symptoms [33]. A lack of family support also contributed to depressive symptoms, substance abuse and suicide attempts. Unmarried, separated, and widowed patients tend to socially isolate themselves, which could have been a possible reason for the impairment [34]. In contrast, married patients demonstrated better treatment outcomes, as reflected by lower FAST impairment scores. This observation aligns with global findings that marital support can positively influence mental health and treatment adherence. The protective effect of marriage could be linked to enhanced social support, which buffers stress and promotes emotional stability [35].

Most patients were on a minimum of two medications. The scores for both manic and depressive symptoms suggest that current treatment regimens, particularly the use of olanzapine and carbamazepine as the most commonly prescribed medications, were effective in symptom control. This coincides with other studies done, which have proven a combination of mood stabilizers and anti-psychotics to be an effective regimen for managing BD [36, 37]. The majority of the patients (52.9%) had also received psychotherapy initially, but not consistently after symptom resolution, often due to symptom improvement, travel distances, or financial constraints. Clinical trials and treatment guidelines have supported the use of combination therapy and psychotherapy in achieving outcomes. However, the functional impairment observed despite this high rate of psychotherapy engagement suggests the need for more targeted interventions focusing on functional recovery and functional remediation [3, 28, 38].

The use of antidepressants was high (58.8%), which was contrary to most studies since the risk of mood switch and long-term destabilization is a concern when using antidepressant medications in BD, especially as monotherapy. The magnitude of this risk has yet to be known, but maintenance use of antidepressant medication has generally not demonstrated a favorable risk-benefit ratio [39, 40].

The comorbidity rates in our study, particularly for substance use disorder (21.6%), are generally consistent with existing literature, though slightly lower than some population-based studies that report rates of 30–50% [41]. This difference might reflect our study setting or selection criteria. It also shows the complexity of managing bipolar disorder in the context of multiple medical conditions. Studies have shown that substance use gravely affects remission and functionality in bipolar and is a major contributor to relapse, suicide risk and recurrence [37, 42, 43]. Studies also show that there is an association between BD and hypertension, though minimal, underscoring the importance of monitoring and managing cardiovascular health and substance use in individuals with BD [44, 45].

A relatively small proportion of participants reported suicidal ideation or a previous suicide attempt. Lower current symptom levels and continued clinical monitoring may be contributing factors, although this study was not designed to evaluate determinants of suicidality. Previous studies indicated that treatment with mood stabilizers and antipsychotics, along with close observation and follow-up, is one of the most effective ways to mitigate suicide in these patients [46]. Lithium has long been known to reduce rates of suicide attempts and completions in depressed and bipolar patients, but its use is still underutilized in clinical practice [47, 48]. Its use in this study was also very limited due to expense and availability. This goes to show that the risk of suicidality does not disappear entirely, but can be mitigated and dealt with at an early stage.

These outcomes must be viewed within the broader systemic context of mental health care in Kenya. Structural barriers within the mental health system may partly contribute to the observed functional gaps. Mental health stigma remains prevalent and may affect employment opportunities, social reintegration, and help-seeking behavior [12, 13]. Additionally, workforce shortages and limited availability of structured psychosocial rehabilitation services in Kenya have been documented [13], potentially restricting access to sustained non-pharmacological support. Financial constraints and the economic burden of mental illness in sub-Saharan Africa may further limit continuity of care [11]. These systemic factors may help explain why functional impairment persists despite relatively low symptom scores at assessment. Mental health remains underprioritized in national health indicators and funding allocations and this calls for the development of comprehensive care models.

In conclusion, these systemic and sociocultural challenges highlight the need for clinical strategies that go beyond symptom management to support functional recovery. Approaches such as structured psychosocial interventions, vocational rehabilitation, and sustained psychotherapy may help address occupational and social deficits, particularly in resource-limited settings. While the present study provides a snapshot of functional outcomes in routine care, longitudinal research is warranted to understand recovery trajectories and inform context-specific interventions.

Limitations

This study should be interpreted with certain limitations. The study’s cross-sectional nature precludes causal inference. The sample size was intended to provide an initial clinical and functional profile rather than to detect small between-group differences or test specific hypotheses requiring high statistical power. The sample size was modest, and reliance on self-reported data may have introduced bias. The sample reflects patients who met eligibility criteria during the study period at a single tertiary referral hospital and had been in care for at least six months. As such, individuals who were more clinically stable and engaged in follow-up may be overrepresented, while those with more severe illness or poor adherence may be underrepresented. This may partly explain the relatively low symptom scores observed at assessment. Although clinical and socioeconomic variables were measured and described, the modest sample size limited the feasibility of reliable multivariable modelling to adjust for potential confounding; therefore, independent associations between these factors and functional outcomes could not be determined. Further longitudinal studies are warranted. Additionally, the FAST instrument used captured information from the recent past, which was short-term in nature; this may not fully reflect long-term functional trajectories. Therefore, findings may not reflect the full spectrum of bipolar disorder in the community, and the relatively small sample size further limits generalizability. Lastly, mixed features were not separately categorized, and this has been acknowledged as a limitation of symptom interpretation. Suicidality was assessed using structured questions rather than a standardized validated instrument, which may introduce potential misclassification bias and limit comparability with other studies.

Although standardized and widely validated instruments were used (FAST, YMRS and HAM-D), formal validation studies in the Kenyan population were limited. Even though these tools have strong psychometric properties in other studies, cultural and contextual differences may influence their validity and reliability. The absence of local validation may affect the precision of measurement and interpretation of the findings of the study. Hence, future research should prioritize cross-cultural adaptation and validation of these scales in the local population.

Conclusion

The majority of the treated patients scored below the threshold for clinically significant symptoms. However, functional impairment remains a major challenge. These findings highlight a gap between symptom status and everyday functioning among individuals with BD in routine care. Targeted interventions addressing occupational rehabilitation, social support, and sustained psychotherapy are essential to bridge this gap in BD management. Longitudinal studies are needed to better understand functional recovery over time. Future studies should include community samples and individuals at different stages of illness to provide a more comprehensive understanding of functional outcomes. Longitudinal studies should also be conducted to better understand functional recovery over time.
