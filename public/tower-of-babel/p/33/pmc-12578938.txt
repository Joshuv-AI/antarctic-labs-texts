The compounding effects of agricultural expansion and snow drought on lake urmia’s drying crisis
Authors: Afshin Shahbazi, Yusuf Aydin, Guluzar Duygu Semiz, Elifnaz Torun, Babak Vaheddoost, Neda Beirami, Alper Unal, Kaveh Madani, Amir AghaKouchak
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Lake Urmia, one of the world’s largest hypersaline lakes, has experienced severe drought in recent decades. This study investigates the combined impacts of agricultural expansion and climate variability on river inflows from 1985 to 2020. A hybrid framework incorporating statistical models and Convolutional Neural Networks was employed to estimate river discharge and disentangle the effects of hydroclimatic and anthropogenic drivers. Results indicate a persistent snow drought beginning in the late 1990s, concurrent with exceeding fourfold increase in irrigated lands. Scenario-based analysis, restoring key parameters to pre-1999 levels revealed that reverting agricultural water use was the dominant factor driving changes in river inflows, accounting for approximately 66% (95% CI: 56%–76%) of the total impact. In contrast, restoring precipitation and evaporation contributed 25% (95% CI: 18%–33%) and 9% (95% CI: 7%–12%), respectively, while restoring both simultaneously explained 34% (95% CI: 26%–43%) of the change. These results underscore the primary role of agricultural water demand amplified by declining snowpack and climatic shifts in altering basin hydrology. The findings highlight the urgent need for integrated water resource management, with a focus on climate adaptation, snowpack monitoring, and sustainable agricultural practices to address ongoing environmental degradation and ensure long-term water security.

Introduction

Compounding effects of climate change and variability along with rapid urban and agricultural land development and unsustainable growth have resulted in major environmental challenges1,2. As a consequence of these changes, some of the world’s water bodies such as the Great Salt Lake in USA, Lake Urmia in Iran, the Dead Sea in the Middle East, Lake Tuz in Türkiye and the Aral Sea in Central Asia have experienced substantial declines in recent decades. These declines are primarily due to anthropogenic drought3,4 developed with notable contributions from climate change and variability5–8, which have led these water bodies into the state of water bankruptcy9.

Lake Urmia, once one of the world’s largest hypersaline lakes located in the northwestern Iran, has gradually lost its water body over the last three decades, with a large fraction of it transforming into a seasonal lake. Drought and a rapid increase in agricultural water withdrawal10 made possible through the construction of dams and increased groundwater extraction11 are the primary causes of lake dryness12. Studies have shown that the compounding effect of climate change and increased water demand intensifies drought and their socioeconomic impacts11. While several studies have focused examined the detrimental effects of climate change on the lake’s water levels13, the majority of recent studies have found indicate that local anthropogenic factors have a more significant impact on the lake’s desiccation than climatic change10,14–18. However, these studies have not adequately considered the role of snow dynamics in the region. The majority of the past studies focusing on the effects of climate change and human-induced activities on lakes have examined factors such as precipitation, temperature, evaporation, and agricultural development, without examining the significance of the snow component15,16,19–22. Seasonal snow is a vital water resource, serving as a naturally distributed water reservoir in mountainous regions23,24. Measuring and monitoring snow is essential for understanding climatic and hydrological cycles, developing water management policy and adaptation strategies, and ensuring the sustainable management of water resources25,26.

There are only a few studies in the literature that investigate the impacts of snow changes on the Lake Urmia Basin (LUB). Nikraftar and Hasanlou (2015)27 used Special Sensor Microwave/Imager (SSM/I) data along with Support Vector Regression (SVR) and a genetic algorithm to estimate snow depth on Sahand Mountain – a major source of snow in the lake’s basin. Habibi et al. (2021)28 applied Standard Precipitation Index (SPI), Standard Precipitation Evaporation Index (SPEI) and Standardized Snow Melt and Rain Index (SMRI) to understand drought dynamics in the LUB from 1981 to 2018. Their findings indicated that reduced snow cover has intensified drought in the region, particularly after 1995. In a recent study, Safari et al. (2023)29 analyzed the variability of Snow Cover Area (SCA) in the LUB from 2000 to 2020. Although their findings indicated no significant long-term trends in SCA, they highlighted a strong association between SCA and the Standardized Evapotranspiration Deficit Index (SEDI), emphasizing SEDI’s function in monitoring snow cover changes.

While these studies have aimed to comprehend the alterations in snow cover within the basin, they have overlooked the exploration of how snow drought and changes in Snow Water Equivalent (SWE) influence the water level fluctuations of Lake Urmia, separating the snow-effect from other climatic and anthropogenic factors. A snow drought refers to periods of abnormally low snowpack conditions and can be classified as either a dry snow drought, driven by reduced precipitation, or a warm snow drought, caused by higher temperatures leading to rain instead of snow or early snowmelt30. Since the LUB is a mountainous region, a comprehensive study considering all factors such as SWE would be need to investigate and understand the dynamics of the water cycle in the region and the corresponding lake level changes. Snow-limited basins such as LUB rely on winter snowpack as a seasonal buffer that releases water in late spring, sustaining recharge and baseflow into the irrigation season. With warming, basins experience less snowfall, more rain, and earlier melt, which collectively advance runoff timing and weaken warm season flows a set of changes widely documented across snow dependent regions31–34. These timing shifts undermine the long-standing supply demand synchrony assumed in water management and are a first order control on water availability for agriculture in spring- summer31–33. Lake Urmia’s water balance reflects the interaction of climate driven snow limitation and management driven demand: earlier snowmelt advances runoff and weakens warm season baseflows, while the irrigation calendar (winter wheat sown Sep–Nov, harvest mid-Apr–Jul; critical spring irrigations at stem-elongation/heading/grain-fill) concentrates demand in spring35,36.

Lake Urmia’s seasonal water balance reflects the interaction of a climate-driven shift toward a snow-limited regime less snowfall with earlier and slower melt that advances runoff and weakens late-spring/summer baseflows and management driven growth in AWU31,33,34,37–43. This interaction narrows the overlap between supply and the spring irrigation calendar. Consistent with basin-wide evidence that both climate and human water use have materially contributed to lake’s decline39–43, we therefore treat their joint role as a first-order control on seasonal inflows and lake storage and test this mechanism with the trend diagnostics and scenario analysis that follow.

Geospatial snow measurements often have temporal and spatial limitations, particularly in high mountainous regions to overcome these challenges, remote sensing methods, such as satellite imagery are employed for monitoring snow cover and estimating SWE. These technologies offer a more comprehensive approach to tracking changes in snow cover and SWE over time. Currently, various types of SWE-related data are available globally, which can be classified into different categories, such as reanalysis, data assimilation and model simulation.

The primary objective of this research is to examine the effects of anthropogenic (agricultural expansion) and climatic (e.g., precipitation, evapotranspiration, snow impact on river inflows) factors on water level fluctuations of the Lake Urmia. A combination of in situ data, satellite data and reanalysis datasets are used to select the most accurate dataset for exploring the impacts of snow variations in the Lake Urmia. A key component of this research is the development of an efficient multi-model approach capable of accurately capturing the complex hydroclimatic dynamics of the LUB and estimating river inflows using statistical and Deep Learning (DL) methods.

Materials and methods

Study area

Lake Urmia (37°06’27’’-38°15’16’’ N, 45°02’10’’-45°49’38’’ E) with an average surface area of 5,000 km2, is located between West Azerbaijan and East Azerbaijan provinces of Iran at an altitude of 1,275 m above sea level. This unique lake is a UNESCO Biosphere Reserve and an international wetland of great importance for its rich ecosystem, housing vital species in the food chain such as Artemia and providing a crucial habitat for migratory birds like flamingos. The LUB is encompassed by elevations ranging from 1222 m to 3,875 m and covers an area of approximately 52,000 km2. Figure 1a indicates the elevation map of the basin produced using ASTER-GDEM (Global Digital Elevation Model) satellite imagery with a spatial resolution of 30 × 30 m (NASA, 2022). Areas with a slope of 0–4°, which are flat or nearly flat, cover the largest section with a 41% ratio and high infiltration occurs in these areas. Areas with a slope of 4–10°, constituting 25% of the landscape and consist of regions surrounding the flat or nearly flat areas with a slight slope. These sections are concentrated in the west and east of the basin, and the highest slope values are found near the Meydan and Mor Mountains on the Turkish border, reaching up to 78°49. Approximately 7 million people inhabit this area, where Lake Urmia plays a significant role in the lives and economy of the local population and country as well15.

Figure 1b indicates the location of Lake Urmia and its basin in Iran. A causeway divides the lake into northern and southern portions and water can only flow beneath a 1,500-meter bridge built on the causeway44,45. The region’s climate is influenced by the surrounding mountains, with temperatures typically ranging from 0 °C to -20 °C in winter and up to 40 °C in summer45. The average annual precipitation in the basin is about 350 mm12. Direct precipitation and groundwater resources account for 25% of Lake Urmia’s water inflow, with rivers supplying the remaining 75%46,47. Several perennial and seasonal rivers feed into Lake Urmia, with Siminehrood and Zarrinehrood being the primary contributors, supplying nearly half of the lake’s water44. Reported evaporation from the lake surface ranges from 580 to 2,000 mm per year47. As of February 2021, the lake’s water surface elevation had dropped to 1,271.3 m, and salinity had exceeded 300 g/L12.

In-situ data

In this study, the hydro-climatic and water storage data in the basin including precipitation (summation of rain, snow and ice), rain (liquid water), temperature, evaporation, river inflows, Agricultural Water Use (AWU) and water level fluctuations of the Lake Urmia were provided from the prior studies15,17. These studies acquired data from several Iranian government organizations and previously processed the raw data. This study utilized runoff data from 16 rivers, comprising 10 main rivers and 6 minor rivers, as well as meteorological data (precipitation, temperature and evaporation) obtained from 36 synoptic stations in the basin and rain data from 110 rain measurement stations in the basin. This data was employed to gain insights into the climatic conditions within the study area. The agricultural development and irrigation demand in the basin were provided from the results of Alizade Govarchin Ghale et al., (2019)15 and Barhagh et al., (2021)17. The in-situ monthly snowfall data, measured between 2007 and 2013 in 81 locations, was obtained from the meteorological organization of Iran. Although access to more recent data was not possible, the 7 years of data were sufficient for the purpose of our study. Figure 1c indicates the location map of 81 ground-observed snow stations used in this study. The main parameters measured at these stations include snow density, snow depth and SWE. Snow measurement stations are typically situated in the higher elevation areas of the basin. The regions with the highest concentration of snow stations are, in order, the south, southwest, northwest, and east. AMSR-E provided a global-scale daily SWE dataset from June 19, 2002, to October 3, 201150. The pixel values of this data range from 0 to 255, with values from 0 to 240 representing SWE values in millimeters. Values of 252-253-254-255 indicate no snow or snow impossibility (252), glacier (253), water (254) and missing data (255), respectively. The real SWE values were divided by 2 for storage51,52. Therefore, to use the data, each pixel value must be multiplied by 2. The algorithm developed by Chang et al., (1987)53, known as the NASA algorithm, utilizes the brightness temperature degrees obtained from AMSR-E to generate SWE data. In this study, monthly AMSR-E SWE data for the years 2002–2011 were obtained from NSIDC in HDF-EOS2 format at Level 3, featuring a spatial resolution of 25 km × 25 km. It should be noted that using a fixed snow density can lead to incorrect results for different regions. MOD10A1

The Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) is a radiometric sensor with dual-sided scanning mirrors, developed by NASA. It was first integrated into the Terra satellite in 199956, followed by the Aqua satellite in 200257. MODIS can cover a wide area of the Earth’s surface with its broad viewing angle and collects data in 36 spectral bands at various resolutions58. This capability facilitates the production of various global-scale products, including snow cover. The high spatial resolution and wide scanning feature of MODIS have enabled cloud-differentiated snow cover data to be available since 200059. Snow and clouds, as captured by MODIS data, can be distinguished through their differing reflectance properties in the VIS and SWIR wavelengths. These differences are easily detected using the Normalized Difference Snow Index (NDSI). This index serves as a fundamental tool for distinguishing between snowy and non-snowy areas60.

For the production of MODIS snow data, three processing levels are applied: L1B, L2, and L3. The L3 snow product is a geophysical product processed spatially and temporally, containing data for a specific region (grid) of the Earth. It is presented in a gridded map projection format as the grids of the global grid61,62. The MOD10A1 snow product is generated from the global observations of MODIS sensors, combined algorithmically with the geographical matching of MOD10_L2 and MOD10_L2G products. The pixels produced through these algorithms contain the optimal NDSI, snow albedo and quality control data.

PERSIANN-CDR

PERSIANN63 is an affective algorithm for reconstructing historical precipitation over the past three decades, utilizing infrared data as its primary source. The model is trained using National Centers for Environmental Prediction (NCEP) Stage IV hourly precipitation data and subsequently applied with fixed parameters. To minimize biases and ensure high-resolution consistency, Global Precipitation Climatology Project (GPCP) data are incorporated. The final product, known as Precipitation Estimation from Remotely Sensed Information using Artificial Neural Networks-Climate Data Record (PERSIANN-CDR), provides a 30-year record of daily precipitation data at 0.25° spatial resolution, covering the 60°N–60°S latitude range64.

Global precipitation measurement (GPM)

The Global Precipitation Measurement (GPM) mission, a collaboration between NASA and JAXA, aims to provide accurate, global precipitation data through a constellation of advanced satellites. These satellites use sophisticated instruments such as the Dual-Frequency Precipitation Radar (DPR) and the GPM Microwave Imager (GMI) to capture both rainfall and snowfall across a broad range of latitudes. The Integrated Multi-Satellite Retrievals for GPM (IMERG) algorithm processes and integrates data from various satellite sensors, offering high-resolution precipitation estimates at 0.1° spatial resolution and 30-minute temporal intervals65,66.

Reanalysis and land data assimilation system data

In this study, we utilized both space-based snow products and the ERA5 and GLDAS datasets to investigate snow changes in the Lake Urmia Basin. We conducted an accuracy assessment to compare all datasets against ground-observed data, enabling us to identify the most reliable dataset for our analysis.

The European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF) developed ERA5 reanalysis data, which are among the leading programs that provide global-scale data67. This dataset offers 8 different snow parameters, including snow albedo, snow cover, snow depth, snowfall, snow layer temperature, snowmelt, snow density and SWE68. The dataset covers the time period from January 1950 to the present with spatial resolutions of 9 km × 9 km on both hourly and monthly timescales.

Land Data Assimilation Systems (LDAS) provide global-scale climatic and hydrological data by running numerical models in addition to ground and satellite observations69. Four different Land Surface Models (LSMs) are used in the production of GLDAS data: NOAH, Catchment (CLSM), Community Land Model (CLM), and Variable Infiltration Capacity (VIC). Surface conditions and flux products simulated by these models, including soil moisture, surface temperature, evaporation and SWE are made accessible through the NASA Goddard Earth Sciences Data and Information Services Center (GES DISC). The 0.25-degree resolution products exclusively contain data from the NOAH model70. In the production of SWE data within the GLDAS/NOAH model, snow cover data obtained from the MODIS sensor on NASA’s Terra satellite is used with the update technique described by Rodell and Houser (2004)69. The modeled SWE data is updated by comparing it with MODIS snow cover data. If MODIS snow cover percentage is greater than 40% whe there is no snow in the model, 10 mm of SWE is added to the pixels69. For GLDAS SWE data, 3-hourly outputs of the NOAH model with a spatial resolution of 0.25° were used in this study.

Data preprocessing

In the first step of data processing, satellite data and reanalysis data were filtered and masked based on the boundaries of the Lake Urmia Basin. The satellite data, referenced to the Earth’s surface, were projected using the Universal Transverse Mercator (UTM) coordinate system, with the WGS 84 Datum and the 38th zone applied to the study area.

To spatially interpolate the point-based SWE measurements across the basin, Co-Kriging82 and Inverse Distance Weighted (IDW) interpolation methods were employed84,85. In the Co-Kriging method, elevation was incorporated as a secondary variable to improve estimation accuracy. The predictive performance of both interpolation techniques was evaluated through cross-validation86. In each iteration, 25% of the snow measurement stations were excluded, and the interpolated values were compared with the actual measurements at these omitted stations. This process enabled the identification of the most accurate interpolation method for generating basin-wide SWE distributions. The interpolated SWE results were compared with SWE estimates derived from AMSR-E, Improved AMSR-E, GLDAS, and ERA5 products, and the dataset that showed the best agreement with SWE measurements was selected.

For the generation of ERA5 SWE maps, monthly output data were used. ERA5 SWE raster data, with a spatial resolution of 9 km × 9 km, were downloaded in NetCDF format from the Copernicus Climate Change Service (C3S, 2023). Similarly, for GLDAS, monthly outputs from the GLDAS NOAH Land Surface Model L4 were employed. These SWE data, with a spatial resolution of 25 km × 25 km, were obtained in NetCDF format from GES DISC87, covering the period from 1985 to 2023.

To classify snow drought intensity, snow accumulation values are compared with the historical climate distribution. The severity of snow drought is classified into four categories based on the snow accumulation percentile thresholds: 1- Light Snow Drought: 50th − 25th percentile, 2- Medium Snow Drought: 25th − 15th percentile, 3- Heavy Snow Drought: 15th − 5th percentile and 4- Extreme Snow Drought: below the 5th percentile.

Statistical indices and models

The accuracy of satellite data and global products based on observational data was evaluated using several performance metrics, including the modified Kling-Gupta Efficiency (KGE)92,93, R-squared94,95, and Root Mean Square Error (RMSE)96. Trend analysis and significance tests

In this study, the Mann-Kendall test108 was used to evaluate trends in SWE, precipitation, water level, evaporation, and other hydro-meteorological parameters.

The Mann-Kendall test, a non-parametric method recommended by the World Meteorological Organization, detects monotonic trends in time series data and is suitable for non-normally distributed datasets common in hydrology and meteorology109–111. The MK test computes a statistic, S, which indicates whether data values increase or decrease over time, with positive or negative S suggesting rising or falling trends, respectively. The standardized statistic, ZS, tests the null hypothesis of no trend. A trend is significant at the 95% (α = 0.05) or 99% (α = 0.01) confidence levels if |ZS| exceeds 1.96 or 2.576, respectively.

In addition, Welch’s T-Test112 was employed to assess the statistical significance of differences in monthly SWE averages across different periods, as well as to evaluate whether changes in river inflows under various scenarios were statistically significant. This test, appropriate for comparing means between groups with unequal variances and sample sizes, was utilized not only to detect notable changes in SWE but also to identify monthly shifts in snowfall patterns.

The first-order polynomial-based RSM, often referred to as MLR, is utilized to illustrate the linear interconnection between the response variable and its predictors. The equation typically integrates intercept and slope parameters, correlating each predictor to the response in a linear fashion. Spatio-Temporal analysis of snow dynamics

To investigate the impacts of climate change and snow drought in the basin, a matched sample T-test was conducted using ERA5 SWE results for two separate periods, spanning November, December, January, February, March, and April. The first period lasts from 1985 to 1998, while the second runs from 1999 to 2023. The time periods were selected based on the snow drought analysis shown in Fig. 7, as well as drought assessments from previous studies3,15.

Snow drought condition for each hydrological year from 1985 to 2023.

The snow drought results in the Fig. 7 revealed no drought or only light drought conditions in the basin before 1999. However, after 1999, drought began to significantly impact changes in snow mass. The T-test is used to determine whether the mean differences between the two groups are statistically significant. The results of T-test revealed that the difference between the two selected periods in terms of SWE were significant. The results indicated consistently lower SWE during the second period (1999–2023). The mean monthly average SWE were 42.3 mm and 23.9 mm for the period 1985–1998 and 1999–2023, respectively (Fig. 8).

The monthly mean SWE during 1985–1998 and 1999–2023.

The mean annual SWE were 253.59 mm and 143.31 mm (43.49%) for the period of 1985–1998 and 1999–2023, respectively. The declines in monthly averages were 30.61%, 37.35%, 33.78%, 34.87%, 54.41%, and 57.62% for November, December, January, February, March, and April, respectively. In the initial period, the peak of the snow season, marked by the highest SWE, transpired in March. Conversely, during the second period, a noticeable shift occurred, with the highest SWE now occurring a month earlier, in February. This shift suggests a dual change in both the volume and seasonal timing of snowfall, which may reflect the intensifying impacts of climate change, as also indicated in Figs. 7 and 8.

Figure 9a illustrates the inverse relationship between SWE and temperature changes in the basin. After 1999, temperature has risen, while SWE has declined during the same period. The mean temperature has increased about 1 Celsius in the basin after 1999. While the minimum mean temperature was observed in 1992 with a value of 9.5 Celsius, the maximum one has observed in 2010 with a value of about 13.5 Celsius. Previous studies show that snow and SWE are sensitive to different levels of warming117–119. For this reason, snow droughts are projected to become increasingly frequent90 due to warming winters, with increasing impacts on the environment. However, lower snow does not necessarily lead to lower available water as the rain-snow ratio may increase over time120. Considering the decline in snow and that the overall precipitation has not changed significantly in Lake Urmia (Fig. 3), it is reasonable to conclude that rain-snow ratio has increased in the Lake Urmia Basin. The advance of peak SWE reflects warmer mid-winter conditions that push precipitation toward rain near the rain–snow transition (~ 0–2 °C), increasing rain-on-snow occurrence, and trigger earlier melt onset that proceeds at lower melt rates in shallower packs mechanisms widely observed in warming, snow-limited basins. Rain data from 110 stations within the basin, spanning from 1985 to 2011, were analyzed to examine changes in the rain-to-SWE ratio across the study area (Fig. 9b). The results showed a noticeable increase in the rain-SWE ratio, particularly after 1999. This increase suggests a shift towards decreasing SWE and increasing rain over time.

To clarify the correlation structure, we added a qualitative path diagram (Fig. 10). It shows that precipitation (P) affects inflow (Q) primarily through snow storage and melt (P→SWE→Q), with a weaker direct rain-runoff path (P→Q). Temperature (T) modulates the partitioning of precipitation into rain or snow and controls the timing/rate of melt, while anthropogenic water use (AWU) reduces Q via withdrawals. Pairwise correlations therefore reflect only part of the system: for example, the correlation between P and Q may appear weak when SWE mediates most of the effect. This framework highlights that the dominant pathway in this basin is P→SWE→Q, while the direct P→Q pathway is secondary and seasonally episodic31,121.

Modeling of hydrological processes

A bivariate simple linear regression model was used to examine the relationship between river inflows and parameters including precipitation, AWU, and evaporation. As shown in the supplementary material, evaporation in the basin is directly linked to temperature changes, so evaporation data were incorporated into the model to predict river inflows, alongside the other parameters. Temperature data for the basin are provided in the supplementary section, with Supplementary Fig. S1 illustrating both temperature trends and the relationship between evaporation and temperature in the study area. Among the evaluated models, the CNN model consistently outperformed MLR and RSM in both training and testing phases, demonstrating lower RMSE and higher R² and KGE scores, which indicates its stable and superior predictive performance. In the 70% training scenario, the CNNs model maintained strong calibration metrics (R2 = 0.86, KGE = 0.86, RMSE = 885), although its verification performance was less stable (R² = 0.43, KGE = 0.73), likely due to the reduced training dataset. The CNN model’s consistent performance in the 80% training scenario, characterized by R² and KGE values near 0.9 and substantially lower RMSE, underscores its reliability and superiority.

The apparent drop in validation R² when moving from an 80%–20% to a 70%–30% split arises from two related factors. Sample size effect; With a 30% validation set, the training set becomes smaller, reducing the effective information available for parameter optimization. As CNNs typically require relatively large training samples to capture spatio-temporal patterns, this reduction naturally leads to lower predictive skill. Interannual variability of inflows; Our dataset (1985–2020) contains strong interannual variability, including several extreme drought years. When a larger fraction of years is allocated to validation, there is a greater likelihood that such extreme years are included in the validation subset, disproportionately lowering performance metrics despite identical architecture and hyperparameters. We confirm that the CNN architecture and hyperparameters (filters, kernel sizes, sequence length, batch size, optimizer) were kept identical between these two configurations, with the only change being the relative proportion of training and validation samples. Overall, the performance drops under a 70%–30% split reflects the trade-off between training size and validation representativeness in a limited dataset, driven primarily by the uneven distribution of extreme years rather than by CNN instability. Consequently, the CNN model was selected for developing predictive scenarios due to its enhanced accuracy and consistency.

Discussion

The absence of adequate snowpack storage or deviations in the timing of snowmelt from the different reservoirs can pose challenges for water resource management31,90. Snow droughts are categorized as either dry or warm, depending on whether they result from insufficient cold-season precipitation (dry) or a failure in snow accumulation despite nearly normal precipitation123. This failure is typically attributed to elevated temperatures, leading to precipitation falling as rain rather than snow, or an exceptionally premature snowmelt (warm) leading to an overall snow amount for the time of the year31,90. Most previous studies did not investigate the effect of snow drought on Lake Urmia and/or primarily concentrated on the influences of precipitation, temperature, and evaporation as climatic factors affecting water level fluctuations in the Lake Urmia, neglecting the significance of snow changes. In mountainous regions such as northwestern Iran, characterized by towering peaks like Sahand Mountain, snow functions as a crucial natural reservoir and a vital component of the water cycle.

This study focused on the intricate interplay of human-related and climatic factors, with a special emphasis on snow dynamics, influencing the rivers that contribute essential inflows to the Lake Urmia. The results highlight the negative impacts of snow drought in conjunction with anthropogenic activities (e.g., agricultural water withdrawal) in the Lake Urmia Basin. The drying up of Lake Urmia can be primarily attributed to the evolution of agriculture and ensuing economic development. This process, intertwined with snow drought and climate variations, especially post 1990s, stand out as critical factors in the lake’s diminishing water levels.

We show that despite enduring snow drought, agricultural expansion has thrived over the past four decades, with irrigated lands expanding over fourfold15. Increasing water discharge for agricultural irrigation resulted in a significant reduction in runoff flow, particularly between 1999 and 2002, when the region also began experiencing a snow drought. After 1999, the basin experienced snow drought, causing the annual runoff into the lake to reach its lowest level during the study period. Despite this, agricultural water withdrawals continued to rise during the same time10,15.

Based on the findings of this study, the root cause of water challenges in the Lake Urmia Basin lies in the inadequate management of water supply and demand, compounded by insufficient preparedness for drought and climate variability. While agricultural expansion and irrigation withdrawals have been the dominant drivers of water scarcity under typical conditions, the analysis reveals that during extreme drought years particularly those associated with snow drought climatic factors can overwhelm even the most efficient management interventions. In such instances, reduced snowpack and early snowmelt significantly diminish inflows, limiting the effectiveness of policy and infrastructure responses.

A shift from snow-dominated to rain-dominated winter precipitation significantly alters the hydrologic regime and challenges existing water management strategies. A rain-heavier winter regime disrupts the timing and reliability of water supply. It requires a fundamental rethinking of reservoir operations, irrigation scheduling, and environmental water management. Proactive adaptation measures such as dynamic reservoir rule curves, updated cropping calendars, and improved forecasting will be critical to sustaining water availability under these shifting climatic conditions. Given that snow plays a critical role in the region’s hydrological balance, as it does in many mountainous basins worldwide, restoration and future development policies must be strategically designed with a nuanced understanding of the basin’s natural coping capacity, projected climatic shifts, and the ecological sustainability of implemented measures. Crucially, water policies should distinguish between normal and extreme hydrological years and incorporate contingency planning to address scenarios in which natural constraints severely limit water availability.

According to a report by the Iran Water Resources Management Organization, approximately 10.3 billion cubic meters of additional water is needed to restore the lake to its ecological level of 1,274.1 meters124. Achieving this target will require significant improvements in agricultural practices, including the adoption of efficient irrigation techniques and precision farming that tailors irrigation and crop management to local conditions. Without sustainable water management systems and effective water policies in place, especially in the context of a changing climate, restoring the lake will be extremely challenging. Additionally, water and environmental policies must be aligned with local realities, supported by a strong and independent judiciary to enforce regulations, and efforts to minimize corruption and illegal water withdrawals within the basin must be intensified.

Conclusion

The primary objectives of this research were to examine the impact of snow drought on river inflows in the Lake Urmia Basin over the last decades. Furthermore, a comprehensive examination was conducted to delineate the compounding impacts of anthropogenic (i.e., agricultural development) and climatic factors on the lake. This encompassed an analysis of agricultural water use, precipitation, evaporation and snow. Notably, this study went beyond previous research efforts by leveraging in situ data, satellite data, and reanalysis datasets to select the most accurate dataset for investigating the effects of snow variations on the lake. A crucial facet of this research was the development of efficient statistical models, namely Multiple Linear Regression (MLR) model, Response Surface Model (RSM), and Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) designed to adeptly capture the intricate hydroclimatic dynamics of the Lake Urmia Basin.

The MLR, RSM, and CNN models demonstrated robust performance in predicting river inflows to the Lake Urmia Basin. The effectiveness of these modeling techniques underscores their reliability in capturing and forecasting the intricate dynamics of inflow. Notably, the CNN model outperformed both MLR and RSM, achieving R² and KGE values of approximately 0.9 in both calibration and verification phases, with significantly lower RMSE values (745 MCM and 347 MCM for calibration and verification, respectively) compared to RSM and MLR. The RSM model exhibited strong explanatory power during both calibration and verification phases, while the MLR model showed weaker calibration performance (R2 = 0.61, KGE = 0.69) despite robust verification results (R2 = 0.96, KGE = 0.97). The CNN model’s consistent and superior performance, particularly in the 80% training scenario, established it as the most reliable approach for predicting river inflows.

Various scenarios were developed based on the model’s outputs to assess their impact on river inflows to Lake Urmia. The first scenario, which maintains AWU at pre-1999 levels, shows a 66% increase in average river inflows. The second scenario, where precipitation is kept at pre-1999 levels, leads to a 25% increase in average inflows. In the third scenario, maintaining evaporation at pre-1999 levels results in a 9% increase in average inflows. Finally, the fourth scenario, which holds both precipitation and evaporation at pre-1999 levels, results in a 34% increase in average inflows.

Based on the results of this study, agricultural development in the basin is identified as the predominant factor influencing river inflows, although its impact varies across different years. While climatic factors, particularly snow drought, were the main drivers during severe drought years such as 1999–2001 and 2008, agricultural factors had a greater influence on river inflows in the intervening years, with a more pronounced difference compared to climatic factors. Overall, it can be concluded that unsustainable agricultural development and inadequate water resource and demand management have caused a significant reduction in inflows to the lake, with climate change further accelerating both the speed and intensity of this decline.

Based on the findings of this study, three key priorities emerge:

Reduce irrigation demand and leakage losses, as these represent the most impactful and immediate management levers under current conditions.Strengthen snowpack monitoring and seasonal forecasting to improve preparedness for shifting winter conditions and earlier melt patterns.Revise water allocation rules to account for increasingly rain-dominated winters and earlier runoff peaks, ensuring timely and equitable supply for both agricultural and environmental needs.
