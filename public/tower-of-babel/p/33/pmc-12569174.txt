Mapping spatiotemporal variations in lakeside populations for sustainable lake management
Authors: Yuhan Sun, Lumeng Liu, Zhuoen Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLakes provide a range of essential ecosystem services and disservices, with their impacts on human well-being closely tied to the spatial proximity between populations and lakes. However, the extent and dynamics of human exposure to lakes remain poorly understood at large scales. In this study, we present a comprehensive spatiotemporal analysis of lakeside populations (LP) across mainland China from 2000 to 2020, evaluating exposure patterns at national, regional, and provincial levels within multiple distance buffers from lakes. We further disentangle the relative contributions of population growth and lake area changes to LP dynamics using a control-variable approach. We found that (1) In 2020, 33.59 million, 18.04 million, 10.47 million, 7.24 million, and 3.59 million people lived within 2000 m, 1000 m, 500 m, 300 m, and 100 m of a lake, respectively, with the highest concentrations observed in the Eastern Plain and around small lakes. (2) Despite the regionally varied temporal dynamics, LP in China showed an overall increasing trend within 500 m, 1000 m, and 2000 m of lakes, but decreased during some periods within 100 m and 300 m buffers. (3) Between 2000 and 2020, LP changes in 70.37% of the examined provinces were dominantly impacted by lake area changes, particularly in northwest and southwest China. Yet, the main direct influencing factors changed dynamically during different periods. The increasing LP in China creates both opportunities and challenges for sustainable lake management. Introduction

There are globally over 3.4 million lakes exceeding 0.03 square kilometers1, providing various ecosystem services (ES) for humans, including freshwater, flood regulation, and climate regulation2,3. Proximity to lakes can enhance residents’ subjective well-being4, improve mental health5, and reduce suicide risk6. However, living near lakes also increases exposure to flooding7, exemplified by the 2024 dike breach of Dongting Lake which forced the evacuation of approximately 5,000 people8. The benefits and risks associated with lakes depend partly on the distance between humans and lakes, i.e., Lakeside Populations (LP). Thus, quantifying the spatial relationship between humans and lakes is necessary for sustainable lake management.

China has a vast number of lakes, with 2,670 lakes larger than 1 km² covering approximately 0.9% of the country’s land area in 20209,10. Climate change and increasing human activities have posed pressure on lakes11. From 1960 to 2015, although the area of lakes in the Tibetan Plateau, Xinjiang, and Northeast Plain and Mountainous regions increased by over 15%, lakes in the Inner Mongolia Plateau decreased by 22%12. During 1990 and 2015, urban lakes declined by 24.22% mainly influenced by urban sprawl13. Despite increased national focus on ecological management, lakes in China still face multiple challenges, including pollution, ecological degradation, and flooding disasters10,14,15. Investigating the spatial relationship between humans and lakes and their drivers is essential to identify opportunities and risks with lake management in China.

In recent years, there has been growing interest in measuring human proximity to natural landscapes, such as global forests16 and urban green spaces17,18. However, human interaction with lakes and rivers remains less studied, with only a few notable contributions. For instance, Kummu et al.2 found that over 50% of the global population lives within 3 km of freshwater bodies, while only 10% reside more than 10 km away. In Wisconsin, USA, more than half of new homes built since the 1960s are near lakes19. Fang and Jawitz20 found that historically, human settlements clustered around major rivers during the pre-industrial era but began moving away from waterways in the late 19th century. Nevertheless, the spatiotemporal dynamics of lakeside populations in China remain largely unexamined.

Therefore, this study aimed to assess the spatiotemporal dynamics and influencing factors of lakeside populations in mainland China (excluding Hong Kong, Macau, and Taiwan) from 2000 to 2020. Specifically, we asked three questions: (1) Where are lakeside populations concentrated in mainland China? (2) How have lakeside populations changed over the past two decades? (3) What are the primary factors directly influencing lakeside populations? To address these questions, we first quantified the spatiotemporal changes in lakeside populations within varying buffer zones of lakes at national, regional, and provincial scales from 2000 to 2020. We then semi-quantitatively analyzed the relative contributions of lake area and population number changes on lakeside populations, and finally proposed lake management strategies.

Materials and methods

Data sources

Data on lake distribution were derived from Zhang21 that mapped lakes with size larger than 1 km2 based on Landsat images (4215 scenes) during the past 60 years. Based on the geographical features and climatic differences, lakes were classified into six major regions: Tibetan Plateau, Xinjiang, Inner Mongolia Plateau, Northeast Plain and Mountain, Eastern Plain, and Yunnan-Guizhou Plateau12.

Population data were obtained from the WorldPop dataset, which estimates the total number of people per grid cell using a Random Forest model and a global database of administrative unit-based census22. The WorldPop dataset spans 20 years with a spatial resolution of 3 arc seconds (approximately 100 m at the equator) and is frequently used in exposure estimation23,24.

Calculating lakeside populations

Lakeside populations were defined as the number of individuals living within buffer zones of 100 m, 300 m, 500 m, 1000 m, and 2000 m from the lake boundary, reflecting commonly adopted distances in environmental exposure assessments23,24 and spatial accessibility evaluations in urban and environmental research25. These buffer distances represent a range of spatial proximities relevant to human–lake interactions, from visual proximity (i.e., 100 m), to neighborhood or block-scale distance (i.e., 500 m), and broader accessible ranges (i.e., 1000 m)26. We further calculated lakeside populations of differently sized lakes. Modified from Ma et al.27, we categorized lakes into three types: small lakes (< 10 km2), medium lakes (10–100 km2), and large lakes (> 100 km2).

Note that due to differences in data sources of populations and lakes, spatial misalignment may occur during their overlay, leading to some population grid centers near the lake shoreline being mistakenly classified as located within the lake. Directly excluding these populations could underestimate the true lakeside population (Fig. S1). To address this, we conducted a comparative analysis to evaluate the differences between including and excluding these populations.

Identifying major factors directly influencing lakeside populations

Lakeside populations are indirectly affected by climate change and socioeconomic development, but directly affected by changes in lake area and population number. In this study, we focused on the direct drivers of LP and identified the key influencing factors using the method proposed by Newton et al.16. For each period (e.g., 2000–2020), we first plotted the observed changes in LP (grey lines) and the assumed changes in LP while holding either population size (red dashed lines) or lake area (purple dashed lines) constant at 2000 values (Fig. 1). We then calculated the angles between the observed and assumed changes to determine the effects of population and lake area changes on LP. The smaller the angles, the greater the impacts.

Conceptual schematic of the approach used to evaluate the relative impacts of lake area and population number changes on LP between 2000 and 2020. Grey lines represent the observed changes in LP, while red and purple lines illustrate the assumed changes in LP when holding either population or lake area constant at their 2000 values. (a) The observed changes in LP from 2000 to 2020. (b) The observed changes in LP closely align with the assumed changes when population is held constant at the 2000 value, indicating that LP changes are dominantly influenced by changes in lake size. (c) The observed changes in LP closely align with the assumed changes when lake size is held constant at the 2000 value, indicating that LP changes are dominantly influenced by changes in population numbers. (d) Changes in lake area and population number both influence LP, but LP values under constant population are closer to observed values than those under constant lake area (i.e., the ratio of angles for scenarios with constant population and constant lake area is smaller than 1), suggesting that lake area changes contribute relatively more to LP variations than population changes.

To quantitatively compare the relative impacts of lake and population changes, we calculated the ratio of the angles for scenarios with constant population number and constant lake area (Fig. 1d). A ratio greater than 1 indicates that LP changes were dominated by population changes, while a ratio less than 1 indicates that lake area changes dominate. For example, in Fig. 1d, LP values under constant population are closer to observed values than those under constant lake area (angle ratio < 1), indicating lake area change as the dominant factor.

Note that all angles were calculated using the arctangent of the ratio between the change in lakeside population (ΔLP) and the change in year (ΔYear). However, directly computing geometric angles from the raw data coordinates is inappropriate, as the numerical values of population are significantly larger than those of year. This imbalance skews the angles toward 90 degrees, failing to accurately represent the actual visual trends depicted in the chart. To address this, we transformed the data coordinates into display coordinates using the standard “window-to-viewport” transformation relation from computer graphics, ensuring that the computed angles align with the visual trajectories perceived by the viewer.

Results

Spatiotemporal changes of LP from 2000 to 2020

In 2020, 33.59 million, 18.04 million,10.47 million, 7.24 million, and 3.59 million people in China lived within 2000 m, 1000 m, 500 m, 300 m, and 100 m of a lake, respectively (Table S1, Fig. 2). When only considering people around lakes, 31.51 million, 16.78 million, 8.67 million, 5.77 million, and 2.07 million people lived within 2000 m, 1000 m, 500 m, 300 m, and 100 m of a lake, respectively (Table S1, Fig. S2). The temporal dynamics of LP within different buffer zones varied (Table S1, Fig. 2). From 2000 to 2005, LP increased slightly across all zones. Between 2005 and 2010, LP declined in the 100 m buffer zones but increased in the remaining zones. From 2010 to 2015, LP decreased within the 100 m and 300 m of lakes, whereas it increased in the other buffer zones. From 2015 to 2020, LP increased across all buffer zones. Overall, LP within 500 m,1000 m, and 2000 m of lakes in China showed an upward trend during the past 20 years.

The number of lakeside population (LP) living within 2000 m, 1000 m, 500 m, 300 m, and 100 m of a lake during 2000 and 2020 in China.

In 2020, the number of LP within 500 m of lakes was higher in the Eastern Plain region, where population density around lakes was relatively high (Fig. 3). Xinjiang and Inner Mongolia Plateau showed relatively small number of LP, corresponding to fewer lakes and lower population densities. Among the provinces, Hubei, Jiangsu, Jiangxi, Anhui, and Hunan showed the largest numbers of LP within 500 m of lakes, cumulatively accounting for 74.88% of the national total in 2020 (Figs. 3 and 4). In contrast, Tianjin, Qinghai, and Tibet had relatively smaller lakeside populations, contributing only 0.019%, 0.073%, and 0.146% of the total, respectively.

Provincial-level lakeside populations over time (within 500 m). The numbers below the names of the provinces represent the proportions of LP to the total population in each province during 2000 and 2020. Fujian, Guangxi, Hainan, and Chongqing only have lakes < 1.0 km2, and thus were not shown here.

Between 2000 and 2020, the temporal trends of LP within 500 m of lakes differed across provinces (Fig. 4). LP generally showed an increasing trend in most provinces, e.g., Guizhou, Ningxia, Xinjiang, and Inner Mongolia. In provinces such as Beijing, Qinghai and Hebei, LP first decreased and then increased. While in provinces like Yunnan and Tianjin, LP remained relatively stable over time. With increasing absolute number of LP, the proportion of LP to China’s total population also increased. During 2000 and 2020, the proportion increased from 0.66% to 0.74% in the 500 m buffer zone, and increased from 2.08% to 2.38% in the 2000 m buffer zone.

Spatiotemporal pattern of LP within different sizes of lakes

The number of small lakes in China is the largest, ranging from approximately 1500 to 2000, while the number of large lakes is the smallest, with fewer than 200 (Fig. 5a). During the past two decades, the number of small and medium lakes decreased first but increased from 2010, while the number of large lakes increased first and declined from 2005. Small lakes predominated in all regions (Fig. 5c).

The number of LP within 500 m around small lakes was the largest among all lake size categories (Fig. 5b). The number of LP around small lakes increased from 2000 to 2010, decreased between 2010 and 2015, while it increased during 2015 and 2020. LP around medium and large lakes both dramatically increased during 2010 and 2015. In almost all regions except for the Yunnan-Guizhou Plateau region, the number of LP around small lakes was the largest (Fig. 5d).

Relative contribution of lake and population changes on LP

The angles between the observed and assumed LP values illustrate the relative influences of population and lake changes (Fig. 6). For example, in Beijing, Inner Mongolia, Guizhou, and Tibet, LP changes between 2000 and 2005 were jointly affected by both lake and population changes. However, lake changes were the primary drivers of LP changes in Tibet and Inner Mongolia (Fig. 6b,d), whereas population changes played a dominant role in Beijing and Guizhou (Fig. 6a,c).

Drivers of LP changes in Beijing (a), Inner Mongolia (b), Guizhou (c), and Tibet (d) during 2000 and 2005. The red dashed lines represent the estimated changes in LP assuming only lake dynamics change, with population held constant at 2000 levels. Similarly, the purple dashed lines represent the estimated changes in LP assuming only population dynamics change, with lake conditions remained at 2000 levels.

Overall, 70.37% of the provinces had an angle ratio of less than 1 between 2000 and 2020, indicating that LP changes in these provinces were mainly influenced by lake changes (Fig. 7). Specifically, LP changes in provinces located in northeast and northwest China (e.g., Liaoning, Jilin, Gansu, and Qinghai) were primarily driven by lake changes, while those in the Yangtze River Delta region (e.g., Shanghai, Jiangsu, Zhejiang) were mainly influenced by population changes.

Direct drivers of LP changes in each province (2000–2020). Lake change angle was calculated as the angle between the observed and assumed LP that only changed lakes, and population change angle was calculated as the angle between the observed and assumed LP that only changed populations. A ratio greater than 1 indicates that LP changes were dominated by population changes, while a ratio less than 1 indicates that lake area changes dominate.

Across the four subperiods, most provinces were primarily affected by lake changes (Fig. 8). The proportion of provinces dominated by lake influences was 62.96% during both 2000–2005 and 2005–2010, 55.56% during 2010–2015, and 88.89% during 2015–2020. Provinces such as Guizhou and Shanghai transitioned from being population-dominated to lake-dominated, while Guangdong shifted from being lake-dominated to population-dominated.

Discussion

Where are lakeside population concentrated in China?

We found that approximately 3.59 million people lived within 100 m of a lake in 2020, a figure comparable to the total population of Tibet28. Meanwhile, 33.59 million people resided within 2 km of a lake, which is equivalent to the total population of Shanxi in 202028. Lakeside populations are predominantly concentrated in the Eastern Plain, where both lakes and human population are densely distributed (Fig. 3). This high concentration is largely driven by the historical development of agricultural, industrial, and urban centers near lakes in this region, which offer accessible water resources for irrigation, drinking, and economic activities. Although Tibetan Plateau also had a vast area of lakes (Fig. 3a), the small lakeside population is possibly caused by the smallest population (Fig. 3b) and inconvenient lake accessibility29. However, compared with Tibetan Plateau and Yunnan-Guizhou Plateau, lakes in Eastern Plain are shallow and turbid30, resulting in lower ecological benefits (e.g., diminished water quality and limited biodiversity) despite the huge beneficiaries.

Associated with the largest number of small lakes, most lakeside populations were concentrated around small lakes, except for the Yunnan-Guizhou Plateau (Fig. 5). This is possibly due to the widespread availability and accessibility of small lakes, which often serve as essential resources for nearby communities1,31. Yet, this high population density imposes significant pressures on water quality and increases the risk of small lake shrinkage from overuse or climate change. Although the number of large lakes was the smallest, populations around large lakes were comparable to those of medium lakes (Fig. 5). This may be due to their large ecological importance and human health benefits. For example, Theeuwes et al.32 suggested that larger waterbodies had higher cooling effects than equally distributed small water bodies; Pearson33 showed that living in close proximity to large, but not small lakes, is associated with improved mental health outcomes.

How did lakeside population change during the past two decades?

We found that the temporal trends of lakeside populations vary across buffer sizes (Fig. 2). This is likely because different buffer zones represent varying degrees of human-water interaction. For example, smaller buffers (e.g., 100 m) mainly capture residents living close to the lake shore, who are more directly affected by local environmental conditions, scenic quality, and protective policies, resulting in greater population fluctuations. In contrast, larger buffers (e.g., 2000 m) cover broader areas that may include diverse urban or rural functional zones, where population changes are influenced more by macro-level factors such as urban expansion and transportation development.

Overall, lakeside populations have grown over the past 20 years, with notable increases in areas farther from lakes (Fig. 2). A longitudinal study by Fang and Jawitz20 found that while human settlements in pre-industrial periods clustered near rivers, populations have moved farther from rivers since 1870. These contrasting patterns highlight differences in urbanization stages in China and the United States, as well as the evolving roles of lakes and rivers in shaping settlement dynamics over time. The increase in LP in China reflects both population growth and greater attractions of the ecological and economic value of lakes34. In earlier times, people tended to settle near lakes to access provisioning services such as fish and water. Over time, however, regulating and cultural services have become more significant factors influencing settlement patterns. By 2020, 15 of China’s 45 national-level tourism and vacation zones were located around lakes, emphasizing their economic and cultural importance10. Policies such as the “Lake Chief System” can enhance water quality and the surrounding environment, potentially increasing the residential and recreational appeal of lakeside areas and attracting new populations35.

Nevertheless, we found that population living within short distances of lakes (e.g., 100 m and 300 m) has declined between 2005 and 2015 (Fig. 2), which is possibly driven by factors such as increasing disasters like flooding10, heavy metal pollution36, and lake-related disservices such as unpleasant odors or disease risks37. People tended to settle farther away to avoid negative ES, while they can also easily obtain benefits with convenient transportation. Strict lake management regulations have also contributed to this decline by restricting human settlement and activities in surrounding areas. For example, the implementation of “Ecological Red Lines” strictly prohibits or limits construction in ecologically sensitive zones, including many lakeside areas38.

The dynamics of lakeside populations varied across regions. In the arid Xinjiang and Inner Mongolia Plateau, lakeside populations have increased, driven by growing water demands for mining and agriculture (Fig. 4). In contrast, lakeside populations in Hunan and Jilin declined sharply (Fig. 4). Between 2005 and 2010, this decline was primarily due to changes in lake conditions, particularly in Jilin, where significant water level reductions were observed. From 2010 to 2015, broader demographic trends, including regional population decline, further contributed to this decrease39,40. Similarly, populations around Dongting Lake in Hunan steadily declined from 2010 to 2020, driven by shrinking lake size and migration-related population shifts41.

Implications for policy making

Over the past 20 years, lakeside populations have generally increased (Fig. 2), offering communities greater access to lake ecosystem services. Improved transportation has further enhanced access by facilitating trade, leisure, and resource distribution. Closer proximity to lakes can benefit human mental and physical health4,5,33 and promote environmentally friendly behavior42,43. However, this growth also poses risks, including increased exposure to floods7, pollution44 and overuse of resources like fish and water45. Therefore, it is crucial for policies to simultaneously focus on sustainable resource management, enhance disaster risk reduction measures, and promote community engagement to ensure effective conservation of lake ecosystems.

Our study suggests that sustainable lake management requires regional-specific strategies. In the densely populated Eastern Plain lake region, with its intensive agriculture and industry, efforts should prioritize protecting ecosystems, improving water quality, and ensuring fair access to ecosystem services. Strengthening levees and flood prevention measures is also vital to safeguard communities. Notably, as urbanization progresses, the water quality of lakes has shown improvement in some areas46, further enhancing their capacity to provide ecosystem services and benefits for local residents. In contrast, in regions where changes in lake area are the primary driving force—such as Qinghai and Inner Mongolia—policy efforts should focus on environmental restoration, integrated water resource management, and society adaptation. In western regions like Tibet, where lakes are expanding due to climate change-induced glacial melt47, management should aim to mitigate the impacts of lake expansion on surrounding communities and infrastructure by improving transportation systems, enhancing disaster preparedness, and ensuring sustainable access to lake ecosystem services.

Limitations and future prospects

While our study provides a comprehensive assessment of LP, improvements can be made in several aspects. First, lakeside residents may differ from actual users48, highlighting the need to measure actual exposure to blue space. Second, employing a control-variable approach (angle ratio), we focused on the relative contributions of direct drivers—lake area change and population dynamics—to LP. However, LP dynamics are also shaped by broader macro-level forces such as climate change and regional economic activities. Future research could utilize structural equation modeling and panel regression to elucidate the multifaceted causal pathways connecting socioeconomic development, climate change, environmental conditions, and LP. Third, although the WorldPop dataset outperforms several other population datasets (e.g., GWP, GRUMP, and LandScan), it still underestimates rural populations by nearly 50% compared to reported Figures49, leading to lower LP estimations in the sparsely populated southwestern provinces. Finally, a more detailed classification of lakes—such as distinguishing urban lakes and protected area lakes—would facilitate a better understanding of lakeside populations. Despite these limitations, by identifying where and how human-lake interactions are evolving, our findings lay the groundwork for more targeted, evidence-based interventions in lake governance and spatial planning.

Conclusions

This study presents one of the first comprehensive assessments of the spatial interplay between human populations and lakes across China. We revealed that by 2020, approximately 3.59 million people resided within 100 m of a lake—comparable to the population of Tibet. Lakeside populations were predominantly concentrated in the Eastern Plain and around smaller lakes. Over the past two decades, the overall trend has been an expansion of lakeside populations, particularly at greater distances from lakes. Through a control variable approach, we demonstrate that lake size dynamics, rather than population growth, have been the principal force shaping the distribution of lakeside populations. The rise in lakeside populations brings both challenges and opportunities, underscoring the need for adaptive, region-specific frameworks for sustainable lake management.
