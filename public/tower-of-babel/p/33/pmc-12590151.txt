Nanodelivery of panobinostat induces cell cycle arrest and apoptosis to suppress hepatocellular carcinoma growth in mice
Authors: Yutong Li, Dong Li, Junjun Chen, Yuqiong Gao, Xingze Zou, Huan Li, Zhuo Yu, Yifang Zou, Jianfeng Guo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), the predominant subtype of primary liver cancer, remains as a globally lethal malignancy with limited therapeutic options. Despite therapeutic efficacy of small-molecule inhibitors, the majority of HCC patients are still incurable due to suboptimal response rate and serious systemic toxicity. Panobinostat (PANO), a potent histone deacetylase (HDAC) small-molecule inhibitor, has established clinical efficacy in hematological malignancies. However, clinical application of PANO in solid tumors (e.g., HCC) is significantly hindered by in vivo delivery barriers. In the present study, we show that PANO triggers cell cycle arrest and apoptosis in HCC cells, demonstrating its therapeutic potential for this malignancy. We also develop a nanocarrier using folic acid-targeted polyethylene glycol-modified poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) to facilitate in vitro and in vivo administration of PANO. In mice with orthotopically allografted HCC, the resultant PANO-loaded nanoformulation achieves potent antitumor efficacy, characterized by significant tumor cell death, remarkable tumor regression, and prolonged animal survival. These findings demonstrate a promising regimen based on nanodelivery of PANO for HCC therapy.Keywords: Liver cancer, Targeted therapy, Epigenetic modulation, Copolymer, Nanoformulation

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remains the leading cause of cancer-related death, giving rise to more than 560,000 mortality in 2022 worldwide [1]. This malignancy is predominantly diagnosed at intermediate or advanced stages, requiring systemic therapeutic interventions. Improved understanding in molecular pathogenesis of HCC profoundly accelerates the development of small-molecule inhibitors [2]. They are a class of low molecular weight compounds that selectively bind oncogenic proteins and inhibit their biological activities. Recently, several tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), represented by Sorafenib, Regorafenib, and Lenvatinib, have demonstrated clinical efficacy in HCC. Despite being the only clinically approved small-molecule inhibitors for HCC, TKIs only offer a modest survival benefit (median overall survival improvement of approximately 3 months) and inevitably develop therapeutic resistance within half a year [3].

As core components of chromatin, histone proteins serve as fundamental regulators of gene expression through epigenetic modifications (e.g., acetylation, methylation, and phosphorylation), which dynamically orchestrate chromatin architecture and transcriptional activity. Histone deacetylases (HDACs), a class of evolutionarily conserved enzymes, catalyze the removal of acetyl groups from lysine residues on histones, resulting in chromatin condensation and transcriptional inhibition [4]. Accumulating evidence demonstrates that HDAC overexpression in cancer cells promotes oncogenesis by epigenetically silencing tumor suppressor genes [5]. HDAC inhibitors reactivate tumor suppressor genes by achieving site-specific histone hyperacetylation, thereby disrupting repressive chromatin architecture and enabling transcriptional machinery recruitment [6]. This epigenetic remodeling not only restores tumor suppressor gene expression but also triggers downstream effects including cell cycle arrest and apoptosis. Among HDAC inhibitors, panobinostat (PANO; marketed under the brand name of Farydak®) represents a clinically validated therapeutic agent approved for multiple myeloma and lymphoma [7]. However, therapeutic impact of this drug in solid malignancies remains limited. This discrepancy is due possibly to the fact that Farydak®, an oral capsule formulation, results in short circulatory half-life, poor tumor distribution, and off-target toxicity, when applied in tumor malignancies.

Nanosystems generally refer to engineered structure or device operating at nanoscale, in order to generate multiple functions across different fields such as materials science, medicine, and biotechnology. Over the past decades, a variety of nano drug delivery carriers have been developed for pharmaceutical applications [8]. Poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA), a copolymer of lactic and glycolic acids, is widely utilized to develop drug nanocarriers [9,10]. The modification with polyethylene glycol (PEG) on nanoparticle (NP) surface can enhance circulatory half-life of drug nanocarriers [11]. The functionalization with folic acid (FA) at distal end of PEG can enable active targeting of HCC cells that highly express folate receptor (FR) [12]. In this study, PLGA-PEG-FA nanocarrier was prepared and used to promote in vitro and in vivo PANO administration to HCC (Fig. 1). The obtained PANO-loaded nanoformulation (T-NP.PANO) significantly promoted circulatory half-life, tumor accumulation, and HCC cell targeting. As a result, T-NP.PANO halted tumor cell cycle and triggered tumor apoptosis, leading to tumor regression and survival extension in orthotopically allografted HCC murine model.

PLGA-PEG-FA NP achieves HCC cell-specific delivery of PANO for triggering cell cycle arrest and apoptosis.

Materials and methods

Materials

PANO (CAS No. 404950-80-7; C21H23N3O2, Mw = 349.43 g/mol) was bought from Macklin (Shanghai, China). PLGA40k-PEG2k-FA (LA:GA = 50:50; referred as to PLGA-PEG-FA) and PLGA40k-PEG2k methyl ether (LA:GA = 50:50; referred as to PLGA-PEG) were sourced from Qiyue Biology (Suzhou, China). Additional reagents, unless otherwise specified, were obtained at Sigma-Aldrich.

Cell culture

Mouse HCC cell line H22 was maintained in RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10 % of fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1 % of streptomycin and penicillin. Luciferase-expressing H22 (H22-luc) was maintained in above-mentioned medium containing 1 μg/mL of puromycin. Human HCC cell line HepG2 was maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10 % of FBS and 1 % of streptomycin and penicillin. These cells were incubated at 37 °C, 5 % of CO2, and 95 % of relative humidity.

In vitro anti-HCC effects of PANO

Cell viability was estimated using CCK-8 assay [13]. Briefly, H22 and HepG2 were seeded at 6 × 103 cells per well in 96-well plate, respectively. Following half a day incubation, two cell lines were added with DMSO (1 % (v/v) used as final concentration, the same below) and PANO ([c] = 1, 10, 20, 40, 80, and 150 nmol/L in DMSO; 1 % (v/v) used as final concentration of DMSO, the same below). After one day incubation, cells were added with CCK-8 reagent (Solarbio, China). After 1 h incubation at 37 °C, absorbances at 450 nm were obtained, and cell viabilities and IC50 values were determined using GraphPad Prism.

Cell cycle phases were determined using DNA Quantitation Kit (Solarbio) with BD FACSCalibur flow cytometry [14]. Briefly, two cell lines were seeded at 5 × 105 cells per well in 6-well plate, respectively. After half a day incubation, cells were added with DMSO and 9 nmol/L of PANO, respectively. After 12 h or 24 h incubation, cells were incubated with pre-chilled 70 % of ethanol for 2 h. According to the manufacturer instruction, cells were processed with RNaseA and propidium iodide (PI) before flow cytometric analysis.

Apoptotic cell populations were quantified using Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Kit (YEASEN, China) with flow cytometry [15]. Briefly, two cell lines were seeded at 5 × 105 cells per well in 6-well plate, respectively. After half a day's incubation, cells were added with DMSO and 9 nmol/L of PANO. After 12 h or 24 h incubation, according to the manufacturer instruction, cells were treated with PI and Annexin V-FITC prior to flow cytometric analysis.

Proteins associated with cell cycle and apoptosis were detected using western blotting assay [16]. Briefly, two cell lines were seeded at 5 × 105 cells per well in 6-well plate, respectively. After half a day incubation, cells were added with DMSO and 9 nmol/L of PANO. After one day incubation, proteins were quantified with BCA Assay Kit (Beyotime, China), and 40 μg of proteins per sample were loaded into 10 % of SDS-PAGE gels for electrophoresis (80–120 V). After 1 h, separated proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes for 90 min at 200 mA. Membranes were then incubated using 5 % of BSA for one and a half hours, treated with primary antibodies (Table S1) overnight, and added with secondary antibodies (Table S1) for 2 h. Protein bands were visualized with the automatic chemiluminescence and fluorescence image system and quantified with ImageJ software.

Nanoformulation preparation and physicochemical characterization

Nanoformulations were prepared using “oil-in-water” single emulsion method, as previously described [17]. In this study, 1 mg of PANO, 1.7 mg of PLGA-PEG-FA, and 8.3 mg of PLGA-PEG were mixed in organic phase (200 μL), which was then emulsified into aqueous phase (5 mL; it contained 2 % (w/v) of sodium cholate). Subsequently, the mixture was sonicated at 150 w power for 10 min and stirred for 4–8 h. The mixture was then transferred to 10 kDa MWCO ultrafiltration tubes and centrifuged at 5000 g for 20 min, aiming for yielding FA-targeted PEGylated PANO-loaded NP (short for targeted nanoformulation or T-NP.PANO in this study). Afterwards, T-NP.PANO was freeze-dried, rehydrated using deionized water, and stored for subsequent experiments. Non-targeted PEGylated PANO-loaded NP (short for non-targeted nanoformulation or NP.PANO in this study) was prepared as mentioned above, except that 10 mg of PLGA-PEG (without PLGA-PEG-FA) were used in organic phase.

Particle morphology and size were characterized using transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEM1230). Particle size, surface charge, and particle stability were determined using Malvern Nano-ZS. High performance liquid chromatography (HPLC; Shimadzu) was utilized to estimate drug concentrations, as follows: C18 column, 25 °C, λ = 254 nm; water (it contained 0.1 % of phosphoric acid) and acetonitrile (v/v = 70:30) were used to prepare for mobile phase). Drug encapsulation was estimated using the equation: (loaded drug mass/fed drug mass) × 100 %. Drug loading was determined using the equation: (loaded drug mass/nanoformulation mass) × 100 %.

Additionally, 5 mL of nanoformulation (it contained 1 mg of PANO) were transferred into a 1K MWCO dialysis bag and immersed in 50 mL of release medium (it was prepared using 1 × PBS containing 0.5 % (v/v) of Tween-80; pH values were adjusted at 5.5 and 7.4, respectively). This system was incubated at 37 °C with continuous stirring. To assess pH-sensitive drug release, 2.5 mL of release medium were taken, and the same volume of fresh release medium was added back, at pre-determined time points. PANO concentrations were quantified as above described.

In vitro anti-HCC effects of targeted nanoformulation

H22 was seeded at 1 × 105 cells per well in a 6-well plate. After half a day incubation, cells were treated with PBS, PANO (5 nmol/L), non-targeted nanoformulation (containing 5 nmol/L of PANO; this concentration was the same below; 0.05 % (w/w) of rhodamine), or targeted nanoformulation (containing 5 nmol/L of PANO; this concentration was the same below; 0.05 % (w/w) of rhodamine). After 4 h incubation, fluorescent cell population was quantified using flow cytometric assay.

H22 was seeded at 6 × 103 cells per well in 96-well plate. Following half a day incubation, cells were treated with PBS, PANO, NP.PANO, or T-NP.PANO. After one day incubation, cell death was assessed as above mentioned using CCK-8 assay.

H22 was seeded at 5 × 105 cells per well in 6-well plate. After half a day incubation, cells were treated with PBS, PANO, NP.PANO, or T-NP.PANO for one day. Cell cycle was determined as above mentioned using DNA Quantitation Kit with flow cytometry. Apoptosis was determined as above mentioned using Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Kit with flow cytometry. Proteins linked to cell cycle and apoptosis were detected as above mentioned using western blotting assay.

In vivo safety, delivery, and therapy of targeted nanoformulation

In this study, five-week-old male BALB/c mice were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology. Experiments were permitted by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Jilin University and performed following the National Research Council's Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Healthy mice were intravenously (i.v.) injected with non-formulated PANO (5, 10, and 15 mg/kg) or targeted nanoformulation (it contained 15 mg/kg of PANO) on Day 1, 3, 5, 7, 9 and 11. Animals were regularly weighted throughout the study (n = 6). Histopathology was determined at Day 30 using hematoxylin/eosin (H&E) staining assay. Hematological samples were processed and analyzed at Day 30 to determine blood, liver, and kidney toxicities (n = 3) (Wuhan Servicebio, China).

H22-luc tumor-bearing mice were established as previously reported [18]. Briefly, 5 × 105 of H22-luc cells were implanted into hepatic lobe of each mouse, 95 μL of fluorescein were intraperitoneally injected to each mouse, and luminescent intensity was analyzed using Perkin Elmer IVIS® device to monitor tumor growth. As luminescent intensities were elevated to 3 × 108 to 5 × 108 p/s/cm2/sr (n = 4), PANO (5 mg/kg) or targeted nanoformulation (containing 5 mg/kg of PANO and 0.05 % (w/w) of DiD) were i.v. administrated once to mice. Subsequently, serum drug concentrations were quantified by HPLC, with half-life calculated using PKSolver software. In a separate experiment, fluorescent intensities in tumors and major organs were measured at 12 h after i.v. injection to determine tissue distribution using IVIS® device.

In addition, when luminescent intensities reached values as above described, HCC mice were i.v. administrated with one of following treatments every two days for a total of six times: PBS, PANO (5 mg/kg), NP.PANO (it contained 5 mg/kg of PANO), or T-NP.PANO (it contained 5 mg/kg of PANO). HCC development was regularly monitored using Perkin Elmer IVIS® device. In a separate experiment, tumor tissues were homogenized five days after six injections, and supernatants were subjected to western blotting assay.

Statistical analysis

Results were illustrated as average ± standard deviation and interpreted using GraphPad Prism. Statistical comparison of two groups was analyzed with Student's t-test. Statistical comparison of three or more groups was analyzed with one-way ANOVA, Tukey's multiple comparisons. Statistical comparison in drug release, animal weight, half-life, and in vivo anti-HCC studies was analyzed with two-way ANOVA, Tukey's multiple comparisons. Animal survival was analyzed with log-rank test. No significance (ns), ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, and ∗∗∗p < 0.001 were presented in this study.

Results and discussion

Cell cycle arrest and apoptosis caused by PANO

CCK-8 results indicate that half-maximal inhibitory concentrations (IC50) of PANO for mouse (H22) and human (HepG2) HCC cell lines were 9 and 9.4 nmol/L, respectively (Fig. 2A). Flow cytometric results demonstrate that PANO induced significant G0/G1 phase arrest (50 % and 79 % for H22 at 12 h and 24 h, respectively, p < 0.01; 53 % and 57 % for HepG2 at 12 h and 24 h, respectively, p < 0.05) as comparison with DMSO (40 % for H22 at 24 h; 45 % for HepG2 at 24 h) (Fig. 2B). Concomitantly, significantly less cell population in S phase was observed following PANO treatment (18 % and 16 % for H22 at 12 h and 24 h, respectively; 30 % and 24 % for HepG2 at 12 h and 24 h, respectively) as comparison with DMSO (39 % for H22 at 24 h; 45 % for HepG2 at 24 h) (Fig. 2B). Western blotting results illustrate that p16 and p21 protein levels were significantly (p < 0.01) upregulated by PANO ([c] = 9 nmol/L, the same below) in two cell lines (Fig. 2C).

In vitro cytotoxicity and cell cycle arrest achieved by PANO. (A) H22 and HepG2 cell viability (%) achieved by PANO at different concentrations for 24 h (n = 3). (B) H22 and HepG2 cell cycle arrest caused by PANO (9 nmol/L) for 12 h and 24 h (n = 3). (C) Expression of p16 and p21 in H22 and HepG2 cells achieved by PANO (9 nmol/L) for 24 h (n = 3).

Flow cytometric results show that PANO profoundly (p < 0.001) triggered apoptosis (17 % and 37 % for H22 at 12 h and 24 h, respectively; 16 % and 48 % for HepG2 at 12 h and 24 h, respectively) as comparison with DMSO (6 % for H22 at 24 h; 6 % for HepG2 at 24 h) (Fig. 3A). Western blotting results present that PANO remarkably (p < 0.05 and p < 0.01) downregulated Bcl-2 expression and upregulated Bax expression, in comparison with DMSO in two cell lines (Fig. 3B). PANO also significantly (p < 0.01 and p < 0.05) augmented activities of Caspases 3 and 9 as comparison with DMSO in two cell lines (Fig. 3B).

Over the past decades, small-molecule inhibitors designed to target cancer hallmarks, including signaling pathways (e.g., proliferation, metabolism, and apoptosis), efflux pumps, and ion channels, have shown therapeutic potential across malignancies. TKIs, represented by Sorafenib, Regorafenib, and Lenvatinib, remain the only viable option of small-molecule inhibitors for intermediate or advanced HCC. However, their clinical utility is largely constrained by low therapeutic efficacy and high adverse effects [19]. These limitations underscore urgent need to advance more small-molecule inhibitors into clinical application for HCC.

DNA and its associated proteins (e.g., histones) assemble into a highly organized structure termed the nucleosome, which serves as fundamental unit of chromatin. In general, epigenetics refers as a subject to study heritable alterations in gene expression that arise from chemical modifications of DNA and histones rather than DNA sequence changes. Increasing knowledge of epigenetic dysregulation associated with human pathologies revolutionizes disease diagnosis, risk stratification, and drug development [20,21]. Among these, two main epigenetic processes namely DNA methylation and histone acetylation/deacetylation are frequently aberrant in cancer cells, serving as critical drivers of tumorigenesis [22]. HDACs, a class of specialized enzymes, exhibit high expression in cancers. The abnormal activity of HDACs leads to the inhibition of tumor suppressor genes through histone hypoacetylation, thereby resulting in malignant transformation, proliferation, and metastasis [23]. There is evidence that pharmacological inhibition of HDACs restores histone acetylation levels, resulting in tumor suppressor gene reactivation. This epigenetic reprogramming triggers cell cycle arrest and apoptosis, positioning HDAC inhibitors as a cornerstone in cancer therapeutics [24,25].

PANO (Farydak®), one of the most successful HDAC small-molecule inhibitors, has achieved clinical success in hematological cancers including relapsed/refractory multiple myeloma and peripheral T-cell lymphoma. Emerging evidence suggests that PANO may also benefit for solid tumors, either as monotherapy or in combination with other therapeutics [26]. In the present study, in vitro anti-HCC effects of PANO were confirmed in murine and human HCC cell lines (Fig. 2, Fig. 3). Mechanistically, PANO blocked cell cycle at G0/G1 phases, concomitant with significant upregulation of p16 and p21 expression (Fig. 2). These tumor suppressor proteins function as pivotal regulators of cell cycle by inhibiting cyclin-dependent kinases, thereby enforcing cell proliferation suppression [27]. In addition, PANO treatment triggered apoptosis by achieving the downregulation of Bcl-2, the upregulation of Bax, and the activation of Caspases 3 and 9 (Fig. 3). Bcl-2 and Bax function antagonistically as anti-apoptotic and pro-apoptotic factors, respectively [28]. Caspases 3 and 9, two important downstream molecules of Bcl-2 and Bax, have been confirmed as pro-apoptotic factors [29]. Collectively, these findings indicate that PANO can trigger cancer cell cycle blockade and apoptosis, demonstrating its promise for HCC targeted therapy.

Physicochemical properties of targeted nanoformulation

T-NP.PANO was prepared by forming “oil-in-water” single emulsion (Fig. 4A), exhibiting a spherical nanostructure as confirmed using TEM (Fig. 4B). T-NP.PANO demonstrated favorable physicochemical profiles with 82 % of encapsulation efficiency (EE), 7 % of loading capacity (LC), 159 nm of particle size, 0.192 of polymer dispersity index (PDI), and −5 mV of surface charge (Fig. 4C). When incubated at 4 °C for seven days or after freeze-drying and rehydration, particle size of T-NP.PANO was not dramatically changed as compared to freshly prepared nanoformulation (Fig. 4D). Additionally, 8 % of loaded PANO were released within half an hour in neutral medium, whereas 30 % of loaded PANO were released within half an hour in acidic medium (Fig. 4E). After one day incubation, 35 % of loaded PANO were released in neutral medium, while 93 % of drug were released in acidic medium (p < 0.05) (Fig. 4E). This pH-sensitive drug release can be attributed to PLGA-associated degradation at acidic environments [30]. Non-targeted nanoformulation (NP.PANO) showed physicochemical properties similar with those of T-NP.PANO as described above.

(D) Particle size of T-NP.PANO at different conditions (n = 4). (D) Drug release at pH 5.5 and pH 7.4 from T-NP.PANO (n = 3).

The majority of HDAC inhibitors demonstrate remarkably reduced efficacy in solid tumors as compared to their potent activity against hematologic malignancies. This disparity can be explained by in vivo delivery challenges, including short circulatory half-life, inefficient tumor accumulation, and non-specific cell targeting. Indeed, clinical efficacy of PANO as oral capsule formulation remains limited in solid tumors, due primarily to the aforementioned delivery challenges. To fulfill therapeutic potential of PANO in HCC, the development of safe, effective, and tumor-specific delivery strategies is urgently needed.

Nanosystems hold significant promise for pharmaceutical applications in disease diagnosis, treatment, and prevention. Recent advances in nano drug delivery systems have opened new avenues for treating solid tumors. NPs modified with stabilizing materials exhibit enhanced circulatory stability following i.v. administration, thereby improving pharmacokinetic characteristics of encapsulated drugs [31]. NPs with particle size below 500 nm penetrate through discontinuous tumor blood vessels during blood circulation [32]. NPs decorated with targeting ligands selectively bind surface receptors or antigens on cancer cells, enabling endocytosis-mediated uptake [33]. NPs on their own or modified with stimulus-responsive groups can be triggered by endogenous or exogenous stimuli to control drug release in cancer cells [34]. Collectively, these properties demonstrate great potential of multifunctional nanocarriers to facilitate HCC delivery of PANO.

Over the past decades, block copolymers based on precisely engineered architectures and functionalized side chains have been developed for pharmaceutical applications [[35], [36], [37], [38], [39]]. As one of the most successful block copolymers, PLGA has been used as a versatile platform for therapeutic delivery and tumor treatment [[40], [41], [42]], due to biocompatible and biodegradable feature, functionalized structure, and controlled drug release [[43], [44], [45], [46]]. To date, no study has investigated therapeutic efficacy of PANO-loaded PLGA-based NPs in HCC. In this study, we developed T-NP.PANO using “oil-in-water” single emulsion method. T-NP.PANO exhibited favorable physicochemical features (Fig. 4), including particle size, surface charge, particle stability, and pH-sensitive drug release [[47], [48], [49]]. These findings provide preliminary evidence supporting therapeutic potential of targeted nanoformulation in HCC, which will be evaluated in following sections.

In vitro anti-HCC effects of targeted nanoformulation

Flow cytometric results reveal that cellular uptake of T-NP.PANO in H22 cells (they are known to overexpress FR [50]) was markedly (p < 0.05) enhanced when compared to NP.PANO (Fig. 5A). In contrast, no remarkable difference in cellular uptake was observed between NP.PANO and T-NP.PANO in A549 cells that exhibit low FR expression [51] (Fig. S1). Furthermore, pretreatment of H22 cells with free FA, a well-established competitive inhibition strategy [52,53], significantly reduced cellular uptake of T-NP.PANO (Fig. S2). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that targeted nanoformulation can achieve ligand-receptor-mediated cellular uptake.

Western blotting results illustrate that T-NP.PANO remarkably (p < 0.05) downregulated the expression of Bcl-2 and upregulated the expression of Bax with comparison to PBS, free PANO, and NP.PANO (Fig. 6B). Although small-molecule inhibitors are designed to selectively target oncogenic pathways in cancer cells, their clinical efficacy is usually limited by off-target effects to healthy cells [54]. FR, a transmembrane glycoprotein mediating endocytosis of folic acid, presents a promising therapeutic target as it exhibits significantly higher expression in malignant tissues as compared to normal counterparts [55]. Notably, FR overexpression is implicated in advanced disease progression and poor patient prognosis [55]. Based on high affinity between FA and FR, FA-targeted drug conjugates have demonstrated therapeutic promise in FR-overexpressing cancers, particularly HCC [56]. Recent advancements have focused on FA-functionalized nanocarriers for HCC delivery of chemotherapeutic agents and small-molecule inhibitors [50,[57], [58], [59], [60], [61], [62]]. However, there is a lack of reported studies investigating FA-targeted nanocarriers for PANO delivery in HCC, representing a critical gap in the field.

In this study, T-NP.PANO achieved significantly improved cellular uptake in HCC cells (Fig. 5). This uptake mechanism was confirmed to be FR-specific, as evidenced by two observations: (1) similar cellular uptake was achieved by NP.PANO and T-NP.PANO in FR-negative A549 cells (Fig. S1), and (2) competitive inhibition with free FA pretreatment significantly reduced T-NP.PANO internalization in FR-positive H22 cells (Fig. S2). In addition to improved HCC cell targeting, T-NP.PANO achieved potent in vitro therapeutic efficacy, as evidenced by cell cycle arrest and apoptosis induction (Fig. 5, Fig. 6). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that T-NP.PANO can specifically deliver PANO to HCC cells through FA-FR-mediated endocytic pathway, thereby promoting therapeutic efficacy of PANO against HCC.

In vivo toxicity, half-life, and tissue delivery of targeted nanoformulation

Following multiple i.v. administration, 15 mg/kg of free PANO caused remarkable (p < 0.05) body weight reduction in healthy mice, while the weight of healthy mice was not affected by T-NP.PANO containing 15 mg/kg of PANO (Fig. 7A). Histopathological results indicate the absence of organ injury in mice treated with T-NP.PANO (Fig. 7B). Hematological analysis shows that no hematotoxicity was caused in mice treated with T-NP.PANO, in terms of red blood cell (RBC), white blood cell (WBC), platelet (PLT), and hemoglobin (HGB) (Fig. 7C). Hematological analysis also illustrates that no damage was found in liver and kidneys of mice treated with T-NP.PANO, based on alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and creatinine (CREA) (Fig. 7D).

In vivo toxicity of T-NP.PANO. (A) Mouse weights were recorded after six i.v. injections in one month (n = 6). (B) Histopathologic analysis was determined at Day 30 by H&E staining assay (bar = 100 μm). (C) Blood toxicity was determined at Day 30 (n = 3). (D) Liver and kidney toxicity was determined at Day 30 (n = 3).

After i.v. administration in H22-luc-derived orthotopic tumor murine model, free PANO exhibited quick systemic clearance (t1/2 = 0.4 h), while T-NP.PANO significantly (p < 0.01) extended circulatory half-life of PANO (t1/2 = 1.7 h; NP.PANO achieved similar circulatory half-life) (Fig. 8A). After i.v. administration in H22-luc-derived orthotopic tumor murine model, T-NP.PANO, but not NP.PANO, was mainly observed in hepatic tumor, as evidenced by the overlap of NP (fluorescence) and tumor (luminescence) (Fig. 8B).

Circulatory half-life and tissue delivery of T-NP.PANO. (A) The graphic of PANO concentrations (either non-formulated PANO or T-NP.PANO; the injected dose of PANO was 5 mg/kg, it was the same below) vs. time points (n = 4). (B) NP distribution to tumors and major organs. Targeted nanoformulation (DiD fluorescence) was observed in hepatic tumor (luminescence) (n = 4).

In general, small-molecule inhibitors suffer from short circulatory half-lives due to their poor solubility and low bioavailability [63]. In this study, blood circulation of PANO in targeted nanoformulation was remarkably increased when compared to free PANO (Fig. 8). This enhancement is possibly due to the fact that PEG forms a hydrophilic layer on particle surface, which improves solubility and bioavailability of T-NP.PANO, thereby prolonging blood circulation of PANO. These findings confirm that T-NP.PANO not only overcomes solubility limitation of free PANO but also extends therapeutic window through improved circulatory half-life.

Notably, although PEGylation offers stealth properties, it may give rise to steric hindrance effect leading to reduced tumor targeting [63]. In this study, T-NP.PANO, but not NP.PANO, significantly enhanced tumor distribution (Fig. 8). This action indicates that targeted nanoformulation, due to FA modification at PEG distal termini, can overcome PEGylation paradox and enhance tumor accumulation.

Collectively, results in Fig. 7, Fig. 8 demonstrate that targeted nanoformulation can promote circulatory half-life and tumor delivery of PANO while mitigating systemic toxicity associated with PANO.

In vivo therapeutic effects of targeted nanoformulation

Therapeutic efficacy of free PANO, NP.PANO, and T-NP.PANO was evaluated in H22-luc-derived orthotopic HCC murine model (Fig. 9A), with 5 mg/kg PANO selected as the starting dose based on in vivo safety profiling (Fig. 7A). Following multiple i.v. injections, free PANO significantly (p < 0.01) reduced HCC growth when compared to PBS, while NP.PANO remarkably (p < 0.05) retarded HCC growth as comparison with free PANO (Fig. 9B and C). T-NP.PANO was able to further (p < 0.01) inhibit tumor growth in comparison with NP.PANO (Fig. 9B and C). As a result, animal survival was significantly (p < 0.01) extended by T-NP.PANO (4 out of 8 mice more than 60 days) relative to PBS (median survival = 16 days), free PANO (21 days), and NP.PANO (26 days) (Fig. 9D).

In vivo anti-HCC effects of T-NP.PANO. (A) Therapeutic schematic of PBS, PANO (5 mg/kg), NP.PANO (5 mg/kg of drug), and T-NP.PANO (5 mg/kg of drug). (B) Representative photos of HCC-bearing mice following different treatments. (C) Tumor growth following different treatments (n = 6). (D) HCC-bearing mouse survival (median survival: PBS = 16 days, PANO = 21 days, NP.PANO = 26 days; T-NP.PANO = 4 out of 8 animals over 60 days) (n = 8). (E) Expression of proteins associated with cell cycle and apoptosis (n = 3).

Western blotting results illustrate that T-NP.PANO profoundly (p < 0.01) upregulated the expression of p16 and p21 in tumor cells (Fig. 9E). Concurrently, T-NP.PANO significantly (p < 0.05) downregulated Bcl-2 expression and upregulated Bax expression in tumor cells, leading to enhancement of Caspase 3 and Caspase 9 activities (p < 0.01, p < 0.05) (Fig. 9E). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that targeted nanoformulation can trigger cancer cell cycle arrest and apoptosis, thereby promoting tumor growth inhibition and animal survival extension (Fig. 9).

Of note, animal models (e.g., patient-derived tumor xenograft), which are more reminiscent of clinical features observed in HCC patients, will be applied in future to evaluate therapeutic efficacy of targeted nanoformulation. Future work also needs to perform therapeutic studies of targeted nanoformulation using different drug doses and administration intervals, in order to comprehensively estimate translation potential for HCC therapy. Additionally, innovative combinatorial strategies integrating targeted nanoformulation with conventional therapies (e.g., chemotherapy and radiotherapy) and emerging immunomodulatory approaches will be explored, aiming for overcoming therapeutic resistance and achieving synergistic tumor eradication.

Conclusions

In the present study, PLGA-PEG-FA NP achieved safe, efficient, and HCC-specific delivery of PANO. The resulting PANO-loaded nanoformulation achieved the blockade of cancer cell cycle and the induction of cancer cell apoptosis. These actions led to tumor suppression and survival extension in orthotopic HCC mouse model. Our preliminary findings provide a proof of concept approach for targeted therapy in HCC.
