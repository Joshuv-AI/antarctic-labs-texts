Vector analog computing via on-demand metasurface dispersive polarization transformation
Authors: Hui Yang, Jie Xu, Meiyu Peng, Hairong He, Yuting Jiang, Dian Yu, Ruibo Jin, Yingjie Gu, Yueqiang Hu, Huigao Duan, Hui Jing
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Optical analog computing can potentially feature high-throughput parallel processing with ultralow power and high speed and is promising for efficient signal processing. Previous platforms have mainly focused on scalar computing with optical intensities, which is highly sensitive to environmental disturbance and has been primarily restricted to single or basic computations because of intrinsic fixed correlation between the input and output signals. To our knowledge, for the first time, we use polarization vectors for optical analog computing with a single-layered metasurface to overcome these restrictions. The underlying mechanism is on-demand polarization transformation on the dispersive Poincaré spheres, constructing intrinsic variable correlations between the incident polarization vectors and output signals. We choose the universal logical gates and mathematical function operations as two specific examples. Experimental results of our vector computing metadevices exhibit minimal errors relative to the target values. Our work opens up an avenue for advanced optical signal processing across both classical and quantum domains.

INTRODUCTION

As we enter the information era, the ever-growing demand for massive data processing drives researchers to explore high-speed and low-consumption alternatives to the current digital computing paradigm. The digital computation, which relies on analog-to-digital conversion of electron flows in electronic circuits, suffers from high-energy consumption, low operation speed, and systematic complexity (1–3). In comparison, photons as massless bosons allow for lossless propagation and manipulation of light through optical materials without analog-to-digital and digital-to-analog conversion, thereby enabling high-speed, in-parallel, and low-consumption computation (4–7). Nowadays, optical analog computing in the temporal or spatial domain has been fulfilled with elaborately designed conventional optical systems using a series of optical elements such as optical lenses, spatial light modulators, and diffractive optical elements (8–11). However, the bulky optical components adopted in these platforms hinder further miniaturization and on-chip integration. Moreover, the microscale addressable pixel sizes lead to a limited spatial resolution and undesirable high-order diffraction loss, which will worsen the final performance of the final systems (12, 13). To fully exploit the potential of photons for analog computing, it is essential to develop a compact platform that can process data at the speed of light with low power consumption and could be integrated into photonic chips with a thin and planar profile.

Recent advancements in optical metasurfaces have made this feasible by arbitrarily manipulating the abundant physical dimensions of photons on an optically thin interface (14–20). Metasurfaces, a two-dimensional version of metamaterial with artificially programmable electromagnetic properties, have been regarded as a promising candidate for compact, highly efficient, and ultrathin analog processors (21–24). So far, metasurface-enabled massive and high-throughput parallel tasks have been demonstrated in various optical data processing scenarios, such as differentiation (25–30), convolution (31, 32), object classification (33–35), logical operation (36, 37), and solving equations (22, 38, 39). Despite these exciting achievements, most of the existing platforms have focused on scalar analog computing with optical intensities, which is susceptible to environmental disturbances such as fluctuations in light source, transmission losses, and atmospheric turbulence. Moreover, these optical computing platforms have primarily been restricted to a finite set of computing operations, such as performing a single image processing with differentiation or convolution, implementing several logical operations, and solving a specific equation. Therefore, a pivotal question arises: whether it is feasible to develop a vector analog computing paradigm that is robust against environmental disturbance while simultaneously transcending simple computing tasks.

Polarization, as one of the fundamental physical dimensions of light, describes the trajectory of the electric field oscillating, revealing the vectorial nature of the electromagnetic field (40). Polarization plays a crucial role in light-matter interactions, carrying valuable information that is essential for applications in science and technology, and therefore, metasurfaces have been widely exploited for versatile and on-demand polarization modulation (41–44). This involves a range of applications in both classical and quantum domains such as polarization imaging (41), remote sensing, optical trapping (45), optical encryption (15, 46), and high-dimensional quantum technology (47, 48). On the other hand, polarization vectors have showcased superiority against environmental disturbances such as fluctuations in light source, transmission losses, and atmospheric turbulence (49, 50). However, developing polarization vectors for optical analog computing in an ultrathin platform remains elusive.

Here, we unlock polarization as the fundamental dimension for optical vector analog computing in the visible wavelength range through a single-layered dispersive Jones matrix metasurface. The metasurfaces are meticulously designed to achieve on-demand dispersive polarization transformation on the dispersive Poincaré spheres (DPSs), thereby establishing intrinsic variable mapping relationships between the incident polarization vectors and the output signals. As a result, one can overcome the restrictions faced with scalar computing and implement complex optical vector analog computing. As a proof of concept, we numerically and experimentally demonstrate the universal logical gates and mathematical function operations. Using the discrete state of polarizations (SoPs) located on the DPSs, the common logical gate operations at red (R), green (G), and blue (B) wavelengths are demonstrated. Using successive SoPs along the equator of a specific Poincaré sphere (PS), the mathematical function operations such as the trigonometric function and complex exponential operations are achieved. The numerical and experimental results demonstrate good agreement and exhibit minimal errors relative to the target values, highlighting the versatility of monoclinic metasurfaces as compact optical analog computing platforms.

RESULTS

Conception and metasurface design

Figure 1 (A and B) schematically shows scalar computing versus vector computing, where the information carriers are intensity and polarization, respectively. Here, we choose the two-channel computation platform as a typical example, where the input signal is split into two by the modulator. For scalar computing, both the two intensity (represent as Gaussian spots) channels are susceptible to environmental disturbance, and therefore, the Gaussian spots are severely distorted in the output ports. Moreover, there is an intrinsic fixed correlation between the input and output intensities (i.e., I0=αI1+βI2 , where the coefficients α and β, which determine the output intensities, are fixed). As shown in Fig. 1B, the situation for vector computing is completely different. The two polarization channels exhibit a disturbance-resilient feature, and the polarization vectors at the output ports remain almost unaffected. In particular, the correlation between the input and output polarizations is intrinsically variable [i.e., I0=sin2(ψ)I1+cos2(ψ)I2 , where the coefficients sin2(ψ) and cos2(ψ) , which determine the output signals, are variable as the change of the incident polarization azimuth angle ψ ], through which one can unlock more complex mathematical computations in principle. This novel polarization vector computing paradigm shows a fundamental difference to the scalar one, and we refer to it as vector analog computing. Figure 1C shows the schematic illustration of the single-layered dispersive Jones matrix metasurface for implementing vector analog computing with polarization. The metasurface is elaborately designed to achieve on-demand dispersive polarization transformation on the DPSs, with specific vectorial focal spots exhibited on the output plane. The vectorial focal spots with certain polarizer-like functionalities are preconfigured, whose amplitudes are controlled by the incident polarization vector |Pin,λ>. As a result, one can construct a mapping relationship between the incident polarization vector and the output signal, thus enabling optical polarization vector analog computing. We choose the universal logical gates and mathematical function operations as two specific examples. Using discrete SoPs located on the DPSs, the common logical gate operations of AND, NAND, NOR, XOR, OR, and XNOR that operate at red (R), green (G), and blue (B) wavelengths are demonstrated. In this process, a pair of orthogonal SoPs and intensity detectors located at specific output positions are designated as the two inputs. The detected intensity values greater than and less than the threshold are designated as output values of “1” and “0.” Using successive SoPs along the equator of a specific PS, the output amplitudes can be directly used to perform mathematical function operations. In this process, as the incident polarization vector |Pin> evolves along the equator of the PS at λG, the amplitudes of the two vectorial focal spots vary from minimum to maximum and vice versa. Supposing that the polarization azimuth angle ψ is equal to the argument θ, the output amplitudes show direct correlation with the argument θ, from which we can achieve polarization vector–enabled mathematical function computing such as the trigonometric function and complex exponential operations.

In this approach, rather than trying to control the far-field intensity distributions for some incident polarization vectors, we are able to control its dispersive polarization transfer functions. For example, if Dλ(kx, ky) corresponds to a polarizer with a fast axis that is parallel to the x axis, light will be transformed into the plane wave component with the direction (kx, ky). The metadevice functions in a way in accordance with a polarizer: bright when the incident polarization vector is |X> and dark when the incident polarization vector changes to the orthogonal one. Here, we seek to design a Jones matrix metasurface capable of implementing on-demand dispersive polarization transformation. The desired far-field polarization–dependent response is described by Dλ(kx, ky), and then the Jones matrix metasurface can be given by inverse Fourier transformation asMλ(x,y)=F−1Dλ(kx,ky)∝∣Pin,λ∗><Pin,λ∣(4)where * denotes complex conjugation. The dispersive Jones matrix in Eq. 4 matches the output intensity characteristic of a dispersive polarizer, while it differs in that the obtained polarization is of flipped-handedness relative to the analyzed one.

The low panel illustration of Fig. 2B shows the side and top views of the meta-atom, which is composed of an elliptical TiO2 nanoblock on the SiO2 substrate. The detailed parameters include a subwavelength lattice constant P = 400 nm, height h = 1000 nm, and in-plane dimensions (major axis Rx, minor axis Ry, and orientation angle θ). The full-wave finite-difference time-domain simulations are performed to characterize the transmission properties of the meta-atoms at working wavelengths of λR = 635 nm, λG = 532 nm, and λB = 450 nm. Figure 2C shows the simulated phase profiles as a function of the nanoblock’s in-plane dimensions under x-linearly polarized incident light with wavelengths of λR, λG, and λB. The requisite phase coverage, spanning from 0 to 2π, is achieved by varying the in-plane geometric parameters within their permissible range, taking into account processing limitations. The corresponding simulated phase profiles under y-linearly polarized incident light can be obtained through a matrix transpose operation. Therefore, using Eqs. 2 and 4 along with a modified particle swarm optimization algorithm, the in-plane dimensions (Rx, Ry, and θ) of the meta-atom at each coordinate point (x, y) of the final dispersive Jones matrix metasurface can be determined. For the designed metadevice consisting of two distinct meta-atoms, it appears to be classified as an interleaved arrangement (51, 52). Actually, the two optimized meta-atoms at each superpixel are almost different, and each meta-atom contributes to determining the final functionality. This characteristic is essentially consistent with that of noninterleaved multifunctional metadevices (53, 54). From this perspective, the designed metadevice is noninterleaved, even though it is composed of two distinct meta-atoms.

Implementation of universal polarization vector logical gates

On the basis of the abovementioned design strategy, we numerically and experimentally demonstrate two metadevices capable of implementing universal polarization vector logical gates and mathematical function operations. For the universal logical gates, four polarizer-like functionalities are preconfigured in R, G, and B wavelengths (section S2). Figure 3A shows the working principle of polarization vector logic gates, where a pair of orthogonal SoPs is set as input I and the intensity detector located at a specific output position is set as input II. The output is the detected intensity, where values greater and less than the threshold are designated as output values of “1” and “0,” respectively. Next, the metadevice sample is fabricated using electron-beam lithography and atomic layer deposition, following the now-standard procedure (see fabrication details in Materials and Methods). The metadevice has a size of 300 μm by 300 μm, containing 375 by 375 supercells with the pixel size of 0.8 μm. Figure 3B shows the overall and enlarged side-view scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of the fabricated metadevice (labeled as M1).

Implementation of mathematical function operation

The sine and cosine functions, as the core mathematical tools for characterizing the fundamental properties of light waves, have tremendous and irreplaceable potential in driving the next generation of high-speed optical computing technology. To be specific, these two basis functions play a pivotal role in diverse optical computing applications, including optical Fourier transform, optical diffraction and interference calculation, and optical information modulation. To further verify our concept of polarization vector analog computing, a metadevice capable of performing complex mathematical function computing has been demonstrated as well. Here, the far-field target Jones matrix function of the metadevice can be expressed asDtar(kx,ky)=ax∣X∗><X∣+ay∣Y∗><Y∣(5)where |X> and |Y> represent the polarization vector of linearly polarized light along the horizontal and vertical directions, respectively. ax and ay represent the far-field wavefront modulation effect (the hyperbolic phase shift is adopted to achieve the far-field Gaussian spot). Figure 4 (A and B) shows the overall and enlarged side-view SEM images of the fabricated metadevice (labeled as M2). Figure 4 (C and D) shows the numerical and experimental intensity distributions of the metadevice under seven different incident linearly polarized lights, which are represented by double-headed arrows (section S6). The corresponding polarization azimuth angles are 0, π/12, π/6, π/4, π/3, 5π/12, and π/2. It can be observed that the two generated focal spots vary from minimum to maximum and vice versa as the incident polarization azimuth angle changes. Note that we measured the intensity values in the experiment, and the amplitude of each focal spot can be obtained by taking the square root (section S7). Then, we quantitatively analyze the amplitude of each focal spot, and the obtained results are depicted in Fig. 4E. The sine and cosine function operations can be directly obtained (via taking the amplitude values of A1 and A2), which can be treated as two primary function operations, while the other trigonometric operations such as tangent and cotangent function operations can be derived from them. Here, we implement trigonometric operations in the domain of 0 to π/2, and such operations can be extended to the whole domain of 2π by using the intrinsic properties of trigonometric functions (section S8). As shown in Fig. 4E, the black solid lines show the ideal trigonometric function shapes, while the lines with orange dots and green circles show the numerical and experimental results, respectively. It is obvious that the numerical and experimental results show good agreement with the ideal values. The small discrepancies of the experimental results from the ideal values can be attributed to fabrication derivations and measurement errors. The average computational errors in the simulation and experimental tests are 0.5 and 3.6%, respectively (section S9). Therefore, we have successfully achieved universe logical gates and high-precision optical trigonometric operations by using the compact and ultrathin metasurface platforms. To show the compactness and advantages of the proposed platforms, we have compared them with state-of-the-art photonic computing platforms (section S10).

Euler’s formula is widely acknowledged as one of the most elegant expressions in mathematics, playing a pivotal role in complex analysis by establishing a profound connection between trigonometric and exponential functions. For the metadevice, we proposed that Euler’s formula operation can be accomplished by respectively designating the two focal spots as the real and imaginary parts of a complex value, as depicted in the left panel of Fig. 4F. To verify the practical polarization vector optical Euler’s formula operation, we depict the numerical and experimental results in a complex axis. As shown in Fig. 4F, the black solid lines show the ideal exponential function (eiθ = isinθ + cosθ) values, while the orange and green dots lines show the numerical and experimental results, respectively. The high accuracy holds considerable importance for the practical application of our designed optical Euler’s formula operator. Beyond processing a single Euler formula, the designed metadevice can be used for more complicated Euler’s formula complex exponential operations, including addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division (section S11). Moreover, by using polarization vectors located on the surface of the high-order PS, polarization vector differentiation operation has been numerically and experimentally demonstrated (section S12).

DISCUSSION

We have proposed and experimentally demonstrated an ultrathin metasurface vector optical analog computing platform. Our design strategy unlocks the polarization vector as the fundamental dimension for optical analog computing. The underlying mechanism relies on at-will dispersive polarization vector transformation on the DPSs, thereby establishing intrinsic variable correlations between the incident polarization vectors and the output signals. As a result, one can overcome the restrictions faced with scalar computing (i.e., susceptible to environmental disturbance and single or basic computing operations because of intrinsic fixed correlation) and implement more complex optical vector analog computing. Without the loss of generality, we choose the universal logical gates and complex mathematical function operations as two typical examples. Using discrete SoPs located on the DPSs and successive SoPs along the equator of a specific PS, the common logical gate and mathematical function operations such as the trigonometric function and complex exponential operations have been numerically and experimentally demonstrated. As expected, the numerical and experimental results demonstrate good agreement and exhibit minimal errors relative to the target values, highlighting the versatility of monoclinic metasurfaces as compact vector analog computing platforms. Our work offers a paradigm for optical analog computing, holding potential applications in chip-scale ultrafast and high-throughput parallel optical signal processing in both classical and quantum realms.

For the demonstrated metadevices, the realization of vector analog computing relies on polarization modulation of the incident light. Mechanical rotation of conventional optical polarization–modulated devices may be slow and impractical in real-world optical computing systems, especially contrary to the trend of integration. Hence, a metasurface-based polarization controller that has ultrafast polarization control and subwavelength configuration could be an excellent choice (55). By leveraging high-precision alignment and integration technologies, this ultrafast polarization controller can be integrated with the metadevices we designed to accomplish ultrafast vector analog computing.

Now, optical analog computing remains in its early stage of development given that some electrical postprocessing techniques have been adopted to facilitate the implementation of its final functionalities (36, 38). For the proposed vector analog computing platform, several electrical postprocessing techniques, including thresholding, division, and square root calculations, have been used. In the future optical analog computing systems, we expect that these mathematical operators can be implemented through optical approaches. The thresholding operation can be accomplished by incorporating an attenuator into the optical path channel. The division operation can be achieved by using a Mach-Zehnder interferometer, in which the dividend beam serves as the input light, while the divisor beam is introduced into one of the interferometer’s arms (56). The square root operation can be achieved by exploiting the nonlinear absorption coefficient of the functional material, which changes in accordance with the light intensity. With an elaborately designed structure, it is feasible to make a portion of its transmission characteristic curve approximately adhere to the square root relationship between the output and input optical signals. These exciting possibilities will be explored in our future works.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Metasurface fabrication

The samples were fabricated using electron beam lithography along with the etching technique. First, a 1000-nm-thick polymethyl methacrylate electron beam resist layer was spin coated at 2000 rpm on the transparent silica substrate with an indium tin oxide film layer and baked on a hot plate for 4 min at 180°C. Next, the sample was exposed by electron beam lithography to a 100-kV voltage and a beam current of 200 pA. Subsequently, we put the exposed sample in a mixed solution of isopropanol and methyl isobutyl ketone (3:1) for 1 min and then fixed it in the isopropanol solution for 1 min at room temperature. Later, we used the atomic layer deposition system to fill the exposed area with 200-nm TiO2. We use polymethyl methacrylate for the positive photoresist exposure process, which is void before deposition. Then, the deposited thickness of TiO2 is related to the semiminor axis of the maximum meta-atom. After this process, there was a layer of 200-nm TiO2 on the top of the entire sample, and we removed it by ion beam etching in the next process. After removing TiO2 on the top layer, we used reactive ion etching to remove the resist. Last, the TiO2 nanostructures with a high aspect ratio (of up to 10) were obtained.
