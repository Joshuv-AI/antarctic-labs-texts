Delayed potentiation effect after high-load resistance priming: Effects of rest-redistribution set structures on athletic performance
Authors: Xingyi Niu, Fei Liu, Yuzhen Chen, Diwei Chen, Zhexiao Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis investigation involved examining whether Rest-Redistribution Set (RR) and Traditional-Set (TS) resistance priming (RP) protocols could induce a Delayed Potentiation Effect (DPE). Additionally, it was analyzed whether the RR protocol demonstrated significant advantages over the TS protocol in various performance parameters across different testing time points following the RP.MethodTwenty-four male collegiate Physical Education majors participated in this study, each completing two resistance protocols involving the back squat exercise: (1) a RR protocol incorporating a 30-s intra-set rest and a 200-s inter-set rest, and (2) a TS protocol without intra-set rest and with a 240-s inter-set rest. Both protocols comprised four sets of five repetitions performed at 85 % of one-repetition maximum (1RM). Performance outcomes, including countermovement jump (CMJ), sprint time, Y-balance test scores, change of direction (COD) ability, and agility, were evaluated at three time points: baseline (Pre), 6 h post-exercise (Post-6h), and 24 h post-exercise (Post-24h). Data were analyzed using a two-way repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) with factors for protocol (RR vs. TS) and time (Pre, Post-6h, Post-24h). When significant main effects or interaction effects were identified, Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted.Results(1) A significant interaction between RP protocols and testing time points was observed for CMJ, sprint, and agility performance (P < 0.05). Simple effects of time: At Post-6h, both RP protocols elicited significant DPE across most metrics. Specifically, the TS protocol improved sprint (P < 0.001) and agility (P < 0.001) compared to Pre, whereas the RR protocol enhanced CMJ (P = 0.003), sprint (P < 0.001), and agility (P < 0.001). By Post-24h, TS showed significant declines in CMJ (P = 0.007) and sprint (P < 0.001) relative to Pre, while RR maintained improvements in agility (P = 0.001). Simple effects of RP protocol: At Post-6h, RR outperformed TS in CMJ (P = 0.005), sprint (P = 0.013), and agility (P = 0.005). At Post-24h, RR demonstrated significantly superior CMJ performance versus TS (P = 0.014). (2) No significant interaction effects between RP and testing time points were observed in COD or Y-balance tests (P > 0.05). However, time-dependent effects emerged: COD improved at Post-6h compared to Pre (P = 0.005), while Y-balance performance showed enhancements at both Post-6h and Post-24h compared to Pre (P < 0.001), with Post-24h values exceeding Post-6h (P < 0.001).ConclusionBoth the RR and TS protocols induced DPE at the Post-6h mark. The RR protocol improved CMJ, sprint, and agility performance, while the TS protocol only enhanced sprint and agility metrics. In comparative terms, RR demonstrated a significantly greater DPE than TS across CMJ, sprint, and agility metrics. At the Post-24h mark, DPE diminished for most measures (with the exception of Y-balance), with RR maintaining CMJ and sprint performance levels close to baseline and demonstrating a significantly enhanced agility performance compared to baseline. Furthermore, RR outperformed TS in CMJ performance. The RP protocol also produced DPE in COD and Y-balance performance, with Y-balance achieving its highest recorded value at Post-24h. Although RR exhibited stronger overall efficacy, its effects were specific to certain performance parameters, indicating a need for further validation.Keywords: Delayed potentiation effect, Resistance priming, Rest-redistribution set, Athletic performance, Neuromuscular capabilities

Introduction

Post-Activation Performance Enhancement (PAPE) is represented as an acute enhancement of athletic performance induced by maximal or near-maximal muscular contractions.1 Research has demonstrated that PAPE protocols have been reported to effectively augment athletes' vertical jump,1,2 Linear sprint,1,3 and change of direction(COD)1 performance. However, as research on PAPE has increased, its implementation in actual sports competition scenarios has revealed significant limitations, primarily due to its transient nature. Most investigations have reported that PAPE becomes evident roughly 4 min after the priming stimulus and persists for about 4–12 min,2,4,5 although some research has suggested it may extend up to 20 min.4 Even at the upper bound, the effect is transient relative to the demands of longer-duration events and challenging to integrate into pre-competition routines. Consequently, the practical deployment of PAPE strategies in competitive settings is constrained.

Recent studies have indicated that resistance training-induced potentiation effects may exhibit an additional enhancement window, with performance improvements manifesting at 1.75h, 6h, 12h, 24h, and even up to 48h.6, 7, 8, 9, 10 During this period, significant improvements in various neuromuscular performance parameters have been observed.6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11 This phenomenon has been termed the "Delayed Potentiation Effect" (DPE) in the academic literature.10 Current evidence indicates that the emergence of DPE is driven by multilevel physiological mechanisms, including fluctuations in diurnal testosterone rhythm,6,11 stiffness modulation,9,12 thermoregulation,6 and higher-order motor unit recruitment,8,9 among other contributing factors. Based on this mechanistic insight, resistance loading protocols don't need to be scheduled immediately before competition, but can be implemented within 24–48 h pre-competition. For instance, when athletic events are scheduled for tomorrow afternoon, the potentiation protocol can be executed either this morning or tomorrow morning. As such, the DPE strategy addresses key limitations associated with PAPE, including its short-lived effectiveness and challenges in practical application, while offering strong applicability across both team-based and individual sports. In the context of team sports, DPE enhances sport-specific physical attributes, such as explosive strength for tackling and jumping in rugby, repeated sprint ability for rapid transitions in soccer, and agility for evasive maneuvers in basketball, thereby directly targeting the neuromuscular demands essential for performance. This contributes to improved competitive effectiveness in dynamic, multifactorial team sport environments.8,13 For individual sports, this methodology has been validated in several studies, demonstrating that athletes can achieve DPE in various athletic performances, including jump,6,9,14,15 sprint,6,14,15 and COD14,15 through pre-competition DPE protocols. Multiple studies have demonstrated that DPE in these performance parameters can be observed 6 h post-resistance training.6,8,15 This suggests that performing resistance exercise 6 h prior to competition may be efficacious, particularly for competitions scheduled in the afternoon or evening. However, other studies have reported that DPE reaches peak effects at 24h post-induction for reactive strength index (RSI) and rate of force development (RFD),9 or at 33h for vertical jump performance,16 with minor benefits persisting up to 48h (RFD).9

The potentiation effects of DPE are contingent upon the priming methods and load parameters utilized. Within this context, resistance training that induces DPE is specifically termed "Resistance Priming" (RP).10 Among various RP protocols, barbell back squats represent the most frequently implemented methodology.6, 7, 8,16 Regarding conditioning loads, current research findings on DPE remain contradictory. Some researchers maintain that squats exceeding 85 % of one-repetition maximum (1RM) significantly enhance neuromuscular function.6,8 For instance, Cook et al.6 employed squats at 93 % 1RM (4 sets, 3 repetitions per set), while Saez et al.8 utilized squats at 85–95 % 1RM (2 sets, 1–4 repetitions per set), both reporting significant improvements in subjects' jumping performance. Conversely, other researchers argue that resistance loads above 85 % 1RM are excessively intense and may impede DPE induction.7 For example, Ekstrand et al.7 found no significant improvement in vertical jump performance following squats at 85 % 1RM (1 set, 6 repetitions per set).The fundamental reason for these divergent views stems from certain scholars' assertions that resistance training at ≥ 85 % 1RM intensity may lead to increased RP total load, subsequently triggering pronounced neuromuscular system responses,17 thus presenting significant challenges to athletes' physiological adaptation. Research by Cheung et al. demonstrated that resistance training at ≥ 85 % 1RM may induce muscle microdamage and inflammatory responses in athletes' musculature, subsequently triggering delayed onset muscle soreness (DOMS) and acute muscle damage during the post-exercise period, ultimately compromising subsequent athletic performance.18 By reducing the intensity of resistance loading, these issues could potentially be addressed; however, this may introduce another concern where the decreased RP load results in less pronounced DPE responses among athletes.

RP load typically comprises three key components: “Intensity”, “Volume”, and “Recovery Interval”.10 The fitness-fatigue model offers a theoretical framework for optimizing athletic performance, positing that increases in training intensity and volume concurrently induce the accumulation of both fitness and fatigue. Excessive fatigue can mask the expression of fitness, whereas the rapid dissipation of fatigue facilitates the manifestation of peak fitness levels.19 Thus, by modifying the recovery interval parameter, it may be possible to reduce the overall RP load while maintaining high intensity and volume. This approach not only preserves the mechanical tension required for DPE induction but also minimizes excessive neuromuscular system responses and severe DOMS in athletes, which could otherwise compromise the implementation of DPE strategies. To address the issue of fatigue accumulation induced by high-load RP in TS structures, the cluster-set (CS) training model offers an optimized solution by incorporating intra-set rest intervals of 10–30 s.20 Unlike TS, which only includes inter-set rest, CS significantly reduces cumulative metabolic stress while maintaining the same intensity (≥85 % 1RM) and volume (total repetitions).21 This design not only effectively alleviates neuromuscular fatigue but also optimizes the transmission efficiency of mechanical tension stimuli by preserving movement technique quality (e.g., reducing compensatory movements or Increase movement speed),20 thereby inducing more pronounced delayed performance enhancement (DPE) at lower fatigue levels.19 Wang et al.15 first introduced the CS paradigm in DPE research, demonstrating that CS back squats significantly improved vertical jump, 20-m sprint, and COD performance in male collegiate athletes on the same afternoon compared to TS. However, the additional rest time in CS may prolong total training duration, limiting its practical application.22 To address this, researchers have proposed rest-redistribution (RR) as an alternative strategy, which reallocates the total rest time of TS into shorter but more frequent intervals. This approach achieves similar benefits to CS in maintaining mechanical performance and reducing fatigue without increasing overall training time.23,24 Unfortunately, only Wang et al.15 have explored the effects of CS back squats on same-day afternoon (6h interval) performance, with no studies examining the impact of CS on DPE over longer periods (e.g., the next day). More importantly, although RR has demonstrated advantages similar to CS in reducing fatigue and maintaining mechanical performance,25 no studies to date have applied RR in the context of DPE. This research gap limits our understanding of the potential role of RR in enhancing athletic performance, particularly its practical value in multi-day competitions or high-intensity training cycles.

In conclusion, pre-competition DPE strategies offer a practical and effective alternative to traditional PAPE approaches by addressing key limitations such as their brief duration and stringent activation requirements. However, the optimal loading parameters for eliciting DPE remain under debate. Excessive loads may suppress potentiation, while insufficient loads may fail to induce meaningful effects. RR protocols incorporating intra-set rest intervals may offer a viable solution, though the current body of research on their efficacy is limited, necessitating further investigation. The aim of the present study was to investigate the efficacy of RR training protocols in inducing DPE among college students majoring in Physical Education. The study maintains key methodological consistencies with prior research, such as the use of back squats at 85 % 1RM (4 × 5 repetitions) as the RP for DPE induction.26 The key methodological divergences include: (1) Extended temporal analysis assessing DPE effects at both Post-6h and Post-24h, versus prior singular Post-6h measurements; and (2) Multi-parametric performance evaluation (vertical jump, linear sprint, agility, COD, dynamic balance) superseding previous single-parameter assessments.9,11,15 The specific objectives of this investigation were: (1) to determine whether RR and TS squat protocols effectively induce DPE; (2) to compare the efficacy of RR versus TS in sustaining DPE at both Post-6h and Post-24h; and (3) to identify whether RR protocols produce domain-specific DPE adaptations distinct from those induced by TS. The research hypotheses were as follows: (1) both TS and RR protocols would elicit DPE across multiple outcomes—CMJ, linear sprint, agility, and COD; and (2) RR would be superior to TS, producing DPE at both 6 h (Post-6 h) and 24 h (Post-24 h) post-intervention, whereas TS would induce DPE only at Post-6 h. Further, RR was expected to yield DPE in a greater number of performance variables across the testing time points than TS.

Materials and methods

Experimental approach to the problem

A randomized balanced crossover design was employed, requiring all subjects to complete both RP interventions (RR and TS), with all five performance measures (CMJ, sprint, COD, YBT, and Agility) assessed at three testing time points (Pre, Post-6h, and Post-24h6, 7, 8, 9). Each testing session consisted of a single RP intervention paired with one preselected performance measure. Prior to the formal experimental phase, participants completed a familiarization session that included protocol orientation, movement technique standardization, and 1RM assessment. Before each intervention, subjects were randomly assigned to either the TS or RR protocol and then completed pre-testing for a randomly selected performance metric. The preselected performance indicator was reassessed at 6 and 24 h post-intervention. All pre-testing and intervention procedures were conducted in the morning to minimize circadian variation. A 72-h washout period followed each Post-24h assessment to allow for full recovery. Throughout the study, participants maintained their usual dietary routines but were instructed to abstain from alcohol and caffeine during the testing periods. Physical activity was prohibited between laboratory visits, and consistent sleep/diet patterns were enforced across conditions. Researchers documented the dietary intake and physical activity of subjects during each visit to verify compliance.

Subjects

This investigation employed a within-subjects repeated measures ANOVA design. Sample size calculation using G∗Power 3.1.9 software indicated a minimum requirement of 24 subjects (Effect size = 0.25, α = 0.05, Power = 0.8). Subject recruitment was conducted via online advertisements targeting university students majoring in Physical Education. Inclusion criteria were established to ensure methodological rigor and the applicability of findings to individuals with relevant resistance training experience. Eligibility required: (1) demonstrated competency in back squat technique supported by a minimum of six months of resistance training history; (2) documented 1RM testing experience within the preceding three months; (3) the ability to consistently execute standardized movement patterns across all performance assessments (CMJ, sprint, COD, YBT, and agility); and (4) availability to complete all experimental sessions. Initial screening of 38 potential participants (12 female, 26 male) identified that the majority of female candidates did not meet key eligibility requirements, with nine lacking recent 1RM testing experience and three exhibiting inconsistent movement execution during familiarization. As a result, 24 male Physical Education majors who satisfied all inclusion criteria were enrolled in the study (age: 23 ± 0.7 years; height: 178.8 ± 5.7 cm; body mass: 78.35 ± 8.14 kg; back squat 1RM: 138.9 ± 16.4 kg). The resulting sex disparity in participant composition arose organically during the recruitment and screening process, reflecting differential eligibility attainment between sexes in structured resistance training contexts, rather than any intentional exclusion based on sex.

The subjects were healthy and physically active male students from the School of Sports Science, with over two years of resistance training experience, no injuries within the past six months, and demonstrated proficiency in executing all test movements with proper form. The experimental protocol was approved by the local Research Ethics Committee in accordance with the latest version of the Declaration of Helsinki (approval number: TY2024046). Prior to the experiment, all subjects were briefed on the testing protocols and safety considerations, and voluntarily provided written informed consent to participate in this investigation.

Procedures

Familiarization sessions

Prior to the commencement of the formal experiment, subjects were assembled at the testing facility where the researchers provided a detailed explanation of the experimental protocol and obtained signed informed consent forms. Standardized attire requirements specified that subjects wear loose-fitting athletic apparel and designated training footwear. To ensure experimental validity, subjects were required to thoroughly familiarize themselves with the testing procedures and standardized execution of all assessment movements, followed by the determination of their 1RM for the back squat exercise.

Experimental conditions

As illustrated in Fig. 1, during the experimental protocol phase, subjects initially completed a standardized warm-up procedure consisting of 5 min of low-intensity aerobic running followed by systematic dynamic flexibility exercises (“World's Greatest Stretch”, “A-Style Jump”, and “B-Style Jump”, among others).27 Following a standardized warm-up, one performance parameter (CMJ, 30-m sprint, agility test, COD test, or Y-balance test) was randomly selected for baseline assessment prior to intervention. After a 10-min recovery period, participants were randomly assigned to perform either the TS or RR squat protocol. Post-intervention assessments of the same parameter were conducted at 6 and 24 h following the intervention. This sequence—comprising pre-test, intervention, and post-tests—constituted a single testing round. After a 72-h washout period, the RP intervention and performance testing were repeated according to the same protocol. This cycle continued until each participant had completed 10 full testing rounds. Movement execution throughout all squat interventions adhered to the technical standards established during the 1RM testing protocol. Both training protocols utilized 85 % 1RM load, comprising 4 sets of 5 repetitions each. The RR protocol implemented a "3 repetitions-30s rest-2 repetitions" structure within each set,21 while the TS protocol involved continuous repetitions without intra-set rest. To standardize total recovery time between protocols, inter-set rest periods were set at 200s for the RR protocol and 240s for the TS protocol. That is, RR: 30s × 4 (intra-set rests) + 200s × 3 (inter-set rests) = 720s total rest; TS: 240s × 3 = 720s total rest, ensuring equivalent total recovery time between protocols. Post-intervention performance parameters were assessed at 6h and 24h intervals.

Experimental Flow chart.

Testing and measurements

1RM test

Given that previous studies6,9,15 on DPE have consistently utilized barbell-based resistance training as the RP, the present study similarly adopted this approach for both RR and TS protocols. Accordingly, prior to the commencement of formal interventions, 1RM assessments for lower-limb resistance loading were conducted for each participant to ensure accurate prescription of training intensities. The 1RM testing protocol was conducted in accordance with the National Strength and Conditioning Association (NSCA, Fourth Edition) standardized testing protocols,28 following a structured loading progression to ensure safety and accuracy. The protocol consisted of: (1) an initial warm-up set using 50 % of the participant's estimated 1RM for 5–10 repetitions, followed by 1 min of rest; (2) a secondary warm-up set at 70 % of estimated 1RM for 3–5 repetitions, followed by 2 min of rest; (3) a tertiary warm-up set at 90 % of estimated 1RM for 2–3 repetitions, followed by 2–4 min of rest; and (4) 3–5 maximal lift attempts to establish the actual 1RM load, with 2–4 min of rest between trials. Prior to testing, subjects performed a comprehensive warm-up and were thoroughly instructed on proper technique. Specific execution requirements were implemented to ensure consistency and safety during all squat trials. The barbell was positioned across the upper trapezius muscles, with the hands adopting a natural grip behind the body. Foot placement was standardized to shoulder-width, with the toes externally rotated approximately 30°. Both the descent and ascent phases of each squat were performed under controlled conditions, synchronized to a metronome set at 40 beats per minute (Largo)29 to regulate movement tempo. For safety, a minimum of three spotters were present during each trial to monitor barbell trajectory, ensure adherence to the metronome pacing, and provide comprehensive support throughout the movement.

CMJ test

The assessment of jump performance was conducted using the CMJ protocol, with vertical jump height (cm) as the primary metric. This parameter has been validated in the existing literature as an effective indicator for monitoring lower limb power output and neuromuscular status.30 The testing procedure was executed as follows: subjects assumed a standing position on a 2D force platform (Kistler 9290DD, Kistler Instruments, Switzerland) with their hands fixed at the anterior superior iliac spine. During the assessment, subjects initiated the movement from an upright stance, rapidly descended to a maximal knee flexion position, and immediately executed a maximum effort vertical jump. The computer program automatically calculates the data for each jump. Subjects were instructed to minimize horizontal displacement upon landing while maintaining consistent hand positioning throughout the movement. Each subject performed three trials with 30-s rest intervals between attempts, with the best performance value recorded for analysis.

Sprint test

A 30-m sprint test was employed to evaluate subjects' linear acceleration performance, a metric that has been extensively validated in previous research studies.31 The 30-m sprint test employed manual timing protocols32,33 with three methodological safeguards to ensure accuracy. First, three certified judges—each holding a Level II certification from the Chinese Athletics Association and possessing collegiate-level timing experience—were assigned to conduct manual timing. This approach is supported by previous research demonstrating its validity and reliability.32,34 Second, the study referenced prior validation studies32,35 confirming high consistency between manual and electronic timing systems. Based on these standards, timers underwent systematic training to accurately identify the initiation of movement (defined as the first visible forward displacement) and the moment the athlete's sternum crossed the finish plane. To further enhance timing precision and correct for typical movement-related errors (e.g., false starts, lateral torso rotation at the finish), high-frequency video replay (GoPro HERO9, 100Hz) was used for frame-by-frame visual analysis. This technology provided a traceable and reproducible review mechanism for disputed cases, enabling precise identification and correction of ambiguous trials through repeated slow-motion playback. Third, a pilot validation study (n = 8), conducted two weeks after timing calibration training, compared manual timing results with laser timing measurements (Brower Timing Systems). The analysis revealed strong agreement between methods (ICC = 0.86, 95 % CI: 0.72–0.94), exceeding established reliability thresholds (ICC ≥0.75). To minimize unnecessary energy expenditure, alternate starting and finishing points were used. Each subject performed three maximal-effort 30-m sprints with 60 s of passive recovery between trials.36,37 The fastest valid trial was retained for analysis.

Agility test

The Y-shaped agility test was employed to evaluate subjects' agility performance, serving as a crucial indicator for assessing athletes' agility performance.38 The testing protocol is as follows: The subject assumes a standing position at the starting point (Point A). Upon receiving the initiation signal, the subjects sprint at maximal velocity in a linear path toward the midpoint (Point B). As the subjects approach Point B, the examiner provides a directional cue, whereupon the subjects execute a rapid 45-degree directional change toward the designated endpoint (either Point C on the left or Point D on the right). The timekeeper recorded each subject's total sprint time from initiation of movement to the moment the torso crossed the finish line, adhering strictly to the standardized start/stop criteria outlined in the sprint test protocol. Each subject completed three trials, with 60-s passive recovery intervals between attempts, and the fastest valid performance was documented for analysis. The spatial layout for the test included a 5-m segment from Point A to Point B, followed by an additional 3-m segment extending from Point B to either terminal point (C or D), depending on the trial configuration.

COD test

The COD90° Test was employed to assess subjects' directional change capability. The validity and reliability of the COD90° have been previously documented, with Sporis et al. demonstrating that the COD90 exhibited the highest reliability among six distinct agility assessments.39 The testing protocol proceeded as follows: Subjects assumed a ready position at the starting point (Point A). Upon receiving the signal, subjects sprinted at maximum velocity to Point B, executing a 90° right turn, then proceeded to Point C where they performed a 90° left turn, continued to Point D for another 90° left turn to reach Point E. Upon reaching Point E, subjects executed a 90° right turn toward Point F. At Point F, they performed a 90° right turn to Point G, and finally completed a 90° left turn at Point G before sprinting to the finish line (Point H). Each subjects completed three trials with 60-s recovery intervals between attempts, with the best performance recorded. The timing methodology followed the standardized protocol described in Section sprint test. Note: The distance between Points A and B was 10m, while all other marker points were spaced 5m apart.

Y-balance test

The Lower Extremity Y-Balance Test (YBT) was employed to assess subjects' dynamic balance capabilities. Previous research has demonstrated that balance performance is not influenced by the dominant or non-dominant side of the leg.40 Therefore, this study collected dynamic balance performance data only from the dominant side (dominant leg determination was conducted using the triple jump test method41). The testing protocol was as follows: subjects maintained an upright posture with their hands positioned on the anterior superior iliac spine while standing on one leg at the center of the testing platform. The non-supporting leg's (right side) toe tip contacted the sliding indicator, and while maintaining trunk stability, subjects maximally pushed the slider in three directions: anterior, posterolateral, and posteromedial. Each directional test was performed three times with 1-min rest intervals, and the optimal value was recorded. The dynamic balance index (%) was calculated using the formula: (anterior distance + posterolateral distance + posteromedial distance)/(3 × lower extremity length) × 100.

Statistical analysis

All data were initially evaluated for normality and homogeneity of variance using the Shapiro–Wilk test and Levene's test, respectively, confirming that the dataset met the assumptions required for parametric analysis (P > 0.05 for both tests). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). A two-way repeated-measures ANOVA (2 [RP: RR squats vs. TS squats] × 3 [testing time points: Pre, Post-6h, Post-24h]) was conducted using SPSS 27.0 (IBM Corp.) to assess changes in vertical jump height, linear sprint time, agility, COD, and dynamic balance performance. The significance level was set at α = 0.05. Mauchly's test was applied to evaluate sphericity, and the Greenhouse-Geisser correction was used when sphericity was violated (P < 0.05). When significant main effects or interactions were observed (P < 0.05), post hoc pairwise comparisons employed Bonferroni-adjusted P-values. For significant interactions, the corrected alpha level was set at α = 0.05/3 ≈ 0.016 for simple effects analysis of RP, and α = 0.05/6 ≈ 0.008 for simple effects analysis of testing time points. In cases of significant main effects, the corrected alpha level was maintained at α = 0.05 for the RP main effect, while for the testing time points main effect it was adjusted to α = 0.05/3 ≈ 0.016.

Results

CMJ performance

The CMJ height analysis (Fig. 2) indicated a significant interaction effect between RP and testing time points (F = 3.48, P = 0.039, ηP2 = 0.131). Following a significant interaction, simple effects analyses were performed to examine time effects within each RP and RP effects at each time point. (1) The RP simple effects analysis demonstrated that the RR protocol produced significantly greater jump heights compared to the TS protocol at Post-6h (56.89 ± 4.67 cm vs. 55.94 ± 5.19 cm, P = 0.005, 95 %CI: 0.318 to 1.582). Similarly, at Post-24h, jump heights for the RR protocol measured significantly greater at 54.85 ± 5.13 cm, compared to 54.08 ± 5.07 cm for the TS (P = 0.014, 95 % CI: 0.171 to 1.354). (2) The time simple effects analyses revealed that RR-induced jump height reached its highest observed value at Post-6h, showing significant improvements compared to Pre (55.05 ± 4.73 cm, P = 0.003, 95 %CI: 0.601 to 3.082) and Post-24h (P < 0.001, 95 %CI: 1.127 to 2.956). Similarly, TS-induced jump height attained its maximum recorded value at Post-6h demonstrating a significant improvement over Post-24h measurements (P < 0.001, 95 % CI: 1.234 to 2.474). However, the difference between Post-6h and Pre (54.96 ± 4.76 cm, P = 0.012, 95 % CI: 0.191 to 1.776) did not reach the adjusted significance threshold. Additionally, a significant decrease in jump height was observed at Post-24h compared to Pre (P = 0.007, 95 %CI: 1.533 to −0.209).

Sprint performance

The sprint performance analysis (Fig. 3) indicated a significant interaction effect between RP and testing time points (F = 3.316, P = 0.045, ηP2 = 0.126). Following a significant interaction, simple effects analyses were performed to examine time effects within each RP and RP effects at each time point. (1) The RP simple effects analysis demonstrated significantly faster sprint times in the RR protocol (4.78 ± 0.26 s) compared to the TS protocol (4.84 ± 0.27 s) at Post-6h (P = 0.013, 95 % CI: 0.099 to −0.013). However, no significant differences were observed at Post-24h (4.88 ± 0.31 s vs. 4.96 ± 0.29 s, P = 0.147, 95 % CI: 0.195 to 0.031). (2) The time simple effects analyses revealed that RR-induced sprint performance reached its highest observed value at Post-6h, showing significant improvements compared to Pre (4.93 ± 0.26s, P < 0.001, 95 %CI: 0.189 to −0.106), but no significant difference compared to Post-24h (P = 0.142, 95 %CI: 0.22 to 0.023). Similarly, TS-induced sprint performance attained its highest recorded value at Post-6h, with significant differences from Pre (4.91 ± 0.28s, P < 0.001, 95 %CI: 0.092 to −0.051) and Post-24h (P < 0.001, 95 %CI: 0.155 to −0.095). Additionally, a significant decrease in sprint performance was observed at Post-24h compared to Pre (P < 0.001, 95 %CI: 0.032 to 0.075).

DPE of sprint performance.

Agility performance

The Agility time analysis (Fig. 4) indicated a significant interaction effect between RP and testing time points (F = 3.645, P = 0.034, ηP2 = 0.137). Following a significant interaction, simple effects analyses were performed to examine time effects within each RP and RP effects at each time point. (1) The RP simple effects analysis showed that the RR protocol produced significantly better agility times compared to the TS protocol at Post-6h (2.29 ± 0.14 s vs. 2.36 ± 0.18 s, P = 0.005, 95 % CI: 0.115 to −0.023). However, at Post-24h, although the RR protocol showed better agility times (2.32 ± 0.21 s vs. 2.39 ± 0.18 s), the difference did not reach the adjusted significance threshold (P = 0.036, 95 % CI: 0.141 to −0.005). (2) The time simple effects analyses revealed that RR-induced agility performance attained its highest observed value at Post-6h, showing significant improvements compared to Pre (2.42 ± 0.18s, P < 0.001, 95 %CI: 0.169 to −0.085), but no significant difference compared to Post-24h (P = 0.631, 95 %CI: 0.1 to 0.033). Additionally, at Post-24h there were significant improvement compared to Pre (P = 0.001, 95 %CI: 0.153 to −0.034). Similarly, TS-induced agility performance reached its maximum recorded value at Post-6h, with significant differences from Pre (2.43 ± 0.18s, P < 0.001, 95 %CI: 0.097 to −0.046) and Post-24h (P = 0.007, 95 %CI: 0.066 to −0.009). Furthermore, at Post-24h, although there was an improvement compared to Pre (P = 0.019, 95 % CI: 0.063 to −0.005), the difference did not reach the adjusted significance threshold.

DPE of agility performance.

COD performance

The COD time analysis (Fig. 5a) indicated no significant interaction effect between RP and testing time points (F = 2.658, P = 0.093, ηP2 = 0.195). Given the absence of a significant interaction, main effects analyses were performed to independently assess the contributions of RP and testing time points. While the main effect of RP was not significant (F = 1.041, P = 0.318, η2 P = 0.043), a significant main effect (Fig. 5b) was observed for testing time points (F = 9.457, P < 0.001, η2 P = 0.291). Post-hoc analysis demonstrated that COD performance at Post-6h (7.08 ± 0.32s) was significantly superior to both Pre (7.19 ± 0.29s, P = 0.005, 95 %CI: 0.189 to −0.031) and Post-24h (7.17 ± 0.32s, P < 0.001, 95 %CI: 0.142 to −0.047).

DPE of COD performance.

Y-balance performance

The YBT analysis (Fig. 6a) indicated no significant interaction effect between RP and testing time points (F = 1.155, P = 0.33, ηP2 = 0.095). Given the absence of a significant interaction, main effects analyses were performed to independently assess the contributions of RP and testing time points. While the main effect of RP was not significant (F = 1.987, P = 0.172, η2 P = 0.08), a significant main effect (Fig. 6b) was observed for testing time points (F = 78.409, P < 0.001, η2 P = 0.773). Post-hoc analysis demonstrated that Y-balance performance at Post-6h (81.53 ± 3.39 %) was significantly superior to Pre (79.22 ± 3.44 %, P < 0.001, 95 %CI: 1.616 to 3.007). The Post-24h Y-balance scores (82.68 ± 3.6 %) were significantly higher than both Pre (P < 0.001, 95 %CI: 2.507 to 4.424) and Post-6h (P < 0.001, 95 %CI: 0.722 to 1.585).

Discussion

The impact of 6-hour DPE on athletic performance

The present results reveal that both RP protocols induced significant DPE in certain athletic performance metrics at Post-6h. Specifically, RR improved CMJ, sprint, and agility, while TS enhanced sprint and agility—results that are generally consistent with previous research conclusions.6, 7, 8,15 Notably, while previous studies using the CS protocol have shown superior improvements over TS in CMJ and sprint at Post-6h,15 the present study is the first to employ its modified variant, RR, and demonstrate significantly greater efficacy in CMJ, sprint, and agility performance at the same time point.

The observed DPE effects may be attributed to underlying physiological mechanisms involving circadian rhythms and neuroendocrine responses. Studies have shown that endogenous testosterone secretion follows a circadian rhythm, peaking upon awakening and declining over 24 h.42 Athletes’ hormonal profiles are closely linked to exercise capacity and functional performance, with elevated free testosterone levels and higher testosterone-to-cortisol ratios strongly associated with enhanced muscle strength and power output.43 Resistance training may regulate circadian hormonal fluctuations,6 while RP interventions increase serum testosterone by ∼17 % during afternoon training.11 In this experimental investigation, both TS and RR protocols represent fundamental resistance movement patterns, which likely engage neuroendocrine mechanisms that suppress diurnal hormonal fluctuations and elevate serum testosterone levels, thereby contributing to enhanced athletic performance in the afternoon. Additionally, McGowan et al.44 demonstrated that athletes who performed RP in the morning exhibited elevated core temperatures during the afternoon testing sessions, and increased muscle temperature has been validated to positively influence athletic performance.45 Based on this analysis, the implementation of RP in the morning may potentially enhance afternoon athletic performance by optimizing endocrine function and improving thermoregulatory capacity.

While the observed superiority of RR over TS in inducing DPE for CMJ and sprint performance at Post-6h aligns with findings reported by Wang et al.,15 the present study extends previous evidence by demonstrating, for the first time, an additional advantage of RR in agility performance at the same time point. A plausible explanation is that the RR configuration, characterized by brief intra-set rest intervals, permits partial ATP resynthesis, which may preserve work capacity and sustain movement quality during repeated efforts, thereby enhancing sprint performance.46 Previous studies have reported that RR exercise exhibits a fast rate of ATP resynthesis compared to TS exercise,46,47 affording greater potential to sustain or optimize barbell velocity and power output during resistance training.48 Consistent with this, Jukic and others report that RR more effectively maintains barbell velocity and power across a session than TS.23,24,49 The superiority of RR for CMJ performance may relate to its influence on the mechanics of the eccentric–concentric transition. The stretch–shortening cycle (SSC) is a biomechanical–neurophysiological mechanism whereby a rapid eccentric action immediately preceding a concentric action augments force production by storing and releasing elastic energy and engaging stretch reflex pathways.50 Repeated RR exposure has been shown to enhance lower-limb SSC capacity, which in turn contributes to improved CMJ performance.51, 52, 53

This investigation uniquely distinguishes between agility and COD performance by categorizing agility as reactive movement in response to external stimuli, and COD as pre-planned directional changes. The Y-shaped agility test was used to assess agility,54 while the COD90° test was utilized to evaluate COD. The findings demonstrate that both RP can induce DPE in COD and agility at Post-6h. While the RR protocol showed significantly better agility than TS at Post-6h, no inter-protocol differences emerged in COD performance. This absence of COD differences may stem from two factors. First, COD performance integrates voluntary directional changes and rotational movements along set trajectories.55 The COD90° test required repeated acceleration-deceleration-reacceleration cycles, emphasizing lower-limb eccentric braking and controlled eccentric contractions.55 However, squats in both RP protocols primarily stimulated concentric rather than eccentric force production in lower limbs, limiting COD-specific adaptations. Second, COD performance is influenced not only by muscular strength but also by technical components such as running velocity, directional change mechanics, and footwork precision.54 As neither RR nor TS protocols targeted these skill-based elements, the absence of inter-protocol differences in COD is consistent with their shared emphasis on strength alone. Nonetheless, the DPE observed in COD at Post-6h is likely attributable to the general neuromuscular potentiation mechanisms discussed earlier. In contrast, RR's superior performance in agility at Post-6h can be attributed to several factors. The agility test (Y-shaped run) evaluated rapid responsiveness, unanticipated directional shifts, and adaptive footwork,55 requiring instantaneous reactions to unexpected stimuli and maximal-velocity sprints in designated directions. Such performance heavily relies on central nervous system excitability. As noted earlier, the TS protocol induced greater exercise-induced fatigue than RR, and elevated fatigue significantly reduces neural excitability.56 Additionally, agility correlates positively with cognitive function,54 while fatigue impairs cognitive processing.57 Thus, the TS protocol's higher physiological fatigue likely compromised athletes' agility via diminished cognitive capacity and neural excitability compared to RR.

The impact of 24-hour DPE on athletic performance

Nevertheless, contrary to previous findings,9 the present study revealed that TS reduced CMJ and sprint performance at Post-24h compared to baseline, whereas RR significantly improved agility while maintaining CMJ and sprint performance near baseline levels. Notably, RR significantly outperformed TS in CMJ performance at Post-24h, while no significant inter-protocol differences were observed for any other performance measures at this time point. Interestingly, neither Y-balance nor COD performance showed protocol-specific effects. Y-balance demonstrated a progressive enhancement in DPE, reaching its peak at Post-24h.

The precise physiological mechanisms underlying DPE at Post-24h remain incompletely understood. Beyond the aforementioned factors, mechanical stiffness has been proposed as a potential mechanism.9 Research has demonstrated that resistance training induces both acute58 and chronic59 increases in musculotendinous stiffness. Given the significant correlation between joint stiffness and neuromuscular performance,60 these alterations in mechanical stiffness may represent a crucial mechanism for enhanced neuromuscular performance following RP (including both immediate and 24–48h DPE).

Except for Y-balance performance, all other metrics declined at Post-24h—a pattern consistent with meta-analytical evidence indicating transient reductions in explosive force following high-load resistance training, primarily attributed to exercise-induced muscle damage (EIMD).61 Specifically, the meta-analysis61 revealed that even elite athletes exhibit performance deterioration due to EIMD-mediated dual neuromuscular effects characterized by progressive delayed-onset muscle soreness and inflammatory cascades.62, 63, 64, 65 Although the current cohort of physical education students had over two years of resistance training experience and a back squat 1RM ≥ 1.5 × body mass, their physiological recovery capacity remained comparatively limited relative to elite athletes. These limitations manifest in critical biological domains including hormonal clearance efficiency,66 neural drive restoration,67 and stress adaptation processes.68 Moreover, the RP protocols used in the present study align closely with those examined in the meta-analysis in terms of loading and modality. Therefore, the partial performance declines observed at Post-24h may reflect similar EIMD-related mechanisms. Overall, these findings emphasize that even moderately trained individuals are vulnerable to EIMD-related functional declines following high-intensity resistance protocols, with outcomes shaped by both intrinsic recovery limitations and program design features. Yet, the absence of direct DOMS biomarkers (e.g., serum CK) in the present study limits definitive attribution to muscle damage, and the observed trends may alternatively reflect context-specific fatigue kinetics rather than generalized EIMD responses.

Further, it was found that although the DPE of agility performance after induction with both RP protocols was slightly lower at Post-24h compared to Post-6h, the RR protocol maintained significantly higher agility performance at Post-24h compared to Pre. The underlying mechanism for this phenomenon may be attributed to the Y-shaped agility test's relatively modest neuromuscular and metabolic demands, given its displacement distance of only approximately 8m compared to sprinting and COD tests. Consequently, agility performance may not require the sustained high-intensity output characteristic of sprinting and directional change capabilities, thus experiencing reduced susceptibility to exercise-induced fatigue. And ultimately showed a significant boost relative to Pre due to previous RP stimulation. Meanwhile, at 24 h post-intervention, the RR protocol yielded significantly greater CMJ performance than TS. This effect likely reflects the inclusion of controlled intra-set rest periods in RR, which expedite physiological recovery.69 According to a study by Glaister et al., 15-s intra-set rest intervals restore power output to 79.7 ± 2.3 % of baseline levels, while 30-s intervals improve maximal power output and reduce lactate buildup.70

Finally, the YBT outcomes warrant focused discussion. The present study is the first to incorporate balance performance as a metric in DPE research, demonstrating that RP protocols can significantly enhance dynamic balance—a novel finding in the field. The improvement in YBT performance at 24 h post-intervention could be ascribed to acute gains in lower-limb strength elicited by RP. This interpretation accords with prior evidence demonstrating robust associations between lower-extremity strength and YBT outcomes. For example, Lee et al.71 identified significant correlations between hip extensor and knee flexor strength levels and reach distances in anterior and posterior directions. Additional studies6,8, 9, 10,72 corroborate that enhanced lower limb strength and power output directly improve postural stability. In the present study, RP protocols notably improved lower limb explosive force (as reflected in countermovement jump performance). Secondly, the absence of significant interaction or main effects related to RP suggests that DPE's impact on balance was predominantly time-dependent rather than protocol-specific. The results of the YBT are influenced by several key factors, including core stability, joint mobility, and neuromuscular coordination. However, both TS and RR protocols primarily emphasized strength and power development, with limited impact on soft tissue extensibility or joint range of motion. This mechanistic emphasis may account for the similar YBT outcomes observed across protocols. Notably, the YBT uniquely demonstrated a progressive enhancement in DPE over time, with Post-24h performance exceeding both Post-6h and baseline values. This temporal trend may be attributed to the YBT's low movement complexity and slow execution speed, which likely reduce susceptibility to acute metabolic stressors such as lactate accumulation or inflammatory responses. Supporting this interpretation, Cooper et al.73 demonstrated that balance performance remains resilient to muscular fatigue-induced declines in power output, aligning with the present observations.

Conclusion

Following the administration of the two RP protocols, most performance parameters exhibited DPE at Post-6h. The RR protocol induced significant improvements in CMJ, sprint, and agility, while the TS protocol elicited DPE in sprint and agility only. RR demonstrated superior enhancements over TS, particularly in CMJ, sprint, and agility performance. By Post-24h, DPE effects had generally attenuated, except for Y-balance. Notably, RR maintained CMJ and sprint performance near baseline, while agility remained significantly elevated compared to Pre. At this time point, RR also demonstrated significantly superior CMJ performance relative to TS. Additionally, both protocols induced DPE in COD and Y-balance, with Y-balance peaking at Post-24h, though no significant inter-protocol differences were observed for these measures. Overall, both TS and RR protocols effectively induced DPE, with RR showing greater efficacy across select performance domains. Nonetheless, the specificity of RR's effects underscores the need for further research to confirm and broaden these findings.

Limitation

Several limitations in this study warrant further investigation and refinement. Firstly, the absence of neurophysiological measurements, including surface electromyography parameters and endocrine hormone levels (such as testosterone concentration), somewhat constrained our in-depth analysis of DPE. Secondly, performance assessments were conducted only at Post-6h and Post-24h, failing to track the complete DPE fluctuations within a 48-h window, thus limiting our ability to fully characterize the temporal dynamics of the phenomenon. Thirdly, due to experimental constraints, the study population was restricted to healthy and physically active male students, potentially limiting the generalizability of the findings to elite athletic populations. Additionally, although %1RM was prescribed from 1RM testing, this method has inherent constraints, including test–retest variability, error in estimating true maximal strength, and limited sensitivity to momentary neuromuscular status across the force–velocity continuum. In the present study, wherein 85 % 1RM back squats served as the priming stimulus, fluctuations in 1RM could have altered the effective load, thereby influencing the magnitude of delayed potentiation and, in some cases, eliciting excess fatigue rather than facilitation. Standardized procedures were implemented to attenuate these effects; nevertheless, future work may benefit from velocity-based training (VBT) and individualized load–velocity profiling to achieve more precise and adaptive load prescription in DPE-oriented protocols. Furthermore, while we recognize that high-intensity RP may effectively induce DPE for same-day afternoon performance, the accumulated fatigue from the substantial loading stimulus did not yield the anticipated performance enhancements across multiple parameters at Post-24h, diverging from our initial hypothesis. Notably, the absence of sessional perceived exertion (RPE) assessment limited our understanding of individual perceptual responses to DPE. The RR protocol demonstrated distinct advantages in inducing DPE, suggesting that future research should further examine how different training intensities and volumes modulate DPE responses. Finally, this study did not compare the CS and RR structures. Future investigations should systematically compare the time course and magnitude of DPE under CS and RR protocols to clarify the relative efficacy of different intra-set rest allocation strategies in optimizing athletic performance.

Practical applications

The RR protocol serves as a scientifically validated induction strategy for maximizing DPE through high-intensity stimulation. For instance, when competitions are scheduled for afternoon sessions, strength and conditioning practitioners can implement high-intensity RP in the morning (6 h pre-competition) to optimize athletic performance. When aiming to maximize DPE activation Post-24h through high-intensity RP, research teams recommend implementing more cautious protocols. Future DPE investigations should explore several key directions: First, conduct an in-depth analysis of optimal loading parameter combinations (including intensity and volume) and examine the mechanistic impacts of various RP on performance-specific adaptations. Second, systematically investigate DPE responses across different genders and strength-level cohorts. Third, employ multi-time point test tracking protocols (1.75–48h) integrated with physiological and biochemical marker monitoring to comprehensively evaluate DPE's temporal dynamics traits and underlying physiological mechanisms. To minimize test-induced fatigue or potentiation and to more objectively characterize DPE at discrete time points, each participant was evaluated at a single, randomly assigned post-intervention time point per session. Across repeated sessions, all participants completed assessments at every designated time point in a randomized, counterbalanced sequence. These research directions will enhance our fundamental understanding of DPE phenomena and provide more robust scientific evidence for practical applications.
