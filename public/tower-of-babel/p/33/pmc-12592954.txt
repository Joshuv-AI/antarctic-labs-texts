Mechanical Power and its Components vs Driving Pressure for Predicting Mortality in Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome: A Prospective Observational Study
Authors: Pratik P Medhi, Souvik Chaudhuri, Vishwas Parampalli, Shubhada Devadiga, Abhilash B Mareguddi, Shubhada Karanth, Shwethapriya Rao, Thejesh Srinivas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimMechanical power (MP) has been proposed as a predictor of acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) mortality, but evidence remains conflicting. We aimed to study its prognostic utility in predicting mortality in ARDS.Patients and methodsThis was a single-center prospective observational study including 137 ARDS patients. The organ dysfunction scores, MP and its components (elastic static, elastic dynamic, and resistive power), driving pressure (DP), severity of acute kidney injury (AKI), lung ultrasound scores, pulmonary artery hypertension, days of intensive care unit (ICU) stay, and mortality outcomes were noted.ResultsOut of 137 ARDS patients, there were 73 (53.3%) non-survivors. Mechanical power was significantly higher with median [interquartile range (IQR)] 29 (24.55-32) J/min in the mortality group and 24 (20-28.75) J/min in the survival group (p-value < 0.001, Mann-Whitney U-test). However, MP was not an independent predictor of mortality. Driving pressure is an independent predictor of mortality with DP >16 cm H2O, hazard ratio (HR) for mortality 2.925 [(95% confidence interval (CI) 1.778-4.810), Cox-proportional hazard p-value < 0.001]. Out of the three components of MP, dynamic elastic power component is the main contributor to high MP (=29 J/min).ConclusionAlthough MP is significantly higher in ARDS non-survivors as compared to survivors, adjustments for confounders showed that it is not an independent predictor of mortality. Driving pressure is an independent predictor of mortality. Introduction

Mechanical power (MP) is the total energy that is transmitted to the pulmonary system from the ventilator per unit time due to the repeated cycles of tidal volume delivered while ventilating a patient.1–3 This leads to stress due to the pressure contributed by the forces that are perpendicular to the point of contact in the alveoli and also a strain caused by the change in volume or alveolar expansion.2 This stress and strain aggravates the lung injury in the already inflamed alveoli in acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS).2 There are three distinct components of MP. First, the work done to overcome the basal tension due to positive end-expiratory pressure or PEEP (elastic static power). Second is the work for inflating the alveoli that depends on pulmonary elastance (elastic dynamic power). The third is called the resistive power (work against the resistance).2 Though theoretically sound, MP involves tedious calculations that are often difficult at the bedside.4 Also, there is conflicting evidence regarding its utility as a ventilator variable predicting outcomes in viral pneumonia and ARDS.2,5,6

On the other hand, driving pressure (DP) is easier to calculate at the bedside and has been shown to prognosticate outcomes in ARDS.7–9 Thus, we aimed to determine the clinical utility of MP to predict mortality outcomes in ARDS.

Aim

Predictive utility of the MP of ventilator as a predictor of mortality in ARDS patients as compared to DP.

Primary Objective

To investigate the prognostic significance of MP of ventilator and DP on all-cause mortality using time-to-event analysis, and to determine whether MP or DP independently predicts adverse outcomes in ARDS patients.

Secondary Objectives

To determine the discrete component of MP that has the highest diagnostic accuracy to predict high MP in ARDS patients.

To determine the discrete component of MP that has the highest diagnostic accuracy to predict high MP in ARDS patients.

To determine the sensitivity and specificity of DP to predict mortality in pulmonary and extra pulmonary ARDS.

To determine the sensitivity and specificity of DP to predict mortality in pulmonary and extra pulmonary ARDS.

Outcome

Intensive care unit (ICU) mortality in patients with ARDS.

Patients And Methods

This study is reported as per the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Study Design

This was a single-center prospective observational study conducted between March 2024 and June 2025 at a medical college. The study was started after due ethical clearance was obtained from the institutional ethical committee (IEC 432/2023) and registered with the Clinical Trials Registry of India (CTRI/2024/03/063731). The patient's accepted representative provided written informed consent as per the due institutional protocol. Consecutive sampling method for recruitment was followed.

Inclusion Criteria

Adults aged 18-90 years of either gender.

Acute respiratory distress syndrome as diagnosed by Berlin's criteria.10

Exclusion Criteria

Patients with an ARDS diagnosis of more than 48 hours at the time of recruitment.

Patients of ARDS who are not on invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV).

Sample size was based on events per variable (EPV) rule in a regression model with a minimum of six independent predictors of mortality in ARDS based on our previous study on ARDS, and mortality in ARDS being about 44%.11,12 Based on a minimum EPV value of 10, the sample size N was13

The demographic parameters—age, gender, co-morbidities, acute physiology and chronic health evaluation II (APACHE II) score, sequential organ failure assessment (SOFA) score, and modified nutrition risk in the critically ill (mNUTRIC) score were recorded on the day of ICU admission. The lung ultrasound score (LUS), echocardiography parameters, pulmonary artery hypertension (PAH), right ventricular systolic pressure (RVSP), and highest partial pressure of arterial oxygen to fraction of inspired oxygen (PaO2/ FiO2) ratio were noted between 24 and 48 hours of ARDS. The IMV parameters on volume control ventilation (VCV) were noted between 24 and 48 hours of ARDS onset and charted on respiratory therapy charts, and the worst values of MP and the corresponding DP were selected from the charts. We took the time duration of 24-48 hours, as studies have shown that ARDS' outcomes are best predicted over 24-72 hours rather than at the onset itself.14–16 The calculations of the various ventilator pressures are as follows, and were measured when the patients were on deep sedation or deep sedation along with neuromuscular blockade, with an inspiratory hold, based on the discretion of the treating intensivist, ensuring that there was no asynchrony:

In the study by Chiumello et al., this was the comprehensive formula for the calculation of MP in patients on VCV, that necessitates an inspiratory hold.4 For the measurement of DP, an inspiratory hold is required. The same inspiratory hold will also serve for calculation of MP using this formula. Thus, this formula would be best suited for the patients on VCV.

Second, it was a comprehensive formula that incorporated both the resistive and elastic components of the mechanical breath delivered, ensuring a more accurate measurement of MP.4

The presence of acute kidney injury (AKI) at 24-48 hours, kidney disease: Improving global outcomes (KDIGO) stage, need for renal replacement therapy during ICU stay, need for prone ventilation was noted. Kidney disease: Improving global outcomes stages 2 and 3 were considered as moderate-severe AKI. The patients were followed up for length of ICU stay and mortality at the end of ICU stay.

Statistical Analysis

The statistical analysis was performed by using the Statistical Software for Social Sciences (SPSS), version 29, IBM, Armonk, USA. For the variables that were continuous like age, APACHE II score, SOFA score, MP, DP, and the normality of distribution was ascertained using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. The parametric continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and the non-parametric continuous variables were expressed as median and interquartile range (IQR). The parameters like gender and the presence of AKI or absence of AKI, that were categorical in nature, were expressed as frequencies and percentages. The ARDS patients were classified based on outcomes into survival and mortality groups, and the variables that were different between the survivors and mortality groups were analyzed. In case of continuous variables with parametric distribution, the Independent Student t-test was used to determine the significant difference between the survival and mortality groups. For continuous variables that were distributed in a non-parametric fashion, the Mann-Whitney U-test was used for the same.

The strength of association between categorical variables and mortality outcomes was analyzed using the Chi-square test. The variables that were found to be significantly different between the survival and mortality outcomes in ARDS were selected for the logistic regression model development.

For each of the selected variables, the univariate analysis was done to predict mortality, and the p-value, odds ratio (OR), and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were determined. The variables that were found to be significant to predict mortality were then moved for the multivariable logistic regression model. However, the principles of multicollinearity and parsimony were also considered while selecting variables for the multivariable logistic regression to predict mortality among ARDS patients. To evaluate possible multicollinearity, variance inflation factor (VIF), tolerance, eigenvalue, and condition index were calculated.

In case of variables that were related, the variable that had the highest OR in the univariate analysis was considered for moving to multivariable logistic regression model. The independent predictors of mortality in ARDS patients were found after multivariable logistic regression, and the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, area under curve (AUC), cut-off value, sensitivity, specificity, 95% CI, and p-value were analyzed.

The time to event (mortality) and probability of survival over time based on the cut-off value of the independent predictors of mortality was plotted using the Kaplan-Meier plot. Log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test p-value was determined to compare the survival plot between two groups. The Cox proportional regression for hazard ratio (HR) for predicting mortality was calculated.

Since we aimed to compare the utility of MP as compared to DP in predicting mortality in ARDS, we included both MP as well as DP in the regression model, though DP is a part of MP. We examined collinearity diagnostics using the VIF, tolerance, eigenvalue, and condition index. When the MP and DP multicollinearity were tested in the multivariable logistic regression model, the values for VIF was 1.20 for MP and 1.19 for DP, with tolerance of 0.83, and acceptable eigen value (<0.5) and condition index (<5). Thus, in the multivariable regression model, there was no concerning multicollinearity that could have made the model unstable.

The AUC of DP to predict mortality was found to be significant. The AUC of DP to predict mortality was 0.804, p-value <0.001, 95% CI (0.734-0.875), cut-off ≥16 cm H2O, sensitivity 69%, specificity 77%, positive predictive value 78.13%, negative predictive value 68.49%, diagnostic accuracy of model was 73%, positive likelihood ratio 3.13, and negative likelihood ratio 0.40. There was a significant association between a high DP (≥16 cm H2O) and mortality. Among the 64 patients who had high DP, 50 (78.1%) were nonsurvivors, whereas among the 73 patients with DP <16 cm H2O, there were 23 (31.5%) non-survivors (Chi-square p-value < 0.001). The Kaplan Meier survival plot showed that the median time of survival in patients with DP ≥16 cm H2O was significantly shorter as compared to patients with DP <16 cm H2O (Fig. 1). The patients with DP ≥16 cm H2O had a higher HR for mortality (Fig. 1).

Kaplan-Meier survival plot of ARDS patients with DP ≥16 cm H2O vs DP <16 cm H2O. The Kaplan-Meier survival plot showed that the median time of survival in patients with DP ≥16 cm H2O was significantly shorter (7 days) as compared to 18 days in patients with DP <16 cm H2O (Log-rank Mantel-Cox p-value <0.001) (Fig. 4). The patients with DP ≥16 cm H2O had a higher HR for mortality (Cox-proportional hazard regression p-value <0.001, HR 2.925, 95% CI: 1.778-4.810)

Diagnostic accuracy of DP to predict mortality in ARDS patients

Among the patients with high MP (≥29 J/min, which is the median MP in the mortality group), the elastic dynamic power had the highest odds of predicting it [OR 1.993, 95% CI (1.510-2.632), p-value < 0.001]. The static dynamic power and “resistive power” components were not found to be significant to predict a high MP ≥29 J/min.

The AUC of elastic dynamic power to predict high MP ≥29 J/min was 0.771, cut-off ≥7.80 J/min, p-value < 0.001, 95% CI (0.693-0.849), sensitivity 70%, specificity 60%, and diagnostic accuracy 69%.

The sensitivity of DP to predict mortality in pulmonary ARDS was 75%, cut-off ≥16 cm H2O, as compared to sensitivity of 55% in extra-pulmonary ARDS.

The AUC of DP to predict mortality in pulmonary ARDS was higher [0.809, p-value <0.001, cut-off ≥16 cm H2O, 95% CI (0.723-0.895), sensitivity 75%, specificity 71%, diagnostic accuracy 72%), as compared to the AUC of DP to predict mortality in extra-pulmonary ARDS [AUC 0.786, p-value <0.001, cut-off ≥16 cm H2O 95% CI (0.655-0.916), sensitivity 55%, specificity 87%, diagnostic accuracy 66%].

Discussion

We found that though MP of ventilator was significantly greater in the mortality group (median 29 J/min) in ARDS patients as compared to the survivors (median 24 J/min), it was not an independent predictor of mortality. In patients with high MP ≥29 J/min, it was the elastic dynamic power (related to DP) that has the maximum contribution, as compared to elastic static power [positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) related], or resistive power (peak pressure-related). Driving pressure was an independent predictor of mortality and has greater sensitivity to predict mortality in pulmonary ARDS as compared to extra-pulmonary ARDS (Figs 2 to 4).

The patients with DP ≥16 cm H2O had a higher HR for mortality (Cox-proportional hazard regression p-value < 0.001, HR 2.925, 95% CI: 1.778-4.810)

Our finding that MP was not a predictor of mortality, was similar to a recent study by Barbeta et al.2 The authors concluded that MP or its components were not a predictor of mortality in 799 COVID-19 acute respiratory failure patients who were mechanically ventilated.2 The DP-related dynamic elastic power was significantly higher in the non-survivors as compared to survivors, in that study.2

Our results, however, were different from a large study on 4,549 ARDS patients by Costa et al., where MP was found to be a predictor of mortality.16 However, the authors also concluded that an easier calculation using DP and RR was also equally good.16 An in-depth reading of the study shows that even though MP was found to be an independent predictor, it was the DP-related elastic dynamic power component of MP that was contributory to MP being a prognostic factor for mortality outcomes.16

There are five variables involved in the calculation of MP-RR, TV, peak pressure, plateau pressure, and PEEP. These variables constitute three discrete components of MP, that is, elastic static power (PEEP-related), elastic dynamic power (DP-related), and resistive power (peak pressure-related).2 Of these, the DP-related elastic dynamic power is the most significant contributor to high MP, whereas elastic static power and resistive power have lesser prognostic value.16

Though we did not find MP to be an independent predictor of mortality after regression analysis, and DP was proven to be an independent predictor, our findings underlie the same conceptual principle that DP being an index of the degree of stress and strain per breath on the lung is the most important factor in ARDS lung injury. That is the reason why DP-related elastic dynamic power is the most important contributor to mortality, as was found by Costa et al.16 We also found that in patients with high MP ≥29 J/ min, the elastic dynamic power had the greatest contribution and the highest OR of mortality among the components of MP (OR 2.025) in the analysis.

Mechanical power can increase with RR, VT, and inspiratory flow (resistive component). But these components may increase airway energy expenditure without proportionally increasing alveolar tissue stress if DP is held constant. Driving pressure intrinsically incorporates alveolar compliance, and thus quantifies the tidal strain applied to the aerated portion of the lung—the so-called “baby lung” in ARDS. This makes it a direct surrogate for the stress (transpulmonary pressure) and strain (change in lung volume based on compliance) that drive ventilator-induced lung injury. In contrast, MP represents the total energy delivered to the pulmonary system over a period, incorporating not only DP but also tidal volume as well as RR, but also resistive pressure, DP, and PEEP. While these additional components influence the calculated power, many do not independently contribute to parenchymal injury in the same way strain does. As a result, the inclusion of these extra terms can dilute the relationship between MP and patient outcomes, whereas DP remains more specifically linked to the pathophysiological mechanism that determines prognosis. This concept is well explained in the study by Costa et al.16 The authors noted that for similar changes in VT, the low compliance condition (<14 mL/cm H2O) has the steepest changes in the OR for mortality, as compared to high compliance (>35 mL/cm H2O).16 This was probably why Coppola et al. had found that it is not MP alone, rather MP normalized to pulmonary compliance that predicted mortality.6 These concepts probably will enable a deeper understanding of the contradictory findings regarding MP and ventilated patients, both ARDS and patients with respiratory failure on IMV, yet all emphasizing the role of the DP component.2,6,16,17

Thus, complicated calculations for MP may be dispensable, and the simpler DP calculation therapeutic strategies may be more beneficial. The algebraic formula for the calculation of MP is difficult at the bedside, and can lead to bias and inaccuracies.18 It is also less utilized as it is difficult to calculate at the bedside.19 Literature on patients who were on IMV and not exclusively low compliance conditions like ARDS, have shown that MP is an independent predictor of mortality.19,20 Mechanical power has also been shown to be high in patients in the mortality group as compared to survival group in ARDS studies similar to this study, but not as an independent predictor after adjusted regression analysis.21–23 However, in patients with low compliance conditions like ARDS, DP may be the most important independent prognostic ventilator variable.2,6

Considering that the elastic dynamic power is the most important component that predicts high MP, a simpler equation at bedside “0.098 × VT × RR × 0.5 DP” may be utilized for incorporating the role of power in predicting outcomes and planning ventilator strategies in ARDS patients.

Strengths and Limitations

We have analyzed whether MP is a predictor of mortality after adjusting confounders like mNUTRIC score, organ dysfunction like moderate-severe AKI, and presence of PAH using multivariable logistic regression. We have also determined that it is the elastic dynamic power component of MP that is the most significant and may be calculated using a simpler available equation. We have considered MP not at the onset of ARDS, but the worst values between 24 and 48 hours, for a better clinical significance. However, being a single-center study has its own limitations. We did not measure the trends of MP over time and MP normalized to compliance, which could have been more significant. We have calculated the MP of ventilator in patients with volume-controlled ventilation, as this is the mode used at our center for ventilating ARDS patients.

Conclusion

Mechanical power is significantly higher among non-survivors than survivors of ARDS, but it is not an independent predictor when adjusted for confounders. The elastic dynamic power is the most significant component of high MP. Driving pressure ≥16 cm H2O is an independent predictor of mortality in ARDS and has greater sensitivity in pulmonary ARDS as compared to extra-pulmonary ARDS.
