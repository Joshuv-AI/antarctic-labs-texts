Large-scale modeling for housing condition prediction using machine learning algorithms
Authors: Kyusik Kim, Tisha Holmes, Emily Powell, Christopher K Uejio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhile housing price prediction is well-studied, the prediction of large-scale housing conditions remains underexplored due to data limitations. This paper addresses this gap by developing a machine-learning model to predict housing conditions across the United States. We integrated property-level data from the Warren Group with neighborhood characteristics from the U.S. Census Bureau’s American Community Survey and trained three gradient-boosting algorithms: CatBoost, LightGBM, and XGBoost. Despite XGBoost’s slightly higher balanced accuracy, CatBoost was selected as the best model due to its superior resistance to overfitting. The final model’s predictions were aggregated to census tracts, ZIP code tabulation areas, and a 36.13 km2 resolution hexagonal grid for national-scale spatial analysis. The resulting comprehensive dataset can serve as a valuable resource for researchers and practitioners to analyze the geography of housing quality with applications in urban planning, disaster management, community resilience, public health, and more.Subject terms: Social sciences, Research data

Background & Summary

Housing is a fundamental determinant of human well-being, extending beyond basic shelter to influence health, safety, and overall quality of life. Residential housing quality, housing standards, the tenants’ lived experiences, and the surrounding neighborhood context can affect degrees of exposure to environmental hazards1,2. For example, living in poor-quality housing can negatively increase exposure to poor indoor air quality, water leaks, thermal extremes, lead, and/or overcrowding1,3. This concomitantly increases the risk of respiratory, cardiovascular, mental and behavioral health disease, childhood development, infectious diseases, falls and injuries, and premature mortality4–6. From urban planning and policy perspectives, understanding the spatial distribution of poor-quality (or high-quality) housing can help identify opportunities to implement community resiliency measures and residential improvements. Moreover, improvements in housing quality contribute significantly to the well-being of residents7.

Although housing condition is a fundamental component in understanding residential environment or community well-being8,9, surprisingly little effort has been made to produce large-scale housing condition data that can allow us to capture the full spatial distribution of housing quality. One exception is the study by Xu et al.10, which used deep learning and crowdsourced images to predict house quality in rural China. However, their prediction focused exclusively on rural houses. Beyond this, similar large-scale housing condition datasets or predictions are scarce in other countries as well.

In the U.S., limitations in existing housing condition data further constrain national-scale analyses. The U.S. Census Bureau’s American Housing Survey11 provides information on housing quality, finance, and maintenance, but housing quality data are available only at the state and metropolitan levels. Relatedly, the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development’s Comprehensive Housing Affordability Strategy’s data12 combine housing affordability, occupant density, and missing amenities (e.g., kitchen, plumbing) but does not directly measure housing conditions. These gaps might stem partly from inconsistent collection and appraisal practices across states and counties, which result in incomplete and inconsistent coverage, particularly at the national level. Consequently, researchers and policymakers lack spatially granular information needed to develop a thorough understanding of housing condition patterns across regions.

In contrast, an extensive body of work has focused on housing price prediction13–16. Yet, because housing prices can be inconsistently related to the adequacy of housing conditions13, a notable gap remains in large-scale studies that specifically aim to predict housing conditions. As a result, no national-scale, spatially detailed dataset currently exists that directly represents housing condition patterns for the entire U.S.

To address this limitation, the present study represents the first nationwide effort to predict housing conditions using machine-learning techniques and produce a complete dataset for the U.S. We develop models that infer housing condition scores based on property-level characteristics (e.g., year built, bedrooms, bathrooms, external materials) and neighborhood-level factors (e.g., population density, mobile homes, median household income). The output provides predicted housing condition scores for all U.S. housing units, aggregated at the census tract level, ZIP code tabulation area (ZCTA), and H3 hexagonal grid cells at resolution 6 (approximately 36.13 km2)17 to facilitate standardized spatial analysis.

Ultimately, our goal extends beyond prediction alone. This high-resolution housing condition dataset can enhance existing analyses related to social vulnerability indices18,19 by enriching their housing components, such as mobile homes, multi-unit structures, and crowding, and thus serves as a valuable resource for both researchers and practitioners. This dataset will enable a deeper understanding of the geographic landscape of housing conditions and their implications across the U.S.

Methods

The Warren Group Real Estate Data

We obtained the 2023 property dataset from Dewey, produced by the Warren Group20,21. This dataset comprises 111,052,962 housing units and includes over 35 property and building characteristics, such as the number of bedrooms and bathrooms, tax amount, and location. The dataset categorizes housing conditions into six classes: Unsound, Poor, Fair, Average, Good, and Excellent. Condition information is available for 55.3% of the records. Our goal is to impute the remaining 44.7% by predicting housing conditions and ultimately to compute the housing condition scores.

Figure 1 shows the spatial distribution of the percentage of houses without condition information at the hexagonal grid level. The map suggests potential regional bias, as the distribution of houses with a high percentage of missing condition information is clearly divided across regions. For instance, most houses in Michigan and Wisconsin lack condition information, whereas those in Georgia and Illinois generally include it.

Training Machine Learning Algorithms and Comparisons

In this project, we selected three gradient-boosting ensemble methods, eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost)22, Categorical Boosting (CatBoost)23, and Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM)24, for housing condition prediction. These algorithms were chosen due to their proven ability to model complex, non-linear relationships between predictors and classes, as well as their robustness to outliers and missing data25–27. They deliver exceptional predictive performance across various domains, such as healthcare28,29, biology30, transportation31, and urban planning32. To identify the best predictive model, we compared performance using balanced accuracy and assessed overfitting, as conventional accuracy metrics often yield overly optimistic estimates33.

Figure 2 shows the proportions of the dataset used in the model training. Of the 55.3% of houses with condition information, we first identified and converted outlier values in predictor variables to missing values using the inclusion criteria (Table 1). We then retained 40,420,280 records with fewer than three missing variables, allowing models with a large number of categorical variables to run within the capacity of the university’s high-performance computing resources. This filtered subset was partitioned into training (80%) and testing (20%) sets via a stratified random split, ensuring proportional representation across all housing condition categories. Because class imbalance can adversely affect model performance34,35, especially for rare categories such as “Unsound” or “Excellent,” this stratified approach was crucial to address inherent imbalances36,37. During model training, the training set was further divided into training and validation folds using three-fold cross-validation.

Second, we incorporated data from the U.S. Census Bureau American Community Survey (ACS) and Rural-Urban Commuting Area (RUCA) codes to capture neighborhood context relevant to housing conditions (e.g., median household income, homeownership rate, poverty level, and urban-rural classification; see Neighborhood Level Tab in Table 2). To include the most granular geographic information, we obtained RUCA and below-poverty attributes, unavailable at the block group level, at the census tract level.

To link census tract and block group attributes to each property record, we performed a spatial join that matches features based on their geographic locations. As a result, every property entry included sociodemographic and economic attributes of its surrounding neighborhood, creating a unified dataset that combines both building-specific characteristics and the contextual factors necessary for robust housing condition prediction.

Hyperparameter Optimization in High-Performance Computing (HPC) environment

To maximize model performance, we applied hyperparameter optimization using a Bayesian approach38 rather than traditional grid or random search methods. Because hyperparameters can enhance predictive performance39,40, Bayesian optimization helps us efficiently navigate the high-dimensional parameter space by sequentially proposing configurations based on prior evaluations41. Given the computational intensity of this process, we utilized an HPC environment. Specifically, we ran GPU-accelerated Bayesian optimization on Florida State University’s research computing cluster42, allowing us to thoroughly explore each algorithm’s parameter space across the large-scale national dataset.

Model Calibration - Maximizing Precision

Evaluation of the prediction models prioritized metrics aligned with the practical applications of housing score assessment. We first maximized balanced accuracy during initial hyperparameter optimization for overall accuracy, which is a general performance indicator. However, we further calibrated the trained model to maximize its predictive capability on the unseen houses. In other words, post-hoc model calibration was then carried out to ensure that the predicted probabilities accurately reflected the true likelihood of each property belonging to its assigned condition category. This calibration step is essential for applications that rely on well-calibrated probability estimates, such as risk assessment or prioritization of housing interventions. We employed isotonic regression for calibration43 and subsequently applied post-processing to adjust the model for maximum precision.

Aggregated Housing Condition Scores

The trained model produced property-level housing condition classifications (Excellent, Good, Average, Fair, Poor, and Unsound). To create a standardized national dataset suitable for spatial analysis and visualization, while protecting individual privacy, these property-level predictions were aggregated to census tracts, ZCTA, and H3 hexagonal grid cells at resolution 6 (approximately 36.13 km²) covering the United States.

A weighted-average approach was used to aggregate property-level housing conditions to the census tract and grid-cell levels. Specifically, each property’s contribution to the census tract and cell’s aggregate score was weighted by its numeric score, where category values were converted as follows: Excellent (6), Good (5), Average (4), Fair (3), Poor (2), and Unsound (1).

Software and Environment

This study utilized Python 3.12.4 for data preprocessing, modeling, and visualization. Core libraries for machine learning algorithms include CatBoost (catboost 1.2.7), LightGBM (lightgbm 4.6.0), and XGBoost (xgboost 2.1.4). The computational demands of national-scale housing condition prediction necessitated deployment on the HPC, which provided access to multi-core processing and GPUs. The computing resource was Intel Xeon CPU E5-2660 2.00 GHz, with 28 cores, 387 GB of memory, and 4 NVIDIA GeForce GTX 1080 Ti GPUs.

Using the optimized hyperparameters (Table 3), we trained the final models and evaluated their training accuracy, validation accuracy, and overfitting gap (Table 4). Among the evaluated algorithms, CatBoost and XGBoost exhibited strong predictive performance for housing condition classification. When comparing balanced accuracy on three-fold stratified cross-validation, XGBoost achieved a marginally higher score. However, assessing overfitting revealed that XGBoost demonstrated a larger discrepancy between training and validation performance (12% vs. 6.4%), indicating greater overfitting than CatBoost.

The selected CatBoost model was then calibrated to maximize precision (Table 5). The version optimized for balanced accuracy yielded very high precision for the “Average” class (0.933) but low precision for “Unsound,” “Poor,” “Fair”, and “Excellent” (0.303, 0.209, 0.315, and 0.365, respectively). In contrast, recall rates for “Unsound” and “Excellent” exceeded 0.8. The macro-average recall (equivalent to balanced accuracy) was 0.724, indicating overall solid performance despite class imbalance. To improve predictive accuracy on unseen data, we performed calibration aimed at maximizing precision. After calibration, precision for “Unsound” increased from 0.303 to 0.610, and precision for “Excellent” rose from 0.365 to 0.621. However, this came at the cost of reduced macro-average recall of 0.624, reflecting a trade-off in sensitivity.

The histogram of conformal prediction illustrates the uncertainty of the predictions made by the selected CatBoost model. A larger number of samples with smaller prediction sets indicates higher confidence in the classification outcomes. Conversely, if a large proportion of samples had wide prediction sets (e.g., four to six classes), it would suggest greater uncertainty. At the 95% confidence level, the empirical coverage was 94.7%, indicating that our model is well calibrated.

Feature Importance

To further validate the selected CatBoost model and understand the drivers of housing condition predictions, we conducted a feature importance analysis. This analysis ranks input features by their contribution to the model’s predictive performance. By examining which property-level characteristics (from the Warren Group data) and neighborhood-level attributes (from the ACS data) are most influential, we gain insight into the underlying factors that determine housing conditions. Feature importance scores were obtained from the trained CatBoost model using permutation importance.

Figure 9 presents the relative feature importance scores. The most impactful feature is tax amount, followed by exterior cover, roof material, and heating type, indicating that tax amount substantially influences predictive accuracy. Year built (fifth rank) and renovation year (seventh rank) also play critical roles in predicting housing conditions. Overall, property-level features dominate the top ranks. Neighborhood-level features (e.g., population density, poverty level, and proportion of mobile homes) exhibit lower importance scores compared to property-level attributes. Intuitively, because neighborhood-level characteristics average over property-specific values, they may contain less information content.

Feature importance scores show the relative importance of each feature on prediction performance. According to the results, tax amount has the greatest impact on predicting housing conditions, followed by exterior cover and roofing material.

Even so, neighborhood-level characteristics are still significant for predicting and understanding housing conditions. Housing condition frameworks broadly consider occupant perception, satisfaction, and demographics, neighborhoods’ socioeconomic position, connection to water, sanitation, and waste services, positive social connections, and/or access to jobs and amenities46–50. People of color, renters, lower-income and/or education level households are more likely to live in households with substandard housing conditions50–52. Correspondingly, social processes such as biased residential lending and investment practices and patchy enforcement of tenant rights regulations may perpetuate these disparities53,54.

In this study, we did not examine in depth the relationships between housing conditions and their predictors, as this was beyond the scope of our objectives. However, future work will investigate these relationships using interpretable machine learning methods such as accumulated local effects plots and SHapely Additive exPlanations (SHAP). In particular, because the CatBoost-based model is highly compatible with these interpretability techniques, it will allow us to explore non-linear relationships between predictors and housing conditions and to provide a more comprehensive understanding of their underlying dynamics.
