Immune signatures in older patients with newly diagnosed multiple myeloma are associated with survival outcomes of first‐line therapy irrespective of frailty levels

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe treatment landscape for older patients with multiple myeloma (MM) has rapidly evolved with the introduction of CD38‐targeting antibodies. Yet, outcomes remain highly variable and are only partially explained by frailty status. To address this, we investigated the impact of the immune system on survival outcomes of 89 newly diagnosed MM patients in the HOVON‐143 trial, where frail or intermediate‐fit patients received daratumumab–ixazomib–dexamethasone. Comprehensive immunophenotyping of lymphoid and myeloid subsets as relative or absolute counts in peripheral blood (PB) and bone marrow revealed comparable immune composition between frail and intermediate‐fit patients at diagnosis, except for reduced naive CD4+ and CD8+ T‐cells and increased effector memory CD4+ T‐cells and CD56bright NK‐cells in frail patients. Among 36 T‐cell and NK‐cell subsets analyzed, 9 subsets—measured as absolute counts in PB—showed strong association with progression‐free survival (PFS) and 5 with overall survival (OS). Four subsets were linked to both PFS and OS: higher absolute counts of naive CD8+ T‐cells, CD38+CD4+ T‐cells, and CD56dimCD57+ NK‐cells were associated with longer survival, whereas elevated EM CD8+ T‐cell counts were linked to shorter survival. Using the most predictive immune parameters, we subsequently developed two immune risk scores—one for PFS and one for OS—which remained strongly associated with survival after adjusting for frailty status, disease stage, and cytogenetic risk. Our findings underscore the importance of a composite analysis of the immune system and demonstrate the association of baseline immune parameters with survival outcomes of first‐line therapy in non‐fit MM patients.

INTRODUCTION

At older age, the clinical outcomes of multiple myeloma (MM) patients are predominantly explained by patient‐related factors, among which the level of physical fitness is a key contributing factor. 1 , 2 , 3 The level of fitness can be quantified by clinical frailty scores, such as the International Myeloma Working Group Frailty Index (IMWG‐FI)—the current gold standard. However, even within the three levels of fitness, ranging from fit to frail, outcomes are heterogeneous. Therefore, a more nuanced understanding of predictive and prognostic factors in older patients is required.

Recent studies suggest that various immunological factors, collectively termed as “immune fitness,” significantly contribute to the therapeutic outcomes of immunotherapy in MM patients. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 Nonetheless, the immune system of frail and intermediate‐fit MM patients, and its implications for clinical outcomes within this vulnerable group, remains poorly studied.

To address this, we conducted comprehensive flow cytometry‐based immune profiling of frail and intermediate‐fit newly diagnosed MM (NDMM) patients enrolled in the HOVON‐143 trial. Patients received a triple regimen consisting of daratumumab—a CD38‐targeting monoclonal antibody exerting anti‐MM activity through multiple mechanisms, including antibody‐dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC), antibody‐dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP), and T‐cell modulation 9 —combined with the oral proteasome inhibitor ixazomib and low‐dose dexamethasone. We first assessed whether frail and intermediate‐fit patients differed in their immune composition by analyzing both relative and absolute counts of various lymphoid and myeloid immune cell subsets in peripheral blood (PB) and bone marrow (BM) at baseline. Subsequently, we investigated the associations between specific (combinations of) immune cell subsets and survival outcomes, and quantified individual patient risks for progression‐free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) by developing two immune risk scores.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study population

The study population consisted of NDMM patients enrolled in the HOVON‐143 clinical trial (EudraCT: 2016‐002600‐90), details of which were previously described. 10 , 11 Briefly, HOVON‐143 was a Dutch/Belgian prospective, multicenter Phase 2 trial specifically designed for NDMM patients classified as frail or intermediate‐fit according to the IMWG‐FI. Patients were treated with nine 28‐day induction cycles consisting of daratumumab 16 mg/kg intravenously (Cycles 1–2: Days 1, 8, 15, and 22; Cycles 3–6: Days 1 and 15; Cycles 7–9: Day 1), ixazomib (oral proteasome inhibitor) 4 mg orally (Days 1, 8, and 15), and dexamethasone orally (only on days that daratumumab was given; Cycles 1–2: 20 mg; Cycles 3–9: 10 mg), followed by 8‐week maintenance therapy cycles consisting of daratumumab (Day 1) and ixazomib (Days 1, 8, 15, 29, 36, and 43) until disease progression for a maximum of 2 years. Trial inclusion and exclusion criteria are described in Table S1. A total of 133 patients were enrolled between 2017 and 2019. Three patients were excluded from clinical outcome analyses because of non‐eligibility. A total of 89 patients were included in our study based on the availability of complete immune profiling data at baseline. Clinical cutoff date was May 2, 2024.

Sample collection

BM and PB samples from patients in HOVON‐143 were collected at baseline and processed centrally at the Amsterdam University Medical Center. After analysis of fresh samples, PB mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and BM mononuclear cells (BM‐MNCs) from remaining material were isolated from each sample via density gradient centrifugation using Ficoll Paque Plus (Cytiva; cat. 17‐1440‐03) and cryopreserved in liquid nitrogen until further use. Collection and storage of samples were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and after approval by the institutional medical ethical committee in each participating center. All patients provided written informed consent for sampling and use of their samples and clinical data.

Basic immunophenotyping of fresh PB and BM samples

Whole PB and BM samples were first analyzed freshly to assess the percentages and absolute counts of main immune subsets including total T‐cells, NK‐cells, B‐cells, monocytes, and granulocytes. White blood cell (WBC) counts were performed using a Beckman Coulter Cell Counter device (Coulter AcT diff Hematology Analyzer). Flow cytometry was conducted after lysis of red blood cells with NH4Cl for 10 min at room temperature. To this end, 1 × 106 cells were stained for 15 min at room temperature with a panel of fluorochrome‐conjugated antibodies consisting of CD45‐Krome Orange (cat. B36294), CD56‐PC7 (cat. A21692), and CD138‐APC (cat. B49219) (all Beckman Coulter), and CD3‐V450 (cat. 560 365), CD14‐PerCP (cat. 345 786), CD16‐PE (cat. 561 313), and CD19‐APC‐H7 (cat. 560 177) (all Becton Dickinson). Cells were measured on a three‐laser (blue, red, and violet) FACSCelesta™ flow cytometer (Becton Dickinson). Raw Flow Cytometric Standard files (FCS 3.1) were manually analyzed using FCS Express Flow Cytometry Software version 7 (De Novo Software, Pasadena, CA, USA). The gating strategy is depicted in Figure S1. Total T‐cells, NK‐cells, monocytes, granulocytes, and B‐cells were expressed as percentages of total leukocytes (i.e., CD45+ cells), whereas monocyte subsets (classical, intermediate, and nonclassical) were expressed as percentages of total monocytes.

Comprehensive immunophenotyping of cryopreserved PBMC and BM‐MNC samples

Comprehensive immune‐profiling of T‐cells and NK‐cells was performed using cryopreserved PBMC (n = 89) and BM‐MNC (n = 69) baseline samples as previously described. 12 Briefly, cells were thawed, treated with DNase, and then stained with a viability dye and four multicolor flow cytometry panels (0.5–1 × 106 viable cells/panel) comprising various fluorochrome‐conjugated antibodies specific for various cell surface markers (Table S2). Cells were then acquired on a five‐laser (blue, yellow/green, red, violet, and ultraviolet) LSRFortessa™ device (Becton Dickinson).

Computational analysis of flow cytometry data from cryopreserved samples

For computational analyses on flow cytometry measurements, raw FCS 3.1 files were first “pre‐gated” for non‐doublet, viable T‐cells (CD45+CD3+ lymphocytes) in Panels 1–2 and non‐doublet, viable NK‐cells (CD45+CD56+CD3− lymphocytes) in Panels 3–4 (Figure S2) using FCS Express Flow Cytometry Software version 7 (De Novo Software, Pasadena, CA, USA). Pre‐gated cell populations were exported as new FCS 3.1 files for downstream computational analyses. To ensure data consistency and comparability across samples, several preprocessing steps were applied including data cleaning with the “PeacoQC” R package, 13 hyperbolic arcsine transformation with optimized cofactors, and min–max scaling across expression parameters. For data clustering, the FlowSOM algorithm 14 (“FlowSOM” R‐package v.2.12.0) was applied to an aggregated dataset of 10,000 T‐cells (Panels 1–2) or 10,000 NK‐cells (Panels 3–4) per FCS file. The number of metaclusters was optimized by visual inspection of star charts and 2D scatterplots until each metacluster displayed clear positive or negative marker expression. This resulted in a final metacluster count of 100 for Panel 1, 80 for Panel 2, 55 for Panel 3, and 35 for Panel 4. T‐cell (Panels 1–2) and NK‐cell (Panels 3–4) metaclusters were mapped over the optimized Minimum Spanning Tree to calculate marker expression levels (median fluorescence intensity) and metacluster percentages. Percentages of T‐cell (e.g., regulatory CD4+ T‐cells) or NK‐cell (e.g., CD56dimCD57+ NK‐cells) subsets were then calculated by summing the percentages of all metaclusters meeting the subset definition.

Quantification of immune cell subsets

To convert relative counts (percentages) of immune subsets to absolute counts in PB, we first calculated the absolute counts of total T‐cells and total NK‐cells in the freshly analyzed samples by multiplying their percentages within the total leukocyte population (flow cytometry) by the WBC count (Coulter AcT diff Hematology Analyzer). For cryopreserved samples, we calculated the absolute counts of each T‐cell or NK‐cell subset by multiplying their percentage with the absolute counts of the total T‐cells or total NK‐cells as derived from the fresh analyses. Absolute counts were expressed as cells per mL of blood. Immune subsets with a median absolute count of <2500 cells/mL were excluded from statistical analyses. Patients with any missing immunological data at baseline were excluded to ensure analyses included only complete cases.

Statistical analysis

A two‐sided Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare the percentages and absolute counts of immune subsets between the frail and intermediate‐fit patient subgroups. Before further analysis, absolute counts of immune subsets in PB were standardized using z‐score scaling. Unsupervised data clustering on z‐scores of immune subsets in PB was conducted using k‐means clustering. 15 The optimal number of clusters was determined using the Elbow and Silhouette methods on the Spearman correlation matrix of the z‐scores. A heatmap of the immune subsets across the k‐means clusters was generated to visualize differences in immune subset distributions, with z‐score standardization applied to ensure that each immune parameter contributed equally to the visualization. Associations between k‐means clusters and survival outcomes (PFS and OS) were assessed with Kaplan–Meier analysis and two‐sided log‐rank tests.

Direct associations of single immune parameters with PFS and OS were evaluated using univariable Cox regression. Multivariable survival analyses were performed using Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO)‐regularized Cox regression models. 16 Models were trained using leave‐one‐out cross‐validation to minimize the risk of overfitting, using z‐score standardized immune subsets as predictors and survival outcomes (PFS or OS) as outcome variables. Predictor variables were selected by the models using the optimal lambda values with the lowest mean squared error. To enhance interpretability, beta values of selected variables were scaled by dividing them by 100.000.

Finally, immune risk scores for PFS and OS were developed by multiplying the LASSO‐selected predictor coefficients (β)—those consistently associated with PFS or OS—by the z‐score standardized counts (χ) of the corresponding immune subsets, according to the formula: immune risk score = Σ(β i × χi ). 17 , 18 , 19 To facilitate interpretability, the risk scores were rescaled to a 0–10 range using min–max normalization. Associations between the immune risk scores and survival outcomes were analyzed using either two‐sided univariable Cox regression analyses with survival area plots from the “contsurvplot” R‐package (v0.2.1) or Kaplan–Meier plots with two‐sided log‐rank tests. To account for other predictor variables, the associations between immune risk scores and survival outcomes (PFS or OS) were also evaluated in two‐sided multivariable Cox regression analyses, with results visualized in forest plots.

The significance level was set at a two‐sided alpha of 0.05. All analyses were conducted and graphics generated using R version 4.4.1.

RESULTS

Patient characteristics

We performed a comprehensive flow cytometry‐based immune profiling analysis on PB and BM samples obtained from 89 NDMM patients (BM available from 69/89 patients) at the time of enrollment in the HOVON‐143 clinical trial. Characteristics of the patient cohort are summarized in Table 1. The median age of the cohort was 77 years (range: 65–89), with 32% classified as International Staging System (ISS) Stage III and 17% had cytogenetic high‐risk abnormalities based on the presence of del17p, t(4;14) and/or t(14;16). According to the IMWG‐FI, approximately half of the patients were classified as frail (47%) and the other half as intermediate‐fit (53%). After a median follow‐up of 61.4 months, median PFS was 13.1 months (95% CI 8.9–22.3) for frail patients, and 14.0 months (95% CI 9.46–34.9) for intermediate‐fit patients. For OS, these numbers were 36.2 months (95% CI 23.5–59.6) and NA (95% CI 56.2–NA), respectively. Clinical characteristics of the 89 patients included in our study were representative of the entire 130‐patient population included in the HOVON‐143 trial (Table S3). 10 , 11

Minimal differences in immune parameters between frail and intermediate‐fit NDMM patients

To assess potential differences in the immunological composition between frail and intermediate‐fit NDMM patients, we first analyzed major lymphoid and myeloid immune subsets (T‐cells, NK‐cells, B‐cells, total monocytes, classical/intermediate/nonclassical monocytes, and granulocytes) in freshly measured PB and BM samples, assessing absolute counts in PB and relative counts (percentages) in both PB and BM. We found no significant differences between frail and intermediate‐fit patients, except for a minimally decreased percentages of classical monocytes in frail patients in PB (80% vs. 83%, P = 0.02) and BM (80% vs. 85%, P = 0.01), and slightly increased percentages of nonclassical monocytes in frail patients in BM (8% vs. 7%, P = 0.04) (Table S4A).

Next, we conducted an in‐depth analysis on cryopreserved samples, analyzing specific T‐cell and NK‐cell subsets. For T‐cells, we focused on CD4+ and CD8+ T‐cells, CD38+ and CD38‐ regulatory T‐cells (Tregs), gamma‐delta T‐cells, naive and memory subsets, T‐cells with a senescent phenotype, and T‐cells expressing markers of activation (CD25, CD38, and HLA‐DR) or exhaustion (PD‐1, TIGIT, TIM‐3, and LAG‐3). Definitions of the studied immune subsets are listed in Table S5. For NK‐cells, we analyzed key maturation stages (CD56bright, CD56dimCD57−, and CD56dimCD57+) along with various activating receptors (DNAM‐1, NKG2D, and NKG2C) and inhibitory receptors (NKG2A, PD‐1, TIM‐3, TIGIT, and KLRG1). These analyses showed that the PB of frail patients on average contained lower absolute counts of naive CD4+ T‐cells (228,343 vs. 408,734 per mL, P = 0.01), naive CD8+ T‐cells (15,585 vs. 34,726 per mL, P = 0.01), central memory CD4+ T‐cells (204,278 vs. 334,673 per mL, P = 0.01), CD38+ CD4+ T‐cells (172,799 vs. 318,456 per mL, P = 0.01), and CD56bright NK‐cells (6874 vs. 11,492 per mL, P = 0.02) than intermediate‐fit patients (Figure 1A, Table S4B). Similarly, frail patients had modestly lower percentages of naive CD8+ T‐cells in PB (9% vs. 16%, P = 0.02) and BM (6% vs. 13%, P < 0.01), a higher percentage of terminally differentiated effector memory (EMRA) CD4+ T‐cells in PB (1% vs. 0%, P = 0.01), and a higher percentage of PD‐1+CD4+ T‐cells both in PB (9% vs. 5%, P < 0.01) and BM (12% vs. 6%, P < 0.01) (Figure 1B,C, Table S4B).

Unsupervised clustering reveals patients with distinct immunotypes and differential survival

Next, we performed an exploratory analysis of the patients' immune composition at diagnosis using unsupervised clustering by k‐means. We applied the Elbow and Silhouette method to determine the optimal number of clusters. Subsequently, k‐means assigned each patient to the cluster with the closest average immune composition. Using this approach, we identified three unique immune profiles within T‐ and NK‐cell subsets in PB, referred to as Immunotypes 1, 2, and 3 (Figure 2A,B).

Immunotype 1 (n = 14; 16% of patients) was defined by higher absolute counts of CD4+ T‐cells, CD38+ Tregs, naive CD4+ and CD8+ T‐cells, and NK‐cells (Figure 2C). In contrast, immunotype 2 (n = 8; 9% of patients) had predominantly higher counts of total CD8+ T‐cells, gamma‐delta T‐cells, CD38− Tregs, late‐differentiated and senescent CD4+ and CD8+ T‐cells, and exhausted CD4+ and CD8+ T‐cells; alongside less NK‐cells, as compared to the other two immunotypes. Lastly, immunotype 3 (n = 67, 75% of patients) demonstrated uniformly average to lower absolute counts across all immune subsets. We observed no significant differences between patients within these different immunotypes in terms of WBC counts, age, WHO performance status, IMWG‐FI, ISS, and cytogenetic risk (Figure S3, Table S6). In addition, dependency in activities of daily living or instrumental activities of daily living did not markedly differ between the three immunotypes. However, given the limited group sizes, these findings remain exploratory and should be interpreted with caution.

To evaluate the clinical relevance of the identified immunotypes, we compared survival outcomes across the groups, revealing significant differences in terms of PFS (log‐rank P‐value = 0.020) and OS (log‐rank P‐value = 0.018) (Figure 2D). Patients within Immunotype 1—characterized by high abundance of naive T‐cells and NK‐cells—had the most favorable survival outcomes, with a median PFS of 32.5 months (95% CI 15.0–NA) and an OS that was not reached (95% CI NA–NA). In contrast, patients within Immunotype 2—characterized by differentiated, senescent, and exhausted T‐cells and lower abundance of NK‐cells—had inferior survival, with a median PFS of only 8.7 months (95% CI 7.2–NA) and OS of 35.8 months (95% CI 10.4–NA). Immunotype 3, characterized by overall moderate to low absolute counts of T‐ and NK‐cell subsets, had intermediate survival outcomes as compared to the other immunotypes, with a median PFS of 12.5 months (95% CI 9.5–22.3) and median OS of 50.2 months (95% CI 38.6–NA).

Identification of specific immune predictors for survival outcomes

To investigate in more detail which specific immune cell subsets were key predictors for PFS and OS, we performed a more targeted univariable Cox regression analysis across all immune subsets and found that baseline immune subsets related to more differentiated/exhausted T‐cells and less cytotoxic NK‐cells were generally associated with inferior PFS and OS (Table S7). Notably, total WBC counts, B‐cells, monocytes and their subsets (classical, intermediate, and nonclassical), and granulocytes were not associated with either PFS or OS in this univariable analysis.

To further refine these findings, we conducted a multivariable analysis using LASSO‐regularized Cox regression models for all T‐cell or NK‐cell immune subsets defined both as absolute counts (in PB) and as percentages (in PB and BM). This analysis demonstrated that nine immune subsets measured as absolute counts in PB were associated with PFS (Table 2). Among the selected immune parameters were naive CD8+ T‐cells, CD38+CD4+ T‐cells, and CD56dimCD57+ NK‐cells, all of which higher absolute counts were associated with prolonged PFS. In contrast, higher absolute counts of EM CD8+ T‐cells and CD56bright NK‐cells were associated with impaired PFS. For OS, a total of five parameters were selected: higher absolute counts of naive CD8+ T‐cells, CD38+CD4+ T‐cells, CD56dimCD57+ NK‐cells, and NKG2A+ NK‐cells were associated with prolonged survival, whereas higher counts of EM CD8+ T‐cells were associated with impaired survival (Table 2). Remarkably, except for a negative association between CD56bright NK‐cells and PFS in PB, none of these associations could be detected when we performed these LASSO‐regularized analyses with percentages of these populations. These findings underscore the importance of measuring absolute counts of immune cell subsets in addition to relative frequencies.

Immune risk scores predict survival outcomes independent of clinical risk factors

Using the coefficients of the immune parameters selected by the LASSO Cox regression models, we developed two distinct immune risk scores to quantify individual patient risk for survival: one for PFS and one for OS. Specifically, the PFS risk score was constructed using the nine immune parameters identified by LASSO as the strongest predictors of PFS, whereas the OS risk score was constructed using the five immune parameters identified as the strongest predictors of OS. For each score, the z‐score standardized absolute counts of the selected immune subsets were multiplied by their respective LASSO‐derived coefficients, and the weighted sums were calculated. Accordingly, the risk scores demonstrated strong associations with PFS (hazard ratio [HR] 1.48 [95% CI 1.25–1.74, P < 0.0001]) and OS (HR 1.44 [95% CI 1.19−1.74, P = 0.0002]) in univariable Cox regression analysis (Figures 3A and S4). In multivariable analysis, the risk scores retained significance for PFS (HR 1.55, 95% CI 1.27–1.88, P < 0.0001) and OS (HR 1.35, 95% CI 1.07–1.71, P = 0.01) after adjusting for established clinical risk factors, including WHO performance status, IMWG frailty status, ISS stage, and cytogenetic risk (Figure 3B).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we comprehensively investigated the immune system of frail and intermediate‐fit NDMM patients treated with daratumumab–ixazomib–dexamethasone in the HOVON‐143 clinical trial. Older patients with NDMM could be clustered into distinct immunotypes, which were associated with differential survival outcomes. To further investigate which specific immune subsets were linked to superior survival, multivariable analyses revealed that higher absolute counts of naive CD8+ T‐cells, CD38+CD4+ T‐cells, and CD56dimCD57+ NK‐cells and reduced EM CD8+ T‐cells were significantly associated with favorable PFS and OS. Building on these findings, two immune risk scores characterized by these parameters were associated with survival independent of other known risk factors such as frailty level, disease stage, and cytogenetic risk. Collectively, these data indicate that the levels of frailty only partially explained the variability in survival of these patients, whereas baseline immune parameters were strongly associated with both PFS and OS. Furthermore, these analyses demonstrated the importance of determining absolute counts of immune subsets in PB for assessing associations of immune parameters with survival.

Our work is the first to link baseline immune signatures with survival outcomes in older, frail and intermediate‐fit NDMM patients—an often underrepresented patient population in clinical trials. Given daratumumab's established mechanism of action, we hypothesize that these associations were largely attributable to the anti‐MM effects of daratumumab. Higher counts of cytotoxic CD56dimCD57+ NK‐cells were linked to superior survival, supporting daratumumab's facilitation of ADCC via cytotoxic NK‐cells. These results align with the association between increased percentages of cytotoxic CD56dim NK‐cells and sustained minimal residual disease negativity in younger NDMM patients treated with daratumumab‐KRD 5 or daratumumab‐VRD, 20 as well as with the observation that a more pronounced response was observed in daratumumab‐treated RRMM patients with higher percentages of CD16+ NK‐cells. 4 In contrast, a study in RRMM found that total NK‐cell counts at baseline were not predictive for outcomes of daratumumab monotherapy, 21 highlighting the importance of analyzing specific subsets rather than total counts.

Perhaps less expected is the association between higher baseline CD38+ Tregs counts and superior PFS. Although the immediate effects of daratumumab are largely related to its capacity to induce ADCC, ADCP, and complement‐dependent cytotoxicity, we and others have demonstrated the powerful immunomodulatory effects of daratumumab via depletion of CD38+ immunosuppressive cells, particularly CD38+ Tregs. 9 Although counterintuitive, daratumumab's immunomodulating effects may be more pronounced in patients with higher baseline level of these strongly immunosuppressive Tregs by virtue of its ability to deplete these cells. Indeed, a previous study in RRMM patients demonstrated that higher baseline CD38+ Tregs counts—but not total Tregs—were linked to improved overall response and durable responses to daratumumab monotherapy. 22

Our data also highlight a correlation between a less‐differentiated T‐cell profile and superior survival, which emphasizes the importance of a well‐preserved T‐cell repertoire in mounting effective antitumor responses during daratumumab therapy. In accordance, several studies have shown that a higher pretreatment T‐cell receptor diversity, most likely reflecting a less mature T‐cell compartment, was associated with improved responses to daratumumab‐KRD 5 or daratumumab‐RD. 23 Interestingly, higher frequencies of naive T‐cells were also found to be associated with improved efficacy of T‐cell redirecting therapies such as bispecific antibodies and chimeric antigen receptor T‐cells. 7 , 8 , 24 , 25 , 26 In addition, responders to daratumumab often exhibit an increase in granzyme B‐positive cytotoxic T‐cells and clonal T‐cell expansion. 23 , 27 , 28 Taken together, the depletion of CD38+ Tregs together with the pre‐existent abundance of less‐differentiated T‐cells may create a favorable immune milieu, where tumor‐specific naive T‐cells can differentiate into functional cytotoxic T‐cells, thereby driving effective anti‐MM responses.

An important consideration in immunomonitoring studies of MM and other hematological malignancies is whether immune subsets in PB accurately reflect the biology of the disease within the BM microenvironment. However, the patchy localization of MM in BM may affect the accuracy of BM sampling in representing the tumor microenvironment. Notably, immune subsets are often reported as relative counts of T‐cells or NK‐cells, 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 21 , 23 which may overlook the potentially critical information provided by absolute cell counts. Indeed, our findings demonstrate that in LASSO‐regularized regression analyses, absolute counts of immune subsets in PB outperformed relative counts in both PB and BM in predicting survival outcomes. This may be explained by that absolute counts quantify immune effector potential more directly, whereas proportions are compositional by nature and may mask biologically meaningful variation in immune competence. Given that PB sampling is significantly less invasive than BM biopsies, these findings highlight the value of assessing absolute cell counts in PB as a practical and effective approach for monitoring the immune status of patients with MM.

The lack of external validation is a limitation that needs to be acknowledged. Due to the unique cohort of frail and intermediate‐fit patients with NDMM, external validation of the immune risk scores was not feasible. However, we employed internal validation using leave‐one‐out cross‐validation to minimize the risk of overfitting of our models. Future studies with larger, independent cohorts should aim to externally validate these immune risk scores and investigate their relevance for other clinical outcomes such as infection‐related deaths. Another potential limitation involved the use of cryopreserved samples for the calculation of absolute immune cell counts. If not handled uniformly, cryopreserved samples are susceptible to cell recovery variability, which can lead to difficulties for reproducibility in external datasets. With these potential drawbacks in mind, we applied standardized sample processing without batching and used z‐score normalization before LASSO modeling to minimize technical variability. Furthermore, we calculated absolute counts anchoring subset frequencies from cryopreserved samples to total cell counts measured in fresh whole blood. Taken together, we consider the use of absolute cell counts in this study to be both methodologically sound and biologically informative.

In conclusion, our study provides the first evidence for the impact of immune status, characterized by a comprehensive profile of absolute counts of T‐ and NK‐cell subsets in PB, on survival outcomes in older MM patients. Ultimately, such immune profiles have the potential to emerge as clinically valuable biomarkers that complement clinical frailty assessments, thereby further enabling personalized treatment in this vulnerable population.
