Multidimensional optimization of stent design for endothelial shear stress regulation: Geometric structuring, surface functionalization strategies to mitigate thrombosis and restenosis
Authors: Mengyuan Che, Liuliu Feng, Xinbing Liu, Liangfeng Zhao, Suying Zhou, Xinyu Zhong, Lingsen You, Yu Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The occurrence of in-stent restenosis (ISR) is closely associated with abnormal distribution of endothelial shear stress (ESS), and optimizing stent design is crucial for improving patient prognosis. This review aims to comprehensively explore how stent design parameters—including geometric structure and surface functionalization—influence neointimal hyperplasia and thrombosis by modulating ESS, while also summarizing the latest technological strategies. A detailed discussion is provided on the design evolution from traditional coronary stents to for non-coronary arteries (cerebral aneurysm) micro-woven stents, analyzing the mechanisms by which factors such as streamlined profiles, reduced strut thickness, and optimized spacing improve hemodynamics. Furthermore, the article critically evaluates the advantages and current limitations of cutting-edge technologies such as computational fluid dynamics (CFD)-based optimization and endothelialization-promoting functional coatings. We conclude that multidimensional stent design optimization represents a future trend in regulating ESS and suppressing restenosis. Future research should focus on integrating personalized design with highly biocompatible materials to advance the clinical translation of next-generation vascular stents.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is the leading cause of death globally, with atherosclerosis (AS) serving as a common pathological basis[1,2]. The development of AS is closely associated with multiple factors, including hemodynamic abnormalities, inflammatory responses, and lipid metabolism disorders. Although this technique effectively restores blood flow in the short term, in the long run, in-stent restenosis (ISR) and stent thrombosis (ST) remain significant challenges that impact patient prognosis.

ESS is the tangential force exerted by blood flow on the vascular endothelial cells (ECs), playing a crucial role in maintaining vascular homeostasis, regulating endothelial function, and influencing the occurrence and progression of AS. Normal ESS promotes the anti-inflammatory, anti-thrombotic, and anti-proliferative functions of ECs, while abnormal ESS can lead to endothelial dysfunction, subsequently triggering AS. After stent implantation, the local hemodynamic environment within the vessel undergoes considerable changes, particularly in the distribution and magnitude of ESS, which are affected by factors such as stent architecture, implantation site, and vessel geometry. Therefore, a comprehensive investigation into the effects of stent implantation on ESS is vital for elucidating the mechanisms underlying ISR and ST, as well as for optimizing stent design, highlighting its significant theoretical and clinical value. In recent years, the rapid advancement of technologies such as computational fluid dynamics (CFD) and optical coherence tomography (OCT) has enabled researchers to simulate and measure hemodynamic changes following stent implantation with greater accuracy, particularly concerning the distribution of ESS. This review focuses on the alterations in ESS after stent implantation and their implications for vascular thrombosis and in-stent restenosis, as well as summerizing the role of stent structural design in modulating ESS. By analyzing the effects of various factors, including stent thickness, shape, length, spacing, and poor apposition, we highlight the critical role that stent design plays in improving the hemodynamic environment. Furthermore, we introduce novel stent design strategies aimed at enhancing ESS regulation, such as geometric structure optimization, the application of CFD models, micro-structuring of the inner wall, and innovative stent designs that promote endothelialization. These strategies provide new insights into reducing complications after stent implantation and improving the long-term efficacy of stents.

Literature search strategy

To ensure the comprehensiveness and objectivity of this review, a systematic literature search was conducted. The retrieval was limited to articles published in the last decade within two core electronic databases, PubMed and Web of Science. A comprehensive search was performed using keywords and their synonyms related to the themes of stent design and endothelial shear stress, constructed with Boolean operators (e.g., (“stent design” OR “stent geometry”) AND (“endothelial shear stress” OR hemodynamics) AND (restenosis OR thrombosis)). All initially identified records were subjected to duplicate removal. Subsequently, two investigators independently screened the records based on pre-defined criteria. An initial screening was performed by reviewing titles and abstracts, followed by a full-text assessment of potentially eligible articles to determine final inclusion.

The inclusion criteria for studies were: original research (including experimental, numerical simulation, and clinical studies) that directly investigated the impact of stent geometrical parameters (e.g., thickness, shape, spacing) on endothelial shear stress, published in English as full-text articles. We excluded conference abstracts and studies focusing solely on drug coatings or biomaterials without addressing geometrical design. Any discrepancies encountered during the screening process were resolved through discussion or adjudication by a third investigator. The detailed process of literature identification and screening, including the number of records at each stage and reasons for exclusion, is presented in a PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 2). Evidence Consideration and limitations of different methodologies

The evidence synthesized in this review originates from a spectrum of methodologies, each with distinct strengths and inherent limitations, which must be considered when interpreting their findings. Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) simulations are powerful for isolating the hemodynamic effects of specific stent design parameters under controlled conditions and providing high-resolution, mechanistic insights into ESS distributions. However, their primary limitation lies in their reliance on often simplified model assumptions regarding vascular geometry, blood rheology, and wall compliance, and they typically cannot capture the full complexity of biological responses, such as inflammation and healing. Animal studies provide a crucial bridge by offering a living biological system to investigate the pathophysiological sequelae of altered ESS, including neointimal hyperplasia and endothelialization. The main sources of potential bias in these models include interspecies anatomical and physiological differences compared to humans, the use of often healthy rather than atherosclerotic vessels, and relatively small sample sizes, which may limit statistical power and generalizability. Finally, clinical studies provide the most direct evidence regarding patient outcomes, such as in-stent restenosis and thrombosis. Nevertheless, the majority are observational in nature, making them susceptible to confounding variables (e.g., patient comorbidities, lesion complexity) and unable to definitively establish causality. Throughout this review, we have striven to contextualize our conclusions by acknowledging the nature of the underlying evidence, and we interpret findings from CFD and animal models as generating hypotheses about mechanistic pathways, which require confirmation through robust clinical investigation.

Endothelial shear stress and AS

ESS denotes the friction exerted by blood flow on ECs lining the interior of blood vessels, representing the mechanical force of blood flow on the vascular endothelium[3]. Atherosclerotic plaques presense a plaque structural stress (PSS). Low ESS and high PSS are associated with AS. Additionally, low ESS is associated with plaque development, high ESS, particularly high ESS gradient (ESSG) due to abrupt changes in plaque-lumen interface topography, can also promote plaque disruption[4]. ESS varies in magnitude and directionality across different vascular regions due to vascular flow dynamics[5]. Hemodynamic shear stress can be classified into steady laminar shear stress (LSS) and oscillatory shear stress (OSS). LSS is defined by a unidirectional flow pattern, which is typically observed in straight arterial regions, with a value of ≈15 dyn/cm[2].As illustrated in Fig. 4, in straight arterial, viscous forces of blood flow exceed inertial forces, resulting in stable unidirectional laminar flow with high shear stress on ECs[6]. In curved, bifurcated regions, where vessels curve and inertial forces predominate, experience disturbed multidirectional oscillating flow, subjecting endothelial surfaces to low shear stress (∼±4 dyn/cm2)[7]. It helps us to understand the distribution of ESS in different blood vessels from clinical and engineering perspectives. ESS is classified into laminar shear stress (LSS) and oscillating shear stress (OSS) based on flow patterns. LSS mainly affects straight vascular segments, offering protection against atherosclerosis, inflammation, and oxidative stress by activating transcription factors such as KLF2 and Nrf2[[8], [9], [10]]. Conversely, OSS affects branch points and bends, promoting atherosclerosis and endothelial dysfunction by inducing proinflammatory pathways like NF-κB and MAPKs. Additionally, OSS exacerbates endoplasmic reticulum stress, apoptosis, and endothelial-mesenchymal transition, thereby advancing atherosclerosis development[[10], [11], [12]].Fig. 4Illustrates a schematic of laminar and disturbed blood flow in arterial branches. ESS influences both the dynamics of the vascular system and the regulation of endothelial gene transduction. ECs directly contacting blood in the arterial lumen. This contact forms a protective barrier between blood and vessel walls under normal physiological conditions. However, due to their direct exposure, ECs are subject to inertial forces from blood flow and ESS[[13], [14], [15]]. Mechanical sensors, adaptor proteins, kinases, and transcription factors in ECs respond to ESS by activating downstream elements and transcription factors, thereby modulating gene expression.

Under physiological conditions, ESS preserves endothelial integrity by modulating vascular tone, inflammatory responses, cell proliferation, and apoptosis[16,17]. Normal ESS facilitates the release of vascular protective mediators like nitric oxide (NO) and activates signaling pathways such as PI3K/Akt, which are essential for endothelial homeostasis[[18], [19], [20]]. Conversely, abnormal ESS, particularly low or oscillatory ESS, can compromise endothelial barrier function. This disruption increases endothelial permeability and promotes the subendothelial infiltration of lipoproteins and inflammatory cells, thereby initiating and exacerbating early atherosclerotic lesions[[21], [22], [23]].

The effects of low vs. Laminar ESS on vascular biology

In this chapter, we focus on the biological effects of low shear and laminar flow ESS on blood vessels. Researches show that large blood vessels are more prone to exhibit turbulent flow. Laminar flow patterns prevail are characteristic of small capillaries. In vascular, a laminar flow pattern of a high magnitude will promote an elongation and alignment of ECs parallel to the direction of flow; it will also trigger their orientation and migration against flow. In capillaries exposed to only a low magnitude of flow, ECs fail to align or to initiate polarity-induced migration[24]. Furthermore, low flow in vivo can induce inward remodeling of blood vessels, which Deng et al. concluded using a partial carotid artery ligation model[25]. Low ESS also attenuates nitric oxide (NO)-dependent atheroprotection, promotes low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL) uptake, synthesis, and permeability and inflammation[26].

In summary, low ESS is not merely the absence of a protective hemodynamic force but an active driver of endothelial dysfunction and vascular disease. It disrupts normal EC physiology, initiates maladaptive remodeling, and activates key molecular pathways that collectively predispose the vessel wall to atherosclerosis. These insights highlight low ESS as a central therapeutic target for preventing and treating vascular pathologies.

Effect of stent implantation on endothelial shear stress distribution

The preceding chapter discusses that AS can lead to the development of plaque lesions within blood vessels in the absence of stent implantation. In such cases, disturbed blood flow near the plaque, can give rise to altered ESS, influencing arterial remodeling and the expression of genes involved in endovascular pathology. Subsequently, we explore the vascular pathological manifestations resulting from alterations in ESS following stent deployment.

In healthy arteries devoid of stent implantation, blood flow typically exhibits a stable laminar state. During this laminar flow, the wall shear stress (WSS) is generally high and uniform, with values ranging from 15 dyn/cm2 to 30 dyn/cm2 [27,28]. However, the implantation of a stent alters the original geometry of the blood vessels, leading to significant changes in the local distribution of wall shear stress[29], especially in the stent edge and stent beam covering area, the ESS is significantly reduced, forming turbulence. In the turbulent state, the ESS is low and oscillating. The ESS range in the turbulent state is between 1dyn/cm2 ∼ 4dyn/cm2 [30], and the blood flows tangentially to the surface of the stent pillar, creating a lower shear force[31].

After the stent implantation, due to the rapid change in blood flow velocity, the ESS distribution is uneven, which will further induce inflammatory response and cell proliferation. At present, numerical simulation and in vitro experimental studies based on computational fluid mechanics (CFD) show that optimizing stent design (such as improving stent beam thickness, shape, etc.) can significantly improve ESS distribution and reduce the range and extent of low ESS regions[[32], [33], [34]].

Endothelial shear stress response signal transduction

ECs detect alterations in ESS via various mechanical sensors, initiating intracellular signaling cascades that govern vascular physiology[35]. These sensors, including integrins, ion channels, G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), and the cytoskeleton, are crucial for signal transduction[[36], [37], [38], [39]]. Integrins are transmembrane receptors that connect the extracellular matrix (ECM) to the cytoskeleton. Under ESS, integrins undergo conformational changes, aggregate and activate signaling molecules such as focal adhesion kinase (FAK) and Src family kinases. Integrin α5β1 is crucial in responding to ESS, facilitating endothelial cell survival and migration[40]. Ion channels, including Piezo1 and TRPV4, serve as mechanosensors on endothelial cell membranes[41]. When activated by ESS, these channels induce Ca2+ influx[42], subsequently activating calmodulin (CaM) and calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase (CaMK). G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) are activated by angiotensin II type 1 receptors (AT1R) and bradykinin B2 receptors (B2R), which stimulate G proteins and downstream effectors. The cytoskeleton, comprising microtubules, microfilaments and intermediate fibers, undergoes rearrangement under ESS, facilitating mechanical force transmission and signal molecule activation[43].

In the domain of ESS, mechanical sensors on ECs initially activate PI3K, followed by Akt. Akt plays a crucial role in cell survival, metabolism, and angiogenesis by modulating downstream targets such as eNOS, glycogen synthase kinase 3β (GSK-3β), and mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR). Due to ECs' vulnerability to stent damage, ESS triggers an antioxidant response via the Nrf2 pathway. In this process, Nrf2 dissociates from Keap1, translocates to the nucleus, binds to the antioxidant response element (ARE), and initiates transcription of antioxidant and protective genes, including heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1). ESS also activates the MAPK pathway, encompassing ERK, JNK, and p38 MAPK, which regulate cell proliferation and differentiation. Aberrant ESS can reduce Klf2 pathway activity, downregulate anti-inflammatory and antithrombotic gene expression, and increase endothelial susceptibility to dysfunction and inflammation. These alterations promote neointimal hyperplasia, vascular remodeling, and thrombosis, potentially leading to in-stent restenosis.

Effect of endothelial shear stress on blood vessels after stent implantation

1. Effect of endothelial shear stress on vascular thrombosis after stent implantation

During stent delivery, ECs are susceptible to damage, leading to platelet activation and increased fibrin, red blood cells, and platelet content, which promote thrombosis and trigger an inflammatory response. In stented arteries, procoagulant factors like thrombin and adenosine diphosphate are more readily released compared to normal vessels, accelerating platelet activation and coagulation responses. This heightened procoagulant environment is a key factor in the increased risk of acute thrombosis following stent implantation[45].

Vascular hemophilia factor VWF is crucial in thrombosis. Fu et al. investigated the interaction between VWF and shear force, revealing that VWF initiates platelet plug formation by binding to platelet GPIbα. At high shear forces (>1000 s−1), platelets attach to the GPIbα receptor and collagen via the VWF A1 domain[46]. Conversely, at lower shear forces (<1000 s−1), platelet aggregation primarily relies on the fibrinogen receptor integrin αIIbβ3. Pathological high shear rates and turbulence enhance platelet adhesion and thrombosis, whereas laminar flow facilitates the release of endothelial cell-derived antithrombotic factors like NO and prostacyclin, thereby inhibiting platelet activation[47].

2 Effect of endothelial shear stress on restenosis in the stent after stent implantation

Normal ESS supports endothelial cell function, maintaining their anti-inflammatory, anti-thrombotic, and anti-proliferative properties, which inhibit smooth muscle cell (SMC) migration and proliferation, thus reducing neointimal hyperplasia and in-stent restenosis (ISR) [26,48]. Post-stent implantation, the interaction with blood vessels causes uneven ESS distribution. Typically, high ESS occurs upstream of blood flow within the stent, while low ESS is found downstream, as shown in Fig. 6. Low ESS can induce endothelial dysfunction, promote inflammatory factor release (NF-κB, IL-6), and facilitate vascular SMC migration, accelerating neointimal hyperplasia. Additionally, low ESS increases vascular wall permeability, encourages lipid deposition and macrophage infiltration, and exacerbates restenosis[49]. Abnormalities in ESS can impede endothelialization on stent surfaces. Optimal endothelialization is crucial for preventing in-stent restenosis (ISR), and uneven ESS distribution may result in incomplete endothelial coverage, heightening the risk of thrombosis[50]. Post-stent implantation, the reparative capacity of ECs may be compromised. Research indicates that stent implantation reduces ESS, with this reduction being unevenly distributed, predominantly affecting the middle third and distal regions of the stent, potentially leading to restenosis within the stent and its segments[51,52].

7.Effect Of stent structure factors on endothelial shear stress

Understanding the issues of vascular thrombosis and restenosis associated with stent implantation allows for optimization through a detailed examination of stent structure, including thickness, shape, length, and wire spacing. Stent design significantly influences coronary fluid mechanics, particularly ESS. Inappropriate stent thickness and shape can lead to inadequate expansion and poor overlap, resulting in abnormal ESS, which exacerbates thrombosis and restenosis post-implantation. Additionally, misalignment of stent pillars, variations in separation distances between pillar walls, and inconsistent stent sizing can alter ESS, further promoting thrombosis and restenosis, thereby hindering patient recovery[[53], [54], [55], [56], [57]].

Thicker stent wires exacerbate blood flow disruption and separation within the lumen, increasing the return area[58]. Non-streamlined stents are more prone to forming spoilers in blood vessels compared to streamlined ones[59]. Excessively long stents introduce more adverse mechanical factors, while small stent spacing results in larger ESS areas[60]. Poor stent expansion slows local blood flow velocity, causes uneven pressure distribution, increases ESS areas, and results in uneven shear force distribution, disordered blood flow, and delayed endothelialization. Misaligned stent pillars create recirculation areas and transition flow from laminar to disturbed. Using 2D and 3D models, researchers found that increased separation distance of pillar walls in an idealized domain raises both the maximum shear rate and the area exposed to high shear rates[60].

1 Effect of stent thickness on endothelial shear stress

Stent wire thickness significantly influences vascular ESS distribution post-implantation. Thicker wires exacerbate blood flow disruption and separation within the lumen, intensifying the obstruction caused by stent struts[58]. This obstruction increases low shear force areas around the stent, elevating thrombosis risk and potentially promoting endothelial hyperplasia, which can lead to in-stent restenosis (ISR) [61]. Thicker stent wires disrupt blood flow uniformity, leading to uneven (ESS) distribution and exacerbating local hemodynamic abnormalities, which can increase thrombus formation[62,63]. Research indicates that thicker wires expand the contact area between the stent and vessel wall, potentially intensifying inflammatory responses and cell proliferation, thereby worsening vascular pathology. Conversely, thinner stent wires minimize flow obstruction, promote uniform blood flow, and help maintain normal ESS distribution. Additionally, thinner wires decrease the contact area with the vessel wall, reducing inflammation and intimal hyperplasia, thereby improving long-term outcomes post-implantation.

Numerous studies have summarized the impact of stent thickness on shear stress distribution[[64], [65], [66]]. Wentzel et al. implanted stents in the coronary arteries of five healthy pigs, revealing that stent implantation alters the curvature at the inflow and outflow ends, leading to maximum and minimum shear stress values. Similarly, LaDisa et al. observed in human coronary arteries that shear stress within the stent decreased by 77 %[67]. Furthermore, increasing strut thickness from 0. 056 to 0. 096 mm resulted in a 570 % rise in areas exposed to low shear stress. Thus, increased stent thickness correlates with a greater distribution of low shear stress within blood vessels.

2 effects of stent shape and length on endothelial shear stress

Stents are typically categorized as streamlined or non-streamlined. In vitro simulations indicate that non-streamlined stents result in reduced ESS and wall shear forces, with an extended blood flow separation distance[68], which adversely affects ESS distribution. Conversely, streamlined stents significantly diminish low ESS regions near the stent[31,59], facilitate endothelialization, and mitigate issues such as flow disturbances and uneven ESS distribution, thereby lowering the risk of arterial restenosis and thrombosis. Endothelialization is crucial for ESS and ISR. To assess the impact of stent design on endothelialization, Duy T Nguyen et al. developed stent pillars with heights ranging from 50 to 150 μm. Using an in vitro re-endothelialization model, they demonstrated that streamlined stents enhance endothelialization under various flow conditions[69].

Stent length significantly influences the distribution of ESS in vascular vessels post-implantation[70]. The required stent length often correlates with lesion size. Overly long stents can cause uneven ESS distribution, particularly forming low ESS regions at the stent ends. These regions elevate thrombosis risk and may induce endothelial hyperplasia, leading to in-stent restenosis. Additionally, a longer stent increases the contact area with the vessel wall, potentially triggering a stronger inflammatory response and cellular proliferation, thereby exacerbating vascular wall pathology. Implanting long stents can result in poor alignment between the stent ends and the ESS vessel wall, heightening the risk of local blood flow disturbances and uneven distribution. This often leads to low ESS areas at the stent ends, raising restenosis risk[70]. Conversely, deploying a short stent may inadequately open the lesion, causing the stent ends to embed in lipid plaques.

To mitigate the adverse clinical outcomes associated with improper stent length selection, advanced imaging technologies such as intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) and optical coherence tomography (OCT) have been increasingly employed. These techniques enable precise measurement of lesion length and vascular anatomy, offering a robust basis for optimal stent length selection.

3 Effect of stent spacing on endothelial shear stress

Stent spacing is a critical factor influencing the intravascular hemodynamic environment and directly impacts the distribution of ESS. The presence of stent struts, depending on their height and width, can lead to the generation of disturbed wall shear stress in between the struts[71]. Minimal stent spacing restricts the space between pillars, impeding blood flow and causing local turbulence and vortex formation. This results in low shear regions near the pillars, while the narrow channels between them can produce high shear area[72]. Low shear force zones can induce endothelial cell dysfunction, trigger the release of inflammatory and prothrombotic factors, and elevate the risk of neointimal hyperplasia and thrombosis. Optimal stent spacing facilitates smoother blood flow between stent struts, minimizing turbulence and vortex formation. Conversely, excessively large stent spacing may result in inadequate contact between the stent and vessel wall, creating stagnation zones where the ESS approaches zero, thereby also promoting endothelial cell dysfunction and thrombosis[73].

Beier et al. [73] investigated the impact of strut spacing on shear force by designing four spacings: 0. 83 mm, 1. 25 mm, 1. 67 mm, and 2 mm. In a pig coronary model, they found that at a spacing of 1. 67 mm, the mean ESS near the stent pillars increased to 12 dyn/cm2. It is evident that increasing stent strut spacing can enhance shear stress. Corti[74] et al. developed a multiscale model framework applied to the human femoral artery. This framework coupled computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations (hemodynamics module) with an agent-based model (ABM) of cellular activities (tissue remodeling module). Their work demonstrated that shear stress directly influences endothelial cell (EC) function, and areas exposed to lower levels of shear stress were associated with luminal re-narrowing and hyperplasia. Consequently, these findings support the hypothesis that increasing strut spacing may reduce the potential for neointimal hyperplasia. Therefore, optimizing stent performance involves reducing the number of pillars and increasing spacing to enhance the hemodynamic environment and lower restenosis risk.

4 Effect of poor stent adherence on endothelial shear force

Stent malapposition occurs when a stent fails to adhere closely to the vessel wall, leading to uneven endothelial coverage during vascular repair[75]. This condition can delay re-endothelialization and contribute to in-stent restenosis (ISR) and late thrombosis (LST), significantly affecting ESS. OCT enables accurate assessment of apposition of stent struts to the arterial wall. If the axial distance between the struts surface to the luminal surface is greater than the strut thickness (including polymer, if present), the strut is considered malapposed.

Due to the poor fit between the stent and the blood vessel wall, a gap forms, as illustrated in Fig. 7. Blood flowing through the stent accumulates in these gaps, creating stagnation zones that significantly reduce local ESS. Additionally, these gaps induce blood flow separation, leading to turbulence and the generation of oscillating shear stress (OSS). The stent design process focuses on reducing stent thickness to minimize low shear force distribution and optimizing the flowline shape for better adaptability to blood vessel walls. Advanced imaging technologies, such as intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) and optical coherence tomography (OCT), are employed for precise lesion length measurement. Key considerations for improving stent performance include optimizing the stent-to-lesion length ratio, reducing stent pillars, increasing stent spacing, enhancing the fit between the stent and blood vessel, improving hemodynamics (especially arterial shear force), and minimizing the risks of thrombosis and restenosis. Stent design strategy based on endothelial shear force

In the previous sections we discussed what consequences some of the undesirable stenting factors can have on endothelial shear, and in the future we can avoid the problems associated with these issues by choosing stents of moderate thickness, length, and streamlining. Next, we also summarize other current strategies for optimizing stents based on ESS. This chapter summarizes factors influencing current stent design and corresponding response strategies, highlighting its innovative nature. Techniques utilizing optical coherence tomography (OCT) and computational fluid dynamics (CFD) integrate high-resolution imaging with hemodynamic simulation to tailor stent structural parameters to patient-specific vascular geometries, improving endothelial shear force distribution and minimizing adverse events. Furthermore, stent designs that encourage endothelialization—such as drug-eluting stents, magnetic particle enrichment of ECs, and surface modifications—and micro-woven inner wall structures, which optimize blood flow velocity and enhance endothelial cell function. These innovative design strategies provide robust technical support for vascular interventional therapy, improving patient outcomes.

1 design of computational fluid mechanics stent based on optical coherence tomography

Stent design utilizing optical coherence tomography (OCT) represents an innovative approach that integrates high-resolution imaging with hemodynamic simulation to optimize ESS distribution post-implantation. OCT generates detailed three-dimensional images of vascular walls, illustrating plaque distribution, vessel wall thickness, lumen geometry, stent adherence, and endothelial coverage post-stent placement[76,77]. This data is critical for Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) simulations, enabling precise calculations of ESS distribution following stent implantation[78].

OCT-based CFD design methods can optimize stent structural parameters, including wire thickness and support unit shape and distribution, tailored to patient-specific vascular geometry. This optimization reduces blood flow disturbances and minimizes low shear areas. By modifying the geometric arrangement of stent wires, blood flow can be stabilized, preventing excessive or insufficient local shear forces[79]. This optimized design facilitates uniform endothelial cell growth and functional maintenance while inhibiting abnormal SMC proliferation, thereby reducing restenosis risk[80]. Additionally, CFD simulations can assess shear force distributions across various stent designs, enabling the selection of optimal configurations and providing a scientific foundation for personalized precision treatment[81].

Research indicates that stent designs informed by Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT) can markedly enhance endothelial shear force distribution and decrease post-implantation adverse events. Chiastra et al. utilizing OCT and Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD), demonstrated that optimized stent designs substantially reduce low shear areas, thereby mitigating thrombosis and neointimal hyperplasia risks[82]. In summary, OCT-based CFD stent design offers robust technical support for precise hemodynamic optimization, improving endothelial shear force distribution and patient outcomes, and represents a significant advancement in vascular interventional therapy.

Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) stent design utilizing optical coherence tomography (OCT) encounters several challenges in clinical application. While OCT provides high-resolution, personalized morphological data of blood vessels, it may lead to distortions in three-dimensional vascular models and inaccurate CFD boundary conditions in cases of significant intravascular calcification.

2 stent design to promote endothelialization

Endothelialization-promoting vascular stents represent a crucial advancement in stent design, enhancing both the longevity of stents within blood vessels and optimizing physiological functions like anti-thrombosis and prevention of vascular stenosis[79,83]. Drug-eluting stents (DES) are widely employed in clinical settings to enhance endothelialization. Research indicates that first-generation DES exhibit a tenfold increase in pro-endothelialization efficiency compared to bare metal stents. With advancements in technology, second-generation DES have been introduced, utilizing polymer materials to decrease drug coating concentration and stent thickness. These improvements have markedly enhanced endothelialization efficiency and significantly lowered stent thrombosis rates compared to first-generation DES[84,85].

Beyond employing drug-eluting stents (DES) to enhance endothelialization, several techniques are utilized to increase endothelialization rates. These include enriching, ECs with magnetic particles, inhibiting enzyme gene expression, and modifying stent surfaces. As illustrated in Fig. 8, Boris Polyak et al. demonstrated through in vivo studies that biodegradable superparamagnetic nanoparticles (MNPs) loaded onto biodegradable stents can, under external magnetic fields, enhance EC enrichment and thereby accelerate stent endothelialization[86]. Sarah T. Hsiao et al. demonstrated that inhibiting Rho-related protein kinases enhances endothelialization rates both in vivo and in vitro[87]. Additionally, modifying the surface of stents with anodized Ni-Ti can further accelerate endothelialization. Wang et al. conducted in vitro experiments revealing that TiO treatment increases the hydrophilicity of Ni-Ti stent pillars. They observed endothelial cell (EC) distribution on the anodized Ni-Ti stent surface 24 h after both static and flow conditions. Under static conditions, the EC density on the anodized Ni-Ti stent surface exceeded that of the control group[88], suggesting that anodized Ni-Ti stents promote endothelialization.Fig. 8Stent loading MNP promotes ECs enrichment under the action of external magnetic field and promotes endothelialization process[86].

The primary challenge in stent design for promoting endothelialization lies in achieving a dynamic balance between ECs adhesion and the inhibition of SMC proliferation. Current coating materials, including anti-proliferative drugs and pro-endothelial factors, suffer from imprecise release control, potentially delaying functional endothelial layer formation or provoking local inflammation. Additionally, the interaction between stent geometric parameters and the hemodynamic microenvironment is intricate. Adequate mechanical support is crucial to minimize blood flow disturbances and prevent complications like platelet activation or abnormal neo-endothelial proliferation. Furthermore, existing clinical evaluation methods, such as OCT and intravascular ultrasound, offer limited dynamic monitoring of endothelial function maturity.

3 design of micro-woven inner wall structured stent

The micro-woven structure of the stent's inner wall optimizes blood flow velocity, enhances ESS distribution, promotes endothelialization, and improves endothelial cell function. It reduces restenosis risk, inhibits SMC proliferation, and enhances the stent's long-term performance by minimizing fatigue damage. Its drag-reducing and anti-adhesion properties also improve fluid flow rate[89]. Introducing microscopic textures to stents can enhance blood flow rate, align it with physiological levels, minimize turbulence and flow separation between the stent's inner wall and the blood vessel, and moderately elevate ESS. This adjustment brings the ESS distribution closer to physiological conditions, thereby reducing endothelial cell damage and inflammatory responses.

Fan et al. [90]present a stent with a fish scale microstructure inspired by bionics. They applied a microstructure to the stent's surface and used finite element analysis to demonstrate that these microstructures reduce blood flow resistance and increase velocity. Furthermore, studies have combined micropatterning with other technologies and found that micropatterned polymer substrates can effectively enhance endothelial cell (EC) proliferation while suppressing platelet adhesion, making them highly biocompatible materials. Therefore, the stent's inner wall microstructure is designed to enhance the hemodynamic environment, facilitating endothelial cell adhesion, migration, and proliferation, thereby accelerating endothelialization[[91], [92], [93]].

The design of micro-woven structured stents for the inner wall presents significant multidisciplinary challenges in clinical applications. Currently, the arrangement of microgrooves within micro-texture structures lacks precision. Post-implantation physiological pulsation and vascular remodeling can compromise the mechanical stability of these micro-textures. Localized stress concentrations may hasten structural fractures or coating delamination, impacting the durability of endothelial repair. Furthermore, the biocompatibility of micro-woven stents requires further investigation. High-resolution imaging is essential to verify their long-term efficacy and safety.

The role of intravascular imaging in stent optimization: IVUS and OCT

The optimization of stent implantation and the accurate assessment of its hemodynamic consequences necessitate detailed visualization of the vascular architecture. Intravascular imaging modalities, particularly Intravascular Ultrasound (IVUS) and Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT), have transitioned from research tools to essential clinical assets for guiding percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). They provide critical data that transcends the limitations of angiography alone, enabling precise stent selection and deployment, which are paramount for regulating Endothelial Shear Stress (ESS) and improving long-term outcomes.

Technical overview of IVUS and OCT

IVUS and OCT are important diagnostic and guiding tools for PCI surgery[94]。IVUS is a catheter-based imaging modality that provides real-time tomographic views of the coronary arteries and allows for a detailed visualization of the plaque[95]. Its principal strength lies in its deeper tissue penetration (4–8 mm), allowing for comprehensive assessment of the vessel wall, plaque burden, remodeling index, and quantitative measurements of lumen and vessel dimensions. On the other hand, OCT has lower tissue penetration (except in calcified plaque) and potential imaging attenuation by red thrombus, lipid, and necrotic cores[96]. OCT employs near-infrared light to achieve a significantly higher axial resolution (10–20 μm). This superior resolution enables exquisite delineation of luminal contours, detailed characterization of plaque morphology (e.g., distinguishing fibrous cap thickness, lipid pools, and calcific nodules), and meticulous evaluation of stent-related features such as strut apposition, tissue coverage, and detection of minute dissections. The complementary nature of IVUS's depth and OCT's resolution provides a more complete picture of the coronary pathology.

Pre-procedural assessment with IVUS and OCT

Before stent implantation, both IVUS and OCT play a pivotal role in procedural planning. They allow for accurate measurement of reference vessel diameter and lesion length, which is crucial for selecting the appropriately sized stent. In recent research, Tanık VO et al. [97]etcompared ostial stent implantation versus crossover stenting for ostial left anterior descending (LAD) lesions and highlighted the essential role of IVUS in delineating true lesion borders, optimizing stent placement, and ultimately improving outcomes in such high-risk scenarios. This evidence reinforces that IVUS and OCT are indispensable not only for morphological assessment but also for guiding precise stent deployment to achieve hemodynamically favorable results. 10.Clinical Perspectives: Translation of ESS-Guided stent design into practice

The multidimensional optimization of stent design for ESS regulation, as detailed in this review, holds immense promise for improving long-term clinical outcomes post-PCI. However, the translation of these biomechanical principles faces several practical considerations and barriers to adoption. But we can facilitate ESS stent selection guidance practices through personalized stent selection and next-generation stent platforms.

Personalized stent selection

The integration of IVUS/OCT with CFD modeling, as discussed in Section 7.1, paves the way for a future where interventional cardiologists can move beyond generic stent selection. In practice, patient-specific CFD simulations could be procedurally to predict ESS distributions for different available stent designs (varying in thickness, shape, and length) within the target lesion.

Next-Generation stent Platforms: Modulation of ESS and clinical implications

Based on the strategy of “hemodynamic optimization,” next-generation stent platforms are likely to focus on advancements in ultra-thin strut stents and bioresorbable stents. The key structural feature of ultra-thin stents lies in their exceptionally slender metallic struts (typically with thicknesses below 60 μm) [98] which minimize flow disruption after implantation and significantly improve the intravascular hemodynamic environment. By reducing areas of flow disturbance, vortex formation, and flow separation, these stents enhance local endothelial shear stress (ESS). This optimized hemodynamic profile promotes rapid and healthy endothelialization and functional recovery, thereby mechanistically mitigating the risks of both restenosis and late stent thrombosis.

Bioresorbable stents, primarily fabricated from biodegradable materials such as polylactic acid (PLA) or magnesium alloys, consist of a temporary mesh-like stent often coated with anti-proliferative drugs. These devices are fully absorbed within 2 to 4 years, reducing risks such as in-stent thrombosis and restenosis, while also restoring vascular compliance and remodeling capacity[96]. It is anticipated that future designs may integrate ultra-thin stent technology with bioresorbable materials, resulting in stents that achieve perfect vascular compatibility and further reduce the incidence of clinical adverse events.

Conclusion and prospect

The design of vascular stents is an inherently interdisciplinary endeavor, uniting materials science, biomechanics, and clinical medicine to overcome the fundamental challenge of post-interventional vascular healing. The ultimate therapeutic objective is to restore the intricate ecological equilibrium of the vessel wall, an equilibrium frequently compromised by suboptimal stent implantation. This disruption manifests as a tripartite dysregulation of vascular biomechanical, cellular, and physicochemical-immune systems—a conceptual framework we have termed the “three balances theory. [99]” Consequently, the development of effective vascular stents mandates a synergistic collaboration across mechanical engineering, materials science, nanotechnology, and pharmacology. Such collaboration is geared toward creating next-generation devices capable of conforming to complex vascular anatomies, incorporating multifunctional surface coatings, and deploying advanced drug-release systems. These innovations are essential for mitigating persistent liabilities such as geometric mismatch, poor biocompatibility, and inefficient drug delivery, which collectively perpetuate this ecological imbalance.

To this end, our group has proposed the “suitcordance theory,” [100]a paradigm predicated on achieving both short-term suitability and long-term concordance to re-establish the three ecological balances between the implanted device and the host vessel.

The foundational prerequisite is short-term suitability, defined as the precise and mechanically sound initial deployment of the stent. Future clinical practice can leverage the distinct advantages of IVUS (deep tissue penetration for assessing vascular remodeling) and OCT (unprecedented resolution for defining luminal contours and vulnerable plaque features) to construct high-fidelity, patient-specific vascular models. By integrating these imaging modalities with computational fluid dynamics (CFD), it becomes possible to transcend the rigid-wall assumption by employing fluid–structure interaction (FSI) models, which more accurately simulate the dynamic interplay between plaque, blood flow, and the device. This deep integration of CFD and intravascular imaging into clinical workflows promises to yield an automated, intelligent platform for precise, image-guided device implantation. Such precision forms the cornerstone of short-term suitability, minimizing acute mechanical trauma and creating an optimal environment for favorable long-term vascular healing.

Following a successful implantation, the therapeutic focus must shift to long-term concordance—the harmonious co-evolution of the vessel and the device. This objective is the primary driver behind the development of next-generation stent platforms. A sophisticated understanding of how stent geometry modulates endothelial shear stress (ESS) and its downstream regulatory pathways is vital for engineering designs that foster a pro-regenerative cellular and physicochemical-immune milieu. Precise implantation, therefore, serves as the catalyst for the long-term co-modulation of the vascular-device fate, ultimately restoring a sustained, stable, and robust equilibrium among the three ecological systems. The suitcordance principle thus provides a guiding philosophy for the design of future interventional devices, redefining the therapeutic objective beyond simple mechanical stenting toward the holistic restoration of long-term vascular health.
