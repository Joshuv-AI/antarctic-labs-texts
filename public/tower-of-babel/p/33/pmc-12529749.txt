The hunt for the last relevant paper: blending the best of humans and AI
Authors: Rens van de Schoot, Bruno Messina Coimbra, Tale Evenhuis, Peter Lombaers, Felix Weijdema, Laurens de Bruin, Rutger Neeleman, Elizabeth Grandfield, Marit Sijbrandij, Jelle Jasper Teijema, Elena Jalsovec, Michiel Pieter Bron, Sonja Winter, Jonathan de Bruin, Mirjam van Zuiden
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: The exponential growth of research literature makes it increasingly difficult to identify all relevant studies for systematic reviews and meta-analyses. While traditional search methods are labour-intensive, modern AI-aided approaches have the potential to act as a powerful ‘super-assistant’ during both the searching and screening phases.Objective: This paper evaluates how a combined, open-source approach – merging traditional and AI-aided search and screening methods – can help identify all relevant literature up to the ‘last relevant paper’ for a systematic review on post-traumatic stress symptom (PTSS) trajectories after traumatic events.Method: We applied eight search strategies, including database searches, snowballing, full-text retrieval, and semantic search via OpenAlex. All records were screened using a combination of human reviewers, active learning, and large language models (LLMs) for quality control.Results: On top of replicating the original 6,701 search results, we identified an additional 3,822 records using AI-aided methods. The combination of AI tools and human screening led to 126 relevant studies, with each method uncovering papers the others missed. Notably, machine-aided techniques helped find studies with missing keywords, unusual phrasing, or limited indexing. Across all AI-assisted strategies, 10 additional studies were identified, and while the overall yield was modest, these papers were unique and relevant and would likely have been missed using traditional methods.Conclusions: Our findings demonstrate that even when returns are low, AI-aided approaches can meaningfully enhance coverage and offer a scalable path forward when combined with screening prioritisation. A transparent, hybrid workflow where AI serves as a ‘super-assistant’ can meaningfully extend the reach of systematic reviews and increase the quality of the findings, but is not ready to replace humans fully.KEYWORDS: Meta-analysis, screening prioritisation, large language model, post-traumatic stress disorder, artificial intelligence

Introduction

Researchers rely on systematic reviews and meta-analyses to summarise data to provide comprehensive overviews of important topics. However, they face an overwhelming and rapidly expanding volume of primary studies on nearly every topic, and the current AI (Artificial Intelligence) revolution is expected to exacerbate this challenge even further (Dennstadt et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2024; Syriani et al., 2023). Systematic literature screening can involve going through tens of thousands of titles, abstracts, and full-text publications, making the process both time-consuming and error-prone (Borah et al., 2017). Compiling a dataset of potentially relevant papers is an iterative process that balances recall (including as many relevant studies as possible) with precision (limiting the total number of studies that must be screened) (Lefebvre et al., 2008). Traditionally, researchers use metadata and Boolean queries (AND/OR statements) to identify candidate publications (Higgins et al., 2019). Moreover, screening the identified literature is extremely labour-intensive, and humans are prone to errors in such repetitive tasks (Wang et al., 2020) – a domain where AI excels, but is not necessarily flawless, as we demonstrate in the current paper. Therefore, we have undertaken a study combining the best of human and AI methods in both searching for relevant papers and screening these with the goal of finding an optimal strategy for uncovering every relevant publication for our use case, engaging in The Hunt for the Last Relevant Paper.

To ensure that all relevant papers are identified and included in the screening dataset, a so-called sensitive search, the strategy should include both the main concepts from the research question and the inclusion criteria and related concepts using Boolean ‘OR’ operators. Although this approach improves comprehensiveness, see Figure 1(A) with hypothetical search results, it also generates more irrelevant records, increasing the time needed for screening. To manage large record volumes, researchers often apply a more precise search incorporating Boolean ‘AND’ statements or limit the research question's scope, potentially narrowing the focus of systematic reviews. Some authors even avoid broad questions altogether if they anticipate a flood of search results, which can lead to many small-scale systematic reviews instead of more comprehensive meta-analyses that could strengthen the evidence base in a field. Another common restriction involves excluding studies published in languages other than English, sometimes due to time constraints and because research teams may lack proficiency in those languages (Greenland & O'Rourke, 2001; Neimann Rasmussen & Montgomery, 2018). Finally, researchers frequently set date ranges or exclude certain publication types, such as books, conference papers, pre-prints, or letters to the editor, to limit the total number of records that must be screened.

Applying the aforementioned restrictions during the search process might increase the risk of selection bias in the results of synthesised research (Dobrescu et al., 2021; Egger et al., 1997; Klassen et al., 2005; Morrison et al., 2012; Pieper & Puljak, 2021; Vera-Baceta et al., 2019). For instance, excluding certain publication types, such as conference papers or pre-prints, may systematically exclude emerging research trends, as many new findings are initially presented in these formats before being published as peer-reviewed journal articles (Vera-Baceta et al., 2019). Additionally, limiting the scope to studies published within a specific date range may bias the results toward contemporary perspectives, potentially excluding foundational studies that provide context or contrast to newer findings (Dobrescu et al., 2021).

Researchers can adopt additional techniques to mitigate these risks, such as snowballing (van Haastrecht et al., 2021). Tracing references from one study to another may uncover relevant articles not indexed in the initial databases or represent perspectives overlooked by the original search terms.

Another option is to use a database with full-text data, because searching directly in full-text content overcomes the limitations of metadata-only approaches, granting access to important concepts that might otherwise remain hidden. However, most databases only provide access to metadata, which means researchers rely on a relatively small portion of the available text for identifying relevant references. A counter-example is the Dimensions database, which indexes the full text of research articles and allows semantic searching instead of keyword-based searching (Herzog et al., 2020). By searching directly in full-text, one can review larger portions of the document, thereby increasing the likelihood of discovering critical studies. On the one hand, full-text searches are more sensitive because they pick up relevant fragments throughout the entire text, reducing the risk of missing key articles. On the other hand, searching large amounts of full-text data significantly increases the number of potentially relevant records, making the screening process far more time-consuming and labour-intensive. Also, searching in full-text requires access to the full-text, advanced machine learning methods and computational resources to deal with the large number of textual data points.

Despite efforts to increase sensitivity and reduce bias, broader search methods inevitably yield more records for screening; see Figure 1(B). As the volume of literature continues to grow – fuelled by AI advancements – adopting methods that can efficiently manage large datasets becomes essential. One promising solution is active learning, a machine-learning strategy where a classification model predicts the relevance of unseen papers based on an initial training set and then requests expert labels for those most likely to be relevant (Cohen et al., 2009; Salton & Buckley, 1990; van de Schoot et al., 2021). This prioritisation ensures that only a portion of the dataset requires screening by a human because, at a certain point, only irrelevant records are presented to the screener, and at some point, the screener may stop screening (Wallace et al., 2010). Numerous simulation studies have validated this screening prioritisation by mimicking real-world screening with manually labelled data, and this human-AI interaction always results in work saved (see, for a systematic review, Teijema, Seuren, et al., 2023). Numerous tools have integrated screening prioritizations to aid systematic reviews, including ASReview (van de Schoot et al., 2021), Abstrackr (Wallace et al., 2012), Colandr (Cheng et al., 2018), Covidence, Rayyan (Ouzzani et al., 2016), RobotAnalyst (Przybył a et al., 2018), Research Screener (Chai et al., 2021), DistillerSR (Hamel et al., 2020), and robotreviewer (Marshall et al., 2017).

Instead of screening fewer papers, one can also use the gain by covering a much larger pool of papers and only screening the most likely relevant papers (van de Schoot et al., 2021). For example, the Center for Urban Mental Health sought to map factors and interactions related to the onset, maintenance, and relapse of anxiety, depression, and substance use disorders. Their initial broad search retrieved 2,739,753 records. After refining the search terms, they still had 165,046 records to screen by title and abstract – an overwhelming number for conventional methods, yet feasible with an active learning pipeline. A team of seven screeners ultimately reviewed 11,571 of the 165,046 records manually (Brouwer et al., 2022), including 246 papers in the final meta-analyses (Franzoi et al., 2024). This outcome illustrates the practical advantage of using active learning with highly sensitive searches.

Another option for handling large volumes of text data is to use large language models (LLMs) for screening, which is becoming ever more popular (Scherbakov et al., 2024). Recent AI breakthroughs, popularised by chatbots like ChatGPT, have triggered a revolutionary paradigm shift across many fields (Qureshi, 2024), potentially affecting systematic reviews as well. In contrast to traditional machine-learning algorithms – trained to classify inputs based on human-labelled examples or active learning protocols – LLMs learn to predict language patterns by processing vast amounts of text. Essentially, they ‘read’ sequences of words in parallel, detecting context and predicting likely subsequent words, which produces a human-like understanding of language. When applying LLMs to screening abstracts, the typical approach involves preparing a prompt for each record with some instructions on the task, followed by the screening criteria and the title and abstract of the paper in question. The LLM then returns an inclusion decision based on the prompt. Studies like Guo et al. (2024) suggest that while LLM performance can sometimes match moderate human-like accuracy for screening tasks, it still varies significantly across different datasets (Dennstadt et al., 2024; Syriani et al., 2023). Moreover, LLMs can hallucinate, producing convincing but incorrect answers, and they rarely respond with ‘I don't know’ even when uncertainty is warranted (Feng et al., 2024; Guerreiro et al., 2023). To address these shortcomings, Bron et al. (2024) propose combining human screening with active learning while leveraging LLM-generated screening decisions. This human–AI hybrid approach may reduce the overall workload without sacrificing screening quality. In the current paper, we build on this hybrid screening approach to enhance labelling decisions while saving screening time – particularly critical when broader searches produce dramatically higher record counts.

Although the hybrid screening approach is promising, some papers may still be overlooked simply because they never appear in the dataset. Figure 1(A) illustrates a hypothetical scenario where five relevant papers are found outside a sensitive search. This issue arises when studies are published in journals not indexed by the searched databases – a problem made worse by the Western bias of many databases, which tend to feature expensive, well-known journals (Khanna et al., 2022). The OpenAlex database has been created as a nonprofit resource devoted to open scholarly principles to address these gaps. An early adopter of the Principles of Open Scholarly Infrastructure, OpenAlex regularly updates a fully open scientific knowledge graph of metadata for more than 250 million works, including journal articles, books, and conference abstracts (Priem et al., 2022). Crucially, its coverage of global settings, such as the global south, surpasses many traditional databases (Alonso-Alvarez & Eck, 2024; Maddi et al., 2024). One approach to reducing bias – especially the overrepresentation of studies from high-income Western countries – is to conduct a traditional keyword-based search within OpenAlex. However, such searches may still face selection bias if the chosen terms miss relevant papers. Another strategy is to use semantic searching when querying OpenAlex. Instead of Boolean queries, this approach employs similarity-based searches: once the titles and abstracts have been vectorised, a search engine like VESPA (Vespa.ai, 2025) can match these vectors to specific inclusion criteria or example papers or even just the inclusion criteria. The output is a ranked list of similarity scores, and top-ranked papers can then be screened for relevance. This method does not rely solely on predefined concepts or Boolean operators, making it more flexible and potentially more comprehensive. The key question, though, is whether machine-aided methods such as these can consistently retrieve all the papers found by traditional search methods – and whether they can also uncover unique studies that conventional strategies might miss.

Therefore, the main aim of this study is to examine whether AI can serve as a super-assistant in the most challenging phases of a systematic review. To explore this, we designed a fully transparent, reproducible, and hybrid workflow that combines traditional and AI-aided methods across both the search and screening stages (see Figure 2 for a schematic overview). Our goal is to assess how such a workflow can support the identification of all relevant studies, up to the point we call the ‘last relevant paper’, defined as the point at which no additional relevant studies can be uncovered despite extensive effort. We explore the potential of modern AI tools to act not as replacements for human reviewers, but as ‘super-assistants’ that enhance efficiency, broaden coverage, and improve label quality across the review pipeline. This is demonstrated through a real-world case: updating a systematic review on latent trajectory analyses of post-traumatic stress symptoms (PTSS) following traumatic events. In the methods section, we present eight search strategies, apply both human and AI-supported screening methods, and perform a simulation of screening prioritisation to test if it would solve the issue of the increased number of studies to screen due to adding more databases. This project has been pre-registered with PROSPERO (CRD42023494027) (van de Schoot et al., 2023), and our entire pipeline is designed to be both transparent and reproducible. All datasets, models, and scripts are publicly available and thoroughly referenced to ensure accountability and facilitate further research. Methods

Data

Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is a psychiatric condition that may develop after exposure to life-threatening or highly distressing events (Friedman et al., 2011). Its lifetime prevalence varies from about 3.9% to 10%, depending on factors like the population under study (e.g. military personnel vs. civilians) and the nature of the trauma (e.g. combat, sexual assault, or severe injury) (Heir et al., 2019; Koenen et al., 2017; Ribeiro et al., 2013). Post-traumatic stress symptoms (PTSS) are characterised by intrusive memories, avoidance of trauma-related stimuli, negative alterations in mood and cognition, and heightened arousal (American Psychiatric Association, 2022), and can follow a variety of trajectories. Most trauma-exposed individuals show little symptoms or recover from initial symptoms while others develop persistent or worsening symptoms (Brooks & Greenberg, 2024).

Given this variability, latent growth mixture modelling (LGMM) has emerged as a valuable and broadly/increasingly used methodological tool to identify unobserved subgroups of trauma-exposed individuals with comparable trajectories and clarify differences herein (Galatzer-Levy et al., 2018). LGMM is a statistical technique used to identify unobserved subgroups (or classes) within a population based on distinct longitudinal symptom trajectories. Unlike traditional growth modelling (LGM) – which assumes a single average trajectory across individuals – LGMM allows for heterogeneity by estimating multiple trajectory classes, each with its own pattern of change over time. This is particularly important in trauma research, where individuals often respond very differently to similar events.

An initial systematic review by Van De Schoot et al. (2017) identified 38 published studies investigating PTSS trajectories via LGMM up to 2016 (see also, van de Schoot et al., 2018, where the included studies were used to inform priors in a Bayesian LGMM). This review led to the development of a quality checklist for LGMM research, including, but not limited to, the field of PTSS trajectories. In the current paper, as part of the FORAS project (Framework for PTSS trajectORies: Analysis and Synthesis – van de Schoot et al., 2023), we update this originally identified PTSS dataset and extend the literature search from 2016 through 2023, capturing the latest work on LGMM-based PTSS trajectory studies.

Search methods

We employed a multi-faceted search strategy encompassing datasets derived from eight different search methods. By ‘data’, we mean the metadata (e.g. titles and abstracts) of scientific works obtained through various searches in different databases. Further details on each method and the construction of the overall dataset are provided in the subsections below.

SYNERGY version of the initial dataset

As a starting point, we used the initial dataset of PTSS trajectories, which covered studies up to 2016 and is publicly accessible at the Open Science Framework (OSF) (Winter & van de Schoot, 2021). The initial dataset contained 7,511 records, of which 38 were deemed relevant in (Van De Schoot et al., 2017) and 34 in (van de Schoot et al., 2018). After conducting an additional de-duplication round and enforcing the stricter inclusion criterion that each study has a persistent identifier (e.g. PubMed ID or DOI), the data was reduced to 4,544 records (including the 38 relevant ones). This refined version is part of the SYNERGY dataset (De Bruin et al., 2023), and we use it in the current paper for both snowballing and machine learning–aided methods.

Human-aided methods

Exact replication

We first created a dataset by replicating the search queries used in the initial study, drawing from the same four databases: PubMed, Embase, PsycINFO, and Scopus. We included a one-year overlap with the original search dates to accommodate any database corrections (Neeleman et al., 2024). We conducted this search on October 16th, 2023, and the specific queries and resulting RIS file can be found on the OSF (Coimbra et al., 2023a). The initial search yielded 13,843 records. After de-duplication, 6,701 records remained for screening.

Comprehensive search

Next, we performed a comprehensive search during the same timeframe, broadening our scope to include two additional databases (PTSDPubs and Web of Science). We also added MeSH terms for PubMed and corresponding thesaurus terms for Embase. This dataset's search queries and RIS file are available on the OSF (Coimbra et al., 2023b). This approach produced 17,008 records, which we reduced to 6,182 after de-duplication.

Snowballing

Finally, we constructed a third dataset using snowballing to capture papers citing any of the initially relevant studies. We ran this search in OpenAlex on December 18th, 2023, identifying 462 records (Lombaers, Van De Schoot, et al., 2024). The code implementing this snowballing approach is integrated into Datatools v1.2 (ASReview LAB developers, 2024a). We also explored a second-level snowballing step – screening papers that cited the first-level citing papers – but this yielded no new eligible studies. Therefore, we excluded this step from the main narrative for clarity and focus.

AI-aided methods

Full-text search via dimensions

We constructed a fourth dataset by using the full-text search option in Dimensions, enabling us to locate papers that mention key inclusion criteria (e.g. use of LGMM, PTSS outcomes) anywhere in the full text – regardless of whether these terms were present in the abstract. We applied the same 2016–2023 timeframe in the replication search; see Coimbra and Van de Schoot (2024) for the exact query. This search yielded 586 records, of which 511 were new additions to our database that had not already been found with any of the other search methods. We marked the remaining as already captured by previous methods, but labelled them to indicate they were also found via the full-text search.

Vectorised search via OpenAlex

The next three methods use a transformer model to encode records from the OpenAlex database (Priem et al., 2022) and perform vector-based searches for records semantically similar to a given query. We used the 2023-10-20 snapshot of OpenAlex, restricting results to works published in 2015 or later that had at least a title or abstract available. This subset comprised approximately 85 million records. We did not impose a language restriction, as we employed a multilingual transformer model (Wang et al. 2024) capable of handling multiple languages. The resulting 640GB of embedded data is hosted on HuggingFace (Global Campus, 2023).

To query this large vector set, we encoded our search text using the same multilingual model and then located the closest vectors based on cosine similarity using Vespa.ai (Vespa.ai, 2025). Efficient searching within the 85 million records was achieved using Vespa.ai, which constructs an HNSW (Hierarchical Navigable Small World) index to optimise retrieval (Malkov & Yashunin, 2020; Vespa.ai, 2024). This approach significantly speeds up the nearest-neighbor search by traversing a hierarchical structure rather than comparing every vector against every other vector. Further implementation details and scripts are available at Lombaers and van de Schoot (2024). Each query text produces a list of similarity scores for records in OpenAlex. The three methods below differ in the query texts used and in how we combine results across queries. In contrast to traditional database queries, the search texts used here were natural-language inputs encoded into vector representations. These included the full inclusion criteria (used in Method 1), full abstracts of the 34 included papers (Method 2), or combinations of both, depending on the approach. The exact texts and implementation details are publicly available in our GitHub repository (Lombaers, Van De Schoot, et al., 2024).

Nearest to inclusion criteria

We vectorised the systematic review’s inclusion criteria for the first OpenAlex method and used that embedding as our query. With an HNSW index based on cosine similarity, Vespa returned the top 5,000 records, from which we added the top 1,000 to the dataset (k = 900 after another round of de-duplication). We also generated rankings for all 5,000 to check whether additional relevant papers would appear further down the list. If relevant records were already in our master dataset, we marked them as discovered by this method; however, we did not add brand-new records at this stage. Relevant records within the larger 5,000-set were flagged accordingly (k = 1712).

Nearest to relevant abstracts

In the second method, we sought the most similar records to each of the vectorised relevant 34 abstracts in the initial review. We retrieved the top 5,000 records for each of the 34 abstracts, giving 170,000 total results. After de-duplication, we had 57,232 unique records. We initially aimed for N = 427 closest works per abstract (yielding roughly 7,000 records) but found this too large to screen. We then selected N = 50 per abstract, ending up with a top 935 records (after de-duplication) for screening and 4863 records flagged as being part of the longer set.

Nearest to work – short-listed with Active Learning

For the third method, we retrieved ten times N (i.e. top 500) closest works for each relevant abstract, resulting in 50,055 unique records. We then used ASReview (version 1.5) to train a logistic model on the original 34 included records. This model produced a relevance score for each of the 50,055 works (see implementation script: https://github.com/IDfuse/foras/). We extracted the top 1,000 records with the highest relevance (k = 930 after de-duplication), plus a top 7,000 subset to see whether more relevant records might appear with additional screening, of which 1733 records were flagged.

Data preparation

We merged the eight datasets into a single dataset (de Bruin & van de Schoot, 2025), flagging each paper by its discovery method and removing overlapping labels with the SYNERGY set (see Figure 2). Next, we searched for Digital Object Identifiers (DOIs; 6.5% still missing), OpenAlex identifiers (3.4% still missing), and titles/abstracts (1.7% still missing) for all records. When fuzzy matching (allowing 95% similarity of the titles accommodating for smal inconsistencies) in OpenAlex returned uncertain titles, we manually verified these records to ensure the identifiers referred to the correct works. If the metadata did not match, we removed the incorrect identifier. Throughout this process, we continued to remove additional duplicates to maintain the highest possible dataset quality.

In some cases (k = 73), duplicates were retained if they involved re-prints of the same paper or different versions of the same work (e.g. pre-print + final version, conference abstract + final version, book chapter + final version, dissertation + final version, or version 1 + version 2 of the same paper); both versions were given the same label. Improve initial set

As part of the current project, we re-evaluated the labelling decisions of the initial dataset published in van de Schoot et al. (2018); Van De Schoot et al. (2017). This re-labelling, referred to here as the Synergy dataset, involved a comprehensive review and re-application of the inclusion criteria to precisely match those used for the update, including updated definition of PTSS, scceptance of treatment studies, inclusion of studies using PTSD cluster sub-scores, allowance for overlapping datasets and correction of a labelling mistake. These refinements yielded 47 relevant papers in total (a net gain of 13 studies); see also Figure 4. Full details of the re-labelling proces are documented in the data codebook for the Synergy dataset, which is available on Dataverse (Van De Schoot, Coimbra, Evenhuis, et al., 2024).

Calibration

Two screeners (BC & RN) conducted an initial calibration session, refining the criteria and updating the screening handbook. Disagreements were discussed with the broader team until consensus was achieved. In a second round, these same screeners evaluated the same subset of 300 records (Kappa = .66, 98% agreement), with a third screener (BG; k = 3) resolving disagreements. Both datasets were created using ASReview datatools 1.3 (ASReview LAB developers, 2024a), and each calibration set included 100 older records, 100 newer records, and 100 randomly chosen records.

Title selection

One screener (BC) excluded records unrelated to the topic, such as animal studies, reviews, protocols, purely biological or psychometric studies, qualitative research, case reports, narrative or methodological studies, and studies on other psychiatric disorders lacking a trauma sample. Cross-sectional designs were also excluded at this stage. 51% of the records were excluded during title screening; see Figure 4.

Abstract selection

Human screening

Next, one screener (BC) reviewed the remaining 5,118 abstracts against each of the inclusion criteria. If one or more of the criteria were deemed irrelevant, the TI-AB label was 0; if all four criteria were labelled Relevant or Questionable, the TI-AB label was 1; see Figure 4 for an overview of the results.

Human – AI screening

Following the SAFE procedure (Boetje & van de Schoot, 2024), a second screener (RN) employed ASReview 1.5 to re-assess the most likely relevant abstracts (as predicted by the machine learning model) that the first screener had excluded either on Title level or on Abstract level. The Synergy and calibration datasets served as prior knowledge for training a model using Sbert for feature extraction and a two-layer neural network as the classifier. We applied a stopping heuristic of 50 irrelevant records in a row to limit screening. In total, 640 records were screened by the second screener, of which there were 71 disagreements with the first screener. After team discussion, 56 of these papers were advanced to full-text screening.

AI screening

As an additional quality check beyond our two-stage human screening process, we used a Large Language Model (LLM) to flag records that may have been mistakenly excluded. This was not intended to replace human judgment, but to explore whether an AI assistant could identify potential oversights or inconsistencies in labelling. As explained in Bron et al. (2024), we used ChatGPT-3.5-v0301 – and supplied the LLM with the inclusion criteria, prompting it to predict whether each record met each of the four criteria based on the title and abstract. For each record, we generated four prompts (one per criterion), allowing the LLM to answer ‘Yes’, ‘No’, or ‘Unknown’ (if the abstract contained insufficient information) and a rationale for each decision. Further details on the exact prompts and implementation are available in (Bron et al., 2024) and in the code repository at Bron and van de Schoot (2025), with data hosted at the OSF (Bron & Van De Schoot, 2024).

From the set of records that were given to the LLM, we selected the records where BC found grounds for exclusion and the LLM strongly disagreed with BC’s decision in (at least) one of the criteria (i.e. BC annotated ‘No’ while the LLM predicts ‘Yes’). Among these, we identified 97 disagreements between the labels provided by BC and the LLM for IC1, 4 for IC2, 128 for IC3, and 45 for IC4. After reviewing all these disagreements, we decided to move five papers to the full-text screening phase to double-check the inclusion criteria. Additionally, independent of the agreementsolving result, we moved four records to the FT screening phase because the LLM flagged these papers as fully relevant (4x TRUE).

Moreover, the LLM highlighted a gap in our inclusion criteria, suggesting that studies using supervised machine learning might qualify as relevant statistical method – something we had overlooked when specifying our criteria. Based on this insight, we searched the full database for records matching this new criterion (k = 17) and added five previously excluded papers to the screening process. So, in total, nine papers identified by the LLM were deemed relevant at TI-AB level.

Full-text selection

We advanced 248 records to the full-text screening round, where two screeners (BC & RN) independently applied the inclusion criteria. For 23 records, we were unable to obtain the full text – mostly conference abstracts – so we reviewed the available abstracts to assess whether they provided sufficient detail for inclusion. Ultimately, none of these 23 abstracts met the criteria, and we excluded them from the study. In total, there were 24 disagreements between the two screeners regarding inclusion. We resolved these discrepancies through group sessions with the broader team until we reached a consensus and included 130 studies, of which 4 are duplicates (3 pre-prints versus journal version, 1 dissertation versus journal article). We consider the dataset size k = 126 for all analyses and the simulation k = 130.

All exclusion reasons and screening decisions on the level of each of the inclusion criteria – both for the TI-AB and FT phase – are documented at the record level and publicly available via Dataverse (Coimbra et al., 2025) and are summarised in Figure 3.

Dataset quality

To ensure the highest-quality labels throughout the screening phases, we conducted comprehensive data quality tests examining label consistency, uniqueness, and completeness of key identifiers (MID values), and logical coherence across related columns. We also verified the synchronisation between primary datasets and second-screener data (van de Schoot, 2025). The resulting cleaned dataset is available at DataverseNL (Coimbra et al., 2025).

Data extraction

We extracted the meta-data characteristics of included papers that were uniquely identified by a single search method, examining how these papers were indexed in other databases. Any information about the content of the included papers – such as specific PTSS trajectories or other findings from the systematic review – falls outside the scope of this manuscript and is described elsewhere.

Analytic strategy

Our first outcome is the accuracy of each search strategy in identifying relevant literature. We evaluate this by comparing the TI-AB and final screening label for every record across the different search methods, focusing on the number of uniquely identified relevant papers per method. We then examine how active learning and a large language model (LLM) further influence the screening process, high-lighting their impact on capturing additional relevant papers. We then qualitatively analysed the unique papers to further examine why certain records were discovered by only one search or screening method. Drawing on meta-data (e.g. journal indexing), content elements (e.g. title/abstract descriptions), and descriptive comparisons, we aimed to determine whether these studies were missed due to search term limitations, coverage bias in specific databases, or other factors affecting their discoverability. Through this lens, we high-light potential gaps in traditional search techniques and underscore how advanced machine-learning approaches, including LLMs, can offer additional value.

Second, we ran a simulation study to assess the effectiveness of active learning in managing the large screening workload generated by multiple search strategies. We used the open-source ASReview software (ASReview LAB developers, 2024b) using the corrected Synergy Dataset as training data. We implemented these simulations using the Makita workflow generator (Teijema et al., 2024), following the ‘prior’ template (ASReview LAB developers, 2024c). We selected mxbai-embed-large-v1 as the feature extractor and Random Forest as the classifier, given their strong performance in previous studies targeting the ‘last relevant paper’ (Teijema, de Bruin, et al., 2023). Our key metric is the time to discover relevant records (Ferdinands et al., 2023). This approach allowed us to compare how effectively each setup could reduce the total screening burden while still capturing all relevant papers.

Results

Effectiveness of search strategies

Figure 5 illustrates how many additional records must be screened at the title and abstract (TI-AB) level when introducing a new search method. Starting with the exact replication approach, 6,700 records would require screening, 202 of which were deemed TI-AB relevant. Adding the comprehensive search introduced 1,120 more records to be screened, with 4 of them relevant at the TI-AB stage, and so on. In total, 3,822 records were added on top of the 6,700 by adding more search strategies, illustrating that each newly applied search method contributes both new relevant studies that might otherwise remain undiscovered and additional screening burden. Of the total 248 TI-AB–relevant records, 163 (65%) were identified by multiple methods, while 2 (17%) were consistently found by all methods. However, 56 records (23%) were uniquely discovered by a single method. Crucially, no combination of approaches captured every TI-AB–relevant record, underscoring the importance of using multiple search strategies to achieve comprehensive coverage.

Effectiveness of screening strategies

Out of the 640 records screened by the second screener, 71 disagreements arose with the first screener’s labels, of which 8 were ultimately deemed fully relevant (7 of these had been excluded at the title level). This means that 8 out of 126 included studies would have been missed without a second screener using active learning to revisit the first screener’s excluded papers. Additionally, nine papers reached TI-AB relevance due to the use of an LLM, and among those, 1 out of 126 was found to be fully relevant. This underscores how an LLM can enhance comprehensiveness in systematic reviews by uncovering studies potentially missed due to fatigue, oversight, or limited search scope.

Qualitative analysis

We found four full-text articles that would not have been identified by traditional search methods alone, along with one article uniquely included based on a suggestion from the large language model (LLM). In this section, we discuss these five articles (see also Figure 6), examining why other search methods did not retrieve them.

The first article, by Lee and Jhone (2022), was identified exclusively via the OpenAlex pipeline, which combined similarity scores with active learning to prioritise articles for inclusion. Written in Korean, it ranked 85th on the prioritisation list. Traditional methods missed this study because it was published in the journal ‘Korean Social Welfare Survey Research’, a journal not indexed by databases such as PTSDPubs, Web of Science, PubMed, Embase, PsycINFO, or Scopus. This case underscores a significant limitation of conventional database approaches, which often provide incomplete coverage for region-specific or niche journals.

A second article, by Schultebraucks et al. (2020), was identified through the OpenAlex pipeline (nearest to relevant abstracts; ranked 2,453rd) and also via snowballing, as it cited one of the initial search’s included articles (Galatzer-Levy et al., 2013). Traditional search queries did not retrieve this paper because its title and abstract used phrasing that did not match the specific terms in our search strategy (e.g. ‘trajectories’, ‘latent growth’, ‘latent factors’, ‘cluster’, ‘latent class analysis’, ‘mixed linear models’, ‘latent profile’, ‘mixture’, ‘typologies’, ‘profile’, ‘discriminant analysis’). Instead, the authors described their work as ‘development and validation of an algorithm for prediction of post-traumatic stress course over 12 months’, none of which triggered the Boolean queries used in our initial approach. This mismatch also applied to keywords and MeSH terms, which excluded the search terms we had specified. Consequently, keyword-based searches failed to locate this article, high-lighting a core limitation of relying exclusively on predefined terms.

Another article, by (Elklit et al., 2023), was uniquely identified through the snowballing method. It was published in November 2023, just two weeks after our formal search cutoff of October 2023, which was used for all other search strategies. Had the search dates been synchronised more precisely across all methods, this paper would likely have been missed – underscoring how arbitrary search cutoffs can affect the comprehensiveness of a systematic review.

A fourth article, by Nowocin et al. (2022), was exclusively identified via the full-text search. It did not appear among the top 7,000 articles prioritised by the OpenAlex pipeline, likely due to the terminology used in the abstract. Instead of terms like ‘trajectory’, the authors described PTSS trajectory analyses as ‘temperament changes’. Although relevant to mood and behaviour alterations, ‘temperament changes’ is neither a standard term in PTSS research nor part of our keyword or similarity-based search queries. As a result, traditional keyword-based searches and even similarity-based pipelines could not effectively identify this abstract due to the divergent language used. interestingly, a closer look at the full text revealed that the authors did indeed discuss trajectories and used terms that were part of our search strategy. This emphasises the added value of full-text searches, which can capture relevant information that is otherwise hidden from traditional metadata searches.

The fifth, paper by Chen et al. (2022), was deemed ineligible by both human reviewers but was identified as relevant by the LLM. The abstract referred to ‘unsupervised machine learning’ for half-year longitudinal trajectories – terminology not part of our original list of acceptable statistical methods. Upon closer inspection of the full text, however, the authors actually used Latent Growth Mixture Modelling (LGMM), which does meet our criteria. The LLM probably inferred that the mention of trajectory analysis could indicate a relevant study, whereas both human screeners dismissed it based on the wording in the abstract. Once Chen et al. (2022) were uncovered, we revised our inclusion criteria to include any mention of ‘unsupervised machine learning’. We then reassessed 17 papers with this phrase in their abstracts, adding five to the TI-AB set. Among those five, only the Chen et al. paper ultimately satisfied all other relevance criteria. This case illustrates how an LLM’s broader conceptual approach can reveal relevant studies that might otherwise be missed when human screeners rely on strictly predefined terminology in abstracts.

Lastly, as shown in Figure 6, several records were not identified through the OpenAlex pipeline, indicating that relying solely on AI-aided approaches does not resolve the problem of missing important studies. Some papers were not even part of the OpenAlex database, so they could never be found as the journal or dissertation repository is not indexed by OpenAlex, as is the case with Wangwang et al. (2023). However, other works are indexed in OpenAlex but obtained such a low similarity ranking it was not selected, as is the case with Roberson (2015). Or papers are overlooked by the semantic similarity pipelines due to unusual terminology, as is the case with the previously discussed paper by Nowocin et al. (2022), but, apparently, also with (Galovski et al., 2016; Martínez Ruiz et al., 2022; Shevlin et al., 2023).

Thus, only by combining multiple methods were we able to uncover all relevant studies. This high-lights how neither human based or AI-aided approaches alone suffice for comprehensive coverage, underscoring the need for a diverse, integrated hybrid search strategy – but at the cost of screening an enormous number of records.

Simulation study

To manage the increased number of records identified by multiple search methods, we explored the potential of active learning to reduce manual screening. Within screening the first 100 records, 61 of the 130 relevant records would have been identified if one had active learning. With 1,848 records screened (17% of the total dataset), the model had located all but one relevant record, the same paper only identified via the full-text search Nowocin et al. (2022). This represents a significant reduction compared to 10,522 papers manually screened by BC, illustrating active learning’s capacity to minimise screening workload while maintaining near-complete recall.

Discussion

In this study, we compared the accuracy of traditional search and screening methods in systematic reviews against AI-driven strategies. Our findings show that combining multiple approaches – Boolean database searches, full-text searches, and machine-aided searches in expansive corpora – collectively uncover all relevant papers, underscoring the complementary strengths of each method. As illustrated in Figure 1(B), the pursuit of the ‘last relevant paper’ cannot end with a limited set of database queries; rather, it requires broader, more inclusive resources to achieve comprehensive coverage. Moreover, active learning-aided screening significantly reduces the manual screening burden while maintaining high recall rates, a pivotal advantage when dealing with resource-intensive systematic reviews. Finally, large language models (LLMs) demonstrated their ability to boost screening label quality by identifying overlooked studies – particularly those obscured by unconventional phrasing or incomplete metadata. Taken together, these results demonstrate the value of blending both traditional and AI-based approaches to ensure a thorough and efficient systematic review process.

Why chase the ‘last relevant paper’ if it demands so much effort? According to the Pareto principle, roughly 20% of included papers yield 80% of the knowledge. One might, therefore, question whether it’s worth screening 3,822 additional records – beyond the initial search – just to find a handful of extra papers. Indeed, yet another Western study replicating the same results may offer little new insight. However, a paper describing genuinely novel results or conducted in a new cultural context can broaden perspectives and enhance the generalizability of findings. For instance, the first Korean study on this topic, by Lee and Jhone (2022), was uniquely identified through the OpenAlex pipeline, while Nowocin et al. (2022) – the only paper describing data from Central Europe – emerged solely via full-text searching. Missing such studies could limit the diversity of insights captured in a systematic review, suggesting that even small gains in coverage may be worth the additional effort.

However, we should not disregard traditional search methods, as the paper by (Wangwang et al., 2023), published in the Chinese Journal of Practical Nursing, was identified exclusively through a traditional search method because the journal is not indexed in OpenAlex. This high-lights that, while OpenAlex aspires to be more inclusive, it still has coverage gaps – particularly for region-specific or less widely recognised journals. Ultimately, as our findings illustrate, no single route – or even a full combination of methods – can guarantee the discovery of every relevant record. Reviewers and readers of this paper are likely to suggest yet another approach that may uncover a study missed by current methods.

Moreover, each method requires different resources. While we did not systematically log time per method, we can offer some general observations based on our experience:

Constructing traditional search queries (e.g. for databases like PsycINFO or Scopus) is time-intensive, often requiring multiple iterations of keyword refinement, Boolean logic tuning, and validation – especially when done to a high standard with the help of information specialists.

In our case, the team had both information specialists and data scientists on board, which made it feasible to apply and compare all these approaches. But our key takeaway is this: no single method – human or AI-driven – will yield a complete set of relevant papers. The most appropriate strategy depends on available expertise, time, and resources.

The challenge, therefore, is to decide how far to push the boundary, acknowledging that the pursuit of absolute completeness can become an ever-more resource-intensive undertaking. The more critical it becomes to find the very last relevant study, the greater the effort required. For instance, vectorising the OpenAlex database and using cosine similarity techniques demands both specialised knowledge and sufficient computing resources, such as cloud credits or robust data infrastructure. Meanwhile, full-text searching often involves commercial platforms – requiring grant funds or institutional subscriptions – and can still be somewhat of a black box, limiting the transparency of the search process. Given these complexities, it is vital to balance the depth of search strategies, the need for open and reproducible methods (Lombaers, de Bruin, et al., 2024), and the practical constraints of time, expertise, and funding. If the team includes information specialists, traditional methods (e.g. database queries, snowballing) can be maximised; if data scientists are on board, machine-learning pipelines or large language models become viable. In either case, each additional method comes at a cost – whether in time, money, or specialised skills – that must be weighed against the potential gain in thoroughness.

Despite offering a variety of methods and open-source approaches, our findings should be interpreted with a few limitations in mind. First, our analyses centred on a specific domain (PTSS and LGMM), and the effectiveness of different approaches may differ in fields with distinct terminologies or publication patterns. Second, none of the databases we used – including OpenAlex or Dimensions – provides complete coverage of all regional or niche journals, underscoring persistent indexing gaps that can still lead to missed publications. Third, the use of a single LLM (ChatGPT-3.5) and a specific active learning setup limits generalizability to other tools and configurations, especially as these technologies rapidly evolve. Fourth, we made iterative updates to our inclusion criteria (e.g. recognising ‘unsupervised machine learning’) during the review process, which may affect reproducibility if not meticulously documented, which we tried to do via the different resources published on the Open Science Framework. Fifth, we could have run additional simulation studies mimicking the screening process with different prior knowledge, but it has been shown that only a high number of records used as training data will impact the performance (Teijema et al., 2022), but not the selection of the records themselves if only two records are used (Byrne et al., 2024). Moreover, although we used the best-performing method based on a large simulation study using the Synergy version of the PTSS dataset (Teijema, de Bruin, et al., 2023), future work could test even better models or improve hyperparameters of the existing model to boost performance even further. Another limitation is that for the vectorised search strategy we used pragmatic cut-off thresholds (e.g. top 1,000 or top 50 per query) rather than theoretically determined stopping points. These decisions were driven by resource constraints and the need to keep the screening workload manageable. While we explored larger candidate sets to assess what might be missed beyond these thresholds, future work could benefit from developing adaptive or data-driven cut-off strategies. Moreover, our reliance on one-time search snapshots and the inherent subjectivity of human screeners – despite calibration and consensus steps – further underscores that no method, whether human-based or AI-aided, can guarantee 100% recall of every relevant study, and we need hybrid methods where AI serves as a super assistant for humans.

Lastly, we show that each new search method – while potentially increasing recall – also introduces a significant number of additional records to screen. This creates a clear tension between sensitivity and feasibility. However, screening prioritisation offers a practical solution: ranking the most likely relevant records first allows teams to focus their effort where it matters most, often stopping well before the whole set is screened. The simulation study in our study shows that this would have saved our screeners an enormous amount of screening time, which is supported by many other of such studies; see (Teijema, Seuren, et al., 2023) for a systematic review of such simulation studies. A common misconception is that AI-assisted screening requires a large, fully labelled training set. However, our results – and findings from prior simulation studies (e.g. Ferdinands et al., 2023) – demonstrate that even a minimal training set (e.g. one relevant and one irrelevant record) is sufficient to yield substantial prioritisation gains. While adding more labelled data leads to incremental improvements, the steepest performance increases occur early in the process. This makes prioritisation not only effective but also highly feasible, even for new reviews that are just beginning their screening phase.

In conclusion, our findings make it evident that even with exhaustive effort and sophisticated technologies, there remains the possibility that certain papers will still be missed. The evolving nature of academic publishing, varying terminologies, and publication delays mean that the pursuit of the ‘last relevant paper’ is inherently a never-ending process. Systematic reviews must remain adaptive, combining manual expertise with the dynamic capabilities of modern technology. Across all AI-assisted strategies, 10 additional studies were identified, and while the overall yield was modest, these papers were unique and relevant and would likely have been missed using traditional methods. This demonstrates that even when returns are low, AI-aided approaches can meaningfully enhance coverage and offer a scalable path forward when combined with screening prioritisation.
