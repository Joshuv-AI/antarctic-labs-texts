INESCO Dataset: indonesian expressive speech corpus for emotion speech synthesis
Authors: Elok Anggrayni, Agus Muhamad Hatta, Dhany Arifianto, Wirawan, Joko Sarwono
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA speech corpus is a crucial component in the development of language models in natural language processing (NLP). A high-quality corpus is a crucial factor in the quality of NLP study, including text-to-speech (TTS), also known as speech synthesis. In Indonesia, developing a high-quality corpus is a priority to ensure that speech synthesis technology better supports digital communications, the creative industries, and public services. Indonesian is still categorized as a low-resourced language, which means its use in digital communication remains limited. Therefore, this study addresses this gap by developing an Indonesian Expressive Speech Corpus (INESCO). INESCO was developed by selecting sentences from various literary sources, including textbooks, magazines, novels, newspapers, movies, and websites. The selected sentences consist of commonly used expressions in everyday conversation. The expressive Indonesian speech corpus contains three emotional styles: happiness (211 sentences), anger (190), and sadness (199 sentences). The total number of sentences in the Indonesian expressive speech corpus were 600. The Indonesian expressive speech corpus was recorded by four professional speakers, consisting of two females and two males. The selected voice artists were professional theater artists who were members of the Indonesian Arts Council, namely Teater Api from Surabaya, East Java, Indonesia and Teater Payung Hitam from Bandung, West Java, Indonesia. The voice artists were selected to record the corpus. Based on the criteria of being allowed to have a regional accent and having more than five years of experience, they were recorded in a soundproof studio with separate recordings and operator rooms to minimize background noise. The recording process used a condenser microphone (Neumann U87 and Studio Project B1) that was equipped with a pop filter. The speakers were asked to stand 2–3 cm in front of the microphone. The microphone was connected to Presonus Studio One version 4.1.4 software for recording and editing. Recorded speech corpus was under configuration with sampling frequency of 44.1 kHz, channel input/output mono, 16 bits/sample and using format “.wav” file. During the recording process, the chair of the Indonesian Arts Council supervised to ensure that every sentence recorded in the database conveyed the correct meaning and evaluated whether all the speakers’ balance was maintained across the three types of emotions. The phonetic annotation process was performed using the Wavesurfer speech software. The emotional validation method was implemented through a set of preliminary experiments on the end-to-end TTS system for Indonesian to demonstrate the dataset's usability. Only files with a mean opinion score (MOS) score of 3.5 or higher were used in the INESCO dataset. The final INESCO dataset compiled 2400 audio files with a text transcription. The total size of the files was 1.33 GB. The total duration of this speech corpus was 3 h, 1 min, and 49 s. This dataset contains comprehensive, carefully curated data to enable reproducible experimentation in end-to-end TTS systems and to support research in speech synthesis, speech recognition, and other areas of natural language processing. The INESCO dataset is publicly accessible and useful for research, academic, and educational purposes.Keywords: Expressive speech synthesis, Indonesian corpus, Low-resource language, Natural language processing, Signal processing, Speech dataset

Background

A speech corpus is the basis for analyzing speech signal characteristics and for developing speech synthesis and recognition systems [1]. A high-quality corpus is very important for high-quality speech synthesis results [2]. In this study, we developed an Indonesian speech corpus comprising three emotional styles (happiness, anger, and sadness) to support a variety of applications. Indonesian is still categorized as a low-resourced language, even though it is spoken by almost 3.47% of the global population, and Indonesia ranks fourth by population [3]. It means its use in digital communication remains limited. To develop a corpus of sentences for digital communication, the corpus must be phonetically balanced. This study addresses this gap by developing the Indonesian Expressive Speech Corpus (INESCO) dataset that has been standardized according to the International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA) standard and evaluated for proper grammar by Indonesian experts. The INESCO dataset meets the criteria for standardized speech corpora.

This INESCO dataset was used in the related article [4], which investigated speech synthesis and demonstrated the dataset's use by using the collected data. This data article has presented the curated dataset used in that research, along with the detailed experimental setup and a reproducible framework for use by other researchers studying text-to-speech (TTS) system in low-resourced language.

This article consists of four sections; the first section explains the value of the data for the scientific community. Section II describes the background and purpose of the study. Section III provides a description of the Indonesian expressive speech corpus (INESCO) dataset for emotion speech synthesis. Section IV provides a complete description of the experimental design, methods used to acquire the INESCO dataset, describes the results of the recorded speech corpus, and ends with future work.

Data Description

The Indonesian expressive speech corpus was recorded in three emotional styles: happiness (211 sentences), anger (190 sentences), and sadness (199 sentences). The sentences used to develop the Indonesian expressive speech database were selected based on phonetic balance criteria that conform to the International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA) standard. This sentence database consisted of 600 sentences, including three sentence lengths: short, medium, and long. Short sentences consisted of 1 or 2 words, medium sentences consist of 3 to 7 words, and long sentences consisted of 8 to 11 words. All sentences in the Indonesian expressive speech corpus have been evaluated and verified for proper grammar by two Indonesian experts from the Department of Indonesian Literature, Faculty of Humanities, Airlangga University.

This Indonesian expressive speech corpus was recorded in a studio or soundproof room with separate recording and control rooms to minimize background noise. The Indonesian expressive speech corpus was recorded by four professional speakers, consisting of two females and two males. The selected voice artists are professional theater artists who were members of the Indonesian Arts Council, namely Teater Api from Surabaya, East Java, Indonesia, and Teater Payung Hitam from Bandung, West Java, Indonesia. The voice artists were selected to record the corpus based on the criteria of being allowed to have a regional accent and having more than five years of experience as a theater artist.

The Indonesian expressive speech corpus (INESCO) [5] is published on Mendeley Data and organized into 2 folders: (a) INESCO Dataset contains the recorded audio for 2400 audio files from four professional speakers (two females and two males) which are saved as “.wav” files and INESCO_sentences.xls (Excel file) contains corresponding transcription and the type of expression in each sentence; (b) guideline of file naming INESCO dataset. Experimental Design, Materials, and Methods

Design and sentence selection

Along with the development of speech processing technology, not only universal languages from big countries like English, Japanese, and Chinese, which have built their own speech corpus, but many other countries have also built their own speech corpus, such as Vietnam [6], Pakistan [7], Ghana [8], and Arabic [9]. In Indonesian, a dataset that was previously developed and published to support research on expressive speech processing technology is called the IndoWaveSentiment: Indonesian Audio Dataset for Emotion Classification [10]. This IndoWaveSentiment dataset was recorded by ten professional speakers, consisting of five female and five male speakers. All speakers uttered the same sentence, namely “kualitas HP ini cukup bagus,” which means “the quality of this smartphone is quite good”, expressing 5 different emotions with normal and strong intensities: neutral, happy, surprised, disgusted, and disappointed. The IndoWaveSentiment dataset consists of 30 audio files per speaker, totaling 300 audio files (30 × 10 speakers). The IndoWaveSentiment dataset used only a one-sentence corpus. The IndoWaveSentiment dataset is not a phonetically balanced speech corpus because it uses only a single-sentence format. The phoneme-based speech corpus produced more natural speech synthesis than the syllable-based speech corpus [11]. Therefore, the speech corpus must be phonetically balanced to produce high-quality speech results [11,12].

Given the importance of a high-quality speech corpus that meets phonetically balanced standards for producing high-quality speech results, this study aims to address this gap. In this study, we developed an Indonesian expressive speech corpus (INESCO) to support research in speech synthesis, speech recognition, and other areas of natural language processing. The selection of database sentences was based on Indonesian phoneme rules conforming to the International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA) standard, which included the silence symbol. Indonesian had a total of 33 phonemes: six vowel phonemes, three diphthong phonemes, and 23 consonant phonemes, as well as one silence symbol [[13], [14], [15]]. In developing the sentence database, consideration was also given to Indonesian linguistic conventions based on the standardized Indonesian Spelling, which has been in use since 1972 in accordance with Presidential Decree No 57 of 1972. This standard regulated three aspects: letter writing, word writing, and punctuation. Additionally, in designing the Indonesian language sentence database, declarative (statement) and interrogative (question) sentences are classified within the natural Indonesian language database. Meanwhile, imperative (command) sentences and exclamative sentences (expressions of surprise or admiration) are classified in the expressive database.

The Indonesian expressive sentences database were uttered in three emotional styles: anger, sadness, and happiness. This sentence database consisted of 600 sentences covering these three expressions, with variations in short, medium, and long sentences. This database of Indonesian expressive sentences was created by selecting sentences from various literary sources, including textbooks, magazines, novels, newspapers, movies, and websites. The selected sentences were those commonly used in everyday conversations. Table 4 below shows examples of sentences from the Indonesian expressive speech database.

Recording process

The database recording was conducted by having the selected speakers record sentences. For the expressive database, the speakers chosen for this study were professional theater artists who were members of the Surabaya Arts Council (represented by Teater Api in Surabaya, East Java, Indonesia) and the Bandung Arts Council (represented by Teater Payung Hitam in Bandung, West Java, Indonesia). These speakers were selected prior to recording, with the criteria that they were permitted to have a regional accent from their hometown and had more than five years of experience as theater artists. The selection of the speakers was conducted through a short interview and audition, during which volunteers were asked to read three sample Indonesian expressive speech sentences. The voice speakers for the Indonesian expressive speech database consisted of two male and two female voice speakers. The recording process was supervised by the chair of the Indonesian Arts Council to ensure that every sentence recorded in the database conveyed the correct meaning.

The recording process was conducted in a recording studio or soundproof room with separate recording and operator rooms as shown in Fig. 13. This setup was chosen to ensure that no background noise was recorded. The recording process used condenser microphones (Neumann U87 and Studio Project B1) equipped with pop filters. The pop filters were used to ensure that the recorded sound was clear and accurate by minimizing background noise. The narrator was asked to stand 2–3 cm in front of the microphone. The recording process uses a microphone set up as shown in Fig. 9. The microphone was connected to the PreSonus Studio One version 4.1.4 software for recording and editing [16]. All the artists whose voices were recorded at different times used the same sentences. The recording process for the Indonesian expressive speech database was conducted at several recording studios in Surabaya, Indonesia. The criteria for selecting recording studio locations were soundproof rooms that were separate from the operator’s room. This was done to ensure that no outside noise was recorded. Database processing

The results of the recording process were sorted before entering the next stage of processing. Database processing was the process of editing raw recording files by splitting the entire recording, consisting of 600 sentences per speaker, into individual sentences. The process involved removing the beginning and end of the signal. These sections were silent for a long time and served as transitional sections at the beginning and end of the recording, linking the previous sentence to the next. As shown in Fig. 12, the red boxes indicated the silent parts for a long time of the speech signal that would be removed. These silent parts were not uploaded to the INESCO dataset’s Mendeley Data repository because they contained absolutely no speech signal information. The results of the Indonesian expressive database recording process were saved as “.wav” files for a single sentence. Thus, the Indonesian expressive speech corpus (INESCO) compiled 2400 audio files (600 sentences × 4 speakers = 2400 audio files).

An example of the silent parts which would be removed.

To ensure the quality of the INESCO dataset, we manually inspected the waveform and the frequency of the single sentences using a spectrogram in MATLAB [17]. A spectrogram was used to analyze speech signals from audio files that result from speech recordings. Speech signal processing was performed by examining the signal’s representation in both the time and frequency domains. To plot a spectrogram, we used the Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) equation [18]. Fig. 14 shows a comparison of the plot waveform and spectrogram of sample sentences from the INESCO dataset representing (a) natural expression for the sentence “boneka beruang di toko itu lucu sekali,” which has the meaning ”the teddy bear in the shop is very cute,” and (b) sadness expression for the sentence “cukup, jangan diteruskan!” which has the meaning “enough, please don't continue!”. The waveform plots have the x-axis representing time and the y-axis representing the amplitude of the speech signal. These waveform plots show information about the amplitude and duration of the sentence utterances. Meanwhile, spectrogram plots show time on the x-axis and frequency on the y-axis; they represent speech in the frequency domain. The graphs show that each voice has a similar frequency pattern. From the two spectrogram examples above, no noise was detected in the audio file resulting from the speech recording. After manually examining each audio waveform in the recording file of the Indonesian expressive speech corpus (INESCO) dataset, it can be concluded that there was no external noise or buzzing noise caused by electrical noise in the recording equipment, such as from adjusting the cables.

Validation of the database

To ensure that the recorded audio files accurately represent the text sentences in the database, the next step was to annotate the database by segmenting and labeling each recorded sentence. Labeling was a process that defines the boundaries of an acoustic unit in an utterance. Segmentation and labeling can be performed using two methods: manual labeling with Wavesurfer or Praat, and automatic labeling with festfox tools. Manual segmentation and labeling are more accurate than automatic segmentation and labeling. In this research, the phonetic annotation process was performed by using Wavesurfer speech software to verify whether the audio file has an accurate transcription for each phoneme [19]. This tool produced the mono label. In a mono label, there was just information about the phoneme start and finish in one sentence. The process involved listening to the audio and marking the location of each phoneme to ensure that the spoken sentence matches the transcript. The Indonesian expressive speech corpus (INESCO) dataset was uttered in three emotional styles: anger, sadness, and happiness. The emotional validation method was implemented through a set of preliminary experiments on the end-to-end text-to-speech (TTS) system for Indonesian to demonstrate the dataset's usability. This INESCO dataset was used in the related article [4], which investigated speech synthesis by using the collected data. We have investigated a low-resource dataset based on a phonetically balanced standard using fine-tuning the Tacotron2 model to synthesize speech. This study proved that using a minimum dataset that met phonetic balance standard was able to produce more closely resembles natural speech. Only files with a mean opinion score (MOS) score of 3.5 or higher were used in the INESCO dataset.

The final INESCO dataset compiled 2400 audio files (600 sentences × 4 speakers = 2400 audio files) with a text transcription. The total size of the files was 1.33 GB. The total duration of this speech corpus was 3 h, 1 min, and 49 s, which were uttered by four professional theater artists. The sentences of the speech database were selected based on phonetic balance criteria that conform to the International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA) standard. In addition, all sentences in the INESCO dataset have been evaluated and verified for proper grammar by Indonesian experts. Based on the result of validation, the INESCO dataset has met the criteria for standardized speech corpora. Thus, the INESCO dataset aimed to advanced expressive Indonesian speech processing technology and enabled reproducible experiments in end-to-end TTS, speech recognition, and other areas of natural language processing.

One of the most significant advancements in speech processing technology is its ability to adapt to local dialects and languages. However, considerable work remains to be done, especially in developing a comprehensive Indonesian speech corpus. In future work, we aim to develop an Indonesian expressive speech corpus with a more diverse variety of local dialects from speakers located throughout Indonesia. The speakers will be selected from various tribes spread throughout Indonesia. Thus, it is hoped that the Indonesian expressive speech corpus will become more diverse in local dialects and be useful in various fields.

Limitations

The recording process for the Indonesian expressive sentence database was conducted by four speakers with accents from two Indonesian tribes: the Javanese tribe, represented by a speaker from East Java, and the Sundanese tribe, represented by a speaker from West Java. For future research, we aim to add more voices featuring speakers from various regions in Indonesia to diversify their accents. This development is expected to support the development of a reliable natural language processing (NLP) system that can be applied widely to low-resource languages and various fields.
