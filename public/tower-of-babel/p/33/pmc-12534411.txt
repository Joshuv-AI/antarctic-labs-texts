Efficient active hydrogen delivery for drug-free radiation enteritis therapy in mice
Authors: Xianggui Yin, Changfen Bi, Yuanfang Chen, Xueyin Hu, Guangyou Shi, Shuqin Li, Wen Zhang, Longbo Ma, Saijun Fan, Luntao Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Radiation enteritis, affecting over 90% of pelvic/abdominal radiotherapy patients, is primarily caused by radiation-induced reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (RONS). Active hydrogens, with broad-spectrum RONS scavenging ability, show radioprotective potential but face delivery challenges due to the intestinal mucus barrier and short lifespan. Here, we show drinkable, self-thermophoretic sodium alginate/chitosan oligosaccharide-coated hydrogenated molybdenum oxide nanomachines (HxMoO3@SA@COSs) that exhibit near-infrared (NIR)-driven directional motility and sustained active hydrogen release. In a male mouse model of radiation enteritis, HxMoO3@SA@COSs overcome the mucus barrier, prolong intestinal retention, and deliver active hydrogen to injury sites, enabling precise enteritis therapy. Beyond RONS scavenging, the released hydrogen induces anti-inflammatory macrophage polarization, increases goblet cell abundance, and modulates gut microbiota, promoting intestinal repair. This hydrogen-based, drug-free strategy demonstrates superior efficacy in treating radiation enteritis.

Introduction

Radiation therapy is a cornerstone modality in cancer treatment, benefiting more than 50% of cancer patients1–5. High-energy ionizing radiation (IR) efficiently destroys cancer cells but can also cause collateral damage to surrounding healthy tissues6–8. This damage primarily arises from enormous quantities of scattered radiation and the excessive generation of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (RONS) during ionization. These RONS cause extensive damage to nucleic acids, lipids and proteins, ultimately triggering inflammation responses9–11. Due to the rapid proliferation and renewal capacity of gut epithelial cells, the intestine is particularly vulnerable to radiation damage12,13. Nearly all patients undergoing pelvic or abdominal radiotherapy experience varying degrees of intestinal dysfunction, manifesting as abdominal pain, diarrhea, hematochezia, and, in severe cases, mucosa ulceration, erosion, and bleeding14. In addition to the dearth of safe and efficient therapeutic agents available in clinical practice, drug delivery is significantly hampered by the extensive mucus barrier15–17. The treatment of radiation enteritis remains a significant clinical challenge.

Hydrogen gas (H2) has been proved to significantly alleviate various radiation-induced pathologies18–21, including damage to the gastrointestinal system22, nervous system23,24, hematopoietic system25 as well as injuries to the brain and skin26,27. This protective effect is primarily attributed to the selective scavenging ability of H2 towards RONS (·OH and ONOO-), which in turn reduces oxidative stress, activates the endogenous antioxidant system, and mitigates inflammation19,28. Moreover, H2 features high biosafety and strong tissue diffusivity, enabling it to penetrate into cellular and subcellular organelle29. Yet, the mechanism of H2, as an inert physiological gas, exerts its radioprotective effects remain inadequately understood. Recent studies have demonstrated that mitochondria may serve as key targets for H2, where hydrogen molecules interact with iron porphyrins to neutralize RONS30,31. The iron porphyrins acted as biocatalysts, similar to noble metals such as platinum and palladium, catalyzing the conversion of H2 into active hydrogen atoms32–34. These active hydrogen atoms, characterized by a low reduction potential (H+/H·), possess broad-spectrum RONS scavenging capability35,36, suggesting their potential for drug-free treatment of radiation damage. Nonetheless, due to the intestinal mucus barrier and the short lifespan of active hydrogens37,38, delivering hydrogens to the injury site has proven to be extremely difficult.

Micro/nanomotors show promise in overcoming physiological barriers–such as mucosal barriers, epithelial barriers, and mucus barriers–enabling more effective drug delivery39–43. This capability is attributed to their unique motility, powered by external stimuli such as ultrasound, light, or magnetic fields44–47. Our previous work demonstrated that asymmetric thermal gradients-mediated thermophoresis-propelled nanomotors by NIR light could powerfully traverse physiological barriers due to their strong directional movement and the spatiotemporal manipulability of NIR light48. The formation of asymmetric thermal gradients usually relies on Janus structures49–51, which often require complex and time-consuming fabrication methods like vacuum deposition, template electrodeposition, or sputtering, resulting in low yield and uncontrollability52–54. Fortunately, thermal field simulations have shown that asymmetrical thermal gradients can also be generated on simply spherical nanostructures under NIR laser irradiation, thus overcoming the manufacturing limitations associated with thermophoresis-powered nanomotors.

Herein, we developed drinkable, spherical and self-thermophoresis-powered hydrogenated molybdenum oxide nanomachines (HxMoO3@SA@COSs) coated with sodium alginate and chitosan oligosaccharide via a wet chemistry method, that exhibits exceptional active-hydrogen storage capacity for efficient delivery. HxMoO3@SA@COSs featured exceptional directional motility driven by near-infrared (NIR) light and the ability to spontaneously and sustainably release active hydrogens, which was applied for treating acute radiation enteritis (Fig. 1). Due to the low redox potential of active hydrogens (H+/H·, -0.34 V vs NHE), HxMoO3@SA@COSs exhibited broad-spectrum RONS scavenging capability. Additionally, the introduction of active hydrogens significantly reduced the band gap of the MoO3 component, resulting in enhanced NIR absorption and excellent photothermal conversion efficiency of approximately 46.5% at 808 nm. In both in vitro and in vivo studies, HxMoO3@SA@COSs exhibited superior motility under 808 nm NIR light, enabling efficient penetration of the mucus barrier and prolonged retention in the intestine. Surprisingly, beyond their efficient scavenging of cytotoxic RONS, the released active hydrogens induced macrophage polarization toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype, increased goblet cell abundance, and modulated gut microbiota. Overall, after HxMoO3@SA@COSs intragastric administration, oxidative stress and inflammation levels were significantly reduced, resulting in improved survival rates without observable side effects. Results

Preparation and characterization of HxMoO3@SA@COS nanomachines

To construct HxMoO3@SA@COS nanomachines, MoO3 nanospheres ( ~ 200 nm in diameter) were synthesized via a citrate-assisted hydrothermal method (Fig. 2a). Active hydrogen atoms were subsequently loaded into MoO3 nanospheres following established protocol55,56. The hydrogen-loaded MoO3 (HxMoO3) nanoparticles retained spherical structure of the original MoO3 nanospheres (Fig. 2b). High-resolution TEM (HRTEM) images revealed a significant increase in lattice spacing of MoO3 from 0.2 nm to 0.27 nm upon hydrogen loading (Fig. 2a inset, Fig. 2b inset, Supplementary Fig. 1). Additionally, the aqueous dispersion of MoO3 changed from white to black upon hydrogenation, indicating successfully hydrogen doping (Fig. 2a inset, Fig. 2b inset). To enhance biocompatibility, the HxMoO3 nanospheres were coated with sodium alginate (SA) and chitosan oligosaccharide (COS) to construct the HxMoO3@SA@COS nanomaterials. TEM images confirmed the successful coating of the biopolymer on the surface of HxMoO3 nanospheres (Fig. 2c, Supplementary Fig. 2a). The significant increase in hydrodynamic size from HxMoO3 to HxMoO3@SA@COS can be primarily attributed to the thickness of the polymer coating, the extension of polymer chains, electrostatic hydration-induced swelling, and the moderate assembly of the HxMoO3@SA@COS nanomachines. Zeta potential analysis showed that hydrogenation had minimal effect on the MoO3 component′s potential, suggesting that protons and electrons were introduced in pairs. Following modification with negatively charged sodium alginate, the uptake of the nanomaterial by negatively charged intestinal epithelial cells was impeded. To overcome this limitation, the surface was subsequently functionalized with positively charged chitosan oligosaccharide, facilitating enhanced cellular uptake (Supplementary Fig. 2b, c). Meanwhile, to preserve the intrinsic advantages of biocompatibility and minimized non-specific interactions, which in turn reduce toxicity risks compared with strongly positively charged particles, a slightly negatively charged HxMoO3@SA@COS (−6 mV) was selected as the active hydrogen delivery nanomachine for this study.Fig. 2Synthesis and Characterization of HxMoO3@SA@COS nanomachines.a−c TEM images of as-synthesized MoO3 (a), HxMoO3 (b), and HxMoO3@SA@COS (c). X-ray powder diffraction (XRD) confirmed the successful synthesis of highly crystalline MoO3. Upon hydrogenation, the diffraction peaks matched those of hydrogen-doped molybdenum oxides (H1.68MoO3, PDF#35-0604; H0.93MoO3, PDF#36-0605). Notably, the MoO3 (020) peak exhibited a slight blue shift and peak splitting, attributed to lattice expansion induced by hydrogen insertion (Fig. 2d). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) further revealed partial reduction of MoO3, with the appearance of Mo4+ and Mo5+ states (Fig. 2e, Supplementary Fig. 3a). Hydrogenation also shifted the MoO3 valence band toward the Fermi level, indicating a modification in the electronic energy levels (Supplementary Fig. 3b). The O 1 s spectrum confirmed Mo–O–H bond formation, validating HxMoO3 fabrication (Fig. 2f). Solid-state ¹H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy further verified hydrogen incorporation and release. Hydrogenated HxMoO3 exhibited a sharp hydrogen peak, which disappeared after complete reaction with methylene blue (MB), returning to a signal level similar to pristine MoO3 (Supplementary Fig. 7a).

Hydrogenation significantly enhanced the optical absorption of HxMoO3 nanospheres beyond 500 nm, while the SA and COS coating did not affect their absorbance (Fig. 2g). Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) analysis confirmed the successful surface modification of HxMoO3 nanospheres with SA, as evidenced by the characteristic peaks at 1629 cm−1, 1416 cm−1, and 998 cm−1, corresponding to the asymmetric stretching, symmetric stretching, and stretching vibrations of -COOH group, respectively, in the FTIR spectrum of HxMoO3@SA@COSs (Fig. 2h). Stepwise changes in zeta potential demonstrated the successful layer-by-layer functionalization of HxMoO3 nanospheres with SA and COS (Supplementary Fig. 2b, c). Additionally, the content of SA and COS in HxMoO3@SA@COSs was approximately 22%, as determined by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) (Fig. 2i). For oral administration in the treatment of intestinal radiation injury, stability studies in varying gastrointestinal pH environments are critical. HxMoO3@SA@COSs demonstrated excellent colloidal stability without aggregation in PBS and cell culture medium (pH 7.4) (Supplementary Fig. 4). Crucially, it also demonstrated strong acid resistance in PBS at pH 1.2. In contrast, gradual degradation of HxMoO3@SA@COSs occurred over 24 h in pH 7.4 or under intestinal mucosa conditions (pH 6.8) due to the formation of MoO42- in alkaline conditions (Supplementary Fig. 5, 6). This pH-responsive degradation property prevents long-term accumulation of the nanomachine in the body, ensuring superior biosafety in biomedical applications.

RONS scavenging performance of HxMoO3@SA@COS nanomachines

MoO3 nanospheres, with their typical layered structures, are capable of loading active hydrogens. Due to the low reduction potential (H+/H· (active hydrogen), −0.34 V vs NHE), these active hydrogens efficiently scavenge various cytotoxic RONS, including ·OH, H2O2, ·O2-, and ONOO- (Fig. 3a, b, Supplementary Fig. 7b, Supplementary Table 1). To confirm the presence and reductive capacity of active hydrogens, methylene blue (MB) was employed as a probe, as it can be reduced to colorless leuco-methylene blue (LMB) upon receiving electrons and re-oxidized to blue when exposed to H2O2 (Fig. 3c). Using MB and CoQ10 as reagents, the active hydrogen content in HxMoO3@SA@COSs was quantified as 1.59 µmol/g (Supplementary Fig. 7c-j). Notably, H2 alone does not induce this color change in MB, but the introduction of Pt nanoparticles (Pt NPs) catalyzes the reduction of MB to LMB due to the active hydrogens generated by Pt NPs57,58. In this study, we observed that HxMoO3@SA@COSs rapidly reduced MB to LMB, causing a color change from dark blue to black (Fig. 3d, Supplementary video 1). Upon adding H2O2, the solution gradually reverted to blue, indicating that HxMoO3@SA@COSs can store and release active hydrogens, displaying reductive activity similar to the H2 and Pt NPs catalytic system. The reversible changes of MB-LMB-MB transitions observed in UV-vis-NIR absorption spectra further confirmed the reductive capabilities of HxMoO3@SA@COSs (Fig. 3e). To verify that MB reduction is driven by active hydrogen rather than Mo valence state transitions, a defective MoO3-x (containing Mo(IV) and Mo(V)) was synthesized (Supplementary Fig. 7k) and evaluated in MB decolorization experiments. MoO3-x exhibited no significant impact on MB absorbance (Supplementary Fig. 7l), indicating that Mo oxidation state transitions do not contribute to MB reduction, further substantiating the critical role of active hydrogen in this process. Additionally, the MB probe was used to assess the reductive activity of HxMoO3@SA@COSs under simulated gastric fluid (SGF) for 2 h and simulated intestinal fluid (SIF) for 8 h. HxMoO3@SA@COSs demonstrate superior and sustained reducibility, effectively scavenging RONS such as ·OH, H2O2, ·O2¯, and ONOO- (Fig. 3f-i, Supplementary Fig. 9-12). The ESR results further confirmed the scavenging of ·OH and ·O2¯ (Supplementary Fig. 6m, n). Furthermore, ESR spectroscopy revealed disappearance of Mo(V) during this process. This is due to the formation of Mo–OH bonds upon hydrogen insertion, where the electron-withdrawing effect of hydroxyl groups reduces the oxidation state of Mo. After full reaction with RONS, hydrogen removal led to the restoration of the Mo oxidation state. The change in the Mo oxidation state follows a similar trend to that of hydrogen, suggesting that the Mo valence state change is induced by the insertion and release of hydrogen (Supplementary Fig. 6o). Additionally, HxMoO3@SA@COSs exhibit a strong total antioxidant capacity, as evidenced by the ABTS assay (Fig. 3j, Supplementary Fig. 13). Radar charts comparing the antioxidant capabilities of HxMoO3@SA@COS and MoO3 highlight the broad-spectrum RONS scavenging ability of HxMoO3@SA@COSs (Fig. 3k). Moreover, the absence of detectable hydrogen gas in the HxMoO3@SA system in PBS indicates that its effect is attributed to active hydrogen (Supplementary Fig. 14). The decolorization ability of HxMoO3@SA@COSs on MB was monitored under different temperatures (25 °C and −20 °C) over a continuous period of 9 days (Supplementary Fig. 15). These results indicate that HxMoO3@SA@COSs retain approximately 80% of its reductive activity after 9 days at both temperatures. Overall, the excellent, robust and long-acting reducibility of HxMoO3@SA@COSs endows it with superior RONS scavenging capability, showing great promise for mitigating oxidative stress.

Photothermal properties and motility of HxMoO3@SA@COS nanomachines

The bandgap of HxMoO3@SA@COSs, as calculated by the Tauc method, was significantly reduced from 2.55 eV to 0.52 eV after hydrogen doping, indicating enhanced light absorption in the NIR region (Fig. 4a). These findings were highly consistent with the UV-vis-NIR spectra of samples before and after hydrogen doping (Fig. 2g). Upon exposure to 808 nm light irradiation (0.2 mg/mL, 1 W·cm-2), the temperature of the HxMoO3@SA@COS aqueous dispersion increased by 32 °C within 10 minutes, showing excellent photothermal properties (Fig. 4b). The photothermal properties of HxMoO3@SA@COSs exhibited notable dependence on concentration and light power, akin to other photothermal agents (Supplementary Fig. 16). The photothermal conversion efficiency of HxMoO3@SA@COSs, calculated from the cooling phase of the heating-cooling curve, was approximately 46.5% (Fig. 4c). Moreover, the photothermal performance of HxMoO3@SA@COSs remained stable after five heating-cooling cycles, highlighting its outstanding photothermal stability (Fig. 4d). The tissue penetration depth of NIR light was assessed by infrared thermal imaging with varying thicknesses of chicken breast tissue as barriers (Fig. 4e). Previous studies have proved that NIR light, within the 650 - 950 nm wavelength range, can penetrate up to approximately 10 mm into tissue59,60. Considering the excellent photothermal performance of HxMoO3@SA@COSs, the in vitro motility driven by 808 nm light was analyzed. Finite element simulations revealed that exposure to 808 nm light induced the formation of an asymmetric thermal field across the 200 nm HxMoO3 nanosphere (Fig. 4h). This manifests that, in addition to asymmetric nanostructures, spherical HxMoO3 nanoparticles can also exhibit strong propulsion via self-thermophoresis (Fig. 4i). The directional motion of HxMoO3@SA@COSs under 808 nm light was further evaluated using MB as an indicator, with HxMoO3@SA@COSs injected at the bottom of a cuvette. Intriguingly, upon exposure to NIR light, HxMoO3@SA@COSs displayed a ballistic motion trajectory with a fast velocity of approximately 2058 μm/s (Fig. 4j, Supplementary Video 2, Supplementary Fig. 17). After 6 min of NIR irradiation, the blue color of the system was completely decolorized, primarily due to the enhanced reduction rate of MB molecules by the active hydrogens released through the nanomachines′ movement. This motion not only enables HxMoO3@SA@COSs to overcome intestinal barriers but also significantly accelerates RONS scavenging. The motility of HxMoO3@SAs under 808 nm light was further investigated in solution with different viscosities, with agarose gels used to simulate intestinal mucus. The velocity (mm/s) of HxMoO3@SA@COS nanomachines in agarose gels is inversely proportional to the gel viscosity (Supplementary Fig. 18). The viscosity-concentration relationship is illustrated in Fig. 4k, with reported small intestine mucosal viscosity ranging from 10 to 19 mPa·s61. Notably, in agarose gel with a viscosity of 26.5 mPa·s, HxMoO3@SA@COSs exhibited excellent directional motion, covering a distance of approximately 23 mm in 3 min under NIR irradiation (Fig. 4l, Supplementary Fig. 19, Supplementary Video 3). This robust mechanical mobility allows HxMoO3@SA@COSs to effectively traverse intestinal mucus barriers and deliver active hydrogens to hard-to-reach injury sites. In summary, the simple spherical HxMoO3@SA@COS nanomachines demonstrate outstanding NIR-driven motility, comparable to that of reported Janus self-thermophoresis-powered micro/nanomotors, which significantly enhances their potential for practical applications. As a conceptual validation, an 808 nm laser, widely used in the literature, was employed for the experiments. Given that HxMoO3@SA@COSs exhibit broad absorption in the near-infrared region, to further explore the application potential of HxMoO3@SA@COSs in deeper tissues, 1064 nm and 1550 nm lasers were used for the driving experiments of HxMoO3@SA@COSs. The results showed that HxMoO3@SA@COSs could be driven to move rapidly by 1064 nm and 1550 nm lasers, further demonstrating their potential for applications in deep tissue (Supplementary Fig. 20, Supplementary Video 4).

In vitro radioprotective effects of HxMoO3@SA@COS nanomachines

The HxMoO3@SA@COS nanomachines exhibit remarkable RONS scavenging capacity and exceptional motility, positioning them as promising candidates for intestinal radioprotection. To further investigate their radioprotective effects and underlying mechanisms at the cellular level, we assessed their RONS scavenging ability, inhibition of cell apoptosis, protection against DNA damage, and modulation of macrophage polarization (Fig. 5a). Cytotoxicity was evaluated using the CCK-8 assay, where HxMoO3@SA@COSs presented excellent biocompatibility across three cell lines-HIEC-6, MODE-K, and RAW264.7 with cell viability exceeding 90% even at high concentrations (Fig. 5b). Notably, HxMoO3@SA@COSs provided significant radioprotection against acute radiation-induced damage in HIEC-6 cells (Fig. 5c), as evidenced by CCK-8 assay results. After radiation exposure to 4 Gy or 10 Gy, cell viability was significantly decreased, while cells treated with HxMoO3@SA@COSs exhibited higher viability, indicating effective protection against radiation-induced apoptosis. Additionally, the NIR-light-propelled motion of HxMoO3@SA@COSs enhanced their ability to penetrate cell membranes, leading to enhanced cellular internalization (Fig. 5d, g, Supplementary Fig. 21). Quantitative internalization analysis via ICP-MS revealed a 2.3-fold increase in the cellular uptake due to the nanomachine′s motility (Supplementary Fig. 22). The intracellular RONS scavenging capability of HxMoO3@SA@COS was assessed using the DCFH-DA fluorescence probe (Fig. 5e, h). After 4 Gy radiation exposure, intracellular RONS levels, indicated by green fluorescence signals, were significantly increased. In contrast, pretreatment with HxMoO3@SA@COS significantly reduced the fluorescence signals, demonstrating its effectiveness in scavenging overexpressed RONS. Notably, the fluorescence intensity in the IR+HxMoO3@SA@COS+NIR group was significantly lower than that in the IR+HxMoO3@SA@COS group, approaching that of control group. This indicates that NIR light-powered movement of HxMoO3@SA@COS enhances cellular internalization, thereby improving RONS scavenging efficiency. Additionally, as radiation induces oxidative stress in cells, it can lead to various forms of cell death, such as apoptosis and ferroptosis. Annexin Ⅴ-FITC/PI flow cytometry results showed that HxMoO3@SA@COS significantly reduced radiation-induced apoptosis, with the IR+ HxMoO3@SA@COS + NIR group exhibiting superior inhibition of cell apoptosis compared to the IR+HxMoO3@SA@COS group (Fig. 5f, i, Supplementary Fig. 23). By detecting lipid peroxidation markers associated with ferroptosis, such as MDA and 4-HNE, it was found that radiation caused a significant increase in these markers, while the levels in the material-treated group were similar to the control group (Supplementary Fig. 24a, b). This suggests that HxMoO3@SA@COS may have a potential effect in alleviating radiation-induced ferroptosis. Analysis of cell sections from the control, IR, and IR+HxMoO3@SA@COS groups revealed significant vacuolization and damage in the mitochondria of IR-treated cells, while mitochondria in the IR+HxMoO3@SA@COS group remained relatively intact, further supporting the notion that radiation induced ferroptosis, and HxMoO3@SA@COS can mitigate this effect (Supplementary Fig. 24c). The γ-H2AX immunofluorescence staining results at 4 Gy (Fig. 5j, l) doses confirmed that HxMoO3@SA@COS significantly mitigates DNA damage, with the IR+HxMoO3@SA@COS+NIR group offering the most effective protection against DNA damage.

Surprisingly, we discovered that active hydrogens could promote macrophages polarization from the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, a crucial mechanism for reducing inflammation in radiation enteritis therapy. Immunofluorescence staining of CD86 (an M1 marker) and CD206 (an M2 marker) was performed to assess macrophage phenotype (Fig. 5k, m, n). After radiation exposure, strong CD86 signals were detected, indicating that the majority of RAW264.7 cells were M1 phenotype, which correlates with elevated inflammation levels. In contrast, treatment with HxMoO3@SA@COSs led to a marked reduction in CD86 signals and a significant increase in CD206 signals, demonstrating that the active hydrogen released from HxMoO3@SA@COS s played a pivotal role in immunoregulation. Previous studies have shown that heat can induce the transition from M1 to M2 macrophages62. Accordingly, NIR irradiation further reduced CD86 expression and enhanced CD206 expression in the IR+HxMoO3@SA@COS + NIR group. Immunofluorescence staining results of other M2 markers (CD163, Arg1) further support this conclusion (Supplementary Fig. 25). To investigate the long-term protective effects of HxMoO3@SA@COS, we conducted a colony formation assay. The ability to form colonies relies on the normal proliferation capacity of cells, which can reflect DNA damage and long-term protection to some extent. The experimental results showed that the colony formation ability of irradiated cells was significantly reduced, while the colony formation ability of cells protected by HxMoO3@SA@COS was significantly improved (Supplementary Fig. 26). This indicates that HxMoO3@SA@COS provides overall radiation protection, effectively alleviating radiation-induced inhibition of cell proliferation. These results demonstrate that HxMoO3@SA@COS nanomachines offer significant protection against radiation enteritis by combining their excellent RONS scavenging ability, potent immunoregulatory effects, and movement-enhanced cellular internalization to reduce oxidative stress, apoptosis, and radiation-induced DNA damage.

In vivo protection effects of radiation enteritis with HxMoO3@SA@COS nanomachines

During radiation therapy for gastrointestinal tumor patients, both tumor cells and normal cells may suffer radiation-induced damage. To verify the DNA damage caused by radiation, comet assays were performed on colorectal cancer cells (CT26) and intestinal epithelial cells (HIEC-6). The results showed that both CT26 cells and HIEC-6 cells formed clear comet images, indicating that radiation induces DNA damage in both cancerous and normal cells (Supplementary Fig. 30). Further analysis of immunogenic cell death (ICD) markers in HIEC-6 cells revealed that after radiation treatment, compared to the control group, HMGB1 expression was reduced in the nucleus and increased in the cytoplasm of the irradiated ICD cells. Additionally, CRT expression in the cytoplasm significantly increased (Supplementary Fig. 31a, b). The HMGB1 and CRT IHC detection in irradiated mouse small intestine tissue further strengthened this observation (Supplementary Fig. 31c, d). These results suggest that during radiation therapy, radiation not only induces DNA damage in normal cells but also triggers immunogenic cell death (ICD), which could contribute to the severe side effects of radiation on normal tissues. Discussion

Radiation enteritis significantly affects the quality of life in patients undergoing pelvic and abdominal radiotherapy, with no effective treatments currently available. Ionizing radiation generates high levels of RONS in the intestine, leading to sustained tissue damage and severe inflammatory responses. Therefore, eliminating excessive RONS and reducing radiation-induced inflammation are fundamental strategies for radioprotection. Hydrogen gas, with its excellent biological safety, selectively scavenges key RONS, such as hydroxyl radicals (·OH) and peroxynitrite anion (ONOO-), reducing oxidative stress, activating endogenous antioxidant systems, and mitigating inflammation. These attributes have established hydrogen as a promising radioprotective agent. However, the underlying mechanisms of hydrogen, an inert physiological gas, in radiation therapy remain poorly understood. Recent findings suggest that hydrogen scavenges RONS via its interaction with ferriporphyrin, which act as endogenous catalyst predominantly located in mitochondria. We hypothesized that ferriporphyrins catalyze hydrogen to generate reactive hydrogens, which subsequently remove RONS. Hydrogen atoms, known for their extremely low reductive potential (H+/H·, -0.34 V vs NHE), exhibit broad-spectrum RONS scavenging capability. Importantly, active hydrogens present huge potential in acute radioprotection. To date, no studies have explored the application of reactive hydrogens in radioprotection, likely due to the lack of effective delivery methods to target damaged tissues, particularly within the intestinal tract, which is protected by a robust mucus barrier.

In this study, drinkable, spherical and self-thermophoresis-powered HxMoO3@SA@COS nanomachines synthesized via wet chemistry method were reported for treating acute radiation enteritis. Upon hydrogenation, the as-prepared HxMoO3@SA@COSs exhibited strong NIR light absorption and achieved a high photothermal conversion efficiency of approximately 46.5% under 808 nm light irradiation. Notably, thermal field simulations revealed that NIR laser exposure generates asymmetric thermal gradients across the spherical nanostructures, driving their directional motility. This unique motility not only enables HxMoO3@SA@COSs to overcome intestinal barriers, facilitating effective hydrogen delivery, but also enhances cellular internalization and prolongs retention time. In vitro and in vivo studies demonstrated that HxMoO3@SA@COSs effectively mitigate radiation-induced DNA damage, scavenge RONS, reduce apoptosis, promote anti-inflammatory macrophage polarization, modulate gut microbiota, and restore intestinal epithelial tissue. The survival rate assay in a mouse model of lethal radiation injury induced by 16 Gy TAI revealed a significant increase in survival from 10%−80% within 30 days following treatment with HxMoO3@SA@COSs combined with NIR irradiation. This hydrogen-based, drug-free therapeutic strategy, coupled with its precise and self-directed delivery, demonstrated excellent biosafety and robust protection against radiation-induced enteritis. These promising results suggest significant clinical potential for this approach.

This study demonstrated hydrogen confers radioprotection in intestinal tissues by scavenging RONS and restoring redox homeostasis67. However, its systemic regulation of the DNA damage response (DDR)-inflammation-immunomodulation axis necessitates a comprehensive mechanistic investigation across various radiation modalities. Although experiments with γ-ray confirm baseline efficacy, translational progress requires stringent evaluation under clinical-grade proton and carbon ion irradiation to address intricate DNA damage patterns and tissue-specific immune remodeling. Additionally, to further enhance the clinical utility of HxMoO3@SA@COS, we propose the use of the internally implanted NIR light sources to drive nanomachine motility in situ, thereby ensuring sustained therapeutic efficacy in deep-seated intestinal tissues. These advancements will be pivotal for refining active hydrogen-based radioprotection and broadening its potential in precision radiation enteritis therapy.

Methods

Animal Experiments

Male C57BL/6 J mice (6−8 weeks old) were purchased from Beijing HFK Bioscience Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) and housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions in a certified animal facility. Mice were maintained on a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle at 23 ± 2 °C with 35%-75% humidity, with standard chow (Xietong Shengwu Co., Ltd., XTI01WC-009) and water provided ad libitum. Only male mice were used in this study as the investigation focused on the scavenging of RONS by active hydrogen, a process for which no sexual dimorphism has been reported. Synthesis of HxMoO3@SA@COS

Synthesis of MoO3: MoO3 was synthesized based on previous reports with some modifications. Briefly, 500 mg of ammonium molybdate and 40 mg of sodium citrate were added to 35 mL of deionized water in a round-bottom flask. The pH of the solution was adjusted to 1 using 6 M hydrochloric acid, followed by magnetic stirring at room temperature for 2 h. The resulting solution was then transferred to a polytetrafluoroethylene-lined autoclave and heated at 150 °C for 24 h. After the reaction, the resulting product was thoroughly washed with deionized water three times and subsequently freeze-dried for 12 h for future use.

Synthesis of HxMoO3: HxMoO3 was prepared via an acid treatment method as previously reported. In brief, 200 mg of MoO3 was dispersed in 10 mL of deionized water, followed by the addition of 5 mL of concentrated hydrochloric acid. The resulting mixture was sonicated for 10 min to ensure uniform dispersion of MoO3. Subsequently, 400 mg of pure aluminum foil was gradually added in small batches under continuous stirring. Upon completion of the reaction, the product was thoroughly washed with deionized water three times to remove residual hydrochloric acid, then freeze-dried for 12 h to obtain the HxMoO3 powder.

Synthesis of HxMoO3@SA@COS: To enhance biocompatibility, 200 mg of HxMoO3 was dispersed in 20 mL of 0.2% sodium alginate (SA) aqueous solution and sonicated in an ice-water bath for 3 h (total power: 700 W, 50% amplitude). After sonication, the product was washed twice with deionized water and freeze-dried for 12 h to obtain HxMoO3@SA. Chitosan oligosaccharide was modified onto the surface of HxMoO3@SA via electrostatic interactions. Specifically, 50 mg of HxMoO3@SA was dispersed in 20 mL of a 0.2 mg/mL aqueous solution of chitosan oligosaccharide and stirred at room temperature for 3 h. Upon completion of the reaction, the product was washed three times with deionized water to yield the chitosan oligosaccharide-modified HxMoO3@SA (noted HxMoO3@SA@COS).

Electrochemical Measurements

The working electrode was prepared by mixing the as-prepared HxMoO3@SA@COS nanobelts, carbon black, and polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) binder at a weight ratio of 7:2:1 in dimethylformamide (DMF). The resulting slurry was uniformly cast onto carbon fiber paper and dried at 60 °C in an oven to remove residual solvent. The electrochemical performance was tested in a three-electrode system, where the HxMoO3@SA@COS-modified electrode served as the working electrode, a graphite rod as the counter electrode, and a saturated Ag/AgCl electrode as the reference electrode. The electrolyte used was an 8 M H2SO₄ aqueous solution. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements were conducted on an electrochemical workstation (CH6504C) over a potential range of -1.25 to 1.50 V (vs. Ag/AgCl) at a scan rate of 10 mV/s. The formal redox potential was estimated as the midpoint between the anodic and cathodic peak potentials observed in the CV curves. The CV curves show one pair of redox peaks at 0.63 V and -0.54 V (vs. Ag/AgCl). According to the formula: ERHE = EAg/AgCl + E0Ag/AgCl (0.1976 V) + 0.059 × pH, ENHE ≈ ERHE, the reduction potential of H+/H· was calculated to be -0.34 V vs NHE.

RONS Scavenging

The scavenging activity of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (RONS) was evaluated using previously reported methods with modifications.

Hydroxyl Radical (·OH) Scavenging: The hydroxyl radical scavenging activity was measured using salicylic acid (SA) as a probe, which forms a colored complex upon reaction with hydroxyl radicals generated via the Fenton reaction. Hydroxyl radicals were prepared by mixing equal volumes of FeSO4 (3 mM) and H2O2 (1 mM). Then, 1 mL of this ·OH solution was added to varying concentrations of MoO3/ HxMoO3@SA@COS to obtain final concentrations of 0, 10, 20, 30, 40, and 50 μg/mL. The mixture was incubated for 10 min, followed by the addition of 0.5 mL of ethanol-dissolved salicylic acid (10 mM). After centrifugation, the absorbance of the supernatant was measured to quantify the scavenging effect.

Hydrogen Peroxide (H2O2) Scavenging: The hydrogen peroxide scavenging capacity was measured using cerium sulfate (Ce(SO4)2), which is bleached by H2O2. Briefly, 1 mL of H2O2 solution (0.05 mM) was added to varying concentrations of MoO3/ HxMoO3@SA@COS, resulting in final concentrations of 0, 20, 40, 60, 80, and 100 μg/mL. After a 10-minute incubation, the mixture was centrifuged, and 0.8 mL of the supernatant was transferred to 0.8 mL of Ce(SO4)2 solution (0.5 mM). After a 5-mins reaction, the absorbance was recorded to determine the H2O2 scavenging efficiency.

Superoxide Anion (·O2¯) Scavenging: The ·O2¯ scavenging activity was measured using pyrogallol, which rapidly undergoes oxidation under alkaline conditions, producing ·O2- and stable colored intermediates. First, 10 mL of EDTA-2Na (5 mM) was added to 40 mL of Tris-HCl solution (0.5 mM), followed by the dropwise addition of NaOH solution (1 M) to adjust the pH to approximately 7.4, forming Solution A. Separately, pyrogallol was dissolved in HCl (1 mM) to a concentration of 60 mM, giving Solution B. Solution A was then mixed with Solution B to dilute pyrogallol to a final concentration of 10 mM, yielding Solution C. Various concentrations of MoO3/HxMoO3@SA@COS (0, 10, 20, 30, 40, 50 μg/mL) were added to Solution C and allowed to react for 15 minutes, after which the absorbance was measured to evaluate ·O2- scavenging activity.

Peroxynitrite Anion (ONOO-) Scavenging: Peroxynitrite was generated following the method described by Beckman et al. Briefly, a mixture of 5 mL of H2O2 (0.7 M) and 5 mL of KNO2 (0.6 M) was cooled in an ice bath. The rapid addition of 0.6 M HCl and 5 mL of ice-cold NaOH (1.2 M), yielding a yellow ONOO- solution. Subsequently, 1 mL of ONOO- solution was mixed to various concentrations of MoO3/ HxMoO3@SA@COS to achieve final concentrations of 0, 20, 40, 60, 80, and 100 μg/mL. The reaction mixture was incubated for 15 min, then centrifuged. The absorbance of the supernatant was measured to assess ONOO- Scavenging activity.

ABTS Decolorization Assay for Total Antioxidant Capacities

The total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC) of all HxMoO3@SA@COS samples was evaluated using the ABTS chromogenic method with a commercial T-AOC assay kit. The ABTS working solution was prepared by mixing equal volumes of ABTS and an oxidizing agent to generate ABTS·+ radical cations, which were allowed to develop over 16 h. Different concentrations of MoO3 and HxMoO3@SA@COS were then added to the ABTS working solution, and the reaction was carried out for 25 minutes. The decrease in absorbance at 734 nm, corresponding to the inhibition of ABTS·+ radicals, was measured. Trolox served as the reference antioxidant standard for quantifying the results.

Hydrogen Molecular Release of HxMoO3@SA@COSs in PBS

Hydrogen release from HxMoO3@SA@COSs was quantified using gas chromatography. Briefly, 4 mg of HxMoO3@SA@COSs was dispersed in 40 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) to achieve a final concentration of 100 μg/mL, with PBS serving as the control group. Samples were incubated in sealed reaction vials at 37 °C for 2 hours. After incubation, 1 mL of headspace gas was collected using a gas-tight syringe and injected into the GC system. The GC was operated with a 5 Å molecular sieve column, high-purity nitrogen as the carrier gas (30 mL/min). A calibration curve was established using standard hydrogen gas to quantify the hydrogen concentration in the samples. Each measurement was performed in triplicate, and the hydrogen release rate was analyzed.

Photothermal Performance Testing

As-prepared HxMoO3@SA@COSs aqueous dispersion was added in to the cuvette. Subsequently, an 808 nm NIR laser (BWT, Beijing) was introduced to irradiate the sample suspension. The temperature and images of the solution was recorded every 30 s with a thermal imager (UTi260E, UNI-T).

Numerical Simulation

The numerical simulation was implemented by COMSOL Multiphysics 6.1. The model of the nanomotor was constructed according to the morphology presented in SEM images. For the simulation of nanospheres, a boundary heat source from the heat transfer module was applied to the surface of the nanospheres to simulate the photothermal absorption of NIR light. Motion Analysis of HxMoO3@SA@COSs

HxMoO3@SA@COSs (2 mg/mL, 0.1 mL) was carefully injected into the bottom of a cuvette containing 2 mL of MB solution. The cuvette was irradiated with an 808 nm laser (1 W/cm²) at its bottom, and the motion of HxMoO3@SA@COSs particles was recorded using a camera.

Agarose gels with concentrations of 0%, 0.01%, 0.02%, 0.03%, 0.04%, and 0.05% were prepared, and their viscosities were measured using a viscometer (NDJ-5s). For each experiment, 3 mL of the agarose gel was added to a cuvette, followed by the gentle injection of 0.1 mL of 2 mg/mL HxMoO3@SA@COSs at the bottom of the cuvette. The 808 nm laser (1 W·cm-2) was then directed at the cuvette base, and the motion of the HxMoO3@SA@COSs particles in the gel was captured by a camera.

The particle velocity was calculated based on the displacement of HxMoO3@SA@COSs particles over time under laser irradiation. Cell Viability Assay

The cytotoxicity of HxMoO3@SA@COSs on HIEC-6, MODE-K, and RAW 264.7 cells was evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells/well and incubated for 24 h. Subsequently, they were treated with varying concentrations (50, 100, 150, 200 μg/mL) of HxMoO3@SA@COSs for an additional 24 h. Cell viability was determined using the CCK-8 assay, followed by incubation with the CCK-8 reagent for 1 h. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm with a microplate reader. Cell survival (%) was calculated using the formula: (ODsamples − ODblank) / (ODcontrol − ODblank) × 100%.

Cellular Uptake of HxMoO3@SA@COSs

HIEC-6 cells were seeded in culture dishes at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well and incubated for 24 h. The cells were divided into two groups: one treated with nanoparticles alone, and the other treated with nanoparticles followed by 808 nm laser irradiation (1 W·cm-2). The cells were treated with 100 μg/mL nanoparticles and incubated for 3 h, either with or without laser irradiation. Following incubation, the supernatant was discarded, and the cells were washed three times with cold PBS (HyClone, UT, USA). The cells were then fixed with 1 mL of 4% formaldehyde per well for 10 minutes at room temperature and washed three times with cold PBS. After DAPI staining, images were captured using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM).

Detection of Intracellular ROS Levels

The levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in HIEC-6 cells post-irradiation were measured using the fluorescent probe 2′, 7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA). HIEC-6 cells (2 × 105) were seeded into confocal dishes and cultured overnight. HxMoO3@SA@COSs (final concentration 50 μg/mL) was added to the cells 6 h before irradiation. Three hours before irradiation, cells were exposed to an 808 nm laser (1 W·cm-2) for 10 minutes. Subsequently, the cells were subjected to 4 Gy radiation and incubated for an additional 12 h. Non-irradiated cells served as a negative control. Afterward, cells were stained with DCFH-DA according to the manufacturer′s protocol, and fluorescence was observed under a fluorescence microscope. The fluorescence intensity was subsequently quantified using ImageJ software.

Detection of Cell Apoptosis

Cell apoptosis was assessed using an apoptosis detection kit, employing Annexin V-FITC to stain phosphatidylserine exposed on the outer membrane of apoptotic cells, and Propidium Iodide (PI) to stain the DNA of late apoptotic and necrotic cells. Cells were seeded into 12-well plates and allowed to adhere overnight. HxMoO3@SA@COSs (final concentration: 50 μg/mL) was added to the cells 6 h prior to irradiation. Three hours before irradiation, the cells were exposed to an 808 nm laser (1 W·cm-2) for 10 minutes, followed by 4 Gy radiation. Cells were then incubated for an additional 24 h. Non-irradiated cells served as negative controls. Following the manufacturer’s protocol, cells were stained with Annexin V-FITC and PI, and apoptosis was quantified via flow cytometry. The apoptosis rate was calculated using FlowJo software.

DNA Damage Detection

HIEC-6 cells were seeded in confocal dishes at a density of 1 × 106 cells per well. After overnight incubation, the complete medium was replaced with serum-free medium containing HxMoO3@SA@COS. Following a 3 hours incubation, the cells were irradiated with an 808 nm laser (1 W·cm-2) for 10 min, then incubated for an additional 3 h before exposure to 4 Gy of radiation. After 24 h, DNA damage was assessed via immunofluorescence by γ-H2AX phosphorylation. Cells were fixed with paraformaldehyde and stained with anti-γ-H2AX antibody overnight at 4 °C. Subsequently, they were incubated with FITC-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG (H&L) at room temperature for 2 h in the dark. Finally, cells were mounted using an anti-fade mounting medium containing DAPI, fluorescence images were captured using a laser scanning confocal microscope.

Phenotypic Polarization of Macrophages

RAW 264.7 cells were seeded in confocal dishes at a density of 1 × 106 cells per well. After overnight incubation, the complete medium was replaced with serum-free medium containing HxMoO3@SA@COSs. After 3 hours incubation, the cells were irradiated with an 808 nm laser (1 W·cm-2) for 10 min. The cells were then incubated for an additional 3 hours before being subjected to 6 Gy of radiation. After 24 h, the cells were fixed with paraformaldehyde and stained overnight at 4 °C with anti-CD86 antibody and anti-CD206 antibody. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with FITC-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG (H&L) in the dark at room temperature for 2 h. Finally, the cells were mounted with anti-fade mounting medium containing DAPI, and fluorescence images were acquired using a laser scanning confocal microscope.

Measurement of MDA and SOD

The levels of malondialdehyde (MDA) and the activity of SOD, two characteristic biomarkers of lipid peroxidation, were quantified using commercial detection kits according to manufacturer protocols. In Vivo Biodistribution

To investigate the distribution of orally administered HxMoO3@SA@COSs in the intestines of mice, male C57BL/6 J mice were fasted for 12 h before receiving an oral dose of 40 mg/kg of Cy5-labeled HxMoO3@SA@COSs. At 1, 2, 4, and 8 h post-administration, intestinal tissues and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys) were harvested, and fluorescence was monitored using an in vivo imaging system. To evaluate the impact of NIR-driven thermophoretic motion on the distribution of HxMoO3@SA@COSs in the intestines, mice were exposed to 808 nm NIR laser irradiation (1 W·cm-2) on the abdomen for 10 min at the 1 h mark, with a total of three irradiations at 10 minute intervals. Temperature changes during laser irradiation were recorded using an infrared thermal imager. To further investigate the adhesion of HxMoO3@SA@COSs in the intestines, the ileum was collected at the 8 h time point, washed thrice with HEPES buffer, and imaged using an in vivo imaging system to confirm adhesion. The ileum was then cryo-sectioned for bright-field and fluorescence imaging under a fluorescence microscope.

Mucus and Intestinal Epithelial Cell Penetration

In vitro experiments were conducted using mouse intestinal tissues to evaluate mucus penetration and epithelial cell permeability. For the mucus penetration assay, FITC-labeled HxMoO3@SA@COSs (0.2 mg/mL, 50 μL) were applied to the inner surface of isolated small intestine tissues, followed by to NIR laser irradiation (808 nm, 1 W·cm-2) for 10 min, with untreated tissues serving as a control. Afterward, the tissues were stained with rhodamine-labeled wheat germ agglutinin for 30 minutes to label the mucus layer. Fluorescence images were acquired using a confocal microscope in Z-scan mode. For the epithelial cell penetration assay, HxMoO3@SA@COSs (0.2 mg/mL, 50 μL) were similarly applied to the inner surface of the intestinal tissues, with NIR laser treatment (808 nm, 1 W·cm-2) for 10 minutes or without laser as a control. The penetration of the materials was evaluated via biological transmission electron microscopy (TEM).

Comet Assay Procedure

Treated cells were collected, washed with PBS, and resuspended at a concentration of 1 × 106 cells/mL. The cell suspension was mixed with agarose and carefully dropped onto microscope slides. The slides were then cooled to allow gel solidification, forming a thin layer that immobilized the cells. Next, the slides were transferred into a pre-chilled lysis solution (2.5 M NaCl, 100 mM Na2EDTA, 10 mM Tris-HCl, 10% DMSO, 1% Triton X-100) and incubated at 4 °C for 2 hours to lyse the cells. Following lysis, the slides were immersed in an electrophoresis buffer and equilibrated for 20 minutes. Electrophoresis was subsequently performed at 30 V for 20 minutes at 4 °C, protected from light. After electrophoresis, the slides were stained with NA-red (Beyotime, D0128), and fluorescence images were acquired using a laser scanning confocal microscope.

Immunofluorescence Staining of HMGB1 and CRT

HIEC-6 cells were seeded in confocal dishes at a density of 1 × 106 cells per well. The cells were then exposed to 4 Gy radiation and incubated for an additional 24 hours. Finally, the cells were mounted using an anti-fade mounting medium containing DAPI, and fluorescence images were captured using a laser scanning confocal microscope.

In vivo Biosafety

To evaluate the acute toxicity of the HxMoO3@SA@COSs nanomachines in vivo, healthy male C57BL/6 J mice (n = 6), weighing 20-22 g, were administered with HxMoO3@SA@COSs orally once daily for five consecutive days. A group of mice administered with DI water serve as the negative control. The mouse body weight was monitored daily throughout the experimental period. On day 14, major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were collected for histopathological analysis. Blood samples were collected immediately for biomedical and hematological analysis, including the evaluation of white blood cell (WBC), red blood cell (RBC), hemoglobin (HGB), platelet (PLT), hematocrit (HCT), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH), mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), uric acid (UA) and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) measurements.

In Vivo Intestinal Radiation Injury Protection

To evaluate the radioprotective effects of HxMoO3@SA@COSs on the intestines, C57BL/6 J mice (average weight of 20 g) were randomly divided into four groups: Control, IR, IR+HxMoO3@SA@COS, and IR+HxMoO3@SA@COS+NIR. HxMoO3@SA@COS was administered orally at a dose of 40 mg/kg once before radiation and daily for three days post-radiation. Mice subjected to 16 Gy total abdominal irradiation (n = 10 for Control, IR and IR+HxMoO3@SA@COS; n = 11 for IR+HxMoO3@SA@COS+NIR) were monitored for 30 day survival and body weight changes. Another cohort, exposed to 12 Gy total abdominal irradiation (n = 6), was sacrificed on day 5 to assess intestinal radioprotection. The small intestines were harvested for histological analyses, including H&E staining, TUNEL staining for apoptosis, and PAS staining for goblet cell quantification. Nrf2+ staining was employed to evaluate antioxidant activity in the intestinal tissue. Immunohistochemistry was performed to assess intestinal epithelial regeneration using Lgr5+ (stem cell marker), Ki67+ (proliferation marker), and Lysozyme+ (secretory marker). Tissue immunofluorescence was used to detect DNA damage and macrophage polarization. Additionally, small intestinal tissues were homogenized to measure antioxidant indicators such as SOD activity, MDA levels, and concentrations of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α and IL-6). To investigate the impact of HxMoO3@SA@COSs on gut microbiota, fresh fecal samples were collected from each group of mice for 16S rDNA gene sequencing analysis, conducted by Novogene Co., Ltd.

ELISA Detection of 3-NT and 4-HNE Levels in Small and Large Intestines

The small and large intestine tissues (approximately 200 mg) from control, IR and IR+HxMoO3@SA@COS groups of mice were collected and homogenized. Total protein concentration was quantified using the BCA assay. Subsequently, the 3-NT and 4-HNE levels were measured according to the instructions provided in the ELISA kit.

Long-Term Safety Assessment at the Animal Level

Mice from the control, IR, and IR+HxMoO3@SA@COS groups were sacrificed, and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were collected. The organs were fixed, embedded, sectioned, and stained with Hematoxylin-Eosin (HE) for histological evaluation. Blood samples were collected from each group, and hematological analysis was performed using a blood cell analyzer to assess routine blood parameters.

Statistical Analysis

Unless otherwise specified, all data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) of at least three independent biological replicates or measurements. Statistical comparisons were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons using GraphPad Prism or OriginPro software. A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Quantification of fluorescence and immunohistochemical analysis were performed using ImageJ software.
