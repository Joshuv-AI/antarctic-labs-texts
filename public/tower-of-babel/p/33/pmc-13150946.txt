Adverse childhood experiences and gastrointestinal symptoms in US youth: Psychosocial pathways and protective contexts
Authors: Sierra L. Martin, Jamie M. Klapp, Jacqueline Shiels, Christin M. Ogle, Jeffrey B. Schwimmer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveAlthough adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) have been linked to gastrointestinal (GI) conditions in children, it remains unclear which specific adversities confer greatest risk and how psychosocial or contextual factors modify these associations. This study identified ACEs most strongly associated with GI symptoms in US youth and evaluated emotional, behavioral, and environmental pathways that may affect these relationships.MethodsWe analyzed nationally representative data from 73,055 US children and adolescents in the 2021–2023 National Survey of Children's Health. Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator regression identified ACEs most predictive of GI symptoms (primary outcome), with co‐occurring GI symptoms and chronic pain examined secondarily. Mediation and moderation analyses assessed psychosocial mechanisms and potential protective factors.ResultsGI symptoms were reported in 6% of children. Five ACEs were retained for the primary outcome; strongest associations were for household mental illness (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 1.56; 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.44–1.70) and economic insecurity (aOR 1.38; 95% CI 1.26–1.50), followed by neighborhood violence (aOR 1.34; 95% CI 1.19–1.52) and racial discrimination (aOR 1.31; 95% CI 1.16–1.47). Emotional/behavioral difficulties mediated 20%–35% of effects; food insecurity mediated 26% for economic insecurity. School and neighborhood safety and community support attenuated several ACE–GI associations.ConclusionsHousehold mental illness and economic insecurity were the strongest ACEs associated with GI symptoms in US youth. Emotional distress and perceived environmental safety mitigated these associations. Integrating psychosocial and contextual factors into evaluation of recurrent GI symptoms may improve care and identify targets for early intervention.

INTRODUCTION

Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) are linked to lasting physiologic and psychological effects, including alterations in stress‐response, immune, and autonomic systems integral to gastrointestinal (GI) homeostasis, offering plausible pathways for ACE–gut–brain connections. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 In adults, ACEs are associated with greater odds of irritable bowel syndrome and with higher GI symptom burden. 9 , 10 , 11 Pediatric studies reflect a similar pattern: cumulative ACE exposure correlates with GI symptoms, with anxiety often mediating the link between adversity and somatic distress. 12 Experimental and human studies further suggest early stress can shape the gut microbiota and neural circuits involved in emotion and visceral pain regulation. 13 However, most pediatric studies aggregate adversity into a cumulative score, limiting insight into which specific adversities confer greatest risk and whether family or community contexts modify these effects.

Diagnosing disorders of gut–brain interaction (DGBI) requires clinical assessment using Rome criteria; such data are not available in large population surveys. 14 , 15 Consequently, epidemiologic research must rely on symptom‐level measures that reflect DGBI's core manifestations. 15 Consistent with pediatric DGBI overviews, these manifestations encompass pain‐related symptoms and GI symptoms such as constipation or diarrhea. 14 Using symptom domains, examined both separately and together, provides a pragmatic proxy for stress‐related somatic burden in youth while avoiding over‐interpretation as clinical diagnoses. 16 This approach aligns with the field's emphasis on heterogeneous DGBI phenotypes and the value of symptom‐based characterization in population studies. 15 , 16

Accordingly, this study examined how specific ACEs relate to GI and pain‐related symptoms in a nationally representative sample of US youth. Objectives were: (1) identify which ACEs were most strongly associated with GI symptoms alone and with co‐occurring GI and chronic pain symptoms; (2) evaluate whether child emotional and behavioral difficulties partially explained these associations; and (3) examine whether protective family and community factors moderated them. Clarifying these symptom‐specific risk and resilience pathways may inform strategies for early identification and integrated care in pediatric gastroenterology.

METHODS

Ethics statement

Data for this study were drawn from the National Survey of Children's Health (NSCH), a nationally representative survey by the US Census Bureau. As NSCH datasets are publicly available and fully de‐identified, this study was exempt from institutional review board oversight.

Study design and data source

We conducted a cross‐sectional analysis of three pooled years (2021–2023) of NSCH data. The NSCH assesses child health, family characteristics, and social determinants of health through mailed and web‐based questionnaires using a two‐phase sampling design in which one child per household is randomly selected. Pooling 3 years increased sample size for subgroup analyses while maintaining cross‐sectional design. Sampling weights, clustering, and stratification were applied to produce nationally representative estimates.

Study population

The study population was children aged 6–17 years with complete data on ACEs, GI symptoms, chronic pain, and covariates. Children under age 6 were excluded due to developmental differences in symptom expression and stress regulation. Cases with missing key variables were excluded.

Adverse childhood experiences

Caregivers reported nine ACEs: economic insecurity, parental separation/divorce, parental death, parental incarceration, household and neighborhood violence, household mental illness, household substance use, and racial/ethnic discrimination. These ACEs represent the subset of standard ACE items available in the NSCH and are not equivalent to a comprehensive assessment of childhood trauma. Abuse and neglect were not assessed because caregiver self‐report may underdetect maltreatment and could trigger mandated‐reporting concerns. ACEs were coded binarily (yes/no).

GI symptoms and chronic pain

Symptom outcomes were based on caregiver‐reported NSCH items. GI symptoms were identified by reports of frequent or chronic difficulty digesting food, including stomach or intestinal problems, constipation, or diarrhea in the past 12 months. Chronic pain was defined by repeated or chronic physical pain, such as headaches, back or abdominal pain. These measures capture common pediatric symptoms but do not permit formal Rome diagnoses or differentiation among GI phenotypes.

To address these limitations, we applied a tiered outcome strategy. The primary outcome was GI symptoms alone, chosen for its direct relevance and strongest face validity for pediatric gastroenterology. The secondary outcome was co‐occurring GI symptoms and chronic pain (GI + pain), reflecting a phenotype most consistent with abdominal pain–related DGBI. Two exploratory outcomes were also examined: chronic pain without GI symptoms (pain‐only) and a composite of GI symptoms and/or chronic pain. This framework prioritized GI‐specific outcomes while capturing overlapping and broader symptom patterns common in youth with stress‐related somatic complaints.

Mediators

We examined psychosocial factors as potential mediators of the association between ACEs and symptom outcomes. Caregivers reported whether their child had “any kind of emotional, developmental, or behavioral problem for which they need treatment or counseling” (yes/no). Although broad, this measure serves as a pragmatic indicator of child mental health needs and captures internalizing difficulties known to mediate links between adversity and stress‐related GI outcomes. 17 Prior longitudinal studies have also associated these factors with GI symptoms, further emphasizing the utility of examining them in a cross‐sectional sample. 18 , 19 In a post‐hoc analysis, food insecurity (caregiver‐reported difficulty affording adequate food in the past year) was also tested as a mediator of the economic insecurity–symptom association, given evidence connecting inadequate nutrition and family stress to somatic and GI distress. 19 Because all variables were measured concurrently, these mediation analyses were conducted to characterize statistical pathways and should be interpreted as associational rather than causal.

Moderators

Family and community characteristics were examined as potential moderators of the association between ACEs and symptom outcomes. Candidate moderators were selected a priori based on evidence that social environments influence children's vulnerability and resilience in the face of adversity. Parental education level was included as a proxy for socioeconomic resources and household stability. Perceived school safety and perceived neighborhood safety were assessed with caregiver items evaluating the extent to which the child felt safe in each setting, reflecting environmental stressors or protective contexts relevant to daily functioning and emotional well‐being. Community support was measured with the item, “When we encounter difficulties, we know where to go for help in our community”—an indicator of social capital and relational resilience. All moderators were re‐coded as binary. Together, these factors captured stable family resources and modifiable contextual influences that may buffer the impact of ACEs on GI and pain‐related symptoms. This approach aligns with resilience frameworks highlighting the role of supportive environments in mitigating the effects of early adversity on child health.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were conducted in R (version 2024.09.0, R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Survey weights, clustering, and stratification were applied using the survey package to account for the complex design of the NSCH and to generate nationally representative estimates. Descriptive statistics summarized sample characteristics, ACE prevalence, and outcome prevalence across demographic subgroups. Weighted percentages were compared using chi‐square tests for categorical variables and t‐tests for continuous variables, with a two‐sided alpha of 0.05.

The primary analysis examined the association between ACEs and GI symptoms alone. Secondary analyses evaluated children reporting both GI symptoms and chronic pain, while exploratory analyses considered chronic pain alone and a broader composite of GI symptoms and/or chronic pain. This tiered approach anchored findings in GI‐specific outcomes, while still capturing overlapping and broader symptom domains.

Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) logistic regression identified ACEs most predictive of each outcome. LASSO addressed multicollinearity and optimized variable selection. The penalty parameter was chosen via 10‐fold cross‐validation using the lambda.1se criterion to favor parsimony. ACEs retained by LASSO were entered into post‐LASSO logistic regression models, yielding adjusted odds ratios (aORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Mediation analyses used the average causal mediation effect (ACME) framework to test whether child emotional and behavioral difficulties were partially associated with ACE–symptom associations. Indirect effects were estimated with 1000 bootstrapped iterations, and the proportion of the total effect associated with each mediator was calculated. In a post‐hoc analysis, food insecurity was tested as a mediator of the association between economic insecurity and symptom outcomes (based on caregiver report of difficulty affording food in the past year).

Moderation was assessed by including interaction terms between each ACE and candidate moderators (parental education, perceived school safety, perceived neighborhood safety, and community support) in multivariable logistic regression models.

RESULTS

Of 160,157 children surveyed in the pooled NSCH datasets, 73,055 met inclusion criteria for the analytic sample. Exclusions were driven primarily by prespecified age‐based eligibility criteria (n = 61,984), with additional exclusions due to missing data on ACEs, GI symptoms, chronic pain, or covariates required for multivariable analyses (Figure S1). The mean age was 11.7 years (SD 3.6), ranging from 6 to 17 years; 48% were female. Most participants identified as White (79%), followed by Asian (6%), Black or African American (5%), and other racial groups (10%). 14% identified as Hispanic, Latino, or of Spanish origin.

ACEs varied in prevalence. Economic insecurity was reported in 9% of children, parental divorce in 17%, parental death in 2%, and parental incarceration in 5%. 4% were exposed to household violence and 3% to neighborhood violence. 10% lived with a household member with a mental illness, and 9% with someone who misused substances. Racial discrimination was reported by 4%.

Overall, 6% of children had GI symptoms, 5% had chronic pain, 2% had both, and 13% had either or both. Children with symptoms were more likely to be older, female, and have a history of ACE exposure (all p < 0.001). Weighted estimates confirmed that the sample closely reflects the national population. Associations between ACEs and GI symptoms

In the primary analysis, five adverse childhood experiences were retained in the model as the most predictive of GI symptoms: household mental illness, economic insecurity, neighborhood violence, racial discrimination, and household substance use. In the post‐LASSO logistic regression, household mental illness showed the strongest independent association with GI symptoms (aOR 1.56; 95% CI 1.44–1.70). Elevated odds were also observed for economic insecurity (aOR 1.38; 95% CI 1.26–1.50), neighborhood violence (aOR 1.34; 95% CI 1.19–1.52), and racial discrimination (aOR 1.31; 95% CI 1.16–1.47). Household substance use was retained in the model but was not statistically significant (aOR 1.07; 95% CI 0.98–1.17; p = 0.14). Effect estimates and linearization paths are illustrated in Figure 1.

Mediation analysis: The role of child emotional and behavioral health

We examined whether children's emotional and behavioral difficulties partially accounted for the relationship between the ACEs retained by the LASSO regression and GI symptoms. In these models, the direct effect represents the portion of the association not accounted for by these difficulties, and the indirect effect represents the portion mediated through them. Emotional and behavioral difficulties significantly mediated the association for all ACEs examined (ps < 0.001). The largest indirect effects were for neighborhood violence (ACME = 0.02; 95% CI 0.014–0.017), accounting for 35% of the total association with GI symptoms. Comparable proportions were mediated for household substance use (ACME = 0.01; 95% CI 0.007–0.009; 34%) and household mental illness (ACME = 0.014; 95% CI 0.013–0.015; 32%). Smaller but significant mediation effects were found for economic insecurity (ACME = 0.01; 95% CI 0.006–0.008; 22%) and racial discrimination (ACME = 0.006; 95% CI 0.004–0.008; 20%). In each model, direct associations between ACEs and GI symptoms remained significant after accounting for emotional and behavioral difficulties, indicating that these factors were significantly associated with part, but not all, of the relationship. Full quantitative results are in Table 2. In a post‐hoc model, food insecurity mediated 26% of the association between economic insecurity and GI symptoms (ACME = 0.011; 95% CI 0.009–0.013; p < 0.001).

Moderation analysis: The influence of protective family and community factors

We evaluated whether protective family and community factors modified associations between ACEs and GI symptoms. In these models, odds ratios below 1.0 indicate that higher levels of the moderator weakened (buffered) the association between adversity and symptoms. Perceived school safety reduced the association between economic insecurity and GI symptoms (aOR = 0.90; 95% CI 0.81–0.99; p = 0.04). Perceived neighborhood safety similarly attenuated effects for neighborhood violence (aOR = 0.80; 95% CI 0.68–0.94; p = 0.003), household mental illness (aOR = 0.84; 95% CI 0.75–0.94; p = 0.001), and household substance misuse (aOR = 0.88; 95% CI 0.78–0.99; p = 0.04). Community support also moderated associations with neighborhood violence (aOR = 0.86; 95% CI 0.76–0.97; p = 0.01) and household mental illness (aOR = 0.90; 95% CI 0.83–0.97; p = 0.01). Parental education did not significantly moderate any associations, nor was racial discrimination moderated by any of these variables. Full results are in Table 3.

Secondary and exploratory analyses

In secondary analyses combining GI symptoms with chronic pain, the same five ACEs were retained in LASSO regressions, with stronger associations overall. Household mental illness (aOR 1.74, 95% CI 1.52–2.00) and neighborhood violence (aOR 1.73, 95% CI 1.45–2.07) displayed the largest effects; household substance use was not significant (Figure S3). Emotional and behavioral difficulties mediated roughly one‐quarter of total effects (Table S4), and school or neighborhood safety and community support buffered several associations (Table S5).

Exploratory analyses for chronic pain alone and for the composite of GI symptoms and/or pain produced similar patterns, though with fewer significant moderators (Figures S6 and S7; Tables S8 and S9). Mediation accounted for 15%–31% of total effects (Tables S10 and S11).

DISCUSSION

In this nationally representative cohort of US children and adolescents, ACEs were significantly associated with GI symptoms. Household mental illness and economic insecurity were the strongest predictors, followed by neighborhood violence, racial discrimination, and household substance misuse. The goal of this study was to identify which specific ACEs are most strongly linked with pediatric GI symptoms, rather than assessing cumulative adversity, to better inform clinical screening and prevention strategies. These findings demonstrate that psychosocial adversity measurably influences pediatric GI health at the population level, extending evidence from smaller clinical and adult studies to a broad community setting. Emotional and behavioral difficulties were significantly associated with part of these associations, and children's perceptions of safety at school and in their neighborhoods moderated several of them.

Among the adverse experiences examined, household mental illness was the most consistent and robust predictor of GI symptoms. Approximately one‐third of this association was associated with children's emotional and behavioral difficulties, consistent with prior evidence linking pediatric anxiety and stress reactivity to pathways between adversity and GI symptom expression. 6 , 12 Children of caregivers with serious mental illness are frequently exposed to chronic stress, disrupted family routines, and emotional unavailability, factors known to interfere with neurodevelopment, attachment, and physiologic stress regulation. 20 These findings parallel adult research demonstrating strong associations between household mental illness and disorders of gut–brain interaction, suggesting these processes begin early in life and may persist across generations. 21 Children of caregivers with anxiety or depression are also more likely to develop internalizing symptoms, independent of shared environment or genetic predisposition. 22 Such maladaptive stress responses can amplify stress‐related GI symptom burden. 23 Addressing caregiver and child mental health should therefore be considered an essential component of comprehensive care for children with recurrent GI symptoms.

Economic insecurity was strongly associated with GI symptoms in this nationally representative cohort, extending evidence that financial strain is linked to abdominal pain and other stress‐related somatic complaints in children. 24 , 25 In post‐hoc analysis, food insecurity was associated with one‐quarter of this association, suggesting a potentially modifiable pathway. Prior research has linked food insecurity to elevated stress, altered gut microbiota, and nutritional deficiencies, each of which may promote GI symptoms. 26 , 27 Broader structural inequities associated with economic insecurity, including limited access to safe outdoor spaces, healthcare, or fresh food, may further increase risk and should be addressed in both clinical assessment and community‐based support. 26 , 27 These findings reinforce the importance of considering socioeconomic context when evaluating persistent GI symptoms and identify opportunities for collaboration among gastroenterology, behavioral health, and social care to better support affected families.

These findings have important implications for clinicians caring for children with recurrent GI symptoms. Recognizing the links between early adversity and GI distress can help clinicians interpret symptoms within a broader biopsychosocial context, reduce unnecessary testing, and guide supportive conversations with families. 28 Because stress‐related symptoms often span multiple domains, including headache, widespread pain, fatigue, and fibromyalgia‐like features, GI complaints should be understood as a clinically salient manifestation of multisystem symptom burden commonly encountered in pediatric gastroenterology practice, rather than as isolated organ‐specific disease. Psychosocial inquiry should be approached thoughtfully and informed by knowledge of local referral options and community resources. Prospective studies are needed to determine whether addressing early adversity, emotional distress, or unmet social needs improves GI outcomes. In the meantime, clinicians can target modifiable contextual factors such as family stress and food insecurity and collaborate with primary care, behavioral health, and community partners to connect families with support. Future research should test feasible, sustainable models for incorporating psychosocial assessment and referral within pediatric gastroenterology care.

This study has several strengths that enhance confidence in its findings. It leverages 3 years of the NSCH, providing a large, nationally representative sample with contemporary data on US children and adolescents. By analyzing individual ACEs rather than cumulative scores, the study identifies specific exposures most strongly associated with GI symptoms. Use of validated survey measures and complex survey weighting strengthens internal and external validity. Analytically, combining LASSO regression with mediation and moderation models enabled examination of psychosocial mechanisms and contextual modifiers, clarifying how adversity relates to symptom development. These features align with emerging standards for population‐based psychogastroenterology research and illustrate how social and emotional determinants can be rigorously integrated into pediatric gastroenterology studies.

Several limitations should be noted. The NSCH symptom items capture common GI and pain complaints but do not permit formal classification under the Rome criteria or distinction among specific DGBI. We applied a tiered outcome strategy to address possible bias resulting from nondifferential misclassification of GI symptoms. Family structure was also missing for a substantial proportion of respondents; complete‐case analysis was therefore used for multivariable models, consistent with prior NSCH analyses. With respect to our mediation analyses, the cross‐sectional design precludes determination of temporal ordering. Our mediating variable could also encompass heterogeneous conditions with distinct etiologies, introducing residual confounding and limiting inference regarding disorder‐specific mechanisms linking ACEs to GI symptoms. Reliance on caregiver report may have also led to underreporting of ACEs, especially direct violence exposures (e.g., domestic and neighborhood violence), attenuating their relative importance in models. 29 Further, abuse and neglect—variables previously associated with DGBI—were not assessed in the NSCH. 18 , 19 Within our penalized regression framework, unmeasured adversities would be expected to influence findings only if they were both prevalent and independently predictive beyond the measured exposures. Future longitudinal studies integrating additional ACEs, adolescent self‐report, clinician‐confirmed DGBI diagnoses, and objective biomarkers are needed to clarify temporal and mechanistic pathways.

CONCLUSION

In this nationally representative study of US children aged 6–17 years, household mental illness and economic insecurity were the most influential ACEs associated with GI symptoms. Emotional and behavioral difficulties were partially associated with these relationships, whereas protective environments such as safe schools and neighborhoods were associated with lower risk. Early psychosocial adversity appears to contribute to pediatric GI symptom burden through pathways involving both emotional distress and contextual stressors. Recognizing these influences can help clinicians interpret symptoms within a biopsychosocial framework and support more comprehensive care. Longitudinal studies incorporating clinical and biologic data are needed to clarify causal pathways and identify targets for prevention and intervention.
