Synthesis, crystal structure at 100 K, Hirshfeld surface analysis, DFT calculations, antioxidant evaluation and molecular docking study of N,N′-diformohydrazide
Authors: Ahlam Bouhraoua, Mouslim Messali, Assia Sid, Hassane Lgaz, Rafika Bouchene, Ilhem Selatnia, Noureddine El Messaoudi, Ahmed G. Soliman, Awad A. Alrashdi, Mortaga M. Abou-krisha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
N,N′-Diformohydrazide was synthesized in 89% yield from hydrazine hydrate and formic acid and characterized by single-crystal X-ray diffraction at 100 K. The compound crystallizes in the monoclinic space group P21/n. The molecule is planar, and the crystal packing is dominated by N—H···O and C—H···O hydrogen bonds that build corrugated two-dimensional sheets, with H···O contacts identified as the major contributor in Hirshfeld surface analysis. DFT calculations at the ωB97X-D/6-311 + + G(d, p) level reveal a large HOMO—LUMO gap and a high ionization potential, consistent with an electronically stable, hard molecule. NBO analysis identifies a strong nitrogen lone-pair → C =O π* delocalization that accounts for the resonance-assisted character of the hydrazide linkage. MESP, atomic-charge, Fukui, and QTAIM analyses converge on the carbonyl oxygens as the principal hydrogen-bond acceptors and the N—H protons as the principal donors. DPPH, CUPRAC, ABTS, and superoxide assays show only weak radical-scavenging activity relative to BHA and BHT, in line with the predicted electronic stability. Molecular docking against Helicobacter pylori protein targets returned weak-to-moderate binding scores (best score − 4.8 kcal/mol) and is reported as a preliminary in silico screen.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-57125-w.

Introduction

N,N′-Diformohydrazide (also known as 1,2-hydrazinedicarboxaldehyde) has long been recognized for its intriguing structural and physicochemical properties, which primarily arise from the presence of short hydrogen bonds and unique molecular geometry1,2. Early crystallographic investigations, beginning with Tomiie et al.3 in the late 1950s, established a foundational understanding of the compound’s three-dimensional arrangement by locating hydrogen atoms at room temperature. Subsequent low-temperature studies refined the structure to progressively higher resolutions and underscored the profound influence of temperature on critical geometric parameters, especially with regard to hydrogen-atom positions4. The continued interest in N,N′-diformohydrazide is partly attributable to its role as a model compound in hydrogen-bonding research and as a blueprint for designing molecules with extended hydrogen-bond networks, which find applications across diverse fields such as crystal engineering, pharmaceutical development, and materials science5,6. While these prior crystallographic efforts have clarified many aspects of N,N′-diformohydrazide, crucial questions remain regarding its behavior at 100 K and the deeper electronic structure and chemical reactivity of this molecule. Even though the fundamental framework of this compound is well established, a more comprehensive theoretical investigation is required to fully characterize the electron density and to elucidate the nature of the non-covalent interactions that hold its extended lattice together. Such an approach is particularly compelling for compounds in which hydrogen-bonding interactions not only drive the crystalline arrangement but may also modulate reactivity and intermolecular recognition in solution or solid phases7–10.

The present work extends the existing literature on N,N′-diformohydrazide in three respects. First, we report a 100 K single-crystal X-ray refinement collected on a modern SuperNova diffractometer with multi-scan absorption correction, complementing the previous determinations of this compound spanning 15 K to room temperature. The crystal structure has been studied by X-ray diffraction at room temperature3,11,12, at 85 K13, at 19 °C and − 165 °C14, by neutron diffraction at 15 K4, and most recently at 100 K with X-ray molecular orbital analysis15,16; all entries refine in the same monoclinic phase, and the present refinement is a redetermination of the known phase. Second, we present the first integrated reactivity profile for this molecule, combining global and local conceptual-DFT descriptors, three independent atomic-charge schemes (Mulliken, ESP, NBO), Natural Bond Orbital analysis, QTAIM, and reduced-density-gradient noncovalent-interaction maps, all anchored to the experimental geometry. Third, we use this profile as the basis for an exploratory in silico screen against Helicobacter pylori protein targets, a class of receptors not previously examined for this scaffold.

The objective of this study is to determine whether the dense hydrogen-bond network of N,N′-diformohydrazide observed at 100 K can be quantitatively reconciled with its electronic-structure descriptors, and whether the resulting picture rationalizes the observed reactivity in solution-phase antioxidant assays and in computational binding screens against bacterial protein targets. Three specific questions are addressed: (i) which atomic sites act as the dominant hydrogen-bond donors and acceptors, and do real-space (QTAIM, RDG, MESP) and orbital-based (NBO, Fukui) descriptors converge on the same assignment; (ii) does the predicted electronic stability translate into the expected weak radical-scavenging activity; and (iii) does the same hydrogen-bond donor–acceptor topology produce favorable, weak, or null binding modes against representative receptors. The antioxidant assays and the docking calculations are reported as exploratory probes of this hydrogen-bond donor–acceptor topology, in solution and in protein binding pockets respectively, rather than as a drug-discovery assessment.

Materials and methods

Synthesis

N,N′-diformohydrazide (1,2-hydrazinedicarboxaldehyde) (3) scheme 1 was obtained as white crystals with 89% Yield. It was synthesized by the treatment of one equivalent of hydrated hydrazine (1) with two equivalents of formic acid (2) in the presence of MeOH under reflux for 4 h. A precipitation of a white powder was separated, washed by ice water, filtered and crystallized in EtOH.

Single-crystal X-ray diffraction

Crystals of N,N′-diformohydrazide (1,2-hydrazinedicarboxaldehyde) (3) were obtained in suitable dimensions for diffraction experiments and mounted on a SuperNova diffractometer equipped with an Atlas detector. Data collection was carried out at 100 K using Mo Kα radiation (λ = 0.71073 Å) and a graphite monochromator. A series of scans was performed to measure the intensities, and the raw data were processed to yield reflection intensities and apply multi-scan absorption corrections. The crystallographic parameters, as well as details of data collection and refinement, are presented in Table 1. Structure solution was accomplished by direct methods, followed by iterative Fourier syntheses to locate the remaining non-hydrogen atoms. Full-matrix least-squares refinement on F2 was then performed, adopting anisotropic displacement parameters for all non-hydrogen atoms. Hydrogen atoms were introduced in geometrically calculated positions and linked to their respective parent atoms under a riding-atom constraint. The isotropic displacement parameters of hydrogen atoms were set at 1.2 times the equivalent isotropic displacement parameter of the parent atom. All computations and refinements were conducted using standard software procedures, with special reference to the guidelines outlined by Sheldrick17,18.

Hirshfeld analysis

The crystallographic information file (CIF) derived from the final X-ray refinement at 100 K was used as the input for Hirshfeld surface computations. All analyses were conducted using Crystal Explorer 17.519. The dnorm property was mapped on the generated Hirshfeld surfaces by calculating di and de at each point, normalizing those distances against the respective van der Waals radii of the atoms involved. Red regions in the dnorm map correspond to distances significantly shorter than the sum of the radii, white indicates interactions close to the sum, and blue suggests longer intermolecular separations. Two-dimensional fingerprint plots were subsequently produced to quantify the fraction of the surface involved in each type of contact. This mapping illuminates the extent of hydrogen-bond participation by showing spikes or wings in the fingerprint profiles corresponding to short H···O or other relevant interactions.

Antioxidant activity assay

The antioxidant potential of compound (3) was assessed in comparison with several reference antioxidants, namely butylated hydroxyl toluene (BHT) and butylated hydroxyl anisole (BHA). The evaluation was carried out through four in vitro assays (DPPH20, CUPRAC21,22, ABTS23, and anion superoxide24,25. All experiments were carried out using a 96-well microplate reader (Spectra Max, Molecular Devices, California, USA). Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) served as the control. Results were expressed as IC50 values (µg mL–1), representing the concentration required to achieve 50% inhibition, except for the CUPRAC assay, where results were reported as A0.5 values (µg mL–1), corresponding to the concentration that produced an absorbance of 0.50.

Computational methodology

All quantum-chemical computations were carried out using density functional theory (DFT) in Gaussian 0926. The ωB97X-D functional was chosen because it includes long-range correction and dispersion terms, ensuring more accurate treatment of intermolecular interactions in hydrogen-bonded systems27,28. The 6-311++G(d, p) basis set was applied to every atom in the molecule. Geometry optimizations were done in the gas phase without any symmetry constraints, employing an ultrafine grid and tight self-consistent field (SCF) convergence criteria for enhanced precision. Frequency calculations confirmed the absence of imaginary vibrational modes, establishing that the optimized geometries correspond to energy minima on the potential energy surface. Wavefunction files were saved for the subsequent Multiwfn analyses (section “Molecular docking workflow”).

HOMO and LUMO energies were extracted from the optimized wavefunctions, and the ionization potential and electron affinity were obtained from Koopmans’ theorem (IP = − EHOMO; EA = − ELUMO). Atomic charge analysis was performed using the Mulliken and natural bond orbital (NBO) schemes. For NBO computations, the Pop=(NBO, Full) keyword in Gaussian 09 was employed to reveal donor–acceptor interactions and assess hyperconjugative effects. To obtain electrostatic potential (ESP) charges, an additional single-point calculation was carried out with Pop=(MK) IOp(6/33 = 2), focusing on fitting the molecular electrostatic potential around the molecule to deduce partial atomic charges that are informative for understanding electrophilic and nucleophilic sites.

Real-space analyses were carried out in Multiwfn32,33 using the ωB97X-D/6-311 + + G(d, p) wavefunction. Reduced density gradient (RDG) isosurfaces and scatter plots were generated to identify noncovalent interactions, and the QTAIM module was used to locate bond critical points and bond paths.

Molecular docking workflow

The 2D structure of N,N′-diformohydrazide was drawn in Marvin JS, and the 3D geometry was energy-minimized in Avogadro 1.2.0 with the MMFF94 force field34. SwissTargetPrediction was used to identify candidate human off-targets from the ligand SMILES. The 3D structures of the H. pylori and human proteins were retrieved from UniProt, and binding sites were assigned from literature reports and validated with CB-DOCK235. Proteins were prepared in AutoDock Tools 1.5.736 by removing water molecules, adding polar hydrogens, and assigning Gasteiger charges. Docking was performed in QVINA237 with grid boxes centered on the predicted binding sites, exhaustiveness set to 16 and the default scoring function. Binding affinities (ΔG) and intermolecular interactions (hydrogen bonds, electrostatic contacts, hydrophobic contacts) were analyzed and visualized with BIOVIA Discovery Studio Visualizer 202038.

Results and discussion

Crystallographic analysis of N,N′-diformohydrazide

The crystal structure of N,N′-diformohydrazide (3) has been the subject of multiple investigations across nearly seven decades. The earliest work by Tomiie, Koo, and Nitta in 1958 established the room-temperature monoclinic structure from photographic two-dimensional X-ray data and located hydrogen atoms despite the inherent difficulty of refining light scatterers3. Tomiie subsequently extended this analysis with electron-count estimates and molecular-orbital comparisons11. Ottersen redetermined the structure at 19 °C and − 165 °C with extended L-shell refinement14, followed by Hope and Ottersen’s 85 K X-ray refinement based on high-order data36, and Tanaka’s diffractometer-based electron-density analysis at 23 °C12. Jeffrey, Ruble, McMullan, DeFrees, and Pople then provided single-crystal neutron diffraction data at 15 K, which remains the reference for free hydrogen-atom positioning in this molecule4. Most recently, Tanaka and Wasada-Tsutsui reported a 100 K X-ray molecular-orbital (XMO) refinement that determined molecular orbitals directly from diffraction data and refined hydrogen positions without geometric constraints15,16. The complete list of crystallographic determinations is summarized in Table S0. All entries refine in the same monoclinic phase (space group P21/n, equivalent to the P21/a setting used in some earlier reports); no polymorph has been reported. The present 100 K refinement is therefore a redetermination of the known phase rather than a new polymorph, and its value lies elsewhere: it provides a modern, multi-scan-corrected single-crystal dataset on a SuperNova diffractometer (R = 0.034) that anchors a comprehensive integrated reactivity analysis (DFT, NBO, QTAIM, RDG, MESP, and condensed Fukui descriptors) - an integration that has not previously been performed on this molecule.

The use of riding-atom constraints for hydrogen positions in the present refinement (N–H = 0.88 Å, C–H = 0.95 Å) merits brief comment in light of more recent free refinements of this same compound. The 100 K XMO refinement of Tanaka and Wasada-Tsutsui16 reported N–H = 1.033(3) Å and C–H = 1.032(2) Å with no geometric constraints, and the 15 K neutron refinement of Jeffrey et al.4 reported N–H = 1.039(7) Å and C–H = 1.100(7) Å. The riding-atom values used in the present work are therefore systematically shorter than the free-refined nuclear positions by approximately 0.15 Å. Crucially, the constraint affects only the H-atom position relative to its bonded heavy atom; the donor–acceptor heavy-atom geometry of the hydrogen-bond network - N⋯O distances, the N–H⋯O angles defined by the heavy atoms, the R22(8) and R42(10) graph-set ring motifs, and the corrugated 2D-sheet topology - is unaffected by this convention. Consistent with this, the N⋯O distance of 2.7591(8) Å reported here at 100 K agrees with the values reported in the free-refined determinations within experimental uncertainty. The integrated reactivity analyses developed in sections “Chemical reactivity descriptors”–“Non-covalent interaction (NCI) analysis” are anchored on the heavy-atom geometry and on the optimized DFT geometry rather than on the X-ray hydrogen positions, and are therefore not sensitive to the riding-model constraint.

An enduring theme in the structural chemistry of (3) is its extensive hydrogen-bond network, which emerges from both the formyl-derived oxygen centers and the amide-like hydrazide moieties. Because hydrogen atoms scatter X-rays relatively weakly, strategic placement protocols are crucial: SHELXL’s HFIX procedures provide a means to constrain the geometries of hydrogen atoms to well-established parameters (0.95 Å for C–H and 0.88 Å for N–H)18.

Table 2 shows the comparison of the molecular dimensions of (3) in this work with previous X-ray and neutron diffraction studies. While modern diffraction data can often locate hydrogen nuclei in electron density maps, systematic errors may remain if the data resolution is limited or if thermal smearing is pronounced. Consequently, employing geometric constraints not only achieves internal consistency for the structure solution but also ensures that the intermolecular hydrogen-bond motifs, key to the molecule’s packing, are accurately delineated.

The thermal ellipsoid plot of the title compound (3), with the atomic labeling scheme, is given in Fig. 1. Comparisons of structural parameters across temperature ranges and methodologies (X-ray at 100 K, 85 K, and neutron at 15 K) confirm that the planarity of (3) persists as a defining structural feature. The asymmetric unit contains half of the molecule, mirrored by an inversion center to form the complete N,N′-diformohydrazide scaffold.

DFT-optimized values agree with the X-ray heavy-atom geometry to within MAD = 0.017 Å for bond lengths and 0.48° for bond angles (Tables 3, 4), confirming that the gas-phase ωB97X-D/6-311 + + G(d, p) calculation reproduces the experimental geometry within the typical accuracy of the method. The largest individual deviation (|Δ| = 0.0244 Å) is observed for the C = O bond, which is shorter in the gas-phase calculation than in the crystal - a difference fully consistent with the resonance-assisted hydrogen-bonding framework discussed in section “Natural Bond Orbital (NBO) analysis”, in which the intermolecular N—H···O = C contacts present in the solid state further elongate the carbonyl bond beyond its isolated-molecule value. The N—N and N—C bond deviations (0.0058 and 0.0194 Å, respectively) are smaller, as expected for bonds that are not directly engaged in the intermolecular hydrogen-bond network. Both the experimental and the DFT-optimized geometries show essentially perfect coplanarity of the hydrazide–formyl framework (N1i—N1—C1—O1 torsion of 0.31° and 0.005°, respectively), consistent with the rigid planar conformation enforced by the resonance-assisted hydrogen-bond network.

The nearly linear torsion angle at the central N–N bond (C1–N1–N1–C1 ≈ 180°) underscores the molecule’s intrinsic planarity and suggests a conjugation pathway that may stabilize the hydrazide’s electronic structure. Chemical reactivity descriptors

Quantum chemical parameters derived from DFT calculation are listed in Table 5. The highly negative energy of the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO, − 9.10 eV) coupled with the significantly positive LUMO energy (+ 1.08 eV) creates a large frontier orbital gap (Egap = 10.18 eV). A gap of this magnitude, beyond the typical range for many conjugated organic molecules, signifies that the compound is likely to be quite inert toward low-level thermal or photochemical excitations40,41.

The high ionization potential (IP = 9.10 eV) and the negative electron affinity (EA = − 1.08 eV) confirm that (3) is unfavorable both as an electron donor and as an electron acceptor in the gas phase42,43. From the perspective of conceptual density functional theory (DFT), this synergy of high IP and negative EA places the compound in a “closed-shell” regime that resists facile electron-transfer processes44–46. The resulting chemical potential (µ = − 4.01 eV) and electronegativity (χ = 4.01 eV) characterize (3) as moderately electronegative but not aggressively so, meaning it can attract electron density from weaker donors while still maintaining a stable electronic shell.

The high hardness (η = 5.09 eV)47 and low softness (σ = 0.196 eV⁻¹)48 point to an electron density that is difficult to polarize, consistent with the molecule’s tendency to form discrete, geometrically well-defined hydrogen bonds rather than to engage in extended π-delocalization.

The electrophilicity index (ω = 1.58 eV) offers additional insight. Although not negligible, this moderate value indicates that while (3) can accept electron density under suitably stabilizing conditions (e.g., formation of strong intermolecular hydrogen bonds with excellent proton donors), it is not a highly active electrophile49. The nucleophilicity index (ε = 0.63 eV–1) is similarly modest, meaning that, in principle, the molecule can donate electron density (for example, via the lone pairs on the hydrazide moieties), but it is unlikely to be a powerful nucleophile in competitive scenarios50.

The disparity between electron donating power (ω– = 8.45 eV) and electron accepting power (ω+ = 0.42 eV) speaks to the molecular architecture dominated by the –CONH–NHCO– backbone. These groups carry significant lone-pair character on nitrogen and oxygen, enabling them to donate electron density in strongly stabilizing environments such as hydrogen bonds or resonance-assisted frameworks. Conversely, the low electron accepting power suggests that any additional electrons introduced must be stabilized through very favorable interactions or resonance effects, reinforcing the molecule’s overall reluctance toward reduction. Consequently, the sizable net electrophilicity (Δω± = 8.87 eV) is less an indication of classical electrophilicity and more a reflection of the compound’s potential to engage in donor–acceptor interactions if the binding partner can compensate energetically for the introduction of electron density.

The HOMO and LUMO are delocalized over the entire molecule (Fig. 6), so no single site acts as a dominant reactive hot spot51–53; the hydrazide and formyl functionalities instead participate cooperatively in the dense hydrogen-bond network observed in the crystal.

MESP and atomic charges

The molecular electrostatic potential serves as a spatial indicator of electron-rich (negative potential) and electron-deficient (positive potential) regions53,54. For N,N′-diformohydrazide, the MESP visualization (Fig. 7a) reveals pronounced red contours around the carbonyl groups (C = O) and an intense blue color in proximity to the N–H fragments. The strong negative potential around oxygen atoms arises from the high electronegativity and the presence of lone pairs, indicating that these sites can act as potential hydrogen-bond acceptors or electrophilic targets. Conversely, the positive potential around the N–H moieties emphasizes their role as hydrogen-bond donors and suggests that these hydrogens could be relatively acidic compared to typical sp²-hybridized C–H sites.

To gain quantitative insight into how this electrostatic environment distributes charge across the molecule, three different partial atomic charge schemes, NBO, ESP-fitted, and Mulliken, were applied. Each method applies a distinct theoretical framework, thus providing complementary views of charge partitioning and electronic polarization.

NBO analysis focuses on identifying localized “natural” atomic orbitals that resemble classical Lewis structures, leading to a chemically intuitive picture of donor–acceptor interactions55–57. In the data reported (Fig. 7c), both oxygen atoms (O9 and O10) are assigned notably large NBO charges (~-0.632), reflecting their role as the most electronegative sites in the molecule. While the nominal magnitudes in NBO often appear high due to the manner in which the algorithm partitions electron density, these values highlight the dominant electron accumulation at the carbonyl oxygens. The nitrogen atoms (N5 and N7) have NBO charges of ~-0.454, indicating significant electron density retention but still less than that of the oxygens. Hydrogen atoms bound to the nitrogen (H6 and H8) show smaller positive values (~ 0.43), consistent with partial proton character. The carbonyl carbons (C1 and C3) exhibit high NBO values (~ 0.52), indicating a strong withdrawal of electron density from the hydrazide backbone by the adjacent oxygen atoms.

In contrast to NBO, electrostatic potential (ESP) charges are derived from fitting the external electrostatic field. As such, they often provide a physically grounded depiction of how the molecule might interact with external species in a real electrostatic environment. Here, Fig. 7b shows that the carbonyl oxygens are each assigned moderately negative charges of about − 0.51, indicating their propensity to attract hydrogen-bond donors or electrophilic centers. The nitrogens (N5 and N7) manifest slight negativity (–0.315 and − 0.262, respectively). In contrast, the hydrogens bonded to these nitrogens (H6 and H8) gain a distinctly positive character (+ 0.33), reinforcing their potential as proton donors in hydrogen bonding. The carbons (C1 and C3), conjugate with the electronegative atoms, maintain small positive values (+ 0.41 to + 0.43). Compared to NBO, the ESP method yields more modest magnitudes, reflecting its direct linkage to the real-space Coulombic potential rather than localized orbital constructs.

Mulliken population analysis distributes electron density based on overlap integrals of atomic orbitals, which can make it highly sensitive to the choice of basis set and the degree of diffuse functions54,58. In the present data (Fig. 7d), the carbonyl oxygens (–0.397) exhibit negative charges of slightly lower magnitude than in the ESP scheme. The N–H fragments again emerge with partially positive hydrogen atoms (H6 and H8 ~ + 0.38) and slightly negative nitrogens (N5 and N7 − 0.16). The carbon atoms in the carbonyl groups display only a small positive shift (+ 0.037). Because Mulliken analysis uses a fundamentally different partitioning approach, it often identifies subtler distinctions across bonded frameworks, especially when compared to NBO. Across all three charge models, the carbonyl oxygens remain the most electron-rich sites, coinciding perfectly with the deep red regions on the MESP surface. These oxygens are thus primed for hydrogen bonding as acceptors and can act as local electrophilic sites. Meanwhile, the N–H hydrogens consistently show positive charges, corroborating their strong donor capacity.

Natural Bond Orbital (NBO) analysis

Second-order perturbation energies E(2) for the most relevant donor–acceptor interactions are listed in Table S2.

The most prominent delocalizations are those involving the lone pairs of nitrogen and the antibonding orbitals of the adjacent C = O bonds. Specifically, LP (1) N5 → BD* (1) C1–O10 and LP (1) N7 → BD* (1) C3–O9 both display exceptionally high E(2) values of 91.73 kcal/mol, underscoring a significant resonance-assisted interaction between the electron pair on nitrogen and the antibonding orbital of the carbonyl group. This finding is consistent with amide-like conjugation phenomena, in which the nitrogen lone pair can donate electron density into the π* orbital of an adjacent C = O, thereby stabilizing the system and partially delocalizing the electronic charge. The relatively small orbital energy gap (ΔE = 0.41 a.u.) in these interactions supports the notion that the nitrogen lone pairs align well energetically with the empty π* states of the carbonyl, facilitating strong delocalization.

Another notable feature is the involvement of oxygen lone pairs (LP (2) O9 and O10) in stabilizing the antibonding orbitals of the adjacent C–N or C–H bonds. Interactions such as LP (2) O9 → BD* (1) C3–N7 (30.26 kcal/mol) and LP (2) O10 → BD* (1) C1–N5 (also 30.26 kcal/mol) highlight significant n → σ* delocalization. Although these are typically weaker than strong n → π* conjugation, the magnitudes here are still considerable, suggesting that the oxygen lone pairs not only engage in π-type resonance with the carbonyl but also donate electron density into certain σ* orbitals of neighboring bonds. Such interactions can modulate bond strengths subtly, affecting both bond angles and bond lengths in the immediate vicinity of the oxygen centers.

More modest resonance is observed between the bonding orbitals of C–H (BD (1) C1–H2 and BD (1) C3–H4) and the antibonding N–N orbital (BD* (1) N5–N7). Each of these interactions contributes about 7.25 kcal/mol in stabilization, implying that even saturated C–H bonds participate to a small degree in hyperconjugative donation toward the N–N antibond. Although such contributions are minor compared to the strong n → π* delocalizations, they still provide incremental stabilization that can influence the overall electron distribution.

Smaller contributions (E(2) = 5.6 kcal/mol) from O core orbitals into Rydberg-type C orbitals are also detected but do not affect the dominant delocalization picture60–62.

The exceptionally high E(2) of 91.7 kcal/mol for LP(N) → π*(C = O) places this molecule firmly in the resonance-assisted hydrogen-bonding (RAHB) regime. This delocalization predicts a measurable elongation of the C = O bond and a shortening of the adjacent N–C bond relative to non-resonance-assisted reference compounds, and both predictions are quantitatively confirmed by the experimental geometry. The C = O distance of 1.2379(9) Å measured at 100 K is approximately 0.015–0.020 Å longer than typical aliphatic aldehyde C = O distances (~ 1.220 Å), and the N–C distance of 1.3305(9) Å is shortened from the typical N–C single-bond value of ~ 1.45 Å by approximately 0.12 Å. Together with the coplanarity of the entire hydrazide–formyl framework (torsion N1ⁱ—N1—C1—O1 = 0.31(13)°), these geometric signatures provide direct experimental evidence for the n→π* delocalization quantified in the NBO analysis. The RAHB framework therefore unifies three otherwise-independent observations: the strong NBO E(2), the geometric distortion away from canonical bond lengths, and the dense, sharply directional hydrogen-bond network observed in the crystal.

Quantum theory of atoms in molecules

The quantum theory of atoms in molecules offers a powerful route to analyze bonding interactions through the properties of bond critical points (BCPs)58,63. In N,N′-diformohydrazide, a total of nine (3, − 1) critical points (Fig. 8a) emerge from the analysis, each representing a unique bonding interaction between pairs of atoms.

Topological descriptors at the BCPs (ρ(r), ∇2ρ(r), G(r), V(r), H(r)) are collected in Table S3. All BCPs show ∇2ρ(r) < 0 and H(r) < 0, indicating that every covalent bond in the molecule has shared-shell character with the potential-energy term dominating at the BCP.

The N–N bond critical point (CP 15, connecting N5 and N7) emerges with an electron density of ρ = 0.341 a.u. and a moderately negative Laplacian (∇2ρ=−0.652 a.u.), consistent with a relatively strong covalent linkage. The partial double-bond character or resonance within the hydrazide moiety often manifests in an appreciable electron density between the two nitrogen atoms. The large negative energy density (H = − 0.313 a.u.) is indicative of a significant electron-sharing interaction that helps rigidify the N–N core.

By contrast, the N–C bonds (CP 13 and CP 17) exhibit electron densities slightly lower than that of N–N, at ρ = 0.316 a.u., but still within a range typical of polar or partially delocalized interactions. The negative Laplacian values (∇2ρ = −0.714 a.u.) underscore a strong electron accumulation, while the total energy density remains negative, confirming substantial covalency. These features align with the resonance concept often invoked in hydrazide and amide-like groups, where lone pairs on nitrogen delocalize into the C = O framework, stabilizing the N–C bond.

The carbonyl bonds (CP 11: O9–C3 and CP 19: O10–C1) show relatively high electron densities (ρ = 0.409 a.u.) and a smaller absolute value for the Laplacian (∇2ρ≈−0.186 a.u.) in comparison to the N–N or N–C bonds. This combination suggests a significant buildup of electron density along the C = O axis, though the Laplacian is not as large (in negative terms) as those for the N–N or N–C bonds. The strong negative total energy density (H = − 0.692 a.u.) corroborates a robust double-bond character with partial ionic polarity, typical of conjugated carbonyl groups. Hence, the topological data align well with the well-known partial double-bond character that fosters resonance stabilization in hydrazide derivatives.

The X–H bonds (where X is C or N) predictably carry a lower electron density than the heavier-atom bonds, as observed at CP 12 (C3–H4), CP 18 (C1–H2), CP 14 (N5–H6), and CP 16 (N7–H8). Here, ρ typically resides in the 0.28–0.34 a.u. range, coupled with negative Laplacian and energy density values. While these negative Laplacians denote electron sharing, the electron density is nonetheless less than in heavier-atom bonds, reflecting the typically polar-covalent nature of C–H and N–H linkages. Notably, the N–H BCPs (CP 14 and CP 16) exhibit ∇2ρ = −1.815 a.u., indicating a strong localization of electron density that contributes to the molecule’s robust hydrogen-bond donating abilities in the crystal. The smaller ρ at these N–H critical points (0.3369 a.u.) relative to the heavier-atom linkages is consistent with short, polar covalent interactions that can easily engage in intermolecular hydrogen bonds.

Bond ellipticities (ε) across the board remain fairly low (on the order of 0.03–0.18), except for certain polar bonds where slight deviations from cylindrical symmetry occur.

Overall, the QTAIM results confirm that N,N′-diformohydrazide possesses a network of predominantly covalent bonds with considerable polarization, especially around the carbonyl and amide-like moieties.

Antioxidant activity

The antioxidant assay was carried out as a direct experimental test of the electronic-structure picture obtained from DFT. The high ionization potential, large HOMO-LUMO gap, and absence of phenolic or other classical hydrogen-atom-transfer motifs all predict that N,N′-diformohydrazide should be a poor radical scavenger. Confirming this prediction experimentally would strengthen the structural–electronic–reactivity correlation that is the central goal of this work. The antioxidant activity of N,N′-diformohydrazide was evaluated using DPPH, CUPRAC, ABTS, and superoxide radical scavenging assays, with BHA and BHT employed as reference standards (Table 6). In both the DPPH and CUPRAC assays, the compound showed no measurable activity at the tested concentrations (IC50 > 800 µM), in sharp contrast to the strong performance of BHA and BHT, which displayed IC50 and A0.5 values in the low micromolar range. In the ABTS assay, N,N′-diformohydrazide exhibited moderate radical scavenging activity (IC50 = 421.25 µM), which was considerably weaker than that of BHA (13.62 µM) and BHT (10.83 µM). Similarly, the compound demonstrated only modest superoxide radical scavenging capacity (IC50 = 152.35 µM), although this still indicates a greater affinity for superoxide compared to its DPPH and CUPRAC responses. Overall, (3) is a weak radical scavenger across the four assays. The relatively higher response in the superoxide assay may reflect a hydrazide-specific reactivity but does not change the overall classification.

Molecular docking study

Helicobacter pylori is a class I carcinogen associated with gastric cancer and peptic ulcer disease, and its rising resistance to standard triple therapy has motivated the search for compounds that engage alternative bacterial targets66,67. As an exploratory in silico screen, (3) was docked against a panel of H. pylori enzymes and a control set of human off-targets to probe whether its hydrogen-bond donor–acceptor topology produces detectable binding signals at non-pharmacological targets.

Our molecular docking calculations returned weak-to-moderate binding scores against several H. pylori proteins. Among the targets examined, dihydroorotase (B5Z6V2) and GTPase Era (B2UTX1) returned the highest scores in our calculations (− 4.8 kcal/mol each (Table 7)), although these values fall in the weak-to-moderate range typically observed for non-optimized ligands. These enzymes are known to play roles in H. pylori survival and pathogenesis, which made them appropriate targets for an exploratory screen. When examining interactions with human proteins, our study found that the compound exhibited generally lower binding affinities compared to bacterial targets, with the highest scores of -6 and − 6.3 kcal/mol for AOC3 and BCHE, respectively (Table 7). The bacterial-versus-human score difference is small (≤ 0.7 kcal/mol) and is well within the typical noise of AutoDock Vina; we therefore do not interpret it as evidence of selectivity.

The interaction analysis with human AOC3 (Fig. 11 and Table S6) showed a conventional hydrogen bonds with SER464, while BCHE (Fig. 12 and Table S6) demonstrated a more extensive network of hydrogen bonds with residues ASN124, SER151, SER152, ASN173, and a carbon hydrogen bond with ALA90. All scores in Table 7 fall in the moderated range, and we caution against extrapolating from these values to in vivo selectivity or therapeutic concentrations.

Dihydroorotase is another candidate target reported in the literature, with pyrimidine-biosynthesis inhibition proposed as an antibacterial strategy. The interactions observed in our docking pose, in particular the salt bridge with ASP239, are geometrically reasonable but do not constitute evidence for specific recognition or inhibition; biochemical assays would be required to make either claim.

Urease binding (− 4.9 kcal/mol) was also examined because of the role of this enzyme in H. pylori survival. The score remains weak, and a redocking against the experimentally determined urease structure with a known inhibitor as positive control would be required before any inhibitory claim could be defended.

Taken together, the docking results call for a brief reconciliation with the electronic-structure picture developed earlier. Frontier-orbital separation governs the energetics of electron transfer and frontier-orbital-controlled reactivity; the large HOMO–LUMO gap reported in section “Chemical reactivity descriptors” therefore predicts, and is corroborated by, the weak radical-scavenging activity in section “Antioxidant activity”. Protein–ligand docking, by contrast, reflects the geometric and electrostatic complementarity between the ligand and the binding pocket and is dominated here by hydrogen bonding and shape fit. The same molecular features that render the compound electronically inert - a rigid, planar hydrazide backbone with sharply localized N-H donors and C = O acceptors - are also what allow the modest but non-zero binding scores reported in Table 7, since these features support discrete, geometrically well-defined hydrogen-bond contacts in protein active sites. The two observations are therefore consistent rather than contradictory: the molecule is a poor electron donor and acceptor, but a competent hydrogen-bond former.

Conclusions

This investigation of N,N′-diformohydrazide at 100 K, combining single-crystal X-ray diffraction and advanced quantum chemical methods, reveals a molecule that achieves both structural rigidity and pronounced hydrogen-bonding capabilities. The low-temperature crystallographic data confirm that the hydrazide adopts a nearly planar conformation, wherein N–H···O = C and C–H···O = C hydrogen bonds assemble into corrugated sheets. Hirshfeld surface analysis corroborates the pivotal role of hydrogen bonding, with more than half of the close contacts in the crystal lattice deriving from H···O interactions. On the electronic front, DFT calculations show a substantial HOMO–LUMO gap (10.18 eV), high ionization potential (9.10 eV), and negative electron affinity (–1.08 eV), underscoring the molecule’s overall electronic stability and low propensity for electron transfer. Local reactivity descriptors, especially Fukui functions and dual descriptors, highlight the carbonyl oxygens as nucleophilic sites and the N–H hydrogens as strong hydrogen-bond donors, consistent with the crystallographic picture. Resonance-assisted interactions, evidenced by substantial n → π* delocalization energies in NBO and by a negative Laplacian of the electron density in QTAIM, underscore how partial double-bond character and polar covalency reinforce the compound’s strong hydrogen-bond framework.

N,N′-Diformohydrazide displayed negligible activity in DPPH and CUPRAC assays and only weak scavenging in ABTS and superoxide tests, far below BHA and BHT. These results indicate limited antioxidant potential, with only modest reactivity toward superoxide species.

Collectively, the experimental and computational results identify (3) as a small, electronically hard molecule whose reactivity is determined almost entirely by the geometric and electrostatic properties of its hydrogen-bond donor–acceptor sites rather than by frontier-orbital chemistry. The docking results reported here are exploratory in silico observations that test, at a coarse level, the protein-binding behavior of the compound’s hydrogen-bond donor–acceptor topology. The absolute binding scores are weak, the score difference between bacterial and human targets is small, and biochemical validation against experimentally determined protein structures with positive controls would be needed before any therapeutic interpretation could be made.
