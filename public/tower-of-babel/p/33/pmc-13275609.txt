Effects of sevoflurane on natural killer cell-induced apoptosis of malignant tumour cells: an in vitro laboratory study
Authors: Valérie Rössler-Fehr, Jonah B Neff, Suellen Darc dos Santos Oliveira, Zhenlong Chen, Richard D Minshall, Martin Schläpfer, Beatrice Beck-Schimmer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeNatural killer (NK) cells contribute to tumour immunosurveillance. They detect and eliminate circulating tumour cells by degranulation, releasing perforin and granzyme. Also involved in NK-cell-mediated tumour cell defense are cytokines with proapoptotic and anti-apoptotic characteristics as well as extracellular vesicles containing perforin. Recent clinical studies suggest that volatile anesthetics might negatively impact cancer outcomes compared with intravenous agents. A possible factor could be the impairment of NK cell activity by volatile anesthetics. In this study, we aimed to investigate the effects of sevoflurane on NK-mediated tumour cell defense in a cell model in vitro.MethodsIn an in vitro laboratory study, we exposed a NK cell line to 2.2% sevoflurane for 2 hr, followed by co-incubation with leukemia target cells. We defined apoptosis of targeted tumour cells and explored effector cell mechanisms by assessing degranulation of NK92 cells and production of cytokine messenger ribonucleic acid (mRNA). Furthermore, we determined NK cell concentrations of extracellular vesicles and perforin protein.ResultsAfter 24 hr of co-incubation of tumour cells with sevoflurane-pretreated NK cells, the number of early apoptotic tumour cells was decreased, with a reduction of 36% on average (P = 0.016). Natural killer cell degranulation, cytokine mRNA, and size and concentration of extracellular vesicles were not significantly different. The perforin concentration of NK cells was reduced when pretreated with sevoflurane. At 48 hr, perforin cell content as well as perforin in the supernatant was reduced (mean reduction for both, 16%; P < 0.001 for both).ConclusionsThis study suggests that the observed transient impaired NK-cell-mediated tumour defense may be linked to the reduced perforin production when NK cells are exposed to sevoflurane.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12630-026-03129-z.Keywords: innate immunity, natural killer cells, sevoflurane, tumour defense, volatile anesthetics

The perioperative period of cancer surgery is crucial, as it can affect the course of the disease and the long-term outcome.1,2 During the surgical procedure, it is unavoidable that tumour cells from solid cancers enter the bloodstream as circulating tumour cells (CTCs).3 These cells can potentially cause metastases and thus need to be eliminated by the immune system.4

Natural killer (NK) cells are large granular lymphocytes, characterized as clusters of differentiation (CD) 56-positive (CD56+) and CD 3-negative (CD3−) and a vital component of the innate immune system.5 Without prior sensitization, they can detect and eliminate malignant cells.6,7 A complex network of activating and inhibitory signals regulates cellular activity.8 Two NK-cell-mediated tumour cell elimination mechanisms are well-established: degranulation (Fig. 1A) and cytokine-mediated death receptor pathways (Fig. 1B).8 Recently, a third mechanism has been suggested: the undirected release of cytotoxic extracellular vesicles (EVs) by NK cells (Fig. 1C).9

The three natural killer-cell-mediated tumour defense mechanisms. The first mechanism is degranulation with fast kinetics and a directed (cell–cell-contact-mediated) mechanism (A). The second mechanism is the induction of the cytokine-induced death receptor pathway (B). This mechanism is not directed (no cell–cell contact is required) and is promoted by TNF-α and IFN-γ and inhibited by IL-6. The third mechanism is extracellular-vesicle/exosome-mediated tumour defense which is not directed (C). Vesicles containing enzymes such as perforin and granzyme are released by NK cells and may induce distant, not directed target cell apoptosis. The experimental setup of this study is displayed in D and E. The natural killer cell line NK92 was treated with 2.2% sevoflurane for 2 hr. In most experiments, NK92 cells were co-incubated with leukemia cells (K562) as a target cell line for varying durations (between 0 and 48 hr); for the perforin measurements in the NK-cell supernatant and NK-cell cytoplasm, no co-incubation was performed.

NK = natural killer; IL-6 = interleukin-6; IFN-γ = interferon-gamma; TNF-α = tumour necrosis factor-alpha; mRNA = messenger RNA

In the degranulation process, lytic granules of the NK cell fuse with the cell membrane of the target cell and release perforin and granzyme, which then initiate apoptosis of the target cell.10–12 Degranulation is considered the mainstay of tumour growth control, and metastasis from solid tumours is 10–100-fold less effective in perforin-deficient mice.13

Of note, two cytokine-mediated death receptor signalling pathways have been described to play a significant role in this process. Both ligands belong to the tumour necrosis factor (TNF) ligand superfamily and activate apoptosis intracellularly.14 Natural killer cells release cytokines such as interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) and tumour necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α). Even though degranulation and the cytokine-dependent death receptor pathways work independently, tumour cell elimination is most effective when both mechanisms are activated.15

Extracellular vesicles are small vesicles released by most cells.16 They are characterized by their origin, size, and shape. A subtype of EVs released by NK cells, called exosomes, is particularly interesting for tumour cell elimination.17 Exosomes from NK cells may carry regulatory micro ribonucleic acids (RNAs),18 killer proteins such as perforin and granzyme, as well as IFN-γ,19,20 which induce apoptotic cell death in cancer cells upon co-incubation.9,20

For general anesthesia, cancer surgery, or diagnostic procedures, anesthesiologists use inhalational or intravenous anesthetics, such as sevoflurane or propofol. Recent clinical studies suggest that volatile anesthetics might negatively impact cancer outcomes.21,22 A possible factor could be the impairment of NK cell activity;23 nevertheless, the exact mechanisms have not yet been explored in detail. Therefore, we sought to determine the effects of sevoflurane on NK-cell-induced apoptosis of malignant tumour cells, focusing on the degranulation-, cytokine-, and EV-induced cell death pathways. We hypothesized that sevoflurane-pretreated NK cells would impair apoptosis of leukemia target cells.

Methods

Cell culture

We conducted an in vitro laboratory study, using the interleukin (IL) 2-dependent NK cell line NK92 (CRL-2407; ATCC Manassas, VA, USA) and the leukemia cell line K562 (ATCC, CCL 243) as effector and target cells, respectively. Both cell lines were cultured in media containing Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; ATCC), and 1 U·mL−1 penicillin and 100 mM·mL−1 streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For NK92 cells, 100 U·mL−1 IL-2, an immunostimulatory cytokine for NK cells, was added to the cell culture medium (PeproTech, Rocky Hill, NJ, USA).

Primary NK cells

We obtained primary NK cells by collecting blood from 10 healthy volunteers who provided written informed consent. The study was conducted at the University of Zurich, Switzerland, in accordance with good laboratory practice, the Declaration of Helsinki, and legal and institutional standards. Inclusion criteria were age ≥ 18 years and no known major diseases. Ethical approval for this study (Cantonal Ethical Committee [CEC] No. 2016-01140) was provided by the Cantonal Ethical Committee of Zurich, Switzerland (Chairperson, Prof. P. Meier Abt) on 21 November 2016.

Magnetic purification of NK cells was performed using the MACSxpress® Whole Blood NK Cell Isolation Kit, human (Miltenyi Biotech, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany). Cells were isolated according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Exposure to sevoflurane

We exposed NK92 cells to one minimum alveolar concentration (MAC) sevoflurane (= 2.2 vol %) in 5% CO2, 21% O2, and 74% N2 in an airtight chamber for 2 hr in accordance with a previous study.24 A fan was placed inside the chamber to ensure the homogeneity of the gas. The anesthetic chamber and the control plate were placed in a cell culture incubator at 37 °C.

For co-incubation experiments, we added target cells after sevoflurane pre-incubation of the effector cells. An overview of the basic experimental setup is given in Fig. 1D and E. The three different mechanisms of tumour defense by NK cells exhibit different kinetics; therefore, the experiments had different exposure times. Cytotoxicity is a fast defense mechanism. Previous research has shown a maximal effect as early as after 2–3 hr of co-incubation with target cells,25 but later time points up to 72 hr have also been investigated.26 Nevertheless, cytokine production, the essential requirement for triggering the cytokine-mediated death receptor pathway, is much slower. Cytokine production by NK cells peaks after 6 hr for many cytokines, including TNF-α and IFN-γ.27 For the exosome measurements, we empirically decided to expose NK92 cells for 24 and 48 hr and primary NK cells for 48 hr without any co-incubation, which revealed differences that aligned with our study. Whether 48 hr is the time point with the most pronounced difference is unclear.

Flow cytometry

For up to 48 hr, we co-incubated 2 × 105 NK92 cells (effector cells) with or without sevoflurane exposure with K562 target cells in a 1:1 ratio in RPMI 1640 Medium containing 10% FBS in a 96-well plate. We performed flow cytometry to quantify live NK and tumour cells as well as tumour cells undergoing early and late apoptosis and necrosis. The Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM) eAppendix describes experimental steps.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction analysis

It is well known that the proinflammatory cytokines IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-6 are involved in the NK cell death pathway. Therefore, we co-incubated NK92 cells, with or without sevoflurane treatment, with K562 cells in RPMI 1640 Medium plus 10% FBS for 6 hr. We chose a ratio of 1:1 (1 × 106 cells of each). Ribonucleic acid isolation and real-time reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction (rt-PCR) are described previously28 as well as in the ESM eAppendix.

Extracellular vesicle characterization

We cultured 6 × 106 NK92 cells in 10 mL of double-filtered RPMI 1640 Medium containing 10% FBS. Cells were exposed to sevoflurane for 2 hr. Thereafter, they were grown for another 46 hr under standard conditions, and subsequently, EVs were isolated and quantified. Control cells were treated simultaneously without sevoflurane exposure. For EV isolation, we used a differential centrifugation protocol:29 300 × g for 5 min, followed by 1,500 × g for 3 min, 3,000 × g for 20 min, and finally at 100,000 × g for 1 hr (at 4 °C). The protocol of EV isolation has been validated previously.30–32 The samples were then either resuspended in double-filtered phosphate-buffered saline for using a Nanosight NS300 particle analysis (Malvern Panalytical Ltd, Westborough, MA, USA) or in radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (Boston Bioproducts, Ashland, MA, USA) for analysis by Western blot.

For particle analysis, we measured each sample in triplicate at a dilution of 1:260.

For Western blot, lysed samples were diluted with b-mercaptoethanol and Laemmli buffer (both, Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc., Hercules, CA, USA) at a ratio of 1:4 and heated to 95 °C for 10 min. We loaded 20 mL of each sample on a 4–12 % gradient of sodium dodecyl sulphate–polyacrylamide for gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) protein separation. After that, proteins were transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane. We used Ponceau red staining to assess total protein loading. After washing with tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween20 (TBST), membranes were blocked with 5% milk (Bio-Rad Laboratories) for 1 hr, followed by incubation with primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. We used the following primary antibodies: lysosomal-associated membrane protein-1 (LAMP-1) (dilution 1:500), perforin (dilution 1:300), and glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) (dilution 1:1000) (all from Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Inc., Dallas, TX, USA). The ESM eAppendix indicates details about the antibodies used. The endosomal origin of the vesicles is indicated by LAMP-1. It is mainly expressed on the surface of small vesicles, namely exosomes.32 After washing with TBST, incubation with a corresponding secondary horseradish peroxidase-coupled antibody was performed (Kirkegaard & Perry Laboratories, Inc. [KPL], Gaithersburg, MD, USA). Chemiluminescent signal was measured using the supersignal chemiluminescent substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and an Odyssey Fc imager (LI-COR, Inc., Lincoln, NE, USA). Densitometry was performed using ImageJ V 2.0 (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

NK92 cells (6 × 106) were seeded in cell culture dishes (Corning Incorporated, Corning, NY, USA) using 10 mL double-filtered RPMI Medium containing 10% FBS. We incubated the control group (“air”) at 37 °C for 24 and 48 hr, while the sevoflurane group was exposed to sevoflurane for 2 hr before incubation for another 22 and 46 hr under standard conditions as described above. After 24 and 48 hr, we harvested the samples and centrifuged them at 200 g for 3 min. The ESM eAppendix contains a description of further steps.

Statistical analyses

Data are presented as mean (standard deviation [SD]). We analyzed the time courses of tumour cell apoptosis and NK cell viability by two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). We used Bonferroni’s post hoc test to correct for multiple comparisons. The number of corrections is indicated in the figure legends. Unpaired Student t tests analyzed polymerase chain reaction (PCR), Western blot, and Nanosight results. Data from the enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) experiments were normalized to the air group for each experiment. We removed outliers according to Grubbs’ outlier analysis with an alpha value of 0.05.

For Fig. 5A–D, we used a mixed linear model to investigate the differences between air and sevoflurane. These models contained a fixed effect for the group comparison and a random effect for the respective experiment. It used robust Design Adaptive Scale estimates, and we assumed independent covariance. Note that we used all values (including the outliers) because robust estimation should be able to handle them. In contrast, we performed a one-sample t test to test whether the sevoflurane values differ from 1.

We used Prism version 8 (GraphPad, La Jolla, CA, USA) for data analysis. The number of independent experiments is indicated in the figure legends.

Results

NK92 viability was not affected by sevoflurane exposure

As shown in Fig. 2A–B, viable NK92 cells (CD56+ , Annexin V [AV]− , FVS [Fixable Viability Stain] 660−) were not affected by sevoflurane treatment. In the short incubation phase, cell viability remained above 81% (4%) in all groups. No difference between air and sevoflurane exposure was observed (Fig. 2A). Mean (SD) viability was comparable between the two groups with 54% (6%) (air) and 52% (3%) (sevoflurane) live cells at 24 hr and 25% (2%) (air) and 27% (3%) (sevoflurane) at 48 hr, respectively (Fig. 2B).

Sevoflurane did not affect natural killer cell viability. Live NK92 cells are defined as Annexin V− and FVS660− cells, determined by flow cytometry analysis. NK92 cells were exposed for 2 hr to sevoflurane (or to air only), followed by a short incubation phase of 1, 2, 4, and 6 hr (A), and a long one of 24 and 48 hr (B). All measurements in Fig. 2 were analyzed using two-way analysis of variance, and multiple comparisons were corrected using the Bonferroni method (four comparisons in A and three comparisons in B). Values represent means (standard deviations). Six independent experiments were included in this analysis.

FVS = Fixable Viability Stain; NK = natural killer; Sevo = sevoflurane

Sevoflurane’s effect on apoptosis and necrosis rate of target tumour cells

Early apoptotic (CD56− , AV+ , FVS660−), late apoptotic (CD56− , AV+ , FVS660+), and necrotic K562 cells (CD56− , AV− , FVS660+) were quantified after short- and long-term exposure to sevoflurane (or air)-exposed NK cells. Sevoflurane pretreatment of NK92 cells did not affect the early apoptosis of target cells between 1 and 6 hr of co-incubation (Fig. 3A). After 24 hr of co-incubation, the mean (SD) early apoptotic rate of tumour cells was 28% (4%) in the air group compared with 18% (6%) in the sevoflurane group (P = 0.02), corresponding to a 36% reduction (Fig. 3B). Late apoptosis (Fig. 3C and D) increased from 1 hr, which was 3% (1%), to 9% (4%) at 6 hr, while in the long-term experiments, the late apoptosis rate was in the range of 1–2%. We observed no statistically significant effect on necrosis of target cells upon sevoflurane treatment (Fig. 3E, F).

Natural killer cells exposed to sevoflurane were less efficient in inducing (early) apoptosis in K562 tumour cells. NK92 cells were pretreated with sevoflurane or without 2.2% sevoflurane (air) and co-exposed to K562 tumour cells for a short incubation phase of 1, 2, 4, and 6 hr (A, C, E) and a long one of 24 and 48 hr (B, D, F). The fraction of early (A and B), late apoptotic (C and D), and necrotic (E and F) tumour cells was determined by flow cytometry. Early apoptotic tumour cells were defined as CD56− , AV+ , FVS 660− ; late apoptotic tumour cells as CD56− , AV+ , FVS 660+ ; and necrotic tumour cells as CD56− , AV− , FVS 660+ . All measurements in Fig. 3 were analyzed using two-way analysis of variance, and multiple comparisons were corrected using the Bonferroni method (four comparisons in A, C, and E and three comparisons in B, D, and F). Values represent means (standard deviations). Six independent experiments were included in this analysis.

Sevoflurane did not significantly affect degranulation or cytokine production in NK92 cells

The release of NK cell granules, confirmed by positive CD107a staining,10,33 was not significantly affected by sevoflurane exposure of NK cells and subsequent incubation with tumour cells (Fig. 4A). Similarly, sevoflurane exposure did not have a statistically significant effect on the messenger RNA (mRNA) levels of the proinflammatory cytokines IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-6 involved in the death pathway when NK cells were exposed to sevoflurane and subsequently incubated with tumour cells (Fig. 4B–D).

Sevoflurane did not affect the size or concentration of exosomes

A nanotrack analysis defining the size and concentration of released EVs showed that, under the influence of sevoflurane, neither the distribution of the size nor the number of particles changed significantly (Fig. 4E, F). We used Lysosomal-associated membrane protein-1 to determine EV origin (ESM eFigure).

Sevoflurane reduced perforin production and secretion by NK92 cells

Perforin protein concentration was determined in lysed NK92 cells and in their supernatant. Upon a 2-hr sevoflurane exposure of NK92, the coefficients for perforin cell concentration in the sevoflurane group vs air were −0.04 at 24 hr (P = 0.13; Fig. 5A) and −0.16 for 48-hr incubation (P < 0.001; Fig. 5C). For the perforin concentration in the supernatant, the following results were calculated: −0.05 at 24 hr (P = 0.05; Fig 5B) and −0.16 at 48 hr (P < 0.001, Fig. 5D).

Sevoflurane’s effects on perforin production and secretion in primary natural killer cells

Perforin protein concentration in lysed primary NK cells was similar in cells exposed to sevoflurane in comparison to air (coefficient, 0.09; P = 0.47; Fig. 5E). In the cell supernatant, the coefficient for sevoflurane was −0.22 (P = 0.049; Fig. 5F).

Discussion

Significant progress in cancer treatment has been achieved in recent years, and yet, cancer remains the second leading cause of death worldwide.34 Therefore, it is crucial to optimize each individual component of therapy, including the perioperative period and the time of anesthesia during surgery.1 Results of our study show that the widely used volatile anesthetic sevoflurane transiently moderately impairs NK-mediated apoptosis of the leukemia target cells and reduces the production and secretion of perforin protein synthesized by NK cells in the NK92 cell line.

In 1989, accelerated progression of metastases was described in animals exposed to the volatile anesthetic halothane or to nitrous oxide.35 Retrospective data analyses support basic research studies, which indicate a lower survival in patients undergoing cancer surgery who received sevoflurane-based vs intravenous anesthesia.21,36 Of note, the impact of volatile anesthetics is cancer-type-dependent.37–39

The immune system has an enormous impact on cancer. Essential components of this defense system include regulatory T-cells, cytotoxic T-cells, and NK cells. According to previous reports, regulatory T-cells of patients with cancer are not significantly modified by intravenous or volatile anesthetics.40 Likewise, the number and apoptosis rate of cytotoxic T-cells was unaffected by the type of anesthetic used in patients with cancer.41

Natural killer cells play a crucial role in cancer cell and metastasis defense.42 This is supported by the fact that a low perioperative level of NK cell activity results in higher cancer recurrence.43 Nevertheless, the number and apoptosis rate of NK cells was not affected by sevoflurane compared with propofol in patients with cancer.41 If volatile anesthetics impact NK-cell-mediated tumour defense, it is thus more likely to be linked to NK cell function than to NK cell count. Consistent with this hypothesis, a clinical trial from our group reported a slight but not significant reduction in apoptotic cancer cells upon incubation with NK cells isolated from patients with breast cancer who were anesthetized with sevoflurane.44

Our current in vitro trial aimed to systematically investigate whether and how the volatile anesthetic sevoflurane affects NK-cell-mediated tumour cell defense in NK92 cells. The reduction in viable NK upon co-exposure to tumour cells was interpreted as the dynamics of NK-cell–tumour-cell interactions, where activation-induced cell death and tumour-mediated immune suppression contribute to reduced viability over time. We obseverved no statistically significant changes in degranulation, cytokine transcription, quantity, or the size of EVs in NK92 cells exposed to sevoflurane. These findings did not explain the reduction in apoptosis. As a next step in exploring the sevoflurane-induced reduction of tumour cells undergoing early apoptosis, we performed quantification of perforin as a cytolytic factor, which is involved in degranulation and in EV-mediated tumour defense. The importance of perforin in the cancer setting has been suggested previously.45 Perforin-deficient mice show a 10–100-fold lower tumour cell elimination rate than wild-type animals.45 The clinical impact of anesthetic agents on cancer survival remains controversial. Nevertheless, a recent systematic review suggests that intravenous anesthesia is generally associated with better overall survival compared with volatile agent-based anesthesia.46

Proinflammatory cytokines can stimulate perforin transcription.47 Although we did not detect altered cytokine mRNA levels, it is still conceivable that sevoflurane-mediated perforin reduction might, at least partially, be linked to an attenuation of inflammation. Sevoflurane is an efficient immune modulator that can reduce the release of proinflammatory cytokines after endotoxin stimulation in vitro and in vivo.48,49 Interestingly, intracellular perforin concentration in primary NK cells was not affected by sevoflurane. Nevertheless, perforin release from primary NK cells was reduced by sevoflurane by 12%, thus to a similar extent as in NK92 cells.

As with all experimental protocols, this study has strengths and limitations. The advantage of this project is its high reproducibility and systematic investigation of the individual points of NK92-cell–cancer-cell interaction. High reproducibility, however, comes with high standardization, which prevents generalizability. The observed sevoflurane-induced reduction in intracellular and released perforin did not consistently translate into differences in early or late apoptosis across all time points, with significance observed only at 24 hr. This may reflect complex, time-dependent interactions between NK cells and tumour cells in vitro, including variability in tumour cell susceptibility or compensatory mechanisms that mitigate the effects of reduced perforin over time. Finally, the observed sevoflurane-induced differences are rather small, and the clinical impact remains questionable. Conducting in vitro studies has the advantage that it is a reductionist approach. Comparison with a control setting allows assessing effects avoiding confounding factors. Nevertheless, in vitro studies do not allow definition of clinical consequences as the in vivo environment is crucial. Further studies are needed to elucidate the precise kinetics of perforin-mediated apoptosis in this model system.

As reported by others, observations may not be uniformly transferred to all cancer types50 since different cancer cells are genetically and biologically diverse.51 The described NK mechanism has been shown in the NK92 and at least in part in NK cells extracted from healthy volunteer blood. Our primary NK cell data show a rather high variability in their sevoflurane response and may present with different functional characteristics.52 Further analysis of the phenotype of all primary NK cells of the different donors might help find explanations for this variability. Phenotype and genotype profiling is, however, beyond the scope of this article. Moreover, other crucial aspects of this study do not fully reflect a clinical scenario. All in vitro studies neglect the interaction of the tumour with the entire immune system, the entire organism, and the clinical situation. Additionally, the inflammatory response was assessed at the mRNA rather than the protein level, which does not fully capture the functional output of secreted cytokines in mediating tumour cell death. For example, perioperative stress and the almost indispensable use of opioids after surgery impact NK activity and tumour biology.23,53 The multitude of interacting factors and the resulting variability make it difficult to explain different disease courses or molecular differences in clinical studies upon modifying one element in a feasible patient cohort.

A positive aspect of the present study is that results are supported by a previous investigation in which NK cells were isolated in patients undergoing breast cancer surgery before and after anesthesia and ex vivo exposure to tumour cells.23 Apoptosis in target cancer cells was lower when patients, and NK cells, were exposed to sevoflurane plus opioids compared with propofol exposure with paravertebral block.

Conclusions

This study shows that sevoflurane exposure may transiently impair tumour cell defense by moderately reducing NK-cell-mediated target cell apoptosis after 24 hr in an NK92 cell line. This effect is likely associated with the observed decrease in perforin content. While these findings suggest that the use of volatile anesthetics could be unfavourable in cancer settings, we are fully aware of the fact that further studies are necessary to confirm and expand upon these results.
