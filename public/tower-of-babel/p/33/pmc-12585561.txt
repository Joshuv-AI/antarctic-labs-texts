Comment on ‘COVID-19 vaccine wastage in private and public healthcare facilities in KwaZulu-Natal, South Africa’
Authors: Amnuay Kleebayoon, Viroj Wiwanitkit
Published: 2025

Dear Editor, we hereby comment on the publication of ‘COVID-19 vaccine wastage in private and public healthcare facilities in KwaZulu-Natal, South Africa’.1 The study on COVID-19 vaccine waste in KwaZulu-Natal (KZN) sheds light on the issues of managing vaccines in practice. However, there are several content and methodological shortcomings that could be rectified. First, while the claimed loss rates for Pfizer and Janssen vaccines appear to be modest, they may not reflect the complete picture of wastage across all immunization locations, particularly in rural or disadvantaged areas. While we acknowledge that the study's methodology is sound, a more detailed investigation within KZN that includes data from different demographics and geographic areas would provide a better understanding of waste dynamics. It could provide a deeper understanding of waste dynamics in these specific contents.

Furthermore, the study's reliance on self-reported data from immunization leads may introduce bias, because individuals reporting may be motivated to present their management tactics in a positive light. However, it is acknowledged during phase two, some of the findings from phase one were further investigated and subjected to sufficient review, which may help mitigate potential biases. To strengthen the findings, future study could consider including firsthand inspections and audits of vaccine storage and handling techniques at various sites. This could assist in detecting differences between claimed tactics and actual behaviors on the ground, resulting in a more accurate picture of vaccination waste.

In addition to quantifying waste, the investigation should look into the underlying causes of the observed waste. While expiry was mentioned as the primary reason, logistical obstacles, vaccine reluctance and staffing issues could all play a part. It is noted that some of these factors, including logistical challenges and vaccine reluctance, have already been reported by Manickum and Mathibe in the original manuscript, and we acknowledge these shortcomings; we hereby restate them and add important points for further consideration. Qualitative interviews with healthcare workers involved in the immunization process could provide significant insights into these barriers and help guide focused actions.

In the future, a more comprehensive strategy could include creating and testing innovative waste reduction solutions, such as dynamic inventory management systems that alter based on real-time vaccination rates and demographic information. Furthermore, incorporating community engagement initiatives to combat vaccination reluctance may be critical, as reducing demand-related waste can have a major impact on overall waste rates. Exploring technology-driven solutions, such as mobile apps for tracking and controlling vaccine inventory, may also improve the efficacy of waste-minimization efforts, resulting in better resource usage in vaccination campaigns.
