Read the Data for Diverse Truths—and Quantitative Myocardial Perfusion Versus Angiography
Authors: Kenneth Lance Gould, Nils P Johnson
Published: 2025

In this issue of the Journal of the American Heart Association (JAHA), Maaniitty et al report their important, usual, meticulous comparison of their hybrid coronary computed tomographic angiography (CCTA)/positron emission tomography (PET) protocol versus anatomic assessment of coronary artery disease (CAD) by CCTA alone. 1 For this study, the CCTA comparator to hybrid CCTA/PET is artificial intelligence quantitative computed tomography (AI‐QCT) for predicting the composite end point of death, myocardial infarction (MI), or unstable angina compared with hybrid CCTA/PET with a median follow‐up of 7 years.

After excluding patients with known CAD, prior MI, prior revascularization, and nonischemic cardiomyopathy or mixed pathology, 2411 symptomatic patients underwent CCTA for CAD. Patients with visual diameter stenosis ≥50% underwent adenosine stress PET perfusion imaging with oxygen‐15 water. Binary classification of “ischemia” was defined as visual diameter stenosis ≥50% on CCTA and stress perfusion <2.3 mL/min per g. AI‐QCT analyzed offline by its supplier was considered nonevaluable if image quality was poor or a stent was present (given exclusion of patients with prior revascularization, there were probably no stents).

A total of 1772 of 2411 patient (73.5%) had both evaluable CCTA and successful PET imaging. Overall, 639 patients were missing 1 or both scans (26.5% of 2441) and thus not evaluable; 391 patients (17% of 2271 with CCTA imaging) were nonevaluable by AI‐QCT and excluded from the per‐protocol analysis. A total of 661 patients (37% of 1772) had CCTA diameter stenosis ≥50% leading to PET perfusion imaging, of which 331 (19% of 1772) had normal perfusion (≥2.3 mL/min per g) and 331 (19% of 1772) had abnormal (perfusion <2.3 mL/min per g). The authors conclude that “the AI‐QCT algorithm demonstrated long‐term prognostic value comparable to hybrid CCTA/PET perfusion imaging in suspected CAD.”

Major Points for Interpreting the Data

Interpreting the results and large issues they raise requires emphasizing 3 major points for the reader here. First, the criteria of “abnormal” or “ischemia” for indicating intervention for a stenosis on CCTA is stress myocardial perfusion in mL/min per g by PET using oxygen‐15 water. Second, the study population is highly selected by excluding patients most commonly seen by a busy invasive cardiology service wherein >90% of patients have known CAD, prior MI, prior revascularization, coronary calcification, nonischemic cardiomyopathy, renal failure, microvascular disease, or commonly a mix of all of these as detailed below. Third, analysis per protocol excludes 17% due to nonevaluable CCTA images even in the highly selected, pristine population of the current report ideally suited for CCTA by excluding patients with these diagnosis.

Results in Main Text Compared With the Supplement

Results in the supplement compared with the main text are reproduced in our Figure 1. The per‐protocol analysis that excluded the nonevaluable AI‐QCT images showed reasonable agreement between hybrid CCTA/PET and AI‐QCT (Figure 1A) in the main text with Cohen’s κ 0.61, χ2 P<0.001. However, by intention‐to‐diagnose analysis reported in their supplemental material and in our Figure 1B, agreement was substantially less with Cohen’s κ 0.39, χ2 P<0.001. Figure 1A from the main text showing analysis per protocol contrasts sharply with our Figure 1B from the supplemental material with the real‐world intention‐to‐treat protocol faced in clinical practice. The AI‐QCT predictions of “ischemia” showed major discordances in 27% (22% plus 5%) using the hybrid CCTA/PET perfusion as the reference standard. Of these discordant AI‐QCT cases, 22% were falsely positive for ischemia, thereby potentially incurring unnecessary interventions.

The authors carefully acknowledge this limitation, stating, “Intention‐to‐diagnose analysis demonstrated superior discriminative ability of a model with clinical variables and hybrid CCTA/PET (C‐index 0.727 [95% CI, 0.696–0.759]) as compared with the model including clinical variables and AI‐QCTischemia (C‐index 0.714 [95% CI, 0.683–0.745]; P=0.022).” They concluded further that hybrid CCTA/PET predicted death better than AI‐QCT but AI‐QCT predicted MI or unstable angina better than hybrid CCTA/PET.

To emphasize their scientific viewpoint, the authors further qualify their above conclusions in the discussion as follows, “However, the anatomical degree of coronary artery stenosis on CCTA does not accurately predict myocardial ischemia, which is an important criterion when considering myocardial revascularization.” And further, “In the current study using real‐world CCTA data, we found that AI‐QCTischemia evaluation was feasible in 89% of the major coronary territories, resulting in complete evaluation in 83% of the patients. For comparison, a previous intention‐to‐diagnose analysis of FFRCT [fractional flow reserve computed tomography] demonstrated successful evaluation in 83% of major coronary arteries, resulting in complete (ie, 3‐vessel) FFRCT assessment in 75% of patients.” 2

Diverse Truths on Close Reading of Text and Supplement

Separate from the conclusions, the meticulous data in the text and appendix of this article support 4 diverse truths depending on viewpoints by readers. For the AI aficionado and commercial distributor of AI‐QCT, the conclusions are definite and favorable for AI‐QCT. For the anatomically oriented invasive cardiologist, the conclusions support AI‐QCT or the CCTA component of the hybrid CCTA/PET by the per‐protocol analysis in the main text.

However, reading the text carefully and the supplement might give pause to a conscientious invasive cardiologist due to the large discordance in analysis by intention to diagnose shown in our Figure 1A and 1B. Similar pause might derive from the main text conclusion for AI‐QCT predicting MI or unstable angina versus PET predicting death. Does the invasive cardiologist have to choose a preselected end point of death or MI/unstable angina before deciding on which technology to use before doing the patient’s study? Finally, in the real‐world practice of a busy invasive cardiology service, one‐third to 90% of patients with symptoms have known CAD, coronary calcification, prior MI, stents, heart failure, nonischemic cardiomyopathy, or a mixture of all of these conditions requiring quantitative perfusion for management that angiographic anatomy cannot provide 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 as acknowledged by the current authors.

For the clinical coronary physiologist, the analysis by intention to diagnose relegated to the supplement provides the definitive evidence for real‐world cardiology practice even for this highly selected population without known CAD, prior MI, procedures, or cardiomyopathy. Even with such a pristine population with maximum evaluability of CCTA, myocardial perfusion efficiently identified ischemia for potential interventions in just 19% with reduced stress perfusion by definition of the protocol. Since the anatomic data provided no additional definitive prognostic guidance for revascularization, quantitative stress perfusion uniquely defined “ischemia” in the protocol for any CCTA stenosis. To the clinical coronary pathophysiologist, the data indicate the most basic truth that quantitative myocardial perfusion supersedes angiographic anatomy for indicating intervention even in this highly selected population ideally suitable for CCTA.

Why Diverse Truths From the Same Data?

The authors clearly report excellent data showing and emphasizing in their discussion the superiority of quantitative myocardial perfusion over all CCTA metrics, AI‐QCT here, 1 and FFRCT in previous reports 2 by conclusions reflecting per‐protocol analysis while relegating the true, correct real‐world intention‐to‐diagnose analysis as faced in clinical practice to the supplement 1 or secondary status in the text 2 . Why generic flat conclusions that AI‐QCT, and previously FFRCT, are equivalent to their hybrid CCTA/PET “gold standard” protocol?

The careful detailed acquisition and analysis of their excellent data for this 1 and prior 2 CCTA comparators reflect the authors’ scientific commitment to pioneering development of CCTA anatomy combined with quantitative myocardial perfusion by PET for managing heart disease. While reporting their complete excellent data, the authors commonly emphasize per‐protocol analysis to make favorable conclusion for AI‐QCT 1 and FFRCT 2 as equal to their own perfusion‐driven standard determinant of ischemia for potential intervention. This favorable emphasis on comparator CCTA technology while reporting good scientific data clearly documenting the superiority of hybrid CCTA/PET myocardial perfusion over pure CCTA may reflect influence of the funding needed for their meticulous research not otherwise feasible. In view of this near‐universal dilemma in academic cardiology, such data selective emphasis or “formatting conclusions” favorable to the commercial funder while publishing data to the contrary may reflect a calculated solution to this dilemma.

In any case, the meticulous, comprehensive data reported here and in prior reports by these established leaders of hybrid CCTA/PET for assessing CAD reveal diverse truths for anatomic–physiologic assessment of CAD but requires reading the data in the appendix as well as the qualifications in the text, not just the conclusion.

Why an Important Article in the Context of Current Literature?

This article is an important study for showing that quantitative myocardial perfusion surpasses all anatomic metrics of severity for predicting outcomes to potentially guide intervention, even for this highly selected population ideally suited for CCTA without confounding known CAD, prior MI, revascularization, stents, or cardiomyopathy that were excluded from this study.

As the next logical step from this article to an extended literature, now compare the superiority of quantitative myocardial perfusion over angiographic anatomy for this highly selected pristine population ideal for CCTA to the more common population of a busy invasive cardiology service illustrated in our Figure 2. For a real‐world invasive cardiology service, up to 98% of all patients referred for PET have ≥1 of the conditions excluded in the current report. In large nonrandomized cohorts undergoing routine diagnostic quantitative PET using Rb‐82 (Bracco Diagnostics Inc, NJ; Figure 2A), 37% of cases (2553/6979) had prior procedures with recurrent symptoms and complex mixed pathology for which quantitative perfusion by PET provides definitive guidance for the initial 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 or additional interventions 8 that significantly reduce death compared with no revascularization for comparable severity. In the randomized CENTURY (Randomized Trial of Comprehensive Lifestyle Modifications, Optimal Pharmacological Treatment, and PET Imaging for Detection and Management of Stable Coronary Artery Disease) trial, comprehensive integrated optimal lifestyle–medical treatment with revascularization reserved for severely reduced coronary flow capacity by PET significantly reduced death by 42%, death or MI by 37%, and revascularization by 35% compared with standard care in chronic CAD 8 —superior outcomes with fewer but physiologically necessary revascularizations.

Even including these diverse complex heart problems imaging all comers, our failure to acquire correct quantitative perfusion using Rb‐82 with our validated HeartSee perfusion model (Food and Drug Administration 510K‐231 731) is <1%, due most commonly to subclavian vein occlusion with delayed arterial input phase or scanner hardware failure. This reliability for all severities and mixed heart pathologies contrasts with 17% failure when acquiring CCTA evaluable images in the highly selected study population ideally sited for CCTA in the current report excluding these conditions. Our typical population of an invasive cardiology service likely precludes the great majority of CCTA images as nonevaluable for AI‐QCT or FFRCT. Even invasive coronary angiography is inadequate for simulating coronary perfusion or pressure with classical fluid dynamic quantitative analysis originally first reported 46 years ago by the senior author using ECG‐triggered, diastolic, high resolution, coronary angiogram by high‐resolution cut film cassette with higher resolution than current coronary techniques. 10 , 11

Of course, there are several reasons. Angiographic resolution cannot resolve the radius of a 3‐ to 4‐mm diameter coronary artery to predict flow dependent on radius to the fourth power that sets limits of anatomic anatomy simulating coronary flow, perfusion, or pressure. 12 This limitation is compounded by stenosis plus diffuse CAD, 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 endothelial dysfunction and microvascular disease 5 , 6 and nonischemic cardiomyopathy plus CAD 9 common in most invasive or academic cardiac centers. Consequently, angiographic‐guided revascularization has failed to reduce composite death and MI in every randomized trial to date, lending credence to this important article and Editorial view for quantitative myocardial perfusion over angiographic anatomy.

With their consistent data on superiority of quantitative myocardial perfusion over CCTA anatomy in this and prior articles, 1 , 2 the author’s ongoing leadership of hybrid CCTA/PET may be due to sufficient cost and complexity of cardiac PET with oxygen‐15 water requiring complex cyclotron operations, its scheduling interfaced with patient scheduling for precise timing due to short half‐life such that a fast, more efficient, less costly CCTA is needed to select only those patients needing definitive quantitative perfusion for guiding intervention in a busy academic cardiac center. In our high‐volume cardiac referral center, the Rb‐82 generator provides rapid, efficient, throughput with definitive guidance for interventions (or not) for all complex heart disease with decreasing cost as volume increases. CCTA is not needed or useful with cardiac PET using Rb‐82, would substantially reduce our rapid throughput, interfere with rapid reporting needed for high‐risk inpatients, incur mostly nonevaluable CCTA due to prevalent diffuse coronary calcification or stents and pose some risks for patients with complex heart disease and common renal dysfunction.

The current important article by pioneering leaders in hybrid CCTA/PET support quantitative myocardial perfusion by PET as primary risk stratification for coronary interventions in chronic CAD even in a pristine population without known CAD ideally suitable for CCTA. In this paradigm documented by a substantial literature, quantitative myocardial perfusion determines whether revascularization is needed (or not) for single stenosis or complex diffuse CAD or mixed cardiac pathology whereas the angiogram determines how it can be done if indicated by PET. For a high case volume over a wide range of heart disease in a busy referral academic center, quantitative PET perfusion provides an efficient, quick, reliable, reproducible quantitative guide to interventions in chronic heart disease.
