Posttraumatic stress symptoms in trauma-exposed plateau children and adolescents: associations with resilience and prosocial behaviour
Authors: Fei Xiao, Buzohre Eli, Yaru Chen, Cong Gao, Pu Gong, Yijin Huang, Meixuan Lv, Zhengkui Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study assessed posttraumatic stress symptom (PTSS) severity and associated factors among trauma-exposed children and adolescents on the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau, focusing on prosocial behaviour and resilience and examining the association of prosocial behaviour with PTSS via resilience.MethodA cross-sectional study involved 2,322 trauma-exposed children and adolescents aged 10–19 years, with boys comprising 49.1%. PTSS, prosocial behaviour, and resilience were measured via three self-report questionnaires: the Child Post-Traumatic Stress Symptom Scale (CPSS), the Prosocial Behaviour Subscale of the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ), and the short version of the Connor–Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC), respectively. Descriptive statistics and correlation analyses were used to summarise participants’ characteristics and the bivariate associations between variables, and a mediation model with bootstrapped confidence intervals was estimated to test the indirect association of prosocial behaviour with PTSS via resilience.ResultsOverall, 40.4% of trauma-exposed children and adolescents scored at or above the CPSS cut-off (≥ 16) and thus screened positive for probable posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD; 95% CI [38.4%, 42.4%]). Prosocial behaviour was positively associated with resilience and negatively associated with PTSS, whereas resilience was negatively associated with PTSS (r = 0.53, − 0.30, and − 0.50, respectively, all p < 0.001). In the mediation model, prosocial behaviour was positively related to resilience (β = 0.52, SE = 0.02, 95% CI [0.48, 0.55]), and resilience was negatively associated with PTSS (β = − 0.50, SE = 0.02, 95% CI [− 0.55, − 0.46]), while the direct path from prosocial behaviour to PTSS was close to zero (β = − 0.02, SE = 0.02, 95% CI [− 0.07, 0.02]). The indirect effect of prosocial behaviour on PTSS via resilience was statistically significant (β = − 0.26, SE = 0.02, 95% CI [− 0.29, − 0.23]), accounting for most of the total association (β = − 0.28, SE = 0.02, 95% CI [− 0.32, − 0.24]).ConclusionsThe results highlight the high burden of PTSS among trauma-exposed children and adolescents in high plateau areas. Prosocial behaviour was associated with lower PTSS, with most of this association statistically accounted for by resilience. These findings are hypothesis-generating and suggest that prosocial behaviour and resilience may represent plausible targets for future longitudinal or experimental research, with practical implications requiring prospective confirmation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04475-9.

Background

Living on the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau entails sustained exposure to hypobaric hypoxia at elevations above 4,000 m, alongside frequent climate- and geology-related hazards. Over generations, long-term residents have developed physiological traits that may support oxygen delivery under low-oxygen conditions, such as more efficient ventilation and circulation as well as relatively lower haemoglobin levels and reduced arterial oxygenation [8]. Although these adaptations confer cardiopulmonary benefits, they have also been associated with elevated risks of depression and suicide, potentially due to diminished serotonin synthesis resulting from decreased oxygen saturation in the bloodstream [42, 44, 52, 105]. The Plateau is also prone to recurrent disasters such as earthquakes, snowstorms, landslides and flooding, which heighten the risk of trauma exposure [34, 97]. For example, the 2010 Yushu earthquake caused extensive casualties and long-term psychological distress. Moreover, economic underdevelopment and limited access to medical and psychosocial services further exacerbate vulnerability [61, 80]. Collectively, these environmental and structural stressors increase the likelihood of trauma exposure and the emergence of posttraumatic stress symptoms, including intrusive memories, hypervigilance, sleep difficulties, poor concentration, and emotional withdrawal [103].

Children and adolescents may be particularly vulnerable to both trauma exposure and subsequent PTSS. Across countries and study designs, reported prevalence of trauma exposure in youth varies widely, with estimates often falling between 31 and 68% [17, 51, 59, 68]. Meta-analytic evidence further suggests that roughly one in six trauma-exposed young people meet criteria for PTSD [2]. This heightened vulnerability is shaped by multiple factors: youth depend on adults for protection and may face a broad range of interpersonal and contextual adversities [18], while having fewer developed coping and emotion-regulation strategies for managing highly distressing experiences [35]. Moreover, ongoing neurobiological maturation and psychosocial development can embed adverse effects more deeply and increase the likelihood of longer-lasting harm [4, 19, 69]. Despite these risks, child and adolescent mental-health services remain scarce in many Tibetan areas, constraining access to timely assessment and support for trauma-exposed youth [102]. Clarifying the extent of PTSS in this context, and identifying factors linked to better adjustment, therefore has clear public-health relevance.

Prosocial behaviour has been proposed as a potentially protective attribute that may offset some adverse effects of traumatic exposure. It encompasses voluntary, other-oriented acts, such as helping, sharing, comforting, guiding, rescuing, and defending, directed toward others’ welfare [23, 26]. A substantial literature links prosocial engagement with positive adjustment in youth, including higher well-being and positive affect as well as better academic and social functioning [11, 54, 62, 87]. Prosocial behaviour is also associated with fewer internalizing difficulties (e.g., depression and anxiety) and lower externalizing problems (e.g., aggression) [29, 43, 72]. From a stress and coping perspective, prosocial behaviour may function as a socially embedded coping strategy that buffers the impact of adverse experiences. The “tend-and-befriend” theory proposes that affiliative and caregiving responses regulate stress physiology and promote safety through social connectedness [91], while communal coping theory emphasises shared appraisal and cooperative action as mechanisms for managing stress [65]. Consistent with these frameworks, prosocial behaviour has been found to promote emotional recovery from naturally occurring stressors and to accelerate physiological regulation following experimentally induced social stress [56, 81]. In more traumatic contexts, higher prosocial behaviour among war-exposed adolescents has been linked to fewer depressive symptoms, and higher baseline prosociality has predicted greater reductions in both depression and anxiety over time [27, 41]. Prosocial behaviour has also been shown to attenuate the association between childhood maltreatment and nonsuicidal self-injury among university students [108], to reduce involuntary trauma-related intrusions in adults [95], and to protect against secondary traumatic stress in professional caregiving settings [84]. Despite this accumulating evidence, the relationship between prosocial behaviour and posttraumatic stress symptoms in children and adolescents remains underexplored.

Resilience is another key factor implicated in positive adaptation following trauma. It is commonly conceptualised as a dynamic capacity shaped by person-environment interactions that supports adaptive functioning under adversity [16, 83]. Studies in children and adolescents indicate that higher resilience is associated with lower levels of depression and posttraumatic stress symptoms following neglect, abuse, and disasters [14, 76, 86]. Beyond its independent role, resilience may also intersect with prosocial behaviour in trauma-exposed youth. Warm-glow theory suggests that individuals may be motivated to help others because the act of giving itself generates an internal positive emotional reward [5], and the broaden-and-build framework proposes that positive affect is associated with the gradual accumulation of personal psychological resources, including resilience needed to meet life challenges [31, 93]. Empirical work has also shown that loving-kindness meditation is linked to increases in positive affect and the subsequent development of resources [32]. More recently, the updated upward spiral theory of lifestyle change further posits a reciprocal, mutually reinforcing association between engagement in health behaviours and positive affect over time, which may in turn support the development of psychosocial resources [33]. A growing body of research has demonstrated that prosocial behaviour is positively associated with resilience and contributes to better psychological states [37, 64, 71, 96]. However, little is known about whether this association also holds in trauma-exposed youth, and how prosocial behaviour may relate to posttraumatic stress symptoms through resilience in this context.

Against this backdrop, the present study addresses two questions: (a) what levels of PTSS are observed among trauma-exposed children and adolescents living on the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau, and (b) whether prosocial behaviour is associated with lower PTSS partly through higher resilience. We expected prosocial behaviour to be negatively associated with PTSS and hypothesised that resilience would account for part of this association.

Method

Procedure

This study draws on a large cross-sectional school survey conducted in Yushu (Qinghai–Tibet Plateau, China) that was coordinated by the Institute of Psychology, Chinese Academy of Sciences, together with the Institute of Science and Technology of the National Health and Family Planning Commission. The project aimed to assess mental health among children and adolescents living in the region. Fieldwork took place from September to November 2016. Prior to assessment, written informed consent was secured from participating students and their guardians, with approvals also obtained through school administration. Data collection was implemented in classroom settings using paper-and-pencil questionnaires. Research assistants were trained using a standardised protocol and subsequently oversaw administration to promote consistent procedures across schools. At the start of each session, assistants delivered the same study introduction and completion instructions, emphasised confidentiality, and clarified that participation was voluntary. Following questionnaire completion, students received a free medical examination. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Review Committee of the Institute of Psychology, Chinese Academy of Sciences (project identification code: H16014).

Participants

Data were collected in Yushu Tibetan Autonomous Prefecture on the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau, China. Schools were selected using a cluster-based approach supplemented by convenience recruitment. In the initial screening stage, 14,228 students (Grades 4–10) from 18 county-level primary and middle schools located in six counties were approached. Details of recruitment and screening are summarised in Fig. 1. Participants were retained for analysis if they (a) were aged 10–19 years, (b) reported exposure to at least one traumatic event according to DSM-referenced PTSD trauma criteria, and (c) provided sufficient data completeness, defined as ≥ 80% item-level responses on each study measure. Applying these eligibility requirements yielded a final analytic sample of 2,322 children and adolescents. Measures

General information

Sociodemographic information, including name, sex, age, religion, parental education level, and annual household income, was collected.

CPSS

Trauma exposure was screened using a single question asking whether participants had experienced or witnessed any scary, dangerous, or violent events [70]. To facilitate comprehension, we provided examples of such events: (a) natural disasters (such as earthquakes and mudslides); (b) severe accidents (such as car crashes, explosions, and terrorist attacks); (c) sudden death or acute illness of a person close to the participant; (d) a seriously painful or distressing medical situation; and (e) violence within the family, at school, or in public. Participants who answered “yes” then completed a brief open-ended item describing the event(s) and, if possible, the approximate time of occurrence. Those who endorsed at least one such event subsequently completed the Chinese version of the Child PTSD Symptom Scale (CPSS) to assess PTSS [30, 106, 107]. The CPSS comprises 17 self-report items reflecting DSM-IV symptom content. Respondents indicate how much each symptom has bothered them during the past two weeks on a 4-point response format from 0 (never) to 3 (always) (e.g., “Having upsetting thoughts or images about the event that came into your head when you didn’t want them to”). Item scores are summed to yield a total between 0 and 51, with higher totals indicating more severe PTSS. A score of 16 has been recommended as a screening cut-point for probable PTSD in children and adolescents [77]. Prior work supports the measure’s psychometric performance, and the Chinese version has been widely used in clinical and research contexts [22, 104, 109, 110, 112]. Using pooled estimates across the multiply imputed datasets, Cronbach’s alpha for this measure was 0.93.

Prosocial behaviour

Prosocial behaviour was assessed using the Chinese version of the Prosocial Behaviour subscale of the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ) [24, 38]. This subscale contains five items capturing supportive and helpful behaviours shown toward others during the past six months (e.g., sharing). Responses are given on a 3-point scale from 1 (not true) to 3 (certainly true), and items are summed such that higher scores indicate greater prosociality. In line with the SDQ copyright and scoring guidelines, the Prosocial Behaviour subscale was administered solely for non-commercial research purposes and scored according to the official SDQ scoring manual, using its original wording, structure, response format, and scoring procedure without any modification. Evidence from prior studies indicates that the SDQ and its Chinese version demonstrate acceptable validity and reliability in school-based samples [40, 47, 75]. Using pooled estimates across the multiply imputed datasets, Cronbach’s alpha for the Prosocial Behaviour subscale was 0.74.

Resilience

Resilience was assessed using the 10-item short form of the Connor–Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC-10) [10], developed from the original 25-item CD-RISC [16]. Items assess attitudes and perceived capacities for coping with adversity over the past month (e.g., “Sees the positive side of situations”). Each item is rated from 1 (never) to 5 (always), and higher summed scores indicate greater resilience. The CD-RISC has demonstrated good psychometric properties in prior research [99, 101]. Using pooled estimates across the multiply imputed datasets, Cronbach’s alpha for the CD-RISC was 0.95.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were conducted in R (version 4.5.1) within the RStudio environment, using the packages lavaan (0.6.19), dplyr (1.1.4), haven (2.5.5), BaylorEdPsych (0.5), mvnmle (0.1.11.2), naniar (1.1.0), broom (1.0.10), mice (3.18.0), MASS (7.3.65), mitml (0.4.5), and miceafter (0.5.0), together with custom R scripts.

Missing data and multiple imputation

Missing data patterns, the multiple imputation procedure, and sensitivity analyses for departures from the missing at random (MAR) assumption (including MCAR tests, logistic-regression–based diagnostics, and pattern–mixture checks for MNAR mechanisms) are described in detail in the Supplementary Materials.

Descriptive statistics and main mediation analysis

Scores for prosocial behaviour, resilience, and PTSS were calculated by summing the scores across all respective items. Continuous variables are reported as means and standard deviations (SD), and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Correlational analyses included Pearson’s correlations for continuous variables and Spearman’s rho and Kendall’s tau for ordinal variables. The hypothesised mediating effect of resilience was tested using regression-based mediation models implemented in lavaan. Indirect effects were estimated using a non-parametric bootstrap procedure with 3,000 resamples. Age, father’s education, mother’s education, and annual household income were included as covariates in the mediation models, and all continuous variables were transformed into standardised z scores to account for differences in measurement scales. All descriptive, correlational, and mediation analyses were carried out on the multiply imputed datasets, and parameter estimates and standard errors were pooled across imputations using Rubin’s rules [60]. Detailed pooling procedures for all analyses are presented in the Supplementary Materials.

Sensitivity analyses

Several sensitivity analyses were conducted to examine the robustness of the findings. First, the potential impact of unmeasured mediator–outcome confounding was assessed using the correlated-residuals approach [48]. Second, potential temporal bias in the cross-sectional mediation model was examined using a phantom-variable structural equation modelling approach [36]. Third, the possible influence of unmeasured school-level clustering was approximated using a design-effect–based sensitivity analysis. Finally, common-method variance arising from the exclusive use of self-report measures was evaluated using an unmeasured latent variable (UMLV) approach [79]. Detailed procedures and results for all sensitivity analyses are presented in the Supplementary Materials.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of the participants

The analytic sample comprised 2,322 students, with a mean age of 14.80 years and 49.1% identifying as male. Most of them adhered to Tibetan Buddhism. The education level of their parents was generally low, with more than 80% reporting no formal schooling or only primary education. Among the 768 participants who reported their trauma type, the majority had experienced an earthquake. More detailed information is presented in Table 1.

Descriptive statistics and correlation analyses

In the trauma-exposed sample (N = 2,322), 40.4% of participants met or exceeded the CPSS screening threshold (≥ 16), indicating probable PTSD (95% CI: 38.4%–42.4%). The distribution of CPSS total scores among trauma-exposed participants is shown in Fig. 2. Descriptive statistics (M, SD) for age, prosocial behaviour, resilience, and posttraumatic stress symptoms, together with the full correlation matrix, are provided in Table 2. At the bivariate level, greater prosocial behaviour co-occurred with higher resilience (r = 0.53, p < 0.001) and with fewer posttraumatic stress symptoms (r = − 0.30, p < 0.001). Resilience also showed an inverse association with posttraumatic stress symptoms (r = − 0.50, p < 0.001). With respect to demographics, age was weakly related to the key constructs: older participants reported slightly more prosocial behaviour (r = 0.11, p < 0.001) but slightly lower resilience (r = − 0.10, p < 0.001) and fewer posttraumatic stress symptoms (r = − 0.07, p < 0.01). Parental education exhibited consistent patterns. Higher paternal education was associated with lower prosocial behaviour (r = − 0.21, p < 0.001) and resilience (r = − 0.32, p < 0.001), and with higher posttraumatic stress symptoms (r = 0.17, p < 0.001). The same directional pattern emerged for maternal education (prosocial behaviour: r = − 0.19, p < 0.001; resilience: r = − 0.28, p < 0.001; posttraumatic stress symptoms: r = 0.16, p < 0.001). Household income showed small negative correlations with prosocial behaviour (r = − 0.07, p < 0.01) and resilience (r = − 0.17, p < 0.001), alongside a positive correlation with posttraumatic stress symptoms (r = 0.13, p < 0.001). As expected, mothers’ and fathers’ education levels were strongly correlated (r = 0.85, p < 0.001). Each parent’s educational attainment was also positively related to annual household income (father: r = 0.31, p < 0.001; mother: r = 0.33, p < 0.001). Table 3 summarises the regression and mediation results with resilience and PTSS as the dependent variables. Prosocial behaviour was positively associated with resilience (β = 0.52, SE = 0.02, 95% CI [0.48, 0.55]). In turn, resilience showed a negative association with PTSS (β = − 0.50, SE = 0.02, 95% CI [− 0.55, − 0.46]), whereas the direct path from prosocial behaviour to PTSS was close to zero and its confidence interval included zero (β = − 0.02, SE = 0.02, 95% CI [− 0.07, 0.02]). The mediation analysis further indicated a significant indirect effect of prosocial behaviour on PTSS via resilience (β = − 0.26, SE = 0.02, 95% CI [− 0.29, − 0.23]), supporting the mediating role of resilience. The total effect of prosocial behaviour on PTSS was also negative (β = − 0.28, SE = 0.02, 95% CI [− 0.32, − 0.24]), with approximately 93% of this total effect accounted for by the indirect path through resilience. Discussion

This study extends the literature by characterising posttraumatic stress symptoms in trauma-exposed youth living on the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau and by testing how prosocial behaviour and resilience relate to these symptoms. In the present sample, 40.4% of participants scored at or above the CPSS screening threshold for probable PTSD. Moreover, higher prosocial behaviour was associated with lower PTSS, and the pattern of results was consistent with an indirect-only association via resilience.

Trauma-exposed children and adolescents on the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau showed substantial posttraumatic stress symptomatology. The observed prevalence rate of 40.4% is similar to that seen among children surviving the Wenchuan earthquake (with a magnitude of 8.0) one year later [111], slightly higher than that in the aftermath of the Ya'an earthquake (with a magnitude of 6.1) at both eight and twenty months [13], and significantly higher than that among children exposed to the Yancheng tornado six to eighteen months post-event [3]. Several contextual features may contribute to this level of posttraumatic stress. As noted earlier, elevated depressive symptoms among long-term high-altitude residents have been linked to physiological characteristics, raising the possibility that altitude-related adaptations may also shape trauma-related symptom expression in youth. In addition, the present study did not differentiate by the number or recurrence of traumatic exposures. Given the environmental context, repeated stressors are plausible. For example, official statistics indicate 984 sudden geological disasters in Qinghai Province between 1990 and 2019, which may translate into recurrent or cumulative adversity for some families. Prior work suggests that repeated trauma during development is associated with greater severity and more complex posttraumatic presentations [15, 90]. Finally, limited service capacity and access barriers in high-altitude areas, including inconvenient transportation, low population density, constrained information exchange, and economic underdevelopment, may restrict timely psychological support for children and adolescents who are experiencing substantial distress [98, 102]. Taken together, these considerations highlight the need to strengthen mental-health awareness, expand targeted interventions, and improve the distribution of mental-health resources in plateau regions.

Consistent with our hypothesis, the results supported a negative correlation between prosocial behaviour and PTSS. This pattern is in line with prior evidence suggesting that helping can support coping and reduce stress-linked distress among trauma-exposed youth [27, 95]. From a developmental perspective, prosocial engagement is often co-developing with perspective-taking, emotion understanding, and moral reasoning, and it may also promote positive affective experiences while strengthening self-regulatory and problem-solving capacities [7, 26, 53, 62, 82]. In turn, these competencies and experiences may confer broader protection against emotional difficulties and contribute to psychological well-being [25, 49, 78]. Notably, previous research examining the correlation between trauma and prosocial behaviour has predominantly centred on whether traumatic experiences precipitate changes in individuals' aggression. The "violence begets violence" theory suggests that those who have endured violence may rationalise engaging in violent behaviour [100]. Moreover, extensive research has established a significant association between prior trauma exposure and heightened levels of aggression, violence, and inclination towards criminal conduct [20, 50, 55, 92]. In this context, trauma-exposed individuals are often characterised chiefly in terms of aggressive tendencies. Interestingly, prosociality and aggression often appear to represent opposing ends of a single behavioural dimension, consistently displaying a negative correlation [28]. The present findings suggest complex interplay among trauma exposure, posttraumatic stress symptoms, prosocial behaviour, and aggression. These observations may be useful for informing interventions that simultaneously target trauma-related distress and social-behavioural functioning in youth.

Our findings were consistent with a model in which resilience serves as a pathway connecting prosocial behaviour with lower posttraumatic stress symptoms. Prior evidence on the psychosocial benefits of prosocial engagement is broadly compatible with the idea that helping behaviours may strengthen adaptive capacity under stress. For example, experimental work conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic suggests that induced prosocial actions can increase perceived meaning, empathy, social connectedness, and positive affect [94]. In daily life, adolescents’ helping behaviours may also meet important interpersonal and emotional needs, particularly for those experiencing depressive symptoms [85]. Media-based studies indicate that exposure to prosocial content (e.g., television programmes or mobile applications) can support young children’s socioemotional learning, including emotion knowledge and regulation strategies [82]. Conceptually, resilience is best understood as a multidetermined capacity that is shaped by interlocking influences across the child’s ecology. Drawing on Bronfenbrenner’s ecological systems framework, adaptive functioning in the context of stress can be viewed as emerging from interactions among biological, psychological, social, structural, and environmental resources [9]. In this sense, resilience reflects patterns of appraisal, coping, and emotional responding that facilitate adjustment when circumstances are demanding [1]. Taken together, the literature suggests that prosocial engagement may contribute to the accumulation or activation of such resources, thereby supporting resilience-relevant processes. Practically, these findings highlight the potential value of embedding prosocial skill-building and opportunities for helping within schools and families as part of trauma-informed mental health promotion for children and adolescents.

There was also a positive correlation between age and prosocial behaviour. At the same time, there was a negative correlation between age and resilience, as well as between age and posttraumatic stress symptoms. This phenomenon can be attributed to the fact that as young people grow older, their social worlds typically expand, providing more varied contexts in which cooperation, sharing, and kindness can be enacted [73, 88]. Evidence on how resilience varies with age is mixed, likely because resilience trajectories depend on multiple accumulating influences and exposures [66]. The negative association between age and resilience observed in this study might suggest that as children and adolescents mature, they face more intricate and challenging circumstances that could pose difficulties in managing, potentially impacting their resilience over time. In contrast, the lower PTSS levels among older participants may indicate that older adolescents are equipped with more advanced coping strategies and emotion-regulation skills for managing trauma-related distress [21]. Overall, these age-related patterns point to a multifactorial set of relationships that should be unpacked with additional contextual and individual-level variables.

Finally, the education level of parents and annual household income were found to be negatively correlated with both prosocial behaviour and resilience but were positively correlated with posttraumatic stress symptoms. The results align with previous studies demonstrating a negative correlation between SES and overall well-being [58]. On the one hand, higher-SES contexts may be more strongly oriented toward individualistic beliefs and self-focused goals [39], which could reduce prosocial engagement and weaken perceived connectedness after trauma exposure. On the other hand, greater material and social resources may buffer children from many adverse experiences, potentially limiting opportunities to practice coping in difficult situations and to build resilience through mastery. In addition, higher parental education may coincide with stronger achievement expectations, which can increase academic stress in children and adolescents [12]. Under heightened academic pressure, young people may reallocate time and effort toward schoolwork in ways that leave fewer opportunities for prosocial behaviours [63]. At the same time, our sensitivity analyses indicated that these SES-related associations were modest in magnitude and sensitive to potential school-level clustering, so they should be interpreted as tentative and with appropriate caution.

Limitations and future directions

Several issues should be noted when interpreting these findings. PTSS was measured via questionnaire report rather than clinician-administered diagnostic procedures. As a result, symptom scores may not map directly onto clinically diagnosed PTSD, and their predictive utility can be constrained. Subsequent work would benefit from incorporating structured clinical interviews or other clinician-rated assessments to corroborate the present results in trauma-exposed samples.

Second, to accommodate the low Mandarin proficiency in the Tibetan region, trauma exposure was assessed using a simplified questionnaire with a single screening item and an optional open-ended follow-up question. Consequently, detailed information on trauma characteristics (e.g., specific event type and timing) was only available for the subset of trauma-exposed children who provided sufficiently detailed written descriptions. This limitation reduced our ability to examine event-specific or dose–response effects and may have introduced bias if these children differed systematically from those who did not respond to the open-ended item. Future studies should consider using culturally and linguistically adapted structured tools to obtain more comprehensive trauma histories in this population.

Third, because identifiers linking participants to their schools or counties were not retained, we could not explicitly model clustering in our primary analyses. This constraint may have led to some underestimation of standard errors and reduced precision of parameter estimates. Nevertheless, sensitivity analyses assuming plausible school-level intraclass correlation coefficients suggested that the main regression, total, and indirect effects were robust to these potential design effects.

Fourth, the age range of the sample was restricted because younger children below a certain school age were excluded from participation, as they were not able to provide reliable self-reports. This exclusion may limit the generalisability of our findings to younger children, whose developmental level and symptom expression could differ from those of older children and adolescents. Future research could address this limitation by incorporating alternative informants, such as parents or teachers, or age-appropriate assessment methods to better capture the psychological characteristics of this younger age group and reduce their underrepresentation in trauma-related research.

Fifth, because the data were collected at a single time point, the current analyses speak to associations only and cannot adjudicate directionality or causal effects. To test temporal ordering and causal mechanisms, future studies could adopt prospective longitudinal designs or intervention-based experiments that manipulate prosocial behaviour or resilience-related processes and track subsequent changes in posttraumatic stress symptoms. Moreover, other socioemotional and cognitive processes, such as social support, emotion regulation, and meaning making, may also mediate the association between prosocial behaviour and posttraumatic stress symptoms. Future longitudinal or experimental studies should therefore examine resilience alongside these alternative pathways within a more integrative framework of mediating processes. Finally, this work draws attention to the region’s particular socio-geographical context as a backdrop for understanding PTSS levels in the surveyed youth. Nevertheless, without an explicit comparison sample, it is difficult to quantify how much the observed symptom pattern is attributable to the Plateau setting per se. Moreover, due to the specificity of the survey area, caution should be taken when generalising the results of this study. Future research could strengthen inference by including matched low-altitude comparison groups with similar age distributions and trauma-exposure profiles, allowing a more direct test of how socioenvironmental conditions, especially high-altitude contexts, relate to posttraumatic stress symptom expression.

Implications

The present findings have practical implications for trauma-exposed youth living in high-altitude regions. The high prevalence of PTSS in this population highlights the importance of strengthening mental health awareness, screening, and access to appropriate support within schools and communities. In addition, prosocial behaviour and resilience emerged as correlates of PTSS and may represent potentially modifiable factors that warrant further investigation. Prior work suggests that cooperative learning approaches and efforts to foster affective empathy, compassion, and altruism can promote prosocial behaviour [6, 45, 46, 57]. However, the extent to which such approaches would translate into reductions in PTSS requires prospective confirmation.

Similarly, supportive family, school, and community contexts, along with adequate resources, have been linked to resilience in youth [67, 74, 89]. Future longitudinal and experimental research is needed to determine whether strengthening prosocial behaviour and resilience can lead to improvements in trauma-related outcomes in this setting, and to inform the design and evaluation of context-appropriate interventions.

Conclusions

In the present study, 40.4% of trauma-exposed children and adolescents had PTSS scores exceeding the screening threshold for probable PTSD. Higher levels of prosocial behaviour were negatively associated with posttraumatic stress symptoms. Furthermore, the pattern of results was consistent with an indirect-only association via resilience, such that higher prosocial behaviour was associated with fewer posttraumatic stress symptoms primarily through higher resilience. Given the cross-sectional design, these findings cannot establish causal direction or mechanism; rather, they are hypothesis-generating and suggest that prosocial behaviour and resilience are promising candidates for future longitudinal, experimental, and intervention research among youth residing on the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau.
