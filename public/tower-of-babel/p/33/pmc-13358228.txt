Health effects of an increased federal minimum wage among US low-wage-earning adults: a difference-in-differences analysis in a population-based cohort
Authors: Laura J Samuel, Rhonda Smith Wright, Hanna Charankevich, Nicholas J Seewald, Jing Tian, Pallavi Dwivedi, Sarah L Szanton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe current US federal minimum wage (US$7.25) translates to incomes below the poverty limit for full-time workers. Prior studies do not provide clear evidence that increasing wages can improve health, but there are limited long-term findings from the low-wage workers directly affected.MethodsWe estimated the health effects of the most recent increased federal minimum wage policy change, which was fully implemented in 2009, comparing residents of states that did and did not rely on the change. Low-income Panel Study of Income Dynamics participants between 1999 and 2019 were included if they were interviewed both before and after the policy change, and they or their partner relied on an hourly wage that was ≤150% of the minimum wage. Adjusted two-way fixed effects difference-in-differences models compared residents of states that relied on the federal minimum wage change in 2009 (1472 observations, 20 states) to those in states with higher minimum wages (1854 observations, 24 states).ResultsParticipants were, on average, 29 years old in 1999 and typically followed for 9.6 years (n=699). Most were female (73%) and Black (50%). The federal policy change temporarily narrowed the mean minimum wage gap between exposed and comparator states. In adjusted fixed-effects models, the federal minimum wage policy change reduced psychological distress (difference-in-differences coefficient=−1.027, p<0.01). There was no effect on body mass index, self-rated health or number of chronic conditions (difference-in-differences interaction term p values >0.05).ConclusionsThese results provide timely evidence suggesting that increased federal minimum wage policy changes may advance mental health equity by reducing psychological distress over 10 years.Keywords: Sociodemographic Factors, Epidemiology, Preventive Medicine

Background

About 1 million Americans earned wages at or below the current federal minimum wage of US$7.25 per hour in 2022, and nearly half of them (49%) resided in the 20 states that relied on the federal minimum wage.1 The current federal minimum wage translates to an income level below the poverty threshold, even for full-time workers, which has prompted recent efforts to increase the federal minimum wage. Despite copious, methodologically strong evidence that poverty contributes to accumulated poor physical and mental health across the lifespan, there is much less evidence about which policies could improve health outcomes for low-income households.2 3 Specifically, there is a need to evaluate whether increasing the federal minimum wage can improve health outcomes for the low-wage workers who are directly affected by the policy. Notably, the theory of cumulative dis/advantage supports the idea that increasing the federal minimum wage should narrow the health gap between rich and poor. Specifically, the concept of cumulative disadvantage proposes that poverty contributes to poor health in a cumulative fashion over the life course, resulting in diverging health trajectories as populations age.4 Conversely, the concept of cumulative advantage suggests that antipoverty strategies such as increased federal minimum wages should narrow health gaps between the rich and poor in a cumulative fashion over time.4

Prior studies have not provided consistent evidence that increased minimum wages improve health outcomes. Although increased minimum wages can reduce the rates of adolescent births,5 infant mortality,6 suicide,7 8 smoking, obesity9 and cardiovascular deaths10 across state populations, a recent review shows that the health effects for low-wage workers are poorly understood,11 partly because of limited longitudinal data for low-wage workers. A new UK minimum wage was associated with improved mental health outcomes among low-wage workers.12 However, US studies have typically selected adults with a high school degree or less13 14 or those without a college degree15 because these groups often have low-wage jobs, and some findings suggest adverse health outcomes. As examples, although one study found evidence of improved mental health among women,15 it also found an increased risk of poor self-rated health in the overall sample,15 and two other studies found an increased body mass index.13 14 However, these studies were limited in three key ways. First, they could not track changes in individual health trajectories because they relied on repeated cross-sectional survey data. Second, they have limited generalisability to the low-wage workers directly affected by the policy change because they were selected based on educational achievement, and 71% of people earning wages at or below the federal minimum at the time of the most recent change in 2009 had at least a high school level of education.16 Finally, they evaluated all minimum wage increases regardless of whether they occurred through federal or state policy action, and this approach may have masked important differences. Federal changes occur less frequently and affect people living much closer to the poverty limit than state changes, and antipoverty policies may inadvertently reduce access to health-promoting services like Medicaid and public benefits. Notably, the most recently increased federal minimum wage caused some Medicaid beneficiaries to lose benefits and did not necessarily increase income levels for low-wage workers.17 18

Therefore, the current study examined the most recent federal policy change in relation to health among a national cohort of low-wage-earning adults. Based on the concept of cumulative advantage, we examine the long-term impact of the policy change for 10 years after full implementation using 20 years of panel data. Because there is no comparison group naive to minimum wage laws, we compared low-wage workers residing in states reliant on the 2009 federal minimum wage change to their peers in states that already had minimum wages exceeding the new federal minimum in 2009, which was essentially an active control condition. Based on the theory of cumulative dis/advantage,4 19 we hypothesise that increasing the federal minimum wage improves health trajectories for low-wage workers by reducing poverty for them.

Methods

Sample

The Panel Study of Income Dynamics (PSID) is an ongoing longitudinal study which recruited a nationally representative sample of about 5000 US households in 1968, oversampling low-income and predominantly Black households. Participants were surveyed annually until 1997 and every 2 years thereafter, typically with ≥90% response rates.20 21 The sample has been supplemented with adult offspring of original participants and an immigrant refresher sample, who were recruited in 1997. Data for the current study were collected between 1999 and 2019. This study time period was selected to avoid a federal minimum wage policy change implemented in 1997 and to avoid the historic pandemic-related economic recession and temporary economic supports in 2020. Therefore, study data covers four PSID survey rounds conducted before the federal minimum wage was implemented, two rounds conducted during implementation (2007 and 2009) and five survey rounds after implementation to assess the long-term cumulative effects of the policy change.4 There were 38 241 PSID participants in 1999, which decreased throughout the years to 25 929 in 2019.

Because minimum wage policy changes predominantly affect the wages of low-wage workers and to enhance generalisability to the low-wage-earning population, we employed a dynamic cohort approach and allowed PSID participants to enter and exit the analytic sample based on whether they were reliant on a low-wage job. Specifically, observations were included in this study if the PSID participant or their partner was employed in an hourly wage job earning a wage that was ≤150% of the minimum wage for their state of residence at the time of their PSID interview. To capture long-term effects and prevent selection bias that might occur if the employment of low-wage workers changes after the policy change, we also included observations if the participant or their partner met selection criteria at the most recent survey round. The 150% threshold was selected a priori to select a low-wage-earning population while also accounting for measurement error and potential spillover effects for those earning wages just above the minimum.22 Because some people may not be financially dependent on a low-wage job as a primary source of income, observations were excluded if the participant’s household income exceeded 200% of the federal poverty threshold, based on the number of adults and children in the household. Finally, participants must have been eligible for the study in at least one survey round prior to 2009 and one round after 2009 to ensure a valid comparison. Overall, the final sample consisted of 699 participants providing 3367 observations.

Outcomes

Outcomes chosen for this study include body mass index (BMI), self-rated health, number of chronic conditions and psychological distress because they are commonly experienced by the population of low-wage-earning adults.23,26 Outcomes were typically measured for the primary respondent and their spouse/partner during each survey, except that psychological distress data were only collected for the primary respondent and only since 2001. BMI was calculated using self-reported height and weight. Self-rated health was ranked as excellent (ref), very good, good, fair or poor. The number of chronic conditions was calculated as a count of up to 12 chronic conditions based on whether a health professional ever told the participant that they have or had each of the following: a stroke, heart attack, heart disease, hypertension, asthma, chronic lung disease, diabetes, arthritis, memory loss or mental disability, learning disorder, cancer or emotional, nervous or psychiatric disorder. Psychological distress level was measured using the K-6 Kessler Psychological Distress Scale based on symptoms over the past 30 days (scores range 0–24).27

Exposure measure

Annual data for state and federal minimum wage levels were obtained from the National Welfare Data maintained by the University of Kentucky Centre for Poverty Research and based on Department of Labour data.28 Two binary indicators were used to examine the effect of the federal policy change. First, a binary variable compared observations made after the policy change was fully implemented (2011–2019) to observations made before or during the implementation period (1999–2009). Based on the principles of the target trial emulation framework,29 this time point was selected to classify exposure to compare those exposed and not exposed at T0 because the federal minimum wage amount was incrementally increased from US$5.15 per hour to US$5.85 in July 2007, then to US$6.55 in July 2008 and to US$7.25 in July 2009, and PSID interviews were conducted in 2007 and 2009. Second, a binary variable classified exposure to the federal minimum wage policy change, comparing residents of states that increased the minimum wage from US$7.25 in July 2009 to a comparator group residing in states with a minimum wage exceeding US$7.25.

Additional independent variables

Individual factors that may differ on average across states were included in these analyses, including age in 1999, sex (female vs male), race (White, Black, Native American, Asian and Native Hawaiian/Pacific Islander), Hispanic ethnicity (Hispanic vs non-Hispanic) and marital status (married vs single/widowed/divorced/separated). Because the increased federal minimum wage coincided with the Great Recession and to address concerns about historic threats to internal validity, analyses evaluated whether the exposed and comparator groups differed with regard to state-level changes in economic conditions, including Gross State Product, per capita income, poverty rate and unemployment rate. However, state-level variables were not treated as covariates in analyses because they likely lie on the causal pathway between federal minimum wage increases and health; there is good evidence that increased minimum wages increase income and reduce poverty.30 31

Statistical analysis

To estimate the average effects of the increased federal minimum wage on health in comparison to reliance on state minimum wages, we leverage the exogenous nature of policy implementation and repeated measures of health outcomes to employ difference-in-differences analyses in fixed effects regression models.32 33 Therefore, Model 1 included both binary exposure indicators described above and their interaction (the difference-in-differences term), which estimates the effect of the policy change on health outcomes. Model 2 additionally adjusted for individual characteristics, including age in 1999, female status, race, ethnicity and marital status. Models 3, 4 and 5 built upon Model 2 by additionally including state fixed effects to account for time-invariant state-level characteristics and/or year fixed effects to account for population trends over time that were consistent across states. The full model (Model 5) equation is provided in online supplemental 1. Linear regression was used for all study outcomes, which is reasonable for ordinal data having a range of values.34 Robust SEs were accounted for the clustering of people within households in all models. Analyses were conducted in R V.4.2.2 using the RStudio integrated development environment provided by Posit Software PBC with the help of the statistical packages ‘stats’ and ‘dplyr’.35

Several additional analyses addressed potential threats to validity. First, because this study used an active control condition (states that had minimum wages exceeding the federal minimum), we used event study regression models and pre-exposure trends regression models using only baseline data (eg, 1999–2007) to assess the reasonableness of the conditional parallel trends assumption. We also characterised the exposure by (1) comparing minimum wage levels over time in states that did and did not rely on the 2009 federal minimum wage and (2) examining the extent to which states reliant on the federal minimum wage in 2009 were reliant on it throughout the study period. Also, to minimise the impact of state minimum wage policy changes on study outcomes, sensitivity analyses used a shorter study period (2003–2013) and separate analyses excluded observations from two states (New Hampshire and Iowa) that had both state and federal minimum wage policy changes implemented between 2007 and 2009. Second, to assess historic threats to internal validity because of the Great Recession, changes in the unemployment rate, poverty rate, per capita income and per capita Gross State Product were compared across exposed versus comparator states and sensitivity analyses were conducted among a placebo sample of high-wage-earning adults (incomes >400% of the minimum wage). Third, to address potential selection bias, additional analyses excluded fewer people based on incomes up to 300% of poverty rather than 200% of poverty, and the sample was compared with the US population of minimum wage workers.

Patient and public involvement

It was not appropriate or possible to involve patients or the public in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

Results

The final sample size included 3326 observations, representing 699 unique PSID participants. Participants were followed for an average of 4.8 survey rounds (SD=2.1), which corresponds to about 9.6 years of follow-up. Half of the sample (50%) were present for at least three consecutive survey rounds, and, on average, it took at most 5.4 years (SD=3.4) to follow up with each participant. Participants were, on average, 29 years old in 1999 (table 1). Most low-wage individuals were female (73%), and they were more likely to be Black (50%) than White (31%). About 18% of the sample were Hispanic, and 38% were married. Sample characteristics were similar to national estimates of US residents earning wages at or below the minimum wage at the time of the most recent minimum wage change in 2009 with regard to age, ethnicity, marital status and educational achievement, except for a higher percentage of people who were Black (50% vs 14%) (online supplemental table A). Low-wage participants exposed to the federal policy change because they resided in a state relying on the federal minimum wage in 2009 were more likely to be Black (75% vs 30%) and less likely to be Hispanic (6% vs 26%) or married (25% vs 48%) than study participants in comparator states but had a similar age (the average age was 29 years vs 30 years) (table 1). Participants resided in 20 of the 23 exposed states (1472 observations) and 24 (1854 observations) of the 28 comparator locations (27 states plus Washington DC) (table 2 and online supplemental table B). The mean minimum wage in comparator states typically exceeded the mean minimum wage in exposed states during the study time period, except that the gap was temporarily narrowed following the federal policy change (figure 1). The majority (n=13) of the exposed states relied on the federal minimum wage not only at the time that the policy change was fully implemented (2009) but also throughout the study time period, suggesting consistency across the study period with regard to reliance on the federal minimum wage. Although some states in the exposed group increased their minimum wage above the federal minimum during the study time period, the minimum wage did not increase as often or as much as it did in comparator states (figure 1 and online supplemental table B). Additionally, the exposed and comparator states did not differ with regard to economic conditions during the Great Recession (online supplemental table B).

Before the federal minimum wage increase was fully implemented, low-wage workers residing in states relying on the federal minimum wage had higher average BMI (mean of 29 vs 28) and more psychological distress (mean scores of 4.5 vs 3.6) than those in states unaffected by the policy change but were similar with regard to self-rated health and number of chronic conditions (table 2). Discussion

This study contributes to the literature by providing timely evidence that federal minimum wage policy changes may reduce psychological distress over 10 years for low-wage workers who are dependent on federal policy updates. This study, grounded in the concept of cumulative advantage,4 is innovative in leveraging 20 years of data to examine the long-term health effects of the most recent US federal minimum wage policy change. Notably, we find that low-wage-earning adults residing in states reliant on the federal minimum wage had more psychological distress at baseline than their peers residing in states with higher state minimum wages, and this is consistent with the concept of cumulative disadvantage. Conversely, after the policy change, the gap between states narrowed over time, and this is consistent with the concept of cumulative advantage. Additionally, this study found that states tend to rely on either the federal minimum wage or state minimum wages, not both, highlighting that federal policy changes should impact the low-wage workers residing in the 20 states reliant on the federal minimum wage. These findings have significance because of longstanding income-based disparities in psychological distress36 by suggesting that minimum wage policy changes can advance income-based health equity. Based on our conceptual understanding of accumulated dis/advantage,4 we interpret these findings as showing that the federal minimum wage policy, which increases income levels for low-income individuals, may alter mental health trajectories and related disparities.

Notably, results are similar to those from a UK study that also used nearly 20 years of data among low-wage workers to estimate the effect of a national policy change12 by showing that national minimum wage laws can improve mental health outcomes. Although these results contradict those of several prior US studies that concluded that increased minimum wages worsen rather than improve adult health outcomes,13,15 there are several methodological differences between the prior US studies reviewed earlier and the current study. First, despite using nationally representative samples, several prior studies13,15 had limited generalisability to low-wage workers because they selected participants based on educational attainment, which is not necessarily correlated with wages. As examples, 88% of people earning wages at or below the federal minimum wage in 2022 had more than a high school level of education, and 22% graduated from college.1 Although the PSID sample, which oversampled people who were Black, included a higher percentage of people who were Black than in the US minimum wage-earning population, the sample was more representative of the low-wage population with regard to educational attainment than samples for prior studies (online supplemental table A). Second, our study estimates the effect of the entire federal policy change, not the effect of each incremental minimum wage increase, as was estimated in prior studies and examines a national policy change that likely has a stronger antipoverty effect than the state-based changes that were examined in other recent studies.13,15 Importantly, our study also tracked individual changes after the policy change instead of the population-level responses that were tracked in prior repeated cross-sectional studies.5,1013 Therefore, although this study did not find evidence that the increased federal minimum wage improved self-rated health or BMI, the lack of evidence for adverse effects on these outcomes is informative because it uses strong methods and contradicts prior findings. Notably, our finding that there was a statistically significant effect in models that used 20 years of data but no statistically significant findings when using only 10 years of data is consistent with findings from a recent review showing that the long-term health effects of minimum wages are typically larger than short-term effects.37 This is also consistent with the idea of cumulative advantage, which suggests that the anti-poverty effect of the policy change should accumulate over time4 and suggests that long-term follow-up may be needed to capture the health benefits of increased minimum wages. Together, these results suggest that more research is needed to track long-term individual health responses to minimum wage policy changes among the low-wage workers who are directly affected by policy changes.

There are potential reasons why increased minimum wages may reduce psychological distress for low-wage-earning adults but not improve other outcomes. First, psychological distress may be more sensitive to economic constraints than other outcomes. Economic constraints related to food insecurity and job loss have been linked with higher rates of psychological distress in national studies,36 38 39 and low wages are believed to contribute to deaths of despair,40 suggesting that efforts to address economic constraints may, conversely, improve distress. Second, low-wage workers are typically young adults with lower rates of obesity, chronic conditions and poor health compared with older adults, and this may explain null findings. Third, participants typically only remained in the low-wage-earning sample for about 9.5 years of follow-up and this may be an insufficient period of time to evaluate the accumulation of chronic conditions or overall health decline.

These findings have important clinical and public health implications. Psychological distress contributes to adverse health outcomes, including a greater risk of obesity and earlier mortality and partly accounts for socioeconomic health disparities,41 42 suggesting that efforts to reduce distress could have a sizeable public health impact. Greater attention should be paid to evaluating the role of other economic policies and programmes in addressing health equity. As examples, unemployment insurance and tax credits may also improve health outcomes for low-income people, but there are gaps in estimating their effects across multiple health outcomes across the life course, which could be used to inform data-driven policy decisions.43 44 Furthermore, national data highlight the urgency in addressing socioeconomic disparities in psychological distress; the prevalence rate had gradually increased during the two decades before the COVID-19 pandemic, dramatically worsened during the pandemic and exhibited widening income-based disparities globally.45 46 Overall, the literature suggests that economic policies such as minimum wage laws can be indirectly linked with multiple types of health disparities and call for greater attention to leveraging policy solutions for addressing health equity.

Limitations and strengths

Some cities have established a minimum wage that exceeds and replaces state minimum wages, and this study could not account for differences within states. Increased state wages during the study period in states exposed to the federal policy change may have biased results but likely would have biased results towards non-statistically significant findings. Although increased minimum wages likely have spillover effects on the wage levels of other low-wage jobs that are exempt from minimum wage requirements (eg, self-employed positions, subminimum wage jobs and jobs paid based on commission and/or tips), the increased federal minimum wage did not require employers to increase wages for all low-wage jobs. Including one additional observational time point for individuals who were no longer low-wage workers may capture employment changes unrelated to the policy change (especially if they occurred several years after the policy change) although it likely reduced selection bias and also accounted for potential employment changes related to the policy change. Sampling weights were not applied because the study sampled a small, low-wage-earning subgroup of the PSID sample; inferences should not be generalised to all US adults. Psychological distress was measured only for the primary respondent in each household; findings may not be generalisable to all low-wage-earning adults. BMI may not be reliably captured for pregnant women who are not identified in PSID. Psychological distress and self-rated health were measured using Likert scales, and the distribution of the underlying latent phenomena may not approximate that of continuous measures as assumed for linear regression. Despite these limitations, this study is strong in providing 20 years of longitudinal data from a national sample of low-wage-earning adults.

Conclusions

This national study, using 20 years of data, which is among the first to examine the long-term impact of federal minimum wage policy changes, estimated that the most recent federal policy change increasing the wage reduced psychological distress among low-wage-earning adults in comparison to their peers in states with higher wages. Unlike some prior studies that suggested that increased minimum wages may worsen health, we found no evidence that the most recent federal policy change worsened self-rated health, BMI or chronic conditions for low-wage US workers. These results contribute to the literature by documenting the relevance of minimum wage policies to public health for low-income adults and provide timely evidence that can be used to inform data-driven policy solutions to address income-based health inequities.
