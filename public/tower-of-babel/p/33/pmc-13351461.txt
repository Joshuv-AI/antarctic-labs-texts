Feasibility of augmented reality-assisted remote consultation for maternal health in Zimbabwe
Authors: Aparna Sridhar, Luke Murphy, Kathleen Chung, Kiran Kavipurapu, Abhiram Mungara, Mary Gacii, Norah Mucheke
Published: 2026

Objective

Sub-Saharan Africa accounts for 70% of global maternal deaths; in Zimbabwe, the maternal mortality ratio is 358 per 100,000 live births.1 Rural Government Medical Officers (GMOs) face acute professional isolation, often practicing several hours away from specialist centers.2 They must manage critical emergencies, including postpartum hemorrhage (PPH) and eclampsia, with limited real-time support. This pilot study evaluated the feasibility of using Augmented Reality (AR) smart glasses to bridge this gap through 2 applications: (1) real-time remote specialist guidance during simulated obstetric emergencies and (2) structured procedural teaching for Emergency Obstetric and Newborn Care (EmONC).

Study design

Following UCLA IRB approval, a feasibility trial was conducted at Bindura Provincial Hospital in Zimbabwe. The intervention utilized AR smart glasses3 and a secure streaming platform4 for 2 scenarios: a "procedural walkthrough" where a provincial obstetrician narrated a PPH emergency from a first-person perspective for remote GMOs, and a "remote mentorship" session where a GMO performed the procedure while an off-site expert provided live guidance. Following the simulations, qualitative interviews were conducted with GMOs and provincial obstetricians to assess usability, clinical value, and implementation barriers. Qualitative data were analyzed using a thematic content analysis approach to identify recurring barriers and facilitators to technology adoption.

Results

The integration of the secure streaming platform and the AR glasses showed promising results in the rural provincial hospital. It was feasible to set up the technology with a remote internet device. The pilot phase received a strongly positive reception, with all participants favoring adoption if technical infrastructure is optimized.

Clinical Value: Providers identified hands-free, real-time mentorship as the most significant benefit. Unlike traditional mobile consultations, AR glasses provide an identical visual field to the mentor while allowing the clinician to use both hands for the patient. The streaming platform made secure sharing of the link as well as streaming feasible. The sharing link could be shared with multiple learners at the same time so they would view it simultaneously on their devices.

Usability: While the devices were rated as comfortable, participants noted the "cognitive load" of technical setup during acute emergencies. They recommended a "tech-lead" model where a secondary staff member manages the device.

Education: Recent graduates highlighted AR’s potential to democratize surgical training, allowing large cohorts to observe procedures without crowding the operating theater.

Barriers: Two critical hurdles emerged. First, connectivity was the primary failure point; internet instability interrupted the pilot and remains the most significant implementation threat. Second, power unreliability in rural districts often coincides with network outages, necessitating parallel investments in infrastructure to ensure device readiness.

Conclusion

There is high provider readiness for AR-assisted mentorship in Zimbabwe. This technology transitions remote consultation from low-fidelity tools to a high-definition, hands-free standard that could reduce preventable maternal mortality. While AR is a viable clinical support tool, its scalable success depends on resolving regional connectivity and power barriers. We will next transition from a pilot phase to a sustainable community-led model with cost benefit analysis.
