The AAA-ATPase Yta4 inhibits the interaction between Ppa2 and Atg43 to promote Atg43 phosphorylation and mitophagy
Authors: Ke Liu, Jiajia He, Yifan Wu, Chenhui Zhao, Shuaijie Yan, Fangyuan Xiong, Xing Liu, Xuebiao Yao, Chuanhai Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAAA-ATPase Yta4/Msp1/ATAD1 is a well-known quality control factor that clears mistargeted tail-anchored proteins and precursor proteins on mitochondria. However, whether Yta4 preserves mitochondrial homeostasis through alternate pathways remains unclear. Traditionally, mitophagy has been recognized as a crucial pathway for eliminating dysfunctional mitochondria, thereby ensuring the maintenance of mitochondrial homeostasis. In this study, we unveil a novel role for Yta4 in sustaining mitochondrial homeostasis by facilitating mitophagy in fission yeast. The absence of Yta4 delays the phosphorylation of the mitophagy receptor Atg43 and specifically inhibits mitophagy. Additionally, Atg43 phosphorylation sites Ser32, Ser35, and Ser36, which are crucial for mitophagy, were identified. We further found that the phosphatase Ppa2 plays a major role in Atg43 dephosphorylation and inhibits excessive mitophagy. Yta4 physically interacts with both Atg43 and Ppa2, and coordinates with Ppa2 to modulate Atg43 phosphorylation and mitophagy. Moreover, Yta4 and Ppa2 bind to the same cytosolic region of Atg43, and Yta4 inhibits the interaction between Atg43 and Ppa2. Collectively, our findings suggest that Yta4 promotes mitophagy by ensuring the effectiveness of Atg43 phosphorylation. Thus, our findings reveal the novel function of Yta4 in regulating mitophagy and expand the understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying mitophagy in fission yeast.Keywords: ATAD1, fission yeast, mitochondria, phosphatase, phosphorylation, PP2A

Mitochondria are dynamic, double-membrane organelles found in eukaryotic cells. They serve as the primary sites for aerobic respiration and ATP synthesis, thereby functioning as the cellular powerhouses (1). A healthy mitochondrial network is crucial for maintaining normal cellular activities. The mitochondrial quality control system is essential for maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis through the ubiquitin-proteasome system, mitochondrial dynamics, and mitophagy (2).

Mitophagy, an important self-protective mechanism, maintains cellular homeostasis by selectively degrading damaged mitochondria (3). The selectivity of mitophagy is demonstrated by the targeting of damaged mitochondria to autophagosomes via the interaction of specific mitochondrial outer membrane receptors with ligand proteins (4). In Schizosaccharomyces pombe, the mitochondrial outer membrane receptor Atg43 promotes mitochondrial degradation by interacting with Atg8 through its Atg8-interacting motif (AIM) (5). In Saccharomyces cerevisiae, the mitophagy receptor Atg32 interacts with Atg8 and Atg11 via its AIM motif and two serine residues (S114 and S119), respectively (6). The interaction between Atg32 and Atg11 is essential for mitophagy and is modulated by Atg32 phosphorylation. Specifically, phosphorylation of Atg32 at S114 and S119 enhances this interaction, thereby promoting mitophagy (7). Under normal culture conditions, Ppg1 and the FAR complex collaborate to dephosphorylate Atg32, thereby preventing excessive mitophagy (8). Ppg1 is a PP2A-like phosphatase in budding yeast. PP2A (Protein phosphatase 2A), a widely distributed serine/threonine phosphatase in eukaryotic cells, comprises catalytic, structural, and regulatory subunits (9, 10). In fission yeast, the PP2A complex includes three catalytic subunits (Ppa1, Ppa2, and Ppa3), two structural subunits (Paa1 and Pab1), and two regulatory subunits (Par1 and Par2). All of these subunits are essential for modulating the cell cycle and cell fate (11, 12). However, the function of PP2A related to mitophagy in fission yeast has not yet been reported.

Yta4, a single transmembrane protein localized to the mitochondrial outer membrane, belongs to the AAA-ATPase family (13). The homologs of Yta4 in budding yeast and mammalian cells are Msp1 (Mitochondrial Sorting of Proteins 1) and ATAD1 (ATPase family AAA domain-containing 1), respectively. Over the past several years, the structure and function of Msp1 have been extensively studied, revealing its role as a hexameric translocase that removes mistargeted tail-anchored (TA) proteins from the mitochondrial outer membrane (14). Msp1 recognizes client proteins via hydrophobic and electrostatic interactions and uses the energy from ATP hydrolysis to extract these clients from the mitochondrial outer membrane (15, 16). The extracted substrate proteins are transported to the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), where some undergo degradation via the ER-associated degradation pathway, while others are redirected to their appropriate organelles (17, 18). Msp1 also plays a crucial role in maintaining mitochondrial protein import homeostasis. Disruption of mitochondrial protein import activates the mitochondrial compromised protein import response, which upregulates Cis1 expression. Cis1 then recruits Msp1 to clear precursor proteins that cannot be imported into the mitochondria (19). The sequences and structures of Yta4, Msp1 and ATAD1 are highly conserved in evolution (20), leading to the preservation of comparable functional roles among these proteins. For example, ATAD1 can also extract viral TA-protein to exert antiviral function (21). Under mitochondrial protein import stress, ATAD1 clears clogged precursor proteins by interacting with the TOM complex and trapped proteins (22). While the roles of Msp1/ATAD1 in preserving mitochondrial protein homeostasis have been extensively reported, their mechanisms and effectiveness in sustaining mitochondrial homeostasis at the organelle level remain poorly understood. Our previous research has demonstrated the mechanism of Yta4 in inhibiting mitochondrial fission (13).

In this study, we identify the novel function of Yta4 in promoting nitrogen starvation-induced mitophagy in fission yeast. We found that mitophagy and Atg43 phosphorylation are significantly delayed in cells lacking Yta4. These phenotypes can be restored by the absence of Ppa2, the PP2A (Protein phosphatase 2A) phosphatase, acting as a negative regulator of Atg43 phosphorylation and mitophagy. Biochemical experiments indicate that Yta4 and Ppa2 bind to the same region of Atg43, and Yta4 inhibits the interaction between Ppa2 and Atg43. Thus, in this study, we reveal a new role of Yta4 in maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis and demonstrate the mechanism of Yta4 in regulating mitophagy.

Results

Yta4 is required to promote nitrogen starvation-induced mitophagy

In our previous research, we discovered that Yta4 modulates mitochondrial fission by interacting with the mitochondrial divisome (13). Given the close interrelation between mitochondrial fission and mitophagy, and considering that mitophagy is an integral process in the regulation of mitochondrial homeostasis (2), we further investigated whether Yta4 also participates in regulating mitophagy. Nitrogen starvation induces autophagy of cytoplasmic proteins, mitochondria, endoplasmic reticulum, and other organelles in S. pombe (23). Given that fluorescent proteins, such as green fluorescent protein (GFP), are relatively resistant to vacuolar proteases, autophagic degradation can be effectively monitored by measuring the release of free fluorescent proteins resulting from the cleavage of their respective fusion proteins (24, 25, 26). To assess mitophagy, we tagged the mitochondrial matrix protein Sdh2 at its endogenous locus with GFP in WT and yta4Δ cells. Free GFP was observed after nitrogen starvation (EMM-N) (Fig. 1A), and the ratio of free GFP to total GFP was significantly lower in yta4Δ cells than in WT cells (Fig. 1A). Ectopic expression of Yta4 from its own promoter in yta4Δ cells (referred as yta4Δ+Yta4) restored mitophagy levels to those of wild-type cells (Fig. S1, A and B). These results indicate that Yta4 is required to promote nitrogen starvation-induced mitophagy.

To verify whether Yta4 specifically regulates mitophagy, we used the same method described above to assess other autophagic degradation pathways in WT and yta4Δ cells. Nitrogen starvation-induced bulk autophagy and ER-phagy were measured by assessing the processing of GFP-tagged cytoplasmic protein Pgk1 (Fig. 1B) and Atg8 (Fig. S1, C and D), and ER membrane protein Yop1 (Fig. 1C), respectively. We found that nitrogen starvation-induced bulk autophagy and ER-phagy were comparable between WT and yta4Δ cells. Msp1 and ATAD1 (Yta4 homologs in Saccharomyces cerevisiae and human sapiens) exhibit a dual localization on mitochondria and peroxisomes, and they play crucial roles in maintaining mitochondrial and peroxisomal homeostasis (14, 27). Thus, we also measured pexophagy by assessing the processing of GFP-tagged peroxisomal membrane protein Pex11, and found that the absence of Yta4 did not impact pexophagy (Fig. 1D). Collectively, these data indicate that Yta4 specifically promotes nitrogen starvation-induced mitophagy, rather than bulk autophagy, ER-phagy, and pexophagy.

We previously established that Yta4 limits excessive mitochondrial fission by inhibiting the Fis1-Mdv1-Dnm1 divisome complex (13). To determine whether the mitophagy defect in yta4Δ cells is a secondary consequence of altered mitochondrial morphology, we assessed mitophagy in dnm1Δ and dnm1Δyta4Δ strains (Fig. S1E). Western blotting analysis revealed that the absence of dnm1 did not significantly impair mitophagy (Fig. S1E). In contrast, mitophagy was markedly reduced in both yta4Δ and dnm1Δyta4Δ cells (Fig. S1E). These results indicate that mitophagy defect in yta4Δ cells are independent of mitochondrial fragmentation and further demonstrate that Yta4 regulates mitochondrial fission and mitophagy through distinct mechanisms.

The absence of Yta4 results in delayed phosphorylation of the mitophagy receptor Atg43

In S. pombe, the mitophagy receptor Atg43 interacts with Atg8 to mediate mitophagy during nitrogen starvation (5). Based on this, we hypothesize that mitophagy defect caused by the absence of Yta4 is dependent of Atg43. To test this hypothesis, we investigated the influence of Yta4 on Atg43. We first quantified basal Atg43 protein levels in WT and yta4Δ cells by using our homemade Atg43 antibody (Fig. 2A). Quantitative analysis revealed that the protein level of Atg43 was comparable in WT and yta4Δ cells (Fig. 2A). Next, we monitored Atg43 levels in WT and yta4Δ cells during nitrogen starvation treatment (Fig. 2B). A pronounced band shift in Atg43 was observed in both strains (Fig. 2B). In Saccharomyces cerevisiae and mammalian cells, mitophagy receptors undergo post-translational modifications, including phosphorylation and ubiquitination (7, 28). Thus, we hypothesized that the observed band shift could result from phosphorylation. To test the hypothesis, we treated cell lysates with λ protein phosphatase and analyzed the alteration of Atg43 band shift. Atg43 band shift was abolished following λ protein phosphatase treatment (Fig. 2C), supporting the conclusion that Atg43 is phosphorylated during nitrogen starvation-induced mitophagy. Additionally, quantification of the shift ratio demonstrated a delayed Atg43 band shift in yta4Δ cells (Fig. 2D), suggesting that during nitrogen starvation, Yta4 ensures efficient mitophagy by promoting Atg43 phosphorylation.

We further assessed the effect of prolonged nitrogen starvation on Atg43 levels in WT and yta4Δ cells. Quantification showed that the protein level of Atg43 initially increased and then decreased in both WT and yta4Δ cells (Fig. 2E). The decrease in Atg43 may reflect mitochondrial degradation. Notably, after 6 h of nitrogen starvation, Atg43 levels remained significantly higher in yta4Δ cells than in WT cells (Fig. 2E). This finding supports the model that the absence of Yta4 impairs mitophagy, leading to delayed Atg43 turnover. Collectively, these data demonstrate that Yta4 regulates mitophagy by promoting Atg43 phosphorylation.

The absence of Yta4 does not affect the localization of Atg43

Atg43 is a typical TA protein localized to the mitochondrial outer membrane (5). Therefore, to test whether Yta4 regulates the mitochondrial localization of Atg43, we fluorescently tagged Atg43. Through serial dilution growth assays, we found that C-terminal tagging significantly impaired the function of Atg43, while N-terminal tagging did not (Fig. 2F). Therefore, we used N-terminal GFP-tagged Atg43 for live-cell imaging to observe the localization of Atg43 in WT and yta4Δ cells during mitophagy (Fig. 2G). Imaging results showed that the absence of Yta4 did not affect Atg43 localization (Fig. 2G). Although GFP tagging did not affect Atg43 localization, we compared the phosphorylation modifications of GFP-tagged and endogenous Atg43 by western blotting (Fig. 2H). The results indicate that GFP tagging significantly delayed the timing of Atg43 phosphorylation (6–8 h after nitrogen starvation), compared to untagged Atg43 (within 1 h of nitrogen starvation) (Fig. 2H). Therefore, in subsequent experiments, we used homemade Atg43 antibodies to detect endogenous Atg43. Collectively, we conclude that despite the alteration in phosphorylation timing by fluorescent tagging of Atg43, the absence of Yta4 does not affect the mitochondrial localization of Atg43.

Atg43 phosphorylation is crucial for mitophagy

To test the functional link between Atg43 phosphorylation and mitophagy, we sought to identify the phosphorylation sites within Atg43. We focused on the N-terminal region of Atg43 (amino acid, 1–90), because Atg43(1–90) contains the AIM motif responsible for interacting with Atg8 (5) (Fig. 3A) and mediates interactions with Yta4 and Ppa2 (as shown below). Based on the distribution of Serine or Threonine residues in Atg43(1–90), we divided this region into three segments: amino acid 1 to 20, 21 to 50, and 51 to 90 for mutational analysis (Fig. 3A). We mutated all Serine and Threonine residues to Alanine in these three segments and expressed these Atg43 mutants in atg43Δ cells. Western blotting analysis revealed that Atg43 mutants containing mutations within the segments 21 to 50 and 51 to 90 exhibited a decrease in the degree of Atg43 band shift (Fig. 3B), suggesting that phosphorylation occurs at residues within these segments. Additionally, we further found that the Atg43 mutant containing mutations within the segment 21 to 50, but not the segment 51 to 90, significantly impaired mitophagy (Fig. 3C). Taken together, these results suggest that the key phosphorylation sites regulating mitophagy are located within the 21 to 50 segment of Atg43.

Next, we analyzed the phosphorylation sites within the segment 21 to 50 by grouping residues into three clusters: Ser22-Ser23, Ser32-Ser35-Ser36, and Ser43-Ser47. Western blotting analysis revealed that the Atg43(S32A-S35A-S36A) mutant significantly inhibited Atg43 phosphorylation (Fig. 3D) and impaired mitophagy (Fig. 3E). We noted that Atg43(S32A-S35A-S36A) did not completely inhibit Atg43 phosphorylation and mitophagy, suggesting that additional Atg43 phosphorylation sites may contribute to the full regulation of efficient mitophagy.

We further generated an Atg43 phospho-mimicking mutant by substituting the serine residues Ser32, Ser35, and Ser36 with aspartic acid (D) and used this mutant, along with the corresponding phospho-deficient mutant (i.e., Atg43(S32A-S35A-S36A)) to assess the effects of these mutants on both Atg43 phosphorylation (Fig. S2A) and mitophagy (Fig. S2B). We found that Atg43(S32D-S35D-S36D) exhibited a significant increase in the degree of Atg43 band shift, whereas Atg43(S32A-S35A-S36A) displayed a decrease in the degree of Atg43 band shift (Fig. S2A). Correspondingly, mitophagy activity in the Atg43(S32D-S35D-S36D) mutant was comparable to Atg43(WT), whereas mitophagy activity in the Atg43(S32A-S35A-S36A) mutant was significantly reduced (Fig. S2B). Collectively, our data demonstrate that Ser32, Ser35, Ser36 residues within Atg43 are critical phosphorylation sites required for promoting mitophagy.

Of note, Atg43(S32D-S35D-S36D) exhibited band shift even under non-starved conditions (Fig. S2A) but did not trigger mitophagy (Fig. S2B). This observation indicates that while Atg43 phosphorylation is necessary, it is not sufficient to induce mitophagy and that additional factors/signals induced by nitrogen starvation are required to trigger mitophagy.

Phosphotase Ppa2 is a negative regulator of Atg43 phosphorylation and mitophagy

To identify proteins involved in regulating Atg43 phosphorylation, we enriched Atg43 using the homemade anti-Atg43 antibody under normal culture conditions and subsequently employed mass spectrometry to identify potential Atg43-binding proteins. We identified several constituents of the mitochondrial outer membrane protein complexes, such as MIM and TOM (Fig. 4A), which have been reported to facilitate Atg43 localization (5). Specifically, we also identified a serine/threonine protein phosphatase Ppa2 (Fig. 4A). Ppa2, the catalytic subunit of protein phosphatase 2A (PP2A), plays a crucial role in regulating the cell cycle (29, 30). To verify the mass spectrometry results, we employed co-immunoprecipitation assays to test the interaction between Atg43 and Ppa2 in vivo. Co-immunoprecipitation assays revealed that Atg43 interacts with Ppa2 (Fig. 4B). Since Ppa2 is a phosphatase, we hypothesize that Ppa2 may be involved in regulating Atg43 phosphorylation. To test this hypothesis, we deleted ppa2 and assessed the phosphorylation ratio of Atg43 (Fig. 4C). Quantification revealed that the absence of ppa2 increased the Atg43 phosphorylation ratio (Fig. 4C). This finding suggests that Ppa2 functions to inhibit Atg43 phosphorylation.

In Saccharomyces cerevisiae, phosphorylation of the mitophagy receptor Atg32 promotes mitophagy (8). Consistently, our data demonstrate that Atg43 phosphorylation is crucial for mitophagy (Fig. 3). Therefore, we further assessed mitophagy in WT and ppa2Δ cells (Fig. 4D). Quantification showed that the absence of ppa2 accelerates mitophagy (Fig. 4D), supporting the model in which Ppa2 acts as a negative regulator of Atg43 phosphorylation and mitophagy.

Yta4 works with Ppa2 to regulate Atg43 phosphorylation and mitophagy

The above findings demonstrate that Yta4 promotes Atg43 phosphorylation and mitophagy, whereas Ppa2 has the opposite effect. Therefore, we further hypothesized that Yta4 works with Ppa2 to regulate Atg43 phosphorylation and mitophagy. To test this hypothesis, we deleted both ppa2 and yta4 (ppa2Δyta4Δ) to assess the level of mitophagy and the phosphorylation ratio of Atg43 (Fig. 4, E and F). Quantification revealed that deleting ppa2 in yta4Δ cells restored Atg43 phosphorylation (Fig. 4E) and rescued the mitophagy defect caused by Yta4 loss (Fig. 4F). Collectively, our results demonstrate that Yta4 and Ppa2 work in an antagonizing manner to regulate Atg43 phosphorylation and mitophagy.

Additionally, we observed that while the phosphorylation levels of Atg43 in ppa2Δ and ppa2Δyta4Δ cells were comparable (Fig. 4E), mitophagy was significantly lower in the double deletion mutant (Fig. 4F). This finding indicates that Yta4 may also promote mitophagy through mechanisms independent of its role in modulating Atg43 phosphorylation (see details in the discussion section).

Atg43 interacts with Yta4 and Ppa2 in cells

To investigate how Yta4 promotes Atg43 phosphorylation and mitophagy in conjunction with Ppa2, we firstly assessed their colocalization to test the potential interaction among Yta4, Atg43, and Ppa2. Wild-type cells expressing Yta4-tdTomato, GFP-Atg43, Ppa2-GFP or Ppa2-mCherry, all tagged endogenously, were imaged by live-cell spinning-disk microscopy (Fig. 5A). Both Yta4 and Atg43 exhibited complete mitochondrial localization (Fig. 5A). Interestingly, Yta4-tdTomato signals were not uniformly distributed among different cells and mitochondria. We speculate that this phenomenon may be attributed to the distinct metabolic states of individual cells. Ppa2 exhibited predominantly cytoplasmic localization, resulting in minimal colocalization with Yta4 or Atg43 (Fig. 5A). To further investigate the localization of Yta4, Atg43 and Ppa2, we isolated mitochondria from cells expressing Ppa2-GFP and Yta4-tdTomato, and analyzed mitochondrial and cytosolic fractions using western blotting (Fig. 5B). Using antibodies against Tubulin (cytosolic fraction), Mti3 (mitochondrial fraction), tdTomato (Yta4-tdTomato), and GFP (Ppa2-GFP), we found that both Ppa2-GFP and Yta4-tdTomato were present in the mitochondrial fraction (Fig. 5B). To test protein-protein interactions, we employed co-immunoprecipitation assays using Atg43 as a bait. As shown in Figure 5C, Atg43 co-precipitated Yta4-13Myc and Ppa2-GFP. These results suggest that Atg43 interacts with Yta4 and Ppa2 in vivo.

Atg43 physically interacts with Yta4 and Ppa2 in vitro

To determine whether Atg43 directly interacts with Yta4 and Ppa2, we purified recombinant proteins from Escherichia coli and performed GST pull-down assays. The domain structure of Atg43, Yta4 and Ppa2 are illustrated in Figure 6A. Both Atg43 and Yta4 contain transmembrane domain, so we purified their soluble cytoplasmic part (referred to as Atg43(ΔTM) and Yta4(ΔTM)). As shown in Figure 6, B and C, GST-Ppa2, but not GST and glutathione resins, precipitated His-SUMO-Atg43(ΔTM) and His-Yta4(ΔTM). Similarly, GST-Yta4(ΔTM), but not GST and glutathione resins, precipitated His-SUMO-Atg43(ΔTM) with the presence of ATP (Fig. 6D). Collectively, these results suggest that Atg43 physically interacts with Ppa2 and Yta4. Given that Ppa2 and Yta4 regulate the phosphorylation of Atg43 in an antagonistic manner, we speculate that Yta4 may compete with Ppa2 for binding to Atg43.

Yta4 inhibits the interaction of Atg43(46–90) and Ppa2

To test whether Yta4 competes with Ppa2 for binding to Atg43, we sought to identify the region of Atg43 that interacts with Yta4 and Ppa2. We divided the cytoplasmic region of Atg43 into two parts (referred to as Atg43(1–90) and Atg43(91–184)) and purified the truncated protein from E. coli (Fig. S3, A and B). As shown in Figure S3, C and D, both GST-Ppa2 and GST-Yta4(ΔTM) precipitated His-SUMO-Atg43(1–90), but not His-SUMO-Atg43(91–184). This suggests that Ppa2 and Yta4 bind to the same region of Atg43, increasing the possibility of competition between Yta4 and Ppa2 for binding to Atg43. Atg43(1–90) contains the AIM motif responsible for interacting with Atg8 during mitophagy (5). Therefore, we further divided this region into two parts (referred to as Atg43(1–45), containing the AIM motif, and Atg43(46–90)) and purified the truncated proteins from E. coli (Fig. 7, A and B). As shown in Figure 7C, GST-Ppa2 primarily precipitated His-SUMO-Atg43(46–90). Similarly, GST-Yta4(ΔTM) also precipitated His-SUMO-Atg43(46–90) (Fig. 7D). Therefore, Ppa2 and Yta4 interact with the same region of Atg43.

We next performed competitive binding assays to test how Yta4 and Ppa2 interact with Atg43. As shown in Figure 7E, the presence of His-Yta4(ΔTM) significantly reduced the affinity of His-SUMO-Atg43(46–90) for GST-Ppa2. This result suggests that Yta4 and Ppa2 interact with Atg43 in a competitive manner, indicating that Yta4 may promote the phosphorylation of Atg43 through inhibiting the interaction of Ppa2 and Atg43.

The reduced recovery of Atg43(46–90) can also be interpretated through an alternative model. Given that Yta4 physically interacts with Ppa2 (Fig. 6C), Yta4 could bind directly to Ppa2, inhibiting the interaction between Ppa2 and Atg43(46–90). Therefore, the observed competition likely results from Yta4 binding to both Atg43(46–90) and Ppa2. Importantly, both interpretations support our conclusion of a competitive interaction among Yta4, Ppa2, and Atg43.

To validate the regulatory role of the interaction between Yta4, Ppa2 and Atg43 in mediating Atg43 phosphorylation and mitophagy in vivo, we expressed an Atg43 mutant (Atg43(Δ46–90)), which lacks the region (a.a., 46–90) required for binding both Ppa2 (Fig. 7C) and Yta4 (Fig. 7D), in atg43Δ cells and assessed its effects on Atg43 phosphorylation and mitophagy. The results showed that the Atg43(Δ46–90) mutant displayed elevated levels of Atg43 phosphorylation (Fig. 8A) and mitophagy (Fig. 8B). These results demonstrate that Yta4 plays a critical role in alleviating the Ppa2-mediated suppression of Atg43 phosphorylation during mitophagy, thereby promoting efficient Atg43 phosphorylation and mitophagy.

Yta4-mediated mitophagy is essential for maintaining cell growth ability in response to long-term nitrogen starvation

Mitophagy in S. pombe is dispensable for short-term survival but becomes essential for maintaining cell growth ability after long-term nitrogen starvation (5). To directly test the role of Yta4-and Atg43-mediated mitophagy in long-term growth, we assessed the ability of cells to resume growth in nutrient-rich medium following 10 days of nitrogen starvation. Microscopic analysis across five independent experiments revealed that, while the restoration time for wild-type cells varied slightly, the restoration time for both the yta4Δ and Atg43(ΔAIM) strains was significantly prolonged relative to wild-type cells (Figs. 9A and S4). These data support the model that Yta4-and Atg43-mediated mitophagy is required to maintain cell growth ability during long-term nitrogen starvation.

Discussion

Msp1/ATAD1, the homolog of Yta4, has been reported to play a crucial role in maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis by extracting mistargeted TA proteins and stuck precursor proteins from the mitochondrial outer membrane (22, 31). However, whether Yta4 contributes to the regulation of mitophagy remains unknown. In this study, we demonstrate that Yta4 promotes mitophagy by facilitating the phosphorylation of the mitophagy receptor Atg43, achieved through the inhibition of the interaction between the phosphatase Ppa2 and Atg43 (Fig. 9B).

Multiple lines of evidence support this model. First, the absence of Yta4 specifically impairs mitophagy (Figs. 1A and S1A) without affecting bulk autophagy (Figs. 1B and S1, C and D), ER-Phagy (Fig. 1C), or pexophagy (Fig. 1D). Interestingly, in budding yeast, the absence of Msp1 (homology of Yta4) alone does not lead to mitophagy deficiency, which was observed only when both Msp1 and the GET pathway component are absent (32). This discrepancy indicates that Msp1/Yta4-dependent regulation of mitophagy may exhibit species-specific differences. Whether ATAD1, the mammalian homolog of Msp1/Yta4, is involved in regulating mitophagy remains an open question for future investigation.

Furthermore, we found that Yta4 modulates the phosphorylation of Atg43, which is the only reported mitophagy receptor in the fission yeast S. pombe (5). Phosphorylation of receptor proteins is a well-established regulatory mechanism in mitophagy (7, 33) and Atg43 indeed undergoes phosphorylation (Figs. 2, B–E and 3). Consistently, in the present work, we demonstrate that the phosphorylation of Atg43 is crucial for mitophagy and that Ser32, Ser35, and Ser36 within Atg43 are crucial phosphorylation sites regulating mitophagy (Fig. 3). However, mutation of Ser32, Ser35, and Ser36 in combination did not completely inhibit Atg43 phosphorylation and mitophagy (Fig. 3, D and E). Therefore, we hypothesized that additional phosphorylation sites within Atg43 are required to fully regulate efficient mitophagy. Intriguingly, Ser32, Ser35, and Ser36 sites are adjacent to the AIM motif (Fig. 3B), which is responsible for mediating interaction with Atg8 (5). It is, therefore, plausible that Atg43 phosphorylation at these residues might influence the interaction between Atg43 and Atg8. However, Atg43 also harbors numerous phosphorylation sites that are not involved in mitophagy regulation (Fig. 3, B and C). This indicates that Atg43 may possess additional cellular functions, which await further investigation. Atg43 phosphorylation is significantly delayed in cells lacking Yta4 (Fig. 2, B and D). Thus, Yta4 likely promotes mitophagy by facilitating Atg43 phosphorylation.

Since Msp1, the budding yeast homolog of Yta4, function as a transmembrane dislocase for TA proteins (20, 34), and Atg43 is a TA protein localized to the mitochondrial outer membrane (5), it is reasonable to hypothesize that Yta4 might regulate Atg43 phosphorylation by altering its mitochondrial localization. However, the absence of Yta4 does not appear to affect the mitochondrial accumulation or localization of Atg43 (Fig. 2G). Therefore, Yta4 modulates Atg43 phosphorylation via a dislocase-independent mechanism.

Biochemical analyses revealed that Yta4 regulates Atg43 phosphorylation in a phosphatase-dependent manner. We identified Ppa2 as the phosphatase responsible for Atg43 dephosphorylation (Fig. 4, A and B). Notably, the absence of Ppa2 led to hyperphosphorylation of Atg43 (Fig. 4, C and E) and enhanced mitophagy (Fig. 4, D and F), even in the absence of nitrogen starvation. Moreover, the absence of Ppa2 restored Atg43 phosphorylation and rescued the mitophagy defect caused by the absence of Yta4 (Fig. 4, E and F). This indicates that Yta4 works with Ppa2 to regulate Atg43 phosphorylation and mitophagy. However, we noted that while Atg43 phosphorylation levels were comparable between ppa2Δyta4Δ cells and ppa2Δ cells (Fig. 4E), mitophagy levels were higher in ppa2Δ cells than in ppa2Δyta4Δ cells (Fig. 4F). These results may also indicate that Atg43 phosphorylation is only one of several requirements for promoting mitophagy and that Yta4 may promote mitophagy through additional mechanisms. It is possible that Yta4 promotes mitophagy by directly facilitating the interaction between Atg43 and Atg8. In addition to Atg43, mitochondrial outer membrane proteins Tom70 and Mim1/2 are involved in facilitating mitophagy (5). Given the critical roles of Yta4, as well as its budding yeast and human homologs Msp1 and ATAD1, in maintaining the homeostasis of mitochondrial outer membrane proteins (31), it is possible that Yta4 promotes mitophagy through modulating mitochondrial outer membrane proteins such as Tom70 and/or Mim1/2. These models await further investigation. Nonetheless, our mechanistic investigation demonstrated that Yta4 inhibits the interaction between Ppa2 and Atg43 (Fig. 7E), resulting in efficient Atg43 phosphorylation and promoting mitophagy. Thus, we propose that Yta4 promotes Atg43 phosphorylation and mitophagy by inhibiting Ppa2-mediated dephosphorylation of Atg43 (Fig. 9B).

While we have identified the regulatory mechanisms through which Yta4 and Ppa2 modulate the Atg43 phosphorylation, we recognize that the regulatory network governing mitophagy in vivo is likely far more complex. Specifically, the precise binding interface between Yta4 and Ppa2, as well as the upstream kinase responsible for Atg43 phosphorylation, remain to be clearly defined. Therefore, we expect that further investigation will be required to characterize the additional regulatory roles of Yta4 and Ppa2 in mitophagy.

In conclusion, our study uncovers a novel function of the conserved AAA-ATPase Yta4 in promoting mitophagy under nitrogen starvation, which is crucial to maintain mitochondrial homeostasis. This work also reports a new regulatory mechanism of mitophagy in fission yeast.

Experimental procedures

Plasmid and strain construction

All plasmids used in this study (Table S1) were constructed using either restriction enzyme digestion (Yeasen) followed by T4 ligase (NEB)-mediated ligation or Exanase II (Vazymebiotech)-mediated recombination cloning. The plasmid pJK210-Pyta4-Yta4-13Myc (pCF.3134) was created as previously described (13). To construct the Atg43(Ser/Thr mutant) and Atg43(Δ46–90) plasmids, we first amplified the Patg43-Atg43 fragment from the S. pombe genomic DNA and integrated it into the pJK148 vector. Then Atg43 mutants were generated based on pJK148-Patg43-Atg43 (PCF.5512) by using the ClonExpress II One Step Cloning Kit (Vazymebiotech).

Yeast strains (Table S2) were generated either by random spore analysis or by tetrad-dissection (Singer instruments). For tagging and deletion of genes, we employed the PCR-based homologous recombination method with the pFA6a series of plasmids. For ectopic Yta4 expression, the plasmid pJK210-Pyta4-Yta4-13Myc was linearized with restriction enzyme StuI and integrated into the genome. For Atg43(WT), Atg43(Ser/Thr mutants) and Atg43(Δ46–90) expression, the related plasmids were linearized with restriction enzyme NruI and integrated into the genome. All PCR products and plasmids were transformed into yeast cells by using the lithium acetate method.

Yeast culture

Yeast strains were cultured in EMM medium (Formedium) supplemented with adenine, leucine, uracil, histidine and lysine (0.225 g/L each; referred to as EMM5S) at 30 °C. For nitrogen starvation experiments, yeast cells were grown to exponential phase (OD600 = 0.6–0.8) in EMM5S, harvested by centrifugation, washed, and resuspended in nitrogen starvation medium (EMM Broth without nitrogen (EMM-N), Formediun). For serial dilution growth assays, exponentially growing cultures (EMM5S) were normalized to equal optical density (OD600), then serially diluted (10-fold increments) in EMM5S, and spotted on YE medium supplemented with adenine, leucine, uracil, histidine and lysine (0.225 g/l each; referred to as YE5S) plates.

Protein extraction and western blotting analysis

Whole-cell protein extracts were prepared using the trichloroacetic acid (TCA) lysis method as previously described (35). Proteins were then analyzed by western blotting assays, using 10% SDS-PAGE. The primary antibodies used for immunoblotting were as follows: anti-Tubulin (BioAcademia, 63–160; 1:10,000 dilution), anti-GFP (Rockland, 600–101–215; 1:2000 dilution), anti-Atg43 (Homemade; 1:2000 dilution), anti- Myc (Thermo Fisher Scientific, SK260674; 1:2000 dilution). The secondary antibodies used in this study were as follows: HRP Goat Anti-Mouse Conjugate (Abclonal, AS003, 1:10,000 dilution), Goat Anti-Rabbit-HRP Conjugate (Bio-rad, 170–5046; 1:10,000 dilution), and Rabbit Anti-Goat HRP Conjugate (Abclonal, AS029; 1:10,000 dilution).

λ protein phosphatase treatment

Exponentially growing WT cells cultured in EMM5S were subjected to nitrogen starvation by transfer to EMM-N medium for the indicated time. Cells were harvested by centrifugation and washed once with distilled deionized water (ddH2O). Cell pellets were then resuspended in phosphatase buffer (Beyotime, P2316S) supplemented with 1 mM PMSF and protease inhibitor cocktail. Cells were further disrupted using glass beads in a Retsch MM400 mixer mill. The lysate was separated from glass beads by centrifugation (1000g, 1 min) and clarified by high-speed centrifugation (13,000g, 20 min, 4 °C). The supernatant was incubated with λ-protein phosphatase (70 U) at 30 °C for 1 h. Reactions were terminated by addition of 2 × SDS sample buffer (60 mM Tris-HCl [pH 6.8], 4% SDS, 4% β-mercaptoethanol, 5% glycerol, 0.002% bromophenol blue) and boiling at 100 °C for 5 min. Finally, protein samples were analyzed by western blotting with antibodies against Tubulin (BioAcademia, 63–160; 1:10,000 dilution) and Atg43 (Homemade; 1:2000 dilution).

Live-cell imaging and data analysis

Fission yeast strains were cultured in EMM5S medium at 30 °C until reaching exponential growth phase. Cells were then harvested and immobilized on microscope slides using 3% agarose pads prepared with EMM5S medium. Images were acquired by using a PerkinElmer UltraVIEW VoX spinning disk confocal microscope equipped with a Hamamatsu C9100-23B electron multiplying charge-coupled device camera and a Nikon CFI Apochromat TIRF 100X objective (NA = 1.49). All stack images, containing 11 planes at 0.5 μm intervals (z-depth, 5 μm), were acquired with Volocity software (PerkinElmer; www.perkinelmer.com). Image analysis was carried out using Metamorph (Molecular Devices; www.moleculardevices.com) and ImageJ (1.52, NIH; www.imagej.nih.gov).

Mitochondria isolation and fractionation

We used the Mitochondrial Yeast Isolation Kit (Abcam) to isolate mitochondria from exponentially growing yeast cultures (in EMM5S medium). According to the manufacturer's protocol, cytosolic and mitochondrial fractions were separated by differential centrifugation. Protein samples were boiled in SDS sample buffer (95 °C, 5 min) and analyzed by western blotting with antibodies against Tubulin (BioAcademia, 63–160; 1:10,000 dilution), GFP (Rockland, 600–101–215; 1:2000 dilution), Mti3 (Homemade, 1:2000 dilution), and tdTomato (Homemade, 1:2000 dilution).

Co-immunoprecipitation assays

Exponentially growing yeast cells in EMM5S medium were harvested by centrifugation (3000g, 5 min, 4 °C) and resuspended in TBS buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.4) containing 1 mM PMSF and protease inhibitor cocktail. Cell lysis was prepared by vortexing with 0.5 mm glass beads. After removal of glass beads, TBST buffer (TBS + 1% Triton X-100) containing 1 mM PMSF and protease inhibitor cocktail was added to lyse cells at 4 °C for 30 min followed by centrifugation (13,000g, 30 min, 4 °C) to obtain cleared lysates. For co-immunoprecipitation, Dynabeads Protein G magnetic beads (Yeasen) were conjugated with anti-Atg43 antibody (Homemade) before incubating with the cleared cell lysates at 4 °C for 2 h by gentle rotation. Beads were washed five times with TBST (0.1% Triton X-100) and once with TBS, then resuspended in SDS sample buffer and boiled at 100 °C for 5 min. Input and immunoprecipitated samples were analyzed by SDS-PAGE and western blotting using antibodies against Atg43 (Homemade; 1:2000 dilution), GFP (Rockland, 600–101–215; 1:2000 dilution) and Myc (Thermo Fisher Scientific, SK260674; 1:2000 dilution).

Protein expression and purification

Recombinant proteins, including His-SUMO-Atg43(1–184) (lacking the C-terminal transmembrane domain), His-SUMO-Atg43(1–90), His-SUMO- Atg43(91–184), His-SUMO- Atg43(1–45), His-SUMO-Atg43(46–90), and His-Yta4(33–355) (lacking the N-terminal transmembrane domain), were expressed in E. coli Rosseta cells induced with 0.5 mM IPTG at 16 °C overnight. All these proteins were purified using Ni-NTA resins (Yeasen) with Lysis/wash buffer (PBS, supplemented with 30 mM imidazole) and eluted with high-imidazole buffer (PBS, supplemented with 500 mM imidazole). Purified proteins were concentrated via molecular weight-cutoff filters concentrator, supplemented with cryoprotectant (5% glycerol for His-tagged proteins), flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored (−80 °C). GST, GST-Ppa2, GST-Yta4(33–355) were purified using glutathione agarose resins (Yeasen) with TBST buffer (Tris-buffered saline supplemented with 0.1% Triton X-100), supplemented with glycerol to a final concentration of 20%, and stored at −20 °C.

GST pull-down assays

Glutathione resins without fusion proteins and GST-bound resins served as negative controls. His-fused proteins were precleaned by centrifugation at 13,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C before incubating bait (GST-fused) and prey (His-SUMO-tagged) proteins in TBST buffer (Tris-buffered saline supplemented with 0.1% Triton X-100) at 4 °C for 2 h. To assess the interaction between GST-Yta4(33–355) and His-SUMO-Atg43(1–184), we additionally added 10 mM ATP. To assess competitive binding, His-Yta4(33–355) was incubated with GST-Ppa2 and His-SUMO-Atg43(46–90) at 4 °C for 2 h. After incubation, glutathione resins were sequentially washed five times with 1 × TBST buffer and once with 1 × TBS buffer, then boiled in SDS sample buffer at 100 °C for 5 min to elute bound proteins. The eluted protein samples were analyzed by SDS-PAGE and western blotting using antibodies against His (Abclonal, AE003; 1:2000 dilution) and GST (Abclonal, AE006; 1:2000 dilution).

Assessment of cell growth ability after long-term nitrogen starvation

Cell growth ability was assessed by microscopic imaging. Yeast cells cultured to exponential phase in EMM5S medium were collected, washed, and subjected to nitrogen starvation in EMM-N medium for 10 days. After treatment, cells were collected, washed, and cultured in YE5S medium for the indicated time. Images were acquired by using a PerkinElmer UltraVIEW VoX spinning disk confocal microscope (equipped with a Hamamatsu C9100–23B electron multiplying charge-coupled device camera and a Nikon CFI Apochromat TIRF 60X objective) or an Olympus IX73 microscope (equipped with an 100X objective and a cooled digital color camera (DP73-1-51)). Microscopic data were analyzed with Metamorph (Molecular Devices) and ImageJ (1.52, NIH).
