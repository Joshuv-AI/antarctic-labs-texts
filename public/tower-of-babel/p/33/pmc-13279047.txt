Association Between Calcium Supplementation and Recurrence of Cardiovascular Events in Patients With Cardiovascular Disease: A Population‐Based Cohort Study
Authors: Xiaowen Zhang, Kathryn Choon‐Beng Tan, Annie Wai‐Chee Kung, Gloria Hoi‐Yee Li, Ching‐Lung Cheung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe cardiovascular safety of calcium supplements in individuals with major cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains unclear. This study investigated the association between calcium supplementation and the risk of CVD recurrence in patients with established CVD.MethodsA retrospective population‐based study was conducted in Hong Kong of individuals aged ≥40 years newly diagnosed with CVD from January 1, 2006, to December 31, 2015. The treatment group consisted of patients with CVD prescribed calcium supplements, while the control group consisted of those who never prescribed calcium supplements from January 1, 2005, to December 31, 2020. Propensity score matching and Cox proportional hazard models were adopted to balance the baseline characteristics and estimate hazards ratios (HRs) for CVD recurrence.ResultsThe matched cohort included 17 720 patients with CVD in each arm. Calcium supplementation was associated with an increased risk of recurrence of adverse CVD events (HR, 1.10 [95% CI, 1.07–1.14]) and CVD‐related hospitalization or accident and emergency department attendance (HR, 1.16 [95% CI, 1.13–1.20]). Calcium‐only supplementation had higher CVD recurrence risk (HR, 1.21 [95% CI, 1.17–1.25]) than the combination of vitamin D (HR, 0.97 [95% CI, 0.93–1.01]), and a more pronounced association was observed in men (HR, 1.15 [95% CI, 1.09–1.20]) than women (HR, 1.07 [95% CI, 1.03–1.11]).ConclusionsIn patients with major CVD, calcium supplementation is associated with an increased risk of CVD recurrence, particularly with calcium‐only use and in men. Caution should be exercised when prescribing or using calcium supplements among patients with CVD.

Calcium supplements are commonly used to treat or prevent calcium deficiency and support bone health, particularly in postmenopausal women. However, concerns have emerged about their cardiovascular safety, 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 with multiple meta‐analyses suggesting an increased risk of myocardial infarction (MI), coronary heart disease (CHD), and stroke associated with the use of calcium supplements. 2 , 4 Nevertheless, these associations are not consistent across all studies. 6 , 7 Importantly, most randomized controlled trials evaluating calcium supplements were not specifically designed or adequately powered to evaluate cardiovascular outcomes, particularly in individuals with established cardiovascular disease (CVD). Consequently, their findings may not apply to this high‐risk population. Furthermore, while circulating calcium is thought to play a role in these associations, many studies have not accounted for serum calcium levels in their analyses.

Patients with established CVD are at heightened risk for recurrent cardiovascular events and often exhibit vascular calcification and compromised cardiac function, a context in which the potential adverse cardiovascular effects of calcium supplementation could be amplified. Nonetheless, the cardiovascular safety of calcium supplementation in individuals with established CVD remains understudied. Moreover, these patients tend to receive pharmacological and nonpharmacological interventions for secondary prevention, which should also be considered when assessing of the impact of calcium supplement use.

Previous observational studies in patients with aortic stenosis have suggested that calcium supplementation may worsen the condition, leading to a higher risk of aortic valve replacement and lower survival rates. 8 , 9 However, these findings are limited by narrow patient populations (restricted to aortic stenosis rather than major CVD) and small sample sizes. Considering that the secondary prevention of CVD is still an area requiring significant improvement, 10 a large‐scale population study evaluating the impact of calcium supplements, a widely used supplement globally, on CVD recurrence holds clinical significance and public health importance for high‐risk populations.

This study aimed to examine the relationship between calcium supplementation and the risk of CVD recurrence among patients with a history of major CVD and the interaction with serum calcium. A retrospective real‐world cohort design was used, leveraging a large‐scale territory‐wide electronic health record (EHR) database encompassing records of calcium supplement prescriptions.

Methods

Data Availability

The EHR data can be accessed for research purposes through the application of the Hong Kong Hospital Authority Data Sharing Portal (https://www3.ha.org.hk/data). The HKOS (Hong Kong Osteoporosis Study) data are not freely available, as the informed consent forms did not include data sharing.

Data Source

This study was based on a territory‐wide EHR database, the Clinical Data Analysis and Reporting System (CDARS), from January 1, 2006, to December 31, 2020. The CDARS, run by the Hospital Authority of Hong Kong, is the sole public health care provider in Hong Kong. The history of diagnosis and prescription in CDARS was obtained via International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision (ICD‐9) codes or British National Formulary codes, respectively. Notably, Hong Kong residents (eastern Asian individuals) generally have lower average dietary calcium intake, 11 and some clinicians may opt to prescribe calcium supplements for individuals with osteoporosis or fractures. All data for this study retrieved from the CDARS are anonymized. Ethics approval was obtained by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Hong Kong/HA HKW, Hong Kong Special Administrative Region (UW 23‐209).

Study Population and Design

A retrospective cohort study was conducted, and the schematic representation of the cohort selection is shown in the Figure1. Patients aged ≥40 years who had a diagnosis of their first‐ever CVD event, mainly involving acute MI, stroke including transient ischemic attack, and CHD, 12 , 13 from January 1, 2006, to December 31, 2015, were included. The ICD‐9 codes for defining the first CVD event are provided in Table S1. Those who died on the same day or during the hospitalization of the event were excluded. A total of 237 782 eligible patients were enrolled in the study.

Among them, patients prescribed oral calcium supplements (calcium carbonate, calcium lactate, calcium gluconate) and calcium supplements with vitamin D (either calcitriol or vitamin D2/D3 prescribed concurrently) are in the treatment group (n=38 586), and patients who had never been prescribed calcium supplements from January 1, 2005, to December 31, 2020, are the controls (n=192 460). Patients who prescribed calcium supplements within 1 year before the first CVD event but not thereafter were considered neither as treatment nor controls (n=6736). For those prescribed calcium supplements, the index date was defined as the prescription start date of calcium supplements; for the controls, they were assigned an index date of the same length of time from the first CVD event to supplementation following the distribution of calcium supplement. Those who were lost to follow‐up at the index date (in the control group) or had missing serum creatinine and serum calcium levels within 1 year before the index date were excluded. The exclusion of the missing data could potentially introduce bias, but this will be addressed by using the propensity score (PS)‐matching method. The treatment group consisted of 31 934 patients with newly prescribed calcium supplements, and the control group had 71 349 patients with no prescription of calcium supplements.

Study Outcomes and Follow‐Up

The primary outcomes were the recurrence of adverse composite CVD events. Similar to other research studying recurrent CVD, 13 the recurrence of adverse composite CVD events was ascertained, including acute MI, stroke, and CHD, the same as the first CVD event (Table S1). To address potential misclassification of CVD events in the EHR due to copy‐forward ICD coding, we further specified recurrence as CVD‐related hospitalization or accident and emergency department attendance. The secondary outcomes are the recurrence of 3 subtypes of CVD events of adverse composite CVD events. Each individual was followed up from the index date to the earliest occurrence of any study outcome, death, or the end of the study period (December 31, 2020).

PS Matching

To estimate the relationship of calcium supplements with CVD recurrence, PS‐matching analysis was adopted to reduce selection bias and minimize the imbalance in baseline characteristics between the treatment and control groups. The method involves matching patients who received treatment with individuals from an external comparator group on the basis of their characteristics. First, the PS for each individual in the study population was estimated using logistic regression, adjusting for all covariates measured at the index date as detailed in Table 1 and Table S1. Covariate selection was informed by literature and domain knowledge to ensure the inclusion of clinically relevant confounders, which includes lifestyle surrogates and hypocalcemia‐, chronic kidney disease (CKD)‐, osteoporosis‐, and CVD‐related conditions. Then, the treatment and control groups were matched through 1:1 PS matching without replacement, using a caliper of 0.2 SD, where 17 720 of 31 934 patients in the treatment arm were matched to 17 720 controls (Figure1). We consider a covariate was balanced if its computed standardized mean difference in treatment and control groups was <0.1.

Statistical Analysis

Cohort demographics were presented as means±SDs for continuous variables or as numbers and percentages for categorical variables. The Cox proportional hazards regression model for matched data was used to evaluate the association of calcium supplementation with the recurrence of adverse CVD events or death. We repeated the analyses by matching the subcategories of the treatment group to the controls (with or without vitamin D) and within each sex and serum calcium group (lower or higher than the median [2.25 mmol/L]).

Sensitivity Analysis

Multiple sensitivity analyses were conducted. First, we restricted the follow‐up only to the first reoccurrence of the CVD event, namely, the second CVD event, and repeated the analyses by excluding individuals who experienced CVD events between their initial event and index date. Second, we further adjusted for the time‐varying exposures using time‐dependent Cox proportional hazard regression and additionally adjusted for total cholesterol and low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol levels as indicators of CVD risk control. Third, we evaluated the dose–response relationship by comparing the calcium supplementation with controls according to average daily elemental calcium groups (<500, 500–1000, ≥1000 mg) and duration groups (<180, 180–365, ≥365 days). Fourth, a competing risk regression was used to account for the competing event of death when estimating hazard ratios (HRs) for CVD recurrence. Fifth, to further assess the robustness of the findings, we excluded those with major fractures before the incidence of CVD recurrence and nonadherent users (proportion of days covered [PDC] <80%) during the follow‐up, further adjusted for frailty on the basis of the Hospital Frailty Risk Score–derived ICD‐9 codes, 14 and repeated analyses on those without the diagnosis of overweight and obesity. Finally, the relationship between calcium supplementation and the risk of peripheral artery disease was further analyzed.

Hong Kong Osteoporosis Study

We further investigated the relationship between self‐reported habitual calcium supplementation and the risk of CVD recurrence in our HKOS cohort. The HKOS is a prospective cohort study aimed at evaluating the relationship of bone and mineral metabolism with various clinical outcomes. Details have been described previously. 15 In brief, 9449 individuals were recruited from the community from 1995 to 2010, and their baseline information on demographic information, drug and medical history, bone and mineral metabolism–related questions (including calcium supplement use in the past 5 years), and lifestyle information were obtained using detailed questionnaires. Moreover, bone mineral density was measured using a dual x‐ray absorptiometry (Hologic, Marlborough, MA). Among the 9449 participants, 636 of them had prevalent CVD. After excluding participants who had missing variables (n=294), 342 were included in the final analysis (Figure S1). Participants were followed up in silico using the data linkage with the CDARS using anonymized reference keys until the event of interest (recurrence of CVD), death, or end of study (June 18, 2022), whichever came first. Patients’ informed consents were collected, and ethics approval was obtained by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Hong Kong/HA HKW, Hong Kong Special Administrative Region (UW 23‐396).

In the HKOS, Cox proportional hazard regression was used to evaluate the self‐reported use of calcium supplements with recurrent CVD. The demographics—age, sex, body mass index, smoking status, drinking status, and physical activeness—were adjusted in the model. The definition of the demographics is provided in Table S1. In addition, bone mineral density at total hip; the number of adverse CVD event diagnoses on and before the index date; a history of CKD, diabetes, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, chronic liver disease, heart failure, major fractures, and thyroid disorders; a history of use of antiosteoporosis medications, renin–angiotensin system agents, β blockers, calcium‐channel blockers, antidiabetic drugs and insulins, antiplatelet agents, and steroids within 5 years before the index date; and baseline serum calcium were adjusted in the model.

In addition, the relationship was further stratified by prescription recorded versus without prescriptions (potential over‐the‐counter [OTC] users) and by body mass index categories (normal weight, 18.5–23 kg/m2; overweight, ≥23 kg/m2; nonobese, 18.5–27.5 kg/m2 in Asian individuals). Sarcopenia, defined by hand grip strength (men, <27 kg; women, <16 kg), was further adjusted to account for potential confounding by sarcopenia.

Results

Before PS matching, the patients prescribed calcium supplements were more likely to be women; be older; and have a history of CKD, osteoporosis, or major fractures, along with their associated common comorbidities and medications. Additionally, they exhibited lower serum calcium and higher serum creatinine levels compared with the controls (Table 1). After PS matching, the baseline characteristics of the treatment (n=17 720) and control groups (n=17 720) are all balanced (standardized mean difference<0.1; Table 1; Figure S2). The overall mean age of the matched cohort was 77.0±11.3 years, and 58.2% were women. The proportions of CKD, diabetes, osteoporosis, and major fractures were 43.0%, 30.6%, 5.1%, and 17.5% in the calcium supplementation group and 43.7%, 30.6%, 4.7%, and 17.1% in the control group, respectively (Table 1).

Patients with CVD were followed longitudinally from their first major CVD event to the index date and then to the outcome of interest or the end of the study. From the index date, the median follow‐up was 1.80 (interquartile range, 0.40–4.60) years, with the longest follow‐up extending to 14.97 years. During the total follow‐up time of 102 969.6 person‐years, 9356 and 8775 recurrences of adverse CVD events were observed in the treatment and control groups, respectively. The incidence rates of the recurrence were 184.9 per 1000 person‐years in the treatment group and 167.5 per 1000 person‐years in the control group (Table 2). Calcium supplementation was associated with an increased risk of recurrence of composite adverse CVD events (HR, 1.10 [95% CI, 1.07–1.14]) and the subtypes: acute MI (HR, 1.10 [95% CI, 1.03–1.18]), stroke (HR, 1.06 [95% CI, 1.01–1.10]), and CHD (HR, 1.09 [95% CI, 1.05–1.13]; Table 2). Additionally, calcium supplementation was associated with a higher risk of CVD‐related hospitalization and accident and emergency department visits (HR, 1.16 [95% CI, 1.13–1.20]; Table 2).

The sensitivity analyses also produced consistent results. When focusing on the outcome of the first reoccurrence of composite adverse CVD events and excluding those with CVD events reoccurring at the baseline, calcium supplementation was associated with an increased risk of reoccurrence (HR, 1.08 [95% CI, 1.04–1.12]; Table S5). Additionally, the results remain consistent after accounting for time‐varying covariates (HR, 1.11 [95% CI, 1.07–1.14]) and the level of CVD risk control on the basis of total cholesterol and low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol levels (Table S6). The dose–response relationship suggests an increasing risk of CVD recurrence as daily intake increases; meanwhile, calcium‐only use showed a greater risk compared with calcium with vitamin D use. For calcium‐only supplementation, a significantly increased risk of CVD recurrence was observed across all dosage and duration groups, with the highest risk at daily doses ≥1000 mg (HR, 1.58 [95% CI, 1.47–1.70]; Table S7).

Moreover, a significantly increased risk of CVD recurrence was also observed when adopting the competing risk regression, after excluding those with major fractures before the CVD recurrence considering the increased risk of CVD after fracture surgeries, after excluding those who are overweight and obese and after additional adjustment for frailty using the Hospital Frailty Risk Score (Table S8). The relationship was stronger after excluding nonadherent users (HR, 1.43 [95% CI, 1.38–1.47]; Table S8). In addition, we also observed increased risks of atherosclerotic peripheral artery disease (atherosclerosis of native arteries of the extremities) and broader peripheral vascular disease (including nonatherosclerotic, diabetic, unspecified) associated with calcium supplementation (Table S9). Given that peripheral artery disease is usually asymptomatic and underdiagnosed in clinical settings, these findings likely reflect risk of clinically severe peripheral artery disease.

In the HKOS cohort of participants with a prevalent CVD, 155 (45.3%) were women and 187 (54.7%) were men. The self‐reported calcium supplementation use was also associated with an increased risk of recurrence of composite adverse CVD events, with an HR of 1.52 (95% CI, 1.05–2.21; Table S10), consistent with the main findings. When stratified by hospital prescription versus potential OTC use, the association appeared stronger among potential OTC users (HR, 2.02 [95% CI, 1.32–3.09]; Table S10). Stratification by body mass index categories suggested a stronger association in participants who were overweight (HR, 2.02 [95% CI, 1.17–3.47]) than normal weight (HR, 1.12 [95% CI, 0.55–2.27]; Table S11). Results were similar after adjustment for sarcopenia (Table S12).

Discussion

The impact of calcium supplements on CVD recurrence risk in patients with preexisting CVD remains uncertain. In this large, retrospective PS‐matched cohort study of individuals with prior major CVD, calcium supplementation was associated with a significantly increased risk of recurrent adverse CVD events. Notably, calcium‐only supplementation was associated with a higher risk than combined calcium–vitamin D supplementation, and the association was more pronounced in men than in women.

To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to investigate the association between calcium supplementation and the recurrence of adverse CVD events in the population with major CVD. While recurrent CVD events are often assumed to share risk factors with initial events, 16 the cardiovascular safety of calcium supplements in the general population remains controversial. Some studies report an increased CVD risk associated with calcium supplementation, 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 whereas others suggest null associations 6 , 17 , 18 or even protective associations. 19 Meta‐analyses indicate that calcium supplements increased the risk of MI by 14% and CHD by 8%, with calcium‐only supplementation showing a greater risk (21% for MI and 20% for CHD). 2 Several other meta‐analyses also report an increased risk of CVD associated with calcium supplements in a range of 15% to 16%. 1 , 4 Our study extends this finding to a high‐risk population, observing an increased risk of CVD recurrence associated with calcium supplementation in patients with established major CVD. The comparable but not more pronounced risk among patients with CVD relative to the general population may be explained by the prevalent use of combined vitamin D, statins, and other cardiovascular medications, which could mitigate the adverse effects. Additionally, the estimated risk may be biased toward the null by exposure misclassification from the potential OTC calcium supplement use. OTC use is more likely to occur in the controls than in the treatment group, thus leading to an underestimation.

In addition, consistent with prior literature, we observed that the combination of calcium and vitamin D supplementation appeared less harmful than calcium‐only supplementation. Two meta‐analyses consistently reported a much higher risk of CVD associated with calcium supplementation without vitamin D compared with supplementation with vitamin D, 2 , 5 and a recent observational study reported a significant association of the increased CVD risk with calcium‐only supplementation but not with calcium with vitamin D supplementation. 3

The connection between calcium supplementation and the recurrence of CVD events seems plausible, as circulating calcium plays an important role in vascular and cardiovascular health. Studies have demonstrated that circulating calcium levels directly contribute to the development of coronary artery calcification 20 and aortic calcification, 21 both of which are strongly related to recurrent CVD. 22 Calcium supplement intake can lead to an instantaneous increase in serum calcium levels for >6 hours, 23 potentially disrupting calcium metabolism and increasing the risk of blood coagulation 24 and vascular calcification. 25 Population‐based studies also confirm the association between calcium supplementation and vascular calcification. 26 , 27

For the differential effects in calcium alone versus combined with vitamin D, a plausible mechanism is that the coadministered vitamin D may protect vessel walls against atherosclerosis, thereby mitigating the adverse effect of calcium. In animal models, vitamin D has been shown to regulate cholesterol efflux and macrophage polarization 28 and inflammatory process via vitamin D receptor in vessel walls. 29 , 30 For example, the activation of vitamin D receptor by calcitriol or its analogs was shown to downregulate angiotensin I expression and reduce local angiotensin II production 29 and stimulate the expression of angiotensin‐converting enzyme 2. 30 Meanwhile, because vitamin D deficiency is associated with higher CVD risk 31 , 32 , 33 and is prevalent in Asian populations, 34 correcting deficiency could improve CVD outcomes among users of calcium with vitamin D. Furthermore, calcium supplements require vitamin D to be absorbed into bones; whether the different degree of absorption results in heterogeneous mineral metabolism in coronary arteries warrants future investigation. Although a recent randomized controlled trial of 21 302 participants demonstrated that vitamin D supplementation may reduce the incidence of major cardiovascular events, 35 the overall evidence remains inconsistent. 36 Additional research is needed to further elucidate the cardiovascular safety differences between calcium supplementation with and without vitamin D.

Sex disparities in our results may reflect men's greater susceptibility to vascular calcification and higher vessel calcium scores and more calcified plaques than women, 37 , 38 explaining their greater risk of CVD recurrence. Meanwhile, in clinical practice, women have a generally lower calcium intake 11 and higher osteoporosis prevalence, 39 leading to more frequent combined prescriptions of calcium and vitamin D with closer monitoring for bone health. This interpretation is consistent with reports of a lower mortality rate associated with calcium supplementation in women than in men. 19 , 40

Notably, no interaction with baseline serum calcium level was observed. This is likely because the study matched calcium supplement users to controls on the basis of variables including baseline calcium levels, confining both groups to a low‐to‐normal range. Furthermore, serum calcium is tightly regulated by homeostatic mechanisms, and the latest baseline measurement may not capture transient postsupplementation increases, which could be more relevant to vascular risk.

In the HKOS cohort, we observed a higher risk in potential OTC users and those who are overweight. These patterns may suggest that the bias from health consciousness of OTC users is not evident, and the stronger association in participants who are overweight may reflect contributions from metabolic factors (eg, diabetes, adiposity) to CVD recurrence risk associated with calcium supplementation. Notably, the estimated HR among normal‐weight participants was >1, but insignificant due to limited sample size. Given the small HKOS sample, these findings should be considered supplementary and exploratory. Further studies are warranted to confirm these results.

Our study has several strengths. First, it is of a large sample size, ensuring sufficient statistical power. Second, a lot of key covariates were balanced after PS matching, including the baseline serum calcium and creatinine, as well as baseline medication of common drugs for treating CVD and osteoporosis that may interact with calcium supplementation. This balancing helps to minimize the potential bias due to confounding by indication. Additionally, stratified analyses help detect the heterogeneity in the association, and consistent results across sensitivity analyses indicate the robustness and reliability of our findings. However, our study has several limitations. First, this is an observational study; therefore, causality cannot be inferred, and the findings should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects. Second, the lack of data on OTC supplement use may underestimate our results, as mentioned above. Nevertheless, the consistent association observed for the self‐reported use of calcium supplements in the HKOS cohort analysis suggested that our finding was unlikely to be false positive. However, the lack of OTC data in CDARS still precludes analysis of CVD risk by indications. HKOS findings suggest that OTC users may have higher risk; however, these estimates are based on a small sample and should be interpreted with caution. Future studies are warranted to clarify differential risks between clinically prescribed and elective supplementation. Third, although PS matching balanced a wide range of covariates, unobserved residual confounders remain possible and limit the interpretation, which includes dietary calcium intake, adiposity, and direct smoking status. Our study assumes that doctors prescribed calcium supplements at appropriate dosages, considering patients' average dietary calcium intake. Nevertheless, the lack of dietary calcium intake restricts our ability to fully interpret the results in the context of total calcium consumption. Fourth, some degree of undercoding in the EHR is possible. However, such undercoding is unlikely to be systematically different between groups and thus would not be expected to bias our results substantially. Finally, the study participants are all Hong Kong residents, who tend to have lower average dietary calcium intake than White individuals 11 and a high prevalence of vitamin D deficiency. 41 Therefore, the generalizability of the results to other regions and populations remains uncertain.

The study has important implications. Given the increased risk of CVD recurrence, caution should be exercised in prescribing or using calcium supplements in patients with CVD. Our observational results, consistent with meta‐analyses, suggest that a combination of calcium and vitamin D supplementation is less concerning. The benefit of routine calcium supplementation for bone health is modest: Meta‐analyses show about a 1% increase in bone mineral density, a change not expected to result in antifracture efficacy, 42 and a trial in premenopausal women found no significant effect on bone mineral density. 43 However, in older adults with coexisting CVD and osteoporosis, calcium supplementation is often necessary during antiosteoporosis treatment to prevent hypocalcemia. In this context, calcium and vitamin D supplementation is preferred over calcium‐only supplementation, particularly for those with insufficient sunlight exposure. Moreover, while guidelines for calcium supplementation in patients with CKD have recently been established, 44 the guidelines for patients with CVD or other patients are still lacking and require future collaborative efforts.

Conclusions

In patients with a history of major CVD, the prescription of calcium supplementation is associated with an increased risk of CVD recurrence. Our study further reveals that higher risks were observed with calcium supplementation without vitamin D compared with calcium supplementation with vitamin D, as well as in men compared with women.
