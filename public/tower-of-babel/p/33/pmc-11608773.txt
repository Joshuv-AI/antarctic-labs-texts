Comparison between a Novel Knee Arthrometer and Simultaneous Stress Radiography for the Diagnosis of Complete and Partial Acute Anterior Cruciate Ligament Tears
Authors: Junqiao Li, Jiayao Zhang, Mingke You, Xiaolong Yang, Wenjing Ma, Qian Deng, Gang Chen, Xin Tang, Weili Fu, Yan Xiong, Qi Li, Jian Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe type of ligamentous tear and the degree of knee laxity have important guiding significance for the diagnosis and management of anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) tears. Instrumental measurement is necessary for ACL tears since physical examination and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) cannot provide an objective and quantitative assessment of knee laxity. This study aimed to compare the application of a novel knee arthrometer and simultaneous stress radiography in differentiating between complete and partial acute ACL tears, and further assess the correlation between the two measurements.MethodsA total of 106 patients with complete acute ACL tears and 52 patients with partial acute ACL tears were included in the study. Preoperative arthrometry and simultaneous stress radiography were performed using the Ligs arthrometer at 90, 120, and 150 N to assess side‐to‐side difference (SSD) in anterior knee laxity. The optimal threshold was determined using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) was used to assess the diagnostic value of the measurement. Pearson's correlation coefficient was used to assess the correlation between the two measurements.ResultsThe optimal differential SSD thresholds in the Ligs arthrometer were 2.7 mm at 90 N, 3.8 mm at 120 N, and 4.6 mm at 150 N. Similarly, the optimal differential SSD thresholds in simultaneous stress radiography were 3.8 mm at 90 N, 5.1 mm at 120 N, and 5.6 mm at 150 N. The AUC analysis revealed that the Ligs arthrometer was fairly informative at 90 N (AUC = 0.851), 120 N (AUC = 0.878), and 150 N (AUC = 0.884), and simultaneous stress radiography was highly informative at 90 N (AUC = 0.910), 120 N (AUC = 0.925), and 150 N (AUC = 0.948). Moreover, the AUC of the combined measurements was 0.914 at 90 N, 0.931 at 120 N, and 0.951 at 150 N. A significantly strong correlation was found between the two measurements at 90 N (r = 0.743, p < 0.001), 120 N (r = 0.802, p < 0.001), and 150 N (r = 0.823, p < 0.001).ConclusionsThe Ligs arthrometer and simultaneous stress radiography proved to be valid diagnostic tools to differentiate between complete and partial acute ACL tears, with a strong correlation between the two measurements in SSD values. Compared with single instrumental measurement, the combination of the two measurements can further improve the diagnostic value in this regard.Keywords: Anterior cruciate ligament, Knee arthrometer, Knee laxity, Stress radiography

Introduction

For the diagnosis and management of anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) tears, the type of ligamentous tear and the degree of knee laxity are two important factors that clinicians need to consider, as these assessments can optimize treatment options for different patients with ACL tears to reduce the weight of the burdens on the healthcare system and society caused by the associated surgical and rehabilitation costs.1, 2, 3 On the one hand, although traditional physical examination is indispensable for clinicians to quantitatively assess knee laxity, the grading is subjective and approximate rather than objective and accurate. On the other hand, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is highly accurate in qualitatively diagnosing ACL tears, but it is unable to provide a quantitative assessment of knee laxity for various types of ACL tears.4, 5, 6, 7 Therefore, additional auxiliary examinations are recommended in clinical practice to complement the above approaches.8, 9

The clinical application and popularization of instrumental measurements in patients with ACL tears have compensated for the shortcomings of physical examination and MRI in the assessment of knee laxity. It can be used not only to measure the specific degree of knee laxity before surgery, but also to assess postoperative outcomes after ACL reconstruction. In this regard, arthrometers such as the KT‐1000 (MEDmetric Corp., San Diego, USA) and GNRB (Genourob, Laval, France) and stress radiography such as the Telos device (GmbH, Hungen, Germany) are the two main measurement techniques used to quantify anterior knee laxity.10, 11, 12 However, the characteristics of the two techniques are not all the same. Arthrometers can be used for simple, rapid, objective, and radiation‐free assessment of knee laxity, while stress radiography can enable visual and direct assessment of anterior tibial translation (ATT) to avoid the influence of soft‐tissue effects on the measurement, but the disadvantages are extra imaging cost and radiation exposure. Overall, the diagnostic value of stress radiography for ACL tears is considered comparable with that of arthrometry.6, 13

The assessment of acute ACL tears is more difficult than that of chronic ACL tears due to multiple factors, such as reactive synovitis, hemarthrosis, and swelling. 14 For acute ACL tears, the diagnostic value of several arthrometers has been previously reported, among which the KT‐1000 and GNRB proved to be valid diagnostic tools.15, 16 As a novel knee arthrometer, the Ligs arthrometer (Innomotion Inc., Shanghai, China) was used not only to measure anterior knee laxity separately but also to assist stress radiography. Although recent studies have assessed the validity of the Ligs arthrometer for ACL tears, the application value of the Ligs arthrometer and simultaneous stress radiography in acute ACL tears still remains to be established.17, 18

The main purpose of this study was to first compare the application of the Ligs arthrometer and simultaneous stress radiography in differentiating between complete and partial acute ACL tears, further assess the correlation between the two measurements, and finally explore whether the combination of the two measurements could improve the diagnostic value. The hypotheses of this study were that (1) both the Ligs arthrometer and simultaneous stress radiography are valid diagnostic tools to differentiate between complete and partial acute ACL tears, that (2) there is a significant correlation between the two measurements, and that (3) the combination of the Ligs arthrometer and simultaneous stress radiography has higher diagnostic value than single instrumental measurement when using the Ligs arthrometer.

Materials and Methods

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee on Biomedical Research, West China Hospital of Sichuan University (No. 2023–282). A retrospective analysis was performed to assess the patients with acute ACL tears confirmed by arthroscopy at our hospital between July 2022 and October 2023. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) acute ACL tears within 6 weeks, (2) complete or partial ACL tears, (3) aged between 18 and 50 years, and (4) undergoing preoperative arthrometry and simultaneous stress radiography with the Ligs arthrometer. 19 The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) incomplete clinical data, (2) concomitant multiple ligament injuries, (3) bilateral ACL tears, (4) skeletal dysplasia, (5) knee valgus or varus deformity, (6) Kellgren‐Lawrence grade III or IV knee osteoarthritis, and (7) prior history of trauma or surgery in either knee.

A total of 158 patients with acute ACL tears were ultimately identified in this study (Figure 1). The patients included 127 males and 31 females, with a mean age of 26.96 ± 6.21 years (range, 18–47 years). The following data were recorded for each patient: the demographic characteristics, injury side of the knee, interval from injury to surgery, preoperative anterior knee laxity, and the type of ACL tear and meniscal status under arthroscopy.

Testing Device

The Ligs arthrometer was developed to measure knee laxity with the characteristics of digitization, convenience, portability, and non‐radiation. It consists of an adjustable fixing bracket and a mainframe with the built‐in computer system and sensors, which is allowed to acquire the corresponding knee displacement at the real‐time load with a precision of 0.1 mm (Figure 2). During the measurement, a continuous load is applied to the knee joint using the operating handle on the mainframe. The load and displacement will be recorded automatically by the sensors on the top of the push rod and shown on the display screen of the mainframe. Furthermore, the computer system will synchronously plot a load‐displacement curve to provide auxiliary reference for the assessment of knee laxity. If necessary, the push rod can be locked to provide a constant load when the load reaches a certain value, thus assisting the radiographic equipment in performing stress radiography.17, 18

Preoperative Arthrometric Measurement

An experienced physical therapist (Q.D.) used the Ligs arthrometer to preoperatively measure anterior knee laxity in all patients with acute ACL tears. Before this study, the examiner passed the assessment after reading the user manual and receiving training from the manufacturer, and had 3 years of practical experience in using the device. During the measurement, the patients were placed in a lateral decubitus position with 30° of knee flexion and neutral rotation. 20 The examiner required the patients to relax their lower limb muscles and checked the muscle contractions before every measurement. After confirming the above preparations, a tolerable anterior load of 0 to 150 N was applied to each patient at a constant speed of 3 N/s. The Ligs arthrometer started to automatically collect load and displacement data when the load exceeded 20 N by default to reduce the impact of soft‐tissue effects. Each knee was measured three times consecutively by the same examiner to determine the intra‐rater reliability. The healthy knee was always measured first, followed by the injured knee. The average of three measurements on each knee was taken to calculate the side‐to‐side difference (SSD) in anterior knee laxity at 90, 120, and 150 N.17, 21

Preoperative Stress Radiographic Measurement

With the assistance of the physical therapist operating the Ligs arthrometer, a trained radiology technician performed stress radiography by an X‐ray machine to obtain true lateral view radiographs of the bilateral knees at baseline, 90, 120, and 150 N (Figure 3A). The radiography protocol was similar to that used in the study by Milano et al. to ensure correct knee flexion position and the superimposition of the medial femoral condyle (MFC) and the lateral femoral condyle (LFC). 22

Preoperative arthrometric and stress radiographic measurements. (A) The patient was placed on the x‐ray table to undergo arthrometry and simultaneous stress radiography using the Ligs arthrometer. (B) Anterior translation in the medial compartment (ATMC) was defined as the distance between the two tangent lines of the most posterior contour of the medial femoral condyle (MFC) and the most posterior contour of the medial tibial plateau (MTP) perpendicular to the MTP joint line on the true lateral view radiograph.

A senior musculoskeletal radiologist measured anterior translation in the medial compartment (ATMC) on the true lateral view radiograph as ATT using the picture archiving and communication system (PACS, Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany). The ATMC was defined as the distance between the two tangent lines of the most posterior contour of the MFC and the most posterior contour of the medial tibial plateau (MTP) perpendicular to the MTP joint line (Figure 3B).12, 20, 23 The measurement was repeated three times to assess the intra‐rater reliability of the stress radiographic measurement on the true lateral view radiograph, and the average of three measurements was recorded as the final result of the ATT. The SSD value was calculated by comparing the ATT of bilateral knees at 90, 120, and 150 N relative to the baseline.

Arthroscopic Assessment of Acute ACL Tears

Two experienced surgeons (Y.X. and Q.L.) assessed the type of acute ACL tears for all patients by reviewing the intraoperative arthroscopic images, and any disputes were resolved by a third senior surgeon (J.L.). Based on the assessment criteria for partial and complete ACL tears, an isolated anteromedial (AM) or posterolateral (PL) bundle tear was classified as a partial ACL tear (Figure 4A,B), and a full‐thickness ACL tear or a lax and nonfunctional ACL was classified as a complete ACL tear (Figure 4C).11, 12, 24, 25

Statistical Analyses

The data were analyzed with Stata software version 15 (Stata Corp., Texas, USA). The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of the variables. Normally distributed quantitative variables were compared between the two groups using the independent samples t‐test. The Mann–Whitney U test was used for comparison of quantitative variables without a normal distribution. Qualitative variables were compared using the Chi‐square test. Intra‐rater reliability was assessed using the intra‐class correlation coefficient (ICC). 26 The sensitivity (Se), specificity (Sp), and optimal threshold were determined using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) was used to assess the diagnostic value of the measurement: null (AUC = 0.5), poorly informative (0.5 < AUC < 0.7), fairly informative (0.7 ≤ AUC < 0.9), highly informative (0.9 ≤ AUC < 1), or perfect (AUC = 1). 27 Pearson's correlation coefficient and Bland–Altman analysis were used to assess the correlation and agreement between the Ligs arthrometer and simultaneous stress radiography. 28 A p value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Among the 158 patients with acute ACL tears included in the analysis, 106 patients had complete ACL tears, and 52 patients had partial ACL tears composed of 37 patients with AM bundle tears and 15 patients with PL bundle tears. Clinical Data

There were significant differences in the Ligs arthrometer and simultaneous stress radiography for SSD measurements at 90, 120, and 150 N between the complete and partial acute ACL tear groups (p < 0.001 for all). Furthermore, patients with complete acute ACL tears had a significantly higher incidence of lateral meniscal tears than those with partial acute ACL tears (p = 0.009), but there was no significant difference in the incidence of medial meniscal tears (p = 0.384) (Table 1).

Intra‐Rater Reliability of the Measurements

In acute ACL tears, the intra‐rater reliability of the Ligs arthrometer for SSD measurements was good at 90 N (ICC = 0.772, 95% CI: 0.642 to 0.868), 120 N (ICC = 0.804, 95% CI: 0.687 to 0.887), and 150 N (ICC = 0.818, 95% CI: 0.708 to 0.896). By comparison, SSD measurements on stress radiographs showed excellent intra‐rater reliability at 90 N (ICC = 0.978, 95% CI: 0.940 to 0.993), 120 N (ICC = 0.969, 95% CI: 0.916 to 0.991), and 150 N (ICC = 0.933, 95% CI: 0.828 to 0.980).

Validity of the Measurements

According to the ROC curve analysis, the optimal differential SSD thresholds in the Ligs arthrometer were 2.7 mm (Se = 78.3%, Sp = 82.7%) at 90 N, 3.8 mm (Se = 72.6%, Sp = 90.4%) at 120 N, and 4.6 mm (Se = 71.7%, Sp = 96.2%) at 150 N for the differentiation between complete and partial acute ACL tears. On the other hand, the optimal differential SSD thresholds in simultaneous stress radiography were 3.8 mm (Se = 83.0%, Sp = 86.5%) at 90 N, 5.1 mm (Se = 84.9%, Sp = 88.5%) at 120 N, and 5.6 mm (Se = 92.5%, Sp = 90.4%) at 150 N (Table 2 and Figure 5).

Diagnostic Value of the Measurements

The AUC analysis revealed that the Ligs arthrometer was fairly informative to differentiate between complete and partial acute ACL tears at 90 N (AUC = 0.851), 120 N (AUC = 0.878), and 150 N (AUC = 0.884). As a comparison, simultaneous stress radiography was highly informative at 90 N (AUC = 0.910), 120 N (AUC = 0.925), and 150 N (AUC = 0.948). Moreover, the AUC of the combined measurements was 0.914 (Se = 81.1%, Sp = 88.5%) at 90 N, 0.931 (Se = 85.8%, Sp = 90.4%) at 120 N, and 0.951 (Se = 93.4%, Sp = 90.4%) at 150 N, respectively (Table 2 and Figure 5). Compared with single arthrometry or stress radiography, the combined measurements had higher diagnostic value at the same load.

Discussion

The main findings of this study were that the Ligs arthrometer and simultaneous stress radiography were valid to differentiate between complete and partial acute ACL tears at 90, 120, and 150 N. Second, there was a significantly strong correlation between the two measurements in SSD values. Furthermore, the combined measurements had higher diagnostic value than single instrumental measurement when using the Ligs arthrometer.

Comparison between Arthrometer and Stress Radiography

Arthrometers are common clinical tools for objectively quantifying knee laxity with the advantages of noninvasiveness, simplicity, and no radiation. The KT‐1000 is the most widely used arthrometer and is regarded as the gold standard in arthrometry for ACL tears. However, it can only measure anterior knee laxity at specific manual loads with relatively low precision. As a contrast, the GNRB is a robotic arthrometer equipped with pressure and motion sensors to conduct automatic measurements with a precision of 0.1 mm.11, 29, 30 Moreover, as a portable and digital arthrometer, the Ligs arthrometer has been used in recent studies for preoperative and postoperative assessment of ACL tears, which can provide a continuous load to measure anterior knee laxity with the same precision as the GNRB.17, 18, 21, 31 Although the validity and reliability of arthrometers for the diagnosis of ACL tears varied between different studies, both generally depended on the familiarity and skill of the examiner to some extent. 6

Stress radiography is another noninvasive technique to objectively quantify knee laxity. The main strength of stress radiography over arthrometer is that it avoids the influence of soft‐tissue effects on the measurement of knee laxity, which can account for more than 50% of anterior displacement.32, 33 Moreover, it provides a visualized and retrievable record for ATT measurements. A variety of stress radiography techniques are available for the diagnosis of ACL tears, with the Telos device being the most widely used. 13 Additionally, the application of arthrometers such as the KT‐1000 and the Genucom in assisting stress radiography has been reported in only a few studies.34, 35 However, a systematic review by James et al. revealed that there was currently no consensus on the optimal stress radiography technique for the diagnosis of ACL tears. 13 In addition, different measurement methods on stress radiographs may affect the results of ATT.36, 37 The ATMC measurement was considered to be superior to that of the lateral compartment with better validity and reliability, which was used in this study to help optimize the measurement on stress radiographs.6, 12, 13, 38, 39

The comparison between arthrometry and stress radiography in patients with ACL tears remains controversial at present. Although Fleming et al. reported that the KT‐1000 showed better validity than stress radiography for ACL tears, other studies havesuggested that a variety of stress radiography techniques have comparable diagnostic performance to the KT‐1000.34, 40, 41, 42 In terms of the GNRB, most current studies agreed that its diagnostic performance was superior to that of stress radiography, which made the GNRB becoming the preferred option for knee laxity measurement.12, 15, 25 Specifically, Ryu et al. found that the diagnostic value of the GNRB at different loads was better than that of the Telos device and the Lachman test for acute ACL tears. 15 In addition, an isolated study comparing the Rolimeter and the Telos device reported that the latter was more accurate than the former for the diagnosis of complete ACL tears. 7

Comparison of SSD Measurements between the Ligs Arthrometer and Simultaneous Stress Radiography

To assess anterior knee laxity in acute ACL tears, we used the Ligs arthrometer to perform arthrometry and simultaneous stress radiography and compared the two measurements. The mean SSD value in simultaneous stress radiography was higher than that in arthrometry at the same load. This may indicate that when using the Ligs arthrometer to assess acute ACL tears, arthrometry underestimated the severity of anterior knee laxity, while the results of simultaneous stress radiography may be closer to the true severity. Similarly, the study by Stäubli et al. also showed that simultaneous stress radiography had a higher mean SSD value in anterior knee laxity than arthrometry when using the KT‐1000 for ACL tears. 34 Besides, Panisset et al. compared different devices and found that the Telos device measured higher SSD values than the Rolimeter for both complete and partial ACL tears. 7 On the other hand, Lee et al. reported that anterior knee laxity in stress radiography was significantly greater than that in arthrometry at different knee flexion positions for patients with complete ACL tears. 37

In the present study, one of the potential reasons that could exacerbate this measurement difference was that arthrometry using the Ligs arthrometer on the affected knee was more susceptible to soft‐tissue effects caused by the patient's pain and muscle guarding during the acute phase. Additionally, the choices of osseous landmarks and reference points had some degree of influence on stress radiographic measurement as it was conducted manually. In particular, the measurement error mainly resulted from the inaccurate positioning of osseous landmarks on stress radiographs.6, 13, 15, 20, 34, 40

Correlation and Agreement between the Ligs Arthrometer and Simultaneous Stress Radiography

Although the measurements obtained using different devices were not considered comparable with one another, a strong correlation (r = 0.743–0.823) was observed in SSD values between arthrometry and simultaneous stress radiography with the Ligs arthrometer in this study.6, 43 Several studies have also reported a positive correlation (r = 0.37–0.67) between the two measurements in ACL tears when using different arthrometers and stress radiography techniques.25, 34, 40 Furthermore, the Bland–Altman analysis indicated acceptable agreement between the two measurements for the entire cohort, with most values falling within the 95% LoA. In this regard, Lerat et al. suggested that arthrometry had the best correspondence with the mean ATT of the bilateral compartments in stress radiography, as the arthrometer was located at the middle of the knee joint to measure the ATT of the center of the knee joint, which corresponded to the mean ATT of the bilateral compartments. 40

Comparison of the Validity and Diagnostic Value between the Ligs Arthrometer and Simultaneous Stress Radiography

With respect to the differentiation between complete and partial acute ACL tears, simultaneous stress radiography showed better validity and diagnostic value than arthrometry with the Ligs arthrometer at the same load. In particular, the Ligs arthrometer at 150 N provided a sensitivity of 71.7% and a specificity of 96.2% with a 4.6‐mm SSD threshold and was fairly informative (AUC = 0.884). By contrast, simultaneous stress radiography at 150 N had a sensitivity of 92.5% and a specificity of 90.4% with a 5.6‐mm SSD threshold and was highly informative (AUC = 0.948). Based on our findings, simultaneous stress radiography was preferred as a more effective diagnostic tool to differentiate between different types of acute ACL tears when using the Ligs arthrometer in clinical settings. Moreover, it was noticed that the optimal differential SSD threshold in simultaneous stress radiography at 120 N (SSD = 5.1 mm) and 150 N (SSD = 5.6 mm) was more than 5 mm, which was generally considered as a SSD threshold for determining surgical treatment in ACL tears. 44 Under these circumstances, a complete acute ACL tear diagnosed through stress radiography assisted by the Ligs arthrometer was often suggestive of a surgical indication. Consequently, the application of the above measurement protocols can help clinicians differentiate between complete and partial acute ACL tears while assisting in the development of treatment plan.3, 45, 46, 47, 48, 49

Different knee flexion positions also have an influence on anterior knee laxity measurement. According to the previous literature, the 30° knee flexion position was significantly superior to other knee positions for both arthrometry and stress radiography. 20 In fact, patients with acute ACL tears usually maintain a slight knee flexion position to reduce knee pain caused by reactive synovitis, hemarthrosis, and swelling.14, 16 Therefore, the use of a 30° knee flexion position in this study helped to improve the diagnostic value of instrumental measurement in differentiating between complete and partial acute ACL tears, as well as increase the maximum pain tolerance threshold of patients during the measurement.

Combined Use of the Ligs Arthrometer and Simultaneous Stress Radiography

In view of the respective advantages of arthrometry and stress radiography, this study combined these two measurements with the Ligs arthrometer, the diagnostic value of which was further improved compared with that of single instrumental measurement. This finding was consistent with that reported by Lee et al., who recommended the combined use of arthrometer and stress radiography in diagnosing complete ACL tears. 20 Additionally, Panisset et al. supported physical examination in combination with the Telos device or the Rolimeter for the diagnosis and management of complete and partial ACL tears. 7 Although some studies have suggested stress radiography as a tool to assist in MRI assessment, given the higher cost and longer waiting period of the latter, the combination of arthrometry and stress radiography may be another potential option for differentiating between different types of acute ACL tears, which is relatively inexpensive and easier to implement.4, 6, 13, 50, 51

Strengths and Limitations

The strength of this study was that we introduced the application of a novel knee arthrometer and simultaneous stress radiography in acute ACL tears, which provided new instrumental measurement approaches to differentiate between complete and partial acute ACL tears. There were also some limitations in the study. First, this was a single‐center retrospective study with an inherent selection bias, and more large‐scale multicenter prospective studies are needed to further validate our findings. Second, the measurement bias caused by instrumental operation, patient awareness, and BMI cannot be fully eliminated. Third, owing to the lack of other proven arthrometers or stress radiography techniques, we were not able to compare the diagnostic performance of the Ligs arthrometer with them. In addition, the present study only selected the optimal knee flexion position of 30° to perform arthrometry and simultaneous stress radiography, and did not compare the conditions at other knee flexion positions. 20 Finally, the inter‐rater reliability of the Ligs arthrometer in acute ACL tears was not assessed in this study, as the measurement was performed by a single experienced physical therapist to reduce the influence related to the examiner's familiarity and skill. 52

Conclusion

The Ligs arthrometer and simultaneous stress radiography proved to be valid diagnostic tools to differentiate between complete and partial acute ACL tears. There was a strong correlation between the two measurements in SSD values. However, simultaneous stress radiography showed a higher diagnostic value in differentiating the type of acute ACL tears than arthrometry at the same load. Compared with single instrumental measurement, the combination of the two measurements can further improve the diagnostic value in this regard.
