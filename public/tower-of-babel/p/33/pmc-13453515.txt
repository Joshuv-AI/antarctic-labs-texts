Research note: Zic5 alters digit morphology during avian limb development☆
Authors: Yining Tang, Shanshan Li, Shibin Bai, Songyang Shang, Ziqiu Jia, Junpeng Zhang, David M. Irwin, Shuyi Zhang, Zhe Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Avian limbs have evolved precise digit lengths and shapes that are optimized for flight and locomotion, but the transcriptional regulators that regulate this architecture remain poorly defined. Although anteriorly biased Zic3 expression constrains digit outgrowth, whether other zinc finger of the cerebellum (Zic) factors—particularly Zic5—shape avian distal limb morphology remains unknown. Here, we define the spatiotemporal dynamics and developmental function of Zic5 expression in the chicken limb and evaluate its role in shaping avian limb and digit morphogenesis. Whole-mount in situ hybridization reveals that Zic5 shows a dynamic, stage-dependent shift from broad distal mesenchymal expression to digit-base–restricted domains in the chicken limb, consistent with a role in constraining digit elongation to shape fore–hind limb morphology. Overexpression of Zic5 in the chicken limb buds resulted in shortened digits and impaired limb ossification. These phenotypes were accompanied with altered expression of skeletal marker genes, including Sox9, Runx2, Col9a1, Col9a3, Ptch2 and Hhip, suggesting that elevated Zic5 expression is sufficient to impair skeletal maturation during limb development. Together, our results indicate that elevated Zic5 expression can impair limb skeletal growth and maturation, with effects on distal digit development that result in digit shortening or loss and suggest that Zic5 may contribute to the regulation of digit architecture during avian morphological diversification.

Introduction

The avian appendicular skeleton represents a case of structural specialization, where both forelimbs and hindlimbs exhibit precise regulation of digit length and number to accommodate the divergent demands of powered flight and terrestrial locomotion. A fundamental commonality across both appendages is the regulation of digit proportions. Digit I (DI) remains the shortest digit in both limbs, despite arising from a homeotic frameshift in the manus (Towers, 2018). DIII is consistently the longest element in both avian fore- and hindlimbs. DIV is shorter than DIII but slightly longer than the positional DII in the avian hindlimbs, and DV is completely lost following the ancestral tetrapod reduction pattern (Seki et al., 2012). Collectively, these proportioning rules in birds ensure a mechanically optimized autopodium that accommodates the distinct biomechanical requirements of aerodynamic flight and terrestrial locomotion. By maintaining this reproducible architectural framework, birds balance developmental robustness with functional performance to facilitate ecological diversification without compromising structural integrity (Seki et al., 2012).

Diversity in avian limb digit morphology has been shaped by the complex interplay of natural selection and developmental constraint. Consistent with a digit-specific regulatory logic, the anteriorly enriched transcription factor Zic3 has been identified as a pivotal regulator of avian digit morphogenesis, since its overexpression results in pronounced digit shortening (Li et al., 2025a). Intriguingly, this regulatory capacity appears to be conserved within the zinc finger of the cerebellum (Zic) gene family, prompting an investigation into related factors, such as Zic5, which are essential for normal growth and embryonic development in vertebrates.

A previous study has reported that Zic5 is largely restricted to the distal limb bud of Xenopus laevis, with persistent distal-anterior enrichment and only transient posterior signal (Hudson et al., 2022). In mice, Zic5 is also distal-enriched early and later becomes regionally restricted within the limb mesenchyme (Inoue et al., 2004). Zic5−/− mutant mice exhibit skeletal defects affecting structures such as the mandible, sternum, vertebral arches, and tail, suggesting a role for Zic5 in skeletal development (Inoue et al., 2004; Kenryo et al., 2005). However, overt limb skeletal defects have not been clearly reported in Zic5-deficient mice, raising the possibility that Zic5 function in the limb may be masked by functional compensation among related Zic family members (Kenryo et al., 2005). Therefore, we conducted in vivo gain-of-function experiments to study the developmental function of Zic5 in avian limbs, and combined with its spatiotemporal expression pattern, aiming to elucidate the molecular mechanisms that contribute to the diverse morphology of avian limbs.

Materials and methods

Compliance and ethics

All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Shenyang Agricultural University (approval ID: 202106038).

Chicken embryo collection

Fertilized specified pathogen-free (SPF) White Leghorn chicken (Gallus gallus) eggs were obtained from a commercial production facility (Boehringer Ingelheim Viton Biotechnology Co., Beijing, China).

Whole-mount in situ hybridization (WISH)

WISH was performed as previously described (Li et al., 2025a, 2025b). Briefly, embryos were fixed overnight in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), dehydrated through a graded methanol series, and stored at −80°C. Prior to hybridization, embryos were rehydrated, treated with 10 μg/mL proteinase K, washed in PBST, and refixed in 0.2% glutaraldehyde/4% PFA. Embryos were then hybridized overnight at 65°C in prewarmed hybridization buffer containing 2 μg/mL DIG-labeled probes. Embryos were then blocked and incubated overnight with alkaline phosphatase–conjugated anti-DIG antibody, followed by washes and color development in buffer containing NBT/BCIP (Roche). Reactions were stopped at the desired signal intensity, and embryos were extensively washed in 1% Triton X-100 to reduce background. Finally, embryos were fixed in 4% PFA and imaged using a Leica stereomicroscope (M165FC). Probes for Zic5 were generated using primers: Forward (F): 5′ –GCCATGTCACCGTGGAG– 3′, Reverse (R): 5′ –GGGAGTGCTTCTTTCTGT– 3′.

Skeletal staining

For cartilage and bone staining, embryonic limbs were dissected in PBS and skinned, fixed overnight in 95% ethanol, incubated overnight in acetone, stained with Alcian blue and Alizarin red, and cleared in 1% KOH until transparent. Digit lengths were measured using ImageJ software (v.1.43o) by tracing the longitudinal midline of each digit from the proximal base to the distal tip.

Sequence alignment

Coding sequences (CDS) for chicken Zic5 and Zic3 were obtained from the NCBI genome database (Accession numbers: Zic5, XM_015274907.4; Zic3, XM_040700079.2). Protein sequences were aligned using Clustal Omega. Functional domains were annotated based on UniProt. The percentage of amino acid identity was determined as:Identity=[NumberofidenticalresiduesLengthofalignment]×100%

Construction and infection of RCAS-Zic5 in chicken embryo limbs

The coding sequence of chicken Zic5 (NCBI Refseq XM_015274907.4) was synthesized and cloned into RCAS at the Spe I / Cla I sites (GENEWIZ, Suzhou, China). The RCAS vector was generously provided by Dr. Clifford J. Tabin. RCAS-Zic5 virus was generated from DF-1 cells (CL-0279, Wuhan, China). Briefly, DF-1 cells were transfected with RCAS-Zic5, RCAS and RCAS-GFP vectors using FuGENE®6 Transfection Reagent (Promega, USA). Cell culture supernatants were collected 15–17 days post-transfection, filtered through a 0.45 μm filter, and concentrated by ultracentrifugation at 50,000 × g for 3.5 h at 4°C using an Optima XPN ultracentrifuge (Beckman Coulter, USA). Viral titers, determined by endpoint dilution on DF-1 cells with PCR-based detection of the viral env gene, consistently exceeded 2 × 10⁷ infectious units (IU)/mL. The viral preparations were aliquoted and stored at −80°C until further use. Using a microinjection system (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany), 0.2–0.3 μL of virus solution containing fast green dye was injected into the expected fore- and hindlimb regions (lateral plate mesoderm, LPM) at HH10. Embryos were collected at HH33, and the uninfected left limb served as a control for assessing Zic5 overexpression induced morphological changes.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from the zeugopods and autopods of hindlimb buds of Zic5-overexpressing and RCAS-injected controls using a RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany). Three independent biological replicates were included for each group, with each replicate derived from an independent embryo. cDNA was synthesized with PrimeScript™ RT Kit (Takara, China). RT–qPCR was performed in triplicate across three independent experiments using TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II (Takara, China) on a LightCycler® 96 system Roche, Switzerland). TBP was used as the internal control, and relative expression were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are listed below: Zic5 F: 5′ – GACTCACACAGGGGAGAAGC – 3′, R: 5′ – TGTGGACATGGGAGTGCTTC – 3′; Runx2 F: 5′ – TGGGAACGACGAGAACTA – 3′, R: 5′ – GGTATGTAGCGACTTGGG – 3′; Col9a1 F: 5′ – GTGGGTTCTACTGCTCTAC – 3′, R: 5′ – GAATCTGCCAAATAGTCC – 3′; Col9a3 F: 5′ – GTGTCCGAGGCTTCCAG – 3′, R: 5′ –GCACCAATAACACCAGCAG – 3′; Sox9 F: 5′ – CTGACCAGTACCCGCATC – 3′, R: 5′ –CGTTCTTCACCGACTTCC – 3′; Ptch2 F: 5′ – GCCACCTTTGACAGAGAC – 3′, R: 5′ – ACCGTCAGGCAGATGTAG – 3′; Hhip F: 5′ – CTGTCTTGGGATTTGGTGA – 3′, R: 5′ –GAGCTGTTCTTTTGCATTCTT – 3′.

Statistical analysis

All results shown are representative of at least three independent samples per probe. Experimental data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and analyzed by Student's t-test. P-values less than 0.05 were considered significant. *, P < 0.05; **, P < 0.01; ***, P < 0.001.

Results and discussion

We firstly examined the Zic5 expression pattern in chicken limbs from embryonic stages 22 to 29 (HH22–29). At HH22, Zic5 was highly expressed in the distal mesenchyme from the anterior to the posterior regions of fore- and hindlimb buds (Fig. 1A). At HH23–25, Zic5 was expressed broadly in the prospective autopod region, but had reduced expression at the prospective DIV positional territory in the fore- and hindlimbs, where digit development generally initiates first in tetrapods (Towers, 2018). From HH26 to HH28, the hindlimb bud only expressed Zic5 at the anterior and the posterior margins, which are the territories for DI and the reduced DV. Due to the developmental heterochrony in avian limbs, the forelimb maintained the broad expression pattern until HH27 and then became similar to the hindlimbs at HH28. Finally, following preliminary digit patterning and the onset of ossification, Zic5 was nearly undetectable in the limb buds at HH29. To elucidate the functional role of Zic5 for limb development, we overexpressed Zic5 in embryonic chick limb buds using an RCAS viral expression system. Zic5 overexpression resulted in morphological defects in both fore- and hindlimbs in 60% (n = 21/35) of the embryos at HH33. The percentage represents the proportion of analyzed embryos displaying morphological defects, rather than the penetrance among all molecularly confirmed infected embryos. These affected embryos could be segregated into two phenotypic categories (Fig. 1B). The mild phenotype was characterized by a shorter ossified region and limited partially unossified areas compared with controls, indicating mild suppression of ossification. The severe phenotype exhibited extensive unossified and markedly reduced ossification. Quantitative analyses of embryos in both mild and severe phenotypes revealed that Zic5 overexpression reduced digit lengths in both fore- and hindlimbs (Fig. 1C). At HH30, Zic5-overexpressing limbs exhibited persistent hypo mineralized regions, whereas control autopods and zeugopods showed clear ossification (Fig. 1D). This indicated that elevated Zic5 activity could suppress skeletal maturation, rather than causing a localized defect alone. These gain-of-function results indicated that increased Zic5 expression was sufficient to perturb skeletal growth and maturation during limb development. Because digit number, phalangeal number, and the overall morphology of the autopod were not altered, these observations suggest that elevated Zic5 expression primarily affects digit length rather than digit identity. Therefore, the shortened digits observed in Zic5-overexpressing embryos may be secondary to impaired ossification and overall skeletal maturation, rather than reflecting a specific defect in digit patterning. To further support this conclusion, transcriptome analysis of each digit before and after Zic5 overexpression could provide molecular evidence. In addition, our RCAS-mediated data should be interpreted as gain-of-function evidence, whereas the endogenous requirement of Zic5 in birds awaits loss-of-function testing using approaches such as shRNA- or morpholino-mediated knockdown and CRISPR/Cas9-mediated mutagenesis.

To examine whether Zic5 overexpression affects molecular programs associated with limb skeletal development, we performed RT-qPCR to examine the expression of key downstream markers found in the Zic3 downstream analysis (Li et al., 2025b). RCAS-injected control embryos displayed a limb morphology comparable to wild type, indicating that the injection and viral procedure itself did not measurably perturb limb development (Fig. 1E). RT-qPCR on regionally dissected autopod and zeugopod tissues confirmed significant upregulation of Zic5 in both segments, demonstrating successful overexpression in the samples used for candidate gene expression analyses (Fig. 1E). Further, we found dysregulation of genes important for chondrocyte differentiation and ossification in chicken limbs after Zic5 overexpression (Fig. 1F). The osteoblast-associated transcription factor Runx2 was decreased in zeugopods after Zic5 overexpression. Since Runx2 is required for osteoblast differentiation and the onset of bone formation, its downregulation is consistent with the impaired ossification observed in RCAS-Zic5 limbs (Mackie et al., 2011). The chondrogenic regulator Sox9 is reduced, consistent with the impaired chondrogenesis phenotype in zeugopod tissues after Zic5 overexpression. Col9a1 and Col9a3, which encode type IX collagen components important for cartilage matrix organization, were significantly reduced in Zic5-overexpressing limbs. Reduced Sox9 and Col9 expression aligned with the skeletal staining phenotype, which showed a delay in the progression from cartilage to mineralized elements (Mackie et al., 2011). These results suggested that Zic5 was associated with altered expression of extracellular matrix (ECM)-related genes during distal limb skeletal development. We also observed significantly reduced expression of Ptch2 in both the autopod and zeugopod, whereas Hhip was significantly reduced only in the autopod of Zic5-overexpressing limbs. A previous study revealed that the loss of Ptch2 in mice enhances the differentiation potential of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (Juuri et al., 2022). Hhip deficiency in mice delayed chondrocyte maturation and endochondral ossification of digits, sternum, and vertebrae (Holmes et al., 2021). Consistent with these findings, the downregulation of Hhip and Ptch2 in Zic5-overexpressing limbs correlated with the observed phenotype of ossification impaired and skeletal maturation. RT-qPCR analyses were performed using three independent biological replicates, and future studies with larger sample sizes will be useful to further validate these expression changes. In addition, it remains unclear whether these genes are directly regulated by Zic5 or represent secondary downstream effects. Future studies using chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) or reporter assays will be required to distinguish between these possibilities.

Our results show that the limb defects caused by Zic5 overexpression resemble our previously reported phenotypes induced by Zic3 overexpression, including impaired skeletal maturation and reduced limb length (Li et al., 2025a, 2025b). Comparison of the chicken Zic5 (531 aa) and Zic3 (464 aa) proteins identified four conserved functional domains, which together share 85.59% amino acid sequence identity when analyzed across the combined domain regions (Fig. 2). While these similarities in sequence and phenotype are notable, they alone do not establish functional redundancy between Zic5 and Zic3. Establishing true redundancy would require comparative identification of shared direct transcriptional targets and biochemical evidence of physical interaction (e.g., co-immunoprecipitation). Therefore, we interpret the observed similarities as convergent phenotypic effects rather than as evidence of equivalent molecular functions. Despite this, they exhibit distinct expression patterns during chicken limb development. Previous studies reported that Zic3 is highly expressed in the first digit of chicken limbs, primarily acting to restrict the growth of DI (Li et al., 2025a, 2025b). In contrast, we found that Zic5 was initially expressed throughout the distal limb buds and later expressed in the anterior and posterior digits. Persistent Zic5 activity impaired skeletal maturation, which may secondarily lead to shortened or lost digit elements in chick embryos. This is a case where homologous gene family members with similar functions are expressed in different regions to modulate morphology, just as seen with the Hox genes in limb development (Seki et al., 2012).Fig. 2Amino acid sequence alignment of chicken Zic5 and Zic3. In summary, our results comprehensively describe the expression pattern of Zic5 in developing chicken limbs and demonstrate that Zic5 can affect digit length by suppressing skeletal maturation, thereby shaping avian limb morphology. Our results help fill a gap in our understanding of the role of Zic5 in avian limb development and identify potential Zic5-associated downstream genes and pathways involved in limb morphogenesis.
