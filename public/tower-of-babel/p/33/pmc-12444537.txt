Study on Prediction Model for Mineral Dissolution Rate in Unconsolidated Sandstone during Steam Flooding
Authors: Yigang Liu, Wei Zhang, Liguo Zhong, Qiuxia Wang, Cheng Wang, Jincheng Shan, Jinxin Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Heavy oil reservoirs are predominantly composed of unconsolidated sandstone formations. During the conversion to steam flooding development, the temperature of the steam chamber in the reservoir typically ranges from 200 to 300 °C, and the steam quality is generally 0 to 0.6. High-temperature steam (hot water) can dissolve minerals, causing changes in the formation permeability. In this study, sand-packed tube experiments were conducted to simulate high-intensity steam flooding in unconsolidated sandstones. Through the analysis of dissolved minerals in produced water and sand samples before and after dissolution, the reaction characteristics of mineral dissolution under different influencing factors were obtained. Considering the effects of the steam injection temperature, steam quality, and injected pore volume (PV) number on the mineral dissolution rate, a prediction model for the mineral dissolution rate in unconsolidated sandstones was established by using the Boltzmann distribution function and density model. The results show that the steam injection temperature, steam quality, and injected PV number all affect the mineral dissolution rate to varying degrees. Among them, the steam injection temperature has a significant impact on the maximum mineral dissolution rate, serving as the main factor determining the degree of mineral dissolution. The model calculations show good agreement with the experimental results, providing theoretical support for steam flooding reservoir engineering research and development scheme design.

Introduction

Heavy oil reservoirs are predominantly composed of unconsolidated sandstone formations, which are characterized by structural fragmentation, weak cementation, high permeability, high porosity, low rock strength, and proneness to sand production. − During the development of heavy oil reservoirs, steam huff and puff is typically implemented first. As steam huff and puff progresses to the middle-late stage with pressure drawdown, the development mode is converted to steam flooding to further enhance oil recovery and achieve production replacement. , Field practices have shown that the steam chamber temperature during steam flooding ranges from 200 to 300 °C, and the steam quality is generally 0 to 0.6. After continuous injection of high-temperature steam into the reservoir, mineral dissolution and clay mineral transformation occur at different distances from the steam injection well, leading to changes in reservoir physical properties and ultimately causing production decline in the late stage of heavy oil reservoir development. , Therefore, accurately predicting the variation of the mineral dissolution rate at different stages of steam flooding is crucial for steam flooding reservoir engineering research.

In terms of experimental studies on mineral dissolution degree, Guo Mingzhe (2023), Wang Shutao (2020), Guo Ping (2007), and Ma Hong (2007) conducted laboratory physical simulation experiments using high-temperature and high-pressure reactors to investigate the dissolution degree of formation sand samples under different temperature conditions. By analyzing the mass changes of sand samples before and after the experiments, they obtained the intensity of mineral dissolution by high-temperature steam/hot water. − However, the precipitation of solutes due to cooling in the reactor after the experiment led to significant deviations in the experimental results. In terms of research on mineral dissolution prediction models, Fournier et al. (1982) proposed a density model based on extensive experimental observations, suggesting that the logarithm of the mineral dissolution rate equilibrium constant exhibits a linear relationship with the logarithm of solvent density, and the equilibrium constant decreases with the decrease in solvent density. Rimstidt (1980) established a differential rate equation for the quartz-water reaction based on the transition state theory, determining the relationship between quartz dissolution rate and solution concentration. Currently, theoretical studies on high-temperature mineral dissolution mainly focus on the relationship between the solubility of single minerals and ion activity or solution pH, while research on the dynamic variation of the formation mineral dissolution rate at different stages of high-temperature steam flooding is relatively limited.

Therefore, in this study, sand-packed tube experiments were conducted to simulate high-intensity steam flooding in unconsolidated sandstones. Through the analysis of dissolved minerals in produced water and sand samples before and after dissolution, the reaction characteristics of mineral dissolution under different influencing factors were obtained. Considering the effects of steam injection temperature, steam quality, and injected PV number on the mineral dissolution rate, a prediction model for the mineral dissolution rate in unconsolidated sandstones was established using the Boltzmann distribution function and density model. The prediction model was validated by the results of the sand-packed tube experiments. The research findings provide theoretical support for steam flooding reservoir engineering research and development scheme design.

Laboratory Experimental Study

Experimental Design

Based on the reservoir permeability conditions, long sand-packed tube experiments were conducted to simulate high-temperature water-rock reactions. Under unsaturated heavy oil conditions, sand-packed tube simulation experiments of steam flooding and hot water flooding were performed at different steam injection temperatures and steam qualities. The dissolution rate of sand samples, phase composition, and surface morphology changes in the high-temperature sand-packed tubes after steam flooding were tested and analyzed to investigate the mineral dissolution characteristics and variations in the mineral dissolution rate at different stages.

Experimental Principle

When liquid water is heated in a closed system, the molecular kinetic energy increases, causing some molecules to escape the liquid surface and form steam. Dynamic equilibrium is achieved when the frequency of steam molecules colliding with the liquid surface equals the frequency of molecules returning to the liquid, at which point the steam pressure is termed the saturation vapor pressure. The saturation vapor pressure of water can be calculated using the Antoine empirical equation. 1 lgPsat=A−BT+C where P sat is saturation vapor pressure, MPa;

T is temperature, K;

A is the strength of intermolecular forces in the substance, which directly influences the absolute value of the saturation vapor pressure; it is generally taken as 8.07;

B is the coefficient related to the heat of vaporization of the substance, which is generally taken as 1730.63;

C is the coefficient related to the critical point temperature of the substance, which is generally taken as 233.43.

When the system pressure exceeds the saturation vapor pressure, water exists in the liquid phase; when the system pressure is lower than the saturation vapor pressure, water converts to the vapor phase. To ensure that the steam quality met experimental requirements, backpressure valves were adjusted and inlet flow rates were controlled throughout the tests.

Materials and Equipment

Experimental Equipment

A steam generator (rated pressure: 40 MPa, maximum temperature: 400 °C, maximum flow rate: 40 mL/min); a dual-cylinder constant-speed constant-pressure pump (cavity volume: 300 mL, rated pressure: 50 MPa, flow rate range: 0.1–50 mL/min); a pressure acquisition system; a TM4000Plus scanning electron microscope (SEM) equipped with an energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) module; a 100 cm Hastelloy-sand-packed tube; a thermostatic oven (maximum temperature: 400 °C).

Experimental Materials

The sand-packed tube was packed with formation sand with a diameter of 0.150 to 0.355 mm. The initial water-measured permeability was 1031–1645 mD, and the initial porosity was 0.30–0.32. Deionized water was used as the experimental water.

As shown in Table , the mineral compositions of formation sand samples under different steam qualities are identical, but significant differences exist in the phase proportions after experiments with different steam qualities, indicating that various mineral components exhibit distinct dissolution capacities under high-temperature conditions. The silicon–oxygen bonds in quartz can undergo complexation reactions with water at high temperatures, leading to quartz dissolution in high-temperature hot water. The lower the steam quality, the smaller the quartz content in the sand sample. When the steam quality is 0, the quartz content in the sand sample is minimized at 56.8%, representing a 16.7% decrease in the quartz phase proportion. This suggests that the dissolution capacity of high-temperature steam for minerals decreases with the increase in the vapor phase proportion in the steam.

To visually understand the dissolution effect of high-temperature steam on the formation rock surface, SEM was performed on sand samples at the same position in the sand-packed tube before and after the experiment to investigate the influence of different steam qualities on the surface morphology of the sand samples. The results are shown in Figure .

Surface morphology changes of sand samples before and after dissolution.

As shown in Figure , the intensity of the dissolution effect on rock surfaces varies with different steam injection qualities. The sand sample surfaces were smooth before the experiment, but they became rougher after high-temperature steam flooding, indicating that chemical dissolution occurred between high-temperature steam/hot water and surface minerals. In high-temperature environments, surface minerals exhibit enhanced reactivity, with quartz chemical bonds more prone to rupture, leading to dissolution and production of quartz minerals by steam. The degree of mineral dissolution differs with steam quality: lower steam quality results in more severe surface dissolution. When steam quality is 0, dissolution pores with a depth of approximately 200 μm appear on sand sample surfaces, whereas at a steam quality of 0.6, the depth of dissolution pores is shallower, and the mineral dissolution effect of steam flooding is significantly weakened. This suggests that in mineral-steam dissolution, liquid-phase components are the primary influencing factor, while vapor-phase components have a minimal impact on mineral dissolution.

Prediction Model for Mineral Dissolution Rate

Based on the transition state theory and combined with experimental results of mineral dissolution reactions during high-temperature steam flooding, a prediction model for the mineral dissolution rate in unconsolidated sandstones was established by considering the effects of steam injection temperature, steam quality, and injected PV number. The model integrates the Boltzmann distribution function and density model to provide theoretical guidance for the development of high-temperature steam flooding in unconsolidated sandstone reservoirs.

The calculation results of the prediction model show small discrepancies with the experimental results. For most experimental cases, the theoretical values calculated by the prediction model are higher than those of the experimental measurements. It is speculated that there are two main reasons: first, trace impurities exist in the samples, which are dissolved and collected by the injected steam during the high-temperature reaction, leading to overestimated experimental results; second, the maximum dissolution rate constant used in theoretical calculations is obtained from experiments, where the reaction energy barrier is relatively high, causing the experimental value of the maximum dissolution rate constant to be smaller than the theoretical value.

The dissolution characteristics of the formation sand samples under different steam qualities, steam injection temperatures, and injected PV were analyzed. The quartz content in sand samples can decrease to 1.1–15.6% of the original value under different steam injection temperatures, and 5.8–13.6% under different steam qualities.

The prediction model for mineral dissolution rate in unconsolidated sandstones, established based on the transition state theory using the Boltzmann distribution function and density model, shows small discrepancies between the calculated results and experimental data, confirming its reliability. The findings provide theoretical guidance for high-temperature steam flooding development in oil reservoirs.
