Cerebellar Contribution to Rapid Cross‐Modal Attentional Shift in Visual‐Tactile Processing
Authors: Simran Mughal, Kayla E. Dolman, Tiffany Y. Stupka, Kylee R. Graham, Danielle Saney, Sean K. Meehan, W. Richard Staines
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTClinical and behavioral studies suggest the right lateral cerebellar hemisphere plays a critical role in cognition, particularly attention. Contralateral connections between the cerebellum and cerebrum via the thalamus support this involvement. When rapid attentional shifts occur between two target stimuli (T1 and T2) in close succession, a phenomenon known as attentional blink (AB) results in decreased accuracy in reporting T2. Continuous theta burst stimulation (cTBS) studies indicate that inhibiting the right cerebellar hemisphere enhances visual AB. However, it is uncertain whether the cerebellum impacts sensory processing when presented with two relevant targets in different sensory modalities, and if lateralization of function is conserved. This study investigated cerebellar involvement in cross‐modal AB, particularly the influence of right cerebellar stimulation on rapid attentional shifts between visual and tactile stimuli. In two experiments, participants completed a cross‐modal attentional blink task pre‐ and post‐cTBS stimulation applied to the left or right cerebellar hemisphere. Stimuli included a visual or tactile T1, followed by a cross‐modal T2 and a mask. In Experiment 1, stimuli were presented to the second and fifth digits of the left hand and left visual space, whereas in Experiment 2, they were presented to the second and fifth digits of the right hand and right visual space. Results revealed that right cerebellar inhibition led to reduced accuracy in reporting tactile T2 targets irrespective of stimulus location. No significant changes following left cerebellar stimulation were observed. These findings suggest hemisphere‐specific cerebellar modulation of cross‐modal AB performance, where the right cerebellum plays a role in managing relevant T2 targets from distractors (mask).

Introduction

To achieve a unified perception of the external world and execute correct behaviors or movements, the brain must process different types of sensory inputs in near unison. This process, known as multisensory integration, allows for maximal extraction of information from the environment (Lalanne and Lorenceau 2004; Macaluso and Driver 2005). At any given time, the brain is exposed to a plethora of sensory stimuli and thus, relies on selection and task relevance of information depending on goals and instructions for successful integration (Talsma et al. 2010). Attentional control plays a critical role in the process of task relevance by guiding the brain's selective attention towards specific sensory inputs, influenced by both bottom‐up factors (such as the saliency of stimuli) and top‐down factors (including cognitive goals) (Corbetta and Shulman 2002; Talsma et al. 2010).

The concept of attention is not a singular system, but rather a collection of mechanisms and significant attributes within various perceptual and cognitive processes that enable the regulation of information processing (Talsma et al. 2010). One critical attribute of attentional control is selecting relevant stimuli to enable efficient processing. One proposed framework explains how attentional selection modulates cross‐modal integration, particularly when there is competition between sensory modalities. When stimuli from different modalities are equally salient, attentional modulation becomes necessary due to increased cognitive load and limited processing capacity (Talsma et al. 2010). Recent evidence shows the involvement of sub‐regions in attentional processing, such as the cerebellum (Brissenden et al. 2016).

Traditionally, the cerebellum has been termed as an accessory motor structure and has been linked to motor control and coordination (Manto et al. 2011). Nevertheless, contemporary evidence suggests that the cerebellar cortex possesses considerable heterogeneity in its anatomical and functional connections with regions outside the cerebellum (Buckner et al. 2011). A significant portion of the cerebellum demonstrates connections with cerebral cortical regions associated with non‐motor functions, including attention, perception and working memory (Brissenden et al. 2016; Schmahmann et al. 2019). Existing research indicates the involvement of cerebellar activity in rapid attentional shifts between attended visual stimuli and shows the presence of cerebellar nodes within the fronto‐parietal attentional network (Arasanz, Staines, and Schweizer 2012; Brissenden et al. 2016; Esterman et al. 2017). Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) studies coupled with visual attentional blink (AB) tasks have demonstrated that transient inhibition of the right cerebellar hemisphere through continuous theta burst stimulation (cTBS) enhances visual AB, whereas stimulation over the left cerebellar hemisphere does not (Arasanz, Staines, and Schweizer 2012). Moreover, transient excitation of the cerebellar nodes through intermittent theta burst stimulation (iTBS) protocols reduces the number of errors reported in detecting the second target post‐iTBS compared to the baseline (Esterman et al. 2017). These outcomes demonstrate that the cerebellum is involved in sensory processing of visual AB and the effect is likely lateralized. However, it remains unclear whether the cerebellum impacts sensory processing when presented with two relevant targets in different sensory modalities compared to two relevant sensory stimuli in the same modality as investigated in previous studies.

AB is a perceptual phenomenon that can be measured by recording the accuracy in reporting the location of the second stimulus target (T2) when two stimulus targets are presented closely in time (200–500 ms), despite accurate detection and reporting of the location of the first target (T1) (Soto‐Faraco et al. 2002). There are multiple frameworks that aim to explain the underlying mechanisms of AB. One idea is related to the limited processing capacity, which suggests that the occurrence of AB is related to transient interference between T1 and T2 processing (Martens and Wyble 2010). When attention is required to process two relevant stimuli presented in close temporal proximity, attending to and processing the first target can lead to delayed processing of the second target. This is because T2 must wait to access a ‘central processing stage’ and during this wait time, the associated impulse is subject to degradation. Studies using a cross‐modal AB task between vision and touch have proposed that visual and tactile stimuli compete for a common limited capacity processor (Soto‐Faraco et al. 2002). Past research has demonstrated that the cross‐modal AB effect is pronounced when a visual target precedes a tactile one within short temporal intervals, as this close timing increases the likelihood that T2 (tactile) processing will be delayed while T1 (visual) still occupies central attentional resources. Importantly, this interference was shown to be time‐locked and independent of task‐switching demands, indicating that visuo‐tactile targets draw on shared attentional mechanisms that are constrained by temporal limitations (Soto‐Faraco et al. 2002).

Lateralization of function implies that certain cognitive processes are predominantly associated with one hemisphere (Schmahmann et al. 2019). For example, language processing is typically lateralized to the left cerebral hemisphere, and previous research has demonstrated that cTBS over only the right cerebellum impairs verbal fluency, while patient studies show that right cerebellar lesions affect general cognition and left cerebellar lesions impair visuospatial functions without disrupting language or attention (Arasanz, Staines, Roy, and Schweizer 2012; Starowicz‐Filip et al. 2021). These findings provide converging evidence that lateralization of function in the cerebrum is mirrored in the cerebellum through contralateral cerebello‐cortical connections and supports the role of the right cerebellar hemisphere in attentional and cognitive processes (Stoodley and Schmahmann 2009; Schmahmann et al. 2019).

Across two experiments we investigated cerebellar influences on attentional control during sensory processing across different modalities and to identify the specific cerebellar functional domains contributing to cross‐modal AB. Experiment 1 examined whether attentional selection of the T1 in one sensory modality would interfere with the selection of a subsequent target (T2) in a different modality. We hypothesized that T2 detection accuracy would be reduced when T1 and T2 were presented within a short temporal window (< 500 ms), reflecting a cross‐modal AB effect. This effect was expected to be most pronounced when a visual target preceded a vibrotactile target, based on previous findings suggesting that visual and tactile stimuli compete for access to a shared, limited‐capacity processor (Soto‐Faraco et al. 2002). Additionally, we hypothesized that cTBS applied to the posterior right lateral cerebellum would enhance cross‐modal AB, particularly when a visual target preceded a tactile target (VT) compared to when a tactile target was followed by a visual target (TV). In contrast, we anticipated no significant difference in T2 detection rates when cTBS was applied to the left lateral cerebellum, serving as an active control, as observed in cTBS studies coupled with visual AB tasks (Arasanz, Staines, and Schweizer 2012).

Experiment 2 was conducted as a follow‐up to Experiment 1 to assess lateralization effects. Experiment 2 used similar procedures to Experiment 1, except that the spatial locations of the visual and tactile stimuli were changed to test whether the effects of cerebellar stimulation would generalize across stimulus positions. This approach allowed us to assess whether lateralization effects are global and independent of stimulus location. This is important because the cerebellum receives sensory feedback primarily ipsilateral to the stimulated limb, whereas it has contralateral connections with the cerebrum. Therefore, changing stimulus locations allowed us to determine whether any observed effects reflect cerebellar hemispheric‐specific lateralization. We predicted that, regardless of stimulus location, cTBS to the right cerebellar hemisphere would impact performance on the cross‐modal AB task. In contrast, cTBS to the left cerebellar hemisphere would have no significant effect, serving as a control for hemispheric lateralization.

Methods

Participants

Thirty‐three healthy young adults (15 males and 18 females; mean age (years) ± SD = 23 ± 3.4 years), who self‐reported no history of neurological deficits participated in the study. Of the group, 31 were right‐hand dominant and 2 were left‐hand dominant, as assessed using the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory (Oldfield 1971). Three participants from this sample took part in both experiments. Thus, 26 participants (13 males and 13 females) were included in Experiment 1 while 10 participants (3 males and 7 females) were involved in Experiment 2. For both experiments, the procedures were approved by the University of Waterloo Research Ethics Board, and all participants provided written consent prior to the start of the experiment.

Experiment 1

Experimental Procedure

The experiment consisted of a cross‐modal attentional blink task with two similar sessions which were randomized for each participant. For both sessions, the participant was seated in a dimly lit section of the lab with the visuo‐tactile device and computer monitor placed within their spatial frame of reference, such that the device and monitor were visible. They were instructed to place the second digit and fifth digit of their left hand onto the vibrotactile stimulators while placing their right hand onto the response device. Secondly, they were instructed to look at the fixation point represented by a rectangular symbol on the screen next to their left hand. This arrangement ensured that both stimuli were represented in the same cerebellar hemisphere due to their spatial alignment. Lastly, they were instructed to identify the location of both targets on each trial, guessing if necessary.

Before the start of the trials, individual tactile and visual detection thresholds were established. To determine these thresholds, participants were exposed to stimuli and were asked to report when they first perceived it. Stimulus intensity was gradually increased after it was perceived. Following this, the intensity was decreased until the stimulus was no longer detectable. This procedure was repeated three times for each modality to ensure consistency. To ensure stimulus saliency during the task, the final sensory threshold values were multiplied by a factor of 1.25 to determine the stimulus intensity used in the experiment. After completing the pre‐cTBS blocks of the behavioral task, the participant received cTBS to either the left or the right cerebellar hemisphere depending on the session. Approximately 5–8 min post‐cTBS, the participants re‐performed the same behavioral task and paradigm. This concluded the first session, which lasted approximately 2 h, with each behavioral testing phase taking approximately 25–30 min. The second session occurred after a washout period of 5–7 days to avoid carry‐over effects (Gamboa et al. 2010). The experimental procedure for the second session was identical to the first session except for the cerebellar cTBS site.

Attentional Blink Task, Apparatus and Stimuli

A custom‐made visuo‐tactile device with two red LED lights and two vibrotactile stimulators was used to deliver the stimuli (Figure 1). The experimental protocol was programmed using LabVIEW, which controlled the delivery and timing of the stimuli (National Instruments, Austin, Texas, USA). Vibrotactile stimuli consisted of discrete vibrations applied to the volar surfaces of the 2nd and 5th digits. Stimulation was controlled by converting digitally generated waveforms to an analog signal (1000 Hz update rate; NI USB‐6341, National Instruments, Austin, Texas) and then amplifying the signal (Bryston 3B‐LP, Peterborough, Ontario) using a custom program written in LabVIEW (National Instruments, Austin, Texas). Varying the amplitude of the driving voltage to the vibrotactile device produced proportional changes in vibration of the device on the finger. The questions and fixation point appeared on a Dell color monitor, and participants recorded their responses using a mouse, which acted as the response device. White noise was provided through headphones via a sound program in the Stim2 stimulus presentation system to mask the noise from the vibrotactile stimulators (Stim2, Compumedics Neuroscan, 2020).

Temporal sequence of stimuli: The diagram displays the temporal sequence of VT (top) and TV (bottom) stimuli during a trial. Each box represents a configuration of LEDs and vibrators. Each trial started with the LEDs (black) and vibrators (black) turned off. Red LEDs and yellow vibrators indicate that they were switched on.

As shown in Figure 1, each trial began with both the vibrators and LED lights turned off. In the visual T1 and tactile T2 (VT) condition, one of the LED lights turned on for 18 ms, followed by a lag/interstimulus interval (ISI) of 200, 300, 450, or 750 ms. After this interval, one of the vibrators turned on for 35 ms and then turned off (Soto‐Faraco et al. 2002). After 100 ms, both vibrators were turned on as part of the masking condition. Masking is a phenomenon of perception. It occurs when the perception of one stimulus, called a target, is reduced by the presence of another stimulus, called a mask. According to the literature, an AB is observed when T2 is followed by a mask that disrupts T2 processing. This is referred to as interruption masking and is critical during cross‐modal AB tasks to induce attentional blinks (Arnell and Jenkins 2004). In the tactile T1 and visual T2 (TV) condition, the trial also started with both vibrators and LED lights turned off. The vibrator turned on for 35 ms, followed by a lag of 200, 300, 450, or 750 ms, after which one of the LED lights turned on for 18 ms. To introduce the visual mask, both LED lights were turned on 100 ms after T2 for 18 ms and then turned off.

Participants completed 8 blocks pre‐ and 8 blocks post‐cTBS during each experimental session for a total of 16 blocks. Twelve of these were experimental blocks, each consisting of 64 experimental trials and 8 T1 control trials, for a total of 72 trials per block. The T1 control trials acted as ‘catch’ trials to ensure participants were actively engaged in the task and accurately reporting the stimulus location to the best of their abilities. Additionally, 4 baseline blocks were presented, each containing unimodal stimuli followed by a mask of the same modality. One baseline block featured 20 trials with only visual stimuli, while the other block presented 20 trials with only tactile stimuli. If the target stimulus was visual, both LED lights turned on for 18 ms simultaneously after the target was presented. If the target was tactile, both vibrotactile stimulators were activated for 35 ms following the tactile stimulus. The purpose of these baseline blocks was to assess participants' proficiency in extracting the relevant T2 target from the mask. This assessment was crucial for understanding participants' perceptual capabilities and AB performance in subsequent experimental conditions.

After each trial, participants answered two questions. The first question asked them to indicate the relative location of the T1 stimulus: for VT blocks, this involved reporting which side the light turned on (Press L or R to indicate where you saw the light); for TV blocks, which side the vibration occurred (Press L or R to indicate where you felt the vibrations). Responses were made by pressing the left or right button on the response device. The second question pertained to the T2 stimulus, where participants had to report the relative location on which they saw the light (TV block) or felt the vibration (VT block) by again pressing either the left or right button. For control trials, where only the T1 stimulus was presented, participants simply indicated the side they saw or felt the stimulus. In the baseline trials, where only unimodal masked stimuli were presented, participants also indicated the side of the stimulus. The order of experimental and baseline blocks was randomized to reduce order effects.

TMS Protocol

The cTBS protocol was administered using the MagPro R30 stimulator (MagVenture, GA, USA) and the MCF‐B65 coil. The participant was seated with their head resting on the table in a U‐shaped pillow and the arm of the coil was positioned such that it was in the correct location for the duration of the stimulation. During cTBS, bursts of 3 stimuli were administered at 50 Hz and repeated at 5 Hz (theta frequency) for 40 s, amounting to 600 pulses (Huang et al. 2007). For right lateral cerebellar stimulation, the centre of the coil was positioned 1 cm below and 3 cm lateral to the right of the inion, and similarly for the left lateral cerebellum, 1 cm below and 3 cm lateral to the left of the inion (Arasanz, Staines, and Schweizer 2012). Stimulation intensity was set at 80% active motor threshold (AMT) which was determined by placing the coil over the scalp to locate the optimal site on the primary motor cortex to evoke motor evoked potentials (MEPs) in the contralateral first dorsal interosseous (FDI) muscle. The AMT was defined as a MEP response greater than 200 μV in 5 out of 10 trials during a 10% isometric contraction of the FDI muscle.

Experiment 2

Experimental Apparatus, Materials, and Procedure

The apparatus, materials, and general procedure for Experiment 2 were similar to those used in Experiment 1. The differences included the delivery of tactile stimuli to the second and fifth digits of the right hand and the visual stimuli presentation to the right visual field in both sessions. This change was implemented to investigate whether the effects of cerebellar stimulation on cross‐modal AB are spatially invariant and lateralized in the cerebellum.

Data Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed in RStudio (version 2022.07.1.554; RStudio Team, 2022), with statistical significance set at p=0.05. To ensure that the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variances were met, the Shapiro–Wilk test and Levene's test were conducted, respectively. Additionally, Mauchly's Test for sphericity was performed, and any observed violations were corrected using Huynh‐Feldt Corrections.

The first aim of this work was to understand whether there was a difference in the ability to report T2 in the presence of T1 in two different block type conditions (VT and TV) pre‐cTBS over the cerebellar hemispheres. This would allow us to assess the presence of the attentional blink and replicate findings presented in the work by Soto‐Faraco et al. (2002). Secondly, to establish effects of cerebellar cTBS on AB performance, we had to ensure that the pre‐cTBS performance was session agnostic. To assess these measures, we conducted a 3‐way repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) on the accuracy of T2 recall rates. The within‐subject factors were Block type (VT/TV), ISI (200, 300, 450, and 750 ms), and Session (cTBS to the right hemisphere/cTBS to the left hemisphere). T2 accuracy was calculated as the ratio of correct responses to total trials according to the following equation: (1) AccuracyT2=NCorrectNTotal where Ncorrect is the number of trials where the participant correctly reported T2 and NTotal is the total number of trials. Furthermore, to ensure there was no difference in extracting either tactile or visual targets from the mask in the baseline blocks before cTBS regardless of session, pairwise t‐tests were conducted for both modality conditions. Lastly, to confirm that the accuracy of detecting and reporting T1 control/catch trials was independent of session pairwise t‐tests were conducted on the mean accuracy values pre‐cTBS. The ratio of accurate T1 control trials was assessed using a modified version of (Equation 1): (2) AccuracyT1=NCorrectNTotal

The second aim of this work was to establish the effects of cerebellar cTBS on cross‐modal AB performance. Firstly, we compared baseline performance in both block type conditions by conducting pair‐wise t‐tests between pre‐ and post‐baseline accuracy in extracting tactile targets from tactile mask (T) and visual targets from visual mask (V) for both sessions. Furthermore, to understand changes in AB performance post‐cTBS and determine whether cerebellar influence on attentional shifts is lateralized, we conducted a three‐way repeated measures ANOVA on the difference score for Experiment 1 and 2. The within‐subject factors were Block Type (VT/TV), ISI (200, 300, 450, and 750 ms), and Session (cTBS to the right hemisphere/cTBS to the left hemisphere). If a significant interaction was observed, it was followed by post hoc pairwise comparison with Bonferroni adjustment for multiple comparisons. Instead of using raw scores (Equation 1), we calculated a difference score (Equation 3) to assess changes in AB performance: (3) ΔAB=PreB–PreE–PstB–PstE where PreB refers to the pre‐cTBS baseline accuracy, PreE refers to pre‐cTBS AB accuracy and PstB & PstE refer to post‐cTBS baseline and experimental accuracy, respectively.

Subtracting PreE from PreB, and subtracting PstE from PstB before evaluating the difference between pre‐ and post‐cTBS results was important due to the masking of all T2 stimuli. By subtracting baseline accuracy, we obtained a true representation of AB, allowing us to evaluate T2 reporting accuracy in the presence of T1 pre‐ and post‐cTBS for each ISI value without the confounding effect of masking. This removal of baseline scores will be referred to as “the baseline correction”.

In addition to the baseline‐corrected difference score analysis, further analyses were conducted on the raw T2 accuracy rates to better characterize the source of any observed effects. Although the difference score provides a measure of AB magnitude that accounts for masking effects, it does not allow for direct assessment of whether changes in performance arise from changes in baseline target detection, experimental AB performance, or both. Therefore, to further examine changes in AB performance following cTBS and to determine whether these changes differed as a function of the stimulated cerebellar hemisphere, additional three‐way repeated measures ANOVAs were conducted on raw T2 accuracy rates separately for each ISI within the VT block type. The within‐subject factors included Modality (VT‐experimental/T‐baseline), Session (cTBS to the right hemisphere/cTBS to the left hemisphere), and Time (pre‐cTBS/post‐cTBS). This approach allowed for a direct comparison of performance across time and session while complementing the baseline‐corrected analyses. Lastly, to assess the accuracy of detecting and reporting T1 control/catch trials, pairwise t‐tests were conducted on the mean accuracy values pre‐ and post‐cTBS for each session.

Results

Experiment 1

Pre‐Cerebellar cTBS Stimulation

Accuracy of Detecting T2 (Pre‐cTBS Raw Scores and AB Condition)

The three‐way repeated measures ANOVA revealed a significant interaction effect between Block type and ISI [F3,75=9.034,p<0.001,ηp2=0.27]. None of the terms involving the factor Session were significant. Thus, regardless of session, there was no difference in the individuals' ability to detect T2 at each ISI value for the two individual block types. Decomposition of the Block Type × ISI interaction revealed a significant difference in the ability to detect T2 when it was either preceded by a visual (T1) or tactile (T1) stimulus at each ISI value regardless of the session as observed in the work done by Soto‐Faraco et al. (2002), demonstrating that an AB was observed in VT block type and not TV block type.

Accuracy of Detecting T2 From Mask (Pre‐cTBS Baseline Condition)

For both sessions, individuals were engaged in extracting T2 tactile targets from the tactile mask and T2 visual targets from the visual mask. For both baseline conditions, the t‐tests indicated no significant difference between the means of the tactile t=0.87,df=25,p=0.38,d=0.16 or visual t=−0.92,df=25,p=0.36,d=−0.13 baseline values for the two experimental sessions before cTBS was administered.

Accuracy of Detecting T1 (Pre‐cTBS Control Condition)

In each experimental block, participants were prompted to detect T1 (either visual for VT blocks or tactile for TV blocks). The group accuracies for the control trials signified that individuals were engaged in the task. The t‐test results showed no significant difference in reporting either visual control trials in the VT block type t=−1.07,df=25,p=0.29,d=−0.26 or tactile control trials in the TV block type t=0.27,df=25,p=0.79,d=0.037 in the two different sessions.

Accuracy of Detecting T2 From Mask Pre‐ Versus Post‐cTBS (Baseline Condition)

Accuracy of Detecting T2 (Difference Score and AB Condition)

The 3‐way ANOVA on difference score (Equation 3) revealed a significant 3‐way interaction effect between Block type, Session and ISI [F3,75=2.75,p=0.049, ηp2=0.099]. Decomposition of the three‐way interaction using separate Block Type × ISI repeated measures ANOVAs for each Session revealed a significant interaction between Block Type and ISI following right cerebellar cTBS but no significant effects following left cerebellar cTBS. These findings suggest that after stimulation to the right lateral cerebellar hemisphere, there is a change in AB performance within the VT condition. Post hoc paired t‐tests, Bonferroni corrected, revealed that this effect was driven by significant differences between VT and TV blocks at the 200 ms (t=−2.18,df=25,p=0.036) and 750 ms (t = –2.58, df = 25, p = 0.017) ISIs as shown in Figure 3A.

Accuracy of Detecting T2 in AB (Raw T2 Accuracy Without Baseline Correction Pre‐ and Post‐cTBS)

To examine raw T2 detection accuracy including the effect of the mask, separate 3‐way repeated‐measures ANOVAs (Modality × Session × Time) were conducted for each ISI (200, 300, 450, 750 ms) within the VT block type.

200 ms ISI

A significant main effect of Modality was observed [F1,25=71.50,p<0.001,ηp2=0.74], indicating that T2 accuracy was lower in VT experimental blocks compared with T baseline blocks, consistent with the AB effect. No other interactions, including the three‐way interaction, reached significance.

300 ms ISI

Experiment 2

Accuracy of Detecting T2 From Mask Pre‐ Versus Post‐cTBS (Baseline Condition)

cTBS to the right lateral cerebellar hemisphere affected the extraction of tactile T2 targets from the mask, consistent with findings from Experiment 1. A t‐test revealed a significant difference in baseline performance pre‐ and post‐cTBS within the tactile modality with a medium effect size (Right cerebellar cTBS: t=3.80,df=9,p=0.004,d=0.70). However, there was no significant effect on extracting tactile targets when cTBS was applied to the left cerebellar hemisphere with a small effect size (Left cerebellar cTBS: t=1.79,df=9,p=0.11,d=0.37). Additionally there was no significant difference in extracting visual targets, regardless of stimulation site, (Right cerebellar cTBS: t = 0.50, df = 9, p = 063, d = 0.22) (Left cerebellar cTBS: t=−1.03,df=9,p=0.33,d=−0.39).

Accuracy of Detecting T2 (Difference Score and AB Condition)

To understand lateralization of function within the cerebellum the 3‐way ANOVA on difference score (Equation 3) revealed a significant interaction effect between block type and session [F1,9=7.45,p=0.02,ηp2=0.45]. The three‐way interaction was not significant (Table 1). Discussion

Cross‐Modal AB Performance Pre‐cTBS

The results of the study demonstrated a cross‐modal AB for tactile (T2) stimuli as a function of their temporal distance from a preceding visual T1 stimulus. In contrast, when a visual stimulus (T2) was preceded by a tactile stimulus (T1), no AB was observed, confirming that TV blocks served as an effective control condition. Importantly, to ensure that both experimental AB performance and baseline performance were session‐agnostic, we confirmed that pre‐cTBS performance did not differ between the right and left cerebellar sessions. Specifically, there were no differences in the ability to extract tactile targets from tactile masks or visual targets from visual masks prior to stimulation at either site. Additionally, analysis of T1 accuracy demonstrated consistently high performance across both sessions, confirming that participants were engaged in the task and that performance on T1 control trials was independent of session order. Together, these findings confirm the presence of a cross‐modal AB and provide confidence that any post‐cTBS changes observed in T2 performance can be attributed to stimulation‐induced modulation of cerebellar output rather than pre‐existing session differences or general task disengagement.

Role of Cerebellum in Cross‐Modal AB

We administered cTBS to the posterior right and left lateral hemispheres of the cerebellum to transiently modulate cerebellar activity and, by extension, influence attentional control. We hypothesized that cTBS applied to the right cerebellar hemisphere would lead to an increase in the number of errors in reporting T2 targets in the VT condition compared to pre‐cTBS performance. Contrary to our initial hypothesis, baseline‐corrected analyses revealed that right cerebellar cTBS was associated with an apparent increase in the ability to recall T2 targets relative to pre‐cTBS performance. However, further examination of baseline performance revealed that stimulation of the right lateral cerebellum significantly impaired the ability to extract tactile targets from the tactile mask during baseline trials. Specifically, post‐cTBS tactile baseline performance was significantly reduced relative to pre‐cTBS performance. Notably, the magnitude of this baseline reduction exceeded the change observed in the experimental VT condition (ΔB > ΔE), indicating that cerebellar stimulation disproportionately affected masking‐related processes. This pattern suggests that the apparent improvement in baseline‐corrected VT performance was driven, at least in part, by a larger decrement in baseline tactile processing following stimulation.

To determine whether changes in cross modal AB performance following right cerebellar cTBS in the VT condition were solely attributable to altered masking performance, we conducted additional analyses on raw T2 accuracy without baseline correction. This analysis allowed us to examine T2 reporting in the experimental VT condition directly, while retaining the influence of the tactile mask. The raw‐score analyses revealed a consistent reduction in tactile T2 accuracy following right cerebellar cTBS across ISIs, with significant decreases observed at the 300 and 450 ms ISIs. These findings demonstrate that, even when masking effects were not removed, VT performance was significantly modulated by cerebellar stimulation. Importantly, although tactile baseline performance also declined following right cerebellar cTBS, the pattern of results across ISIs differed between baseline and experimental conditions. This indicates that changes in VT performance cannot be fully explained by a general impairment in tactile target extraction from the mask alone. In comparison, there was no significant difference in extracting tactile targets from the mask or AB performance in the VT condition after stimulating the left lateral cerebellar hemisphere. This hemispheric specificity suggests a dominant role of the right cerebellum in regulating tactile processing under conditions of temporal and perceptual competition.

Together, the raw and baseline‐corrected analyses indicate that changes in AB performance following cTBS to the right cerebellum are driven by two interacting mechanisms: the temporal constraints between T1 and T2 as they compete for access to a capacity‐limited central processing stage and the ability to extract T2 from a subsequent tactile mask. As previously described, AB is a phenomenon where the accurate detection of a T2 is reduced when it appears in close temporal proximity to T1 (Soto‐Faraco et al. 2002; Martens and Wyble 2010). According to the central bottleneck theory, information from different sensory modalities converges onto a shared, capacity‐limited processing stage responsible for conscious report, response selection, or working memory consolidation (Martens and Wyble 2010). When this central processing stage is engaged in processing visual (T1), its capacity to simultaneously process T2 is diminished, causing T2 to “wait” to be processed (Soto‐Faraco et al. 2002; Martens and Wyble 2010). During this waiting period, if a mask of equal saliency and same modality is presented approximately 100 ms after T2, the bottom‐up processing of the mask stimuli forms a strong tactile representation, which can interfere with the processing of the T2 stimulus. This interference is thought to result from “masking by object substitution,” where the perception of a target is disrupted by a masking stimulus that appears shortly after it (Arnell and Jenkins 2004). At the same time, T2 processing is occurring via top‐down processes, which rely on higher cognitive functions such as expectation, prediction, and prior knowledge. Due to limited attentional resources, the bottom‐up processing of the mask competes with the top‐down processing of T2, increasing the likelihood of a decrease in T2 processing. To manage the competition for attentional resources between these processes, our results indicate that the right cerebellar hemisphere is involved. Specifically, the cerebellum attenuates the bottom‐up processing such that the representation of the mask is weakened. This allows the top‐down process to effectively process the T2 target. Following cTBS to the right lateral cerebellum, we observed a significant reduction in the ability to detect tactile targets during baseline masking trials, even in the absence of a preceding T1 [ΔB > ΔE (t)] (Figure 2A). This implies that a transient decrease in cerebellar function by cTBS impairs one's ability to manage bottom‐up processing of the mask which likely interacts with top‐down mechanisms and increases the probability of interference occurring in the processing of T2.

This leads to the question of how the cerebellum modulates the observed competition. In a cross‐modal AB task, T1 and T2 are considered task‐relevant stimuli because the participants are instructed to respond to their locations, while the mask acts as a distractor. One neural process that ensures task‐relevant stimuli are not overwhelmed by distractors is sensory gating. This process is the brain's intrinsic ability to filter irrelevant afferent stimuli, preventing an overload of information within higher cortical areas and occurring in the early stages of cortical processing (Staines et al. 2002). Anatomically, tactile afferent information is relayed to the cortex via the thalamus, where sensory gating is known to occur through inhibitory mechanisms involving the thalamic reticular nucleus (TRN) (McCormick and Bal 1994; McAlonan et al. 2000). Recent evidence demonstrating cerebellar projections to the TRN provides a plausible pathway through which the cerebellum may influence sensory gating (Pisano et al. 2021). Decreasing cerebellar output via the deep‐cerebellar nuclei through cTBS may therefore reduce the efficiency of this gating mechanism. This results in stronger representations of the masking stimuli that interfere with T2 target processing. Although top‐down processes contribute to performance in the experimental condition, the present results indicate that cerebellar modulation of bottom‐up sensory gating plays a more dominant role. This is supported by the finding that baseline masking performance was more strongly affected by right cerebellar stimulation than experimental performance, suggesting that the cerebellum primarily regulates the suppression of irrelevant tactile input rather than directly enhancing target processing. Together, these findings position the right cerebellum as a key modulator of sensory gating under conditions of high perceptual competition, particularly when task‐relevant tactile information must be extracted from salient distractors. Additionally, focusing on the differences observed in the experimental trials, we conclude that the right cerebellum also modulates top‐down processes by re‐weighting sensory information based on task relevance. However, after cTBS, the difference in T2 extraction pre‐ and post‐cTBS from the mask (baseline blocks) is greater than the difference in T2 extraction pre‐ and post‐cTBS from T1 in the presence of the mask (experimental blocks). Considering this, we believe that while the cerebellum's inhibition may reduce the incidence of AB, it also impairs the precise extraction of relevant stimuli, indicating a trade‐off between these two aspects of attentional control. Finally, after baseline correction, we see an overall decrease in AB; hence, we theorize that the cerebellar modulation of bottom‐up processes is more significant. Focusing on these results, we believe that output signals from the right cerebellar hemisphere are combined with signals in the sensory processing circuits to control the input gain of sensory information by balancing attentional resources allocated to bottom‐up and top‐down processes.

Additionally, our results support the hypothesis that in the TV condition, which served as a control, there was no significant difference in extracting the visual (T2) targets from the mask, regardless of stimulation site. This pattern of results can be attributed to the difference in neural processing time between tactile and visual stimuli, as tactile stimuli are processed quicker than visual stimuli (Godlove et al. 2014). Specifically, the tactile stimulus (T1) precedes the visual (T2) and visual mask, allowing sufficient temporal separation for the attentional system to process each event without interference. This reduces the likelihood of a bottleneck in processing; thereby, the demand for cerebellar‐mediated sensory gating is reduced when temporal overlap and cross‐modal competition are minimal. This supports our broader interpretation of the cerebellum as a dynamic sensory modulator, especially under conditions of high attentional load or close temporal proximity between competing stimuli. Lastly, cTBS to either site did not impact performance in detecting T1 catch/control trials. This outcome implies that the cerebellum is not involved in stimuli detection, irrespective of modality. Rather, it is involved in accurate target stimulus extraction from a series of target and non‐target stimuli presented in close temporal proximity (Arasanz, Staines, and Schweizer 2012).

Lateralization of Function

Our findings add to the growing body of evidence supporting the existence of distinct functional domains within the cerebellum. The results from Experiment 2 mirrored our Experiment 1 findings that cTBS to the right cerebellum selectively impaired performance in the VT condition, while cTBS to the left led to no change in performance (Figures 3 and 4). Specifically, after cTBS to the right cerebellar hemisphere, accuracy in extracting tactile targets from the mask in the VT condition significantly decreased. This change in performance was absent following left cerebellar stimulation, serving as further support for functional asymmetry. As expected, TV conditions, regardless of stimulation site, showed no significant differences, confirming their role as controls. A limitation of the current work is the relatively small sample size in Experiment 2. The main purpose of Experiment 2 was to serve as a control experiment to assess lateralization effects irrespective of stimuli location. While the effect sizes and pattern of results are comparable for Experiment 1 and Experiment 2 to draw conclusions about lateralization of function, future work should focus on replicating the experimental paradigm for Experiment 2 with a larger sample size.

Cerebellum and Repetitive TMS

Across the two experiments post‐cTBS to the right cerebellum impacted cross‐modal AB performance, thus, it is important to consider how cTBS‐induced disruption may have altered this process. Administration of cTBS can be thought to reduce the functional efficacy of the cerebellum by decreasing excitatory cerebellar output (Piochon et al. 2010). Although we did not administer iTBS to the same hemisphere as a control to assess cTBS effects, our results show a negative change in behavior. One possibility is that cTBS introduces noise into the system from the stimulation, making it harder for the cerebellum to modulate the cortical networks in question. This is a limitation and to address it, iTBS could be used as a control to assess the pattern of stimulation for cTBS. However, as one of the main questions of this research was to assess lateralization; it was imperative to stimulate the left hemisphere as an active control with the same type of stimulation.

A further limitation lies in the variability of outcomes from cerebellar non‐invasive brain stimulation. Meta‐analysis findings show substantial heterogeneity across studies in cognitive effects, due to differences in stimulation parameters, individual variability, and cerebellar‐cortical dynamics (Pezzetta et al. 2024). Despite this, our group‐level findings demonstrated condition‐specific modulation of tactile performance, with moderate to large effect sizes observed following right cerebellar cTBS only. This pattern indicates that, although the magnitude of stimulation effects may vary across individuals, cerebellar cTBS was sufficient to elicit functionally meaningful effects at the group level in the present study. Hence, our results contribute to the growing understanding of how cerebellar stimulation influences attentional processes.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we found that the right lateral cerebellum is a key contributor in modulating sensory processes that compete for attentional resources during complex tasks. Moreover, across these two experiments, we demonstrated that irrespective of stimulus location, lateralization of function is present in the cerebellum. Lastly, we emphasized the physiological mechanism by which the cerebellum is modulating sensory gating during sensory processing of tactile stimuli driven by attention. Although more work is needed, this study lays the groundwork for understanding the physiological mechanisms and the role of the cerebellum in functional networks underlying attention.
