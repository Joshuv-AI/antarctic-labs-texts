Photophysics of Organic Fluorophore Photobluing and Its Applications in Fluorescence and Super-Resolution Microscopy
Authors: Jung Bae Son, Youmin Ahn, Sanghun Yeou, Yoonjung Cho, Hyeong Jeon An, Chulbom Lee, Nam Ki Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ConspectusPhotoconversionthe light-induced shift of a fluorophore’s absorption and emission spectrahas attracted increasing attention across diverse fields, including single-molecule spectroscopy and super-resolution microscopy. Although blinking and photobleaching are well-established photophysical phenomena of organic dyes and have been extensively studied, photoconversion has historically received comparatively limited attention. First reported decades ago in rhodamine dyes, photoconversion has since been identified in a broad range of organic fluorophores and has become increasingly evident with advances in fluorescence imaging technologies. Unintended photoconversion can introduce spectral crosstalk and lead to misinterpretation in multicolor experiments, motivating intensified efforts to elucidate its underlying mechanisms and develop effective mitigation strategies. In this Account, we focus on the occurrence and molecular mechanisms of photoconversion in rhodamine- and cyanine-based dyes, which are widely used in commercial fluorescent probes, and discuss practical approaches to suppress its impact in fluorescence experiments. Beyond the challenge of controlling unwanted spectral shifts, we also highlight emerging opportunities to exploit photoconversion as an alternative form of “photo-switching” for biophysical applications. We first provide a historical overview of photoconversion phenomena observed in organic fluorophores, including rhodamine, cyanine, and other synthetic dye families. We then summarize the current state of research on photoconversion mechanisms. In rhodamine dyes, photobluing proceeds primarily through progressive N-dealkylation upon irradiation, which has motivated the development of synthetic strategies aimed at producing photoconversion-resistant rhodamine derivatives. Studies on cyanine dyes have revealed that photoconversion requires singlet oxygen and is strongly modulated by buffer pH. Notably, we have demonstrated by NMR spectroscopy that the photoconverted form of Cy5 is structurally identical to Cy3, a troubling observation in light of the widespread use of the Cy3–Cy5 pair as a fluorophore combination for single-molecule fluorescence resonance energy transfer (smFRET) without awareness of the underlying photoconversion. For the C2H2 excision reaction that converts Cy5 into Cy3, both intramolecular and intermolecular mechanisms have been proposed. Here, we present detailed experimental evidence supporting each photobluing pathway and offer mechanistic insights into why two distinct routes can emerge. Depending on the specific cyanine dye and experimental conditions, either pathway may dominate, or both pathways may operate concurrently within the same reaction system. We then focus on the consequences of photobluing for smFRET measurements. Building on our initial observation that even ambient light can induce Cy5 photobluing, we demonstrate that this process not only generates false donor signals but can also produce spurious FRET efficiencies. Finally, we highlight emerging applications that deliberately harness photoconversion as a new class of tools for biophysical research. By addressing its potential, we aim not only to facilitate the regulation of photoconversion in commercial dyes but also to accelerate its purposeful exploitation as a new asset in super-resolution microscopy and single-molecule spectroscopy.

Introduction

Understanding the photophysical and photochemical reactions of organic fluorophore that occur during single-molecule spectroscopy and super-resolution microscopy is crucial, as these imaging techniques play a vital role in advancing biochemical and biophysical studies. One of the most common photochemical processes is irreversible photobleaching, defined as the permanent loss of fluorescence resulting from chemical bond cleavage or the formation of nonradiative products. − In addition, reversible fluorescence quenching frequently occurs through energy transfer processes, such as fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET). , Fluorophore blinking has long been recognized as an artifact in single-molecule measurements. Consequently, significant efforts have been devoted to suppressing blinking by employing triplet-state quenchers, such as Trolox (TX) and β-mercaptoethylamine (MEA). − However, it has become evident that various “switching-on” and “switching-off” behaviors of fluorophores are not nuisances but essential tools for super-resolution imaging and high-density single-particle tracking (spt). − Indeed, photoinduced switching of photoactivable fluorescent proteins, cyanine dyes, and quantum dots has been widely used for super-resolution microscopy, with each class of probes offering distinct advantages and limitations. The development of new photoswitching mechanisms therefore holds great promise for expanding the scope of super-resolution and single-molecule fluorescence imaging, enabling broader applications in the study of biological processes.

Recently, a new mode of photoswitching, known as photoconversion, has emerged. , Photoconversion reactions generate fluorescent species whose optical properties differ from those of the parent fluorophore. − ,, Photobluing , and photoredding, which describe hypsochromic or bathochromic shifts in excitation and emission spectra resulting from photoconversion, have been regarded as artifacts in multicolor super-resolution imaging, colocalization analysis, and single-molecule FRET (smFRET) experiments. ,− In contrast to conventional photoactivatable dyes, which switch from a nonemissive to an emissive state, photoconversion involves interconversion between distinct emissive states with spectrally resolved fluorescence, effectively serving as a “switching-on” mechanism. This feature enables imaging without the need for photoactivation, which often relies on cell-damaging UV irradiation. Furthermore, synthetic efforts are increasingly focused on engineering fluorophores specifically optimized for photoconversion, thereby opening new opportunities for applications in biological imaging.

Understanding the photophysics of fluorescent dyes is essential for the accurate interpretation and rational design of single-molecule and super-resolution imaging experiments. Accordingly, extensive efforts have been devoted to identifying fluorophores that undergo photoconversion, elucidating the conditions under which photoconversion occurs, and uncovering the underlying molecular reaction mechanisms. , These advances not only improve strategies for mitigating unwanted photoconversion artifacts but also enable the deliberate exploitation of photoconversion as a powerful new tool in biophysical studies. ,, In this Account, we review recent advances in the photoconversion of organic fluorophores, with a particular focus on cyanine- and rhodamine-based dyes. We emphasize the underlying photochemical and photophysical mechanisms and discuss the emerging potential of photoconversion as a tool for single-molecule and super-resolution microscopy.

Emergence of Photoconversion in Various Fluorescent Molecules

Photobluing in Various Organic Dyes

Photobluing is now recognized as a widespread photophysical phenomenon rather than a special case confined to a single dye class. It was first reported nearly a half century ago in rhodamine-based dye, ,, where sequential photooxidative N-dealkylation induces a 10–15 nm blue shift in both absorption and emission maxima (Figure a). , Further studies revealed that other triarylmethane dyes can undergo photobluing via N-demethylation and N-de-ethylation, implying a shared mechanistic pathway among structurally related fluorophores. , Beyond rhodamine derivatives, several other dye classes also exhibit photobluing, underscoring its broad occurrence: coumarins (a well-known class of laser dyes), terrylene diimides (utilized in live-cell chemosensor), BODIPY-based dyes such as Lysotracker Red (a commercial lysosome marker), and Nile Red dyes such as NR12A (environment-sensitive membrane probe). ,−

More recently, cyanine-based dyes have also been shown to photoconvert toward shorter wavelengths (Figure b). The first case, reported in 2013, involved DiR (a lipophilic Cy7 derivative) showing irreversible hypsochromic shifts upon irradiation. A year later, a blue-shifted emission signal was observed in a dye stock solution and cell membrane proteins labeled with Cy5 and Alexa Fluor (AF) 647, initially attributed to impurities. After that, it was reported that photoconverted product of AF647 emits blue-shifted fluorescence under two-photon excitation.

These discoveries raised concerns over potential artifacts in super-resolution imaging and single-molecule experiments, prompting investigations on which commercial dyes undergo photobluing. Consequently, it is now established that photobluing occurs in a wide range of commercial rhodamine- and cyanine-based dyes: rhodamine (Rhodamine 6G, Rhodamine B, TMR, JF549, JF525, Atto565, CF633, CF660R) and carborhodamine (Atto647N), cyanine (indocyanine green, Cy7, Cy5, Cy5.5, Cy3B, AF 750, AF 700, AF 647, AF 680, CF647, CF660C, Dylight650, and Dyomics 649p1) (Figure c). − ,

Photoredding in Synthetic Organic Dyes

Photoredding, bathochromic shifts of absorption and emission maxima, was first observed in fluorescent protein (FP) rather than in organic dye. In 1997, green fluorescent protein (GFP) was shown to photoconvert to a red-emissive form under anaerobic conditions upon blue-light irradiation, leading to the development of monomeric, environment-independent photoredding FPs that are now widely used in biophysical studies. −

Although photoredding was not observed in commercial dyes and occurred only in FPs, recent strategies in which photoirradiation extends conjugation lengths have enabled photoredding in synthetic organic dyes. Early examples include photocaged BODIPY derivatives, followed by diazaxanthilidene systems and subsequent extensions to trioxatriangulenium derivatives and dihydroalkaloid-based dyes. , Recently, photoredding has been demonstrated using sulfoxide-bridged dimeric BODIPYs, and a detailed mechanism was reported by Gong et al.

Photophysics of Rhodamine Dye Photobluing

Mechanism of Rhodamine Dye Photobluing

Rhodamine-based dyes are widely used in fluorescence imaging, due to their high quantum yields and photostability, especially in STimulated Emission Depletion (STED) microscopy. − The mechanism underlying photobluing in rhodamine and related coumarin dyes has been elucidated through the identification of various photoproducts by multiple research groups, leading to a consensus model based on oxidative N-dealkylation (Figure a). ,,−

(b) Possible strategies for suppressing step (i), (i)′, and (ii) for mitigating photobluing of rhodamine-based dyes. (c) Photobluing resistant dyes with strategies for blocking each steps of photobluing are represented. [Panels (a–c) were reproduced from ref . Copyright 2019, American Chemical Society.]

The photodealkylation process is initiated by photon absorption, generating an amine radical cation (aminium radical). This species can form either via intramolecular electron transfer from a (di)­alkylamino substituent to the dye core, populating a twisted intramolecular charge-transfer (TICT) excited state, or through one-electron photo-oxidation by an external oxidant (i). Then, the amine radical cation undergoes α-deprotonation (ii), forming an α-aminoalkyl radical. After the formation of peroxide radical (iii) in oxygen-containing media, peroxyhemiaminal species are formed by external reductant and H+ transfer (iv). After O–O bond cleavage, either an N-acylated product (v′) or an iminium-type species (v) is formed. In protic solvents, addition of alcohol or water to the iminium could occur to form a hemiaminal species followed by N-dealkylation (vi) entailing the loss of an alkyl substituent in the form of an aldehyde. Recently, another phobluing phenomenon of silicon rhodamine dye was reported in which central silicon atom is replaced by oxygen atom. However, apart from the involvement of oxygen, the detailed reaction mechanism remains unclear and warrants further investigation.

Strategies to Suppress Rhodamine Dye Photobluing

Several strategies have been developed to suppress photobluing in rhodamine-based dyes by targeting the key steps involved in the N-dealkylation mechanism: one-electron photo-oxidation, transition into a twisted intramolecular charge transfer (TICT) excited state leading to amine radical cation formation, and α-deprotonation leading to an α-aminoalkyl radical (Figure b).

At step (i), amine radical cation formation via external oxidants can be attenuated by reducing the electron-donating ability of the N-substituent. Incorporation of electron-withdrawing groups on nitrogen, as exemplified by fluorinated rhodamines, effectively suppresses this process and diminishes photobluing. In step (ii), α-deprotonation adjacent to the nitrogen atom generates an α-aminoalkyl radical that reacts with molecular oxygen. Dyes lacking an α-hydrogen, such as Rhodamine 110 or tert-alkyl-substituted rhodamines, effectively block this pathway and exhibit strong resistance to photobluing. An alternative pathway (i)′ involves amine radical cation formation through a TICT excited state caused by C–N bond rotation. Restricting this rotation, such as with 7-azabicyclo[2.2.1]­heptane substituents, reduces photobluing, whereas smaller rings such as azetidine or aziridine are less effective. This observation is consistent with the weak correlation previously noted between TICT formation and photobluing efficiency. Recently, isotopic substitution of hydrogen with deuterium in N-alkyl groups has been shown to mitigate photobluing. This substitution reduces electron-donating strength of the N-alkyl amine through isotope effects. This would inhibit oxidant-mediated photoinduced electron transfer in step (i) and suppress transition into a TICT excited state in step (i)′. Additionally, step (ii) could be suppressed by the slower deprotonation rate associated with the stronger C–D bond.

Enhancing the Spectral Shift by Rhodamine Dye Photoconversion

To date, the photoconversion of rhodamine dyes has seen limited application in super-resolution microscopy due to their tendency to exhibit a small (∼10–15 nm) hypochromic shift, rendering the photoconverted species difficult to distinguish spectrally from the parent dye. Overcoming this limitation requires molecular designs that amplify the spectral separation between the two states. Recently, a successful strategy has emerged based on a 1,1′-diazaxanthilidene scaffold that shares the xanthene fluorophore core of rhodamines. In this system, two xanthene cores are connected through photocyclization induced by visible light, leading to a pronounced bathochromic shift of up to 69 nm upon photoconversion. This large spectral displacement enables clear discrimination between prephotoconverted and postphotoconverted species, rendering engineered rhodamine-based dyes viable for imaging applications, with further refinements improving photoconversion efficiency and spectral shift. , In a separate study, tris­(diethylamino)-trioxatriangulenium, which are synthetic dyes structurally similar to rhodamines, , undergo photoconversion via an amine-to-amide transition. This chemical change reduces electron donation to the fluorophore core, narrows the energy gap, and yields an ∼40 nm red shift, allowing spectral discrimination of the photoconverted species.

Photobluing Mechanism of Coumarine and BODIPY Dyes

Basically, photobluing in conventional coumarin-based dyes proceeds through an N-dealkylation mechanism that parallels that of rhodamine dyes. ,, Recently, a distinct photobluing mechanism has been demonstrated in synthesized styryl-coumarin containing aromatic singlet oxygen reactive moieties (ASORMs) like pyrrole or furan. , Upon irradiation, singlet oxygen preferentially oxidizes the ASORM rather than the coumarin core. This leads to dearomatization of the ASORM and disruption of the conjugation inducing an ∼68 nm blue shift. In LysoTracker Red, a commercial BODIPY dye, photobluing arises from double-bond rearrangement or reduction at the pyrrole ring that disrupts conjugation. Subsequently, several BODIPY-based dyes have been deliberately designed to undergo photobluing through distinct mechanism, including oxidative CC bond cleavage in alkene-substituted BODIPYs, photodecaging-induced tautomerization in nitro-benzyloxy BODIPYs without singlet oxygen involvement, and singlet oxygen-mediated photo-oxidation of pyrrolyl substituents in pyrrolyl-BODIPY dyes, all of which shorten the effective conjugation length and induce pronounced blue shifts.

Photophysics of Photobluing of Cyanine Dyes

Factors Affecting Photobluing of Cyanine Dyes

Because cyanine dyes are widely used in fluorescence microscopy, it is essential to investigate the conditions under which photoconversion occurs in order to understand its underlying mechanisms and to prevent it from introducing artifacts.

Singlet Oxygen

Singlet oxygen (1O2) is a major factor in the cleavage of the polyene bridge of cyanine dyes. Consistent with this, photobleaching of cyanine dyes was enhanced by 1O2 generation via Erythrosin B mediated sensitization (Figure a). A substantial body of work has established that 1O2 is also a key factor driving Cy5 photobluing. , The involvement of 1O2 has been independently confirmed by multiple groups using sodium azide (NaN3), a specific quencher of 1O2, and deuterium oxide (D2O), which prolongs the lifetime of 1O2. In contrast, Schnermann and co-workers reported that although 1O2 efficiently induces Cy5 photobluing, other reactive oxygen species do not elicit comparable photobluing effects.

Oxygen Scavenging Systems and Triplet-State Quenchers

Oxygen scavenging systems and triplet-state quenchers are popularly used in single-molecule and super-resolution microscopy to suppress photobleaching and photochemical side reactions. Because 1O2 is required for Cy5 photobluing, the use of an oxygen scavenging system based on glucose oxidase and catalase (GOC) and the addition of ascorbic acid, 1O2 reducing agent, significantly reduced the efficiency of Cy5 photobluing (Figure a and b). The triplet state of Cy5 is responsible for the generation of 1O2 during fluorescence imaging; accordingly, triplet-state quenchers such as TX and MEA also inhibit Cy5 photobluing (Figure a and b). , Importantly, neither oxygen scavenging systems nor triplet-state quenchers completely suppress Cy5 photobluing. As a result, continuous generation of Cy3 from Cy5 photobluing is unavoidable, although its extent depends on the specific imaging conditions. Notably, Cy5 photobluing can occur during any stage of sample handling, including commercial dye production and labeling procedures. Consequently, even nominally Cy5-labeled samples may contain a small population of Cy3 species generated via photobluing. This contribution should therefore be considered when interpreting fluorescence measurements involving Cy5.

pH Dependence

Schnermann and co-workers examined the pH dependence of Cy5 photobluing (Figure c). Cy5 photobluing was most efficient at pH 9, with a conversion efficiency of approximately 4.1%. In contrast, the photobluing efficiency decreased sharply at pH values below 7.5 and above 10.0. Based on this trend, the authors proposed that the reaction is favored under mildly basic conditions.

Mechanism of Cy5 Photobluing: Intramolecular or Intermolecular Reaction

The mechanism underlying cyanine dye photobluing remains incompletely understood. Both intramolecular and intermolecular pathways have been proposed, and substantial experimental evidence has been reported in support of each pathway. , These observations suggest that two pathways may coexist. Alternatively, one pathway may dominate, depending on the experimental conditions and on additional functionalization of the basic cyanine scaffold.

Intramolecular Reaction Mechanism

Initially, the photobluing reaction of AF647 followed first-order kinetics. Based on this observation, Dirix et al. proposed an intramolecular reaction mechanism for AF647 photobluing. More direct molecular-level evidence supporting an intramolecular process was later provided by Schnermann and co-workers. Using asymmetrically deuterated variants of Cy5 in photobluing experiments, they observed exclusively asymmetric Cy3 species as the photoproducts, consistent with an intramolecular reaction mechanism. Based on these results, together with the experimental factors influencing Cy5 photobluing, Schnermann and co-workers proposed a model with the help of ab initio calculation. The proposed intramolecular reaction mechanism is shown in Figure a (upper panel). First, 1O2 undergoes an asynchronous attack on Cy5 at the C1′ position to form the peroxy intermediate 1_a-b, which may alternatively arise from ring-opening of the 1,2-dioxetane generated via [2 + 2] cycloaddition of 1O2 across the C1′–C2′ alkene. In contrast, the addition of 1O2 at the C2′ or C3′ positions leads to singlet oxygen-mediated bond cleavage. Then, because of its high electrophilicity, the dicationic peroxy intermediate 1_a-b undergoes hydration, yielding hydroperoxyethanol 2_a-b. Subsequent rotation around the C2′–C1′ bond enables intramolecular cyclization to form hydroperoxycyclobutanol 3_a-b, which then releases hydroperoxyethanol 4 to ultimately generate Cy3.

Intermolecular Reaction Mechanism

The intermolecular reaction mechanism was primarily proposed by our group (Figure a, bottom panel). Singlet oxygen generated by laser irradiation mediates oxidative cleavage of the polymethine chain. Among the carbonyl compounds generated, the highly reactive iminium aldehydes 6a and 6b may produce 8a and 8b via hydration followed by retro-aldol-type carbon–carbon bond cleavage, while 7a and 7b can further yield 8a and 8b through ROS-mediated decarbonylation. Subsequently, Cy3 can be formed through the coupling reaction between 7a, 7b (Fischer’s aldehydes) and 8a, 8b (Fischer’s bases).

Intermolecular reaction mechanism was supported by various experimental results. The most direct approach to demonstrating an intermolecular process is to use an asymmetrically substituted Cy5 derivative. We used sulfo-Cy5 containing a long carbon chain with a carboxyl group. Upon examining the HPLC chromatogram of asymmetric Cy5 photoproducts, two minor peaks on either side of a main peak were detected with an approximately 1:2:1 ratio (Figure b, middle). The chemical identities of two minor peaks were confirmed by the HRMS that they are the analogues of symmetric Cy3 (Figure b, right). This result clearly demonstrated that sulfo-Cy5 undergoes intermolecular photobluing process. In addition to Cy5, Cy5.5 photobluing also generated three peaks in the HPLC, which also indicates the intermolecular process of photobluing.

This mechanism was further supported by the dependence of Cy5 photobluing on the distance between Cy5 molecules, as demonstrated using dual-labeled DNA constructs with varying interdye separations and antibodies labeled with different degrees of labeling (DOL) (Figure c). Cy5 photobluing occurred more efficiently in dual-labeled DNAs than in single-labeled DNA, with higher efficiency observed as the distance between Cy5 molecules decreased among the dual-labeled DNAs. Similarly, the photobluing conversion ratio increased in antibody samples with higher DOL, where the local distance between Cy5 molecules was reduced due to their higher labeling density (Figure d). We further compared Cy5 photobluing efficiencies at the single-molecule and bulk levels. At the single-molecule level (i.e., corresponding to an extremely low concentration), the photobluing efficiency was only 0.024%, whereas it increased to 2.58% under bulk conditions (0.5 mM). This concentration dependence also supports for an intermolecular mechanism.

In addition to the three Cy3 photoproducts by C5 photobluing, we also identified carbonyl products 7a and 7b (Fischer’s aldehyde) and enamine products 8a and 8b (Fischer’s base) by HRMS (Figure a, bottom panel). When the corresponding HPLC eluates containing both Fischer’s aldehydes and bases were subjected to a 60 °C heating for 24 h, a change to a pink color was observed. Subsequent HRMS analysis confirmed the formation of three types of Cy3 analogues. These observations indicates that 7a, 7b, and 8a, 8b serve as intermediates in the conversion to Cy3, supporting again the intermolecular mechanism.

Intramolecular versus Intermolecular Pathways?

As discussed above, experimental evidence supports both mechanistic pathways. Depending on the specific cyanine dye and experimental conditions, either pathway seems to predominate, or two pathways may operate concurrently within the same reaction. The photobluing efficiencies of cyanine dyes are strongly influenced by their local environments, including conjugation to antibodies and other biomolecular contexts. ,, For example, photoconversion occurs more readily for AF647 maleimide than for AF647 carboxylic acid (Figure e). We summarized Cy5 derivatives that predominantly generate photobluing products via apparently intramolecular or intermolecular processes (Figure f). Functionalization of the parent cyanine scaffoldsuch as the introduction of sulfonate groups on the heterocyclic moiety or the attachment of extended alkyl linkers, including NHS esters and maleimidesmay significantly alter the preferred photobluing pathway. Interestingly, Cy7 photobluing has thus far been observed exclusively via an intramolecular pathway. , In sharp contrast to Cy5, however, Cy7 exhibited a low photobluing efficiency at elevated temperatures and showed no detectable dependence on singlet oxygen for photobluing. Khan et al. proposed that photoexcited Cy7 forms a cyanine radical dication intermediate via electron transfer to oxygen, which subsequently reacts with another oxygen molecule in the presence of ethanolamine to generate Cy5. These observations indicate that Cy7 photobluing may be mechanistically distinct from Cy5 photobluing.

Evidence supporting the coexistence of both pathways has also been reported. At extremely low surface densities, the efficiency of Cy5 photobluing was measured to be as low as 0.024%, which is attributable primarily to an intramolecular process. In contrast, under conditions where Cy5 molecules are densely packed (0.5 mM), the photobluing efficiency increased to 2.54%, with typical values of 2%–3% observed for measurements performed on cell surfaces. These observations suggest that the intramolecular pathway may dominate at low dye concentrations, whereas under confined environmentssuch as total internal reflection fluorescence (TIRF) microscopy measurements at the cell membranethe local accumulation of Cy5 photoproducts favors the intermolecular pathway. In addition to photoinduced C2H2 excision, thermally induced reactions, , i.e., intermolecular pathway, may also contribute concurrently to Cy5 photobluing during the fluorescence imaging of living cells.

Photobluing of Cyanine Dye under Ambient Light and Its Effect on smFRET Experiments

In smFRET experiments, fluorophores that undergo spectral shifts are not suitable, because apparent FRET efficiencies can change even without donor–acceptor distance change. Because Cy5 photoconverts to Cy3, smFRET experiments employing Cy3–Cy5 pair require a special care for the potential artifacts by Cy5 photobluing. We have shown previously that Cy5 can be photoconverted to Cy3 by room light, and here we reinvestigated its impact on smFRET using alternating-laser excitation (ALEX-FRET). ALEX-FRET can accurately measure FRET efficiency of a freely diffusing single molecule. , Also, structural changes of a biomolecule, − binding affinity, , and fast dynamic transition can be acquired by ALEX-FRET. Stock solutions of Cy5-labeled 20-nucleotide poly dT single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) overhangs (dT20) and its complementary ssDNA (d20C) were exposed to ambient light before DNA hybridization (Figure a), after which dsDNA was observed by ALEX-FRET. The ratio of photogenerated Cy3-labeled sample substantially increased, depending on the light exposure time (Figure b and c), which indicates the Cy5 photoconversion by ambient light can be detected in smFRET measurements. Since dsDNA labeled by Cy5 and photogenerated Cy3 can probabilistically exist, the FRET signal was also observed in the acceptor-only sample (Cy5–Cy5 sample) (Figure b). The FRET signal from samples with the S ≈ 0.5 value gradually increased with light exposure time (Figure b). These effects are expected to be more pronounced in multicolor smFRET experiments, , highlighting that unavoidable ambient light exposure during sample preparation can introduce false donor signals.

Photoconversion as New Tools for Biophysics

Photoswitching phenomena have since been harnessed as foundational principles for developing indispensable tools in super-resolution imaging and biophysics. In a similar way, photoconversion is now emerging as a novel photoswitching strategy, as illustrated by the examples discussed below.

Long-Term Single-Cell Tracking in Live Animals and in 3D Cultured Cells

Photoconversion of organic dyes enables long-term, single-cell tracking in vivo and surmounts the drawbacks of existing approaches (Figure a). ,, Population-based labeling buries individual cellular behaviors, while photoconvertible fluorescent proteins require transfection, short wavelength irradiation, and suffer from photoconverted signal lose within about 24 h. − By contrast, organic dyes can undergo irreversible photoconversion with visible to near-IR light, thereby reducing phototoxicity, improving tissue penetration without genetic modification. Early work demonstrated long-term tracking of hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells in mouse bone marrow for over 130 h using photoconversion of the lipophilic dye DiR. Subsequent applications extended to spatiotemporal labeling in 2D monolayers, 3D tumor spheroids, and xenografts. In parallel, multiphoton excitation enabled axial confinement of photoconversion for 3D single-cell tracking, ,, while chemically modified Cy7 derivatives with enhanced photoconversion efficiency further improved sensitivity for monitoring tumor cell migration under physiological conditions.

Single-Molecule Localization Microscopy (SMLM) Imaging

Recent work has adapted photoconversion as an alternative photoswitching strategy for SMLM by harnessing the photobluing of Cy7 into Cy5, effectively treating Cy7 as a dark-state reservoir for Cy5. This method enables single-molecule localization and detection by balancing bleaching and photoconversion. However, the relatively low photoconversion yield of Cy7 results in sparse localizations, limiting image completeness (Figure b, left). To address this limitation, an artificial neural networks-based reconstruction method leveraged structural redundancy to consolidate these sparse localizations into a complete microtubule image (Figure b, right). Related work further demonstrated photoconversion-regulated switching between two photoblinking-capable emissive states of a BODIPY-based dye, enabling sequential SMLM imaging and extended localization capacity.

DNA-Based Point Accumulation for Imaging Nanoscale Topography (DNA-PAINT) with Higher Imager Concentration

Another application exploited Cy7 photobluing to fast DNA-PAINT by allowing higher imager-strand concentrations without increasing background noise (Figure c). In conventional DNA-PAINT, high imager concentrations lead to elevated background signals, necessitating lower imager levels and prolonged acquisition times. In contrast, photobluing-based DNA-PAINT employs Cy7 as a dark reservoir for Cy5, predominantly activating target-bound imagers under TIRF illumination. Even if photoconversion occurs for unbound imagers within the evanescent field, these photoconverted imagers rapidly diffuse away, with newly arriving imagers being largely unphotoconverted. As a result, background signal does not accumulate, enabling single-molecule localization even at 1000-fold higher imager concentrations, achieving approximately 2-fold faster acquisition while maintaining comparable resolution.

High-Density Single-Particle Tracking (spt) in a Living Cell without UV and Additives

AF647 and Cy5 can be photoconverted by 642 nm illumination in standard cell culture media without chemical additives. Building on this observation, we demonstrated that photobluing of AF647 and Cy5 enables high-density spt for membrane proteins in living cells (Figure d). Conventional spt methods typically rely on UV photoactivation along with oxygen-scavenging systems or reducing agents, which can induce cytotoxicity. ,, Under physiological conditions, continuous photoconversion maintains a steady influx of new emitters, preventing the rapid loss of trajectory density caused by photobleaching. Also, photoconverted AF647 exhibited an average trajectory length of about 21.5 frames which was approximately 3-fold longer than AF647 without oxygen scavenging, thereby facilitating the investigation of anomalous diffusive behavior and microheterogeneity within individual trajectories. ,

Developing a New Class of High-Performance Organic Dyes via Photoconversion

Recently, Ryu and co-workers reported the discovery of a high-performance organic dye generated through Cy5 photobluing. By isolating photoproducts from tetra-sulfonated Cy5, they identified and purified a highly photostable dye, termed Phoenix Fluor 555 (PF555). Conventional Cy5 photobluing typically involves net C2H2 excision without alteration of the heterocyclic framework. In contrast, PF555 formation is accompanied by both C2H2 excision and a structural modification of one indole moiety, which is converted to a 3-oxo-quinoline group. Remarkably, PF555 exhibits an order-of-magnitude improvement in photostability compared with conventional dyes. This high photostability enabled long-term live-cell imaging with sustained fluorescence signals, unlike conventional dyes that rapidly lose signal upon repeated imaging (Figure e).

Organelle Tracking in Living Cells

Since intracellular organelles are dynamic and smaller than the diffraction limit of the visible light, super-resolution imaging is required for localization and tracking in living cells. , Synthetically engineered photoconvertible dyes which accumulate in specific organelles enable photoconversion to act as a population control for organelle tracking in living cells without cytotoxic additives (Figure e). By photobluing, a styryl-coumarin derivative localized to lipid droplets enabled tracking of lipid droplets in living cells.

CONCLUSION AND FUTURE OUTLOOK

In recent years, photoconversion has been identified in a wide range of commercially available organic dyes through detailed spectroscopic and chemical characterization. The conditions under which photoconversion occurs, as well as the underlying mechanisms, have now been extensively elucidated. Moreover, advances in our ability to control photoconversion have enabled the development of new super-resolution imaging methodologies. In particular, the use of photoconversion as a means of population control has shown considerable promise in SMLM. Its occurrence under cell-growth conditions and its minimal cytotoxicity further suggests broad potential for biophysical applications, including the tracking of biomolecules in live cells and even in vivo systems. Despite these advances, significant challenges remain. Most notably, the relatively low efficiency of photoconversion remains insufficient for robust SMLM, limiting complete imaging of target populations. Recently, Klan and co-workers reported an improvement in Cy7 photobluing efficiency to approximately 30%, a development that is expected to substantially enhance achievable resolution in SMLM. In parallel, further progress is required to improve dye cell permeability and targeting strategies for specific labeling in live-cell applications.

It is now evident that the goal of photoconversion research is no longer merely to suppress these phenomena, but rather to harness them as a novel “switch-on” mechanism. Chemistry-driven enhancements in photoconversion efficiency and spectral tuning, combined with deep-learning-based image reconstruction and advanced biomolecular labeling strategies, are poised to establish a new generation of photoconversion-based fluorescence imaging methodologies. These advances will enable the investigation of biophysical problems that were previously inaccessible.
