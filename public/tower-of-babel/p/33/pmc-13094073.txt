Short-term outcomes of health belief model-based brief virtual education on HPV infection and vaccination among pharmacy students: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Sümeyye Kavak, Abdikarim Abdi, Pınar Ay, Yeliz Şahin, Mesut Sancar, Betul Okuyan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGenital Human Papillomavirus (HPV) infection was a global public health concern, with low vaccination rates in Türkiye highlighting a critical need for effective interventions. Pharmacists, as future health advocates, were a crucial target population. This study evaluated the short-term outcomes of brief virtual education program based on the Health Belief Model (HBM) on pharmacy students’ knowledge and health beliefs on HPV infection and vaccination.MethodsA randomized controlled trial with a pre-test/post-test design was conducted with pharmacy students in Istanbul, Türkiye. Participants were randomized to an intervention group that viewed an educational video based on HBM which took almost five minutes or a control group. The primary outcomes were HPV knowledge and health beliefs, measured using validated scales at baseline and one day post-intervention, while the secondary outcome was vaccination intention. Statistical analyses, including the Mann-Whitney U test, Wilcoxon signed-rank test, Chi-square test, and McNemar test were performed to compare between and within groups, with a significance level of p < 0.05.ResultsThe intervention group (n = 59) showed significant increases in HPV knowledge and positive changes in all HBM constructs (p < 0.05). No significant changes were observed in the control group (n = 68). While both groups increased their intention to get vaccinated after the assessments, there was no significant difference in the final intention rate between them (p > 0.05).ConclusionThe HBM-based brief virtual education program slightly improved HPV knowledge and health beliefs among pharmacy students in Türkiye, a population with critically low vaccination rates.Trial registrationNCT05175482 (date of registration 24/12/2021) https://clinicaltrials.gov/ct2/show/NCT05175482?term=betul+okuyan&draw=2&rank=1.

Introduction

Genital Human Papillomavirus (HPV) infection is the most prevalent sexually transmitted infection globally, posing a major public health concern due to its strong association with several cancers [1]. Most sexually active individuals acquire at least one genital HPV infection during their lifetime. Global estimates indicate that the prevalence of HPV is 12% among women and up to 31% among men, with higher rates reported in Eastern Europe and sub-Saharan Africa [2]. A substantial proportion of young people, particularly those aged 14–59 years, are at increases risk of infection [2].

HPV is classified into high and low-risk subtypes. High-risk strains like HPV-16 and HPV-18 are strongly associated with several cancers, accounting for 91% of cervical, 91% of anal, and 70% of oropharyngeal cancers [3]. Globally, cervical cancer ranked as the fourth most common cancer and the fourth leading cause of cancer-related mortality among women in 2022 [4].

HPV vaccines are highly effective in preventing HPV-related cancers, particularly cervical cancer, and genital warts. For instance, the nine-valent HPV vaccine provides nearly 100% protection against its targeted strains, which are responsible for approximately 90% of all cervical and other anogenital cancers [4, 5].

In Türkiye, HPV prevalence has been reported to range from 2.0% to 46.0% [6]. Cervical cancer is a significant public health challenge, ranking as the 12th most frequent cancer among women [7]. However, despite existing national recommendations for cervical cancer screening Türkiye lacks a national HPV vaccination program [7]. This gap highlights the importance of educational interventions, particularly for future healthcare providers and advocates.

Pharmacists are crucial for increasing HPV vaccine coverage and reducing HPV-related diseases through patient education, administration, and advocacy [8]. However, global data have revealed significant variations in HPV knowledge and vaccination uptake among pharmacy and medical students, with low levels in some regions and moderate to high rates in others [9–11]. This indicated a need for targeted educational interventions, as many pharmacy students fall within the recommended age group for vaccination but may lack sufficient knowledge [12]. Such programs not only improve students’ personal health literacy and vaccination rates but also prepare them to be effective future healthcare advocates [13]. This dual benefit is particularly important since lack of strong healthcare provider recommendations is a major barrier to achieving higher vaccination rates in the general population [14].

The Health Belief Model (HBM) is a widely recognized theoretical framework used to understand, predict, and modify health behaviours, including vaccination uptake [15, 16]. This model assumes that an individual’s likelihood of engaging in a health action is determined by their perceptions of perceived susceptibility, perceived severity, perceived benefits, perceived barriers, self-efficacy, and cues to action [15]. Thus, this model offers an ideal foundation for designing targeted educational interventions, allowing researchers to identify and address specific determinants influencing health behaviours. By systematically addressing misinformation, enhancing accurate risk perception, highlighting benefits of preventative measures, mitigating barriers, and fostering self-efficacy, HBM-based interventions can effectively influence attitudes, intentions, and ultimately, immunization uptake [17].

Virtual education has rapidly evolved into a powerful modality, particularly accelerated by recent global health crises [18]. While both traditional face-to-face and online learning methods require students to attend, comprehend the subject, and complete assignments, they differ significantly in their approach. Traditional learning is typically teacher-centred and occurs at a fixed time and location, whereas online learning is student-centred, promotes active learning, and is not restricted by time or location [18]. The ability to deliver education through internet-based information systems offers significant advantages, including enhanced accessibility, unparalleled flexibility, and potential for fostering interdisciplinary collaboration. Systematic reviews suggest that virtual education can be as effective as, or even more effective than traditional methods for knowledge transfer and attitudinal change [19].

This study aimed to evaluate the short-term outcomes of a brief, HBM-based Virtual Education Program on pharmacy students’ knowledge and health beliefs regarding HPV infection and vaccination. Given that HPV vaccination is not covered by health insurance in Türkiye, we also assessed the impact of this virtual education program on their intention to receive the HPV vaccine, as a secondary outcome.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study was a two-arm, parallel-group, randomized controlled trial (RCT) measuring outcomes at baseline and post-intervention (Fig. 1). The trial was conducted at a public university in Istanbul, Türkiye, between December 30, 2021, and June 15, 2022. The study design and results are reported in accordance with the CONSORT (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials) 2025 Statements [20]. The study was carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and the study protocol was approved by the Marmara University Institute of Health Sciences Ethical Committee (reference number: 14.12.2020/104). Trial registration was done (Clinicaltrials.gov- NCT05175482- date of registration 24/12/2021).

Participants

All pharmacy students (n = 718) enrolled at the university were invited to participate in the study through an official email sent by the research team. Eligibility criteria included being 18 years of age or older, not having previously received the HPV vaccine, and providing electronic informed consent. Those who completed the pre-test questionnaire but did not complete the post-test questionnaire were excluded from the final analysis.

Randomization and blinding

After completing the baseline questionnaire, participants were randomized electronically into either the intervention group or the control group. A computer-generated sequence of random numbers was used for allocation in a 1:1 ratio. The randomization sequence was generated and maintained by a researcher who was not involved in data collection or analysis. Due to the nature of the intervention, participants were not blinded to their group assignment.

Intervention

The intervention was a tailored, animated virtual education program on HPV infection and vaccination, based on the HBM. The content focused on the following HBM domains: perceived susceptibility (e.g., global and national HPV rates, risk factors), perceived severity (e.g., link between HPV and cancer, consequences of infection), perceived benefits (e.g., benefits of screening and vaccination), and perceived barriers (e.g., vaccine side effects, cost, and reimbursement). A five-minute animated video was developed to enhance engagement and support learning. The content was developed by the research team (SK and BO) and aligned with materials from major international and national health organizations, including the Centres for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), World Health Organization (WHO), the Turkish Ministry of Health and other relevant institutions (Table 1). An expert panel of three public health specialists and two clinical pharmacists reviewed the content for accuracy and clarity, and a pilot test with eight pharmacy students was conducted to ensure comprehensibility and effectiveness before finalization. The duration of the animation video was 5 min.

The link to the video was sent to participants in the intervention group via email immediately after the baseline survey.

Participants in the control group did not receive any intervention but completed the same baseline and post-intervention surveys as the intervention group, allowing for comparisons of change over time.

Outcome variables

There were three outcome variables: HPV knowledge score, HBM score and vaccination intention.

The HPV knowledge score (HPV-KS) developed by Guvenç et al. [21] evaluates knowledge about HPV infection and vaccination. The scale consists of ten items, and the total score is calculated by summing correct responses. The maximum possible score is ten, with higher scores representing greater knowledge [21].

The HBM Scale for HPV and vaccination (HBMS-HPVV) developed by Guvenc et al. [21] consists of 14 items rated on a 4-point scale. The constructs of the scale were perceived severity (n = 4 items; min-max score: 4–16), perceived susceptibility (n = 2 items; min-max score:2–8), perceived benefits of vaccination (n = 3 items; min-max score:3–12), and perceived barriers to vaccination (n = 5 items; min-max score: 5–20). Response options were 1 (strongly disagree), 2 (somewhat agree), 3 (agree), and 4 (strongly agree) [21]. Higher scores indicate greater perceived benefits of vaccination, as well as higher perceived susceptibility to and severity of infection. The scores of perceived barriers were reserved.

Vaccination intention was assessed with a single question assessing participants’ likelihood of getting the HPV vaccine within the following six months, using a 5-point Likert scale (5: strongly agree to 1: strongly disagree) [22]. A positive intention was defined as selecting either ‘strongly agree’ or ‘agree’.

Data collection

Baseline and post-intervention electronic surveys were administered via the LimeSurvey platform. The survey included demographic questions and three parts corresponding to the outcomes defined above. The baseline survey was sent to all students and upon completion, participants were randomized. The post-intervention survey was sent to both groups approximately one day after the baseline survey. To maximize the response rate, two email reminders were sent to non-responders within one month. Permission to use the scales was obtained from the original developers.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated using G*Power software. According to the estimated difference between the mean score of participants’ knowledge level in both groups [17], the sample size was calculated using alpha 0.05 and power 0.90. It was estimated that at least 54 participants should be in each group. Considering a potential dropout rate of 10%, we aimed to recruit approximately 59 participants per group.

Statistical methods

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (Statistical Package for Social Sciences) Windows 11.0. Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk-W tests. Continuous variables were presented as median [Interquartile Range]. The Mann-Whitney U test (for between-group comparisons) and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test (for within-group comparisons) were done. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages and compared using the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests or McNemar test (for pre-post evaluation) as appropriate. The significance level was set at p < 0.05.

Results

A total of 718 pharmacy students were invited to participate in the study. Of these, 528 (73.5%) were excluded for various reasons (Fig. 1). The remaining 190 participants were randomized to either the intervention (n = 104) or the control (n = 86) groups. A total of 127 participants (66.8%) completed both the baseline and post-intervention assessments (Fig. 1). There were no significant differences in baseline characteristics of the participants who completed the study versus those who dropped out (p > 0.05).

Demographic characteristics of the participants in the intervention and the control groups are presented in Table 2. At baseline, there were no significant differences between the two groups in HPV-KS or in any of the HBMS-HPVV domains (p > 0.05 for all) (Table 3). At baseline, the rate of intention to receive the HPV vaccination in the next six months was higher in control group (26.5%) when compared with intervention group (10.2%) and there was no significant difference in intention to receive HPV vaccination between the two groups (p > 0.05).

Between-group and within-group comparisons of outcome variables in the intervention and control groups

The intervention group showed a statistically significant increase in HPV-KS (p < 0.05), along with positive changes in perceived vaccination benefits, susceptibility to HPV infection, and perceived severity from baseline to post-intervention (p < 0.05 for all). A significant decrease in the perceived barriers score was also observed in the intervention group (p < 0.05). Participants in the control group showed no significant changes in HPV-KS scores or in any of the HBMS-HPVV domains over the same period (p > 0.05 for all).

In the intervention group, the proportion of participants reporting an intention to receive the HPV vaccination in the next six months increased from 10.2% (6 participants) at baseline to 30.5% (18 participants) post-intervention. In the control group, the corresponding proportions were 26.5% (18 participants) and 39.7% (27 participants), respectively (p < 0.001). There was no significant difference between the groups in the postintervention proportion of participants with the intention to receive the HPV vaccine (p > 0.05).

Discussion

In this randomized controlled trial, the HBM-based brief virtual education program slightly improved HPV knowledge and health beliefs among pharmacy students in Türkiye, a population with critically low vaccination rates. This outcome was consistent with prior research showing that educational interventions effectively enhance vaccine-related knowledge and attitudes, which are essential precursors to vaccine uptake [23].

These findings have important public health implications. Previous studies have consistently linked HPV vaccine uptake to cognitive factors like higher knowledge, positive attitudes toward vaccination, and receiving information from a healthcare provider [23]. By targeting the perceived severity of HPV infection, the program might have strengthened the perceived connection between HPV infection and cancer, a powerful motivator for vaccination [24]. The reduction in perceived barriers, such as concerns about side effects, suggested the program effectively addressed common sources of vaccine hesitancy [17]. This is particularly relevant in Türkiye, where the absence of a national vaccination program and insurance coverage creates a significant financial barrier to HPV vaccine access. By enhancing the health literacy of future healthcare providers, we can enable them to become effective public health advocates. This, in turn increase public acceptance of HPV vaccines, generating the demand needed to influence policymakers to include them in the national immunization program [25].

A striking finding of our study was the extremely low baseline vaccination rate among our students: only 2.64% (n = 19) of the 718 students reported being vaccinated. This rate was even lower than that reported among medical students in Türkiye, where a study found a 3.5% uptake rate in a sample of 687 students [26]. That study also reported higher awareness levels and a greater proportion of female participants, both of which were associated with higher vaccine uptake [27]. In contrast, a recent systematic review by Levy et al. on health professional students in other countries reported much higher HPV vaccine initiation rates, ranging from 34.6% to 70.3%, with 28.3% to 58.3% of students being fully vaccinated [28]. This disparity underscored the urgent need for targeted interventions in Türkiye. Our students, before the intervention, showed partial awareness and knowledge, with a median score of 5.0 out of 10. While 99% of students in other studies knew HPV caused cervical cancer, fewer knew about its link to other cancers (40–47%) [28].

The effectiveness of the intervention was likely attributable to its theory-driven approach, consistent with the commentary highlighting the success of interventions grounded in established behavioural models, such as the HBM used in the current study and the Theory of Planned Behaviour (TPB) [29]. Levy et al.‘s review identified eight intervention studies published between 2015 and 2021 targeting health professional students: however, none focused specifically on pharmacy students [28]. Furthermore, none of these studies reported using a theoretical framework to design the intervention, and none included a control group. The interventions reviewed comprised team-based learning and lectures, both of which were associated with improvements in vaccine knowledge and attitudes [28]. A study revealed that medical students with lower knowledge levels of HPV infection were more hesitant to recommend vaccination at baseline however, they became more likely to recommend it following an educational session [12]. Levy et al. also noted that educational interventions can improve healthcare providers’ counselling intentions, a crucial outcome that was not assessed in our study [12, 28].

A significant portion of interventional educational studies on HPV vaccination reviewed by Levy et al. [28] have utilized digital tools, specifically videos and online interventions similar to our study [12, 24, 30–32]. These technology-based approaches offered a practical solution to the common barriers of traditional educational methods, such as scheduling conflicts and high costs, while effectively promoting HPV vaccine uptake [33].

Our study had several limitations. Despite the rigorous randomized controlled trial (RCT) design and the use of a technology-based, theory-driven intervention, our study highlighted significant methodological complexities inherent to educational research. We observed no substantial difference in vaccination intention between the intervention and control groups, a finding that might be explained by several challenges. The inability to blind participants was a common issue in educational interventions, and it could have led to a compensatory rivalry, or the John Henry effect, where control group participants, aware of their non-intervention status, increased their efforts to seek information to close a perceived knowledge gap [34]. This might be compounded by participant reactivity, as students in a learning environment might actively seek information after an initial assessment to improve their future performance, thereby influencing their responses in subsequent assessments [34]. These challenges, also noted in similar studies [17, 30], could have led to an underestimation of the intervention’s true effect. The high attrition rate in the intervention group (43.3%) and the control groups (20.9%) could have led to selection bias, though baseline analysis showed no significant differences between those who completed the study and those who dropped out. The reliance on a self-reported survey might be subject to social desirability bias. Most importantly, the short follow-up period (one day) limited our ability to assess the long-term retention of knowledge and, more critically, whether the increased intention translated into actual vaccination behaviour. Future studies could be designed to test the long-term impact. Finally, the study was conducted on a single cohort of pharmacy students, which limited the generalizability of our findings to other healthcare professions or the wider Turkish population. There could be John Henry effect and contamination bias. Future studies could reduce the risk of contamination bias by adopting a clustered design, in which groups are allocated to different campuses and/or institutions. Additionally, future research could develop and evaluate complex interventions targeting financial barriers in combination with brief virtual education based on the HBM. The awareness and information gained by the intervention group might have diffused through their peer and social networks, contaminating the control group and blurring the lines between the study arms. This diffusion of benefits made it difficult to attribute the outcomes solely to the intervention and was a critical factor to consider when interpreting the results of such studies [35]. A further possible explanation for the lack of improvement in vaccination intention is the cost of the HPV vaccine, which was not reimbursed by national health insurance at the time of the study.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this randomized controlled trial showed that a brief HBM-based virtual education program, slightly enhanced HPV knowledge levels and positively shifts key health beliefs among pharmacy students. The intervention slightly increased participants’ perceived benefits, susceptibility, and severity related HPV infection while reducing perceived barriers. This is particularly significant given the alarmingly low baseline HPV vaccination rate among the students surveyed, underscoring the urgent need for educational initiatives in this population.

The lack of a significant difference in vaccination intention between the groups may reflect methodological challenges—such as the John Henry effect and diffusion of benefits—as well as the cost of the HPV vaccine, which was not covered by national health insurance at the time. As well as educational initiatives, behavioural interventions could be developed to enhance motivation and opportunities to receive the HPV vaccination in the future studies.
