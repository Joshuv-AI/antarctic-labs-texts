Evaluation of squamous cell carcinoma antigen as a diagnostic biomarker in pulmonary diseases: a male cohort study
Authors: Lin Lirong, Wang Kangying
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEarly pulmonary disease recognition in men is often hindered by nonspecific clinical features and overlapping laboratory parameters. Squamous Cell Carcinoma Antigen (SCCA), a glycoprotein traditionally associated with malignancy, has been proposed as a potential biomarker in a wider spectrum of pulmonary disorders. This study evaluated the diagnostic performance of serum SCCA in pulmonary diseases among the male cohort and determined its relationship with inflammatory markers.MethodsWe analyzed serum SCCA levels in 533 male patients diagnosed with squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), pneumonia, pulmonary nodules, or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) at the Affiliated Hospital of Putian University (January 2017–November 2021), and compared them to 80 healthy male controls. Intergroup differences were assessed, correlations between SCCA and inflammatory markers were analyzed, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were used to determine disease-specific diagnostic performance.ResultsSerum SCCA levels differed significantly across disease groups. SCC patients had markedly higher concentrations (7.14 ± 3.64 ng/ml) than all other groups, whereas pulmonary nodules showed the lowest levels (1.45 ± 0.81 ng/ml). SCCA achieved its highest diagnostic accuracy in SCC (AUC = 0.792), outperforming its ability to differentiate other pulmonary conditions.ConclusionSerum SCCA expression shows a promising and supportive biomarker in the diagnostic evaluation of pulmonary diseases among the male patients, meriting further validation in larger, multi-centre studies.

Introduction

Lung diseases, or respiratory diseases (RDs), encompass pathological conditions that impair gas exchange in the respiratory system [1]. Globally, RDs disproportionately affect men, driven by occupational exposures, hormonal and biological factors, and behavioral patterns [2, 3]. Since 2019, lung diseases among the males have been ranked as the third leading cause of death worldwide, hence contributing substantially to healthcare costs and societal burden [4]. Early-stage lung diseases often present without specific symptoms; when clinical signs such as cough or dyspnea do arise, diagnosis is typically incidental via chest X-ray or computed tomography (CT). Despite the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) aiming for a one-third reduction in premature mortality from non-communicable diseases by 2030 [5], diagnostic challenges threaten progress, delaying timely and accurate treatment.

Conventional diagnostic tools for lung disease include bronchoscopy, serological testing, and imaging. While bronchoscopy allows direct visualization of bronchial changes, it is invasive, poorly tolerated by some patients, and limited in detecting concealed lesions. Serum tumor markers offer a non-invasive alternative, detecting antigens or bioactive substances aberrantly expressed by tumor tissues but minimally present in normal cells [6]. Introduced to clinical practice in 1978, tumor markers have since become integral to cancer detection, monitoring, and prognosis.

Squamous cell carcinoma antigen (SCCA), encoded on chromosome 18q21.3, is a serine protease inhibitor expressed at low levels in normal epithelial tissues but elevated in malignant squamous lesions [7]. First purified in 1977 from cervical squamous cell carcinoma [8], SCCA is closely associated with tumor development, metastasis, staging, and prognosis [9–11]. Its serum concentration rises with disease progression and decreases following effective treatment; renewed elevation often signals recurrence or metastasis [12–14]. Beyond malignancies such as cervical, lung, and liver cancers [15–17], clinical observations suggest SCCA may also be elevated in certain non-malignant lung conditions involving epithelial regeneration or inflammation.

However, despite widespread use of SCCA in cancer diagnostics, its expression profile across the spectrum of pulmonary diseases, both malignant and non-malignant, remains unexplored. Given the distinct epidemiological, biological, and exposure-related characteristics of respiratory diseases, defining disease-specific SCCA patterns could enhance early, non-invasive diagnosis and aid in differential diagnosis. This study addresses this gap by systematically evaluating serum SCCA levels in lung cancer, pneumonia, pulmonary nodules, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) among the male cohort, compared with healthy controls, to clarify its diagnostic utility in pulmonary pathology. To our knowledge, this is the first study to profile serum SCCA levels across multiple malignant and non-malignant pulmonary diseases among male patients, providing male-specific diagnostic insights that have been largely overlooked in biomarker research.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval and research subjects

This retrospective cohort study received ethical approval from the Affiliated Hospital of Putian University ethics committee (Puyi Fulun [202409]) and was conducted under the Helsinki Declaration principles. The research group included 533 male lung disease patients who visited the Affiliated Hospital of Putian University between January 2017 and November 2021. The participants were included after written consent. The study sequence was as follows: (a) written informed consent was obtained from the patient; (b) vital signs, including body temperature, were recorded during the initial physical examination; (c) auscultation findings, including wheezing, were documented by three attending respiratory physicians; and (d) fasting venous blood samples were collected within 24 h of admission for routine laboratory tests, including SCCA measurement.

Inclusion criteria: The study targeted predominantly male subjects, since RDs are highly prevalent in males compared to females. The age range of the participants was from 16 to 92 years old. The lung cancer subtype studied was squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), which is strongly associated with smoking and is more prevalent in men due to tobacco-related carcinogenesis [18].

SCC was diagnosed based on clinical symptoms, including hemoptysis, dyspnea, hoarseness, loss of weight, fatigue, chest pains, and coughs. All cases of pulmonary squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) included in this study were pathologically confirmed through histopathological examination of biopsy or surgical specimens. Chest CT scan was used to confirm and define the tumor size, invasion, and location [19]. Inclusion criteria for lung cancer patients included individuals meeting either of the following factors: a pathologically diagnosed lung cancer case, or clinically diagnosed benign pulmonary nodules (confirmed by follow-up as guided by the 2018 Chinese Consensus on Pulmonary Nodule Diagnosis and Treatment, control, or by pathology), and other benign pulmonary diseases such as COPD, sarcoidosis, and pulmonary fibrosis.

Lung nodules were diagnosed using chest X-ray and CT scan. Lung nodules characteristics included the following: low-risk nodules were < 6 mm, while high-risk lung nodules were ≥ 8 mm. Nodules with irregular borders, eccentric or central/popcorn calcification, part-solid, solid, or ground-glass opacity, and the location of the nodules, where upper lobe nodules are mostly malignant. In the pulmonary nodule group, malignancy was excluded through radiological follow-up, histopathological assessment when clinically indicated, and absence of progressive malignant features during the follow-up period. Nodules demonstrating benign imaging characteristics and clinical stability were classified as non-malignant.

The Chinese Guidelines for Diagnosis and Treatment of Community-acquired Pneumonia (2016 edition) [20] was used for pneumonia diagnosis. These included: (1) The onset of community disease; (2) manifesting the following related pneumonia clinical signs: (i) recent cough, existing respiratory disease signs with or without dyspnea/ chest pain/ purulent sputum/hemoptysis; or expectoration (ii) fever; (iii) peripheral WBCs > 10 × 109/L in the presence or absence of a neutrophilic left shift; (iv) pulmonary consolidation symptoms and wet rales; and 3. chest imaging showing leaf or segment contrast, patchy infiltration, interstitial changes in the presence or absence of pleural effusion, or ground glass shadow. Patients with criteria 1 and 3 and any one of the 2 criteria were confirmed to have pneumonia, except for those with pulmonary tumor, pulmonary tuberculosis, non-infectious interstitial infection, infiltration of pulmonary eosinophil, pulmonary edema and embolism, atelectasis, and pulmonary vasculitis. The COPD diagnosis was done according to the GOLD 2019 criteria using spirometry, which assesses lung functioning and airflow obstruction. A post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC ratio < 70% indicated a remarkable obstructive defect. Patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) were recruited during clinically stable phases of disease and not during acute exacerbations. Stability was defined by the absence of acute worsening of respiratory symptoms, hospitalization, or systemic corticosteroid treatment within the four weeks preceding enrollment. Patients in the normal control and lung disease group (including lung cancer, lung nodules, pneumonia, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease) were to provide 3 mL of venous blood samples.

Exclusion criteria: Patients with serious organic diseases such as liver and kidney disease or abnormal blood biochemistry; patients with a recent infection history; patients without valid blood samples; Patients with systemic inflammation, such as bronchial asthma or rheumatoid arthritis, and patients who do not meet the age range.

Eighty healthy control group was recruited from age-matched male volunteers attending routine health check-ups at the same institution during the study period. All controls had no clinical history or symptoms of pulmonary disease, malignancy, or chronic inflammatory conditions. Eligibility was confirmed through medical history, physical examination, chest radiography, and routine laboratory investigations. Only individuals with normal findings were included as healthy controls. To be included in the healthy group, the participants were to have: abdominal ultrasound and X-ray examinations (taken within 3 months before enrolment) that showed no organic diseases such as lung, liver, and kidney diseases; no pulmonary nodules, blood biochemical examinations that showed normal ranges of liver and kidney functions; and no recent history of infection.

Measurements

After the ethical clearance and patients’ consents, 3 mL of fasting venous blood was drawn, and the serum SCCA was quantitatively detected. A chemiluminescence immunoassay was done using the MAGLUMI 2000 plus automatic chemiluminescence instrument (Shenzhen New Industrial Biomedical Engineering Co. Ltd, Shenzhen, China). The chemiluminescence immunoassay was carried out using the reagents contained in the instrument’s supporting kit. The quality control reagents and products used were purchased from Bio-Rad (Hercules, California, United States). The procedure was done in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines. The specimens were centrifuged at 3000 r/min for 5 min within 1 h after collection. The serum was separated and tested within 2 h. The standard operating procedures were strictly followed. If the test could not be performed on the same day, the separated serum was placed in a -20° refrigerator for further testing.

We measured the body temperature with a digital thermometer (CTH6500, Klingenberg, Germany). The “increase in body temperature” refers to a body temperature > 37.3 °C, based on the upper limit of the normal morning body temperatures, consistent with national clinical guidelines and local hospital practice [21]. According to the standard definition, wheezing was described as a high-pitched, musical, continuous adventitious lung sound produced by airflow through narrowed or compressed airways (≈ 400 Hz), often more prominent during expiration, lasting > 250 ms. Assessment was done by three attending respiratory physicians, and discrepancies were resolved by consensus [22].

Observation indicators

The cut-off value reference range of the serum SCCA index level (provided by the reference reagent manufacturer) was set as SCCA < 1.5 ng/mL as the normal value, and a value higher than this value was considered positive.

Statistical methods

Data analysis was done using the GraphPad Prism 8 statistical analysis software. Data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed data and median (interquartile range) for non-normally distributed data. The serum SCCA test value was presented as the mean ± standard error, and the sensitivity and specificity of SCCA in different male lung diseases were calculated. Associations between SCCA and continuous inflammatory markers (SAA, CRP, and WBC counts) were examined using correlation analyses. Pearson correlation coefficients were applied when both variables were normally distributed; otherwise, Spearman’s rank correlation was used. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of serum SCCA and inflammatory markers in distinguishing patients with respiratory diseases from healthy controls. The optimal cut-off value for serum SCCA was determined using the Youden index, defined as the point maximizing the sum of sensitivity and specificity. The area under the curve (AUC), its 95% confidence interval, sensitivity, specificity, and optimal cutoff values were calculated to assess their clinical utility. The P value of SCCA in different male lung diseases was calculated. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient demographics

Eventually, we included 533 patients, comprising 229 outpatients and 304 inpatients, with SCCA levels within our experimental range. Patients in the research group were aged 16 to 92 years, with an average age of 61.8 ± 12.7 years. The control group had 80 patients aged 19 to 89 years, with an average age of 59.9 ± 10.0 years, and there was no significant age difference between the research and the control group (data not shown). The research group was further divided into four groups of diseases including 161 cases of lung cancer, aged 30 to 88 years old, with an average age of 63.5 ± 10.8 years; 144 lung nodules cases, aged 16 to 78 years, with an average age of 54.5 ± 12.6 years; 112 cases of pneumonia, aged 22 to 92 years, with an average age of 62.8 ± 13.5 years; and 116 cases of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, aged 47 to 89 years, with an average age of 68.2 ± 9.4 years old.

Clinically, patients with elevated SCCA levels had an increased probability of wheezing and presented with higher body temperatures(P < 0.05). These findings suggest that SCCA levels may be partially influenced by systemic inflammation.

Discussion

In this research, patients with elevated SCCA levels were older than those with reduced SCCA expression, had an increased probability of wheezing, and presented with higher body temperatures. In addition, inflammatory indicators, including SAA and Hs-CRP were significantly increased in the experimental group, compared to the healthy controls. SCCA expression was highest in the lung cancer group but lowest in the pulmonary nodules group. The ROC curve of serum SCCA was highest in the lung cancer group, but lowest in the pulmonary nodules group.

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a preventable and treatable disease characterized by limited airflow, which is progressive and not completely reversible. COPD is highly prevalent with a high mortality rate, ranking fourth among the causes of death globally [23]. COPD is one of the major global public health issues, and the World Health Organization predicts that the disease will rank fifth in the disease economic burden globally by 2020 [24]. Acute exacerbation of COPD is one of the most common reasons for hospitalization, and its cause is unclear. COPD is related to various causes, including smoking, dust and chemical inhalation, air pollution, and respiratory infections. Among the 116 COPD patients in this study, the SCCA test value was the lowest. 0.54ng/ml, up to 4.44ng/ml, with an AUC of 0.769, second only to lung cancer. In summary, early detection and treatment can delay the progression of COPD. Giving patients individualized combined treatment plans and precise individual care is necessary to promote patient recovery. Therefore, it is important to find highly specific indicators to detect lung diseases [25].

Pulmonary nodules are shadows with an increased density of less than 3 cm in size. Pulmonary nodules are different from lung cancer and are mostly benign. Sarcoidosis is a non-necrotizing granulomatous disease of unknown cause that can affect multiple systems. Sarcoidosis occurs worldwide, with the highest incidence in people aged 20 to 50 [26]. According to the previous investigations, the incidence of sarcoidosis in European countries is 64.0/100,000 in Sweden and 20.0/100,000 in the UK, while the incidence of sarcoidosis in Asian countries is 1.4/100,000 in Japan [27]. Our study showed that when the lung parenchyma of patients with sarcoidosis is affected, the most common manifestation of chest high-resolution CT (HRCT) examination is nodules. Granulomas may appear as small nodules in the early stage, and large nodules may occur after the disease progresses, or several small nodules may merge to form large nodules [28].

The present study found that the serum SCCA level of patients with benign lung diseases would increase to some levels, but the degree of increase in different diseases varied. The highest SCCA increase was observed in lung cancer. Our study reported that the SCCA level in lung diseases among the male cohort was most significantly increased in the lung cancer group, followed by chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and then the pneumonia group. The lung nodule group indicated the least increase. The receiver operating characteristic curve decreased from lung cancer, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, pneumonia, and the lung nodules group.

While SCCA is classically regarded as a tumor marker for squamous cell carcinoma, its assessment in benign pulmonary diseases is justified. Conditions such as COPD, pulmonary nodules, and pneumonia might induce epithelial injury, squamous metaplasia, and repair processes, which are known to release SCCA into circulation. Prior reports have documented such non-malignant elevations, and by including these groups in our analysis, we sought to address potential diagnostic overlap and demonstrate that the degree of elevation remains significantly higher in lung cancer, supporting the clinical relevance of SCCA as a discriminative biomarker.

Our findings indicated that increased body temperature leads to elevated SCCA in RD patients. Fever, as a critical mechanism in host defense, is achieved by integrating the neural and physiological circuits [29]. A cold-sensing neuron, transient receptor potential cation channel subfamily M member 8-TRPM8, is expressed in the sensory neurons’ nerve endings and the skin epidermis keratinocytes. Activation of this neuron induces cold defense processes, including thermogenesis of brown adipose tissue, shivering, and skin vasoconstriction [30]. The aim of comparing the levels of SCCA with the body temperature and wheezing was to determine whether this biomarker is influenced by concurrent inflammatory or obstructive processes. Because fever and wheezing are typical features of pneumonia and COPD, their correlation with SCCA provides insight into the extent to which non-malignant conditions contribute to elevated levels, thereby clarifying the marker’s specificity for lung cancer in clinical practice.

Since skin tissue is the outermost barrier of the human body, it contains the epidermal, dermal, and the subcutis layer [31]. Meanwhile, the skin also has type 2 innate lymphoid cells (ILC2s) [31], which are activated by cold-sensing neurons-derived signals [30]. Consequently, the signals respond to changes in ambient temperature to control the skin thermal homeostasis.

A previous study by Rianne and colleagues also confirmed greater ROC values in lung cancer cases compared to COPD patients [32], which is consistent with our findings. Therefore, changes in SCCA levels have a guiding significance for different lung diseases in clinical practice. This study shows that SCCA levels also increase to varying degrees in benign diseases, and this variation is dependent on the disease. In the chronic obstructive pulmonary disease in this study, the degree of SCCA increase was higher than that in pneumonia and the lung nodule group, and the area under the ROC curve reached 0.769, the lower limit of the 95% confidence interval was 0.701, and the upper limit was 0.836. The significant positive correlations observed between serum SCCA and inflammatory markers (r ≈ 0.7) suggest that SCCA expression is not exclusively tumor-specific and may also be influenced by systemic inflammatory activity. This finding has important implications for the diagnostic specificity of SCCA in pulmonary diseases. While elevated levels were most pronounced in patients with pulmonary squamous cell carcinoma, increases observed in non-malignant conditions may partly reflect inflammatory epithelial activation rather than malignant transformation alone.

SCCA is known to be expressed in squamous epithelial cells, and its release into circulation may be enhanced in both neoplastic and inflammatory states involving epithelial injury. Therefore, its diagnostic utility should be interpreted in the context of concomitant inflammatory processes. Rather than functioning as a cancer-specific biomarker, SCCA may better represent a broader indicator of epithelial stress and disease activity, which partially explains its elevation in conditions such as pneumonia and COPD.

These findings highlight the importance of integrating SCCA with clinical assessment and other diagnostic modalities rather than relying on it in isolation. Future studies should further explore combined biomarker panels and multivariable models to better distinguish malignant from inflammatory-driven elevations of SCCA.

Renal failure and skin diseases are the most common causes of false-positive results. In addition, SCCA levels will be significantly elevated in patients with liver effusion. Although SCCA has great clinical significance, when using SCCA for differential diagnosis, disease monitoring, and prognosis, it is important to pay attention to increases caused by other factors. The SCCA sensitivity and specificity for auxiliary diagnosis, recurrence monitoring, and prognosis of squamous cell carcinoma are not very high. It is possible to consider combining SCCA with other tumor markers to improve its sensitivity and specificity.

A key novel aspect of this study lies in its comparative evaluation of SCCA across a spectrum of clinically relevant pulmonary conditions within an exclusively male cohort. The inclusion of pneumonia, pulmonary nodules, and COPD provides a realistic clinical framework in which SCCA might be applied, as these conditions commonly mimic or coexist with lung cancer. Moreover, limiting the analysis to male participants reduces biological variability related to sex-specific hormonal influences on biomarker expression, allowing for a clearer interpretation of SCCA dynamics in pulmonary disease.

The findings of this study should be interpreted in the context of its study design. This was a single-centre, cross-sectional analysis, which allowed for controlled comparison of serum SCCA levels among male patients with different pulmonary conditions and healthy controls under uniform laboratory conditions. However, the cross-sectional nature of the study limits causal inference and precludes assessment of temporal changes in SCCA levels or their prognostic significance. The study design also limits the ability to assess changes in SCCA levels over time or in response to treatment. Additionally, although inflammatory pulmonary conditions were included for comparison, subclinical inflammation may still influence biomarker levels. Future prospective studies should explore longitudinal SCCA monitoring, evaluate combined biomarker panels, and investigate the prognostic and therapeutic monitoring potential of SCCA in pulmonary SCC. Further, the relatively modest sample size may have affected the statistical power and generalizability of the results. The restriction of the study population to male participants was intended to reduce biological variability related to sex hormones but may limit extrapolation of the findings to female populations. Another limitation of this study is the absence of a multivariable logistic regression analysis. Therefore, potential confounding factors such as age, smoking burden, and inflammatory status could not be adjusted for. Given that SCCA levels may be influenced by smoking-related epithelial changes and systemic inflammation, future studies should incorporate multivariable models to determine whether SCCA independently discriminates pulmonary squamous cell carcinoma from other pulmonary diseases. Despite these limitations, the standardized recruitment criteria and exclusion of conditions known to influence SCCA levels strengthen the internal validity of the observed diagnostic performance.

Conclusion

Serum SCCA demonstrated distinct expression patterns across different pulmonary diseases within this male study cohort, with the highest levels observed in patients with pulmonary squamous cell carcinoma. Although SCCA should not be considered a stand-alone diagnostic marker, its non-invasive, rapid, and cost-effective characteristics support its potential role as an adjunctive tool for early detection, differential diagnosis, and disease monitoring in pulmonary disease evaluation.

By comparing malignant and non-malignant pulmonary conditions within a clinically relevant cohort, this study contributes additional evidence supporting the diagnostic relevance of SCCA in pulmonary squamous cell carcinoma. The findings suggest that SCCA measurement may assist in refining diagnostic pathways and supporting timely clinical decision-making when used alongside established radiological and histopathological assessments. However, because the study included only male participants, the results should not be interpreted as evidence of sex-specific biomarker behavior. Further large-scale studies including both male and female populations are warranted to validate these findings and clarify the broader clinical applicability of SCCA in pulmonary disease diagnosis.
