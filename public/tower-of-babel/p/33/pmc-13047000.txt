Cutaneous Skin Toxicities of Oncological Targeted Therapies: Comprehensive Overview of Mechanisms, Management, and Research Gaps—A Narrative Review
Authors: Piotr Janicki, Anna Makowska, Anna Jakubczyk-Słabicka, Renata Zaucha, Wioletta Barańska-Rybak, Witold Owczarek, Magdalena Górska-Ponikowska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Targeted therapies in oncology have become a mainstay in therapy of numerous types of cancers. They work by targeting and utilizing (activating, inhibiting, or modulating) molecular pathways that are crucial in pathogenesis of certain cancers and can be divided into numerous classes according to their mechanism. However, since the antigens and molecules they target can take part in homeostasis of the skin and the integument, cutaneous toxicities are a common occurrence. The frequency of certain cutaneous adverse events (CAEs) depends on the causal drug and varies even between drugs of the same class and mechanism. Rashes, pruritus, and xerosis are among the most frequent CAEs. If severe, they can negatively impact the course of treatment by causing dose reductions and even treatment cessation. Additionally, patients with cancer often exhibit a further loss in quality of life due to the CAEs. When managed accordingly and promptly, skin, hair, and nail-related adverse events can be minimized in order to avoid treatment modifications and improve patients’ quality of life. The vast majority of summarized data and management guidelines regarding this topic focus on immune checkpoint inhibitors, with a much smaller amount dedicated to other targeted therapies. This narrative review presents the current state of knowledge about the mechanisms of cutaneous toxicities during treatment with targeted oncological drugs and identifies gaps in research.

Key Summary Points

Introduction

The introduction of targeted therapies in oncology has transformed cancer treatment. A significant portion of oncological patients are receiving therapeutic agents targeting specific molecules and pathways. For example, epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) mutations play a crucial role in multiple malignancies, leading to a growing number of therapies specifically targeting this pathway in recent years [1]. However, side effects frequently occur, posing a major obstacle both to oncologists and other clinicians including dermatologists [2]. Depending on the drug and its mechanism of action, cutaneous side effects of targeted treatments can occur in up to 90% of patients receiving them [3]. These cutaneous toxicities can result in preventable dose reduction or treatment cessation, especially when treated inadequately. There is high-quality evidence that supports the introduction of skin-targeted dermatological management of cutaneous adverse events (CAEs) in order to reduce their severity and impact on patients’ quality of life (QoL) [4]. With multiple new molecules and entire drug classes currently in development, this field of dermatology is dynamically expanding. However, most research and numerous guidelines are focused on immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI; drugs targeting checkpoint inhibitors such as PD-1, PD-L1, or CTLA4), leaving other drug classes underrepresented. The scope of this review is to summarize the relevant CAEs of currently utilized targeted therapies (excluding ICI) as well as to outline their management, supportive care, and identify gaps in research.

Methodology

The objective of this narrative review was to identify, summarize, and critically evaluate clinical evidence regarding current epidemiology and management of non-ICI targeted therapy-related dermatological AEs. The literature search was performed in PubMed/MEDLINE and Google Scholar, using the following terms and their combinations: “cutaneous toxicities,” “dermatological adverse events,” “targeted therapies,” “oncology,” “dermatology,” “skin adverse events,” “management,” “EGFR inhibitors,” “BRAF inhibitors,” “MEK inhibitors,” “HER2 inhibitors,” “CDK4/6 inhibitors,” “VEGFR inhibitors,” “Hedgehog inhibitors,” “FGFR inhibitors,” “antibody–drug conjugates,” “CCR-4″,“talquetamab,” “tebentafusp,” “rash,” “acneiform rash,” “pruritus,” “alopecia,” “hand–foot syndrome,” “palmar-plantar dysesthesia,” “lichenoid reactions,” “lichen planus,” “supportive care,” “exosomes“,“bioferments,” “skincare.” Synonyms and related terms were included in the search. Titles and abstracts were screened for relevance. Only peer-reviewed articles published in English published between January 1, 2000 and October 30, 2025 were considered. The search was extended to relevant papers cited in reference lists of included articles. Data was synthesized narratively and, where possible, summarized in tables. This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Targeted Therapy Drug Classes

EGFR Inhibitors (EGFR-i) and Anti-EGFR Antibodies

Common side effects of treatments that target the EGFR include rash, erythema, and acneiform dermatitis [5]. Xerosis and pruritus are present in the majority of patients, most commonly in those treated with panitumumab [6, 7]. Acneiform dermatitis, an acne-like monomorphic pustulopapular eruption, is observed in up to 90% of patients receiving anti-EGFR treatment, more commonly with monoclonal antibodies (i.e., panitumumab, cetuximab) than with tyrosine kinase inhibitors (i.e., erlotinib, gefitinib). The lesions can be pruritic and cover large areas of the face, neck, and body in patterns that are not typical for acne. The incidence of acneiform eruption is associated with a favorable response to treatment [8]. An exact mechanism of EGFR-i-associated acneiform eruptions has yet to be completely elucidated, but upregulation of the p27 protein, which causes abnormal keratinocyte proliferation, is currently considered to be one of the main causal factors [9]. Nail and hair toxicities such as paronychia and trichomegaly have also been frequently reported [10, 11]. Skin toxicities are the most common cause of treatment dose reduction during EGFR therapy with first-generation (gefitinib, erlotinib) and second-generation (afatinib, dacomitinib) tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), and frequently multiple skin toxicities can occur in one patient at one time [12].

Amivantamab, a newer-generation antibody blocking both EGFR and mesenchymal epithelial transition factor, has caused erosive pustular dermatosis of the scalp (EPDS), in 21.9% of patients, with a significant percentage of the cases (28.5%) requiring dose reduction or treatment cessation [13]. Other EGFR-i are not very frequently associated with EPDS, but its incidence during EGFR-i treatment has been reported [14].

BRAF and MEK Inhibitors

BRAF inhibitors can be used as monotherapy or combined with MEK inhibitors in treating BRAF-mutated melanoma. The most common cutaneous toxicities of these drugs include rashes, alopecia, hyperkeratosis, and photosensitivity, which are more frequently observed with vemurafenib than with dabrafenib [15, 16] (Table 1). It is, however, notable that combination BRAF/MEK therapy causes fewer adverse cutaneous events than BRAF monotherapy. Incidence of cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma post-BRAF therapy has been reported [17]. HER2/HER

The frequency of cutaneous toxicities depends on the molecular mechanism of human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)/HER inhibition due to different ways of interaction with the pathway (Table 2). Whereas monoclonal antibodies induce cutaneous adverse reactions in approximately 1% of patients, TKIs cause these events more frequently as a result of direct inhibition of the EGFR pathway in keratinocytes [19–21]. Palmar-plantar erythrodysesthesia (PPE), also known as hand‒foot syndrome (HFS), is reported during treatment with multiple TKIs, including those targeting HER2/HER [22]. CDK4/6 Inhibitors

Inhibitors of CDK (cyclin-dependent kinase) 4 and 6 are used in treating metastatic breast carcinoma. They are associated with numerous AEs, and cutaneous toxicities account for 15% of them [25] (Table 3). However, despite their common occurrence they are mostly mild and rarely cause treatment cessation or dose adjustment [26]. CDK4/6 inhibitors might commonly cause pruritus (62%) alopecia (32%), eczema (31%), and vitiligo-like lesions (27%), with significant differences between the three drugs of this class, probably due to their varying selectivity towards CDK4 and CDK6 [27]. Multikinase and VEGFR Inhibitors

Most multikinase inhibitors, including those targeting the vascular epithelial growth factor receptor (VEGFR) pathway, inhibit other kinase pathways with varying specificity and effectiveness. AEs including pruritus, rashes, and HFS are regularly seen in patients receiving anti-VEGFR drugs [32]. HFS in patients treated with sorafenib for metastatic renal cell carcinoma is an independent favorable prognostic factor for treatment outcomes such as tumor response and progression-free survival [22]. The incidence of HFS varies between different agents, e.g., with sorafenib causing HFS in 37% of patients, pazopanib in only 9%, and no significant incidence observed in groups treated with vandetanib [33]. The lack of significant incidence of HFS in patients receiving vandetanib is explained by the fact that concurrent inhibition of VEGFR and platelet-derived growth factor receptor (PDGFR) is postulated to be a prerequisite to cause HFS [34]. Vandetanib inhibits VEGFR, but does not impact the PDGFR pathway. Imatinib mesylate, a multikinase inhibitor that mainly inhibits the Bcr-Abl kinase, also inhibits PDGFR, but not VEGFR. It does not cause HFS [35]. Imatinib mesylate causes dermatological AEs in 31–44% of patients, most commonly a maculopapular rash on the trunk and forearms, but pigmentation disorders, lichenoid or morbilliform reactions may appear as well [36].

Hedgehog Pathway Inhibitors

Sonic hedgehog (Shh) pathway inhibitors—vismodegib and sonidegib—are a mainstay in therapy of locally advanced and metastatic basal cell carcinoma. Since the Shh pathway is linked to homeostasis of the hair follicle, absence of signalling causes decrease in hair growth and prevents hair follicles from entering the anagen phase [37]. Diffuse, gradual alopecia is the most common CAE of Hedgehog inhibitors and it has a large impact on patients’ QoL. Despite this fact, there are no established treatments and no studies have been published on the effectiveness of any intervention to prevent or treat Shh inhibitor-associated alopecia. Sonidegib is associated with lower incidence of alopecia than vismodegib, but extracutaneous AEs are more common in sonidegib [38] (Table 4). Antibody–Drug Conjugates

Antibody–drug conjugates (ADC) use monoclonal antibodies to deliver targeted drugs to tumor-associated antigens in a precise manner. However, since those antigens can be expressed in non-neoplastic tissue, on-target off-tumor effects may occur, resulting in a range of toxicities [41] (Table 5). For example, CD44v6, a target antigen for bivatuzumab mertansine (BM, an ADC carrying an antimicrotubule agent) investigated for SCC of the head and neck, is expressed in healthy squamous epithelium which causes desquamation in up to 80% of patients treated with BM [42, 43]. FGFR1–4 Inhibitors

Inhibitors of fibroblast growth factor receptors (FGFRs) are used to treat cholangiocarcinoma and urothelial carcinoma. Since FGFRs are widely expressed in the skin, hair follicles and nail tissues, cutaneous toxicities are often observed during treatment with FGFR inhibitors, as FGF regulates keratinocyte proliferation, tissue repair, and can impact hair growth [51] (Table 6). Alopecia is the most commonly reported dermatological AE [51]. CAEs are more common in FGFR2-selective inhibitors than in pan-FGFR inhibitors [52]. CC Chemokine Receptor 4

Mogamulizumab is a monoclonal anti-CC chemokine receptor 4 (CCR-4) antibody used in treatment of cutaneous T cell lymphoma (CTCL). CCR-4 is expressed on malignant T cells in 30–65% of patients with peripheral T cell lymphomas, including CTCL [54]. CCR-4 is a molecule that is implicated in skin accumulation—epidermotropism—of T cells [55]. However, since CCR-4 is expressed on regulatory T cells (Treg) and Th17 cells, whose depletion impacts skin homeostasis, mogamulizumab causes an increase in proliferation of CD8+ T cells [56]. Those CD8+ cells are mostly newly formed autoreactive clones which have been linked to the high frequency of CAEs during the treatment with mogamulizumab [57].

Up to 50–63% of patients receiving mogamulizumab may develop cutaneous toxicities—including maculopapular and papulosquamous rashes, psoriasis, toxic eruptions, and vitiligo [58–60]. However, these rashes may resemble CTCL progression and histopatological examination with T cell clonality testing may be helpful to differentiate them [61]. Development of dermatological AEs may predict a favorable treatment response [62]. The onset of these AEs is reported usually after the fourth or subsequent infusions [59].

Anti-(HLA)-A*02:01

Tebentafusp, approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and European Medicines Agency (EMA) for treatment of metastatic uveal melanoma, is an immune cell-mobilizing monoclonal T cell receptor against cancer, combining an HLA-A*02:01-restricted T cell receptor specific for the glycoprotein 100 (gp100) peptide, binding to gp100-positive cells. Cutaneous toxicities appear in approximately 80% of patients, with rash being the most common presentation and a predictor of longer median overall survival rate, especially when developed after three doses of the drug at least once [63, 64]. Tebentafusp-related rash presents as symmetrical erythematous patches on the face, neck, and trunk, frequently involving periorbital areas with accompanying periorbital edema [63]. Vitiligo-like lesions can also be observed [65].

Anti-CD3 and GPRC5D

Bispecific antibodies target two antigens simultaneously. Talquetamab is a bispecific antibody targeting CD3 and G protein-coupled receptor, family C, group 5, member D (GPRC5D) in a 1:1 configuration to treat multiple myeloma by recruiting and activating T cells. Since GPRC5D can be expressed in the skin and hair follicles, on-target and off-tumor cutaneous toxicities of talquetamab are frequent. Skin-related AEs observed in a phase one study were noticed in 67% of patients receiving the drug weekly and 70% of patients receiving the drug every second week [66]. In the phase 2 MonumenTAL-1 study 56–71% of patients developed CAEs and 61% reported nail AEs [67]. Rashes were reported in 35% of patients receiving talquetamab [68].

Between bispecific antibodies that target the same antigens the difference in affinity towards those antigens can result in a vastly varying AE profile. Forimtamig, an antibody targeting CD3 and GPRC5D in a 2:1 configuration, has been investigated in a phase 1 clinical study with 78–86% of patients exhibiting skin AEs, 24–28% nail AEs and 73–77% mucosal toxicity-associated AEs [68].

Dose modification and especially discontinuation of the treatment should be a shared decision-making process between the dermatologist and oncologist, involving a careful risk/benefit assessment [70]. Oncologists are more likely to overestimate the grade of a CAE than dermatologists, which can cause premature treatment decisions [71]. The decision to discontinue treatment should incorporate a patient’s preferences as well.

Since non-ICI targeted therapies are remarkably diverse in their mechanisms of action and profiles of AEs, the data on the treatment of CAEs related to them is relatively scarce, with the notable exception of EGFR-related acneiform rashes. In clinical practice management of non-ICI CAEs is oftentimes similar to the management of respective ICI CAEs, with treatment regimens yielding results despite the mechanistic differences between them. There is also a lack of research directly comparing the epidemiology, timing, and morphology of ICI-related and non-ICI-related CAEs of different classes. However, we hope that with an increasing number of patients treated with these drugs, more research dedicated to other targeted therapies might result in dedicated treatment guidelines in the future, closing this notable research gap and improving patient outcomes.

Oral lichen planus can present as reticulated, erosive or bullous lesions and should be differentiated from autoimmune bullous diseases with histopathology and immunofluorescence to be managed properly. Appropriate topical preparations of CS including mouthwashes and solutions are helpful in grade 1–2 oral lesions, and systemic treatment (Table 12) is often needed in more severe grade 2–3 lesions [75]. The incidence rate of oral lichen planus-like lesions in non-ICI therapy has not been reported.

Hand–Foot Syndrome

Palmar–plantar erythrodysesthesia, known as hand‒foot syndrome (HFS), is the most commonly reported adverse reaction to classical chemotherapeutics including 5-fluorouracil (5-FU), capecitabine, and liposomal doxorubicin. HFS and hand‒foot skin reaction (HFSR) can also be induced by multikinase inhibitors (TKI). Topical 10% urea emollients are helpful for HFS prevention [82]. However, good-quality data regarding reactive treatment in TKI-related HFS is scarce. Grade 2 HFS can be managed by transient dose reduction, and grade 3 HFS treatment discontinuation is advised [83].

Alopecia

Alopecia is a frequent CAE reported during treatment with multiple targeted therapies. Prophylactic measures used in prevention of chemotherapy-induced alopecia such as scalp cooling are not recommended for use in targeted therapy-related alopecia and there is no evidence to support it. Most research is focused on FGFR inhibitor-related alopecia, in all cases of which topical minoxidil is recommended [51]. Oral minoxidil has been explored in a cohort study of patients receiving anti-CDK4/6 therapies with 80% of patients showing improvement in hair density after 4–6 months of treatment with no serious adverse effects [84]. The favorable safety profile and high efficacy of low-dose oral minoxidil in treating hair loss warrants its further exploration in alopecia caused by other targeted therapies.

Erosive Pustular Dermatosis of the Scalp (EPDS)

EDPS is an emerging CAE frequently reported during treatment with amivantamab and rarely with EGFR-i [13, 85]. EPDS is an inflammatory disorder of the scalp in which erosions, thick crusts, and pustules exuding a serum-hematic liquid appear, leading to skin atrophy and scarring alopecia [86]. About 18% of patients require dose alteration or treatment discontinuation due to EPDS [14]. Prophylaxis with an adequate skincare regimen and systemic tetracyclines can decrease its severity [4]. Mild forms of EPDS are usually treated with high-potency topical corticosteroids, topical antibiotics, and oral tetracyclines (100–200 mg daily) [87]. More severe and recalcitrant cases may require systemic corticosteroids or systemic retinoids [14].

Stevens–Johnson Syndrome (SJS) and Toxic Epidermal Necrolysis (TEN)

In cases of severe drug reactions such as SJS and TEN the causal targeted therapy should be discontinued. Other than urgent admission to a specialized ward with adequate supportive care, treatment with glucocorticosteroids and cyclosporine is usually recommended [74]. However, etanercept, a tumor necrosis factor alpha inhibitor, has been shown to improve survival and skin healing time in TEN [88]. One case report describing the use of etanercept combined with other treatment modalities in the treatment of TEN caused by anti-PD-1 therapy has been published [89].

Supportive Care: Future Perspectives

Future strategies for the prevention and management of treatment-related cutaneous toxicities are exploring approaches centered in skin biology, aimed towards restoring skin barrier function and cutaneous homeostasis. Among these, the use of bioferments—components obtained from natural raw materials of plant origin through fermentation with selected strains of microorganisms—emerge as a potential option for managing xerosis and barrier impairment, primarily owing to their antipruritic and moisturizing properties [90–92]. Although microbiome-supporting interventions have not yet been validated in controlled clinical studies, the growing recognition of the skin microbiome’s role in epidermal integrity underscores their relevance as promising avenues for further investigation.

In parallel, exosomes represent an additional emerging therapeutic perspective. These nanosized extracellular vesicles (30–150 nm) secreted by various cell types, including mesenchymal stem cells (MSC), immune cells, and keratinocytes, involved in intercellular communication, have demonstrated anti-inflammatory and regenerative effects in preclinical models [93], suggesting a potential role in counteracting inflammation-driven keratinocyte damage and barrier dysfunction induced by targeted therapies. While clinical evidence remains limited, the possibility of integrating exosome-based approaches with established dermatologic supportive care [94], as well as the development of personalized formulations [95], highlights their translational potential. Further clinical validation will be required before these strategies can be incorporated into routine practice.

Conclusions

A tailor-made regimen consisting of skincare and, where applicable, medications should be implemented from the beginning or even before oncological therapy to help minimize the incidence of AEs. Reactive treatment is necessary to avoid unnecessary treatment modifications. Interdisciplinary collaboration between dermatologists, oncologists, and other professionals as well as further research in this field is necessary since the current strategies for skin toxicity management are still evolving.
