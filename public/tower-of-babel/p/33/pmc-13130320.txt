Emotion contagion through interaction with generative artificial intelligence chatbots may contribute to development and maintenance of mania
Authors: Søren Dinesen Østergaard
Published: 2025

Chatbots and delusions

Chatbots driven by generative artificial intelligence (henceforth, chatbots) have become ubiquitous. Reportedly, the apparent market leader, ChatGPT from OpenAI, surpassed 900 million downloads by July 2025 (Storyboard 18, 2025 a) and handles more than 2.5 billion prompts per day (Storyboard 18, 2025 b).

While there are, likely, many positive use cases for these chatbots, as indirectly supported by their huge uptake, there may be risks associated with using them as well. Already in August 2023, concerns were raised over the potential of the chatbots to trigger and/or maintain delusional thinking in people prone to psychosis (Østergaard, 2023). At that time, this concern was merely based on speculation, but it has since been substantiated by several reports of cases in which interaction with chatbots seem to have fueled delusions, one of which had a fatal outcome (Morrin et al. 2025).

Does it extend to mania?

The aim of this editorial is to raise the hypothesis that chatbots may not only trigger and maintain delusions but also has a specific effect on mania. In the reports on delusions occurring in relation to use of chatbots, there have been some cases that, judged by the descriptions, may well be psychotic mania (Morrin et al. 2025; VICE, 2025). In the reporting on these cases, the emphasis has been on delusions, but I would argue that the chatbots are likely to have a direct effect on mania via a mechanism that is analog to, yet slightly different from, that seeming to be driving delusions.

With regard to the mechanism at play regarding delusional development/maintenance, it has been proposed (Østergaard, 2025) that the culprit is likely the chatbots’ inherent tendency to confirm and flatter the user (referred to as ‘sycophancy’ (OpenAI, 2025 a)). Specifically, if a user prone to psychosis introduces a thought/belief that is slightly unusual/improbable/false, the chatbot may well support/confirm it. Iteratively, such a correspondence may lead this user’s thoughts to spiral out of control (and reality), potentially resulting in development/maintenance of outright delusions (Østergaard, 2025).

As for the potential mania-inducing effect of interaction with chatbots, it is likely not reinforcement of false beliefs that is the main mechanism at play. Rather, I would propose that it is driven by one or more of the following four mechanisms: i) reinforcement of elated mood/self-esteem, ii) pacing of racing thoughts/talkativeness, iii) fueling of hypersexuality, and iv) sleep deprivation.

To summarize the proposed mechanisms i–iv, they are all ‘activating’ in relation to core symptoms of mania in a manner that seems akin to what has been labeled as emotion contagion between humans (i.e. ‘ ‘catching’ the emotions of others, as one would with a physical disease.’ (Horesh et al. 2021)) – a phenomenon, which extends to interaction via digital (social) media (Goldenberg and Gross, 2020). In the present version of emotion contagion, a human has been replaced by an anthropomorphized chatbot, which mirrors and potentially worsens the pathologically elated mood of the human user.

I am, of course, aware of the fact that this is a single correspondence and that I engaged in it to prove a point – potentially leading on the chatbot. However, I find it more likely than unlikely that the tone/reciprocity of its responses may well be quite representative of what those prone to – or with manifest – bipolar disorder can be confronted with when interacting with chatbots, and this is somewhat worrying.

The chatbot can see the problem when asked – a part of the solution?

‘l will be honest with you. What if I told you that this entire correspondence was to prove that use of chatbots driven by large language models can be risky for people prone to mental disorder?

Can you see the problem?’ (OpenAI, 2025 c). ChatGPT responded:

This makes me wonder if it would be technically possible for chatbots to have such a meta-level analysis of chats running without being prompted and adjusting the tone/responses in the chat accordingly. This could potentially be part of the solution to this problem.

A call for research, clinical awareness and legislation

There is no doubt that research is needed to firmly establish whether interactions with chatbots may indeed contribute to development and maintenance of mania. Here, the lines of research recently suggested for the analog case of delusions, seems equally relevant: case reports written by mental health professionals (moving beyond self-report), interview-based qualitative studies of cases, and experimental designs to explore the mechanisms underlying the putative effect (Østergaard, 2025).

But what do we do now – in the absence of firm evidence of chatbots driving mania (and delusions)? In my opinion, there is both a clinical/public health task and a political/regulatory/legal task to be carried out sooner rather than later; First, to err on the side of caution, it seems reasonable to suggest that people who know that they are vulnerable to psychotic or bipolar disorder (having manifest mental illness or a family history of severe mental illness) should be very careful when interacting with chatbots. Accordingly, such advice should be part of psychoeducation provided by psychiatric services to patients and their relatives. Second, governments should pass legislation requiring that chatbots based on generative artificial intelligence must pass thorough ‘psychological safety tests’ prior to being released on the market. Hopefully, such initiatives will prevent us from making the same mistake as in the case of social media, where awareness of their harmful impact on mental health (US Surgeon General, 2023) and appropriate political/legislative counteraction came way too late (BBC, 2024; Politico, 2025).
