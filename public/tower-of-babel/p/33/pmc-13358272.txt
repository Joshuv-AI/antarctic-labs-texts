Real-world experience with personalised belimumab spacing in sustained SLE remission: a multicentre study
Authors: Alice Bartoletti, Lorenza Maria Argolini, Giuseppe Alvise Ramirez, Luca Moroni, Tommaso Schioppo, Chiara Bellocchi, Sofia Aliprandi, Biancamaria Venerandi, Annalaura Fasiello, Gabriele Domenico Gallina, Lorenzo Beretta, Lorenzo Dagna, Roberto Caporali, Maria Gerosa, on behalf of SMILE, Milan Lupus Consortium
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the feasibility and safety of belimumab dose spacing and withdrawal in patients with SLE in long-standing remission on belimumab.MethodsPatients with SLE fulfilling the 2019 European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR)/American College of Rheumatology (ACR) criteria who underwent belimumab spacing and/or discontinuation were identified across four tertiary rheumatology centres. The shared belimumab step-down strategy consisted of spacing to subcutaneous (SC) 200 mg/10 days (or intravenous (IV) 10 mg/kg/6 weeks). In the absence of flares, belimumab could be progressively tapered every 6–12 months to SC 200 mg/14 days (or IV 10 mg/kg/8 weeks), SC 200 mg/21 days (or IV 10 mg/kg/12 weeks), SC 200 mg/28 days (or IV 10 mg/kg/16 weeks) and eventually discontinued. Primary endpoint: SLE flares occurrence.ResultsAmong 152 patients with SLE treated with belimumab, 22 (14.5%) underwent spacing for sustained remission (21 females (95.5%), mean±SD age 47±11 years). Spacing patients had longer mean remission duration compared with standard-dose belimumab (67±30 vs 33±32 months, p<0.001), less serological activity (low complement and/or positive anti-double stranded DNA 31.8% vs 54.6%, p=0.048), lower SLE Disease Activity Index 2000 (0.77±1.23 vs 1.98±2.01, p<0.001) and were less often on glucocorticoids (22.7% vs 47.7%, p=0.029) or additional immunosuppressants (0.0% vs 50.8%, p<0.001). During the 21±5.4 months of follow-up, no SLE flares occurred. None of the patients required glucocorticoid dose escalation or reduced the dose interval. Seven patients discontinued belimumab.ConclusionProgressive belimumab dose spacing and withdrawal, coupled with clinical monitoring, could be a practicable approach in selected patients with SLE with long-standing disease remission on minimal glucocorticoid doses, no additional immunosuppressants and low serological activity.Keywords: Lupus Erythematosus, Systemic; Lupus Nephritis; B-Lymphocytes; Therapeutics

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

The latest recommendations for the management of SLE suggest considering gradual immunosuppressive treatment tapering in patients on prolonged disease remission. However, evidence guiding immunosuppressant de-escalation in clinical practice is scarce. In particular, there is no consensus on which patients can safely discontinue belimumab, and no belimumab discontinuation protocols exist in literature.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This is the first multicentre real-world study evaluating belimumab dose spacing and withdrawal in patients with SLE in long-standing remission. Our findings demonstrate that, in carefully selected patients with prolonged disease remission, minimal glucocorticoid doses, no additional immunosuppressants and low serological activity, progressive belimumab dose interval extension and eventually withdrawal can be achieved with a low relapse risk over 24 months.

This is the first multicentre real-world study evaluating belimumab dose spacing and withdrawal in patients with SLE in long-standing remission. Our findings demonstrate that, in carefully selected patients with prolonged disease remission, minimal glucocorticoid doses, no additional immunosuppressants and low serological activity, progressive belimumab dose interval extension and eventually withdrawal can be achieved with a low relapse risk over 24 months.

Introduction

SLE is a complex systemic autoimmune rheumatic disease characterised by a highly variable clinical course.1 The majority of patients experience disease flares and periods of persistent activity, leading to long-term immunosuppression and organ damage. Recent advances in therapy have significantly improved the prognosis of SLE. Belimumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting B-lymphocyte stimulator (Blys, also called B-cell-activating factor), is the first biological drug approved for the treatment of SLE.2 3 For over a decade, belimumab has been part of the therapeutic armamentarium of SLE to achieve and maintain disease control and reduce the need for glucocorticoids. Belimumab is usually well tolerated and is continued for many years.4 However, infections secondary to immunosuppressive therapies are a common cause of morbidity and mortality among patients with SLE.5 Despite all the data regarding belimumab effectiveness and safety, little is known about when and how it should be discontinued in patients reaching sustained disease remission. Managing SLE during remission requires a careful balance between disease control and the risks associated with overtreatment. Patients in sustained remission require a different therapeutic strategy compared with those with frequent relapses or chronically active disease. The 2023 European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) recommendations for the management of SLE suggest considering gradual tapering of the immunosuppressive treatment in quiescent disease if the patient is on treatment for at least 3–5 years and in remission for at least 2 years.6 However, a recent trial of mycophenolate or azathioprine discontinuation after 2–3 years of therapy in lupus nephritis failed to demonstrate non-inferiority in the risk of renal relapses and increased the rate of severe extrarenal flares compared with continuing the immunosuppressive treatment.7 A retrospective study observing patients with lupus nephritis discontinuing belimumab for any reason found that 50% relapsed after 1 year.8 According to the authors, potential indicators of a low risk of relapse on belimumab discontinuation were normal complement, negative anti-double stranded DNA (anti-dsDNA) antibodies, SLE Disease Activity Index 2000 (SLEDAI-2K) <4 and glucocorticoid dose <5 mg/day prednisone equivalents. According to two studies, a minority of the patients with lupus nephritis flared after immunosuppressive treatment withdrawal, especially if hydroxychloroquine was maintained, immunosuppressants were continued for a longer time and if the patient was in prolonged disease remission.9 10 A temporary discontinuation of belimumab for 24 weeks did not result in an increased risk of severe (including renal) flares in patients with SLE on stable low disease activity.11 The risk of renal and extrarenal SLE flares after belimumab spacing or withdrawal is unknown. There is no systematic strategy to successfully taper and stop belimumab.

The aim of the study is to evaluate the feasibility and safety of belimumab dose spacing and withdrawal in patients with SLE who were in long-standing remission on belimumab, primarily the occurrence of SLE flares.

Materials and methods

A multicentre retrospective study was conducted on subjects followed-up between 1 January 2013 and 31 December 2025 within the Milan Lupus Consortium (SMiLE), involving four tertiary rheumatology centres in the Milan area (ASST Gaetano Pini-CTO, IRCCS Ospedale San Raffaele, Fondazione IRCCS Ca’ Granda Ospedale Maggiore Policlinico and Ospedale San Paolo). A shared protocol for gradual belimumab tapering to be followed across the participating centres was devised. This reference step-down strategy consisted of initial spacing to subcutaneous (SC) 200 mg/10 days or intravenous (IV) 10 mg/kg/6 weeks (step 1). In the absence of disease flares, belimumab could be progressively tapered every 6–12 months to SC 200 mg/14 days or IV 10 mg/kg/8 weeks (step 2), SC 200 mg/21 days or IV 10 mg/kg/12 weeks (step 3), SC 200 mg/28 days or IV 10 mg/kg/16 weeks (step 4) and eventually discontinued (step 5). Deviations from the reference schedule were permitted based on clinical judgement.

Adult patients with a diagnosis of SLE fulfilling the 2019 EULAR/American College of Rheumatology (ACR) classification criteria12 and receiving stable belimumab treatment for ≥24 consecutive months were eligible. Among these, patients without SLE flares for >6 months who underwent belimumab dose spacing and/or discontinuation were identified. Flares were defined as new or worsening clinical signs or symptoms of active SLE associated with increased disease activity indices (SLEDAI-2K >4 and ≥1 British Isles Lupus Assessment Group A or B item) requiring escalation of immunosuppressive therapy. Demographic, laboratory and clinical data were collected through retrospective chart review. The primary endpoint was the occurrence of SLE flares. Secondary endpoints included death and progression of damage accrual defined as an increase in Systemic Lupus Collaborating Clinics (SLICC)/ACR Damage Index (SDI).13

The study was conducted in accordance with the Helsinki declaration. All patients provided written informed consent.

Continuous variables were expressed as mean±SD. Categorical variables were summarised as numbers with percentages. Characteristics at enrolment of the two groups were compared using χ2 for categorical variables, and Student’s t-test or Welch’s t-test for continuous data with and without equal variances, respectively.14 Normality of data distribution and homoscedasticity were assessed with Q–Q plots. Kruskal-Wallis’ test was used for statistical comparison of data across the different time points.

A multivariate generalised linear mixed model (GLMM) with a binomial logit link function was built to identify clinical predictors of belimumab tapering and address the potential influence of the participating centre on this decision. Fixed effects included disease duration, history of lupus nephritis, baseline SLEDAI-2K, baseline glucocorticoid dose in mg/day prednisone equivalents, ongoing hydroxychloroquine and remission duration. The treating centre was included in the model as the random intercept. A linear mixed-effects model (LMM) was employed for the analysis of belimumab tapering steps and glucocorticoid dose during the follow-up and to investigate for possible differences in the therapeutic strategies among centres. The regression coefficient β was computed to estimate belimumab and glucocorticoid dose reduction over time. Random effects included the individual patient and the centre. For all the models, the proportion of variance explained by the centre was calculated with the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC).

Data were analysed using RStudio V.2025.09.0+387 (Posit Software, PBC, Boston, Massachusetts, USA). A p value<0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Among 152 patients with SLE treated with belimumab across the four centres, 22 (14.5%) underwent belimumab dose spacing. 21 were females (95.5%), with a mean age of 47±11 years at enrolment. Eight patients (36.4%) had a history of lupus nephritis. Mean disease duration was 22±11 years. Patients were treated with belimumab standard-dose for an average of 6±2 years before spacing. 21 (95.5%) were on SC belimumab and 1 (4.5%) on IV belimumab. Demographics, organ involvement and belimumab duration were similar between the spacing and the standard-dose group (table 1). Patients undergoing spacing had a longer remission duration compared with those on standard-dose belimumab (67±30 vs 33±32 months, p<0.001), lower frequency of serological activity (low complement and/or positive anti-dsDNA 31.8% vs 54.6%, p=0.048), lower SLEDAI-2K (0.77±1.23 vs 1.98±2.01, p<0.001) and clinical SLEDAI (0.05±0.21 vs 0.55±1.26, p<0.001) and were less frequently on glucocorticoids (22.7% vs 47.7%, p=0.029). Hydroxychloroquine treatment was similar between the two groups (72.7% spacing vs 78.5% standard-dose, p=0.55), whereas concomitant immunosuppressants were more frequent in the standard-dose group (50.8% vs 0.0% spacing, p<0.001).

GLMM analysis detected a greater duration of SLE remission (OR 1.02, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.04, p=0.006) and a lower SLEDAI-2K score (OR 0.62, 95% CI 0.42 to 0.92, p=0.018) as predictors for allocation to belimumab spacing (online supplemental table S1). Although a history of lupus nephritis was associated with a lower probability of undergoing spacing (OR 0.69, 95% CI 0.23 to 2.03), this was not statistically significant. Similarly, disease duration, daily glucocorticoid dose and ongoing hydroxychloroquine treatment did not correlate with whether belimumab was tapered or not. The variance attributed to centre-related random effect was 0.38, resulting in an ICC of 10%.

LMM analysis of patients on belimumab spacing identified a significant advancement in belimumab reduction steps (β=0.056, 95% CI 0.043 to 0.069, p<0.001) and a decrease in daily glucocorticoid dose (β=−0.016, 95% CI −0.025 to −0.008, p<0.001) during the follow-up (online supplemental table S2). Patient-related variance on belimumab tapering speed constituted 70% of the heterogeneity (variance 1.789), whereas centre-related variability was limited to 14% (variance 0.374). No centre-related variability of the reduction of the daily glucocorticoid dose was detected.

Discussion

This proof-of-concept study observed individuals affected by SLE in long-standing remission following a personalised belimumab de-escalation tailored to disease activity. Interestingly, patients not candidate to spacing had a shorter disease remission duration, greater frequency of low C3 and/or C4, anti-dsDNA positivity, with consequent higher SLEDAI-2K scores and were more often on glucocorticoid treatment. Subjects with these features were reported to be less likely to completely discontinue the immunosuppressive treatment.1 8 10 11 In turn, evaluating belimumab tapering and discontinuation after many years of treatment in those at low risk of SLE flare according to individualised risk-based stratification strategies could be a sensible approach.

The aim of this preliminary study was to evaluate the feasibility of belimumab tapering and withdrawal in patients with long-standing disease remission, focusing on safety and risk of relapse. The therapeutic decision to reduce or continue the standard-of-care belimumab dose was compliant with the latest international guidelines on the management of SLE, recommending immunosuppressant de-escalation in patients who achieved long-standing remission. Our shared protocol is also rooted in the real-world clinical practice, where immunosuppressive treatment reduction is attempted only in patients with quiescent disease. Our findings confirm that decreasing the immunosuppressive burden is possible in selected individuals on stable immunosuppressive treatment with long-standing clinical and serological remission (the mean remission duration was 5.5 years in our spacing cohort). According to our experience, approximately 15% patients with SLE successfully underwent belimumab spacing and/or discontinuation. Of note, no organ-threatening or life-threatening relapses were observed during belimumab spacing or withdrawal.

Random effects model analysis showed a limited heterogeneity between the participating centres, meaning that clinicians followed the inclusion criteria of the study to select eligible patients for belimumab spacing, and mostly attained to the shared tapering protocol. Our results indicate a good homogeneity in clinical decision-making across the centres, mainly focused on periodic disease activity monitoring with remission as a target.

A strength of this study is the invention of a prespecified protocol for belimumab spacing that can be applied on a larger scale. Limitations consisted of the small number of patients candidates for belimumab spacing and the retrospective design of the study. The decision to enrol only patients in a state of quiescent disease and the absence of a control group were potential sources of bias, as a subpopulation with long-standing remission on minimal immunosuppressive therapy was analysed.

Nevertheless, the results of this study support the possibility to gradually discontinue belimumab in carefully selected patients. Further research involving larger cohorts with randomised controlled trials is necessary to validate this approach. Future studies should also aim to identify reliable predictors of successful belimumab withdrawal and clarify optimal timing for tapering immunosuppressive therapy. We believe that a personalised approach is essential in SLE, as treatment decisions during remission should be made carefully weighing the relapse risk against that of continuing immunosuppression. Future strategies should focus on safely tapering and discontinuing immunosuppressants, particularly in patients who achieved long-term remission.

In conclusion, personalised belimumab dose spacing and withdrawal, coupled with close clinical monitoring, could be a safe and feasible approach for selected patients with SLE in long-standing remission on belimumab, minimal glucocorticoid doses, no additional immunosuppressants and low serological activity. Further randomised controlled studies with larger cohorts will be required to validate this approach and define which patients would be suitable for belimumab tapering and discontinuation.
