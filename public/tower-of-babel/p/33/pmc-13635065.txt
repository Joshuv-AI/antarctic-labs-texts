Harnessing the potential of lignocellulosic biomass for biofuels production
Authors: Nutan Mhetras, Digambar Gokhale
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCurrently, fossil fuels are the main and dominant sources for producing fuels and commodity chemicals. The rising demand for fossil fuels continues to entrench global dependency on non-renewable sources creating the climate risk, the economic instability and sustainability challenges. Hence, there is a need to shift towards globally available, sustainable and renewable resources such as lignocellulosic biomass (LCB) that offers a sustainable pathway for producing biofuels and chemicals. Though various technologies are available for LCB conversion to biofuels and chemicals, scaling up of biorefineries remains stifled by its recalcitrance nature and volatile supply chain economics. LCB processing often requires pretreatment to disrupt the rigid lignin–hemicellulose barrier and decrystallize cellulose. This structural opening is essential to maximize the enzymatic hydrolysis and sugar yields for biofuel production. The pretreatment process is energy-intensive and expensive accounting for 40% of the overall biofuel production cost followed by hydrolysis using expensive enzyme cocktails. These economic barriers currently limit the adoption of LCB as a cost-competitive fuel resource. The promising strategy for low cost LCB derived ethanol production is to adopt integrated biorefinery approach utilizing physical, chemical and biological processes. The integrated biorefinery approach tackles the high costs of second generation ethanol production by mimicking traditional petroleum refineries. It valorizes all three LCB components to marketable fuels and high-value chemicals maximizing the overall process profitability. Introduction

The world population and industrial development are expanding rapidly, which depend heavily on fossil fuels for meeting their energy needs. Fossil fuels take millions of years to form and are readily accessible and affordable over several decades. Their rate of consumption is far in excess of the rate of formation, making them non-renewable resources. Global dependence (85%) on fossil fuels and its consumption release massive amounts of greenhouse gases into the environment. These emissions cause rapid global warming, environmental pollution, and climate change. The global crude oil demand in 2025 was around 850,000 barrels/day and was expected to increase to more than 930,000 barrels/day in 2026 (https://www.iea.org/reports/oil-market-report-january-2026). To meet these growing demands, continuous efforts are being made by the scientific community to explore sustainable alternatives to fossil-based fuels (Oloyede et al. 2024). This shift from non-renewable fossil fuels to renewable natural sources is endorsed by international policies and regulatory directives of keeping global warming at well below 1.50 C being ideal (Stechemesser et al. 2024). Majority of the countries have pledged to reach zero emissions by 2050 in response to Paris Agreement (https://eciu.net/analysis/briefings/net-zero/net-zero-why). Alternative resources include solar energy, wind energy, algae and biobased feedstock such as starch, oils, with a major focus placed on LCB to limit global warming. Solar and wind are used to generate power without GHG emissions, and algae-based resources produce biofuels, nutraceuticals, and biopolymers (Goh et al. 2024; Mhetras and Gokhale 2025; Su et al. 2025). Food-based resources such as starch, vegetable oils (first generation) are not attractive for production of biofuels and chemicals because they cause an imbalance in the food and feed supply chain. Non-food-based feedstock such as lignocellulosic biomass (LCB) does not interfere with the food and feed supply chain. The abundance of LCB makes it a potential low cost feedstock for production of fuels and chemicals. Conventional methods for managing LCB waste rely on landfilling or incineration that are energy-inefficient. In addition, these methods release GHGs leading to environmental pollution and soil degeneration (Patel and Panwar 2023). Traditional methods for converting LCB to fermentable sugars include thermochemical, biochemical, and chemical processes. However, they consume high energy and generate toxic by-products leading to environmental issues. These challenges compel us to search for sustainable, less energy-intensive, and environment-friendly innovative solutions for LCB transformation.

Conventional oil refinery converts non-renewable crude oil to produce naphtha which is the product of oil refining serving as crucial feedstock. Naphtha is processed further to generate platform chemicals such as ethylene, propylene, C4-olefins, benzene, toluene and xylene. These platform chemicals serve as a base for production of other chemicals and products (Jiang et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2024a). Oil refineries rely on thermo-catalytic processes like distillation and cracking. Biorefinery concept emerged during late 1990s as a result of limited availability of fossil fuels. It acts as a sustainable alternative to oil refineries by converting plant biomass through advanced extraction and conversion techniques into fuels and chemicals. Integration of biorefinery processes is essential for transforming LCB into fuels and chemicals thereby fostering a circular bio-based economy. Adoption of integrated approach (combination of biochemical, thermochemical and physical methods) can reduce the production of waste steams thereby increasing the economic value of biomass and lowering the environmental impact (Pandey et al. 2025; Woiciechowski et al. 2025). Many LCB-based products are produced in the new generations of biorefineries, but their commercial scale production is hampered by high operating costs (OPEX) and capital expenditures (CAPEX) making them more expensive than their petroleum derived counterparts. Significant improvements are required in feedstock logistics, pretreatment efficiency, enzyme production and utilization of all LCB components to achieve cost-effective and sustainable biofuels production during scaled up operations. (Calvo-Flores and Martin-Martinez 2022). Additionally, developing new catalysts and processing technologies is of paramount importance enabling the synthesis of substances that are not synthesized by any other means. Traditionally, pulping industries focus on increasing the yield and purity of cellulose for papermaking and biorefineries prioritized carbohydrates for fuels often treating lignin as a waste by-product. Lignin streams obtained from pretreatments are presently burnt to produce steam and electricity, giving only fuel grade value to lignin. The biorefineries are now keen on transforming lignin into high value chemicals and materials. This strategic approach is crucial to reduce the dependence on cellulose-based revenue thereby improving sustainability and economic feasibility of LCB-derived biofuels. In recent decades a new strategy known as “lignin-first” has been developed in which the availability of lignin fraction is prioritized together with carbohydrate preservation. It is also known as Reductive Catalytic Fractionation (RCF) that extracts and depolymerizes lignin from LCB immediately. This approach targets the prompt stabilization of reactive lignin intermediates to minimize lignin condensation. It also provides homogeneous lignin suitable for conversion to biobased fuels, sustainable plastics, pharmaceuticals and other high-demand aromatic chemicals through chemical or biological routes. The “lignin-first” approach enables the complete utilization of LCB components creating a more profitable and environmentally benign process for producing biofuels and chemicals (Martins et al. 2024).

Recently, biorefineries are producing many fuels, polymers, and platform chemicals from LCB through processes that are expected to reach an industrial scale. However, the cost of the bio-products produced by biorefineries exceeds the cost of fossil fuel-based products which currently dominate the market (Iwogbemi et al. 2025). Traditional approaches primarily focus on production of a single product such as bioethanol or biogas from LCB that fail to offer economic viability. The biorefinery concept tackles these drawbacks by enabling simultaneous production of multiple products from LCB. One can also integrate biorefinery processes and chemical processes to get bulk products and high-value products at one place. This integrated approach generates additional revenue streams and minimizes the waste generation leading to economic sustainability of the biofuel industries. Many reviews focused on the promising chemicals that can be produced from LCB which may reduce the cellulosic ethanol production cost. However, most of the technologies developed so far are still under developmental stage and their commercial feasibility still remains uncertain. Also recent reviews have been published which primarily highlighted the potential of LCB as sustainable feedstock for producing biofuels and chemicals. Many of them point out the major challenges in LCB pretreatment and unaffordable cost of biocatalysts (enzymes) resulting in high cost of biofuels production. The recent advances in LCB pretreatment processes, integrated biorefinery concept and “lignin first” approach show promise in development of cost-effective and sustainable technologies for biofuels production. However, the technology still faces difficulties in achieving economic competitiveness with petroleum-based fuels. Technologies developed so far for the production of LCB-derived biofuels are still at developmental stage and their commercial feasibility is still uncertain. The low operational cost technologies need to be developed for effectively valorizing each component of LCB including lignin to value-added products. Chemo-catalytic routes for converting LCB to ethanol is considered to be the greatest option since it produces ethanol in high yields with no carbon loss and GHG release (Gong et al. 2022; Niaze et al. 2025). This route involves cascade reactions such as hydrolysis, retro-aldol condensation and hydrogenolysis. The techno-economic analysis also proved its superiority over fermentation as well as gasification routes with respect to the maximum fuel selling price (Zhuang et al. 2024). However, there are many challenges in the optimization of catalysts which are involved in retro-aldol reaction followed by hydrogenation which balance the main and side reactions. In addition, the major challenge is instability of highly reactive glycolaldehyde involved in side reactions that suppress the target alcohol yield. These hurdles need to be properly addressed before this technology is commercialized. Our review discusses the recent approaches in harnessing LCB for biofuels mainly ethanol and butanol. We try to address the gaps in current LCB valorization processes which enable the development of commercial processes for sustainable production of biofuels. This review also aims to provide recent information (2020–2026) on critical challenges primarily regarding LCB pretreatment and enzyme cost and scale up limitations that are faced by the processes employed in LCB conversion to biofuels.

LCB sources and structure

LCB includes agricultural residues, energy crops, forest residues, wood by-products and municipal solid waste, aquatic plants such as water hyacinth. Around 140–170 billion metric tons of biomass is produced annually but hardly 8.2 billion tons are exploited for various applications. LCB is recognized as lucrative feedstock having enormous potential for producing fuels and chemicals. However, the traditional management methods (incineration or disposal) are inefficient, causing not only environmental pollution but also failing to unlock its potential (Ingle et al. 2025). Though LCB resources offer sustainable advantages over fossil fuels, they still present some trade-offs which include intrinsic heterogeneity of LCB, market price fluctuations, requirements of complex, energy-intensivepretreatment methods with high operating cost which adds to the overall cost of LCB-based products (Seger et al. 2024; Bytyqi et al. 2025). Biorefinery processes are still in the early stage of development due to many barriers and hence are not as mature as fossil refineries. Typically, LCB is a carbohydrate polymer containing cellulose (35–55%), hemicellulose (20–35%) and lignin (10–25%) predominantly distributed in secondary walls of plant cell walls. Cellulose and hemicellulose are interconnected with each other by hydrogen bonds and covalent bonds and both are linked to lignin. This interconnected nature of components in LCB makes them a robust material which provides structural rigidity and defense for plants but creates a major hurdle in utilizing plant biomass efficiently (Tamo et al. 2025).

Cellulose is an integral part of LCB comprising D-glucose units linked by β-(1,4)-glycosidic bonds with cellobiose as functional unit. The molecular weight of cellulose polymer ranges from 50,000 to 2,500,000 that corresponds to 300–15000 glucose units. The cellulose chains are aggregated to form microfibrils which are packed together to form cellulose fibrils. The microfibrils and fibrils are tightly interconnected giving rigidity and high tensile strength to plant cell walls. Recent studies have revised the understanding of cellulose synthesis in land plants with strong evidence that cellulose is synthesized by a multi-protein cellulose synthase complex (CSC). It was also found that the land plants synthesize a single cellulose microfibril with maximally 18 glucan units having 3 nm width (Cosgrove et al. 2024). However, they are still not sure about the precise chain numbers and arrangement of these chains in single cellulose microfibril which remains a topic of active scientific debate. Degree of polymerization (DP) of cellulose is also thought to influence its susceptibility to enzymatic hydrolysis. However, the exact role of DP in enzyme hydrolysis is not fully understood. It is assumed that the higher DP hinders the enzyme attack due to presence of more hydrogen bonds and the lower DP facilitates enzyme accessibility due to weaker hydrogen bonding. Cellulose has crystalline and amorphous regions that are intertwined to form cellulose structure. Cellulose structure exists in two forms namely crystalline (highly ordered) and amorphous (randomly distributed), and the crystallinity is due to intramolecular and intermolecular hydrogen bonds. Crystalline cellulose exhibits a variety of polymorphs namely, Iα, Iβ, II and III distinguished by hydrogen bonding patterns between glucose units. The cellulose has versatile properties such as low toxicity, biocompatibility, biodegradability and mechanical strength making it suitable for use in drug delivery systems. Softwood cellulose fibers are used for packaging, sanitary as well as hygiene products due to their length, coarseness and absorbency. Hardwood cellulose fibers with high chemical purity (90%) are used to make textiles such as rayon and Lyocell. These fibers are also processed into cellulose derivatives (CMC, cellulose acetate) having applications in food and medical industries (Marinho 2025; Sulis et al. 2025). Nanocellulose is an emerging and sustainable biomaterial with remarkable mechanical strength. Based on its morphology, particle size and isolation methods, nanocellulose is classified into nanocrystalline cellulose (NCC), nanofibrillar cellulose (NFC) and bacterial nanocellulose (BNC). A recent review highlights a shift toward greener and more sustainable methodologies to produce NCC aiming at replacing mineral acids, enzymes, mechanical, oxidative and radiation-based methods (Dagnino et al. 2025).

Hemicellulose (xylan) is widely considered as the second most abundant hetero-polymeric polysaccharide coexisting with cellulose in plant cell walls. Xylan is classified as glucuronoxylan (GX), glucuronoarabinoxylan (GAX) or arabinoglucuronoxylan (AGX) that are held together by β-(1,4)-and β-(1,3)-glycosidic bonds. It is a highly branched hetero-polymer consisting of predominantly pentoses (xylose, arabinose), hexoses (glucose, mannose and galactose). It also contains minor sugars (rhamnose and fructose), uronic acids and acetyl groups. All these components are interconnected through hydrogen bonds and covalent bonds. Hardwood hemicelluloses contain mostly xylan and softwood hemicelluloses contain mostly glucomannans with DP much lower (100–200 units) than cellulose. They are appended with a high degree of complex substituents such as 4-O-methyl group, α−1,2-and α−1,3-L-arabinofuranosyl or 2-O and 3-O-acetyl groups (Ye and Zhong 2022; Curry et al. 2023). Extensively acetylated hemicelluloses create steric hindrance and block the active sites of cellulase enzymes that interfere with the binding of cellulases to cellulose. Thus deacetylation of hemicellulose is known to improve the accessibility of cellulose to enzymes leading to more cellulose hydrolysis.

Lignin is an integral part of LCB providing mechanical support to the plant and is a complex and amorphous three dimensional hetero-polymer. The pulp and paper industry currently generates high amounts of lignin (roughly 100 million tons) as by-product annually during pulping process and it is expected to reach 225 million tons per year by 2030. The lignin market size in 2025 was USD 921 billion and is expected to reach USD 969 billion in 2026 with the set target to exceed USD 1.6 trillion by 2035 (https://www.researchnester.com/reports/lignin-waste-market/6166). Biorefining of LCB produced large quantities of lignin as a by-product with up to 1.5 kg of lignin produced per liter of cellulosic ethanol. However, almost all (99%) of lignin produced is burnt to generate heat and electricity and only small portion (1% or 2%) is, recovered for high value applications (Gan et al. 2022). Lignin is composed of three phenylpropanoid units namely p-coumaryl alcohol (H), coniferyl alcohol (G) and synapyl alcohol (S). These units are connected by C-O and C–C bonds which include α-O-4, β-O-4, β-β, β−5, β−1 bonds (Sun 2020). Lignin strongly binds to both cellulose and hemicelluloses in the cell wall and thus acting as a barrier to the accessibility of the biomass,degrading enzymes to cellulose and hemicellulose. It also irreversibly adsorbs enzymes and deactivates them resulting in reduced hydrolytic efficiency during enzymatic hydrolysis. This non-productive binding of lignin to enzymes is attributed to, hydrophobic interactions and electrostatic attraction (Zhang et al. 2024b). There are two types of lignins namely native lignin and industrial or technical lignin. Lignin in LCB exists as native lignin in unaltered form which holds great potential in the synthesis of high-value chemicals through “lignin-first” strategies. The technical lignin is generated from industrial sources such as pulp and paper industries using harsh chemical processes (Bilal et al. 2021). Kraft lignin is most widely produced technical lignin amounting to 85% (130 million tons) of the total lignin produced worldwide having applications in making fillers, resin materials, carbon fibres and activated car,bon (Giardino et al. 2024). Other lignins include organosolvent lignin, pyrolytic lignin, hydrolysis lignin and the properties of these l.ignins differ depending on methods of extraction. Based on recent research, an attempt was made by Wu et al (2024) to valorize the poplar the sawdust by converting it into well-defined lignin oil and bioethanol. They aintegrated the RCF process with ethanol fermentation process. This integrated biorefinery approach provided promising method to effectively fractionate and utilize all the components of poplar saw dust. Ralstonia eutropha, no,n-lignin degrading bacterium, was engineered through multi-module genome integration and directed evolution which converted underutilized lignin into polyhydroxybutyrate (PHB). The engineered organism produced 2.38 g/L of PHB from lignin derived aromatics (Zhao et al. 2024). The earlier misconception was that anything “except money” can be made from lignin. However, the waste-to-resource transition of lignin is slowly moving towards industrial reality due to the improvements in “lignin-first” processes. These “lignin first” processes prioritize the extraction and stabilization of lignin which can be easily valorized to produce phenolic monomers. This strategy,creates multiple high-value aromatic product streams rather than generating a single low-value waste stream thus offsetting the costs associated with cellulosic ethanol production. Recently, the interest has been shifted to advanced “lignin-first” biorefining processes aiming to depolymerize the lignin matrix early. This approach yields high value biogenic aromatics (vanillin, bio-oils) leaving behind cellulose and hemicellulose highly accessible for enzymatic hydrolysis and subsequent conversion to biofuels (Brienza et al. 2024; Kang et al. 2025). By actively preventing lignin condensation, this holistic approach aims to valorize each and every component of LCB. LCB pretreatment

LCB is a heterogeneous, three-dimensional matrix in which cellulose, hemicellulose and lignin are intricately associated, which contribute to its recalcitrant nature. In addition, high crystallinity of cellulose, hydrophobicity of lignin and encapsulation of cellulose within lignin–hemicellulose skeleton make the LCB resistant to degradation (Singhvi and Gokhale 2019; Gokhale 2021). Pretreatment is a mandatory step in LCB valorization which brings LCB into a reactive state from its recalcitrant nature. It breaks down the complex and rigid structure of lignocellulose by disrupting the tight association between lignin, cellulose and hemicellulose that improves the accessibility of its carbohydrate components for conversion. It also reduces the cellulose crystallinity so that it becomes easier for enzymes to degrade cellulose and hemicellulose into fermentable sugars. Recent reviews highlight several LCB pretreatment techniques to overcome the LCB recalcitrance along with their advantages and disadvantages (Shukla et al. 2023; Jain and Kumar 2024; Kululo et al. 2025; Wo'zniak et al. 2025). Hence, we shortly introduce some of the LCB pretreatment methods which may provide clear guidance on searching cost-effective methods of LCB pretreatment. The key LCB pretreatment methods are categorized into physical, chemical, organosolv and biological methods.

Physical methods

Physical treatment methods include grinding, extrusion, shredding and cutting which require mechanical energy. These methods decrease the biomass particle size, improve the surface area and reduce the crystallinity and degree of molecular polymerization. This approach offers advantages such as environmentally benignity, low production cost and no generation of toxic products. Recently, ball milling was shown to be effective to produce bioethanol from LCB which reduced the crystallinity index and particle sizes of the biomass. Chen et al. (2022) demonstrated that ball-milled corn stover generated considerable fermentable sugars through enzymatic hydrolysis with enzyme loading of 5 FPU/g of solids at 30% ball-milled corn stover. Extrusion is a well-known thermo-mechanical method generally used in plastic industry for mixing and giving shape to the polymers and recently it was also used in LCB pretreatment (Gallego-Garcia et al. 2023; Karimipour-Fard et al. 2023). It involves the mechanical shearing and thermal energy for disruption of complex LCB structure. This pretreatment leads to separation of lignin from cellulose and hemicellulose thereby increasing the porosity (Konan et al. 2022). The effect of reactive extrusion was studied using corn stover and spruce chips. The optimization of extrusion conditions through response surface methodology led to improvement in delignification of both the biomasses. The pretreated biomass gave high yield of glucose and xylose after saccharification. The delignification was achieved without the formation of p-cresol, furfural and 5-hydroxymethylfurfural which inhibit enzymes as well as microbial growth (Konan et al. 2025). In another study, blasting extrusion pretreatment was shown to enhance ethanol production from sweet sorghum bagasse. It was found that there was higher release of sugar from bagasse compared to conventional extrusion methods during enzymatic saccharification that resulted in enhanced ethanol production (Vázquez-Rodríguez et al. 2025). Though mechanical methods offer advantages, they require higher mechanical and electrical energy compared to inherent biomass energy making them costly processes. In addition, they are generally ineffective in lignin removal from LCB which is difficult to valorize restricting their commercial feasibility. Pretreatments such as microwave, ultrasound and high-energy radiations can be used as alternative pretreatment approaches. Microwave radiations disintegrate LCB structures by exerting thermal effect which increases cellulose accessibility (Chen et al. 2019). Ultrasound pretreatment uses acoustic cavitation to disrupt rigid LCB structure and weakens the hydrogen bonds. It enhances the biomass porosity, reduces the cellulose crystallinity and dissolves lignin. This physical modification improves enzyme access, accelerating the hydrolysis of carbohydrates into fermentable sugars (Abolore et al. 2024). These methods really offer several advantages such as high efficiency energy saving, minimal by-products, short reaction times, and no pollution issues. However, there are practical issues such as low penetration rates and uneven power distribution of radiations when dealing with large amounts of biomass resulting in low sugar content. In general, physical methods are less chemically invasive but pose industrial scale up challenges due to their high energy dependency and also difficulty in controlling particle size (Muryanto et al. 2024). These challenges can be resolved by extensive optimization to make the processes efficient and cost-effective at industrial scale.

Chemical methods

Chemical methods are extensively employed for LCB pretreatment due to their cost-effectiveness and efficiency which uses acid, alkali, ionic liquids, organic solvents. These chemicals alter the chemical bonds and functional groups of LCB regulating its physical structure and also chemical composition. Chemical pretreatments reduce cellulose crystallinity, enhance surface area of biomass and remove lignin and hemicellulose which contribute to reducing the recalcitrance of LCB (Yang et al. 2024). These are generally employed in pulp and paper industry to remove controlled amount of lignin from the pulp for further biomass processing (Mboowa 2024). The acid pretreatment involves the use of concentrated or/and dilute acids. The use of concentrated sulfuric acid and hydrochloric acid for LCB pretreatment are not considered safe due to their poisonous, hazardous and corrosive properties even though higher sugars are released from pretreated LCB through enzymatic hydrolysis. In addition, inhibitors such as furfural, hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF) generated during severe acid pretreatment are toxic to microorganisms (yeasts and bacteria) which affect the fermentation by slowing microbial growth and in turn reducing the product yield. Alkaline pretreatment of LCB has gained significant interest since it removes lignin and some hemicellulose improving the accessibility of biomass to enzymes thereby enhancing the saccharification of LCB. The most commonly used alkali for LCB pretreatment are sodium, potassium, ammonium and calcium hydroxides. Alkali is non-corrosive and hence the alkali pretreatment does not need corrosion-resistant reaction equipment which is the main advantage of this process. It generates low levels of by-products/inhibitors compared to acid-based pretreatment. In addition, alkaline pretreatment delignifies the biomass efficiently but requirement of large amount of non-recoverable alkali reagents limits their use (Kininge and Gogate 2022). The issues such as alkali recovery, waste generation and longer time of pretreatment need to be addressed for its economic viability. Calcium oxide was found to be more effective in alkaline pretreatment since it can be recovered and regenerated and also reused. It is a mild alkali that also disrupts lignin-carbohydrate complex resulting in enhanced cellulose accessibility. The possibility of using recoverable alkali such as calcium oxide (CaO) or calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)2) for LCB pretreatment shows promise in sustainability, cost-effectiveness and waste reduction which aligns with the green chemistry principles (Mazaheri et al. 2021; Worku et al. 2024). Sugarcane bagasse (Hu et al. 2018) and corn stalks (Wu et al. 2019) were pretreated with aqueous calcium oxide at 50 °C for 8 h along with surfactant. This pretreatment improved enzymatic hydrolysis of the biomasses with the production of 190 g of ethanol/kg of biomass. Though it is an inexpensive alkali and also recoverable, its low solubility makes it a time-consuming pretreatment. Further research is required for its applicability to all types of biomasses since lignin recovery is not as effective as in the case of NaOH pretreatment. The application of the alkali pretreatment requires further research to tackle the challenges such as alkali recovery, waste generation and longer treatment times to make the process sustainable and economically viable. Recent studies present the upscaling of sugarcane bagasse pretreatment using soda process with the new redox mediator, 2-hydroxynaphthalene-1,4-dione. This pretreatment removed lignin without inhibiting subsequent enzymatic hydrolysis or fermentation. The pretreated bagasse (15%) was hydrolyzed with less enzyme loading (3FPU/g) that led to 142 g/L of reducing sugars after 72 h. The hydrolysate was further fermented using co-cultures (C6 and C5 fermenting yeast strains) to yield 61.3 g/L of ethanol (Barreto et al. 2025). The authors claim that the process is robust, having potential in 2G-ethanol production. They also claim that there is enough scope for integrating 1G/2G biorefineries to improve energy efficiency and economic viability.

Organosolv pretreatments

Organosolv pretreatment is highly effective for LCB processing which uses volatile solvents such as methanol, ethanol, acetone in combination with mineral acids (HCl and H2SO4) as catalysts. This process dissolves lignin and breaks the lignin carbohydrate linkage to improve enzymatic saccharification. The mechanism of action involves breaking down of aryl ether bonds between LCB components (Nair et al. 2023). One of the key features of the process is the removal of highly pure lignin fraction, which can be used in various chemical reactions. Another advantage is recovery of solvents followed by their recycling, which is critical for the economic and environmental sustainability of the process. The challenges such as equipment development, solvent recycling, process optimization require careful management at industrial scale since solvents are volatile and inflammable. In addition, when operated as an integrated biorefinery, the solvents extract both lignin and hemicellulose. This multi-product yield offsets the high capital and operating costs of the traditional extraction processes. The scaling up processes face hurdles like high capital costs and solvent losses, which directly impact operational costs also.

Ionic liquid and deep eutectic solvents pretreatments

Extensive work has been conducted on the use of ionic liquids (ILs) for LCB pretreatment. Presently, ILs are used for LCB pretreatment due to their ability to completely dissolve lignin and carbohydrates (Dharmaraja et al. 2023; Rahman et al. 2025). ILs consists of large organic cations and small inorganic or organic anions exhibiting high thermal stability. They possess unique properties such as negligible vapour pressure and their ability to remain in liquid form in wide range of temperatures. These properties make them most versatile greener and recyclable solvents for using them in various industrial processes compared to conventional solvents. Recently, reducing sugars and lignin were recovered from sugarcane bagasse using two novel protic ionic liquids, N,N-diisopropylethylammonium hydrogen sulphate and N,N-diisopropylethylammonium perchlorate. The pretreated biomass was hydrolysed by enzymes which showed 3.9–4.7fold increase in saccharification efficiency (Sunar et al. 2024). It is also demonstrated that both the ILs were recycled with high pretreatment efficacy for subsequent biomass processing. However, the challenges remain in adopting ILs-based pretreatment to cost effective industrial scale applications due to limitations in techniques such as distillation, membrane separation, accumulation of biomass derived impurities causing poor performance over repeated recycles. Hence there is urgent need for energy efficient and cost competitive recycling technologies (Rahman et al. 2025). Very recent review also explored the various ILs recovery techniques highlighting their efficiencies and limitations in the context of increasing the commercial viability and sustainability of ILs (Rai et al. 2026). In addition, further research should also focus on valorization of recovered fractions in to high value products (Ovejero-Parez et al. 2024). Deep eutectic solvents (DESs) have emerged as superior green alternatives to traditional solvents for LCB pretreatment. They disrupt the rigid/tough lignin-carbohydrate complex and remove lignin with high efficiency even after multiple cycles leaving cellulose mostly intact. They are easily synthesized from readily available and inexpensive raw materials such as choline chloride, urea and glycerol. Their low cost, high biodegradability, efficient recyclability and reusability may contribute to the development of sustainable and economically viable LCB pretreatment processes in biorefineries. The key factors such as lignin removal rate, sugar conversion rate, and the chemical structure of the products in streams generated through DESs pretreated LCB have been critically discussed in recent reviews (Yao et al. 2024b; Bao et al. 2025). A comparative study was carried out using three DESs (choline chloride:glycerol, choline chloride:ethylele glycol and choline chloride:formic acid) for pre-treatment of rice straw (Igbojionu et al. 2025). The results showed that choline chloride:formic acid pretreated rice straw was significantly hydrolysed with 79.7% glucose yield after 72 h. Life cycle assessment indicates that DES-assisted LCB pretreatment reduces environmental impacts by 67% compared to conventional methods. However, this pretreatment technology requires high solvent recovery rates (95%) and high solid loadings (Wang et al. 2025a, b, c). Very recently. technoeconomic and life cycle assessment of ethanol production from sugarcane bagasse using MW-assisted ternary DES pretreatment was performed (Joshikumar et al. 2026). The ternary DES was composed of choline chloride:ethylene glycol:nickel chloride hexahydrate in a molar ratio of 1:2:0.016. The process was performed in one pot which included MS-assisted DES pretreatment, enzyme hydrolysis and fermentation followed by downstream separation and purification. This study demonstrated that ethanol production cost for a 100 tonne-per-day was USD 1098 per ton, with 5-year payback period with 10% solid loading during pretreatment and no DESs recycling. Here, the assumption was made that the plant was operation in the viscinity of sugar mill. The production cost and global warming potential can be reduced by 30% and 91% respectively by increasing the solid and DES loading as per the sensitivity analysis. These results tell us that adjusting the critical operation variables may lead to environmental sustainability with commercial viability of cellulosic ethanol plant.

Combined pretreatment methods

Relying on a single pretreatment method inevitably presents certain bottlenecks such as excessive energy use, production of toxic chemicals or high chemical cost. Combination of various pretreatment methods like physico-chemical, physical-biological or biological-chemical methods creates a synergistic effect that overcomes these limitations and enhances overall bioconversion efficiency. For example, ball milling with chemicals was shown to facilitate lignin removal, decrease the cellulose crystallinity which ultimately enhances the LCB digestibility (Sitotaw et al. 2023). Alkali induced dry ball milling (chemo-mechanical) pretreatment of corn stover biomass was found to be effective which gave highest yields of glucose (97%) and xylose (72%) after enzymatic hydrolysis (Lin et al. 2024). The combination of ultrasound and chemical treatments (alkali, acid, organic solvent or ionic liquids) intensifies the overall process that offers higher efficiencies, shorter reaction times and lower temperatures compared to conventional approaches. The alkaline pretreatment combined with ultrasound was studied using sawdust obtained from furniture manufacturing which resulted in higher delignification compared to conventional pretreatment. The combined pretreatment led to more effective enzyme accessibility to LCB which improved the hydrolysis (Chen et al. 2024; Gavrila et al. 2024). Ultrasound irradiation coupled with low cost ionic liquid, triethylammonium hydrogen sulphate [TEA][HSO4] treatment was performed at 130 °C for 30 min with high biomass (wheat straw) loading which removed 75% lignin and 52% hemicellulose. This pretreatment resulted in almost 80% saccharification yields using commercial cellulase (CelluMax) and the ethanol generation rate was 42 g/L with 82% yield (Ziaei-Rad et al. 2023). These findings show promise in developing ultrasound pretreatment aided with low cost chemicals for LCB conversion to ethanol. Microwave (MW) assisted DES pretreatment enables ultrafast deconstruction of biomass. It was found that MW enhanced the delignification efficiency of DES leading to 96% lignin removal from wheat straw just in 150 s at 130 °C, significantly improving enzymatic digestibility (92%) and reducing biorefinery costs (Wang et al. 2025a, b, c). The life cycle cost analysis suggests that the MW-DES pretreatment showed 44% reduction in cost compared to conventional heating-DES pretreatment. The combined pretreatment technologies offer great potential in LCB utilization and make a critical contribution to achieving global climate goals and the circular bioeconomy.

Biological pre-treatment

Biological pretreatment is performed by microorganisms or enzymes to delignify LCB. It is sustainable, mild, cost-effective and eco-friendly and is operating without any chemicals and high energy demands. This pretreatment rarely generates the toxic byproducts which commonly inhibit enzymatic saccharification and fermentation steps. Lignin decomposition in this process is carried out by both bacteria and fungi especially white or brown rot fungi. White-rot fungi are generally preferred because they have a unique extracellular enzyme system which includes laccases, lignin peroxidases, manganese peroxidases. These enzymes preferentially depolymerize lignin by an oxidative action without severely damaging the cellulose and hemicellulose in LCB (Kumar et al. 2025). Laccases are restricted to attack phenolic lignin units to form phenoxy radicals but they cannot oxidize non-phenolic lignin units due to their low redox potential. They generally require low molecular weight mediators such as syringaldehyde, acetosyringone and lignin-derived monophenolics to oxidize non-phenolic lignins. Lignin peroxidases are powerful enzymes which depolymerize both phenolic and non-phenolic lignins because of their high redox potential. They generate highly active radical intermediates which trigger the spontaneous chemical reactions leading to breakdown of strong C–C bonds (Nguyen et al. 2024). Manganese peroxidases are key enzymes used in biological pretreatment which degrade lignin in LCB. These enzymes oxidize Mn2+ to highly reactive Mn3+ which forms a complex with the chelators (oxalate or malonate). This stabilized Mn complex diffuses away from the enzyme and attacks phenolic lignin units to generate free radicals which effectively disintegrate both phenolic and non-phenolic lignins (Kumar and Arora 2022). Though biological pretreatments offer the inherent advantages of low energy requirement and no use of harsh chemicals, they suffer from several shortcomings which hinder the commercial scalability. The shortcomings include the requirement of costly sterilization process, long incubation time needed for microbial growth, undesirable carbohydrate loss due to consumption of sugars by microbes and the variable efficiency of lignin degradation due to structural variation in the feedstock. These bottlenecks can be addressed by integrating biological methods with mild physical treatments to achieve desired delignification. These combined or hybrid pretreatment methods may reduce the incubation time and also prevent unwanted sugar loss (Chen et al. 2024). In addition, these pretreatments remain less studied in comparison to physical, chemical and physico-chemical methods mainly due to their lengthy incubation periods (Kumar et al. 2025; Sulis et al. 2025).

In general, many reviews highlight the importance of pretreatment processes which account for 40% of the total production cost of LCB derived biofuels (Awoyale and Lokhat 2021). Many pretreatments processes have been examined and it was observed that each method has its own advantages and limitations. For example, steam explosion is most cost-effective method which is widely used at commercial scale but it is less effective with high lignin containing biomasses. LCB pretreatment using DESs is highly regarded as a promising and sustainable approach because it removes lignin substantially and also supports environmentally friendly processing. However, it faces significant hurdles toward commercialization due to high viscosity and poor mass transfer which hinder effective LCB deconstruction especially at higher solid loadings. Also DESs pretreatment requires high solvent-to-biomass ratio which reduces its economic viability. Therefore, a “one-size-fits-all” approach is not applicable for ethanol production due to varying composition of LCB feedstock and hence no single pretreatment works universally for all types of LCB feedstock. The high cost of LCB pretreatment is still a major issue in commercial production of second generation ethanol. Further research in areas like process efficiency, energy reduction and recovery of high-value by-products is required to improve the overall process economics of the LCB pretreatment at industrial scale.

Enzymatic hydrolysis: key challenge in LCB bio-refinery

Dilute sulfuric acid is commonly employed for the hydrolysis of a wide variety of biomass with more than 80% sugar yields. However, it releases undesired by-products from degradation of hemicellulose which inhibit the fermentation microbes. Enzymatic hydrolysis is considered to be the most promising method for LCB conversion to monosaccharides because it requires less energy and mild operating conditions. The cellulose and hemicellulose in pretreated LCB are hydrolyzed to their respective monomers with the help of hydrolytic and oxidative enzymes acting in synergy. The cellulases, namely cellulolytic glycoside hydrolases (GHs) can be classified into cellobiohydrolases (exo-1,4-β-D-glucanases, EC 3.2.1.91), endoglucanases (endo-β−1,4-glucanases EC 3.2.1.4) and β-D-glucosidases (EC 3.2.1.21). Cellobiohydrolases (CBHs) cleave cellobiose units from both reducing and non-reducing ends of cellulose chains. They depolymerize cellulose by processive hydrolysis along the linear cellulose chain releasing cellobiose from the ends. The processive action is essential for degrading highly crystalline structure of cellulose. Endoglucanases (EGs) hydrolyze cellulose chains at amorphous regions randomly and increase the number of chain ends for the action of cellobiohydrolases. The β-D-glucosidases (BGs) catalyze the final step of cellulose hydrolysis hydrolyzing cellobiose and cello-oligosaccharides released by CBHs and/or EGs to glucose (Payne et al. 2015). Exo- and endo-glucanases are inhibited by cellobiose and β-D-glucosidases are inhibited by glucose (Da Silva et al. 2020). Globally, the cellulase market is experiencing strong growth with valuations reaching USD 1.9 billion in 2025 and is projected to exceed USD 2.4 billion by 2035. The expected increase in the market is due to rising demands from textile, biofuels, detergent and food industries (https://www.futuremarketinsights.com/reports/cellulase-market). Fungal cellulases are dominating with approximately 39% market share in 2025 because of their ease of cultivation, high enzyme yields and stability. Bacterial and actinomycetes cellulases hold 25% and 15% market shares respectively because of their complex production processes and lower enzyme yields.

Lytic polysaccharide monooxygenases (LPMOs) and cellobiose dehydrogenases (CDHs) are auxiliary enzymes which are known to accelerate cellulose hydrolysis (Smuts et al. 2023). These two enzymes act synergistically on highly ordered crystalline surfaces of cellulose creating new chain ends (both native and oxidized) for the action of CBHs. The LPMO is a copper-dependent enzyme responsible for oxidative cleavage of glycosidic bonds in the presence of reductant. This process of oxidative cleavage requires an external reducing agent such as ascorbic acid and molecular oxygen to produce reactive oxygen which cleaves the glycosidic bonds in cellulose. Thus, LPMOs use an oxidative process to break down cellulose chains and create oxidized cellulose fragments which are more accessible to cellulase enzymes. The oxidation occurs at C1 or C4 positions of the glucose units within the cellulose chain depending on the type of LPMOs. Sometimes LPMOs can undergo auto-oxidative deactivation using reducing agents as electron donors (Christensen et al. 2023). LPMOs are classified into eight families (AA9, AA10, AA11, AA13, AA14, AA15, AA16, and AA17) based on their origin and substrate specificity. LPMOs from AA9 family are predominantly from fungal sources and AA10 are from bacteria, yeast and fungi. A recent review by Contato and Conte-Junior (2025) highlighted the importance of fungal LPMOs as indispensable enzymes for the future of the circular bioeconomy and green industrial processes. CDHs catalyze the oxidation of cello-oligosaccharides and cellobiose at their reducing ends and relieve the product inhibition of cellulases by oxidizing cellobiose and cello-oligosaccharides. During oxidation, CDHs support LPMOs' function by supplying electrons to LPMOs' copper center at the active site (Xie et al. 2023). Recent studies established that LPMOs play a crucial role in LCB degradation and act synergistically with cellulases for hydrolysis of recalcitrant biomass. Hence, they are known as “booster enzymes” acting oxidatively on cellulose, hemicellulose and lignin. A protein engineering approach is being employed to improve the performance of LPMOs required for their application in biorefinery processes (Kumar et al. 2024; Hall et al. 2025). Recently, the impact of LPMOs on the efficiency of cellulase cocktails was studied by Angeltveit et al. (2024). They used LPMO-deficient Celluclast 1.5L and supplemented with LPMOs from two fungal strains (Thermothielavioides terrestris and Thermoascus auranticus) for hydrolysis of Avicel and steam-exploded wheat straw. The results confirmed that the cellulase cocktail with 10% LPMO supplementation not only increased the cellulose solubilization but also improved the saccharification (75%) of both Avicel and wheat straw. Commercial cellulase Cellic® CTec2 supplemented with LPMO or LPMO with its co-substrate H2O2 improved the enzymatic hydrolysis of sugarcane bagasse pretreated by hydrodynamic cavitation-assisted oxidative process at 10% solid loading (Balaguer Moya et al. 2022). Thus, the blending of traditional cellulases with LPMOs or CDH-like enzymes could improve the hydrolytic step in biorefineries. In addition, enzyme blends with LPMOs can reduce the enzyme doses for LCB hydrolysis leading to economic viability of the saccharification process. The LPMO enzyme structure, its substrate preference and mechanism of its action on cellulosic substrates have been well discussed in a recent review (Rezik et al. 2023; Hall et al. 2025). These studies are necessary for the selection of bioprocess conditions in relation to LPMO activation/stabilization in biorefinery processes.

Microbes that degrade plant cell walls produce either free enzyme systems extracellularly in the medium or multi-enzyme complexes called cellulosomes (Payne et al. 2015). Aerobic bacteria and fungi (Trichoderma and Aspergillus) produce free enzyme systems containing cellulase enzymes acting synergistically to degrade cellulose. Anaerobic bacteria such as Clostridium produce cellulosomes which are anchored to the bacterial cell surface. The cellulosome systems of C. thermocellum and C. cellulolyticum are well characterized. The enzymes in cellulosomes are held together by non-enzymatic protein called scaffoldin which contains carbohydrate domain (CBD) to bind the cellulosome tightly to the substrate. This tight binding enables the enzymes to degrade LCB efficiently and synergistically. Consolidated biosaccharification (CBS) has been developed in which Clostridium thermocellum and its cellulosomes are utilized for LCB saccharification (Liu et al. 2020; Qi et al. 2021). Recently an exhaustive review has been published on digestive tract microbiota cellulosome system which focused on structural and functional attributes of cellulosomes in digestive tracts of various animals (Qi et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2025). Cellulosomes from animal digestive tracts differ from Clostridial cellulosomes and hence further research is needed in relation to their structural and functional aspects. Recently, about 305,693 prokaryotic genome assemblies were searched from NCIB sequence database to identify cellulosome containing bacteria (Minor et al. 2024). The genomic analysis revealed 33 bacterial species which have the ability to produce cellulosomal proteins. Out of 33 bacterial species 10 species were not previously reported in the literature. The majority of these cellulosome producing bacteria belong to genera Acetivibrio, Clostridium, Ruminoclostridium and Ruminococcus. The comparison of scaffoldins, enzymes and mechanism of cellulosomal gene regulation in these bacteria displayed conserved properties. Each enzyme (like cellulase) has a specific non-catalytic module called Dockerin which acts as a key. Scaffoldin contains repeated binding sites called cohesins which act as the locks. Dockerin tightly binds to complementary cohesin present on scaffoldin. This lock-and-key system brings together different enzymes into a single and highly efficient complex which helps bacteria to degrade biomass effectively. Designer cellulosomes can also be created by precisely controlling not only the placement but also the combination of enzymes on artificially created scaffoldins. These designer cellulosomes degrade biomass more effectively compared to natural enzyme systems and hence they have applications in the field of LCB biorefinery for producing biofuels and chemicals (Lindik and Vodovnik 2025). Fungal cellulosomes have been isolated from anaerobic fungi, especially from the phylum Neocallimastigomycota, residing in herbivores' digestive systems (Lillington et al. 2021; Brown et al. 2023). They are indeed poorly understood, which could be due to difficulties in their isolation and cultivation in the laboratory settings. Ambiguity still remains in the precise architecture and regulation of fungal cellulosomes especially produced by anaerobic rumen fungi, which are the areas of active research.

Free LCB degrading enzymes systems are found in aerobic filamentous fungi and some bacteria which secrete individual enzymes in the medium. These are enzyme cocktails containing exoglucanases, endoglucanases, xylanases, β-glucosidases which act synergistically to effectively break down complex LCB. Other enzymes such as laccases, lignin peroxidases and ferulyl esterases help in breaking down the lignin thereby enhancing the efficiency of LCB degradation. These enzymes degrade carbohydrates (cellulose and hemicellulose) into fermentable sugars which are the main sources for producing biofuels, chemicals and other value-added products. Trichoderma reesei is the most studied fungus producing a highly effective blend of cellulases and hence is considered to be a model organism for cellulase research. It has the robust secretory capacity making it a suitable host for producing heterologous proteins (Li et al. 2025). Its mutant, T. reesei RUT-30 developed through random mutagenesis, produces high levels of cellulases and hence is used for commercial cellulase production. The genetic make-up of T. reesei RUT-30 includes many mutations with primary alteration in regulatory genes such as deletion or truncation of cre 1 gene encoding for carbon catabolite repression. Deletion of cre 1 gene allows the strain to produce cellulases even in the presence of glucose which represses cellulase production in the wild strain. Aspergillus and Penicillium are also recognized as potent cellulase producers due to their ability to degrade LCB into simple sugars (Singh et al. 2021; Ilic et al. 2023). The cost of cellulases still remains one of the key barriers for cellulosic ethanol production. Significant efforts have been made to reduce the cost of the hydrolytic process using cellulase enzymes. Several approaches have been employed to improve the production and performance of cellulase enzymes which include optimization of fermentation processes to produce enzymes with higher yields, use of advanced genetic engineering techniques to develop strains with hyperactive cellulase enzymes (Yang et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2024c). Combination of synthetic biology and artificial intelligence (AI) is being used for developing fungal strains capable of producing hyperactive enzyme cocktails suitable for efficient degradation of LCB. Researchers are also engaged in isolating robust organisms from extreme environments which thrive on cellulose and produce highly stable and efficient extremozymes. Especially thermophiles and halophiles are considered to be ideal microbes for biofuel production due to their ability to withstand extreme environments such as high temperatures, extreme pH and high salinity. Many leading companies produce tailored cellulases used for specific applications such as textile and pulp and paper industries. Some of the companies such as Novozymes (now Novonesis) are producing cellulase and hemicellulase cocktails from improved fungal strains designed to degrade LCB into fermentable sugars. Notable products from Novozymes such as Cellic® CTec3, Cellic® HTec3 are advanced and efficient enzyme complexes used for conversion of pretreated LCBs. Cellic® CTec3 HS is another proprietary product from Novozymes with highly efficient cellulase cocktail boosted with advanced LPMO-AA9 molecules, improved β-glucosidases and an array of hemicellulases (Ilic et al. 2023). One of the mutants of P. janthinellum NCIM 1366 isolated in our laboratory produced high titers of cellulases in both submerged fermentation (SmF) and solid state fermentation (SSF) which efficiently hydrolyzed natural biomass resources such as wheat straw (Singhania et al. 2014). The mutant strain encodes more than 400 CAzymes of which about 50% are predicted to be extracellular. Though all of these CAZymes are not involved in biomass hydrolysis, they have important biochemical and metabolic function needed for the survival and proliferation of the fungus (Christopher et al. 2022). The types of proteins produced in SmF and SSF were similar to the abundance of key enzymes especially LPMOs, β-glucosidases and hemicellulases. The SSF-derived enzymes exhibited increased hydrolytic performance with high rates of hydrolysis and low cellobiose accumulation. In addition, SSF enzymes showed lower protease abundance allowing stability of the enzyme preparations (Sankar et al. 2023). LCB-degrading enzyme cocktail produced by P. janthinellum NCIM 1366 contains 4 CBHs, 7EGs and 2 β-glucosidases which is more diverse than that of T. reesei C30 strain (Christopher et al. 2023). The key advantage of this strain is its ability to produce a complete enzyme system with a high amount of β-glucosidase compared to T. reesei. In addition, the presence of LPMOs creates oxidized cellulose fragments that are more amenable to cellulase attack thereby enhancing overall efficiency of cellulose degradation. The inclusion of LPMOs in an enzymatic system makes it a potential strain for developing efficient and cost-effective enzyme systems for producing biofuels and chemicals from LCB. Although the commercial cellulase mixtures/blends are highly efficient, they are not cost-effective for 2G ethanol production. Researchers have been focusing on appropriate enzyme selection, immobilization and recycling, developing more efficient enzyme cocktails and on-site enzyme production to lower the overall biofuel production cost. Enzyme doses of 5–15 Filter Paper Units (FPU)/g of Dry Solids (DS) have been reported for cellulosic ethanol production from pretreated LCB (Ntimbani et al. 2022; van Dyk et al. 2024). However, these values are indeed high and cannot compromise the economics of cellulosic ethanol production. The possible alternative could be the use of consolidated bioprocessing (CBP) in which fermentative microbes engineered for cellulase production are employed for ethanol production from LCB. Such a CBP approach has been used in which less exogenous enzyme (< 2FPU/g of DS) was required for achieving the desired ethanol yield and concentration required for economically viable process (Lee et al. 2017; Bunga et al. 2025). Economics of cellulosic ethanol production will work only when the cost of enzyme is reduced to approximately USD 0.1 per liter of ethanol which has long been considered as appropriate. To our knowledge, combined cost of biomass pretreatment and enzymes should be between USD 0.15 and 0.USD 0.35 per gallon of ethanol since the key drivers are doses of enzymes and pretreatment methods used for LCB conversion to fermentable sugars. Cellulosic ethanol should reach the Minimum Ethanol Selling Price (MESP) of roughly USD 3.5 per gallon which is the present MESP of 1G ethanol.

Opportunities to produce cellulosic ethanol

The global ethanol market accounts for USD 109 billion in 2024 and it is expected to increase to USD 182.88 billion by 2034 (https://www.precedenceresearch.com/ethanol-market). As per the Statista report, global ethanol production is about 31 billion gallons in 2024. The global cellulosic ethanol market was only USD 4.1 billion in 2024 and it is expected to reach around USD 99.8 billion by 2034 (https://market.us/report/global-cellulosic-ethanol-market/). The ethanol market in India is valued at USD 3.28 billion in 2025 and may reach to USD 9.3 billion by 2032 with the growth rate of 16% (https://www.coherentmi.com/industry-reports/india-ethanol-market). The total ethanol production capacity in India is 1990 crore liters in 2025 from sugarcane and grains out of which 45% of the total capacity is grain-based (https://www.igrain.in/posts/indias-ethanol-capacity-reaches-199-billion-litres). Cellulosic ethanol production in India is still at pilot scale because much work is still being done to commercialize the technology. Beta Renewables started the first industrial ethanol plant (40MMgy) in 2015 in Crescentino, Italy but ceased the operations due to technical failures such as contaminations in pretreatment systems and failure to meet production targets. The company was sold in 2018 to pay off the debts from its own bankrupt parent company (Mossi & Ghisolfi Group). DuPont produced cellulosic ethanol using 30MMgy plant in Nevada, USA. After the merger of DuPont with Dow Chemicals, DowDuPont sold its first USD 200 million cellulosic ethanol plant in Nevada to VERBIO (Vereinigte BioEnergie) AG in late 2018 for production of natural gas. The facility was repurposed and retrofitted for conversion of agricultural residues to renewable natural gas. Raizen started two plants at its Costa Pinto and Bonfirm mills with capacity of 30MMgy. The company’s aim is to develop 180MMgy capacity plant in place by 2027–2028 (https://www.argusmedia.com/en/news-and-insights/latest-market-news/2580045-brazil-s-raizen-ships-2g-ethanol-cargo-to-eu). GranBio along with sugarcane-based ethanol producing companies announced the production of 40 million gallons of cellulosic ethanol in 2025 using sugarcane waste along with the facility producing 50 million cubic meter of biomethane per year (https://valorinternational.globo.com/agribusiness/news/2025/01/28/after-setbacks-granbio-redesigns-project-in-alagoas.ghtml). The company uses enzyme (Novozymes) mediated LCB pretreatment process developed by Beta Renewables and yeasts from DSM to produce 2G ethanol. Now it is aiming at producing 60 million liters of 2G ethanol in 2026 and 200 million liters in 2027 by combining 1G and 2G ethanol processes. Pinheiro et al. (2025) integrated 1G and 2G processes for ethanol production using immobilized recombinant S. cerevisiae. They used molasses (1G substrate) which was mixed with crude sugarcane bagasse acid hydrolysate (2G substrate). The authors found that it was possible to increase ethanol production per ton of sugarcane processed without decreasing the environmental performance of the process. Blue Biofuels developed its proprietary cellulose to sugar (CTS) technology which will be used to produce cellulosic ethanol at commercial scale (https://bluebiofuels.com/blue-biofuels-makes-first-cellulosic-ethanol/). The ethanol produced will be converted to Sustainable Aviation Fuels (SAF) through Consolidated Alcohol Deoxygenation and Oligomerization (CADO) technology with the help of Vertimass company. The Spanish biotech company, Evolgene, developed a new proprietary enzymatic technology which produces cellulosic ethanol from cellulosic waste. They are planning to construct two plants by 2030 at the start and further twenty plants in the following 5 years. They will use the ethanol as feedstock for producing SAF through Alcohol-to-Jet (ATJ) pathway. (https://stillwaterassociates.com/cellulosic-ethanol-is-a-revival-underway/). Though all these companies are putting efforts to commercialize the cellulosic ethanol technology, they are still facing challenges in LCB pretreatment, hydrolysis and complete use of LCB components. Hence, the affordable ethanol cost remains a prime challenge irrespective of whether it is used as fuel or the feedstock for SAF production. In addition, the commercial production of ethanol from LCB derived sugars has been restricted due to lower petroleum prices. Most of the chemicals are still produced from petroleum due to its low price and well optimized processes for their production. Careful evaluation in relation to energy consumption and environmental impact involved in different processes (especially LCB pretreatment and hydrolysis) is required for 2G-ethanol production (Yaverino-Gutierrez et al. 2024). In addition, 2G-ethanol processes often lead to diluted ethanol concentration (40–60 g/L) compared to 1G-ethanol processes (85–110 g/L). Thus, the low 2G-ethanol concentrations make the traditional distillation energy intensive and expensive since large volumes of water are required to be removed which demand high heat inputs to get appropriate ethanol purity (Ayodele et al. 2020).

Strategies for 2G-ethanol production

The common processes representing the evolution of 2G ethanol production are: separate hydrolysis and fermentation (SHF), simultaneous saccharification and fermentation (SSF), simultaneous saccharification and co-fermentation (SSCF) and consolidated bioprocessing (CBP). Each process has its own advantages and disadvantages with respect to the efficiency of operations (Devos and Colla 2022). Usually, LCB hydrolysis and ethanol fermentation are accomplished separately but can also be performed in same reactor which eliminates both the substrate and end product inhibition (Derman et al. 2022). Yousefian et al (2024) evaluated bioethanol production from alkali-pretreated sweet sorghum bagasse using SHF and SSF methods. They found that SHF yielded higher ethanol production (48.7 g/L) compared to SSF (29.5 g/L). However, SHF is still not commercially attractive due to significant longer time taken for ethanol production. SSF is highly efficient because it integrates both hydrolysis and fermentation processes within a single reactor that improves the economics and reduces the residence time during ethanol formation. Though SSF approach has been used in ethanol production from LCB, finding a compromised optimum temperature for SSF is a major bottleneck because the two processes involved (enzyme hydrolysis and fermentation) have inherently different optimal temperature profiles. (Amandio et al. 2023). The inability of wild type yeasts to ferment xylose is another bottleneck in ethanol production from LCB using SSF. Even if engineered yeasts are used in SSF, glucose consumption precedes xylose uptake thereby prolonging the fermentation time. Xylose is usually consumed only after depletion of glucose leading to diauxic fermentation which is the key challenge in LCB ethanol production (Jahangeer et al. 2025). Diauxic fermentation issue has been well addressed through metabolic engineering approach to achieve simultaneous fermentation of both glucose and xylose by engineered yeast strains. The approach involves the introduction of heterologous xylose transporters (less sensitive to glucose inhibition) from xylose fermenting yeast to S. cerevisiae. This strategy allows the strains to metabolize both sugars simultaneously to improve fermentation efficiency in the context of advanced ethanol production (Qiu et al. 2023). The pathway engineering has successfully developed engineered S. cerevisiae strains capable of co-fermenting glucose and xylose efficiently. In this context, the successful introduction of xylose reductase and xylitol dehydrogenase (XR-XDH) or xylose isomerase (XI) pathways into S. cerevisiae is considered a landmark achievement in metabolic engineering. This achievement transformed S. cerevisiae into a robust and flexible cell factory capable of co-fermenting glucose and xylose sugars from LCB into ethanol (Wagner and Gasch 2023). Very recently, nonconventional, Clavispora lusitaniae, isolated from mescal musts was known to assimilate xylose, glucose and cellobiose. The strain produced maximum ethanol (31.8 g/L) from saccharified sugarcane bagasse under anaerobic followed by combined anaerobic-microaerobic conditions and xylitol (14.3 g/L) under micro-aerobic conditions (Guzman-Hernandez et al. 2025). These findings show that C. lusitaniae has significant potential for coproduction of ethanol and xylitol simultaneously from LCB. CBP approach is widely recognized as promising technology for LCB conversion since it combines three critical steps, (enzyme production, hydrolysis and fermentation) into a single consolidated process. It reduces significantly the operational cost and capital investment associated with biofuels production from LCB. It uses a single organism or a consortium of microbes performing enzyme production, LCB hydrolysis and fermentation in single vessel (Li et al. 2024). CBP does not currently eliminate the need to disrupt the recalcitrance structure of biomass, the pretreatment is still required in current state-of-art biofuel production. Therefore, LCB pretreatment still remains a crucial step for commercial viability of cellulosic ethanol. Fungi belonging to basidiomycetes and zygomycetes grow on LCB by penetrating their hyphae deeper into the substrates and convert it to ethanol in one step (Carrillo-Nieves et al. 2019). However, most of cellulosic fungi are aerobic and hence face difficulties for their survival under anaerobic condition during fermentation. The metabolic engineering of thermophilic cellulolytic fungus, Myceliophthora thermophile, to produce ethanol directly from cellulose mimics the Crabtree effect (aerobic ethanol fermentation) observed in yeast (Zhang et al. 2023a). The engineering strategy includes 1) optimization of ethanol synthetic pathway by introducing ADH gene from S. cerevisiae, 2) enhancement of glycolytic pathway by increasing the sugar uptake capabilities 3) inhibition of mitochondrial NADH shuttles which directs metabolic flux away from respiration to ethanol production and 4) elimination of ethanol consumption pathway. This engineered thermophilic fungus produced high titers of ethanol (52.8 g/L) from Avicel cellulose and (39.8 g/L) from pulverized raw corn cob without the need of cellulase supplementation via CBP. S. cerevisiae is considered as most suitable candidate for CBP to convert LCB to ethanol provided it can be successfully engineered to produce complete suite of LCB degrading enzymes. In addition, it is robust, tolerates ethanol and low pH, ferments sugars efficiently and its genome is well-mapped. These features enable the engineering of S. cerevisiae capable of converting complex and low-cost LCB into biofuels. Several strategies for construction of cellulolytic S. cerevisiae for CBP have been discussed which mainly focus on optimizing expression, secretion and synthetic activity of heterologous cellulase cocktail (Fortuin et al. 2025). The main focus is on engineering S. cerevisiae for cellulase production through CRISPR/Cas9 approach. The developed strains secrete core CBH, EG and BGL for CBP. Using CRISPR approach multiple cellulase genes (cbh1, cbh2, eg2, and bgl1) can be integrated into yeast genome for the efficient production cellulases minimizing the requirement of external enzymes. Robust natural strains are preferred for engineering due to their superior performance under stress (heat and inhibitors). Key strategies involve free secretion of enzyme; cell wall tethering via glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI) anchors or creating multi-enzyme complexes mimicking cellulosomes in bacteria ensuring high accessibility of substrates to the enzymes. Improved metabolic engineering tools such as gene attenuation is powerful strategy that allows the microbial host to optimize the desired product yields. The strategy does not entirely knockout the specific gene or entire pathway but reduces their expression level or activity without impeding the overall viability of the microbial host (Zhang et al. 2025). This strategy has been used to reengineer S. cerevisiae to improve ethanol production from LCB (Sahoo et al. 2025). Such attempt was made by deletion of four genes GPD2, FPS1, ADH2 and DLD3 corresponding to glycerol synthesis, glycerol release, ethanol breakdown and D-lactate dehydrogenase respectively using CRISPR-Cas9 approach (Yang et al. 2022). The main aim of this strategy is to redirect the carbon flux towards ethanol production and reducing the by-products formation. The engineered yeast produced 18.58% more ethanol by reducing the byproducts such as glycerol and lactic acid. Though the primary aim is to use this strain for ethanol production from glucose and sucrose, the ultimate goal is to exploit these strains in biorefineries for conversion of LCB sugars into ethanol. The rational metabolic engineering has been the primary approach for improving ethanol production in S. cerevisiae. This traditional approach targets specific gene modifications to redirect carbon flux towards ethanol. However, it is increasingly hindered by complexity of cellular systems and unpredictability of systemic effects. For example, introducing new or modified pathways may consume essential precursors, produce toxic intermediates causing slow grow or cell death. To mitigate these limitations, evolutionary engineering, also known as Adaptive Laboratory Evolution (ALE) is a powerful, non-rational engineering strategy to improve yeast performance in ethanol production from LCB. This approach improves consumption of both glucose and xylose which is crucial for maximizing fermentation efficiency from LCB and also increasing the tolerance to inhibitors (furfural, acetic acid) generated through LCB pretreatment. It involves random mutagenesis and selection to improve yeast strains for better ethanol production. It works by inducing random genetic changes followed by selection of the mutants that survive harsh conditions thereby evolving a more robust strain (Topaloglu et al. 2023, Wang et al. 2025a, b, c). The recent studies by Yao et al. (2024a) showed that the ALE strategy is highly effective for generating S. cerevisiae strains with improved furfural tolerance for bioethanol production. In this study, complete genome sequencing and comparative genomic analysis of the evolved strains was conducted to identify the genetic mutations and alterations responsible for the improved furfural tolerance. The identified key mutations were reconstructed using CRISPR/Cas9 approach to understand the specific mechanism of tolerance. Such strains generated through ALE approach make the entire process of transforming LCB to ethanol more robust and economically viable improving the efficiency of converting LCB to ethanol even in presence of inhibitors. The ALE approach was used to obtain cellulolytic S. cerevisiae Z100 strain after 91 days’ laboratory adaptive evolution in wheat straw hydrolysate enabling it to maintain high fermentability at 50 °C. The improved strain showed 127% enhancement in ethanol production from pretreated wheat straw at 50 °C within 24 h (Zhang et al. 2023b).

Among all the strategies, CBP is the ideal strategy for cost-effective ethanol production from LCB. CBP aims to cut high costs associated with traditional fermentation processes (SHF, SSF, SSCF) by integrating enzyme production, hydrolysis and fermentation into one step. However, the technology still remains in the developmental stage due to the lack of robust microorganisms or consortia capable of performing all three functions (enzyme production, hydrolysis and fermentation) simultaneously and efficiently. Though natural microbial communities are highly efficient at degrading LCB, it is difficult to exploit them at industrial scale due to their instability and complexity. By employing synthetic biology tools, scientists may design and also fine-tune the microbial communities capable of enhancing LCB conversion to biofuels. (Troiano and Studer 2025). Opportunities and strategies for producing biobutanol from LCB

Biobutanol has gained significant attention over the last few years as a potential replacement for petrol due to its advantageous properties which include high energy and density, immiscibility with water, less corrosive nature and superior fuel properties (Serrano-Echeverry et al. 2025). It is often considered a superior direct drop-in replacement for ethanol because it can be blended with gasoline at higher ratios without needing specialized or modified engines. The market size of butanol is estimated to be USD 5.8 billion in 2025 and is expected to reach USD 8.6 billion by 2033–2035 owing to its applications in producing butyl acetate, butyl acrylate used for coatings, paints, adhesives, sealants and construction sectors. (https://www.cognitivemarketresearch.com/n-butanol-market-report#tab_order_report).

Presently, the majority of commercial butanol is obtained from petroleum-derived chemicals primarily propylene via the Oxo process where propylene and syngas (CO and H2) are reacted in liquid phase in the presence of cobalt as catalyst yielding a mixture of 75% butanol and 25% isobutanol. This is a high-pressure process with operating pressure of 200–300 kg/cm2 and temperature 100–180 °C. The modified new process is operated at low-pressure (7–20 kg/cm2) using Rhodium (Rh) catalyst which yields about 95% butanol and 5% isobutanol. The process involves the conversion of propylene to butyraldehydes (n-butyraldehyde and isobutyraldehyde) through hydroformylation which are subsequently hydrogenated to butanol and isobutanol. Therefore, butanol production from petroleum is susceptible to fluctuations in the crude oil prices and hence is certainly not a viable alternative both economically and environmentally. Dow Chemicals, BASF SE and Oxea Group (now OQ chemicals) are the biggest players producing butanol using Oxo-process.

Butanol can be produced through acetone-butanol-ethanol (ABE) process which is an anaerobic fermentation using solventogenic bacteria. Clostridial strains convert various carbon sources into acetone-butanol-ethanol mixtures using their metabolic pathways. This process had become an important way for producing acetone during the first World War and the acetone produced via ABE process was used for cordite synthesis (Bunch 2014). Recent reviews provide extensive literature on fermentative butanol production from LCB and also provide the performance indicators to be used as valuable tools for guiding the further research and development in this field with the ultimate aim of making the process economically competitive (Singhvi and Gokhale 2019; Guo et al. 2022; Ganeshan and Tulbek 2025). The technology of biobutanol production is still in its infancy stage due to lack of potent microbes capable of converting LCB. The researchers are focusing on improving the bacterial strains to increase butanol yields and butanol tolerance since butanol toxicity is the major obstacle in commercializing the biobutanol technology. The use of genetic and metabolic engineering, evolutionary engineering and synthetic biology approaches may overcome these limitations to make the biobutanol production technology economically viable (Capilla et al. 2024; Moore et al. 2025). A naturally occurring bacterium Thermoanaerobacterium thermosaccharolyticum TG57 was isolated from mushroom harvesting waste. The strain efficiently converts cellulose into biobutanol through simple fermentation. The process does not require complicated pretreatment or genetic modification of the organism (Ethanol Producer Magazine – The Latest News and Data About Ethanol Production). Gevo and Butamax are the two companies producing biobutanol with Gevo focusing more on isobutanol and related hydrocarbon production. Gevo successfully produced biobutanol from wood waste feedstock which was converted to jet fuel for commercial airlines. The biobutanol produced meets the requirements of the ASTM D7566 standards (https://biomassmagazine.com/articles/gevo-produces-cellulosic-jet-fuel-that-meets-astm-specification-13801). Easel Biotechnologies, LLC developed Bio-Oxo process alternative to chemo-oxo process for producing isobutyraldehyde from corn stover hydrolysate using metabolically engineered E. coli designed to co-utilize both glucose and xylose (https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/1437008). Deletion of specific alcohol dehydrogenases and reductases resulted in minimizing the conversion of isobutyraldehyde to isobutanol by the engineered strain. They claimed that the Bio-Oxo process yielded 56 g/L of isobutyraldehyde in 14L fermenter. Clostridium co-culture systems show promise in improving butanol production compared to monocultures especially dealing with complex substrates such as LCB. Such attempt was made by co-culture of S. cerevisiae and C. acetobutylicum which enhanced butanol production from starch-based media (Capilla et al. 2024). Anaerobic fungi and Clostridium acetobutylicum were used in co-culture which led to increased butyrate and butanol production (Brown et al. 2022). Such co-culture formulations could be the promising strategy for industrial butyrate and butanol production from LCB. The thermal stability of biobutanol producing mesophilic C. beijerinkii was enhanced by protoplast fusion between C. thermocellum and Thermoanaerobacterium saccharolyticum. The fusants produced enhanced levels of butanol (13.82 g/L) at 45 °C compared to 6.25 g/L of butanol produced by their co-culture counterparts. The fusants also produced 68–75 FPU/ml of cellulase which is comparatively more than commercial cellulase (Accelerase 1500) with an activity of 43.21 FPU/ml (Mohtasebi et al. 2021). The use of C1 gases such as carbon monoxide (CO) and carbon dioxide (CO2) is an innovative approach for sustainable butanol production. This method presents significant environmental benefits by utilizing greenhouse gases emitted into the atmosphere which otherwise lead to environmental pollution. The process involves the use of anaerobic, autotrophic bacteria that convert CO and CO2/H2 into intermediate which is further converted to butanol by autotrophic fermentation. Organic co-substrates like sugars or waste materials also can be used along with C1 gases to enhance microbial growth and product yield (Mixotrophic fermentation). Different bacterial cultures (co-culture system) are used in tandem for butanol production from gases via two stage process. One culture produces intermediate acids from gases (CO, CO2 and H2) and the second culture converts these acids into butanol (Du et al. 2020). The viability of co-culture approach was evaluated to produce butanol in mixotrophic conditions. The mixotrophic co-culture fermentation was carried out using C. carboxidivorans and C. beijerinkii which resulted in 7 g/L of butanol at pH 7.0 in presence of Feo (Fernandez-Delgado et al. 2025). Very recently similar attempt was made for production of butanol and other value-added products. The C1 gases (CO and CO2) were converted to 12 g/L of butanol, 2.7 g/L of acetic acid and 7.g/L of butyric acid using C. carboxidivorans and C. beijerinkii in gas-lift bioreactor. The hydrolysate of carrot discard was used as a co-substrate supplemented with Feo (Lopez-Linares et al. 2026).

In addition to LCB pretreatment issues, the primary bottleneck for butanol production is the performance of microbial strain. The main issue is the low butanol tolerance of the strains that leads to low final butanol concentrations (10–16 g/L) which makes the recovery of butanol uneconomical. In addition, Clostridium cultures undergo biphasic metabolism shifting from acidogenesis to solventogenesis. Hence maintaining solventogenic phase to produce butanol without acetone and ethanol is rather difficult. The only solution is to construct the microbes (Clostridium) with enhanced butanol/inhibitor tolerance using genetic and metabolic engineering so that the butanol can be produced at high concentration required for cost-effective recovery by simple distillation. The constructed strain should utilize both sugars glucose and xylose derived from LCB simultaneously with metabolic pathways redirected for higher butanol selectivity. The integrated in situ product recovery to surpass the natural toxicity levels of butanol to fermenting strain would also help in achieving much higher concentration for easy recovery of butanol. The maximum butanol concentration reported in the literature is 113.2 g/L using hyper-butanol producing Clostridium acetobutylicum JB200 from glucose (474.9 g/L) in 326 h. This was achieved using fed-batch fermentation process integrated with intermittent gas stripping butanol recovery. Similar study was performed using cassava bagasse hydrolysate that produced 76.4 g/L of butanol. Very recently, Muharja et al (2026) used co-extraction of butanol and acetone from the lime buffered glucose fermented broth leading to 67.9 g/L of butanol and 34.1 g/L of acetone. They used biocompatible mixture of oleyl alcohol and tributyrin (1:1 v/v) as extractant with extractant to broth ratio of 10 to overcome product inhibition. The study on recyclability of the extractant was not performed which is required for economic assessment of the total butanol production process. Certainly these processes represent a major technical breakthrough which slightly improves the process economics compared to traditional process. However, there are several challenges to be addressed before proving its competitiveness with petrochemical-based butanol production. Economics of the process depends on many factors such as cost of the feedstock, energy inputs required for recovery process, scaling up of the complex process without hurdles in operation.

Conclusions and future perspectives

Global economy still depends on fossil fuels (85%) with a demand of 105 million barrels in 2025 ((https://www.statista.com/statistics/271823/global-crude-oil-demand/). Alternative sources for production of fuels and chemicals to reduce the fossil fuels dependence focus on three renewable biomasses. Conventional first generation biofuels derived from food-based biomass threaten the global food security. First generation biofuels derived from food-based biomass are technologically mature but raise food versus fuel concerns. Non-food-based feedstock such as lignocellulosic biomass (LCB) does not interfere with food and feed supply chain. Abundance of LCB makes it a potential low cost feedstock for production of second generation biofuels. Second generation biofuels derived from LCB are technically mature with technology readiness level 8–9. However, commercial scalability remains constrained by economic barriers and complex pretreatment requirements which inflate the minimum selling price compared to fossil fuels. Though third and fourth generation biofuels represent the future of sustainable energy, they are at early development or pilot stages due to technological and economic challenges. Despite path-breaking discoveries and inventions by NREL and many other institutions engaged in 2G ethanol production, the LCB-based bioethanol technology is still at pilot-plant stage without any commercial and economic dominance. Several challenges remain to be addressed in the economic development of LCB-biorefinery. The key challenges include sustainable biomass supply chain, development of cost-effective biomass pretreatment, optimizing enzymatic hydrolysis and valorization of entire biomass components. In addition, the issues such as transportation of LCB, pests and disease control and the land use contribute to high cost of 2G-ethanol production. Analysis of complex lignocellulosic matrix in various LCB sources is crucial for designing pretreatments. Tailoring specific pretreatment and fractionation technology is essential for deconstructing the structural matrix of LCB. The future of LCB biorefinery depends heavily on overcoming the natural recalcitrant nature of plant cell walls. Hence, efficient and cost-effective pretreatment methods are required to deconstruct the LCB and maximize the sugar yields. In addition, it is essential to substantially reduce the chemicals required for pretreatment to make the biomass processing and wastewater treatment economically and environmentally competitive.

Many biomass degrading enzyme cocktails are available in the market but their high production cost makes the commercial production of cellulosic ethanol economically challenging. Adopting strategies such as on-site enzyme production, enzyme engineering, microbial bioprospecting is essential to reduce the cost of ethanol. Understanding the synergism helps in designing tailor-made enzyme cocktails. Such optimized mixtures lower the biorefinery cost by maximizing the sugar yields and reducing the total dosage required for cellulose hydrolysis. The other challenge is the presence of mixed sugars (glucose and xylose) in the LCB hydrolysates which creates the metabolic bottleneck. This bottleneck can be addressed by forcing or engineering the yeasts to co-consume glucose and xylose. This approach can significantly reduce fermentation time and improve the overall commercial viability of cellulosic ethanol production. Recent reviews provide a comprehensive overview on ABE fermentation and highlight the limitations in large scale butanol production from LCB Commercial scale butanol production (ABE fermentation) still struggles to compete with fossil fuels due to expensive pretreatment, costly enzymes and mainly inefficient microbes. The microbes produce low titers (10–20 g/L) due to butanol toxicity at even low concentrations (0.5% to 2%) which further increase downstream separation cost. The commercial scale biorefineries for LCB-derived butanol are still in the demonstration and development stages. Re-commercializing ABE process using engineered Clostridium strains is expected to overcome these bottlenecks like feedstock costs, low butanol concentration and butanol toxicity to cells.
