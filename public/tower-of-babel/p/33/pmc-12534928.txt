Burden of environmental heat and cold exposure and its attributable risk factors in 204 countries and territories (1990–2021)
Authors: Zhongyong Xie, Zhihua Huang, Wenmin Luo, Wei Du
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEnvironmental heat and cold exposure poses significant public health challenges worldwide. Understanding the global, regional, and national burden of disease, as well as sociodemographic and risk factor associations, is essential for targeted interventions.MethodsThis secondary data analysis utilized age-standardized incidence rates (ASIR), mortality rates (ASMR), and Disability-Adjusted Life Years (DALYs) rates from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021 to assess the health impacts of environmental heat and cold exposure globally from 1990 to 2021. Regional and national variations, as well as age, sex, and Socio-demographic Index (SDI) patterns, were analyzed. Health workforce density data from WHO were incorporated to examine associations with temperature-attributable burden.ResultsIn 2021, 3.4 million incident cases (ASIR: 45.1 per 100,000; 95% UI: 42.3–48.1) and 36,000 deaths (ASMR: 0.4 per 100,000; 95% UI: 0.3–0.5) were attributed to environmental heat and cold exposure worldwide, with 1.68 million DALYs (age-standardized rate: 20.4 per 100,000; 95% UI: 18.9–22.1). From 1990 to 2021, DALYs decreased by 53% (95% UI: 49–57%), highlighting global progress. However, disparities persist, with Central Asia and sub-Saharan Africa exhibiting the highest burden. Mongolia reported the highest national rates (ASIR: 366.4; ASMR: 5.2; DALY rate: 238.1 per 100,000). Men generally faced greater risks than women, particularly in older age groups. Cold exposure contributed 68% of total temperature-related DALYs, with opposing SDI gradients for heat (negative correlation) and cold (positive correlation) burden.ConclusionsWhile global reductions in temperature-attributable burden demonstrate progress, significant regional and national disparities persist, particularly in low-SDI regions. Effective healthcare policies, health workforce strengthening, and targeted interventions are critical to reducing the persistent burden in vulnerable areas.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03263-2.

Introduction

Environmental heat and cold exposure presents a significant public health challenge, exacerbated by the increasing frequency and intensity of extreme temperature events due to global climate change [1]. These exposures are defined as ambient temperatures exceeding the theoretical minimum risk exposure level (TMREL)—the temperature associated with minimum mortality risk for each location [2]. Temperature exposures are associated with a wide range of health outcomes, including direct effects (heat stroke, hypothermia) coded as ICD-10 X30 and X31, and indirect effects through cardiovascular and respiratory pathways, contributing to the global burden of disease [2–4]. Low- and middle-income regions with inadequate infrastructure and healthcare systems bear a disproportionate burden of non-optimal temperature exposure [3]. Recent studies reveal significant disparities across socioeconomic, geographic, and demographic groups, particularly among older adults, residents of low-SDI regions, and men [3, 5, 6].

The mechanisms underlying temperature-related health impacts are multifaceted. Heat exposure causes peripheral vasodilation, dehydration, and increased cardiovascular strain, while cold exposure induces vasoconstriction, increased blood viscosity, and an elevated risk of thrombotic events [7]. These physiological responses are modified by individual factors including age and pre-existing conditions; behavioral risk factors such as alcohol consumption can alter vasomotor control and thermal perception and have been epidemiologically linked to higher heat-related risk, although laboratory evidence on direct thermophysiologic impairment is mixed [8].

Cardiovascular diseases, significantly impacted by non-optimal temperature exposure, contribute to a substantial share of DALYs, particularly in low- and middle-income regions where insufficient adaptation measures exacerbate the burden [3]. This is consistent with findings showing a disproportionately high burden of temperature-related cardiovascular mortality in sub-Saharan Africa and Central Asia and large cerebrovascular burdens in these settings [5, 9]. At the national level, the temperature-attributable stroke burden is notably high in countries such as Mongolia; plausible contributors include extreme continental climate conditions (approximately − 40 °C to + 40 °C), dispersed settlement patterns, and traditional housing with low insulation and limited infrastructure [9–12]. Conversely, regions with robust health systems and comprehensive adaptation strategies, including Western Europe and Australasia, demonstrate much lower burdens [3, 13].

Health system capacity—especially adequate resources and timely accessibility of care—plays a crucial role in mitigating temperature-related health impacts; better access is associated with lower heat-wave mortality [14]. Furthermore, disparities in health outcomes by sex and age persist, with men generally experiencing higher burdens than women until advanced age groups, where women face greater risks due to longer life expectancy and higher prevalence of chronic conditions [5, 6].

This study builds upon prior research by leveraging data from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 study to provide a comprehensive assessment of the global, regional, and national burden attributable to environmental heat and cold exposure [3]. Unlike previous analyses focusing on either heat or cold separately, we examine both exposures simultaneously while incorporating modifying factors, including alcohol consumption and health-resource access [3, 8, 14]. Using data from 204 countries and territories over a 32-year period, this analysis examines trends in incidence, mortality, and DALYs across age, sex, and SDI levels while exploring associations between disease burden and multiple sociodemographic factors through advanced statistical approaches [3].

Methods

Study design

Secondary data analysis of the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021.

Study data

The Global Burden of Disease 2021 study (released March 2023, version 1.1) assessed 376 diseases and injuries and 87 risk factors across 204 countries and territories from 1990 to 2021. Environmental heat and cold exposure were evaluated using the comparative risk assessment framework, which estimates the burden attributable to risk factors by comparing current exposure levels to the theoretical minimum risk exposure level (TMREL). Detailed methodologies and estimates are publicly available (https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbd-compare/ and https://ghdx.healthdata.org/gbd-results-tool, accessed January 15, 2024).

Definition of environmental heat and cold exposure

In the GBD framework, environmental heat and cold exposure encompass health outcomes directly caused by ambient temperatures deviating from the TMREL. The TMREL represents the temperature associated with minimum mortality risk, determined through systematic review and meta-analysis of temperature–mortality relationships for each location. Data extraction and analysis

Data on incident cases, deaths, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) attributable to environmental heat and cold exposure were retrieved using the GBD Results Tool. All estimates included counts and age-standardized rates per 100,000 population with 95% uncertainty intervals (UI), calculated from 1000 draws of the posterior distribution. Age standardization used the GBD 2021 global age structure as the reference population.

Health workforce analysis

Health workforce density data (physicians, nurses, and midwives per 10,000 population) were obtained from the WHO Global Health Observatory (accessed January 20, 2024). Countries were categorized into tertiles based on workforce density to examine associations with temperature-attributable burden.

Results

Global level

In 2021, environmental heat and cold exposure resulted in 3.4 million incident cases globally (95% UI: 3.2–3.6 million), with an age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR) of 45.1 per 100,000 population (95% UI: 42.3–48.1), representing a 37.2% decrease (95% UI: 34.1–40.5%) since 1990. Global deaths totaled 36,000 (95% UI: 31,000–42,000), with an age-standardized mortality rate (ASMR) of 0.4 per 100,000 (95% UI: 0.3–0.5), reflecting a 48.8% decline (95% UI: 44.2–53.1%) over the study period. Disability-Adjusted Life Years (DALYs) reached 1.68 million (95% UI: 1.51–1.87 million), with an age-standardized rate of 20.4 per 100,000 (95% UI: 18.9–22.1), showing a significant 53% decrease (95% UI: 49–57%) from 1990 (Table 1). Decomposition analysis revealed that cold exposure contributed 68% (95% UI: 64–72%) of total temperature-related DALYs globally, with heat exposure accounting for the remaining 32% (95% UI: 28–36%). The EAPC for DALYs was − 2.1% (95% CI − 2.3 to − 1.9%), with joinpoint analysis identifying acceleration in decline after 2003 (EAPC 2003–2021: − 3.2%; 95% CI − 3.5 to − 2.9%).

Regional level

Central Asia exhibited the highest age-standardized incidence rate at 113.6 per 100,000 (95% UI: 98.4–131.2), followed by Eastern Europe (87.3; 95% UI: 76.5–99.8) and Australasia (49.5; 95% UI: 43.2–56.9). The lowest rates were observed in Southeast Asia (23.8; 95% UI: 20.1–28.2) and High-income Asia Pacific (26.9; 95% UI: 23.4–30.8) (Table S1).

For mortality, Central Asia reported the highest ASMR at 1.3 per 100,000 (95% UI: 1.0–1.7), while Australasia, High-income North America, and Western Europe demonstrated the lowest rates at 0.1 per 100,000 (95% UI: 0.08–0.13). The pattern for DALYs showed Eastern Europe with the highest rate (154.5 per 100,000; 95% UI: 135.2–176.8), contrasting with Southeast Asia (4.6; 95% UI: 3.8–5.5) and Australasia (8.4; 95% UI: 7.1–9.9) at the lower end (Tables S2, S3).

Between 1990 and 2021, all regions experienced reductions in age-standardized DALY rates, with the most substantial decreases in Eastern Europe (− 56.1%; 95% UI: − 60.3 to − 51.2%), Baltic states (− 58.4%; 95% UI: − 63.1 to − 52.8%), and Western Europe (− 41.2%; 95% UI: − 45.8 to − 35.9%). Central Asia showed the smallest decline (− 7.0%; 95% UI: − 12.4 to − 1.2%), indicating persistent challenges in managing temperature-related health impacts (Table S3).

National level

Country-level analysis revealed extreme heterogeneity. In 2021, Mongolia reported the highest burden across all metrics: ASIR of 366.4 per 100,000 (95% UI: 312.7–428.5), ASMR of 5.2 per 100,000 (95% UI: 4.3–6.4), and DALY rate of 238.1 per 100,000 (95% UI: 198.4–285.3). This exceptional burden reflects Mongolia’s extreme continental climate, with temperature variations exceeding 80 °C annually, combined with a nomadic population (30%) living in traditional gers with limited temperature control, and sparse healthcare infrastructure (2.8 physicians per 1000 population versus 3.8 in high-income countries).

In contrast, the lowest ASIR was observed in Singapore (17.7 per 100,000; 95% UI: 14.9–21.1), benefiting from near-universal air conditioning coverage (> 95% of buildings) and comprehensive heat-health action plans. Association with sociodemographic index and health systems

Regional analysis revealed a significant positive correlation between SDI and age-standardized DALY rates (Spearman ρ = 0.16; 95% CI 0.08–0.23; p < 0.001), driven primarily by cold-related burden in high-latitude, high-SDI regions. When decomposed, heat-attributable DALYs showed a negative correlation with SDI (ρ = − 0.34; 95% CI − 0.42 to − 0.25; p < 0.001), while cold-attributable DALYs demonstrated a positive correlation (ρ = 0.28; 95% CI 0.19–0.36; p < 0.001).

Countries substantially exceeding SDI-expected burden included Mongolia (observed/expected ratio: 4.2), Afghanistan (3.8), and Kazakhstan (3.1), while Japan (0.3), Singapore (0.4), and Switzerland (0.4) performed better than predicted. Risk factors

Alcohol consumption emerged as a significant modifying factor for temperature-related health impacts. From 1990 to 2021, the proportion of temperature-attributable DALYs with alcohol as a contributing factor decreased slightly in males (5.7%–5.5%) but increased in females (1.3%–1.5%). Regional variation was substantial: Eastern Europe (males: 8.0%; females: 3.2%) and Central Asia (males: 7.9%; females: 2.8%) showed the highest alcohol-attributable proportions in 2021.

Notably, high-income regions demonstrated concerning trends among females, with alcohol-attributable proportions increasing by 38% in North America and 31% in Australasia, reflecting evolving consumption patterns. Discussion

This comprehensive analysis of the global burden attributable to environmental heat and cold exposure from 1990 to 2021 reveals both encouraging progress and persistent challenges [2, 15, 16].

The 53% reduction in global age-standardized DALY rates represents a significant public health achievement, grounded in Global Burden of Disease (GBD) risk-factor methods applied to non-optimal temperature [3, 15].

Improvements in healthcare capacity, heat–health action plans and early-warning systems, and enhanced population awareness are all documented contributors to reduced heat-related health impacts [2, 17].

However, the substantial heterogeneity across regions and countries underscores the complex interplay between climate exposure, socioeconomic development, and health-system capacity [2, 15, 16].

Our finding that cold exposure contributes 68% of temperature-related DALYs aligns with multi-country and global assessments showing that cold still accounts for the majority of temperature-attributable mortality despite warming trends [2, 17].

This cold-dominant pattern reflects both direct hypothermia effects and substantial indirect burden through cardiovascular and respiratory pathways, particularly in aging populations at higher latitudes [17].

Opposing SDI gradients—higher cold burden in higher-SDI settings and higher heat burden in lower-SDI settings—are consistent with GBD risk-factor gradients by development level [3].

Mechanistic insights and regional variations

Mongolia’s exceptional burden exemplifies how extreme continental climate and socioeconomic factors can amplify health risks [2, 18–21].

A sizeable share of households in Ulaanbaatar’s urban settlements live in traditional gers with limited insulation, shaping exposure and indoor environments [21].

Coal-based heating in ger districts drives indoor CO and particulate pollution, which increase cardiopulmonary risk and can interact with temperature stressors [21].

Conversely, the dramatic improvements in the Baltic states—illustrated by Estonia’s post-EU-accession health-system reforms (including nationwide electronic health records and modernized emergency care)—show that multi-sector action can rapidly reduce vulnerability [22, 23]. Housing-efficiency and energy-poverty programs in the EU context are documented to reduce exposure and vulnerability, providing a policy template for transitioning economies [22].

Paradoxical increases in some developing countries likely reflect a vital-registration catch-up (bringing previously uncounted deaths into the system) alongside rapid urbanization that intensifies heat-island exposure before infrastructure catches up [24, 25].

Health workforce as a critical modifier

Across settings, health-workforce density is a strong predictor of health outcomes, often more so than macroeconomic covariates, and is central to climate-resilient response capacity (pre-hospital care, outreach, and management of temperature-exacerbated chronic disease) [19].

The oft-cited 4.45 per 1000 threshold refers to the combined density of doctors, nurses, and midwives needed for UHC/SDG targets, highlighting why investments in people—not matter for adaptation [19].

Countries exemplifying this include Cuba, which maintains very high physician density in the region (≈8 + per 1000) and strong prevention-oriented primary care, and Rwanda, where an extensive community health worker network provides village-level capacity even with limited resources [20, 26, 27].

Alcohol as an emerging concern

Alcohol impairs thermoregulation via peripheral vasodilation, altered thermal perception, dehydration, and reduced heat-production capacity, increasing risks during both heat and cold [8, 28]. Recent US and Canadian data show rising alcohol-related mortality and emergencies, with faster increases among women in recent years, underscoring the need to integrate alcohol messaging into heat/cold advisories [29–32].

Strengths and limitations

Our study uses standardized GBD methodology for non-optimal temperature, enabling valid cross-setting comparisons and building on established risk-function estimation and exposure–response modeling [15].

Model-based estimates can mask local variation, particularly in data-sparse regions, and urban–rural differentiation remains a key evidence gap [15, 16].

Associations between workforce density and temperature-attributable burden are observational and do not establish causality, although the consistency across specifications supports plausibility [19].

Additionally, while GBD 2023 updates are now available, we used 1990–2021 risk-factor series for comparability and will validate findings with newer estimates in future iterations [16].

Conclusions

Global temperature-attributable DALY rates have declined markedly over three decades, but between- and within-country disparities are widening, demanding urgent action [15, 16].

Our results reinforce health-workforce capacity and behavioral risk reduction as actionable levers beyond infrastructure alone [19, 28, 32].

As extremes intensify with climate change, the contrasting trajectories of countries such as Mongolia and Estonia show that policy and investment choices can rapidly change risk [2, 22, 23].

The persistence of cold-related burden alongside emerging heat challenges requires nuanced, context-specific strategies, regional collaboration, and learning from both successes and setbacks [2, 3, 33].
