Bone marrow–derived emergency monopoiesis drives brain–lung axis injury after traumatic brain injury via IL-1
Authors: Tianrui Ma, Jingjing Liu, Zhuyu Gao, Yang Li, Jie Lin, Kaifeng Pang, Rui Li, Xiaoyang Zhang, Lujia Tang, Yafan Liu, Hao Zhang, Yichi Zhang, Lorie Chen Cai, Zijian Zhou, Kaibin Shi, Tao Zhu, Jianning Zhang, Jieli Chen, Zhigang Cai, Ying Fu, Zilong Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTraumatic brain injury (TBI) presents a major clinical burden, often resulting in both acute neurological impairment and pulmonary dysfunction, underscoring the complex and poorly understood brain–lung axis. Although dual-organ injury is well-documented, the mechanistic basis linking brain trauma to peripheral organ damage has remained elusive. In this study, we integrate clinical observations, single-cell RNA sequencing of skull bone marrow (BM) from TBI patients, and preclinical mouse models to uncover a rapid, IL-1-driven emergency monopoiesis response initiated within 24 h post-injury. We demonstrate that TBI induces accelerated, myeloid-skewed hematopoiesis in the cranial BM, marked by the expansion and mobilization of proinflammatory monocytes. This immune activation correlates strongly with both neurological decline and acute lung injury. Mechanistically, we identify IL-1 signaling as a master regulator of this pathological hematopoietic reprogramming. Disruption of IL-1R1 signaling, either globally or selectively within the hematopoietic compartment, attenuates emergency monopoiesis, preserves blood-brain barrier integrity, and reduces inflammatory infiltration in both the brain and lungs. These findings establish emergency monopoiesis as a central driver of multi-organ injury in TBI and reveal the BM niche as a critical yet underappreciated target in post-traumatic systemic pathology. By targeting IL-1R1-dependent hematopoietic activation, this study proposes a novel therapeutic strategy to interrupt the harmful brain–lung immune crosstalk and improve clinical outcomes in acute TBI.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12974-025-03589-y.Keywords: Bone marrow, Interleukin-1, Monopoiesis, Traumatic brain injury, Lung injury

Introduction

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) constitutes a global health crisis, with annual incidence exceeding 50 million cases and acute mortality reaching 46% in affected populations [1]. Clinically, pulmonary complications emerge as the predominant extracranial manifestation, occurring in 81% of severe TBI cases and significantly contributing to deaths related to multi-organ dysfunction syndrome [2–4]. TBI can aggravate alveolar wall damage and interstitial alveolar edema through sympathetic nerve excitation and the release of pro-inflammatory factors [5–7]. Importantly, pulmonary dysfunction can further interact with brain, which is termed as brain-lung axis, by aggravating cerebral hypoxia, dysregulating immunity and amplifying secondary brain damage [8]. Despite strong clinical and experimental supporting the existence of this axis and its association with poor clinical outcomes [7, 9, 10], the underlying mechanisms of TBI-associated brain and lung damage remains poorly understood. Current clinical interventions targeting systemic inflammation, including glucocorticoid receptor agonists, steroidal anti-inflammatory agents, and β-adrenergic blockade such as propranolol, have showed limited clinical efficacy in recent controlled trials [11, 12].

Imbalance in the immune cells compartment is a major contributor to both lung and brain injures post TBI [13]. Monocytes, which are rapidly elevated in peripheral blood within hours after TBI, play a central role in the early inflammatory response. These cells infiltrate the injured brain and also migrate to the lungs, representing a critical early step in the development of both neuroinflammation and acute lung injury after TBI [14, 15]. However, as short-lived innate immune cells, monocytes are rapidly depleted from the circulation following acute injury in order to maintain systematic homeostasis [16–18]. In response, the bone marrow (BM) initiates extensive hematopoietic proliferation to replenish the peripheral pool, a compensatory mechanism commonly observed in acute injuries including TBI [19–22]. Recently, studies have demonstrated sympathetic nervous system drives BM to undergoing emergency hematopoiesis in TBI [23, 24]. In the chronic phase after TBI, mouse BM remains in a perpetually activated state, resulting in biological deficiencies in innate immunity and neurological function [1, 25]. However, the phenotypic and functional alterations of skull BM cells in TBI patients, as well as the potential mechanisms underlying BM-mediated brain-lung axis injury during the acute phase of TBI, remain largely unexplored.

Here we present results from a study designed to test the hypothesis that BM monopoiesis during the hyper-acute phase (4–24 h) contributes to simultaneous development of brain-lung injury after TBI. We collected BM samples from TBI patients and performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analyses to investigate hematopoietic changes and their underlying mechanisms during this critical window. To validate our findings, we utilized in-vivo molecular imaging in a TBI mouse model and employed multimodal imaging techniques to assess the impact of acute BM responses as potential intervention targets for brain and lung axis injury after TBI.

Methods

Human study

Patient studies were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The inclusion of human subjects and supporting documentation were approved by the Ethics Committees of Tianjin Medical University General Hospital (IRB2023-YX-074-01). Informed consent was obtained from all subjects at the time of enrollment. Inclusion criteria for patients with TBI were as follows: (i) Patients are older than 18 years old; (ii) Patients with severe TBI (GCS ≤ 8) who have cerebral herniation and dilated pupils, CT scans reveal significant signs of brain compression, including cerebral contusions, hemorrhaging, brain swelling, cerebral edema, and strokes (evidenced by midline shift and compression of basal cisterns) [26]. (iii) Surgeries were conducted within 24 h post TBI onset. Inclusion criteria for atlanto-occipital deformity patients were as follows: (i) Patients were older than 18 years old. (ii) Patients had no signs of TBI before surgery. Exclusion criteria were inflammation, acute myocardial infarction, heart failure, liver diseases (including liver cirrhosis, fatty liver, and viral hepatitis), tumor, autoimmune diseases, hematological system diseases or any hemodynamic instabilities at the time of consent and any infections before disease onset, soft tissue injury of the chest, rib fractures and concomitant use of antineoplastic or immunomodulating therapies. 8 TBI and 3 control patients were included in the first cohort from the year 2021 to 2024 for single-cell sequencing analysis. The second cohort consisted of 10 control subjects​ and ​27 TBI patients enrolled between ​2022 and 2024 were employed for interleukin-1β (IL-1β) level detection (Supplemental Table S1 and S2).

Mice

All studies were performed in adult male mice of 8 to 10 weeks old. C57BL/6 were purchased from the Vital River Corporation (Beijing, China) and B6. Cg-Gt (ROSA) 26 S, Fgd5-CreERT2 were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME, USA). Fgd5-creER-tdTomato mice were obtained by crossing Fgd5-CreERT2 mice with B6. Cg-Gt (ROSA) 26 S mice. Il1r1−/− mouse were purchased from Cyagen (Suzhou, China). All the mice were maintained under Specific Pathogen Free (SPF) conditions and housed with no more than five animals per cage with standardized light-dark cycle conditions and provided with plenty of water and food.

Mouse FPI model

Mice were subjected to isolated TBI using a fluid percussion injury (FPI) device (Custom Design & Fabrication, Richmond, VA) [27]. Briefly, a mouse was anesthetized with 4% isoflurane in 70% N2O and 30% O2 (0.8 L/min) and remained under anesthesia with 2% isoflurane (0.8 L/min) during TBI. A 3-mm-diameter cranial aperture was precisely created (2.0 mm posterior to the bregma and 2.0 mm lateral to the sagittal suture), exposing the intact dura mater of the mouse brain. A Luer-Lok syringe hub with a hollow structure was subsequently affixed onto the craniotomy window using cranioplastic cement, ensuring optimal fluid transmission and providing necessary reinforcement. Subsequently, the mouse was connected to a FPI device through the syringe hub and subjected to fluid pressure ranging from 15 to 18 psi. Once breathing resumed, the syringe hub was detached, and the wound was stitched and closed immediately. The mouse was placed on a heating pad to maintain body temperature for 30 min after injury and then housed individually in a cage. Sham mice underwent identical procedures without FPI.

Isolation of bone marrow and bone marrow mononuclear cells (BMMCs)

For human samples, skull bone flaps were collected from TBI patients who underwent decompressive craniectomy. BM cells were flushed out from the removed skull bone flaps with RPMI-Medium 1640 and filtered by 50 mm filters. BM cells collected from patients with atlanto-occipital deformity were used as non-TBI controls. BM cells were then centrifuged in Ficoll (Sigma-Aldrich) at 700 g for 30 min at room temperature to collect bone marrow mononuclear cells (BMMCs) for further analysis. For mice samples, mice were anesthetized under isoflurane and then euthanized. Skull and femur were harvested for cell dissociation. BM cells were obtained by rinsing bone marrow from skull and femur with cold 1640 medium (Thermo Fisher). The skull was crushed and the BM from the femur was extracted with 1640 medium, filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer, treated with lysis buffer, washed with PBS and resuspended in 1% BSA for further analysis.

Single-cell sequencing and analysis

Library construction and sequencing of human single-cell RNA-seq

BMMCs of human were captured using the 10X Chromium System (10X Genomics, USA). The single cells were encapsulated into droplets according to the manufacturer’s instructions and the cDNA library was prepared, and subsequently sequenced using Illumina HiSeq PE150 at Novogene (Beijing, China).

ScRNA-seq data processing

The reads of each gene in the scRNA-seq sample of human were quantified against the GRCh38 human reference genome using the Cell Ranger (version 8.0) single-cell software suite. Seurat objects were generated using Seurat 4.4.0, and the quality control criteria were that the number of cell gene expression was greater than 200 and less than 7500, and the proportion of mitochondrial genes was less than 20%. After filtering, 96,262 cells and 27,930 genes were obtained in human samples. NormalizeData was applied for normalization; FindVariableFeatures was used to find high-variable genes, ScaleData was converted to Z-score for PCA dimensionality reduction; RunHarmony was used to remove batch effect; FindNeighbors and FindClusters function were used for cell cluster analysis; RunUMAP was nonlinear dimensionality reduction and visualization.

To integrate data sets from different batches into a shared space for unsupervised clustering, we used the harmony algorithm for batch effect correction. The highly expressed genes in each cluster were identified according to FindAllMarkers, and the top genes were regarded as marker genes. We annotated cell types for all clusters based on known marker genes.

Differentially Expressed Gene (DEG) and pathway enrichment

The differential gene between the disease group and the control group was calculated by Findmarkers with the parameter “min.pct” = 0.25 and “logfc.threshold” = 0.25. GSVA pathway enrichment based on differential genes via C2 set.

Gene set scoring

The gene set scores of each cell were estimated using the AddModuleScore function in Seurat, which calculated the average expression levels of each program (cluster) on single cell level, subtracted by the aggregated expression of control feature sets. All analyzed features are binned based on averaged expression, and the control features are randomly selected from each bin. Inflammation response gene sets are described in supplemental information (Supplemental Table S3).Cell-cell communication analysis

Cell-cell communication analysis

The interaction intensity of information flow between cells was calculated using Cellchat (version 1.6.1). To infer the cell state-specific communications, it first identified differentially expressed signaling genes across all cell groups within scRNA-seq dataset, using the Wilcoxon rank sum test with the significance level of 0.05, and then modeled ligand-receptor mediated signaling interactions using the law of mass action.

The communication between HSPCs and myeloid cells was repeatedly predicted by Nichenet (version 2.0.4). It assessed target genes and ligands with high activity ranking and inferences ligand-to-target signal transduction pathways.

Drug administration

Tamoxifen (Sigma-Aldrich) was dissolved in ethanol/corn oil (1:9) at 30 mg/ml. Fgd5-CreER-tdTomato mice received intraperitoneal injections of 100 µL tamoxifen solution (3 mg per mouse) daily for 5 consecutive days. Fgd5-CreER-tdTomato reporter mice underwent this 5-day tamoxifen regimen prior to TBI induction. Following TBI, mice proceeded to subsequent experimental analyses. Mice administered with tamoxifen but without TBI induction served as the sham control group [28].​

18F-FDG PET imaging

On Day 1 after TBI, we conducted in vivo assessment of BM activity in TBI mice using 18F-FDG PET-CT imaging. The mice were euthanized under isoflurane anesthesia, and then injected with 100 µCi of 18F-FDG via the tail vein. After 1 h, they underwent scanning using a merged micro-PET-CT scanner (Novel Imaging, Beijing). Images were obtained within an 80 cm by 80 cm field of view (160 pixels x 160 pixels) and reconstructed into a three-dimensional projection (336 pixels x 160 pixels x 160 pixels). CT images were used to locate and measure the skull, vertebrae, and femurs on both sides. The radioactivity was quantified using the average standard uptake value (SUV).

Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI)

MRI scanning was performed on a 9.4-T Bruker BioSpec 94/30 USR (Bruker, Karlsruhe, GER) at 1, 3, and 7 day post FPI. T2, T2* map, and contrast enhanced-T1 (CE-T1) were obtained, which were independently analyzed by 2 trained, blinded neurologists in MRI processing. T2 images were acquired by using fast spin-echo pulse sequence with the following parameters: TR = 2802.4 msec, TE = 33 msec, spatial resolution = 78 × 78 µm2, and 0.5 mm thick slices. In this study, the T2* map was acquired using a multi-echo gradient echo pulse sequence with the following parameters: TR = 947.4 msec; TE = 3.5, 8.5, 13.5, 18.5, 23.5, 28.5, 33.5, 38.5 msec; FA = 50°; matrix size = 256 × 256; FOV = 20 × 20 mm2; resolution = 78 × 78 µm2, and 0.5 mm thick slices. Five minutes after intraperitoneal injection of 46.9 mg gadopentetate dimeglumine (Magnevist; Bayer, Guangzhou, CHN), the CE-T1 was conducted to detect contrast enhancement of the cerebral lesion due to BBB breakdown and acquired by using fast spoiled gradient-recalled echo sequence with the following parameters: TR = 200 msec, TE = 3 msec, spatial resolution = 78 × 78 µm2, and 0.5 mm thick slices [29]. The lesion volume change over 7 days post injury was assessed on T2 and CE-T1 scans, which was outlined along the border of the hyperintense signal area. 3D Slicer (version 5.6.2) was used for measurement. As we previously described, R2* values of lesion were acquired by RadiAnt DICOM Viewer (version 2024.1; Medixant, Poznan, Poland) after the T2* map reconstruction [30].

Construction of BM-chimeric mice

BM cells were obtained from the femur of Il1r1−/− (CD45.2+) mice and Il1r1+/+ (CD45.2+) mice. Briefly, BM cells from donor mice were collected and washed with PBS consisting of 2% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS), followed by centrifugation at 300 g for 5 min. Prior to transplantation of BM cells, recipient mice (Il1r1+/+, CD45.2+) were subjected to sublethal irradiation (7 Gy + 4 Gy). Within 24 h after irradiation, BM cells were administered to recipient mice via tail vein injection at a dosage of 1 × 106 BM cells per mouse. Following injection, the mice were housed in recovery cages and provided with water containing antibiotics. Four weeks post-BM cell transplantation, BM chimeric mice underwent FPI to induce a TBI model.

IL-1β quantification

For human, 1 cm x 1 cm size skull bone flap was taken from TBI patients. The skull bone marrow was flushed out with 2 ml cold PBS and centrifuged at 300 g for 10 min at 4℃ to collect supernatant. Peripheral blood was collected from patients and centrifuged at 300 g for 10 min at room temperature to obtain peripheral blood plasma. The content of IL-1β in skull bone marrow supernatant and peripheral blood plasma was detected by using Cytometric Bead Array (CBA) Flex sets (BD Biosciences). For mice, BM supernatants were obtained by rinsing bone marrow from skull with cold PBS. Subsequently, the BM supernatant and plasma were analyzed for the levels of IL-1β using commercial ELISA assays (FineTest, Wuhan, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Lung histopathology and lung injury scoring

Lung sections underwent hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining as previously reported [31]. Two blinded pathologists independently assessed pathology using a semi-quantitative scoring system evaluating five parameters per field: alveolar leukocytes, interstitial leukocytes, hyaline membranes, airspace proteinaceous debris, and alveolar septal thickening. Each parameter was scored with the scores 0 ~ 2: 0 (absent/minimal), 1 (moderate), or 2 (severe) based on predefined thresholds. Scores from all fields were summed to generate total pathology scores per animal, with three randomly selected sections analyzed per mouse and final scores averaged [32].​

Statistical analysis

The animals were assigned to treatment groups based on a set of pre-generated random numbers created using Excel. A two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test was employed to compare data between two independent groups. To compare data from three or more groups with one variable, we used one-way Analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey post hoc test. Associations between cells of BM, BM supernatant, Rotterdam score, Ichikado score, and GCS score were evaluated using Pearson correlation coefficients. For comparing two or more variables among multiple groups, we utilized two-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni post hoc test. Statistical significance was considered at P < 0.05 level. GraphPad Prism 10.0 software was used for data analysis. All results are expressed as mean ± SEM.

Results

Emergency monocytosis in bone marrow are associated with brain and lung injury in TBI patients

To assess alterations of hematopoietic compartments during the hyperacute phase post TBI, we collected skull BM samples from 11 individuals, including 8 TBI patients and 3 non-TBI controls. All 8 TBI patients underwent decompressive craniectomy within 24 h, while the 3 control patients, who had atlanto-occipital deformity as non-TBI controls (Fig. 1A and Supplemental Table S1). Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) was performed on bone marrow mononuclear cells (BMMCs). Following rigorous quality control, a total of 96,262 high-quality cells were acquired and 17 distinct cell clusters were identified (Fig. 1B and S1A), which were subsequently classified into four hematopoietic lineages: hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs), myeloid cells, lymphoid cells, and erythroid cells (Fig. 1B), based on the expression of lineage-specific marker genes (Fig. S1B). Compared to non-TBI controls, TBI patients exhibited distinct alterations in skull BM myeloid cells. Specifically, the proportion of ​monocytes, ​neutrophils, and ​megakaryocyte-erythroid progenitors (MEPs) were elevated, while ​neutrophil progenitors, ​monocyte progenitors, HSPCs, and ​erythroid were reduced (Fig. 1C).

Moreover, we observed a stronger inflammatory response signature in TBI patients, particularly in monocytes, DC and mature neutrophils with significant upregulation of inflammatory genes including ​IL1B, ​IL18, PTGS2 and TLR4 (Fig. 1D and E). Compared to different BM cell populations in controls, ​monocytes in TBI patients demonstrated a markedly elevated ​hallmark inflammatory response AUCell Score, which was the highest among all evaluated cell types (Fig. 1F). Importantly, these monopoiesis in BM significantly correlated with brain injury severity, as quantified by Rotterdam brain CT scores (r = 0.89, p = 0.003) and with lung injury, as measured by Ichikado scores (r = 0.91, p = 0.002, Fig. 1G) [33, 34]. Together, these findings suggest that TBI patients exhibit obvious BM emergency monopoiesis, which are closely associated with brain and lung injury.

Inflammatory signaling, particularly IL-1β, is elevated in bone marrow after TBI and is associated with brain-lung axis injury following TBI

To investigate the mechanisms involved in emergency monocytosis post TBI, we performed CellChat analysis on scRNA-seq datasets and observed a significant increase in IL1B information flow (Fig. 2A). Previous studies have found that inflammatory factors can lead to acute hematopoiesis in the bone marrow, we hypothesized that the emergency monocytosis induced by TBI maybe caused by the pro-inflammatory factors. Several lines of evidence support this hypothesis. First, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) showed that HSPC had a higher degree of inflammatory factor response, such as IL-1, IL-6, TNF and IFN II in TBI patients (Fig. 2B). Second, pseudotime analysis revealed stem progenitor cells of TBI group had higher expression of FOS, JUNB and KLF6, which positively regulated myeloid cell differentiation [35], compared to control group (Fig. 2C).

To validate these findings, we selected IL-1β as a representative inflammatory mediator (Fig. 2A). IL-1β levels were significantly elevated in the skull BM supernatant and peripheral blood plasma of TBI patients within 24 h post-injury (Fig. 2D). Consistently, in the mouse TBI model, IL-1β levels were markedly increased in both skull BM supernatant and peripheral blood plasma at 24 h post-TBI compared to sham controls (Fig. 2E). These results indicate that TBI induces early IL-1β production both in the bone marrow compartment and peripheral blood, potentially contributing to systemic immune activation and inter-organ communication.

We next assessed the clinical relevance of IL-1β in brain and lung injury. Plasma levels of IL-1β were negatively correlated with the Glasgow Coma Scale (GSC) score, and positively correlated with brain and lung injury severity, as measured by Rotterdam and Ichikado score (Fig. 2F). Together, these results demonstrate that inflammatory factors, particularly IL-1β, are markedly elevated in the BM following TBI, and are closely associated with the severity of brain and lung injury.

BM emergency monopoiesis promotes lung and brain injury during the acute phase of TBI in mice

Pulmonary injury occurs within 24 h post-injury in TBI patients (Fig. 1G). Consistent with TBI patients’ experiments, mice subjected to TBI developed lung injury, defined by thickened septa in the alveolar cavity, increased leukocytes infiltration and lung injury score (Fig. S2A and S2B), and worse pulmonary function, detected by increased inspiratory resistance (RL) and expiratory resistance (RE) but decreased dynamic compliance of lung (Cdyn) at days 1 and 3 after TBI, with partial improvement at day 7 after TBI (Fig. S2C). These findings suggest that the types of injuries in human data are similar to those in FPI mice models.

To investigate the role of BM emergency monopoiesis in TBI-induced brain and lung injury, we adopted Fgd5-CreER; tdTomato mouse line, which enables lineage tracing of HSCs and downstream cell populations after TBI (Fig. 3A). We observed a significant proliferation of BM cells in skull within 24 h after TBI, with a majority of these expanded cells exhibiting tdTomato fluorescence indicating HSCs (Lineage−c-Kit+Sca-1+CD48−CD150+) origin (Figs. 3B and 4D). Immune cells in both skull and femur BM, including tdTomato+ neutrophils (CD45+CD11b+Ly6G+)​​ and tdTomato+ monocyte (CD45+CD11b+Ly6G−F4/80−) were notably enriched and skewed toward pro-inflammatory phenotypes (Figs. 3C and 4I, S3A and S3B).

Flow cytometry detected an increase infiltration of tdTomato+ macrophages into the brain, including both pro-inflammatory (tdTomato+Ly6c+) and anti-inflammatory (tdTomato+Ly6c−) subtypes, along with tdTomato⁺ neutrophils in TBI mice (Fig. 3D). Consistent with the finding, we further detected a significant accumulation of BM-derived pro-inflammatory macrophages in both brain injury region and lungs of TBI mice, compared to sham control mice. Notably, BM-derived anti-inflammatory macrophages were increased in the brain, but not in the lungs (Fig. 3E-H). Neutrophils (CD45+CD11b+Ly6G+) and monocytes infiltrated in brain (CD45+CD11b+Ly6G−F4/80−) and lung (CD45+Ly6G−SiglecF−F4/80+CD11b+) tissues exhibited significantly elevated IL-1β expression.​​ (Fig. 3I and J). These results indicate that emergency monopoiesis in the BM gives rise to inflammatory monocyte and macrophage that are mobilized from BM niche to the lungs and brain, and they contribute to acute injury following TBI.

Inhibition of emergency myelopoiesis through global Il1r1 deficits attenuates brain and lung injury post-TBI

To further test the direct impact of IL-1 signaling in BM emergency monopoiesis and its contribution to brain and lung injury post TBI, we studied the TBI on IL-1R1 deficient mice [36] (Il1r1−/−, Fig. 4A). Positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET-CT) revealed significantly reduced metabolic activity in skull, vertebra and femur BM of Il1r1−/− mice subjected to TBI compared to Il1r1+/+ mice (Fig. 4B and C and S4A). This finding indicates that TBI induced systemic metabolic alterations in the bone marrow microenvironment across different skeletal compartments, including flat bones (skull), irregular bones (vertebrae), and long bones (femur) as shown in Fig. 4B. These changes were significantly attenuated in Il1r1−/− mice, as quantitatively demonstrated in Fig. 4C.

Additionally, flow cytometric analysis revealed that LSK cells (Lin−Sca-1+c-Kit+) and HSCs (LSK CD150+CD48−) were significantly increased in both skull and femur BM of Il1r1+/+ mice at day 1 post-TBI (Fig. 4D, E and G, S4B and S4D). Subsequently, their downstream myeloid progenitors including CMPs​ (Lin−Sca−1−c-Kit+CD34+CD16/32−), ​GMPs (Lin−Sca-1−c-Kit+CD34+CD16/32+)​, and ​MDPs ​(Lin−Sca-1−c-Kit+CD34+CD16/32+CD115+), were observed in both bone compartments (Fig. 4F and H, S4C and S4E). These TBI-induced changes were significantly blunted in Il1r1−/− mice, suggesting a critical role for IL-1 signaling in driving emergency hematopoiesis post-TBI. Furthermore, the proliferation of monocytes (CD45+CD11b+Ly6G−F4/80−) and Ly6C+ monocytes in skull and femur marrow was significantly reduced in Il1r1−/− mice compared to Il1r1+/+ controls (Fig. 4I and K). Collectively, these results indicate that IL-1 signaling mediates emergency hematopoiesis and promotes monopoiesis in the BM during the acute phase of TBI.

To determine whether attenuating BM emergency monopoiesis through global IL1 receptor 1 deficiency confers therapeutic benefits in TBI, we evaluated brain and lung injury outcomes. First, Il1r1−/− mice subjected to TBI exhibited significantly decreased infiltration of pro-inflammatory macrophages (CD68+CD86+) in both brain and lung, as detected by immunofluorescent staining compared to Il1r1+/+ mice (Fig. 5A and B, S5A and S5B). However, the number of anti-inflammatory macrophages (CD68+CD206+) remained comparable between Il1r1+/+ and Il1r1−/− mice (Fig. 5A and B, S5A and S5B). Second, flow cytometry analysis revealed the total number of macrophages, neutrophils and pro-inflammatory Ly6c+ macrophages in the brain were increased in Il1r1+/+ mice (Fig. 5C and D and S5C), and this was significantly reduced in Il1r1−/− mice (Fig. 5C and D). Third, neuroimaging assessments demonstrated that Il1r1−/− mice subjected to TBI developed smaller brain lesion volume as evidenced by decreased T2-weighted (T2) signal, diminished blood-brain-barrier (BBB) leakage identified by decreased Contrast-enhanced T1 -weighted (CE-T1) signal, and less brain iron deposition alongside restored of cerebral blood volume (CBV) defined by decreased T2* mapping signal quantified by R2*, compared to Il1r1+/+ mice (Fig. 5E and F and S5D). Fourth, Il1r1−/− mice developed significantly improved neurological outcomes, measured by mNSS, and had improved motor function measured by rotarod and corner test (Fig. 5G and S5E-S5G). Additionally, lung 18F-FDG uptake was decreased in Il1r1−/− mice relative to Il1r1+/+ mice, reflecting decreased pulmonary metabolic activity (Fig. 5H). Histopathology and respiratory function tests revealed that Il1r1−/− mice subjected to TBI exhibited ​reduced leukocytes infiltration and lung injury score (Fig. 5I and J)​, along with ​decreased inspiratory resistance (RL), expiratory resistance (RE), and increased dynamic compliance (Cdyn) on day 3 after TBI (Fig. 5K)​. These results indicate that inhibition of inflammatory-derived emergency BM monopoiesis via global Il1r1 deficiency mitigates both brain and lung injury following TBI.

Hematopoiesis-specific loss of Il1r1 alleviated brain and lung injury post-TBI

To further investigate the direct role of BM IL1 signaling to brain and lung injury post-TBI, we generated BM chimeric mice harboring Il1r1−/− or Il1r1+/+ BM cells. Femur BM cells from Il1r1−/− (CD45.2) or Il1r1+/+ (CD45.2) mice were isolated and transplanted into non-lethally irradiated CD45.1 recipient mice via the tail vein injection (Fig. 6A). Following TBI, mice receiving Il1r1−/− BM exhibited significantly reduced infiltration of CD45.2+ pro-inflammatory macrophages, anti-inflammatory macrophages, neutrophils and total macrophages into the brain (Fig. 6B C and S6A). Consistently, immunofluorescence staining showed that Il1r1−/− BM transplantation prevented the TBI-induced pro-inflammatory macrophages infiltration in both brain and lungs (Fig. 6D and E, S6B and S6C), relative to those receiving Il1r1+/+ BM transplantation. MRI analysis revealed that mice received Il1r1−/− BM cells had significantly decreased cerebral lesion volumes at 7 days post TBI, along with reduced BBB leakage and brain iron accumulation, and restored CBV as detected by CE-T1 and T2*mapping at 3 days post-TBI (Fig. 6F and G and S6D). Functionally, mice reconstituted with Il1r1−/− BM demonstrated significantly improved neurological behavioral and motor performance, as measured by mNSS, rotarod, and corner test at 3 days post-TBI compared to mice received wild type (WT) BM (Fig. 6H, and S6E-S6G).

Histopathology examination of lung tissue further showed that Il1r1−/− BM recipients had markedly reduced infiltration of inflammatory cells, decreased alveolar wall thickness and reduced lung injury score (Fig. 6I). Furthermore, ​IL-1β expression levels were significantly reduced​ in ​monocytes and neutrophils that infiltrated​​ in brain or ​lung tissues of the TBI mice following Anakinra administration (Fig. 7E and F). Discussion

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) often results in multi-organ dysfunction, with acute brain and lung injury being major contributors to poor outcomes and mortality. Here, we identify emergency monopoiesis—as a key pathological driver of secondary brain-lung axis injury following TBI. Our study integrates clinical observations with mechanistic validation in mouse models, revealing a critical early intervention window within 24 h post-injury during which pathological hematopoietic responses are initiated.

We report several novel findings that together establish a causal link between dysregulated emergency monopoiesis and TBI-induced brain and lung injury. TBI patients demonstrated rapid activation of emergency monopoiesis in the skull BM. Moreover, immune profiling of the skull BM revealed expansion of innate immune cells with proinflammatory phenotypes (Fig. 1D and F), suggesting that the BM response is not merely reactive but actively pathogenic. Another significant finding is the strong association between emergency monopoiesis and the extent of brain and lung injury following TBI. This was demonstrated by two major observations: (1) the number of monocytes in skull BM were associated with both brain and pulmonary injury severity (>Fig. 1G), and (2) consistent with the TBI patients’ findings, we also detected mice subjected to TBI developed brain and lung injury (Fig. S2A-S2C and 5). Inflammatory monocyte/macrophage originating from BM were mobilized to both lung and brain, where they contribute to TBI-induced brain and lung injury (Fig. 3).

Several mechanisms may underline this pathological cascade. First, TBI induces increased production of proinflammatory innate immune cells within the BM (Fig. 1B and F). These hyperactive immune cells subsequently infiltrate both the brain and lung, where they mediate pathological destruction through excessive inflammatory responses(Figs. 1 and 3). Second, emergency monopoiesis leads to the massive release of inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β (Fig. 3I and J), contributing to acute lung injury [37–39]. Notably, this myeloid skewing occurs at the expense of dendritic cell development, thereby compromising local immune regulation and making the lungs more vulnerable to secondary infections, such as Streptococcus pneumoniae following influenza virus infection [40, 41].

Initial single-cell sequencing of skull BM specimens from TBI patients revealed heightened inflammatory cytokine gene activity, including IL-1B, TNF, IFN II, all of which are known to orchestrate emergency monopoiesis. Among these, IL-1 signaling emerged as the most dynamically regulated inflammatory pathway in the cranial BM niche following TBI. We observed elevated levels of IL-1β were detected in the BM after TBI, implicating it as a key upstream driver of both emergency hematopoiesis and monopoiesis. This notion is supported by several key findings: (1) TBI activated the hematopoietic system through IL-1β signaling, leading to up-regulation of downstream myeloid cell differentiation transcription factors (FOS, JUNB, KLF6) in HSPCs (Fig. 2C). HSCs exposed to acute IL-1β demonstrate enhanced proliferation and myeloid differentiation mediated by the adaptor protein MyD88 and various signaling pathways including NF-κB and PU.1; [42–44] (2) IL1R1-deficient (Il1r1⁻/⁻) mice showed markedly reduced BM-derived cells and diminished monopoiesis, indicating the necessity of IL-1 signaling for the pathological hematopoietic response observed post-TBI (Fig. 4). While prior studies have linked IL-1 signaling to emergency hematopoiesis in trauma settings [45], it remained unclear whether and how the described immunomodulatory effects of TBI translated into aggravating brain and lung injury.

Our current findings address this gap by demonstrating that IL-1 dependent emergency monopoiesis is a key contributor to TBI-indued brain-lungs axis injury. In both Il1r1−/− mice and BM chimeric models subjected to TBI, we observed significantly reduced inflammatory cellular infiltration in the brain and lungs (Figs. 5A and D and 6B and E). Moreover, these mice exhibited reduced BBB leakage and less lesions around the TBI core, as visualized by multimodal imaging (Figs. 5E and F and 6F and G). Additionally, PET imaging, histopathological and respiratory function tests analysis revealed marked mitigation of lung injury in these mice following TBI (Figs. 5H and K and 6I), further supporting the systemic impact of IL-1-driven hematopoietic dysregulation. These data establish IL-1 signaling as a central regulator of emergency monopoiesis and a therapeutically actionable link between bone marrow activity and secondary organ damage in TBI.

These findings highlight a critical opportunity to develop more effective therapeutic strategies for managing TBI-induced brain and lung injury. Although suppressing the immune response can effectively reduce neuroinflammation after acute brain injury, it frequently compromises pulmonary immunity, increasing susceptibility to infection and worsening the risk of pneumonia [10, 11]. Similarly, sympathetic nerve stimulation, while previously shown to modulate BM responses and alleviate brain injury [23, 28, 46], has also been reported to exacerbates lung injury by disrupting the alveolar-capillary membrane and increasing vascular permeability [7, 47, 48]. Given the dual vulnerability of the brain and lungs in TBI, our data support a targeted translational strategy that avoids immunosuppression and instead focuses on selectively inhibiting pathological emergency monopoiesis through BM–specific IL-1 signaling blockade. By intervening at the level of the hematopoietic niche, it may be possible to attenuate neuroinflammation and brain injury while simultaneously preserving pulmonary immune defenses and reducing systemic complications. This precision approach offers a promising avenue for improving outcomes in acute TBI patients with multi-organ involvement.

There are, however, limitations that warrant consideration. Our patient cohort, while well-characterized, remains relatively small, and larger studies are needed to confirm these associations. Moreover, while IL-1β was the focus of our mechanistic exploration, other inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6, TNF, IFN-γ) may also contribute to emergency monopoiesis in TBI and should be evaluated in future studies. Finally, the long-term fate and functional role of BM-derived immune cells in chronic neurodegeneration post-TBI remains to be fully elucidated.

In summary, our study demonstrates that emergency monopoiesis is a key pathological driver of TBI-induced brain and lung injury, primarily through the mobilization and accumulation of proinflammatory monocytes and macrophages from the bone marrow niche to peripheral organs. These insights not only advance our understanding of the brain-lung axis in TBI but also identify novel therapeutic targets, particularly within the bone marrow microenvironment, for the prevention and treatment of TBI-associated multi-organ injury.
