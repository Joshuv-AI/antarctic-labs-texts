AI in peer review: can artificial intelligence be an ally in reducing gender and geographical gaps in peer review? A randomized trial
Authors: André L Teixeira
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGender and geographical disparities have been widely reported in the peer-review process of biomedical journals. Artificial Intelligence (AI) is increasingly transforming the publishing system; however, its potential to identify suitable reviewers, and whether it might reduce, replicate or reinforce existing biases in peer review has never been comprehensively investigated. This study sought to determine the usefulness of AI in identifying expert scientists in medicine taking into consideration gender and geographical diversity, equity and inclusion (DEI).MethodsThe title and abstract of 50 research articles published in high-impact biomedical journals between November 2023 and September 2024 were fed into a large language model software (GPT-4o), which was prompted to identify 20 distinguished scientists in the study’s field. Two trials were randomly performed with and without a gender and geographical DEI prompt. Scientists were classified based on gender, geographical location, and country of affiliation income level. Furthermore, the number of peer-reviewed publications, Google Scholar-derived total citations and h-index were computed.ResultsWithout a DEI prompt, GPT-4o primarily identified male scientists (68%) and those affiliated to high-income countries (95.3%). Conversely, when DEI was explicitly prompted, GPT-4o generated a gender-balanced (51% females) and geographically diverse list of scientists. Specifically, the proportion of scientists from high-income countries decreased to 42.3%, while representation from upper-middle (3.2% to 26.2%), lower-middle (1.2% to 26.1%), and low-income (0.2% to 5.4%) countries significantly increased. The number of publications (without vs. with DEI: 284 ± 237 vs. 281 ± 245, P = 0.77), citations (48,445 ± 60,270 vs. 53,792 ± 71,903, P = 0.13), and h-index (79 ± 43 vs. 76 ± 43, P = 0.15) did not differ between groups.ConclusionsWhen not prompted to consider DEI, GPT-4o successfully identified expert scientists, but primarily males and those from high-income countries. However, when DEI was explicitly prompted, GPT-4o generated a gender-balanced and geographically diverse list of scientists. The academic productivity was considerably high and comparable between groups, suggesting that GPT-4o identified potentially skilled scientists who could reasonably serve as reviewers for scientific journals. These findings provide evidence that AI can be an ally in combating gender and geographical gaps in peer review, though DEI should be explicitly prompted. Introduction

The peer-review process is crucial for ensuring the integrity and quality of scientific literature [1, 2]. However, gender and geographical disparities, including the underrepresentation of females, ethnic minorities, and scientists from lower-income and non-Western countries, have been widely reported in the peer review of high-impact biomedical journals [3–10]. Indeed, the majority of peer reviewers of The British Medical Journal, Lancet, Nature, and The New England Journal of Medicine are males and affiliated with institutions in high-income countries, especially in North America and Europe [3–10]. Consequently, effective strategies to enhance gender and geographical diversity, equity, and inclusion (DEI) in the reviewer pool of scientific journals are urgently needed.

While peer review is at the heart of the publishing system, securing expert reviewers has become increasingly difficult over the past decades [11, 12], making the role of associate editors both challenging and time-consuming. Prior reports demonstrated that only ~35% of invited reviewers agree to review a manuscript for major biomedical journals [9, 13]. Consequently, automated tools such as the Clarivate Reviewer Locator and the Elsevier EVISE Reviewer Finder have been developed to assist associate editors in identifying suitable reviewers across different scientific fields [14, 15]. However, to the best of the author’s knowledge, these automated tools and algorithms are not designed to consider gender and geographical DEI in their suggested reviewer pool.

Artificial Intelligence (AI), especially large language models (LLMs), has been extensively used in science and medicine [16–23]. In the context of journal peer review, AI technologies have been explored for several tasks, including (but not limited to) accelerating the time to secure reviewers, providing initial quality control of submitted manuscripts, detecting plagiarism, generating review reports and recommendations, and assessing the quality of peer reviews [15, 24–28]. Given its ability to process, synthesize, and generate complex text-based information, it is reasonable to suggest that AI has the potential to enhance DEI in science. For example, Bardejo-Espinola and Amano [29] suggested that AI can aid non-native English speakers in improving their writing and readability skills. However, whether AI could be employed to identify expert reviewers in different medical fields while accounting for gender and geographical DEI is currently unknown. Noteworthy, AI technologies are trained in existing data and given the well-established gender and geographical inequalities in the publishing system [30, 31], it is plausible to hypothesize that AI could replicate existing bias. Nevertheless, the extent to which AI could reduce, replicate, or reinforce historical bias in peer review has never been comprehensively investigated.

Therefore, the present study sought to determine the usefulness of AI in identifying expert scientists in medicine while accounting for gender and geographical DEI. It was hypothesized that: (i) when not specifically prompted to consider gender and geographical DEI, AI models would primarily identify male scientists affiliated with high-income countries, especially North America and Europe; (ii) when explicitly prompted to consider gender and geographical DEI, AI would generate a gender-balanced and geographically diverse list of experts from both low-, middle- and high-income countries; and (iii) the academic metrics would be comparable between the groups with and without a DEI prompt. In addition, a secondary aim was to assess the repeatability of the method by determining whether AI would consistently suggest the same list of scientists across multiple independent trials. Given that LLMs are inherently generative rather than deterministic [32], it was hypothesized that the repeatability would be limited.

Methods

Study design and data selection

This study employs a cross-sectional design to assess the ability of an advanced AI language model to identify expert reviewers in different medical fields. For this, a sample of 50 research articles published in five different (10 per journal) high-impact medical journals (i.e., British Medical Journal, Journal of the American Medical Association, Nature Medicine, Lancet, and New England Journal of Medicine) was randomly selected (https://www.randomizer.org). Eligible manuscripts included both original and review articles published between November 2023 and September 2024, regardless of research topic, author affiliation or gender. Although all procedures were conducted unblinded by a single researcher., the article selection and reviewer name generation were fully automated using randomization software and AI, respectively, minimizing subjective risk of bias. The research topics covered a wide range of medical fields including, but not limited to, vaccination, Parkinson’s disease, Covid-19, chronic kidney disease, dyslipidemias, pregnancy, breast cancer, physical activity, hypertension, diabetes, obesity, nutrition, and smoking. The title and abstract of each study were fed into an advanced natural language processing model software (Generative Pretrained Transformer 4 Omni [GPT-4o], OpenAI; https://openai.com), which was prompted to provide a list of 20 expert scientists in the study’s field. Each study randomly underwent two analyses where GPT-4o was prompted with and without a specific request to consider gender and geographical DEI (Table 1). The rationale for requesting 20 names per article was based upon the fact that only ~35% of reviewers agree to review manuscripts for high-impact biomedical journals [9, 13] and, although the number of reviewers varies considerably within and between journals, most biomedical journals use multiple (≥ 3) reviewers per article, with 15–20 individuals often invited to achieve this target [13].

Gender determination

Following the task completion by GPT-4o, the gender of each scientist was determined using a modified sequential four-step model [9, 33]. Firstly, the scientist’s institutional or personal website was accessed where their gender was determined according to the pronouns or salutations used to identify them, attributing male to “he”, “him”, “his”, and “Mr”, and female to “she”, “her”, “hers”, “Miss”, “Mrs”, and “Ms”. If the gender was not determined based on the pronouns or salutations, the individual’s first or middle name, as well as the country of born (if identifiable) were used to search on Gender API (https://gender-api.com/en) and genderize.io (http://genderize.io) websites, where an accuracy above 80% was considered acceptable for gender determination [9, 33]. If none of these methods determined the gender, it was recorded as undetermined.

Geographical location and income level

The geographical location of each individual was extracted based on the country of their institutional affiliation and classified into one of six groups (Africa, Asia, Europe, North America, Oceania or South America). Furthermore, the wealth of the country was classified into one of four levels (low income, lower middle income, upper middle income, and high income) according to the World Bank Atlas method 2023–2024 [34].

Academic metrics

To provide evidence of the academic productivity and potential impact of each scientist, the total number of published articles in PubMed, and Google Scholar-derived total number of citations and h-index (if available) were computed. Furthermore, individuals were clustered into six categories based on the h-index range (< 10, 11–25, 26–50, 51–75, 76–100, and > 100).

Repeatability analysis

A repeatability sub-analysis was conducted to assess whether GPT-4o would consistently provide the same names across multiple trials. For this, 10 of 50 manuscripts from the original analysis were randomly selected, and four independent trials were performed for each (i.e., two with and two without a DEI prompt). The gender, geographical location and academic metrics of each scientist were extracted as previously described.

Statistical analysis

The normality of all variables was confirmed using the Komolgorov-Smirnof test. Chi-square tests were used to determine group differences in the proportion of scientists according to gender, geographical location, and country of affiliation income level. The academic metrics (i.e., number of publications, citations, and h-index) were compared between groups using independent sample t-tests. For the repeatability analysis, the proportions of identical and different names that was generated across the two trials for each group (i.e., without DEI Trial 1 vs. Trial 2, and with DEI Trial 1 vs. Trial 2), were compared using chi-square tests. Comparisons between the academic metrics of scientists across all trials were performed using 2 x 2 repeated measures ANOVA, where group (with vs. without a DEI prompt) and condition (Trial 1 vs. Trial 2) were the main factors. Analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS 26 (SPSS, Chicago, IL). Data are presented as means ± standard deviation (SD) unless otherwise stated, and statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

GPT-4o successfully generated a list of 20 names per trial, comprising a total of 2,000 names (i.e., 1,000 per group). Of these, five names could not be identified as scientists (one from the group without DEI and four from the group with DEI), and 14 of the identified scientists were deceased (11 from the group without DEI and three from the group with DEI). These individuals were therefore excluded from the data analysis. As a result, the final sample comprised 1,979 scientists (986 without and 993 with a DEI prompt). The gender, geographical location, and total number of publications were successfully identified for all 1,979 scientists. However, only 1,427 of these individuals (653 without and 774 with a DEI prompt) had a Google Scholar research profile and were therefore included in the analyses of total number of citations and h-index.

Figure 1 shows the geographical distribution, country wealth, gender, and the Google Scholar-derived h-index of the scientists generated by GPT-4o with and without a DEI prompt. Without DEI, the vast majority of scientists identified by GPT-4o were male (68% vs. 32% female), and affiliated with high-income countries, primarily North America (60%) and Europe (28%), with the United States alone accounting for 56%. Additionally, the number of scientists affiliated with institutions in Africa (0.7%), Asia (4.9%), Oceania (4.7%), and South America (1.2%) was extremely low, and only two scientists were affiliated with low-income countries. Conversely, when gender and geographical DEI was explicitly prompted, the list of scientists was gender balanced (51% females and 49% males) and geographically diverse. Specifically, the proportion of scientists affiliated with high-income countries decreased from 95.3% to 42.3%, while scientists from upper-middle (3.2% to 26.2%), lower-middle (1.2% to 26.1%), and low-income (0.2% to 5.4%) countries significantly increased. In addition, the proportion of scientists affiliated with North America (60% to 19%) and Europe (28% to 18%) reduced, while representation from Africa (0.7% to 21.3%), Asia (4.9% to 26.4%) and South America (1.2% to 11.8%) increased. The number of scientists affiliated with Oceania remained relatively stable (4.7 without vs. 3.0% with a DEI prompt). Chi-square tests revealed significant group differences in the proportion of scientists by gender (x2 = 7.75, P = 0.005), geographical location (x2 = 66.4, P < 0.001), and country of affiliation income level (x2 = 65.9, P < 0.001). The total number of countries represented increased from 45 (without a DEI prompt) to 103 (with a DEI prompt). A detailed overview of the number of scientists from each country provided by GPT-4o with and without a DEI prompt is presented in Supplementary Table 1. Noteworthy, the time required by GPT-4o to complete each trial was similar between tasks without (21 ± 10 s) and with (25 ± 13 s) a DEI prompt.

The repeatability analysis demonstrated that GPT-4o generated the same scientists in 43% of the cases without a DEI prompt and in 19% of the cases with a DEI prompt. This difference was statistically significant (x2 = 13.46, P < 0.001), indicating that incorporating gender and geographical DEI significantly reduces the repeatability of the model. Figure 3 shows that the gender and geographical distribution closely mirrored the overall pattern observed in the original analysis. Specifically, without a DEI prompt, the majority of scientists were males (Trial 1: 66% vs. Trial 2: 67%) and affiliated with institutions from high income countries (Trial 1: 95% vs. Trial 2: 94%), particularly North America (Trial 1: 63% vs. Trial 2: 60%) and Europe (Trial 1: 28% vs. Trial 2: 27%). In contrast, following a DEI prompt, the pool of scientists became gender balanced (Trial 1: 52% females vs. Trial 2: 51% females) and geographically diverse. In fact, affiliations were better distributed across high (Trial 1: 51% vs. Trial 2: 49%), upper middle (Trial 1: 23% vs. Trial 2: 22%), lower middle (Trial 1: 21% vs. Trial 2: 25%), and low income (Trial 1: 5% vs. Trial 2: 5%) countries. Additionally, the proportions of scientists affiliated with North America and Europe significantly decreased, while representation from Africa, Asia, Oceania and South America consistently increased in both trials (Fig. 3). The total number of published articles (without DEI Trial 1: 326 ± 238 vs. Trial 2: 327 ± 238; with DEI Trial 1: 318 ± 216 vs. Trial 2: 321 ± 218; P > 0.05), citations (63,069 ± 78,359 vs. 68,842 ± 81,820 vs. 65,150 ± 75,282 vs. 75,511 ± 82,267, P > 0.05) and h-index (95 ± 44 vs. 99 ± 43 vs. 84 ± 37 vs. 87 ± 39, P > 0.05) were comparable across all groups and trials.

Discussion

This is the first study to explore the utility of AI in aiding associate editors to identify expert reviewers in different medical fields taking into consideration gender and geographical DEI. In accordance with the primary hypothesis, when not prompted to consider DEI, GPT-4o primarily identified male scientists and those affiliated with high-income countries, especially North America and Europe. In contrast, a DEI prompt led to a gender-balanced and geographically diverse list of scientists. The academic metrics were comparably high in both groups, demonstrating that GPT-4o provided a list of potentially skilled scientists in their respective medical fields that could have been considered to participate in the peer-review process of a biomedical journal. Collectively, these findings suggest that, if wisely managed, AI has the potential to serve as a valuable ally in reducing gender and geographical disparities in peer review.

The pros and cons of AI in science and medicine have been broadly discussed elsewhere [21–23, 35–44]. Despite ongoing concerns, such as the increased risk of plagiarism, misinformation, and lack of transparency, there is no doubt that AI technologies are reshaping both academic publishing and clinical practice. The exponential growth of AI has prompted top-tier medical journals to publish specific guidelines on their use in research and publication [45]. Regarding DEI, Bardejo-Espinola and Amano [29] suggested that AI tools may help overcome language barriers faced by non-native English speakers when writing scientific manuscripts [29]. Conversely, von Wedel et al. [46] found that AI-assisted reviews tended to favor medical abstracts from renowned institutions over those from “low-tier” institutions, highlighting that AI may replicate existing affiliation bias in peer review. Furthermore, by using AI technologies to evaluate peer reviewers’ reports, Verharen [47] found that although the vast majority of comments were polite, manuscripts with female first authors were more likely to receive less polite comments than manuscripts with male first authors. More recently, Chan et al. [48] explored several strategies to establish equity in AI tools as a means to reduce bias in healthcare. The present study adds to this growing body of knowledge by demonstrating, for the first time, that AI can be a useful tool for reducing gender and geographical disparities in peer review. However, such balance was only achieved when gender and geographical DEI was explicitly highlighted in the prompt. While the factors contributing to gender and geographical inequalities in peer review are complex and not fully understood, several biomedical journals, scientific societies, and publishing companies have consistently advocated for and reinforced their commitment to improving DEI in science [49–57]. Nevertheless, original studies investigating the effectiveness of these strategies are still lacking.

Reviewing manuscripts is often considered extra work and is not formally recognized or rewarded as part of a researcher’s job [58]. Barnett et al. [59] found that peer reviews were more likely to be submitted during leisure time (i.e., weekends, holidays, or outside typical work hours) when compared to manuscript submissions, a pattern that has remained consistent over time. Additionally, not only has the number of manuscripts submitted to specialized biomedical journals increased exponentially, but recruiting expert reviewers has become harder over the past decades [11, 12]. Indeed, it has been shown that > 60% of invited reviewers declined to review a manuscript for major biomedical journals [9, 13]. On top of that, the implementation of effective strategies to enhance DEI in the journal reviewer pool often falls almost entirely on the shoulders of the associate editor. Some may argue that associate editors should focus solely on scientific expertise when selecting reviewers, without being expected to consider DEI. However, a lack of DEI in the reviewer pool may, in fact, make the task of finding suitable reviewers even more difficult [12]. Therefore, providing an automated and reliable tool to identify potentially skilled reviewers while also accounting for gender and geographical DEI could be a valuable resource for handling editors. The findings of the present study suggest that AI can assist editors in identifying potential reviewers in different medical fields. However, a small proportion of the names provided by GPT-4o where either untraceable or belonged to individuals who had passed away. Consequently, while AI can bring substantial benefits in streamlining the reviewer selection, it should be used with caution, considering data privacy, accuracy, and the continuous requirement for editorial oversight. Ultimately, AI should be viewed as a complement to, rather than a replacement for, human judgment in the peer-review process.

The repeatability sub-analysis revealed limited consistency of the model, particularly when DEI prompts were included. Given that LLMs are fundamentally generative rather than deterministic [32], this finding is not entirely surprising. Generative models learn and sample from a probability distribution to produce multiple plausible outputs for the same input, whereas deterministic models consistently yield identical outputs without stochastic variation [32]. While this variability could be viewed as a methodological limitation, it may also represent a strength of the model, as performing multiple trials could yield a broader and even more diverse pool of potential reviewers worldwide. Notably, the academic metrics remained comparable across all trials and conditions, indicating that despite exhibiting limited repeatability, GPT-4o consistently identified expert scientists in different medical fields with similar levels of academic productivity.

Limitations

While the results of the present study are promising, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, GPT-4o, like all LLMs, relies on the datasets it was trained on, which may reflect historical biases in publication and authorship. For example, ~90% of handling editors of the BMJ publishing group (comprising 21 biomedical journals) and > 60% of the senior authors of manuscripts submitted between 2018 and 2021 are geographically located in Europe and North America [9]. This trend is reflected in the geographical distribution of reviewers, with > 70% of invited reviewers being also based in Europe and North America [9]. Therefore, it is not surprising that AI technologies would shadow this pattern and reinforce historical disparities in the academic publishing system when not explicitly guided to consider DEI. Indeed, the majority of scientists identified by GPT-4o without a DEI prompt was affiliated with institutions in North America and Europe. However, this geographical gap significantly shrunk when DEI were explicitly prompted. Yet, while an attempt to mitigate existing biases was made by using a DEI prompt, the proportion of scientists affiliated with high-income countries remained predominant (i.e., 42%), suggesting that GPT-4o might still exhibit residual bias. The underlying reasons for this residual bias are unclear, but historical bias could skew AI's ability to identify truly diverse and representative experts, particularly from regions or demographics that are underrepresented in existing literature. As such, handling editors may consider explicitly prompting for experts from lower-income countries, but this strategy warrants further investigation.

It is important to acknowledge that there is currently no universally accepted threshold or specific metric that defines a “qualified” reviewer. In the present study, the reported academic metrics (i.e., peer-reviewed publications, citations and h-index) provide insight into the academic productivity and potential impact of the suggested scientists. However, ranking or selecting scientists solely based on these metrics presents significant caveats. Ultimately, the specific credentials that determine whether a scientist is suitable to serve as a potential reviewer remain largely unknown. While this study demonstrated that AI could generate a more diverse and inclusive pool of potential reviewers, it remains unclear whether these individuals would agree to participate in the peer review. Nonetheless, despite being underrepresented, reviewers from emerging countries are more likely to accept invitations to review [9]. Moreover, although AI appears capable of improving DEI in reviewer selection, the extent to which these changes would impact the quality of peer review and the broader scientific discourse are yet to be determined. Additionally, employing AI tools to identify potential reviewers raises important ethical considerations, particularly regarding the handling of sensitive or personal information, which warrants careful evaluation in future studies. Finally, the present study did not examine other types of bias, such as ethnic or institutional bias, which should be addressed in future research.

Conclusions

In closing, the present study demonstrated that without a DEI prompt, GPT-4o can successfully identify expert scientists in different medical fields, but predominantly males and those affiliated with high income countries. However, a gender-balanced and geographically diverse pool of scientists can be achieved by incorporating DEI into the prompt. The academic metrics were considerably high and did not differ between groups with and without a DEI prompt, suggesting that GPT-4o is capable of broadly identifying potentially skilled scientists who might be suitable to review for a scientific journal. Moreover, the observed low repeatability may be interpreted as a methodological strength of the model, as running multiple trials could generate an even more diverse pool of potential reviewers without compromising academic productivity. Collectively, these findings suggest that AI can be an ally in reducing gender and geographical gaps in peer review, though DEI should be explicitly highlighted in the prompt. On the other hand, AI could perpetuate existing biases if not carefully managed. Future studies are warranted to build upon these preliminary findings and to further characterize and validate the potential role of AI in addressing gender and geographical disparities in peer review.
