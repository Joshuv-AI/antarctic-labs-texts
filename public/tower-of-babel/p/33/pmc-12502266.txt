Surface roughness and biofilm formation on tooth-colored restorative materials immersed in food-simulating liquids
Authors: Mehmet Alperen Sahin, Ozge Gizem Yenidunya, Ilknur Kaleli, Mehmet Atca
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate the surface roughness and biofilm formation of different restorative materials immersed in food-simulating liquids (FSLs), and to investigate the relationship between these parameters.MethodsA total of 220 disc-shaped specimens (8 mm diameter × 2 mm depth) were prepared using five restorative materials: alkasite [Cention N], giomer [Beautifil II], ormocer [Admira Fusion], direct composite [G-ænial A’Chord], and indirect composite [Gradia Plus] (n = 44 per material). Each material group was divided into four subgroups (n = 11), immersed in one of four solutions—heptane, ethanol, citric acid, or artificial saliva (control)—for 7 days, resulting in a total of 20 experimental subgroups. In each subgroup of 11 specimens, 10 were used for both surface roughness measurements (before and after immersion) and bacterial adhesion assessment using the colony-forming unit (CFU) method, while one was reserved for scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis. Additionally, data were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and statistical analyses were performed using robust ANOVA and Bonferroni post hoc tests (p < 0.05).ResultsAlkasite and giomer exhibited significantly higher surface roughness values, whereas indirect composite presented the lowest (p < 0.001). Regarding the immersion solutions, citric acid led to the most pronounced increase in surface roughness compared to the other solutions, while heptane had the least impact (p < 0.001). Consistent with these findings, alkasite and giomer demonstrated the highest levels of bacterial adhesion, in contrast to direct and indirect composite, which showed the lowest (p = 0.008). Furthermore, citric acid resulted in the greatest microbial retention among the solutions, and heptane the least (p < 0.001).ConclusionsThe restorative materials exhibited varying degrees of susceptibility to the tested solutions. Alkasite and giomer showed the most pronounced surface changes, whereas the indirect composite group was the least affected. Among the solutions, citric acid caused the greatest surface alterations, while heptane had the least impact. Surface roughness emerged as a key factor influencing microbial retention on restorative materials.Keywords: Bacterial adhesion, Food-simulating liquids, Surface roughness, Tooth-colored restorative materials

Background

In contemporary restorative dentistry, the demand for materials that are not only durable and biocompatible but also capable of delivering superior esthetic outcomes continues to rise. As patient expectations increase and minimally invasive treatment philosophies gain prominence, the development of advanced tooth-colored materials has become a primary focus in dental biomaterials research [1]. Traditional options such as amalgam and glass ionomer cements, though once widely used, have seen a marked decline due to their esthetic limitations and insufficient mechanical performance. Consequently, resin composites have emerged as the material of choice, offering excellent esthetics and the ability to preserve sound tooth structure [2].

Aligned with these evolving clinical needs, the incorporation of biomimetic and bioemulation principles has driven the development of materials that not only replicate the natural form and function of dental tissues but also interact dynamically with the oral environment [3]. Recently developed material classes have gained increasing attention due to their enhanced mechanical properties, ion-releasing capabilities, and improved biocompatibility. While direct resin composites remain the cornerstone of chairside restorations due to their favorable mechanical and esthetic properties, they are not without limitations—such as polymerization shrinkage, incomplete curing, and long-term degradation within the oral cavity. To address these challenges, several innovative material classes have been developed, including indirect resin composites, ormocers, giomers, and alkasites. These materials introduce novel chemistries and therapeutic functionalities, broadening the clinical applicability of resin-based restoratives [4].

The restorative materials examined in this study—indirect resin composites, ormocers, giomers, and alkasites—differ considerably in terms of chemical composition, curing mechanisms, and clinical functionality. Indirect resin composites are light-, heat-, and pressure-cured outside the oral cavity, achieving a high degree of monomer conversion, enhanced mechanical properties, and improved biocompatibility due to reduced residual monomer content [5, 6]. Ormocers are organically modified ceramic systems composed of polymerizable groups chemically bonded to a siloxane (Si–O–Si) network formed via sol–gel processing [7]. This composition leads to minimal polymerization shrinkage and high resistance to wear and dimensional changes [7, 8]. Giomers are resin-based materials containing surface pre-reacted glass (S-PRG) fillers that release therapeutic ions such as fluoride and strontium. These fillers promote remineralization and acid resistance while maintaining the mechanical and esthetic properties of conventional composites [9, 10]. Alkasites are self-adhesive materials containing alkaline glass fillers that release hydroxide, calcium, and fluoride ions. They are designed to buffer acidic environments and support remineralization, and can be applied with or without light activation [11–13].

The long-term clinical performance of these materials is largely influenced by their resistance to degradation in the dynamic oral environment. The oral cavity presents continuous challenges due to variations in pH, temperature, and humidity, as well as frequent exposure to dietary and chemical agents. Acidic beverages, alcohol-based mouthrinses, and fatty foods introduce substances that may compromise the structural and surface integrity of restorative materials. To replicate these conditions in vitro, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) recommends the use of standardized food-simulating liquids such as citric acid, ethanol, and heptane. In addition, artificial saliva is widely employed to mimic the humid and enzymatically active oral environment [14, 15].

Among the various material properties, surface roughness plays a critical role in both esthetic performance and biological response. It is typically quantified by the Ra value, which represents the arithmetic average of surface deviations from a central plane [16]. Increased surface roughness can result in reduced gloss and compromised esthetics while also promoting microbial adhesion by creating favorable niches for biofilm formation [17]. Previous studies have suggested a threshold Ra value of 0.2 µm, below which the influence of roughness on bacterial attachment is considered clinically negligible [18].

One of the most studied species in this context is Streptococcus mutans, a highly cariogenic microorganism known for its acidogenicity, aciduricity, and its ability to synthesize extracellular polysaccharides (EPS). These properties enable strong adhesion and stable biofilm development on both enamel and restorative surfaces [17, 19]. Therefore, understanding the relationship between surface properties and S. mutans adhesion is essential for the development of restorative materials that support long-term oral health.

Given the clinical relevance of these interactions, the present study aimed to evaluate the effects of food-simulating liquids on the surface roughness and microbial adhesion of different restorative materials with varying compositions. The null hypotheses tested in this study were as follows: i. the type of FSL would not significantly affect surface roughness; ii. there would be no significant difference in surface roughness among the restorative materials following exposure to FSLs; iii. bacterial adhesion would not differ significantly among the restorative materials; and iv. the type of FSL would have no significant effect on bacterial adhesion.

A total of 220 disc-shaped specimens (8 mm in diameter × 2 mm in thickness) were prepared from five different tooth-colored restorative materials (n = 44 per material) and randomly assigned to four subgroups (n = 11) for immersion in different FSLs. Of the 11 specimens prepared for each experimental subgroup, 10 were utilized for both surface roughness assessments conducted before and after immersion, as well as bacterial adhesion analysis. The remaining specimen from each subgroup was reserved for scanning electron microscopy to evaluate biofilm morphology. To facilitate understanding of the study design, the organization of experimental subgroups is illustrated in Fig. 1. Each material was inserted into a silicone mold and gently covered with a Mylar strip (Hawe™ Transparent Strips, KerrHawe, Bioggio, Switzerland) and a thin glass slab to obtain a flat and void-free surface. All specimens, except those belonging to the indirect composite group and the alkasite group, were photopolymerized using an LED curing unit (Bluephase PowerCure, Ivoclar Vivadent AG, Schaan, Liechtenstein) operating at a light intensity of 1200 mW/cm2. The curing light output was verified prior to each use with the device’s integrated radiometer. The indirect composite specimens were polymerized using a heat-curing protocol in full mode for 3 min in a pressure-curing oven (Labolight Duo, GC Europe, Leuven, Belgium), as recommended by the manufacturer. In contrast, the alkasite material was mixed manually in a 1:1 ratio on a mixing pad with a plastic spatula until a uniform consistency was achieved, transferred into the mold, adapted with a condenser, and allowed to set through chemical polymerization alone. The specimens were stored in distilled water at 37 °C for 24 h to ensure complete polymerization before testing.

Schematic representation of the experimental design

Each specimen was labeled with a numerical code to allow identification while preserving group blinding during measurement. All specimens were finished using a polishing machine (Presi Mecapol P230, Madrid, Spain) with 600- and 800-grit silicon carbide papers, applied sequentially for 20 s each under water cooling. The cured surfaces were then polished using the Ultradent/Jiffy system (Ultradent, Salt Lake City, Utah, USA) for 15 s at a maximum speed of 3000 rpm, following the manufacturer’s instructions. Finally, the specimens were ultrasonically cleaned in distilled water for 5 min to remove surface residues.

All surface roughness measurements were performed by a single trained operator who was blinded to the group identities. Surface roughness was evaluated at two time points: first, baseline measurements (Ra₁) were obtained immediately after specimen preparation and polishing, from the upper surfaces of each specimen. A mechanical profilometer (Perthometer M2, Mahr, Göttingen, Germany; profile resolution: 12 nm) was used with a 0.25 mm cutoff and a 2 mm tracing length. For each specimen, three measurements were recorded at the center in varying directions, and the mean Ra₁ value was calculated in micrometers (µm). The device was calibrated before each session using a reference block with a known Ra value of 3.22 µm. Following baseline measurements, each specimen was placed in a glass Petri dish containing 5 mL of the respective food-simulating liquid and incubated in a drying oven (EN025, Nüve, Turkey) at 37 ± 1 °C for seven days. To ensure pH stability and prevent microbial contamination, FSLs were refreshed daily. After the 7-day immersion, the second surface roughness measurements (Ra₂) were performed on the same surface areas using the same procedure.

Microbial adhesion testing was conducted using Streptococcus mutans ATCC 25175. After surface roughness measurements, all specimens were sterilized with ethylene oxide. A standardized bacterial suspension (1 × 10⁸ CFU/mL) was prepared in Brain Heart Infusion (BHI) broth, adjusted to 0.5 McFarland turbidity, and diluted 1:10 before inoculation. Unstimulated human whole saliva was collected, immediately frozen, and thawed prior to use; to ensure sterility, it was passed through a 0.22 µm syringe filter immediately before application. The study was approved by the Pamukkale University Ethics Committee (Approval No: E-60116787–020-358576) and conducted in accordance with institutional ethical standards. To simulate pellicle formation, 500 µL of the sterile, filtered saliva was applied to each specimen in sterile 24-well plates and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h under static conditions, ensuring complete surface coverage and enabling initial protein adsorption. After incubation, specimens were rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and transferred to tubes containing 1.6 mL of BHI broth supplemented with 5% sucrose and 200 µL of the bacterial suspension. The samples were then incubated at 37 °C in a 5–7% CO₂ atmosphere for 24 h. Non-adherent bacteria were removed by rinsing with sterile saline. To detach the remaining adherent Streptococcus mutans cells, each specimen was individually placed into a sterile 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube containing 1 mL of sterile saline and vortexed for 60 s at maximum speed using a benchtop vortex mixer (MX-F Vortex, DLAB Scientific Inc., China). The resulting suspensions were serially diluted and plated onto Mitis Salivarius Bacitracin (MSB) agar. Colony-forming units (CFU) were counted after 48 h of incubation at 37 °C under CO₂ and expressed as log₁₀ CFU/mL.

To visualize biofilm morphology, one specimen from each of the 20 experimental subgroups was randomly selected prior to the vortexing step, immediately after incubation and rinsing. To remove non-adherent microorganisms, each specimen was gently rinsed with sterile isotonic saline, mounted on an aluminum stub, and sputter-coated with a 4 nm gold–palladium (Au–Pd) layer to ensure conductivity. Imaging was performed with a field-emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM; Regulus 8230, Hitachi, Ibaraki, Japan) at magnifications of 5,000 × and 10,000 ×.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 23.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and Jamovi (Version 2.3.28; The Jamovi Project, Sydney, Australia). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances using Levene’s test. Several subsets violated these assumptions; therefore, robust ANOVA methods were selected, as they provide reliable estimates under non-normality and variance heterogeneity while allowing analysis of both main and interaction effects. Specifically, a three-way robust ANOVA (material × solution × time) was applied to evaluate surface roughness, and a two-way robust ANOVA (material × solution) was used for bacterial adhesion. These analyses were performed using the WALRUS module in Jamovi, which implements trimmed means–based robust techniques for factorial designs. Post hoc multiple comparisons were adjusted using the Bonferroni correction. Results were expressed as median (minimum–maximum) and mean ± standard deviation, with the significance level set at p < 0.05.

Results

Surface roughness

The effects of restorative material, food-simulating liquid (FSL), and time on surface roughness (Ra) are presented in Table 3. At baseline, there were no statistically significant differences in surface roughness between solution groups (p > 0.05). After 7 days of immersion, specimens stored in citric acid exhibited the highest Ra values (0.322 ± 0.017 μm), independent of restorative material, followed by ethanol (0.186 ± 0.010 μm), artificial saliva (0.178 ± 0.008 μm), and heptane (0.142 ± 0.008 μm) (p = 0.001). When Ra values were analyzed independently of time, citric acid resulted in significantly higher roughness (0.199 ± 0.015 μm) compared to saliva (0.134 ± 0.007 μm), ethanol (0.138 ± 0.008 μm), and heptane (0.118 ± 0.005 μm) (p < 0.001).

Discussion

Restorative materials are continuously challenged by the complex oral environment, which includes dietary acids, salivary enzymes, chemical agents, pharmaceuticals, microbial activity, and fluctuations in temperature and pH [22, 23]. These factors can progressively undermine the mechanical and physical stability of restorations [24, 25], contributing to surface degradation, increased roughness, discoloration, plaque accumulation, mucosal inflammation, and secondary caries development [26, 27].

Surface characteristics play a decisive role in the clinical longevity of restorations, as surface irregularities promote microbial adhesion and biofilm development—both of which are implicated in the etiology of caries and periodontal disease [28, 29]. Surface roughness is governed by intrinsic material factors such as filler content, particle size, monomer composition, material hardness, filler–matrix interfacial strength, and the degree of polymerization [30]. Additionally, procedural variables—including the properties of polishing abrasives, technique employed, and operator-dependent factors like applied pressure, polishing duration, and rotational speed—also significantly influence surface texture outcomes [30, 31]. In this study, a contact-type mechanical profilometer was used, as it is widely accepted and has proven reliability for the quantitative assessment of surface roughness in dental materials. It has sufficient sensitivity to detect micrometer-scale topographical changes, particularly on polished and standardized resin-based surfaces. Furthermore, taking three measurements from each specimen and calculating the mean value is a standard approach that enhances accuracy and reduces surface variability, and this method has been consistently applied in similar in vitro studies [19, 32].

Beyond material- and technique-related factors, exposure to dietary-simulating media has emerged as a critical extrinsic factor influencing the surface integrity of restoratives [33, 34]. Among these, citric acid has been consistently reported to exert a markedly erosive effect on resin-based materials [35–37]. Its low pH facilitates filler dissolution and induces matrix softening, leading to surface degradation and topographical alterations [36, 37]. Giomer-based materials, in particular, appear highly susceptible, exhibiting both significant discoloration [38] and increased roughness [39]—likely due to the acid-labile nature of their fluorosilicate glass fillers. In agreement with these observations, the present study identified the highest surface roughness values in the citric acid group for Cention N and Beautifil II. These findings highlight the potential vulnerability of bioactive, ion-releasing materials to acidic environments, which may compromise their esthetic and functional stability over time.

The influence of ethanol, another frequently employed food-simulating liquid, appears to involve a different mechanism of surface alteration. Ethanol interacts with the polymer network by facilitating solvent penetration, promoting monomer elution, and expanding intermolecular spaces within the resin matrix—mechanisms that may ultimately result in surface softening or microcrack formation [40, 41]. A review of the current literature reveals considerable variability in the effects of food-simulating liquids (FSLs) on the physicochemical properties of dental materials. Vouvoudi and Sideridou demonstrated that immersion in a 75% ethanol–water solution led to more pronounced alterations in the viscoelastic properties of resin composites compared to artificial saliva, which was attributed to the organophilic nature of ethanol and its enhanced ability to penetrate the polymer matrix [42]. Interestingly, in the current study, ethanol exposure yielded surface roughness values statistically indistinguishable from those observed in artificial saliva, both in solution-specific and interaction analyses. This finding is consistent with the results of Cabadag and Gonulol, who similarly reported that both media exerted similar effects on the surface roughness of bulk-fill composites [15]. The observed similarity may be attributed to the short immersion duration and the relatively mild degradation potential of both solutions. Heterogeneity among studies may be explained by differences in the material composition, immersion protocols, and testing conditions. Artificial saliva, selected as the control medium, simulates the ionic composition of natural saliva while maintaining chemical neutrality. It contains key electrolytes (e.g., Na⁺, K⁺, Ca2⁺, Cl⁻, HCO₃⁻) but lacks enzymes or acidic components that could aggressively interact with resin-based materials, thus allowing for baseline comparisons without introducing additional degradative variables. This suggests that ethanol’s impact on surface morphology may be comparable to that of a neutral physiological medium, potentially reflecting similar plasticizing or solvation behavior. Although heptane has been reported to promote filler particle loss and weaken the resin matrix in restorative materials [34], other studies have indicated that its effect on surface properties is minimal, possibly contributing to the stabilization of the filler–matrix interface and the preservation of mechanical integrity [27, 43]. In the present study, specimens stored in heptane exhibited the lowest surface roughness value, which may be associated with reduced interfacial leakage within the filler domains during exposure to food-simulating liquids. These observations demonstrate that the type of food-simulating liquid significantly affected surface roughness, thereby supporting the rejection of the first null hypothesis.

Due to its distinct chemical composition, Cention N demonstrated a markedly adverse response to acidic exposure in the present study. The increased surface roughness observed in the citric acid group can be attributed to the material’s documented release of unreacted monomers and leachable ions, a property previously associated with increased susceptibility to chemical degradation compared to conventional glass ionomers and composite resins [44–46]. This ion release is strongly pH-dependent and intensifies under acidic conditions, thereby accelerating surface degradation and undermining structural integrity [47]. These findings are consistent with degradation mechanisms proposed for ion-releasing systems and are further corroborated by the relatively large filler particle size of Cention N (0.1–7 µm), which limits polishability and contributes to increased surface irregularities, in agreement with prior studies [48–50].

The bioactivity of giomer materials is primarily conferred by the incorporation of S-PRG fillers, which facilitate the controlled release of therapeutic ions. However, this ion-releasing mechanism may also promote localized surface degradation and microstructural deterioration, particularly under acidic conditions. Prior research has shown that such ion-exchange-induced surface alterations can compromise the physical and mechanical integrity of giomer-based restoratives [51, 52]. In line with these findings, the present study revealed marked surface deterioration in the Beautifil II group following exposure to citric acid, likely attributable to the acid sensitivity of its fluoroaluminosilicate glass fillers. These results underscore the importance of considering ion release characteristics when evaluating the long-term surface stability of giomer materials in low-pH environments.

Surface roughness varied notably among the materials evaluated, with Gradia Plus yielding the lowest values. This superior outcome may be attributed to a combination of enhanced polymerization efficiency—achieved through additional light-curing—and favorable intrinsic characteristics, including a relatively low filler content (75 wt%) and fine particle size (300 nm). These features likely promoted better polishability and reduced the formation of surface irregularities, ultimately resulting in smoother surfaces. The present findings are consistent with previous reports highlighting the influence of filler composition and curing protocols on the surface performance of indirect composite resins [53–55]. Based on these findings, the second null hypothesis—that there would be no significant difference in surface roughness among the restorative materials—was rejected.

Surface roughness is a well-documented factor influencing bacterial adhesion, as surface irregularities provide retention sites that facilitate microbial colonization and protection [16, 56–58]. Although this association is widely supported, other surface characteristics—such as surface energy, hydrophilicity, and salivary pellicle composition—have also been identified as contributing factors to bacterial retention on restorative materials [59–61]. Nonetheless, increased surface roughness has been consistently correlated with higher levels of bacterial adhesion, underscoring the importance of adequate finishing and polishing protocols in clinical practice [19, 58, 62–64]. These procedures help reduce surface irregularities and may effectively limit biofilm formation. From a material standpoint, composites with smaller filler particles and optimized resin matrices typically yield smoother surfaces with improved polishability, resulting in reduced bacterial adhesion, whereas larger filler particles are associated with increased roughness and greater microbial retention [58, 64].

In the present study, bacterial adhesion patterns closely reflected surface roughness outcomes. Cention N exhibited the highest microbial retention (7.140 log CFU), likely due to its relatively large filler particle size (0.1–7 µm) and associated surface irregularities. In contrast, Gradia Plus demonstrated the lowest bacterial adhesion (6.934 log CFU), which may be attributed to its finer filler particles, lower filler content, and enhanced polymerization protocol—factors contributing to a smoother surface. These findings underscore the importance of filler characteristics and surface topography in modulating bacterial adhesion to resin-based restorative materials. In light of these findings, the third null hypothesis—that bacterial adhesion would not differ significantly among the restorative materials—was rejected.

Acid-induced chemical degradation emerged as a key contributor to microbial colonization patterns observed in the current study. Specimens immersed in citric acid exhibited significantly greater bacterial adhesion, likely due to surface breakdown, microstructural deterioration, and filler particle loss—mechanisms consistently reported in previous studies [65, 66]. In contrast, the lowest bacterial retention was recorded in specimens stored in heptane, possibly owing to its stabilizing effect on the filler–matrix interface and preservation of surface integrity [43]. This was further supported by SEM imaging, which revealed substantial S. mutans accumulation on citric acid–exposed specimens, in contrast to the sparsely colonized surfaces observed in the heptane group. Accordingly, the fourth null hypothesis—that the type of food-simulating liquid would have no significant effect on bacterial adhesion—was also rejected.

Bacterial adhesion to resin-based restorative materials is a multifactorial phenomenon influenced by various parameters beyond surface roughness. Factors such as surface free energy, surface charge, hydrophilicity, the antimicrobial potential of certain materials, and the formation of the salivary pellicle have all been shown to modulate microbial colonization dynamics [59, 67–69]. These variables were beyond the scope of the present study; however, their reported influence in the literature should be acknowledged when interpreting the multifactorial nature of bacterial adhesion. Recognizing these aspects provides a broader context for understanding the complexity of biofilm development on restorative surfaces.

While the present study offers valuable insights into the surface behavior of restorative materials, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the in vitro model used cannot fully replicate the multifactorial nature of the oral environment, including temperature fluctuations, salivary dynamics, and the complex microbial ecology. Second, although flat, standardized specimens were produced to ensure surface consistency, these do not reflect the anatomical and procedural variability encountered in clinical settings, particularly regarding polishing effectiveness. Finally, the use of a single bacterial strain (Streptococcus mutans ATCC 25175) fails to represent the polymicrobial interactions inherent to natural oral biofilms, thereby limiting the ecological validity of the findings.

Conclusions

Within the limitations of this in vitro investigation, it can be concluded that the surface integrity and microbial susceptibility of restorative materials are significantly influenced by both their intrinsic composition and the chemical characteristics of the surrounding environment. Materials containing bioactive or ion-releasing components, such as alkasite and giomer, exhibited greater surface degradation and bacterial colonization, particularly under acidic conditions. Conversely, the indirect composite demonstrated superior resistance to surface alteration and microbial adhesion, likely attributable to its higher degree of conversion and lower solubility.

These findings underscore the necessity of tailoring material selection not only to mechanical and aesthetic demands but also to the patient’s individual oral environment, especially in cases where high dietary acidity or elevated cariogenic risk is present. While the results provide mechanistic insights into material-environment interactions, clinical trials are warranted to determine the extent to which these laboratory findings translate into long-term restorative performance.
