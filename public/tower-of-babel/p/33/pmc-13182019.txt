Efficacy of hip strengthening on balance and function outcomes in chronic ankle instability: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Zhi-Qiang Hou, Ju-Hong Li, Xin-Yue Xu, Ning Zuo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic ankle instability (CAI) involves not only local mechanical damage to the ankle joint but also accompanying muscle control deficits in the proximal limb. Although hip muscles play a crucial role in postural control within the lower limb, the overall effectiveness of hip strengthening exercises in the rehabilitation of CAI patients remains unclear.MethodsMajor databases including PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, and Sport Discus were searched to identify randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing hip strengthening exercises with control. Primary outcome measures included dynamic balance assessed by the Star Excursion Balance Test (SEBT/YBT), static balance assessed by center of pressure (COP) parameters, and foot and ankle ability measure (FAAM) scores. Effect sizes were pooled using a random-effects model.ResultsNine RCTs involving 306 participants were included. Meta-analysis results showed that hip strengthening exercises significantly improved dynamic balance in patients with CAI, specifically demonstrated by increased anterior (SMD = 0.72, 95% CI: 0.25–1.18, P = 0.003), posterolateral (SMD = 1.35, P < 0.0001), and posteromedial (SMD = 1.66, P < 0.0001) reach distances in the SEBT/YBT test. In terms of static balance, the intervention group showed a significant reduction in COP ellipse area (MD = -11.62 mm², 95% CI: -20.77 to -2.47, P = 0.013) and a significant shortening of the movement path length (MD = -56.33 mm, P < 0.05), indicating improved postural control. Regarding foot and ankle function, the intervention group had higher FAAM-ADL scores (MD = 4.32, 95%CI: 1.06–7.58, P = 0.010) and higher FAAM-Sport scores (MD = 5.26, P = 0.007), indicating improved foot and ankle function.ConclusionHip strengthening exercises can effectively improve dynamic and static balance control abilities in patients with chronic ankle instability, especially posterior dynamic stability, and improve ankle function. It is recommended to include targeted hip strengthening exercises in the routine rehabilitation program for CAI to optimize treatment outcomes and prevent re-injury.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-026-09733-w.

Lateral ankle sprains (LAS) are one of the most common injuries in sports activities, characterized by a high rate of recurrence [1]. Chronic ankle instability (CAI) is a condition associated with residual symptoms and recurrent instability, characterized by pain, a feeling of ankle instability, and other functional deficits that persist for more than six months after a severe LAS [2, 3]. A cohort study has shown that improper and insufficient rehabilitation of LAS would lead to approximately 40% of patients that eventually develop CAI [4]. CAI limits daily life and sports activities, and in severe cases, it can gradually progress to traumatic osteoarthritis [5].

Traditional rehabilitation programs for CAI primarily focus on the ankle joint itself, utilizing clinical interventions such as resistance band strengthening and balance board exercises to improve function and stability [6]. Evidence suggests that foot and ankle muscle strengthening exercises can effectively improve proprioception [7], and balance training can enhance functional and dynamic balance [8]. However, despite benefits from these interventions, the optimal rehabilitation strategy for CAI has yet to be established.

New evidence suggests that CAI-related deficits are not limited to the distal joints but extend proximally along the kinetic chain [9]. Recent study [10] has found significant neuromuscular impairments in the hip musculature of CAI patients. Compared to healthy controls, CAI patients exhibit reduced electromyographic (EMG) activity in proximal muscles (such as the gluteus medius and biceps femoris) during functional rehabilitation tasks [11]. Furthermore, biomechanical assessments show that CAI patients exhibit altered landing kinematics, including reduced hip abduction and significantly deficient hip external rotation, extension, and abduction strength [12]. These strength deficits appear to persist across different age groups, with middle-aged patients demonstrating significantly lower hip extension strength [13].

Given these proximal deficits, it is crucial to include proximal muscle strengthening in the rehabilitation program when evaluating the entire kinetic chain in CAI [12]. Although hip weakness may not always be directly related to altered landing mechanics, strengthening these proximal muscles is believed to improve overall functional outcomes [13]. A recent randomized controlled trial [14] demonstrated that incorporating hip strengthening into conventional ankle rehabilitation such as balance training, led to better patient-reported outcomes.

In recent years, emerging clinical trials have supported proximal interventions, whereas a consensus on the specific efficacy of hip strengthening exercises for the rehabilitation of CAI has not yet been established. Although previous systematic reviews have explored proximal muscle involvement in chronic ankle instability (CAI), they either focused on muscle strength deficits alone or included mixed interventions (e.g., balance and strength combined). To our knowledge, this is the first meta-analysis specifically designed to quantify the isolated effect of hip strengthening exercises on balance and functional outcomes in individuals with CAI. Our review updates the evidence by incorporating recently published randomized controlled trials (RCTs) from 2020 to 2025 (e.g., Yeum et al. 2024; Suttmiller et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2025; Huang et al. 2025), which were not covered in prior syntheses. Furthermore, we applied several methodological enhancements: prospective registration of the review protocol in PROSPERO to ensure transparency; comprehensive searches of both Western and Eastern databases (including CNKI, Wanfang, and KoreaMed) to reduce language and publication bias; rigorous risk of bias assessment using the Cochrane RoB 2.0 tool; and exploration of heterogeneity through subgroup and cumulative meta-analyses. These methodological strengths provide a more robust and up‑to‑date estimate of the true effect of hip strengthening, thereby offering clearer guidance for clinical practice and future research directions.Therefore, the purpose of this study is to evaluate the effectiveness of hip strengthening exercises for CAI.

Materials and methods

Registration and protocol

This systematic review was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO). The registration number is CRD420261288466 and the full protocol is publicly available at: https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD420261288466. There were no amendments to the information provided at registration or in the protocol.

Databases and search strategy

Relevant literature published before December 27, 2025 was searched in the following databases: PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Sport Discus, CNKI (China), Wanfang (China), and KoreaMed (Korea). Language restrictions: The search was limited to studies published in English, Chinese, and Korean, the databases searched included those from China (CNKI, Wanfang) and Korea (KoreaMed).Taking PubMed as an example, we first obtained MeSH keywords related to CAI, and then constructed the advanced search expression: (“Hip strengthen program” [tiab] OR “Hip muscle strength exercise” [tiab] OR “Hip strengthening program” [tiab] OR “Isometric Hip Strength” [tiab]) AND (“Ankle Injuries” [mesh] OR “lateral ankle sprain” [tiab] OR “LAS” [tiab] OR “Chronic ankle instability” [tiab] OR “CAI” [tiab] OR “functional ankle instability” [tiab] OR “FAI” [tiab] OR “Ankle fractures” [tiab]). To minimize publication bias, we supplemented the database searches by manually screening the reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews. Additionally, we searched Clinicaltrials.org and Google Scholar for unpublished or ongoing trials. However, conference abstracts and dissertations were only included if they provided full-text data and met all eligibility criteria.The complete search strings for all databases are provided in Supplementary File 1.

Eligibility criteria

Study type

The literature included in this review only comprises RCTs.

Study participants

All included studies involved patients with CAI, who had a history of ankle sprains and experienced recurrent ankle pain and instability.

Intervention

All studies included at least one intervention group that received hip strengthening exercises for rehabilitation. Hip strengthening exercises were defined as strengthening exercises for the hip muscles, such as hip external rotation and flexion in side-lying, sitting, or standing positions, single-plane hip joint movements using resistance bands, or isokinetic muscle strength training targeting the hip adductor and abductor muscles. Other rehabilitation methods, such as calf and ankle muscle exercises, could be combined in this intervention group, but hip-strengthening exercises remained the primary intervention. Due to the anticipated variability in hip strengthening protocols across studies, we extracted detailed information on intervention characteristics, including targeted muscles, exercise type, intensity/dosage, frequency, duration, progression criteria, and co-interventions. These details are summarized in Table 2 to facilitate clinical interpretation and highlight areas of protocol heterogeneity.

Control

In the control group, patients received simple ankle muscle exercises, no strength training, or no rehabilitation program at all.

Outcome measures

The studies must include outcome measures related to ankle stability before and after the intervention, such as dynamic balance ability, static balance ability, muscle strength, and ankle function scale scores.

Exclusion criteria

Single-arm controlled studies (without a control group), observational studies, and case series will be excluded. Studies enroll participants without CAI would be excluded. Studies comparing two different intervention methods (e.g., strength training versus balance training) will be excluded; studies using hop-stabilization training as the intervention method will be excluded; and studies using multimodal comprehensive exercise (involving dorsiflexion, ankle strength, hip strength, dynamic postural control, and static postural control) as the intervention method will be excluded. Studies with missing outcome measures, unavailable data, or data that cannot be converted for use will be excluded.

Screening process

Duplicate entries were removed by reviewing the title and abstracts. Two researchers then read the full texts and screened the studies according to the criteria.

Quality assessment

The risk of bias in the included studies was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool V2.0 [15] provided by the Cochrane Collaboration. Three review authors Zhi-Qiang Hou, Xin-Yue Xu and Ning Zuo independently assessed the risk of bias for each included study across the following domains: randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported result. Each domain and the overall risk of bias were judged as “low risk of bias,” “some concerns,” or “high risk of bias” according to the criteria specified in the RoB 2.0 tool. Any disagreements between the three reviewers were resolved through consultation with a fourth review author.

Data extraction

Two researchers independently extracted the structured data from the literature. They cross-checked each other’s results and discussed any discrepancies before finalizing the data.

Priori power

Priori power is used to estimate the approximate number of studies and the average sample size of studies needed in a meta-analysis [16]. By using an estimated standardized mean difference (SMD) = 0.75, number of studies = 10, and average sample size per study = 15 as initial values, and a heterogeneity parameter of “random,” we calculated the prior power using the `power.analysis` function from the `dmetar` package in R and plotted the resulting curve.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were defined as the changes in dynamic balance ability, the post-intervention static balance ability, and FAAM scores. Dynamic balance ability was measured using the Y-Balance Test (YBT) or Star Excursion Balance Test (SEBT), including angular changes in the anterior, posteromedial, and posterolateral directions. Static balance ability was measured using the center of pressure (COP), including three parameters: ellipse area (EA) (mm²), path length (PL) (mm), and average velocity (AV) (mm/s). The FAAM includes two dimensions: ADL and SPORT.

Effect sizes and syntheses

The comparison of changes in dynamic balance ability between groups was analyzed using the SMD as the effect size. SMD was chosen because dynamic balance was assessed using different measurement tools across studies (i.e., the SEBT and the YBT), SMD allows these different scales to be combined by expressing the effect in units of standard deviation, thereby enabling a pooled analysis while accounting for measurement variability.For static balance outcomes, and average velocity and foot and ankle function, the MD was used as the effect measure. MD was appropriate because these outcomes were measured using identical or directly comparable units across studies.Using MD preserves the original scale of measurement, facilitating clinical interpretation of the results.

Despite the use of different COP parameters across studies, we considered pooling these outcomes appropriate for the following reasons. First, all three parameters are valid and complementary measures of postural control during static stance, reflecting different aspects of balance: EA represents the spatial extent of sway, PL reflects the total distance traveled by the COP, and AV indicates the speed of postural adjustments. Second, previous meta-analyses in the field of CAI and balance research have commonly pooled different COP parameters under the domain of “static balance”.However, we acknowledge that the variability in measurement protocols and parameter selection across studies may contribute to heterogeneity and affect the robustness of the findings, which we address in the Discussion.

Heterogeneity statistics and source investigation

Heterogeneity was assessed using I² analysis and Q test, with I² > 50% or P < 0.1 indicating the presence of heterogeneity. Subgroup analysis was used to explore the source of heterogeneity.

Cumulative analysis

We also conducted a cumulative meta-analysis on the outcomes based on publication year.

Publication bias detection

The funnel plots were drawn to show the publication bias.

Statistical analysis

The analysis was performed using the “meta” package in R (version 4.4.1), with P < 0.05 indicating a statistically significant overall effect size.

Sensitivity analysis

To assess the robustness of our findings and examine the impact of studies with high risk of bias, we conducted sensitivity analyses by excluding studies rated as “high risk” according to the Cochrane RoB 2.0 tool. For dynamic balance outcomes, after excluding these three high-risk studies, the pooled effect size for anterior direction remained significant, which was comparable to the main analysis.Similarly, the results for posterolateral and posteromedial directions remained robust. For static balance outcomes (ellipse area), excluding the high-risk studies did not substantially alter the pooled estimate consistent with the main analysis. For FAAM-ADL scores, the pooled effect size remained significant after excluding high-risk studies. These sensitivity analyses suggest that the overall conclusions are not solely driven by studies with high risk of bias, although the results should still be interpreted with caution given the methodological limitations of the included studies.

Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of hip strengthening exercises in improving rehabilitation outcomes for patients with CAI. Our main findings indicate that hip strengthening exercises significantly improve both dynamic and static balance abilities in CAI patients. Specifically, the meta-analysis results showed that the intervention group demonstrated more changes in all three directions (anterior, posterolateral, and posteromedial) of the SEBT or YBT compared to the control group (SMD values of 0.72, 1.35, and 1.66, respectively). Furthermore, in terms of static balance, hip strengthening significantly reduced the ellipse area (MD = -11.62 mm²) and path length (MD = -56.33 mm) of the center of pressure (COP), indicating a substantial improvement in postural control. Regarding foot and ankle function, According to previous literature, the Minimum Clinically Important Difference (MCID) for FAAM-ADL and FAAM-Sport typically ranges from 8 to 9 points., the intervention group had higher FAAM-ADL scores (MD = 4.32, 95%CI: 1.06–7.58, P = 0.010) and higher FAAM-Sport scores (MD = 5.26, P = 0.007),This suggests that while hip strengthening leads to statistically significant improvements in patient-reported function, the magnitude of improvement may not reach the threshold that patients perceive as clinically meaningful, The observed differences in results may be attributed to several factors: the MCID values were derived from distinct patient populations and may not directly apply to our specific cohort. Additionally, isolated hip strengthening exercises without concurrent functional training may enhance capability but fail to translate fully into clinical performance.

Studies [26–28] have shown that patients with CAI experience proprioceptive deficits, weakened neuromuscular control, decreased postural control, and reduced ankle muscle strength. Currently, most clinical rehabilitation methods for CAI focus on the ankle joint itself, such as training the strength of the muscles around the ankle, improving ankle proprioception, and enhancing neuromuscular control through neuromuscular electrical stimulation of the ankle muscles [29]. However, CAI patients are rarely treated holistically. All movements of the lower limbs depend on the stabilizing function of the trunk and pelvis, which are considered the core of the body. Individuals with a history of lower limb injuries require stronger core muscle activation to maintain postural stability during dynamic tasks [30]. A clinical controlled study [31] comparing strength training and balance training in the rehabilitation of CAI patients found that both had similar effects on the patients’ balance abilities.

Our results are consistent with several previous studies, further confirming the crucial role of hip function in ankle joint stability. Smith et al. [19] found that targeted hip abduction and external rotation training significantly improved SEBT performance, which aligns with our pooled effect size (especially the significant improvements in the posterolateral and posteromedial directions). This is likely because extension in the posterolateral and posteromedial directions is highly dependent on hip flexion and external rotation control, and patients with CAI often exhibit insufficient hip abductor strength [32]. The study by Wang et al. [20] also supports this, finding that combined hip and ankle isokinetic training improved SEBT scores more than ankle training alone, suggesting an additive effect of proximal muscle training.

Furthermore, in terms of static balance, our results showed a significant reduction in COP sway. This is consistent with the findings of study [17] and study [21]. Yeum et al. [17] further indicated that hip strengthening not only increased hip abduction strength but also significantly improved static balance ability under closed-eye conditions. This suggests that the activation of hip muscles may enhance the stability of the entire lower limb kinetic chain through a feed-forward mechanism, thereby compensating for the lack of ankle proprioception.

The mechanism by which hip strengthening improves balance in CAI patients may be explained by a biomechanical concept of “proximal control influencing distal function” [33]. Patients with CAI often have impaired proximal compensatory strategies. When ankle proprioceptive input is impaired, the central nervous system increasingly relies on hip strategies to maintain balance. However, the weakness of hip muscles (especially the gluteus medius and gluteus maximus) limits the compensatory mechanism, resulting in a decreased postural control ability [34, 35]. Targeted hip strengthening, such as the elastic band training used by Smith et al. [19] or the isokinetic training used by Wang et al. [20], enhances the stability of the proximal limb, thereby reducing excessive swaying of the lower limb during landing or single-leg stance, which in turn improves distal ankle stability and further enhances foot and ankle function.

The findings of this study provide evidence-based medical support for the development of rehabilitation programs for CAI. Given the significant effects of hip training in improving dynamic and static balance, clinicians and physical therapists managing CAI should extend beyond isolated ankle rehabilitation and routinely incorporate strengthening exercises targeting the hip abductors, extensors, and external rotators. The integrated approach not only help improve athletic performance but may also reduce the risk of future ankle sprains. Supporting this perspective, the study by Powden et al. [36] inplemented multimodal intervention program to CAI patients over four weeks, incorporating weight-bearing lunge training, isometric ankle strength training, isometric hip strength training, dynamic postural control training, and static postural control training, and reported promising outcomes.

This study has several limitations. First, the included studies exhibited substantial heterogeneity in their intervention protocols (e.g., intervention durations ranging from 4 to 12 weeks, and training methods including isokinetic training and elastic band training), which may have contributed to the statistical heterogeneity observed in our dynamic balance analysis (I²=58.2%). Second, the small sample sizes in some studies may adversely affected the robustness of the analyses. Third, Although we analyzed EA, PL, and AV separately to maintain within-parameter comparability, the fact that different studies reported different parameters introduces potential inconsistency, This discrepancy may reflect genuine differences in how hip strengthening affects distinct dimensions of postural control—EA may be more sensitive to changes in overall sway area, while PL and AV capture temporal and dynamic aspects of balance that may require longer intervention periods or more sensitive measurement tools to detect change.Fourth, some useful outcomes, such as post-intervention muscle strength results, could not be included in the meta-analysis due to the limited number of studies reporting such outcomes. Although our sensitivity analyses indicated that excluding high-risk studies did not substantially alter the pooled effect sizes, the presence of studies with methodological flaws—particularly those with high attrition rates and inadequate randomization procedures —may still introduce bias that cannot be fully corrected through statistical adjustments.

In the meta-analysis assessing the impact of hip strengthening exercises on anterior dynamic balance, significant statistical heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 58.2%, P = 0.04). To further investigate the potential sources of this heterogeneity, we conducted subgroup analyses based on different dynamic balance testing tools (YBT vs. SEBT) and intervention durations. The results suggest neither of them were the primary sources. We speculate that the high heterogeneity may stem from several under-quantified clinical and methodological factors.

First, intervention protocol characteristics varied substantially across studies. Hip strengthening approaches included isokinetic training, resistance band exercises, and bodyweight training, each involving different contraction modes (eccentric, concentric, or isometric) and kinetic chain patterns (open vs. closed chain), which may differentially influence outcomes. However, a subgroup analysis wasn’t performed based on the intervention protocols, because they couldn’t be quantified. Second, baseline participant characteristics differed across studies. Third, control group conditions were inconsistent: some studies used a no-intervention control, whereas others implemented conventional ankle rehabilitation, potentially amplifying variability in effect sizes due to differing baseline comparisons. Future research should prioritize standardization of hip training (e.g., intensity and frequency) and employ more rigorous population stratification to better isolate and clarify these potential confounding factors.

In this study, the included literature primarily recruited male patients with CAI, with fewer female patients, which may introduce a gender bias. Beyond the imbalance in sex distribution, the potential sex differences in hip muscle function and neuromuscular control warrant deeper discussion. Emerging evidence suggests that the relationship between hip strength and functional outcomes in CAI may differ between males and females. Lu J et al. [37] demonstrated that in female CAI patients, normalized hip abductor strength was significantly and positively correlated with self-reported ankle instability scores (CAIT), whereas no such correlation was observed in males. This indicates that hip abductor strength may play a more critical role in perceived ankle stability for females than for males.

These findings have important clinical implications.If hip strength is more strongly associated with functional outcomes in females, our pooled effect sizes might either underestimate or overestimate the true effect in females, depending on the sex distribution of the original studies. Second, rehabilitation programs may need to be sex-specific. For female CAI patients, targeted hip strengthening—particularly of the hip abductors—might be especially beneficial, as suggested by the significant correlations reported by Lu et al. In contrast, male patients might respond differently or require alternative intervention emphases.Future research should prioritize sex-specific analyses to determine whether the efficacy of hip strengthening differs between males and females. clinicians should be aware of potential sex differences and consider individualizing hip strengthening protocols based on patient characteristics.

In this study, the included literature on hip strengthening exercises primarily focused on muscle strengthening. However, the study by Lee et al. [38] indicated that stability training of the lumbopelvic structure not only helps reduce pain and improve hip joint function but can also be applied to treat CAI, stabilize ankle function, and reduce the frequency of re-injury. Future research could incorporate pelvic structural stability exercises into hip strengthening programs, which may yield additional benefits in the treatment of CAI.

In this study, the prior power calculation indicated that the sample size and number of included studies met the general requirements for meta-analysis. Publication bias result suggest minimal bias, and cumulative meta-analysis showed that the results gradually converged and stabilized with increasing publication year. However, the quality of the included studies was still not high. This topic still warrants further investigation with the inclusion of more high-quality studies.

Conclusion

In summary, this meta-analysis suggests that hip strengthening exercises may effectively improve dynamic and static balance abilities and self-reported function in patients with chronic ankle instability. As an intervention targeting the proximal kinetic chain, it could be considered an integral part of a comprehensive rehabilitation program. However, given the methodological limitations and heterogeneity of the included studies, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Future high-quality RCTs are warranted to confirm the optimal dosage and long-term effects of hip strengthening in this population.
