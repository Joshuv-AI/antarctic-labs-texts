Transvenous embolization of patent ductus arteriosus in 16 dogs using the Amplatzer Vascular Plug II device: description of a modified technique and complications
Authors: Antoine DUBÈS, Jean-Benoît THAMBO, Virginie LOYER, Pierre MENAUT
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) is a deadly congenital disease in dogs if left untreated. Occlusion of the defect is the treatment of choice and can be achieved by surgical ligation or interventional closure. In this retrospective study of 16 dogs, an alternative to the classically used canine device which is placed by an arterial route is described. The Amplatzer Vascular Plug II® (AVPII) can be deployed by a transvenous approach, using a modified and simplified technique using a single catheter to perform angiography and device delivery. This allows the percutaneous treatment of smaller dogs <3 kg and the concomitant treatment of pulmonic stenosis if present. Successful and complete closure was achieved in all dogs with a mean device/ampulla diameter ratio of 1.28 and a mean device/ostium ratio of 3.6. Embolization into the pulmonary artery was observed in one dog where the device/ampulla diameter ratio was <1.1 and device/ostium ratio was <2.1. In conclusion, our study confirms that PDA transvenous closure using the AVPII appears to be a viable alternative to transarterial closure, allowing the treatment of smaller dogs and a fully percutaneous approach. Care should be taken in patients with very large ducti where undersizing might result in device embolization.

Left-to-right shunting patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) is one of the most common congenital cardiac diseases in dogs [21]. When left untreated, the disease leads to left-sided congestive heart failure and carries a high mortality rate [5]. Occlusion of the PDA must be performed early to prevent this complication or the development of right-to-left shunting through the ductus. Occlusion can be achieved via surgical or catheter-based techniques. For the latter, performed under fluoroscopy, multiple devices can be used and have been described [12, 25]. Currently, the most commonly used device in dogs, is the Amplatz Canine Duct Occluder® (ACDO®, Infiniti Medical, Redwood City, CA, USA) [19, 20]. However, it has to be placed with an arterial access, which requires a small incision and dissection of the femoral artery and cannot be used in small animals weighing less than 3 or 4 kg. A low profile device for small animals has been described [27] but has not been marketed yet to the best of our knowledge.

Recently, other devices have been used in small animals [3, 14,15,16]. One of them is the Amplatzer Vascular Plug II® (ABBOTT Cardiovascular, Plymouth, MN, USA), whose primary indication in human medicine is arterial and venous embolization in the peripheral vasculature. This device has also been described to close PDA in children and infants [9, 10, 18, 24]. One of the advantages of this device is the deployment via a venous access, allowing a percutaneous approach and a simplified access for small dogs. The deployment can also be done with an arterial access if needed.

The objective of our study was to describe our experience with the use of the Amplatzer Vascular Plug II® (AVPII) in dogs with left-to-right shunting PDA and provide additional data to studies conducted by other institutions during an overlapping period. In addition, we propose a modified technique using a single catheter that could simplify the procedure, in terms of manipulation, duration and cost.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

Medical records of dogs with left-to-right PDA treated with the AVPII between December 2019 and April 2022 at the Centre Hospitalier Vétérinaire AQUIVET were reviewed. For all of them, informed client consent was obtained before the procedure and the study received ethical approval by the VetAgroSup ethical Committee (approval n°2221).

Diagnosis of PDA was made by physical examination (auscultation of a continuous left basilar heart murmur), and echocardiography. Full echocardiographic examination was performed according to veterinary guidelines [4, 8, 28] including two-dimensional, M-mode and Doppler examination. Left ventricular and atrial dimensions were noted as well as ductal morphology and dimensions and concurrent congenital malformations. Descriptive data including age, weight, breed were reviewed.

Description of the device

The AVPII is a multi-layered nitinol mesh device with six planes of cross-sectional coverage (Fig. 1Fig. 1.Photograph of the Amplatzer vascular plug II device (used with permission).). The indicated diameter is the external maximal diameter of the cylinders. It is available from size 3 to 22 mm, permitting the occlusion of blood vessels up to 17 mm, according to the manufacturer.

Photograph of the Amplatzer vascular plug II device (used with permission).

Procedure

A premedication was made, with morphine (0.2 mg/kg IV) and diazepam (0.25 mg/kg IV). A prophylactic antibiotic was delivered (cefazoline, 30 mg/kg IV). Animals were anesthetized with propofol in titration and intubated in order to maintain narcosis with isoflurane. They were positioned in lateral recumbency (right for 9 of them and left for 7 of them). The jugular zone was clipped and surgically scrubbed.

Jugular access was obtained by the modified Seldinger technique using a 18G needle and an introducer sheath (Avanti+, CORDIS, Miami, FL, USA) was placed. A multipurpose (MP) catheter (MP1 MERIT MEDICAL, South Jordan, UT, USA) was then advanced in the right heart and pulmonary trunk over a wire (Guide Wire M straight 0.035” 150 cm, TERUMO EUROPE, Leuven, Belgium). In 5 cases, advancing the catheter into the right ventricular outflow tract was difficult and a balloon wedge pressure catheter (Balloon Wedge Pressure Catheter, ARROW, Reading, PA, USA) was used over a J-Tip wire (Guide Wire M J 0.035” 150 cm, TERUMO EUROPE), before being exchanged for the MP catheter. A first angiography was then performed to visualize the anatomy of the pulmonary trunk and visualize the PDA ostium. In some cases (depending on the animal size and the quality of transthoracic echocardiography), transesophageal echocardiography was used to help visualize the PDA. With the MP catheter positioned in the pulmonary trunk, the straight guidewire was then passed through the PDA and descending aorta, followed by the MP catheter. Another angiography was then performed in order to visualize the ductus and make appropriate measurements for device selection (Fig. 2Fig. 2.Patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) angiogram in lateral projection, performed with contrast injection through the Multipurpose A1 catheter. The catheter is placed at the entrance of the PDA. PDA ampulla is visualized between the yellow arrows.).

Device selection was made based on echocardiographic (Fig. 3Fig. 3.Echocardiographic visualization and measurements of the patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) using a left parasternal cranial view at the level of the pulmonary artery. (1) Ostium diameter. (2) Base diameter. (3) Ampulla diameter.) and angiographic measurements of the ductal ampulla. A general rule was to use a device whose discs were 20 to 30% larger than the ductal ampulla. However, in some cases, depending on availability of devices during the COVID pandemic, patient size and ostium size, it was decided to use smaller or larger device size/ampulla ratios. The guide was then removed, and the AVPII delivered through the MP catheter. Two disks of the device were deployed in the aorta, then the device was force-fitted in the PDA ampulla. The third disk was deployed in the pulmonary trunk. Fitting of the device was then tested using gentle back and forth manipulation of the delivery wire. An angiographic control was performed to confirm adequate positioning of the device using saved reference angiographic images (Fig. 4Fig. 4.Deployment of the Amplatzer vascular plug II® (AVPII) in a dog with left-to-right patent ductus arteriosus (PDA), under fluoroscopy (lateral projection). The device was then released from the delivery wire by counterclockwise rotation. The delivery wire, catheter and introducer sheath were then removed and compression of the jugular vein was applied for five min.

An echocardiogram was performed a few hours after the procedure to check chamber size and occlusion of the PDA (Fig. 5Fig. 5.Postoperative echocardiographic visualization of the device (yellow arrows), using a right parasternal short axis view at the level of the pulmonary trunk.). Thoracic radiographs were obtained for future comparison if needed. The animals were discharged the following day. Recheck echocardiogram and radiographs were performed at one-month post-procedure.

Long-term follow-up information was obtained at one year visit and by phone call at the time of preparation of the manuscript.

Statistical methods

Retrospectively, ratios of echocardiographic and angiographic ostium to device diameter and ampulla to device diameter were calculated off-line. Percentage of constriction of the device were also calculated. Angiographic measurements were performed with digital calibration using the MP catheter. Duration of the procedure was also recorded.

Descriptive statistics were performed using an online commercial software (www.sthda.com). Normality was assessed with Shapiro-Wilk test. Data was summarized with mean and standard deviation when normally distributed, or median and range if not normally distributed. Statistical significance was set at P<0.05.

All devices were set through a jugular venous access, and successful deployment was achieved in all dogs. In one dog with concurrent pulmonic stenosis and PDA, pulmonary valvuloplasty was successfully performed before occlusion of the PDA. The mean duration of the procedure was 68.2 min (Table 1). For all animals, echocardiographic values were stable or improved on the post-procedural echocardiography. Cardiac abnormalities remained stable, except for the dog with severe mitral insufficiency, that developed atrial fibrillation a few weeks later. For the other dogs, normalization of the echocardiographic values was observed after one month.

Post-procedural echocardiography revealed complete closure of the PDA in 15 dogs. In one dog, embolization of the device into the pulmonary artery occurred a few hours after the procedure. This was noticed on Doppler echocardiography, with persistent continuous flow across the ductus, absence of visualization of the prosthesis and confirmation of embolization on thoracic radiographs. No clinical consequences were observed following embolization. Percutaneous retrieval of the device and placement of a larger AVPII was offered but declined by the owner.

Short term outcome was assessed 4 to 6 weeks after the procedure in 15 cases. Complete closure of the PDA was observed in all of them. Long term (>6 months) outcome was available in all cases. All cases showed persistent and complete occlusion of the PDA. Fourteen dogs remained asymptomatic. One dog with atrial fibrillation, was successfully cardioverted under general anesthesia using direct current cardioversion 2 months after the procedure. One dog with severe mitral regurgitation, atrial fibrillation and congestive heart failure was followed for 13 months and euthanized after developing acute kidney failure. The dog in which device embolization occurred was managed medically with pimobendane and was still alive 8 months after the procedure.

Retrospective calculation of ductus echocardiographic measurements (ostium and ampulla) to AVPII diameter ratios are summarized in Table 1. Constrained device size was measured after delivery on angiographic pictures. The mean device/ampulla diameter ratio was 1.28 (SD=0.22). The mean device/ostium ratio was 3.6 (SD=0.86).

Measurements of the device in place revealed a reduction of 1 to 16% of its initial size.

DISCUSSION

Interventional closure of patent ductus arteriosus is currently the method of choice because of its minimally invasive nature and low complication rate compared to surgery [11, 23]. Several devices have been used in this purpose [1, 7, 12, 13, 25], but ACDO® closure is currently the most common technique used in dogs [19, 20]. The criteria for the ideal device for PDA occlusion would be the effectiveness and ease of the procedure, the possibility to treat patients of all sizes, and PDA morphologies. At the time of writing, no device that meets all these criteria has been developed. The ACDO® is probably the device that is the closest to this definition, but the size of the femoral artery is a limiting factor to treat small dogs (the smallest device (3 mm) requires a 5Fr sheath or 6Fr guiding catheter for delivery [20]). A pilot study involving a prototype low-profile ACDO® has been published but this device has not been marketed yet [27]. Coils are more challenging to place than duct occluders, and multiple coils are often needed. They are most commonly used today to treat small dogs (usually <3–4 kg) where ACDO® is not a viable option [13].

Compared to ACDO®, AVPII may be advantageous from different points of view. First, this technique uses a venous access only, which is slightly less invasive than the ACDO® technique requiring most of the time a short surgical time to isolate the femoral artery. So, this technique is truly percutaneous. Second, the venous access allows the treatment of smaller animals than the ACDO® technique, as veins are more compliant than arteries, and allow the use of larger introducers. Our smallest patient was 2.5 kg and it is possible that smaller patients be treated as it is done in infants and reported in another institution [3]. Third, this technique used the ampulla diameter as a guidance for device selection. Being a larger structure, ampulla diameter is more easily measured than the PDA ostium which is sometimes difficult to see on echocardiography or angiography. Further studies would be needed but we could hypothesize this technique could decrease the risk for under or oversizing during device selection. Finally, in some countries, AVPII device might be cheaper than ACDO.

Recently two devices of the Amplatzer family have been tested: the Amplatzer Vascular Plug IV® which was delivered by an arterial approach [15, 16] and the Amplatzer Vascular Plug II which was delivered transvenously [2, 3, 14].

We report here our experience with 16 cases treated with the AVPII device over a period overlapping the studies mentioned above. Our study confirms AVPII is a viable alternative to ACDO and brings some new data compared to the available literature:

i) Description of a modified and simplified technique, using a single catheter to perform angiography and device delivery.

ii) Description of the treatment of both PDA and pulmonic stenosis using a single vessel access.

iii) Description of spontaneous embolization of the device which was not reported before.

i) The methodology used in our case series differs slightly from other studies published on the use of AVPII in dogs [2, 3, 14]. When compared to other studies, our study shows two levels of simplification. First our approach was simplified, as we used only a venous approach, not an arterial and venous approach as in Hildebrandt’s study. Indeed, in our study, angiography was performed retrogradely through the guiding catheter placed in the descending aorta. We need to mention this approach provides less qualitative angiographic images compared to an antegrade aortic injection. Measurements of the PDA may also be affected by the presence of the catheter, decreasing their accuracy. Residual shunting post-embolization could also be missed on the angiographic control performed from the pulmonary artery side if transesophageal or transthoracic echocardiography is not available during the procedure. Despite this limitation, AVPII device seems to provide no residual shunting in almost all cases in our study and others’ (only one case was reported with residual shunting due to mispositioning of the device [14]). This could be explained by the structure of the device, which, with 2 discs in the ductal ampulla could reduce the risk of residual shunting. Another explanation would be that selection of the device is based mainly on ampulla diameter and not only on ostium diameter. This may decrease the risk of error by measuring a structire that is larger than a very small structure as the ostium.

This method yielded certainly less qualitative PDA angiography but was judged sufficient to make adequate measurements in all dogs. Most of the time, selection of the device was based on echocardiographic measurements rather than angiographic measurements. Angiography was used for device selection when adequate imaging of the duct was not acquired with transthoracic or transesophageal echocardiography. In Bagardi’s study [2], device selection was based only on echocardiographic measurements and complete occlusion was achieved in all dogs. Comparison of angiographic and transthoracic echocardiographic measurements were recently analyzed in low-weight children with PDA, and echocardiographic measurements compared favorably with angiographic ones, avoiding the need for arterial access in very low-weight infants [22]. A second level of simplification compared to other studies on AVPII, was that we used the same catheter to perform the angiography and deliver the device, allowing a reduced number of manipulations, hence reducing potentially the duration of the procedure, radiation time and the environmental and economic cost of the procedure.

ii) In our study, seven dogs had comorbidities. Among them, one had a concurrent pulmonic stenosis, which was corrected in the same anesthetic time. Treatment of concurrent PDA and pulmonic stenosis has already been described [6, 26], but with a dual (arterial and venous) access. The technique employed here permitted to use a single jugular venous access to treat both conditions, shortening the procedure. To the author’s knowledge, this technique has not been described in the veterinary literature.

iii) In one case of our series, embolization of the device occurred. Spontaneous device embolization has been described with the ACDO® but not with the AVPII. Undersizing of the device is suspected to be the reason for embolization. In this case, AVPII closure was a rescue procedure following failure of a standard surgery on a very large ductus. Due to Covid-19 pandemic, there was shortage of devices and we used the largest device available in our stock. Looking at the device size/ampulla diameter ratio, one can see that the dog who experienced embolization of the device had a ratio of 1.08 whereas the mean ratio for the other dogs where the procedure was successful was 1.29. However, one can also note that two dogs that were successfully treated had a ratio inferior to that dog (both <1). In these dogs (two French Bulldogs), the ratio device size/ostium was high (4 and 3.75 respectively) whereas it was only of 2.06 in the dog which experienced embolization of the device.

Looking at dogs where the procedure was successful long term, device/ampulla ratios ranged from 0.94 to 1.88. Two dogs had a device/ampulla ratio <1, four dogs had a device/ampulla ratio between 1.1 and 1.2, three dogs had a device/ampulla ratio between 1.2 and 1.3, and six dogs had a device/ampulla ratio >1.3. In these successful procedures, the device/ostium ratio ranged from 2.67 to 6, and for dogs where the device/ampulla ratio was less than 1.2, device/ostium ratio ranged from 3.08 to 4. We can conclude from these observations, that one should aim for a ratio device/ampulla >1.2, but our series shows that dogs with ratios <1.2 can be successfully treated if their device/ostium ratio is >3.08. Other studies on AVPII [2, 3, 14] have also shown that the device is relatively permissive in terms of sizing with successful occlusions with device/ampulla ratio <1. This can be explained by the shape of PDA in dogs where a narrow ostium is most often present, whereas over sizing of 30–50% is recommended in human medicine as AVPII is marketed for peripheral vessels that have no or minimal narrowing. Looking at the literature published in humans for PDA closure with the AVPII, device size selection was based on a device size/minimal ductal diameter ratio >2 and device diameter equal to 2 mm larger than the mid-portion of the ampulla [9, 10, 18, 24]. No case of embolization was reported in these case series, but device “pull-through” was observed in some cases indicating that these guidelines remain indicative. This is confirmed by the fact that these criteria would have been met in the dog of our study that experienced device embolization. Despite the fact that dogs and humans have different ductal morphologies, it appears that selection of the device size has to take into account both the minimal ductal and ampulla diameter, and care should be taken in animals with large ducti and significant flow.

Of course, these observations should be confirmed by collecting a larger number of cases which will allow to formulate stronger statistical data and more precise guidelines.

In conclusion, our study confirms that PDA transvenous closure using the AVPII appears to be a viable alternative to the classic transarterial closure, allowing the treatment of smaller dogs and a fully percutaneous approach. It also describes a single catheter technique to perform both angiography and device delivery, which could simplify the procedure in some dogs.
