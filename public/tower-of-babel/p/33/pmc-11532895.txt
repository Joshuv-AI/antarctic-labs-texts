Lobular carcinoma metastasis to endometrial polyps: Insights from a case report and literature analysis
Authors: Abdelhamid Benlghazi, Hamza Messaoudi, Saad Benali, Imane Tazi, Moulay Mehdi Elhassani, Jaouad Kouach
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction and importanceEndometrial polyps are rare sites of metastatic breast carcinoma. Such cases have mainly been reported in tamoxifen-related polyps.Case presentationWe describe the case of a 56-year-old woman with invasive lobular carcinoma who was experiencing vaginal bleeding. Introduction

Metastatic tumors rarely affect the female reproductive system. The ovaries and vagina are the most frequently affected sites, while the endometrium is a less common site for metastases. Non-uterine cancers that can spread to the endometrium include those of the ovary, breast, gastrointestinal tract, and especially the colon [1]. Metastases to an endometrial polyp (EMP) are even more unusual. Some polyps with metastases have been reported to be associated with tamoxifen use [2]. We present a case of metastatic breast carcinoma in an endometrial polyp in a patient with a history of tamoxifen treatment. This case was presented in accordance with the updated consensus Surgical Case Report (SCARE) guidelines [3].

Case report

We report the case of a 56-year-old post-menopausal patient who presented to the gynecology department with persistent abnormal uterine bleeding of the post-menopausal metrorrhagia type, which had been evolving for a period of two months. The patient also had a significant history of breast cancer, for which she had undergone a left mastectomy with axillary lymph node dissection five years previously.

Pathological examination of the breast tissue removed during the mastectomy showed the presence of grade III infiltrating lobular carcinoma, according to the Scarff-Bloom-Richardson classification. In addition, lymph node involvement was observed,with 19 out of 24 lymph nodes were positive.

An immunohistochemical study was performed to further characterize the breast tumor. The results showed strong positivity for hormone receptors. However, the HER2 receptor was negative. In addition, the cell proliferation marker ki67 was at a level of 25 %.

The patient subsequently underwent adjuvant treatment with six cycles of chemotherapy (3 Fluorouracile, Epirubicine et Cyclophosphamide + 3 Docétaxel), followed by radiotherapy to the chest wall and supra- and sub-clavicular relays (total dose 45 Gray). She also received tamoxifen-based hormone therapy for five years.

No abnormalities were noted on clinical examination, including examination of the mastectomy scar and the right breast. Pelvic ultrasound revealed inhomogeneous endometrial thickening. A sagittal section revealed a suspicious polyp located at the uterine fundus, measuring 20 × 10 mm. No abnormalities were detected on mammography or breast ultrasound.

Diagnostic hysteroscopy confirmed a 25 mm bloody endocavitary polyp implanted on the hyper-vascularised uterine fundus, and a directed biopsy was performed (Fig. 1). The anatomopathological analysis revealed the presence of an endometrial polyp characterized by large cells exhibiting anisokaryotic, vesicular, and nucleolated nuclei. Immunohistochemistry results showed CKAE1/AE2 positivity; E-cadherin, GATA3, and estrogen receptors were positive; Mamaglobin was negative; progesterone receptors were negative; and HER2 score was 0 (Fig. 2).

These factors suggested cancerous spread from the breast in the form of an endometrial polyp.MRI of the pelvis and lumbar-aortic region revealed a lesion in the endometrium, with no extension to the myometrium, and no lymph node involvement was observed. This lesion was classified as stage IA (Fig. 3). A PET scan performed to assess the extent of the disease revealed no other lesions.

Following the multidisciplinary meeting, the medical team decided to perform a total hysterectomy with bilateral adnexectomy.

The patient underwent the operation. Her post-operative recovery was uneventful and favorable.

The definitive antomopathological study showed the presence of a polypoid formation measuring 17 × 5 × 15 mm corresponding to the uterine location of a lobular type breast carcinoma.

The multidisciplinary consultation committee recommended initiating hormonal treatment for the patient. It established a clinical follow-up plan, with assessments every three months for the first year and every six months for the following two years. As of now, the patient has not exhibited any signs of recurrence after 18 months of monitoring.

Discussion

Metastatic malignant tumors in the female reproductive tract generally originate from cancers of the breast, stomach, and colon. The ovary and vagina are the sites most frequently affected by these metastases. In the uterus, the metastatic process mainly involves myometrium rather than the endometrium [1]. Although endometrial deposits are rare, they tend to be symptomatic, often manifesting as uterine bleeding that may be mistaken for tamoxifen side effects. Therefore, breast cancer patients with abnormal uterine bleeding must be examined to rule out metastatic endometrial involvement [2].

We conducted a literature search and found that metastasis to an endometrial polyp is extremely rare, with only 17 documented cases of metastatic breast carcinoma involving EMP.

Invasive lobular carcinoma of the breast is the most likely to metastasize to the female reproductive tract. Of the seventeen documented cases of breast carcinoma metastasizing to an EMP, nine were lobular, seven ductal, and one apocrine. In thirteen cases, the endometrial polyp was associated with tamoxifen use. One case did not involve tamoxifen treatment, and the other three reports did not specify whether the patients were taking tamoxifen.

An absence of association with tamoxifen is extremely rare. The histological characteristics of tamoxifen-associated polyps have been widely documented in the literature [4].

The typical image of tamoxifen-related polyps is multiple cystically dilated glands with metaplasia of the lining epithelium, fibrous stroma, and peri glandular stromal condensation [5]. The microscopic features of our case were similar.

Regarding the risk and prognosis of endometrial cancer after tamoxifen treatment, it is important to note that metastases from breast carcinoma generally spread via the arterial route. Thus, the presence of uterine involvement often indicates advanced disease and extensive metastases, as was observed in our case. There are, however, a few exceptions where metastases are confined to the uterus. The time between the initial diagnosis of breast cancer and the detection of metastases in an endometrial polyp can vary from three to seventy-two months [6].

The risk and prognosis of endometrial cancer after tamoxifen treatment was investigated by Bergman et al. in a nationwide case-control study. This research revealed a significant incidence of endometrial cancer in the years following the start of treatment. Furthermore, the prognosis of endometrial cancers in women with long-term tamoxifen use is less favorable, due to less favorable histology and more advanced stage of diagnosis. However, the survival benefits of tamoxifen for breast cancer far outweigh the increase in endometrial cancer mortality [7].

Conclusion

Ultimately, our report underscores the critical need for meticulous microscopic evaluation of all endometrial polyps, particularly in older women, to avoid overlooking the possibility of hidden, albeit rare, metastatic disease that is clinically significant. The common association of lobular breast carcinoma in these instances is particularly noteworthy, as its cells can be difficult to differentiate from stromal or inflammatory cells due to their small size, uniform appearance, and lack of tubular or nest-like structures.
