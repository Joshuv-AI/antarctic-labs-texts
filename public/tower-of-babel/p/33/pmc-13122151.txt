Early Individualized Psychological Intervention Using Virtual Reality-assisted Decision System Reduces Decisional Conflict in Patients with Pulmonary Nodules: A Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Yuhang Hu, Qiong Wu, Xidong Jin, Hao Meng, Shumin Wang, Dazhi Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo investigate the impact of early individualized psychological intervention utilizing a virtual reality-assisted decision system (VR-ADS) on preoperative decisional conflict and postoperative recovery quality in patients with pulmonary nodules.MethodsA prospective, single-center, randomized controlled trial enrolled 304 surgical patients with pulmonary nodules (June 2022–June 2024). Patients were randomized into an early intervention group (n = 155) and a conventional intervention group (n = 149). The early intervention group received individualized psychological intervention within 24–48 hours after diagnosis, incorporating decision-making style assessment, VR-ADS, and a decision partner mechanism. The primary endpoint was the Decisional Conflict Scale (DCS) score. Secondary endpoints included Hamilton Anxiety Scale (HAMA), Hamilton Depression Scale (HAMD), EORTC QLQ-C30, postoperative pain, chest tube duration, hospital stay, pulmonary function, and complication rates over a 6-month follow-up.ResultsBaseline characteristics were comparable between groups (p > 0.05). The early intervention group showed significantly lower DCS total scores (32.4 ± 12.8 vs. 45.7 ± 15.3, p < 0.001). Intervention timing was the strongest independent predictor of decisional conflict (β = −0.412, p < 0.001). Preoperative HAMA and HAMD scores were significantly lower in the early intervention group (both p < 0.001), with sustained improvement through follow-up. The early intervention group also demonstrated lower postoperative pain scores, shorter chest tube duration (2.8 ± 1.2 vs. 3.6 ± 1.5 days, p = 0.008), fewer hospital days (5.2 ± 1.8 vs. 6.7 ± 2.3 days, p < 0.001), lower complication rates (12.9% vs. 22.6%, p = 0.032), higher decision satisfaction (85.7 ± 12.3 vs. 78.2 ± 15.6, p < 0.001), and lower decision regret rates (8.6% vs. 18.3%, p = 0.014).ConclusionEarly individualized psychological intervention combined with VR-ADS reduces preoperative decisional conflict, improves psychological status, promotes postoperative recovery, and enhances quality of life in patients with pulmonary nodules.

INTRODUCTION

Systematic reviews indicate that the overall detection rate of pulmonary nodules has reached 27%, and even as high as 50% in certain high-risk populations [1]. Studies show that the proportion of patients with reported pulmonary nodules on chest CT examinations increased from 38−50% between 2008 and 2019 [2]. Although most pulmonary nodules are benign lesions, 0.7−6% of patients still develop lung cancer during follow-up.

Pulmonary nodules are defined as round or oval opacity lesions within the lung parenchyma with a diameter less than 30 mm, completely surrounded by aerated lung tissue, without associated atelectasis, hilar lymphadenopathy, or pleural effusion [3]. Epidemiological studies show that the detection rate of solitary pulmonary nodules ranges from 2−24% in the general population and 17−53% in high-risk screening populations [4].

The management of pulmonary nodules faces complex clinical decision-making challenges. On one hand, there is a need to identify potential malignant lesions promptly to improve patient outcomes; on the other hand, excessive examination and treatment may impose unnecessary medical burden and patient suffering. This uncertainty often causes patients to experience significant psychological distress and decisional conflict when facing surgical decisions. Decisional conflict refers to the psychological state that occurs when individuals face choices involving risk or uncertain outcomes, requiring evaluation of potential benefits and risks corresponding to each option, weighing decision values, and preparing to accept losses from rejected options [5].

Lung cancer, as the second most common malignant tumor globally and a leading cause of cancer-related deaths, presents particularly prominent psychological burden during diagnosis and treatment [6]. Studies show that the incidence of psychological distress in lung cancer patients is approximately 40%, significantly higher than in patients with other types of cancer. For patients with pulmonary nodules, although malignancy has not been confirmed, the uncertainty of the disease and fear of cancer subject them to similar psychological challenges.

Preoperative psychological status has profound effects on postoperative recovery in lung cancer patients. Research has found that lung cancer patients with preoperative psychological distress demonstrate more severe symptom burden and poorer self-perceived health status within one year postoperatively, including significant deterioration in functional status and sleep quality [7]. Studies further indicate that 30.1−49.6% of postoperative non-small cell lung cancer patients experience anxiety or depression, 6−52% have decreased quality of life, and these psychological problems may reduce treatment adherence and even negatively impact treatment outcomes [8].

Shared decision making (SDM), as a core element of patient-centered care, holds special importance in the management of pulmonary nodules. SDM is defined as a process in which healthcare providers and patients collaborate to make optimal medical decisions for patients [9]. Research demonstrates that effective SDM can improve patient understanding of treatment options, reduce decisional conflict, and increase patient active participation in the decision-making process. A systematic review showed that providing decision support tools to patients can significantly improve patient knowledge levels, reduce decisional conflict, and enhance patients’ positive role in the decision-making process [10].

Individualized psychological intervention represents a key advancement beyond conventional standardized education. While traditional preoperative education typically delivers uniform information to all patients regardless of their psychological characteristics or decision-making preferences, individualized intervention tailors support based on patients’ specific cognitive styles, coping strategies, and social support levels. This personalized approach acknowledges that patients differ substantially in how they process medical information, manage uncertainty, and engage in decision-making. By categorizing patients according to their decision-making styles and providing targeted support, individualized intervention can more effectively address each patient’s unique psychological needs and enhance their capacity to make informed treatment decisions.

Virtual reality (VR) technology has emerged as a promising tool to enhance patient understanding and engagement in medical decision-making. VR-assisted decision systems can provide three-dimensional, immersive visualizations of surgical procedures and treatment outcomes, allowing patients to gain intuitive understanding of complex medical information. Research indicates that VR technology can significantly improve patients’ comprehension of surgical plans and reduce uncertainty in the decision-making process. Unlike traditional two-dimensional educational materials, VR systems can present personalized information tailored to individual patients’ clinical conditions, helping them form more realistic expectations about treatment options and potential outcomes. The integration of VR technology with individualized psychological intervention may therefore offer synergistic benefits in reducing decisional conflict and improving patient engagement.

Psychological intervention, as an important means to improve patients’ preoperative decision-making capacity and postoperative recovery quality, involves mechanisms at multiple levels. Cognitive behavioral stress management (CBSM), as an important psychological treatment method, improves mental health and quality of life by helping patients recognize and manage stress. Studies demonstrate that CBSM intervention can significantly improve anxiety, depression, and quality of life in non-small cell lung cancer patients [11].

The timing of psychological intervention has important effects on intervention outcomes. Early intervention can provide support during critical stages of patients’ psychological adaptation process, helping patients establish effective coping strategies and reduce decision-related psychological distress. In contrast, conventional preoperative education usually occurs a few days before surgery, when patients’ psychological states may have already solidified, resulting in relatively limited intervention effects [12].

Currently, research on the impact of psychological intervention timing on decisional conflict and postoperative recovery in patients with pulmonary nodules remains limited. This study aims to explore the impact of early individualized psychological intervention utilizing a virtual reality-assisted decision system (VR-ADS) on the degree of preoperative decisional conflict and postoperative recovery quality in patients with pulmonary nodules through rigorous randomized controlled trial design.

METHODS

Study Design and Ethical Review

This study employed a prospective, single-center, randomized controlled trial design, strictly adhering to the Declaration of Helsinki and international medical research ethical guidelines. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of General Hospital of Northern Theater Command (ethics approval number: 2025-062), and registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry. All patients participating in the study signed informed consent forms after fully understanding the study purpose, methods, potential risks, and benefits.

Study Population and Inclusion/Exclusion Criteria

The study population consisted of patients with pulmonary nodules treated at the Department of Thoracic Surgery in our hospital from June 2022 to June 2024. Inclusion criteria included: age 18 to 75 years, regardless of sex; high-resolution chest CT showing single or multiple pulmonary nodules with maximum diameter ≤ 30 mm; basic Chinese reading and writing ability, capable of understanding and completing relevant scale assessments; expected survival ≥ 12 months; voluntary participation in the study and signing of informed consent.

Exclusion criteria were: previous history of confirmed malignant tumors; concurrent severe mental illness or cognitive dysfunction, unable to cooperate with research assessments; extremely high expected surgical risk, American Society of Anesthesiologists classification ≥ Grade IV; pregnant or lactating women; receipt of formal psychological treatment or anti-anxiety/depression medications within the past 3 months; concurrent other diseases requiring surgical treatment; expected inability to complete 6-month follow-up; refusal to participate in the study or withdrawal of informed consent.

Sample Size Calculation and Randomization

Based on preliminary trial results, the clinically meaningful difference in the primary endpoint Decisional Conflict Scale (DCS) score was set at 8 points, with a standard deviation of 15 points. Setting α = 0.05, β = 0.2, test power 1 − β = 0.8, using two-sided testing. According to the sample size calculation formula for comparing means between two independent samples, at least 112 patients were needed in each group. Considering a 20% dropout rate, the final total sample size was determined to be 304 patients.

Stratified block randomization was used for grouping to ensure balance in baseline characteristics between the two groups. Stratification factors included age (< 60 years vs. ≥ 60 years), nodule size (< 20 mm vs. ≥ 20 mm), and education level (high school and below vs college and above). SAS 9.4 software (SAS Institute) was used to generate random number tables, with an independent statistician responsible for random sequence generation and allocation concealment. Research nurses obtained corresponding randomization numbers and group information according to patient enrollment order, allocating patients to either the early intervention group or conventional intervention group. To ensure allocation concealment, randomization sequences were sealed and stored in opaque envelopes, which could only be opened after patients completed baseline assessments.

Blinding

Due to the nature of the psychological intervention, it was not feasible to blind participants or intervention providers to group allocation. However, outcome assessors responsible for collecting scale-based measurements (DCS, Hamilton Anxiety Scale [HAMA], Hamilton Depression Scale [HAMD], European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality of Life Core Questionnaire [EORTC QLQ-C30]) were blinded to group assignment throughout the study period. Data analysts were also blinded to group allocation until the completion of statistical analyses. Clinical outcome measures such as chest tube duration, hospital length of stay, and complication rates were objective and recorded by clinical staff who were not directly involved in the intervention delivery.

Intervention Protocol Design

Early intervention group protocol

Patients in the early intervention group received individualized psychological intervention within 24 to 48 hours after diagnosis of pulmonary nodules and decision for surgical treatment. The intervention protocol was constructed based on the Transtheoretical Model and Self-Determination Theory, including the following elements:

First, an individualized assessment phase was implemented, using the Medical Decision Making Style Scale (MDMSS) combined with the Social Support Rating Scale (SSRS) and Simplified Coping Style Questionnaire (SCSQ) for comprehensive evaluation. The MDMSS scale contains 20 items, assessing patients’ style preferences in medical decision-making, divided into three types: active participation, collaborative participation, and passive acceptance. The SSRS scale evaluates patients’ social support status, containing 10 items covering three dimensions: objective support, subjective support, and utilization of support. The SCSQ scale evaluates patients’ coping styles, containing 20 items divided into two dimensions: positive coping and negative coping.

Based on the comprehensive evaluation results from the three validated scales, patients were classified into four decision-making types using the following systematic approach: (1) rational decision-making type was defined as patients with MDMSS active participation scores ≥ 15 (out of 20) combined with SCSQ positive coping scores ≥ 30 (out of 40), indicating preference for evidence-based information and logical analysis; (2) emotion-dominated type was identified when patients scored ≥ 14 on HAMA or ≥ 8 on HAMD at baseline assessment, combined with SCSQ negative coping scores ≥ 20, reflecting anxiety-driven decision patterns; (3) support-dependent type was characterized by MDMSS passive acceptance scores ≥ 12 and SSRS total scores < 35, indicating reliance on family and healthcare providers; and (4) conflict-avoidant type was assigned when patients showed MDMSS collaborative scores in the middle range (10−14) combined with high decisional conflict (DCS screening score > 40 at initial assessment), reflecting difficulty engaging with decision-making. These classification criteria were established through a pilot study of 50 patients prior to the main trial and were applied consistently by trained assessors. Each patient was assigned to one primary decision-making type based on the dominant pattern identified through this algorithm.

Differentiated intervention strategies were implemented for different decision-making types. For rational decision-making patients, focus was placed on providing evidence-based medical evidence support, including pulmonary nodule malignancy risk prediction models, benefit-risk comparisons of different treatment options, and domestic and international authoritative guideline recommendations. For emotion-dominated patients, emphasis was placed on emotional counseling and psychological support, using cognitive behavioral therapy techniques to help patients identify and modify irrational cognitions and establish positive coping strategies. For support-dependent patients, family and social support network building was strengthened, inviting family members to participate in the decision-making process and providing peer support and patient communication platforms. For conflict-avoidant patients, gradual information disclosure and decision support tools were used to reduce decision pressure and provide step-by-step decision guidance.

A VR-ADS was introduced, which simulates the entire surgical process, allowing patients to intuitively understand surgical steps, possible complications, and postoperative recovery processes. The VR system, combined with patients’ specific conditions, provides personalized displays of expected effects of different treatment options, helping patients form clearer treatment expectations. The VR content was tailored according to decision-making types: for rational decision-making patients, the VR system emphasized quantitative surgical outcome data, complication rates, and evidence-based comparisons between treatment options; for emotion-dominated patients, the VR focused on visualizing successful recovery trajectories and positive patient testimonials to reduce anxiety; for support-dependent patients, the VR included simulated family consultation scenarios and peer support narratives; for conflict-avoidant patients, the VR provided step-by-step guided exploration with pause-and-reflect prompts to facilitate gradual decision engagement.

Simultaneously, a “decision partner” mechanism was established, matching each patient with a professionally trained volunteer (recovered patients or family members of recovered patients) to provide peer support and experience sharing. Decision partners underwent standardized training comprising 8 hours of instruction on active listening, empathetic communication, boundary maintenance, and recognizing situations requiring referral to healthcare professionals. Partners were matched with patients based on similarity in demographic characteristics (age, sex) and clinical features (nodule size, surgical approach) when possible. Each decision partner maintained contact with their matched patient through at least two face-to-face meetings (30−45 minutes each) and was available for telephone consultation as needed throughout the preoperative period.

Interventions were implemented by specialized nurses with formal psychological training backgrounds. All intervention nurses held national psychological counselor certifications (Level 2 or 3) and had completed additional specialized training in oncology psychology and decision support interventions. To ensure intervention consistency and quality, all nurses completed a 16-hour standardized training program covering the theoretical framework, assessment procedures, type-specific intervention protocols, VR equipment operation, and communication techniques. Intervention fidelity was monitored through regular case discussions, random audio recording reviews (with patient consent), and monthly group supervision sessions led by a clinical psychologist. Each intervention session lasted 60 to 90 minutes, and 1 to 3 individualized intervention sessions were arranged according to patient needs and assessment results.

Conventional intervention group protocol

Patients in the conventional intervention group received standardized preoperative education and psychological care from the Department of Thoracic Surgery. Three to seven days before surgery, uniform health education was provided by responsible nurses, including basic disease knowledge, surgical procedure introduction, preoperative preparation items, and postoperative precautions. Psychological care used traditional one-on-one communication methods, focusing on patients’ emotional states and providing general psychological support and comfort. Educational materials used standardized written materials and videos, not adjusted according to individual patient differences. The entire intervention process lasted 20 to 30 minutes, mainly focusing on information delivery.

Outcome Measure Assessment

Primary outcome indicator

The Chinese version of the DCS was used to assess patients’ degree of decisional conflict. This scale has undergone rigorous reliability and validity testing, with Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.916 and test-retest reliability of 0.892. The scale contains 16 items divided into 5 dimensions: uncertainty (4 items), informed (3 items), values clarity (3 items), support (3 items), and effective decision (3 items). A 5-point Likert scale was used for scoring (1 = strongly agree, 5 = strongly disagree), with total scores converted to 0−100 points; higher scores indicate more severe decisional conflict. Assessment time point was 1 day preoperatively.

Secondary outcome indicators

Psychological status assessment used the HAMA and HAMD. HAMA contains 14 items, with total scores 0 to 56 points, ≥ 14 points indicating anxiety symptoms. HAMD contains 17 items, with total scores 0 to 52 points, ≥ 8 points indicating depressive symptoms. Assessment time points were at enrollment, 1 day preoperatively, 1 week postoperatively, and 1 month postoperatively.

Quality of life assessment used the Chinese version of the EORTC QLQ-C30. This scale contains 30 items covering global health status, functional domains (physical function, role function, cognitive function, emotional function, social function), and symptom domains (fatigue, pain, nausea and vomiting, etc.). Higher scores in functional domains and global health status indicate better functional status and quality of life; higher scores in symptom domains indicate more severe symptoms. Assessment time points were at enrollment, 1 month postoperatively, 3 months postoperatively, and 6 months postoperatively.

Postoperative recovery indicators included: postoperative pain intensity assessed using the Numerical Rating Scale, 0 to 10 points, assessed at 24 hours, 48 hours, and 72 hours postoperatively; chest tube duration recorded until removal; hospital length of stay calculated from admission to discharge; pulmonary function measured using spirometry, recording forced vital capacity (FVC), forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1), and other indicators, assessed 1 day preoperatively, 1 week postoperatively, 1 month postoperatively, and 3 months postoperatively; postoperative complications including pulmonary infection, pleural effusion, cardiac arrhythmia, wound infection, etc., followed up to 30 days postoperatively.

Quality Control Measures

All medical staff participating in the study received uniform training, including study protocol interpretation, scale usage methods, intervention technique specifications, etc., and could only participate in the study after passing examinations. Training content included VR equipment operation, psychological intervention techniques, standardized scale assessment procedures, etc., with training duration not less than 16 hours. Standardized operating procedures were established with detailed specifications for each research stage, including patient screening, randomization, intervention implementation, data collection, follow-up management, etc.

RESULTS

Patient Characteristics and Study Flow

From June 2022 to June 2024, a total of 347 patients with pulmonary nodules were assessed for eligibility. After applying inclusion and exclusion criteria, 304 patients were randomized into the study, with 155 patients assigned to the early intervention group and 149 patients to the conventional intervention group. During the 6-month follow-up period, 8 patients in the early intervention group and 12 patients in the conventional intervention group were lost to follow-up, resulting in completion rates of 94.8% and 91.9%, respectively. No significant differences were observed in baseline characteristics between the two groups (Table 1). The mean age of participants was 59.0 ± 12.1 years, with 142 (46.7%) being male. Most patients (58.9%) had education levels of high school or below. The average nodule size was 18.0 ± 7.1 mm, with 253 (83.2%) patients having single nodules. No statistically significant differences were found between groups in terms of demographic characteristics, clinical parameters, or social support levels (all p > 0.05). The distribution of patients across the four decision-making types was: rational decision-making type (n = 78, 25.7%), emotion-dominated type (n = 92, 30.3%), support-dependent type (n = 81, 26.6%), and conflict-avoidant type (n = 53, 17.4%). The proportions were similar between the early intervention group and conventional intervention group (p = 0.624).

Primary Outcome: Decisional Conflict

The primary analysis revealed significant differences in decisional conflict scores between the two groups. Patients in the early intervention group demonstrated substantially lower DCS total scores compared to the conventional intervention group (32.4 ± 12.8 vs. 45.7 ± 15.3, p < 0.001). Analysis of individual DCS subscales showed consistent improvements across all dimensions in the early intervention group (Table 2).The effect sizes (Cohen’s d) for DCS subscales ranged from 0.51 to 1.31, indicating moderate to large clinical effects. The uncertainty subscale showed the largest effect size (d = 1.31), followed by the informed subscale (d = 1.23).

Multiple Linear Regression Analysis

Multiple linear regression analysis was performed with DCS total score as the dependent variable and age, sex, education level, nodule size, number of nodules, family income, social support, previous surgery history, number of comorbidities, and intervention timing as independent variables. The results demonstrated that intervention timing was the most significant independent factor influencing decisional conflict (standardized coefficient β = −0.412, p < 0.001), with early intervention reducing DCS scores by an average of 11.3 points (95% CI: −14.2 to −8.4). Other significant factors included education level (β = −0.298, p < 0.001), social support level (β = −0.235, p < 0.01), and nodule size (β = 0.186, p < 0.05). The overall model showed good fit with R2 = 0.547, adjusted R2 = 0.531, F = 34.28, p < 0.001 (Table 3).

Secondary Outcomes

Psychological status

Psychological assessments using HAMA and HAMD scales revealed significant improvements in the early intervention group. At baseline, no significant differences existed between groups (HAMA: 11.2 ± 4.3 vs. 11.6 ± 4.7, p = 0.432; HAMD: 9.8 ± 3.6 vs. 10.1 ± 3.9, p = 0.521). However, preoperative assessments showed significantly lower anxiety and depression scores in the early intervention group (HAMA: 7.3 ± 3.2 vs. 11.6 ± 4.1, p < 0.001; HAMD: 6.8 ± 2.9 vs. 10.2 ± 3.8, p < 0.001). These improvements persisted through postoperative follow-up periods (Supplementary Table 1; available online, Fig. 1). Notably, a transient increase in HAMA scores was observed at one week postoperatively in both groups, likely reflecting the acute stress response associated with surgical recovery and early mobilization. However, the early intervention group maintained significantly lower anxiety levels compared to the conventional group even during this period (9.1 ± 3.5 vs. 12.8 ± 4.3, p < 0.001) (1-month postoperative: HAMA 6.8 ± 3.1 vs. 9.7 ± 3.9, p < 0.001; HAMD 6.2 ± 2.7 vs. 8.9 ± 3.4, p < 0.001).

Postoperative recovery outcomes

Significant differences were observed in multiple postoperative recovery indicators. The early intervention group demonstrated lower pain scores, shorter chest tube duration, and reduced hospital length of stay. Postoperative complications were also less frequent in the early intervention group (Table 4).

Pulmonary function recovery

Pulmonary function assessments showed better recovery patterns in the early intervention group. While baseline measurements were comparable between groups (FEV1% predicted: 89.3 ± 12.4 vs. 88.7 ± 13.1, p = 0.682; FVC% predicted: 91.2 ± 11.8 vs. 90.6 ± 12.3, p = 0.654), the early intervention group demonstrated superior recovery at 1-week and 1-month postoperative assessments (1-week: FEV1% 78.4 ± 10.2 vs. 72.6 ± 11.8, p < 0.001; FVC% 80.1 ± 9.7 vs. 75.3 ± 11.2, p = 0.001; 1-month: FEV1% 84.7 ± 9.3 vs. 79.2 ± 10.7, p < 0.001; FVC% 86.2 ± 8.9 vs. 81.4 ± 10.1, p < 0.001; 3-month: FEV1% 87.9 ± 8.6 vs. 84.1 ± 9.8, p = 0.002; FVC% 89.1 ± 8.2 vs. 85.7 ± 9.4, p = 0.004) (Fig. 2). Complete pulmonary function data across all time points are presented in Supplementary Table 2 (available online).

Quality of life assessment

EORTC QLQ-C30 assessments revealed sustained improvements in quality of life for the early intervention group across multiple domains and follow-up periods (Table 5). Statistically significant differences (p < 0.05) were observed between groups across most quality of life domains from 1-month post-operation onwards, with differences maintained through the 6-month follow-up period.

Subgroup Analysis

Subgroup analyses based on patient characteristics revealed that the benefits of early intervention were consistent across different demographic and clinical subgroups. However, the magnitude of effect varied, with larger benefits observed in patients with higher education levels, stronger social support, and smaller nodule sizes (Fig. 3). These subgroup trends support the value of individualized intervention, as patients with different baseline characteristics showed varying degrees of response to the intervention. While formal subgroup analyses by decision-making type were not conducted due to sample size limitations, the overall pattern suggests that tailored psychological support may be particularly beneficial for patients with specific risk profiles.

Safety and Feasibility

No serious adverse events related to the psychological interventions were reported in either group. The VR-assisted decision system was well-tolerated, with only 3 patients (1.9%) in the early intervention group experiencing mild motion sickness that resolved within 10 minutes. The completion rate for the full intervention protocol was 94.2% in the early intervention group.

Long-term Follow-up Outcomes

At 6-month follow-up, patients in the early intervention group continued to show superior outcomes in psychological adjustment and quality of life measures. The decision satisfaction scores, measured using the Satisfaction with Decision scale, were significantly higher in the early intervention group (85.7 ± 12.3 vs. 78.2 ± 15.6, p < 0.001). Additionally, fewer patients in the early intervention group reported decision regret (8.6% vs. 18.3%, p = 0.014) (Fig. 4).

DISCUSSION

This study investigated the impact of individualized psychological intervention timing on preoperative decisional conflict and postoperative recovery quality in patients with pulmonary nodules. The results demonstrate that early implementation of individualized psychological intervention can reduce patients’ decisional conflict, improve psychological status, promote postoperative recovery, and continuously enhance quality of life.

Our study found that the early intervention group had significantly lower total decisional conflict scores compared to the conventional intervention group, a finding highly consistent with recent systematic research results. Zhang et al. [13] demonstrated that patients with pulmonary nodules commonly experience moderate to severe decisional conflict, with an average DCS score of 37.35 ± 16.65 points, and decisional conflict was significantly positively correlated with patients’ psychological distress levels. The decisional conflict scores in our early intervention group were markedly lower than this baseline level, suggesting the effectiveness of early individualized psychological intervention.

Analysis of individual dimensions of decisional conflict revealed that the uncertainty dimension showed the most significant improvement, which may be closely related to the VR-ADS employed in this study. VR technology significantly enhances patients’ understanding of treatment options by providing three-dimensional visualization of surgical procedures and expected outcomes [14,15]. Research confirms that VR technology can help patients understand surgical plans more clearly and significantly reduce uncertainty in the decision-making process [16]. This study innovatively combined VR technology with individualized psychological intervention, not only providing intuitive visual information but, more importantly, offering personalized presentations based on patients’ cognitive characteristics and decision-making styles, thereby more effectively reducing decision uncertainty.

Multiple linear regression analysis showed that intervention timing was the most important independent factor affecting decisional conflict, a finding that supports the core concept of readiness for change in the Transtheoretical Model. According to the Transtheoretical Model, individuals have different cognitive and emotional characteristics at different stages of change, and early intervention can provide appropriate support when patients are in the contemplation or preparation stage, promoting their transition to the action stage [17,18]. The individualized intervention protocol constructed in this study based on Self-Determination Theory enhanced patients’ intrinsic motivation by satisfying their needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness, thereby reducing decisional conflict [19,20].

In this study, the early intervention group showed significantly lower anxiety and depression scores preoperatively compared to the conventional group, and this improvement effect persisted through the postoperative follow-up period. From a neuroscience perspective, psychological intervention may exert its effects by regulating the activity of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis. Chronic stress conditions with excessive HPA axis activation lead to elevated cortisol levels, which subsequently affect immune function and tissue repair processes [21,22].

Cognitive behavioral therapy techniques, as an important component of psychological intervention in this study, have been proven to improve emotional regulation ability by reshaping brain neural network connections. Functional magnetic resonance imaging studies show that cognitive behavioral therapy can enhance prefrontal cortex regulation of the amygdala, thereby reducing fear and anxiety responses [23,24]. The emotional counseling and cognitive restructuring techniques included in the individualized psychological intervention in this study may function through similar neural regulatory mechanisms.

Furthermore, strengthening social support is also an important therapeutic mechanism. The decision partner mechanism introduced in this study activated the oxytocin system through peer support, a neuroendocrine system closely related to trust, attachment, and social connection [25,26]. Oxytocin release not only reduces stress responses but also promotes tissue repair and immune function recovery, which may partially explain the improvement in postoperative recovery indicators in the early intervention group.

This study found that early psychological intervention significantly improved patients’ postoperative recovery indicators, including reduced pain scores, shorter chest tube duration, fewer hospital days, and lower complication rates. These results are consistent with recent research findings on the impact of psychological factors on postoperative recovery. Downey et al. [27] conducted a large-scale cohort study demonstrating that postoperative complications have sustained negative impacts on patients’ quality of life, while psychologically well-prepared patients had significantly lower postoperative complication rates.

The improvement in pain perception may be related to psychological intervention’s regulatory effects on pain processing neural networks. Pain is not only a physiological sensation but also a complex psychosocial experience. Anxiety and depression states amplify pain perception through dysfunction of the descending pain modulation system [28,29]. Early psychological intervention in this study may have enhanced the function of the endogenous pain modulation system by improving patients’ psychological status, thereby reducing postoperative pain.

The reduction in hospital length of stay and chest tube duration reflects accelerated overall patient recovery. This improvement may be related to psychological status’s regulatory effects on immune function and inflammatory responses. Research indicates that positive psychological states can promote the release of anti-inflammatory cytokines and reduce pro-inflammatory mediator production, thereby accelerating tissue healing processes [30,31]. The reduced postoperative complication rates in the early intervention group further support the positive impact of psychological intervention on immune function.

It should be acknowledged that the comparison groups in this study differed not only in intervention timing, but also in content and intensity. The early intervention group received a comprehensive, individualized intervention program that integrated decision-making style assessment, VR-assisted decision support, and peer decision partners within 24 to 48 hours of diagnosis. In contrast, the conventional group received standardized preoperative education 3 to 7 days before surgery without these individualized components. Therefore, the observed benefits likely reflect the combined effects of earlier timing, personalized content, and enhanced intervention delivery, rather than timing alone. This integrated approach represents a clinically meaningful advancement in perioperative psychological care, and future research with factorial designs may help disentangle the relative contributions of timing, individualization, and specific intervention components.

This study has certain limitations that need to be considered when interpreting results. First, this was a single-center study with samples primarily from urban tertiary hospitals, which may limit the generalizability of findings. Future multicenter studies are needed, including patient populations from different regions and economic levels, to validate the universality of intervention effects. Second, although this study’s follow-up period was 6 months, it may still be insufficient for evaluating long-term effects of psychological intervention. The psychological status and quality of life of patients with pulmonary nodules may undergo longer-term changes, and future studies should consider extending follow-up time to 1 to 2 years to comprehensively evaluate the sustainability of intervention effects. Third, although this study controlled for major confounding factors, unobserved bias may still exist. For example, patients’ personality characteristics, previous psychological trauma experiences, and family support quality may affect intervention outcomes. Future studies should consider including more psychological variables to more accurately evaluate intervention mechanisms. Fourth, the intervention protocol allowed for flexibility in the number of sessions (1−3) and session duration (60−90 minutes) based on individual patient needs, which may have introduced heterogeneity in intervention exposure. While this variability was intentional to accommodate the individualized approach, it represents a potential source of bias. Future research should consider conducting sensitivity analyses or subgroup comparisons among patients receiving similar intervention doses to better understand the dose-response relationship. Fifth, although subgroup analyses suggested consistent benefits across different patient characteristics, formal analyses stratified by decision-making type were not performed due to sample size constraints. Future larger-scale studies should examine whether specific decision-making types benefit differentially from tailored intervention strategies to further refine the individualized approach.

In conclusion, through randomized controlled trial design, this study systematically confirmed the beneficial effects of early individualized psychological intervention on preoperative decisional conflict and postoperative recovery quality in patients with pulmonary nodules. The results demonstrate that individualized psychological intervention strategies based on the Transtheoretical Model and Self-Determination Theory, combined with innovative VR-assisted decision systems, can effectively reduce patients’ decisional conflict, improve psychological status, promote postoperative recovery, and continuously enhance quality of life. These findings provide important evidence-based support for optimizing perioperative psychological care in patients with pulmonary nodules and hold significant implications for advancing precision medicine and humanized healthcare development.
