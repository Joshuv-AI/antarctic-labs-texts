Bee sting-induced hypervirulent Klebsiella pneumoniae infection causing fulminant necrotizing fasciitis: case report and narrative review
Authors: Yi Hu, Dongyue Lyu, Hanyu Sha, Meng Xiao, Shuai Qin, Linxuan Yang, Jun Zhang, Gang Li, Deming Tang, Ran Duan, Huaiqi Jing, Xin Wang, Tao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Necrotizing fasciitis induced by hypervirulent Klebsiella pneumoniae (hvKp) represents a rare yet life-threatening soft tissue infection, with an increasing incidence observed in recent years. This condition is characterized by rapid progression and a high mortality rate, thereby posing a serious risk to patient health. This article presents a case of acute necrotizing fasciitis of the right upper limb caused by an infection of hypervirulent K. pneumoniae following a bee sting. Additionally, it reviews the current research on K. pneumoniae-associated necrotizing fasciitis, emphasizing its pathogenic mechanisms, clinical manifestations, and diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. Clinicians are encouraged to exercise heightened awareness regarding skin wound infections, especially those arising from outdoor activities, and to undertake timely surgical evaluation and debridement when necrotizing infection is suspected. Diagnostic efforts should prioritize the combined implementation of the string test, molecular typing, and virulence gene detection to facilitate the swift identification of the hvKp.

Introduction

Klebsiella pneumoniae is an important Gram-negative enteric bacterium and a common opportunistic pathogen in humans. It was first described in 1875 and formally named in 1886 [1]. Classical K. pneumoniae (cKp) has long been recognized as a major cause of nosocomial infections. However, in recent decades, a distinct hypervirulent pathotype has emerged and attracted increasing attention.

Hypervirulent K. pneumoniae (hvKp) was first clinically described in 1986 in Taiwan, China, in patients with pyogenic liver abscess and metastatic complications [2]. Early observations noted that hvKp isolates frequently exhibited a hypermucoviscous phenotype in vitro, which led to the initial designation of “hypermucoviscous” or “hypervirulent” strains. The string test—performed by stretching a bacterial colony with an inoculation loop—was widely used as a simple screening method; a positive result (≥5 mm viscous string) suggested the presence of hvKp [3]. However, accumulating evidence indicates that the string test has limited specificity for identifying hypervirulent strains and should be interpreted in conjunction with molecular typing and virulence gene analysis [4].

HvKp infections have attracted widespread attention from the World Health Organization [5] due to their increasing prevalence. Compared with classical K. pneumoniae, hvKp is more likely to cause severe infections in healthy individuals, often accompanied by metastatic infections, leading to complex and uncontrollable conditions. Recent studies indicated associations of hvKp with various conditions, including liver abscesses, lung abscesses, ocular infections, and—as reported in our previous investigation—renal abscesses [6,7]. The ability of hvKp to form biofilms and produce a protective capsule enhances its resistance to phagocytosis and immune clearance mechanisms. This characteristic not only promotes bacterial adhesion and aggregation [4], but also obstructs the penetration of antimicrobial agents, fosters bacterial colonization and persistent infections [8].

Necrotizing fasciitis is a critical soft-tissue infection characterized by rapid dissemination of the infection along the subcutaneous fascia, resulting in significant tissue necrosis. The management of necrotizing fasciitis poses considerable challenges and, without proper control, the condition can lead to life-threatening complications [9]. We here present a case of sepsis associated with acute necrotizing fasciitis of the right forearm, which was attributed to an hvKp infection.

Case report

Case presentation

A 20-year-old female with no underlying diseases developed right forearm swelling after being stung by Apis cerana in an outdoor setting. The initial swelling was localized to the sting site and resolved spontaneously, with minimal pain and no rash, fever, or cough. Therefore, she did not seek medical attention at that time. Approximately five days after the sting, the swelling reappeared at the same site and was accompanied by local warmth and a burning sensation (Fig. 1A). Topical mercurochrome was then obtained from a primary clinic and applied locally without any oral or systemic medication. However, no significant improvement was observed. Given that stingers of Apis cerana are barbed and often remain embedded in the skin after stinging, the possibility of residual stinger components persisting locally cannot be excluded. Over the following weeks, the swelling and burning pain persisted and progressively extended from the forearm to the right upper limb. After one month of progressive symptoms, the patient presented to a hospital in Ningxia, China, presenting with a fever of 38 °C, a productive cough with white viscous sputum, swelling in the right upper limb, edema in both lower limbs, wheezing, dyspnea, and oliguria. Upon admission, laboratory tests showed a white blood cell count of 1.85 × 109/L, neutrophil percentage of 80%, lymphocyte count of 0.26 × 109/L, procalcitonin levels exceeding 100 ng/mL, and myoglobin levels greater than 900 ng/mL. A chest CT scan revealed bilateral pneumonia, pericardial effusion, and bilateral pleural effusions (Fig. 1B). The initial diagnoses were septic shock, cellulitis, pulmonary infection, and multiple organ failure. The patient underwent emergency pericardiocentesis with catheter drainage, which alleviated her chest tightness and dyspnea. Empirical anti-infective treatment was initiated with imipenem (1 g every 12 h) and linezolid (600 mg daily), alongside fluid therapy.

Significant swelling of the right upper limb developed (Fig. 1C), with blood cultures identifying K. pneumoniae. Within 24 h, diffuse hemorrhagic bullae appeared on the right limb (Fig. 1D), and concurrent wound drainage cultures confirmed K. pneumoniae. The two isolates tested positive in the string test (Fig. 1E). Despite multiple emergency debridement and decompression surgeries revealing significant exudate and tissue tension (Fig. 1F), K. pneumoniae persisted in wound cultures and even in pericardial effusion. Susceptibility testing demonstrated that the K. pneumoniae isolates were sensitive to cephalosporins, carbapenems, ciprofloxacin, tigecycline, and other antibiotics, prompting initiation of targeted therapy with meropenem and linezolid. Given the biofilm-forming propensity of hvKp and the risk of enzyme-mediated resistance, the regimen was adjusted to meropenem plus ciprofloxacin and later to ceftazidime-avibactam (post-treatment chest CT, Fig. 1G). However, progressive limb necrosis and multi-organ dysfunction necessitated amputation. The amputation was performed approximately 10 cm below the greater tubercle of the humerus (Fig. 1H). Postoperatively, with continued anti-infective therapy and nutritional support, the patient gradually improved and was discharged after 71 days of comprehensive treatment.

Bacterial investigation

To identify the characteristics of the strains, whole-genome analysis was conducted on the K. pneumoniae isolated from this case. Average nucleotide analysis revealed 100% homology between the K. pneumoniae strains isolated from the patient's blood (BioSample accession: SAMN46159633; size: 5,470,624 bp; GC content: 57.24%) and the wound drainage fluid (BioSample accession: SAMN46159635; size: 5,470,472 bp; GC content: 57.24%), indicating a high degree of genetic similarity. Both strains were classified as sequence type 86 (ST86), with capsular serogroup (K antigen) K2 and lipopolysaccharide serotype (O antigen) O1. The global prevalence of hvKp is predominantly characterized by increasing detection of strains such as ST86-K2, ST11-K64, and ST23-K1 [2,3]. The ST86-K2 K. pneumoniae possessed several virulence-associated genes, including the K2-specific gene k2A; rmpA2, responsible for capsule synthesis regulation; the lipopolysaccharide-related virulence genes uge and wabG; the biofilm production genes luxS, pgaA [10]; the fimbrial genes fimH and mrk; and the siderophore virulence genes entB, irp2, iroBCDN, and iucABCD. Phylogenetic analysis based on multilocus sequence typing and capsular typing demonstrated that the K. pneumoniae isolates from this case clustered within the same branch as the ST86-K2 hvKp strain (Fig. 1I). Comparative genomic analysis against representative multidrug-resistant strains in the Virulence Factor Database (including ST23-K1 and ST11-K125) revealed that the clinical isolates carried a complete repertoire of virulence genes, with even greater abundance of virulence determinants (Fig. 1J). In conclusion, the strains were confirmed as hvKp based on a combination of clinical manifestations, string test results, molecular typing, and virulence gene detection. While the phenotypic susceptibility profiles indicated broad-spectrum sensitivity, genomic analysis detected the presence of SHV-1 (a β-lactamase), fosA (conferring fosfomycin resistance), and OqxA (a plasmid-mediated quinolone resistance determinant) genes, suggesting potential for developing resistance mutations.

Discussion

Klebsiella pneumoniae is increasingly recognized as a cause of necrotizing fasciitis, a rapidly progressing and life-threatening soft tissue infection. Necrotizing fasciitis often develops following trauma or metastatic infection, with the extremities being the most commonly affected sites [11,12]. It can also manifest in diverse anatomical regions, including the periorbital area, upper lip, neck, psoas muscle, or perineum [[13], [14], [15], [16]]. While traditionally rare and mostly reported in East Asia—a geographic clustering potentially linked to the endemic distribution of specific strains and host genetic susceptibility [17,18]—sporadic cases with severe outcomes have recently been documented in Europe and North America, suggesting global dissemination of this pathogen [19].

Immunocompromised individuals, particularly those with diabetes mellitus, constitute the most vulnerable population. Poorly controlled diabetes significantly elevates infection risk, as hyperglycemia impairs neutrophil chemotaxis and macrophage activity, creating favorable conditions for bacterial invasion [20,21]. Beyond diabetes, patients with underlying conditions such as cirrhosis, malignancies, or those on long-term immunosuppressive therapy also demonstrate heightened susceptibility [17,21]. These individuals are more prone to hematogenous dissemination and distant abscess formation upon infection, exacerbating disease severity [22,23].

The worldwide prevalence of hvKp is on the rise, with particular emphasis in the literature on the increasing detection of strains such as ST86-K2, ST11-K64, and ST23-K1 [2,3]. The hvKp strain involved in this case was also identified as ST86-K2, which is prevalent in Asia [6] and is often associated with severe human infections [24]. Additionally, an ST35-K108 hvKp strain was isolated from the renal abscess drainage fluid of a patient in our recent study [6,7], underscoring the expanding diversity of hvKp in terms of multilocus sequence typing and capsular serotypes. The hvKp strain in this case was found to carry the capsular gene k2A, the mucoid phenotype-associated gene rmpA, and genes encoding siderophores, all of which are closely associated with hypervirulence and the potential for metastatic infections [25]. This case demonstrates that the dual detection of sequence type and virulence genes can facilitate the rapid identification of hvKp infections. Therefore, relying solely on MLST sequence typing for assessing hypervirulent strains is insufficient, and comprehensive detection combining sequence typing, string test, and virulence gene analysis is crucial for rapid diagnosis.

The pathological mechanisms of K. pneumoniae necrotizing fasciitis involve complex interactions between bacterial virulence factors and host immune responses. The bacteria's unique capsular polysaccharides and biofilm formation create physical barriers that effectively resist neutrophil phagocytosis and complement-mediated killing [17,22]. Virulence genes such as k2A and rmpA upregulate capsule synthesis, while iron acquisition systems (iucABCD for aerobactin, entABCDEF for enterobactin, ybtA for yersiniabactin, and allABCDS for salmonelin) competitively sequester host iron to support rapid bacterial proliferation [14,19,26]. These multifaceted virulence strategies enable the pathogen to spread rapidly through hypoxic fascial layers while evading immune surveillance. During tissue destruction, bacterial-secreted elastases and hyaluronidases play pivotal roles. These enzymes directly degrade extracellular matrix components and disrupt microvascular structures, leading to local ischemia and necrosis [26,27]. Consequently, the hyperglycemic environment in diabetics, which compromises immune function and causes microvascular complications leading to poor tissue perfusion, significantly increases infection risk [11,13,20].

The pathogen also carries multiple virulence island gene clusters. The stfBD gene (fimbrial adhesion determinant) upregulates type III fimbriae expression, enhancing endothelial cell adhesion [18,28]. Its specific invasive characteristics enable hematogenous spread and formation of metastatic foci, particularly in the liver and lungs [12,22]. Notably, hvKp has the capacity to induce severe infections even in healthy individuals and can lead to invasive and metastatic infections. The patient in this case was a healthy young woman with no pre-existing medical conditions prior to the infection. A skin wound resulting from a bee sting provided an entry point for K. pneumoniae to penetrate the skin barrier, while the wound microenvironment during tissue repair may have facilitated bacterial persistence. Furthermore, bees may encounter various bacterial sources in their natural environment, including sewage, soil, and plant surfaces [29]. Due to the delayed presentation and the inability to obtain samples from the bee or the initial wound, the exact source of hvKp could not be determined. However, the clinical course allows for a reasonable inference. Five days after the sting, swelling recurred with local warmth and a burning sensation. Over the following weeks, symptoms persisted and progressively extended from the forearm to the upper limb, without fever or systemic signs—strongly suggesting local bacterial colonization at the sting site for weeks before dissemination. Thus, infection originating from the wound appears more likely.

Another possibility cannot be entirely excluded: the patient may have developed hvKp pneumonia and bacteremia from another source, with the prior sting site—where local microcirculation was altered by the earlier sting and had not fully recovered—becoming secondarily seeded during bacteremia, leading to the explosive development of necrotizing fasciitis. Regardless of whether the hvKp originated from the bee or from subsequent environmental exposure, the bee sting provided a portal of entry for bacterial invasion, and the wound microenvironment during tissue repair may have further facilitated bacterial persistence. Therefore, clinicians should maintain high vigilance for skin wound infections, particularly those originating from outdoor environments, and should perform immediate debridement along with comprehensive clinical evaluation.

K. pneumoniae infection triggers excessive inflammatory responses, with massive release of proinflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6, leading to microvascular thrombosis and "cytokine storm" that accelerates tissue necrosis [23]. This pathophysiological change aligns with the "biphasic injury" pattern characteristic of necrotizing fasciitis: an initial phase dominated by direct bacterial toxicity followed by a secondary phase of systemic damage primarily mediated by host hyperinflammatory responses [13]. In our study patient, the clinical course strongly suggests that the initial phase most likely represented persistent local infection at the sting site. As antibiotics could not penetrate the established bacterial biofilm, local antimicrobial therapy showed limited efficacy, potentially contributing to the subsequent rapid progression to necrotizing fasciitis, bacteremia [30], and other systemic infections, including pulmonary involvement, pericardial effusion, and multiple organ failure.

The standard management protocol includes fluid resuscitation, antimicrobial therapy, urgent surgical debridement of necrotic tissues, and supportive care for compromised organ systems [31]. Laboratory markers such as significantly elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) and procalcitonin (PCT) levels often indicate severe infection and can serve as important diagnostic references [21]. Local debridement alone is frequently insufficient for infection control, necessitating systemic antibiotic therapy [28]. Carbapenems like meropenem remain first-line treatments, but regimens should be adjusted based on antimicrobial susceptibility testing for resistant strains, with common alternatives including tigecycline and polymyxin [30]. Clinical studies show that combination therapy with β-lactams and aminoglycosides can produce synergistic effects, while clindamycin is often used adjunctively for its antitoxin properties [11,30]. Notably, for patients with metastatic infections like liver abscesses, percutaneous drainage combined with antibiotics significantly improves outcomes [12,23].

According to current clinical guidelines [[31], [32], [33]], surgical intervention in suspected necrotizing soft tissue infection should not be delayed until the onset of fever or systemic toxicity. Instead, prompt surgical exploration is recommended when there is strong clinical suspicion, based on features such as rapidly progressive swelling, pain disproportionate to physical findings, bullae formation, crepitus, or failure to respond to initial antimicrobial therapy. Early surgical source control is critical, as delays are associated with increased mortality. Although the patient initially presented with localized symptoms, retrospective review of the clinical course suggests that progressive and persistent local manifestations may have already emerged during the pre-hospital period. At the time of admission, the presence of severe limb swelling, hemorrhagic bullae, and systemic deterioration strongly supported the diagnosis of a severe soft tissue infection, prompting immediate surgical intervention. This highlights the importance of early recognition and timely surgical management in similar cases.

In this case, despite appropriate antibiotic therapy guided by susceptibility testing and timely debridement, the hvKp infection continued to spread - biofilm formation impeded drug penetration, while microcirculatory failure from necrotizing fasciitis further limited antibiotic distribution. Importantly, hvKP strains demonstrate strong biofilm-forming capacity. The isolate in this study was found to carry biofilm-related regulatory genes. This biofilm-producing capability significantly enhances bacterial pathogenicity and drug resistance, creating substantial treatment challenges. Even for strains sensitive to multiple drugs, once biofilm forms, antibiotic susceptibility decreases markedly, resulting in poor treatment response and increased risk of persistent/recurrent infections [30]. Given the invasive nature of hvKp infections (with high amputation rates for KP infections), combined with observed proximal spread of necrotic lesions and the young patient's developing multiple organ dysfunction, limb amputation ultimately became necessary for infection control. Although initial antimicrobial susceptibility testing showed sensitivity to multiple drugs, implementing a dynamic multidrug regimen after debridement and amputation helped minimize further resistance development.

In this study, the clinical course of the patient with this hvKp infection can be divided into two phases: an initial phase of persistent local infection and a later phase of rapid necrotizing fasciitis development. The persistent local infection phase was characterized by right forearm swelling lasting nearly one month after the bee sting, representing a prolonged period of pathogen implantation and host immune confrontation. The bee sting served as an entry portal for K. pneumoniae [29], while the wound microenvironment during tissue repair may have facilitated bacterial persistence. The patient neglected the condition and delayed seeking medical attention. Although their initial immune response may have temporarily controlled the infection, it did not completely eliminate the pathogen. Due to inability of drugs to penetrate established bacterial biofilms, topical antimicrobial therapy demonstrates limited efficacy, leading to a potential risk of localized hvKp infection that may progress to necrotizing fasciitis. Moreover, the bacteria can enter the bloodstream, resulting in bacteremia [34], and other systemic infections, including pulmonary infection, pericardial effusion, and multiple organ failure. Therefore, clinicians should be maintain a high index of suspicion for skin wound infections, especially those originating from wild environments, and should perform prompt debridement alongside comprehensive clinical evaluation.

While the ST86-K2 strain is frequently characterized as hypervirulent, reliance on the assessment based solely on the sequence type is not sufficiently reliable. An ST35-K108 hvKp strain was isolated from the renal abscess drainage fluid of a patient in our recent study [6,7], underscoring the expanding diversity of hvKp in terms of multilocus sequence typing and capsular serotype. The strains in this study harbor multiple virulence genes, which correlate with their high pathogenicity in clinical manifestations. The hvKp were found to carry the capsular gene k2A, the mucoid phenotype-associated gene rmpA, as well as a complete set of siderophore-encoding genes including iucABCD (Aerobactin), entABCDEF (Ent siderophore), ybtA (Salmochelin), and allABCDS (Yersiniabactin), all of which are closely associated with hypervirulence and the potential for metastatic infections [25]. Notably, the strains possessed the rare ureBG genes (encoding the α-subunit of urease) and stfBD genes (fimbrial adherence determinants), which may collectively enhance its invasive potential. Therefore, the dual detection of sequence type and virulence genes can facilitate the rapid identification of hvKp infections.

The clinical manifestations and isolated pathogen collectively confirmed the diagnosis of necrotizing fasciitis and sepsis affecting multiple organs. Necrotizing fasciitis caused by hvKp is a rare but extremely severe condition, characterized by high invasiveness and mortality [18]. This aggressive soft tissue infection spreads rapidly along subcutaneous fascia, resulting in significant tissue necrosis. The management of necrotizing fasciitis poses considerable challenges and, without proper control, the condition can lead to life-threatening complications [9]. The standard management protocol includes fluid resuscitation, antimicrobial therapy, urgent surgical debridement of necrotic tissues, and supportive care for compromised organ systems [31]. Raising public awareness of the importance of seeking timely medical care for non-healing wounds may reduce delays in treatment and the risk of secondary infection. In addition, it is particularly important to improve diagnostic awareness among primary healthcare workers, who are the first point of contact for such patients, as early identification and intervention can effectively prevent the progression of local infection to severe systemic disease.
