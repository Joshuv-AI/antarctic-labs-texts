Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances and adolescent bone mineral density: assessing periods of susceptibility
Authors: Jessie P Buckley, Katherine M Marquess, Joseph M Braun, Antonia M Calafat, Kim M Cecil, Aimin Chen, Bruce P Lanphear, Halley Wasserman, Yingying Xu, Kimberly Yolton, Jordan R Kuiper
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractContextPer- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) may affect adolescent bone mineral density (BMD), but few studies have examined periods of susceptibility.ObjectiveTo assess associations of repeated serum PFAS concentrations with early adolescent BMD.MethodsAmong 218 children in a prospective pregnancy and birth cohort, we quantified serum concentrations of perfluorohexane sulfonic acid (PFHxS), perfluoroctane sulfonic acid (PFOS), perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA), and perfluorononanoic acid (PFNA) at delivery (cord) and ages 3, 8, and 12 years. We measured BMD at 6 skeletal sites using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry at age 12 years. We estimated covariate-adjusted differences and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) in BMD Z-scores per interquartile range (IQR) increase in each PFAS at each time point using multiple informant linear regression models.ResultsHigher PFOA concentrations were associated with lower 1/3 distal radius BMD at every time point: delivery (β: −0.39; 95% CI: −0.69, −0.10), 3 years (β: −0.36; 95% CI: −0.86, 0.14), 8 years (β: −0.54; 95% CI: −0.91, −0.16), and 12 years (β: −0.40; 95% CI: −0.75, −0.04). Higher 3-year PFHxS and PFOS concentrations were significantly associated with higher whole body, total hip, and femoral neck BMD Z-scores, while higher 12-year PFNA concentrations were associated with lower BMD Z-scores. Associations of 12-year PFAS with lower 1/3 distal radius BMD were stronger among females than males.ConclusionSerum PFOA concentrations from delivery to age 12 years were associated with lower 1/3 distal radius BMD in early adolescence, while associations of other PFAS with BMD varied by exposure assessment time point, skeletal site, and sex.

Achieving optimal bone mineral accrual during adolescence is important for lifelong bone health and prevention of fractures and osteoporosis in adulthood [1]. Bone mineral accrual begins during the fetal period [2] and proceeds rapidly during adolescence; peak mineral accretion rates occur at an average age of 12.5 years for females and 14.1 years for males [3] and almost 40% of total body bone mineral is acquired during the 4 years surrounding peak accretion rates [4]. Lifestyle factors such as calcium intake and physical activity increase bone mineral accretion [5], while exposure to environmental chemicals such as per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) may be detrimental [6].

Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances are a class of >4000 fluorinated compounds used in a myriad of industrial and consumer products for their stain- and water-resistant properties, with human exposures occurring through consumer products, indoor air, dust, drinking water, seafood, and proximity to contaminated sites [7]. Perfluorohexane sulfonic acid (PFHxS), perfluorooctane sulfonic acid (PFOS), perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA), and perfluorononanoic acid (PFNA) are widely detected in serum samples from the US general population [8, 9]. These 4 PFAS are persistent in the environment and have biological half-lives in humans of 2 to 9 years [10, 11]. Moreover, some PFAS have been detected in human bone tissue or marrow [12-16]. Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances may influence bone accrual and maintenance by competitively binding with the vitamin D receptor [17, 18] to reduce bone mineralization [18], by acting as peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR) agonists [19, 20], or through other endocrine-related pathways such as sex steroid or thyroid hormone disruption [21-26]. Four toxicological studies reported that PFOA, PFOS, or PFHxS exposure in mice caused decreases in bone mineral density (BMD) measured using micro-computed tomography [27-30].

In humans, higher serum or plasma PFAS concentrations have been associated with lower BMD and bone mineral content (BMC) during childhood or adolescence in prospective studies of prenatal and early postnatal exposures [31-35] and in cross-sectional studies of childhood exposures [36-38]. Three studies have evaluated PFAS concentrations at >1 time point, and all reported stronger associations of postnatal compared with prenatal or infancy PFAS concentrations with whole-body BMD at age 3 [35], 7 [33], or 9 [34, 39] years, suggesting that children's bone health may be more impacted by childhood than early life exposures. Still, prior studies examining postnatal PFAS exposures measured BMD before adolescence, a critical period for bone accrual. Understanding periods of heightened susceptibility to PFAS may aid in targeting interventions to reduce PFAS exposures during relevant life stages, given that certain sources of PFAS exposure change over the course of early life. For example, major PFAS sources include maternal–fetal transfer across the placenta during gestation [40, 41], exposure through breastfeeding during infancy [42, 43], and ingestion through diet [44] and drinking water [45] in later childhood and adolescence.

We aimed to assess associations of 4 PFAS and their mixture measured in children's serum collected at delivery (cord) and at ages 3, 8, and 12 years with BMD assessed at age 12 years in the Health Outcomes and Measures of the Environment (HOME) Study. Based on prior literature and the ongoing nature of bone turnover and growth, we hypothesized that serum PFAS concentrations measured in later childhood (ie, ages 8 and 12 years) would have stronger associations with lower BMD than earlier measures (ie, delivery and age 3 years). In secondary analyses, we explored differences in periods of susceptibility by adolescent's sex given our prior findings that associations of maternal gestational serum concentrations of certain PFAS with BMD were sex specific at some skeletal sites [31].

Materials and methods

Study participants

We used data from the HOME Study, a prospective pregnancy and birth cohort study that recruited women from 9 prenatal practices affiliated with 3 hospitals in Cincinnati, OH, from March 2003 to January 2006 [46, 47]. Eligibility criteria for enrollment were: ≥18 years of age; 13 to 19 weeks gestation; living in a residence built prior to 1978 that was not a mobile/trailer home; HIV negative; and not receiving seizure, thyroid, or chemotherapy/radiation medications; intention to continue prenatal care and deliver at a collaborating clinic; English fluency; no diagnosis of diabetes, bipolar disorder, or schizophrenia; and no cancer requiring radiation or chemotherapy. There were 1263 eligible pregnant women; 468 of these were enrolled and 401 were retained in the study at delivery. There were 67 women who dropped from the study prior to delivery, and 11 of these were re-enrolled at a later study visit. Of these women, 398 gave birth to singleton live-born infants. Participants were invited to participate in a study visit at age 12 years and 242 singletons completed the visit [46]. For the current study, we excluded participants that did not complete dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) scans at the 12-year visit (n = 19) and those without serum PFAS concentrations measured at least once at delivery or at age 3, 8, or 12 years of age (n = 5). Thus, our final sample size was 218 (a flowchart detailing study eligibility and enrollment is shown in Fig. S1 [48]).

The Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Cincinnati Children's Hospital Medical Center (CCHMC) and collaborating hospitals approved the HOME Study protocol and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) deferred to CCHMC IRB as the IRB of record. The analysis of de-identified children's 12-year serum specimens at the CDC laboratory was determined not to constitute engagement in human subject research. Women provided written informed consent for themselves and their children, and adolescents provided written informed assent at the 12-year study visit.

Serum PFAS quantification

Among our study sample, we collected venous blood samples from children at delivery (cord blood; n = 121) and ages 3 (n = 136), 8 (n = 165), and 12 (n = 192) years as well as from mothers during clinic visits at ∼16 weeks gestation (n = 182), ∼26 weeks gestation (n = 15), and within 48 hours of delivery (n = 9). We stored serum at −80 °C before shipment on dry ice to the CDC laboratory for quantitation of PFAS concentrations using on-line solid-phase extraction coupled with high-performance liquid chromatography-isotope dilution tandem mass spectrometry [49]. Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substance concentrations have previously been shown to be stable in frozen serum [50]. Reagent blanks, serum blanks, and high- and low-concentration quality control materials were included in each analytic batch. Quality control material coefficients of variation were ∼6%. Limits of detection (LOD) were 0.2 ng/mL for PFOS at delivery, age 3, and age 8 years and 0.1 ng/mL for all other PFAS and time points. As most PFAS were detected in >98% of serum samples, we replaced concentrations below the LOD with the LOD divided by the square root of 2 [51]. In serum samples collected at age 12 years, we measured concentrations of linear and branched isomers of PFOS (n-PFOS and perfluoromethylheptane sulfonic acid isomers) and PFOA (n-PFOA and branched PFOA isomers), which we summed to obtain the total concentrations of PFOS and PFOA, respectively, that were used in the analysis.

Bone mineral density measures

Trained technicians conducted triplicate measures of height (Ayrton Stadiometer) and weight and performed DXA scans of the whole body, lumbar spine, hip, and radius (Hologic Horizon densitometer) at a clinic visit occurring at approximately age 12 years. We analyzed scans to estimate BMC (g) and areal BMD (BMD = BMC/projected area, g/cm2) using Apex 5.5 software and calculated bone mineral apparent density (BMAD, g/cm3) of the spine following published methods [52]. Next, we calculated age-, sex-, and race-standardized Z-scores for whole body (less head), lumbar spine, total hip, femoral neck, 1/3 distal radius, and ultradistal radius BMD and spine BMAD using reference ranges from the Bone Mineral Density in Childhood Study (BMDCS) [52-54]. We used height adjustment to prevent bias from the influence of bone size on 2-dimensional DXA measurements. The Z-score calculations relied on maternal reports of the child's race (Black or non-Black). We focused analyses on BMD rather than BMC because BMD tracks more strongly across time during skeletal and pubertal maturation [55] and because precision error for BMD is lower compared with BMC at all sites [56].

Covariates

We identified confounders and precision variables using a directed acyclic graph (DAG) based on prior literature (Fig. S2 [48]). Using computer-assisted interviews and medical chart reviews, trained research assistants collected information on mothers and children during prenatal, perinatal, and/or postnatal study visits. We collected information on maternal characteristics, including age at delivery, self-identified race/ethnicity, household income at enrollment, parity at enrollment, mid-pregnancy body mass index (BMI), and duration of human milk feeding up to the age 3-year follow-up visit. For maternal mid-pregnancy BMI, we internally standardized to calculate Z-scores. We quantified concentrations of lead in whole blood [57] and cotinine in serum [58] from maternal pregnancy or delivery samples and calculated the average of available log2-transformed concentrations. We measured child anthropometry at every visit and calculated age- and sex-standardized BMI Z-scores using CDC reference standards [59].

As described previously [60, 61], we collected adolescent characteristics at the 12-year visit including age, healthy eating index (HEI) score, Physical Activity Questionnaire for Older Children (PAQ-C) score, and Tanner Stage. We calculated HEI 2015 scores ranging from 0 (least healthy) to 100 (most healthy) [62, 63] based on the average of up to three 24-hour dietary recalls [64]. We calculated the PAQ-C based on a self-administered 7-day recall of age-appropriate moderate to vigorous physical activities during the school year [65]. Adolescents used standardized instruments for Tanner stage self-assessment based on degree of pubic hair development (Tanner Stages I–V) [66].

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics

We calculated the proportion of participants within categories of discrete variables and the mean and SD of continuous variables in the study sample, the full HOME Study cohort, and among participants with PFAS measures at each exposure assessment time point (delivery, 3 years, 8 years, and 12 years). We calculated the mean (SD) BMD Z-score for each skeletal site by levels of covariates. For each PFAS time point, we calculated the detection frequency of each PFAS as well as the geometric mean (GM), geometric SD (GSD), and percentiles of PFAS concentrations. We calculated Pearson correlations among BMD Z-scores at the 6 skeletal sites (overall and by sex) as well as Spearman correlations (due to skewed distributions) among PFAS concentrations within and across time points.

Periods of susceptibility to individual PFAS

We used a multiple informants approach to assess for periods of heightened susceptibility [67]. Specifically, we fit separate covariate-adjusted linear regression models for each PFAS time point with each BMD outcome. Then, we applied a seemingly unrelated regression equations approach, with cluster-based sandwich estimation of standard errors, to perform Wald F-tests for global differences in effect estimates across time points, as previously described [68]. We used log2-transformed PFAS concentrations standardized by the interquartile range (IQR) to facilitate comparisons.

In adjusted models, we included predictors of PFAS exposure or BMD that were not on the causal pathway according to our DAG (Fig. S2 [48]). For all models, we included maternal age at delivery, self-identified race/ethnicity (White non-Hispanic; all other races and/or Hispanic ethnicity), household income at enrollment, parity (nulliparous, primiparous, and multiparous), average blood lead concentration during pregnancy, average serum cotinine concentration during pregnancy, and maternal mid-pregnancy BMI Z-score as well as child's sex and age, HEI score, and PAQ-C score at the 12-year follow-up visit. For the 3-, 8-, and 12-year and area under the curve (AUC) models, we also adjusted for duration of human milk feeding in weeks; we did not include this variable in delivery models because it may be on the causal pathway for this time point [69] (Fig. S2 [48]). For all analyses, we multiply imputed missing covariate information via chained equations, resulting in 50 imputed datasets, and combined using Rubin's rules.

Periods of susceptibility to PFAS mixtures

For each time point, we estimated the overall effect (Ψ) of a simultaneous IQR increase in serum concentrations of all 4 PFAS using a parametric, generalized linear model-based implementation of g-computation. We fit models using the R package qgcomp using a similar approach to quantile-based g-computation [70], but with continuous exposure variables (scaled by the IQR) instead of quantized exposure variables (ie, “q=_null_”). Similar to single-PFAS analyses, models for each time point were restricted to participants with measured PFAS concentrations at that time point.

Cumulative exposure to individual PFAS

We estimated the cumulative exposure to each PFAS from delivery through early adolescence by calculating the AUC of concentrations from delivery to age 12 years (ng/mL × months) as described in the Supplementary Methods [48]. In subsequent analyses, we log2 transformed and IQR scaled each PFAS AUC variable and estimated associations with BMD Z-scores using covariate-adjusted linear regression models with multiply imputed data as described for primary analyses.

Cumulative exposure to PFAS mixtures

We used the g-computation approach described above to estimate the effect on BMD Z-scores of a simultaneous IQR increase in all 4 PFAS at all 4 time points (ie, a simultaneous increase in 16 variables). We estimated the overall effect (Ψ) and weights that represent the contribution of each PFAS at each time point to the positive or negative partial effects. For each dataset, the negative weights sum to 1.0 and the positive weights sum to 1.0. As we used 50 imputed datasets and weights cannot be combined using Rubin's rules, we report descriptive statistics of the weights from the 50 datasets as median (IQR).

Sex-specific associations

We assessed associations of individual PFAS concentrations, AUCs, and PFAS mixtures with BMD outcomes in models stratified by child's sex using the modeling approaches described above. For AUC models, we tested effect measure modification (EMM) by child's sex using a 2-sample Z-test [71].

Sensitivity analyses

Primary analyses did not adjust for pubertal stage [61] or child BMI [68, 72, 73] because they may be affected by PFAS exposure and thus may mediate associations of PFAS with BMD. In sensitivity analyses, we conducted models as above but with additional adjustment for continuous pubic hair Tanner stage (all time points) or child BMI Z-score at the same visit as the PFAS time point (3-, 8-, and 12-year models only).

We conducted analyses in Stata version 15 and R version 4.3.1. We determined statistical significance as P < .05 for main effects. For periods of susceptibility and EMM by sex, we selected a less conservative significance threshold of P < .1 to identify potential differences despite lower statistical power for these tests [74].

Results

Descriptive statistics

At enrollment, mothers in our sample were predominantly non-Hispanic White (58%), had an annual household income ≥$40 000 (59%), and were parous (58%; Table 1). Maternal baseline characteristics of the study sample were similar to the full HOME Study cohort (Table S1 [48]). Adolescents in the study were fed any human milk on average for 23 weeks (SD: 24 weeks) with an average BMI at age 12 years of 20.9 kg/m2 (Table 1). Bone mineral density Z-scores tended to be slightly lower among males than females, and more females attained later stages of pubertal development compared with males (Table S2 [48]). Pearson correlations of BMD Z-scores by skeletal site ranged from 0.45 for 1/3 distal radius and spine to 0.91 for total hip and femoral neck (Table S3 [48]). BMD Z-scores were generally lower among those whose mothers were non-Hispanic White, age ≥35 years at delivery, or had a mid-pregnancy BMI <25 compared with other groups (Table S4 [48]). BMD Z-scores were lower among those who had not reached higher stages of pubertal development, who had longer durations of human milk feeding, and among those with lower PAQ-C scores (Table S4 [48]).

For PFHxS, PFOS, and PFNA, the magnitude and direction of associations differed by time point and skeletal site (Table 2). Delivery associations for these PFAS were null except for an association of PFHxS with higher ultradistal radius BMD Z-score (β per IQR increase in PFHxS: 0.35, 95% CI: 0.07, 0.62). The PFHxS, PFOS, and PFNA concentrations measured at the 3-year time point were generally associated with higher BMD Z-scores for the whole body less head (Fig. 3B), total hip, femoral neck, and ultradistal radius (Table 2). In contrast, 8- and 12-year concentrations of PFHxS, PFOS, and PFNA tended to be associated with lower BMD Z-scores (Table 2). Higher 8- and 12-year PFOS concentrations were significantly associated with lower 1/3 distal radius BMD (Fig. 2C and 2D), and 12-year PFNA concentrations were significantly associated with lower whole body, total hip, femoral neck, and ultradistal radius BMD Z-scores (Table 2; Fig. 3D).

Sex-specific associations for individual PFAS

In unadjusted and adjusted multiple informant models stratified by sex, time point differences in PFAS-BMD Z-score associations were more evident among females (Tables S9 and S10 [48]) than males (Tables S11 and S12 [48]). Adjusted associations of PFOA with lower 1/3 distal radius Z-scores were strongest at 3 years for males (β: −0.93; 95% CI: −1.59, −0.27; Fig. 2B) vs at 12 years for females (β: −0.74; 95% CI: −1.26, −0.21; Fig. 2D). Among females, 12-year PFOS, PFOA, PFNA, and the PFAS mixture were significantly associated with lower 1/3 distal radius BMD Z-scores (Fig. 2D, Table S10 [48]).

For other skeletal sites, adjusted estimates among females mirrored the pattern found in the overall sample, with 3-year PFHxS, PFOS, and PFNA being generally associated with higher BMD Z-scores (Fig. 3B, Table S10 [48]). Adjusted associations among males were mainly null for these other skeletal sites with the exceptions of 3-year PFOA with lower ultradistal radius BMD (β: −0.93; 95% CI: −1.59, −0.27; Table S12 [48]) as well as 8-year PFHxS with lower whole body less head BMD (β: −0.29; 95% CI: −0.55, −0.03; Fig. 3C) and spine BMAD (β: −0.45; 95% CI: −0.83, −0.07; Table S12 [48]) Z-scores.

Cumulative exposure to individual PFAS

Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substance AUCs were not associated with differences in BMD Z-scores, apart from the association between the PFOA AUC with 1/3 distal radius BMD Z-score (β per IQR increase in PFOA AUC: −0.27; 95% CI: −0.52, −0.02; Table S13 [48]). In sex-stratified analyses, PFAS AUCs tended to be weakly associated with lower BMD Z-scores in males and higher BMD Z-scores in females, though few sex-stratified estimates were statistically significant and all EMM P-values were >.1 (Table S13 [48]). An IQR increase in the PFOA AUC was associated with 0.40 (95% CI: −0.73, −0.07) lower 1/3 distal radius and 0.39 (95% CI: −0.75, −0.02) lower ultradistal radius BMD Z-scores among males while estimates among females were null (Table S13 [48]).

Periods of susceptibility to PFAS mixtures

Estimates of the overall effect (Ψ) on BMD Z-scores of a simultaneous IQR increase in all serum PFAS concentrations at each time point were imprecise, with most confidence intervals (CIs) crossing the null (Table S14 [48]). Similar to single-PFAS models, the 3-year PFAS mixture tended to be associated with higher BMD Z-scores (Fig. 3B; Table S14 [48]) with the exception of the 1/3 distal radius, where the PFAS mixture at the 8- and 12-year time points was significantly associated with lower BMD (Fig. 2A–2D; Table S14 [48]). When assessing mixtures by child's sex, an IQR increase in all serum PFAS concentrations at the 12-year time point was associated with lower 1/3 distal radius (Ψ: −0.88; 95% CI: −1.54, −0.22) and ultradistal radius (Ψ: −0.82; 95% CI: −1.62, −0.02) Z-scores among females (Fig. 2D).

Cumulative exposure to PFAS mixtures

An IQR increase in all 4 PFAS at all 4 time points was associated with lower 1/3 distal radius Z-scores (Ψ: −0.48, 95% CI: −1.10, 0.13; Table S14 [48]). Based on the weights from the g-computation model (Table S15 [48]), the largest contributions to this overall effect were 12-year PFOS (median weight = −0.23), 8-year PFOA (median weight = −0.18), and delivery PFOA (median weight = −0.13).

Sensitivity analyses

Additionally, adjusting for pubic hair Tanner stage did not alter interpretation of findings, as estimates were similar to primary analyses in overall and sex-stratified models (Tables S16–S18 [48]). Adjusting for child BMI at the time of the PFAS measurement tended to slightly attenuate associations of 8- and 12-year PFAS with BMD Z-scores (Tables S19-S21 [48]).

Discussion

In the HOME Study cohort, higher serum PFOA concentrations were consistently associated with lower BMD Z-scores at the 1/3 distal radius. For other PFAS, associations differed by time point, with 3-year concentrations associated with higher BMD Z-scores and 8- and 12-year concentrations associated with lower BMD Z-scores, particularly among females. Bone mineral density Z-score differences ranged from 0.2 to 0.8 SD per IQR increase in PFAS concentrations. If replicated, this is a clinically significant difference given that a 1 SD change in BMD is associated with 1.3 to 1.4 greater odds of forearm fracture in children [75]. For comparison, previous studies of adolescents and young adults reported 0.01 to 0.06 BMD Z-score differences per unit increase in HEI score [76], per hour of daily high-impact physical activity [77], or per unit of genetic risk score [77]. The Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry [78] minimal risk level for intermediate duration PFOA oral exposure is based on skeletal effects observed in mice [27]; our study provides critical epidemiologic evidence to support bone as a key endpoint for PFAS toxicity. Interventions to reduce PFAS osteotoxicity may include exposure reduction or mitigation of PFAS impacts through healthy lifestyle factors such as improved diet quality, higher calcium or dairy intake, and greater physical activity [79, 80].

Associations of PFAS with lower adolescent BMD tended to be strongest for PFAS biomarkers measured at 8 and 12 years, indicating that childhood PFAS exposure may have a greater impact than prenatal exposure. Prior prospective studies of prenatal PFAS biomarkers with child or adolescent BMD also report weak negative or null associations for most PFAS [32-34, 39, 81] or a weak positive association for PFHxS [34]. Two of these studies also evaluated childhood PFAS biomarkers [34, 39]. In a Faroese Cohort, cord serum PFAS concentrations at birth were not associated with 9-year whole-body BMD Z-scores, while serum PFOS, PFOA, and PFNA at age 5 years and PFOA at age 9 years were associated with lower BMD Z-scores [34]. In the Odense Child Cohort, PFAS concentrations in maternal pregnancy serum and child serum at ages 18 months and 5 years were weakly associated with lower whole-body BMC and BMD Z-scores at ages 7 [33] and 9 years [39], although no associations were statistically significant. Several additional studies reported associations of higher childhood PFAS concentrations with lower BMD in adolescence or early adulthood. In the Boston-based Project Viva cohort (n = 484), higher plasma PFOA and PFDA concentrations measured at age 8 years were associated with lower whole-body BMD Z-scores at 13 and 18 years, particularly among females [80]. In a prospective study of adolescent PFAS concentrations and BMD, higher plasma PFOS concentrations at age 8 to 11 years were associated with decreases in trunk, but not total, BMD over an average of 1.4 years of follow-up among Hispanic adolescents with overweight and obesity from the Study of Latino Adolescents at Risk of Type 2 Diabetes (SOLAR) cohort (n = 304) [82]. Other PFAS were not significantly associated with baseline or change in BMD and there were no sex differences [82]. Finally, 3 cross-sectional studies reported associations of PFAS biomarker concentrations with lower BMD, including 2 studies of US adolescents aged 12 to 19 years [36, 38] and an earlier report from Project Viva assessing 8-year-old children [37]. Overall, the existing studies of mid-childhood and adolescent PFAS exposure suggest that higher PFAS concentrations, particularly PFOA, may negatively affect adolescent bone health.

Notably, higher PFOA concentrations at every time point were associated with lower 1/3 distal radius BMD at age 12 years, consistent with our previous study of maternal gestational serum concentrations in this cohort [31]. As the 1/3 distal radius represents the status of cortical bone [83], associations at this skeletal site but not others suggest that PFOA may differentially affect cortical over trabecular bone density. This finding is consistent with a study of developmental PFOA exposure (gestation and lactation) in female mice, which reported decreased cortical tibia BMD, with effects lasting to adulthood, but no effects on trabecular bone [27]. A study of 12-week PFHxS exposure in adult male mice reported decreases in both trabecular and cortical bone volume, with stronger magnitude for trabecular bone [29]. A study of developmental PFOS exposure also reported decreased trabecular BMD in exposed mice, though cortical parameters were not assessed [30]. We are not aware of toxicological studies of PFNA or studies that assessed >1 PFAS. Still, mechanisms of action likely differ between PFAS based on structure-related differences in their ability to activate PPARs alpha and gamma [19, 20], binding affinity for the vitamin D receptor [17, 18], or influence on sex and thyroid steroid hormones [21-26].

In contrast to other time points, 3-year PFHxS and PFOS were associated with higher whole body, total hip, and femoral neck BMD Z-scores, skeletal sites comprised of a mix of trabecular and cortical bone. This observation was not expected based on findings of 2 prior studies of early childhood PFAS [33, 35]. In the Odense Child Cohort, higher PFOS and PFNA concentrations at age 18 months were associated with lower whole-body BMD and BMC Z-scores at age 7 years (n = 668) [33], though these associations weakened when outcomes were assessed again at age 9 years [39]. In the Sophia Pluto study in The Netherlands, summed concentrations of 5 PFAS at age 2 years were associated with lower 3-year age- and sex-adjusted BMD SD scores (n = 237) [35]. Differences in our results from prior studies of early childhood PFAS exposure may be due to differences in study populations, covariate adjustment, and ages of BMD assessment. Notably, we measured BMD in early adolescence while the prior studies assessed BMD at age 3 and 7 years. The opposite direction of PFAS associations at 3 years compared with other time points in our study may be due to differences in biologic susceptibility in early childhood or higher PFAS concentrations at 3 years than other time points. While decreasing trends in levels over time are consistent with temporal declines in PFAS concentrations in the US population [9], higher 3-year levels may also reflect lactational PFAS transfer [84]. We adjusted for human milk feeding duration, as it is associated with higher PFAS concentrations in our cohort [68]. Still, positive associations at the 3-year time point could be due to residual confounding as exclusivity of human milk feeding may be more important for adolescent BMD than duration [85]. Other unmeasured behavioral or environmental exposures related to both higher BMD and human milk feeding may also play a role. Larger studies may be able to disentangle relations among PFAS concentrations, human milk feeding, and bone health. We emphasize that associations with higher BMD do not necessarily indicate a beneficial effect given limitations of BMD as a measure of bone strength. For example, fluoride exposure has been linked to higher BMD but also greater risk of fractures [86, 87]—thus, environmental exposures may increase BMD while also disrupting bone microarchitecture to reduce strength. Future studies assessing impacts on risk of fracture and osteoporosis are needed to determine impacts on bone strength.

An important consideration for interpreting results of our study is the timing of BMD measurement at age 12 years, an age before most bone mineral is accrued and when, on average, females will have accrued more of their total body bone mineral compared with males. Studies with long-term follow-up are necessary to determine if associations observed in early adolescence persist, disappear, or strengthen as participants attain peak bone mineral in early adulthood. Relatedly, although we observed some differences by sex, our sample size was limited, and larger studies are required to more definitively examine sexual dimorphism in periods of susceptibility. We also assumed linear concentration-response relations due to our limited statistical power; larger future studies could explore the potential for nonlinear relations. We used height-adjusted and age-, sex-, and race-specific BMD Z-scores developed by the BMDCS to account for variation in BMD during growth and development; however, we acknowledge ongoing debate regarding race standardization given that race is a misclassified proxy for ancestry [88, 89]. In addition, we conducted many statistical tests given our uniquely comprehensive data on 4 PFAS measured at 4 time points as well as BMD measured at 6 skeletal sites. We acknowledge that some statistically significant findings may have arisen due to chance, and we focused our inference on consistent patterns of association and comparisons with prior literature.

Strengths of our study include the prospective study design with repeated PFAS measures to examine multiple exposure assessment periods during childhood and assess whether PFAS associations with early adolescent bone health differ by exposure assessment timing. We characterized BMD at 6 unique skeletal sites, enabling comparisons of associations at bone sites comprised of cortical or trabecular bone, providing insight into possible mechanisms by which PFAS impact bone strength and structure for follow-up in future laboratory studies. We also prospectively measured a wide range of important covariates related to PFAS exposure and bone strength to reduce confounding bias.

Conclusion

In the HOME Study, we found that higher serum PFOA concentrations at multiple time points were associated with lower BMD Z-scores at the 1/3 distal radius, a predominantly cortical bone site, while associations for other PFAS differed by exposure assessment timing, skeletal site, and sex. Continued research is needed to assess whether these associations persist or evolve into adulthood.
