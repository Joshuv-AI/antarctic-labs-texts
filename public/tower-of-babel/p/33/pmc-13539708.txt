Sparse multi-way DMDC for longitudinal classification in high dimension low sample size data
Authors: Zahra Zolghadr, Hamid Alavi Majd, Mohammad Rouzbeh, Yadollah Mehrabi, Amir Eftekhari Moghadam, Salman Safdari, Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI)
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecent advances in data registration techniques have enabled researchers to analyze high-dimensional, low sample size (HDLSS) data, which poses challenges for traditional statistical and machine learning methods—especially in fields like neurodevelopmental disorder classification. Among various proposed solutions, the Data Maximum Dispersion Classifier (DMDC) has demonstrated superior performance. This study introduces two extensions: Sparse DMDC (SDMDC) and Longitudinal Sparse DMDC (LSDMDC), with the latter leveraging multi-way data frameworks. Simulated HDLSS datasets were generated from multivariate normal distributions with sparse structures. Evaluation of SDMDC involved varying class imbalance, predictor count, and correlation structures. Performance was measured via average F-measure and signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). Results showed that SDMDC outperformed other sparse models. Moreover, promising improvement was observed in model classification performance and the selection of effective predictors when the correlation between predictor variables was increased. In longitudinal simulations, LSDMDC maintained performance even as the number of predictors increased along the first dimension, effectively handling rank-1 and rank-2 data. Notably, LSDMDC achieved the best results under autoregressive correlation. In conclusion, SDMDC demonstrated clear advantages over other sparse classifiers, while LSDMDC proved highly effective for complex longitudinal structures.Keywords: Classification, Data maximum dispersion classifier, Multiway data, Longitudinal sparse DMDC, High-dimensional low-sample-size data

Introduction

Classification is a powerful tool that can be utilized to provide a solution for a wide variety of problems. It plays a major role in statistics and machine learning and applied in a broad knowledge-based areas such as disease diagnosis, image segregation, audio and speech processing and bioinformatics [1]. In general, machine learning algorithms that works well with statistical methods such as Naïve Bayes Classifier [2], Logistic Regression [3], Neural Networks [4] and Random Forest [5] perform a reasonably acceptable job when there are no special requirements either in data or in algorithm itself. However, when we are dealing with a high dimensional data for which there are not many samples available, these classical algorithms fail to provide a feasible estimation. This special case where the number of samples are negligible compared to the number of dimensions d, i.e. n <<d, is called High-Dimensional Low-Sample-Size in the literature or abbreviated as HDLSS [1]. fMRI data [6] or gene expression [7] are good examples of when there are few samples with high dimension available. While feature selection methods can reduce dimensionality during preprocessing, they can be time-consuming. Therefore, preference is often given to algorithms that can efficiently handle high-dimensional data without requiring explicit dimensionality reduction [8]. Certain classifiers exist for this specific task of HDLSS model training, such as Support Vector Machine (SVM) [9], distance weighted discrimination (DWD) [10] and DMDC [8].

Recent advancements in high-dimensional data classification have introduced novel frameworks to improve accuracy and scalability. Mo and Chen (2023) proposed a classification framework leveraging pairwise distance ranks to identify patterns overlooked by previous methods, demonstrating superior performance across diverse datasets [11]. Zhou et al. (2024) developed a multiobjective genetic programming approach (PS-MOGP) that integrates recursive feature elimination and a progressive Pareto archive strategy to address solution diversity, multiclass imbalance, and large feature spaces. These approaches contribute significantly to tackling the challenges of high-dimensional classification [12].

In 2020 Shen and Yin having simulated a scenario with high dimensional data proved that DWD and SVM face overfitting and so called data-piling problem. In the same study they introduced a new method called Data Maximum Dispersion Classifier (DMDC) which, by maximizing the dispersion of data in the projection space and the gap between classes, overcomes the aforementioned issues of data-piling. Classically, DWD, SVM, DMDC and all other applicable methods make use of all variables in the training procedure. Practically however, there may only be few variables playing a decisive role in determining the outcome of the model. Having all variables participated in the training phase would unequivocally introduce noise into the system, unimportant parameters can inject more uncertainty and thus reduce performance [13]. This, on its own, suggests the possibility of omitting a handful of variables and applying a form of feature selection on the model. Committing to this idea will produce a sparse representation of the model while providing a more meaningful interpretation [14].

Zhu et. al in 2004 [15] introduced L1-Norm SVM as an approach to come up with sparse SVM, although many other versions of sparsity in SVM have introduced based on different penalty terms by zhang [16] and wang [17]. Following the work of Zhu et. al, in 2016 Zhang and Lin applied L1-Norm and elastic-net on DWD and presented the first sparse DWD model [18].

In numerous fields of research, often we require a more complex structure than the classic two-way data matrix (samples x variables). Data structure in these cases are best represented in the form of a multi-way array or as is called a tensor. A clarifying example can be fMRI data taken from different regions of brain over different points of time. To present this, a multi-way structure is best suited and defined in the form of samples x regions x time-points. A comprehensive research on multi-way data classifiers was proposed by LYU et. al in 2017 [19] where a discriminant framework is mathematically introduced. Coefficients specific to each way were computed by taking into account the effects of the correlation of predictors in each way. The core of the framework mainly consists of DWD that utilizes rank-1 and rank-r versions of multi-way classification. Sparsity was later on added to framework core by Guo et. al [20] in 2023 and extend the model functionality to work with two-way data and beyond.

In this study, we are tackling data-piling problem and, with regards to the undeniable advantage of introducing sparsity into the model, as heavily studied by aforementioned researchers, proposing a new method that combines sparsity with DMDC to be applied in HDLSS environments.

This study focuses on data types where the second way concerns different time-points which facilitates longitudinal classification. Framework core is based on sparse DMDC which all in all, makes it feasible to handle HDLSS data using Longitudinal Sparse DMDC.

Methods

Sparse DMDC

The data maximum dispersion classifier is an advantageous model for high-dimensional low-sample-size datasets that can perform better than the conventional models by overcoming the problem of data-piling. Similar to the standard SVM and DWD models, which use L2-norm penalty (ridge penalty) on their optimization function, DMDC may also exhibit suboptimal performance for two reasons. First, based on scientific assumptions, in a high-dimensional data environment only a limited number of variables can affect the overall model performance, and a good classifier should be able to identify effective variables and discard irrelevant ones; whereas the standard SVM, DWD and DMDC make use of all the variables in the model building process. Second, having all the variables enlisted and participated in creating such a model may result in classification inefficiency due to the accumulation of errors in the estimation of a large number of noisy parameters. Based on these two considerations, and to compensate for the overall decline in the performance, sparse models are usually utilized for high-dimensional data classification task. The adoption of penalty functions to L1-norm (LASSO penalty), in classification models for sparse modeling, constitutes a prevalent approach in this context. Replacing with in the optimization function of classification models results in the automatic selection of relevant variables. This is due to the fact that the LASSO penalty corresponds to a double-exponential distribution for coefficients, while the ridge penalty corresponds to a Gaussian distribution for obtaining coefficients. As the exponential distribution has heavier tails than the Normal distribution, it tends to estimate larger coefficients for important variables and consequently leave others at zero. In these sparse models, coefficients of less important variables will be exactly zero. Thus, the lasso penalty does a kind of continuous feature selection, while this is not the case for the ridge penalty. Unlike simplex method, which, in order to reach the optimal point, moves along the edges of a multifaceted space constructed from the constraints to the vertex where the value of the cost function is lower. In the interior point method, the iterations of the algorithm start inside the multifaceted space and regardless of the edges move towards the lowest cost vertex. This approach reduces the number of iterations required to reach that vertex and thus speeds up the computation time deemed necessary to produce a viable solution. In the realm of linear programming optimization the interior point method removes the constraints from an optimization problem and reaches the optimal point using Newton's numerical calculation method (usually in 20 to 100 iterations). This is achieved by obtaining the standard or focal form of the linear problem, changing the form of the initial and dual optimization function and adding the logarithmic Brier function and applying Lagrange in the interior point method.

To evaluate sparse models in simulated data, two indices—signal and noise—were used. The signal index represents the number of nonzero coefficients estimated by the model for predictor variables whose mean was considered nonzero in the simulation. The noise index refers to the number of nonzero coefficients estimated by the model for predictor variables whose mean was considered zero in the simulation. To combine these indices, the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) was used. A higher SNR (greater than one) indicates a better ability of the sparse model to select important predictors.

Real data analysis

The proposed methods, SDMDC and LSDMDC, were applied to baseline and longitudinal real data obtained from the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) database and compared to corresponding versions of SVM and DWD.

Data source

Data for this study were obtained from the ADNI database, which was initiated in 2003 as a public–private partnership led by Principal Investigator Michael W. Weiner, MD. The ADNI aims to combine serial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), positron emission tomography (PET), other biological markers, and clinical and neuropsychological assessments to measure the progression of mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and early Alzheimer’s disease (AD). For more information, see adni.loni.usc.edu.

Considering the data requirements for this study, all individuals with longitudinal fMRI images from at least three consecutive time points were selected as samples in two groups: healthy and AD (Alzheimer's Disease). Accordingly, a total of 153 individuals were included, comprising 66 AD individuals (43.2%) and 87 healthy individuals (56.8%), each having fMRI images taken at three time points (at diagnosis, three months after diagnosis, and six months after diagnosis). The data were downloaded in August 2022.

Data preprocessing

After downloading the research data, preprocessing of fMRI images was conducted using the CONN toolbox in MATLAB. This process involved multiple steps. First, skull-stripping was performed to remove non-brain tissue from the MRI images. Motion correction and slice-timing correction were then applied, with the interleaved acquisition method used for the latter. Spatial smoothing with a full-width at half maximum (FWHM) of 8 mm was conducted to improve signal quality. Group registration and normalization were performed to align individual brain images to a standard template. Finally, the brain was segmented into 132 regions using the Automated Anatomical Labeling (AAL) atlas. These preprocessing steps ensured that the data were standardized and suitable for further analysis.

Analytical steps

Evaluation framework

Due to the limited number of observations (87 healthy and 66 Alzheimer’s patients), a fivefold cross-validation strategy was adopted. Observations were evenly distributed across folds to maintain the same response variable distribution. In each fold, four subsets were used for training the models, and one subset was reserved for testing.

Correlation matrix calculation

Correlation matrices (132 × 132) were computed for each individual, representing the connectivity between brain regions. Each matrix element reflected the correlation between the activities of two brain regions.

Feature engineering for classification models

Upper triangular elements of the correlation matrices (excluding the diagonal) were extracted, resulting in 8,646 features per individual. This feature set was augmented with demographic variables (gender, age, marital status, handedness, education, retirement status, and race) and clinical statuses. To standardize the data, Min–Max scaling was applied, normalizing all features to the range [−1, 1].

Parameter optimization

Using the training data, penalty parameters for each model were optimized via a tenfold cross-validation within the training set. For each iteration, independent t-tests were performed on values for healthy and Alzheimer’s groups, and the parameter maximizing the t-statistic was selected.

For the SDMDC and SSVM models implemented in Python, the sparse penalty parameter λ was selected from a linear grid . Larger values of λ were not considered, as they resulted in an insufficient number of non-zero coefficients. For the corresponding multi-way sparse models, including rank-1 and rank-2 SSVM and SDMDC, a wider linear grid was used to account for the increased flexibility of the multi-way structure.

For the SDWD model implemented in R using the package, the default-tuning scheme was adopted, with the lambda sequence automatically generated using and set according to the package defaults. For the multi-way SDWD models (rank-1 and rank-2), the predefined penalty values were used. In all cases, the optimal parameter was selected using cross-validation.

Model fitting

The SDMDC, SSVM, and SDWD models were trained using the optimized parameters on baseline data (time = 1). Additionally, the rank-1 and rank-2 variants of these models were trained with the optimized parameters on longitudinal data spanning multiple time points (time = 1, 2, 3). Each model underwent evaluation five times based on the initial fivefold cross-validation split.

Model testing

Testing data were classified using the trained models. Predictions were compared with actual classifications to calculate evaluation metrics, including the mean F-score and the number of non-zero coefficients. Final metrics were averaged across the five folds to provide robust performance measures. To assess whether the observed differences in model performance were statistically significant, we employed a bootstrap-based pairwise comparison. For each pair of models, the F-scores average obtained across cross-validation folds were resampled with replacement for a large number of bootstrap iterations. At each iteration, the mean difference in F-score average between the two models was computed, and a 95% confidence interval was constructed from the empirical distribution of these differences. Performance differences were considered statistically significant if the confidence interval did not include zero.

The results indicate that adding structural constraints leads to improved performance. Specifically, the Rank-1 DMDC model (F = 0.47) improves upon the baseline DMDC (F = 0.38), highlighting the benefit of the longitudinal extension.

More importantly, the sparse and longitudinal SDMDC variants consistently achieve higher F-measure average than the original DMDC, with Rank-2 DMDC showing statistically significant differences in favor of all SDMDC variants (confidence intervals excluding zero). These findings suggest that incorporating sparsity enhances feature selection stability in this high-dimensional setting, while the longitudinal formulation better captures temporal progression patterns in ADNI data. Together, these modifications contribute to more robust and improved classification performance compared with the original non-sparse, non-longitudinal formulation.

Discussion

In this article, we have proposed a sparse DMDC data model to be utilized as the core for the framework of classifying multi-way data. Here, we considered time as the second way to reach longitudinal SDMDC to handle classification of HDLSS data. Performance-wise, SDMDC confirmed superior in classifying high dimensional data and in general, proved to be the best choice in selecting effective predictors. Additionally, promising improvement was observed in model classification performance and the selection of effective predictors when the correlation between predictor variables was increased. Moving towards longitudinal model, LSDMDC showed a great deal of robustness against increasing the number of predictors in the first way.

LSDMDC estimated class labels best when it dealt with autoregressive structure and similar to SDMDC, presented higher accuracy in predictor selection when correlation in predictors was likely to increase. Finally yet importantly, this study demonstrated that our sparse models have a propensity towards facilitating and simplifying interpretation.

Since second way contained time, and usually the number of time-points is limited, LSDMDC rank may not go beyond a certain figure.

In the real data analysis, SDMDC demonstrated competitive and generally strong performance in both baseline and longitudinal classification tasks. Bootstrap-based significance analyses indicate that SDMDC performs significantly better than SDWD. In the longitudinal setting, both Rank-1 and Rank-2 SDMDC, as well as Rank-1 SDWD, achieve significantly higher F-measure compared to Rank-1 SSVM in distinguishing Alzheimer's patients from healthy controls.

This study has several limitations. The computational complexity of the simulation and real data analyses was substantial, and the use of alternative optimization tools due to solver and hardware constraints may have affected computational efficiency. In addition, in the real data application—particularly for the ADNI dataset—the sample size was relatively limited, which may restrict the stability and generalizability of the results. Future studies with larger cohorts and additional clinically relevant variables with fewer missing values may further improve model performance.

A possible future direction to extend this work could involve considering other types of penalty instead of l1-Norm such as elastic-net. In addition, one could go beyond binary classes and encompass multi-category classification, which is worthwhile idea for research.
