Dual-targeted albumin nanoparticles for the Co-delivery of low-dose paclitaxel and PCSK9 inhibitor in melanoma treatment
Authors: Ling Chen, Meng Hu, Hui-min Yan, Xue-Qing Ding, Qiu-xiang Yang, Ling Wang, Hong Pan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe immunosuppressive microenvironment and impaired antigen presentation represent significant challenges in melanoma treatment. This study explores strategies to overcome these barriers by simultaneously inducing tumor cell immunogenic cell death (ICD) and enhancing antigen presentation. We developed hyaluronic acid/R8-RGD dual-modified albumin nanoparticles (HR/LDPP-NPs) designed to co-deliver low-dose paclitaxel (PTX) and the PCSK9 inhibitor PF-06446846. PTX induces ICD, releasing calreticulin (CRT), ATP, and HMGB1 danger signals, thereby transforming tumor cells into an in situ antigen source and initiating dendritic cell-mediated T-cell responses, akin to an intrinsic vaccine effect. Simultaneously, the inhibition of PCSK9 significantly upregulates tumor MHC-I expression, counteracting immune evasion. This synergistic approach, combining in situ vaccination with enhanced antigen presentation, significantly amplifies antitumor immunity. Both in vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrate that these spatiotemporally coordinated dual-targeting nanoparticles achieve substantial therapeutic efficacy with significantly reduced toxicity, presenting a novel chemo-immunotherapeutic strategy for melanoma treatment.Keywords: Nanoparticles, Dual targeting, Paclitaxel, PCSK9 inhibitor, Melanoma, Immune regulation

Introduction

Melanoma is a highly aggressive neoplasm characterized by an unfavorable prognosis and a relatively brief survival duration for affected individuals [1,2]. Contemporary therapeutic strategies encompass surgical excision, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy, among others [3]. While surgical excision facilitates the direct removal of the tumor, it is associated with a high propensity for recurrence and inflicts considerable trauma on the patient. Radiotherapy, although employed, demonstrates limited efficacy and poses the risk of collateral damage to adjacent healthy tissues. Chemotherapy, despite its capacity to inhibit tumor cell proliferation, suffers from a lack of specificity and is accompanied by substantial adverse effects. Immunotherapy, which aims to stimulate the immune system to target tumor cells, is constrained by limited activation efficiency [4].

The concurrent application of chemotherapy and immunotherapy capitalizes on the advantages of both modalities, producing a synergistic effect, and has emerged as a prevalent treatment strategy for melanoma [5,6]. PTX, a widely utilized chemotherapeutic agent, can directly induce apoptosis in cancer cells, expose "damage-associated molecular patterns" (DAMPs), and generate "eat me" signals that recruit DCs. This process facilitates the establishment of an anti-tumor immune microenvironment and activates tumor immune responses [7,8]. Nevertheless, PTX lacks specificity and is distributed systemically upon entering the bloodstream, necessitating higher doses to achieve its anti-tumor effects, which consequently results in significant toxicity and side effects [9]. Consequently, enhancing drug targeting has become a critical focus in recent years to optimize PTX-related therapies [10].

Research indicates that tumor-secreted PCSK9 can interact with MHC-I, facilitating its lysosomal degradation and impeding its recycling on the tumor cell membrane. This process results in decreased MHC-I expression on the surface of tumor cells, thereby hindering T cell-mediated recognition and contributing to the immune evasion of cancer cells [11,12]. In light of this mechanism, we propose that the concurrent administration of PTX and PCSK9 inhibitors PF-06446846 may enhance anti-tumor immunity while mitigating immune escape, potentially leading to a synergistic anti-tumor effect.

In recent years, nanomaterials have demonstrated significant potential in cancer treatment, particularly in the realms of chemotherapy and immunotherapy. These materials enhance anti-tumor efficacy through various mechanisms, such as augmenting drug concentration at tumor sites and improving drug stability and biocompatibility [13,14]. Furthermore, the modification of nanoparticles facilitates precise drug delivery, thereby enhancing drug bioavailability and specificity, effectively addressing the challenges of poor targeting and high toxicity associated with conventional treatments [15]. Additionally, by specifically binding to tumor cell surface receptors, nanomaterials can increase drug accumulation within tumor cells and overcome multidrug resistance through multiple strategies [16,17]. In the context of cancer immunotherapy, nanomaterials also play a crucial role by enhancing the immune response efficacy of immunotherapeutic agents through modification [18,19]. The favorable biocompatibility and facile functionalization of nanomaterials render them vital tools in the development of novel immunotherapy strategies.

While nanotechnology has introduced numerous innovative strategies for cancer treatment, current solutions continue to face significant limitations. A primary challenge is the suboptimal targeting of nanoparticles, which can result in inefficient delivery, thereby compromising treatment efficacy and elevating the risk of adverse side effects [[20], [21], [22]]. Additionally, physiological barriers, including cell membranes and endothelial cell layers, pose substantial obstacles to nanoparticle penetration, significantly limiting their access to tumor tissues. Consequently, the full potential of their anti-cancer properties cannot be realized, adversely affecting overall treatment outcomes and patient recovery [[23], [24], [25]].

HA, a naturally occurring polysaccharide, exhibits the capability to selectively target the CD44 receptor, which is frequently overexpressed in a variety of malignant tumors and cancer stem cells. Consequently, nanoparticles modified with HA can enhance their targeting efficacy by accurately recognizing and binding to CD44 on tumor cells [[26], [27], [28], [29]]. Furthermore, under physiological conditions, the negative charge of HA can mitigate the adsorption of nanoparticles to blood proteins, thereby reducing the likelihood of recognition and clearance by immune cells [[30], [31], [32]]. The R8-RGD peptide is endowed with both targeting and deep tissue penetration capabilities [33,34]. The RGD sequence can specifically bind to the integrin αvβ3 receptor, facilitating the guidance of nanoparticles to tumor sites and enhancing their penetration into tumor tissues [35,36]. Meanwhile, R8, functioning as a cell-penetrating peptide [37,38], can aid nanoparticles in penetrating deeply into solid tumors [39,40].

We propose the hypothesis that dual-targeting nanoparticles, through the delivery of low-dose paclitaxel (PTX) to induce immunogenic cell death (ICD) and facilitate the release of endogenous tumor antigens, initiate an in situ vaccine response. Concurrently, the inhibition of proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9) prevents the degradation of major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I). This synergistic approach, termed "in situ vaccination coupled with presentation enhancement," establishes a self-amplifying immunostimulatory loop capable of overcoming the immunosuppressive microenvironment characteristic of melanoma.

Materials and methods

Materials

Cationic Bovine Serum Albumin (cBSA) was procured from MedChemExpress Inc. (Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA). Sodium HA, with an average molecular weight of 35 kDa, was acquired from Shandong Freda Biochem Co., Ltd. (Shandong, China). R8-RGD peptides, featuring a terminal cysteine [Cys-RRRRRRRR-c(RGDfK), cysteine-modified octa-arginine conjugated to the branch of lysine], were synthesized by Chinapeptides Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China) utilizing the standard solid-phase peptide synthesis protocol. The compound 3-(4,5-Dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) was sourced from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). The Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis detection kit was obtained from Becton, Dickinson and Company (Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). PTX and Nile Red (NR) were purchased from Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The PF-06446846 was also acquired from MedChemExpress Inc. (Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA). Alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) colorimetric assay kits were supplied by Roche Diagnostics, Shanghai, China. Mouse high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) and adenosine triphosphate (ATP) ELISA kits were procured from Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China. ELISA kits for interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) were sourced from MultiSciences Biotech Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, China. Primary antibodies against BAX (2772S) and BCL-2 (3498S) were acquired from Cell Signaling Technology Inc., Denver, CO, USA. Naïve CD4+ and CD8+ T cell isolation kits were obtained from Stemcell Technologies, Vancouver, BC, Canada. Primary antibodies targeting Calreticulin (CRT, 27298–1-AP) were sourced from Proteintech Group Inc., Wuhan, China. All antibodies for flow cytometry were purchased from BioLegend, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA. DCFH-DA fluorescent probe was purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China. All other chemical reagents utilized in the experiments were obtained from Sinopharm Chemical Reagents Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China, and were of analytical or chemical grade.

Cell lines and animals

The B16F10 and B16F10-OVA cell lines were procured from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, while the YUMM1.7 cell line was acquired from Cyagen Biosciences Co., Ltd., located in Guangzhou, China. All the cells were cultured in ulbecco's Modified Eagle's Medium (DMEM) medium supplemented with 1 % streptomycin and penicillin, along with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS). The cultures were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5 % CO2. For in vivo experiments, C57BL/6 mice and OT-I transgenic mice were obtained from SPF Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China).

Preparation, Characterization, and labeling of HR/LDPP-NPs nanoparticles

The synthesis of HR/LDPP-NPs is predicated on the principles of electrostatic and hydrophobic interactions. Initially, 150 mg of cBSA was dissolved in 10 mL of ultrapure water to create an aqueous phase. Subsequently, 10 mg of PTX, 50 mg of the PF-06446846, and 80 mg of soybean oil were dissolved in 1 mL of dichloromethane to constitute an organic phase. The aqueous phase was then introduced into the organic phase, followed by ultrasonic emulsification (300 W,5 min), rotary evaporation(40 °C,100 rpm), and high-pressure homogenization to yield the initial nanoparticle dispersion (LDPP-NPs). Subsequently, negatively charged HA and R8-RGD peptide were adsorbed onto the surface of the LDPP-NPs via electrostatic interactions, resulting in the formation of HR/LDPP-NPs. The preparation protocols for the control nanoparticles, namely HR/LDP-NPs, HR/P-NPs, and LDPP-NPs, were analogous, with each variant incorporating only PTX, only PF-06446846, or lacking modification, respectively.

The particle size and zeta potential of HR/LDPP-NPs and the control particles were assessed using dynamic light scattering (DLS) with a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS90 (Malvern, UK). The morphological characteristics of the nanoparticles were examined via transmission electron microscopy (TEM) employing a JEOL JEM-1200EX (Tokyo, Japan). The encapsulation efficiency (EE%) of PTX and PF-06446846F-06446846 was quantified using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). This was calculated as the ratio of the weight of PTX or PF-06446846 in HR/LDPP-NPs or control nanoparticles to the total weight of PTX or PF-06446846. Similarly, the drug loading (DL%) was determined by dividing the weight of PTX or PF-06446846 in HR/LDPP-NPs or control nanoparticles by the weight of the nanoparticles [41].

To synthesize fluorescently labeled nanoparticles, Nile Red (NR, Aladdin, Shanghai, China) was initially dissolved in dichloromethane (DCM) at a concentration of 10 mg/mL. A 200 μL aliquot of the NR solution was subsequently mixed with 10 mg of paclitaxel, 50 mg of PF-06446846, and 80 mg of soybean oil in 1 mL of DCM to constitute the organic phase. The aqueous phase was then incorporated, followed by probe sonication to achieve emulsification. Rotary evaporation was employed to remove DCM, and high-pressure homogenization was conducted to produce NR-loaded nanoparticles (LDPP-NPs). The resulting dispersion was shielded from light and subjected to dialysis against deionized water (MWCO 3 kDa) to remove unbound NR. Finally, negatively charged hyaluronic acid and R8-RGD peptide were adsorbed onto the LDPP-NPs through electrostatic interactions, resulting in the formation of NR-labeled HR/LDPP-NPs.

In vitro stability and release assay

The stability of HR/LDPP-NPs was evaluated by incubating them in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 10 % (v/v) fetal bovine serum at 37 °C with gentle agitation, as outlined in Ref. [42]. The in vitro release study was conducted using the dialysis method described in Ref. [43]. Free PTX, PF-06446846, and HR/LDPP-NPs were placed in dialysis bags with a molecular weight cutoff of 3000 Da and immersed in a PBS solution containing 1 % Tween 80. The samples were continuously agitated at 100 rpm and maintained at 37 °C. Periodic sampling was performed, with the withdrawn volume being replenished with an equivalent volume of fresh medium. The pH was adjusted to 7.4 to replicate physiological conditions and to 6.5 to simulate the tumor microenvironment. The cumulative release percentages of PTX and PF-06446846 were subsequently quantified using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC).

Assessment of Cellular Uptake Efficiency

B16F10 or YUMM1.7 cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well in 6-well plates and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Subsequently, NR-labeled HR/LDPP-NPs or control formulations were introduced, and the incubation was extended for an additional 48 h. The cells were then harvested using trypsin, washed three times with PBS, resuspended in FACS buffer, and analyzed using a BD Fortessa flow cytometer. The data were processed with FlowJo version 10.8. For the assessment of intracellular localization, B16F10 cells cultured on coverslips were exposed to nanoparticles for 48 h, followed by three rinses with cold PBS. The cells were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15 min at room temperature and washed again three times for 5 min each. The nuclei were stained with 300 nM DAPI for 5 min in the dark, followed by three additional PBS washes. The coverslips were then mounted and imaged using an Olympus IX73 microscope, and fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software.

Competitive uptake assay

To assess the role of CD44 and integrin αvβ3 in nanoparticle internalization, B16F10 melanoma cells were pre-incubated for 30 min with an excess of free hyaluronic acid to saturate CD44 receptors or with RGD peptide to saturate αvβ3 integrins. Subsequently, NR-labeled HR/LDP nanoparticles were introduced for a specified duration, and the proportion of NR-positive cells was quantified using flow cytometry.

Evaluation of In vivo tumor targeting capability of HR/LDPP-NPs

NR-labeled HR/LDPP nanoparticles and control formulations were administered intravenously to mice bearing B16F10 tumors. After a period of 16 h, tumors and major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys, were harvested and processed into single-cell suspensions. A consistent gating strategy was employed across all samples: debris was excluded based on forward scatter area (FSC-A) and side scatter area (SSC-A), single cells were identified using forward scatter area (FSC-A) and forward scatter height (FSC-H), and NR-positive events were quantified to ascertain the percentage of NR+ cells. Tumor targeting efficacy was assessed by calculating the tumor-to-liver ratio (TLR), defined as the mean fluorescence intensity in the tumor divided by the mean fluorescence intensity in the liver. Fluorescence images of tumor and liver sections were captured using an Olympus IX73 microscope and subsequently analyzed with the software ImageJ.

Flow cytometry assay

B16F10, B16F10-OVA, YUMM1.7 cells, along with immune cells, underwent three washes with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), followed by detachment using trypsin. Subsequently, the cells were resuspended in appropriate buffers in preparation for staining with specific antibodies or dyes, in accordance with the manufacturers' protocols. For the detection of apoptosis, the cells were initially washed with ice-cold PBS. Adherent cells were gently dissociated using EDTA-free trypsin, centrifuged at 300×g for 5 min, and then resuspended in 100 μL of Annexin V binding buffer. Annexin V-FITC (5 μL) was added, and the mixture was incubated at room temperature in the dark for 10–15 min. Following this, the cells were washed three times with PBS to eliminate any unbound dye, after which 5 μL of propidium iodide (PI) was added and incubated at room temperature for 5 min prior to immediate analysis. For the analysis of protein expression, cells underwent a series of preparatory steps. Initially, they were washed with cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), subjected to trypsinization, and subsequently collected via centrifugation before being resuspended in PBS. A viability dye was then applied at 4 °C for a duration of 30 min. Following three additional washes with PBS, the cells were resuspended in a flow buffer consisting of PBS supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS). They were then incubated with fluorophore-conjugated primary antibodies at 4 °C for 30 min in the dark. Post-incubation, the cells were washed three times with PBS and finally resuspended in 200 μL of flow buffer for analysis. Data acquisition was performed using a BD Fortessa cytometer, and subsequent analysis was conducted with FlowJo software version 10.8. Apoptosis results are presented as total apoptosis, encompassing both early and late stages. Protein expression levels are quantified and reported as either mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) or the percentage of cells expressing the protein of interest.

MTT assay

The MTT assay was employed to assess the impact of HR/LDPP-NPs and their corresponding control nanoparticles on the viability of B16F10 cells. Following a 48-h co-incubation period with B16F10 or YUMM1.7 cells, MTT reagent (5 mg/mL) was introduced, and incubation was continued for an additional 4 h. Subsequently, dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was added to solubilize the resulting formazan crystals. Absorbance values were then measured at a wavelength of 570 nm using a microplate reader (Thermo Scientific Varioskan Flash, Massachusetts, USA).

Colony formation assay

The colony formation assay was conducted to assess the impact of HR/LDPP-NPs and various control nanoparticles on the proliferative capacity of B16F10 or YUMM1.7 cells. Initially, the cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 500 cells per well. Following a 48-h exposure to the respective treatments, the medium was replaced with fresh medium, and the cells were cultured for an additional week. Subsequently, the cells were fixed and stained with crystal violet. The colonies formed were quantified using image analysis software (ImageJ, Bethesda, Maryland, USA).

Western blot analysis

The Western blot technique was utilized to assess the expression changes of the pro-apoptotic protein BAX and the anti-apoptotic protein BCL-2 in B16F10 cells treated with HR/LDPP-NPs, as well as their respective control nanoparticles. Following cell collection, total proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer, and the protein concentration was quantified using the BCA method. The protein samples underwent electrophoresis on a 10 % SDS-PAGE gel and were subsequently transferred onto a PVDF membrane. After blocking, the membrane was incubated with antibodies specific to BAX, BCL-2, and GAPDH. The bands were visualized using an ECL detection system (GE Healthcare, UK), and the gray values of the protein bands were analyzed using ImageJ software.

In vivo anti-melanoma therapy of HR/LDPP-NPs

Male C57BL/6 mice, aged 6–8 weeks, were subcutaneously inoculated with either B16F10 or YUMM1.7 cells at a concentration of 1 × 106 cells per mouse. The mice were then randomly assigned to one of seven distinct groups (n = 5 per group). The groups were defined as follows: (1) control group (C), (2) free conventional-dose PTX (F-CDP) group, administered at a dosage of 10 mg/kg, (3) HR/LDPP-NPs group, with an equivalent PTX dose of 1 mg/kg, (4) dual-modified nanoparticles only loaded with Low-Dose PTX (HR/LDP-NPs) group, (5) dual-modified nanoparticles only loaded with PF-06446846 (HR/P-NPs) group, (6) unmodified nanoparticles co-loaded with low-dose PTX and PF-06446846 (LDPP-NPs) group, and (7) combination treatment of free low-dose PTX and free PF-06446846 (F-LDP + F-P) group.

The treatment protocol commenced with a tail-vein injection administered seven days post-tumor inoculation and was subsequently repeated at three-day intervals. Following the conclusion of the treatment regimen, serum, tumor tissue, and the associated draining lymph nodes were harvested for further analysis. Survival rates were monitored on a daily basis, and Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated to assess survival outcomes. Statistical significance was evaluated using the log-rank test, with data analysis conducted utilizing ImageJ software.

Co-culture of tumor cells and DCs

B16F10 cells treated with HR/LDPP-NPs and their respective controls were co-cultured with bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs). Initially, bone marrow cells were harvested from mice and differentiated into BMDCs using RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with GM-CSF (20 ng/mL) and IL-4 (10 ng/mL). Subsequently, BMDCs were co-cultured with the treated B16F10 cells at a ratio of 1:3 for 24 h. The expression of mature DC markers was assessed by flow cytometry (BD Fortessa).

Co-culture of DCs and T cells

Naive CD8+ and CD4+ T lymphocytes were isolated from spleen and subsequently co-cultured with BMDCs subjected to differential treatments, maintaining a cell ratio of 5:1 for a duration of 72 h. Then the expression levels of CD25 and CD69 on the T cell surface were quantified via flow cytometry. Concurrently, the concentrations of TNF-α and IFN-γ in the culture supernatant were measured.

Co-culture of tumor cells and CD8+T cells

CD8+ T cells were extracted from the tumor tissues of C57BL/6 mice bearing B16F10 tumors, as well as OT-I CD8+ T cells from OT-I transgenic mice with B16F10-OVA tumors. Each T cell population was co-cultured at a 1:1 ratio with B16F10 or B16F10-OVA cells that had been subjected to differential treatments. The expression of MHC-I on tumor cells was assessed using flow cytometry, while TNF-α secretion by CD8+ T cells was quantified in the supernatants using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA).

Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining and histopathological examination

Following the processes of fixation, dehydration, paraffin embedding, and sectioning (with a thickness of 5 μm) of the collected tissue samples, the sections were examined using an optical microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) and subsequently photographed for documentation purposes.

ROS detection

B16F10 cells were exposed to the specified nanoparticles for a duration of 48 h, followed by incubation with 10 μM DCFH-DA for 30 min. Subsequently, the cells underwent three washes with PBS. The nuclei were then counterstained with 300 nM DAPI for 5 min at room temperature under dark conditions, followed by three further PBS washes. Coverslips were mounted, and fluorescence images were captured using an Olympus IX73 fluorescence microscope. The fluorescence intensities were quantified utilizing ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted utilizing Prism software (GraphPad version 8.0), employing either T-tests or analysis of variance (ANOVA). Statistical significance was determined at the levels of ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, and ∗∗∗p < 0.001.

Results and discussion

Preparation and Characterization of HR/LDPP-NPs

The mean hydrodynamic diameter of HR/LDPP-NPs was 248.5 ± 17.4 nm, with a polydispersity index (PDI) of 0.26 ± 0.03 and a zeta potential of −21.4 ± 1.9 mV. In comparison, the HR/LDP-NPs demonstrated an average particle size of 235.8 ± 15.6 nm, a PDI of 0.28 ± 0.02, and a zeta potential of −19.8 ± 1.4 mV. The HR/P-NPs were characterized by an average particle size of 228.6 ± 14.3 nm, a PDI of 0.27 ± 0.02, and a zeta potential of −20.6 ± 1.7 mV. Lastly, the LDPP-NPs displayed an average particle size of 220.7 ± 13.5 nm, a PDI of 0.29 ± 0.04, and a zeta potential of 28.5 ± 1.6 mV (Fig. 1A–C). The anionic surface of the engineered nanoparticles aligns with the literature-reported mechanism in which HA electrostatically repels plasma proteins [[30], [31], [32]]. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis further confirmed that all nanoparticles were approximately spherical and exhibited uniform morphology (Fig. 1D).

The in vitro Stability results indicated minimal changes in particle size, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential over a 14-day period (Fig. 1E–G), thereby demonstrating robust stability. Additionally, under both pH 7.4 (physiological conditions) and pH 6.5 (tumor microenvironment), HR/LDPP-NPs demonstrated notable sustained-release properties for both PTX and PF-06446846 (Fig. 1H and I).

These physicochemical properties suggest that HR/LDPP-NPs possess excellent stability and drug-loading capacity, which are advantageous for extending the circulation time of drugs in the body and thereby enhancing therapeutic efficacy.

Cellular Uptake Efficiency

Before assessing the cellular uptake of HR/LDPP-NPs, we quantified the expression levels of CD44 and integrin αvβ3 on B16F10 and YUMM1.7 melanoma cells. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that approximately 95 % of B16F10 cells were CD44-positive, while 53 % were positive for αvβ3 (Fig. 2A–C). Similarly, 87 % of YUMM1.7 cells expressed CD44, and 41 % expressed αvβ3 (Fig. S2A–C). These findings indicate that a significant proportion of melanoma cells express the αvβ3 integrin, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target.

Cellular Uptake Efficiency of B16F10 Cells for HR/LDPP-NPs

A-C. CD44 and integrin αvβ3 expression on B16F10 cells. D-E. Cellular uptake efficiency of HR/LDPP-NPs. F-G. Competitive uptake experiment. Data are presented as mean ± SD (n = 3). ∗P < 0.05, ∗∗P < 0.01, ∗∗∗P < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001.

Flow cytometry analysis demonstrated a significantly enhanced uptake of Nile Red-labeled HR/LDPP nanoparticles by B16F10 cells in comparison to free Nile Red and non-targeted nanoparticles (Fig. 2D and E). This finding was corroborated by fluorescence imaging, which confirmed the intracellular accumulation of HR/LDPP nanoparticles, exhibiting markedly stronger fluorescence than either free Nile Red or unmodified particles (Fig. S1A–B). Similarly, HR/LDPP nanoparticles showed superior internalization in YUMM1.7 cells compared to control formulations (Fig. S2D and E). These results collectively indicate that dual functionalization with hyaluronic acid (HA) and R8-RGD peptides significantly enhances nanoparticle interaction with melanoma cells, thereby increasing cellular uptake.

The results of competitive uptake experiment indicated a significant inhibition of nanoparticle uptake when an excess of free HA or RGD peptides was pre-added, with the inhibitory effect being more pronounced when both peptides were introduced simultaneously (Fig. 2F and G). These results corroborate that the intracellular uptake of HR/LDPP-NPs is predominantly mediated by the dual receptors CD44 and integrin αvβ3.

Consequently, dual-targeting modification with HA and R8-RGD peptides substantially enhances the interaction between HR/LDPP-NPs and B16F10 cells, thereby increasing their absorption rate, which is pivotal for achieving precise drug delivery.

Antitumor efficacy of HR/LDPP-NPs

In vitro antitumor efficacy of HR/LDPP-NPs

Initially, we assessed the in vitro antitumor efficacy of HR/LDPP-NPs in B16F10 melanoma cells. HR/LDPP-NPs significantly decreased the viability of B16F10 cells (Fig. 3A) and markedly inhibited their colony-forming ability (Fig. 3B and C). Concurrently, there was a significant increase in the apoptosis rate (Fig. 3D and E), along with a notable upregulation of the BAX, resulting in a significant elevation of the BAX/BCL-2 protein expression ratio (Fig. 3F and G). Importantly, the inhibitory effects of HR/LDPP-NPs on B16F10 cells surpassed those observed in the F-CDP group, as indicated by reduced cell viability and proliferation, as well as increased apoptosis rate and BAX/BCL-2 protein expression ratio (Fig. 3A–G). These results suggest that HR/LDPP-NPs exhibit substantial in vitro activity against B16F10 melanoma cells.

A further comparative analysis demonstrated that B16F10 cells treated with HR/LDPP-NPs exhibited reduced viability and proliferation, alongside a significantly increased apoptosis rate and elevated BAX/BCL-2 protein expression ratio, compared to those treated with single-drug-loaded HR/LDP-NPs or HR/P-NPs (Fig. 3A–G). This suggests a synergistic interaction between low-dose PTX and the PF-06446846.

Moreover, relative to the free-form combination group (F-LDP + F-P) and the unmodified LDPP-NPs group, B16F10 cells exposed to HR/LDPP-NPs displayed diminished viability and proliferation, coupled with a higher apoptosis rate and BAX/BCL-2 protein expression ratio (Fig. 3A–G). These findings indicate that the dual-targeting modification with HA and R8-RGD substantially enhances drug targeting, thereby augmenting the antitumor efficacy of HR/LDPP-NPs.

In alignment with findings observed in B16F10 cells, HR/LDPP-NPs demonstrated significant drug synergy and dual-targeting effects in YUMM1.7 melanoma cells. This was evidenced by decreased cell viability (Fig. S3A), reduced colony formation (Fig. S3B–C), and increased apoptosis rates (Fig. S3D–E).

In summary, the in vitro experimental data confirm that HR/LDPP-NPs effectively inhibit melanoma cell growth through mechanisms of drug synergy and dual-targeting precision delivery.

In vivo antitumor Efficacy and Biocompatibility of HR/LDPP-NPs

Prior to assessing the in vivo antitumor efficacy of HR/LDPP-NPs, the tumor-targeting capabilities in vivo was evaluated. Nile Red-labeled HR/LDPP-NPs demonstrated significantly enhanced intratumoral accumulation and an increased tumor-to-liver ratio (TLR) compared to unmodified LDPP-NPs, highlighting their superior tumor-targeting capability (Fig. 4A). Examination of major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney, revealed that approximately 10 % of NR-positive cells were present in the liver of mice treated with both HR/LDPP-NPs and LDPP-NPs, whereas the proportion of positive cells in other organs was below 3 % (Fig. 4A). Fluorescence imaging of excised tumors and liver tissues further corroborated a significantly increased accumulation of HR/LDPP-NPs within the tumor tissue (Fig. S5A). Histological analysis of liver sections stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), along with serum biochemical assessments, indicated no notable hepatotoxicity, as evidenced by aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels that remained comparable to those of the control group (Fig. 4B–D).

The in vivo anticancer efficacy of HR/LDPP-NPs showed that relative to the control group, the HR/LDPP-NPs group exhibited a significant reduction in B16F10 tumor size, alongside a marked elevation in the apoptosis rate and the expression levels of BAX/BCL-2 proteins (Fig. 4E–J). These outcomes surpassed those observed in the F-CDP group, suggesting that HR/LDPP-NPs possess potent in vivo antitumor efficacy.

Furthermore, when compared to single-drug-loaded HR/LDP-NPs or HR/P-NPs, the HR/LDPP-NPs treatment resulted in smaller tumors and higher apoptosis rates and BAX/BCL-2 protein expression levels (Fig. 4E–J), thereby confirming the synergistic antitumor effect of combining low-dose PTX with PF-06446846.

In addition, a comparative analysis of the free F-LDP + F-P combination group with the unmodified LDPP-NPs group revealed that tumors were smaller, and there was an increased rate of apoptosis along with elevated expression levels of BAX/BCL-2 proteins following treatment with HR/LDPP-NPs (Fig. 4E–J). This underscores the efficacy of HA and R8-RGD dual-modified nanoparticles in enhancing the targeting capabilities of drug therapy.

In alignment with the experimental findings observed in B16F10 tumor-bearing mice, the administration of HR/LDPP-NP in mice with YUMM1.7 tumors led to a significant decrease in tumor volume (Fig. S4A–B) and a pronounced increase in tumor cell apoptosis (Fig. S4C–D). These results suggest a robust drug synergy and effective dual-targeting capability.

During the safety assessment, the body-weight profiles remained consistent across all groups (Fig. 4K), and no statistically significant differences in survival rates were observed (see Fig. 4N). Additionally, liver function was assessed through the measurement of ALT and AST levels, revealing no significant liver damage in any group, with the exception of the F-CDP group (Fig. 4L and M).

In conclusion, the in vivo experimental outcomes of this study corroborate the findings from previous in vitro experiments, demonstrating that HR/LDPP-NPs exhibit a potent anti-melanoma effect in vivo while ensuring favorable biosafety profiles. The two tumor models validated the generalizability of HR/LDPP-NPs, and their synergistic antitumor mechanism was further elucidated through immunomodulatory analyses, as detailed in Section 3.3.3.

HR/LDPP-NPs elicit an In situ vaccine response and stimulate immune activation via PTX-induced immunogenic cell death

The immunogenic death of tumor cells is known to activate anti-tumor immune responses [44,45]. A critical aspect of preventing tumor immune evasion involves inhibiting the downregulation of MHC-I expression on the surface of tumor cells [[46], [47], [48]]. PTX is capable of inducing apoptosis in tumor cells while simultaneously enhancing the expression or release of key ICD markers, including CRT, ATP, and HMGB1, on the tumor cell surface. This process increases the immunogenicity of the tumor cells and activates anti-tumor immune responses [49,50]. DCs, as professional antigen-presenting cells (APCs), play a pivotal role in efficiently capturing and presenting antigens, thereby inducing T cell activation and initiating anti-tumor immune responses [[51], [52], [53]]. Based on these insights, we propose that HR/LDPP-NPs may exert anti-melanoma effects by activating anti-tumor immunity and inhibiting immune escape mechanisms.

To investigate whether PTX-driven antigen release and PCSK9-blockade-mediated antigen presentation constitute a synergistic feedback loop, we initially assessed the in situ vaccine response elicited by HR/LDPP-NPs. In comparison to the control group, HR/LDPP-NPs significantly enhanced calreticulin exposure on both B16F10 and YUMM1.7 cells and upregulated MHC-I expression (Fig. 5A–B, Fig. S6A, C, E, F). Additionally, serum levels of ATP and HMGB1 were markedly increased in both tumor models (Fig. 5C–D, Fig. S6G–H). Within tumor tissues, there was an elevation in the proportions of CD25+ and CD69+ activated CD8+ and CD4+ T cells (Fig. 5E–H, Fig. S6A, C, I-L). Furthermore, in the draining lymph nodes, the percentage of dendritic cells increased, accompanied by significant upregulation of the maturation markers CD80 and CD86 (Fig. 5I–K, Fig. S6B, D, M − O). Importantly, HR/LDPP-NPs demonstrated superior efficacy compared to F-CDP across all measured parameters, thereby confirming their enhanced capability to activate anti-tumor immunity and inhibit immune evasion in vivo.

In both tumor models, the administration of HR/LDPP-NPs, as opposed to the single-drug groups HR/LDP-NPs or HR/P-NPs, resulted in a significantly elevated degree of CRT exposure and MHC-I expression within the tumor tissues of mice. This treatment was also associated with a marked increase in the infiltration of activated CD4+ and CD8+ T cells into the tissues, alongside a significant rise in serum concentrations of ATP and HMGB1. Moreover, there was a substantial enhancement in the proportion of dendritic cells (DCs) in tumor-draining lymph nodes, as well as in the expression levels of the surface maturation markers CD80 and CD86 (Fig. 5A–K, Fig. S6A–O). These results indicate a synergistic interaction between low-dose PTX and PF-06446846, which collectively augment the immune regulatory function of HR/LDPP-NPs.

In comparison to free F-LDP + P or unmodified LDPP-NPs within both the B16F10 and YUMM1.7 tumor models, HR/LDPP-NPs demonstrated significantly enhanced efficacy (Fig. 5A–K, Fig. S6A–O). This underscores the benefits of HA- and R8-RGD-mediated dual targeting. Such modifications facilitate precise and efficient drug delivery to the tumor microenvironment, thereby enhancing immunomodulatory activity.

In conclusion, HR/LDPP-NPs facilitate low-dose PTX-induced immunogenic cell death (ICD), which stimulates dendritic cells to phagocytose tumor-associated antigens and enhance the expression of maturation markers CD80 and CD86. This process subsequently activates CD8+ and CD4+ T cells, thereby creating a self-perpetuating antitumor immune response. This phenomenon, described as an "in situ vaccine" effect, initiates systemic immunity in the absence of exogenous antigen administration.

HR/LDPP-NPs augment tumor immunogenicity and stimulate the dendritic cell–T-cell axis through paclitaxel-induced immunogenic Cell death

To evaluate the potential of HR/LDPP-NPs in inducing immunogenic cell death (ICD) and initiating antitumor immunity, we assessed their impact on the immunogenicity of B16F10 cells, the maturation of dendritic cells, and the activation of T cells. In comparison to the control groups, B16F10 cells treated with HR/LDPP-NPs demonstrated significantly enhanced immunogenicity, increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, improved dendritic cell maturation, and heightened T-cell activation, surpassing the performance of the F-CDP group in all evaluated parameters. Notably, there was a substantial increase in calreticulin (CRT) surface exposure on B16F10 cells, along with significantly elevated levels of ATP and HMGB1 in the supernatant (Fig. 6A–C, Fig. S7A). Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were significantly elevated (Fig. 6D). The co-incubation of bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (DCs) with HR/LDPP-NP-treated B16F10 cells resulted in a marked up-regulation of the maturation markers CD80 and CD86 (Fig. 6E–F, Fig. S7B). Subsequent co-culture of these mature DCs with naive CD8+ and CD4+ T cells led to a significant increase in the expression of CD25 and CD69 on T cells, as well as an enhancement in the secretion of TNF-α by CD8+ T cells and IFN-γ by CD4+ T cells (Fig. 6G–J, Fig. S7C–D). Collectively, these findings indicate that HR/LDPP-NPs, through ROS-mediated endoplasmic reticulum stress, effectively enhance the immunogenicity of tumor cells, promote robust DC maturation, and elicit potent T-cell responses.

In comparison to single-drug-loaded HR/LDP-NPs or HR/P-NPs, the administration of HR/LDPP-NPs to B16F10 cells resulted in significantly enhanced outcomes, including increased CRT exposure, elevated ATP and HMGB1 content, heightened ROS levels, and upregulated expression of CD80 and CD86 on BMDCs, as well as the activation of naive T cells (Fig. 6A–L, Fig. S7A–D). These findings indicate a synergistic interaction between low-dose PTX and PF-06446846, which collectively augment the potent anti-tumor efficacy of HR/LDPP-NPs.

Moreover, the HR/LDPP-NPs demonstrated superior performance compared to both the free F-LDP + F-P combination group and the unmodified LDPP-NPs group, as evidenced by elevated ROS levels, enhanced tumor cell immunogenicity, promotion of dendritic cell maturation, and activation of T cells (Fig. 6A–L, Fig. S7A–D). This highlights the targeting advantage provided by the dual modification with HA and R8-RGD, which enables precise and efficient drug delivery, thereby enhancing the anti-tumor efficacy.

In summary, through the dual mechanisms of drug synergy and dual-targeted precise delivery, HR/LDPP-NPs effectively enhance the immunogenicity of tumor cells, induce DC maturation, and activate T cell immune responses.

Inhibition of PCSK9 enhances MHC-I expression to improve antigen presentation and T-cell recognition in the context of In situ vaccine response

We next examined whether PCSK9 inhibition potentiated MHC-I–mediated antigen presentation. The findings revealed that, in comparison to the control group, B16F10 pre-treated with HR/LDPP-NPs exhibited a marked upregulation of MHC-I expression (Fig. 7A). Additionally, the TNF-α levels secreted by CD8+ T cells were markedly elevated (Fig. 7B). These metrics surpassed those observed in the F-CDP pre-group, demonstrating that HR/LDPP-NPs substantially enhance MHC-I expression in tumor cells, facilitate the recognition and activation of CD8+ T cells, and effectively inhibit immune escape.

A further comparison of the groups, specifically HR/LDP-NPs and HR/P-NPs, demonstrated that B16F10 treated with HR/LDPP-NPs exhibited significantly elevated MHC-I expression and TNF-α levels (Fig. 7A and B). This finding suggests a synergistic interaction between low-dose PTX and PF-06446846, which collectively enhance the inhibitory effect of HR/LDPP-NPs on immune escape.

Moreover, the efficacy of HR/LDPP-NPs surpassed that of both the free F-LDP + F-P combination group and the unmodified LDPP-NPs group (Fig. 7A and B), underscoring the targeting advantage conferred by the dual modification with HA and R8-RGD. This dual modification facilitates precise and efficient drug delivery, leading to more effective inhibition of immune escape.

We further demonstrated that HR/LDPP-NPs enhance tumor antigen presentation and activate antigen-specific CD8+ T cells through the inhibition of PCSK9. The surface expression of MHC-I on B16F10-OVA cells was significantly upregulated following HR/LDPP-NP treatment compared to all control groups (Fig. 7C). In co-culture experiments, OT-I CD8+ T cells isolated from tumor-bearing mice secreted substantially higher levels of TNF-α when incubated with HR/LDPP-NP–pre-treated B16F10-OVA cells (Fig. 7D). These findings confirm that HR/LDPP-NPs synergistically enhance tumor antigen-specific immune recognition and response at both the antigen presentation and T-cell activation stages.

In conclusion, the inhibition of PCSK9 prevents the degradation of MHC-I, significantly increasing the density of peptide–MHC-I complexes on tumor cells. When combined with PTX-induced immunogenic cell death (ICD), this process establishes a positive feedback loop wherein stabilized MHC-I presents abundant tumor antigens, thereby promoting robust CD8+ T-cell activation and substantial TNF-α secretion. These findings demonstrate that the synergistic mechanism of HR/LDPP-NPs effectively counteracts melanoma immune evasion and significantly enhances T-cell recognition within the in situ vaccine response.

Conclusion

This study presents and validates a novel nanoparticle-based "chemo-immunological in situ vaccine" strategy. The administration of low-dose paclitaxel induces immunogenic cell death (ICD), transforming tumor cells into an endogenous antigen reservoir while releasing danger signals that initiate canonical in situ vaccination, thereby promoting dendritic cell maturation and T-cell activation. Simultaneously, the PCSK9 inhibitor maintains elevated tumor-surface MHC-I expression, addressing antigen-presentation deficiencies that are a fundamental mechanism of immune evasion. The dual targeting of HR/LDPP-NPs with hyaluronic acid and R8-RGD ensures the spatiotemporally co-localized delivery of both therapeutic agents, effectively linking antigen release (induced by paclitaxel-mediated ICD) to antigen presentation (sustained by PCSK9 inhibitor-maintained MHC-I), thereby establishing an efficient immune-activation loop (see Fig. 7E). This approach enhances the immunomodulatory efficacy of low-dose chemotherapy and proposes a novel paradigm for overcoming the tumor's immunosuppressive microenvironment.
