Diffusion and contrast-enhancement MRI phenotypes associated with immune checkpoint inhibitor exposure and with immune cell infiltration in brain metastases
Authors: Francesco Sanvito, Gianluca Nocera, Zexi Wang, Eileen Shiuan, Lu Sun, Sonoko Oshima, Asher Kim, Irina Kryukov, Guowen Shao, Nicholas S Cho, Noriko Salamon, Benjamin M Ellingson, Robert Prins, Won Kim, Jingwen Yao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMRI-derived apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) and contrast-enhancement (CE) may represent noninvasive biomarkers to evaluate microstructural tissue changes and histopathological immune cell infiltration induced by immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI).MethodsOne hundred and twenty-five lesions across 93 patients were analyzed. ADC (reflecting water diffusivity) and CE T1-subtraction maps (reflecting blood-brain barrier permeability) tumor values were used for bivariate histograms of ADC and CE, and to quantify voxel fractions belonging to highADC-highCE and lowADC-lowCE quadrants. The association between ICI exposure and voxel frequency in ADC-CE quadrants was evaluated with multivariate mixed-effects models accounting for corticosteroid exposure and other variables. In a subset of patients (n = 23), the association between immune cell counts (CD45 + cell density) from tissue samples and ADC-CE phenotypes was tested.ResultsICI and corticosteroid exposure were associated with distinct ADC-CE phenotypes on bivariate histograms. ICI-exposed lesions showed significantly higher voxel frequency in the highADC-highCE quadrant (P < .001) and lower voxel frequency in the lowADC-lowCE quadrant (P < .01), compared to ICI-naïve lesions. Multivariate analyses confirmed that ICI exposure was an independent determinant of higher voxel frequency in the highADC-highCE quadrant (P = .001) and lower voxel frequency in the lowADC-lowCE quadrant (P = .01) in the absence of corticosteroid treatment. Corticosteroid usage significantly influenced the relationship between ADC-CE phenotypes and ICI exposure. Voxel frequency in ADC-CE quadrants was correlated with CD45 + cell density in histopathology (partial ρ = 0.56 P = .01 for highADC-highCE, partial ρ = –0.64 P < .01 for lowADC-lowCE).ConclusionsADC-CE phenotypes are associated with ICI exposure and immune cell infiltration in BM, representing potential noninvasive markers to monitor ICI-induced pathophysiological changes.

Importance of the Study

This study, conducted on 125 brain metastases, demonstrates that exposure to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) determines distinct diffusion and contrast-enhancement MRI phenotypes (ADC-CE phenotypes). These phenotypes also correlate with histopathological immune cell infiltration, suggesting that ADC-CE phenotypes may serve as in-vivo noninvasive biomarkers of pathophysiological changes induced by ICI treatments, and may potentially be used to monitor treatment response. Notably, corticosteroid administration influences the relationship between ICI exposure and ADC-CE phenotypes, suggesting that the reliability of this biomarker may be limited to lesions off corticosteroid treatment.

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) are a type of immunotherapy currently included among the systemic treatment strategies to manage brain metastases (BM),1 especially for melanoma.2 ICI have become the standard of care for many advanced-stage malignancies, including certain subtypes that commonly metastasize to the brain such as melanoma, non-small cell lung cancer without actionable genomic alterations, triple-negative breast cancer, and renal cell carcinoma.3 In these cases, ICI are typically combined with chemotherapy or targeted therapy, as well as local radiotherapy or surgery, with the notable exception of melanoma in which ICI alone have shown significant response in patients with BM.4 By targeting immune checkpoints that inhibit T-cell activation, such as PD-1 and CTLA-4 pathways,5 ICI administration promotes T-cell activation and favors the infiltration of T cells and, more in general, immune cells within brain tumor tissue.6–9 Indeed, a more pronounced immune cell infiltration, particularly of T cells, was shown to be associated with ICI treatment success.5 Therefore, noninvasive biomarkers reflecting immune cell infiltration in BM would represent a valuable tool to monitor the response to ICI treatment.

The apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) derived from diffusion MRI10,11 is a noninvasive metric that reflects tissue microstructure and is influenced by tissue factors such as cell density, extracellular matrix composition, and vasogenic edema.12–17 Recent studies reported that ADC-derived measurements may be associated with ICI efficacy in glioblastoma.18,19 In particular, a more favorable prognosis following ICI treatment was seen in lesions showing higher post-ICI ADC values18 and higher post-ICI ADC elevation compared to pre-ICI.19 Similar findings were also seen using voxel-wise ADC analyses applied to pediatric brain tumors treated with peptide-based vaccines.20 Additionally, anecdotal findings from patients treated at our institution suggest that pronounced immune cell infiltration found in histological samples from BM can correspond to regions with high ADC values on MRI, accompanied by prominent contrast enhancement (CE).

In the present study, we investigated how diffusion and CE MRI phenotypes are associated with ICI treatment and with histopathological immune cell infiltration in BM. Since ICI treatment can promote immune cell infiltration in brain tumor tissue, we hypothesized that a distinctive MRI phenotype with higher diffusivity and more pronounced CE can be seen in lesions exposed to ICI treatment and in lesions with higher immune cell counts on surgical tissue samples.

Methods

Patient Selection and Clinical Information

The cohort of patients evaluated in this study was formed by combining 3 separate sub-cohorts of patients with treatment-naïve and/or newly diagnosed brain metastases (BM) who were part of dedicated IRB-approved studies, for which they provided written informed consent:

Sub-cohort A (IRB-18-0300) included patients who underwent craniotomy and surgical resection, previously described in a different study that analyzed their histopathological data but not MRI data.9 For sub-cohort A, the presurgical MRI timepoint was used for imaging analysis.

Sub-cohort B (IRB-19-20-84) was selected by filtering patients with a diagnosis of BM among patients who were scanned with a standardized brain tumor imaging protocol.21

Sub-cohort B (IRB-19-20-84) was selected by filtering patients with a diagnosis of BM among patients who were scanned with a standardized brain tumor imaging protocol.21

Sub-cohort C (IRB-21-1070) is composed of patients with BM who received laser interstitial thermal therapy (LITT), previously analyzed in a study on LITT efficacy.22 For sub-cohort C, the most recent pre-LITT MRI scan was used for the imaging analysis.

Sub-cohort C (IRB-21-1070) is composed of patients with BM who received laser interstitial thermal therapy (LITT), previously analyzed in a study on LITT efficacy.22 For sub-cohort C, the most recent pre-LITT MRI scan was used for the imaging analysis.

All patients were consecutive within their sub-cohort. The final cohort of the study was obtained after combining the sub-cohorts, excluding patients with MRI artifacts or non-measurable disease (see next paragraph), and excluding duplicates (patients belonging to more than one sub-cohort). Ultimately, the final cohort included one single MRI timepoint for each patient, with no duplicate patients. The whole final cohort was used for the analysis testing the association between MRI phenotypes and prior treatments or clinical variables. Only sub-cohort A had available histopathological tissue for the analysis of immune cell infiltration.

For each patient within the final cohort, clinical characteristics and medical history were annotated, including age, sex category, systemic treatment with ICI in the 6 months prior to MRI (“ICI exposure”), primary tumor (grouped as “lung,” “melanoma,” or “other”—due to limitations related to the number of items). Since ADC and CE can be influenced by corticosteroids23,24 and anti-VEGF treatments,25–27 we also annotated extended systemic treatment (for at least 2 months) with corticosteroids, in the 6 months prior to MRI (“steroid exposure”), and anti-VEGF treatments (including both monoclonal antibodies and tyrosine kinase inhibitors) in the 6 months prior to MRI (“anti-VEGF exposure”). For each patient, all measurable lesions were included in the analyses (see “Imaging Acquisition and Processing” paragraph), and lesion-specific treatment history was annotated, in particular, whether the lesion was previously treated with radiation (“prior RT” within 6 months, and/or at any time).

Imaging Acquisition and Processing

MR images were acquired according to BTIPs21 in all sub-cohorts, and the imaging protocol included pre- and post-contrast T1-weighted images (T1w-pre and T1w-post), and diffusion-weighted images (DWI) acquired with b-value 0 and 1000 s/mm2 (Figure 1). T1w-pre and T1w-post were normalized, co-registered, and subtracted voxel-wise to obtain T1-subtraction maps (Figure 1E), as previously described in other studies.28,29 ADC (Figure 1D) maps were calculated on the scanner from DWI images and were registered to T1w-post with an affine linear registration, using the flirt function (FSL; University of Oxford; https://fsl.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl/).

MRI acquisition and processing. Post-contrast (A) and pre-contrast (B) T1-weighted images, were used for tumor segmentation (C). Tumor values of apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) (D) and T1-subtraction maps (E) were extracted from the segmentation and used to obtain bivariate scatter dot plots (F) and bivariate histograms (G).

A neuroradiologist (F.S.) and a neurosurgeon (G.N.) with a background in brain tumor imaging were selected as experienced readers to perform the following evaluations, while blinded to the clinical information and treatment history. First, either one of the experienced readers excluded cases with excessive susceptibility artifacts due to metal or blood products in the tumor area, which would affect the accuracy of ADC measurements. Second, they reviewed each MRI scan to identify all BM and to determine which ones were considered “measurable” according to RANO 2.0 criteria (ie, at least ≥1 × ≥1 × ≥1 cm on 3D imaging).30 Since the ADC measurements in smaller lesions are more easily contaminated by partial volume effects, “non-measurable” lesions were not included in the analysis. The NS-HGlio v.2.0 deep learning model (Neosoma Inc, neosomainc.com)31 was used to automatically obtain a preliminary tumor segmentation of the contrast-enhancing lesions. Then, either one of the experienced readers curated the final contrast-enhancing segmentation of each “measurable” lesion (Figure 1C), by separating different lesions into different segmentations and applying manual edits when needed, using ITK snap (itk-snap.org).32 The final segmentation included the lesion-specific contrast-enhancing tumor tissue, without cystic or necrotic components, nor peri-lesional edema.

From each lesion, voxel-wise measurements of ADC and T1-subtraction (used as a semi-quantitative surrogate of CE intensity) were extracted and used to compile a bivariate scatter dot plot (Figure 1F), using an in-house MATLAB script (MathWorks). By applying thresholds to the scatter dot plot, it was possible to quantify the fraction of voxels belonging to 4 different quadrants: lowADC-lowCE, lowADC-highCE, highADC-lowCE, and highADC-highCE. The thresholds were decided empirically (see “Statistical Analysis” paragraph). Bivariate histograms were also obtained (Figure 1G), where each bivariate bin represents the fraction of voxels exhibiting a certain combination of ADC and CE.

Histological Immune Cell Infiltration

In sub-cohort A, tissue samples were available from the surgical resection performed after the MRI scan. Surgical tissue samples were fixed in formalin (10%) and embedded in paraffin. Sections with a thickness of 5 μm were used for immunofluorescence staining with markers including CD45. Slides were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and heat-induced antigen retrieval was obtained. After staining, a Leica Aperio Versa 200 Slide Scanning Microscope was used to image the slides with a resolution of 20×. HALO Image Analysis software (Indica Labs) was used to quantify cell staining per mm2 tissue, as previously described.9 First, slides that were not stained with the primary antibody were used as negative control, to measure the fluorescent signal attributable to nonspecific background and autofluorescence staining. Then, on samples stained with the primary antibody, fluorescence exceeding the nonspecific signal was considered true positive. The tissue area for analysis was selected by excluding regions with autofluorescent and nonspecific backgrounds. The CD45 + cell density (count of CD45 + cells per mm2 tissue) was used as a marker of immune cell infiltration within the BM tissue samples. CD45 is an antigen expressed in all leukocytes,33 and CD45 + cells infiltrating brain metastasis tissue include both lymphoid (eg, CD4 + T lymphocytes and CD8 + T lymphocytes) and myeloid cells (eg, macrophages).9 For a subset of samples, also CD4 and CD8 stainings were available, and CD4 + cell density and CD8 + cell density were calculated.

Statistical Analysis

The ADC-CE bivariate histograms, representing voxel fractions in each bin, were averaged across all lesions, and across lesion groups of interest, to visualize group differences in ADC-CE distributions. Additionally, the voxel fractions within each bin of the bivariate histograms were compared between ICI-naïve and ICI-exposed BM, with a bin-wise t-test. The distribution of the bin-wise t-statistic was used to empirically determine an ADC threshold and a CE threshold to distinguish lowADC-lowCE, lowADC-highCE, highADC-lowCE, and highADC-highCE quadrants. Since our hypothesis was that ICI exposure would determine higher ADC values and a more pronounced CE, our analyses focused on 2 quadrants of interest: highADC-highCE and lowADC-lowCE—which we anticipated to be more represented in ICI-exposed and in ICI-naïve BM, respectively. The same thresholds were consistently applied to each lesion-specific ADC-CE scatter dot plot to calculate the fraction of voxels belonging to each quadrant, and then compare the resulting values between ICI-naïve and ICI-exposed BM using a t-test. Since corticosteroid use is known to affect both ADC23,34,35 and CE,24 these analyses were repeated in the subset of lesions that did not have a recent steroid exposure.

To evaluate more rigorously the association between ICI exposure and ADC-CE phenotypes, while accounting for confounding factors and for multiple observations within the same patient, we developed a multivariate mixed-effects model:

In this model, voxel fractionij is the fraction of voxels belonging to the quadrant of interest, representing the dependent variable for the j-th observation of the i-th patient. β0 is the intercept. β1 to β8 are the coefficients for the fixed effects. β1 is the coefficient for ICIi, the ICI exposure (coded as: 1 = yes, 0 = no). β2 is the coefficient for Steroidi, the steroid exposure (coded as: 1 = yes, 0 = no). β3 is the coefficient for the interaction variable of ICI and steroid exposure. Of note, the presence of the interaction variable determines that β1 should be interpreted as the effect of ICI exposure in the absence of steroid exposure (ICI | No steroids) and that β2 should be interpreted as the effect of steroid exposure in the absence of ICI exposure (Steroids | No ICI). β3 not only can be read as the interaction coefficient but can also be used to calculate the coefficient β1+β3, corresponding to the effect of ICI exposure in the presence of steroid exposure (ICI | Steroids). The mathematical procedure to calculate a p-value corresponding to the combined coefficient β1+β3 is described in Supplementary Methods. β4 is the coefficient for RTij, the prior RT treatment within 6 months (coded as: 1 = yes, 0 = no; note that this covariate is specific to each lesion). The primary tumor is coded as 2 variables: β5 is the coefficient for a variable encoding whether the primary tumor was a melanoma (Primary Melanomai, coded as: 0 = lung or other, 1 = melanoma), β6 is the coefficient for a variable encoding whether the primary tumor belonged to the “other” category (Primary Otheri, coded as: 0 = lung or melanoma, 1 = other). Hence, in this model, β5 can be interpreted as the coefficient for melanoma compared to lung, and β6 as the coefficient for other primary tumors compared to lung (since lung represents the baseline condition, coded as 0 in both variables). β7 and β8 represent the coefficients for age and sex category. Patienti accounts for the random effect linked to certain lesions belonging to the same patient and the same MRI scan (therefore, it accounts for both biological and technical aspects that can make 2 lesions from the same patient not independent). Finally, ϵij is the residual error term. This model was repeated twice, using as dependent variables the voxel fraction for either one of the quadrants of interest: highADC-highCE and lowADC-lowCE.

As a sensitivity analysis, 2 alternative models were also run. In the first alternative model, the previous RT within 6 months was replaced with the previous RT at any time. While covarying for RT within 6 months was aimed at adjusting for potential early inflammatory effects induced by RT, covarying for RT at all timepoint was aimed at also accounting for late effects such as radiation necrosis. In the second alternative model, steroid exposure was replaced with steroid and/or anti-VEGF exposure, to also adjust for potential ADC changes25,26 and CE changes27 introduced by anti-VEGF treatments. It was not possible to covary for anti-VEGF treatments using a stand-alone covariate, due to the small number of lesions treated with these therapies within our cohort. Finally, other 2 alternative models were implemented to verify that adjusting for lesion location and side would not modify the results of the main model.

In sub-cohort A, the correlation between the log-transformed histological CD45 + cell density and the voxel fractions within each bin of the bivariate histograms was evaluated with Spearman correlations, to obtain bin-wise Spearman coefficients (ρ). Additionally, the correlation between the histological CD45 + cell density and the fraction of voxels belonging to each quadrant of the bivariate scatter dot plot was evaluated by calculating a partial Spearman’s ρ coefficient, which allowed to assess the correlation while controlling for steroid and RT exposure.

Results

Patients’ Characteristics

The final cohort consisted of 93 patients, with a total of 125 lesions that were selected for analysis. Histopathological data was available for 23 lesions from 23 different patients (sub-cohort A). Clinical characteristics and medical history are summarized in Table 1. Twenty-two lesions (4 with histopathological data) were exposed to ICI. Primary tumor was categorized as: lung (n = 40), melanoma (n = 28), or other (n = 57), due to the relatively low sample size of non-lung non-melanoma primary tumors. The primary tumor origins categorized as “other” included: breast (n = 25), kidney and urinary tract (n = 7), gastrointestinal tract (n = 5), head and neck (n = 4), gynecological (n = 8), and carcinoma of unknown origin (n = 8). 49 lesions received prior local radiation (n = 46 received a single SRS, n = 2 received multiple SRS, n = 1 received IMRT), and 24 of these lesions were irradiated within 6 months before MRI.

Group Comparisons According to ICI-Exposure

BM recently treated with ICI showed a distinctive ADC-CE phenotype with bivariate histograms compared to ICI-naïve BM, when considering lesions without recent steroid exposure. As expected, ICI-treated BM visually showed a higher voxel frequency in bins with high ADC and high CE values, compared to ICI-naïve BM (Figure 2D vs Figure 2B). Indeed, high-frequency bins (red) lie more superiorly (more intense CE) and more to the right side of the histogram (higher ADC values) in lesions treated with ICI (Figure 2D vs Figure 2B). Conversely, when considering lesions recently exposed to steroids, this visual distinction between ICI-treated and ICI-naïve is less clear (Figure 2E vs Figure 2C). This is probably due to steroids interfering with ICI. Indeed, considering ICI-treated lesions, it can be noted that high-frequency bins (red) are located more inferiorly and more to the left of the histogram in steroid-exposed lesions compared to non-steroid-exposed ones (Figure 2E vs Figure 2D). As a result, ICI-treated lesions that recently received steroids (Figure 2E) present a histogram pattern similar to the ICI-naïve lesions (Figure 2B), suggesting that the steroid usage may interfere with a potential ICI-induced MRI phenotype switch. Analogous distinctive phenotypes can be seen when stratifying histograms according to primary tumors (Supplementary Figure 1).

Group comparison of ADC-CE phenotypes according to ICI exposure. Lesions that were treated with ICI had a distinct ADC-CE phenotype, with a greater fraction of highADC-highCE voxels and a smaller fraction of lowADC-lowCE voxels (D vs B), which was not appreciable in the case of corticosteroid exposure (E vs. C). Bin-wise (F, G) and quadrant-wise (H–K) statistical tests confirmed that these differences in ADC-CE phenotypes between ICI groups are significant and that the effect size is greater when the steroid-naïve lesions are isolated (G and J–K) compared to analyses conducted on all lesions (F and H–I).

The bin-wise t-test based on bivariate histograms showed that bins with a positive t-statistic (red) clustered in the upper right corner of the histogram, while negative t-statistics (blue) clustered on the left side and in particular in the lower left corner (Figure 2F–G). This distribution is appreciable when considering all lesions (Figure 2F), and even more pronounced when lesions without recent steroid exposure are isolated (Figure 2G). The visualization of these t-statistic histograms was used to empirically determine an ADC threshold and a CE threshold. Since the highly positive t-statistics clustered in bins corresponding to ADC ≥ 1 × 10−3 mm2/s and T1-subtraction ≥ 0.3, these thresholds were used to define the upper right highADC-highCE quadrant, and ADC < 1 × 10−3 mm2/s and T1-subtraction <0.3 were used as thresholds to define the lowADC-lowCE quadrant. As expected, the ICI-exposed lesions showed significantly greater voxel fractions in the highADC-highCE quadrant and smaller voxel fractions in the lowADC-lowCE quadrant (P < .05, Figure 2H–I), and this group difference was even more pronounced in the subset of lesions with no confounding effects from recent steroid exposure (P < .001 for the highADC-highCE quadrant and P < .01 for the lowADC-lowCE quadrant, Figure 2J–K). This analysis confirmed that ADC-CE phenotypes are influenced by ICI exposure and that ADC-CE differences due to ICI exposure can be clouded by steroid exposure.

Determinants of MRI Phenotypes From Multivariate Analysis

Multivariate analysis using mixed-effects models revealed significant variables influencing diffusion and CE phenotypes (Table 2, the adjusted R2 of the models were 0.57 for the highADC-highCE model, and 0.82 for the lowADC-lowCE model). First, as previously shown in the group comparison analyses, the multivariate analysis confirmed, more rigorously, that ICI exposure was a significant independent determinant of ADC-CE phenotypes in the absence of steroid exposure (“ICI | No steroids”). In more detail, ICI exposure determines a greater fraction of highADC-highCE voxels (P = .001) and a smaller fraction of lowADC-lowCE voxels (P = .01). Notably, ICI exposure did not have a significant effect on ADC-CE phenotypes in the presence of steroid exposure (“ICI | Steroids,” P = .22 for highADC-highCE quadrants, P = .41 for lowADC-lowCE quadrants). Indeed, the interaction between ICI exposure and steroid exposure in influencing ADC-CE phenotypes was significant (P = .002 highADC-highCE, P = .02 lowADC-lowCE). Prior RT was also an independent determinant of higher ADC and more pronounced CE (P = .001 highADC-highCE, P = .03 lowADC-lowCE). Primary tumor was also an independent determinant of ADC-CE phenotypes, with melanoma and “other” primary tumors both showing significantly lower ADC and less pronounced CE compared to lung primary, after adjusting for other covariates. Finally, steroid exposure in the absence of ICI (“Steroids | No ICI”) did not significantly influence ADC-CE phenotypes after adjusting for other covariates. The results of the alternative models used for the sensitivity analysis were comparable to the main model, demonstrating that the association between ADC-CE phenotypes and ICI exposure is still significant when adjusting for RT at any time or including steroid and/or anti-VEGF treatments as an aggregate covariate (Supplementary Table 1–2). Similarly, also adjusting for lesion location or size did not affect the main results of the model (Supplementary Table 3–4), and neither location nor size significantly impacted ADC-CE phenotypes.

Correlation With Immune Cell Infiltration

In the subset of lesions with histological samples available (sub-cohort A), the bin-wise Spearman’s correlation showed that bins in the upper right corner of the histogram (bins with high ADC and high CE) had a positive correlation (red) with immune cell infiltration (quantified as CD45 + cell density from the tumor tissue), while bins in the lower left corner had a negative correlation (blue; Figure 3A). When applying the same consistent thresholds of ADC ≥ 1 × 10-3 mm2/s and T1-subtraction ≥0.3, the voxel fractions in the quadrants of interest were significantly correlated with immune cell infiltration (Figure 3B–C). In more detail, voxel fractions in the highADC-highCE quadrant were directly correlated with tissue immune cell density (partial ρ = 0.56, P = .01, Figure 3B) and voxel fractions in the lowADC-lowCE quadrant were inversely correlated with tissue immune cell density (partial ρ = −0.64, P < .01, Figure 3C). This is consistent with immune cell infiltration being associated with higher diffusivity and more pronounced contrast enhancement. In a subset of lesions (n = 8), descriptive data suggests that lesions with higher values of highADC-highCE voxel fractions (and lower fractions of lowADC-lowCE voxels) exhibit a more prominent increase of CD8 + cell density within tumor tissue and a less prominent increase of CD4 + cell density (Supplementary Table 5).

Correlation between ADC-CE MRI phenotypes and immune cell infiltration on histology. A greater immune cell density on histology positively correlated with the tumor voxel fraction in highADC-highCE bins (upper right corner in A) and in the highADC-highCE quadrant (B). Conversely, it negatively correlated with the tumor voxel fraction in lowADC-lowCE bins (lower left corner in A) and in the lowADC-lowCE quadrant (C).

Discussion

Noninvasive tools for monitoring therapy-induced pathophysiological changes are continuously sought in neuro-oncology. The efficacy of immunotherapies such as ICI is thought to be associated with boosted anti-tumor immunity, which is reflected in the increased presence of T cells and other immune cells within the tumor tissue,5 as evident from recent work proposing that ICI promote transition in BM from an immune exclusion state to an immune infiltration state.9 Our study shows that exposure to ICI treatment and histopathological immune cell infiltration in BM are associated with a distinctive MRI phenotype, consisting of a high prevalence of tumor regions with combined high water diffusivity (as measured with ADC) and pronounced CE (as measured with T1-subtraction maps). This finding was evident both from univariate group analyses and from more rigorous multivariate analyses accounting for covariate interplay and random effects, which supports the study’s hypothesis that ADC-CE phenotypes can potentially serve as noninvasive markers to monitor the ability of ICI treatments in inducing immune cell infiltration within the tumor tissue. Notably, this composite marker can be obtained easily from sequences included in standardized BTIPs21 (ie, parameter-matched T1w-pre and T1w-post, and DWI with b-value = 1000 s/mm2), therefore also on retrospective datasets.

This study’s results are consistent with the current understanding of ICI mechanisms and diffusion MRI and CE semeiology. While an ICI-naïve tumor microenvironment may present immunosuppressive features and promote immune surveillance escape through immune checkpoint signaling, ICI promote immune cell activation and production of pro-inflammatory cytokines.36 Cytokines and arachidonic acid are responsible for endothelial fenestrations and defective tight junctions37,38 as well as local inflammation, ultimately leading to increased blood-brain barrier permeability and promoting immune cell tissue infiltration. Notably, recent evidence suggests that increased vascular permeability is induced by ICI not only through the inflammatory cascade but also through T cells directly targeting endothelial cells.39–41 On MRI, an increased vascular permeability would result in a higher degree of contrast agent extravasation, showing more pronounced CE along with vasogenic edema in the extravascular extracellular space that leads to increased diffusivity and ADC values, consistent with our observations. This interpretation has also been proposed by other authors, who suggested that immunotherapies cause an edema-induced ADC increase, as well as more prominent contrast enhancement.20 An additional possible explanation for our findings is that ICI-induced immune cell infiltration may potentially cause ADC elevation through changes in tissue cell density (eg, due to T-cell mediated tumor cell death), or through changes in other microstructural features such as extracellular matrix composition.12–17 Our main result is also consistent with previous reports of elevation in ADC-derived metrics after ICI treatment being associated with improved prognosis in recurrent glioblastoma, potentially related to a more boosted anti-tumoral immune response with more pronounced immune cell infiltration.18,19 In more detail, Hagiwara et al. reported that a higher relative ADC in post-ICI scans was associated with longer overall survival in patients with recurrent gliomastoma.18 Consistently, Song et al. found that recurrent glioblastomas responding to ICI showed an increase in relative ADC when comparing pre-ICI to post-ICI scans, while glioblastomas progressing under ICI treatment showed a decrease in relative ADC.19

Interestingly, our analyses also showed a significant interplay between ICI and corticosteroids in determining ADC-CE phenotypes, where lesions exposed to both ICI and corticosteroids were, to some extent, similar to ICI-naïve lesions in terms of ADC-CE phenotypes. Indeed, ICI exposure in the absence of corticosteroids was a significant determinant of ADC-CE phenotypes in multivariate analysis, but not ICI exposure in the absence of corticosteroids. This finding aligns with the empirical knowledge that corticosteroids interfere with ICI efficacy, likely by suppressing immune cell response. Therefore, corticosteroids may interfere with vascular permeability and immune cell infiltration, preventing the distinctive ADC-CE elevation induced by ICI, as we observed. Indeed, corticosteroids are known to decrease vascular permeability in brain tumors both by inhibiting the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and by directly up-regulating occludin expression in the endothelium.38 This is in line with previous reports showing how corticosteroid administration can cause an ADC decrease,23,34,35 a reduction in vasogenic edema,42 and less vivid CE24 in brain tumors.

This study has some limitations. First, we were not able to evaluate longitudinal MRI data from the same lesion to demonstrate pre- to post-ICI changes in ADC-CE phenotypes. However, this analysis is very challenging to achieve in practice because most patients eligible for ICI treatment may start this systemic therapy for their underlying disease before developing BM. Therefore, it is rare to have pre-ICI MRI scans of BM lesions in patients eligible for ICI. Related to this consideration, another limitation is that the ICI-exposed lesions are not matched by primary histology to the ICI-naïve ones because some ICI-naïve lesions may include histotypes and molecular types that are not ICI-eligible. However, our multivariate model was designed to adjust for these confounding factors. Related to the absence of longitudinal data, it was not possible to use ADC-CE phenotypes in ICI-treated lesions to stratify ICI-responders and non-responders. While the present study should be considered a first proposal of this promising imaging biomarker, future studies should assess its value for treatment response assessment. Additionally, some peculiar primary tumors had a relatively small number of items in the cohort and had to be grouped with other tumor types (eg, n = 3 SCLC was grouped together with non-small cell lung cancer within the lung cancer category, despite biological differences). Similarly, anti-VEGF-treated lesions were not numerous enough to disentangle the anti-VEGF contribution to the ADC-CE phenotype. Another limitation is that histological tissue was available only for a relatively small sub-cohort, for which only CD45 staining and, for a further subset of lesions, CD4 and CD8 stainings were available, which did not allow a comprehensive characterization of the immune cell populations infiltrating the tumor tissue. Finally, our approach to measure the degree of CE using T1-subtraction maps is approximative. Future studies should quantify CE by measuring changes in longitudinal relaxation rate (ΔR1) or volume transfer constant (Ktrans) from perfusion sequences,29 or using quantitative T1 relaxometry.43 Future studies may also validate the usefulness of ADC-CE phenotypes in predicting patient prognosis and confirm its value as a potential tool for treatment response assessment using datasets from immunotherapy trials.

Conclusions

MRI phenotypes derived from CE and diffusion are associated with ICI exposure and with histological immune cell infiltration in BM. Immune cell infiltration induced by ICI treatment may result in a higher prevalence of regions with combined higher diffusivity and more pronounced CE within the tumor. Therefore, if further refined and validated, combined CE and diffusivity phenotypes have the potential to serve as a noninvasive marker to monitor ICI-induced immune cell infiltration.
