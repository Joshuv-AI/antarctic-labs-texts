Moderate agreement between the spot albumin‒Creatinine ratio (ACR) and urine albumin (UA) among virally suppressed adults living with HIV in botswana: a cross sectional study
Authors: Mosepele Mosepele, Ponego L Ponatshego, Kesaobaka Molebatsi, Kago Kebotsamang, Christopher Williams, Lucky Mokgatlhe, Shahin Lockman, Nabila Youssouf, Robert Gross, Joseph Jarvis, Thato Moshomo, Shabbar Jaffar, Duolao Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlbuminuria predicts end-organ dysfunction in the general population. Its detection in people living with HIV (PLWH) is understudied, despite their heightened risk for end-organ dysfunction. We assessed agreement between albumin‒creatinine ratio (ACR) and urine albumin (UA) tests for albuminuria in a cohort of PLWH in Botswana, where females are more affected by HIV than males.MethodsWe used cross-sectional convenience sampling to enrol 1533 virally suppressed adults aged ≥ 21 years who were receiving antiretroviral therapy (ART) in Gaborone, Botswana. We measured urine creatinine levels using a colorimetric assay (Jaffe method) and UA levels using an immunoturbidimetric assay. The UA was used due to it being less costly and simpler, making it more feasible for routine use in resource-limited settings compared to the ACR. We defined albuminuria as 30mg/g versus 30 mg/Lusing ACR and UA levels respectively. Agreement between the two tests was assessed using Cohen’s kappa for the entire cohort and then separately by sex groups. The optimal UA cut-off point that matched the sex-specific ACR was assessed using Youden’s J index.ResultsThe ACR detected similar proportion of participants with albuminuria as UA test (17.1% versus 17.6%). There was moderate agreement in the detection of albuminuria using the ACR versus UA test in the entire cohort, among males and females with Kappa statistics of 0.71 (95% CI: 0.67, 0.76; P < 0.001), 0.74 (95% CI: 0.68, 0.80; P < 0.001), and 0.68 (95% CI: 0.60, 0.76; P < 0.001) respectively. Using Youden’s J, the optimal cut points for UA levels were 17.9 mg/L (sensitivity = 88.9%, specificity = 87.3%, AUC = 0.94), 25.0 mg/L (sensitivity = 90.4%, specificity = 90.1%, AUC = 0.94) and 15.1 mg/L (sensitivity = 89.7%, specificity = 87.5%, AUC = 0.94) for the entire cohort, males only and females only, respectively.ConclusionUA and ACR show moderate agreement overall. Optimal UA cutoffs differ slightly by sex, suggesting tailored thresholds may improve detection. Future studies should explore clinical indication(s) for using the ACR versus UA levels among PLWH.Keywords: People living with HIV, Albuminuria, Albumin-creatinine ratio, Cardiovascular disease

Background

Albuminuria is an established marker for cardiovascular and renal disease. The rates of albuminuria range between 4% and 61% globally [1–6]. The main reason for this wide variability in reported rates of albuminuria may relate to the different clinical cohorts (presence of comorbidities), differing definitions of albuminuria, including any detectable albuminuria, the geometric mean albumin‒creatinine ratio (ACR), the standard mean ACR (which does not take into account skewed ACRs), and not using sex-specific cutoff points for the ACR (25–355 mg/g for females versus 17–250 mg/g for males) [7–10], and more recently non-sex-specific cutoff point for ACR (30 mg/g) [11].

HIV infection has been linked with an increased prevalence of albuminuria. Given that the risk for developing cardiovascular disease (CVD) in people living with HIV (PLWH) is almost 1.5- to 2-fold higher than that in their counterparts without HIV, this increased prevalence of albuminuria is associated with increased morbidity and mortality from cardiovascular and renal disease [12–15].

Detecting any albuminuria or microalbuminuria is a relatively noninvasive, technically easy test with several licenced point-of-care tests (bedside tests) [16–18]. Despite this, the prevalence and predictive utility of albuminuria (for end-organ disease) for morbidity and mortality remain unknown among treated black African patients living with HIV. Urinary albumin can be assessed using an albumin-specific dipstick, immunochemical techniques, and size-exclusion high-performance liquid chromatography. Urine specimens can be obtained from either a timed collection or a spot urine sample for assessment. Spot urine protein- or albumin-to-creatinine ratios are preferred to a 24-hour urine sample in routine practice, as they are more pragmatic [19, 20]. On the other hand, the use of urine albumin (UA) measurement alone has potential advantages in certain contexts: ACR testing requires measurement of both albumin and creatinine, which can be more resource-intensive. In many low-resource settings, including Botswana and other parts of sub-Saharan Africa, UA is more readily available and less costly. Furthermore, UA semi-quantitative assays are available for use in primary care settings, making UA more accessible for early screening. Lastly, there are limited data comparing UA with ACR in people living with HIV in sub-Saharan Africa.

These measurement and sampling procedures for albuminuria assessment have not yet been standardized even in the general population, with persisting differences of opinion over whether the employment of untimed ‘spot’ urine samples is as accurate as using timed 24-hour urine specimens and the relative merits of measuring total protein versus albumin to detect and monitor kidney malfunction, among others [21, 22]. Additionally, since the risk of HIV acquisition, pathogenesis and prevalence differ by sex group in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) [23, 24], it is important to evaluate cut-off points for albuminuria by both sex and non-sex specific cut-off points. The aim of this study was to assess agreement in the detection of albuminuria by the ACR versus urine albumin (UA) clinical cut-off points, overall using both non-sex specific (main analysis) and then by sex specific group cut-off points (in sensitivity analysis) in a cohort of PLWH in SSA.

Methods

Design, setting and participants

Baseline data from an existing database of 1533 virally suppressed PLWH enrolled in a prospective study of albuminuria at Princess Marina Hospital Infectious Disease Care Clinic (PMH-IDCC), Gaborone, Botswana, were extracted for this analysis. All participants attended their baseline study visits between January 2020 and January 2021. Participants had to be aged 21 years or older. To be enrolled, participants had to (1) be living with HIV as per their medical records, (2) be taking antiretroviral therapy, and (3) agree to provide a morning mid-stream urine sample during their scheduled HIV care clinic visit. Participants were instructed to provide this first morning urine sample during their scheduled clinic visit. Potential enrollees were excluded if medical records or self report indicated any recent medication use, medical diagnosis, medical procedure or personal activity that would be associated with muscle breakdown [25, 26].

Study procedures

Briefly, participants in the main albuminuria study were recruited (convenience sampling) when they attended their routine HIV care visits. After consenting to participate, they underwent clinical evaluation and provided urine and blood samples for subsequent assessment of albuminuria. Participants were shown a sterile urine bottle, and shown how to open and close it properly. They then were instructed on collection of a mid-stream sample to fill the sterile urine bottle up to three-quarters full. Once the urine bottle lid was tightly screwed back on, the participant brought the urine bottle back to a member of the study team. Samples were then kept in the refrigerator at 2–8 degrees celsius, and subsequently transported in a cooler box on the same day. If samples were not tested the same day, they were then stored in the refrigerator at 2–8 degrees celsius in a dark place until tested. The samples were kept for a maximum of 2 weeks before being tested. All study procedures were performed in accordance with IRB approval and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Laboratory testing

In the main albuminuria study, all testing was performed on a Cobas Integra© 400 plus analyzer at the Botswana-Harvard HIV Reference Laboratory (BHHRL) in Gaborone, Botswana. At this laboratory, urine creatinine levels were measured using a colorimetric method while urine albumin was measured using an immunoturbidimetric method. Specifically, Tina-quant albumin Gen.2 (urine albumin detection) and Creatinine Jaffe Gen.2 (urine creatinine detection) kits were used as per manufacturer's instructions. The ACR was calculated by dividing the albumin concentration in milligrams by the creatinine concentration in grams [19, 20], and these results were directly imported from the main albuminuria database (already calculated) for use in the current study.

Statistical analysis

The albuminuria cut-off point using ACR was 30 mg/g or higher [11].The albuminuria cut-off criterion using UA was defined as 30 mg/L or more for all participants irrespective of sex group [27–29]. We reported the baseline characteristics of all participants and those who were classified as having albuminuria or not by the ACR criteria (used as the gold standard in this study). Since albuminuria has been observed to vary significantly by sex in other studies, we ran our agreement analysis in three parts: (1) in the entire cohort, (2) among males only and (3) among females only. Agreement between the two tests at each step was assessed using Cohen’s kappa statistic. We also obtained the optimal cut-off point that matched the ACR and UA levels using Youden’s J index calculated as J = sensitivity + specificity − 1. The maximum value of J may be used to determine an optimal cut-off in scenarios where the costs associated with false-positives and false-negatives are equal [30]. This index was used along with the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and corresponding area under the curve (AUC). In sensitivity analysis, the sex-specific cut-off points for albuminuria using the ACR were 25–355 mg/g for females and 17–250 mg/g for males. These sex-specific cut-off points help to account for sex differences in urinary creatinine excretion, which may result in misclassification of albuminuria since urinary creatinine excretion is lower in females than in males. Further, since HIV pathogenesis and prevalence may differ by sex group, it is important to revisit sex specific albuminuria cut-off points [23, 24, 31].

Discussion

We report moderate agreement between ACR and UA [32] based on a one-time assessment of albuminuria using two commonly used criteria for determining albuminuria in a cohort of PLWH in Botswana, sub-Saharan Africa. Our results further suggest that follow-up studies need to explore lower cut-off points for albuminuria among PLWH. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to assess agreement between the ACR and UA levels among a chronically ART-treated (>10 years) cohort of black PLWH in sub-Saharan Africa.

Because PLWH are ageing and already known to be at an increased risk for cardiovascular morbidity and mortality, careful attention to the early detection and treatment of albuminuria is urgently needed, as has been established for other patient groups. For instance, among persons diagnosed with diabetes mellitus and hypertension, the American Diabetes Association recommends annual testing for albuminuria in 12-hr or 24-hr urine samples to predict the risk of CVD, as well as renal dysfunction [33]. However, there are no similar recommendations for albuminuria screening among PLWH, including from the US National Kidney Foundation (NKF), which probably explains the paucity of data on the comparative analysis of different screening tests for albuminuria among PLWH. These preliminary results provide a foundation for how the ACR and UA levels can be studied to develop recommendations for screening PLWH for albuminuria using a feasible, low-cost strategy in low-income settings. Unfortunately, we did not assess the performance of point-of-care (POC) tests vis-à-vis the laboratory-based tests used in this study. Future studies should test the performance of POC tests in this patient group.

Although the gold standard method for detecting albuminuria is measuring 24-hr urine, in practice, collecting urine for 12–24 hr is difficult and sometimes unreliable if the urine sample is incomplete [34]. As a result of these challenges, measurement of the albumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR) in spot urine samples has also been recommended for screening purposes [35]. Therefore, our approach in screening for albuminuria using the ACR does not deviate from this US NKF recommendation, although the NKF recommendation is not specific to PLWH.

In this study, we tested for albuminuria using two tests, urine albumin tests and the ACR, to assess the agreement between the two tests in PLWH. Albuminuria was defined by a clinical cut-off point of 30 mg/L for random urine albumin (UA) versus 30 mg/g for ACR. The moderate agreement between UA and ACR implies that UA is a potential alternative, pending further evaluation in a context where only one of the tests is available. However, since the sex-specific cut-off points for ACR identified more cases of albuminuria, revised sex specific ACR cut-off point would likely be preferred among those at higher risk for albuminuria, especially if their UA test came back negative using the current approved cut-off points. It is worth noting that agreement between ACR and UA was consistently higher for males as compared to females- highlighting how overall agreement may be driven by the male sex specific cut-off point. However, since females in SSA are at a substantially higher risk of acquiring HIV as compared to their male counterparts [36], and have been disproportionately affected by HIV since 2000, and projected to continue to experience a higher prevalence of HIV by 2050 [23, 24], global non-sex specific agreement between ACR versus UA cutoff points may inadvertently lead to underdiagnosis of albuminuria among the majority of females living with HIV in SSA. This is particularly concerning as a growing body of evidence suggests that HIV pathogenesis, and therefore, end organ dysfunction may differ between female and male adults living with HIV [24, 31, 37]. In either case, we conclude that ACR remains the guideline-recommended standard for assessing albuminuria.

UA, while simpler and potentially more accessible in resource-limited settings, shows only moderate agreement with ACR. Our results suggest that UA may serve as a pragmatic screening tool where ACR is not feasible, but confirmatory testing with ACR should remain the standard of care.

Limitations

Our study had several limitations. Enrolment in our study followed convenience sampling, and participants provided a morning sample once they were in clinic (rather than upon waking up at home). Therefore, our results may be biased to those who chose to participate, thereby limiting the generalizability of our observations to many other PLWH in SSA. We relied on rapid recruitment (convenience sampling) of those who were available to attend in person during the COVID-19 pandemic (January 2020-January 2021). Our study used a one-time assessment of both the ACR and UA levels despite a one-time ACR having a higher negative predictive value (~ 98%) and a slightly lower positive predictive value (~ 74%) [4]. Therefore, we may have overestimated cases of albuminuria (using one-time assessment with the ACR as the gold standard), which would explain the moderate (rather than high) agreement between the ACR and UA levels. We compared UA levels to the ACR, rather than to 24-hour urine results - the traditional reference standard, or to repeat ACR testing. Importantly, while we explored optimal UA cut-off points against the ACR, our study was not designed to evaluate whether low-level albuminuria—or discordant results between UA and ACR near diagnostic thresholds—is associated with adverse clinical outcomes. Adequately powered longitudinal studies are needed to confirm the persistence of albuminuria and to establish whether such findings have prognostic significance. Additionally, we may have under-reported degree of albuminuria because in some instances where enrolment was slow, samples were batched for a maximum of 1–2 weeks prior to testing.

Despite these limitations, our results provide new and potentially useful insights into the performance of the different ACR cut-off points versus UA levels in an ageing high-risk patient group (PLWH) comprising more than 30 million people in sub-Saharan Africa.
