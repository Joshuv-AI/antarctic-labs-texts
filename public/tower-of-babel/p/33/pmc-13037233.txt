Examining the association between neck pain and depressive symptoms in middle-aged and older Chinese adults: findings from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS)
Authors: Jie Huang, Shuai Xu, Hongzhen Li, Weiwei Xia, Chenjun Liu, Haiying Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNeck pain is a prevalent musculoskeletal condition among middle-aged and older adults and may contribute to the development of depressive symptoms. However, evidence on the longitudinal relationship between neck pain and depression remains limited in large population-based cohorts.MethodsData were obtained from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), including adults aged ≥ 45 years who participated in the 2011 baseline survey. Cross-sectional associations between neck pain and depressive symptoms were evaluated using multivariable logistic regression, while longitudinal associations over a 9-year follow-up (2011–2020) were examined using Cox proportional hazards models. An elastic net (ENET) regularization method was used for predictor selection. Subgroup and interaction analyses were performed to assess effect modification, and E-values were calculated to evaluate robustness to unmeasured confounding.ResultsAt baseline in 2011, 9,452 participants (mean age 59.11 ± 9.56 years; 49.97% women) were included in the cross-sectional analysis, and 3,530 participants free of depressive symptoms at baseline were included in the longitudinal analysis. In cross-sectional analyses, neck pain was independently associated with increased odds of depressive symptoms (adjusted OR = 3.019, 95% CI: 2.341–3.899). In longitudinal analyses, neck pain predicted a higher risk of developing depressive symptoms over time (adjusted HR = 1.102, 95% CI: 1.081–1.124). Subgroup analyses confirmed the robustness of these associations across sociodemographic and health strata, with no significant interactions observed. Sensitivity analysis showed moderate to strong resistance to unmeasured confounding.ConclusionNeck pain is significantly associated with the presence and future development of depressive symptoms in middle-aged and older Chinese adults. These findings highlight the importance of early identification and integrated management of neck pain in efforts to prevent late-life depression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-26750-5.

Introduction

Neck pain and depressive symptoms represent prevalent and debilitating conditions that significantly impact the health of aging populations globally [1]. The Global Burden of Disease study identifies neck pain as a leading contributor to years lived with disability, exhibiting an annual global prevalence surpassing 30% [2, 3]. In China, epidemiological studies report neck pain prevalence rates between 3.8% and 17.6%, underscoring its substantial public health burden [4, 5]. Although traditionally viewed through a biomechanical lens, persistent neck pain frequently associates with emotional distress, functional impairment, and diminished quality of life [6–8].

Depressive symptoms remain a major public health concern, especially among middle-aged and older adults, as they accelerate functional decline, promote social withdrawal, and elevate mortality risk. China's rapidly aging population has exacerbated the burden of late-life depressive symptoms [9, 10], with recent surveys indicating clinically relevant symptoms in 30–37% of older adults, particularly women and rural residents [11, 12]. Research consistently associates late-life depression with low educational attainment, impaired self-care capacity, multimorbidity, and chronic conditions including arthritis and cardiovascular disease (CVD) [13], highlighting the multifactorial nature of depression in aging populations and the urgency to pinpoint modifiable risk factors specific to China. The bidirectional relationship between chronic pain and mood disorders is well-documented [14, 15], yet neck pain's role in depression remains understudied despite its high prevalence and quality-of-life implications. This overlooked association may represent a critical target for integrated geriatric mental and physical health strategies. From a biopsychosocial perspective, several pathways may link neck pain to depressive symptoms [16, 17]. Persistent pain may contribute to functional limitation and disability, sleep disturbance, and reductions in physical activity and social participation, which in turn increase vulnerability to depressive symptoms [18–21]. Psychological factors such as pain catastrophizing and reduced self-efficacy may further amplify emotional distress [22]. In addition, neuroendocrine stress responses and low-grade inflammation have been proposed as biological mechanisms potentially connecting chronic pain conditions with depression [23, 24]. These plausible pathways underscore the need to clarify the temporal association between neck pain and depressive symptoms in aging populations.

Existing studies, such as meta-analyses and cross-sectional analyses, have examined the association between musculoskeletal pain and depressive symptoms, with few longitudinal cohort studies focusing specifically on neck pain as a risk factor [25, 26]. Moreover, most prior research has relied on localized samples, limiting generalizability. To address these gaps, the present study investigates the association between neck pain and depressive symptoms using data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a nationally representative cohort of adults aged 45 years and older. By examining this relationship in a large and diverse population, we aim to clarify the potential mental health impact of neck pain and identify high-risk groups for early intervention.

Methods

Study design and population

We analyzed data from the CHARLS, a nationally representative cohort survey of Chinese adults aged 45 years and older. The study employed a stratified, multistage probability-proportional-to-size sampling design, recruiting approximately 20,000 participants from 100,000 households across 450 communities in 150 counties spanning 28 Chinese provinces [27]. Baseline data collection occurred during 2011–2012, with follow-up waves conducted in 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020. Trained interviewers administered computer-assisted personal interviews to minimize input errors through real-time validation. Interviewers underwent rigorous training involving standardized assessments and mock field exercises before deployment, with on-site supervision ensuring protocol compliance. When direct participation proved impossible, proxy respondents, typically immediate family members, provided information to reduce recall bias [28]. The Peking University Ethical Review Committee approved the study protocol (IRB00001052-11015), and all participants provided written informed consent [29]. We designated the 2011 survey as baseline and included participants without depressive symptoms, with 10-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D-10 score) < 10 at enrollment, tracking incident cases (CES-D-10 ≥ 10) during subsequent waves. Neck pain status, determined through self-reports in each wave, was considered present if reported before or concurrent with depressive symptom onset. All data collection followed standardized protocols through face-to-face interviews conducted by trained personnel.

The cross-sectional analysis initially included 17,705 participants from the 2011 baseline wave. Exclusion criteria comprised age below 45 years (n = 404), missing gender data (n = 12), absent BMI values (n = 4,070), incomplete neck pain information (n = 3,513), or incomplete CES-D-10 responses (n = 254), yielding a final cross-sectional sample of 9,452 participants. For longitudinal analysis, we additionally excluded participants with baseline depressive symptoms (n = 2,838) or lacking follow-up data after 2011 (n = 3,084), resulting in 3,530 individuals for assessing the prospective association between neck pain and incident depressive symptoms from 2011 to 2020 (Fig. 1). This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. The STROBE checklist is provided as Supplementary Table S1.

Assessment of neck pain

Neck pain was assessed based on participants’ responses to a standardized questionnaire item administered during each wave of CHARLS (2011–2020). Participants were asked: “On what part of your body do you feel pain?”. They were encouraged to report all areas of the body where they were currently experiencing pain (CHARLS question code: Da042). A human body diagram was simultaneously presented to aid accurate identification. Individuals who indicated the neck as one of the pain sites were classified as having neck pain.

Assessment of depressive symptoms

Depressive symptoms were assessed with the CES-D-10, a widely used measure of psychological distress in population studies [30]. Responses to each item were recorded on a 4-point Likert scale (0 = "rarely or none of the time" to 3 = "most or all of the time"), yielding total scores between 0 and 30. We reverse-coded items 5 and 8 prior to analysis. The scale captures symptom frequency over the preceding week, measuring depressed mood, sleep disturbances, irritability, sadness, loneliness, hopelessness, lack of energy, difficulty concentrating, fearfulness, and perceived effortfulness. Following established epidemiological conventions [31], participants scoring ≥ 10 were considered positive for depressive symptoms.

Ascertainment of covariates

Covariates included sociodemographic characteristics, lifestyle behaviors, physical activity, and chronic health conditions. Sociodemographic variables comprised age, gender, place of residence (urban or rural), educational level (categorized as illiterate, junior high school or below, and high school or above), and marital status (living with a partner or living alone). Body mass index (BMI) was classified as normal (< 25), overweight (25–29.9), or obese (≥ 30). Physical activity was assessed using metabolic equivalent task (MET) values, which were derived from self-reported duration and intensity of activities performed during a typical week. Lifestyle factors included smoking and alcohol use. Smoking status was defined based on self-reported history and categorized as never or ever smoker, with the latter including any prior tobacco use regardless of frequency or duration. Alcohol consumption was similarly classified as never or ever drinker, based on lifetime use. Chronic conditions included hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, CVD, cancer, low back pain, and joint pain, all of which were identified through self-reported physician diagnoses or participant-reported symptoms confirmed through standardized questionnaires.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables were expressed as means with standard deviations (SDs), and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Differences between participants with and without neck pain were assessed using Welch’s two-sample t-test for continuous variables and Pearson’s chi-squared test for categorical variables.

To identify the key predictors of depressive symptoms, an elastic net (ENET) regularization approach was applied, integrating both ridge and LASSO penalties to optimize variable selection and reduce overfitting. Furthermore, three logistic regression models and Cox proportional hazards regression were respectively constructed: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 adjusted for gender, marital status, educational attainment, and place of residence; and Model 3 further included BMI, hypertension, diabetes, and CVD. Moreover, subgroup analyses and interaction tests were performed to assess effect modification by gender, marital status, education, residence, and BMI. Finally, E-values were conducted to evaluate the robustness of the observed associations to potential unmeasured confounding.

Data were extracted and initially managed using R statistical software (version 4.4.2). All subsequent statistical analyses, including model fitting, covariate adjustment, subgroup and sensitivity analyses, and visualization, were conducted in DecisionLinnc (version 1.0). The elastic net procedure (including tuning and coefficient visualization) was implemented in DecisionLinnc. Statistical significance was determined as a p-value below 0.05 through two-sided testing.

Results

Participant characteristics

At baseline in 2011, 9,452 participants were included in the analysis. Among them, 2,838 (30.02%) had depressive symptoms; the mean age was 59.11 ± 9.56 years, and 49.97% were women (Table 1). Participants with depressive symptoms were predominantly female, lived alone, had lower education levels, and resided in rural areas more frequently than those without symptoms. They also showed higher rates of chronic conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, and CVD. Lifestyle differences emerged, with depressed individuals more often abstaining from smoking and alcohol consumption. While most participants in both groups were overweight, those with depressive symptoms had a marginally higher proportion in the normal weight range and a lower proportion in the obese category. MET-measured physical activity levels did not differ significantly between groups. Participants with depressive symptoms reported substantially higher rates of musculoskeletal pain, including neck pain, low back pain, and joint pain.

Cross-sectional association between neck pain and depressive symptoms at 2011

To identify the most relevant predictors of depressive symptoms, we applied an ENET regularization approach using a comprehensive set of sociodemographic, lifestyle, and health-related variables. As shown in Figure S1, several variables were selected including neck pain, low back pain, joint pain, CVD, BMI, and other key demographic and health factors. Among these, neck pain showed one of the strongest positive associations with depressive symptoms, highlighting its potential importance in mental health risk stratification.

The independent association between neck pain and depressive symptoms was further evaluated using multivariable logistic regression models (Table 2). In the unadjusted model (Model 1), individuals reporting neck pain had significantly higher odds of depressive symptoms compared to those without neck pain (OR = 7.275, 95% CI: 6.258–8.479, P < 0.001). This association remained robust after adjusting for sociodemographic variables (Model 2: OR = 6.553, 95% CI: 5.619–7.663, P < 0.001) and further for clinical comorbidities and other pain conditions (Model 3: OR = 3.019, 95% CI: 2.341–3.899, P < 0.001). These findings suggest that neck pain is independently associated with a significantly increased risk of depressive symptoms, even after accounting for multiple confounders.

Longitudinal association between neck pain and depressive symptoms at follow-up, 2011–2020

As shown in Table 3, individuals with neck pain at baseline had a significantly higher risk of developing depressive symptoms over time. In the unadjusted model (Model 1), neck pain was associated with an 18.3% increased hazard of depression onset (HR = 1.183, 95% CI: 1.182–1.185, P < 0.001). This association remained statistically significant after adjusting for demographic factors including age, gender, marital status, education, and residence (Model 2: HR = 1.150, 95% CI: 1.119–1.183, P < 0.001). After further controlling for low back pain, joint pain, CVD, and BMI, the association was slightly attenuated but remained significant (Model 3: HR = 1.102, 95% CI: 1.081–1.124, P < 0.001).

Cross-sectional subgroup analysis

To explore whether the association between neck pain and depressive symptoms varied across population subgroups at baseline, we conducted stratified multivariable logistic regression analyses. As shown in Fig. 2, neck pain was significantly associated with depressive symptoms in all predefined subgroups. Specifically, the association appeared stronger among individuals with CVD, compared to those without CVD, and this was the only subgroup showing a statistically significant interaction (P for interaction = 0.022). However, no significant heterogeneity were observed across subgroups of gender, marital status, education level, residence, and BMI (all P for interaction > 0.05).Fig. 2Cross-sectional analysis using multivariable logistic regression models at baseline (2011). Longitudinal subgroup analysis

We further assessed whether baseline neck pain was differentially associated with the risk of developing depressive symptoms during the 9-year follow-up period by conducting subgroup analyses using Cox proportional hazards models. As illustrated in Fig. 3, HRs across all subgroups consistently indicated an elevated risk, although the magnitude of association was more modest compared to the cross-sectional analysis. However, none of the interaction terms reached statistical significance (all P for interaction > 0.05). These findings suggest that the association between neck pain and subsequent depressive symptoms was stable and robust across different subgroups.

Sensitivity analyses

Finally, we conducted sensitivity analyses using E-values based on the fully adjusted models (Model 3) from both the cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses to assess the potential impact of unmeasured confounding on our findings. In the cross-sectional logistic regression model, neck pain showed a strong association with depressive symptoms. Other variables such as low back pain, joint pain, and living alone also exhibited moderate robustness. The relatively high E-values across multiple variables, particularly for neck pain, suggest that the observed cross-sectional associations are unlikely to be fully attributable to unmeasured confounding (Table 4).

Discussion

Our study demonstrates consistent cross-sectional and longitudinal associations between neck pain and depressive symptoms in middle-aged and older Chinese adults. Baseline data revealed that participants with neck pain were predominantly female, lived alone, had lower education levels, and exhibited higher rates of comorbid cardiometabolic conditions including hypertension, diabetes, and CVD. Depressive symptoms affected 70.1% of individuals with neck pain, a prevalence nearly three times greater than in those without neck pain. After adjusting for demographic, socioeconomic, and clinical covariates, neck pain maintained a significant association with depressive symptoms in cross-sectional analyses (adjusted OR = 3.019, 95% CI: 2.341–3.899). Longitudinal follow-up over nine years showed that baseline neck pain predicted increased risk of developing depressive symptoms (adjusted HR = 1.102, 95% CI: 1.081–1.124), confirming its role as a prospective risk factor for depression in aging populations.

The temporal association was reinforced by ENET modeling, which identified neck pain as the strongest predictor of depressive symptoms, outweighing both psychosocial and physical health factors. E-value sensitivity analyses demonstrated the robustness of these associations against potential unmeasured confounding. Subgroup analyses revealed consistent associations across sociodemographic and clinical groups, with a pronounced effect in CVD patients, implying a potential interaction between chronic pain and systemic illness in depression pathogenesis.​

The observed association between neck pain and depressive symptoms aligns with mounting evidence highlighting the bidirectional relationship between chronic pain and psychological distress. Neck pain, frequently linked to poor posture and sustained static neck positions, represents a multifactorial condition with complex etiology [32–34]. This condition often follows a recurrent or chronic course, substantially impairing daily functioning [35, 36]. Persistent pain may chronically activate stress response systems, potentially fostering emotional disturbances including anxiety and depression [37]. Conversely, psychological distress can amplify pain perception and functional limitations, perpetuating a detrimental cycle [38–40].

Prospective and cross-sectional studies consistently link psychological factors, including anxiety, emotional distress, and mood dysregulation, to the development and maintenance of neck pain [41–43]. Depressive symptoms correlate with modified central pain processing and reduced pressure pain thresholds, reflecting neurophysiological mechanisms underlying heightened pain sensitivity [44, 45]. Both adolescent and adult populations with elevated state or trait anxiety demonstrate greater neck pain prevalence, severity, and chronicity [46]. The China Mental Health Survey corroborates these findings, revealing significantly higher rates of chronic neck or back pain among individuals with mood disorders than in mentally healthy counterparts [47]. Major depressive disorder exhibits the strongest comorbidity with neck pain among psychiatric conditions. Mood-related symptoms consistently emerge as critical psychosocial risk factors in chronic musculoskeletal pain cohorts [48, 49]. Functional limitations imposed by pain often trigger social withdrawal and reduced quality of life, exacerbating depressive symptoms. Pain catastrophizing, a cognitive pattern characterized by exaggerated negative appraisal of pain, mediates the relationship between chronic pain and depression. This maladaptive response may initiate a self-reinforcing cycle where pain intensifies emotional distress, subsequently lowering pain thresholds. Chronic neck pain patients also commonly experience sleep disturbances, which both signal and potentially exacerbate depression. Poor sleep quality likely impairs emotional regulation while increasing vulnerability to depressive episodes.

Biological mechanisms may also underlie this relationship. Neuroimaging studies reveal altered activity in brain regions associated with affective pain processing, including the insular cortex and prefrontal cortex, which may mediate depression's influence on pain perception [50, 51]. Chronic pain can induce central sensitization, a state of heightened nervous system responsiveness to pain stimuli that amplifies both pain perception and emotional distress. These changes occur in pain-processing regions such as the amygdala and prefrontal cortex, areas also involved in mood regulation. Dysregulation of neuroimmune pathways and the HPA axis may further connect chronic pain and depression through conserved inflammatory and neuroendocrine mechanisms [52, 53]. These findings collectively demonstrate the intricate biological, psychological, and behavioral interactions driving the co-occurrence of neck pain and depressive symptoms.

Social determinants substantially contribute to depressive symptoms in individuals with chronic neck pain. The stigma associated with chronic pain often generates shame and self-perceived inadequacy, exacerbating psychological distress. Persistent medical expenses and reduced work capacity create financial strain, a well-established risk factor for depression. Insufficient social support may compound isolation and emotional distress, ultimately impairing the individual's capacity to manage chronic pain effectively.

This study has several notable strengths. The analysis draws on the nationally representative CHARLS database, providing a robust foundation for the findings. Rigorous sampling methods and data screening protocols ensure the reliability and validity of the results. The large sample size and thorough adjustment for sociodemographic, behavioral, and clinical covariates enhance the robustness of the observed associations. ENET regression was employed for variable selection, effectively mitigating multicollinearity and overfitting. Sensitivity analyses using E-values further corroborate the findings, while subgroup analyses explore potential effect modifications across clinically relevant strata.

Several limitations warrant consideration. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference between neck pain and depressive symptoms. Both conditions were assessed through self-reports, potentially introducing recall bias. The CES-D-10, though validated, serves as a screening tool rather than a diagnostic instrument, so findings reflect associations with depressive symptomatology rather than clinical diagnoses. In addition, neck pain in CHARLS was captured using a body-site pain item and did not provide information on pain intensity or pain duration; this limited granularity may result in non-differential exposure misclassification and likely bias effect estimates toward the null. It also precluded dose–response analyses and sensitivity analyses stratified by pain severity or chronicity. Despite extensive covariate adjustment, unmeasured psychological, behavioral, or clinical factors, such as pain duration, sleep quality, and medication use, may contribute to residual confounding. While the CES-D-10 is widely used, its role as a screening tool rather than a diagnostic measure should be acknowledged.

Conclusion

In this nationally representative cohort study, neck pain was independently associated with a significantly higher risk of depressive symptoms among middle-aged and older Chinese adults. This association remained robust after adjusting for key demographic and clinical variables and across all subgroups. Given its high prevalence and psychological relevance, neck pain may serve as a potential marker for identifying individuals at risk of depressive symptoms. Further longitudinal and mechanistic research is needed to explore causal pathways and assess the effectiveness of integrated management strategies.
