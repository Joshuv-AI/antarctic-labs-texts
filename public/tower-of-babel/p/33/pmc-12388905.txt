Development and validation of a computational method to predict unintended auditory brainstem response during transcranial ultrasound neuromodulation in mice
Authors: Mi Hyun Choi, Ningrui Li, Gerald Popelka, Kim Butts Pauly
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Background:Transcranial ultrasound stimulation (TUS) is a promising noninvasive neuromodulation modality. The inadvertent and unpredictable activation of the auditory system in response to TUS obfuscates the interpretation of non-auditory neuromodulatory responses.Objective:The objective was to develop and validate a computational metric to quantify the susceptibility to unintended auditory brainstem response (ABR) in mice premised on time frequency analyses of TUS signals and auditory sensitivity.Methods:Ultrasound pulses with varying amplitudes, pulse repetition frequencies (PRFs), envelope smoothing profiles, and sinusoidal modulation frequencies were selected. Each pulse’s time-varying frequency spectrum was differentiated across time, weighted by the mouse hearing sensitivity, then summed across frequencies. The resulting time-varying function, computationally predicting the ABR, was validated against experimental ABR in mice during TUS with the corresponding pulse.Results:There was a significant correlation between experimental ABRs and the computational predictions for 19 TUS signals (R2 = 0.97).Conclusions:To reduce ABR in mice during in vivo TUS studies, 1) reduce the amplitude of a rectangular continuous wave envelope, 2) increase the rise/fall times of a smoothed continuous wave envelope, and/or 3) change the PRF and/or duty cycle of a rectangular or sinusoidal pulsed wave to reduce the gap between pulses and increase the rise/fall time of the overall envelope. This metric can aid researchers performing in vivo mouse studies in selecting TUS signal parameters that minimize unintended ABR. The methods for developing this metric can be adapted to other animal models.

Introduction

Transcranial ultrasound stimulation (TUS) has the potential to stimulate deep brain targets non-invasively at high spatial resolution [1]. In the last decade, TUS has been demonstrated to affect various target brain regions in rodents [2–28], rabbits [29], pigs [30,31], sheep [32–34], non-human primates [35–42], and humans [43–60].

TUS may also produce auditory system responses even when non-auditory regions are targeted, despite the fact that the fundamental frequencies of the signals commonly used for neuromodulation (0.25–2 MHz) are far above the subject’s hearing frequency range (mice: 2–90 kHz [61]; humans: 20 Hz–20 kHz [62]). This was shown with cochlear microphonics in cats [63], auditory perception in humans [64–74], auditory cortex calcium images in mice [9], auditory extracellular electrophysiology recordings in guinea pigs [16], and auditory brainstem responses (ABRs) in mice [11]. The unintended auditory activation could confound the interpretation of observed neural responses to TUS, which vary depending on targeted region and TUS signal parameters [1], and thereby hinders the development of TUS as a neuromodulatory tool that reliably produces predictable neural responses.

Previous studies have shown that the pitch of the perceived sound during TUS in humans is related to the modulating frequency of the sinusoidal or pulsed envelope of the TUS waveform [67,71]. Based on this finding, a more recent study showed that ABR in mice can be eliminated by smoothing the rectangular modulating envelope and thereby reducing the frequency content in the hearing range without affecting the intended neuromodulation of the motor cortex [11]. However, this was demonstrated with only a single continuous TUS waveform of a long duration of 80 ms, while many current stimulation paradigms use trains of pulses of shorter duration.

In the present work, we develop a framework for predicting the ABR in mice for any given TUS waveform based on the spectral content of the TUS signal and the average characteristics of the mouse auditory system. We then validate the framework by comparing the predictions with experimental ABRs to sonication for systematically varied parameters of the TUS waveforms. ABR is a well-accepted and highly repeatable measure of synchronous neural fiber activity along the lower auditory pathways, from the cochlea to the inferior colliculus, commonly used for hearing threshold detection [75]. The synchronous activity occurs after changes in the sound, such as the rise and fall of a signal envelope [76]. Anesthesia, required to eliminate myogenic noise in mice, may affect suprathreshold auditory perception, but results in insignificant changes in ABR thresholds [77,78]. It is important to study the ABR during TUS of non-auditory regions because it could be a major confounding factor downstream when examining the relationship between the TUS signal and the resulting neural effects. The findings of this study can be used to predict and mitigate inadvertent ABR during TUS, setting the stage for causal and robust neuromodulation using TUS.

Materials and methods

Animals

All experiments were performed in accordance with procedures approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Stanford University. C57BL/6J mice (8 M, 8 F; 14–24 g) (JAX000664; The Jackson Laboratory, ME, USA) were used in this study. The mice were housed in the vivarium under standard conditions (temperature 20–26 °C; humidity 30–70%; 12/12 h light/dark) with ad libitum access to food and water. They were 6 weeks old at the start of the experiment, and the experiment lasted up to a maximum of 12 weeks of age to avoid age-related changes in auditory threshold. The experiment was conducted over a maximum of 13 sonication sessions per mouse. For each session, each mouse was anesthetized using intraperitoneal injection of 80/8–100/10 mg/kg ketamine/xylazine. Ophthalmic ointment (Paralube; Dechra, Northwich, UK) was applied to both eyes. Respiration and temperature were continuously monitored throughout the study. Depilatory cream (Veet, Reckitt Benckiser, NJ, USA) was used on the scalp to facilitate ultrasound transmission. Three single electroencephalogram (EEG) subdermal needle electrodes (Technomed Europe, Maastricht Airport, Netherlands) were placed at the top of the head shifted 3 mm to the left of center to avoid interference with the ultrasound waves (+), behind the left ear (−), and in the hindlimb muscle (ground) (Fig. 1A).

Stimulation

The experimental setup is shown in Fig. 1A. To measure auditory thresholds, 5 ms tonal airborne sounds at 2, 5, 8, 11.3, 16, 22.6, 32, and 45.3 kHz at 0–80 dB SPL with 0.5 ms rise/fall times (to avoid spectral splatter [79]) were presented every second through a free field loudspeaker (R3004/6020–00 1’’ Tweeter; ScanSpeak, Jutland, Denmark) connected to a high frequency auditory signal processor (RZ6; Tucker-Davis Technologies, FL, USA). The speaker was placed 10 cm away from the left of the mouse’s ear. The airborne sounds were generated using the SigGenRP software and played and calibrated using the BioSigRZ software in conjunction with a microphone connected to a signal conditioner (PCB Piezotronics, NY, USA). The microphone was placed where the center of the mouse head would be.

For TUS, a single-element (2.54 cm diameter) 500 kHz immersion transducer (V301-SU; Olympus, MA, USA) was placed on a 3-D printed translucent resin conical waveguide with a water outlet and inlet. The distal end of the waveguide had a 3 mm diameter opening, where a latex ultrasound probe cover (Bio-scanguard™; EMT Medical, GA, USA) was placed and secured using an O-ring. The waveguide was filled with degassed deionized water. Degassed ultrasound gel (Aquasonic 100; Parker Laboratories, Inc., NJ, USA) was used to couple the tip of the waveguide to the mouse’s head parallel to the skull surface as to achieve perpendicular incidence between TUS waves and the targeted brain region and positioned above the right sensorimotor cortical region. Based on a previous finding that 500 kHz signals normal to the skull surface travel through the mouse skull without significant distortions or attenuation [2], it was assumed that the beam was transmitted straight, continuing to the sensorimotor cortex.

TUS signal parameter commands from MATLAB were sent to two function generators (33220A, 33250A; Agilent, CA, USA) connected by a mixer (ZAY-3+; Mini-circuits, NY, USA). The mixer output was connected to an amplifier (A150; Electronics & Innovation, NY, USA) then the transducer. The pressures listed below are as measured in free water, but representative of the pressures in the brain as there is no significant attenuation through the mouse skull at 500 kHz central frequency (fc) [2]. All signals were 80 ms long with 5 s intervals (Figs. 2 and 3). It is known that a 0.077–0.10 s interval is sufficient in minimizing adaptation during ABR recordings [80]. Detailed information on the timing of the waveforms can be found in Table S1.

Eight mice received 260 repetitions of 14 waveforms, four mice received 260 repetitions of 4 other waveforms, and four other mice received 260 repetitions of 4 other waveforms (Table S2). Hydrophone measurement

The acoustic output of the transducer placed on the waveguide was measured with a hydrophone system (Onda Corporation, CA, USA). The peak-to-peak voltage was increased from 0–550 mV in 50 mV increments. The corresponding measured peak negative pressure values at the center of the focus were used to find the line-of-best-fit for voltage-to-pressure conversion (P=0.0006Vpeak-to-peak+0.0581; V in mV, P in MPa). Additionally, the acoustic field map was obtained along the transverse (0.4 mm step size) and longitudinal (1 mm step size) planes (Fig. S1).

Experimental ABR recording

The ABR electrodes were connected to a headstage, connected to a preamplifier, which fed into a high frequency auditory signal processor (RZ6; Tucker-Davis Technologies, FL, USA). The data were obtained at a sampling rate of 195.3 kHz (BioSigRZ; Tucker-Davis Technologies, FL, USA). To eliminate breathing and electronic artifacts, each run was filtered using a bandpass filter (300–3000 Hz) and a Hampel filter (k = 20000, n = 3.5). For each stimulation type and mouse, ABRs were synchronously averaged in the time domain over all stimulus presentations (260 runs). The final average trace was represented as mean ±standard deviation.

For airborne sound stimulation, the auditory thresholds of each mouse were determined based on the presence of an ABR response (≥3 standard deviations above the noise floor) for each frequency-amplitude pair. If there was no ABR response up to 80 dB SPL for a frequency, the threshold was assigned a value of 80 dB SPL rather than using higher level sounds that may be damaging to the ears.

Computational prediction

ABR can be modeled as a convolutional process (Equation (7)), where a(t) is the ABR, s(t) is the stimulation (suprathreshold impulse at the beginning of each stimulation event above noise level), i(t) is the ABR impulse response, and n0(t) is the noise, which was considered 0 with s(t) being suprathreshold (Fig. 1B) [82]. It was assumed that the TUS signal that travels through the mouse skull would reach the cochlea [83] as a scaled version of the signal without a significant amount of distortion and would be detected by the cochlea the same way an airborne sound would be detected [84]. The signal that reaches the cochlea was assumed to be the Fourier components of the square of TUS signal pressure (𝓕(p2(f,t))=I(f,t)) based on Ohm’s acoustic law [85]. Ohm’s acoustic law has been used in modeling human ABR, and humans and mice share very similar cochlear structures [86,87]. Since spiral ganglion neurons at the cochlea generate action potentials based on the slopes in neural signal levels [88], s(t) was assumed to be the time-varying frequency content of the TUS signal (I(f,t)) differentiated over time (∂l(f,t)∂t), and scaled by the inverse of the ABR thresholds of mice by frequency (h−1(f)) (Equation (8)) (Fig. 1B and C). Fig. 4B shows the average ABR threshold (n = 12) used for all calculations (h(f)) (Table S3). The Fourier window size was set to 1 ms (mouse behavioral minimum auditory gap threshold for sequential signals [89]) with 50% overlap. Since the unit is arbitrary, it was normalized by a coefficient R=1.17e+4, which is the peak ABR response to the rectangular wave with prms = 0.22 MPa. This coefficient was selected because the ABR voltage amplitude in response to sonication with this rectangular wave is known and can be compared to airborne sound that evokes equivalent amplitude ABR (Fig. S2). No parametric study was done to select the coefficient.

The 10 ms power (ABR impulse response is 10 ms long [90]) of the ABR prediction metric and the ABR responses were compared at the beginning (0 ms) and in the middle (40 ms) of each 80 ms TUS signal. Data points above the experimental ABR threshold were fitted to a line that best defines the relationship between the experimental data and the computational prediction.

Sound measurement

To determine whether the TUS transducer produces any detectable airborne sound (Fig. S5), a sound level meter (SVAN 977C; SvanTek, Warsaw, Poland) was used to measure sound next to the contralateral ear during TUS with a 3 MPa peak-to-peak pressure, which is higher than any pressure used in the ABR experiments. Both rectangular and smoothed envelopes (12 ms ramp) were studied. The frequency and time varying sound level data (resolution: 0.01 dB, 1 s, 25 Hz) was collected using the SvanPC++ software (SvanTek, Warsaw, Poland). For comparison, the levels of 30 dB SPL 1, 5, and 16 kHz airborne sound presented every 10 s were also measured. As no airborne sound could be measured next to the external ear during TUS (Fig. S5), it was confirmed that ABRs to TUS are due to sound propagation within the mouse (e.g., through cerebrospinal fluid, soft tissue, or bone conduction) [83]. We are aware that modulating the envelope and thereby the frequency content of the sound propagating through the mouse may change the ABR. However, determining which pathway within the mouse is most affected by changes in the TUS envelope is beyond the scope of this study but worthy of future studies.

Results

ABRs depend on TUS waveform parameters

Fig. 2 shows experimentally measured ABRs and predicted ABRs in response to stimulation with continuous TUS waveforms. Two types of common waveform modulations were studied: pressure amplitude modulation (Fig. 2A–C) and ramp duration modulation (Fig. 2D–I).

For rectangular envelope waveforms, as the TUS pressure decreased, the experimental ABR amplitudes at the start and end of the TUS signal decreased; the peak ABR amplitudes for 0.22, 0.17, 0.13, and 0.085 MPa prms were 0.35, 0.16, 0.086, 0.065 μV respectively (Fig. 2B). Any TUS waveform with instantaneous rise to a peak pressure that results in peak ABR amplitude ≤ 0.084 μV was considered subthreshold, based on the average noise floor (3 standard deviations above mean over 20 ms pre-stimulation). The experimental data shows that the threshold for ABR lies between prms = 0.085 and 0.13 MPa for rectangular waveforms, which correspond to peak pressures 0.12 and 0.18 MPa respectively. The ABR to an instantaneous rise to and instantaneous fall from the same pressure differed, which the model prediction did not reflect. Any instantaneous rise to ≥ 0.18 MPa and fall from ≥ 0.24 MPa is likely to result in an ABR, and the evoked response increases linearly with the pressure. The prediction overestimated the ABR with prms = 0.085 MPa.

When the waveform envelope was smoothed, the ABRs diminished (Fig. 2E, H). At prms = 0.17 MPa, 4, 8, 12, and 40 ms smoothing durations for the 80 ms waveform resulted in no ABR, despite the fact that the peak pressures of the TUS waveforms were higher than that of the rectangular waveform with corresponding prms. With a shorter ramp time of 2 ms, ABR was found at the end of the 2 ms increasing ramps (Fig. S6A), with suprathreshold peak ABR amplitude of 0.097 μV. Therefore, the ABR threshold fell between 2 and 4 ms ramp times for prms = 0.17 MPa pulses. The ABR threshold for the slope to maximum envelope plateau region was between 0.12 MPa/ms (Ppeakdurramp=0.24MPa2ms) and 0.063 MPa/ms (Ppeakdurramp=0.25MPa4ms) for smoothed waveforms with prms = 0.17 MPa. The predictions showed a small response with the 2 ms ramp signal as well (Fig. S6B). We noted that TUS with a smoothed waveform that invokes no ABR may still be perceptually audible, because ABR only detects synchronous signals and sounds above auditory threshold may be asynchronous [91]. In fact, the middle of a smoothed continuous sound may be more audible than its rectangular counterpart with the same prms due to the higher pressure in the middle of the TUS signal if higher pressures are used.

Fig. 3 shows experimental and predicted ABRs with pulsed waveforms. PRF modulation with rectangular pulses at 50% DC (Fig. 3A–C) and sinusoidally smoothed pulses at 100% DC (Fig. 3D–F) were studied.

With an instantaneous rise at TUS onset, every pulsed signal with rectangular pulses had ABRs above the noise floor (0.084 μV) (Fig. 3B). ABR evoked with the instantaneous rise to 0.48 MPa at the onset of the pulsed waves is in line with the previous observation from continuous rectangular waves: an instantaneous rise to ≥ 0.18 MPa is suprathreshold. The maximum experimental ABR at onset for 0.5, 1.5, 8, and 16 kHz were 0.38, 0.81, 0.67, and 0.82 μV, and the metric predicted that the onset ABR would increase with PRF. Closely placed short pulses beyond the onset of the signal resulted in more ABR in the middle of the TUS signal and less ABR at the end depending on the PRFrect. Increasing the PRFrect decreased the ABR in the middle of the TUS signal, despite moving close to the frequencies with lower thresholds based on airborne sound measurements. The 10 ms power of experimental ABR from 40 to 50 ms for 0.5, 1.5, 8, and 16 kHz were 0.092, 0.0070, 0.0043, and 0.0039 μV2. This shows that ABR activation is less dependent on the hearing frequency range and thresholds for airborne sinusoids, but more on the number of detectable sharp edges, related to the gap detection threshold [89]. Specifically, for the studied pulsed waveforms with prms = 0.17 MPa and 50% DC, the threshold for ABR in the middle portion of the TUS signal was between PRFs of 1.5 and 8 kHz, with increasing ABRs with lower PRFs (Fig. 3B and C). The experimental results and predictions followed the same general trend.

The same PRFs were used for sinusoidally pulsed waves. To reduce the ABR with the instantaneous rise time, the overall envelope of the pulsed signals with sinusoidal pulses was smoothed (12 ms) (Fig. 3D, G). A similar trend as the smoothed continuous waveforms was found at the beginning and end of the waveform, where there was no ABR above the noise level (Fig. 3E, H). Experimental data showed that ABR peaks at 0.5 kHz PRFsin, within the plateau region of the overall envelope (12–68 ms) (Fig. 3E, H). TUS with 8 kHz PRFsin resulted in just suprathreshold ABR at 12 ms. Below 0.5 kHz, the ABR amplitude decreased with decreasing PRFsin. The metric suggested that 8 and 16 kHz waveforms would result in no significant ABR and peak at PRFsin = 1.5 kHz (Fig. 3F, I), which did not align with the experimental results.

When suprathreshold ABRs were close in time based on ABR recordings, desensitization due to adaptation was observed. Of the waveforms studied, the desensitization is most prominent in the middle region of responses to 0.5 kHz rectangular (Fig. 3B) and sinusoidal pulsed (Fig. 3H) waveforms, as can be seen by the decreasing response over the duration of the signal.

Frequency content changes over time in TUS pressure waveforms can be used as predictors of ABR

The predicted ABRs showed similar parametric trends as their experimental counterparts (Figs. 2 and 3): ABR decreases with decreasing pressure amplitude, increasing ramp time, and increasing PRF. Since the prediction was made in arbitrary units, for a quantitative comparison, the 10 ms power of experimental ABR was compared with that of the prediction at t = 0 ms and 40 ms of each of the studied TUS signal waveforms in Fig. 3 (Fig. 4). The data points at t = 80 ms were not included, because our model did not include differential response to a decreasing sound.

Note that the mean ABR threshold from this work’s experimental setup was comparable to the mean ABR thresholds in the literature [61] (Fig. 4B). Using our mean ABR threshold data, a best fit equation was obtained for the data points above the ABR threshold (3 standard deviations) with a significant correlation value of R2 = 0.97 (Fig. 4A). The best fit equation is shown in log-log scale, more representative of how sound levels are processed in the auditory pathway compared to a linear scale [92].

Discussion

The results of this study demonstrate that a time-based frequency analysis of TUS waveforms predicts ABRs in mice during TUS. The method can be used to select and design waveforms that result in lower ABRs and are therefore less audible. In general, smoothing the onset and offset of TUS waveforms reduces ABRs, eliminating them altogether with continuous pulses when the ramp time is sufficiently long. With pulsed waveforms, when the temporal gap between the pulses is decreased, ABRs are eliminated in the middle of the pulse train. Pulse trains can therefore be used without ABR if the rise and fall times are smoothed and a PRF and duty cycle combination results in a small temporal gap. Lastly, ABRs to sinusoidally enveloped waveforms depend on the sinusoidal frequency and is shifted to lower frequencies than the hearing sensitivity curves would suggest.

For mice, based on the TUS waveforms studied in the current work, the experimental ABR threshold in mice in vivo lies between 1) prms = 0.085 MPa and 0.13 MPa for rectangular continuous wave envelopes, 2) 0.063 MPa/ms and 0.12 MPa/ms envelope rise slope for smoothed continuous waves, 3) PRFrect = 1.5 kHz and 8 kHz at 50% DC for pulsed waves with the overall envelope smoothed, or 4) PRFsin = 8 kHz and 16 kHz pulsed sinusoidal waves with the overall envelope smoothed.

The metric introduced in this study had a significant prediction power for suprathreshold ABRs in the mouse model (R2 = 0.97). The trends in the ABRs predicted using the metric aligned well with the experimental data, especially with the most common type of waveforms used in TUS studies – continuous rectangular, continuous smoothed, and pulsed rectangular. For sinusoidal enveloped waveforms, the predicted ABR matched with experimental ABRs from different sinusoidal frequencies. Experimental ABRs were the highest for sinusoidal TUS waveforms at 0.5 kHz, which is lower than the expected 1.5 kHz according to our metric that assumes the ultrasound wave propagation is linear. This suggests some nonlinearity in the system that produces harmonics for sinusoidal signals, as previously reported in humans [64–72,74].

There are several potential improvements to be made to the model presented in this study. Firstly, a comparison between predictive and experimental ABRs for a wider variety of TUS signals could improve the accuracy of this model. We did not study the impacts of changing the fundamental frequency. Gavrilov et al. (1977) found in humans that fundamental frequency has an effect on ultrasound thresholds in humans [67], but given the thin and small mouse skull, it is unclear if the fundamental frequency affects ABRs or audibility in the mouse. Secondly, the metric could have been improved to account for more characteristics of the mammalian auditory system. Previous auditory studies have shown that an airborne sound with a sharp increase results in a higher amplitude ABR than a sound with a sharp decrease [93–98], and we have observed the same with TUS. This can be explained by how the basilar membrane inside the cochlea functions. When a tone starts, the sharp envelope rise triggers a forced oscillation of the basilar membrane. On the other hand, when the tone stops at the envelope fall, the oscillation gets gradually dampened, which results in a smaller signal at the envelope fall [95]. Every signal we have observed with an ABR at the end of the signal had a larger ABR at the beginning of the signal. Thus, our metric is still useful in gaining information on highest ABRs during the progression of the TUS signal. Also, adaptation due to spike rate saturation from repeated or long presentations of a sound was also not considered [99]. Adaptation was most notably observed in experimental ABR recordings for 0.5 kHz PRF pulsed TUS signals. However, no attempts have been made to mimic this decreasing response trend in the prediction model. Thirdly, the correlation was fit to a linear function in a log-log scale consistent with known processing in the mammalian auditory system, despite known nonlinear considerations for suprathreshold signals. This can be further investigated with known nonlinear considerations. Lastly, ABRs measured in this study were specifically in response to targeting the sensorimotor cortex. If other neural regions are targeted, the distance from the target to the cochlea may change and subsequently the amplitudes of ABRs may change accordingly.

Researchers studying other animal models could identify their specific animal’s ABR thresholds and auditory system characteristics to adapt the prediction model introduced in this study. It is likely that the general parametric trends we have observed in ABR in mice will hold. However, specific thresholds and recommendations may change. For example, since the gap detection threshold in humans is 5 ms as opposed to 1 ms in mice [100], the recommendation on PRF and duty cycle will be different. Additionally, individual skull anatomy would need to be included in the equation to detect whether the ABRs are above the detection threshold. It is possible that there may be more complications with larger animals with thicker skulls with varying bone densities, since larger mammalian skulls result in more aberrations and absorption [101]. TUS signal may be distorted and resonate in unexpected ways, especially based on individual differences in skull thickness and composition. Lastly, if this study were to be repeated in humans or awake animals, ABRs may be susceptible to modulation by cortical involvement including learning, task-related suppression, and attention [102].

An important next step would be to assess the downstream impact of TUS signal modulation on central auditory activation, beyond what is measurable with ABR, up to cortical levels to understand the auditory perception of TUS. ABR can be used to estimate hearing sensitivity to sharp changes in the sound envelope, but not the overall audibility. A continuous sound without any ABR in the middle may still be audible from asynchronous responses. Sounds above the auditory threshold that result in no ABR may still be audible, but inaudible sounds will not have any ABR. In humans, a pitch matching study, which is beyond the scope of what is achievable in mice, can be conducted. Understanding pitch perception in humans could help mitigate audibility through signal modulation or masking.

Finally, it must be noted that we cannot forgo the assessment of other potentially confounding sensory modes. Loud airborne sounds and bone conducted vibration can activate otolithic organs in the vestibular system [103]. However, a preliminary experiment sonicating the motor cortex of transgenic mice without otoliths showed that their motor response is not significantly different from that of the wildtypes [104], showing otoliths do not contribute significantly to the behavioral response. However, the vestibular system may still be involved in this case because vestibular hair cells themselves were still functional. It is also possible that the sonication affects tactile receptors in the skin at the point of contact with the transducer. Another experiment showed that the elicited motor response in mice injected with the local anesthetic agent lidocaine at the transducer site was not significantly different from that of mice injected with saline [104], suggesting limited role of the tactile system as well. More robust studies are needed before the field fully understands the effects of TUS signal parameters on neural responses of different sensory cell types.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that key TUS signal parameters can serve as predictors of unintended ABR during neuromodulation. Furthermore, it introduced the method of using spectral information of the TUS signals and background on the specific mammalian auditory system to predict ABRs.
