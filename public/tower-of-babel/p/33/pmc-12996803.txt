Alcohol cue-induced heart rate variability and drinking behavior: The role of impulsivity
Authors: Nayla Taniajura, Christopher Rincon, Sabiq Kennedy, Joel Erblich
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Impulsivity predicted alcohol-cue-induced changes in heart-rate variability.•Impulsivity predicted higher levels of alcohol consumption.•Heart rate variability may reflect a key regulatory process in reducing drinking.

Introduction

Alcohol consumption is a major public health concern, associated with a range of adverse outcomes including chronic diseases, cancer, and impaired mental health (CDC, 2025). This issue is particularly salient among adolescents and young adults, especially those attending college, as drinking habits formed during this critical developmental period, such as binge or heavy drinking, can increase long-term risks for alcohol use disorders and other health problems (Attwood et al., 2012, Buckman et al., 2010, Foster et al., 2021).

Multiple factors contribute to problematic alcohol use, including environmental conditions, alcohol availability, social norms, and individual differences in personality. Among these, trait impulsivity has emerged as a robust predictor of risky drinking (Coskunpinar et al., 2013). Impulsivity is defined as a tendency to act quickly without adequate forethought and is consistently associated with higher rates of substance use, including alcohol (Burnette et al., 2019, Stamates and Lau-Barraco, 2017). Individuals high in impulsivity, especially those with clinical diagnoses characterized by disinhibition (e.g., ADHD, conduct disorder), are more likely to engage in excessive alcohol consumption and develop alcohol-related problems (Fillmore et al., 2009, Luderer et al., 2021).

Impulsivity has also been linked to heightened self-reported and psychophysiological reactivity to alcohol-related cues, such as the sight or smell of alcohol (Burnette et al., 2019, Papachristou et al., 2013). Cue exposure evokes physiological reactivity, including increased skin conductance, salivation, and autonomic nervous system activity—particularly changes in heart rate (HR) and heart rate variability (HRV) in individuals with alcohol dependence [e.g., Rajan et al., 1998, Szegedi et al., 2000]. Such changes have been reported in both directions across multiple substances and populations [e.g., Carter and Tiffany, 1999, Erblich et al., 2011].

HR is an indicator of both cognitive and emotional activation (Skora et al., 2022, Wascher, 2021). HRV, defined as the variation in time intervals between successive heartbeats, is a well-established marker of autonomic regulation, reflecting the balance between sympathetic and parasympathetic nervous system activity (Buckman et al., 2010, Ingjaldsson et al., 2003). Lower HRV has been associated with poorer executive functioning and increased impulsivity, while higher HRV has been linked to greater inhibitory control and adaptive self-regulation in both clinical and non-clinical samples of users of a variety of substances (Allen et al., 2009, Booij et al., 2006, Ingjaldsson et al., 2003, Ottaviani et al., 2019, Papachristou et al., 2012, Rajan et al., 1998).

Past studies have also examined changes in HRV in the presence of challenges such as laboratory stressors or exposure to alcohol or other substance cues. Machado et. al (2021) showed that among undergraduates, HRV decreased in the presence of a stressor (e.g., oral speech task). Similarly, Vaschillo et. al (2008) found that HRV decreased in the presence of alcohol cues. Other research among both healthy adults and smokers has suggested that anticipation of substance-related and stressful stimuli can increase HRV reactions that might be important for the potential recruitment of self-regulatory processes (Hilgarter et al., 2021, Kroczek et al., 2024). Thus, the relationship between exposure to challenging stimuli and short-term changes in HRV remains somewhat unclear.

The ability of HRV to predict drinking behavior outside the laboratory environment remains underexplored. Existing studies suggest that individuals who drink more display lower alcohol cue-induced HRV responses, indicative of poor self-regulation (Buckman et al., 2010, Ingjaldsson et al., 2003). On the other hand, individuals with high impulsivity often demonstrate stronger HRV reactivity in response to alcohol cues (Papachristou et al., 2012), raising the possibility of a potential physiological pathway linking trait impulsivity and drinking behaviour.

Despite evidence linking impulsivity and cue-reactivity, it remains unclear whether these physiological responses are associated with actual drinking behavior in key non-clinical populations such as young adult drinkers, who remain at risk for the development of alcohol use disorders (Meque et al., 2019). The current study aimed to examine whether impulsivity predicts cue-induced changes in HR and HRV, and whether these responses, in turn mediate the relationship between impulsivity and alcohol consumption. We hypothesized that (1) individuals higher in trait impulsivity would exhibit greater HR and HRV reactivity during exposure to alcohol cues, and (2) these physiological responses would, in turn, predict drinking behavior.

Method

Participants

Seventy-three students from a major urban university participated in this study. Participants were recruited via advertisements posted in and around the university campus. In order to be eligible, participants were required to be between the ages of 18 and 25 and have reported some alcohol use within the past 90 days. Exclusions were a history of cardiovascular disease, alcohol-related problems, illicit drug use, and treatment for major mental illness (e.g., schizophrenia). Women were excluded if the reported current pregnancy. Participants were instructed to abstain from drinking on the study day, which was confirmed with an Intoximeters (St. Louis, MO) breath alcohol monitor. All participants provided informed consent in accordance with the City University of New York’s University-Integrated Institutional Review Board (IRB).

Measures

Craving. We assessed self-reported craving (0–100) immediately before and after each of the two exposures (neutral, alcohol) using a well-established 5-item scale (Erblich & Bovbjerg, 2004) to confirm the effectiveness of the cue manipulation. Cronbach’s α ranged from 0.95 to 0.96 for the four administrations.

Trait Impulsivity. Trait impulsivity was assessed using the UPPS-P Impulsive Behavior Scale. The UPPS-P is a validated self-report questionnaire with a number of drug-relevant subscales, including positive and negative urgency and sensation seeking, that measures impulsivity and has been employed extensively in the literature (Cyders, 2013). Cronbach’s α was high for the full scale (0.89), with α-values for the subscales ranging from 0.68 (Negative Urgency) to 0.87 (Perseveration).

Drinking Behavior. Alcohol consumption over the past 90 days was assessed using the Timeline Followback (TLFB) method, a calendar-based self-report tool in which participants recorded the total number of alcoholic drinks consumed each day (Sobell et al., 1996). For this study, we focused on the total number of drinks consumed, and the number of binge episodes that occurred in the past ninety days, as longer intervals are associated with reduced accuracy (Hoeppner et al., 2010). Following NIAAA criteria, a binge drinking episode was defined as four or more drinks in a single occasion for women and five or more for men (NIAAA, 2007).

Procedure

Participants came to the study site for a single session, scheduled between 1PM and 5PM to reduce diurnal variability in psychophysiological measures, during which time they provided signed consent, completed basic demographic, UPPS-P, and TLFB questionnaires, and participated in a classic alcohol cue exposure task. During this task, participants were connected to twin lead subclavicular ECG electrolyte-gelled monitoring electrodes, sat quietly for a 10-minute baseline period, and were exposed to two counterbalanced conditions: a neutral cue (a glass of water) and an alcohol cue (a glass of the participant’s preferred alcoholic beverage). Each cue was presented for 90 s while participants held and viewed the beverage, without consuming it. Between the two cue presentations, participants viewed a nature video for two minutes that has been previously shown to help return psychophysiological indices to baseline (Piferi et al., 2000). Heart rate (HR) and heart rate variability (HRV) were recorded continuously throughout each cue exposure using a Biopac MP150 system with an ECG100C amplifier, with standard filtering of 1 (high-pass) and 35 (low-pass) Hz. Waveforms were acquired and analyzed by AcKnowledge 3.1 software, with automated QRS detection. We visually examined traces for ectopic beats and other artifacts and manually corrected any beats when misidentified. Low-frequency HRV (0.04–0.15 Hz) was analyzed using power spectral density methods in AcKnowledge.

Results

Demographics, Descriptive Statistics, and Craving. The study sample was 54.8% female (n = 40) and 45.2% male (N = 33). Mean age of the sample was 21.5 years (SD = 2.0). Of the 73 participants, 28.8% (n = 21) reported being Asian, 27.4% (n = 20) reported being White, 19.2% (n = 14) reported being Latino/Hispanic, 13.7% (n = 10) reported being Black/African American, and 10.9% (n = 8) reported other or multiple race/ethnicities. Participants scored an average of 2.31 (SD = 0.36) on the UPPS-P scale. They reported consuming 74.03 (SD = 65.98) total drinks over the past ninety days, and 6.84 (SD = 7.80) NIAAA-defined binge drinking episodes in the same time period. We confirmed that the alcohol cue significantly increased craving with a Cue (Neutral, Alcohol) by Time (Pre-Exposure, Post-Exposure) repeated-measures ANOVA; F(1,71) = 39.1, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.35. Mean cravings before and after the neutral cue were 12.7 (SE = 2.3) and 11.2 (SE = 2.1), and before and after the alcohol cue were 11.5 (SE = 2.2) and 25.7 (SE = 3.1), respectively. Results remained significant even after including gender and total drinks as covariates.

Impulsivity and Cue Reactivity. We performed two repeated-measures ANOVAs to evaluate the effects of neutral and alcohol cue exposures on HR and HRV, respectively. In both, we included UPPS-P scores as a between-subjects predictor. In the first ANOVA, we evaluated effects of cue exposure (neutral vs. alcohol) and impulsivity on HR. The main effect of cue type failed to reach statistical significance; F(1,71) = 3.45, p < 0.07, η2 = 0.04, with heart rates of 73.84 beats per minute (SE = 0.95) during the neutral cue exposure, and 72.72 beats per minute (SE = 0.93) during the alcohol cue exposure. The interaction of UPPS-P and cue exposure was not significant; F(1,71) = 0.41, p > 0.52, η2 = 0.004. Similar patterns of results were observed when analyzing the individual subscales, and when including gender and total drinks as covariates.

Next, we conducted analyses of the effects of cue exposure and impulsivity on HRV. In this case, there was no main effect of cue exposure on HRV; F(1,71) = 2.21, p > 0.14, η2 = 0.03, but there was a significant interaction between impulsivity and cue exposure; F(1,71) = 6.21, p < 0.02, η2 = 0.09. Simple effects analyses revealed that higher levels of impulsivity were related to higher HRV reactions to the alcohol cue, but not to the neutral cue (Fig. 1). To formally assess the direct and indirect pathways between impulsivity, HRV reactivity, and drinking behavior, we conducted a series of mediational analyses following the methods described by Hayes (2022). As already reported in the regression analyses above, there were significant direct effects of impulsivity on both total drinks and binge episodes. Interestingly however, we found a significant negative indirect effect of impulsivity on drinking behavior, such that impulsivity was associated with higher HRV reactions to alcohol cues, which in turn was associated with fewer total drinks (β = -0.09, bootstrapped CI’s: −0.23, −0.002) and fewer binge episodes (β = -0.09, bootstrapped CI’s: −0.25, −0.002). Inclusion of sex as a covariate did not impact results.

Discussion

The objective of this study was to examine the effects of trait impulsivity on alcohol-cue induced cardiovascular responses and drinking behavior. We did not observe significant main effects of cue exposure on either HR or HRV. Partially consistent with expectations, however, trait impulsivity predicted higher levels of HRV, but not HR, reactivity to alcohol cues. Not surprisingly, impulsivity also predicted higher levels of past 90-day drinking. Interestingly, however, lower levels of cue-induced HRV, not higher, predicted drinking. Consistent with this finding, mediational analysis revealed a positive direct effect of impulsivity on drinking, but a negative indirect effect, operating through increased HRV.

Trait impulsivity is reliably related to poorer drinking outcomes in the broader literature (Crews and Boettiger, 2009, Stojek and Fischer, 2013), and direct effects of impulsivity on drinking behavior in the present sample was no exception. Somewhat paradoxically, however, the indirect effect of impulsivity on drinking via HRV changes was in the opposite direction; impulsivity predicted higher cue-induced HRV reactions, which in turn predicted lower levels of drinking. Increased cue-induced HRV has been linked to enhanced regulatory capacity (McCraty & Shaffer, 2015), which is consistent with our finding that higher HRV was associated with lower levels of past 90-day drinking. Classic dual-process models of addiction (Strack & Deutsch, 2004) highlight the complex interplay between the “hot,” impulsive, and cue-reactive “fast” processes, and the “cooler,” top down, and reflective slower processes. Appetitive cues are considerably more salient to impulsive individuals who smoke (Doran et al., 2008, Erblich and Michalowski, 2015), and this salience may explain the robust momentary increases in HRV during a cue-exposure challenge that requires them to mount an increased regulatory response to cope with the immediate urge. Findings may suggest that although impulsive individuals may generally be at risk for increased drinking, they are also capable of recruiting powerful regulatory responses that set the stage for slower, reflective self-regulatory processes necessary for drink refusal. It is tempting to speculate that although the “net” effect of impulsivity is higher risk for increased drinking, exaggerated cue-induced HRV among impulsive individuals may reflect an underlying physiological capacity to self-regulate, and may be viewed as a biomarker of resilience. Indeed, recent work has raised the possibility that HRV can predict self-regulation even within impulsive individuals (Ottaviani et al., 2019). If so, interventions (e.g., episodic future thinking (Athamneh et al., 2022) that further encourage top-down, slower processing may be particularly useful for impulsive individuals who are trying to moderate alcohol consumption. By integrating psychophysiological measures into research on impulsivity and drinking, this study contributes to our understanding of the mechanisms underlying alcohol cue-reactivity and its implications for risky drinking. Clarifying these mechanisms may inform early intervention strategies, particularly among young adults at risk of problem drinking. Of course, changes in HRV may also be associated with other important processes, including changes in physiological effort, attentional engagement, and others [e.g., Park et al. (2022)], and therefore we emphasize that our interpretation here is but one of numerous alternatives.

The study has a number of important limitations. First, the non-clinical study sample consisted of college students who, on average, did not score exceptionally highly on the UPPS-P. We also did not assess menstrual cycle in our female participants, which could have influenced results. In addition, the drinking assessment was retrospective, limiting the predictive value of the results and somewhat limiting the interpretation of the directionality of the path analyses. Nevertheless, the results suggest that impulsivity is a risk factor for increased drinking independent of HRV, and that HRV may actually reflect an increased momentary self-regulatory capacity that can be drawn upon when developing targeted intervention to reduce alcohol consumption.
