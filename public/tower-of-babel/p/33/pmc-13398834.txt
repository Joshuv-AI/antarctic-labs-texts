Comparative Clinical and Radiological Outcomes of Arthroscopic‐Assisted Uniportal Spinal Surgery Versus Unilateral Biportal Endoscopy for Unilateral Laminotomy With Bilateral Decompression in Lumbar Spinal Stenosis: A 2‐Year Follow‐Up Study
Authors: Abdul Waheed Bahir, Wang Daxing, Liu Bailian, Gan Xuewen, Xiong Ying, Xiao Jiayu, Gu Shao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveUnilateral laminotomy with bilateral decompression (ULBD) is a standard procedure for relieving neural compression. Recently, minimally invasive techniques such as Arthroscopic‐assisted uniportal spinal surgery (AUSS) and unilateral biportal endoscopy (UBE) have gained attention as alternatives to traditional methods. However, limited data are available on the comparative clinical and radiological outcomes of AUSS and UBE for ULBD in patients with lumbar spinal stenosis (LSS). This study aimed to compare these outcomes in patients with LSS who underwent ULBD using either AUSS or UBE, with a follow‐up period of 2 years.MethodsThis retrospective study evaluated 87 patients with LSS who underwent ULBD using either the AUSS or UBE technique between March 2021 and November 2022. Patients were followed up for at least 2 years. Perioperative outcomes, pain relief assessed using the VAS and ODI, and radiological parameters, including the Cross‐Sectional Dural Area (CSA), Disc Height Index (DHI), Translation Motion, Preservation Rate of Facet Joint, and Radiological Progression, were compared between the two groups. Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS version 29. Continuous variables were analyzed using the independent‐samples t‐test or Mann–Whitney U test, and categorical variables were analyzed using the chi‐squared test or Fisher's exact test.ResultsBoth AUSS and UBE techniques showed significant reductions in VAS and ODI scores postoperatively, with no significant differences between the groups at any follow‐up. The AUSS group had shorter operative times, less blood loss, and smaller incisions compared to the UBE group. Postoperative CSA of the dural sac increased similarly in both groups. Radiological progression was observed in one patient in the AUSS group and one in the UBE group, but no major complications occurred. More than 90% of patients in both groups reported favorable outcomes based on the Modified Macnab criteria at the final follow‐up.ConclusionsAUSS and UBE are both effective minimally invasive techniques for treating LSS through ULBD, achieving similar clinical and radiological outcomes at the two‐year follow‐up. Although AUSS demonstrated perioperative advantages, including shorter operative time and lower estimated blood loss, patient‐centered clinical outcomes were comparable between the two groups.

Introduction

Lumbar spinal stenosis (LSS) frequently affects the aging population and results from progressive narrowing of the spinal canal, which places pressure on neural structures [1]. This compression typically causes symptoms such as discomfort in the lower back or legs, along with decreased walking endurance [2]. In the early stages, symptoms are often managed with nonsurgical approaches such as exercise‐based rehabilitation, medication, and lifestyle modifications [3]. However, when these approaches fail, particularly in cases of progressive neurological deficits or declining mobility, surgical intervention becomes necessary [4].

Traditional surgical techniques, including complete or partial laminectomy with transforaminal decompression, have long been effective in relieving nerve compression. However, these procedures typically involve the removal of substantial portions of bone and soft tissue, which may lead to complications such as chronic postoperative pain and accelerated degeneration of adjacent spinal segments [5]. Techniques aimed at reducing these risks have gained popularity through a minimally invasive spinal surgery (MISS) approach, which focuses on minimizing tissue damage, preserving spinal stability, and promoting faster recovery after surgery [6].

A commonly adopted technique involves unilateral excision of the lamina and ligamentum flavum to achieve bilateral neural decompression, known as unilateral laminotomy for bilateral decompression (ULBD) [7]. By sparing key anatomical structures, such as the paraspinal muscles and posterior ligament complex, ULBD helps maintain spinal integrity and reduces the likelihood of postoperative complications [8, 9, 10].

In recent years, two advanced techniques have emerged as promising alternatives: unilateral biportal endoscopy (UBE) and Arthroscopic‐assisted uniportal spinal surgery (AUSS), also referred to as uniportal noncoaxial spinal endoscopic surgery (UNSES) [11]. The presence of two separate portals in UBE allows for greater flexibility during surgery. However, it may also increase technical complexity, as instrument coordination and maintenance of stable positioning can be more challenging [9, 12].

The AUSS, first introduced by a Chinese scholar, uses a single portal that combines both visualization and instrumentation channels [13]. This configuration allows for more intuitive instrument handling and the wider surgical field, which enhances the surgical adaptability. This procedure is a combination of intervertebral foraminoscopy and unilateral biportal endoscopy, which allows the effective decomposition of the spinal canal and nerve roots. By merging the simplicity of the uniportal access with the visual advantages of biportal systems, AUSS has become increasingly popular among spinal surgeons in China for the treatment of LSS [14, 15].

Despite the increasing clinical use of AUSS and UBE, comparative evidence regarding their long‐term clinical and radiological outcomes in ULBD for LSS remains limited. Existing studies have not fully clarified the relative advantages, safety profiles, radiological stability, and perioperative features of these two minimally invasive approaches. Therefore, further evaluation is needed to guide surgical decision‐making and optimize patient care. The purpose of this retrospective study was to: (i) compare the clinical effectiveness of AUSS and UBE by evaluating pain relief, functional recovery, and patient satisfaction using VAS, ODI, and modified Macnab criteria; (ii) assess radiological decompression and postoperative segmental stability by analyzing the CSA of the dural sac, DHI, translation motion, facet joint preservation, and radiological progression; and (iii) compare perioperative outcomes and surgical features, including operation time, intraoperative blood loss, incision length, hospital stay, and complications.

These findings may help clarify the advantages and features of AUSS and UBE in the treatment of LSS and provide direction for future prospective, multicenter studies evaluating minimally invasive decompression techniques.

Methods

This retrospective study included patients who underwent ULBD using either UBE or AUSS for LSS at our hospital between March 2021 and November 2022. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Yan'an Hospital, affiliated to Kunming Medical University (Approval No. 2025‐082‐01).

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (i) confirmed diagnosis of lumbar spinal stenosis; (ii) persistence of symptoms despite at least 10–12 weeks of conservative treatment; (iii) presence of activity‐related lower‐extremity symptoms consistent with neurogenic claudication, including radiating leg pain, numbness, heaviness, or weakness, relieved by rest or lumbar flexion; (iv) imaging‐confirmed narrowing of the central canal or lateral recess at a single spinal level; and (v) completion of a minimum 24‐month follow‐up.

The criteria for exclusion were as follows: (i) lumbar tuberculosis, trauma, or infection; (ii) multilevel lumbar spinal stenosis requiring surgical intervention; and (iii) previous lumbar surgery at the affected spinal level.

Patient Data

This study involved 87 participants who met the selection criteria. Among them, 41 underwent AUSS, while 46 underwent UBE. Both AUSS and UBE were applied for the same surgical indication, ULBD, for LSS. The choice of surgical approach was guided by clinical assessment and shared decision‐making between the surgeon and patient, with the same indication criteria applied to both groups. All procedures were performed by the same senior spinal surgeon experienced in both techniques, assisted by the same surgical team. This helped reduce inter‐operator variability and improve procedural consistency.

Surgical Procedure

AUSS Group

The AUSS combines both endoscopic and surgical techniques to facilitate a minimally invasive approach for unilateral laminotomy to achieve bilateral decompression of the spinal canal. The effective nature of the procedure for lumbar spinal stenosis is that it is created using a uniportal procedure, which causes minimal tissue damage and improves visualization of the surgical site and access to it. After the administration of general anesthesia, the patient was placed in the prone position on a well‐padded surgical table to avoid causing pressure sores and to keep the patient comfortable. Normal fluoroscopic impressions in the C‐arm were obtained to ensure that the vertebrae were targeted by performing anteroposterior and lateral vertebral scans of the lumbar spine. The intended interspace was marked on the skin, and the area was sterilized and covered with a sterile drape. An approximately 1.5 cm incision was made at the marked interspace. This procedure was performed using a single deep fascial opening under a single skin incision. The incision area was progressively expanded by a dilation tube which minimized the disturbance of the muscles. Following dilation, a trocar was used as a working one to aid in the placement of endoscopes. The paravertebral muscles and underlying bone were separated by an electric coagulator to maximize the inferior border of the upper lamina and interlaminar ligament. The procedure involved ipsilateral laminotomy and partial removal of the base of the spinous process using high‐speed diamond burrs and Kerrison rongeurs for precision in the surgical field. After ipsilateral laminotomy, the ligamentum flavum was excised (flavectomy). Both the ipsilateral and contralateral recesses can be clearly visible and the endoscope is able to flex and angle allowing the extensive decompression of both dural sac and nerve roots. Adequacy of decompression was evaluated by normal pulsatility of the dura mater in the course of respiration. Endoscopic observation confirmed that both the central spinal canal and nerve roots were adequately decompressed. Careful examination of the bilateral nerve roots and lateral recesses for signs of compression is critical to confirm decompression success. After confirming adequate decompression, meticulous hemostasis was achieved. In cases of significant blood loss or concerns about seroma formation, a drain may be placed. The incision was then closed in layers using 2–0 absorbable sutures, and a sterile dressing was applied to complete the procedure (Figure 1).

UBE Group

General anesthesia was administered prior to positioning the patient in the prone position for surgery. The operative posture was secured to ensure stability throughout the procedure. The target vertebral level was then identified using intraoperative fluoroscopic imaging with a C‐arm. Once localization was confirmed, the surgical field was sterilized and covered with drapes to maintain a sterile environment. Two small incisions were made in the medial pedicle line: a 5–6 mm incision was used for endoscope insertion and continued saline irrigation, whereas an 8–10 mm caudal portal was used for saline outflow and insertion of instruments used in decompression and the overall procedure. A blunt dilator was utilized within the working channel to separate the soft tissues of the upper and lower laminae and the inner joint capsule, ensuring clear visibility through electrocautery hemostasis. The remaining steps for decompression are the same as AUSS, which are described in the AUSS procedure in detail.

Postoperative Management and Efficacy Evaluation Indicator

Postoperative management was consistent across both groups. All patients received routine postoperative care. On the first postoperative day, straight leg raise exercises and ankle pump training were initiated, and patients were encouraged to ambulate with lumbar support. Although limited bed rest was recommended during the first month, guidance on lumbar and back muscle exercises was incorporated to enhance recovery. After 1 month, patients were gradually encouraged to resume normal daily activities. Follow‐up evaluations were scheduled at 3 months, 12 months, and 2 years postoperatively.

To assess the efficacy of the surgical interventions, various perioperative indicators were documented, including operative time (recorded from skin incision to wound closure, excluding anesthesia induction and patient positioning), intraoperative blood loss (estimated from preoperative and postoperative day 3 hemoglobin levels), surgical incision length, hospital stay, and complications.

Clinical outcomes including pain and functional improvement were assessed using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) for the pain in the legs and back, as well as the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI), measured preoperatively and during the postoperative follow‐up visits.

Radiological assessments were conducted with the help of three main imaging techniques: T2‐weighted magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), computed tomography (CT), and dynamic flexion‐extension lateral radiographs, to measure anatomical and functional alterations during the pre‐ and postsurgery period.

Radiological measurements were performed by two experienced spine surgeons using the hospital PACS system. Due to the availability of surgical information within the hospital system, the observers were not formally blinded to the surgical technique; however, all parameters were evaluated independently using standardized measurement protocols to minimize potential measurement bias.

The cross‐sectional area (CSA) of the dural sac was measured on axial T2‐weighted MRI at the level of maximal spinal canal compression, using manual tracings performed with the Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS). The traced areas were calculated in square millimeters (mm2) to objectively assess decompression effectiveness.

The Disc Height Index (DHI) was calculated from lateral lumbar radiographs by averaging the anterior, middle, and posterior disc heights at the operated level and expressing this value as a percentage relative to the height of the adjacent vertebral body, providing a standardized measure of disc preservation and degenerative progression.

Translation motion between adjacent vertebral bodies was evaluated on the flexion–extension lateral radiographs by the measuring of anteroposterior displacement and normalizing it to the anteroposterior length of the inferior vertebral body, expressed as a percentage using the formula: (displacement ÷ vertebral body length) × 100%. This method allowed the comparison of the segmental stability among patients with varying spinal morphologies, with increased translation motion indicating potential instability.

Facet joint preservation was evaluated on axial CT scans by measuring the facet joint surface area on both ipsilateral and contralateral sides before and after surgery. The preservation rate was calculated as (postoperative area ÷ preoperative area) × 100%, reflecting the extent of joint preservation associated with postoperative spinal stability (Figure 2).

Radiological progression was characterized by the decrease in DHI by at least 5% or the increase in vertebral slippage by at least 5% at follow up. In general, it can be concluded that decompression can affect disc integrity and spinal stability, which is why close monitoring after surgery is very important [16].

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyzes were performed with IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 29). For continuous variables adhering to a normal distribution, data were expressed as means along with standard deviations, and intergroup comparisons were conducted utilizing the independent samples t‐test. In the absence of normal distribution, the Mann–Whitney U test was utilized for comparison. Categorical data were described through frequencies and percentages, then evaluated using either the chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test, contingent upon the data distribution and sample size. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

A similar pattern was observed in ODI scores, which significantly decreased as early as 3 days postoperatively and remained lower at all subsequent follow‐up visits (p < 0.05). Postoperative Complications and Additional Procedures

Regarding perioperative complications, the AUSS group had one case of dural laceration, which was relatively minor and did not require any repair. In the UBE group, one patient experienced nerve injury and one required reoperation because of Slippage Progression after 7 months. No infections, epidural hematomas, or dural lacerations were observed in the UBE group. The frequency of complications and additional procedures was rare and had no significant differences between the two groups.

Modified Macnab Evaluation at Final Follow‐Up

At the final follow‐up, both techniques demonstrated high patient satisfaction, with over 90% of patients in both the AUSS (38/41) and UBE (43/46) groups achieving excellent or good outcomes. According to the Modified Macnab criteria, outcomes in the AUSS group (n = 41) were excellent in 24 patients, good in 14, fair in 2, and poor in 1. In the UBE group (n = 46), outcomes were excellent in 27 patients, good in 16, fair in 3, and no patients were classified as poor. There was no significant difference between the two groups (p = 0.696). The small number of fair or poor outcomes was primarily associated with persistent postoperative symptoms or radiological progression.

Discussion

LSS is among the most common degenerative disorders which are affecting the elderly population and often leads to significant deterioration in mobility and quality of life [17]. Spinal canal stenosis can compromise neural structures and commonly manifests as neurogenic claudication and radicular pain [18, 19]. Conservative treatments such as physical therapy and analgesics are typically the first‐line management strategies and surgical intervention is necessary when symptoms persist or worsen [20].

With the development of minimally invasive spine surgery, endoscopic decompression techniques have been increasingly applied to reduce tissue disruption while achieving adequate neural decompression.

In the present study, we compared the 2‐year clinical and radiological outcomes of AUSS and UBE for ULBD in patients with LSS. The main findings showed that both techniques resulted in significant improvements in VAS and ODI scores, high excellent/good rates according to the modified Macnab criterion, effective radiological decompression, and preservation of segmental stability. Overall, the long‐term clinical and radiological outcomes were comparable between the two groups.

Clinical Effectiveness of AUSS and UBE

Our analysis shows that AUSS and UBE are both reliable methods for LSS surgery, as patients experienced substantial reductions in VAS and ODI scores after 2 years. The scores for both groups before treatment were similar, which improved the study's fairness. Additionally, the absence of a significant difference in postoperative VAS and ODI scores indicates that AUSS and UBE have the same effectiveness in managing LSS‐related symptoms. The outcomes of these studies match those of earlier investigations that found similar clinical results for MISS. A comparative study showed that both endoscopic methods resulted in significant reductions in pain and improvements in functional status, comparable to conventional open surgeries [21]. The clinical success in both groups was high, with over 90% of individuals reporting an excellent or good outcome as per the modified Macnab criterion at the 2‐year follow‐up. The results show that there is no big disparity between the two methods in the area of patient‐reported outcomes. This high level of patient satisfaction is consistent with findings from systematic reviews and meta‐analyzes, which report that more than 90% of patients undergoing minimally invasive procedures achieve excellent or good outcomes. This consistency further supports the clinical equivalence of these techniques in providing effective symptom relief and improving quality of life [22].

Perioperative Outcomes and Surgical Efficiency

However, the perioperative outcomes showed significant differences between the groups. The AUSS was also linked to a short duration of operation, less intraoperative blood loss, and smaller incisions as compared to UBE. The AUSS single‐incision design, which integrates a view‐working channel, offers the added benefit of enhancing the surgical procedure organization, thus allowing for improved handling of the endoscope and instrument [11, 13]. In contrast, the dual‐portal configuration of UBE may limit instrument coordination, which could partly explain the longer operative time required [23]. However, it should be acknowledged that variations in operative time and blood loss may also reflect surgeon proficiency, case complexity, or familiarity with each technique, rather than differences in system design alone. Previous studies have also noted that UBE presents a steeper learning curve than open surgery, particularly for less experienced surgeons. However, if training and experience are sufficient, both approaches can be successful, resulting in better outcomes [24].

Safety Profile and Complications

Both methods had similar complication rates, suggesting that either technique is safe for treating LSS. During treatment in the AUSS group, a dural laceration occurred during surgery; however, it was relatively small, and no additional procedure was needed. In the UBE group, one case of nerve injury occurred, and reoperation was performed in one case because of progressive vertebral slippage. These findings are consistent with the existing literature highlighting the safety of minimally invasive techniques. Minimally invasive surgery tends to result in fewer complications than conventional surgeries. This contributes to its growing preference in the clinical practices. Radiological Outcomes and Segmental Stability

Both groups showed an increase in the CSA of the dural sac on radiological evaluation, suggesting successful management of the conditions. Furthermore the key biomechanical parameters, including the DHI, segmental translation and facet joint integrity remained the stable postoperatively in both groups, with no significant differences observed between them. The preservation of the spinal anatomy indicates that neither surgical technique resulted in postoperative spinal instability, which is crucial for long‐term spinal health. Maintaining the integrity of these structures is essential to prevent adjacent segment disease and reduce the risk of future fusion surgery, which could significantly impact patients' quality of life. These findings are consistent with those of previous studies demonstrating the effectiveness of minimally invasive spine procedures in preserving spinal structures and achieving favorable radiological outcomes. Our results are consistent with those of studies comparing various MIS techniques, including uniportal, biportal, and conventional microscopic approaches. These studies consistently show that MIS provides effective decompression while maintaining spinal stability and achieving positive radiological outcomes comparable to traditional open surgery [3, 26]. These findings align with biomechanical studies emphasizing the importance of preserving midline structures during ULBD to maintain spinal stability [27]. Recent advances in minimally invasive spine surgery have emphasized the importance of preserving spinal stability by minimizing injury to the posterior elements [28]. Facet‐preserving and midline‐structure‐sparing endoscopic approaches, including microendoscopic decompression, unilateral laminotomy with bilateral decompression, and delta‐channel endoscopy [29], share the common goal of maintaining biomechanical integrity while ensuring sufficient neural decompression. In this context, the AUSS may represent an advanced iteration of anatomy‐preserving endoscopic techniques which integrate surgical efficacy with maintenance of spinal stability. Our findings are consistent with this principle, showing that both the AUSS and UBE preserved segmental stability.

Technical Considerations and Learning Curve

Within this framework AUSS may represent an advanced development of endoscopic procedures that preserve the anatomy and achieve surgical efficacy and spinal stability conservation. From a practical standpoint the logistical advantages of the AUSS especially in decreased time of operation and blood loss are worth mentioning and could lead to the increase in the efficiency of surgery in the high‐volume or resource‐limited context. the decreased time of operations can reduce anesthesia‐related risks and lower the incidence of intraoperative complications and also improve overall surgical throughput. Although both the AUSS and the UBE are minimally invasive endoscopic procedures designed to decompress the lumbar spine, their learning curves differ. Studies suggest that surgeons may require approximately 30 cases to achieve UBE proficiency [30]. Conversely, the initial assessment of the AUSS suggests that the learning curve is more favorable, which may be taken with less difficulty by surgeons who are making a transition or those who are new to the endoscopic surgeries. This relative ease of learning with the AUSS may facilitate its broader integration into clinical practice and thereby improve patient access to advanced surgical care. Combining the visual and operative portals into one incision, the AUSS will provide a fast instrumentation process and superior ergonomics compared to the two‐portals design chosen by UBE. After mastering the technique of the AUSS, it can enhance surgical versatility and workflow in the management of the various lumbar pathologies, ultimately improving clinical outcomes.

Limitations and Strengths

This study has several strengths. First, it directly compared AUSS and UBE for the same surgical indication, namely ULBD for LSS. Second, all patients completed a minimum 2‐year follow‐up, allowing assessment of both clinical improvement and radiological stability. Third, multiple outcome measures were evaluated, including perioperative parameters, VAS, ODI, modified Macnab outcomes, complications, and radiological indicators. In addition, all procedures were performed by the same senior spinal surgeon and surgical team, which helped reduce variability related to surgical technique.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, the retrospective and single‐center design may introduce selection bias and limit generalizability. Second, the relatively small sample size and lack of randomization may reduce statistical power and allow potential confounding. Third, objective assessments of paraspinal muscle injury, such as MRI or serum biomarkers, were not included. Fourth, the surgical learning curve was not formally evaluated using case‐sequence stratification or CUSUM analysis. Finally, this study only compared AUSS and UBE without including other minimally invasive techniques. Future prospective, multicenter, randomized studies with larger sample sizes and longer follow‐up are needed to further validate these findings.

Conclusion

Both AUSS and UBE are effective minimally invasive techniques for performing ULBD in patients with lumbar spinal stenosis, demonstrating significant clinical and radiological improvement at the 2‐year follow‐up. Although AUSS showed perioperative advantages, including shorter operative time and lower estimated blood loss, patient‐centered outcomes such as pain relief, functional improvement, hospital stay, and overall satisfaction were comparable between the two groups.
