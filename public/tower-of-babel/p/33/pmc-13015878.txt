Echocardiographic Pulmonary–Left Atrial Ratio and Epicardial Adipose Tissue as Noninvasive Markers of Obstructive Sleep Apnea
Authors: Yemlihan Ceylan, Mehmet Hakan Bilgin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundObstructive sleep apnea syndrome (OSAS) is linked to pulmonary hypertension and cardiovascular risk, yet simple echocardiographic indices related to cardiopulmonary hemodynamics and cardiometabolic burden are limited. We evaluated ePLAR and epicardial adipose tissue (EAT) thickness as complementary, noninvasive echocardiographic markers associated with OSAS presence and severity in newly diagnosed patients.MethodsIn this cross‐sectional study, 63 polysomnography‐confirmed OSAS patients and 63 controls with a similar distribution of age, sex, and major comorbidities underwent transthoracic echocardiography. OSAS severity was classified as mild, moderate, or severe. ePLAR (TRVmax/E/e′) and EAT were measured. Diagnostic performance was assessed by ROC analysis (apparent discrimination), and prediction models for severe OSAS were fitted using Firth penalized logistic regression with bootstrap optimism correction.ResultsOSAS patients had higher EAT (0.62 ± 0.11 vs. 0.53 ± 0.06 cm) and ePLAR (0.37 ± 0.09 vs. 0.23 ± 0.04 m/s) than controls (both p < 0.001). Within OSAS, both measures increased stepwise with severity (p < 0.001). In internally validated models, adding ePLAR and/or EAT to age/sex/BMI improved discrimination for severe OSAS.ConclusionsEPLAR and EAT were associated with OSAS presence and severity and showed good apparent discrimination for severe OSAS in this cohort; these findings are hypothesis‐generating and require external validation in clinically representative populations before any clinical implementation.Trial RegistrationThis study was observational and cross‐sectional in design and was not registered in a clinical trial registry because it was not an interventional clinical trial.

Introduction

Obstructive sleep apnea syndrome (OSAS) is a respiratory disorder characterized by repeated upper airway obstructions during sleep, significantly increasing the risk of cardiovascular diseases (CVD) [1]. OSAS is strongly linked to cardiovascular complications such as heart failure, coronary artery disease (CAD), and resistant hypertension [1, 2]. Especially in severe OSAS cases, the risk of developing pulmonary hypertension (PH) and right ventricular (RV) dysfunction is higher, leading to increased cardiovascular morbidity and mortality [3, 4].

The echocardiographic pulmonary left atrial ratio (ePLAR) is a new noninvasive parameter calculated by dividing the peak tricuspid regurgitation velocity (TRVmax) by the mitral E/e′ ratio (TRVmax/E/e′) [5]. This method has gained attention as a noninvasive index associated with pulmonary hemodynamic differences in selected clinical settings. Studies in pulmonary embolism and PH have reported correlations between ePLAR and invasive hemodynamic measurements and suggested that ePLAR may reflect differences in transpulmonary gradients [5, 6]. Given the established association of OSAS with pulmonary vascular stress and PH, ePLAR may provide a practical echocardiographic index associated with OSAS severity and cardiopulmonary hemodynamic surrogates in this population. Nevertheless, interpreting ePLAR as a surrogate of pulmonary vascular disease in OSAS requires validation against invasive hemodynamic measurements.

EAT thickness, a metabolically active parameter, is elevated in OSAS patients and correlated with disease severity [7]. Increased EAT thickness is regarded as a marker of cardiovascular risk and a potential predictor of cardiac complications in OSAS [8]. However, whether EAT and ePLAR add clinically relevant information beyond anthropometric measures such as BMI remains an important question.

To our knowledge, ePLAR has not been systematically evaluated in polysomnography (PSG)‐confirmed OSAS cohorts. Combining ePLAR and EAT thickness measurements may provide complementary echocardiographic markers of OSAS presence and severity. These findings require confirmation in larger, clinically representative cohorts with external validation. Because conditions that can independently elevate pulmonary pressures may confound TRVmax‐based indices, we evaluated ePLAR and EAT in a newly diagnosed OSAS cohort without chronic lung disease or suspected/confirmed PH.

We aimed (i) to compare ePLAR and EAT between PSG‐confirmed OSAS and PSG‐normal controls, and (ii) to evaluate their ability to identify severe OSAS within the OSAS cohort. The results may offer new insights into echocardiographic correlates of OSAS presence and severity and may inform future validation studies.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Setting

This study was designed as a cross‐sectional investigation following the STROBE guidelines. From January 2024 to January 2025, patients diagnosed with OSAS based on PSG results and referred to the Cardiology Department from the Pulmonary Diseases Department at Van Yüzüncü Yıl University were consecutively enrolled.

Participants and Study Flow

The sample size for this study was determined based on the findings of previous research by Scalia et al. [6]. on the echocardiographic pulmonary to left atrial ratio (ePLAR). To achieve a statistical power of 80% (β = 0.20) with a significance level of 5% (α = 0.05), the minimum required sample size was calculated as 25 participants per group. However, a total of 126 participants were included in the final study group, ensuring sufficient statistical power. A total of 126 participants were enrolled: 63 patients with polysomnography‐confirmed OSAS and 63 controls with normal PSG findings. The control group had a similar distribution of age, sex, and major comorbidities compared with the OSAS group.

The study included 63 consecutive patients with polysomnography (PSG)‐confirmed OSAS and 63 control participants. All participants underwent overnight PSG in the sleep laboratory. OSAS was defined as an apnea–hypopnea index (AHI) ≥5 events/hour. Controls were selected among individuals with normal PSG findings (AHI <5 events/hour) and no symptoms suggestive of sleep‐disordered breathing (e.g., habitual snoring, witnessed apneas, or excessive daytime sleepiness). Individuals with borderline or technically inadequate PSG recordings were not included in the control group. The participant flow, including group allocation, is shown in Figure 1. All data were collected at a single time point, and no data were missing for any participant.

TRVmax and ePLAR distributions may also be shifted toward lower values compared with broader sleep‐clinic populations.

Polysomnography (PSG) Study

All participants underwent overnight PSG using a 16‐channel Embla N7000 (Medcare, Reykjavik, Iceland) sleep monitoring system in a laboratory setting. PSG recordings were performed following the guidelines of the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) [9]. The AHI was calculated based on the number of apnea and hypopnea events per hour of sleep, in line with AASM criteria. Control subjects were selected among individuals whose PSG results were within normal limits (AHI <5 events/hour) and who subsequently attended the cardiology outpatient clinic for routine evaluation. In addition, control subjects reported no history of habitual snoring, witnessed apneas, or excessive daytime sleepiness, and had no prior diagnosis of sleep‐disordered breathing.

Clinical and Biochemical Assessments

Demographic data of patients referred to the cardiology clinic were recorded. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters (kg/m2). Body surface area (BSA) was measured in square meters using the Du Bois formula. CAD was defined as angiographically documented disease or a history of coronary artery bypass graft surgery. Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) was diagnosed according to the ADA guidelines as fasting plasma glucose (FPG) ≥126 mg/dL, 2‐h plasma glucose ≥200 mg/dL during an oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), HbA1c ≥6.5%, random plasma glucose ≥200 mg/dL, or a history of oral antidiabetic medication use [9]. Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥90 mmHg on repeated office measurements, confirmed by out‐of‐office measurements, or current use of antihypertensive medication [10]. Hyperlipidemia was defined according to the 2019 ESC/EAS dyslipidemia guidelines as elevated LDL cholesterol (≥130 mg/dL), triglycerides (≥150 mg/dL), total cholesterol (≥200 mg/dL), or the use of lipid‐lowering medications [11]. Active smokers were classified as smokers.

Venous blood samples were drawn after overnight fasting to analyze hemogram, C‐reactive protein (CRP), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C), low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C), and liver and kidney function tests.

Echocardiographic Measurements

Transthoracic echocardiography was performed using a GE Vivid E9 system (GE Healthcare, Norway) in accordance with current guidelines. Standard measurements of cardiac chambers and the aorta were obtained. Transmitral inflow velocities and tissue Doppler measurements of the lateral and septal mitral annulus were recorded from the apical four‐chamber view at end‐expiration, and averaged over ≥3 consecutive cardiac cycles. Mitral E/e′ was calculated using the average of septal and lateral e′ velocities. Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was calculated using the modified Simpson method. Peak tricuspid regurgitation velocity (TRVmax) was obtained using continuous‐wave Doppler from multiple windows at end‐expiration, using the highest‐quality envelope, and averaged over ≥3 cardiac cycles. Systolic pulmonary artery pressure (SPAP) was estimated using the modified Bernoulli equation (SPAP = 4×TRVmax2 + right atrial pressure). Right atrial pressure was approximated based on inferior vena cava diameter and collapsibility (3, 8, or 15 mmHg, as appropriate). When the tricuspid regurgitation jet was inadequate, agitated saline was used to enhance the Doppler signal (n = 8). Left atrial (LA) volume was measured using the biplane area–length method from apical 4‐ and 2‐chamber views at end‐systole, and LA volume index (LAVi) was calculated by indexing LA volume to body surface area (mL/m2), in accordance with current echocardiography guidelines. EAT thickness was measured at end‐systole on the RV free wall in the parasternal long‐axis view, and the average of three cardiac cycles was recorded [12]. ePLAR was calculated as TRVmax divided by the mitral E/e′ ratio (TRVmax/[E/e′]), yielding values expressed in m/s, as previously described [5]. All baseline echocardiographic measurements were performed by a single experienced echocardiographer blinded to clinical and polysomnographic data.

Intraobserver reproducibility was evaluated in a randomly selected subset of participants (n = 35). The same echocardiographer repeated measurements after a 2‐week interval, blinded to the initial measurements and to clinical and polysomnographic data. Repeated measurements included TRVmax, mitral E, mitral e′, E/e′, ePLAR, and EAT. Agreement was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC [3,1]; two‐way mixed‐effects model, absolute agreement) and Bland–Altman analysis (Table S3).

Bias Minimization Strategies

To minimize bias, participants were enrolled consecutively. Controls were required to have normal PSG findings (AHI <5 events/hour) and no symptoms suggestive of sleep‐disordered breathing, thereby reducing misclassification. Echocardiographic measurements were performed according to American Society of Echocardiography (ASE) recommendations by an experienced echocardiographer blinded to clinical and polysomnographic data. Strict exclusion criteria were applied to limit confounding by conditions known to affect pulmonary pressures and cardiac function. Confounding was further addressed by multivariable modeling, including established clinical factors (age, sex, and BMI). Given the limited number of severe cases and evidence of quasi‐separation, penalized regression methods were used for severe OSAS models to reduce small‐sample bias. These design choices also define a selected study population (PSG‐normal, asymptomatic controls and a lower‐comorbidity OSAS cohort), which may accentuate case–control separation and limit generalizability to broader sleep‐clinic populations; ePLAR/TRVmax distributions may therefore be shifted toward lower values.

Statistics

Demographic, laboratory, and echocardiographic variables were compared between the OSAS and control groups. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of distributions. Continuous variables were compared using the independent‐samples t‐test for normally distributed data and the Mann–Whitney U test otherwise. Categorical variables were compared using the chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or n (%).

Differences among OSAS severity groups (mild, moderate, severe) were analyzed using Welch's one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Games–Howell post hoc tests.

The diagnostic performance of ePLAR and EAT was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis in two settings: [1] discrimination between OSAS patients and controls and [2] identification of severe OSAS within the OSAS cohort (severe vs. non‐severe). For each parameter, area under the curve (AUC), optimal cut‐off value, sensitivity, specificity, and Youden index were calculated. A combined logistic‐regression–derived score including ePLAR and EAT was also evaluated by ROC analysis, with cut‐offs determined by maximizing Youden's J statistic.

Because of the limited number of severe cases and evidence of quasi‐separation, multivariable models for severe OSAS were fitted using Firth penalized logistic regression. Model discrimination was assessed using AUC, and internal validation was performed using bootstrap optimism correction (1000 resamples) to obtain optimism‐corrected AUC estimates and 95% confidence intervals (Table S1). The ROC analyses for individual markers and the combined score represent apparent (non‐validated) discrimination within the study dataset; therefore, internally validated discrimination metrics from penalized multivariable models were emphasized for clinical interpretation. For the reduced model including EAT and ePLAR only, calibration was additionally assessed using bootstrap‐optimism‐corrected calibration intercept and slope (1000 bootstrap iterations; Table S2), and calibration was visualized with a calibration plot (Figure S1).

Intraobserver reproducibility was assessed in a randomly selected subset using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC [3,1]; two‐way mixed‐effects model, absolute agreement) and Bland–Altman analysis (Table S3). In additional prespecified sensitivity analyses within the OSAS cohort, we explored the contribution of the ePLAR components by fitting a Firth penalized logistic regression model including TRVmax and E/e′ simultaneously (adjusted for age, sex, and BMI), alongside a separate Firth model including ePLAR (adjusted for age, sex, and BMI) (Table S4). For interpretability across different measurement scales and to mitigate unit‐dependence, standardized (z‐score) versions of continuous predictors were also examined (Table S4). Given the unexpected direction of E/e′ differences, we provide descriptive subgroup summaries of E/e′ within the OSAS cohort stratified by obesity and hypertension (Table S5). For these subgroup comparisons, exploratory unadjusted two‐sided Mann–Whitney U p values were additionally reported in Table S5.

A two‐sided p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant for group comparisons and univariable analyses; for penalized multivariable models, emphasis was placed on internally validated discrimination rather than p values. Analyses were performed using SPSS version 23 and R software.

Results

Table 1 summarizes the demographic, biochemical, and echocardiographic characteristics of the OSAS and control groups. There were no statistically significant differences between the groups in terms of mean age or sex distribution. Similarly, the prevalence of hypertension, T2DM, hyperlipidemia, CAD, and smoking status did not differ significantly between the two groups (all p > 0.05). BMI and BSA were significantly higher in the OSAS group compared to controls (both p < 0.001). Among laboratory variables, only CRP levels were significantly elevated in the OSAS group (p < 0.05), while all other biochemical and hemogram parameters were similar between the groups. According to polysomnographic findings, OSAS severity was classified as mild in 11 patients (17.5%), moderate in 28 (44.4%), and severe in 24 (38.1%). There were no significant differences in OSAS severity distribution by sex (p = 0.827).

Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve Analysis

Figure 3 shows ROC curves illustrating the apparent diagnostic performance of ePLAR and EAT in discriminating OSAS patients from PSG‐normal controls. In this comparison, ePLAR demonstrated excellent discrimination (AUC = 0.97), whereas EAT showed moderate discrimination (AUC = 0.78). ROC analysis was also performed to evaluate discrimination for severe OSAS within the OSAS cohort. The AUC for ePLAR was 0.937 (95% CI: 0.87–1.00); the optimal cut‐off value was 0.401, yielding 75% sensitivity and 100% specificity for severe OSAS. For EAT, the AUC was 0.939 (95% CI: 0.88–1.00), with an optimal cut‐off of 0.620 (sensitivity 79%, specificity 92%). A combined logistic‐regression–derived score incorporating both ePLAR and EAT showed the highest apparent discrimination (AUC = 0.949; 95% CI: 0.89–1.00) with an optimal cut‐off of 0.375 (sensitivity 83%, specificity 92%) (Table 3, Figure 4). These ROC results represent apparent (non‐validated) discrimination within the study sample; internally validated discrimination of multivariable prediction models is reported separately using bootstrap optimism correction (Table S1).

Multivariable Analysis for Severe OSAS

To explore factors associated with severe OSAS within the OSAS cohort, we fitted penalized multivariable logistic regression models (Table S1). Given the limited sample size and evidence of quasi‐separation in marker distributions, models were estimated using Firth penalized logistic regression, and discrimination was evaluated using bootstrap optimism‐corrected AUC (1000 resamples). The clinical model including age, sex, and BMI demonstrated modest discrimination (AUC = 0.65; 95% CI 0.50–0.79). Adding ePLAR improved discrimination (AUC = 0.72; 95% CI 0.59–0.85), and adding EAT provided further improvement (AUC = 0.78; 95% CI 0.65–0.88). The full model including both ePLAR and EAT achieved the highest discrimination (AUC = 0.83; 95% CI 0.72–0.92). In additional prespecified Firth sensitivity analyses within the OSAS cohort (adjusted for age, sex, and BMI), we examined the contribution of the ePLAR components using a joint component model including TRVmax and E/e′ simultaneously, and separately adjusted models including TRVmax alone and E/e′ alone. We also fitted a separate adjusted model including ePLAR. In the joint component model, higher TRVmax and lower E/e′ were independently associated with severe OSAS. Results are presented in both clinical units and standardized (z‐score) form (Table S4). Because quasi‐separation can inflate effect estimates, odds ratios should be interpreted cautiously; therefore, internally validated discrimination metrics and ROC‐derived cut‐offs (Table 3, Figure 4) are emphasized for clinical interpretation. For the reduced model including EAT and ePLAR only, internal validation showed excellent discrimination (bootstrap optimism‐corrected AUC = 0.945; 95% CI 0.897–1.00). Calibration of this reduced model was acceptable on average (intercept −0.087), but the calibration slope was 0.677, suggesting some overfitting and indicating that predicted probabilities may be overly extreme; calibration results are provided in Table S2 and Figure S1.

Intraobserver Reproducibility

In a randomly selected subset (n = 35), intraobserver agreement was excellent for ePLAR (ICC = 0.986; bias 0.0001 m/s; 95% limits of agreement −0.030 to 0.031) and EAT thickness (ICC = 0.957; bias −0.009 cm; 95% limits of agreement −0.071 to 0.052) (Table S3).

Discussion

This cross‐sectional study demonstrated that both ePLAR and EAT thickness are significantly higher in patients with OSAS compared with controls and that their values rise progressively with increasing OSAS severity. Moreover, both markers showed good apparent discrimination for severe OSAS, and multivariable analyses indicated incremental value beyond standard clinical factors such as age, sex, and BMI (Table S1).

The rise of ePLAR in OSAS patients represents a novel finding. To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate ePLAR in a PSG‐confirmed OSAS cohort, thereby addressing an important knowledge gap. ePLAR, calculated as the ratio of peak tricuspid regurgitation velocity to the mitral E/e′ ratio, has been shown to correlate with invasive hemodynamic measurements and to differentiate pre‐capillary from post‐capillary PH in other clinical settings [5, 6]. In our cohort, ePLAR values were higher in OSAS than in controls and increased in parallel with OSAS severity, suggesting that OSAS severity may be associated with greater cardiopulmonary hemodynamic surrogates even before overt RV systolic dysfunction becomes evident. However, the interpretation of ePLAR in OSAS should be confirmed in future studies incorporating invasive hemodynamic assessment. An important interpretative point is that ePLAR is a composite Doppler ratio (TRVmax/E/e′), and therefore changes in either component can influence the overall value. In our cohort, TRVmax was higher, and E/e′ was lower in OSAS compared with controls; thus, ePLAR elevation should not be interpreted as a direct surrogate of pulmonary vascular disease in the absence of invasive validation. Component‐based Firth sensitivity analyses (including both a joint TRVmax+E/e′ model and separately adjusted TRVmax‐only and E/e′‐only models) suggested that higher TRVmax and lower E/e′ were independently associated with severe OSAS in the joint model (Table S4). Because TRVmax and E/e′ are correlated, these sensitivity analyses are interpreted primarily for directionality and component contribution rather than definitive risk quantification (Table S4). The strong association between ePLAR and OSAS severity is particularly relevant given the well‐established link between OSAS, PH, and RV dysfunction [3, 4]. Recurrent episodes of hypoxia and hypercapnia, together with large negative intrathoracic pressure swings, can induce pulmonary vasoconstriction and vascular remodeling, leading to increased RV afterload and, ultimately, PH [3, 4]. The stepwise increase in ePLAR with OSAS severity observed in our study supports the concept that this noninvasive composite index—integrating TRVmax (a Doppler‐derived marker related to pulmonary pressure) and E/e′ (a surrogate of left‐sided filling pressure)—may reflect differences in echocardiographic hemodynamic surrogates across OSAS severity. However, invasive hemodynamic validation is needed before ePLAR can be interpreted as a surrogate of pulmonary vascular disease or pulmonary hemodynamics in OSAS.

Our observation of increased EAT thickness in OSAS patients aligns with previous studies connecting OSAS to heightened epicardial adiposity [7, 8, 13, 14]. EAT is a metabolically active depot implicated in systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and cardiometabolic risk [15, 16]. As a visceral adipose tissue compartment, EAT contributes to local and systemic inflammation by releasing pro‐inflammatory cytokines and adipokines [15, 16]. Intermittent hypoxia and sleep fragmentation in OSAS may provoke inflammatory changes in adipose tissue and alter adipokine profiles [17], while the anatomical proximity of EAT to the coronary arteries and myocardium suggests that local paracrine effects could directly influence cardiovascular function [18]. In our study, EAT thickness not only differed between OSAS patients and controls but also increased stepwise from mild to severe OSAS, supporting its role as a marker of cardiometabolic and inflammatory burden in this population. Importantly, in penalized multivariable models, EAT provided incremental discrimination for severe OSAS beyond age, sex, and BMI (Table S1), suggesting that its association is not fully explained by anthropometric measures alone. However, in the typical OSAS setting with a high prevalence of overweight and obesity, the measurement and interpretation of EAT thickness may be less straightforward, as overall adiposity is increased and acquisition may be more dependent on imaging quality and operator technique. These practical considerations may partially explain why combining EAT with ePLAR provided only a modest incremental gain in discrimination in our cohort.

An intriguing finding of our study was that the mitral E/e′ ratio was lower in OSAS patients than in controls, which contrasts with some previous reports suggesting impaired left ventricular diastolic function in OSAS [19, 20]. Several explanations are possible. First, our cohort comprised relatively younger patients with fewer comorbidities, in whom diastolic function may still be preserved; thus, differences in age and clinical profile across studies may partly explain discrepant E/e′ findings. In addition, E/e′ is an imperfect surrogate of left‐sided filling pressure and may be influenced by loading conditions and measurement context. To contextualize this unexpected direction, we provide descriptive subgroup summaries of E/e′ within the OSAS cohort stratified by obesity (BMI ≥30 kg/m2) and hypertension status, demonstrating variability across clinically relevant strata (Table S5). Moreover, our exclusion criteria yielded a selected OSAS cohort, which may also contribute to differences from studies reporting more uniform LV diastolic impairment. Second, OSAS‐related increases in RV afterload and interventricular septal shift may modify left ventricular filling dynamics and E/e′ measurements in complex ways [21]. Negative intrathoracic pressure swings and hypoxia‐induced pulmonary vasoconstriction may increase RV afterload and potentially contribute to septal shift toward the left ventricle [3, 4, 21], thereby affecting left ventricular compliance and filling patterns and leading to unexpected variations in E/e′, particularly in earlier disease stages. Importantly, TRVmax was also significantly higher in OSAS than in controls (Table 1), supporting that the observed increase in ePLAR was not solely driven by lower E/e′ values. Nevertheless, because ePLAR is a composite index, future studies incorporating invasive hemodynamics are needed to clarify the relative contribution of ePLAR components (TRVmax and E/e′) in OSAS. Accordingly, we interpret E/e′ differences cautiously and view them as load‐dependent echocardiographic surrogates rather than definitive measures of LV filling pressure.

Despite higher echocardiography‐estimated SPAP, TAPSE values were overall preserved (within normal range) in the OSAS group, despite a small between‐group difference, suggesting that RV systolic function may remain compensated in the setting of early pulmonary hemodynamic stress. This observation underscores the potential value of detecting pulmonary hemodynamic changes before overt RV systolic dysfunction becomes evident. TAPSE is a commonly used echocardiographic measure of RV longitudinal systolic function and may remain preserved during the early phases of increased afterload [22]. In our cohort, the combination of elevated SPAP with preserved TAPSE (Table 1) is consistent with a compensated stage, although comprehensive RV assessment and longitudinal follow‐up are needed to confirm progression over time.

Beyond conventional RV systolic indices such as TAPSE, myocardial deformation imaging has emerged as a sensitive tool to detect subclinical ventricular dysfunction in OSAS. Both LV and RV global longitudinal strain have been reported to identify early functional impairment even when standard parameters are within normal ranges. Strain imaging was not systematically available in our study and was not part of the prespecified echocardiographic protocol; therefore, we could not evaluate its incremental value. Future prospective studies should integrate LV/RV strain with ePLAR and EAT to provide a more comprehensive echocardiographic characterization of OSAS‐related cardiopulmonary involvement.

Furthermore, although left atrial (LA) volume did not differ significantly between the OSAS and control groups, LAVi was lower in the OSAS group (Table 1). This finding may be explained, at least in part, by higher body surface area (BSA) in OSAS and the limitations of BSA‐based indexing in overweight and obese individuals. A large cohort study from Brazil reported that indexing LA volume to BSA in obese and overweight individuals can underestimate LA size [23]. In addition, OSAS‐related negative intrathoracic pressure swings and elevated right‐sided pressures could potentially influence cardiac chamber interaction and LA geometry, contributing to heterogeneous LA structural and functional findings across studies [24]. Taken together, our results suggest that LA changes in OSAS may not follow a uniform pattern and may be influenced by body size indexation and loading conditions.

From an interpretative standpoint, our multivariable analyses provide additional insights. A simple model including age, sex, and BMI had only modest ability to discriminate severe OSAS within the OSAS cohort. In contrast, univariable ROC analyses and the ROC‐based combined score showed good apparent discrimination (AUC ∼0.94–0.95), which should be interpreted cautiously given the risk of overestimation in small samples. In internally validated penalized multivariable models, discrimination improved from 0.65 to 0.83 after adding ePLAR and EAT (Table S1), supporting incremental value beyond conventional clinical parameters. When both markers were included in the same model, the incremental gain in AUC was small, and the independent contribution of each marker was attenuated, consistent with collinearity and the limited number of severe cases. Moreover, because patients with chronic lung disease or suspected/confirmed PH were excluded, generalizability to unselected OSAS populations is limited. In addition, because controls were PSG‐normal individuals rather than symptomatic sleep‐clinic comparators, the separation between groups and apparent discrimination may be overestimated. From a feasibility standpoint, ePLAR has a practical advantage because it is calculated from Doppler indices routinely obtained in standard echocardiographic examinations (TRVmax and mitral E/e′), without requiring additional dedicated measurements or prolonging the examination. In contrast, EAT thickness is not uniformly measured in routine echocardiography and may be more operator‐ and protocol‐dependent. Therefore, where resources or acquisition time are limited, ePLAR may represent a more readily obtainable echocardiographic index for research‐oriented phenotyping in OSAS; however, these results are hypothesis‐generating and require external validation before any clinical use. Overall, these findings suggest that jointly considering Doppler indices related to pulmonary hemodynamics (ePLAR) and epicardial adiposity (EAT) may help characterize cardiopulmonary phenotypes in OSAS, although external validation is required.

Logistic regression models produced extremely large odds ratios with very wide confidence intervals for ePLAR and EAT. This is most likely due to quasi‐separation, whereby a limited number of severe cases and a clear separation of marker values between severe and non‐severe OSAS lead to numerically unstable coefficient estimates. In this context, the very large odds ratios should be viewed as qualitative evidence of a strong association rather than precise quantitative risk measures. Therefore, from a practical standpoint, apparent ROC‐derived cut‐off values and discrimination metrics (AUC), together with internally validated model performance, may be more interpretable for the clinical context than exact odds ratios in this dataset.

Our findings have several potential implications for future research and clinical phenotyping in OSAS. First, ePLAR and EAT were associated with OSAS severity in this cohort, suggesting that routinely derived Doppler indices (ePLAR) and measures of epicardial adiposity (EAT) may capture complementary echocardiographic correlates of disease severity, even when TAPSE and conventional diastolic parameters are within normal ranges. Second, although our study was cross‐sectional, these markers merit prospective evaluation as candidate noninvasive indices for longitudinal assessment of echocardiographic surrogates and cardiometabolic/inflammatory burden in response to therapy. This may be particularly relevant for continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) treatment, which has been associated with improvements in cardiopulmonary hemodynamics and inflammatory profiles [17]. Third, while combining ePLAR and EAT improved discrimination in our dataset, external validation and (for hemodynamic interpretation) invasive confirmation are required before any clinical use.

Limitations of the Study

This study has several limitations. First, the cross‐sectional design precludes causal inference regarding the relationship between OSAS and the observed differences in ePLAR and EAT. Prospective studies are needed to determine whether these echocardiographic markers predict longitudinal changes in cardiopulmonary status and clinical outcomes, and to validate their hemodynamic interpretation against invasive measurements. Second, this was a single‐center study with a modest sample size, particularly in the severe OSAS subgroup, which limits generalizability. In addition, the prevalence of severe OSAS in our cohort (38%) may contribute to optimistic estimates of apparent discrimination and limits direct extrapolation to lower‐prevalence clinical settings. The limited number of severe cases and clear separation of marker values led to quasi‐separation and numerically unstable effect estimates, resulting in very large odds ratios with wide confidence intervals; thus, odds ratios should be interpreted as qualitative evidence of association rather than precise risk estimates. Although internal validation supported good discrimination, a calibration slope <1 suggested some overfitting and that predicted probabilities may be overly extreme at the individual level. Accordingly, the reduced model and proposed thresholds should be considered exploratory and require external validation and, if necessary, recalibration before clinical use (Table S2; Figure S1). To reduce confounding, we excluded chronic lung disease and suspected/confirmed PH. While this improves internal validity, it narrows applicability to real‐world OSAS populations with common cardiopulmonary comorbidity. Moreover, controls were PSG‐normal individuals rather than symptomatic sleep‐clinic comparators, which may accentuate case–control separation and overestimate apparent discrimination in real‐world referral populations. Exclusion criteria and the use of PSG‐normal controls may have shifted marker distributions and inflated apparent discrimination compared with symptomatic sleep‐clinic populations. In addition, diastolic indices such as E/e′ are load‐dependent surrogates and may not reliably reflect LV filling pressures across all OSAS phenotypes; therefore, our diastolic findings should be interpreted cautiously.

Third, while all participants underwent overnight PSG and controls had AHI <5 events/hour, a single‐night PSG may not fully capture night‐to‐night variability; therefore, misclassification of very mild or positional OSAS cannot be completely excluded.

Fourth, invasive hemodynamic data were not available to directly validate ePLAR against measured pulmonary pressures, and we did not collect post‐treatment follow‐up data (e.g., after CPAP). Thus, we could not assess reversibility of ePLAR or EAT changes or their responsiveness to therapy.

Additionally, EAT thickness measurement is not universally incorporated into routine echocardiography, and its acquisition and interpretability may be more challenging in obese individuals—who are common in OSAS—potentially limiting generalizability across laboratories.

Finally, interobserver variability was not assessed. However, intraobserver reproducibility for ePLAR and EAT was excellent in a randomly selected subset (Table S3).

Conclusions

ePLAR and EAT thickness were higher in OSAS than in PSG‐normal controls and increased stepwise with OSAS severity. These noninvasive echocardiographic findings indicate an association between ePLAR/EAT and OSAS severity and support further prospective validation of these measures as candidate echocardiographic markers in OSAS. Because ePLAR is derived from routinely acquired Doppler parameters, it may be more readily obtainable in standard echocardiographic examinations than dedicated EAT thickness measurements. Given the cross‐sectional design, single‐center setting, limited sample size, and lack of invasive hemodynamic validation and the absence of interobserver reproducibility assessment, these findings should be considered hypothesis‐generating and require confirmation in larger prospective multicenter studies with external validation.
