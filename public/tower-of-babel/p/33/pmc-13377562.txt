Synergistic adsorption–oxidation of phenol by a magnetically recoverable LNC/MMT-stabilized NZVI composite
Authors: Zhao Baiyun, Meng Ling, Zhang Bo, Wang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Phenolic wastewater remains a critical environmental concern owing to its high toxicity and resistance to natural attenuation. To overcome the inherent drawbacks of bare nano-zero-valent iron (NZVI)—namely severe agglomeration and rapid surface passivation—we designed a novel ternary composite (LNC/MMT@NZVI) by immobilizing NZVI particles onto a lignocellulose/montmorillonite (LNC/MMT) binary support via liquid-phase reduction. This strategy achieved excellent NZVI dispersion and generated a mesoporous architecture with markedly enhanced thermal stability, as confirmed by N2 adsorption–desorption, XRD, FTIR, SEM-EDS, TEM, TG-DSC, and VSM analyses. The composite exhibited an outstanding phenol adsorption capacity of 127.58 mg g−1 under optimized conditions (pH 8, 50 °C, 240 min). Kinetic and isotherm studies revealed that phenol removal follows the pseudo-second-order model (R2 = 0.9857) and Langmuir isotherm (R2 = 0.9999), indicating a chemisorption-dominated monolayer process. XPS and EPR spectroscopic evidence unambiguously demonstrated that the embedded Fe0 initiates a sustained Fenton-like redox cycle, generating hydroxyl radicals (·OH) as the predominant reactive species, while the LNC/MMT support concurrently enriches phenol molecules near the active sites via hydrogen bonding and surface complexation. This synergistic integration of adsorptive enrichment and catalytic oxidation not only overcomes the intrinsic limitations of standalone NZVI but also provides a highly efficient, magnetically recoverable platform for treating recalcitrant organic wastewater.

Introduction

Industrial wastewater containing refractory organic pollutants, such as phenols, poses a severe threat to environmental and human health due to its toxicity and persistence.1–3 The development of efficient and sustainable treatment technologies is therefore imperative.4,5 Among various methods, adsorption is widely adopted for its simplicity and effectiveness,6 while nano-zero-valent iron (NZVI) has emerged as a powerful reactive material for the degradation of both common and emergent pollutants via reduction or Fenton-like oxidation.7,8 However, the practical application of bare NZVI is hindered by its tendency to agglomerate and undergo rapid surface passivation, which drastically reduces its available active sites and long-term reactivity.

The redox potential of iron-based materials has been well demonstrated even at larger scales. Pioneering studies using standalone micro- and nanoscale zero-valent iron (ZVI) particles have proven their effectiveness in eliminating various pharmaceuticals from water. For instance, Ghauch et al. reported the rapid removal of the fungicide flutriafol by ZVI powder,9 the efficient elimination of amoxicillin and ampicillin by microscale and nanoscale iron particles,10 and the aqueous removal of diclofenac using plated elemental iron in bimetallic systems.11 These works highlighted the powerful reductive capability of iron particles but also implicitly underscored the necessity of supportive matrices to prevent aggregation and maintain reactivity as particle sizes decrease.

Although the use of support materials can improve the dispersion efficiency of NZVI, a critical evaluation reveals that many conventional supports (e.g., silica, activated carbon) function primarily as passive scaffolds, offering limited enhancement beyond physical separation.12,13 These inert hosts do not actively participate in the pollutant removal process, and the resulting composites often fail to achieve a truly synergistic effect. Consequently, there is a growing need for “smart” supports that not only stabilize NZVI but also actively contribute to contaminant sequestration through complementary mechanisms, thereby creating an integrated synergistic system.

In this context, hybrid materials derived from natural and abundant resources present a compelling avenue. Lignocellulose (LNC), a renewable biopolymer rich in functional groups (e.g., –OH, –COOH), exhibits intrinsic affinity for organic contaminants through adsorption and complexation.14 Chen et al. demonstrated that LNC-based materials possess high specific surface area, porosity, and enhanced adsorption capacity for dyes.15 Montmorillonite (MMT), a low-cost aluminosilicate clay, possesses a high surface area, cation exchange capacity, and a stable layered structure ideal for hosting nanoparticles.16 Owing to its large specific surface area and high cation exchange capacity, MMT exhibits considerable adsorption performance and has been widely applied in wastewater treatment.17 We hypothesize that an LNC/MMT composite can serve as an ideal multifunctional host for NZVI. Unlike simple single-component supports, this binary matrix is designed with a clear functional division: the LNC component provides abundant adsorption sites, while the MMT layers offer confined spaces to limit NZVI particle growth and aggregation. More importantly, this hybrid matrix is designed to work synergistically with NZVI: it first concentrates phenol molecules near the reactive iron sites via adsorption, thereby promoting subsequent surface-mediated oxidative degradation.

Despite the existence of studies on clay- or biomass-supported NZVI, most of them treat the support as a mere dispersant, and a systematic design that deliberately integrates the superior adsorption capacity of LNC/MMT with the redox activity of NZVI into a unified, synergistic platform has not been reported. Furthermore, a clear mechanistic understanding distinguishing the contributions of adsorption, surface complexation, and catalytic oxidation within such a ternary system is often lacking.

To address these gaps, this study reports the rational design and synthesis of a novel lignocellulose/montmorillonite-supported NZVI composite (LNC/MMT@NZVI). The specific objectives are to: (1) synthesize and optimize the composite via a facile liquid-phase reduction method; (2) comprehensively characterize its physicochemical and structural properties; (3) evaluate its performance for phenol removal under varying conditions; (4) analyze the adsorption kinetics and isotherms; and (5) elucidate the synergistic removal mechanism by correlating spectroscopic evidence with performance data. This work aims to demonstrate that strategic material design can yield a highly effective composite that overcomes the limitations of standalone NZVI, while providing new insights into cooperative pollutant-removal mechanisms in complex hybrid systems.

Experimental

Instruments and reagents

Analytical-grade reagents and deionized water were employed to prepare all solutions. The specific reagents and instruments used are detailed in Tables S1 and S2.

Preparation of LNC/MMT

The synthesis process of LNC/MMT is shown in Fig. S1. Initially, LNC flocs were pulverized and passed through a 200-mesh sieve to obtain LNC powder, which was then stored in a sealed bag for further use. A NaOH solution (120 mL, 15 wt%) was prepared, into which 4 g of LNC powder was added. The mixture was magnetically stirred at room temperature for 30 min to form a homogeneous LNC suspension. Separately, an MMT suspension was prepared by dispersing 8 g of MMT in 240 mL of deionized water. The LNC suspension and the MMT suspension were slowly introduced into a three-necked flask and allowed to react under continuous electric stirring at 70 °C for 4 h. After the reaction, the product was thoroughly washed with distilled water until neutrality was achieved, followed by vacuum filtration. The collected solid was dried at 105 °C for 4 h, ground, and sieved through a 200-mesh sieve to obtain the final LNC/MMT, which was then packed in sealed bags for subsequent use.

Preparation of LNC/MMT@NZVI

The LNC/MMT@NZVI was synthesized via a liquid-phase reduction method (Fig. 1). Firstly, 9.66 g of FeCl3·6H2O and 2.0 g of LNC/MMT were added to a three-necked flask containing a solvent mixture of 10 mL deionized water and 40 mL absolute ethanol. The suspension was homogenized under continuous electric stirring for 30 minutes at room temperature. Subsequently, 100 mL of a freshly prepared 2.0 M aqueous NaBH4 solution was added dropwise into the flask under vigorous stirring to reduce Fe3+ to Fe0. Throughout the reduction process, a continuous flow of N2 gas was maintained to provide an inert atmosphere, minimizing the oxidation of the as-formed nanoparticles. After the reduction was complete, the solid product was separated from the liquid phase by vacuum filtration. The collected solid was thoroughly washed with absolute ethanol to remove residual salts and by-products, followed by drying at 50 °C for 6 h under ambient pressure to yield the final LNC/MMT@NZVI.

Adsorption experiments

The adsorption performance of LNC/MMT@NZVI toward phenol was evaluated through batch experiments. In a typical procedure, 50 mL of a phenol solution (60 mg L−1) was transferred into a 100 mL conical flask. The initial pH of the solution was adjusted to 8.0. Subsequently, 80 mg of LNC/MMT@NZVI was added to the flask, which was then placed in a thermostatic water bath shaker. The mixture was shaken at 120 rpm and 50 °C for 3 h to reach adsorption equilibrium. After the reaction, the solid was separated from the liquid using a 0.22 µm organic microporous filter. The filtrate was collected, and the residual phenol concentration was determined at a wavelength of 270 nm using a UV-visible spectrophotometer. All experiments were conducted in triplicate, and the average values were reported. Results and discussion

Preparation mechanism

The synthesis of LNC/MMT@NZVI involves two concurrent reduction processes mediated by sodium borohydride. First, –CHO present on the LNC/MMT support is reduced to –CH2OH, thereby increasing the overall content of active hydroxyl moieties within the composite (reaction (2)). Simultaneously, Fe3+ are reduced to Fe0, which nucleates and forms nanoparticles on the support. This step is accompanied by the generation of additional surface functional groups, including –COOH and –OH, further enhancing the chemical activity of the final LNC/MMT@NZVI (reaction (3)).22R-CHO + 3BH4− + 9H2O → 2R-CH2OH + 3H2BO3− + 10H2↑34Fe3+ + 3BH4− + 9H2O → 4Fe0↓ + 3H2BO3− + 12H+ + 6H2↑where, R = cellulose, hemicellulose or lignin.

The adsorption capacity exhibited a distinct trend, initially increasing and then decreasing as the mass ratio of Fe3+ to BH4− was varied (Fig. 2a). The optimal adsorption capacity of 123.60 mg g−1 was achieved at a mass ratio of 1 : 2. This trend can be attributed to the completeness of the reduction reaction. At lower BH4− concentrations (ratio <1 : 2), the insufficient reducing agent failed to completely reduce all Fe3+, resulting in a lower actual content of NZVI within the LNC/MMT@NZVI. The presence of unreduced Fe3+ species and the exposed LNC/MMT support likely compromised the overall adsorption efficacy. Conversely, when the BH4− concentration was in excess (ratio >1 : 2), the rapid reduction process promoted the formation of NZVI nanoparticles. This accelerated formation can lead to severe agglomeration and poor dispersion of the NZVI particles on the support, ultimately diminishing the available active surface area and, consequently, the adsorption capacity.

Effects of the mass ratio of Fe3+ to BH4− (a) and the mass ratio of NZVI to LNC/MMT (b) on phenol adsorption onto LNC/MMT@NZVI.

A similar non-monotonic relationship was observed between the adsorption capacity and the mass ratio of NZVI to LNC/MMT (Fig. 2b). In this composite system, LNC/MMT acts as a dispersive support to mitigate NZVI aggregation. When the proportion of LNC/MMT is too high (i.e., a low NZVI ratio), an excessive amount of the support material may physically encapsulate the NZVI particles, thereby shielding them and hindering direct contact between the active Fe0 sites and the phenol molecules in solution. On the other hand, an excessively high loading of NZVI (i.e., a high NZVI ratio) overwhelms the dispersive capacity of the LNC/MMT support. This leads to inevitable nanoparticle agglomeration, which reduces the effective surface area and results in a decrease in adsorption capacity, despite the higher nominal iron content.

Characterizations

The N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms of LNC/MMT, NZVI, and LNC/MMT@NZVI (Fig. 3a and b) exhibited type-IV curves with H3-type hysteresis loops, characteristic of mesoporous materials with slit-shaped pores.19 The pore size distribution concentrated near 5 nm (Fig. 3c), indicating the prevalence of mesopores. Notably, compared to pristine LNC/MMT, the composite displayed a significant increase in average pore diameter (from 16.29 to 31.83 nm) and pore volume (from 0.0507 to 0.0706 cm3 g−1), alongside a reduction in specific surface area (from 12.442 to 8.875 m2 g−1) (Table 1). This contradictory phenomenon (larger pores but lower specific surface area) is compelling evidence that NZVI nanoparticles were preferentially intercalated into the interlayers of MMT, acting as “pillars” that expanded the layer spacing. Concurrently, the partial coverage of the external surface by NZVI aggregates also accounts for the decreased specific surface area. This expanded mesoporous framework is highly advantageous for facilitating intraparticle diffusion of phenol molecules, thereby enhancing the accessibility of internal active sites.

Compared with pristine NZVI, the bands at 1640, 941 and 829 cm−1 were enhanced in the LNC/MMT@NZVI, which can be ascribed to the formation of alcohol hydroxyl groups during the redox reaction. The intensified absorption near 464 cm−1 suggests the partial substitution of Al3+ by Fe3+ in the silicate layers, leading to the formation of Si–O–Fe bonds.21 Among the samples synthesized with different Fe3+/BH4− ratios, LNC/MMT@NZVI III (1 : 2) displayed the most intense peaks at 3354 and 464 cm−1, corresponding to –OH and Si–O–Fe stretching vibrations, respectively. Moreover, the CO peak at 1695 cm−1 shifted to a lower wavenumber (1660 cm−1) in the composite, owing to the formation of intermolecular hydrogen bonds between CO and the –OH groups of LNC/MMT. These observations collectively confirm that a redox reaction took place during the preparation, resulting in the successful loading of NZVI onto the LNC/MMT support.

The XRD patterns (Fig. 4b) of both NZVI and LNC/MMT@NZVI exhibited characteristic peaks of highly crystalline Fe0 at the (110)22 and (112)23 planes.20 The persistence of these peaks in the composite confirms that the reductive capability of NZVI was retained post-synthesis. However, the presence of secondary peaks corresponding to Fe2O3 [(012),19 (104) and (110)20,24 ] and Fe3O4 [(220), (311),25 and (400)] suggests the inevitable surface oxidation of Fe0, forming a typical core–shell structure (Fe0@FeOx).26 This thin oxide shell is not entirely detrimental; it serves as a passivation layer that prevents immediate, catastrophic corrosion while allowing for sustained electron release via Fe2+/Fe3+ redox cycling, as further substantiated by XPS analysis.

SEM analysis (Fig. 4c and d) elucidates the pivotal role of LNC/MMT in preventing the aggregation of NZVI particles. As observed in Fig. 4c, pristine NZVI exhibits significant agglomeration, forming dense, core–shell-like structures attributable to magnetic interactions and high surface energy. In contrast, after incorporation via mechanical stirring, the NZVI particles are effectively dispersed across the surface and within the interlayers of the porous LNC/MMT matrix (Fig. 4d). This homogeneous distribution yields the LNC/MMT@NZVI composite with a notably rougher texture and enhanced porosity compared to NZVI alone. The improved dispersion directly increases the accessibility of active sites, which accounts for the composite's superior adsorption performance toward phenol.

This homogeneous distribution was corroborated by TEM (Fig. 4e), which visualized spherical NZVI nanoparticles (∼20–50 nm) anchored firmly onto the sheet-like MMT layers. EDS parameters (Table 2) further confirmed the successful integration, as evidenced by the concurrent presence of Fe (43.87%), Si (11.20%), and C (10.72%) in the composite, alongside a dramatic decrease in pure Fe content relative to pristine NZVI. The presence of Na, Ca, and Si—characteristic elements of the LNC/MMT matrix—along with a significant decrease in Fe content and a notable increase in C content, confirms the successful incorporation of NZVI into the LNC/MMT support.

To elucidate the synergistic removal mechanism,24 XPS and EPR were employed to probe the surface chemical transformations (Fig. 5). The survey spectra (Fig. 5a) confirmed the presence of Fe, O, C, and Na in the composite. High-resolution O1s spectra (Fig. 5b) revealed a shift from 530.68 eV (ref. 27) to 531.48 eV after phenol adsorption, indicating the involvement of oxygen-containing moieties (e.g., CO, Fe–O) in surface complexation or hydrogen bonding with phenol. The Na 1s peak weakened slightly and shifted from 1071.08 eV to 1071.28 eV (Fig. 5c). The C 1s peak at approximately 285 eV (Fig. 5e), attributed to CC bonds,28 showed no pronounced change, indicating that the CC structure did not participate directly in the redox process. Together, these observations imply that oxygen-containing functional groups (e.g., CO) play a critical role in phenol removal, whereas the carbon framework remains largely inert during the reaction.

XPS spectra (a–e) of LNC/MMT@NZVI before and after phenol degradation, and EPR spectrum (f) using DMPO as spin-trapping agent for free radical detection.

Crucially, the Fe 2p spectra (Fig. 5d) provided direct evidence of the redox mechanism. For the fresh LNC/MMT@NZVI, peaks at 706.0 eV (Fe 2p3/2), 710.1 eV (FeO/Fe2+), and 719.5 eV (Fe2O3/Fe3+)27 were identified. Upon phenol degradation, the Fe0 signal nearly vanished, while the Fe2+ peak shifted to 710.8 eV and the Fe3+ peak decreased in intensity. This spectroscopic evolution substantiates that the sacrificial oxidation of Fe0 to Fe2+ initiated the Fenton-like reaction, and the concurrent consumption of Fe3+ suggests its involvement in the catalytic cycle.

Furthermore, EPR spectroscopy using DMPO as a spin-trapping agent (Fig. 5f) unambiguously identified the characteristic 1 : 2 : 2 : 1 quartet signals, assignable to the DMPO–·OH adduct. The emergence of these distinct peaks confirms that ·OH are the predominant reactive oxygen species responsible for the oxidative mineralization of phenol. Additionally, the adsorption and enrichment effect of the composite ensures an adequate supply of phenolic substrates around the iron active sites, thereby efficiently scavenging free radicals and facilitating the smooth operation of the Fe0/Fe2+/Fe3+ redox cycle, as illustrated in possible redox pathways (eqn (4)–(7)).4Fe0 → Fe(OH)2 → Fe2O3 → (γ-FeO)52Fe0 + 4H+ + O2 → 2Fe2+ + 2H2O672Fe3+ + 6OH− → 2Fe(OH)3

The thermal stability of the materials was evaluated by TG and DSC.27 The TG data (Table 3) and corresponding thermograms of LNC/MMT@NZVI (Fig. 6a, c–e) indicate a three-stage decomposition process. The first stage, occurring between 25 °C and 179 °C, is characterized by a mass loss of 2.81%, primarily attributed to the evaporation of physically adsorbed water. This value is lower than the 5.61% loss observed for pristine LNC/MMT in the same temperature range. The second stage, from 179 °C to 679 °C, involves a mass loss of 4.05%. This stage corresponds to the cleavage of hydrogen bonds and surface hydroxyl groups, leading to the release of water, coupled with the carbonization and pyrolysis of LNC/MMT components which volatilize organic matter. The reduced mass loss in this region, compared to the individual components, may be ascribed to enhanced stability resulting from electrostatic interactions between NZVI and LNC/MMT formed during composite synthesis. The final stage, between 679 °C and 800 °C, shows a minimal further mass loss of only 0.26%. The composite ultimately achieves a high residual mass (char yield) of 92.88%, which stabilizes by the end of the analysis. Notably, this residual mass is significantly higher than that of LNC/MMT alone (75.95%), demonstrating the superior thermal stability of the LNC/MMT@NZVI composite.

The effects of reaction conditions on the performance of LNC/MMT@NZVI treatment of phenol wastewater

The initial pH of the solution is a critical parameter influencing both the surface charge of the adsorbent and the speciation of phenol, thereby governing adsorption performance. As shown in Fig. 8a, the isoelectric point of LNC/MMT@NZVI is determined to be 8.89. Fig. 8b reveals that the maximum adsorption capacity for phenol (128.35 mg g−1) is achieved at an initial pH of 8. When the solution pH is below the isoelectric point, the surface of LNC/MMT@NZVI is positively charged, however, because phenol exists predominantly as neutral molecules, electrostatic attraction does not play a major role in adsorption under these conditions. Moreover, under strongly acidic conditions (low pH), the high concentration of H+ competes with phenol for adsorption sites, thereby limiting uptake, and the LNC/MMT@NZVI composite may undergo corrosion or react with water, compromising its structural stability.

(a) Isoelectric point of LNC/MMT@NZVI; effects of (b) pH, (c) initial phenol concentration, (d) adsorbent dosage, (e) reaction time, and (f) reaction temperature on phenol adsorption by LNC/MMT@NZVI.

As the pH increases toward the isoelectric point, the surface charge becomes less positive, which reduces H+ competition and enhances phenol adsorption through hydrogen bonding, coordination, or other non-electrostatic interactions. At pH 8, just below the isoelectric point, the adsorbent surface is only weakly charged, optimizing phenol adsorption affinity. At pH values above the isoelectric point, the surface of LNC/MMT@NZVI acquires a negative charge. While this could lead to electrostatic repulsion against phenol molecules in their anionic form, the formation of iron hydroxide or iron carbonate passivation layers on the particle surface also inhibits direct contact between phenol and active sites. Additionally, elevated OH− concentrations under alkaline conditions can suppress redox reactions and reduce the effectiveness of phenol adsorption onto LNC/MMT@NZVI. Thus, the adsorption performance is a result of combined electrostatic interactions, surface speciation, competitive ion effects, and material stability across the pH range.

Fig. 8c shows that the adsorption capacity of phenol onto LNC/MMT@NZVI initially increased with rising phenol concentration, reaching a maximum at 700 mg L−1, beyond which it gradually declined. The initial enhancement can be attributed to the greater mass transfer driving force at higher concentrations, which facilitates the diffusion of phenol molecules toward the abundant active sites on the adsorbent surface. However, at excessively high phenol concentrations, the adsorption capacity decreases. This decline likely results from intensified competition among phenol molecules for the limited active sites, possible desorption of weakly bound phenol, or a reduction in effective adsorbent utilization once the surface approaches saturation.

Fig. 8d shows that the phenol adsorption capacity initially increased with LNC/MMT@NZVI dosage, attaining a maximum value at 80 mg, beyond which a gradual decrease was observed. This trend is attributed to the increased availability of active sites and enhanced phenol-adsorbent contact at lower dosages, promoting adsorption until the system approaches saturation. At higher dosages, however, the adsorption capacity per unit mass declines, consistent with the relationship defined in eqn (1). This decline can be ascribed to several factors, including aggregation of adsorbent particles, reduction in effective surface area, and the excess of adsorption sites relative to the remaining phenol concentration, which collectively lower the utilization efficiency of the adsorbent.

Fig. 8e shows that the phenol adsorption capacity of LNC/MMT@NZVI initially increased with reaction time, reaching a maximum at 240 min, and then gradually declined upon further exposure. During the initial stage, the abundant available adsorption sites on both the surface and within the pores of LNC/MMT@NZVI, coupled with the reductive activity of NZVI, contributed to a rapid increase in phenol uptake. The subsequent decline more plausibly arises from the partial desorption of phenol or its reaction intermediates back into the solution under prolonged agitation, together with gradual surface passivation or structural alteration of the adsorbent that reduces the number or accessibility of effective sites.

Fig. 8f shows that the adsorption capacity exhibited a non-monotonic dependence on temperature, increasing gradually to a maximum at 50 °C, beyond which a decline was observed. The initial enhancement in adsorption with rising temperature can be attributed to the accelerated thermal motion of molecules, which facilitates the adsorption and redox reactions. However, when the temperature exceeds 50 °C, the elevated thermal energy likely intensifies molecular motion to the extent that desorption of phenol molecules from the adsorbent surface becomes more favorable, thereby reducing the overall adsorption capacity. This pattern suggests that the adsorption process is governed by a balance between kinetic facilitation at moderate temperatures and thermodynamic limitations at higher temperatures.

Adsorption mechanism

The adsorption kinetics was investigated to determine the adsorption rate. Experiments were conducted at 50 °C using six parallel samples. The initial concentration of phenol was set to 700 mg L−1 with shaking at 120 rpm. Samples were collected at different time intervals, filtered, and the supernatant was analyzed. The experimental data were fitted with two widely used kinetic models: the pseudo-first-order kinetic model (eqn (8))30 and the pseudo-second-order kinetic model (eqn (9)).3189where, the adsorption capacity of the adsorption equilibrium and at time t are denoted by qe (mg g−1) and qt (mg g−1), respectively. The pseudo-first-order rate constant and pseudo-second-order rate constants are denoted by k1, and k2, respectively.

The kinetic analysis results are summarized in Fig. 9a and b and Table 4. Although the pseudo-first-order model presents a slightly higher correlation coefficient (R2 = 0.9966) than the pseudo-second-order model (R2 = 0.9857), the equilibrium adsorption capacity predicted by the pseudo-second-order model (qe = 118.77 mg g−1) is much closer to the experimental value (127.58 mg g−1) than that of the pseudo-first-order model (277.47 mg g−1). This indicates that the pseudo-second-order model better describes the overall adsorption process, suggesting that chemisorption, likely involving redox reactions as evidenced by XPS, is the rate-limiting step. Meanwhile, the high R2 of the pseudo-first-order model implies that physical adsorption and intraparticle diffusion also contribute to phenol uptake, which is consistent with the porous structure of the composite.

The LNC/MMT@NZVI ternary composite features a core–shell structure (crystalline Fe0 core and amorphous iron oxide shell) and is synthesized via liquid-phase reduction, which immobilizes NZVI particles within the layered support. Conclusion

In this study, the LNC/MMT@NZVI composite was successfully synthesized via a facile liquid-phase reduction method and systematically characterized for its structural, thermal, and magnetic properties. The results demonstrate that the LNC/MMT binary support plays a dual role: it serves as a dispersive scaffold that effectively prevents NZVI agglomeration and simultaneously acts as an adsorptive component that concentrates phenol molecules through hydrogen bonding and surface complexation. The embedded NZVI particles retain their reductive capacity, as evidenced by XRD and XPS, and drive a sustained Fenton-like redox cycle (Fe0 → Fe2+ → Fe3+) that generates ·OH radicals—the primary oxidants responsible for phenol mineralization. The adsorption process is well described by the pseudo-second-order kinetic model and the Langmuir isotherm, confirming that monolayer chemisorption is the rate-controlling step. The composite's mesoporous structure (average pore diameter: 31.83 nm) and high thermal stability further contribute to its superior performance, while its soft ferromagnetic behavior (saturation magnetization: 12.87 emu g−1) enables convenient magnetic separation and reusability. Collectively, the LNC/MMT@NZVI composite integrates adsorptive enrichment and oxidative degradation into a synergistic system, effectively addressing the critical limitations of bare NZVI and offering a promising, sustainable solution for the efficient treatment of phenolic wastewater and related refractory organic pollutants. Future work may focus on long-term stability assessment, continuous-flow reactor performance, and scale-up feasibility for practical industrial applications.
