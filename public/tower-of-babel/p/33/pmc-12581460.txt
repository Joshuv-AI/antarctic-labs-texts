ABSTRACT
BackgroundNigeria is experiencing a growing burden of diet-related non-communicable diseases, particularly hypertension and cardiovascular disease, largely driven by high sodium intake and poor diet quality. Unhealthy dietary patterns characterized by excessive sodium, sugars, and unhealthy fats, and low intake of essential nutrients contribute to approximately 31% of global deaths annually. However, there is limited data on the sodium content and nutritional quality of foods in Nigerian markets. This study assessed and compared the availability, healthiness, and sodium content of packaged and unpackaged foods in Nigerian markets.MethodsFrom August 2022 to December 2022, we collected and compared nutrition data on packaged and unpackaged foods from 35 outlets across three states in Nigeria. Data collection utilized the Food Switch Data Collector App. Healthiness was assessed using the Health Star Rating (HSR) system, which rates products from 0.5 (least healthy) to 5 stars (most healthy) based on their nutritional profile. Statistical analysis included descriptive statistics for packaged/unpackaged foods, calculating mean health star rating values and comparing them using Welch’s t-test, and analyzing sodium content using descriptive statistics and Mann-Whitney U tests.ResultsPackaged foods and beverages (n = 6636) were more available than unpackaged foods (n = 507). Overall, unpackaged foods had a higher mean HSR than packaged foods (4.6 vs. 3.4, p < 0.001). Packaged meat and meat alternatives had significantly higher sodium (760.0 mg/100 g, IQR 560.0) compared to unpackaged (74.0 mg/100 g, IQR 45.0) (p < 0.001).ConclusionsPackaged foods and beverages in Nigeria were less healthy and contained higher sodium levels than unpackaged foods. Background

Unhealthy dietary patterns, characterized by the consumption of foods that are high in sodium, sugars, saturated, trans fats, and equally low in essential nutrients including vitamins, minerals, and dietary fiber, are associated with an increased risk of hypertension and cardiovascular disease (CVD). CVD contributes to approximately 31% of all global deaths. This includes an estimated 11 million deaths linked to unhealthy diets each [1–3]. Unhealthy foods and beverages are widely available in open markets, supermarkets, convenience stores, and fast food outlets [4–6].

National estimates indicate that hypertension affects about 30% of Nigerian adults, with prevalence rates ranging from 29.5% to 38.1% [7, 8]. In sub-Saharan Africa, most sodium intake comes from discretionary salt, though contributions from processed and packaged foods are steadily increasing [9]. Globally, lower-income countries rely more on discretionary sources than on packaged products, largely due to the high availability and widespread use of salt in home cooking and seasoning [10].

In Nigeria, the primary contributors to sodium intake are bouillon cubes and table salt added during cooking and seasoning; however, sodium from packaged foods is on the rise due to their increasing availability in markets and retail outlets [11]. The wide availability of unhealthy food and beverage products in supply chains is a significant concern to global public health, with an increased incidence of diet-related diseases such as obesity, hypertension, CVDs, and diabetes [12–16]. The availability of food and beverage products is influenced by several factors, including consumer demand, market trends, affordability, advertising and marketing strategies, cultural and taste preferences, and very importantly, profitability [17–19]. In both high-income and low- and middle-income countries, unhealthy food products are prominently displayed in food retail stores and heavily marketed to consumers, incentivizing purchase and consumption, partly because unhealthy foods and beverages tend to be more affordable [20, 21].

In 2019, Nigeria introduced the National Multi-Sectoral Action Plan for the Prevention and Control of Non-Communicable Diseases (NCDs), incorporating a goal to decrease the average population intake of dietary sodium by 30% by 2025, aligning with the global target set by the World Health Organization (WHO) [22]. In addition to establishing sodium intake guidelines, WHO has identified cost-effective interventions with high potential for positive health impact, known as “best buys”, to reduce excess dietary sodium intake and prevent and control hypertension [23]. These Best-Buy interventions include sodium reformulation targets for processed and packaged foods, front-of-pack labelling, provision of low sodium options in public settings such as schools and hospitals, and behaviour change through mass media campaigns.

These recommendations align with the first National Multi-Sectoral Action Plan for the Prevention and Control of NCDs 2019–2025 in Nigeria, with targets and a roadmap to address the high rate of CVDs and associated risk factors through Nigeria’s Federal Ministry of Health (FMoH) in partnership with other key stakeholders including the National Agency for Food and Drug Administration and Control and Standards Organization of Nigeria [22]. The primary focus of the National Multi-Sectoral Action Plan is to implement evidence-informed strategies and interventions to prevent and control NCDs [22, 24]. However, there is limited data to design, implement, and evaluate the effectiveness of sodium reduction strategies in Nigeria. However, there is limited data to design, implement, and evaluate the effectiveness of sodium reduction strategies in Nigeria. While previous reports have primarily focused on packaged foods, comprehensive breakdowns of caloric and sodium contributions from packaged versus unpackaged foods remain limited [25]. Therefore, this study aimed to quantify and compare the availability, healthiness, and sodium content of packaged and unpackaged foods and beverages in Nigeria.

Methods

Setting

The Nigeria Sodium Study was conducted in the Federal Capital Territory, Abuja, Kano State and Ogun State in Nigeria. These locations provide a good representation of Nigeria, covering diverse regions including Abuja in the North-Central, Kano in the North-West, and Ogun in the South-West. Data on commercially available packaged food and beverage products were collected between August and December 2022 from 35 retail outlets located in the capital cities of each state. These included major retail stores such as high-traffic supermarkets and grocery chains with a large market share, known for stocking a wide variety of packaged food and beverage items. (i.e., Shoprite, Sahad Stores, Grandsquares, Justright Stores, SPAR, H-Medix, Jafatu Stores and Shopwell Stores). Data on commercially available unpackaged food products were collected between August and October 2022 at the largest open food markets in each study site: Karmo Market in Abuja, Dawanau Market in Kano, and Kuto Market in Ogun State. These markets were selected because they represent the primary retail setting for unpackaged foods, which are typically not available in formal retail outlets such as supermarkets. Unlike packaged products, unpackaged foods are rarely sold in prominent retail stores due to storage, display, and supply chain differences. Therefore, traditional open markets were more appropriate for capturing the availability and characteristics of unpackaged food items.

Packaged vs. unpackaged food and beverage products

In the context of Nigeria, the terms packaged foods and unpackaged foods and beverages refer to different types of food products based on their presentation and packaging. Packaged foods and beverages are those sold in pre-packaged, sealed containers or wrappers, and are typically processed with labels providing information on ingredients, nutritional value, and expiration dates. Examples include snacks like biscuits, chips, and cookies; beverages such as bottled water, soft or soda drinks, and fruit juices; canned goods like beans, tomatoes, and fish; and processed foods including instant noodles, breakfast cereals, and frozen items. These products offer convenience, a longer shelf life due to preservatives and packaging, and detailed information through labeling. In contrast, unpackaged foods are sold without packaging, often found in local markets or from street vendors, and are usually less processed. Examples include fresh produce like fruits and vegetables sold in bulk, staples such as rice, beans, and maize in large sacks, and local foods like freshly made bread, local snacks (such as puff-puff made from flour or akara made from beans), and cooked meals from street vendors, as well as non-alcoholic beverages like kunu drink, kunun gero, and lime juice. Each unique product was counted once, regardless of variations in packaging size, to avoid duplication and overestimation of product availability. Product uniqueness was determined based on brand name andproduct type and nutritional content per 100 g.

Data collection

Data were collected on smartphones using the FoodSwitch Data Collector App (DCA), a specialized digital tool designed to gather information on food and beverage products using a standardized protocol [26]. For packaged products, trained data collection officers scanned the barcode of each packaged food and beverage item available for sale in each store using the FoodSwitch DCA. Photographs of the front and back of the packaging were used to capture information about the product. For unpackaged food and beverage products, a database was also created using the FoodSwitch DCA to capture photos of all available products sold at the included markets. When nutrient values were unavailable in the Nigerian Food Composition Table or the West African Food Composition Table, data were supplemented using the Australian Food, Supplement and Nutrient Database (AUSNUT), particularly for basic food items with close equivalents. Although AUSNUT provides comprehensive data, its use may present a risk of mismatch due to regional formulation differences. Therefore, proxy values were used conservatively, and matching was done based on similar ingredients and preparation techniques. This limitation is acknowledged in the discussion. The data entry team matched photographs of unpackaged products with relevant nutrition information drawn from these databases (Table S1). To ensure data quality and reliability, trained field researchers were supervised throughout the data collection process. Supervisors reviewed data entries daily to identify and address inconsistencies or incomplete entries. Product images were cross-checked for clarity, and nutrition information was verified against packaging labels before upload.

Data categorization

The collected data were mapped to standardized food and beverage categories using the classification system established by the Global Food Monitoring Group. This ensured consistency and comparability across different products [25, 26]. The categorization system utilized in this study employed a hierarchical structure to group foods and beverages into 16 major and 89 minor subcategories. Major food categories included (a) bread and bakery products, (b) cereal and grain products, (c) confectionery, (d) convenience foods, (e) dairy, (f) edible oils and oil emulsions, (g) alcohol, (h) foods for specific dietary use, (i) fruits, vegetables, nuts and legumes, (j) meat and meat alternatives, (k) non-alcoholic beverages, (l) sauces, dressings, spreads, and dips, (m) seafood and seafood products, (n) snack foods, (o) sugars, honey and related products, and (p) vitamins and supplements [25].

Classification rationale for food categories

The categorization of foods in this study was guided by a combination of the following principles: Primary Mode of Processing and Form: Foods were grouped based on their dominant method of processing (e.g., baked, fried, fermented, raw), aligned with the INFORMAS and Global Food Monitoring Group food categorization frameworks; Ingredient Base: In cases where processing methods overlapped, the main ingredient (e.g., cereals, legumes, dairy) informed the category allocation; Functional use and consumption context: Some foods were classified based on how they are typically consumed in Nigerian contexts (e.g., snacks, breakfast staples, accompaniments), to reflect consumer-facing categorization.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes of this study were the availability, healthiness, and sodium content of both packaged and unpackaged foods and beverages. Availability was defined as the number of food and beverage products present where the products were sampled. The healthiness of foods and beverages was determined based on the Australian Health Star Rating (HSR) nutrient profiling system [26]. This system considers several key food components, including energy, saturated fat, sodium and sugars to calculate a composite score. Additionally, it factors in the positive contributions of certain components like fruit, vegetable, nut, and legume (FVNL) content and dietary fibre. This rating is used as a front-of-pack nutrition label to help consumers make informed choices about the healthiness of food products at a glance. Where the required nutrition data were missing, we created proxy nutrient information by using category averages, similar products in the FoodSwitch database, or generic food composition databases. This method has been used in previous studies, including in Nigeria [14, 25, 27, 28]. Based on this calculation, the HSR system assigns a rating to packaged food and beverage products, ranging from 0.5 stars (least healthy) to 5 stars (most healthy), for their overall nutritional quality. The FoodSwitch system uses the nutrient information available on a food’s packaging label to automatically calculate an estimate of the HSR. In this study, a product was classified as “unhealthy” if it had an HSR score < 3.5 and “healthy” if its HSR score ≥ 3.5 based on previous research [28–30]. Sodium content was assessed directly from the nutritional information label of the packaged foods.

Statistical analysis

The number of packaged and unpackaged food products in each major food category was reported using counts and percentages. Mean (standard deviation, SD) HSR values for each major food category were calculated separately for packaged and unpackaged foods and compared using Welch’s t-test due to unequal sample sizes in the groups. A chart was used to visually compare the healthiness of packaged and unpackaged foods and beverages in Nigeria, as measured by mean HSR. Sodium composition of foods was reported using descriptive statistics (mean, median, standard deviation, and interquartile range) expressed as mg/100 g for each major food category. The median (interquartile range, IQR) sodium content between packaged and unpackaged foods and beverages was compared using the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U tests. Statistical analyses were carried out using Stata (Version 17.0, Standard Edition, StataCorp LLC, USA) using a complete case analysis approach. All statistical tests were performed at a two-sided significance level of α = 0.05 with no adjustment for multiple testing.

Discussion

This research evaluates the availability, healthiness, and sodium content of packaged and unpackaged foods and beverages in Nigerian retail stores and open markets. Packaged foods and beverages comprised 92.9% of the total products surveyed, indicating a significant disparity in availability compared to unpackaged foods, which made up only 7.1%. In terms of healthiness, unpackaged foods had a higher mean HSR score of 4.6 out of 5, compared to 3.4 for packaged products. This suggests that, despite their lower availability, unpackaged options are generally healthier. The use of the HSR nutrient profiling system allows for consistent health assessments and comparisons with international standards. Additionally, the study found that packaged foods and beverages had a substantially higher median sodium content than unpackaged foods, raising concerns about the associated health risks, such as increased risk of elevated blood pressure and cardiovascular diseases. These findings highlight the need for public awareness of the unhealthiness of the packaged food and beverage supply, as well as targeted interventions to promote healthier foods and beverages, including unpackaged products.

Similar to other countries like India, Nigeria exhibits a higher number of packaged foods and beverages compared to their unpackaged counterparts, as indicated by a study conducted in India [4]. Similarly, the United States food supply heavily relies on packaged foods and beverages that are high in calories, unhealthy fats, sugar, and salt [31]. A study analyzing over 80% of packaged foods and beverages available in the US found that 71% were classified as ultra-processed, and the overall average health rating was low with an average HSR value of 2.7 (1.4) [32]. A comparable trend has emerged in South Africa with the rise in packaged food availability in South Africa reflecting a global shift toward convenience-based eating, influenced by urbanization and changing lifestyles [33]. This nutrition transition has been linked to increased risks of diet-related diseases, such as obesity and diabetes, due to the high sugar, sodium, and unhealthy fat content in processed foods. In contrast, fresh unpackaged foods, like fruits and vegetables, are viewed as healthier but often come with higher costs, limited shelf life, and less convenience, which are factors that can be barriers to healthy eating, especially in low-income areas [34–36]. In China, pre-packaged foods, particularly processed foods and ultra-processed foods, constitute a significant portion of food in the market, with 75% of pre-packaged products falling into these categories. A majority of these foods are unhealthy, with over 70% of processed foods and ultra-processed foods containing excessive quantities of at least one negative nutrient according to various nutrient profile models, whereas unpackaged foods were reported to positively influence perceived healthfulness compared to packaged foods [37, 38].

The persistent concern regarding the nutritional quality of processed products in Nigeria necessitates specific interventions [39, 40]. The Pre-Packaged (Labelling) Regulations of 2022 in Nigeria, while comprehensive in requiring essential information on product labels [41], does not mandate front-of-pack nutritional labels, including front-of-pack warning labels. The regulations only require mandatory nutrient declarations on the back of pre-packaged foods and beverages that make a health or nutrient claim. These omissions limit consumer access to easily understandable nutritional information and warnings about potentially harmful ingredients such as high levels of sugar, salt, or saturated fats. Without front-of-pack nutritional labels, consumers will likely find it challenging to quickly assess the healthfulness of products, potentially leading to unintended consumption of unhealthy foods. Similarly, the absence of front-of-pack warning labels reduces the visibility of critical health warnings, which are crucial for informing consumer choices and promoting healthier dietary habits. These shortcomings highlight the need for future amendments to enhance the effectiveness of the regulations in promoting public health through clearer and more informative labeling practices [42].

Encouraging reformulation by reducing sodium, saturated fats, added sugars, and harmful additives, alongside promoting healthier ingredients and cooking methods, is crucial to improving healthy diet intake in Nigeria. Implementing and enforcing nutrient profiling guidelines can guide reformulation efforts and consumer choices. Nigeria has policies to reduce dietary sodium, but implementation and accountability remain weak. Stronger regulatory measures, like South Africa’s legislated sodium limits for food, are needed to drive reformulation and improve public. By setting these limits, Nigeria can effectively encourage food manufacturers to reduce sodium levels in their products, thereby addressing the rising rates of hypertension and related health issues among its population. Such regulations would not only improve public health outcomes but also foster a culture of healthier eating habits, ultimately contributing to a more sustainable food environment [43, 44]. However, despite these strategies being widely recognized as effective, their implementation and accountability mechanisms often fall short [45]. Nonetheless, this strategy may work if it is implemented properly in Nigeria following international guidelines and best practices [46].

The predominance of packaged foods in Nigeria’s food environment [25, 47], combined with their low health ratings and high sodium content, highlights the urgent need for comprehensive and enforceable nutrition regulations. These regulations should include front-of-pack labeling and nutrient-specific limits to address consumer awareness and encourage healthier food choices. Moreover, lessons from countries demonstrate that robust policy enforcement, combined with public education campaigns, can drive meaningful change. Nigeria must prioritize these interventions as part of a broader strategy to mitigate the rising burden of diet-related diseases and create a food environment that supports healthier dietary habits and long-term public health outcomes. The observed patterns in food availability and healthiness are influenced by cultural and economic factors. Unpackaged foods, such as fresh produce, grains, and locally prepared meals are often perceived as fresher, more affordable, and culturally familiar, making them widely accepted and consumed across diverse Nigerian communities. Promoting these foods offers potential benefits, including improved diet quality and support for local food systems. However, challenges remain. Their availability can be inconsistent, especially in urban areas where modern retail dominates. In addition, concerns about food hygiene, storage, and standardization may limit broader acceptance. While unpackaged foods present a culturally acceptable and cost-effective alternative, scaling up their consumption in a safe and sustainable way will require targeted investments in food safety, infrastructure, and consumer education.

Future research should explore the effectiveness of policy interventions, such as front-of-pack labeling, sodium reduction targets, and reformulation strategies on consumer behavior and health outcomes in Nigeria. Longitudinal studies assessing shifts in purchasing habits, sodium intake, and associated health indicators following such interventions would provide valuable insights. Additionally, research should investigate how education campaigns and changes in food environments influence consumer choices, particularly in low-income and urban settings where packaged and ultra-processed food consumption is rising. Understanding these dynamics is critical for designing context-specific, evidence-based policies to promote healthier dietary practices.

The strengths of this study include systematic and rigorous data collection methods to ensure a comprehensive and reliable dataset directly comparable to other countries. In addition, the inclusion of data from diverse locations, spanning different states, stores, and markets, enhances the study’s robustness and geographic coverage. The study also had some limitations. Some food products lacked HSR ratings because the HSR could not be calculated due to the unavailability of some specific nutritional information required by the HSR algorithm. Furthermore, the HSR system was not designed for the specific context of Nigeria, potentially influencing its applicability in this setting. Another potential limitation of this study is the urban-centric selection of data collection sites, which may contribute to the observed imbalance between unpackaged and packaged foods and beverages that were identified. Urban areas typically have a higher concentration of retail establishments offering a variety of packaged foods, and the study considered the largest retail outlets in these areas, which generally offered the largest number of products. Moreso, the unpackaged food products were sampled only from large traditional open markets and not from prominent retail stores such as supermarkets, which may also sell a limited selection of unpackaged items. This may have resulted in underrepresentation of certain unpackaged products available in formal retail settings. The data collection period spanned only 4–6 months which may not fully capture seasonal variations in the availability or sodium content of food products. Additionally, the study was conducted in only three states, all of which are major urban areas where popular food and beverage products are widely sold. This limited geographic scope and sampling frame of unpackaged foods may not fully represent the full availability and characteristics of unpackaged foods in rural settings; however, the included products are likely among the most commonly sold and consumed. This study used the AUSNUT database to supplement missing nutrient values where local data were unavailable. While efforts were made to match similar food types, the use of a non-local database may introduce inaccuracies due to differences in formulation and processing. This represents a potential limitation in the nutrient estimates. Future research should aim to include a broader representation of different geographic areas to provide a more comprehensive analysis of the food landscape in Nigeria. Finally, the study assigned uniform nutritional values to types of unpackaged products, which may oversimplify the assessment of their healthiness compared to packaged foods and beverages.

Conclusion

This study documented a greater number of unique packaged foods and beverages available to consumers compared to unpackaged options. Additionally, the healthiness of packaged food and beverage items in Nigeria was lower than that of unpackaged foods, as indicated by lower HSR and higher sodium content. Given the significant burden that unhealthy diets impose on health outcomes, the Nigerian government, in collaboration with stakeholders, should implement a comprehensive approach to improving the healthiness of the food and beverage supply, including reformulation strategies to enhance the nutritional quality of packaged products, mandatory front of package nutrition labels to improve consumer choice, and promotion of unpackaged foods and beverages, which are generally healthier. Establishing standards to restrict the production and consumption of unhealthy packaged foods, particularly those high in sodium, alongside public health education, may yield significant health benefits for the Nigerian population by reducing the burden of hypertension, CVD, and other diet-sensitive conditions.
