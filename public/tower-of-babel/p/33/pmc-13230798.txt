Quizartinib and omacetaxine mepesuccinate combination therapy in FLT3-ITD AML: a phase II trial
Authors: Li-Chuan Zheng, Kelvin K W Wong, Stephen S Y Lam, Garret M K Leung, Chenqinyao Li, Kwui-Wa Tong, Wing Lam, Xiao-yuan Zeng, Koon-Chuen Chan, Natalie Nok-Man Chan, Ka-Lam Ng, Chee-Chean Dang, Tsz-Ho Kwok, Sze-Pui Tsui, Rakesh Sharma, Jason W H Wong, Suet-Yi Leung, Anskar Y H Leung, Cheuk-Him Man
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFLT3-ITD inhibitors are approved for acute myeloid leukemia (AML) treatment but relapse is common. In this study, the combined inhibition of FLT3-ITD signal and protein translation by QUIZartinib and Omacetaxine Mepesuccinate (QUIZOM) synergistically suppressed the most critical FLT3-ITD survival signals including mitochondrial respiration and proteostasis, which induced apoptosis and pro-inflammatory response. In a Phase 2 trial (NCT03135054) involving 40 chemo-refractory/unfit FLT3-ITD AML patients, QUIZOM achieved a composite complete remission (CRc) of 83%, a median leukemia-free survival (LFS) of 10 months (Range: 0.7-68.2 months) and a median overall survival (OS) of 12.9 months (Range: 1.8-69.2 months). 13/33 (39%) received allogeneic HSCT after a median of 143 days (Range: 53-367 days). Higher CRc rates were observed in patients with NPM1 mutations, DNMT3A mutations, and wild-type WT1. Single-cell RNA-sequencing of QUIZOM cohort revealed positive correlation between pro-inflammatory response in blasts, CD8 + T activation and clinical responsiveness. Further, we identified a leukemic stem cell (LSC) subpopulation with activated JNK/JUN/HSPA1B axis via PLD1-driven phosphatidylcholine metabolism, which promoted proteostasis and drove QUIZOM resistance. PLD1-inhibitor remodeled phospholipid metabolism, induced ferroptosis and restored QUIZOM response in LSC. Our findings provided the therapeutic and resistant mechanisms of QUIZOM and paved the way for targeted interventions in this AML subtype.Subject terms: Acute myeloid leukaemia, Targeted therapies, Cancer

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is characterized by an abnormal increase in myeloblasts in blood and bone marrow (BM) due to differentiation blockade and increased proliferation. FMS-like tyrosine kinase 3-internal tandem duplication (FLT3-ITD) is one of the most common mutations in AML, accounting for up to 30% of cases. In-frame insertion of the duplicated sequence leads to constitutive activation of FLT3, which drives cellular proliferation via activation of oncogenic pathways, viz. PI3K, STAT5 and RAS1. AML carrying FLT3-ITD is associated with a higher risk of relapse and inferior prognosis2.

FLT3 inhibitors are now available for clinical use at relapse3–6. Despite an improved outcome compared with standard of care (SOC), the clinical benefit of FLT3 inhibitor monotherapy, notably gilteritinib and quizartinib, is characteristically transient, and relapse is uniform unless allogeneic HSCT can be timely arranged7,8. There is an unmet clinical need to identify the oncogenic vulnerabilities in FLT3-ITD AML and develop mechanism-driven therapeutic strategies at relapse so that more patients can be bridged to HSCT.

In this study, we examined the oncogenic vulnerabilities in FLT3-ITD AML and showed that they were specifically sensitive to perturbation of the FLT3-ITD signal and protein translation, which was proven both in the laboratory and in a Phase II clinical trial. Combination treatment with Quizartinib and Omacetaxine mepesuccinate (referred herein as QUIZOM) disrupted proteostasis, leading to apoptosis, DNA damage, and activation of inflammatory pathways. Clinical resistance to QUIZOM is associated with increased activity of PLD1-driven phosphatidylcholine metabolism, which promoted proteostasis in a leukemic stem cell (LSC) subpopulation. Inhibition of PLD1 mitigated the resistance in the LSC population by remodeling metabolism and inducing ferroptosis. The mechanisms underlying the therapeutic efficacy and subsequent relapses have become important leads for further research that may further improve the outcome in this AML subtype.

Results

Summary of patient recruitment, primary and secondary outcomes in QUIZOM clinical trial

Forty patients, including 35 relapse/refractory (R/R) and 5 newly diagnosed FLT3-ITD AML patients, were recruited and treated with the combination of quizartinib and omacetaxine mepesuccinate (QUIZOM) between November 2017 and September 2020.

The treatment achieved a composite complete remission (CRc) rate of 83% (33/40 patients). For the entire cohort, the median leukemia-free survival (LFS) was 10 months (range: 0.7–68.2 months). Subgroup analysis showed a median LFS of 6.9 months (range: 0.9–15.7 months) in the newly diagnosed group and 10 months (range: 0.7–68.2 months) in the R/R group.

FLT3-ITD AML is vulnerable to the co-inhibition of FLT3-ITD and protein translation

To identify the vulnerability of FLT3-ITD AML that may become a target for intervention, we analyzed the DepMap CRISPR-Cas9 knockout database (https://depmap.org/portal/). FLT3-ITD AML dependency involved genes associated with FLT3 signaling, protein translation and respiratory electron transport (Fig. 1A, Supplementary Datasets 5, 6). Functional validation was performed. There was preferential sensitivity of FLT3-ITD AML towards the inhibitor of FLT3-ITD (Quizartinib) and protein translation (Omecetaxine Mepesuccinate, OM) (Fig. 1B, C, Supplementary Fig. 1A, B). Combination treatment (QUIZOM) synergistically reduced protein synthesis and induced apoptosis in human FLT3-ITD AML cell lines (Fig. 1D–F, Supplementary Fig. 1C–E) and isogenic Ba/F3-FLT3-ITD cell lines (Fig. 1G–I). The synergism was further validated in primary FLT3-ITD AML samples (Fig. 1J, K) and in the xenotransplantation model (Fig. 1L–O, Supplementary Fig. 1F), confirming the therapeutic efficacy of QUIZOM in this AML subtype. At the drug doses used, there was no significant toxicity of QUIZOM in animals (Fig. 1P).

QUIZOM synergistically remodeled the translatome in FLT3-ITD AML

To study the mechanism underlying the vulnerability to QUIZOM in FLT3-ITD AML, we examined the effects on newly synthesized proteins (NSPs) (see also Supplementary Note 2) (Fig. 2A) and identified 5917 NSPs that showed ≥1 unique peptide in all samples (Supplementary Fig. 2A, Supplementary Datasets 7–10). Substantial decrease in NSPs associated with various biological processes, such as protein folding, cell cycle and RNA metabolism, was observed upon drug treatment (Supplementary Fig. 2B–D). A total of 165 NSPs (Fig. 2B) were selected for subsequent analyses based on pre-determined criteria: log2 fold-change <−0.5 (downregulated) and synergistic score >0.5 (non-antagonistic QUIZOM v.s. monotherapy). Among them, MCL1 (a reported OM target)9 and the 2 most downregulated NSPs (HSP70 and UBL3) was selected for validation by Western blot (Fig. 2C). Reportedly, the anti-leukemic effect of OM might be driven by MCL1 downregulation9. However, forced expression of MCL1 only partially mitigated the QUIZOM-induced apoptosis, suggesting that alternative mechanisms might account for QUIZOM-induced cytotoxicity (Fig. 2D, Supplementary Fig. 2E). Gene Ontology analysis of the 165 NSPs showed that they were solely enriched with mitochondrial respiration and chaperone-mediated protein folding functions (Supplementary Dataset 11, Fig. 2E), suggesting these processes might account for QUIZOM cytotoxicity.

To investigate the molecular events involved in the QUIZOM-induced translatome remodeling, RNA-seq was performed. QUIZOM significantly upregulated 1689 and 1327 genes and downregulated 1160 and 1577 genes in MV4-11 and MOLM-13, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 2F). Pathway analysis and Western blot confirmed the suppression of FLT3-ITD downstream signal, mTOR/S6K/4E-BP1 axis10, that might account for the synergistic inhibition of protein translation by QUIZOM (Supplementary Fig. 2G–I). In addition, there was negative enrichment of pathways pertaining to mitochondrial metabolism and chaperone-mediated protein folding (Supplementary Fig. 2J) based on GSEA, consistent with the translatome findings.

The observations of DepMap dependency, translatome and transcriptome aforementioned underscored a key therapeutic effect of QUIZOM via perturbation of mitochondrial function. The latter was further investigated. QUIZOM decreased mitochondrial mass and membrane potential in FLT3-ITD AML (Fig. 2F, G). There was ultrastructural evidence of mitochondrial rupture (Fig. 2H, I).

Functionally, QUIZOM significantly reduced both glycolysis (glucose uptake and extracellular acidification rate (ECAR)), mitochondrial respiration and ATP level (Supplementary Fig. 2K–P), underscoring a significant reduction of functional mitochondria. An untargeted metabolomics was performed, and principal component analysis (PCA) confirmed a unique spectrum of metabolic remodeling by QUIZOM, which was distinct from those of single drug treatment (Supplementary Fig. 2Q). In particular, QUIZOM significantly reduced glucose, amino acid and nucleotide metabolism, which were reported to be essential in supporting the survival of FLT3-ITD AML (Fig. 2K)11–13. However, pathway analysis of metabolites with significant changes upon QUIZOM treatment showed that lipid metabolism was not affected, indicating the dispensable role of lipid metabolism in mitochondrial respiration in parental FLT3-ITD AML (Supplementary Fig. 2R). Nicotinamide riboside (NR), a precursor of the NAD+ cofactor, could ameliorate the growth inhibitory effect of QUIZOM (Fig. 2L, M). NR has been shown to promote mitochondrial biogenesis and mitochondrial respiration14,15. In this study, NR alleviated the adverse effects of QUIZOM on mitochondria and ATP level (Fig. 2N–Q). Untargeted metabolomics showed that NR induced an increase in sugar and glycerophospholipid metabolism in FLT3-ITD AML upon treatment with QUIZOM (Supplementary Fig. 2S, T). Similar effects of QUIZOM and NR rescue were seen in primary FLT3-ITD AML samples (Fig. 2R–T).

QUIZOM abrogated mitochondrial-assisted protein refolding and led to apoptosis

Potential effects of QUIZOM on chaperone-mediated protein folding were also examined. QUIZOM was shown to reduce the amount of chaperone protein HSP70 (Fig. 2C) and induce the formation of protein aggregates in multiple independent assay platforms (Fig. 3A, B, Supplementary Fig. 3A–C). Pharmacologic inhibition of HSP70 similarly enhanced the formation of protein aggregates (Fig. 3C, D) and induced apoptosis (Fig. 3E, F) in AML cell lines and primary samples. Bortezomib, a proteasomal inhibitor, was reported to generate protein aggregates by blocking the degradation of misfolded protein16. Addition of Bortezomib potentiated the formation of protein aggregates and apoptosis upon HSP70 inhibition, underscoring the significant role of protein aggregates in QUIZOM-induced cytotoxicity (Supplementary Fig. 3D, E). QUIZOM-induced protein aggregate formation was abolished by NR (Fig. 3G), whereas further inhibition of metabolic respiration by PKM2 inhibition (Compound 3k) potentiated HSP70 inhibitor-induced apoptosis (Supplementary Fig. 3F), supporting the proposition of mitochondrial collapse promoting PAs-driven apoptosis (Fig. 3H). Furthermore, increased protein aggregates were associated with DNA damage (Fig. 3I). DNA damage was reduced by NR supplement, suggesting a causal relationship between mitochondrial function, protein aggregate formation and cytotoxicity (Fig. 3J). The aforementioned transcriptome analysis revealed activation of p53, which might be triggered by protein aggregates-inducing DNA damage (Supplementary Fig. 3G). In fact, QUIZOM-induced apoptosis was p53-dependent, as demonstrated by its lack of effect in the TP53 loss-of-function mutant MV4-11 isogenic line (Fig. 3K). The role of HSP70 in QUIZOM-associated cytotoxicity was further examined by forced expression of HSP70 (HSP70-OE) in AML cell lines, which resulted in the reduced formation of protein aggregates and apoptosis upon QUIZOM treatment in vitro (Fig. 3L, M, Supplementary Fig. 3H) and mitigated the survival benefit of QUIZOM in animals engrafted by MV4-11 (Fig. 3N).

QUIZOM activated proinflammatory response in FLT3-ITD AML

In addition to apoptosis, RNA-seq data showed the QUIZOM-induced increase in CDKN1A, a decrease in LMNB1 expression (Supplementary Fig. 4A) and a positive enrichment of pathways associated with senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) (Fig. 4A, B, Supplementary Dataset 12). QUIZOM induced DNA damage, as shown by the increase in γH2AX in FLT3-ITD AML (Fig. 3I). Furthermore, mitochondrial rupture led to an increase in the release of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) to cytoplasm (Fig. 4C)17. These changes were associated with STING dimerization, 2’3’-cGAMP production and SASP cytokines (Fig. 4D–F, Supplementary Fig. 4B), characteristic of cyclic GMP-AMP synthase-stimulator of interferon genes (cGAS-STING) activation. Treatment with cGAS inhibitor (RU.521), HSP70-OE, and NR treatment ameliorated the increases in pro-inflammatory cytokines (Fig. 4F), supporting the proposition that cGAS-STING activation resulted from the aberration of the aforementioned intracellular signal. Functionally, QUIZOM promoted phagocytosis by macrophages in co-culture with FLT3-ITD AML and the process was also ameliorated by NR or HSP70 overexpression (Fig. 4G). Phagocytosis was also ameliorated by the treatment of CCL3 and CSF1 neutralizing antibodies (Fig. 4H), suggesting that these cytokines might induce phagocytosis in response to QUIZOM treatment. Importantly, macrophage activation was observed in vivo, as evidenced by their increase in MHC II and CD40 expression (Fig. 4I) when NSG mice engrafted by FLT3-ITD AML were treated with QUIZOM.

Phase 2 clinical trial of QUIZOM in FLT3-ITD AML

The reported synergism and therapeutic mechanisms of QUIZOM laid the foundation of a Phase 2 clinical trial evaluating its clinical efficacy and toxicity in FLT3-ITD AML patients. Forty FLT3-ITD AML patients (35 R/R and 5 newly diagnosed) were recruited as previously described. Their clinicopathologic characteristics were shown in Supplementary Dataset 13. The median age was 57.5 years (range: 23–81). Among 35 R/R patients, 11 were primary refractory to “7 + 3” induction chemotherapy and 14 had two or more prior lines of chemotherapy. Fourteen patients had prior exposure to FLT3 inhibitors, including midostaurin (N = 11) and sorafenib (N = 3). Seven patients were at post-HSCT relapse (Supplementary Note 2). Median pre-treatment FLT3-ITD allelic ratio was 0.39 (range: 0.03 – 5.72). Targeted gene sequencing of 38 available diagnostic or pre-treatment BM samples showed a median of 3 mutations (range 1–6) (Fig. 5A). Similar to other reported cohorts18, recurrent mutations were seen in DNMT3A (49%), NPM1 (49%), TET2 (17%), RUNX1 (15%), IDH2 (15%) and WT1 (12%). A tyrosine kinase domain (TKD) mutation (p.D835V) of FLT3 was detected in one patient. However, there was no significant change in the top 10 common mutations in the cohort between pretreatment and relapse samples, indicating the genetic changes might not account for the emergence of QUIZOM resistance (Supplementary Fig. 5A).

Composite complete remission (CRc) rate was 83% (N = 33), including 15% (N = 6) CR and 68% (N = 27) CRi (Fig. 5B). Twenty-nine patients achieved remission after 1 cycle of QUIZOM, and 4 patients after 2 cycles. Median time to response is 23 days (Range: 17–62 days). The median number of cycles received was 3 (range 1–20). The CRc rates were 60% and 86% in the upfront treatment and R/R group, respectively. Higher CRc rate was observed in patients with mutated NPM1 (95% vs 67%, p = 0.038), mutated DNMT3A (95% vs 67%, p = 0.038) and wild-type WT1 (91% vs 20%, p = 0.002) (Fig. 5A) among other clinical parameters (Supplementary Dataset 14). The peripheral blood white cell count decreased within the first 48–72 h of treatment, compatible with induction of apoptosis by QUIZOM (Supplementary Fig. 5B).

Among 33 patients who achieved remission, 13 (39%) received allogeneic HSCT after a median of 143 days (Range: 53–367 days), including 3 from HLA-identical siblings, 4 from haploidentical relatives and 6 from matched unrelated donors (MUD). Twenty-two (63%) patients relapsed, including all who did not receive HSCT (N = 19) and 3 patients at 4, 12 and 41 months after HSCT. One patient died of transplant-related mortality, and another patient died of infective complication during remission. The median LFS and OS were 10 months (range: 0.7–68.2 months) and 12.9 months (range: 1.8–69.2 months). For patients not bridged to HSCT, the median LFS and OS were 3.3 months and 9.9 months, respectively. For patients who were bridged to HSCT, the median LFS and OS were not reached (Fig. 5C) at the time of analysis, with a median follow-up time of 4.7 years. Nine patients remained disease-free at the time of analysis with a median LFS at 58.5 months (range: 20.5–68.5 months) and OS at 79 months (range: 22.5–106.3 months). Among 13 relapsed patients with available samples for evaluation, 8 (62%) patients had loss of FLT3-ITD clones at relapse. They had a smaller pre-treatment ITD clone size (Supplementary Fig. 5C).

Univariate analysis showed that better overall survival was associated with age ≤ 60 years, achieving CRc, prior FLT3 inhibitor exposure, allogeneic HSCT and wild-type WT1. Superior LFS was associated with age ≤ 60 years and allogeneic HSCT (Supplementary Fig. 5D). Multivariate analysis was not performed due to small cohort size.

There were two early deaths on days 29 and 53 from QUIZOM treatment. They were both non-responding patients, and the causes of death were pneumonia and pulmonary hemorrhage, and pneumonia respectively, considered to be related to underlying leukemia progression. Treatment-emergent adverse events were summarized in Supplementary Dataset 15. Grade 3/4 cytopenia and febrile neutropenia were common adverse events. Of note, 20% patients developed bacteremia (grade 4 sepsis) requiring intravenous antibiotics. Importantly, prolongation of QTc was not observed at the current study dose.

Activation of immune response was associated with clinical QUIZOM response

To delineate the mechanism of QUIZOM responsiveness, single-cell RNA sequencing was performed in 45 samples from 19 unselected patients receiving QUIZOM, from whom serial samples (pre-treatment, remission and relapse/refractory stages) were available (Supplementary Fig. 5E). After integration and unsupervised clustering, 15 major cell populations were identified (Fig. 5D, E). Cell barcode calling was performed to identify AML cells carrying defined mutations (Supplementary Fig. 5F). 19,830 (19,169 with 1 mutation; 661 with ≥2 mutations) out of 439,320 cells were found positive, including mostly haematopoietic stem cell - multipotent progenitor (HSC-MPP), granulocyte-monocyte progenitor (GMP), megakaryocyte progenitor (MkP), and monocyte populations (Fig. 5F). A machine learning framework was applied to define the leukemic and immune populations with high precision and correlation with flow cytometry findings (Supplementary Fig. 5G–I). The largest leukemic cell cluster, HSC-MPP, exhibited strong transcriptomic similarity to HSC-MPP cells in previously reported FLT3-ITD samples (Supplementary Fig. 5J)19. QUIZOM treatment resulted in a more drastic reduction of HSC-MPP and significant decreases in GMP and MkP in responders compared to non-responders. Moreover, there were increases in immune populations, including CD4-T, CD8-T, Natural Killer T cell (NKT cell), and Natural Killer cell (NK cells), which were not found in non-responders (Fig. 5G, Supplementary Fig. 5K).

Single-cell transcriptome was analysed to address whether the aforementioned activation of immune and inflammatory responses to QUIZOM in the laboratory could be generalized to patients receiving such treatment. Specifically in the responders, QUIZOM induced immune activation, exemplified by the upregulation of interferon-responsive genes and IRF2 regulon in the leukemic HSC-MPP population (Fig. 5H, Supplementary Fig. 5L). In addition, QUIZOM resulted in the activation of the T cell Receptor (TCR) pathway in CD4+ and CD8 + T cells, and cytotoxicity gene signature in CD8 + T cells at remission (Fig. 5I–K). Gene regulatory network analysis showed a remarkable increase in activity of EOMES and T-bet pertinent to T cell activation and differentiation in CD4+ and CD8 + T cells (Fig. 5L, M). Furthermore, QUIZOM induced a significant increase in terminally differentiated effector memory CD8 + T cells (TEMRA), a decrease in effector memory CD8 + T cells, exhausted CD8 + T cells (TEX) and CD4 + Th17 cells (Fig. 5N, Supplementary Fig. 5M). Collectively, the transcriptomic changes in both the leukemic and immune cell compartments supported the proposition from laboratory studies that the immune system was remodeled by QUIZOM-mediated cytotoxicity on leukemic blast and such a mechanism might correlate with the clinical responsiveness to QUIZOM.

Identification of distinct leukemic stem cell signature associated with QUIZOM-resistance

Despite the overall satisfactory response to QUIZOM, 4/19 patients did not respond. We further examined the transcriptomic changes after QUIZOM with a view to identifying differential changes in the HSC-MPP population of responders compared to non-responders (Supplementary Fig. 6A). Significant and preferential changes were observed in the activity of 29 transcription factors in the HSC-MPP population among responders to QUIZOM (Supplementary Dataset 16), including the down-regulation of HSC genes, such as the HOX cluster (Supplementary Fig. 6B)20. Furthermore, the primitive FLT3-ITD-bearing HSC-MPP blast was divided into 7 sub-clusters by unsupervised clustering. Intriguingly, non-responders showed persistence of sub-cluster 0 and 1 (resistant clusters) in the post-treatment samples (Fig. 6A, B, Supplementary Fig. 6C), indicating these clusters might underlie clinical drug resistance. These resistant sub-clusters exhibited up-regulation of gene expression pertinent to leukemic stem cell, replicative quiescence and lipid metabolism, whereas those associated with protein translation and amino acid metabolism were reduced (Supplementary Fig. 6D, E). Furthermore, HSC-MPP cells of non-responders on Day 28 of QUIZOM treatment showed a significantly lower pseudotime compared with the responders, attesting to its primitive nature (Fig. 6C). We analysed Beat AML public RNA-seq cohort for the impact of LSC signature from this study on overall survival in AML patients. Based on the single cell matrix, AML patients were accorded with high, medium and low LSC fractions using CibersortX. Those with the highest LSC fraction were associated with the worst OS, and those with the lowest LSC fraction showed the best OS (Fig. 6D). Importantly, QUIZOM-resistant LSCs displayed a transcriptome profile distinct from that of chemo-resistant LSC in pediatric AML21, suggesting diverse mechanisms of drug resistance in LSC (Supplementary Fig. 6F).

Putative functions of LSC were examined. LSC preferentially expressed JUN and FOS (key regulators of quiescence, unfolded protein response)22, HSPA1B (encoding HSP70), and genes promoting lipid metabolism such as PLD1, MTMR3, NR1H2, and SREBF123–25 (Fig. 6E, F, Supplementary Fig. 6G). LSC isolated from primary FLT3-ITD AML samples based on our LSC phenotypic markers CD33 + CD44 + CD69 + (Fig. 6F) [viz. triple positive (TP) cells] were more replicatively quiescent and less sensitive to QUIZOM than the bulk population (Fig. 6G–I). To validate the functional role of JUN/FOS and PLD1 (phospholipase D1, catalyzing the hydrolysis of phosphatidylcholine (PC) to phosphatidate and choline) in the treatment response to QUIZOM, TP of FLT3-ITD primary AML samples were treated by QUIZOM combined with inhibitors to c-Fos/c-Jun (T-5224) and PLD1 inhibitor (VU0155069). T-5224 and VU0155069 synergized with QUIZOM in inducing apoptosis in the TP subpopulation (Fig. 6J, K). Combination of PLD1 and QUIZOM resulted in profound suppression of leukemic engraftment of primary AML (Fig. 6L).

To investigate the molecular mechanisms of PLD1 in mediating LSC activity, untargeted metabolomic analysis of TP and the bulk population was performed. Glycerophospholipid metabolism was the sole metabolic pathway enriched in TP compared with the bulk population (Fig. 6M, Supplementary Fig. 6H). In particular, pathway analysis of TP-enriched metabolites reveals the activation of phosphotidylcholine metabolism, which was consistent with the upregulation of PLD1 in TP compared to the bulk population (Supplementary Fig. 6I). Furthermore, PLD1 inhibition suppressed lipid uptake in TP but not in the bulk population (Fig. 6N). PLD1 inhibition remodeled lipid metabolism, leading to an increase in ROS and lipid peroxidation (Fig. 6O, P). Furthermore, treatment with ferrostatin but not necrostatin mitigated the QUIZOM and PLD1 inhibition-induced cell death, underscoring the induction of ferroptosis by the latter treatment in the TP LSC subpopulation (Fig. 6Q). PLD1 overexpression induced lipid uptake (Fig. 6R) and ameliorated the QUIZOM-induced apoptosis (Fig. 6S), supporting the role of PLD1 in mediating QUIZOM resistance in AML. Supplementation of phosphatidylcholine (PC), the substrate of PLD1, also induced QUIZOM resistance in AML (Supplementary Fig. 6J, K). Furthermore, PC significantly reduced QUIZOM-induced formation of protein aggregates (Supplementary Fig. 6L). Mechanistically, PLD1 inhibition preferentially reduced glycerophospholipid metabolism (Supplementary Fig. 6M) and resulted in accumulation of phosphatidylcholines via inhibiting its hydrolysis (Supplementary Fig. 6N). Among the phosphatidylcholines, diacyl-PUFA phosphatidylcholines (PC-PUFA2), a driver of ferroptosis26, were increased. The observations supported the proposition that PLD1 inhibitor and QUIZOM showed synergism in suppressing the growth of FLT3-ITD AML via simultaneous induction of apoptosis and ferroptosis.

The putative pathogenetic link between PLD1 expression and the JNK/JUN/HSPA1B axis in the LSC population was evaluated (Supplementary Fig. 6O). Inhibition of PLD1 reduced phosphorylation of JNK (Fig. 6T). Moreover, inhibition of JNK signaling, FOS/JUN transcriptional activity or PLD1 suppressed the expression of HSPA1B (Fig. 6U). In primary AML samples, the therapeutic effects of FOS/JUN and PLD1 inhibition on HSPA1B expression and protein aggregate formation were only observed in TP LSC but not the bulk population (Fig. 6V, W).

Discussion

FLT3-ITD AML is associated with a high risk of relapse after conventional chemotherapy. Midostaurin3 and quizartinib6, as an adjunct to chemotherapy, could improve patient survival. Once relapsed, gilteritinib4 and quizartinib27 monotherapy have been shown to confer a survival advantage compared with salvage chemotherapy, but response is typically transient, and overall outcome is unsatisfactory. There is an unmet clinical need to develop effective and tolerable means of salvage treatment. In this study, we identified the vulnerability to perturbed protein translation and refolding in FLT3-ITD AML. The results were consistent with those from in vitro drug sensitivity testing that identified OM as an inhibitory agent for FLT3-ITD AML. The latter showed activated mTOR signal and higher rate of protein synthesis10,28. In addition, we performed multi-omics analyses and mechanistic studies and provided a new treatment paradigm for FLT3-ITD AML based on metabolic remodeling and cGAS-STING activation.

First, we found that the overreactive translational activity in FLT3-ITD AML supported the protein refolding machinery via overexpressing HSP70. Combined inhibition of FLT3-ITD signal and protein synthesis (QUIZOM treatment) synergistically reduced HSP70 expression and therefore suppressed the protein refolding activity, exerted proteostatic stress, and subsequently induced DNA damage by accumulation of misfolded protein, i.e., protein aggregates. Simultaneously, QUIZOM disrupted mitochondria, which generate ATP for maintaining the bioenergetic pathway for supporting leukemic survival and also the proper function of ATP-dependent HSP7029. Therefore, QUIZOM potentiated the accumulation of protein aggregates via ablating the mitochondria-HSP70 axis. Induction of protein aggregates by HSP70 inhibition has been reported in lymphoma and colon cancer30,31. Therapeutic targeting of HSP70 has been reported in FLT3-ITD AML32, but the mechanism is unclear. Our results supported the proposition of a distinct mitochondria-HSP70-protein aggregates-DNA damage axis, hitherto undescribed in FLT3-ITD AML and might open a new avenue for therapeutic intervention. Inhibition of HSP70 might also suppress autophagy and subsequent recycling of misfolded proteins33. Whether it might account for the anti-leukemia effects of QUIZOM would have to be investigated.

Second, we demonstrated the cell extrinsic effect of QUIZOM treatment in FLT3-ITD AML. QUIZOM disrupted the proteostatic and mitochondrial homeostasis that resulted in the formation of protein aggregates in FLT3-ITD AML. The mitochondrial collapse and protein aggregates then potentiated the activation of the cGAS-STING pathway and subsequent increase in the expression of SASP cytokines. We have recently described cGAS-STING activation in TP53 mutated AML upon treatment by PLK4 inhibitor34 and decitabine35. Functionally, there was evidence of macrophage activation both in vitro and in vivo secondary to HSP70 suppression and disruption of mitochondrial integrity. Our results underscore a new paradigm in AML treatment whereby hitherto cell-intrinsic therapeutic intervention might result in immune activation and leukemia cell eradication. Intriguingly, the repertoire of SASPs induced by QUIZOM in FLT3-ITD AML, viz. MCP-1a (CCL3), RANTES (CCL5), and M-CSF (CSF1) were distinct from those induced by decitabine or PLK4 inhibition in TP53-mutated AML, suggesting that the inflammatory response induced via the cGAS-STING pathway might be agent and disease-specific.

Third, the leukemia inhibitory activity of QUIZOM was successfully translated into clinical application. Specifically, patients with R/R AML or unfit for conventional treatment showed a high response rate to QUIZOM. Notwithstanding the difference in study design and patient cohort, our results compared very favorably with those of gilteritinib4 and quizartinib27 monotherapy, and the regimen provided an effective bridging therapy to HSCT (Supplementary Dataset 17). Specifically, 7 patients recruited into this study had relapse after the first HSCT, and 4 were effectively bridged to the second HSCT, attesting to the safety and tolerability of the regimen. With post-HSCT quizartinib maintenance, 3 remained in remission after 5 years, underscoring the efficacy of the QUIZOM regimen.

Finally, single-cell transcriptome analysis of the serial samples from the clinical cohort revealed potential mechanisms of QUIZOM responsiveness and subsequent refractoriness. Specific molecular changes in responders included down-regulation of HOX gene activity in AML at remission, suggesting that the latter underwent a critical loss of leukemic-maintaining signals that accounted for the therapeutic efficacy36. Moreover, immune activation at remission was evident by the enrichment of CD8-TEMRA, increased cytotoxic signature and reduction of CD8-TEX37–39, while there were no significant changes in the immune compartment in non-responder. However, macrophage population was not annotated, due to its rarity in BM samples40. The results were consistent with our preclinical studies that QUIZOM induced immune activation via the cGAS-STING pathway. Furthermore, the data have provided insights into the QUIZOM refractoriness. Specifically, our single-cell RNAseq data on the clinical cohorts revealed that FLT3-ITD AML is highly heterogeneous. Subclusters in the non-responders’ HSC-MPP population showing a strong LSC signature might account for the drug resistance41,42. Genes associated with quiescence (JUN), protein folding (HSPA1B) and lipid metabolism (PLD1) were abundant in the LSC population that might be crucial to the maintenance of leukemia stemness. However, amino acid-associated oxidative phosphorylation and protein translation were not active in that cluster. Interestingly, LSC with quiescent phenotype and active lipid metabolism have been found to confer resistance to chemotherapy and venetoclax, suggesting a potential therapeutic strategy via targeting LSC quiescence and lipid metabolism43. Notably, PLD1 might act as a signal transducer and drive the JNK/JUN pathway via modulating lipid mediator (phosphatidic acid). The latter supports the chaperone-mediated protein refolding capacity by inducing HSP70 expression and confers drug resistance. Inhibition of PLD1 remodeled phosphatidylcholine metabolism and resulted in the accumulation of PLD1 substrate, PC-PUFA2, which triggered ferroptosis in FLT3-ITD AML. PLD1 was also found to crosstalk with Wnt/β-catenin and S6K/AKT pathway and promoted the progression of different solid tumors, suggesting its essential role in cancer stemness44,45. More importantly, additional somatic mutation was not observed at relapse; instead, the transcriptome data revealed that QUIZOM treatment upregulated PLD1 expression in FLT3-ITD AML cell lines. It suggested that QUIZOM resistance at relapse might be the consequence of both QUIZOM-mediated transcriptomic change and selection of PLD1-high LSC subclone during QUIZOM treatment in patients. Most importantly, our data validated the therapeutic efficacy of c-Fos/c-Jun and PLD1 inhibitors that can sensitize LSC to QUIZOM, providing an advanced regimen for FLT3-ITD AML. Collectively, our work suggested that CD8 T cell activation or PLD1 inhibition abrogated alternative chaperone protein refolding machinery and might improve the treatment outcome of QUIZOM in FLT3-ITD AML.

There were limitations to the present study. Despite the high CRc rate to QUIZOM, most cases failed hematological recovery. In fact, two-thirds of the responding patients remained transfusion-dependent until allo-HSCT, limiting the benefits in transplantation-ineligible patients. Future trials should consider dose modifications and measurable residual disease-guided strategies to avoid treatment-related marrow aplasia. FLT3-ITD might represent an amplifier rather than driver of leukemia initiation in some patients46 and FLT3-wildtype (FLT3-WT) leukemia relapse was often encountered47. In addition, relapse could occur despite effective FLT3 inhibition48,49 due to adaptation by leukemia cells to survival on alternative signals, including but not limited to activation of NHE150, remodeled drug metabolism51, dysregulation of apoptotic pathway52 and the emergence of leukemia clones carrying distinct mutations associated with chromatin modifier, spliceosome and signaling pathways53. Furthermore, the extent of immune activation by QUIZOM might be limited by simultaneous immune suppression mediated by leukemia cells54 in the BM milieu or prior leukemia treatment55,56. These limitations should not negate the findings of the present study, but the reported therapeutic mechanisms of QUIZOM might open up new avenues for intervention to potentiate the disruption of proteostasis and immune activation.

Methods

Detailed descriptions of materials and methods, including cell culture, xenotransplantation studies, translatome and transcriptome analyses, are described in the Supplementary Note 2.

Isotope-metabolic pulse labeling and drug treatments

AML cells were cultured for 24 h, rinsed with PBS, and cultured in arginine, lysine, and methionine-depleted RPMI for 30 min and modified RPMI containing heavy lysine (13C6-13N2, 0.8 mM Lys8), heavy arginine (13C6-13N4, 0.8 mM Arg10), azidohomoalanine (1 mM AHA) and 10% dialyzed fetal bovine serum. They were treated with quizartinib (2 nM), omacetaxine mepesuccinate (OM, 10 nM), QUIZOM (quizartinib 2 nM + OM 10 nM), or 0.1% DMSO control for 4 hours and lysed. Newly synthesized proteins (NSPs) were isolated by click-chemistry, digested, subjected to tandem mass tag (TMT) multiplexed labeling and deep-scale liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry / mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) (Supplementary Note 2). Translatome data were quantified using Proteome Discoverer and MSstatsTMT. Changes in NSP upon drug treatments with a q-value < 0.05 and a log2 Fold change (FC) ≥ 0.38 or ≤−0.38 were further analyzed. Translatome analysis was described in detail in the Supplementary Note 2.

Single-cell RNA sequencing and data processing

Cryopreserved serial samples from AML patients enrolled in the QUIZOM trial were thawed and subjected to encapsulation and library preparation immediately with ChromiumTM Single Cell 5’ v2 Reagent Kit. Paired-end 151 bp reads were generated by Illumina NovaSeq 6000. Cell Ranger (version 6.0.0) was applied for quantification57. Seurat was then used for downstream analysis58. Cells with fewer than 200 detectable genes or with more than 15% counts mapped to mitochondrial genes were removed59. Data normalization was carried out with SCTransform60. Data integration was performed using “FindIntegrationAnchors” and “IntegrateData” functions in Seurat. Unsupervised clustering was conducted with Louvain algorithm and serial resolutions from 0.4 to 561. The stability of clusters was examined and an optimal resolution was determined by Clustree62. Marker genes for each cluster were identified and published haematopoietic cell markers were used to annotate 15 major cell types40,63. Details about single-cell analysis were included in the Supplementary Note 2.

Patients and treatment

Relapse/refractory FLT3-ITD AML patients ≥18 years old and newly diagnosed FLT3-ITD AML patients ≥65 years old unfit for conventional chemotherapy were recruited into a phase II single-arm open-labeled study (ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT03135054) with informed consent and approval from Institutional Review Board of the University of Hong Kong/Hospital Authority Hong Kong West Cluster (Ref no.: UW 17-099 and UW 05-183 T/846). Among the 45 patients recruited, one patient died before treatment, and 4 patients subsequently withdrew from the trial based on the discretion of treating physicians.

Treatment comprised quizartinib 30 mg daily p.o. and OM at 2 mg daily i.v. for 7 days (1st cycle) or 5 days (subsequent cycles) for every 21–28 days until HSCT or disease progression. Patients achieving complete remission (CR) or complete remission with incomplete hematologic recovery (CRi) were referred for HSCT. Quizartinib was given as post-HSCT maintenance for at least 24 months after engraftment at doses ranging from 30 mg once per day twice weekly to 30 mg daily depending on tolerability.

Primary endpoint was composite complete remission (CRc) defined by CR or CRi according to the ELN guideline 20222. Secondary endpoints were the percentage of patients who were bridged to HSCT, leukemia-free (LFS) and overall survivals (OS) (Details refer to the Study Protocol).

Untargeted metabolomics analyses

Around 150 samples comprising FLT3-ITD cells under different combinations of QUIZOM treatments, as well as primary FLT3-ITD AML enriched with LSC, i.e., the triple positive (TP) cells or unselected AML cells in bulk, underwent untargeted metabolomics profiling by liquid chromatography-high resolution mass spectrometry. The untargeted metabolomics procedures were described in detail in the Supplementary Note 2.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square test and logistic regression, whereas continuous variables were compared using the t-test, Mann-Whitney U test, and Wilcoxon signed rank test. Survival analysis was performed using the Kaplan-Meier estimator. LFS and OS were visualized by Kaplan-Meier curves and described by median survival time. Statistical comparisons between two populations were performed by the log-rank test. The impacts of various clinical factors on disease outcome were analyzed by univariate analysis using Cox regression.

Experimental data were presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) from at least three independent experiments. Mouse survival after AML cell line engraftment was evaluated by Kaplan-Meier analysis. All statistical tests were carried out using GraphPad Prism 8.0 or R. p-values were shown in all figures.
