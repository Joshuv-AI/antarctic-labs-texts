Promoting Electrocatalytic Oxygen Reactions Using Advanced Heterostructures for Rechargeable Zinc–Air Battery Applications
Authors: Dingrong Qiu, Huihui Wang, Tingting Ma, Jiangdu Huang, Zhen Meng, Dayong Fan, Chris R Bowen, Huidan Lu, Yongping Liu, Sundaram Chandrasekaran
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn order to facilitate electrochemical oxygen reactions in electrically rechargeable zinc–air batteries (ZABs), there is a need to develop innovative approaches for efficient oxygen electrocatalysts. Due to their reliability, high energy density, material abundance, and ecofriendliness, rechargeable ZABs hold promise as next-generation energy storage and conversion devices. However, the large-scale application of ZABs is currently hindered by the slow kinetics of the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) and the oxygen evolution reaction (OER). However, the development of heterostructure-based electrocatalysts has the potential to surpass the limitations imposed by the intrinsic properties of a single material. This Account begins with an explanation of the configurations of ZABs and the fundamentals of the oxygen electrochemistry of the air electrode. Then, we summarize recent progress with respect to the variety of heterostructures that exploit bifunctional electrocatalytic reactions and overview their impact on ZAB performance. The range of heterointerfacial engineering strategies for improving the ORR/OER and ZAB performance includes tailoring the surface chemistry, dimensionality of catalysts, interfacial charge transfer, mass and charge transport, and morphology. We highlight the multicomponent design approaches that take these features into account to create advanced highly active bifunctional catalysts. Finally, we discuss the challenges and future perspectives on this important topic that aim to enhance the bifunctional activity and performance of zinc–air batteries.Keywords: Bifunctional electrocatalysts, Heterointerfaces, Oxygen reduction reaction, Oxygen evolution reaction, ORR/OER, Zinc-air batteries, Rechargeable batteries, Energy storage

Introduction

Highly efficient sustainable energy conversion and storage technologies have received significant interest due to the rising energy demand from net zero carbon emissions, electric vehicles, and consumable electronics.1−3 Due to their range of potential benefits, which include safety of operation and high theoretical energy density, metal–air batteries, such as zinc–air batteries (ZABs), show significant promise compared to other batteries,4,5 where the redox reaction between the metal electrode and air electrode in a zinc–air battery (ZAB) results in the generation of electrical energy.6−8 Notably, platinum (Pt) group-based noble metal materials such as Pt/C are regarded as an ideal electrocatalyst for oxygen reduction (ORR), while RuO2 and IrO2 are regarded as effective electrocatalysts for oxygen evolution reactions (OER); however, the major challenges include not only their high-cost and scarcity but also the effect of catalyst poisoning.4,9−12 Additional shortcomings include a lack of catalytic bifunctionality and low durability.13−16 Therefore, it is common practice to combine both ORR and OER catalysts, where each provides an exceptional function, to develop a bifunctional oxygen electrocatalyst for the discharge/charge process in rechargeable ZABs. However, their low power density and poor stability due to a slow oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) during discharge and a slow oxygen evolution reaction (OER) during charging limits their widespread use.14,17−19 As a result, there is a need to create reliable, effective, and low-cost robust bifunctional oxygen electrocatalysts to improve the power density, recharge capability, and stability of ZABs by increasing the oxygen reaction kinetics.

Generally, bifunctional ORR and OER electrocatalysis for rechargeable ZABs occur only at the interface of a solid catalyst, liquid electrolyte, and gaseous products.20−24 Hence, the development of low-dimensional electrocatalysts has advanced significantly over the past few decades, in particular in the areas of active site engineering and the creation of efficient catalysts. However, the performance of commonly studied bifunctional electrocatalysts based on low-dimensional nanocatalysts remains unsatisfactory.25−27 The bifunctional electrocatalytic activity and the corresponding ZAB performance are rarely improved by optimizing a single physical property, such as the Gibbs free energy of adsorption–desorption for reaction intermediates. This is a result of the stability and activity of catalysts being also influenced by the local reaction environment, which is highly connected to the performance of a rechargeable ZAB.28−30 This indicates that there is a significant gap between present electrocatalyst research, which has a focus on conditions of high half-wave potential for ORR and low overpotential for the OER, and the practical applications of rechargeable ZABs. Research on heterostructures to date are either based on metal-free and/or transition metals, and their combinations, that have emerged as alternatives to the bifunctional precious metal-based oxygen electrocatalysts, where the performance of the heterostructures is closely related to the practical application of ZAB technologies.31−36 In the past few years, an increasing number of heterostructured catalysts have been designed and tested for ORR, OER, and rechargeable ZABs with industrially relevant performance (see Scheme 1); this has included tailoring a range of functional properties by increasing the number of exposed active sites, electronic structure modulation, heterointerface formation, strain engineering, defect engineering, and electrical conductivity enhancement by synergetic coupling of various metals or nonmetals.33,37−40 For example, there are more than 6000 papers related to ORR, 5000 papers on the OER, and 2000 papers on traditional bifunctional electrocatalysis in 2019, among more than 4000 papers with the area of heterostructured bifunctional electrocatalysts for ZABs (Figure 1). However, the number of papers on heterostructured bifunctional electrocatalysts for ZABs continues to grow; the research field is still relatively progressive when compared to research of highly active ORR and OER catalysts. Also, in the past decades, several reviews have focused on carbon based materials as cathode catalysts for ZABs.41−45 In addition, Dias et al.46 discussed transition metal chalcogenides carbon-based cathode catalysts, while Chen et al.47 overviewed recent advances in the bifunctional activity of ternary metal sulfides, without emphasis on the ZABs. Leong et al.48 discussed advancements in the Zn-anode, electrolytes, and oxygen catalysts and, recently, Lv et al.49 and Nazir et al.43 provided a general discussion on rechargeable ZABs. In addition, Bi et al.50 discussed the fundamental promises of rechargeable zinc–air versus lithium–air battery for industrial applications. However, there is a need to review the most recent advances in the heterostructured electrocatalysts in rechargeable ZABs, where it is clear that more work is necessary to develop heterostructured bifunctional electrocatalysts for ZABs, in particular due to their importance for the widespread practical application of ZAB technologies.

In light of recent advances in the intriguing area of the design of heterostructured bifunctional electrocatalysts for rechargeable ZAB technologies, this review first highlights developments in the design of a range of heterostructured bifunctional electrocatalysts. The heterostructured catalysts considered here are those commonly examined as bifunctional ORR and OER reactions for rechargeable ZABs unless otherwise indicated. Such a threshold in the bifunctional ORR and OER potential difference (ΔE = Ej=10 – E1/2) of the robust heterostructures was selected since the bifunctional operation and performance is usually higher than the combination of precious metal-based electrocatalysts (e.g., Pt/C+RuO2 or Pt/C+IrO2) for commercial ZAB technologies. Following a discussion of recent developments and prospects in the multiscale design of heterostructured catalysts, several important factors influencing the bifunctional electrocatalytic reactions and rechargeable ZAB performance of catalysts are examined; these include catalyst dimensionality, surface chemistry, morphology, electron transport pathway, and catalyst–electrolyte interactions for a variety of heterostructured catalysts. Finally, we propose future research directions with existing challenges in this vital topic to further enhance the bifunctional electrocatalytic and rechargeable ZAB performance.

Impact of Bifunctional Oxygen Reactions in Rechargeable Zn–Air Batteries

A zinc–air battery (ZAB) is a form of renewable energy technology that generates electricity through a redox reaction between a Zn metal electrode and an air electrode.25,39,51,52 Rechargeable ZAB technologies have received significant attention recently because of their potential in future mobile power supplies and consumer electronics industries. Typically, rechargeable ZABs offer significant benefits compared to other battery systems in several ways. For example, because of their high open circuit voltage, high power density, extended operational life, and relatively low objective energy density (<350 W h kg–1), metal-ion batteries (particularly lithium and sodium ion batteries) have demonstrated widespread applicability in electronic devices. Furthermore, the higher energy and power density of proton exchange membrane fuel cells (PEMFCs) makes them a desirable alternative.25,36,39,51 In contrast, rechargeable ZABs exhibit several favorable capabilities when compared to other potential alternatives. These include an economical cost (today ∼$100 per kW h; and potentially expected to be as low as ∼$10 per kW h in the future), high theoretical energy density (∼1353 W h kg–1), environmentally sustainable practices, and exceptional safety.25,53 As a result, ZABs are regarded as a promising alternative for next-generation innovative energy devices.

Rechargeable ZABs generally consist of a zinc (Zn) electrode, an air electrode (which contains an oxygen electrocatalyst(s), a gas diffusion layer, and a current collector), an electrolyte (typically an alkaline solution at a high concentration), and a separator, as illustrated in Figure 2. In general, rechargeable ZABs can be categorized into two different types, namely electrically and mechanically rechargeable ZABs.47,54 The replacement of the Zn electrode and electrolyte can be used to externally recharge mechanically rechargeable ZABs.55−57 However, it is worth noting that both types of recharging are possible and are done in rechargeable ZAB. Mechanically rechargeable ZABs were less common due to their high cost, short lifespan, erratic operation, and the emerging popularity of Li-ion batteries.57 Though, materials engineering offers fascinating potential for the design of electrically rechargeable ZABs that require bifunctional oxygen electrocatalysts that enable both the OER during the recharging process and the ORR during the discharging process. Typically, the working principle of rechargeable ZAB is based on redox reactions between the zinc metal electrode and air electrode.58−60 The metallic Zn electrode will experience an oxidation reaction as the discharging process is taking place. This process is also accompanied by the generation and transportation of electrons, while oxygen at the air electrode and the triple interface created by solid (electrocatalysts)–liquid (electrolyte) and gas (oxygen) undergoes a reduction process to produce OH–.3,25,61−63 During the charging process, OH– at the triple-phase interfaces of the air electrode experiences an oxidation reaction to generate O2, while the divalent zinc on the zinc electrode takes part in a reduction reaction to form metallic Zn. Owing to the multiple electron transfer process, the primary reaction sites of rechargeable ZABs are at the surface/interface of the cathode and anode, and the characteristic properties of these sites have a direct impact on the overall performance of rechargeable ZABs.32,63,68 More importantly, whatever zinc electrode structure is used, the formation of zinc dendrites can significantly impact battery security, lifetime, and performance, regardless of the electrode structure.69−75 In addition, the non-negligible hydrogen evolution (HER) process in alkaline conditions can cause the electrode to corrode and decrease its Coulombic efficiency.76−79 Under strong alkaline conditions, zinc would also be passivated to some extent, producing a nonconductive layer of zinc oxide. These issues have severely impacted the lifetime and consistency of ZABs, and they create several risk considerations that hinder their widespread application. Currently, two major approaches have been used to resolve zinc electrode challenges, this includes surface treatment or adding additives (e.g., copper oxide) to zinc electrodes without modifying the zinc itself.80 The alternative option is to modify the zinc material to create a zinc sponge or composite material that can replace pure zinc as the electrode material for ZABs, such as by modifying or alloying with metallic components.69,81,82 Before assessing rechargeable ZABs, several considerations must be taken into account. (i) The overall reaction indicates that a ZAB has a high open circuit voltage of ∼1.66 V; however, polarization of the zinc electrode and air electrode can result in a voltage drop. To evaluate the degree of polarization of a ZAB, an important metric in this regard is the open circuit voltage. (ii) The ratio of discharge capacity to total zinc metal consumed can be used to evaluate the energy density of a ZAB. (iii) The power density of a ZAB is directly proportional to the catalyst activity and oxygen transfer efficiency, since it is the ratio of power output to catalyst weight or electrode area. (iv) The rate performance of a ZAB refers to the output voltage at a variety of discharge current densities. (v) During the charging and discharging process, the polarization of the charge–discharge process is represented by the discrepancy between the theoretical open circuit voltage and the charge–discharge voltage. (vi) Stability is often measured in terms of charge–discharge cycles for prolonged time periods. (vii) A rechargeable ZAB energy utilization efficiency is measured by its round-trip efficiency, defined as the ratio of the charge to discharge energy capacity where the voltage is also considered with units of mWh, e.g., 5 min at −5 mA and 1.2 V, 5 min at +5 mA and 2.1 V, and the efficiency of ∼57%. Typically, the complex triple-phase reaction environment in the charging (OER process) and discharging (ORR process) processes leads to slow kinetic activity and mass transfer resistance, which typically limits the round-trip efficiency of ZABs (∼55–65%); it also greatly limits the large-scale application of rechargeable ZABs.25,32,68,83 Hence, the rational engineering of the surface and interface of an air electrode is of significance (see Scheme 2). As a result, the use of surface/interface engineering to regulate the reaction energy barrier, electrical conductivity, reaction surface area, and mass transfer behavior in rechargeable ZABs can play a vital role in enhancing performance and is crucial for addressing the aforementioned issues. The electrocatalysts and air electrode in the electrode of a rechargeable ZAB form a triple-phase interface reaction environment (solid–liquid–gas). The kinetic activity of the ORR and OER is determined by the surface/interface properties of electrocatalysts, whereas the surface/interface sites of air electrodes are crucial for the efficient loading of an electrocatalyst and the rapid transportation of oxygen molecules, electrolyte, and electrons.19,25,84−87 Therefore, careful multiscale interfacial regulation may effectively regulate the electrochemical efficiency, which is highly dependent on the triple-phase reaction interface of the air electrode and the interface structure of bifunctional electrocatalysts. The next sections provide a more in-depth discussion of these challenges.

Strategies on Interfacial Engineering of Bifunctional Oxygen Electrocatalysts for Rechargeable Zinc–Air Batteries (ZABs)

Impact of Heterostructure Design for Bifunctional Electrocatalysis and ZABs

In oxygen-involved heterogeneous electrocatalytic reactions, the reactant molecules are adsorbed, activated, and transformed into oxygen-containing species at the electrocatalyst surface and interface sites.24,25,33,88 For example, during the OER process, hydroxide ions (OH–) initially migrate from the bulk electrolyte and subsequently adsorb onto the active sites. Then, there is an oxidation process to form an adsorbed OH* intermediate, which is then further oxidized to form O* and OOH* intermediates. Following the sequence of surface reactions, such as bond formation/breaking, it is transformed to O2 and released from active sites.88−91 It is worth noting that ORR proceeds via a reversed version of OER; however, it is difficult for an electrocatalyst to simultaneously possess the optimum catalytic performance for both reactions due to their distinctive needs since the oxygen reaction intermediates such as O*, OH*, OOH*, and O2 can have an impact on the catalytic capabilities of the electrocatalysts for both the ORR and OER. In addition, based on the Sabatier principle, the adsorption strength of oxygen reaction intermediates on active sites should neither be too strong, nor too weak. During the ORR process, the rate-determining step (RDS) is small and the reaction steps are a downhill Gibbs free energy.53,91,92 However, every OER reaction step is upward with the RDS being the largest step. As a result, an optimal ORR catalyst is often ineffective for the OER reaction and vice versa. The need for a potential difference (ΔE) between the OER low overpotential to reach a current density of 10 mA cm–2 (E10) and the ORR half-wave potential (E1/2), where ΔE = Ej=10 – E1/2, is a crucial factor in the development of high-performance bifunctional ORR/OER electrocatalysts and is often used as a measure of bifunctional activity.53,92 Thus optimizing the adsorption/desorption behavior of oxygen reaction using rich active sites and heterointerface engineering is a viable technique for creating high-performance ORR/OER bifunctional electrocatalysts.

The ORR/OER reaction kinetic activity and the oxygen mass transfer at the triple-phase interfaces are both influenced by the surface/interface architectures of the electrocatalyst.39,93,94 Notably, in a rechargeable ZAB, the enrichment of electrocatalytic efficiency is highly dependent on the triple-phase (solid–liquid–gas) interface environment.92,95 Hence, developing bifunctional electrocatalysts with an optimum interface for effective mass transfer and more exposed active sites for improving activity is of importance. An adequate amount of contact between electrocatalysts, liquid aqueous electrolytes, and oxygen (O2) reactants is necessary for the gas-consuming ORR process that occurs during the discharge reaction. As a gas evolution process, the OER requires the rapid removal of the O2 product for optimal performance. By balancing these oxygen gas effects, a bifunctional electrocatalyst with a well-designed structure can meet the combined requirements of the ORR and OER processes.95,96 From this perspective, the bifunctional electrocatalytic ORR and OER performance is highly influenced by the mass transport capacity, charge transfer efficiency, density of the accessible active sites, and intrinsic reactivity of the electrocatalysts. These behaviors can be regulated and enriched using surface and interface engineering technologies to form heterostructure-based catalysts. In recent years, heterostructure-based surface and interface engineering has received significant attention for the ORR/OER and ZABs.25,33,96 To improve electrocatalytic performance, approaches such as morphology tuning, strain/defecting engineering, electronic structure engineering via constructing heterointerfaces, nanoconfinement effects, crystallographic tailoring, regulation of the catalyst/substrate interface, and catalyst/electrolyte interface engineering have been developed and employed (see Scheme 2).97−99

Fine-tuning the electronic structure of an electrocatalyst is a common technique to regulate the adsorption behavior of any reaction intermediates,25,53 since electronic interactions at the interface of a catalyst can play a critical role in modulating the electronic structure of the active species.99 Interfacial effects are a result of the combination of two or more components in a heterostructure that can be triggered by changes in the Fermi level, geometric structure, and electron affinity. The Fermi level mismatch can lead to a redistribution of charge at the interface, and the built-in electric field that results from this process can lead to charge separation. Considering the geometric structure, dislocations at the lattice can lead to a generation of a tensile or compressive strain, where a shift in the d-band center also leads to the formation of defects.93,100 Furthermore, charge transfer via interfacial chemical bonding can be triggered by differences in electronic affinities, and by carefully modulating the electronic nature, these activities can increase the intrinsic activity of the catalyst.12,101,102 In addition, it is possible to increase the number of exposed active sites by reducing the particle size to attain a large exposed surface area. There is also potential to modulate the interface through heterostructure formation, which can preserve the structural integrity of the high density of exposed active sites while maintaining structural stability in the long term. The structure–activity scaling relationship of heterogeneous catalysts is another aspect that limits catalyst activity.103−105 Typically, oxygen electrocatalysis involves a number of reaction intermediates with a multistep electron transfer mechanism, and the adsorption of these intermediates is interconnected, so that optimizing the bonding strength is beneficial. Breaking such a scaling law is possible through the formation of a heterostructure and interfacial engineering, to thereby combine robust catalysts with complementary activities.62,103,106−109 Notably, the aforementioned effects are typically connected, either directly or indirectly, and collectively they form the foundation for heterointerface engineering for energy conversion and storage.

Strategic Heterostructures for Enhancing ORR/OER and ZABs

Metal-Free Heterostructures for ORR/OER and ZABs

Carbon-based metal-free materials have excellent electrical conductivity and diversified morphologies, high specific surface area, and high mechanical strength and can achieve synergistic effects by composite design, defect engineering, and doping modification, thereby improving bifunctional electrocatalytic activity and stability. These factors can enhance the energy density, power density, and life cycle of ZABs.84,110−113 Therefore, it is of interest to employ bifunctional catalysts with excellent activity toward both the ORR and the OER by fabricating heterogeneous structures containing carbon materials and active OER catalytic materials. For example, as shown in Figure 3(i), Zhao et al.114 reported that a N-SMCTs@N-rGO heterostructure with a directional interface electron transfer resulted in dual-site collaborative adsorption and significantly lower internal resistance. In addition, rapid electron transfer and more active sites were produced by coupling higher Fermi level N-SMCTs with N-rGO to activate directional interfacial electron transfer. In addition, the creation of submicron-sized N-SMCTs prevented the stacking of two-dimensional graphene sheets and provided a highly open and rapid mass diffusion pathway. As a result, the N-SMCTs@N-rGO catalyst exhibited an excellent E1/2 of ∼0.87 V vs RHE with a durability of over 20 h, greater than N-SMCTs and N-rGO. In addition, during the OER reaction, the N-SMCTs@N-rGO catalyst required a low OER potential of ∼351 mV to reach a current density of 10 mA cm–2, it was higher than those of N-SMCTs (∼518 mV) and N-rGO (∼496 mV). Moreover, the N-SMCTs@N-rGO based ZAB exhibited a high power density of ∼126 mW cm–2 at 165 mA cm–2, greater than that of Pt/C+IrO2 (∼112 mW cm–2).

Xu et al.24 developed a fabrication strategy based on metal–organic frameworks to produce a hybrid material based on 2D nitrogen-doped carbon nanotubes/graphene (GNCNTs) nanocatalysts; see Figure 3(ii). Due to the enriched electronic structure modulation as a result of a synergistic hybrid coupling between the hierarchical structure and doping of heteroatom effects, the optimal GNCNTs-4 based rechargeable ZAB delivered a high specific capacity of 801 mAh g Zn1– and maximum power density of ∼253 mW cm–2 with excellent cyclic stability of over 3000 h at 5 mA cm–2, this outperformed commercial precious metal catalyst-based ZAB. In addition, in a N,S-GOQD-RGO/CNT heterostructure, the incorporation of CNTs prevented the stacking of graphene nanosheets, while the incorporation of N and S doping and graphene quantum dots (GOQDs) could provide an active heterointerface and thus enhance charge transport via electronic structure modulation.115 As a consequence, the N,S-GOQD-RGO/CNT heterostructured hybrid showed an excellent E1/2 value of ∼0.84 V vs RHE and a higher current density of ∼5.88 mA cm–2. As a bifunctional catalyst, the N,S-GOQD-RGO/CNT heterostructured catalyst exhibited a lower potential difference (ΔE) of ∼0.78 V and a long-term stability. Furthermore, the N,S-GOQD-RGO/CNT based ZAB exhibited an excellent open circuit potential of ∼1.414 V, and a maximum power density of ∼134.3 mW cm–2, this was higher than a Pt/C+Ru/C based ZAB. As shown in Figure 3(iii), it has been shown that van der Waals heterostructures produced from a combination of 2D materials and graphitic carbon significantly enhance the bifunctional electrocatalytic reactions and rechargeable ZAB performance through improved electronic conduction.116 In addition, nitrogen and phosphorus-doped carbon nanotubes (CNTs) loaded on the microfolds of graphene (CGFs) exhibited a mesoporous nature with a large specific surface area, which allows more exposed carbon skeleton of CGFs with abundant active sites.110 As a result, the hybrid catalyst exhibited excellent bifunctional electrocatalytic performance with a ΔE of ∼0.9 V, long-term durability, and high methanol tolerance, see Table 1.

Robust Bifunctional Electrocatalytic ORR/OER Activity and Their Zinc–Air Battery (ZAB) Performance of Reported Advanced Heterostructured Catalysts

MXene-Based Heterostructures for ORR/OER and ZABs

MXene is an emerging family of 2D nanomaterials with excellent electronic, optical, mechanical, and electrochemical properties.117−119 Due to its large specific surface area and flexibility of surface termination, MXene-based heterostructures have been used as an air electrode catalyst to mediate the ORR/OER in ZABs.118,120 For example, Han et al.121 prepared a multidimensional hierarchical CoS2@MXene using CoS2 nanowires and MXene nanosheets through selective etching and exfoliation by a facile hydrothermal reaction followed by a sulfurization process. The CoS2@MXene material was produced by using 10 mL of MXene (referred to as CoS2@10MXene) and exhibited the best OER performance with the lowest overpotential of ∼270 mV at 10 mA cm–2. Specifically, the overpotential was smaller than those of other samples including CoS2 (∼323 mV), bare MXene (∼431 mV), and RuO2 (∼302 mV). Notably, the mixed CoS2 and MXene (CoS2+MXene) showed a higher OER overpotential compared to CoS2@MXene. This result indicates that, in a CoS2@MXene electrocatalyst, the stronger electronegativity of MXene compared to CoS2 can accelerate facile charge transfer from CoS2 to MXene, which results in the modulation of surface electronic structure. In addition, CoS2@MXene also exhibited an excellent electrocatalytic HER activity in terms of a smaller overpotential of 175 mV compared to CoS2 (∼225 mV) and MXene (∼260 mV) at 10 mA cm–2; this was ascribed to the synergistic effect between CoS2 and MXene. Moreover, the CoS2@MXene exhibited a higher ORR onset potential (Eonset) of ∼0.87 V vs RHE and a higher E1/2 value (0.80 V vs RHE) compared to CoS2 and MXene. This enhancement in electrocatalytic performance was attributed to a synergistic coupling between CoS2 and MXene, while the isolated 2D MXene with a large specific surface area can provide more anchored sites for active CoS2. In addition, the distinctive structure based on CoS2 nanowires parallelly grown on MXene nanosheets can improve interfacial effects between the electrode and electrolyte; it also provides rich reaction sites and promotes the mass transport of reactants to enhance the electrocatalytic activity and stability. As a consequence, a typical sandwich structured ZAB made by CoS2@MXene-based air electrode exhibited a higher peak power density of ∼29 mW cm–2 compared to Pt/C based ZAB (∼21 mW cm–2). During discharge performance testing, CoS2@MXene is stable at ∼1.28 and 1.23 V with current densities of ∼1 and 3 mA cm–2, respectively. Furthermore, the CoS2@MXene-based air electrode displayed significant long-term durability with a small decay in the voltage gap.

Lei et al.122 prepared NiCo2O4/MXene, where the ultrasmall-sized NiCo2O4 nanoparticles were decorated on a few-layered MXene surface by an in situ one-pot hydro-thermal process; see Figure 4(i). Interestingly, the NiCo2O4/MXene achieves a higher E1/2 value of ∼0.7 V vs RHE and a larger limiting current density (−5.82 mA cm–2) compared to bare MXene and NiCo2O4. The ORR limiting current density of NiCo2O4/MXene was similar to that of Pt/C, indicating the considerable catalytic performance of the ORR. In addition, the NiCo2O4/MXene catalyst reached a low OER overpotential of ∼310 mV at 10 mA cm–2, which is superior to those of NiCo2O4 and MXene individually. As a consequence, the ZAB manufactured using the NiCo2O4/MXene electrode attained a high open-circuit potential (Voc) of ∼1.40 V, and a power density of ∼277 mW cm–2. This suggests the superior activity of the NiCo2O4/MXene electrode could be attributed to the NiCo2O4 nanocrystals that are grown and stabilized by Ni/Co–F bonds that acted as electrocatalytic sites for ORR and OER. Notably, using NEXAFS analysis, it was observed that Ni/Co–F bonds lead to a synergistic effect in terms of electron cloud migration, resulting in a facile charge transport during catalytic reactions. In addition, a heterostructure based on (MnCo2O4/N-GQD/MXene) and Ti3C2Tx (MXene) nanosheets decorated with spinel manganese–cobalt oxide (MnCo2O4) nanoparticles and N-doped-graphene quantum dots (N-GQD) is also a high performance electrocatalyst for the reversible ORR and OER in rechargeable ZABs.123 Notably, the MnCo2O4/NGQD/MXene based ZAB exhibited an excellent power density of 152.8 mW cm–2, this was higher than that of the Pt/C+IrO2/C (133.5 mW cm–2). Notably, no voltage loss was observed during long discharge times. Furthermore, the specific capacity of MnCo2O4/NGQD/MXene based battery, that was standardized to the amount of the Zn used, was 753 mAh gZn–1 at j = 10 mA cm–2; this was higher than a Pt/C-IrO2 based battery (722 mAh gZn–1). A zinc–air battery with MnCo2O4/NGQD/MXene also shows a smaller charge–discharge gap from 0.92 to 0.94 V after 300 h. This result is indicative of superior charge–discharge behavior and stability of the MnCo2O4/NGQD/MXene bifunctional ORR/OER electrocatalyst. This enhancement was associated with the high surface area, highly porous structure, and enriched electron conductivity, thereby providing a large number of active sites and channels for electron/ion transfer. As a result of the exceptional morphological features and enriched charge transfer kinetics, the NiCoFe-layered double hydroxides/MXene/N-doped carbon nanotube composite exhibited a higher E1/2 value of ∼0.78 V vs RHE than NiCoFe-LDH and was similar to the Pt/C catalyst.124 During an OER test, the NiCoFe-LDH/MXene/NCNT exhibited a low OER potential of ∼332 mV; this was less than NiCoFe-LDH (∼336 mV), IrO2/C (∼353 mV), Pt/C (∼492 mV) and MXene (∼525 mV) at a current density of 10 mA cm–2. These results demonstrate that the heterostructure formed based on NiCoFe-LDH/MXene/NCNT has the potential to be a promising nonprecious electrocatalyst for the ORR/OER. As a consequence, a ZAB based on the NiCoFeLDH/MXene/NCNT electrocatalyst showed a high specific capacity of ∼753 mAh g–1 at a current density of 10 mA cm–2 and a large energy density of 843 Wh kg–1; this was higher than the Pt/C+IrO2 (826 Wh kg–1). Moreover, the battery retains its high open circuit voltage (OCP) at 1.54 V for a long time period of 80 h. Zong et al.120 observed that in a Nb2CO2@COF nanocatalyst, the oxygen-terminated Nb2CO2 MXene plays an active role in enhancing the hysteresis reaction of oxygen intermediates. Hui et al.125 reported that, in a Ti3C2@SrTiO3 hybrid heterostructure, abundant Ti-vacancy defects formed in the Ti3C2 MXene can optimize the electronic structure and thus accelerate electron transfer to promote electrocatalytic redox kinetics, while the oxygen vacancies produced in the SrTiO3 crystals could strengthen the absorption capability of reaction oxygen intermediates and further improve the bifunctional catalytic activity of SrTiO3, see Figure 4(ii).

Wang et al.126 proposed that spray-lyophilization of 2D sheets of Ti3C2Tx and in situ growth of BCN nanotubes between MXene sheets may increase the specific surface area of the catalyst and expose more active sites; it may also restrict the stacking of MXenes. In this regard, Zhang et al.127 developed a facile one-pot pyrolysis method to prepare a catalyst based on a nanoalloy of FeCo that was encapsulated with N-doped carbon nanotubes (CNT) along with anchored N-doped MXene (Fe/Co-CNT@MXene-T); see Figure 4(iii). Among the range of Fe/Co-CNT@MXene samples examined, Fe/Co-CNT@MXene-8 exhibited the highest E1/2 value of ∼0.85 V vs RHE. In addition, the Fe/Co-CNT@MXene-8 has a smaller OER overpotential than the other compositions and RuO2 at the current density of 10 mA cm–2, while the overpotential of Fe/Co-CNT@MXene-7, Fe/Co-CNT@MXene-8, Fe/Co-CNT@MXene-9 and RuO2 is ∼379 mV, 360 mV, 390 mV, 380 mV. Among all the as-prepared catalysts, Fe/Co-CNT@MXene-8 also shows the smallest ΔE of ∼0.73 V, this value was lesser than a benchmark Pt/C∥RuO2. As a result, the maximum power density of ZAB with Fe/Co-CNT@MXene-8 was as high as 138 mW cm–2; this activity was higher than that of Pt/C∥RuO2. The specific capacity of ZAB with Fe–Co/CNT@MXene-8 reached as high as ∼759 mA h g–1 at 10 mA cm–2; this was 18 mA h g–1 higher than that with Pt/C∥RuO2. In addition, the Fe–Co/CNT@MXene-8 based ZAB exhibited a steady voltage plateau during a 375 h long-term charge–discharge test operation. It has been observed that the high performance and stability could be attributed to the 3D structure. The CNT reorganizes the alignment of the Ti3C2 flakes, leading to facile electronic transport pathways being developed on heterostructures, while the MXene acts as the electrically conductive material and the CoFe nanoalloy embedded CNT acts as the origin of active sites. Thus, due to their enhanced electronic nature, MXene-based heterostructural design and optimization of the composition can be promising approaches for the design of ZABs with high current densities and high capacities.

Precious Metal-Based Heterostructures for ORR/OER and ZABs

Noble metal materials, such as Pt, Pd, RuO2, and IrO2, have proven to be effective electrocatalysts for both the ORR and OER.128−134 However, their large-scale application is hindered by their poor operational stability, CO2 poisoning effects, scarcity, and the high cost of precious metals. To reduce production costs, an effective strategy to improve the activity of catalysts is to combine two or more nonprecious catalytic materials with a small amount of precious metals to construct heterostructures.135,136 These metals are used as catalysts for the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) and oxygen evolution reaction (OER), which are vital for the application of rechargeable zinc–air batteries (ZABs). For example, Kang et al.137 reported that for a Pd/CoOx/d-NC nanocatalyst, the electron-deficient palladium (Pd) and free electron at oxygen vacancies can facilitate the adsorption of oxygen species during the OER reaction, while the hybridization of defect-rich N-doped carbon with catalytically active Pd nanoparticles can improve the ORR activity and enhance the bifunctional activity. As a consequence, an aqueous Zn-air battery based on a Pd/CoOx/d-NC air electrode exhibited a high power density of 184 mW cm–2; this was higher than the benchmark of Pt/C+IrO2. Wang et al.138 reported that in a Ni SAs-Pd@NC, Pd nanocrystals with sub-10 nm sized strongly contribute to the ORR activity, while the Ni–N4 sites with a high atom utilization efficiency and low catalytic barriers are responsible for the OER; this results in a higher selectivity toward the respective ORR in terms of the highest onset potential (Eonset ∼ 0.94 V vs RHE) and half-wave potentials (E1/2 ∼ 0.84 V vs RHE). For the OER the Ni SAs-Pd@NC exhibited a small overpotential of ∼380 mV at 10 mA cm–2, close to that of RuO2 and higher than Ni SAs@NC. As a consequence, a Zn-air battery assembled with a Ni SAs-Pd@NC exhibited a high open circuit voltage (OCV) of ∼1.44 V. In addition, the Ni SAs-Pd@NC ZAB exhibited a maximum power density of ∼134.2 mW cm–2, higher than that of a Pt/C+RuO2-based ZAB (∼109.7 mW cm–2).

Li et al.139 outlined that alloying Pd and Ni could lead to a modulation of both the geometry and electronic structure of reaction sites of a PdNi/Ni@N–C catalyst, which could lead to improved performance of a bifunctional catalyst compared to monometallic Pd and Ni; see Figure 5(i). As a consequence, PdNi/Ni@N–C-based ZABs achieved a specific capacity of ∼719.2 mA h gZn–1 at 5 mA cm–2, corresponding to an energy density of ∼923.3 Wh kgZn–1; this was superior to that of a Pd/C+RuO2 driven battery. As a bifunctional catalyst, the optimal amount of Ru-loaded Ni0.5Co0.5(OH)2/CC nanoneedles (2% Ru-NCO/CC) exhibited excellent OER performance of η50 = 233 mV, η100 = 269 mV compared to 0.02% Ru-NCO (η50 = 291 mV, η100 = 318 mV), 0.2% Ru-NCO (η50 = 281 mV, η100 = 303 mV), and 4% Ru-NCO (η50 = 295 mV, η100 = 318 mV).140 In addition, during ORR testing, 2% Ru-NCO/CC showed a high E1/2 value of ∼0.88 V vs RHE and a high kinetic current density (Jk@0.85 V = 14.65 mA cm–2), greater than that of a commercial noble metal Pt/C (E1/2 = 0.86 V, Jk@0.85 V = 11.83 mA cm–2). It has been observed that Ru doping can modulate the electronic energy state of a NiO/Co3O4 heterostructure and thereby regulate the activation energy and Gibbs free energy of the catalyst. As shown in Figure 5(ii), the excellent water splitting and Zn-air battery performance of the Ru-FeRu@C/NC catalyst was attributed to effective charge transfer from the Fe atom to Ru at the interface of Ru-FeRu@C/NC, that modulated the electronic structure of the heterostructured catalyst and thus improved the reaction kinetics between the electrocatalyst and electrolyte. As a consequence, the Ru-FeRu@C/NC-based battery exhibited a high specific capacity of ∼754.2 mAh g–1, greater than that of an IrO2||Pt/C assembled cell (∼736.8 mAh g–1).141 In addition, the loading of Ru in Co9S8 (Ru–Co9S8) exhibits superior OER activity compared to that in Co9S8. Specially, the 10Ru–Co9S8 material required a low OER overpotential of only 163 mV to reach 10 mA cm–2; this was lower than that of Co9S8 (∼300 mV), 5Ru–Co9S8 (∼268 mV), 20Ru–Co9S8 (∼325 mV), and RuO2 (306 mV).142 In addition, the catalyst demonstrated an excellent durability over 48 h. As an ORR electrocatalyst, the samples had E1/2 values of ∼0.75, 0.79, 0.80, 0.81, and 0.83 V vs RHE, for Co9S8, 5Ru–Co9S8, 10Ru–Co9S8, 20Ru–Co9S8, and Pt/C respectively. In addition, it has been observed that the introduction of Ru can regulate the electronic structure of Co9S8, and hence, the reaction barriers for the OER and ORR were greatly reduced. As a result, the 10Ru–Co9S8 based ZAB exhibited a high open circuit voltage of ∼1.36 V. In addition, the ZAB displayed a maximum peak power density of ∼92.0 mW cm–2; this was comparable to that of Pt/C+RuO2 based ZAB. Of particular interest is that the 10Ru–Co9S8-based ZAB exhibited a long durability over 500 h at 5 mA·cm–2 without any degradation of the charge–discharge voltage gap.

Transition Metal Oxide/Hydroxide-Based Heterostructures for ORR/OER and ZABs

Transition metal oxide/hydroxide-based heterostructures (e.g., Co, Fe, Mn, Ni, and Cu) are electrocatalysts that have attracted interest due to their abundant availability, low cost, and comparable catalytic performance to noble metals.143−150 These electrocatalysts have been used in electrochemical water splitting for sustainable hydrogen production and ZAB applications. Gao et al.151 reported on a heterostructured bifunctional catalyst based on oxides and layered hydroxides (LDH) of CoFeRu-oxides/LDH that were prepared by partially oxidizing LDH to provide additional active sites for ORR and OER. Notably, as a bifunctional catalyst, RuO2, Fe2O3, and LDH exhibited a high activity toward OER, whereas the Co3O4 showed a high reactivity toward ORR. In addition, as shown in Figure 6(i), due to the high electrical contact through the bimetal coupling interactions, the Fe/Co-DHO nanoparticles strongly anchored on the carbon nanotubes (FeCo-DHO/NCNTs) to provide a high ORR half-wave potential (E1/2 ∼ 0.86 V vs RHE) and a low OER potential of ∼1.55 V than Fe-NCNTs and Co-NCNTs, respectively.152 As a ZAB air electrode, the FeCo-DHO/NCNTs showed a high specific capacity of ∼793.0 mAh g–1, corresponding to energy densities of ∼930.6 Wh kg–1; this was higher than that of a Pt/C + RuO2 catalyst (854.3 Wh kg–1). Mondal et al.153 utilized the hydrothermal method to produce a 2D heterojunction of <20 nm thick double perovskite oxide NSs of Pr0.5Ba0.5Mn1.8–xNbxCo0.2O6-δ (PBMNC-x, x = 0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, δ = 0.38–0.59) with NiFe-LDH (PBMNC/LDH-y). Notably, it was observed that the Nb-doping at the B-site of perovskites may increase the net cationic charge, which lowers the oxygen nonstoichiometry. As a result, 5 at. % Nb-doping was identified to exhibit the best ORR/OER bifunctional activity compared to other samples. As shown in Figure 6(ii), Moloudi et al.154 proposed that a Ni–Co–Fe LDH on a Ni nanocone (NiNC)-decorated Ni foam with amorphous trimetal borate (TMB@NiNC), can act as a high performance bifunctional electrocatalyst. As a result of its heterointerface-rich nature and self-reconstruction properties, ZAB based on TMB@NiNC exhibited a high open-circuit voltage of ∼1.477 V and a specific energy of ∼918 W h kgZn–1. In addition, due to the synergetic coupling of NiCo2O4 and NiCoFe–hydroxide, the NiCo2O4@NiCoFe–hydroxide nanoarrays exhibited an excellent ORR and OER activity and good ZAB recharging performance.155 In addition, it has been reported that in a CoN/Co3O4 HNPs@NCNWs catalyst, interfacial engineering of CoN and Co3O4 HNPs with NCNWs can modify the electronic structure, improve the electronic conductivity, enhance the number of active sites, and thereby shorten the transport pathways for electron/reactants during bifunctional electrocatalytic reactions and ZAB testing.156 Zhu et al.157 indicated that establishing a triphase interfacial structure by integrating a highly crystallized FexNi metal alloy, carbon shell, and tunable outer active FeOy layer could lead to facile charge transfer across a thin oxide layer at the interface, thus significantly increasing intrinsic activity during oxygen catalysis.

Transition Metal Chalcogenide-Based Heterostructures for ORR/OER and ZABs

Recently, transition metal chalcogenides have been increasingly important in energy conversion and storage applications due to their low cost, high electronic conductivity, abundant redox chemistry, excellent electrochemical activities, and ease of production.158−160 Moreover, innovative features, functionalities, and improved performance for a variety of applications have been achieved by combining two or more constituents into a heterostructure. For example, Gui et al.161 reported that the synergistic coupling among ultrafine Co0.85Se nanoparticles and N-doped CNFs heterostructures can lead to enriched electrochemical active sites for both the ORR and OER. As a result, a Co0.85Se@CNFs heterostructure exhibited a high E1/2 value of ∼0.84 V vs RHE and a low OER potential of ∼1.58 V vs RHE at 10 mA cm–2. It was observed that, in a CoSe@NMC heterostructure, a nitrogen-doped mesoporous carbon (NMC) may also modify the electronic nature of CoSe nanocrystals.162 In addition, as a result of the formation of a heterointerface, the high percentage of Ni3+ in the nanoparticles of Ni0.85Se–NHCS could serve as active sites for a bifunctional electrocatalyst toward ORR/OER.163 As a result, ZAB formed from a Ni0.85Se–NHCS electrode exhibited a high discharging–charging performance and extended lifetime. Zheng et al.164 outlined the synthesis of different interfacial densities of NiSe2/CoSe2 heterostructures by an ion injection method. Their investigation revealed that the introduction of an interface at atomic levels can potentially affect the bimetallic Ni–Co active sites (while the Ni2+ is easier to activate than Co2+), and facilitate electronic coupling between the selenides located within the core and the in situ formed oxides and hydroxides to decrease the oxidation overpotential. Due to enriched active sites as a result of the heterointerface formed the Co0.85Se-NHCS mixed with Ni0.85Se-NHCS (i.e., Ni0.85Se/Co0.85Se-NHCS-2) exhibited an enhanced ORR and OER catalytic activity compared to single selenide catalysts, see Figure 7(i).165 Notably, the OER and ORR enhancement of Ni0.85Se/Co0.85Se-NHCS-2 was attributed to the enriched Ni3+ in Ni0.85Se-NHCS and abundant Co2+ in Co0.85Se-NHCS active sites, respectively. Moreover, a ZAB based on a Ni0.85Se/Co0.85Se-NHCS-2 catalyst exhibited an outstanding charge and discharge performance and high stability compared with precious metal-based catalysts. In addition, due to modulation of the electronic structure, a ZAB based on a bifunctional electrocatalysts of Co9S8/Co1–xS@NSC demonstrated an excellent open circuit potential of ∼1.48 V, while the maximum power density was ∼141.9 mW cm–2 at 189.2 mA cm–2, and outperformed the Pt/C+IrO2 catalyst-based system (∼116.3 mW cm–2 at 157.1 mA cm–2).166 Peng et al.167 proposed that the incorporation of an optimal amount of ZnS into N and S-doped carbon could be beneficial to regulate the bifunctional catalytic properties through electronic coupling and an increase in the number of active sites for the bifunctional electrocatalytic reactions. In addition, Shang et al.168 reported on a MoS2@Co–Fe heterojunction structure with a nitrogen-doped carbon, which could optimize the electronic structure of the active centers for ORR and OER, and thereby enhance ZAB performance. Cao et al.169 demonstrated that creating heterointerfaces of NiS2/CoS2 nanohybrids could accelerate the rapid charge transfer rate during bifunctional catalysis, see Figure 7(ii). As a result, the NiS2/CoS2 displayed OER potential of ∼295 mV at 10 mA cm–2; this was ∼61, 92 and 101, 61, and 92 mV lower than that of Ir/C, NiS2, and CoS2, respectively. However, after the OER reaction, the structure of NiS2/CoS2 showed some agglomeration and an amorphous nature, which may be due to the formation of an amorphous layer on the surface of the NiS2/CoS2 nano hybrid electrode. Meanwhile, the NiS2/CoS2 displayed a low ORR onset potential (Eonset) of ∼0.90 V vs RHE and a E1/2 value of ∼0.79 V vs RHE. In addition, the ZAB based on NiS2/CoS2 electrode displayed a high power density of 101 mW cm–2, higher than a Pt/C+Ir/C system. The improved ZAB performance of NiS2/CoS2 was attributed to the synergistic effect between the two domains that resulted in improved electron conductivity and increased catalytic active sites as well as optimized the chemisorption/desorption efficiency and improved rate of mass transfer.

Transition Metal Phosphides-Based Heterostructures for ORR/OER and ZABs

Recent research has shown that heterostructured transition metal phosphides (TMPs) are superior to single transition metal phosphides in terms of catalytic activity.170−174 This is because TMP-based heterostructures can enhance conductivity and surface reaction active sites, leading to superior catalytic and ZAB performance. For example, Shi et al.175 proposed that, in a Co2P-NPC/CF catalyst, the simultaneous doping of P and N atoms in carbon may induce an enhanced coupling of Co2P NPs with N, P-co-doped carbon, which can improve the exposure of bifunctional catalytic active sites and ZAB performance. In addition, due to the use of multicomponent hollow spheres of CoPx wrapped in N-doped graphitic carbon, the CoxP-NC-420 showed the highest ORR activity with an excellent E1/2 value of 0.860 V vs RHE; this was higher than that of 0.824 V vs RHE for Pt/C.176 In addition, CoxP-NC-420 showed the lowest OER overpotential of 273 mV and a Tafel slope of 81 mV dec–1. As a result of the effect of the use of a multicomponent system, a charge transfer resistance of 87.8 Ω was attained for CoxP-NC-420, indicating a rapid electron transfer rate for the catalyst. Notably, the CoxP-NC-420 based ZAB exhibited an open-circuit potential of 1.374 V, this was ∼27 mV lower than 1.401 V for the Pt/C based ZAB. Also, the incorporation of Cu atoms on the Co2P/carbon nanofiber (Cu–Co2P/CNFs) prepared by electrospinning, followed by high-temperature heat treatment, showed an excellent electronic reconfiguration.177 As a consequence, the optimal amount of Cu doping of 7% Cu–Co2P/CNFs showed a high E1/2 value of ∼0.792 V vs RHE, it was higher than 4% Cu–Co2P/CNFs; the 10% Cu–Co2P/CNFs and showed a similar activity to Pt/C. In addition, the OER overpotential of 7% Cu–Co2P/CNFs was ∼360 mV and was larger than 4% Cu–Co2P/CNFs (∼370 mV), Cu–Co2P/CNFs (∼400 mV), and Co2P/CNFs (∼410 mV), signifying the electronic coupling of Cu and the formation of active hydroxide species. Moreover, the ZAB based on 7% Cu–Co2P/CNFs exhibited a high discharge current density and the power density of ∼375.0 mA cm–2 and ∼230 mW cm–2, respectively; this activity was higher than the Pt/C-RuO2 based battery (∼156.3 mA cm–2 and ∼79.6 mW cm–2), and a long-term durability of over 80 h was also observed. In addition, Co2P@N,P-doped carbon polyhedral derived from CoCo-PBA and melamine have been shown to promote a high charge transfer rate and enhance the ORR/OER kinetics compared to Co@NC-1, Co@NC-2 and CoP@NPPC.178 Notably, the open-circuit voltage of Co2P@NPPC-based ZABs (∼1.4 V) was higher than that of Pt/C+RuO2-based ZABs (∼1.373 V). As a result, the peak power density of Co2P@NPPC-based primary ZABs was ∼226 mW cm–2; this was superior to that of the Pt/C-RuO2-based ZAB (∼215 mW cm–2). In addition, the Co2P@NPPC-based ZAB showed an excellent recharge ability for 160 h at 10 mA cm–2. Nanohybrids of the NixP nanoparticle anchored N, namely P-doped porous carbon (NixP-NP-C900) were fabricated from hexagonal NiZn-MOFs and demonstrated a high ORR Eonset and E1/2 values of 0.9 and 0.76 V vs RHE, respectively; this was much higher than the NixP-NP-C1000, and exhibited similar activity to 20%Pt/C.179 In addition, it was predicted that the small Ni2P nanoparticles could act as active sites to enhance the bifunctional ORR and the OER catalytic activity and increase durability. In addition, a synergetic coupling between the NixP and N–P doped carbon skeleton may induce structural defects and thereby regulate the electronic structure to result in a greater number of exposed active sites and improved catalytic activity. As a consequence, the NixP-NP-C900 based ZAB delivered a high power density of ∼266 mW cm–2 and a small charge–discharge voltage gap. It has also been demonstrated that, in a Ni–Fe based heterostructured catalysts, the Ni/Fe metal ratios could influence the ORR and OER activity via regulated coupling effect and electronic structure modulation, see Figure 8(i).180 As a consequence, the NiFe(1:2)P/Pi catalyst exhibited a high ORR Eonset value of ∼0.98 V vs RHE and a low OER overpotential of ∼0.21 V at 10 mA cm–2 with the lowest potential difference (ΔE = E10 – E1/2) of ∼0.62 V; this surpasses a benchmark Pt/C+RuO2 catalysts. In addition, the NiFe(1:2)P/Pi-based ZAB demonstrated an outstanding discharge capacity of 900 mAh g–1 @10 mA cm–2 and a high power density of 395 mW cm–2 along with steady cycles. Due to the synergetic coupling between the nanocrystals of CoFeP and N–P doped carbon, the CoFeP@C exhibited an excellent bifunctional electrocatalytic reaction and ZAB performance, see Figure 8(ii).181 It has also been observed that there is in situ conversion of metal derivatives, such as metal chalcogenides and metal phosphides, to corresponding metal oxides, hydroxides, and/or oxyhydroxide under accelerated environments during OER and/or ORR of rechargeable ZABs, which reduces the Gibbs free energies.182,183

Other Mixed Heterostructures for ORR/OER and ZABs

Mixed-heterostructures, that involve the combination of materials with multiple dimensionalities, provide a wide range of multifunctionality that cannot be simply achieved by a pure heterostructure material.39,184,185 Typically, across the mixed-interfaces, the degree of electrostatic screening and the discontinuous changes in the energy dependence of the density of states can alter the magnetic, electrical, optical, and thermal properties, thereby introducing innovative and/or enhanced functionalities.186−189 For example, due to the enriched electronic charge density by the coupling of CeO2 with CoSe2–NCs, the CeO2@CoSe2–NCs, the CeO2@CoSe2–NCs required a low OER overpotential of ∼0.32 V to reach a current density of 10 mA cm–2, while it exhibited the high E1/2 value of ∼0.76 V vs RHE. Notably, the ORR performance of CeO2@CoSe2–NCs is almost similar to the Pt/C. In addition, the peak power density of CeO2@CoSe2–NCs based ZABs was ∼153 mW cm–2, and was higher than that of the ZABs assembled with the Pt/C-IrO2 (∼138 mW cm–2).190 Zhang et al.191 proposed that due to the combined effect of the formation of a heterostructure with a large pore volume via a porous structure of Co/Co9S8@SNC-900, rapid charge and mass transfer rates were facilitated. As a result, Co/Co9S8@SNC-900 exhibited a high diffusion-limited current density of ∼5.2 mA cm–2; this activity was similar to commercial Pt/C (5.5 mA cm–2). The Co/Co9S8@SNC-900 exhibited a high E1/2 value of ∼0.82 V vs RHE, and this performance was similar to commercial Pt/C (∼0.84 V vs RHE). The electrocatalytic OER activity of Co/Co9S8@SNC-900 showed a low overpotential of ∼0.31 V at a current density of 10 mA cm–2, indicating that its OER activity is the best among all other prepared samples including RuO2. Moreover, after OER stability testing, Co/Co9S8@SNC-900 maintained a stable rod structure, signifying the robustness of Co/Co9S8@SNC900. As a bifunctional electrocatalyst, Co/Co9S8@SNC-900 exhibited a potential difference (ΔE) of ∼0.73 V, which was lower than that of a precious metal catalyst (∼0.78 V). In addition, the Co/Co9S8@SNC-900 based ZAB delivered a high power density of ∼106.6 mW cm–2 at a current density of 163.2 mA cm–2; this was higher than that of a ZAB based on Pt/C+ RuO2 (∼105 mW cm–2 at 161.8 mA cm–2). As shown in Figure 9(i), due to the enriched active sites through the formation of a heterostructure of Co3W3C/CoP nanoparticles anchored on N, the P-doped hierarchically porous carbon fibers showed excellent bifunctional ORR and OER performance compared to Co@WC1–x/NCNTs (N-doped carbon nanotubes), W2N/WC, FeCo/Co2P@NPCF, Co/CNFs (1000).192 As a consequence, the CoWCP-NPC-2:1-based Zn-air battery exhibited a high open circuit voltage of ∼1.41 V and was stable for 12 h, where the open circuit voltage was ∼31 mV higher than that of Pt/C based ZAB. The CoWCP-NPC-2:1 achieved a higher peak power density of ∼205.5 mW cm–2 than that of Pt/C (∼75.1 mW cm–2). In addition, it has been validated that the formation of a Mott–Schottky heterojunction based on Co/Co2P nanoparticles encapsulated in the intertwined NPCNT skeletons could improve the bifunctional electrocatalytic activity through the formation of a favorable pathway for electron transport, see Figure 9(ii).193

The CoSe2/Co@NCNF-3 heterostructure helps to regulate the adsorption of reaction intermediates and improve catalytic efficiency.195 In addition, the catalyst demonstrated long-term durability compared to Pt/C and IrO2 in ORR and OER respectively. Hu et al.196 reported that oxygen vacancies/defects formed during the formation of a heterostructure of NiFe/NiSe2@NCNT catalyst could promote the bifunctional catalytic activity toward the ORR (E1/2 = 0.811 V vs RHE), and OER (η = 292 mV@10 mA cm–2) with a low ΔE of ∼0.711 V, which greatly improved the ZAB performance; see Figure 10(i). Notably, a ZAB based on CoS/CoO PNRs delivered a high open circuit voltage (OCV) of ∼1.35 V and a peak power density of ∼58.8 mW cm–2 that was higher than that of a Pt/C + RuO2-based ZAB (∼55.1 mW cm–2). Due to the highly exposed active surface area and synergistic coupling at its interfaces, the FeCo2O4@FeCo2S4/NF showed an excellent bifunctional ORR and OER performance.198 Moreover, FeCo2O4@FeCo2S4/NF-based ZAB achieved a peak power density of ∼189.75 mW cm–2 at 10 mA cm–2. Notably, the FeCo2O4@FeCo2S4/NF-based ZAB displayed a negligible charge/discharge voltage gap and good cycling capacity of up to ∼227 h at 10 mA cm–2. In addition, it has been reported that the CoNi/CoNiP heterostructure anchored on a 3D MXene framework with a hierarchical structure can modulate the electron transfer rate via a synergetic coupling that facilitates the mass transfer of the reactants and provide facile paths for reactant diffusion, thereby significantly enhancing electrocatalytic bifunctional reaction kinetics and ZAB performance; see Figure 10(iii).119 It has also been revealed that the synergistic coupling effect between CoP and CoO could enrich the active sites and lower the energy barrier for adsorption of reaction intermediate species in a bifunctional CoP/CoO@MNC–CNT electrocatalyst.199

Core–Shell Heterostructures for ORR/OER and ZABs

Recently, numerous bifunctional core–shell heterostructured catalysts with a range of material features, including a hollow core–shell porous structure, metal–metal core–shell structure, and metal–carbon core–shell structure, have been produced to enhance bifunctional reactions and ZAB performance.33,200−202 In addition to the size and shape of the nanoparticles, the method of manufacturing has a major effect on the final material properties.

Metal–Carbon Core–Shell Heterostructures for ORR/OER and ZABs

The primary approach for enhancing catalytic performance and safeguarding the metal-core against corrosion involves constructing a metal–carbon core–shell heterostructure using carbon materials.203−205 This can be achieved by forming heterointerfaces and exploiting the protective properties of the carbon shell. For example, Yu et al.206 reported that the core–shell structure of N-doped graphitic carbon-coated Co nanoparticles (Co@NrC) can optimize the oxygen adsorption/dissociation. In addition, the optimal compositional and core–shell architectural features revealed excellent electronic conductivity, regulated electronic configuration, rich defects, and a greater number of exposed active sites to provide enhanced charge transport for ORR and OER. As a consequence, the Co@NrC-0.3 system showed higher onset and halfwave potentials (Eonset = 0.97 V vs RHE; E1/2 = 0.85 V vs RHE) compared to other Co@NrC catalysts (Co@NrC-0.6: Eonset = 0.91 V vs RHE; E1/2 = 0.81 V vs RHE; Co@NrC-0.45: Eonset = 0.92 V vs RHE; E1/2 = 0.81 V vs RHE; Co@NrC-0.15: Eonset = 0.97 V vs RHE; E1/2 = 0.85 V vs RHE;); this was almost similar to commercial Pt/C (Eonset = 0.99 V vs RHE, E1/2 = 0.85 V vs RHE). In addition, during the OER, the Co@NrC-0.3 sample required a small overpotential of ∼386 mV to reach 10 mAcm–2; this was higher than other Co@NrC materials (Co@NrC-0.6: ∼ 407 mV; Co@NrC-0.45: ∼ 406 mV; Co@NrC-0.15: ∼ 466 mV) and commercial RuO2 (∼440 mV). Notably, the Co@NrC-0.3 exhibited excellent bifunctional activity with a ΔE of ∼0.766 V. In addition, a Co@NrC-0.3-based ZAB demonstrated a larger open-circuit voltage of ∼1.47 V compared to a Pt/C+RuO2-based ZAB (∼1.44 V). Xu et al.207 identified that FeCo core–shell carbon particles embedded on porous nitrogen-doped carbon-nanosheets (FeCo/NUCSs) can facilitate the coexistence of Fe-Nx and Co-Nx active sites, which increase ORR activity. As a result, the FeCo/NUCSs showed a high E1/2 value of ∼0.89 V vs RHE, higher than pristine FeCo-NPs (∼0.835 V vs RHE) and NUCSs (∼0.815 V vs RHE). In addition, the nitrogen-doped carbon nanosheets (NUCSs) can enhance the number of catalytic active sites on the surface of the carbon by facilitating electron transport from the FeCo alloy to carbon, while the catalytic active sites Fe–Nx and Co–Nx can change charge distribution across the carbon layer. Moreover, catalytic FeOOH/CoOOH species are produced on the FeCo alloy during electrocatalysis that can act as OER active sites. The FeCo/NUCSs exhibited an OER overpotential of ∼310 mV at 10 mA cm–2; this was lower than FeCo-NPs (∼440 mV), Co/NUCSs (∼450 mV), Fe/NUCSs (∼480 mV), NUCSs (∼750 mV) and RuO2 (∼390 mV). As a consequence, the FeCo/NUCSs based ZAB exhibited a higher open circuit voltage (∼1.51 V) compared to a Pt/C+RuO2 based ZAB (∼1.47 V). In addition, the FeCo/NUCSs based ZAB showed a maximum power density of ∼152.38 mW cm–2 at 207.16 mA cm–2, which was higher than that of Pt/C+RuO2 based ZAB (∼113.81 mW cm–2). The FeCo/NUCSs based ZAB was also stably cycled for over 100 h.

Chen et al.208 observed that, due to the introduction of defects and multiple activity centers formed by the core–shell heterostructure of nitrogen-doped carbon encapsulated FeNi3 (FeNi3@NC), there was an increase in the bifunctional electrocatalytic activity. Due to the different polarity between the FeCo alloy components in the N-doped carbon (FeCoNC/D), the reaction energy barrier was lowered when the FeCo alloy nanoparticles were uniformly dispersed, which led to an internal electron redistribution; see Figure 11(i).209 Meanwhile, the strong bond between the core of the alloy nanoparticle and the N-doped carbon shell prevents the metal from being corroded during the electrocatalytic process. Zhao et al.210 reported that the use of alloying and sulfurization techniques could be an effective strategy for enhancing the number of exposed catalytic active sites within the catalyst. This includes cobalt or cobalt ferroalloy sulfides, along with their heterojunctions, core–shell structure, and S- and N-doped carbon/carbon nanotube (CoFe-S@3D-S-NCNT) electrocatalysts. Consequently, this strategy results in a simultaneous improvement in the catalytic activity for ORR and OER, while minimizing the potential gap (ΔE) to 0.71 V. As a result, liquid ZABs made from a CoFe-S@3D-S-NCNT electrocatalyst exhibited a high peak power density of 223 mW cm–2, a superior specific capacity of 815 mA h gZn–1, and exceptional stability for almost 900 h at a current density of 5 mA cm–2. In addition, the quasi-solid-state zinc–air batteries (ZABs) demonstrate a high peak power density of 490 mW cm–2 and an excellent voltage round-trip efficiency of 64%. Choi et al.57 reported that Co nanoparticles-encapsulated in holey N-doped carbon nanotubes (Co@H-NCNT) could provide a greater number of Co/N–C moieties. Notably, the Co@H-NCNT microstructure formation was effective in having high catalytic activity and stability toward the ORR, the OER, and the ZAB performance (Figure 11(ii)). In addition, due to the core–shell features, the MOF-derived CoP-nitrogen-doped carbon@NiFeP (CoP-NC@NFP) nanoflakes electrocatalyst exhibited an excellent bifunctional electrocatalytic activity, along with good stability and ZAB performance; see Figure 11(iii).211

Metal–Metal Core–Shell Heterostructures for ORR/OER and ZABs

Recent investigations have shown that metal–metal core–shell structured nanomaterials with tailored chemical and physical properties, in both the cores and shells, can act as powerful platforms for bifunctional electrocatalysis.212,213 In such metal–metal core–shell heterostructures, the lattice mismatch and deficiency of chemical interaction between metals (or metal oxides or sulfides or phosphides) often results in a large interfacial energy, which could lead to strain-rich and defect-rich features, and an edge-terminated environment, thereby increasing the number of exposed active sites for adsorption and desorption of oxygen reactants and increasing the electronic conductivity for facile charge transport.10,12,39,212,213 As shown in Figure 12(i), a layer of 1T Co-WS2 shell-nanochannels at the NiTe2/Ni metallic core–nanoskeleton sheet can provide high electrocatalytic activity in terms of ηOER of 290 mV @ 30 mA cm–2 and E1/2 of 0.781 V for ORR. It was predicted that the interfacial heterojunction of 1T Co-WS2/NiTe2/Ni may exhibit outstanding electrochemical performance.214 In addition, the high electrocatalytic bifunctional activity of NiPS3 nanosheets was achieved by their unusual core–shell heterostructure with NiOOH (i.e., NiPS3@NiOOH core–shell heterostructure), where the presence of P and S modulates the surface electronic state of nickel and their high density of exposed active metallic-edge sites assisted in improving water oxidation. It was predicted that, during an OER, electron-transport to the NiOOH shell would be facilitated by the metallic edge sites of the NiPS3 core.215 In addition, due to an improved electronic conductivity with a greater number of exposed active sites, the metallic Ni core and Fe-doped NiCo alloy shell on carbon nanotubes (Fe/Ni@NiCo-CNT) exhibited superior ORR and OER activities. As a result, the Fe/Ni@NiCo-CNT Zn-air batteries (ZABs) showed a high specific capacity of ∼796.5 mA h gZn–1 and an excellent open circuit potential of 1.427 V.216 Yan et al.217 reported that in a MoS2@FeN-C NSs core–shell heterostructure, the core–shell of Fe–N–C shields the MoS2 core from the corrosive effects of the alkaline electrolyte during the ORR and OER reactions, thereby ensuring the as-built ZABs demonstrate high durability. As a consequence, when repeatedly charged and discharged for 22 h at a high current density of 10 mA cm–2, the ZAB based on MoS2@Fe–N–C NSs showed a high cycling stability. In addition, for a Co core@Co oxide shell (Co@CoOx) catalyst, the surface of the Co oxide was observed to be more electron enriched.218 This heterostructure effect and modification of charge conductivity, oxygen affinity, and intermediate transfer channel make the metal@metal oxide catalyst a potential candidate for ORR and the OER. Notably, the aqueous ZAB based on Co@CoOx showed a high specific capacity of ∼694 mAh gZn–1 with excellent performance retention after a durability test. Pan et al.219 prepared the heterostructure of amino-functionalized CNTs with CoNi@CoO–NiO (CoNi@CoO-NiO/NH2–CNTs) core/shell structure by hydrothermal method followed by calcination. Notably, the bifunctional catalysis performance is significantly enhanced by a synergy between the bimetallic CoNi alloy and its corresponding oxides. As a consequence, the CoNi@CoOeNiO/NH2–CNT-3 catalyst exhibited an excellent E1/2 value of ∼0.794 V vs RHE and a low OER potential of ∼1.607 V vs RHE at 10 mA cm–2. Interestingly, the CoNi@CoOeNiO/NH2–CNTs-3 based rechargeable ZAB provided a high power density of ∼128.7 mW cm–2; this was higher than that of Pt/CeRuO2 (∼88.1 mW cm–2). Furthermore, the CoNi@CoOeNiO/NH2–CNTs-3 based ZAB exhibited a high open circuit voltage (OCV) of ∼1.458 V and the discharge voltage was ∼1.40 V at 10 mA cm–2. In addition, due to the small core@shell CoSx@Cu2MoS4 hybrids in a CoSx@Cu2MoS4–MoS2/NSG heterostructured catalyst, the formation of a core–shell structure could lead to defect-rich features with a more edge terminated nature and an increase in the number of exposed active sites for adsorption and desorption of oxygen reactants and an increase in electronic conductivity for facile charge transport; see Figure 12(ii).220 A ZAB based on the CoSx@Cu2MoS4–MoS2/NSG heterostructure exhibited an open-circuit voltage of ∼1.44 V and a maximum power density of ∼40 mW cm–2 at 58 mA cm–2, which was higher than Pt/C-based ZAB (∼32 mW cm–2 at 50 mA cm–2).

Hollow Core–Shell Heterostructures for ORR/OER and ZABs

The energy conversion and storage applications of core–shell materials are increasing due to their multifunctional nature that can be achieved by forming several shells and manipulating the core–shell materials and structure.33,221,222 Furthermore, the removal of the inner core from core–shell structures presents an excellent opportunity to create multipurpose hollow core architectures with large electrochemically active surface area, more exposed active sites, improved charge and mass transfer rates, and interfaces with optimal electronic properties.222−224 In this regard, a hollow yolk@shell Co3O4/HNCP-40 structure demonstrated excellent performance for the ORR and OER reactions, due to several advantageous factors.225 These include the presence of more oxygen vacancies and tetrahedral Co2+ ions, which contribute to enhanced catalytic ORR activity. Furthermore, the hollow yolk–shell interfacial structure facilitated efficient mass transfer, leading to an improved catalytic activity. As a result, the hollow yolk@shell Co3O4/HNCP-40 catalyst exhibited similar catalytic activity to Pt/C for the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) with an excellent E1/2 value of ∼0.834 V vs RHE and also exhibited an enhanced stability and methanol tolerance compared to Pt/C. In addition, the CoOx species and nitrogen-doped carbon in the structure exhibited potential synergistic effects of the optimal yolk@shell Co3O4/HNCP-40 catalyst; this resulted in the enhancement of OER performance by a low overpotential of ∼333 mV at 10 mA cm–2 and a small Tafel slope of ∼69 mV dec–1. Notably, these values surpass those of an IrO2 catalyst (∼409 mV at 10 mA cm–2). In addition, the catalyst has similar catalytic activity for the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR), while exhibiting improved stability and methanol tolerance compared to commercially available Pt/C (20 wt %). These findings suggest that the catalyst has the potential to serve as a bifunctional electrocatalyst for both the OER and ORR. Wang et al.226 reported that in a porous Ni–Fe–S/xNCQDs hollow nanocage heterostructure, the higher oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) activity can be attributed to the intrinsic heterostructure properties of FeS/NiS and nitrogen doped carbon quantum dots (NCQDs), which resulted in an increased adsorption affinity for O2; see Figure 13 (i). In addition, the superior mass transfer was facilitated by the presence of a hollow nanocage structure. The coupling and interaction between nickel oxyhydroxide (NiOOH) and iron oxyhydroxide (FeOOH) significantly enhanced the process of charge transfer and catalytic performance for the oxygen evolution reaction (OER). The porous hollow features can also improve the rate of mass transfer and promote the exposure of a larger number of active sites (ORR/OER) on the surface of Ni–Fe–S/3NCQDs, thereby resulting in a promising bifunctional activity (ΔE ∼ 0.675 V). In addition, the cage structure of the material can prevent the active species from aggregating, thereby ensuring the long-term stability. As a result of these features, the Ni–Fe–S/3NCQDs-based ZAB showed an open-circuit voltage and maximum power density of ∼1.389 V and 94 mW cm–2, respectively. When a cyclic test was performed at ∼10.0 mA cm–2 for 1700 h (40 min per cycle), the initial discharge and charge voltages were ∼1.19 and 2.11 V, respectively, where the voltage gaps were ∼0.92 V and ∼56.3% of the round-trip efficiency was achieved. Following 360 cycles of discharge and recharge, there was a small increase in the voltage gap (∼1.27 V) and a slight decrease in the energy efficiency (∼44.4%). In addition, the modulated Fe-d band center of the Fe–N4 sites in a NiFe-LDH/Fe1–N–C hollow nanorod heterostructure can significantly reduce the energy barriers of the ORR rate-limiting steps, while the NiFe-layered double hydroxide (NiFe-LDH) nanodots could provide active sites for OER.227 As a result, the optimal NiFe-LDH/Fe1–N–C hollow nanorod heterostructured catalysts showed excellent outstanding bifunctional activity with a small ΔE of ∼0.65 V. The ZABs made using the NiFe-LDH/Fe1–N–C catalyst exhibited a peak power density of ∼205 mW cm–2 and long-term cycling stability of up to 400 h. Furthermore, dual-sided Fe/Fe3C@N-doped carbon nanotubes (FNCHFs) containing hierarchical hollow frameworks exhibited excellent electrochemical activity and ZAB performance through the regulated electronic conductivity and more exposed active sites.228 Due to the synergistic effect between S-dopant and CoFe–N–C active sites in the Fe and Co atoms impregnated N, the S-co-doped hollow carbon spheres (CoFe-SNC) can improve the intrinsic bifunctional electrocatalytic activity, accelerate the reaction kinetics, and enhance ZAB.95 Moreover, a nanospheroidal structure of CuSA@HNCNx provided more active sites and increased accessibility of reactive species.229 As shown in Figure 13 (ii), due to its hollow structural features, the Janus-structured Ni-SAs/HCNFs/Co-NAs exhibited an outstanding bifunctional catalytic activity with a low ΔE value of ∼0.65 V.101 Notably, the Ni–As/HCNFs/Co-NAs-based ZAB showed high open-circuit potential, and high specific capacity of ∼1.45 V and 808 mAh g–1 Zn, respectively; it is also possessed excellent long life over 200 h at 10 mA cm–2. Due to the formation of Mott–Schottky heterojunction, the oxygen defects-rich CoFe/CoFe2O4 on hollow N-doped carbon sphere (Vo-CoFe/CoFe2O4@NC) showed an excellent E1/2 value of ∼0.858 V vs RHE, while the high limiting current of ∼5.98 mA cm–2 at 0.5 V was achieved, see Figure 13 (iii).230 Besides, the current density for Vo-CoFe/CoFe2O4@NC was similar (∼98.3%) after a 100 h stability test. In addition, VoCoFe/CoFe2O4@NC required a lower OER potential of ∼360 mV to reach 10 mA cm–2. With a current density of ∼155.2 mA cm–2 at 0.8 V and a peak power density of ∼138.5 mW cm–2 at 230.2 mA cm–2, the Vo-CoFe/CoFe2O4@NC-based ZAB was more effective than the Pt/C+RuO2-based ZAB (∼135.2 mA cm–2 at 0.8 V and ∼121.6 mW cm–2 at 207.2 mA cm–2). The Vo-CoFe/CoFe2O4@NC-based ZAB had an initial voltaic energy efficiency of ∼63%, calculated by dividing the discharge voltage by the charge voltage.

Density Functional Theory Calculations

Computational catalysis has emerged as an effective method for analyzing, understanding, and even predicting the activity and selectivity of various catalytic materials.231−233 The results of computational studies are utilized to rationally design materials that are more catalytically active. This approach reveals innovative catalysts that could be further tested experimentally. Particularly, density functional theory (DFT)-based methods have shown to be essential for understanding the mechanistic details of catalytic features and properties to be tackled, such as identification of active catalytic species, adsorption energies of intermediates, activation energy barriers (i.e., transition states), and electronic structure information which are difficult to investigate through experimental methods because of their high instability at the atomic scale.233−235 Theoretical research on electrocatalytic oxygen reactions (ORR and OER) has shown that the free energy of the intermediates (*O, *OH, and *OOH) on the catalyst surface strongly influences the reaction activity.232,236 Besides, the catalytic activity is significantly influenced by the nature of the catalyst surface, as the adsorption energies of the reaction intermediates are subject to variation. Rather than simply combining several materials with different levels of superiority, a heterostructured catalyst offers several advantages, and the microstructure of heterointerfaces is closely associated with the distinctive advantages of the heterostructure.236−238 When two building blocks developed into contact, band alignments may occur at heterointerface, causing charge redistribution around the interfaces.239 While the Fermi levels (EF) of these materials eventually approach equilibrium. Consequently, a built-in potential is formed as the holes and electrons accumulate near the heterointerface and are separated by a completely ionized depletion area. This built-in potential makes heterostructured materials as highly conductive in forward bias and nearly insulated in reverse bias.238 Notably, the holes and electrons accumulated near the heterointerface may easily adsorb the opposite charge carriers, which can act as active sites for electrocatalytic reactions.109

As shown in Figure 14(i), hybridization of NiCoP with NiO may induce a strong synergistic electronic coupling effect.240 Notably, by giving 0.3420 e– to NiCoP, NiO acts as an electron donor. These findings indicate that the heterojunction of NiCoP/NiO interfaces may result in an inhomogeneous electron aggregation, enriched bonding, and facile regulation of catalytic reactions, and thus enhances the reaction kinetics of bifunctional oxygen reactions. Also, the density of states calculation (DOS) indicates that, in comparison to NiO, NiO with the oxygen vacancy exhibits metallic properties and the charge carrier increases the density at the Fermi level, signifying that the vacancy structure of the vacancy promotes fast electron transfer because it has no band gap and is nearer to the EF. Also, the formation of CoP3/CeO2 heterojunction in 3D flower-like CoP3/CeO2/C heterostructure may modify the distribution of charges at the interface, lower the energy barriers for electrocatalytic processes, and produce an enhanced electronic environment for OER and ORR.241 The CoS2/Cu2S heterostructured catalyst catalyzing the oxygen reaction had the lowest reaction energy barrier than CoS2 and Cu2S, indicating that the strong electronic interaction between CoS2 and Cu2S in a CoS2/Cu2S heterostructure could increase conversion step.159 Also, compared to CoS2, and Cu2S, the DOS of the CoS2/Cu2S heterostructure exhibited an electron delocalization nature. As a result, the CoS2/Cu2S heterostructure required an energy of ∼0.644 eV for the third step, which is the rate-determining step in the ORR process, which was lower than that of CoS2 (∼0.656 eV, from *OH to *O) and Cu2S (∼0.660 eV, from *+O2 to *OOH). In the OER, the conversion of *OH and *O was the crucial step between CoS2/Cu2S and Cu2S, with free energy differences of ∼1.311 eV and ∼1.391 eV, respectively. Recently Yao et al.38 proposed that in a Fe3N-FeN0.0324 heterojunction, the electron-rich FeN0.0324 will be an active site for ORR, where the electrons (e–) can be easily transported from the surface of FeN0.0324 to the adsorbed oxygen-related intermediates, creating a negative charge center for proton (H+) to attack, thus significantly accelerating the ORR kinetics (Figure 14(ii)). Besides, the hole-rich Fe3N could be an active site for OER, subsequently the h+ may transfer from the surface of catalyst to the adsorbed –OH group intermediates, and thus encouraging the deprotonation process. As a result, the electron-/hole-rich center in the Fe3N-FeN0.0324 heterojunction showed excellent bifunctional oxygen reaction kinetics.

Also, Hammer and Nørskov developed the d-band hypothesis to describe the electronic structure of adsorbates on metal surfaces.242,243 In oxygen electrocatalysis, various oxygen intermediates including O*, OH*, OOH*, and OO* interact on the catalyst surface to create adsorbed oxygenated species.20,243 The localized d-band center mostly regulates the bonding strengths of the adsorption species on the surface of the catalyst. When a bond is formed between metal d-orbitals and oxygen p-orbitals, two states are created, namely, the filled bonding state and the partially occupied anticoding state. The d-band center theory states that the adsorption energy of a particular species on a metal surface is influenced by the position of the d-band center (Ed) relative to the Fermi level (EF).31,45 Therefore, the energy level of the d-band center (Ed) directly influences the number of electrons occupying the antibonding orbital, thereby affecting the strength and stability of the adsorption bond. Additionally, a downshift in the energy state of the d-band center (Ed) can improve the desorption capacity of the oxygen-containing intermediates on the surface of the catalyst (owing to a weaker metal–adsorbate bond), hence accelerating the ORR/OER reaction rate. Therefore, the d-band center can be employed as an activity descriptor because this model allows a basic correlation between surface electronic features and catalytic activity with reliable consistency.244 Recently, it was suggested that a d-band center model based on DFT simulation, could be used to effectively control the electronic structure of cobalt or Iron nanoclusters coupled heterointerfaces.31,238,245 For example, in a Ni3FeN/VN heterostructure, after forming the interface of the Ni3FeN/VN heterojunction, a small downshift in Ed has been observed for V 3d and Ni 3d states (see Figure 14(iii)).246 Whereas a slight upshifting to be near to EF was observed for the Ed of Fe 3d, displaying an improved electron occupied Fe 3d states near EF and enhancing the electron transfer. The d-band theory states that reducing Ed to the Fermi level increases the binding strength between reactants and active sites, facilitating the adsorption of reactive molecules. Conversely, it may lead to a weak binding strength, which would make a facile desorption of the reaction intermediate product, hence, accelerating the ORR/OER reaction rate.

In addition, several other approaches including multisite adsorption strategies,247 doping schemes,157 multiple descriptors,248 and strain schemes,249 have been executed for the development of robust heterostructured bifunctional oxygen electrocatalysts with outstanding performance. However, it may be challenging to regulate the adsorption free energies to the appropriate ranges in an attempt to enhance the efficiency of heterostructured electrocatalysts in experiments. To further rationally design electrocatalysts, it is also important to carefully choose physically feasible descriptors, such as electronic descriptors (d and/or p electrons), geometrical descriptors (coordination environment), activity descriptors (electron affinity, electronegativity, and first ionization energy), and composite descriptors (bond length and electronegativity of neighboring atoms).

Challenges and Future Perspectives

Recent research has revealed that a range of nonprecious metal heterostructured catalysts have provided significant advancements in terms of design, processing, and performance; see Scheme 3. Due to their distinctive interfacial topology, highly exposed active sites, and electrically conductive nature, such catalysts have been employed as electrocatalysts, in particular for improving oxygen reduction and evolution reactions (ORR/OER). Notably, rechargeable zinc–air batteries (ZABs) can benefit greatly from the use of heterostructured electrocatalysts to improve bifunctional oxygen reactions (ORR/OER) since they are low-cost, effective, durable, and environmentally friendly. In addition, inspired by the advantageous characteristics of the variety of architectures formed, the electrocatalytic performance can be efficiently optimized through the integration of a diverse range of nanostructures. To better understand how to improve the electrochemical performance of rechargeable ZABs, we have surveyed in detail recently developed approaches to regulate heterointerfaces and have focused on several key parameters; these include a synergetic coupling of the different constituents, defect/strain engineering, surface engineering, incorporation of metal-free or precious or nonprecious metal heteroatoms, the formation of a range tailored heterostructures, electrocatalyst reaction adsorption, enhancing the electrical conductivity, active sites engineering, increasing the reaction surface area, decreasing the reaction energy barrier, and improving electron and mass transfer. Judicious control of interfacial electronic effects such as electronic charge redistribution, chemical bonding at the interface, and lattice strain effects can optimize the electronic structure and modulate the chemisorption of diffusion/transportation behavior of reaction intermediates during the ORR and OER. Tailoring such characteristics can improve the reaction kinetics for the ORR/OER to achieve high electrocatalytic performance for rechargeable ZABs.

Challenges and Future Outlook on the Progress of Rechargeable Zinc–Air Batteries (ZABs)

Despite their promising performance and wide-ranging potential application, these heterostructured materials require further development for widespread industrial-level ZAB implementation. Typically, the surface and interface engineering of air electrodes and electrocatalysts is crucial for rechargeable Zn–air batteries (ZABs) due to the complex triple-phase gas–solid–liquid reaction environment. Since several catalysts have a high potential difference (ΔE), this indicates that additional effort needs to be made to improve their efficiency. Limited research to date has been conducted regarding heterostructured electrocatalysts, despite the fact that their long-term durability makes them of particular interest in enabling practical applications; there is therefore a need for substantial bifunctional catalytic current densities at low potentials. In addition, the interfacial bonding and active surface areas critically depend on the chemistry, structure, and mismatch stresses involved in the heterostructures. As a result, the commercialization of ZABs would benefit greatly from efforts in the optimization of structural configuration, improvement in reaction kinetics, and optimal loading of constituents. The primary limitation of heterostructures is the large number of defects induced by lattice mismatches, and typically, the formation of a large number of defects can result in a decrease in the bifunctional electrocatalytic activity and ZAB device performance. In addition, depending on the thickness of the layers and the degree of stress, the heterointerface can be relaxed by creating undesired defects and dislocations, and these defects often result in inferior functionality. Therefore, optimization and regulation of defects and strain are areas worthy of further investigation. Similarly, further investigations into the reaction mechanism of heterostructures in the context of electrocatalysis are required, in particular for the OER, due to the growing complexity of catalyst components. In an effort to monitor structural reconstruction through real-time examinations and identify active sites for targeted reactions, advanced characterization techniques, such as in situ Raman, TEM, and XANES spectroscopy, are essential. This will aid in the design of more efficient bifunctional heterostructures for electrocatalytic reactions by understanding the reaction kinetics and thermodynamics and screening the structure–performance relationship of interfacially modified catalysts. The OER and ORR reactions have relevance to the recharging and discharging processes of rechargeable ZABs and, due to the contrasting reaction requirements and complex multielectron reaction processes, there remains a gap between the ORR and the OER for single heterostructure catalysts. This difference renders a significant number of electrocatalysts unsuitable for use in rechargeable zinc–air battery applications. Thus, it is essential to narrow the gap in the potential difference between the ORR and the OER, where the power density, round-trip efficiency, and lifetime of rechargeable ZABs are directly influenced by both the ORR and the OER on the air electrodes. It is of particular interest that common alkaline electrolytes can create insoluble zinc oxide precipitates and/or zinc dendrites from zinc ions and membrane disintegration. Alkaline solutions can quickly disintegrate the air electrode polymer binder, leading to the loss of active material and high interfacial impedance, which increase the cell overpotential. To solve the alkaline electrolyte problem, alternate electrolytes must therefore be developed. However, integrating multiscale heterointerfacial architectures remains a challenge, where the inherent activity of many microscale interfacial regulatory approaches leads to a decrease in performance due to unavoidable obscuring of active site. Thus, the development of high-performance ORR and OER electrocatalysts for ZABs requires coordinated multiscale heterointerfacial regulation. Besides, single-cathode and dual-cathode zinc–air batteries are also of great interest to enhance the performance. Finally, it may be possible to achieve improved electrochemical performance by precise control of the interface at the micro/atomic level, which can only be realized through a combination of theoretical and experimental research that can provide innovative insights into interface effects. Effort in these directions provides potential to improve the bifunctional ORR/OER electrocatalytic activity and thereby induce the ZAB performance in large-scale applications
