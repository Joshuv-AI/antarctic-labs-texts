A transcriptomic and metabolomic approach to the physiological adaptation of ‘Bayushizi’ pomegranate fruit to sunburn browning
Authors: Chunyan Liu, Na Ma, Liqing Lu, Haiqi Zhang, Yongjie Qi, Zhenghui Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSunburn browning is a major physiological disease of the ‘Bayushizi’ pomegranate fruit that significantly affects its appearance and internal quality. At present, the underlying mechanisms of sunburn browning in ‘Baiyushizi’ pomegranates remain unclear. This study aims to elucidate the physiological adaptations, metabolic shifts and potential genetic regulation underlying sunburn browning in ‘Baiyushizi’ pomegranates.ResultsSunburn browning induces pericarp cell shrinkage and lignification, leading to increases in H2O2, O2− and MDA levels, indicating that sunburn browning causes membrane lipid peroxidation. This further leads to enzymatic reactions (increased SOD and CAT contents) and nonenzymatic reactions (increased total flavonoid, carotenoid and total phenolic contents) in the pericarp of the pomegranate. Metabolomic analysis revealed that flavonoids, phenolic acids, coumarins and lignans characterized the sunburn browning of pomegranates. The physiological characteristics were integrated with transcriptomic data for weighted correlation network analysis (WGCNA). Regulatory networks were used to identify key genes and transcription factors (TFs) that influence flavonoid, phenolic acid, coumarin and lignan metabolism during sunburn browning.ConclusionsThese findings provide insight into the physiological adaptation, metabolic shifts and potential genetic regulation of the effects of pomegranates on sunburn browning.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08685-6.Keywords: Pomegranate, Sunburn browning, Transcriptomics, Metabolomics

Introduction

Climate change has imposed significant negative impacts on agricultural production, with rising temperatures exacerbating sunburn damage to fruit trees [1]. Sunburn is a physiological disorder caused by excessive solar radiation and elevated temperatures and adversely affects both the external appearance and internal quality of fruits, thereby reducing their market value [2]. Three distinct types of sunburn have been identified and characterized in apple fruits: photooxidative sunburn, sunburn necrosis and sunburn browning [3, 4]. Photooxidative sunburn is characterized by surface bleaching of the fruit, resulting from sudden exposure to intense light. Sunburn necrosis results from heat-induced cell death and appears as dark brown or black spots on the fruit surface. In contrast, sunburn browning does not involve cell death and is characterized by yellowing or browning of the pericarp. This discolouration has been associated with decreased chlorophyll and anthocyanin concentrations, along with increased levels of carotenoids and quercetin glycosides [5, 6].

Sunburn-induced damage in fruits is closely linked to their antioxidant capacity. Under normal physiological conditions, the generation and scavenging of reactive oxygen species (ROS) are maintained in dynamic equilibrium [7]. However, under stress conditions such as high temperature and intense light, ROS levels increase significantly. In response, plant cells upregulate their antioxidant defence systems, which contain both enzymatic and nonenzymatic components, to mitigate oxidative damage by eliminating excess ROS [8].

To mitigate the adverse effects of abiotic stress and the subsequent generation of ROS, plants have developed a sophisticated antioxidant enzyme system. This system includes peroxidase (POD), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and catalase (CAT) enzymes, whose activities are closely linked to plant stress resistance [9]. Under different types of sunburn stress, the activities of SOD, POD, and CAT in apple peel generally increase initially but decline with prolonged exposure [10]. In tomato leaves subjected to high temperature or intense light, the activities of ascorbate peroxidase (APX) and CAT increase, whereas those of SOD, POD, and glutathione reductase (GR) decrease [11]. Similarly, in pear peel, antioxidant enzyme activities vary with sunburn severity: CAT activity decreases significantly as sunburn intensifies, whereas APX and SOD activities increase [12]. Overall, the responses of antioxidant enzymes depend on both the plant species and the severity of sunburn stress.

The nonenzymatic antioxidant system constitutes the other essential component of the plant’s antioxidant machinery, encompassing flavonoids, phenolics, carotenoids, and others [13]. For instance, in tomato plants, defence mechanisms such as chlorophyll degradation and carotenoid synthesis are activated to mitigate light and heat stress [14]. Similarly, in sunburned ‘Beurré D'Anjou’ pears, the antioxidant capacity increases with the accumulation of polyphenolic compounds to counteract sunburn damage [12]. Flavonoids, in particular, function as effective ROS scavengers and can absorb high-energy, short-wavelength solar radiation [15].

Pomegranate (Punica granatum L.), originating from Central Asia, is now widely cultivated in subtropical and tropical regions. During its fruiting period from summer to autumn, the fruit is subjected to intense solar radiation and high temperatures, thereby increasing its vulnerability to sunburn damage [16]. Sunburn can lead to losses exceeding 30% of the total pomegranate harvest [17]. Studies on coloured varieties such as ‘Hongyushizi’ have shown that sunburn stimulates an increase in antioxidant content, although the levels of metabolites associated with phenylpropanoid and flavonoid biosynthesis tend to decrease in sunburn-necrotic tissue [18]. Research in apples further indicates fundamental differences in sunburn responses between red and green cultivars [6]. In white ‘Baiyushizi’ pomegranates, sunburn browning represents the predominant form of sunburn injury. Nevertheless, there is a lack of knowledge regarding changes in antioxidant enzymes and nonenzymatic antioxidants, as well as the metabolic shifts and underlying genetic mechanisms involved in the response to sunburn browning in white ‘Baiyushizi’ pomegranates.

To address these gaps, this study investigated changes in antioxidant enzymes and nonenzymatic antioxidants, along with microstructural observations, in the white pomegranate variety ‘Baiyushizi’ under different degrees of sunburn browning. Transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses were also performed on pericarps with different levels of sunburn browning. These findings help elucidate the response mechanisms of ‘Baiyushizi’ pomegranate pericarps to sunburn browning from physiological, cellular, metabolic, and molecular perspectives, laying a theoretical foundation for understanding the genetic basis of sunburn tolerance in pomegranates.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and sampling

Experiments were performed on 10-year-old pomegranate trees (P. granatum L. ‘Baiyushizi’). This cultivar is of significant commercial value and is widely grown in China. It was officially approved by the Anhui Provincial Forest Variety Approval Committee in 2003. Varietal identification was carried out in accordance with the characteristics described by Liwu Zhu et al. [19]. The trees were planted at a spacing of 4.0 × 3 m in an orchard located in Huaiyuan, Anhui Province, and were maintained under standard irrigation and fertilization practices. Sampling was performed with the explicit permission of the orchard grower. We declare that the pomegranate materials in this study complied with institutional, national, and international guidelines for collection and cultivation.

On September 19, 2024, approximately 130 days after full bloom, three replicates of fruits (three trees for each replicate) of uniform size were harvested. Within each replicate, the samples were graded by browning severity into three categories (10 fruits each): SB0 (no sunburn browning, yellowish-white pericarp), SB1 (mild sunburn browning, yellow discolouration with few brown spots), and SB2 (moderate sunburn browning, brown discolouration with dark tan spots) (Fig. 1a). Pericarp slices (~ 1 mm thick, ~ 10 g per replicate) were excised from each category, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C for analysis.

Analysis of the pomegranate pericarp microstructure

The microstructures of pericarps representing the three sunburn grades were examined with paraffin sections. Cubes (5 × 5 × 1 mm) were fixed in 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde and then subjected to trimming, dehydration, paraffin embedding, microtome sectioning (8 µm), safranin-fast green staining, mounting, and light microscopy imaging following standard histological protocols [20].

Determination of antioxidant enzyme activity and membrane lipid peroxidation

Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was measured as the inhibition of photochemical nitroblue tetrazolium chloride (NBT) reduction [21]. Catalase (CAT) activity was assessed using a hydrogen peroxide assay based on the formation of a stable complex with ammonium molybdate [22]. The activity of guaiacol peroxidase (POD) was determined by monitoring the increase in absorbance at 470 nm during guaiacol oxidation [23]. Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and superoxide anion (O2−) levels were quantified by the titanium sulfate colorimetric method and the hydroxylamine hydrochloride-sulfanilic acid-naphthylamine colorimetric method, respectively [24]. The malondialdehyde (MDA) content was determined using the thiobarbituric acid colorimetric method [24]. Total phenolics (TPs), total flavonoids (TFs), and ferric-reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) were determined according to Liu et al. [18]. The chlorophyll and carotenoid contents were extracted and analysed according to the methods of Huang et al. [25] and Gong et al. [26], respectively. Analysis of variance followed by Duncan’s new multiple range test (SPSS 16.0) was applied, and differences were considered significant at p < 0.05.

LC‒MS/MS analysis

The pericarps were lyophilized and ground to a fine powder. Aliquots (100 mg) were extracted overnight at 4 °C with 1.0 mL 70% aqueous methanol and filtered through 0.22 μm nylon membranes (SCAA-104; ANPEL, Shanghai, China). Sample extracts were analysed using an ultra‑performance liquid chromatography system (ExionLC™ AD) coupled with an electrospray ionization tandem mass spectrometry system (SCIEX). Separation was achieved with an Agilent SB-C18 column (1.8 µm, 2.1 × 100 mm) maintained at 40 °C. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (B) delivered at 0.35 mL min⁻1. The gradient profile was as follows: 0–9 min, 5–95% B; 9–10 min, 95% B; 10–11.1 min, 95–5% B; and 11.1–14 min, 5% B. The injection volume was 2 µL. The effluent was alternatively connected to an ESI-triple quadrupole-linear ion trap (QTRAP)-MS [27].

The mass spectrometry conditions primarily included an electrospray ionization (ESI) source temperature of 500 °C; an ion spray voltage (IS) of 5500 V (positive ion mode)/−4500 V (negative ion mode); ion source gas I (GSI), gas II (GSII), and curtain gas (CUR) set to 50, 60, and 25 psi, respectively; and the collision-induced ionization parameter set to high. Multiple-reaction monitoring (MRM) was employed for both identification and quantification. Metabolites were identified against the MetWare spectral library and annotated via the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes enrichment analyses (KEGG) database. Differentially accumulated metabolites were defined by a fold change ≥ 2 or ≤ 0.5 and a VIP > 1 [27].

Transcriptomic analysis

RNA was extracted from pomegranate pericarp with an RNAprep Pure Plant Kit (Tiangen, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For sequencing, libraries were prepared using the NEBNext Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit from Illumina (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Illumina sequencing was conducted by MetWare Biotechnology (Wuhan, China). Three independent biological replicates were sequenced for each group. Transcript abundances were quantified as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using |log2 (fold change)|≥ 1 and a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and subsequently subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway analyses.

To identify coexpression modules associated with physiological traits, weighted gene coexpression network analysis (WGCNA) was performed using the WGCNA package in R [28]. Module‒trait relationships were assessed by correlating module eigengenes with physiological traits using Pearson correlation, and the significance of each correlation was determined by calculating raw p values (q values) followed by false discovery rate (FDR) correction for multiple comparisons.

Reverse transcription quantitative PCR(RT‒qPCR) analysis

cDNA was synthesized using a PrimeScript™ RT reagent kit (TaKaRa, Japan). Reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT‒qPCR) was performed on a CFX96™ Real-Time System (Bio-Rad, USA), with PgrActin serving as the internal reference gene. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method. The primer sequences are listed in Table S1.

Results

Sunburn browning alters the cell structure and physiological–biochemical characteristics of the ‘Baiyushizi’ pomegranate pericarp

The pericarp of SB0 fruit is covered by a 2–3-layered epidermis of small, irregular yet regularly aligned cells. In this layer lies a tightly packed subepidermal zone of thin-walled, circular-to-elliptical cells, whereas the mesocarp consists of larger, thin-walled cells that are only loosely arranged (Fig. 1b). The pericarp of SB1 exhibited a more compact epidermis characterized by smaller cells. The subepidermal cells appeared shrunken and densely packed, with occasional red-stained cells indicating incipient lignification. In the SB2 pericarp, the epidermal cells were further collapsed, the degree of lignification became pronounced, and the mesocarp cells displayed both rupture and extensive lignification. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that sunburn browning progressively reduces cell volume and promotes lignification in the ‘Bayushizi’ pomegranate pericarp.

To characterize the physiological response, we quantified membrane lipid peroxidation (MDA, H2O2, and O2−), antioxidant enzyme activities (SOD, POD, and CAT), nonenzymatic antioxidants (TPs, TFs, chlorophyll, and carotenoids) and FRAP (Table 1). H2O2 and O2− concentrations increased significantly with increasing sunburn severity. MDA remained unchanged in SB1 but significantly increased in SB2, confirming accelerated membrane injury at advanced stages. SOD and CAT activities increased under stress, whereas POD activity was below the detection limit. The TPs content was greater in SB1 and SB2, while the TFs and carotenoid contents increased markedly, and the chlorophyll content decreased gradually. These changes increased the FRAP by 36% in SB1 and by 106% in SB2. Overall, sunburn browning evokes the activity of antioxidant enzymes and antioxidant responses that attempt to limit but cannot fully prevent ROS-mediated damage to the pomegranate pericarp.

Metabolomic analysis and screening for differentially accumulated metabolites

To better understand the metabolic changes in pomegranate pericarp during sunburn browning, we conducted an untargeted LC‒MS-based metabolomics analysis. A total of 2,491 metabolites were identified after quality control, including 1,181 differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs) (Table S2a). Specifically, 637 DAMs (551 upregulated and 86 downregulated), 1,077 DAMs (913 upregulated and 164 downregulated), and 581 DAMs (512 upregulated and 69 downregulated) were identified in the SB1 vs. SB0, SB2 vs. SB0, and SB2 vs. SB1 comparisons, respectively. A Venn diagram revealed 248 DAMs common to all three comparison groups (Fig. 2A), most of which were flavonoids (61.69%), phenolic acids (12.50%), terpenoids (5.65%), lignans and coumarins (5.24%) (Table S2b).

To identify metabolites linked to sunburn severity, we performed KEGG pathway enrichment analysis on the DAMs. The pathways were enriched in flavonoid biosynthesis (ko00941), flavone and flavonol biosynthesis (ko00944) and isoflavonoid biosynthesis (ko00943) (Fig. 2B–D). Based on orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) and multivariate analysis p values, the top 20 DAMs were further selected (Fig. 2C–E). Most metabolites were upregulated; only four metabolites (okanin-4'-O-glucoside, enterodiol, 3-dihydrocadambine, and nicotine) were downregulated. Flavonoids, lignans, and coumarins constituted the majority of these top 20 DAMs. Together, these findings suggest that flavonoids, lignans, and coumarins may play key roles in the defence response of ‘Baiyushizi’ pomegranates against sunburn browning.

Transcriptome analysis in response to sunburn browning

To identify genes underlying metabolite responses to sunburn browning, RNA-seq libraries were constructed using the Illumina platform. Nine cDNA libraries generated 62.39–86.14 million high-quality clean reads per replicate; 97.45% of the reads uniquely mapped to the pomegranate reference genome. Q20 ≥ 98%, Q30 ≥ 94% and GC 50.38–51.08% confirmed the sequencing reliability (Table S3). Boxplots of log10 (FPKM) values across all the samples revealed consistent distribution shapes, suggesting successful normalization without obvious systematic bias towards low or high expression ranges (Fig. S1).

Pairwise comparisons across the three sunburn grades revealed 2,198 DEGs (Table S4). SB1 vs. SB0, SB2 vs. SB0 and SB2 vs. SB1 yielded 464, 1,648 and 1,071 DEGs, respectively, with only 41 genes shared among all the comparisons (Fig. 3A, B). Thus, sunburn browning elicits a graded transcriptional response, with SB2 showing the greatest reprogramming.

Gene Ontology (GO) analysis was performed to annotate gene functions and identify enriched terms. The assigned GO terms fell into three main categories: biological process, cellular component, and molecular function. Among the DEGs, 348 of 464 in SB1/SB0, 1156 of 1648 in SB2/SB0, and 734 of 1071 in SB2/SB1 were assigned to at least one GO term related to sunburn browning. In SB1/SB0, the most enriched subcategories were response to heat, response to oxidative stress, and response to reactive oxygen species. In SB2/SB0, the top enriched terms were tetrapyrrole binding, secondary metabolic process, and heme binding. For SB2/SB1, secondary metabolic processes, phenylpropanoid metabolic processes, and secondary metabolite biosynthetic processes were the most enriched (Fig. 3C).

To further investigate the key pathways involved in the sunburn browning response, the DEGs were annotated using the KEGG database. In SB1/SB0, the enriched pathways included protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum, circadian rhythm–plant, and photosynthesis. In SB2/SB0, the main pathways were metabolic pathways; photosynthesis–antenna proteins; and cutin, suberine, and wax biosynthesis. For SB2/SB1, the top enriched pathways were stilbenoid, diarylheptanoid and gingerol biosynthesis; biosynthesis of various plant secondary metabolites; and cutin, suberine and wax biosynthesis (Fig. 3D). These KEGG enrichment results demonstrate that genes in ‘Baiyushizi’ pomegranate pericarp were differentially expressed across the comparison groups, suggesting that different molecular mechanisms are activated in SB1 and SB2.

Weighted gene coexpression network analysis

To link transcriptomic changes to the pericarp physiological index, we applied WGCNA to 2198 DEGs from the ‘Baiyushizi’ pomegranate. Six modules containing 118–1,240 genes were identified (Fig. 4A). The turquoise module was strongly positively correlated with the physiological index (R ≥ 0.82, q < 0.05), whereas the brown module was negatively correlated (R ≤ − 0.80, q < 0.05) (Fig. 4B), indicating that genes within these modules drive either the accumulation or the degradation of metabolites associated with sunburn browning.

KEGG enrichment of the turquoise module revealed significant overrepresentation of cutin, suberine and wax biosynthesis, photosynthesis–antenna proteins, secondary metabolite biosynthesis, glycerolipid metabolism and plant–pathogen interactions (Fig. 4C). In contrast, the brown module was dominated by genes related to starch and sucrose metabolism (Fig. 4D). Several pathways related to the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, including flavonoid, phenylpropanoid, isoflavonoid, and plant secondary metabolite biosynthesis, were also enriched within the turquoise module, underscoring their pivotal role in the pericarp’s protective response to sunburn browning.

Transcriptomics and metabolomics integration revealed key metabolic pathways regulating sunburn browning

To identify the candidate genes associated with key secondary metabolites involved in the response to sunburn browning, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of the DAMs and DEGs.

Metabolomic analysis revealed 24 DEMs involved in flavonoid biosynthesis. Compared with SB0 pericarps, three flavonoid metabolites (eriodictyol chalcone, eriodictyol, and naringenin chalcone) decreased significantly under SB1, whereas 13 flavonoid metabolites increased. Under SB2, 20 flavonoid metabolites increased significantly, whereas (-)-epicatechin decreased. Total flavonoid levels were strongly correlated with the ME-turquoise module and negatively associated with the ME-brown module. A regulatory network was constructed using the 24 metabolites and their associated structural genes involved in flavonoid synthesis within these two modules. In total, 17 flavonoid synthesis-related genes were identified, including 10 shikimate O-hydroxycinnamoyltransferase (HCT), trans-cinnamate 4-monooxygenase (CYP73A), two flavonol synthases (FLS), chalcone isomerase (CHI), naringenin 3-dioxygenase (F3H), leucoanthocyanidin reductase (LAR) and flavonoid 3'-monooxygenase (CYP75B1). The expression level of HCT3, a key regulator of flavonoid synthesis, was positively correlated with the accumulation of four key metabolites (kaempferol, pinocembrin, luteolin, and pinocembrin chalcone) (R > 0.85) (Fig. 5A). TFs from the ME-turquoise and ME-brown modules associated with 16 structural genes were further examined, and 40 TFs strongly correlated with 12 structural genes (|R|> 0.9) (Fig. 5B). These TFs belong to 19 families, including WRKY, NAC, MYB, bHLH, and bZIP (Table S5a). Among the 40 TFs, 26 exhibited the strongest coexpression with HCT1. These results showed that these TFs likely modulate flavonoid metabolism by influencing the levels of these structural genes during sunburn browning.

Phenolic acids constitute the major fraction of total phenolics. The accumulation profiles of four phenylpropanoid pathway-associated metabolites, namely, 1-O-sinapoyl-β-D-glucose, sinapoyl aldehyde, 5-O-caffeoylshikimic acid, and 4-coumarate, differed during sunburn browning. Specifically, 1-O-sinapoyl-β-D-glucose levels decreased steadily with increasing severity; sinapoyl aldehyde levels decreased in SB1 but increased in SB2; and 5-O-caffeoylshikimic acid levels increased specifically in SB2; in contrast, 4-coumarate accumulated in both SB1 and SB2. Twenty-six structural genes of the pathway were captured in the ME-turquoise and ME-brown modules. Although most genes were upregulated with increasing stress, HCT1, HCT2 and peroxidase5 showed the opposite trend. The accumulation of 5-O-caffeoylshikimic acid was positively correlated with the expression levels of ten genes, particularly HCT5; likewise, the levels of 1-O-sinapoyl-β-D-glucose were positively associated with the expression levels of CCR1, CCR2, and HCT2 (Fig. 5C). We constructed a high-confidence correlation network (|R|> 0.95) connecting 38 TFs with 19 structural genes (Fig. 5D, Table S5b), among which 18 TFs showed specific associations with CYP73A. Together, these findings establish an integrated transcriptional‒metabolic framework that governs phenolic–acid homeostasis during sunburn browning.

In pomegranate, coumarins and lignans serve as important antioxidants within the pericarp. Their levels changed significantly in response to sunburn browning. Notably, the three main compounds, scopoletin, xanthotoxol, and secoisolariciresinol, decreased with increasing sunburn severity, with the most pronounced decline observed in the SB2 samples. We identified five fraxetin 5-hydroxylase (CYP82C4) genes and one pinoresinol/lariciresinol reductase (PLR) gene associated with coumarin and lignan synthesis in the ME-turquoise and ME-brown modules. Sunburn browning induced the expression of these genes, with transcript levels significantly upregulated in SB2. Among them, CYP82C4.5 showed the strongest correlation with secoisolariciresinol and scopoletin metabolism (Fig. 5E). Furthermore, we identified TFs strongly coexpressed (|R|> 0.90) with these five key genes and constructed a regulatory network (Fig. 5F and Table S5c). Among the 55 TFs identified, 36 (including members of the bZIP, MYB, and bHLH families) exhibited the strongest coexpression with CYP82C4.4. Integrated analysis revealed that these TFs likely regulate coumarin and lignan metabolism by modulating the expression of these structural genes during sunburn browning.

Verification of the transcriptome data by RT‒qPCR

To validate the accuracy of our transcriptomic sequencing, we performed RT‒qPCR analysis on seven structural genes and five transcription factors. Their expression patterns were consistent with the RNA-seq data (Fig. 6), confirming the reliability of our transcriptome dataset for evaluating gene expression in pomegranate pericarps with varying degrees of sunburn browning.

Discussion

Sunburn in pomegranates is caused primarily by high solar radiation and elevated temperatures. Given that the species is widely cultivated in arid zones, drought stress further increases the risk of sunburn. This occurs by reducing canopy transpiration, which diminishes cooling and humidity within the bunch zone, and by promoting smaller canopies that expose bunches to more direct sunlight [29]. In recent years, changing climatic conditions have exacerbated sunburn damage, increasingly limiting pomegranate production and quality. The mechanisms by which fruit pericarp respond to sunburn vary across cultivars and peel colours [2]. Sunburn browning is often the most prevalent type of sunburn in white pomegranate varieties. Most existing studies have focused primarily on influencing factors and prevention strategies [16, 30, 31]; however, the underlying molecular and physiological mechanisms in white pomegranate varieties remain unclear. By integrating physiological, cellular, metabolomic, and transcriptomic analyses, this study comprehensively elucidates the mechanism underlying sunburn browning in white ‘Baiyushizi’ pomegranates.

Sunburn browning primarily affects fruit appearance through pericarp discolouration. In ‘Bayushizi’ pomegranates, the pericarp colour shifts from yellow to brown with increasing damage severity—a phenomenon also observed in sunburned apple and pear peels [10, 12]. These phenotypic alterations reflect dynamic changes in photosynthetic and photoprotective pigments. Unlike red varieties, white varieties accumulate chlorophylls and carotenoids but lack anthocyanins [32]. Sunburn triggers ROS-mediated chlorophyll catabolism, which manifests as pericarp yellowing [33]. This degradation plays dual roles: as an oxidative stress biomarker and in photoprotective adaptation via reduced light harvesting [34]. Concomitant carotenoid accumulation in the exposed pericarp enhances photoprotection through nonphotochemical quenching and reactive oxygen species scavenging [2]. In red ‘Hongyushizi’ pomegranates, sunburn stress disrupts anthocyanin biosynthesis, leading to a marked reduction in pelargonidin content [18]. Similarly, in red berries, sunburn affects anthocyanin biosynthesis and manifests as poor colour development and bleached spots [29]. From a physical perspective, fruits containing anthocyanins reach higher temperatures upon illumination than those lacking these pigments do owing to their lower albedo, a phenomenon that may counteract the photoprotective effects of anthocyanins [35]. This also explains why in the anthocyanin-rich ‘Hongyushizi’ pomegranate, severe sunburn necrosis predominates [18], whereas in the white ‘Baiyushizi’ pomegranate, sunburn browning is dominant.

Fruits mitigate sunburn stress through diverse physiological strategies. Sunburn induces ROS bursts that trigger membrane lipid peroxidation, disrupting membrane integrity [9, 34]. In this study, sunburn browning significantly increased H2O2, O2−, and MDA levels, indicating the occurrence of ROS-driven membrane damage. ROS act as signalling molecules, triggering lignin and suberin synthesis to reinforce cell walls in sun-exposed tissues [36]. Histological observations revealed significantly increased lignin accumulation and elevated ROS levels in sunburned ‘Baiyushizi’ pomegranate pericarps. This deposition enhances structural integrity and inhibits pathogen ingress [37]. To counteract ROS, fruits activate enzymatic antioxidant systems. Under stress, SOD constitutes the first line of defence, whereas CAT and POD further scavenge ROS [9]. In pomegranate pericarps, SOD and CAT activities increased under sunburn stress, indicating their protective role. However, no POD activity was detected, suggesting that POD does not contribute to ROS removal in this context. The lack of anthocyanins in the white ‘Baiyushizi’ pomegranate may be the primary reason for the undetected POD activity, as POD activity has been found to be regulated by the anthocyanin content [38]. In addition to enzymatic antioxidants, non‑enzymatic compounds also alleviate oxidative injury. The TPs and TFs contents increased with increasing sunburn severity, suggesting that their induced accumulation is involved in stress resistance. To further elucidate the metabolic responses to sunburn, we conducted an integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analysis.

Under stress, plants often increase secondary metabolite production for protection. The phenylpropanoid pathway is a major metabolic pathway whose branches produce flavonoids, phenolic acids, lignans, and coumarins, all of which contribute to stress resistance [39]. Among these compounds, flavonoids are particularly important secondary metabolites that mitigate various stresses [18, 39, 40]. In this study, sunburn browning significantly increased the total flavonoid content. Metabolomic analysis revealed that 24 flavonoid metabolites were affected, with the expression levels of key biosynthesis genes (FLS, CHI, and HCT) upregulated. Consequently, the levels of several flavonoid metabolites, including myricetin, kaempferol, pinocembrin, naringenin, and eriodictyol chalcone, significantly increased (Tables S2, S4), suggesting that these compounds function as antioxidants in the scavenging of free radicals [41]. However, this response contrasts sharply with findings in coloured ‘Hongyushizi’ pomegranate pericarps exhibiting sunburn necrosis, where key flavonoid biosynthesis genes (CHI, CHS, LAR and F3'5'H) were upregulated, but most differential flavonoid metabolites were downregulated [18]. This discrepancy likely stems from differences in sunburn severity: our study examined pericarps with sunburn browning, whereas the earlier study used necrotic tissues. These contrasting patterns suggest that sunburn browning stimulates flavonoid accumulation to counteract damage, whereas sunburn necrosis may shift the metabolic balance towards flavonoid degradation [40]. Such differential responses reflect the complex homeostatic regulation among different branches of phenylpropanoid metabolism. Redirection of metabolic flux results in extraordinary plasticity in response to environmental stimuli [42]. The activation of phenylpropanoid and flavonoid biosynthesis genes and consequent flavonoid upregulation in our study came at the cost of the downregulation of three major lignans and coumarins (scopoletin, xanthotoxol, secoisolariciresinol), suggesting that metabolic flux was redirected towards flavonoid production to mitigate ROS-induced damage [43]. These findings contrast with those of red ‘Hongyushizi’ pomegranates, which exhibit necrotic sunburn, where the phenylpropanoid pathway favours coumarin synthesis, increasing scopoletin levels [18]. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that metabolic responses to sunburn stress differ substantially depending on both the type of sunburn injury (browning vs. necrosis) and varietal differences, highlighting the highly plastic nature of phenylpropanoid pathway regulation under environmental stress. The extrapolation of these findings to other white or coloured varieties requires further comparative studies involving multiple genotypes.

Sunburn browning induced the expression of genes involved in phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, flavonoid biosynthesis, and coumarin and lignan biosynthesis in this study. Genes in these pathways were coregulated by TFs from families such as MYB, WRKY, NAC, and bHLH. Among these genes, the MYB family contained the greatest number of members involved in the response to sunburn browning (Table S5). MYB proteins are extensively involved in the response to various stresses, including high temperature, light, and UV radiation [44, 45]. For instance, MYB123, which is closely related to TT2, has been proposed to regulate heat stress responses in Arabidopsis [46]. In the present study, the induction of MYB123 promoted the expression of CYP82C4.1 and CYP82C4.4 in phenylpropanoid biosynthesis. In Melilotus albus, MaMYB4 enhances drought tolerance by activating MaUGT79 expression and promoting scopolin biosynthesis [47]. These findings suggest that MYB4 may promote the expression of target genes (PLR, CYP82C4.1, CYP82C4.4, and CYP82C4.5), thereby influencing scopolin accumulation in response to sunburn browning. The exact functions of these candidate TFs require further validation through virus-induced gene silencing (VIGS) or heterologous overexpression systems to establish their roles in the sunburn response.

Conclusions

In this study, integrated metabolomic, physiological and transcriptomic analyses were conducted to elucidate how the white ‘Baiyushizi’ pomegranate pericarp responds to sunburn browning. These data reveal that the phenylpropanoid pathway and its downstream metabolites constitute the key line of defence against sunburn browning-induced oxidative damage. Based on these findings, we suggest a possible working model for the physiological and molecular responses underlying sunburn browning in the white pomegranate cultivar studied (Fig. 7), although further functional validation is needed to confirm the regulatory mechanisms proposed. Under SB1, the fruit experiences modest osmotic and oxidative stress, whereas SB2 triggers a burst of ROS. In both scenarios, sunburn browning rapidly upregulates the contents of antioxidant enzymes (SOD and CAT) and nonenzymatic antioxidants (carotenoids, TFs, and TPs) to repair the damage caused by oxidative stress. The marked accumulation of flavonoids and phenolic acids is the hallmark of this protective response. Conversely, the levels of chlorophyll and some coumarins/lignans decline progressively as the severity of sunburn browning intensifies. Key structural genes and TFs governing flavonoids, phenolic acids, coumarins and lignans were identified. Collectively, our findings elucidate the mechanism underlying sunburn browning in the white ‘Baiyushizi’ pomegranate. Future research should include comparative analyses across multiple pomegranate cultivars with varying degrees of susceptibility to validate the universality of the proposed regulatory network and to validate the exact functions of these candidate TFs (MYB 4 and MYB 123).
