Rheopheresis in hemodialysis patients with limb ulcers or amputation: safety and outcomes from a multicenter cohort
Authors: Yuri Bonsante, Cyril Ferdynus, Ehshaneraza Asgarali, Ludovic Di Ascia, Henri Vacher-Coponat, Asma Allal, Anabelle Baillet, Gabriel Gruget, Stéphanie Delelis, Reuben Veerapen, Julien Gayon, Hamza Naciri Bennani, Arnaud Le Flecher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLimb ulcers are common in hemodialysis (HD) and often have a poor prognosis despite standard care. Rheopheresis is a plasmapheresis technique that reduces blood viscosity and erythrocyte aggregation and has been proposed as an adjunctive therapy, but no study has yet compared it to standard care alone.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective multicenter study comparing 61 hemodialysis (HD) patients treated with rheopheresis for limb ulcer or amputation in five French dialysis centers with 119 HD patients receiving standard care matched on age, lesion type, and lesion site. Outcomes were analyzed using propensity score–matched cohorts. The primary outcome was 1-year amputation-free survival (AFS).ResultsOne-year AFS reached 58.9% (95% CI: 45.1; 70.4) in the rheopheresis cohort and 28.8% (95% CI: 20.9; 37.2) in the standard care cohort. This difference did not reach statistical significance after adjustment for immortal-time bias (HR: 1.28, 95% CI: 0.72–2.25). Secondary outcomes followed similar trends, slightly in favor of rheopheresis without reaching significance. Major adverse events occurred in only 2.4% of rheopheresis sessions, and no treatment-related deaths were reported.ConclusionsRheopheresis was well tolerated and demonstrated a favorable safety profile. Clinical outcomes were numerically better with rheopheresis compared to standard care, although differences did not reach statistical significance. These findings suggest a potential benefit, warranting larger prospective studies.

Introduction

Peripheral artery disease (PAD) and diabetic foot ulcer (DFU) are two common comorbidities in Hemodialysis (HD) patients and are associated with worse clinical outcomes compared to non-HD patients. HD patients have a 7.6-fold higher incidence of foot ulceration and a 15.0 fold higher incidence for limb amputation than CKD stage 3 patients [1]. The amputation incidence rate has been slowly decreasing but remains high in HD patients (2 to 3 per 100 patients/year) [2–4]. In addition, HD patients have an increased risk of wound recurrence regardless of revascularization and a low 1-year amputation-free-survival (AFS) after revascularization, ranging between 47% and 70% [5–8]. Survival rates at 1 and 5 years in HD patients with foot ulcers remain poor, at approximately 60 to 65% and 5 to 25% respectively, despite revascularization [5, 9].

Chronic limb ulcers are often the combined result of impaired macrocirculation and microcirculation. Impaired rheology and an increased blood viscosity have been associated with ischemic heart disease, stroke, progression of foot ulcers [10–15]; and cardiovascular mortality [16, 17]. The current standard of care for limb ulcers includes wound management, revascularization when indicated, and optimization of cardiovascular risk factors. Antiplatelet therapy and LDL-C lowering treatments (e.g. statins) are recommended for treating the microvascular component of PAD [18, 19]. Despite those medications being widely prescribed among HD patients, new treatment options are needed to prevent amputation and mortality in this population.

Rheopheresis is a double-filtration plasmapheresis technique that removes a range of high-molecular-weight proteins involved in blood viscosity, including fibrinogen, LDL-C, IgM, and von Willebrand factor (vWF) [20]. This process lowers blood viscosity, reduces erythrocyte aggregation, and exerts an acute anti-inflammatory effect, which may be beneficial in microcirculation disorders [21, 22]. Rheopheresis and hemodialysis can be performed simultaneously as tandem procedures (Fig. 1) with HD patients who usually have an optimal vascular access [23].

The efficacy of rheopheresis has been hypothesized for sudden sensorineural hearing loss [24, 25]; and age-related macular degeneration [26–28]. The 2023 ASFA (American Society for Apheresis) guidelines recommend rheopheresis as a treatment for these diseases (respectively with grade 2 A and 2B). Further literature suggests its efficacy for treating limb ulcers [29]. Recent case reports and case series advocate for the efficacy of rheopheresis in treating calciphylaxis in HD patients [30–32].

Recently, a retrospective cohort of 28 HD patients that underwent rheopheresis for a PAD-related chronic ulceration or a PAD-related amputation in 3 French centers assessed major clinical outcomes such as 1-year AFS and overall survival [33]. Additionally, a prospective multicenter study conducted on 25 HD patients demonstrated an improvement in foot lesion-related pain 10 weeks after starting rheopheresis treatment [34]. To our knowledge, no study has, to date compared clinical outcomes between HD patients treated and untreated with rheopheresis. The ongoing national RHEOPAD study in France is a prospective, randomized, controlled, single-blind, multicenter trial that aims to evaluate the efficacy of rheopheresis in this high-risk population. This trial assesses clinical outcomes as well as biological parameters including blood viscosity, skin microcirculation, and coagulation.

The overseas French region of Reunion Island has the highest incidence rate of hospitalization for PAD among all French regions, exceeding 200 per 100,000 people and the fourth highest incidence rate of hospitalization for DFU related amputation [35, 36]. In addition, the incidence rate of dialysis (HD or peritoneal dialysis) was over 351 per million persons in 2022, more than twice the national average [37].

The aim of this study was to compare clinical outcomes in a cohort of HD patients with DFU, PAD-related chronic ulcers, or amputations treated with rheopheresis, to those in a matched cohort receiving standard care.

Materials and methods

Study population

We conducted a retrospective multicenter study involving HD patients who, at baseline, presented with limb ulceration [peripheral artery disease (PAD) – stage IV of the Fontaine classification or stages 5 or 6 of the Rutherford classification], or who had undergone baseline limb amputation leading to rheopheresis treatment.

Patients in the rheopheresis cohort were identified across five HD centers in Reunion Island. Patients were selected for rheopheresis treatment based on clinicians’ discretion.

Exclusion criteria included initiation of rheopheresis more than 6 months after diagnosis of limb ulcer or amputation, initiation of HD after the diagnosis of limb ulcer, and missing data.

To form the standard care cohort, we selected two HD patients for each rheopheresis-treated patient, matched by age (± 10 years), lesion type (limb ulcer or amputation), and lesion site (foot, lower leg, thigh, or hand). Patients were identified using diagnosis codes for limb ulcer and amputation from the University Hospital of La Réunion database.

Standard practice

Aside from rheopheresis, treatment patterns for limb ulceration and peripheral artery disease were similar across all participating centers. Patients were routinely prescribed statin and antiplatelet therapy when PAD was confirmed. In addition, following the diagnosis of limb ulceration, patients underwent a contrast-enhanced CT angiography of the lower limbs, followed by a vascular surgery consultation to discuss revascularization.

Rheopheresis management and monitoring

Rheopheresis treatment was performed using a Plasauto Σ supplied by Hema. T Medical (Ramonville Saint Agne, France) with a Plasmaflo 0P-08 W® for plasma separation and Rheofilter ER-4000® for removal of high-molecular-weight proteins with an internal fiber diameter of 175 μm (Asahi Kasei Medical, Tokyo, Japan).

Rheopheresis was performed during the first two hours of hemodialysis by connecting the rheopheresis and dialysis circuits in tandem (Fig. 1). The initial plasma treatment volume was set at 25 mL/kg for the first two sessions, then increased to 40 mL/kg for subsequent sessions. Thereafter the plasma volume was adjusted to reach a post-session fibrinogen level of 1–1.5 g/L. Blood flow was 150 ml/min and plasma separation was 25% of maximum blood-flow rate. Vascular access consisted either of an arteriovenous fistula or a central venous catheter (jugular or femoral), according to the patient’s established hemodialysis access.

All patients received plasma exchange replacement fluid with 20 g of intravenous albumin at the beginning of the session. Additional fluid replacement with 0.9% saline or an additional 20 g albumin infusion was administered at the physician’s discretion. Anticoagulation was achieved with a low molecular weight heparin bolus administered upstream of the plasma filter.

At the beginning of rheopheresis treatment “standard regimen” consisted of biweekly sessions for 2 weeks, followed by weekly sessions for 8 weeks. Thereafter, treatment frequency was tapered at the physician’s discretion, typically until complete wound healing.

Dialysis sessions were prescribed three times a week.

Adverse events were recorded for each rheopheresis session with an available monitoring report and classified as either minor or major (the latter leading to session discontinuation).

Data collection

This retrospective study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All data were fully anonymized before analysis. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (CER-BDX 2024-36). In accordance with French regulations, patients received an information letter and were provided the opportunity to opt out of data use.

For each patient, the following data were extracted from medical records: medical history, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), lesion type and site, presence of osteitis and any surgical debridement or irrigation performed. Baseline laboratory results were recorded when available and included C-Reactive Protein (CRP), fibrinogen, albuminemia, proteinemia, hemoglobin and mean corpuscular volume (MCV).

Outcomes assessed after baseline diagnosis comprised death, amputation, complete wound healing, any surgical debridement procedure, cumulative hospitalization duration within six months, the highest level of amputation at one year, and ambulatory status at follow-up end. Optimal revascularization was defined as angioplasty-eligible vascular stenosis 3 months post- baseline.

For patients undergoing rheopheresis, we assessed the number of sessions and the adverse events observed during the sessions (severe hypotension was defined by a systolic blood pressure lower than 80mmHg). Additionally, we collected the following laboratory results for each patient treated with rheopheresis, when available: CRP, fibrinogen, albuminemia, proteinemia, hemoglobin, LDL-C, total cholesterol, and gamma-globulins on the first day of treatment, as well as at 2 and 4 weeks after rheopheresis initiation. All blood samples were drawn at the start of the corresponding sessions.

Study end points

The primary composite endpoint was 1-year AFS after diagnosis of limb ulcer or after baseline amputation.

Secondary endpoints included: 1-year overall survival, 1-year amputation-free rate, complete wound healing (complete healing of baseline ulcer or amputation site), and highest level of amputation at 1 year.

We also described the adverse events observed in the rheopheresis cohort.

Statistical analysis

To control for potential confounding, a propensity score (PS) was calculated for each patient using a multivariable logistic regression model including baseline covariates likely to influence treatment assignment: age, sex, presence of diabetes, prior amputation, lesion type (ulcer or amputation), anatomical level, CCI, and CRP at diagnosis.

Patients treated with rheopheresis were matched to those receiving standard care using nearest-neighbor matching without replacement, applying a caliper width of 0.2 of the standard deviation of the logit of the PS. Match quality was assessed by visual inspection of covariate distributions and calculation of standardized mean differences (SMDs).

Baseline characteristics were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median [interquartile range, IQR] for continuous variables, and counts (percentages) for categorical variables. Between-group comparisons used Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, and Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. An SMD < 0.1 indicated good covariate balance.

Survival analyses used stratified Cox proportional hazards models incorporating rheopheresis as a time-dependent covariate to address immortal time bias, with stratification by matched sets. In this framework, rheopheresis patients contributed to the “untreated” risk set until treatment initiation, and to the “treated” risk set thereafter. This approach allowed unbiased hazard ratio (HR) estimation for outcomes such as 1-year AFS, overall survival, wound healing, and amputation rates.

All analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC). A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline population

We estimated that a total of 240 patients (a 3:1 ratio of matched patients) would provide 80% power to detect a 30% risk reduction in 1-year AFS in the rheopheresis cohort.

Across five dialysis centers in Reunion Island, 72 HD patients who received rheopheresis were screened for inclusion, of whom 61 were ultimately included. Additionally, 275 HD patients presenting with baseline limb ulceration or amputation who did not receive rheopheresis were screened, of whom 119 were included in the standard care cohort, matched for age (± 10 years), lesion type, and lesion site. Baseline CCI was similar between cohorts. Patients in the rheopheresis cohort had lower prevalence of diabetes (88.5% vs. 96.6%; p = 0.05) and diabetic retinopathy (p < 0.001). A history of prior amputation was more common in the rheopheresis cohort (52.5% vs. 37.3%; p = 0.05), as was previous lower limb revascularization (p = 0.04). Osteitis at baseline was significantly less prevalent in the rheopheresis cohort (23.0% vs. 46.2%; p = 0.003).

Laboratory data indicated significantly higher CRP in the standard care cohort (110.0 mg/L vs. 55.3 mg/L; p = 0.007). Optimal revascularization at 3 months was achieved more frequently in the rheopheresis cohort (100% vs. 85,7%; p < 0.001).

Clinical outcomes on primary and secondary endpoints

For the primary endpoint, the 1-year AFS was 58.9% (95% CI: 45.1–70.4) in the rheopheresis cohort and 28.8% (95% CI: 20.9–37.2) in the standard care cohort. However, the difference was not statistically significant after adjustment for immortal time bias (HR: 1.28, 95% CI: 0.72–2.25).

The one-year overall survival was 81% (95% CI: 68.0–89.0) in the rheopheresis cohort and 69% (95% CI: 60.0–77.0) in the standard care cohort (HR: 1.41, 95% CI: 0.67–2.99). Similar, non-significant trends were observed for secondary endpoints, including the 1-year amputation-free rate (HR: 1.29, 95% CI: 0.65–2.54) and complete wound healing (HR: 2.06, 95% CI: 0.92–4.57). These results are summarized in Fig. 3. Progression to a higher amputation level occurred more often in the standard care group (p < 0.001).

The main causes of death at 1-year follow-up are presented in Table 3. The leading causes were limb lesion–related sepsis (n = 16, 36.4%) and dialysis discontinuation (n = 8, 18.2%), with similar proportions in both cohorts. The cause of death could not be determined for four patients in each cohort.

At 6 months from baseline, median hospitalization duration was 7 days (IQR: 3.8–25) in the rheopheresis cohort and 24 days (IQR: 10–42.8) in the standard care cohort (p = 0.01).

Rheopheresis sessions

Data on the number of sessions were available for 57 of the 61 treated patients. The median time from ulcer or amputation diagnosis to initiation of rheopheresis was 57.0 days (95% CI: 44.4–69.7). Among these patients, the median number of sessions was 21 (IQR: 12–36).

A total of 49 patients received the “standard regimen” of 12 sessions in 10 weeks. In two dialysis centers, 9 patients received one weekly session for 12 weeks. Physicians would then adapt treatment’s frequency, as patients would either undergo another 12-sessions cycle or weekly sessions until treatment discontinuation.

In total, 1,201 rheopheresis sessions were available for adverse event analysis (Table 4). Adverse events occurred in 197 sessions (16.4%), and major adverse events (leading to session discontinuation) in 29 sessions (2.4%). Severe hypotension was the most frequent adverse event, occurring in 172 sessions (14.3%) and leading to discontinuation in 22 sessions (1.8%).

One episode of cardiopulmonary arrest occurred but was successfully reversed, with no fatal outcome. Rheopheresis was discontinued after this event, the patient died 16 months later following withdrawal from HD, unrelated to rheopheresis. One patient on antiplatelet therapy experienced prolonged bleeding time at the end of a session; however, no major hemorrhagic events were observed.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to compare AFS between HD patients receiving rheopheresis and a matched standard care cohort. Notably, the rheopheresis cohort represents the largest group of patients treated with rheopheresis evaluated for robust clinical endpoints.

In this study, rheopheresis was not associated with a statistically significant improvement in 1-year amputation-free survival compared to standard care. While point estimates for 1-year mortality, amputation rate, and complete wound healing all favored rheopheresis, none of these differences reached statistical significance after adjustment. To further investigate the potential effect of rheopheresis on these clinical outcomes, adequately powered randomized trials are needed before any clinical recommendation can be made.

In our rheopheresis cohort, the 1-year AFS rate was consistent with previously reported outcomes. Solignac et al., in a French cohort study, reported a 1-year AFS rate of 53.6% [33]. However, direct comparison is limited by methodological differences, particularly the definition of baseline, which in their study corresponded to the initiation of rheopheresis. The 1-year AFS rate in our standard care cohort was 28.8% (95% CI: 20.9–37.2), which is lower than what has been reported in the literature. For instance, Fallon et al. documented a 1-year AFS rate of 40% in a cohort of 394 HD patients with severe PAD following revascularization [5]. Similarly, in the CRITISCH cohort, the 1-year AFS rate was 47% among 102 hemodialysis patients with PAD [8]. Comparisons with our results are limited by substantial differences in initial management strategies: primary amputation was performed as first-line treatment in 34.4% of our patients, compared with only 0% and 3% in the Fallon and CRITISCH cohorts, respectively.

This difference may be explained by the likelihood that patients in the standard care cohort more frequently presented with infected lesions, as suggested by the higher baseline inflammatory markers in this group. In such cases, physicians typically aim to perform the most distal amputation possible, often requiring later revision to a higher level. This approach could partially explain the lower AFS rate observed in our standard care cohort. In contrast, progression to a higher amputation level occurred less frequently in the rheopheresis cohort. Although the generalizability of our findings is limited by cohort characteristics, these results raise the possibility that rheopheresis may help limit the extent of amputations.

Our findings also support the favorable safety profile of rheopheresis in HD patients with limb ulcers or recent amputations, both during treatment and in long-term follow-up. No deaths were attributable to rheopheresis, and most adverse events were consistent with those typically observed during standard HD sessions.

This study has several limitations. First, it is subject to the inherent biases of retrospective analyses. Second, comparing a specific treatment with no treatment retrospectively introduces immortal time bias, which we attempted to address through statistical adjustment. Third, despite propensity score matching, residual baseline imbalances persisted between the two cohorts and may have contributed to residual confounding. Rheopheresis-treated patients were identified through public and private ambulatory HD centers, whereas standard care patients were included via the University Hospital of Reunion Island, raising the possibility of selection bias, as patients managed at a tertiary academic center may have presented with more advanced disease compared to those managed in ambulatory dialysis centers. Consistent with this, they exhibited higher rates of diabetes and diabetic retinopathy. While this likely limits the generalizability of our findings, both populations remain representative of patients typically considered for rheopheresis in clinical practice, and all participating centers were able to manage a wide range of comorbidities, as reflected by similar CCI scores in both cohorts. Higher baseline CRP levels and a greater prevalence of osteitis in the standard care cohort further suggest more frequent initial wound infections, which are known to impair healing. Additionally, optimal revascularization at 3 months was achieved less frequently in the standard care cohort, potentially due to early amputations. Although this difference was modest, it may have increased the risk of subsequent amputation, thereby biasing the results in favor of rheopheresis efficacy. Overall, these imbalances point toward greater disease severity in the standard care group, and the residual confounding they introduce is unlikely to be fully resolved by statistical adjustment alone. Fourth, non-fatal complications such as infectious or hemorrhagic events occurring outside rheopheresis sessions during follow-up were not systematically recorded in our dataset and could therefore not be compared between groups. Fifth, the multicentric design of our study limited the collection of outcomes such as pain measurement or quality of life. Finally, the relatively small sample size of our study, which fell short of the number initially estimated in the power calculations, likely limited statistical power, and may partially explain the wide confidence intervals observed for our estimates. This limitation should be considered when interpreting non-significant findings, as the absence of statistically significant differences does not necessarily exclude a clinically meaningful effect of rheopheresis.

It is also worth noting that our inclusion criteria may be open to discussion, as only patients with trophic lesions were enrolled. In addition, the long delay between diagnosis and initiation of rheopheresis may have exposed patients to more advanced disease, as trophic lesions represent late stages with partially necrotic tissue in which the benefit of microcirculatory optimization may be limited. Altobelli et al. reported a reduction in PAD-related lower-limb pain among patients with trophic lesions treated with rheopheresis. Since ischemic pain generally precedes the development of trophic lesions, these findings raise the possibility that rheopheresis may provide greater benefit when initiated at earlier stages of PAD, before tissue damage becomes too advanced for improvements in microcirculatory perfusion to meaningfully alter the course of the disease [34]. One might therefore hypothesize that rheopheresis could be more beneficial if initiated earlier in PAD patients before tissue loss occurs.

Finally, rheopheresis is a resource-intensive technique, with a single session costing approximately 20 times that of a standard HD session. To date, no study has formally assessed the cost-effectiveness of rheopheresis in the treatment of ischemic trophic lesions, and the retrospective multicenter design of our study did not allow for such an evaluation. Beyond cost, feasibility is also limited by the requirement for specialized equipment and trained personnel. Nonetheless, real-world implementation is achievable: in Reunion Island, where CKD and PAD represent a major public health burden, all in-center HD units have integrated rheopheresis into their practice over the past six years (since 2020).

Dedicated prospective studies, ideally embedded within randomized trials such as the ongoing French RHEO-PAD study (NCT03975946), will be needed to determine whether the clinical benefit of rheopheresis justifies its cost and to guide broader implementation.

Conclusion

In this retrospective cohort study, rheopheresis was not associated with a statistically significant improvement in 1-year AFS, overall survival, or complete wound healing in HD patients with severe PAD-related limb lesions, although point estimates consistently favored rheopheresis across all outcomes. Given its favorable safety profile and potential to reduce amputation-related complications, further prospective investigations are warranted. Determining the optimal timing for initiating rheopheresis also appears relevant; including patients with chronic limb ischemia at the stage of rest pain without tissue loss could be of particular interest.
