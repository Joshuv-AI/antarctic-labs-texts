Retrospective study of minimally invasive percutaneous ultrasonic tenotomy for chronic patellar tendinosis
Authors: Roy Akarakian, Andreea Geamanu, Audrey Moffat, Chaoyang Chen, Sadiq Haque, Nicholas Moore, Brandon Kakos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundJumper’s knee or patellar tendinosis is a debilitating condition occurring in up to 40% of athletes that perform jumping, rapid acceleration and deceleration movements. Minimally invasive percutaneous ultrasonic tenotomy (MIPUT) provides an efficacious, non-surgical procedure for treating this condition. In this study, we hypothesized that patients with patellar tendinitis would achieve a reduced visual analog scale (VAS) pain score after MIPUT.MethodsA total of 42 knees with chronic patellar tendinosis which had previously failed conservative treatment before undergoing MIPUT were retrospectively reviewed. Patient demographics and medical history were recorded and VAS pain score was assessed at the 2-, 6-, and 12-week follow-up visits. Patients were instructed to follow physician specific activity modifications for 1–2 weeks following the procedure.ResultsPatients enrolled included 15 males and 27 females, averaging 39 years old. Post treatment, 38 patients were seen at 2 weeks, 24 at 6 weeks, and 10 at 12 weeks. The average VAS pain score pre-MIPUT was 5.12. After MIPUT, the average VAS score was 2.95, 2.96, and 2.60 at 2, 6, and 12 weeks, respectively. There was a statistically significant difference between pre-treatment pain score and all follow-up visits (p = 0.04, observed power of 66.9%). For patients in the 18–39 age group, the average VAS score was 4.54 pre-MIPUT and 2.05, 2.77, and 2.33 at 2-, 6-, and 12-week follow-ups, respectively (p < 0.024). A significant reduction in VAS pain scores was also seen in the 40–59 group when comparing the pre-treatment score of 5.80 to 2.78 and 3.0 at 6 and 12 weeks, respectively (p < 0.032). Overall, there was not a difference in pain score between age groups over the follow-up period (p = 0.681, observed power 6.6%).ConclusionsMIPUT is a safe and effective procedure for pain relief in those with patellar tendinopathy and no complications were seen in this study population. Future research could target longer term outcomes and improvement in quality of life.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-026-09703-2.Keywords: Conservative treatment, Athletes, Quality of Life, Basketball, Visual Analog Scale

Background

Chronic patellar tendinosis, also known as “Jumper’s knee” is a degenerative knee condition caused by repetitive jumping motions. During such jumping motions, high amounts of stress are applied to the extensor mechanism, creating small tears in the patellar tendon [1]. The overall incidence of chronic patellar tendinosis in athletes is 14%; this increases to 30–40% in sports that involve jumping, rapid acceleration or deceleration and kicking, such as basketball, volleyball, soccer and tennis [2, 3]. Blazina et al. first used the term patellar tendinitis in 1973, and further classified it into four stages [4]. Patellar tendinitis can become chronic in professional athletes and lead to early retirement [5].

Histopathologically, Alfredson et al. demonstrated a lack of inflammatory cells in patellar tendinosis [6]. The tendon often has loss of demarcation of collagen bundles and increased mucoid degeneration, intratendinous calcification, and fibrinoid necrosis. Further histological studies show elevated high molecular weight proteoglycans and type III collagen, which is suggested to be more consistent with compressive loads and adaptive changes [7].

Ultrasound has been used to diagnose patellar tendinosis for years. In 1997, Cook et al. studied 57 athletes with patellar tendinitis using ultrasound. In all 57 participants, a sonographic hypoechoic region was present at the proximal patellar attachment of the symptomatic patellar tendon. This corresponded to the region of tenderness on exam [8]. Color Doppler was shown to be a useful adjunct as it is able to identify vascularity and neovascularization signifying structural changes in the tendon [9, 10].

Historically, both non-surgical and surgical treatment options have been suggested. Non-surgical treatment includes activity restriction/modification, ice, non-steroidal anti-inflammatories (NSAIDs), stem cell therapies, platelet-rich plasma (PRP) and eccentric muscle training/stretching [2, 11, 12]. Approximately 10% of patients do not respond to non-surgical treatment, therefore, open or arthroscopic surgical procedures are often recommended. A previous systematic review showed an average success rate of 87% for open treatment with an average return to sport being 8.3 months, and 91% for arthroscopy with 3.9 month return, respectively [2].

A novel technique, Minimally Invasive Percutaneous Ultrasonic Tenotomy (MIPUT), was first studied by Koh et al. in 2013 for chronic lateral elbow tendinopathy. The study included 20 patients with diagnostic ultrasound evidence of hypervascularity and hypoechogenicity who had failed non-operative medical management. The procedure was shown to be well received and demonstrated a high level of efficacy (95%) at 6 months post intervention. Additionally, an average Visual Analog Scale (VAS) score drop of 40% (VAS score 5.5 to 3.3) and 91% drop (VAS score 5.5 to 0.5) were recorded at the 1-week and 12-month follow-up post treatment, respectively [13]. Seng et al. demonstrated 36 months post procedure that 100% of the same cohort had ultrasonic reduction in tendon thickness and hypoechoic lesion size; 94.4% achieved resolution of hypervascularity, and 100% of the patients were satisfied with the procedure [14].

Furthermore, a study done by Battista et al. with 7 subjects showed a preoperative to postoperative American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) score improvement from 55.6 to 94.1, which was maintained through the 24 months following MIPUT [15]. Traister et al. (unpublished data, 2014) also showed ~ 73% improvement in VAS pain scores at 12 weeks for lateral epicondylitis, ~ 91% in patellar tendinosis, and ~ 76% in plantar fasciitis. In all of the previous cases, the smaller sample size significantly limits the generalizability of the results. To our knowledge, no previous study has been conducted using ultrasonic tenotomy to treat patellar tendinopathy for a sample size of 42 knees.

In a previous study by Kwan et al., it is noted that the healing properties of tendons can be reduced with aging. This is due to lowered cell mobility and proliferation, as well as an overall decrease in tendon cell quantity [16]. Another factor that may affect tendon healing is body mass index (BMI) [17]. However, in a previous study by Chimenti et al. which retrospectively observed 34 patients following percutaneous ultrasonic tenotomy for insertional Achilles tendinopathy pain, average BMI was 32.9- classified as obese class I- but an increased quality of life and overall satisfaction with the procedure was still observed among patients [18].

In this study we hypothesized that patients with similar tendinopathy, such as chronic patellar tendinosis with ultrasound evidence of hypervascularity and hypoechoicity, can achieve success with MIPUT. The aim of this study is to evaluate the efficacy of percutaneous ultrasonic tenotomy for chronic patellar tendinosis at 2, 6 and 12 weeks post treatment. Patients were stratified by age and BMI in order to investigate their possible role in tendon healing. The correlation of these variables with outcome scores over time were then analyzed.

Methodology

Following approval from Wayne State University’s Institutional Review Board (IRB# 064018M1X), 60 chronic patellar tendinosis patients who underwent minimally invasive percutaneous ultrasonic tenotomy between January 2016 and January 2021 were retrospectively reviewed. Patients over the age of 18 with documented chronic pain, previously failed conservative treatment, and ultrasound evidence of hypoechoicity and/or hypervascularity within 12 weeks of the procedure were included in the study. Conservative treatment included physical therapy for at least 4 weeks and analgesics such as NSAIDs or acetaminophen. Patients were excluded if they had an active infection, coagulopathy, history of arthrofibrosis, complex regional pain syndrome, previous surgery on their lower extremity or had suffered a fracture within 6 months of the procedure. Nineteen patients were excluded because they did not meet the inclusion criteria.

Device characteristics

The TXI system (Tenex Health, Lake Forest, California) is a novel device used to perform minimally invasive treatment for tendinopathy conditions including plantar fasciitis, Achilles tendinitis, Tennis and Golfer’s elbow, and patellar tendinitis [19, 20]. The device includes a main console with an on-screen display indicating the levels of irrigation, aspiration and tissue debridement (Fig. 1) [21].

The TX1 system (Tenex Health, Lake Forest, California). A Tenex Health TX1 Probe. B Tenex Health Console [21]

MIPUT is completed in a stepwise fashion. Prior to performing the procedure, a diagnostic ultrasound of the affected knee was performed. Standardized ultrasounds were performed by three Sports Medicine trained physicians, all with 5–10 years of experience post-fellowship. The American Medical Society for Sports Medicine (AMSSM) protocol for musculoskeletal ultrasounds of the anterior knee was used by all participating physicians during the procedure. A copy of this protocol can be found in Supplementary Files as Appendix A. During the ultrasound, a thickened, heterogeneous, hypoechoic defect along the proximal patellar tendon was identified and recorded (Fig. 2).

A Ultrasound of proximal patellar tendon, showing common sonographic findings of chronic patellar tendinosis. B Ultrasound of the proximal patellar tendon prior to percutaneous ultrasonic tenotomy. P: patella, T: tendinosis, SC: subcutaneous tissue, HFP: Hoffa fat pad, N: needle, TT: tibial tubercle. Both images were captured at an Orthopaedic and Sports Medicine clinic in Metro Detroit, MI

Following sterile skin preparation, 5 mL of 1% lidocaine was injected to anesthetize the skin and subcutaneous tissue. A #11 blade was used to make a vertically oriented stab incision through the skin and into the fascia to accommodate the device. An 18-gauge needle was then introduced to make multiple fenestrations of the tendon to provide a tract for the device. This was followed by the insertion of the device tip into the hypoechoic region. Utilizing real-time ultrasound guidance, a gentle back and forth motion with the device was used to achieve microresection of the pathological tissue (Fig. 3).

Set-up for sonographically guided percutaneous ultrasonic tenotomy. Captured at an Orthopaedic and Sports Medicine clinic in Metro Detroit, MI

Controlled by pedal activation, the device tip oscillates at specific ultrasonic frequencies, allowing for improved precision. The hollow needle utilizes suction to remove the cut tissue. The needle was surrounded by a sleeve that provides continuous irrigation. Together, the instrument can perform accurate irrigation and debridement without affecting the surrounding healthy tissue. After treatment, the incision was cleaned, pressure was applied to the skin to express any additional fluid, and skin closure occurred through use of adhesive strips. The skin was then wrapped with gauze and overwrapped with a compression ace bandage.

Outcomes assessment and follow-up visits

Immediately after the procedure, the patients were instructed to use a knee brace and crutches with toe-touch weight bearing ambulation restrictions. After 3 days, the patients transitioned to partial weight-bearing with discontinuation of the brace and initiation of gentle range of motion exercises. After 2 weeks, patients started physical therapy for gentle hip/knee range of motion using a stationary bike, straight leg raises, and progressed as tolerated. At 6 weeks, patients began more sport specific rehabilitation and progressed through strengthening exercises including: leg press, body weight squats, jogging, running, sprinting, and jumping in a stepwise approach. Rehabilitation protocols varied by patient at the discretion of both the physician and physical therapist.

The follow-up visits were designed to document safety, tolerability, and early efficacy of the procedure. Data accrued included the following: procedure/device related complications, adverse wound healing, and VAS pain score, based on a scale of 0 to 10 (0 = absence of pain, 10 = worst possible pain). As is standard procedure during clinic visits, VAS pain scores were recorded by medical assistants. All patients were required to return to the clinic 2, 6 and 12 weeks after the tenotomy procedure. During the visits, patients provided the following information: vital signs, gender, age, race, BMI, concomitant medications, comorbidities, VAS scores, and adverse events.

The repeated measures of ANOVA was used for statistical analysis of VAS over the follow-up period. Two-way repeated-measures ANOVA was used for VAS score analysis between different BMI groups and age groups over the follow-up period. Statistical analysis was performed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software (version 25, IBM, Armonk, NY). The means and standard deviations (SD), effect sizes, and confidence intervals were obtained together with p-values and observed power. P values less than 0.05 were considered significant.

One-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) with Post Hoc Least Significant Difference (LSD) was used to determine if there was statistical difference of VAS over the follow-up period for each individual weight and age group. Statistical analysis was performed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software (version 25, IBM, Armonk, NY). P values less than 0.05 were considered significant.

Results

A total of 41 patients (42 total knees), 15 males and 27 females, had an average age of 39 years (range, 18–72 years; standard deviation (SD), +/- 15.25). Twenty patients were diagnosed with patellar tendinosis in the right knee, while 22 patients had pathology in the left knee. The average BMI was reported as 31.9 (range 23.8–49). Thirty-eight patients provided follow-up data at the 2-week mark, 24 at 6 weeks, and 10 at 12 weeks. Following the procedure, many patients experienced minor post-procedure pain, however, no adverse events were recorded among study subjects.

The average VAS pain score was 5.12 before treatment (range, 1–10; SD, +/-2.19), and 2.95 (range, 0–8; SD, +/- 2.13), 2.96 (range, 0–8; SD, +/- 2.42), and 2.60 (range, 0–8; SD, +/- 2.76), at 2, 6, and 12 weeks post-treatment, respectively. The greatest change in VAS pain score occurred between the preoperative and 12-week follow-up visit with an average decrease of 2.52. VAS pain score dropped most significantly between the pre-treatment and 2-week visit. Pain score then plateaued between 2 and 6 weeks with a final decrease being seen at the 12-week mark (Fig. 4). A repeated-measure ANOVA showed a statistically significant difference between pre-treatment pain score and all follow-up visits (p = 0.04, observed power of 66.9%).

Visual Analog Scale (VAS) pain score over time. Statistically significant difference between pre-treatment pain score and all follow-up visits denoted as *p = 0.04

Statistical analysis was done to determine if BMI affected VAS pain scores. BMI was categorized by healthy weight (BMI 18 to < 25), overweight (BMI 25 to < 30), obese class I (BMI 30 to < 35), and obese class II (BMI 35 to < 40). In the pre-treatment group, 7 patients were healthy weight, 10 were overweight, 14 were obese class I, and 11 were obese class II. Two-way repeated-measures of ANOVA showed that there was not a significant difference of pain scores between the BMI groups (p = 0.255, observed power of 25.2%). Multivariate tests demonstrated that there was a significant difference between time points interacted with BMI groups (p = 0.004).

The individual BMI group comparisons were done using one-way ANOVA and Post Hoc analysis. The healthy weight group showed a significant difference in VAS pain score over time with an initial decrease at the 2-week mark followed with an increased pain score at the 6- and 12-week follow-up visits (p = 0.025). Further Post Hoc analysis was not possible due to only one patient reporting a VAS pain score at the 12-week follow-up visit. For those with an overweight BMI, there was a significant reduction in pain scores between pre-treatment and both the 2-week (p = 0.003) and 6-week mark (p = 0.018), but not between pre-treatment and the 12-week mark. The obese class I patients showed a statistically significant pain score when comparing pre-treatment and 2-week visit (p = 0.012), 6-week visit (p < 0.001), and 12-week visit pain scores (p < 0.001). There was not an overall significant difference in pain score over time for the obese II patient population (Fig. 5).

Impact of Body Mass Index (BMI) on VAS scores. Statistically significant difference between pre-treatment pain score and follow-up visits denoted as *p ≤ 0.025 and **p ≤ 0.003

Further analysis was done to determine if age had an effect on VAS pain score over time. The patients were divided into three age groups: 18–39, 40–59, 60–72. Using a two-way repeated-measures ANOVA, no significant difference in pain score between age groups was found over the follow-up period (p = 0.681, observed power 6.6%). The highest reported VAS scores for all age groups were documented during the pre-treatment visit. On average, the age group 18–39 reported a VAS score of 4.54, 40–59 reported 5.80, and 60–72 had a score of 6.33. At the 2-week follow-up, ages 18–39, 40–59, and 60–72, reported a VAS of 2.05, 4.57, and 1.67, respectively. At the 6-week follow-up, 18–39, 40–59, and 60–72 reported a VAS of 2.77, 2.78, and 5.0, respectively. At the 12-week follow-up, patients in the 18–39 age group reported a VAS pain score of 2.33, while those in the 40–59 group reported an average pain score of 3.0. The patients in the 60–72 age group had no follow up data at this time point.

When comparing age groups individually using one-way ANOVA and Post Hoc analysis, it was found that the 18–39-year-old group had a significant difference in pain score when comparing pre-treatment with 2-week (p < 0.001), 6-week (p = 0.017), and 12-week visits (p = 0.024). The 40–59-year-old group showed significant difference when comparing pre-treatment to 6-week pain scores (p = 0.003) and 12-week pain scores (p = 0.032). There was not a significant difference in pain scores over time in the 60–72-year-old grouping (Fig. 6). Overall, patients 40–59 years old reported a statistically higher pain score than the 18–39 age group (p = 0.003).

Impact of age on visual analog scale (VAS) scores. Statistically significant difference between pre-treatment pain score and follow-up visits denoted as *p ≤ 0.032 and **p ≤ 0.003

Discussion

This study showed that patients benefited from ultrasound guided tenotomy and saw significant results in as little as 2 weeks. These outcomes were generally sustained over the course of 6 and 12 weeks, which highlights the potential for lasting improvement.

Similar to Koh et al. who looked at lateral elbow tendinopathy, our results showed a statistically significant improvement within the first 2 weeks with down trending VAS pain scores over time. In Koh’s study, researchers saw a statistically significant decrease in pain at 3 months, 6 months and 1 year. In comparison, our VAS pain scores plateaued; however, the frequency of follow-up differed [13].

Importantly, there was a significant difference between time points interacted with BMI groups. The procedure yielded significant benefit and reduction in pain scores to those who were overweight (BMI 25 to < 30) and in obese class I (BMI 30 to < 35). There was an overall difference in pain score for patients in the healthy weight range (BMI 18 to < 25) with improvement seen at 2 weeks, but not at 6 and 12 weeks. However, it is difficult to draw conclusions based on the healthy weight group, as only one patient reported for the 12-week follow-up visit. The obese class II patients also had a low follow-up rate complicating Post Hoc LSD analysis. One-way ANOVA showed no statistical significance in pain score overall for this BMI group. BMI effects on MIPUT outcomes is an area that has not been well studied. Further analysis is needed to determine the significance of BMI groups on procedure outcomes as it may give insight into targeted treatment management and patient selection for MIPUT.

Age also impacted outcomes, as results were statistically significant for age groups 18 to 39 and 40 to 59, but not for those over 60. These results could indicate that MIPUT is most effective in those that are younger or middle aged. Although follow-up visits at the 12-week mark were significantly decreased, it could be assumed that patients over 60 accumulate more scar tissue and require longer healing time. Further studies or meta-analyses with larger sample sizes are needed to determine the effectiveness of treatment in those over the age of 60 as well as in relation to degree of injury.

Many different treatment options for patellar tendinopathy have been referenced. The fact that a control group was not used, in addition to the lack of comparison with other known treatment modalities, poses a clear limitation in our study. While this does prompt areas for future research, our current results provide insight into a novel treatment option.

Conclusion

During clinic follow-up, MIPUT has been shown to be a safe treatment option for patellar tendinosis in our patient population. In this study, patients in the overweight and obese class I BMI groups saw a significant reduction of VAS scores over 6 and 12 weeks, respectively. Additionally, patients between the age of 18 and 59 also experienced a significant reduction of VAS scores over the study period. Based on these results, MIPUT is a reasonable treatment option for patients that fall within these BMI and/or age categories.

Limitations

It is well recognized that patellar tendinopathy is a self-limiting condition and can be subject to placebo effects of perceived therapeutic procedures. Although recruited patients underwent significant conservative treatments, the true effects of ultrasound tenotomy would be best proven with a blinded randomized controlled clinical trial. In this study, however, we did not use any type of control group. Over the course of their recovery, several patients were lost to full follow-up. At study onset, 41 patients (42 knees) were enrolled in the pre-MIPUT group, which dropped to 38 at 2 weeks, 24 at 6 weeks, and 10 at 12 weeks. The decreased rate of patient participation over time was possibly correlated with factors such as reduced/resolved pain, patients seeking care elsewhere, or being content with current condition. Additional limitations to this study include a relatively short follow-up time frame of 12 weeks as well as the lack of a functional score in addition to the VAS pain score to measure patient progress following the procedure, standardized post-procedural rehabilitation protocol for all participants, and imaging follow-up.
