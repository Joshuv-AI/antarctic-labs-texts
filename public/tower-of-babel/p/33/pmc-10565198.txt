How to predict the death risk after an in-hospital cardiac arrest (IHCA) in intensive care unit? A retrospective double-centre cohort study from a tertiary hospital in China
Authors: Youping Zhang, Caijun Rao, Xiao Ran, Hongjie Hu, Liang Jing, Shu Peng, Wei Zhu, Shusheng Li
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesOur objective is to develop a prediction tool to predict the death after in-hospital cardiac arrest (IHCA).DesignWe conducted a retrospective double-centre observational study of IHCA patients from January 2015 to December 2021. Data including prearrest diagnosis, clinical features of the IHCA and laboratory results after admission were collected and analysed. Logistic regression analysis was used for multivariate analyses to identify the risk factors for death. A nomogram was formulated and internally evaluated by the boot validation and the area under the curve (AUC). Performance of the nomogram was further accessed by Kaplan-Meier survival curves for patients who survived the initial IHCA.SettingIntensive care unit, Tongji Hospital, China.ParticipantsAdult patients (≥18 years) with IHCA after admission. Pregnant women, patients with ‘do not resuscitation’ order and patients treated with extracorporeal membrane oxygenation were excluded.InterventionsNone.Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe primary outcome was the death after IHCA.ResultsPatients (n=561) were divided into two groups: non-sustained return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) group (n=241) and sustained ROSC group (n=320). Significant differences were found in sex (p=0.006), cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) duration (p<0.001), total duration of CPR (p=0.014), rearrest (p<0.001) and length of stay (p=0.004) between two groups. Multivariate analysis identified that rearrest, duration of CPR and length of stay were independently associated with death. The nomogram including these three factors was well validated using boot calibration plot and exhibited excellent discriminative ability (AUC 0.88, 95% CI 0.83 to 0.93). The tertiles of patients in sustained ROSC group stratified by anticipated probability of death revealed significantly different survival rate (p<0.001).ConclusionsOur proposed nomogram based on these three factors is a simple, robust prediction model to accurately predict the death after IHCA.

Introduction

In-hospital cardiac arrest (IHCA) is defined as a cardiac arrest that takes place in a hospital (regardless of whether the patient is hospitalised). IHCA is a common life-threatening event with poor outcomes.1 2 Meanwhile, survival from IHCA has been reported extremely low, ranging from 0% to 37.5%,3 although most larger studies reported survival at 20%–30%.2 4 5 Although recent data suggest improved survival over the past two decades, the outcomes remain poor.6 7

IHCA has received less level of research focus compared with out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA). Thus much of the knowledge concerning IHCA is based on extrapolation of results from trials of OHCA.2 8 Currently, guidelines for IHCA and OHCA are almost identical, but there are significant distinctions, such as cause, prevention and treatment, between the conditions that should be taken into account.2 What’s worse, studies about cardiac arrest informing treatment and prognostication are mostly focused on OHCA and few studies have been done on the relationship between the death risk and IHCA. Considering the knowledge gap between IHCA and the poor outcomes it may lead to, to further evaluate the clinical features of IHCA and develop a reliable prediction for the probability of death is a high priority.

Although there are several clinical models available to predict mortality in adult patients who experience IHCA,9–11 the use of these predictions have been limited by their complicated scoring systems. Short duration of cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) has been reported as a prognostic parameter for a favourable outcome in many studies,12–14 other features concerning IHCA—rearrest,15 length of stay16—are also reported associated with decreased survival. To the best of our knowledge, there are few studies dedicated to evaluating these clinical factors and their relation to the outcomes of IHCA in intensive care unit (ICU).17 To create a postarrest prediction model is of great significance.

We sought to test the hypothesis that the clinical features concerning IHCA were associated with death for critically ill patients and develop a simple, bedside prediction tool regarding the probability of death for patients after IHCA.

Materials and methods

Study design

This was a retrospective double-centre observational study of IHCA patients in ICU, Department of Emergency Medicine, Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical college, Huazhong University of Science and Technology during January 2015 and December 2021. The two centres were two tertiary hospitals affiliated to Tongji Hospital: the Optical Valley campus and the Hankou campus. Data including prearrest diagnosis, clinical features of the IHCA and laboratory results after admission were collected and analysed.

Population

Adult patients (≥18 years) with any IHCA after admission were included. Pregnant women, patients with ‘do not resuscitation’ order in the initial IHCA, patients with any missing data and patients treated with extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) before or after an IHCA were excluded.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Data collection

We collected data on prearrest diagnosis, clinical features of the IHCA and laboratory results after admission. Diagnosis were divided into 10 categories: sepsis, trauma, tumour, cardiovascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, lung infection, major bleeding, respiratory failure, perioperative period and other (including but not limited to renal failure, poisoning, decompensated liver failure and acute pancreatitis). When there were more than one diagnosis, the first and the senior author (SL) would make a discussion over it and then the primary diagnosis would be determined by consensus. Clinical features about IHCA were defined as follows: rearrest, another cardiac arrest occurring after >20 min of continuously maintained achieve return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC); ROSC is defined as clinical signs with a palpable pulse or generating a blood pressure. CPR duration, the duration of the initial CPR; total duration, the sums of all CPR duration; survival time, the survival time (hours) from the initial successful resuscitation to endpoints (death or discharge); length of stay, length of the stay prior to arrest. Sustained ROSC was defined as evidence of a palpable pulse or a measurable blood pressure for more than 20 min after CPR procedures initiated. Working shifts were classified as day (07:00–17:59 hours) and night (18:00–06:59 hours). Laboratory results were tested during the stay, but only means of each targeted laboratory result were collected. Clinical feature was retrieved through the electronic patient record by two clinicians. The data on age, the data on sex and laboratory results were extracted from the electronic medical records by engineers blinded to the study design in the Department of Information Management, Tongji Hospital.

Endpoints and outcome

All eligible patients were thoroughly scrutinised for clinical features and followed up through the electronic patient record. If the treatment was withdrawn with the request of the family, the outcomes would be determined after discussion by the first and the senior author. The primary outcome was the death after IHCA.

Statistical analysis

In accordance with the data’s normality, continuous variables were reported as means and SD or medians and IQR, as applicable. Counts and proportions were used to depict categorical data. χ2 or Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to compare group differences.

All analyses were performed in R V.2.14.1 (http://www.r-project.org/). The Kaplan-Meier method was used to represent the survival curves, and the log-rank test was performed to compare them. Logistic regression analysis was used for multivariate analyses. The Akaike information criterion was employed in both-way stepwise selection to choose variables for the final multivariable logistic regression model.

A nomogram was formulated based on the final logistic regression model using the R package of rms. The performance of the nomogram was evaluated by the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. Calibration curve was derived based on bootstraps with 1000 resample. All analyses were two sided and a p<0.05 was considered as statistically significant.

Results

Clinical features of IHCA

We evaluated 572 patients between 2015 and 2021 who experienced IHCA. After exclusions, 561 patients were included in the final analysis (online supplemental figure).

Clinical features of IHCA were presented in table 1 and online supplemental table. Of 561 patients meeting study inclusion criteria, the median age was 64.00 (95% CI 52.00 to 73.00) years old. The top three diagnoses in our study were sepsis (21.0%), trauma (17.6%) and cerebrovascular disease (11.8%). The median length of stay, the median duration of CPR and the median total duration were 5 (95% CI 2 to 13) days, 20 (95% CI 7 to 30) min and 30 (95% CI 25 to 45) min, respectively. The median of survival time was 1 (95% CI 1 to 10) hour. A total of 365 (65.1%) patients experienced single cardiac arrest and 196 (34.9%) patients experienced rearrest more than once. Only 66 (11.8%) patients survived to discharge.

According to the responsiveness to CPR procedures, patients were divided into two groups: non-sustained ROSC group (n=241) and sustained ROSC group (n=320). The median duration of CPR in sustained ROSC group was 8 (95% CI 3.00 to 15.00) min, which was much shorter than that in non-sustained ROSC group: 30 (95% CI 30.00 to 40.00) min (p<0.001). For those who survived the initial IHCA, the median survival time was 7 (95% CI 2.00 to 63.00) hours. In these two groups, significant differences were also found in sex (p=0.006), total duration of CPR (p=0.014), rearrest (p<0.001) and length of stay (p<0.001). However, no significant difference was found in any laboratory result between the two groups.

Model specifications and predictors of death

Patients (n=561) were enrolled in further analysis for predictors of death. To select candidate variables for the prediction model, the logistic regression was performed. We used both-way stepwise selection in multivariate analysis to identify variables that had the strongest association with death. Finally, only rearrest, duration of CPR and length of stay were selected in the final model. Specifically, rearrest (OR 11.493; 95% CI 5.603 to 25.309; p<0.001), duration of CPR (OR 1.129; 95% CI 1.096 to 1.167; p<0.001), length of stay in 2–7 days (OR 1.797; 95% CI 0.951 to 3.462; p=0.074) and length of stay less or equal to 1 day (OR 23.896; 95% CI 4.725 to 437.293; p=0.002) were each independently associated with the death of IHCA (table 2).

Nomogram validation and performance

Nomogram to predict the probability of death of patients with IHCA was shown in figure 1. The nomogram to predict the probability of death was created based on three independent prognostic factors: rearrest (yes or no), duration of CPR (min) and length of stay (≤1 day, 2–7 days or >7 days). Bootstrap validation with 1000 resamplings was used to test model accuracy and potential model overfit. The bootstrapped calibration plot for the prediction of the probability of death was shown in figure 2A. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) of the nomogram was 0.88 (95% CI 0.83 to 0.93) and was showed in figure 2B.

Discussion

The outcomes of patients with IHCA in this study significantly varied according to the responsiveness of CPR. Compared with non-sustained ROSC group, patients in sustained ROSC group had a shorter duration of CPR and longer length of stay. Rearrest (yes or no), duration of CPR (min) and length of stay (≤1 day, 2–7 days or >7 days) were identified as independent prognostic factors for the probability of death. Our nomogram containing these three prognostic factors is a simple, bedside prediction tool and provides excellent ability to assess the death of IHCA.

When an IHCA occurs, some patients act responsive to CPR procedures and achieved sustained ROSC in a short time, the others show non-responsive and die eventually. The contrast is much clearer in ICU patients, as a large portion of them are critically ill patients. Some models regarded advanced age as a strong predictor for mortality,9 10 and elevated lactate levels or poor lactate clearance were also reported helpful in stratifying mortality risk after IHCA.18 19 In this study, neither age nor lactate acid was identified as an independent risk factor for death, partly because of the unique demographic characteristics (relatively small age range and elevated lactate levels) in the ICU ward population.

Defining an optimal duration for resuscitation attempts proves to be difficult.2 20 21 It is generally acknowledged that resuscitation efforts should be stopped if an advanced life support patient has asystole for longer than 20 min without a reversible reason.21 22 In a Swedish cohort study, the median duration of CPR with ROSC was 5 (IQR 2–12) min.12 Data from the GWTG-R (Get With the Guidelines-Resuscitation) registry have shown that the median time to achieve ROSC was 11 (IQR 6–21) min.10 In our study, the median duration of CPR in sustained ROSC group was 8 (95% CI 3 to 15) min, which indicated that the majority of patients survived the initial resuscitation within 15 min. Although longer duration of resuscitation is associated with worse outcomes, individuals may still benefit from prolonged CPR. In another study from the AHA GWTG-R registry, 88% of patients achieved sustained ROSC within 30 min.23 Decisions on medical futility regarding CPR should not be made based on CPR duration alone. We should bear in mind that a cut-off point of CPR duration beyond which resuscitation is futile is unlikely to exist. Thus, in our prediction model, the CPR duration was evaluated as a continuous variable.

Rearrest is an important risk factor related to outcome and is not uncommon in patients that recover spontaneous circulation after the initial resuscitation.15 24 The incidence and related risk factors for the complication of rearrest are still not well reported. Previous research on the subject of rearrest has mostly concentrated on the causes, effects and incidence of rearrest in the prehospital setting.25 In IHCA patients, Salcido et al found that rearrest occurred frequently after resuscitation and it was associated with a decreased likelihood of survival.26 A retrospective cohort study of patients with OHCA or ICHA also suggested that rearrest was common and occurred early in the course of their postarrest care.15 In our study, the incidence of rearrest was high up to 61.8%. The OR for death in rearrest patients was 11.493 (95% CI 5.603 to 25.309) compared with single arrest patients, which was consistent with the conclusions of researches referred above.

The aetiology of cardiac arrests varies as length of stay increases, thus the length of stay carries high weight when predicting the survival after IHCA. However, studies focus on the relation between the length of stay and the survival after IHCA are rare. Tran et al proposed that arrests occurring after longer hospitalisations were associated with worse neurological outcomes and lower probability of death.16 Qazi et al reported that timing of cardiac arrest during hospitalisation had an effect on survival outcomes and IHCA after three hospital days had lower rates of survival to discharge.27 These conclusions seemed contradicted to ours. In our study, the short length of stay was found associated with the higher rate of death after IHCA. In fact, our study focused on the critically ill patients in ICU, and the top three diagnosis were sepsis, trauma and cerebrovascular disease, while the studies referred above included patients both in the general ward and ICU. The aetiology of IHCA and the population of patients in ICU may contribute to the different conclusions about length of stay regarding the probability of death.

There are several nomograms to predict mortality in IHCA patients, but those tools were either designed as complicated scoring system with too many variables9 10 17 or designed mostly for patients with chronic cardiovascular disease.28 29 These predictions were too complicated to be suitable for bedside decision making, regarding on the subsequent treatment. To the best of our knowledge, there is few prediction tools to predict the death for ICU patients with IHCA. Our proposed nomogram was a simple, bedside prediction tool for the probability of death, with an excellent discriminative ability (AUC 0.88, 95% CI 0.83 to 0.93). Indeed, our nomogram was successful in identifying different patient groups with varying chances of the probability of death and was well validated using bootstrapped method. A simple but robust prediction model can be of great help in risk identification and clinical decision evaluation.

There are several limitations to be noted in this study. First, as we chose to limit our analytical cohort to only critically ill patients without ECMO treatment in ICU, our score should be applied cautiously when generalise to other settings. ECMO was recently shown to be highly effective in improving survival compared with conventional CPR.30 The selection of the study population has had an impact on survival in our study. A review reported that the survival until hospital discharge for IHCA ranged between 11.6% and 28.5% for elderly patients.31 In our study, survival to hospital discharge was 11.8%, which is lower than reported in most other cardiac arrest registries.2 31 The possible reason is that our study did not include patients treated with ECMO. Second, our nomogram is based partly on rearrest, which means the death risk after the rearrest will change dynamically comparing with that after the initial arrest. Thus, the dynamic evaluation after each arrest is recommended. Third, although our study included patients from two different hospitals to minimise the selection bias, the sample was still small. Fourth, our findings may be subject to comorbidity, as all patients were classified by the primary diagnosis. Subgroup analysis stratified by primary diagnosis or comorbidity would be helpful in further revealing the correlation between aetiology and IHCA. Fifth, we did not conduct validation on a separate cohort. Although internally validated using bootstrap validation, our nomogram still needs to be externally validated in the future.

Conclusion

Patients with IHCA showed different responsiveness to CPR procedures. CPR duration, length of stay and rearrest were identified as independent risk factors for the death of IHCA. Our proposed nomogram is a simple, robust prediction model and is able to stratify IHCA patients into distinct prognostic groups regarding the probability of death.
