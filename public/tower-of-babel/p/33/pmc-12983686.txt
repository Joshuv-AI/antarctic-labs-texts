‘We just give the phone so they stay quiet’: a qualitative exploration of screen time practices among caregivers of children younger than 5 years in south India
Authors: Samya Varadarajan, Akila Govindarajan Venguidesvarane, Muthukumar Rajamohan, Murugesan Krupa, Karthik Narayanan Ramaswamy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveTo explore the perceptions, challenges and strategies of caregivers in managing screen time among children younger than 5 years in the urban and rural areas of Tamil Nadu, India.DesignQualitative study using focus group discussions (FGDs).SettingRural Health and Training Centre in Vayalanallur, Thiruvallur district and Urban Health and Training Centre, Thiruvanmyur in Chennai district, Tamil Nadu, India.Participants54 caregivers (27 rural, 27 urban, 46 mothers, 6 grandmothers, 2 fathers) of children younger than 5 years whose screen time exceeded WHO age-specific recommendations participated. Six FGDs were conducted, each with 8–10 participants.MethodsFGDs were conducted in familiar community locations near the participants’ homes to ensure comfort and accessibility. The audio recordings were transcribed verbatim in Tamil and translated into English and analysed using inductive thematic analysis. Coding was manually performed by two independent researchers.ResultsSix major themes emerged: (1) circumstances leading to screen exposure, (2) perceived advantages, (3) perceived disadvantages, (4) challenges in reducing screen time, (5) methods adopted to reduce screen time and (6) strategies for sustaining reduction. Safety concerns, lack of play space and the need to manage household chores were commonly cited reasons for screen use. Although most caregivers were aware of the potential harms, screens were often used to feed or pacify children. Emotional resistance from children, inconsistent family norms and grandparents’ screen use were common barriers. Caregivers employed strategies, such as limiting access, engaging in outdoor play and enrolling children in structured activities. Urban–rural differences were minimal, and thematic saturation was achieved after six FGDs.ConclusionsCaregivers face multiple, structural and family level challenges in managing screen time among young children. Awareness and interventions need to be tailored to address family dynamics, caregiver fatigue and lack of alternatives. Health providers, community workers and policymakers should collaborate to offer structured support, promote screen-free engagement and ensure child-friendly spaces in urban and rural communities.Keywords: PUBLIC HEALTH, QUALITATIVE RESEARCH, Community child health

Introduction

The use of digital media by young children has become a growing global concern. The WHO1 and Indian Academy of Paediatrics2 recommend no screening for children younger than 2 years and a supervised 1 hour limit for those aged 2–5 years. Excessive screen time during early childhood is associated with a range of negative developmental outcomes, including language delay,3 4 attention problems, emotional dysregulation,5 behavioural issues,6 sleep disturbances7 8 and obesity.9,11 Growing evidence suggests that excessive screen exposure in early childhood may have effects that extend beyond the preschool years, including later difficulties with attention, emotional regulation, academic functioning and mental well-being.12 13 Globally, only a minority of children aged 5 years and younger meet recommended screen time guidelines. A recent meta-analysis reported that fewer than one in four children globally adhered to screen time recommendations, with lower adherence observed in low- and middle-income settings.14 In India, evidence from multiple regions suggests that excessive screen exposure during early childhood is common. More than half of children younger than 5 years exceed the recommended screen time limits, with a prevalence ranging from approximately 55% to nearly 90%.15,17 Our previous study from Tamil Nadu found excessive screen exposure in >70% of children younger than 5 years, and a recent systematic review reported a mean daily screen time exceeding 2 hour among young children in India.3 18

Although excessive screen exposure has been reported worldwide, the contexts in which young children are cared for differ across settings. Screen use among young children is shaped by multiple factors, such as caregiver work demands, parental stress, family screen habits, availability of alternative activities and the increasing role of digital media in education.5 19 In many high-income settings, children’s screen use is shaped by regulated childcare systems, safer neighbourhood play spaces and greater access to organised early learning and recreational activities.20 In contrast, caregiving in India often takes place within nuclear households, with mothers and grandmothers bearing the primary responsibility for childcare, limited access to affordable formal childcare and fewer safe or supervised alternatives for young children’s play. National data show that women spend substantially more time on unpaid care work than men, reflecting persistent gendered caregiving roles.21 Simultaneously, limited availability of safe outdoor play spaces, traffic and safety concerns, air pollution and neighbourhood restrictions reduce opportunities for active play, especially in cities.22 Evidence from multi-country and Indian studies has suggested that caregivers’ perceptions of these structural and environmental constraints strongly shape children’s activity patterns, often resulting in greater reliance on screens as an indoor alternative rather than reflecting individual caregiver choices alone.23

Although international and national guidelines exist, they are largely informed by evidence from high-income settings and may not fully reflect caregiving realities in diverse Indian contexts. The coronavirus disease 2019 pandemic further accelerated children’s exposure to digital media in India, with an increased reliance on screens for online learning, communication and daily routines, even among preschool-aged Indian children.24,26 While quantitative studies have documented rising screen exposure and its associated developmental risks, they provide limited insight into why caregivers rely on screens in everyday life and the practical challenges they face in trying to reduce screen time.

Caregivers, including parents and grandparents, play a central role in shaping young children’s media habits through daily supervision, rule setting and screen use modelling. Once introduced, screen exposure during early childhood may become embedded in daily routines, making subsequent reductions difficult. A recent meta-synthesis of qualitative studies on caregivers’ perceptions of screen time identified common themes across settings; however, none of the included studies were conducted in India.27 Given the differences in family structures, caregiving roles and environmental constraints, there is a clear need to explore how caregivers in Indian settings perceive, negotiate and manage screen use among young children.

The current study aimed to address the literature gap by conducting focus group discussions (FGDs) among parents and caregivers of children younger than 5 years with excess screen time in the rural and urban areas of Tamil Nadu, India.

Materials and methods

Study design

This descriptive qualitative study used FGDs to explore caregivers’ perceptions, challenges and strategies related to reducing excess screen time among children younger than 5 years (figure 1). The data were analysed using an inductive thematic analysis approach. No a priori theoretical framework was applied to guide the data collection or analysis; instead, themes were directly derived from the participants’ narratives. Existing literature and WHO screen time recommendations were used to contextualise the findings during interpretation.

Flow of methodology. FGDs, focus group discussions; RHTC, Rural Health and Training Centre; UHTC, Urban Health and Training Centre.

Setting

This study was conducted in the rural and urban field practice areas attached to a medical college in Chennai, India. The Rural Health and Training Centre (RHTC) is located in Vayalanallur village, Thiruvallur district, approximately 20 km from the medical college, and caters to a population of approximately 30 000. The Urban Health and Training Centre (UHTC) is located in Thiruvanmiyur, Chennai district, approximately 14 km from the medical college and provides free healthcare services to a population of >50 000.

Participants and recruitment

Participants were recruited from the field practice areas of the rural and urban centres attached to a medical college in Chennai, India. The caregivers of children aged younger than 5 years were initially identified by health inspectors and community health nurses from registers with information on those younger than 5 years. Home visits were conducted to assess children’s screen exposure based on WHO recommendations.

Caregivers of children with screen times exceeding age-appropriate recommendations were considered eligible for inclusion. Eligible participants included mothers, fathers and grandmothers who were the primary caregivers and directly involved in the child’s day-to-day care. Caregivers who were not regularly involved in caregiving or who were unwilling to participate in group discussions were excluded.

Eligible caregivers were invited in person to participate in the FGDs and informed about the date, time and location of the discussions. Written informed consent was obtained from all the participants before their participation. A small number of eligible caregivers (n=5) declined to participate because of time-related and work-related commitments. Only caregivers who provided informed consent were present during the FGDs. No financial or material incentives were offered for participation in this study.

Six FGDs were conducted, three in a rural setting and three in an urban setting, with 8–10 participants in each group. Caregivers unwilling to participate or unable to provide consent were excluded.

Data collection

The interview guide (online supplemental file 1) and prompts were developed in consultation with co-faculty members, paediatricians and speech and language pathologists. The interview guide was pretested among a small group of caregivers of children younger than 5 years at the rural centre during an immunisation clinic day, when parents had brought their children for routine vaccinations. Based on the feedback, a few modifications were made to improve the clarity and ease of language for better understanding. FGDs were conducted in Tamil, the local language. The discussions were conducted in a common space located near the participants’ residences in both the RHTC and UHTC areas, routinely used for community outreach and meetings, as caregivers preferred a familiar setting close to home to avoid disruption to household work and to remain available for childcare. The guide included open-ended questions designed to elicit children’s perceptions of screen time, challenges in limiting screen exposure, family influences and strategies used to reduce and sustain screen time. The sociodemographic details of the participants and their children were also collected.

Each discussion lasted between 45 min and 1 hour and was audio-recorded with permission. The sessions were moderated by the principal investigator and assisted by co-investigators who took notes. All the investigators are permanent faculty members of tertiary medical colleges and research institutes with adequate training in conducting qualitative research. The moderators acknowledged the participants’ contributions and balanced their participation by gently redirecting the discussion when the dominant voices emerged. Ground rules were set at the outset to encourage active listening and minimise potential power imbalances within the group.

Repeat interviews were not conducted. At the end of each discussion, the participants were informed that they could review the transcripts if they wished; however, they did not express interest in doing so. The caregivers expressed confidence that their views were adequately captured and accurately represented in the research. A likely contributing factor was familiarity between the participants and the research team, as all investigators were faculty members regularly engaged in home visits and community outreach activities through rural and urban health centres. This study adhered to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research 32-item checklist to ensure methodological rigour and transparency.

Data analysis

The inductive thematic analysis approach described by Braun and Clarke28 was used to identify and interpret data patterns. Data collection and analysis were performed iteratively. Following each FGD, the transcripts were reviewed, and preliminary codes were developed. Saturation was assessed based on the recurrence of themes and absence of new codes emerging in successive discussions. In the sixth FGD, no new substantive themes were identified. Saturation was examined across the overall dataset, focusing on both rural and urban groups that showed similar patterns. The analysis was descriptive in nature and aimed to capture the explicit meanings and lived experiences of caregivers regarding screen time practices. Urban–rural differences were descriptively explored by comparing themes and patterns across focus groups conducted in each setting, rather than through a formal comparative analysis. The audio recordings were transcribed verbatim in Tamil and then translated into English. The translated transcripts were checked against the original Tamil versions by bilingual members of the research team to ensure accuracy. Differences in wording or meaning were discussed and resolved to preserve the intended meaning of the participants’ accounts. Manual coding was independently conducted by the principal investigator and co-investigator. The codes were inductively developed and grouped into categories based on semantic similarity. A third researcher reviewed and cross-verified the codes to ensure inter-coder reliability and resolve discrepancies through consensus. A visual coding tree was not created; however, the research team systematically documented and collaboratively reviewed all the codes and categories.

Results

Participant profile

Six FGDs were conducted, three in rural settings and three in urban settings, with 54 caregivers (mostly mothers, some grandmothers and one father). The caregivers were predominantly homemakers or informal workers, with educational levels ranging from primary to postgraduate. Most participants belonged to nuclear families with 1–3 children younger than 5 years (table 1). A summary of the thematic analysis is shown in figure 2.

Theme 1: circumstances leading to excess screen time

Caregivers cited safety concerns, household responsibilities and lack of outdoor play options as the primary reasons for screen exposure. As the toddlers were challenging to care for, some parents found it comfortable to allow their children in front of screens for safety reasons, so that they did not run around or get injured. Digital media often served as a babysitter during chores:

My child followed me to the kitchen and touched hot plates and food and injured herself, so I prefer to make her sit in front of the TV while cooking.(FGD-3, rural, mother)

Lack of safe outdoor spaces was commonly reported, particularly in urban settings:

There is no space to play outside. The road is unsafe, and neighbours complain if kids touch their vehicles. (FGD-2, urban, mother)

Some caregivers linked their reliance on screens to changes in family structure. Living in nuclear families meant that caregiving responsibilities were often managed by a single adult, without the support of grandparents or other relatives during the day. In the absence of extended family members, who traditionally shared childcare, caregivers described using screens to keep children occupied while attending to household tasks or work-related responsibilities:

I have to attend online meetings in the evenings related to work; I tend to give my child a phone or turn on the TV so that he does not disturb me. As I am the only caregiver at that time I don’t have a choice. (FGD-5, urban, mother, field note: expressed visible distress while explaining her situation)

Theme 2: perceived advantages of screen time

Although many participants acknowledged the risks, some saw perceived benefits, such as language learning, educational content, and support in managing daily routines:

My son learns a lot of rhymes. But I know he shouldn’t watch for too long. (FGD-6, urban)

Some caregivers felt that screen use helped compensate for their own limitations, especially language skills:

In school, they asked us to use YouTube videos to teach certain topics. As I don’t know English, I find it helpful. (FGD-5, urban grandmother)

Digital media was also viewed as a substitute for storytelling in the absence of joint family support:

My girl loves to listen to stories – but I don’t find time as I am juggling between with household chores and work – so I use YouTube videos for moral stories (FGD-4, rural, mother)

A mother, in particular, mentioned, “my child eats well only while watching phone – especially while giving him vegetables it’s easy to feed him while is engaged watching videos.” (FGD-1, rural, mother).

However, this view had some conflicts among others, who felt that the child was not able to taste or relish the food given and felt it was wrong to do.

Some caregivers felt proud that their children were able to use touch screens and watch videos to which they did not have access as kids.

Theme 3: perceived disadvantages of screen time

Caregivers expressed deep concern over behavioural changes, reduced social interaction, physical inactivity and eye-related problems associated with screen use.

Some caregivers reported behavioural issues, such as aggression, tantrums and violent behaviours:

My child becomes aggressive and throws tantrums when the phone is taken away. (FGD-1, rural)

He watches fight scenes and then imitates them at home using sticks. (FGD-4, rural)

My child watches TV serials with her grandparents; influenced by the scenes; she began to normalise behaviours like lying to others, and scheming parents for getting what she wants. (FGD-6, urban, mother)

There were concerns regarding the child’s social interaction and peer engagement:

I feel my child does not interact well with peers. Even when I take him out for family functions or parties, he prefers to sit in the corner with my phone. (FGD-5, urban, father)

Although some caregivers described using screens during meals as a practical way to ensure food intake, others were concerned about the children becoming dependent on screens for eating and losing interest in food:

She will eat only if the phone is on. Otherwise, she skips meals. (FGD-5, urban)

Reflection on broader development of the child, such as reduced imagination and lack of interest in books, was also expressed by caregivers:

One of the parents in the urban setting felt, “I feel the children these days lose the ability to imagine and think creatively when they start watching screens. As a child, I used to love reading books and loved imagining the characters. It’s disappointing to see these generation children hardly showing interest in books” (FGD-2, urban, mother)

Theme 4: Challenges in Reducing Screen Time

Attempts to limit screen use led to emotional outbursts, defiance and household conflict:

If I turn off the TV, my son breaks the remote and cries for 20 minutes. It is challenging to calm him down (FGD-3, rural, mother)

My daughter said — ‘when grandma watches TV, why do you turn off mine?’ (FGD-2, urban, mother)

My child just 5 years, turned completely aggressive when I tried to get the phone from him, he threw the phone down, shattering it. (FGD-6, urban, mother)

Caregivers also described the challenges arising from differing screen use practices among adults within the household, which made it difficult to enforce consistent limits:

When I try to discipline my child by taking back the phone, my husband gives his phone to the child and tries to win him. This is not a way to raise a child. (FGD-1, rural, mother)

Theme 5: methods adopted to reduce screen time

Caregivers described a range of low-cost strategies for reducing screen use, including redirecting children to toys or colouring activities, encouraging outdoor play, gradually reducing screen exposure and limiting access through measures, such as password locks or temporary disconnection from television services:

I explained that if she watches too much, she may have to wear spectacles. That worked for a few days. (FGD-3, rural)

I disconnected cable TV for two months. My child now plays outside more and sleeps better. (FGD-5, urban, father)

Caregivers also noted that maintaining limits was difficult when screen use practices varied among the household adults.

Some caregivers suggested engaging children in extracurricular activities, such as dance, singing, art or martial arts, to reduce screen use. However, this approach was not universally accepted, with several caregivers raising concerns about the costs and practical challenges of arranging transportation for children.

Disagreements also emerged when punishment was suggested as a strategy, such as deliberately skipping a meal. Other caregivers strongly opposed this approach, leading to visible tension during discussions.

Theme 6: sustaining reduced screen time

Caregivers emphasised the need for structured alternatives and acknowledged the difficulty in long-term maintenance, especially during school holidays, when children spent more time at home:

It is possible to reduce screen time — but only if parents are consistent and make it a habit from early on. (FGD-2, urban grandmother)

I enrolled her in dance class and Hindi tuition. She doesn't ask for the mobile anymore. (FGD-5, urban)

Enrolment in organised classes was mainly described by caregivers who reported having access to such activities in their neighbourhoods.

Caregivers also expressed concerns that screen use might increase again as children grow older because of peer influence and school-related requirements. Parents of older children described receiving homework instructions and school communications through messaging applications, such as WhatsApp, which increased their exposure to mobile phones.

Although many experiences were shared across the settings, some differences emerged in how caregivers described screen use. Urban caregivers spoke more about the lack of safe outdoor spaces, neighbour-related constraints, work-from-home demands and organised extracurricular activities. Rural caregivers more commonly described safety concerns within the home environment and fewer structured alternatives for children’s engagement. Differences were also noted across caregiver roles, with mothers frequently describing daily caregiving pressures, grandmothers referring to television viewing as routine or acceptable, and fathers expressing concerns about their children’s social interactions and behaviours. These observations were not uniform, and overlapping views were observed across groups.

Discussion

The present study explored caregivers’ perspectives, challenges and strategies related to digital media usage among children younger than 5 years in the urban and rural areas of Tamil Nadu. The findings highlight how everyday caregiving responsibilities, family expectations and child well-being intersect in a digital environment. Consistent with our earlier quantitative findings in the same population, the caregivers frequently acknowledged their overreliance on screens.3 Most caregivers were aware of the potential harm, including sleep problems, eyestrain, reduced social interaction and limited outdoor play, which is consistent with findings from studies in other settings.729,33 Despite this awareness, screens were often rationalised as a babysitter or pacifier that enabled caregivers to complete household work, which was consistent with other qualitative studies.5 29 34 35

When interpreted through family systems and sociostructural lenses, screen use among young children in this study appeared less as an individual caregiving choice and more as an adaptive response to everyday caregiving demands. Caregiving was largely managed by mothers and grandmothers within nuclear households, with limited extended family support and minimal daytime involvement of fathers, thus increasing the reliance on screens to manage competing responsibilities. These family dynamics interacted with environmental constraints, such as limited play space, safety concerns, long work hours and the cost of organised activities, making sustained screen time reduction difficult. This framing underscores screen use as a systemic issue, rather than an individual caregiver’s failure. Urban caregivers more often described a lack of safe play spaces and neighbour-related restrictions, whereas rural caregivers highlighted difficulties in supervising children in open environments. Urban–rural differences were descriptively examined through a comparison of FGDs rather than formal analytic comparisons.

Parental perceptions of benefits included educational values, learning new languages and keeping the child calm, similar to a meta-synthesis.27 The contradicting statements regarding the benefits and harms of screen use shed light on the deep tensions and stress involved in parenting. The behavioural and emotional changes that children undergo while restricting screens are among the most important themes generated. Temper tantrums, physical outbursts of throwing and breaking things, arguments that evolved and threats highlighted how screens had become a ‘norm’ in most of the families.29 Caregivers also expressed uncertainty and a lack of guidance on how to manage these reactions, making it difficult to sustain limits on screen use despite their concerns. Although we did not formally analyse caregiver age, differences in familiarity with digital media and physical capacity for active engagement may influence how caregivers experience and manage screen time challenges, particularly older caregivers.

The caregiver-suggested strategies in this study can be broadly grouped into three practical categories: first, immediate, low-cost behavioural approaches, such as restricting access, gradual reduction and redirecting children to non-screen activities within the home; second, engagement-based strategies, including outdoor play, storytelling and structured routines to replace passive screen use; and finally, longer-term or resource-dependent options, such as enrolment in extracurricular activities, which were perceived as helpful but not universally feasible. Although caregivers made active efforts to implement these strategies, access to structured activities often depended on family income and neighbourhood availability, making them easier to adopt in urban settings than in resource-limited contexts. Engaging children during holidays and weekends was more challenging, as echoed in a study by Sigmundová and Sigmund.36 Tensions between partners and differences in opinions among caregivers where multiple caregivers were involved were frequently cited as common reasons for the difficulty in consistently limiting screen use. Similar to our study, parental conflicts were a reason for high screen time in children in other studies.33 37 This suggests that screen time reduction efforts should include low-cost and community-based play opportunities that are feasible across different settings. Emerging global research has raised concerns that early and prolonged exposure to digital media may have implications for later mental health and well-being, reinforcing the importance of addressing screen use early in life through preventive and caregiver-supported approaches.38

A key strength of our study was the inclusion of diverse caregiver voices from multiple settings, which enhanced the transferability of the findings to similar low- and middle-income contexts. FGDs were chosen to facilitate interactions among caregivers and uncover shared norms and tensions that may not have been present in the individual interviews. Despite the study’s rigour, our sample lacked gender diversity, with minimal representation of fathers or male caregivers, which may have restricted the perspectives on intra-household decision-making. The participants were primarily from lower- to middle-income backgrounds, limiting their generalisability to higher socioeconomic strata. The study setting in our own field area could have introduced a selection bias, as caregivers had better access to health and were more knowledgeable. Familiarity between the participants and the research team may also have influenced how caregiving practices and challenges were described, potentially shaping the openness or framing of responses during discussions. Our study exclusively included caregivers of children with excessive screen time, and the perspectives of those who successfully maintained limited screen exposure were not captured. Including such caregivers may have provided additional strategies, stronger counterarguments and more diverse solutions to the challenges discussed.

Future studies can be directed towards a longitudinal qualitative approach to track changes in caregivers’ attitudes and perceptions as the child grows. The inclusion of fathers and other family members is critical to better understand family dynamics and tensions. In addition, involving caregivers who have successfully limited screen time for their children can help identify effective and practical strategies that may not have emerged in the present study. Intervention studies on structured caregiver programmes provide better evidence for scalable solutions.

Conclusion

This study highlights the multifaceted and culturally rooted challenges that caregivers face in managing screen time among children younger than 5 years. Voices from the community emphasise that screen use in early childhood is not only a matter of awareness but also of circumstance, support and available alternatives. Interventions should be designed with a family systems lens and implemented through accessible platforms, such as well-baby clinics, community health workers and early childcare centres.

At the policy level, urban planning must prioritise the creation of child-friendly spaces, such as parks and playgrounds, especially in low-income neighbourhoods. Community-based programmes led by non-governmental organisations, self-help groups and government-supported Anganwadi centres should be leveraged to limit the screen time for young children. These efforts, if sustained and context-sensitive, can help reverse early screen dependency and foster healthier developmental environments for young children in India.
