Efficacy and safety of low-temperature plasma ablation combined with collagenase in the treatment of lumbar disc herniation
Authors: Deng-Hui Li, Gao-Feng Wu, Ze-Yu Huang, Zhong-Hua Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLumbar disc herniation (LDH) is a common cause of radicular pain and disability. Evidence regarding the incremental effectiveness of combining plasma-based intradiscal decompression with collagenase chemonucleolysis remains limited.MethodsThis single-center retrospective cohort study included consecutive hospitalized adults with imaging-confirmed LDH treated between July 2021 and July 2025. Patients underwent low-temperature plasma ablation alone (control group, n = 108) or plasma ablation plus intradiscal collagenase (observation group, n = 116). The primary outcomes were longitudinal Visual Analog Scale (VAS) and Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) scores assessed at baseline, 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, and 6 months. Linear mixed-effects models evaluated treatment group, time, and group × time interactions after adjustment for clinical and imaging covariates. Secondary outcomes included clinically meaningful improvement, symptomatic imaging-confirmed recurrence, reintervention, perioperative characteristics, and complications. Propensity score matching and sensitivity analyses were performed.ResultsVAS and ODI scores decreased over time in both groups (both time effects, P < 0.001), with significant group × time interactions for VAS (P = 0.002) and ODI (P = 0.006). At 6 months, the adjusted mean differences favored the observation group for VAS (− 0.54; 95% CI, − 0.81 to − 0.27) and ODI (− 5.10% points; 95% CI, − 7.47 to − 2.73). Six-month VAS responder rates were 91.2% and 96.3% (RR, 1.06; 95% CI, 0.98–1.13), while ODI responder rates were 83.2% and 91.6% (RR, 1.10; 95% CI, 0.99–1.22), respectively. Recurrence and reintervention were numerically less frequent in the observation group, but confidence intervals were wide and crossed 1. Overall complications occurred in 11.1% and 7.8% of patients, respectively (RR, 0.70; 95% CI, 0.31–1.59).ConclusionsPlasma ablation combined with intradiscal collagenase was associated with modestly lower VAS and ODI scores over 6 months than plasma ablation alone. Differences in clinically meaningful response rates were not statistically significant, and low event numbers precluded firm conclusions regarding recurrence, reintervention, or comparative safety.

Introduction

Lumbar disc herniation (LDH) is a common cause of radicular pain, functional limitation, and work disability. Current clinical guidance generally recommends structured nonoperative management as the initial approach for patients without urgent surgical indications, while persistent disabling radiculopathy, progressive neurologic impairment, or other concerning clinical features may warrant procedural or surgical intervention [1, 2] .Treatment selection should therefore account for symptom duration and severity, neurologic status, imaging findings, response to conservative treatment, and patient preferences.

Minimally invasive decompressive procedures have been developed to reduce tissue disruption and perioperative burden while achieving pain relief and functional recovery. Endoscopic lumbar discectomy and microdiscectomy both provide substantial improvements in appropriately selected patients, although their perioperative profiles and complication patterns may differ [3, 4]. Percutaneous intradiscal decompression represents a less invasive option for selected patients without urgent indications for direct surgical decompression. Plasma- or coblation-based nucleoplasty is intended to remove a small amount of nucleus pulposus tissue through controlled ablation, thereby reducing intradiscal pressure and nerve-root irritation [5]. Randomized and comparative evidence also indicates that full-endoscopic discectomy may reduce blood loss and hospitalization compared with open microdiscectomy while producing similar improvements in pain and disability in selected LDH populations [6]. However, comparisons between surgical decompression and intradiscal procedures remain strongly influenced by differences in indication, anatomical phenotype, and baseline disease severity [7].

Chemonucleolysis has re-emerged as an intermediate treatment option between conservative management and surgical decompression. Enzymatic agents are intended to degrade components of the nucleus pulposus and reduce the volume of herniated disc material, although their efficacy and adverse-event profiles vary by agent, dose, delivery technique, and patient selection [8, 9]. Collagenase chemonucleolysis has been used extensively in China, either alone or in combination with other percutaneous procedures, but procedural protocols and eligibility criteria remain heterogeneous [10, 11]. Consequently, the optimal role of collagenase as an adjunct to plasma decompression has not been fully established.

A directly relevant retrospective study by Zhang et al. compared low-temperature plasma ablation combined with collagenase injection against plasma ablation alone and reported lower postoperative Visual Analog Scale (VAS) and Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) scores in the combination group, together with differences in circulating inflammatory mediators [12]. Nevertheless, that study principally assessed early and 3-month clinical outcomes and did not comprehensively address longitudinal recovery trajectories, individual clinically meaningful improvement, expanded imaging confounding, recurrence, reintervention, or the uncertainty surrounding uncommon complications.

The present retrospective cohort study compared low-temperature plasma ablation combined with intradiscal collagenase with plasma ablation alone in patients with imaging-confirmed LDH. We hypothesized that combination treatment would be associated with greater reductions in VAS and ODI scores over the 6-month follow-up period, as reflected by significant treatment group × time interactions in longitudinal analyses. The primary objective was to compare the adjusted longitudinal trajectories of pain and disability between the two treatment groups. Secondary objectives were to compare the proportions of patients achieving prespecified clinically meaningful improvement thresholds, perioperative characteristics, symptomatic imaging-confirmed recurrence, reintervention, and complications.

Methods

Study design and participants

This single-center retrospective, nonrandomized cohort study consecutively included hospitalized adults with imaging-confirmed lumbar disc herniation (LDH) who underwent percutaneous interventional treatment at Hengdian Wenrong Hospital between July 2021 and July 2025. Patients were classified according to the treatment actually received into an observation group treated with low-temperature plasma ablation combined with intradiscal collagenase and a control group treated with low-temperature plasma ablation alone. The clinical factors influencing treatment selection are described in “Treatment selection and interventions”.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) a primary diagnosis of LDH based on concordant clinical symptoms and magnetic resonance imaging and/or computed tomography evidence of disc herniation at the symptomatic level; (3) persistent symptoms despite standardized conservative treatment, including medication and/or physical therapy, or symptoms considered to warrant procedural intervention; and (4) availability of sufficient baseline and postoperative follow-up information for outcome assessment. The exclusion criteria were: (1) previous lumbar surgery at the index level; (2) concomitant spinal disorders likely to confound outcome assessment, including lumbar fracture, spinal infection, spinal tumor, inflammatory spondyloarthropathy, or severe scoliosis; (3) clinically or radiologically significant spinal instability, including spondylolisthesis requiring fusion, or severe central canal stenosis requiring surgical decompression; (4) cauda equina syndrome or a progressive severe neurologic deficit requiring emergency open surgery; (5) a known allergy or contraindication to collagenase in patients considered for combination treatment, or another contraindication to the planned procedure; (6) pregnancy or lactation; and (7) absence of key outcome data or inability to ascertain 6-month follow-up status. Patients with partial missing data for individual baseline covariates were not excluded solely on that basis and were handled according to the missing-data strategy described “Statistical analysis”.

A uniform analytic follow-up window of 6 months after the index procedure was prespecified for all eligible patients. Although patients treated earlier in the study period had the potential for longer follow-up, outpatient reassessment and repeat imaging beyond 6 months were not performed according to a standardized schedule in routine clinical practice. Longer-term data were therefore not included in the primary analysis to avoid unequal follow-up opportunities, symptom-driven detection of recurrent events, and calendar-time differences across enrollment years.

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Hengdian Wenrong Hospital. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and applicable institutional requirements. Written informed consent for treatment and the use of clinical data for research purposes was obtained from all participants. All data were anonymized before analysis.

Treatment selection and interventions

Treatment allocation was nonrandomized and reflected routine clinical decision-making. The decision to add intradiscal collagenase was made by the treating physician after reviewing the patient’s clinical presentation and preoperative imaging findings and was subsequently discussed with the patient. Factors considered included the size and containment status of the herniated disc material, the degree of disc degeneration, the anticipated adequacy of decompression with plasma ablation alone, contraindications to collagenase, the patient’s treatment preference, and acceptance of the additional treatment cost. Combination treatment was generally considered for patients with a relatively large contained protrusion or extrusion in whom plasma ablation alone was expected to provide insufficient volume reduction. Plasma ablation alone was more commonly selected for smaller lesions, when collagenase was contraindicated or considered inappropriate, or when the patient declined the additional drug treatment. The final treatment strategy was determined through shared decision-making between the treating physician and the patient. Because treatment allocation was based on clinical judgment rather than randomization, confounding by indication could not be excluded and was addressed analytically.

All patients underwent standardized percutaneous low-temperature plasma ablation under general anesthesia. After the symptomatic level was confirmed by preoperative imaging and intraoperative fluoroscopy, percutaneous puncture was performed through a posterolateral approach. A working cannula was advanced into the target intervertebral disc, and its position within the posterior third of the disc was confirmed on anteroposterior and lateral fluoroscopic views. The ablation probe was then introduced into the nucleus pulposus. Intradiscal decompression was performed by advancing the probe in ablation mode and withdrawing it in coagulation mode. Three to six intradiscal channels were created by rotating the probe within the disc according to disc size, herniation morphology, and intraoperative resistance. The probe tip was maintained within the disc space and away from the vertebral endplates and posterior annulus to minimize the risk of endplate or neural injury. The probe and working cannula were withdrawn after satisfactory intradiscal decompression had been achieved, and hemostasis was secured as required.

In the combination group, collagenase was administered during the same operative session immediately after completion of plasma ablation. Collagenase for injection (Andejian; Liaoning Weibang Biopharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Shenyang, China; 1200 U/vial) was used according to the institutional medication protocol. One vial containing 1200 U of collagenase was reconstituted with 2.0 mL of sterile 0.9% saline immediately before administration, yielding a final concentration of 600 U/mL. The solution was prepared under aseptic conditions by the operating-room nursing team. The product lot number, preparation time, administered dose, and route of administration were recorded in the medication administration record.

After fluoroscopic reconfirmation of the working-channel position, the needle tip was positioned within the posterior nucleus pulposus or at the base of the herniated disc material. Negative aspiration was performed before injection to exclude blood or cerebrospinal fluid return and reduce the risk of intravascular or intrathecal administration. The collagenase solution was injected slowly over approximately 1–2 min. The needle was maintained in position for approximately 2 min after injection to permit local dwell time and reduce reflux before withdrawal. Patients were subsequently monitored for new or worsening radicular pain, neurologic deficit, hypersensitivity reactions, fever, infection, and local inflammatory responses.

All procedures were performed by the same surgical/interventional team using a standardized institutional protocol. Preoperative assessment, perioperative medication, postoperative analgesia, early activity restrictions, progressive mobilization, rehabilitation guidance, and follow-up schedules were applied consistently across the two groups as far as permitted by routine clinical practice. Discharge criteria included stable vital signs, adequate pain control with oral medication, absence of a procedure-related complication requiring continued inpatient treatment, and safe ambulation.

Data collection and imaging assessment

Demographic, clinical, perioperative, and follow-up data were retrieved from the electronic medical record using a standardized data-extraction form. Demographic and clinical variables included age, sex, body mass index, occupational workload, symptom duration, predominant symptoms, including low back pain, radicular leg pain, and numbness or paresthesia, and documented comorbidities and lifestyle factors, including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, osteoporosis, smoking history, and alcohol use. Baseline VAS and ODI scores and the calendar year of treatment were also recorded.

Perioperative variables included procedure duration, estimated blood loss, length of hospital stay, number of ablation channels, postoperative analgesic strategy, perioperative antibiotic use, and initiation of rehabilitation within 24 h. For patients in the combination group, the collagenase product, specification, lot-number documentation, administered dose, dilution volume, and final concentration were additionally recorded. Procedure-related adverse events were identified from operative notes, inpatient records, medication records, discharge summaries, readmission records, and follow-up documentation.

Preoperative lumbar MRI examinations were independently reviewed by two assessors who were blinded to treatment allocation and postoperative outcomes. The symptomatic level was determined by concordance between clinical findings and imaging abnormalities. Imaging variables included the index level, herniation morphology, axial location, spinal canal stenosis severity, Pfirrmann grade, maximum anteroposterior diameter of the herniated component, containment status, and degree of nerve root compression. Herniation morphology was classified as protrusion, extrusion, or sequestration, and axial location as central, paracentral, foraminal, or extraforaminal. Disc degeneration was graded on sagittal T2-weighted MRI according to the Pfirrmann classification from grade I to V. The maximum anteroposterior diameter of the herniated component was measured in millimeters on the axial image demonstrating the greatest extension beyond the disc margin. Herniations were classified as contained when the displaced disc material remained covered by the outer annulus and/or posterior longitudinal ligament and as uncontained when this continuity was disrupted or a free fragment was present. Nerve root compression was graded as contact without displacement, displacement without marked deformation, or definite compression with deformation of the affected nerve root. Spinal canal stenosis was categorized as none, mild, moderate, or severe.

Disagreements between the two imaging assessors were resolved by consensus; unresolved cases were adjudicated by a senior spine specialist. Interobserver agreement was evaluated using weighted κ for ordinal variables and Cohen’s κ for nominal variables. The maximum anteroposterior herniation diameter was measured independently by both assessors, and the mean of the two measurements was used for analysis; interobserver reliability for this continuous measurement was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient.

VAS and ODI assessments were completed by the patients using standardized forms at baseline and at the prespecified postoperative follow-up visits. Because of the nature of the interventions, patients and treating physicians were not blinded to treatment allocation. Follow-up personnel used standardized instructions when administering the questionnaires and did not interpret individual items or suggest responses. Whenever feasible, scores were entered directly by the patient or transcribed without modification from the completed form.

Two investigators independently extracted and cross-checked the nonimaging clinical data. Discrepancies were resolved by review of the source records and, when necessary, consultation with a third investigator. Missingness was audited for each variable and follow-up time point and summarized separately by treatment group. The number and proportion of missing observations, together with the corresponding analysis method, are reported in Supplementary Table S1.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were the longitudinal trajectories of pain intensity and functional disability during the 6-month postoperative follow-up period. Pain intensity was assessed using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS; 0–10 points), with higher scores indicating greater pain, and functional disability was evaluated using the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI; 0–100%), with higher percentages indicating greater disability. VAS and ODI were assessed at baseline and at 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, and 6 months after the index procedure. To accommodate variability in routine follow-up scheduling, assessments were considered valid when completed within ± 7 days of the 1-week visit and within ± 14 days of the 1-, 3-, and 6-month visits.

To complement the analysis of mean score trajectories, clinically meaningful improvement was evaluated at 6 months. A VAS responder was defined as a reduction of at least 2 points from baseline, and an ODI responder was defined as a reduction of at least 10% points from baseline. These responder thresholds were used to assess individual-level clinical improvement and were interpreted separately from the between-group mean differences.

Secondary outcomes included symptomatic imaging-confirmed recurrence, reintervention, length of hospital stay, and procedure-related complications. Symptomatic imaging-confirmed recurrence was defined as the reappearance or worsening of low back pain and/or radicular leg symptoms after initial postoperative improvement, accompanied by MRI- or CT-confirmed recurrent disc herniation or enlargement of residual herniated disc material at the same index level during the 6-month follow-up period. Transient postoperative pain fluctuations without confirmatory imaging evidence were not classified as recurrence. New symptoms attributable to a different lumbar level were not counted as recurrence of the index lesion. Imaging was obtained when patients reported recurrent or aggravated symptoms during follow-up or when recurrence was clinically suspected. All suspected recurrence events were reviewed using outpatient records, readmission records, follow-up notes, and imaging reports. Final recurrence status was independently assessed by two investigators, and disagreements were resolved through discussion with a senior spine specialist.

Reintervention was defined as repeat low-temperature plasma ablation, repeat injection therapy, including intradiscal injection, or conversion to open lumbar surgery within 6 months after the index procedure. Length of hospital stay was calculated as the number of inpatient days from admission to discharge. Procedure-related complications occurring within 6 months after the index procedure were identified from operative notes, inpatient records, readmission records, and follow-up documentation. Prespecified complications included neurologic injury, surgical-site or systemic infection, and hypersensitivity or allergic reactions.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and R, version 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Continuous variables were assessed for distributional normality using graphical methods and the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation and non-normally distributed variables as median with interquartile range. Categorical variables are presented as number and percentage. Unadjusted between-group comparisons were performed using the independent-samples t test, Welch t test, Mann–Whitney U test, χ² test, or Fisher exact test, as appropriate. Baseline balance was additionally assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs), with an absolute SMD < 0.10 considered indicative of acceptable balance. Baseline comparability was not inferred solely from hypothesis-test P values.

The number and proportion of missing observations were summarized for each variable and follow-up time point. Missing baseline covariates were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations under a missing-at-random assumption. Twenty imputed datasets were generated using predictive mean matching for continuous variables, binary logistic regression for dichotomous variables, and ordinal logistic regression for ordered categorical variables. The imputation model included treatment group, demographic and clinical variables, imaging characteristics, baseline VAS and ODI scores, postoperative VAS and ODI measurements, and secondary outcomes. Estimates from the imputed datasets were combined using Rubin’s rules. The longitudinal mixed-effects models used all available outcome observations and did not require exclusion of patients with an isolated missing follow-up assessment. Complete-case analyses were performed as sensitivity analyses.

Longitudinal VAS and ODI trajectories were analyzed separately using linear mixed-effects models. Baseline and postoperative assessments at 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, and 6 months were included, with time modeled as a categorical variable. Each model included fixed effects for treatment group, time, and the treatment group × time interaction, together with prespecified baseline covariates selected on clinical grounds: age, sex, body mass index, symptom duration, osteoporosis, treatment year, index level, herniation morphology, maximum anteroposterior herniation diameter, containment status, axial location, nerve root compression grade, spinal canal stenosis severity, and Pfirrmann grade. A patient-specific random intercept was included to account for within-patient correlation. An unstructured covariance matrix was used initially; a simpler covariance structure was adopted when required for model convergence and selected according to the Akaike information criterion. The principal longitudinal hypothesis was assessed using the overall treatment group × time interaction. Estimated marginal means, adjusted between-group mean differences, 95% confidence intervals, and multiplicity-adjusted P values were reported for each postoperative time point.

To further address confounding by indication, propensity scores for receipt of combination treatment were estimated using multivariable logistic regression including all prespecified pretreatment demographic, clinical, and imaging covariates. One-to-one nearest-neighbor matching without replacement was performed using a caliper width of 0.20 standard deviations of the logit of the propensity score. Covariate balance before and after matching was evaluated using SMDs, with an absolute SMD < 0.10 considered acceptable. The longitudinal mixed-effects models were repeated in the matched cohort as a sensitivity analysis. Because propensity score methods account only for measured covariates, residual confounding from physician judgment, patient preference, and other unrecorded factors could not be eliminated.

At 6 months, clinically meaningful improvement was evaluated using responder analyses. VAS response was defined as a reduction of at least 2 points from baseline, and ODI response as a reduction of at least 10% points. Between-group differences in responder rates were summarized using risk ratios with 95% confidence intervals and were additionally evaluated using multivariable logistic regression adjusted for the same prespecified baseline covariates.

Symptomatic imaging-confirmed recurrence, reintervention, and complications were summarized using risk ratios and 95% confidence intervals. Because these events were infrequent, analyses of these outcomes were considered exploratory, and nonsignificant results were not interpreted as evidence of equivalence or absence of a clinically meaningful difference. Sensitivity analyses included complete-case analysis, analysis of the propensity score–matched cohort, and exclusion of patients with uncontained or sequestered herniations.

Because this retrospective cohort included all consecutive eligible patients during the study period, no prospective sample size calculation was performed. With 108 patients in the control group and 116 in the observation group, the available sample provided 80% power to detect a standardized between-group mean difference of approximately 0.38 at a two-sided α level of 0.05. Post hoc power estimates were calculated for the observed VAS, ODI, recurrence, reintervention, and overall complication effects to quantify the limitations of the available sample. These estimates were interpreted descriptively and were not used to redefine statistically nonsignificant findings. All statistical tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient selection, missing data, and analysis cohorts

Between July 2021 and July 2025, 245 hospitalized patients with imaging-confirmed lumbar disc herniation were screened for eligibility. Twenty-one patients were excluded because of previous lumbar surgery at the index level (n = 6), concomitant spinal disorders likely to confound outcome assessment (n = 4), significant spinal instability or severe central canal stenosis requiring open decompression and/or fusion (n = 5), cauda equina syndrome or a progressive severe neurologic deficit requiring emergency surgery (n = 2), a contraindication to collagenase or a history suggestive of hypersensitivity (n = 2), or absence of key outcome data or inability to ascertain 6-month follow-up status (n = 2). The final cohort comprised 224 patients, including 108 who received low-temperature plasma ablation alone and 116 who received low-temperature plasma ablation combined with intradiscal collagenase (Fig. 1).

Flowchart of patient selection and construction of the analytic cohorts

Baseline VAS and ODI scores were available for all 224 patients. The numbers of available VAS assessments were 219 at 1 week, 217 at 1 month, 213 at 3 months, and 210 at 6 months. The corresponding numbers of available ODI assessments were 218, 215, 211, and 208, respectively. Missingness in baseline demographic, clinical, and imaging variables was limited, with no individual baseline variable exceeding 3.2% missing data. The greatest proportion of missing outcome data occurred for the 6-month ODI assessment (16/224, 7.1%). Missing observations were distributed across both treatment groups without evidence of marked differential missingness (Supplementary Table S1).

Missing baseline covariates were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations, with 20 imputed datasets generated as prespecified. All available longitudinal VAS and ODI observations were retained in the mixed-effects analyses. Complete-case analyses were conducted as sensitivity analyses. After multiple imputation and 1:1 propensity score matching, 102 matched pairs were retained, yielding a matched cohort of 204 patients; 6 patients from the control group and 14 from the observation group were not matched because no suitable counterpart was available within the prespecified caliper.

Treatment selection and baseline characteristics

Before propensity score matching, the control and observation groups were similar in age, sex, baseline VAS, baseline ODI, index level, and Pfirrmann grade. The observation group had a higher BMI than the control group (24.9 ± 3.3 vs. 24.3 ± 2.9 kg/m²; SMD = 0.193), a larger maximum anteroposterior herniation diameter (7.4 ± 1.6 vs. 6.8 ± 1.5 mm; SMD = 0.386), a higher proportion of contained herniations (82.8% vs. 72.2%; SMD = 0.301), and a higher proportion of definite nerve root compression with deformation (34.5% vs. 24.1%; overall SMD = 0.322). Additional imbalances were observed for hypertension, osteoporosis, occupational workload, and treatment year (Table 1).

Perioperative characteristics

The mean procedure duration was 47.3 ± 9.6 min in the control group and 50.1 ± 11.7 min in the observation group, with a between-group difference of 2.8 min (P = 0.052). Median estimated blood loss was higher in the observation group than in the control group (20.3 [IQR, 14.5–28.2] mL vs. 16.0 [IQR, 12.2–22.1] mL; P = 0.001). Median length of hospital stay was 3.0 days in both groups (P = 0.515).

Postoperative analgesia consisted of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs alone in 72.2% of patients in the control group and 69.0% in the observation group, whereas opioid rescue was required in 27.8% and 31.0%, respectively (P = 0.698). Perioperative antibiotics were administered to 79.6% of patients in the control group and 82.8% in the observation group (P = 0.668). Rehabilitation was initiated within 24 h in 76.9% and 79.3% of patients, respectively (P = 0.777) (Table 2).

Longitudinal trajectories of VAS and ODI

In the adjusted linear mixed-effects models, VAS scores decreased over time in both groups (overall time effect, P < 0.001). The treatment group × time interaction was significant (P = 0.002), indicating different VAS trajectories between the two groups. Baseline adjusted VAS scores did not differ between the control and observation groups (adjusted mean difference, − 0.06; 95% CI, − 0.34 to 0.22; adjusted P = 0.674). At postoperative week 1, the adjusted VAS score was 0.47 points lower in the observation group (95% CI, − 0.79 to − 0.15; adjusted P = 0.004). The adjusted between-group differences were − 0.56 points at 1 month, − 0.54 points at 3 months, and − 0.54 points at 6 months, with all corresponding confidence intervals excluding zero.

ODI scores also decreased over time in both groups (overall time effect, P < 0.001), with a significant treatment group × time interaction (P = 0.006). The adjusted baseline difference was − 0.40% points (95% CI, − 2.97 to 2.17; adjusted P = 0.759). The observation group had lower adjusted ODI scores at 1 week (adjusted mean difference, − 3.50% points; 95% CI, − 6.25 to − 0.75; adjusted P = 0.018), 1 month (− 4.60; 95% CI, − 7.16 to − 2.04; adjusted P = 0.001), 3 months (− 5.30; 95% CI, − 7.72 to − 2.88; adjusted P < 0.001), and 6 months (− 5.10; 95% CI, − 7.47 to − 2.73; adjusted P < 0.001) (Table 3).

Safety and complications

The overall complication rate was 11.1% (12/108) in the control group and 7.8% (9/116) in the observation group (RR, 0.70; 95% CI, 0.31–1.59; P = 0.395). Neurologic injury occurred in 2.8% and 1.7% of patients, respectively (P = 0.675), surgical-site or systemic infection in 5.6% and 3.4% (P = 0.456), and hypersensitivity reactions in 2.8% and 2.6% (P = 1.000). The wide confidence intervals and small numbers of events precluded a definitive assessment of comparative safety (Table 5).

After excluding patients with uncontained or sequestered herniations, the corresponding differences were − 0.50 points (95% CI, − 0.80 to − 0.20; P = 0.001) and − 4.82% points (95% CI, − 7.48 to − 2.16; P < 0.001), respectively.

The available sample provided 80% power to detect a standardized between-group mean difference of approximately 0.38. Discussion

In this retrospective, nonrandomized cohort study, VAS and ODI scores decreased over time in both treatment groups, while significant treatment group × time interactions indicated different short-term recovery trajectories. After adjustment for demographic, clinical, and imaging characteristics, low-temperature plasma ablation combined with intradiscal collagenase was associated with lower VAS and ODI scores from postoperative week 1 through month 6 than plasma ablation alone. At 6 months, the adjusted between-group mean differences were − 0.54 points for VAS and − 5.10% points for ODI. Similar results were obtained in the propensity score–matched cohort, complete-case analysis, and analysis excluding patients with uncontained or sequestered herniations. However, the proportions achieving the prespecified individual improvement thresholds did not differ significantly between groups. Estimates for recurrence, reintervention, and complications were imprecise because of the small numbers of events.

These findings extend the directly relevant observational study by Zhang et al., which reported lower postoperative VAS and ODI scores and changes in circulating inflammatory mediators after low-temperature plasma ablation combined with collagenase compared with plasma ablation alone [12]. That study focused primarily on early and 3-month outcomes. The present study adds a uniform 6-month follow-up period, standardized reassessment of Pfirrmann grade, herniation size, containment status, and nerve root compression, longitudinal mixed-effects modeling of repeated VAS and ODI measurements, propensity score matching, responder analyses, and explicit evaluation of recurrence, reintervention, and complications. Its incremental contribution therefore lies in estimating the adjusted longitudinal association, evaluating the clinical magnitude of the observed differences, and quantifying the uncertainty surrounding infrequent secondary and safety outcomes.

The wider evidence base for enzymatic chemonucleolysis remains heterogeneous. A systematic review and meta-analysis reported an overall treatment success rate of approximately 79%, but the included studies differed substantially in enzyme type, treatment era, patient selection, outcome definition, comparator, and methodological quality [9]. A recent review of collagenase chemonucleolysis similarly emphasized that treatment response may depend on herniation morphology, annular integrity, degeneration severity, collagenase dose, injection location, and procedural standardization [10]. The current findings are consistent with a possible incremental short-term benefit from adding collagenase to plasma ablation, but they do not establish uniform benefit across all LDH phenotypes.

The magnitude of the treatment effect warrants cautious interpretation. The adjusted 6-month differences of 0.54 VAS points and 5.10 ODI percentage points were smaller than several commonly cited individual-level MCID estimates in lumbar spine populations. Copay et al. reported MCID estimates of approximately 1.2 to 1.6 points for pain scales and 12.8% points for ODI, although these thresholds vary according to the population, baseline severity, intervention, follow-up interval, external anchor, and analytical method [13]. An individual MCID is not mathematically equivalent to a between-group mean effect because the former classifies improvement within a patient, whereas the latter estimates the average incremental effect associated with treatment. We therefore supplemented the continuous-outcome analyses with responder analyses. At 6 months, the VAS response rate was 91.2% in the control group and 96.3% in the observation group, while the ODI response rate was 83.2% and 91.6%, respectively. Although both estimates numerically favored combination treatment, neither comparison reached statistical significance. Collectively, the findings indicate a statistically detectable but modest average incremental benefit rather than a large or uniformly patient-perceptible advantage.

Several mechanisms could hypothetically explain the observed differences in recovery trajectories. Previous clinical studies have evaluated collagenase chemonucleolysis alone or in combination with radiofrequency-based procedures and have proposed complementary enzymatic and mechanical effects [14–16]. Clinical experience with condoliase, another chemonucleolytic enzyme, also suggests that enzymatic degradation of nucleus pulposus components can reduce symptoms in selected patients with LDH [17]. Plasma ablation may reduce nucleus pulposus volume and intradiscal pressure, whereas collagenase may facilitate degradation of collagen-containing disc material. Changes in the local inflammatory environment surrounding the affected nerve root have also been proposed [12]. However, the present study did not include serial disc-volume measurements, postoperative quantitative MRI, intradiscal pressure assessment, inflammatory biomarkers, or tissue-level evidence of matrix degradation. These proposed pathways therefore remain hypotheses rather than mechanisms demonstrated by the present data. Prospective studies incorporating serial MRI volumetry, standardized assessment of herniation resorption, and inflammatory or matrix-degradation biomarkers are required to determine whether symptomatic improvement corresponds to structural resorption, biochemical modulation, or both.

The imaging variables incorporated into the revised analyses are clinically relevant because anatomical phenotype may influence both treatment allocation and response. Pfirrmann grade, maximum anteroposterior herniation diameter, containment status, morphology, axial location, nerve root compression, and canal stenosis severity were included in the baseline assessment and adjusted models. Nevertheless, these measurements cannot fully characterize annular competence, disc biomechanics, inflammatory activity, fragment mobility, or technical accessibility. Residual anatomical confounding may therefore remain despite multivariable adjustment and propensity score matching.

Symptomatic imaging-confirmed recurrence occurred in 11.1% of the control group and 5.2% of the observation group, while reintervention occurred in 9.3% and 3.4%, respectively. Although the corresponding risk ratios were below 1, their confidence intervals crossed 1 and were wide. Recurrence after LDH treatment is multifactorial and may be influenced by degeneration severity, annular defects, herniation morphology, mechanical loading, rehabilitation, and follow-up intensity [18]. Statistical power was only 37.9% for recurrence and 44.9% for reintervention. These findings should therefore be regarded as exploratory and cannot establish that adjunctive collagenase reduces recurrence or subsequent intervention. In addition, a 6-month follow-up period is insufficient to characterize delayed recurrence or long-term structural durability.

The perioperative differences were small in absolute terms. Median estimated blood loss was 20.3 mL in the observation group and 16.0 mL in the control group, corresponding to an absolute median difference of approximately 4.3 mL. Although statistically significant, a difference of this magnitude is unlikely to be clinically consequential for most patients undergoing a percutaneous intradiscal procedure. It may reflect minor puncture-site bleeding, procedural manipulation, or limited precision in the retrospective estimation of very small blood volumes. Mean procedure duration was only 2.8 min longer in the observation group and did not differ significantly. Length of stay and postoperative management measures were also similar. Thus, the addition of collagenase did not appear to materially increase measured short-term procedural or inpatient burden.

The overall complication rate was 11.1% in the control group and 7.8% in the observation group, but the 95% confidence interval for the risk ratio ranged from 0.31 to 1.59. Confidence intervals for neurologic injury, infection, and hypersensitivity reactions were even wider, and statistical power for the overall complication comparison was only 13.8%. The absence of statistically significant differences therefore does not establish equivalent or noninferior safety. The available chemonucleolysis literature suggests that serious adverse events are uncommon overall, but event profiles vary according to the enzyme, dose, delivery technique, and study era [9, 10]. Larger multicenter cohorts or prospective registries are needed to characterize uncommon neurologic, infectious, and hypersensitivity complications reliably.

The clinical role of combination intradiscal treatment should be distinguished from that of established surgical decompression. Microdiscectomy remains a standard surgical option for persistent disabling radiculopathy with concordant nerve root compression, while endoscopic discectomy is an increasingly used minimally invasive surgical alternative. Comparative evidence indicates that endoscopic discectomy and microdiscectomy generally produce substantial improvements in pain and disability, although differences in operative time, blood loss, hospitalization, recurrence, and complications vary across techniques and studies [3]. A systematic review and meta-analysis found a lower pooled overall complication rate with full-endoscopic discectomy than with open or microscopic discectomy, although the certainty of this finding was limited by heterogeneity and relatively small event numbers [19]. Another systematic review found broadly similar clinical effectiveness between full-endoscopic and open or microendoscopic discectomy, while emphasizing variability in surgical technique and study quality [20]. Current guidance supports individualized treatment selection according to symptom persistence, neurologic status, imaging findings, response to conservative management, and patient preference [1]. Because the present study did not include microdiscectomy, endoscopic discectomy, or continued nonoperative care as comparator groups, it cannot establish equivalence or superiority over these approaches. The findings should not be interpreted as supporting replacement of direct surgical decompression in patients with progressive neurologic deficits, cauda equina syndrome, severe compression, or other established surgical indications.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, treatment allocation was based on clinical judgment and shared decision-making rather than randomization. Although multivariable adjustment and propensity score matching improved balance in measured characteristics, residual confounding from physician expectations, patient preference, treatment cost, and undocumented anatomical or technical factors remains possible. Second, patients and treating clinicians were not blinded. Because VAS and ODI are self-reported outcomes, expectancy and contextual treatment effects may have contributed to the observed differences. Third, missing baseline covariates were addressed using multiple imputation, and mixed-effects models incorporated available repeated measurements, but these approaches depend on assumptions regarding the missing-data mechanism. Fourth, follow-up was limited to 6 months, and recurrence imaging was symptom-triggered rather than routinely scheduled. Fifth, the study was underpowered for recurrence, reintervention, and complications. Sixth, estimated blood loss may have been measured imprecisely in a procedure involving very small blood volumes. Seventh, patient satisfaction, return to work, analgesic cessation, health-related quality of life, and return to usual activity were not collected systematically. Finally, the absence of serial imaging, biomarkers, intradiscal pressure measurements, or biomechanical assessments prevents mechanistic conclusions.

Future studies should use prospective multicenter designs with explicit treatment-allocation criteria, standardized procedural protocols, blinded or independently administered outcome assessment where feasible, and longer follow-up. They should include patient-centered outcomes, clinically comparable surgical comparator groups, and serial quantitative imaging or biomarker analyses to clarify comparative effectiveness, long-term durability, safety, and potential mechanisms.

Conclusion

In this retrospective cohort, low-temperature plasma ablation combined with intradiscal collagenase was associated with lower adjusted VAS and ODI scores over 6 months than plasma ablation alone, with significant differences in longitudinal recovery trajectories. However, the adjusted average between-group differences were modest, and the proportions achieving prespecified individual clinically meaningful improvement thresholds did not differ significantly. Recurrence, reintervention, and complication comparisons were limited by small event numbers, wide confidence intervals, and low statistical power. The findings support further evaluation of the combination strategy but do not establish comparative safety, long-term durability, or superiority over established surgical treatments. Prospective studies with standardized selection criteria, blinded outcome assessment, surgical comparator groups, mechanistic measurements, and longer follow-up are required.
