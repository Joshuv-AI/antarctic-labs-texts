Midlife ICAM-1 levels may predict cardiovascular disease and cognitive decline in latelife: Insights from the HeartSCORE study
Authors: Khaled Abdoun, Justin Swanson, Ian Pollack, Yuefang Chang, Kevin Kip, Oscar L Lopez, Ann Cohen, Steven Reis, Anum Saeed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSystemic inflammation is a well-established risk factor for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) and has also been implicated in the progression of neuroinflammation. However, the relationship between inflammation and the combined risk of ASCVD and cognitive decline—particularly during the critical transition from midlife to late life—remains poorly understood. Identifying a shared inflammatory marker that signals vulnerability to both conditions may be an important tool for early intervention. In this study, we examined how baseline inflammatory markers, as well as their changes over one year, relate to long-term risk of ASCVD and cognitive outcomes.MethodsWe analyzed baseline and one-year change (1y-Δ) in an inflammatory biomarker panel [high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), interleukin-6 (IL-6), intracellular Adhesion Molecule 1 (ICAM-1) and CD40 ligand (CD40L_)] in the Heart Strategies Concentrating on Risk Evaluation (Heart SCORE) study. Using logistic and linear Cox regression models, the association of inflammatory markers and 1y-Δ, cognitive assessment by MoCA score, coronary artery calcium (CAC), carotid intima-media thickness (CIMT), and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) events were assessed longitudinally. All data were adjusted for the pooled cohort equation (PCE) risk factors and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) were calculated for incremental risk prediction.ResultsAmong 673 participants (mean age 59 ± 6.8 years; 63.9 % female; 31.6 % Black) were followed for 12 years. While both hs-CRP and IL-6 were associated with MACE events at 12 years, only ICAM-1 was linked with long-term MACE (HR 2.34 [1.02–5.37], p < 0.05) as well as lower MoCA scores (β: 0.47 [95 % CI: 0.93 to −0.02], p < 0.05). Compared to the PCE model, inflammatory biomarkers improved risk prediction indices for MACE (0.812, ΔAUC +0.056, p = 0.02) and MoCA (0.664, ΔAUC +0.04, p = 0.048). One-year biomarker changes were not significant for endpoint association.ConclusionsIn a community cohort of adults, midlife levels of three inflammatory markers (hs-CRP, IL-6, and ICAM-1) were predictive of late life ASCVD; however, only ICAM-1 was identified as a dual marker for ASCVD and cognitive impairment. The role of ICAM-1 as a prognostic marker for adverse cardiovascular and cognitive health should be explored in future studies.Keywords: Inflammaging, Atherosclerosis, Neuroinflammation, Heart Brain Axis, Cognitive decline

Introduction

The Heart-Brain Axis (HBA) describes the bidirectional relationship between the cardiovascular and central nervous systems, with growing evidence linking atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) with increased risk of Alzheimer's disease and other neurodegenerative disorders [[1], [2], [3]]. With an aging population worldwide, in whom ASCVD is increasingly prevalent and the leading cause of morbidity, it is crucial to identify modifiable risk factors for preventive interventions [4,5]. Aging itself is a major, non-modifiable risk factor for both ASCVD and neurodegenerative diseases in which many maladaptive processes overlap [6,7]. As individuals age, chronic low-grade inflammation becomes more prominent, contributing to vascular dysfunction, atherosclerosis, and neurodegeneration [8]. Inflammatory biomarkers such as interleukin-6 (IL-6), high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), intracellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1), vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1), and CD40 ligand (CD40L) have been implicated in these processes [[9], [10], [11], [12]]. Notably, many of these markers have been associated with both ASCVD and cognitive decline - likely a sequela of inflammaging – the deleterious effects of chronic inflammation on aging-related diseases [8].

Since ASCVD and neurodegeneration share overlapping risk factors—including aging—it is critical to improve risk identification during the mid-to-late life transitions. Traditional risk factors have less pronounced prognostic power in late life due to physiological changes, accumulated damage, and shifting pathophysiology [13]. Although adhering to American Heart Association's (AHA) proposed Life's Essential 8 (LE8) behaviors [14] plays a fundamental role in ASCVD prevention, they may not fully capture an individual's risk, particularly in mid-to-late life transitions [15]. Inflammatory markers may offer more personalized risk assessment for optimal cardiovascular and cognitive health during mid to late life transition.

To address this gap, we evaluated whether mid-life inflammatory biomarkers and their one-year change (1-yΔ) are associated with clinical and subclinical ASCVD, as well as cognitive function leveraging data from the Heart Strategies Concentrating on Risk Evaluation (Heart SCORE) longitudinal cohort study.

Methods

Study design

The Heart SCORE study is a longitudinal cohort study started by the University of Pittsburgh recruited baseline visits between 2003 and 2005. The study, aimed at identifying disparities in ASCVD risk in a middle-aged population, enrolled 2000 adults without a previous diagnosis of ASCVD, who mainly self-identified as Black or White and continues to follow them until present day [16]. More detailed descriptions of the Heart SCORE study have been previously published [16,17]. Participants with a life expectancy of <5 years, existing coronary artery disease, or who were unable to undergo annual follow up were excluded [17].

Assessment of covariates

Participants underwent a comprehensive assessment that included the collection of demographic information, psychosocial circumstances, body habitus measurements, dietary patterns, exercise habits, and sleep behaviors. In addition, participants were evaluated for ASCVD risk factors to determine their cardiovascular risk profile using the standard Pooled Cohort Equations (PCE) variables [6]. Additional clinical and lifestyle factors were also considered, including smoking status, lipid profile (triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein [HDL] cholesterol, and total cholesterol), and blood pressure measurements (systolic and diastolic). The presence of hypertension, diabetes, and metabolic syndrome was documented, along with the use of antihypertensive medications, statins, and aspirin therapy. Diabetes mellitus was identified based on a fasting serum glucose level of ≥126 mg/dL, a non-fasting serum glucose level of ≥200 mg/dL, a self-reported physician diagnosis of diabetes, or the use of hypoglycemic medication. Smoking status was categorized as “never,” “former,” or “current” according to self-reported data. Blood pressure (BP) measurements were taken by trained personnel following standardized procedures [17].

Measurement of serum inflammatory markers

Serum inflammatory panel inclusive of hs-CRP, IL-6, ICAM-1, and CD40L was measured via standard assays at the University of Pittsburgh Medical Center. Baseline serum markers were drawn at baseline, then one year later to calculate a 1-yΔ to reflect the chronicity of each participant's inflammatory state. IL-6 levels were analyzed using commercially available ELISA kits (Quantikine high-sensitivity human kits, R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN). Hs-CRP was measured using a high-sensitivity assay on a Hitachi 911 analyzer (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland) with reagents sourced from Denka Seiken (Niigata, Japan). Baseline plasma samples were stored for future evaluations [17].

Assessment of ASCVD

Since biomarkers were measured at midlife in a cohort free of ASCVD, we used measurements of coronary artery calcium (CAC), carotid intima-media thickness (CIMT), and major adverse cardiac events (MACE) as predictors of cross-sectional, short-term, and long-term cardiovascular risk, respectively.

Subclinical ASCVD burden was assessed via baseline measurement of CAC (as Agatston score) at baseline, then again 2 years later [16]. CAC in this population was obtained using electron beam computed tomography and has been previously described by Shpilsky et al. [18]. Positive CAC was defined as having an Agatston score >0, indicating any presence of coronary calcium.

CIMT, a validated independent predictor of cardiovascular risk, was measured in participants 5 years later [19]. The technique for CIMT acquisition in this group has been previously described [18]. CIMT ≥1.3 mm was considered significant.

Clinical ASCVD was defined as any MACE including fatal and nonfatal myocardial infarction, revascularization, ischemic stroke, and cardiovascular death. The median follow-up time to outcome assessment was 12 years.

Assessment of cognition

Cognition was assessed using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) [20]. Participants enrolled underwent MoCA evaluation ∼10 years after enrollment.

Statistical analysis

Characteristics of study participants were assessed at baseline. Descriptive statistics are presented as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and means and standard deviations for numeric variables. Changs in biomarker levels from baseline to one year were evaluated using paired t-tests.

Logistic regression was applied to estimate odds ratios (ORs) for the relationship between biomarker log-concentration and baseline CAC as well as CIMT. Cox regression was used to calculate hazard ratios (HRs) for the association between each biomarker's log-concentration and time to first MACE. Linear regression was performed to assess the relationship between biomarker log-concentration and MoCA performance at year 10. When using regression to estimate the relationship between inflammatory biomarkers and various outcomes, we developed two types of models: 1) a model including only the biomarker in question and adjusting for PCE covariates, and 2) a larger model including all biomarkers, their 1-yΔ, and adjusting for PCE covariates. To improve model fit and mitigate violations of various regression assumptions, inflammatory markers were treated as natural log-transformed values. Interpretation of statistics such as means or mean difference, beta coefficients, and ORs/HRs should be considered in terms of log-units.

To assess the impact of biomarker information on model discrimination, we fit models using PCE variables with and without biomarkers. First, logistic regression was used to predict a MoCA score less than 26, and area under the receiver operating curve (AUC) was assessed using DeLong's Test [21]. Next, Cox regression was used to predict MACE; model fit was assessed using concordance indices and a likelihood ratio test.

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.3 [22].

Ethics

All participants in the Heart SCORE study provided written informed consent at the time of enrollment, and the study was approved by the University of Pittsburgh Institutional Review Board (IRB). Data analysis was performed by Justin Swanson, who had full access to all study data and takes responsibility for its integrity.

Results

Baseline participant characteristics

Among 673 participants, baseline age 59 ± 6.8 years, 63.9 % were female and 31.6 % were self-reported Black (Table 1). This population was overweight with a mean BMI of 29.3 ± 5.6 kg/m2 with 34.1 % classified as hypertensive and, 7.4 % and 17.2 % with diabetes, and metabolic syndrome, respectively. The total prevalence at follow-up of CAC with an Agatston score >0 was 75.7 % (N = 368). CIMT measuring ≥1.30 mm was present in 15.8 % of participants (N = 106). There was a total of 77 occurrences of MACE over 7005.5 person-years of follow-up, or 1.1 cases per 100 person-years.

Assessment of model discrimination

Using only PCE variables to predict a MoCA score less than 26 resulted in an AUC of 0.624, while the model including both PCE and biomarker variables resulted in an AUC of 0.664. DeLong's test yielded a p-value of 0.048, indicating a statistically significant improvement of AUC. When predicting MACE, the concordance index of the model using only PCE variables was 0.756, while the model including both PCE and biomarker variables had a concordance index of 0.812. A likelihood ratio test of these models yielded a p-value of 0.020, indicating a statistically significant improvement to model fit with the inclusion of biomarker values.

Discussion

In this longitudinal cohort study, we examined the association of mid-life (baseline) inflammatory biomarker levels and their 1-y change (1-yΔ) with ASCVD events and cognitive impairment (measured by MoCA score). We demonstrate 3 central findings; 1) Over a longitudinal time period, hs-CRP, IL-6, and ICAM-1 were associated with ASCVD events, 2) ICAM-1 was not only associated with ASCVD events but also with lower MoCA score risk and, 3) addition of these inflammatory markers to the PCE made an incremental improvement in risk prediction C-statistic metrics for both ASCVD and cognitive impairment.

Our findings align with prior evidence showing causal role of inflammation in ASCVD pathogenesis. We observed that mid-life levels of hs-CRP and IL-6, which are known ASCVD risk modifiers, correlate with cross-sectional CAC>0 and short-term incremental CIMT increase, respectively [11,12]. Both were also significantly associated with MACE risk in the long-term follow-up. IL-6 has been extensively implicated in vascular remodeling and subclinical atherosclerosis, and our findings mirror those of the Stabilization of Atherosclerotic Plaque by Initiation of Darapladib Therapy (STABILITY) trial, where IL-6 independently predicted MACE risk in patients with stable CAD [12]. Unlike the STABILITY trial, which only assessed baseline inflammatory marker levels, our analysis allowed us to study the effect of chronic inflammation on ASCVD outcomes by using baseline IL-6 and hs-CRP levels and their change over one year. Furthermore, the STABILITY trial found a similar association of hs-CRP with MACE and MI, however, in fully adjusted models accounting for PCE and other inflammatory markers, no association was found [12]. We demonstrate that mid-life serum levels of IL-6 and hs-CRP are associated with worse cardiovascular outcomes in participants without established CAD, suggesting that they may not only be implicated in plaque instability, but also in plaque development given their association with CIMT and CAC, respectively. Previous studies have shown a positive correlation between CD40L and CIMT; however, we found an inverse association [23]. This descrepancy maybe explained by the current study's threshold for CIMT <1.30 mm versus prior work citing normal as CIMT <0.9 mm and subclinical atherosclerosis as 0.9 mm ≤ CIMT <1.2 mm [23]. Additional studies should expand on these relationships.

Our findings on intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1) in relation to both ASCVD and cognitive function offer novel insights and also warrant further investigation. Although ICAM-1 has been individually linked to CVD and cognitive decline, our study is the first to identify it as a shared predictive biomarker for both outcomes within the same cohort. Previously, Hwang et al. reported that ICAM-1 was an independent predictor of coronary artery disease and higher CIMT in the ARIC Study [24,25]. In our analysis, elevated ICAM-1 was strongly associated with adverse cardiovascular outcomes, though we did not observe a significant association with CIMT.

Importantly, these results contribute to a growing body of literature connecting systemic inflammation with neurocognitive impairment and systemic vascular disease. Prior studies suggest that ICAM-1, a marker of endothelial dysfunction, may facilitate leukocyte transmigration across the blood-brain barrier, promoting neuroinflammatory processes that contribute to cognitive decline [10,26]. Supporting this mechanism, Yoon et al. (CARDIA Study) found that elevated ICAM-1 levels in early adulthood were associated with poorer cognitive performance in midlife [27]. The current finding extends these results by demonstrating that ICAM-1 may be a relevant predictor of disease during the mid-to-late life transitions as well.

Recent research has also highlighted the utility of ASCVD risk factors in predicting adverse brain outcomes such as neurodegeneration [28]. However, standard risk models like the PCE may underestimate ASCVD risk in certain populations, particularly older adults [15]. Integration of PCE variables with inflammatory markers outperformed PCE alone in MACE and cognitive outcomes prediction in this aging cohort. These results underscore the potential value of including routinely measured inflammatory biomarkers in clinical risk assessments for cardiovascular and cognitive health. Further research is warranted to determine whether interventions targeting systemic inflammation could reduce the burden of both ASCVD and neurocognitive impairment.

Study limitations

This study had a relatively small number of participants (N = 673). Although multiple statistical tests were performed, we did not apply a formal adjustment for multiple comparisons; individual p-values should be interpreted cautiously, and study findings should be considered exploratory. Additionally, the study may be underpowered to detect smaller but potentially meaningful associations between inflammatory biomarkers and various clinical outcomes, and future studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to more robustly assess these effects. While inflammatory biomarkers were measured at baseline and one-year follow-up, longer-term trends were not assessed. The reliance on serum biomarker levels without repeated measures over extended follow-up periods may limit the ability to capture more chronic inflammatory processes. While the Heart SCORE population is healthy at relatively normal hs-CRP and IL-6 levels, the study may be underpowered to detect a significant association between hs-CRP and IL-6 with cognition. CIMT measurements lacked a baseline for direct comparison, limiting our ability to assess progression accurately. Finally, while the study included a high proportion of Black and White participants, other racial and ethnic groups were underrepresented, which may affect the generalizability of the findings.

Conclusions

In this community study, ICAM-1 was identified as a dual marker of ASCVD and cognitive decline. Further, an inflammatory panel improved risk prediction metrics for both ASCVD and cognitive decline beyond traditional risk factors. The utility of ICAM-1 for risk assessment of ASCVD and cognitive risk during mid to latelife should be explored further.
