Exploring and identifying the imaging biomarkers for predicting anti-VEGF treatment response in polypoidal choroidal vasculopathy: a prospective multicenter study
Authors: Wenfei Zhang, Xingwang Gu, Bing Li, Shulin Liu, Jingyuan Yang, Yuelin Wang, Shiyu Cheng, Lin Lv, Zhiqing Li, Linna Lu, Jinghong Zhang, Qi Zeng, Zefeng Xiao, Guangfeng Liu, Cai Xin, Tianyu Zhang, Yingdan Su, Minyu Chen, Chengshu Wang, Yonkang Cun, Xinyu Zhao, Youxin Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPolypoidal choroidal vasculopathy (PCV) is a hemorrhagic fundus disease that can lead to permanent vision loss. Predicting the treatment response to anti-VEGF monotherapy in PCV is consistently challenging. We aimed to conduct a prospective multicenter study to explore and identify the imaging biomarkers for predicting the anti-VEGF treatment response in PCV patients, establish predictive model, and undergo multicenter validation.MethodsThis prospective multicenter study utilized clinical characteristics and images of treatment naïve PCV patients from 15 ophthalmic centers nationwide to screen biomarkers, develop model, and validate its performance. Patients from Peking Union Medical College Hospital were randomly divided into a training set and an internal validation set. A nomogram was established by univariate, LASSO regression, and multivariate regression analysis. Patients from the other 14 centers served as an external test set. Area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy were calculated. Decision curve analysis (DCA) and clinical impact curve (CIC) were utilized to evaluate the practical utility in clinical decision-making.FindingsThe eye distribution for the training set, internal validation set, and external test set were 66, 31, and 71, respectively. The ‘Good responder’ exhibited a thinner subfoveal choroidal thickness (SFCT) (230.67 ± 61.96 vs. 314.42 ± 88.00 μm, p < 0.001), lower choroidal vascularity index (CVI) (0.31 ± 0.08 vs. 0.36 ± 0.05, p = 0.006), fewer choroidal vascular hyperpermeability (CVH) (31.0 vs. 62.2%, p = 0.012), and more intraretinal fluid (IRF) (58.6 vs. 29.7%, p = 0.018). SFCT (OR 0.990; 95% CI 0.981–0.999; p = 0.033) and CVI (OR 0.844; 95% CI 0.732–0.971; p = 0.018) were ultimately included as the optimal predictive biomarkers and presented in the form of a nomogram. The model demonstrated AUC of 0.837 (95% CI 0.738–0.936), 0.891 (95% CI 0.765–1.000), and 0.901 (95% CI 0.824–0.978) for predicting ‘Good responder’ in the training set, internal validation set, and external test set, respectively, with excellent sensitivity, specificity, and practical utility.InterpretationThinner SFCT and lower CVI can serve as imaging biomarkers for predicting good treatment response to anti-VEGF monotherapy in PCV patients. The nomogram based on these biomarkers exhibited satisfactory performances.Keywords: Polypoidal choroidal vasculopathy, subfoveal choroidal thickness, choroidal vascularity index, anti-VEGF, nomogram

Introduction

Polypoidal choroidal vasculopathy (PCV) is a blinding disease characterized by branching neovascular network (BNN) and polypoidal lesions, which can result in recurrent multilevel retinal and vitreous hemorrhage, and even hemorrhagic retinal detachment, ultimately leading to retinal atrophy and permanent visual loss [1–3].

The treatment of PCV is complex, and there is a lack of standardized therapeutic guidelines. Recent randomized trials and accumulating clinical experiences consistently indicate that, in the majority of PCV cases, anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) monotherapy could lead to significant improvements in visual acuity and induce dry macula. However, these pivotal clinical trials also underscore the marked heterogeneity within PCV. As demonstrated in the EVEREST-II study, one-third of patients undergoing ranibizumab monotherapy exhibited suboptimal responses, requiring 10–12 injections within the first 12 months [4,5]. Additionally, findings from the FUJISAN study revealed that nearly 50% of patients receiving ranibizumab monotherapy necessitated additional deferred PDT [6].

Despite numerous studies attempting to explore imaging biomarkers influencing the prognosis of PCV, many unresolved challenges persist. Firstly, as one of the earliest proposed biomarkers, subfoveal choroidal thickness (SFCT) exhibits higher specificity but only 63.2% sensitivity and the area under the curve (AUC) of 0.769 in Asian population [7]. This suggests that relying solely on SFCT as a predictor for the response to anti-VEGF monotherapy may be insufficient. There remained a need to identify other parameters that could potentially contribute to predicting treatment outcome. Secondly, while some studies have proposed other biomarkers affecting the prognosis of anti-VEGF monotherapy in PCV, such as intraretinal fluid (IRF) [8,9], lesion area, polyp configuration [10], conditions of pigment epithelial detachment (PED) [11], and choroidal vascular hyperpermeability (CVH) [12–14], the conclusions are inconsistent due to the retrospective nature, single center and small sample size of these studies. At the same time, with the proposal of the term ‘pachychoroid’ and advancements in imaging technologies, there has been a surge in interest in relatively novel choroidal vascular indicators, such as choroidal vascularity index (CVI) [15–17], and choroidal capillary vessel density (CCVD) [18] in the context of PCV [19].

Considering the lack of widely recognized and sufficiently large-scale studies on the prediction of therapeutic efficacy for PCV, we conducted a prospective multicenter study to incorporate and screen all possible imaging biomarkers, including controversial indicators and relatively novel choroidal vascular indicators. Through the establishment of a nomogram prediction model and nationwide validation, we aim to identify imaging biomarkers influencing the therapeutic effects of anti-VEGF monotherapy in PCV and provide reference for therapeutic prediction and classification of PCV.

Methods

Data sources

This prospective multicenter study, led by the Ophthalmology Department of Peking Union Medical College Hospital (PUMCH), involved a total of 15 participating centers: Zhongshan Ophthalmic Center of Sun Yat-Sen University, The First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University, Tianjin Medical University Eye Hospital, Ninth People’s Hospital of Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine, The No.4 Hospital of Zhangjiakou, The First Affiliated Hospital of Hunan Normal University, Wuhan Hospital of Integrated Chinese and Western Medicine, Peking University International Hospital, Shaoguan Aier Eye Hospital, BaoTou Eighth Hospital, Foshan Fosun Chancheng Hospital, The Tenth Affiliated Hospital of Southern Medical University, Guannan County First People’s Hospital, Dehong People’s Hospital of Yunnan Province. Treatment naive PCV patients were enrolled from May 2019 to September 2023. Eyes from PUMCH were randomly divided into training set and internal validation set in a 7:3 ratio, while data from other centers served as the external test set. This study was approved by the institutional Review Board/Ethics Committee of PUMCH (No. K4490) and conducted following the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients signed informed consent before treatment. Figure 1 illustrated the workflow of the study.

The workflow of the study.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were: (1) treatment naive PCV diagnosed according to EVEREST criteria [20], namely focal hyperfluorescent lesions appearing in first 6 min with or without BNN on indocyanine green angiograph (ICGA); or non-ICGA diagnostic criteria from the Asia-Pacific Ocular Imaging Society PCV Workgroup [3], mainly sub-retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) ring-like lesion, sharp-peaked PED on optical coherence tomography (OCT) and en face OCT complex RPE elevation; (2) patients received at least 3-monthly intravitreal anti-VEGF according to the 3 + pro re nata (PRN) regimen and anti-VEGF agents included ranibizumab (Lucentis; Genentech, San Francisco, CA), aflibercept (Eylea; Regeneron, Tarrytown, NY) and Conbercept (KH902; Chengdu Kanghong Biotech Co., China). The same anti-VEGF agent was used for 3 initial consecutive injections.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) concomitant retinochoroidal and ocular inflammatory diseases; (2) history of ocular surgeries other than cataract surgery; (3) associated systemic diseases contraindicating anti-VEGF therapy; (4) extensive PED or hemorrhage obscuring the underlying BNN and polypoidal lesions or affecting choroidal indicator measurements; (5) severe opacity of the refractive media.

Measurement of imaging parameters

Patients underwent a comprehensive ophthalmologic examination at baseline, including slit-lamp examination, best corrected visual acuity (BCVA), intraocular pressure, fundus photograph (FP, CX-1, Topcon, Topcon Corporation, Tokyo, Japan), fluorescein angiography (FA), ICGA (Heidelberg Retina Angiography 2, HRA2; Heidelberg Engineering, Germany), swept-source OCT and OCT angiography (OCTA, Intalight, Ltd., China). Monthly OCT and OCTA were conducted to assess treatment efficacy and determine the need for additional injections. The OCT(A) scanned both the fovea-centered and lesion-centered areas within a 6 mm*6mm range (512*512 with 4 repeats) in all the participating centers. ‘Good responder’ referred to those who exhibited complete absorption of subretinal fluid (SRF) and IRF, with no expansion of PED after 3 months of initiating anti-VEGF treatment. ‘Poor responder’ was defined as patients with persistent SRF and/or IRF after 3 consecutive injection.

The Intalight’s OCT(A) employed a deep learning-based artificial intelligence algorithm for the identification of segmentation layers in the retina and in the choroid. Automated segmentation was complemented by manual adjustments to clearly visualize polypoidal lesions and BNN. Central retinal thickness (CRT), SFCT, and maximum PED height referred to the distances between the retinal anterior surface at the fovea to the outer surface of the RPE, the outer boundary of Bruch’s membrane below the fovea to the inner edge of the sclera, and the maximum distance between the RPE and Bruch’s membrane, respectively.

After accurately delineating the RPE, Bruch’s membrane, and choroidal layers, the software automatically identified the contours of PED in each B-scan and performed simultaneous 2D area calculations and 3D volume calculations (Figure 2). The upper boundary of CCVD was Bruch’s membrane, while the lower boundary was defined as the interface between the capillaries and the Sattler layer. CCVD was calculated as the ratio of blood flow area of the choriocapillaris to the entire en face scanning area on foveal centered OCTA images. The three-dimensional CVI referred to the ratio of the choroidal vessel lumen volume in the Haller and Sattler layers to the corresponding total choroidal volume, reflecting choroidal vascular volume density (Figure 2) [21]. CVH was defined as multifocal area of hyperfluorescence with blurred margins during late-phase (10–15 min) ICGA [12]. The above examinations and measurements were performed by two experienced ophthalmologists (WFZ and BL) and the measurements of each eye were repeated for three times. Medical records were reviewed to obtain basic information about patients and treatments.

The upper and lower boundaries of PED were defined by the RPE and Bruch’s membrane, respectively (b). The measurements of three-dimensional CVI were shown in (c,d). Model establishment and evaluation

The preliminary selection of predictor variables for the predictive model was conducted through univariate analysis and LASSO regression analysis. Univariate analysis was performed on the training set, selecting biomarkers that were correlated with the treatment response (p < 0.05). To enhance the model’s generalization performance, the LASSO regression was also employed for biomarkers selection. A 20-fold cross-validation was applied to the included biomarkers, with ‘Lambda. min’ chosen as the optimal regularization parameter. Subsequently, the biomarkers selected through univariate and LASSO regression analysis were incorporated into a multivariate logistic regression analysis, where variables with p < 0.05 were identified as independent risk factors to construct a nomogram predictive model.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were separately plotted for the training set, internal validation set, and external test set. The area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy were calculated to evaluate the predictive performance of the nomogram model. The goodness of fit of the model was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test, and a calibration curve was drawn to intuitively evaluate the model’s calibration. A p-value >0.05 in the H-L test or scatter points closer to the diagonal line in the calibration curve indicated a well-fitted predictive model [22]. Additionally, to assess the practical utility of the predictive model in clinical decision-making, this study presented decision curve analysis (DCA) and clinical impact curves (CIC), quantifying the net benefit of the predictive model at different probability thresholds [23].

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables were reported in terms of frequencies and percentages. Independent t-tests, paired t-tests, or non-parametric tests (if not normally distributed) were employed for the analysis of continuous variables. Chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests were used to analyze categorical variables. Statistical analysis was conducted using R software (version 4.3.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). p < 0.05 was considered indicative of statistically significant differences.

Results

Ninety-seven eyes of 94 patients at PUMCH were randomly allocated into a training set and an internal validation set in a 7:3 ratio, with 66 and 31 eyes, respectively. Seventy-one eyes of 71 patients from other centers were used as the external test set. In training set, the average age was 65.5 ± 9.86, with 37 (58.7%) being male. Twenty-seven eyes involved the right eye. The mean follow-up time was 27.25 ± 15.61 months. Table 1 illustrated the changes in indicators at baseline and the last follow-up. SFCT decreased from 277.62 ± 93.46 μm at baseline to 243.65 ± 86.14 μm (p = 0.008), and SRF was fully absorbed in ∼30% of the eyes. The number of polyps significantly decreased. The maximum PED height, PED area, and volume at baseline were 414.07 ± 313.30 μm, 16.68 ± 13.68 mm2, and 2.11 ± 2.98 mm3, respectively. After anti-VEGF treatment, these values decreased to 321.77 ± 271.22 μm (p = 0.001), 13.46 ± 19.33 mm2 (p < 0.001), and 1.18 ± 1.69 mm3 (p = 0.009), respectively. CCVD decreased to 0.64 ± 0.18 (p = 0.034) and CVI increased from 0.34 ± 0.07 at baseline to 0.36 ± 0.05 (p = 0.006).

Selection of predictive features and model establishment

Univariate regression analysis identified SFCT (OR 0.988; 95% CI 0.981–0.995; p = 0.001), CVH (OR 0.274; 95% CI 0.098–0.767; p = 0.014), IRF (OR 0.299; 95% CI 0.108–0.829; p = 0.020), and CVI (OR 0.844; 95% CI 0.747–0.954; p = 0.007) as factors correlated with response at 3 m. According to the optimal lambda of 0.066, LASSO regression selected SFCT, CVH, IRF, CVI, and CCVD with non-zero coefficients as relevant factors (Figure 5). The results of multivariate regression analysis ultimately incorporated SFCT (OR 0.990; 95% CI 0.981–0.999; p = 0.033) and CVI (OR 0.844; 95% CI 0.732–0.971; p = 0.018) as the optimal predictive biomarkers to develop model and presented in the form of a nomogram (Figure 6).

Model evaluation

As shown in Table 3 and Figure 7, the AUC for the nomogram predicting ‘Good responder’ in the training, internal validation, and external test set was 0.837 (95% CI 0.738–0.936), 0.891 (95% CI 0.765–1.000), and 0.901 (95% CI 0.824–0.978), respectively. Sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy were all above 0.79. The p-values from the Hosmer-Lemeshow test were 0.177, 0.410, and 0.207, respectively, all >0.05.

Discussion

Given the heterogeneity in treatment response among PCV patients, there is a need for clinical and imaging biomarkers to predict treatment outcome, which will aid in formulating treatment plans [2]. However, research attempting to identify predictive factors has yielded controversial results and has often been characterized by retrospective and single-center designs. To the best of our knowledge, our study is the first prospective multicenter study to explore and identify the imaging biomarkers for predicting anti-VEGF treatment response in PCV, as well as integrating SFCT and CVI to construct a predictive model. In this study, we compared changes in imaging parameters before and after anti-VEGF treatment and identified a series of imaging biomarkers associated with treatment outcomes. In both univariate and LASSO regression analyses, SFCT, CVI, CVH, and IRF were identified as indicators associated with treatment response at 3 months. Multivariate logistic regression further identified SFCT and CVI as independent clinical factors for therapeutic effects. Importantly, a nomogram predictive model based on these two imaging factors achieved an AUC above 0.89 in both internal validation and external test sets, demonstrating good sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy. The model exhibited excellent discriminative ability and calibration. DCA and CIC across the three datasets indicated the favorable clinical utility of this predictive model.

Similar to other studies, SFCT, PED height, area, and volume all showed significant reductions following anti-VEGF treatment, while CVI exhibited a significant increase [24–29]. It was postulated that PCV lesions exhibited low resistance, high blood flow, and increased choroidal permeability, resulting in fluid transudation, increased choroidal stromal volume, and elevated subretinal pigment epithelium hydrostatic pressure. Thus, at baseline, the choroid was thicker with relatively lower CVI, accompanied by significant PED. After treatment, there was a reduction in exudation from lesions, absorption of excessive stromal exudates, and a decrease in the hydrostatic pressure beneath the RPE, leading to choroidal thinning and resolution of PED. Additionally, the absorption of choroidal leakage contributed to an increase in CVI [15]. PCV was considered as pachychoroid spectrum disease, characterized by attenuation of the choriocapillaris overlying dilated choroidal veins. In our study, despite anti-VEGF treatment, CCVD continued to decrease, indicating an ongoing atrophy of the choriocapillaris.

Some studies have attempted to explore imaging indicators influencing treatment response. SFCT was one of the earliest proposed biomarkers. In our study, the relationship between increased SFCT in eyes with PCV and poor response to anti-VEGF treatment was similar to the findings reported by others [30,31]. While SFCT demonstrated a high level of specificity as a predictive indicator, its sensitivity remained at a moderate level. This implied that relying solely on a single parameter of SFCT may be insufficient and necessitated the exploration of other potential parameters that could contribute to predicting the response to anti-VEGF monotherapy [7]. Moreover, choroidal thickness cannot reflect the functional status of the choroid. If the increase in outer choroidal vascular luminal volume was counteracted by the decrease in inner choroidal volume due to inner choroidal atrophy, an eye may exhibit choroidal thickness that was normal or even subnormal.

In contrast to the variability of choroidal thickness influenced by factors, such as age, gender, and refractive status, CVI generally remained stable across different age groups and macular regions [32]. Changes in CVI, associated with the relative variations in choroidal vascular volume and choroidal stromal volume, can provide indications of choroidal congestion and ischemic states and serve as a more reliable and stable biomarker for choroidal diseases compared to choroidal thickness. Similar to Vyas’s study, our research also suggested a correlation between CVI and the disease activity [17].

The existence of CVH remained controversial. In some studies, it has been proposed as a risk factor predicting poor responsiveness to anti-VEGF monotherapy in PCV [12], while others believed that it was an unrelated factor [7,30]. However, these studies consistently indicated a strong positive correlation between CVH and SFCT. Liu et al. categorized PCV into two groups based on the presence of CVH. In the presence of CVH, CVI tended to be higher. The occurrence of CVH was attributed to the dilation of choroidal vascular lumens, leading to an increase in the relative area of vascular lumens to the total choroidal area, which may explain the association between CVH and CVI [16]. In our study, the proportion of CVH was higher in the ‘Poor responder’ group, and CVI was larger. Although CVH emerged as a risk factor in both univariate and LASSO regression analyses, it was excluded in the multivariate regression. Therefore, as a non-invasive imaging biomarker, CVI might serve as a potential substitute for the invasive marker CVH obtained through ICGA in predicting treatment outcomes.

For IRF, our study suggested similar results to those of Jang et al. [8]. In their study, the polypoidal choroidal neovascularization group exhibited a higher incidence of IRF compared to the typical PCV group. We speculated that good responders with thinner choroid and lower CVI might have backgrounds more closely resembling polypoidal choroidal neovascularization. They primarily manifested as lesions secondary to changes in RPE degeneration and choroidal neovascularization, resembling neovascular AMD. These abnormal vessels exhibited higher permeability, potentially involving factors associated with VEGF-induced angiogenesis. Thus, those patients with IRF would have good response for anti-VEGF treatment.

In model construction, our objective was to obtain the optimal model with the fewest indicators. The key features of PCV involved changes in choroidal structure [19]. SFCT and CVI, as non-invasive indicators, can reflect the structural and functional status of the choroid. The model based on these indicators demonstrated satisfactory predictive performance in terms of discrimination, accuracy, calibration, and clinical applicability. The different responses to anti-VEGF monotherapy may also correspond to different subtypes of PCV. As indicated in our study, the ‘Poor responder’ group exhibited increased choroidal thickness and higher CVI, suggesting that this subtype may be categorized as ‘pachychoroid PCV’, where the role of dilated choroidal vessels was more prominent. The primary pathogenic mechanisms in these eyes may be associated with choroidal venous hypertension, rather than being solely mediated by the VEGF process [33]. Therefore, monotherapy with anti-VEGF exhibited limited efficacy, necessitating combination with PDT. In PDT Treatment, free radicals released upon activation of the photosensitizer extensively damaged choroidal vessels, leading to vascular atrophy and alleviation of pachychoroid, thereby improving therapeutic outcomes. This phenomenon may differ from the pathogenic mechanisms observed in non-pachychoroid PCV, where monotherapy with anti-VEGF demonstrated efficacy. Understanding these distinctions is of paramount importance, as it can offer valuable insights for the precise diagnosis and treatment of PCV. In the future, it is possible to differentiate various subtypes of PCV through the combined use of SFCT and CVI.

Our study still had several limitations. While the research data spanned across 15 multicenter institutions, the study primarily focused on Asian populations. Nevertheless, given the higher incidence of PCV among individuals of East Asians, our research retained significant implications. Simultaneously, for the convenience of multicenter, we uniformly employed the 6*6mm OCT/A scanning mode, making it challenging to investigate choroidal changes beyond this range. Additionally, despite regression analyses on anti-VEGF agents were conducted to exclude their potential influence on prognosis, the variation among different anti-VEGF agents could still serve as a confounding factor. Therefore, larger-scale studies focusing on a single type of anti-VEGF agent might be necessary in the future. Nevertheless, our study was currently the sole nationwide prospective multicenter investigation that explored all imaging biomarkers, established a predictive model, and underwent multicenter validation for the response of anti-VEGF monotherapy in PCV, thus holding great clinical significance.

Conclusion

In this prospective multicenter study, thinner SFCT and lower CVI were identified as imaging biomarkers to predict good treatment response, and a nomogram predictive model for the response of anti-VEGF monotherapy in PCV was constructed. The model demonstrated excellent predictive performance in both internal validation and external test sets.
