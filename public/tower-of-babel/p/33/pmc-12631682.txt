Nanomedicine Solutions for Alzheimer’s Disease: A Critical Review of Therapeutic Nanoparticle Strategies
Authors: Teboho E. Tutubala, Ayodeji O. Egunlusi, David Fisher, Admire Dube, Jacques Joubert
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a debilitating disorder marked by progressive memory and cognitive function loss. Current treatments, including acetylcholinesterase and N-methyl-d-aspartate receptor inhibitors, offer symptomatic relief but lack disease-modifying effects. The recent approval of aducanumab, an antibody clearing amyloid beta plaques, brings hope, though its therapeutic benefits are controversial. AD’s etiology is multifactorial, involving over 40 genetic variants, and remains poorly defined. Nanotechnology offers a promising avenue for optimized drug candidates, addressing challenges such as solubility, stability, and blood–brain barrier permeation. This review explores nanoformulations targeting key AD aspects, including amyloid beta, Tau protein, oxidative stress, and neuroinflammation. Notably, multifunctional nanocarriers present a comprehensive approach, demonstrating the potential for effective AD therapy. Despite extensive research, only a small fraction of these studies progress to clinical trials. Continuous nanomedicine research is poised to play a vital role in future AD management, providing innovative solutions to this devastating disease.

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a neurodegenerative disease characterized by deterioration of memory and cognitive function, personality changes, and resulting challenges in daily activities. , The World Alzheimer’s Report 2021 reveals that 55 million elderly individuals predominantly live with dementia and AD, accounting for just 25% of diagnosed cases. However, statistics are less accurate in low- and middle-income countries as 90% of cases are undiagnosed. This suggests a high AD prevalence masked by underreporting. − The financial burden associated with AD significantly impacts government fiscal and household expenditures, as it leads to excessively high medical and caregiving expenses. The global societal burden of AD was estimated at approximately 958 billion US dollars in 2015 and is projected to rise to 1.5 trillion by 2050.

Despite significant effort, AD currently lacks any treatments that can prevent or reverse the death of brain cells, and various treatment strategies slow only the progression of cognitive decline. The United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has approved pharmacological treatments for AD that primarily focus on symptom relief (Figure ) − and include acetylcholinesterase (AChE) inhibitors such as donepezil, galantamine, and rivastigmine, which are available in various forms such as oral solutions, tablets, capsules, and transdermal patches. These drugs inhibit AChE enzymes, increasing acetylcholine (ACh) levels, a key neurotransmitter involved in learning, memory, and cognitive functions. Another drug type approved for AD treatment is memantine, an N-methyl-d-aspartate receptor inhibitor, and it is administered orally as a solution or tablets, particularly in moderate to severe stages of the disease. For moderate to severe AD treatment, a combination of an AChE inhibitor and an N-methyl-d-aspartate (NMDA) inhibitor is available worldwide. In June 2021, the United States FDA approved aducanumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting amyloid-beta plaques. Considerable disputes about aducanumab’s ability to effectively reduce amyloid beta plaques and actual clinical improvement in patients were raised by the European Medicine Agency and Japan Health Ministry, and ongoing clinical trials are expected to continue until 2026. , Additionally, research efforts are directed toward developing multitarget drugs that utilize pharmacodynamic structural scaffolds and bioactive molecules to simultaneously target multiple pathological pathways in AD (Table ).

Potential Pharmacological Targets for the Treatment of AD

This approach involves the formation of hybrids or conjugates, which may include a linker. Several hybrids or conjugates have been explored, and they include: (1) Ladostigil, an inhibitor for monoamine oxidase (MAO) A/B and AChE, which has been shown to treat mild cognitive impairment; (2) T-817MA, a drug molecule targeting a sigma-1 receptor agonist to reduce Aβ induced neurotoxicity and Tau pathology; (3) M30, a MAO A/B inhibitor and an iron chelator that improve neuronal acetylcholine and scavenge free radicals; (4) Curcumin derivatives which target Aβ, Tau, oxidative stress, and inflammation; (5) ANAVEXZ-73, a sigma-1 receptor agonist and muscarine modulator that contributes to the reduction of Aβ and Tau pathologies and improves cellular stress response and synaptic function; (6) SB-742457, a 5-HT6 receptor antagonist, capable of modulating glutaminergic, cholinergic, and dopaminergic neurotransmission, activating a proactive signal; (7) VB-037, a GSK-3 inhibitor, which reduces Tau and Aβ pathologies.

The etiology of AD is not fully understood, but both genetic and environmental factors contribute to its onset, similar to Parkinson’s disease. Alois Alzheimer’s pioneering work in 1907 highlighted the presence of amyloid plaques and neurofibrillary tangles, which are classic indicators of AD pathology. However, the exact mechanism of action for the more than 40 genetic variants associated with AD remains unclear, and sporadic cases of AD cannot be explained. Recent research has identified various potential drug targets for AD, encompassing amyloid beta, Tau proteins, apolipoprotein E, lipids, lipoprotein receptors, neurotransmitter receptors/enzymes, neurogenesis, inflammation, oxidative stress, cell death, proteostasis/proteinopathies, metabolism/bioenergetics, vasculature, growth factors/hormones, synaptic plasticity/neuroprotection, epigenetics, and others. ,−

There are four general hypotheses regarding the pathogenesis of AD, which have led to extensive preclinical therapeutic research. These hypotheses include the Aβ, Tau protein, neuroinflammation, and antioxidant hypotheses. The amyloid cascade hypothesis, proposed almost three decades ago, suggests that the accumulation of Aβ, which forms plaques, triggers events that lead to neuronal and synaptic toxicity in the brain. − However, some disputes exist within the scientific community regarding the correlation between Aβ accumulation and cognitive decline, as well as whether Aβ is the main trigger of neurodegeneration in AD. This emanated from studies that found no correlation between a high-level Aβ deposit and cognitive decline or cognitive stability. Additionally, reports indicate that the administration of Aβ antibodies, such as aducanumab, shows no clinical benefits despite reducing Aβ levels. , Tau phosphorylation dysfunction, on the other hand, is associated with mutations in the microtubule-associated protein Tau gene and is observed in other inherited forms of frontotemporal dementia and Parkinsonism characterized by high Tau aggregation without Aβ deposition. The complex mechanisms induced by oxidative stress result in hyper-phosphorylation of Tau through the release of Ca2+, the activation of cyclin-dependent kinase-5 (CDK-5), the inhibition of calcineurin, and the direct activation of mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathways. , Neurofibrillary tangles, which are considered important indicators in AD development, have led to the hypothesis of therapeutic targeting of Tau proteins therapeutically. Strategies involve targeting Tau deposition, phosphorylation, and immunotherapy, with one approach being the inhibition of glycogen synthase kinase-3 (GSK-3) to control Tau phosphorylation. Chronic neuroinflammation in AD involves the activation of microglia and astrocytes, resulting in the secretion of proinflammatory cytokines and nitric oxide. This leads to increased levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and dysfunctional mitochondria, causing cytotoxic effects on AD neurons. Additionally, neuroinflammation contributes to arterial stiffness, Aβ neurotoxicity, Tau plaques, iron accumulation, and microglial activation. − Due to the brain’s high oxygen consumption and glucose utilization, there is a significant production of ROS. However, only if an imbalance exists between high levels of ROS and antioxidants will it result in oxidative stress. ROS plays a role in normal cell signaling and cellular immunity, but their accumulation disrupts cell functioning, leading to oxidative stress, cellular apoptosis, activation of transcription factors, and protein phosphorylation. , Moreover, abnormal ROS production by plasma membrane oxidases specific to cytokines alters gene expression and contributes to oxidative stress.

Currently, numerous new drugs are undergoing clinical trials at various stages, targeting the different hypotheses of AD through various approaches in an attempt to potentially treat the disease. , Unfortunately, many of these drugs that are unable to modulate these targets fail due to minimal or no change in Aβ burden, worsening cognitive decline, and poor pharmacokinetics. Additionally, the effective delivery of drugs to the central nervous system (CNS) is severely blocked by several barriers, including the blood–brain barrier (BBB), blood–brain tumor barrier, blood–cerebrospinal fluid barrier, and efflux proteins (Figure ). The complexity of the brain’s three biological barriers restricts the entry of the majority of drug molecules. These challenges complicate the effective delivery of potential AD drugs to the brain’s target site. Under normal physiological conditions, components of BBB like microvascular endothelial cells, are known to form tight junctions that are necessary to maintain brain homeostasis and limit the penetration of various neurotherapeutic drugs. In AD, the blood-brain barrier (BBB) exhibits dysregulated receptors, including lipoprotein receptor-related protein 1 (LRP1) and the receptor for advanced glycation end products (RAGE). These receptors normally facilitate amyloid beta (Aβ) clearance from the brain, but their dysfunction permits the entry of bacteria and toxins. ,, The blood–cerebrospinal fluid barrier, formed by ependymal cells in the choroid plexus, acts as a barrier separating the blood from the cerebrospinal fluid and contains an active organic acid transport system that removes several drugs. Efflux transporters, primarily located on the membrane of BBB epithelial cells, play a role in clearing metabolites and catabolites produced in the brain. Various strategies have been attempted to facilitate AD drug penetration across the BBB, including osmotic disruption, ultrasound, intracerebroventricular, intranasal, and nanoparticle drug delivery approaches. , However, despite these efforts, there remain gaps in our understanding and limitations in drug delivery to effectively target AD in the CNS. Further research and innovative approaches are needed to overcome these barriers and enhance the delivery of therapeutic agents to the brain for the treatment of AD and to improve the outcome of AD drugs in clinical trials. In recent years, nanoparticle (NP) drug delivery systems have been regarded as a promising approach due to their ability to interact and deliver drugs across the BBB. ,

Enhancing Therapeutic Strategies for AD through Nanotechnology

The concept of delivering an effective and optimum concentration of drugs into the CNS remains a subject of debate within the scientific community, primarily due to the complexity of brain biochemistry and structural features. Drug-loaded nanoparticles (NPs), also known as nanocarriers, are nanoscale systems composed of organic, inorganic, or biological matrices. These have demonstrated improved drug bioavailability, pharmacokinetics, and distribution. Additionally, these NPs have been designed for targeted and intercellular drug delivery, demonstrating reduced systemic toxicity and side effects. Efficient drug delivery is crucial in addressing CNS diseases as there is minimal control over drug selectivity or specificity toward diseased brain tissues, as well as challenges related to the duration of treatment, the need for high systemic doses, and multiple administrations to achieve therapeutic concentrations. ,, However, functionalized NPs have intrinsic properties, including unique physicochemical composition, size (1–1000 nm), and shape, which influence their interactions with biological systems. These NPs, including liposomes, micelles, dendrimers, and polymeric nanoparticles, have demonstrated considerable advantages over conventional drug delivery methods and are the most widely investigated. , These unique characteristics provide an avenue for combination therapies that can potentially modulate multiple pathways involved in neurodegeneration. In AD studies, polymeric NPs are the most studied, as they offer versatility in the formulation and meet specific requirements that enable BBB permeation, controlled release, and improved stability during systemic circulation. ,

The limited efficacy of conventional AD drugs is primarily due to the multifactorial nature of the disease and the physiological barriers such as the blood-brain barrier (BBB), blood–cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) barrier, and ependymal barrier. Additionally, drug efflux mechanisms, including P-glycoprotein, restrict drug entry into the central nervous system (CNS). In addition to the challenge of drug entry into the brain, drug physicochemical properties also hinder access due to low solubility, short stability, and high molecular weight, possessed by some drugs including AD approved drugs, further reducing bioavailability and impacting the therapy. , A wide range of NP approaches has been studied for mental disorders, including various nanoparticles that enhance BBB penetration such as polymeric, solid lipid NPs, nanostructured lipid carriers, nanoemulsions, extracellular vesicles, iron, yeast cells, layered double hydroxide, nanofibers, sol based, ceria oxide, and ruthenium dioxide among other NPs.

Studies on NP engineering highlight improved pharmacokinetics, high stability in biological environments, and surface-engineered adhesion. These enhancements are achieved through positively charged surfaces conjugated with chemical groups that interact with the negatively charged endothelial cells of the BBB. This results in NP penetration with enhanced drug absorption and bioavailability. , Bioengineering of molecular targets in nanosystems can functionalize NPs for successful BBB penetration through absorption or receptor-mediated transcytosis using peptides such as transferrin, insulin, apolipoprotein, and β-amyloid binding proteins, as well as penetrating vectors such as proteins and antibodies, among others. , Rabies virus (RVG29) and glucose receptor vectors have been shown to conjugate with nanocarriers like exosomes and polyamidoamine (PAMAM) dendrimers, successfully penetrating the BBB. Furthermore, engineered NPs can target intracellular components such as mitochondrial dysfunction and the extracellular space of synaptic sites where NMDA and Aβ plaques are inhibited. , The modified surface charge of NPs and ligands that promote bypassing oral and gut uptake barriers to deliver medicines and prevent phagocytic optimization are examples of the proactive functions of NPs. , Moreover, NPs greatly contribute to drug penetration into the brain. This is due to their greater stability, biocompatibility, biodegradability, and protection against enzymatic degradation. Additionally, they offer increased half-life, controlled and sustained drug release, minimized quantity and frequency of dosage, and improved bioavailability, as observed in estradiol PLGA NPs, resulting in a 10-fold increase in patient compliance. As such, these NPs can overcome physicochemical constraints such as molecular weight, net charge, polarity, solubility, and affinity for hydrophobic or lipid moieties. ,

The review by Cano et al. (2021) illustrates various AD targeting strategies of NPs demonstrating their versatility and enormous benefit in diagnostic methods and drug delivery, including clearance and inhibition of Aβ and Tau plaques. Among drug administration routes to the brain, intranasal delivery has gained popularity in brain therapy due to its noninvasiveness, ease of self-medication, rapid absorption, and capacity to bypass therapeutic metabolism. Concrete evidence from studies shows that NP formulations administered via the intranasal route result in higher drug concentrations in the brain compared to oral 0.34-fold, intravenous 1.11-fold, and intranasal 3.42-fold after one and half hours. , The intranasal route also promotes drug brain bioavailability and is reported to be as fast as the intravenous route while surpassing the oral route significantly and promotes high delivery of poorly soluble drugs. In addition, the nasal mucosa provides a large surface area with rich vasculature which facilitates the penetration of nanoencapsulated drugs into the brain. Several studies have also investigated the modification of ligand peptides for conjugation in nanocarriers, demonstrating increased transport across the BBB and improved neuronal targeting efficiency.

Some noteworthy ligand peptides comprising amino acids have demonstrated significant potential in enhancing drug delivery into the BBB. For instance ten-11 translocation 1 (Tet-1) (HLNILSTLWKYR) has shown to increase neuronal uptake of curcumin-PLGA, while TGNYKALHPHNG (TGN) significantly improved cellular uptake of methoxy-poly­(2-(dimethylamino) ethyl methacrylate)-MPEG-PDMAEMA/DNA compared to unmodified NPs. Angiopep-2 (Ang-2) (TFFYGGSRGKRNNFKTEEY) integration with liposomes loaded salidroside and icariin exhibited an increased drug accumulation within the brain and enhanced uptake in cell models. Another study by Guo et al. (2020) introduced a dual-ligand fusion peptide Tet-1 and TCN or CGN (d-CGNHPHLAKYNGT) called TPL (TGNYKALHPHNG-GGGG-HLNILSTLWKYR) and NAP (NAPVSIPQ) peptides, incorporated into poly­(ethylene glycol)–poly­(lactic acid) (PEG–PLA) NPs. The modified NAP nanocomposite demonstrated a remarkable accumulation within the central neurons of mice, within the minimum dose of 6 g of NAP/kg reduced Tau protein.

To date, different nanomedicines are available in the market for cancer treatment, CNS disorders, diabetes, lung diseases, cardiovascular diseases, and inflammatory disorders.

Nanotechnology Advancements in FDA-Approved Drugs for AD

Several recent studies have explored different nanoencapsulation strategies to improve the delivery of FDA-approved AD drugs such as donepezil, tacrine, rivastigmine, galantamine, and memantine, leading to enhanced therapeutic outcomes (Figure ). In recent years, many FDA-approved NP drugs with clinical applications have been developed. These include Doxil, a PEGylated-liposome loaded with doxorubicin for targeting breast cancer; Abraxane, an albumin-bound Paclitaxel NP targeting various cancers; Onivyde, a liposomal irinotecan formulation targeting pancreatic cancer; Depocyt, a cytarabine liposome targeting lymphomatous meningitis; and Mepact (mifamurtide), targeting nonmetastatic osterosarcoma. Passive targeting NPs for CNS are also reported, such as in the FDA-approved Plegridy formulation, made of PEGylated interferon beta 1a, and in clinical trial dendrimer NPs targeting adrenoleukodystrophy (ALD) therapy. ,

Donepezil, a widely used cholinesterase inhibitor, has been encapsulated in various nanocarriers to enhance brain penetration and sustained drug release. Lipid-based NPs functionalized to target the apolipoprotein E (ApoE) protein have shown increased brain penetration, improved bioavailability, and reduced cytotoxicity. Nanoencapsulation using chitosan, poly­(lactide-co-glycolic acid)-block-poly­(ethylene glycol) (PLGA-b-PEG), nanostructured lipid coated with chitosan, hybrid polymeric lipid, and liposome in situ gel have also been explored to improve the delivery of donepezil. , Tacrine, the first approved cholinesterase inhibitor for AD, exhibited side effects that led to its withdrawal from the market. However, nanoencapsulation strategies have shown promise in improving its delivery and reducing adverse effects. Encapsulation in chitosan and PLGA NPs administered intranasally has demonstrated sustained release and improved drug efficiency. , Optimization of drug delivery using poloxamer and liposomes has also been explored. The encapsulation of 7-methytacrine (7-MEOTA) in poly­(ε-caprolactone) (PCL) nanocapsules was reported to result in a modified release form of 7-MEOTA as evidenced by a release percentage of 90% and 95% at 24 and 72 h, respectively, from a dialysis assay. Using a mathematical model proposed by Korsmeyer and Peppas, values of 3.86 min-1 and 0.25 were obtained for the release constant and the release exponent, respectively, indicating that diffusion controlled the release process. Rivastigmine, the first intranasal formulation for AD treatment, has been extensively studied using nanoencapsulation approaches such as nanostructured lipid NPs, nasal gels, nanoemulsions, PLGA–PEG, and polysorbate-coated mesoporous silica NPs, with enhanced pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of NP encapsulated drug. − Intranasal administration of lipid loaded with rivastigmine was found to improve pharmacokinetic parameters including nasal bioavailability (F = 118.65) when compared to the oral route (F = 4.39) and ex-vivo nasal permeation of nanoformulation (356 g/cm2) when compared to the pure drug (146 g/cm2). However, these nanoformulations exhibit a low drug deposition of 10%, which could lead to poor bioavailability and reduced therapeutic effect. Additionally, the need for multiple doses of rivastigmine may increase the risk of peripheral nervous system side effects, such as diarrhea, indigestion, and stomach. Intravenous rivastigmine-loaded PEG–PLGA NPs improved drug release kinetics by nearly 65%, and encapsulated rivastigmine reached a concentration of 1058 ng/mL with a circulation time of 17.7 h as compared to 421 ng/mL in 5.70 h of the free rivastigmine. Both polysorbate-80-coated and uncoated mesoporous silica NPs loaded with rivastigmine have been shown to enhance brain parenchyma drug uptake clearance by 10.71 and 3.42 times, respectively, as compared to the free drug in a rat model. Galantamine, when loaded in chitosan and administered intranasally, demonstrated enhanced spatial memory, cholinergic transmission, and improved drug efficacy when compared to an oral route in mice and rat models. , Nanomicelles employing Quality by Design (QbD) principles enhanced optimal drug delivery of encapsulated galantamine with a 4.31-fold increase in brain concentration after oral administration and a 5.40-fold increase in maximum brain drug levels after 8 h when compared to the free drug. Moreover, considerable elevation of drug concentration in the brain improved drug efficacy and biochemical parameters in brain homogenate, decreasing A plaques and promoting cognitive functions. Another study compared three routes of galantamine drug administration (intravenous, oral, and nasal) and galantamine-bound chitosan NP administered via the nasal route of a rat model at a dose of 3 mg/kg. The maximum brain concentration was found to be 89 ng/mL intravenous, 59 ng/mL oral, 26 ng/mL intranasal, and 74 ng/mL intranasal with NPs. LS-MS was used to detect and measure brain uptake of galantamine found in plasma, CSF, and brain homogenate, and a high recovery percentage was observed in the brain homogenate sample. Higher concentrations of galantamine chitosan NPs were also observed in both brain homogenate and CSF compared to the plasma concentration. Memantine, another FDA-approved drug for AD, has been encapsulated using various nanocarriers to enhance its bioavailability by 7.87 times compared to free drugs and improve its pharmacokinetic parameters. Encapsulation of memantine in PAMAM NPs and its conjugation with lactoferrin result in a 10-fold increase in memantine brain bioavailability compared to pure memantine drug. This enhancement significantly improved locomotive activity and memory in a rat behavioral study. , Nanoprecipitation with ultrasonication and homogenization methods using PLGA NPs with encapsulated memantine have been compared through different administration routes, including oral, intravenous, and intranasal, with the intranasal route significantly inhibiting the effect of scopolamine and restoring cognitive function. The developed memantine NP administered intranasally was shown to exhibit enhanced penetration, maintaining concentration and sustaining a release pattern as observed in gamma radiolabeled images of memantine. Additionally, nanofibers made of poly­(vinyl alcohol) (PVA) or polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP), encapsulating donepezil, memantine, and curcumin, have been made. These nanofibers were shown to increase the expression of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (13.5-fold), glial fibrillary acidic protein (115-fold), TUBB3 (8.9-fold), Nestin (166-fold), Neurog2 (5.6-fold), and NeuroD1 (5.8-fold) when compared to pure nanofibers. The encapsulation efficiency was recorded at 80%, and they were shown to decrease levels of AChE, Aβ aggregation, Tau protein, APP, tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and GSK-3 in rat models after encapsulated nanofibers were administered sublingually.

Nanoparticles for the Modulation of the Amyloid-Beta Protein

The Aβ hypothesis strongly suggests that blocking Aβ aggregation could have therapeutic benefits for AD, as the presence of Aβ in the brain is linked to the progression from mild cognitive impairment to AD. Recent studies have demonstrated that engineered NPs with unique features of large surface-to-volume ratio interact with Aβ plaque formation through hydrophobic binding and specific ligands, and the inorganic NPs show good inhibition of Aβ fibrillation either as a bare formulation or with conjugated drugs. The most synthesized NPs for Aβ targets are based on polymers, inorganic materials, and lipids (Figure ). Nano-Encapsulated Drug Formulations Targeting Amyloid Beta

Several studies have also focused on the use of the FDA-approved biodegradable polymer PLGA to deliver conjugated or encapsulated AD drugs like donepezil, memantine, curcumin, quercetin, and galantamine, and each NP has demonstrated successful neuroprotective effects in animal models. ,− Additional studies on PLGA have demonstrated its capability to suppress Aβ1–42 aggregation in a temperature-dependent manner (27–40 °C), unlike other polymer NPs. Furthermore, it has been suggested that the inhibitory effect of PLGA against Aβ aggregation is due to the chirality of PLGA and the binding of Aβ oligomers to PLGA via π–π and hydrophobic interactions, as evidenced in its ability to attenuate memory deficits in a 5xFAD mouse AD model. In this study, the special interaction between PLGA NPs and Aβ isoforms and the neuroprotective effects of PLGA NPs were demonstrated in both the in vitro and in vivo experiments. Encapsulation of naturally occurring flavonoid chrysin using chitosan NPs successfully delivered the drug across the BBB in a zebrafish model. The study demonstrated how chrysin-chitosan NPs mitigate the effects of injected Aβ1–42 by maintaining synaptic integrity and reducing ROS production. A polyphenolic xanthone compound, alpha-mangostin (α-M), has been extensively reviewed for its multifunctional approach to combating AD. Encapsulation of α-M has been shown to enhance the ratio between brain cells and blood by 79%, as well as between microglial cells and the brain by 68%. This encapsulation also exhibits 2.3-fold higher brain distribution, demonstrating promising effects in clearing Aβ peptides and reducing the low-density lipoprotein receptor responsible for neuroinflammation. ,

Several studies have recently produced some inorganic NPs incorporated into different drug compounds. These include TiO2 NP, silver (Ag) NP, , nickel (Ni) NP, metal-phenolic network of zinc II-EGCG coated with gold (Au) NP, γ-FE2O3–Au core–shell magneto-plasmonic NP, and fulvic acid coated iron oxide NP, which inhibit Aβ aggregation in rats. Inorganic NPs have attracted significant interest in drug delivery primarily due to their smaller size, which allows efficient BBB penetration and targeted delivery to the site of interest. However, these particles are often nonbiodegradable and may exhibit toxic effects, albeit the conjugation of these inorganic NPs with drugs and other molecules has proven to be successful in drug delivery. A facile coating method was used in the conjugation of α-casein with Au NPs, and the in vitro results reveal the enhanced binding capacity of α-casein@AuNP to Aβ, leading to an increase in mitigation of Aβ toxicity, ROS, and behavioral and neurological dysfunction biomarkers in a zebrafish larvae model. In a different study conducted on an AD model of C-elegans, α-casein incorporated in liposomes significantly inhibited Aβ aggregation, when compared to α-casein alone. The potency study of levodopa conjugated on Au nanoroses (fiber-like morphology) was reported by Kour et al. (2022) where both amyloid inhibitory and disintegration tendencies of levodopa-Au nanoroses were demonstrated on amyloid animal models. Functionalized Au-levodopa NP successfully inhibits and disaggregates Aβ1–40 and Aβ1–42 fibrils in a Thioflavin T (ThT) assay, rescues SH-SY5Y cells from Aβ-induced cytotoxicity, and enhances permeation as a permeability assessment of 45.64% was observed on a porcine brain, which suggests passive diffusion of NPs through the BBB. A dynamic construction of a metal-phenolic network made of Zn­(II) ion was used to coat the Au conjugated epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) nanocomposite (MPN@AuNP-EGCG) which demonstrated inhibitory ability against Aβ1–42 aggregation and toxicity in cell model studies. The study further revealed the atomic coordination of Zn­(II)-EGCG network inhibitory effects of Aβ fibrillations by sequestering peptides within the mesoporous network mechanism of antiamyloidosis. It also revealed peptide binding by EGCG on a discrete molecular dynamics simulation. Another recent study by Jomepour et al. (2023) demonstrated the antiamyloid and anti-inflammatory effect of fulvic acid coated with an iron oxide NP. This NP was shown to reduce and eradicate amyloid aggregation in an in vitro model, with no toxicity experienced in cell models.

In targeting Aβ aggregation, lipid NPs have also been investigated in preclinical studies, and they have been efficient in transporting drugs across the BBB to reach specific targets. ,− A different study reported the synthesis of two forms of lipid NPs (solid lipid and liquid lipid) used in encapsulating flavonoid myricetin. A preclinical study showed an improved memory by ameliorating Aβ1–42 induced rats and ensuring nanostructured lipid carriers (NLC) NPs sustain the drug release. The lipids-myricetin showed a 2.77-fold increase in plasma and drug targeting efficiency and displayed enhanced brain uptake of (127.05%) compared to pure myricetin. These myricetin-lipid carrier NPs were also found to decrease Aβ levels, reduce BuChE and AChE activities in the brain and serum, and promote neurogenesis activity. A recent study conducted by Andrade et al. (2023) involved the loading of caffeic acid into liposome NPs, which was further modified to include transferrin. In vitro studies of novel NPs showed significant disaggregation of 15% mature Aβ fibrils and delayed aggregation of Aβ42 monomers. In the treatment of neurological diseases, intranasal drug administration has played a significant role, and utilizing NPs as carriers to target the brain through the olfactory region is well described in existing literature.

There are three drug candidates administered intranasally that target Aβ and are currently in clinical trials: (1) insulin, (2) a novel rivastigmine nasal spray (Morgan and Soh, 2017; ANZCTR), and (3) APH-1105 (NCT03806478) reported on some chitosan-coated and uncoated solid lipid and PLGA NPs that were loaded with insulin and delivered intranasally. In this study, a high diffusion of insulin was observed in the brain cell line model used, and chitosan effectiveness in mucoadhesion was demonstrated. Rivastigmine-loaded lipid-based NPs designed as in situ hydrogels had an extra barrier that prevented drug diffusion compared to nanoemulsion or NLC via nasopharynx. These rivastigmine loaded NPs were shown to exhibit improved stability and prolonged drug release properties. In situ, hydrogels displayed prolonged drug release and 8% high drug deposition when compared to 4% in loaded nanoemulsion and NLC NPs. APH-1105 is in clinical trials as a secretase modulator that has been shown to be successful in gene editing in in vivo preclinical studies. It uses a CRISPR-Cas9 system made of lipid nanocarriers to reduce transthyretin misfolding and treat transthyretin amyloidosis. , Another successful liposome nanocarrier used for mRNA-based therapy is ALN-TTR02, approved for the treatment of amyloid dysfunction. ,

Nanoencapsulated Drug Formulations Targeting Neuroinflammation

The formulation and characterization of PEGylated PLGA-encapsulated EGCG/AA (epigallocatechin gallate/ascorbic acid) NPs have demonstrated significant efficacy and a 5-fold increase in concentration when compared to free EGCG/AA. These NPs were shown to inhibit Aβ plaques, increase synaptophysin expression, and reduce neuroinflammation. Moreover, these nanocarriers, with sizes less than 100 nm, have shown the ability to penetrate the BBB through diffusion. In vitro BBB studies have revealed the concentration-dependent passage of EGCG/AA NPs loaded with rhodamine, and an in vivo study that assessed its distribution and neuroprotective activity suggests 52% and 60% Aβ deposit reduction in the hippocampus and frontal cortex, respectively, of the APPswe/PS 1dE9 (APP/PS1) AD mice model. This suggests their potential for targeted delivery and therapeutic effects on the brain. Another noteworthy approach involves the utilization of a polymer to encapsulate carnosic acid in sulfobutyl ether-β-cyclodextrin-based NPs, and these NPs demonstrated pro and anti-inflammatory therapeutic effects, through inhibition of Aβ aggregation, in an APP/PS1 mice model. This strategy holds promise for modulating neuroinflammation and addressing the pathological processes associated with AD. In a separate study, polysorbate 80 was employed to coat extracted yeast cells loaded with sategliptin, resulting in enhanced anti-inflammatory effects linked to a 10.90-fold increase in plasma drug concentration when compared to the normal drug, sitagliptin, when administered in a nanocarrier to Bagg Albino-c (BALB/c) mice.

Most lipid NPs have successfully been evaluated in clinical practice for cancer therapy, infections, and neurological diseases. A potent anti-inflammatory and antioxidant drug, sinomenine, was encapsulated in a 4-PAMAM dendrimer to afford a prolonged release formulation that slowly releases its contents, as evidenced by a drug release profile of 90% within 2 weeks when subjected to diffusion by the dialysis bag method. This formulation was able to successfully attenuate lipopolysaccharide (LPS) induced inflammation by inhibiting nuclear factor κB (NF-κB), suppressing cytokines and reducing oxidation stress in marine macrophages. Nanovaccine lipid NPs (LNP-R/As) were fabricated by loading rapamycin and an anti-A antibody, which was shown to induce specific Treg cells and reverse cognitive dysfunction. Furthermore, the study demonstrated in vivo efficient drug delivery of rapamycin through intradermal and IV administration, and LNP-R/As may suppress the hyperphosphorylation of Tau and cause secretion of anti-inflammatory cytokines in 5xFAD mice. Additionally, α-lipoic acid encapsulated in chitosan NPs and SLNs attenuated neurotoxicity induced by aluminum chloride (AlCl3) and showed a drug release profile of 94% within 18 h, with the chitosan NP, when subjected to diffusion in a dialysis bag method. The neuroprotective effects of this NP were attributed to its anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antiapoptotic activities.

Certain inorganic NPs have exhibited potential in targeting neuroinflammation and providing a promising therapeutic approach for AD. For instance, biogenic silver (Ag) NPs were synthesized from natural honeyberry and were shown to mitigate LPS-induced inflammation in HMC3 cells. These AgNPs not only exhibited a reduction in microglia (M) M1 phenotype (class type) biomarker CNS damage but also increased M2 phenotype biomarkers’ neuroinflammatory control, thereby alleviating ROS generation. Moreover, the intranasal administration of AgNPs has been found to activate microglia. This activation of microglia is of significant interest in the context of neuroinflammation and its potential therapeutic implications for AD. Additionally, another noteworthy development involves iron oxide NPs (IONPs) that are incorporated with human Wharton’s jelly derived mesenchymal stem cells (MSC-IONPs). These MSC-IONPs have shown remarkable retention in the brain and have been observed to promote the release of neurotrophic factors and anti-inflammatory cytokines. Notably, they have also exhibited a substantial ability to reduce Aβ plaques in the brain of 5xFAD mice and improve cognitive functions.

Extracellular vesicles loaded with long intergenic noncoding RNA-cyclooxygenase-2 (LincRNA-Cox2 siRNA) have demonstrated their potential to attenuate microglial proliferation induced by LPS. These stem cell nanoconjugates have been engineered for targeted delivery and controlled drug release, effectively modulating microglial activities. To develop nanocarriers that can address multiple targets in microglial activity, researchers have screened molecules targeting enzymes involved in neuroinflammatory response. One such nanocarrier is apolipoprotein E3 reconstituted high-density lipoprotein (ApoE3-rHDL) with 94.6% brain accumulation, which binds to Aβ plaques, leading to their clearance and a reduction in neuron damage and microglial activity in SAMP8 and SAMR1 mice models. Another multitarget NP has been fabricated using layered double hydroxide, loaded with rutin, and BACE1 siRNA or GSK3β siRNA. Additionally, the NP has surface targeting delivery modules Ang-2 and RVG29 peptides to facilitate BBB penetration. In vivo studies have shown a high concentration of this nanocarrier in the brain, resulting in the downregulation of target genes, inhibition of Aβ deposits, reduced hyperphosphorylation of Tau, alleviation of neuroinflammation, and restoration of cognitive function.

Limitations and Biosafety Measure Nanomedicine in AD

There are potential risks associated with long-term exposure to NPs, which require further critical investigation in preclinical and clinical studies. The requirements set by the FDA and other health organizations have established standardized protocols that are continuously reviewed, particularly concerning the toxicity profiles of NPs and associated biomarkers. , Despite the materials used to fabricate NPs being considered biodegradable, there are significant variations in physicochemical properties such as size, shape, and surface chemistry, which impact their interactions with different biological constituents, posing serious safety concerns. As such, the following concerns necessitate long-term studies to further validate the use of these NP carriers in addressing CNS disorders: (1) for polymeric NPs, there are risks of inflammatory response and cytotoxicity associated with long-term exposure, accumulation in tissues, and degradation of functionalization moieties; (2) lipid-based NPs undergoing lipid oxidation, which could trigger immune response and oxidative stress in certain biological environments; (3) inorganic NPs are primarily made from materials such as gold, silver, and silica, and they present significant risks of nonbiodegradability and potential toxicity due to prolonged exposure in the body. Although numerous studies have evaluated biosafety concerning cytotoxicity, oxidation stress, and inflammatory responses following acute exposure to viable cells, the long-term effects remain a concern; (4) biological conjugated NPs offer a natural and biocompatible platform that is less likely to provoke an immune response and can be engineered to target specific cell receptors. However, concerns in clinical applications include scalability, storage, stability, and the potential for horizontal gene transfer; (5) although hybrid NPs provide multiple benefits in drug delivery and diagnostic platforms, these can introduce new risk factors that need to be monitored for unpredictable interactions. It is suggested that a safety profile should be developed based on monitoring the excretion and metabolism, and the biodistribution of hybrid NPs should be considered. Despite numerous studies on the safety profiles of various NPs, the majority have been conducted under conditions of acute toxicity or short-time exposure. Long-term effects still need to be investigated, particularly in chronic conditions like AD.

Conclusions

AD remains one of the most challenging neurodegenerative disorders, particularly due to the increasing elderly population. Its complex and multifactorial nature has hindered the development of effective drugs targeting a single pathway to halt its progression. As a result, researchers have redirected their focus toward investigating multipurpose drugs obtained through repurposing, re-engineering, and chemical modification. However, the current drug formulations lack the efficacy to effectively address the diverse mechanisms and properties to effectively overcome the physiological barriers involved in neurological diseases, including the formidable BBB. Promising strides have been made in synthesizing NPs for brain therapy across various diseases, but further efforts are essential to optimize NPs combined with highly effective drugs for targeting and reversing the multifactorial AD pathogenesis, which include AChE, amyloid beta, Tau phosphorylation, neuroinflammation, and antioxidant related conditions. Continuous research and development in nanomedicine is poised to play a vital role in the future management of AD, presenting innovative and effective solutions to combat this devastating disease. By harnessing the properties of NPs, researchers aim to develop targeted and efficient drug delivery systems capable of crossing the BBB, penetrating the affected brain regions, and exerting therapeutic effects. Although several NP formulations have been explored, only a handful have made it to clinical trials. As such, there is a need for more extensive in vivo studies, encompassing nonhuman primate models and clinical trials, to validate these formulations. Moreover, exploring alternative routes of administration, such as the intranasal route, presents an avenue for further NP investigation. The design simplicity of an effective NP is crucial as it could facilitate scalability and promote batch manufacturing under good manufacturing practice (GMP) conditions suitable for clinical trials.
