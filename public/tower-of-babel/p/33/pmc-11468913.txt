Self-healable and stretchable perovskite-elastomer gas-solid triboelectric nanogenerator for gesture recognition and gripper sensing
Authors: Feng Jiang, Gurunathan Thangavel, Jin Pyo Lee, Adit Gupta, Yufei Zhang, Jian Yu, Tomoyuki Yokota, Kento Yamagishi, Yihui Zhang, Takao Someya, Pooi See Lee
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Gas-solid triboelectric nanogenerators (GS-TENGs) based on porous elastomers provide a promising avenue for the design of self-powered sensors. However, the intrinsic limitation of low electric output in GS-TENGs could affect the accuracy and sensitivity of sensing systems. Here, we develop a porous composite by integrating an adhesive poly(siloxane-diphenylglyoxime-urethane) (PSDU) elastomer with ferroelectric (3,3-difluorocyclobutylammonium)2CuCl4 [(DF-CBA)2CuCl4] fillers. PSDU, an intrinsically triboelectric negative material with alternating hard-soft segments and supramolecular bonds, imparts excellent compressibility, adhesion, and self-healing properties to the composite. Simultaneously, the incorporation of (DF-CBA)2CuCl4 as functional fillers leverages the formation of a hydrogen bonding network to enhance charge transfer processes. These fillers facilitate charge accumulation through an electric poling process, resulting in a power output improvement that surpasses 1400 times higher than that of a dense PSDU-based GS-TENG. Tapping onto the versatile properties of porous poly(siloxane-diphenylglyoxime-urethane)-perovskite (PSDU-PK) GS-TENGs, applications such as hand gesture/food recognition and dual-mode sensing system have been demonstrated, suggesting their promising potential in wearable electronics and smart agriculture.

INTRODUCTION

Triboelectric nanogenerators (TENGs) convert low-frequency mechanical energy into electricity, offering a sustainable and self-powered technique for advanced smart sensing applications (1–8). Nevertheless, conventional solid-solid and solid-liquid TENGs necessitate the complete contact and separation of two distinct materials with an essential physical gap, normally with the aid of additional spacers or hydrophobic materials. This requirement imposes notable constraints on their application scenarios. Furthermore, their functionality is hindered by factors such as ambient environmental conditions, effective contact area, surface morphology, and wear resistance of the materials, thereby placing restrictions on the stability and precision of triboelectricity-driven sensors.

In addressing these challenges, gas-solid TENGs (GS-TENGs) using porous elastomers were reported, allowing a more stable and straightforward gap-free operation for self-powered sensors (9, 10). Functional polyurethanes, serving as promising elastomer candidates, have excellent self-healing, adhesive, and stretching abilities through diverse supramolecular interactions and also provide an ideal platform for designing closed porous structures through customized synthesis routes (11–14). As the gas is well encapsulated within the voids, the output performance of GS-TENGs becomes less susceptible to the ambient environment and surface conditions of the solids. Moreover, the device durability can be also markedly enhanced, as a result of the low friction coefficient between air and elastomeric solids. Consequently, the design of a stable GS-TENG with customizable pore size and structure is imperative to achieve robust and precise sensing functions. We envisage that GS-TENG could deliver smart sensors with outstanding mechanical deformability, stretchability, self-healing capability, and self-powering features.

Despite the stable outputs and excellent durability, the electric performance of GS-TENGs is still constrained by the relatively low friction generated from the pores. A promising strategy to enhance electric outputs involves designing a triboelectric material with a high charge storage capacity (15). To achieve this objective, the incorporation of functional fillers into a polymer matrix becomes essential (16). This not only enhances output performance through dielectric or charge trapping improvement but also ensures the mechanical flexibility of the composite. Among various functional fillers, hybrid organic-inorganic perovskites distinguish themselves for their tunable energy levels and bandgaps, efficient charge trapping capabilities, exceptional dielectric properties, and ferroelectric polarization (17). These attributes position halide perovskites as attractive additives that enhance both the charge-trapping capacity and static surface potential of elastomers. However, the introduction of perovskites as fillers may also introduce more complex interfacial interactions (e.g., hydrogen bonding and ferroelectric dipoles). The critical concerns are whether these interfacial interactions affect the charge transport performance of GS-TENGs and how they cross-couple with gas-solid interactions for electrical enhancement.

In this work, a stretchable and self-healing composite, consisting of porous [poly(siloxane-diphenylglyoxime-urethane) (PSDU)] elastomers and ferroelectric {(3,3-difluorocyclobutylammonium)2CuCl4 [(DF-CBA)2CuCl4]} halide perovskites, has been prepared (Fig. 1A). PSDU-PKs have excellent stretchability (~2100%), compressibility (~80%), adhesion (~1.35-MPa adhesive strength), and self-healing properties, owing to the alternative hard and soft segments in the polymer backbone as well as diverse hydrogen bonding interactions (NH···O═C, NH···O─C═O, NH···Cl, and NH···F interactions; Fig. 1B). The incorporated (DF-CBA)2CuCl4 perovskites further enhance the charge trapping capacity through the dielectric enhancement and ferroelectric polarization (18, 19), resulting in heightened charge accumulation of the elastomer. Consequently, PSDU-perovskite (PSDU-PK) composite demonstrates substantially improved electrical outputs (23.2 V, 232.0 nA cm−2, and 1.9 W m−2) after an electric poling process, with the power density exceeding 10-fold that of pure porous PSDU (5.1 V, 44.4 nA cm−2, and 141.6 mW m−2) and over 1400-fold greater than that of dense PSDU-based TENG (0.4 V, 4.6 nA cm−2, and 1.3 mW m−2). Capitalizing on these remarkable advantages, the PSDU-PK–based GS-TENGs can be used as multifunctional sensors for hand gesture recognition, machine learning–enabled food identification, and dual-mode sensing systems. The excellent sensitivity and recognition accuracy of these GS-TENG–based sensors display their huge potential in human-machine interface and smart agriculture.

RESULTS

Synthesis and characterization of porous PSDU-PKs

The synthesis route of PSDU is illustrated in figs. S1 to S3, showing the preparation of poly(dimethylsiloxane)-based segmented urea-siloxanes prepolymer (PDMS-SUSP) and diphenylglyoxime-terminated polyurethane prepolymer (DPG-PUP). The design of PDMS-SUSP was based on precipitation polymerization of bis(3-aminopropyl)-terminated poly(dimethylsiloxane) (NH2-PDMS-NH2) and isophorone diisocyanate (IPDI), while DPG-PUP was obtained through a two-step polycondensation process by mixing IPDI, polytetrahydrofuran glycol (PTMG), DPG, and triethanolamine (TEA). The molar ratio of NH2-PDMS-NH2 to IPDI was optimized using a ratio of 2:1.6 to impart the elastomer with both excellent stretchability and robust mechanical strength (fig. S4A). In the preparation of DPG-PUP, the precursors PTMG and IPDI serve as the soft and hard segments, while DPG function as the chain extender. This design inhibits hard segment crystallization due to the adjacent oxime groups and steric hindrance of phenyl groups (20, 21). In addition, the incorporation of TEA group in the polymer backbone further improves the flexibility of the elastomer and generates more covalent cross-linked networks. A reaction time of at least 7 hours was optimized to ensure complete polycondensation and the formation of a permanent covalent urethane network in DPG-PUP (fig. S4B). Combining both advantages of PDMS-SUSP and DPG-PUP, the resulting PSDU demonstrates multiple molecular interactions, such as reversible carbamate-oxime covalent (─NH─CO─O···N═C) bonds and dipole-dipole and intramolecular hydrogen (─C═O···H─N─) bonds between diisocyanate (─NCO) and amino (─NH2) groups (figs. S5 and S6). Further analysis via nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy, encompassing 1H NMR, 13C NMR, and 29Si NMR, substantiates the successful synthesis of PSDU, evidenced by the presence of characteristic peaks corresponding to PDMS-SUSP, IPDI, PTMG, DPG, and TEA segments (figs. S7 to S13).

The chemical structure and synthesis process of PSDU are also confirmed by Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) measurements (figs. S14 and S15). Notably, characteristic hydrogen bonding peaks were identified at 3318 to 3325 cm−1 (hydrogen-bonded N─H stretching) and 1718 cm−1 (hydrogen bonded ─C═O stretching) (fig. S15). Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and dynamic mechanical analysis (DMA) were also conducted to characterize the physical properties of elastomers. TGA results (fig. S16) reveal three distinct degradation temperature ranges (280° to 320°C, 330° to 380°C, and 390° to 450°C) for both PSDU and PSDU-PKs. The first temperature region is attributed to the decomposition of labile carbamate-oxime groups, while the subsequent regions correspond to chain scission in different polyols. In DMA tests (fig. S17), PSDU-PKs exhibit two distinct glass transition temperatures (Tg, −20.2° and −70.1°C), contrasting with the broader temperature range (−16.2° to 18.5°C) observed for PSDU. The lower Tg of PSDU-PKs indicates the strong intermolecular hydrogen bonding between PSDU and (DF-CBA)2CuCl4. To assess the potential impact of voids on the inherent chemical structure of PSDU-PKs, small-angle x-ray scattering (SAXS) was performed on PDMS-SUSP, dense PSDU, and porous PSDU-PKs. As shown in fig. S18, dense PSDU and porous PSDU-PKs display similar peak features corresponding to the crystallized hard segment domains, indicating that porous PSDU-PKs maintain a similar ratio of hard segments as dense PSDU. Compared to PDMS-SUSP (q = 1.26 to 1.32 Å), both PSDU and PSDU-PKs show larger scattering vectors (q = 1.36 to 1.42 Å), suggesting that their polymer chains are more closely packed. This increased chain packing is attributed to enhanced hydrogen bonding interactions and reduced microphase separation induced by the diphenylglyoxime and urethane groups.

The schematic structure of (DF-CBA)2CuCl4 is shown in fig. S19, which features an octahedral CuCl6 inorganic framework intercalated by two layers of organic cations DF-CBA+. Figure S20 illustrates the morphology of the (DF-CBA)2CuCl4. Optical microscopy images reveal the flake-shaped and crystalline structures, while scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images display the distinct layered structures of perovskites, confirming their two-dimensional characteristics. As per the Aizu notion (22), (DF-CBA)2CuCl4 aligns with a 4/mmmFm-type phase transition, where the symmetry shift results in the reduction of symmetric elements from 16 to 2. This alteration gives way to eight crystallographically equivalent polarization directions (18). In contrast to most uniaxial ferroelectrics, the multiaxial ferroelectricity in (DF-CBA)2CuCl4 allows for more accessible polarization modulation across multiple directions, thereby substantially enhancing its charge accumulation through the electric poling–induced polarization (19). The ferroelectric property of (DF-CBA)2CuCl4 has been established through polarization electric field (P-E) hysteresis loop measurements, revealing a saturated polarization (Ps) of approximately 0.3 μC cm−2 and a low coercive field of around 12 kV cm−1 (fig. S21). Figure 1C shows the optical microscopic images and SEM images of dense PSDU, porous PSDU, and porous PSDU-PKs (perovskite content: 30 wt %) composites, respectively. These images reveal that the voids in porous PSDU and PSDU-PKs are encapsulated within the elastomer, suggesting effective confinement of air within these enclosed spaces. In addition, the perovskites demonstrate a uniform dispersion throughout the composite, effectively shielded by the PSDU from ambient moisture, which ensures exceptional stability and resilience for the entire composite system.

Hydrogen bonding interaction between PSDU and perovskites

We incorporated (DF-CBA)2CuCl4 as functional fillers into the PSDU, enabling diverse hydrogen bonding interactions with the carbamate and urea groups within the polymer matrix. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations indicate that PSDU can engage in hydrogen bond interactions, forming connections with both the organic ligands and the inorganic framework of perovskites through N─H···F and N─H···Cl bonds (Fig. 2A). Notably, the binding energies (EHB) of the interfacial N─H···F (−10.66 kcal mol−1) and N─H···Cl hydrogen bonds (−9.76 kcal mol−1) surpass that of the intramolecular N─H···O hydrogen bonds (−5.89 kcal mol−1), which fall within the typical region of hydrogen bonding energies (1 to 12 kcal mol−1). The hydrogen bonding between perovskite and elastomer leads to the formation of interfacial dipole layers and charge redistribution at the interface. This interaction modulates the electronic structure of both materials, including the energy levels of their respective highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) (23). The presence of hydrogen bonding reduces the HOMO-LUMO gap in the composite (from 5.79 to 4.39 eV; fig S22). This reduction occurs due to the stabilization of molecular orbitals at the interface, which leads to a more favorable energy match between the HOMO of the perovskite and the LUMO of the elastomer (24). The diminished HOMO-LUMO gap heightens the likelihood of charge transfer between the two materials. When the composite experiences mechanical deformations, such as bending or compression, these interfacial charge transfer processes are further enhanced. As a result, more electrons are transferred between the perovskite and elastomer, leading to higher electrical outputs. These findings highlight that perovskite-induced interfacial hydrogen bonds not only enhance the mechanical properties of the elastomer but also play a pivotal role in augmenting charge transfer capabilities.

Furthermore, we also performed x-ray diffraction (XRD), FTIR, and x-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) experiments to verify the hydrogen bonding interaction between PSDU and (DF-CBA)2CuCl4. As plotted in Fig. 2B, the XRD patterns are highly consistent with the simulated single-crystal results (18, 19), affirming the purity and lattice structure of (DF-CBA)2CuCl4. The perovskites also display excellent stability, and their lattice structure can be maintained in an ambient environment (25°C, 40% relative humidity) for 6 months without any degradation (fig. S23). The pure PSDU shows two characteristic broad peaks at 12.3° and 22.2°, indicating its amorphous behavior. The PSDU-PKs exhibit an overlapping feature of both amorphous peaks from PSDU and lattice characteristics from perovskites. From FTIR results (Fig. 2C), the stretching vibration peaks of hydrogen-bonded N─H peaks of PSDU-PKs (3318 cm−1) become broader and shift to lower wave numbers with respect to those from perovskites (3334 cm−1), suggesting the formation of hydrogen bonds between perovskites and PSDU (25, 26). These hydrogen bonding interactions were further verified by XPS measurements (Fig. 2, D and E). Compared to pure perovskites, all the peaks of PSDU-PKs in the Cl2p and F1s spectrum shift toward low binding energy regions with a decrease in peak intensity. This phenomenon can be ascribed to the diminished strength of Cu─Cl and C─F bonds and the redistribution of electron density because of hydrogen bonding interaction (27, 28), consistent with both DFT calculations and FTIR results.

Mechanical properties of porous PSDU-PKs

PSDU-PKs exhibit remarkable adhesion and can be readily attached to various substrates such as stainless steel, curved glass, and plastic films (Fig. 3A). As depicted in Fig. 3B, the maximum adhesion strength of PSDU-PKs on stainless steel can reach 1.35 MPa [standard American Society for Testing and Materials (ASTM) D1002 method], surpassing pure PSDU by approximately 60% (0.86 MPa) and conventional polyurethane (PU) by twice as much (~0.6 MPa) (9). This robust and enduring adhesive strength stems from the oximes and hydroxyl groups, along with a cohesive hydrogen bonding network between perovskites and polymers, which provides inherent strength in bonding across diverse materials. The adhesive performance of PSDU-PKs was further evaluated by bonding two stainless steel plates using a mixed solution of diverse PSDU monomers and perovskites (shown in the lower right image of Fig. 3A). The in situ polymerization process (80°C, 3 hours) fostered more bonding interactions, resulting in a notably enhanced adhesion strength between the metal plates. This augmented adhesion exhibits exceptional strength, supporting substantial weights ranging from 1 to 5 kg (fig. S24 and movie S1).

The creep and recovery tests of PSDU-PKs under different applied pressures and temperatures were also conducted, as shown in Fig. 3C and fig. S25. At room temperature (25°C), the creep strain demonstrates an increase from 15 to 140% as the applied pressure rises from 0.1 to 1.6 MPa (Fig. 3C). Upon stress release, immediate elastic recovery with a notable strain reduction occurs across all creep-recovery curves. When subjected to an applied pressure of 0.2 MPa, the strain exhibits a rise from 16 to 128% as the temperature escalated from −50° to 90°C (fig. S25). Subsequent stress release also prompts rapid strain reduction back to the initial elastic response, affirming the excellent elastomeric behavior of PSDU-PKs across a broad range of pressures and temperatures.

Owing to the stronger hydrogen bonding interactions facilitated by halide perovskites, PSDU-PKs demonstrate superior stretchability (~2100%) and increased tensile strength (7.32 MPa) compared to the pure PSDU elastomer (~1600%, 6.91 MPa), as illustrated in Fig. 3D. The reinforcement of the elastomer’s hard segment (cyclohexyl-carbamate units) through these hydrogen bonds also enhances the toughness and tear resistance of PSDU-PKs, which can retard the tearing process and promote the dissipation of elastic energy within the notched region (sample width: 10 mm, notched size: 2.5 mm) during stretching (29). As a result, PSDU-PKs maintain a high tensile strain (~1500%) even after experiencing cutting damage (Fig. 3E). Cyclic loading-unloading tests of uniaxial tension were also performed to evaluate the mechanical recoverability of PSDU-PKs. Figure 3F depicts the small residual strain (5 to 10%) observed throughout continuous tensile tests spanning 100 to 400% strain. The porous structures remained intact even after a 400% tensile strain (fig. S26). The same PSDU-PKs underwent additional stretching to 1000% strain, allowing a relaxation period of 2 hours between two consecutive cyclic tests (fig. S27). The tensile stress-strain curve shows minimal residual strain, low hysteresis ratios with tiny change, as well as notable recovery even under such a high tensile strain, indicating the remarkable energy dissipation capacity and elasticity of PSDU-PKs.

PSDU-PKs also exhibit outstanding self-healing performance (72% tensile strain recovery at 80°C for 3 hours) and demonstrate superior tensile stress (2.58 MPa) and strain (~1540%) compared to pure PSDU (1.06 MPa, ~920%) under identical healing conditions (Fig. 3, D and G, and fig. S28). This remarkable self-healing ability primarily originates from three key factors: (i) Diverse supramolecular interactions (Fig. 2A and fig. S6), including urethane-urea-oxime ether bonds (NH···O═C, NH···O─C═O, and NH···O─N═C) and hydrogen bonds between perovskites and PSDU (NH···Cl and NH···F), empower the composite with excellent adhesion and chain reentanglement. These dynamic supramolecular interactions on the fractured surfaces enables reversible dissociation and association reactions. (ii) The incorporation of TEA group in the polymer backbone generates more covalent cross-linked networks, which effectively disperse the concentrated stress and facilitate the rearrangement and reentanglement of polymer chains after damage. (iii) The highly dynamic cyclohexyl and phenyl groups promote more efficient reconstruction of diverse supramolecular interactions.

Apart from the excellent adhesion, stretchability, and self-healing ability, PSDU-PKs also display remarkable compressibility. As shown in Fig. 3H, the porous PSDU-PKs exhibit exceptional resilience, smoothly recovering their original state with minimal residual strain despite enduring continuous compressive strains ranging from 32 to 80%. In addition, fatigue cyclic compression tests, comprising 20 consecutive loading-unloading cycles at a strain amplitude of 80% (figs. S29 and S30), demonstrate that PSDU-PKs can fully recover with a high degree of curve coincidence. The closed voids remain undamaged and nonsealed under this high compressive deformation (fig. S31), indicating their outstanding compressive stability and recoverability. For rheological properties, both PSDU-PKs and PSDU demonstrate comparable storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) with parallel changing trends (Fig. 3I). The dominance of G′ over G″ in both materials signifies their exceptional elastic properties (30). Notably, at lower frequencies, both G′ and G″ of PSDU-PKs surpass those of pure PSDU, affirming that PSDU-PKs have a higher elastic modulus and lower energy dissipation. In addition, viscosity curves for PSDU-PKs and PSDU are also obtained with typical shear-thinning characteristics (fig. S32). Particularly noteworthy is the consistently higher viscosity of PSDU-PKs across all shear rates compared to PSDU, contributing to the composite’s robust adhesion strength. These findings align well with the superior mechanical strength, elastic recovery performance, and adhesion strength exhibited by PSDU-PKs.

Working mechanism of porous PSDU-PK GS-TENGs

On the basis of a typical porous PSDU-PK elastomer (perovskite content: 30 wt %, thickness: 2 mm, void size: 0.44 mm), a double-electrode GS-TENG was constructed according to the design depicted in Fig. 4A. The cross-sectional optical microscopic image of the device was illustrated in fig. S33. Al foils were adopted as both top and bottom electrodes, while an additional PSDU dense layer (~0.2 mm) was further coated on the surface to protect the device from potential contamination and ambient moisture. Within the composite system, the hydrogen bonding interaction and ferroelectric polarization play substantial roles in an efficient charge transfer and trapping process (31, 32). As illustrated in Fig. 4A (a), perovskites are randomly distributed in the system when there is no electric poling, so the output performance is mainly contributed by gas-elastomer interactions between air and the composite. During the cyclic compression process, the sealed voids within the composite undergo marked deformation, resulting in instantaneous pressure changes within these enclosed spaces and enabling intense friction between the air and elastomer. In this case, electrons would transfer from the air to the elastomer via the triboelectric effect, causing an instantaneous asymmetric distribution of the positive charges in air and the negative charges in elastomer. Upon releasing the compression, the composite reverts to its original state, prompting the redistribution of mobile positive charges to equalize the potential difference within the void. This charge redistribution process generates instantaneous electric signals through the electrostatic effect, highlighting the operational mechanism of GS-TENG.

Once the electric poling is applied to the composite, the perovskite dipoles would be aligned along the electric field direction [Fig. 4A (b)]. This alignment not only contributes to the surface charge accumulation on the elastomer but also facilitates charge separation and redistribution within the voids, leading to a substantial enhancement in the electric outputs (33, 34). To confirm the impact of electric poling process on the output performance of the device, both simulation analysis and experimental measurements were performed as shown in Fig. 4 (B, E, and F). As illustrated in the above equation and simulation results in Fig. 4B (d), porous PSDU-PKs after poling generate the largest potential, affirming the crucial contributions of perovskites and electric poling process toward the electric enhancement. Figure S34 records the evolution of internal electrostatic potential during the compression process of porous PSDU-PKs. We observed that during the gradual application of external force, charges progressively accumulated at the bottoms of the closed voids and perovskites. When the force was removed, the charges gradually returned to their initial state. Besides, the electrical tests consistently align with the simulation outcomes. As illustrated in Fig. 4 (E and F) and figs. S35 and S36, the dense PSDU-based TENG exhibited minimal outputs of 0.4 V, 4.6 nA cm−2, 1.3 mW m−2, and 3.8 nC, primarily resulting from charge transfer induced by contact deformation during compression. Conversely, the porous PSDU elastomer displays higher electric outputs of 5.1 V, 44.4 nA cm−2, 141.6 mW m−2, and 20.6 nC. This stark contrast confirms the efficacy of gas-solid interactions in power generation.

To further verify that the electric performance is influenced by gas-solid interaction rather than external contacts, we conducted experiments with different substrates [acrylic, polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE), polyethylene, and wood] of varying thickness attached to the top and bottom sides of the PSDU for electrical measurements. As depicted in fig. S37, all devices exhibit similar electric performance irrespective of the substrate type, verifying that gas-solid interactions govern the charge transfer process, while external contacts have minimal impact on the output performance of the devices. The high output performance of porous PSDU stems from the highly dynamic nature of cyclohexyl and phenyl groups present within the material. These groups facilitate the reconstruction of oxime-urethane units, thereby enhancing the electron affinity of PSDU and endowing it with a notably negative triboelectric property. Consequently, PSDU achieves a substantial static surface potential of −1.23 kV, surpassing commonly used triboelectric negative materials like PTFE (−0.53 kV) and PVDF (−0.36 kV) (Fig. 4C).

When ferroelectric perovskites are combined with PSDU to form a PSDU-PK composite, the resulting electric outputs would increase to 10.8 V, 124.0 nA cm−2, and 55.2 nC. These outputs can be further heightened to 23.2 V, 232.0 nA cm−2, and 74.4 nC after electric poling process (Fig. 4, E and F, and figs. S36 and S38), surpassing most state-of-the-art GS-TENGs (table S1). Notably, the output power density (1.9 W m−2) of poled porous PSDU-PK composite was more than 10 times higher than the pure porous PSDU elastomer (141.6 mW m−2) and 1400 times higher than the dense PSDU elastomer (1.3 mW m−2). This exceptional electric performance is attributed to three pivotal factors. First, (DF-CBA)2CuCl4 perovskites exhibit superior dielectric properties compared to PSDU. Consequently, the capacitance changes within PSDU-PKs exceed that of pure PSDU during compression (fig. S39). This is also supported by the enhanced static surface potential of PSDU-PKs (−2.15 kV) than that of PSDU (−1.23 kV). Second, the unique multiaxial ferroelectricity of perovskites enables their dipoles to align along the electric poling field. This alignment not only improves electric outputs by fostering gas-solid interactions and polarization-induced charge accumulation but also guides charge distribution within voids (38). Surface potential (−2.99 kV) and capacitance change measurements (Fig. 4, C and D, and fig. S39) also validate the substantial enhancement in triboelectric negativity after electric poling. Last, the hydrogen bonding networks formed between perovskites and PSDU also promote the charge transfer and trapping process. These diverse supramolecular interactions contribute to the electric enhancements, corroborated by both DFT calculations (fig. S22) and experimental results (Fig. 4, C to F).

Electric performance of porous PSDU-PK GS-TENGs

To achieve an optimal output performance of porous PSDU-PK GS-TENGs, various parameters, such as perovskite content, void size, composite thickness, compressive strain, and frequency, were further optimized. As illustrated in Fig. 5 (A to C), both electric outputs and transferred charge exhibit an increase with the rise in perovskite content from 0 to 30 wt %. This elevation is attributed to the uniform distribution and entire encapsulation of perovskite flakes within the PSDU elastomer, as illustrated in the SEM images in fig. S40B. This configuration leads to the strong charge-trapping capability of perovskites and the efficient charge transfer network facilitated by hydrogen bonding. However, a decline in output performance is observed as the perovskite content surpasses 40 wt %. This phenomenon arises from the agglomeration of large perovskite flakes, which cover the surface of the composite at high content levels (>50 wt %) (figs. S40C and S41). Consequently, the exposed perovskites may accelerate surface charge dissipation, resulting in diminished electric outputs. This observation also corroborates findings from previous studies (39). Our experimental results also rule out the impact of solid-solid electrification. If perovskite-elastomer interactions dominated the electrification process, we would expect the electrical output to increase with higher concentrations of exposed perovskites (above 30 wt %) due to the increased contact area and more effective friction between the perovskites and elastomer. The opposite experimental trend confirms that gas-solid interactions play a more crucial role in the output performance.

Apart from the perovskite content, the void size also stands as a critical parameter influencing the gas-solid interaction and electric performance of the GS-TENGs. To explore its impact on the electric outputs of the devices, porous PSDU-PKs with varying void sizes ranging from 0.16 to 1.66 mm were prepared (fig. S42). As displayed in Fig. 5 (D to F), a notable rise in output voltage, current density, and transferred charge is observed with an increase in void size from 0.16 to 0.44 mm. However, a subsequent decline in performance emerged as the void size expanded to 1.66 mm. The findings underscore the critical importance of an optimal void size for achieving peak electric performance. Smaller voids in GS-TENGs present challenges in effective compression due to limited deformation, while excessively large voids lead to a reduction in PSDU-PK density (9). This decrease restricts the elastomer presence between voids, affecting the charge trapping capacity and reducing efficient interaction area during deformation.

Furthermore, the influences of composite thickness, compressive strain, and frequency on the electric outputs of porous PSDU-PK GS-TENGs were also explored, as illustrated in figs. S43 to S45. The triboelectric performance demonstrates a steady increase with the growth in PSDU-PK thickness from 0.5 to 2 mm, followed by a slight decline upon further increasing the thickness to 2.5 mm (fig. S43). A thinner PSDU-PK elastomer with fewer voids deforms less during compression, whereas a thicker composite undergoes enhanced deformation owing to increased voids. This increased deformation facilitates more gas-elastomer interactions, potentially improving electron transfer and, consequently, improving output performance. However, if the composite exceeds an optimal thickness, then the increased distance between the electrode and voids’ internal surfaces would hinder electrostatic induction and diminish electric outputs. This underscores the significance of maintaining an appropriate thickness to achieve optimal electric performance.

In view of the remarkable compressive recoverability of PSDU-PKs (Fig. 3H), electrical measurements of GS-TENGs were also conducted under different compressive strains (fig. S44). The results reveal an increase in electric outputs as the compressive strain increased from 10 to 50% (from 2.0 V, 19.2 nA cm−2, and 12.3 nC to 14.8 V, 176.2 nA cm−2, and 55.3 nC), followed by a gradual rise as the strain reached 60% (15.1 V, 181.5 nA cm−2, and 57.2 nC). This phenomenon is reasonable in that the increased compression strengthens the interaction between air and PSDU-PKs, thereby promoting power generation. Once a sufficiently high compressive strain is achieved (e.g., 40%), the gas-elastomer interaction reaches saturation, diminishing the subsequent impact of strain increase on electric performance. Figure S45 also shows the influence of compressive frequency on power generation. The results display that higher electric outputs are achievable at lower frequencies (3 to 5 Hz), suggesting that this frequency range facilitates an optimal gas-solid interaction.

Given the exceptional self-healing ability and elasticity of PSDU-PKs, we conducted electric measurements upon the device’s recovery from both mechanical damage and severe deformations. As depicted in Fig. 3G, PSDU-PKs exhibit impressive self-healing ability after cutting (80°C, 3 hours), with no discernible impact on electric outputs (Fig. 5G). In addition, the output performance of GS-TENGs was also tested under varied stretching conditions (Fig. 5H). The results reveal a gradual decline in output voltages as tensile strain increased, which is attributed to the diminished void volume and composite thickness. Nonetheless, the composite showcases excellent mechanical recovery upon release, maintaining its original output performance. This resilience underscores its immense potential for applications in extreme conditions, emphasizing both its remarkable recoverability and adaptability. The composite further displays remarkable recyclability, allowing PSDU-PKs to be reused for device preparation through recasting or thermal pressing methods, as demonstrated in figs. S46 and S47. While the output voltage experiences a slight degradation (from 11 to 9 V) owing to the diminished hydrogen bonding between perovskites and PSDU that weakens their charge transfer and trapping (fig. S48), it remains higher than that of the pure PSDU (5 V).

Furthermore, the tensile strain of the composite remains above 2000% even after undergoing two consecutive recycling processes (fig. S49). This is because the strong hydrogen bonding network within the PSDU-PKs helps restore the internal cross-linked framework, endowing it with exceptional recycling capability. The composite also exhibits excellent hydrophobicity with a water contact angle of 122.8° (fig. S50), which imparts exceptional humidity resistance, maintaining consistent electrical performance across a relative humidity range of 50 to 99% (fig. S51). This is attributed to the effective protection of the perovskites by the PSDU elastomer, as supported by the SEM image of PSDU-PKs (30 wt %) in fig. S40B.

To evaluate the electric stability of the porous PSDU-PK GS-TENGs, long-term durability tests were also conducted as shown in Fig. 5I and fig. S52. The findings reveal that the electric outputs remain consistently stable even after enduring 50,000 compressive cycles (at 30% compressive strain), confirming their viability for applications in long-lasting wearables and self-powered sensors. Compared to solid-solid, liquid-solid, and liquid-liquid TENGs, GS-TENGs offer notable advantages in terms of electrical stability, mechanical performance, operational convenience, durability, and material selection, as illustrated in fig. S53.

GS-TENG for gesture recognition, food identification, and dual-mode sensing

Considering the remarkable electrical performance and stability of the porous PSDU-PK GS-TENGs, a smart glove was engineered, integrating customized electrode circuits and GS-TENG devices. This glove serves as a self-powered sensing array tailored for recognizing diverse hand gestures. As depicted in Fig. 6A, the bottom Ag electrode circuit was fabricated using a screen printing method, enabling customized designs to meet wearable and stretchable needs. Positioned at the finger locations of the glove were five porous PSDU-PK GS-TENGs (effective area: 2.5 cm by 0.5 cm) functioning as the sensing unit. For the top electrode, Al foil was selected due to its flexibility and cost-effectiveness. The GS-TENGs were designed to cover the first two knuckles of the fingers, ensuring notable deformation for accurate sensing. To validate the practicality of this smart glove, 12 distinct hand gestures were used, as shown in Fig. 6A. The testing results (Fig. 6C and movie S2) reveal that different sensors would undergo various deformations (such as bending and touching) while executing diverse hand gestures. These deformations led to instantaneous pressure changes within the composite material, causing marked gas-solid interaction and electron transfer via the triboelectric and electrostatic effect. Consequently, the electrical outputs generated by the sensing array effectively discern these gestures, providing clear and reliable gesture recognition. To further verify the electrical stability of the smart sensors under sweating conditions, the output voltage was tested before and after 15- and 30-min running sessions. As shown in fig. S54, the voltage signals remained almost unchanged during the index finger bending process, confirming that sweating does not affect the electrical performance of the PSDU-PK GS-TENG devices.

Figure 6B displays another application of porous PSDU-PK GS-TENGs in machine learning–assisted food identification. In this design, six GS-TENG devices were affixed to the inner surface of pneumatically controlled soft grippers. As these grippers grasped objects, the GS-TENG sensors made contact with the food surface, undergoing deformations and generating triboelectric signals. These signals, influenced by the food’s shape, surface texture, and softness, serve as indicators of distinct food features. Leveraging a convolutional neural network (CNN) model (fig. S55 and table S2), our sensing system successfully identifies 10 types of food (Fig. 6B). To ensure data reliability, we collected 100 sets of electrical signals from each food, randomly assigning 70% as the training set and the remaining 30% as the testing set. A representative set of output signals is displayed in Fig. 6D. After the training and optimization process, the model achieves an accuracy of 99.2% (Fig. 6E), which enables a real-time food prediction and classification with exceptional precision (movie S3), highlighting the potential of our multichannel sensing system in food recognition, smart agriculture, and intelligent production lines.

The PSDU-PK composite also demonstrates reversible thermochromic behaviors attributed to variations in the Cu─Cl bond angle and distance within the perovskite structure at different temperatures (19). This intriguing characteristic inspires the design of a dual-mode sensing system capable of detecting heat and pressure changes, respectively. As an illustrative example, six PSDU-PK composites (1 cm by 1 cm) and one PSDU-PK GS-TENG (effective area: 2 cm by 2 cm) were affixed to a glass surface, serving as heat and pressure sensors, respectively (Fig. 6F). The color of the composite would change from light green to brown once the hot water is poured into the glass. Upon cooling to room temperature, the color reverts to light green, showing its excellent thermal recoverability. The electric performance of porous PSDU-PK GS-TENGs (effective area: 1 cm × 1 cm) was also conducted to estimate their sensing performance under different temperatures. As depicted in Fig. 6G, the output voltage remains stable below 75°C; however, higher temperatures led to diminished performance due to the thermionic emission effect (18, 40). The electric outputs were further examined under various pressures at room temperature. As revealed in Fig. 6H, the electric outputs demonstrate a gradual increase corresponding to the incremental compressive force ranging from 5 to 40 kPa. These results enable the establishment of a good linear correlation between the output voltage and applied pressure (Fig. 6I), further affirming the exceptional sensing capabilities of PSDU-PK GS-TENGs (sensitivity: 0.029 V kPa−1) within the dual-mode sensing system (see table S3 for the performance comparison with the state-of-the-art pressure sensors).

DISCUSSION

In summary, we have developed a stretchable and porous PSDU-PK composite by combining an adhesive PSDU matrix with ferroelectric (DF-CBA)2CuCl4 fillers for GS-TENG fabrication. It displays outstanding stretchability (2100%), compressibility (80%), adhesion (1.35-MPa adhesive strength), and self-healing properties. Moreover, the incorporation of ferroelectric (DF-CBA)2CuCl4 serves to reinforce internal hydrogen bonding interactions, thereby improving charge transfer and trapping processes while also facilitating charge accumulation through an electric poling process. This results in a remarkable 1400-fold electric enhancement compared to a dense PSDU-based device, reaching 23.2 V, 232.0 nA cm−2, and 74.4 nC, which exceeds most state-of-the-art GS-TENGs. It consistently maintains stable output performance even after enduring cutting damage, extreme stretching, and prolonged compressive tests up to 50,000 cycles. In view of these advantages, diverse porous PSDU-PK GS-TENGs have been devised, showcasing applications in hand gesture recognition, machine learning–enabled food identification, and dual-mode sensing of both heat and pressure. The devices presented here introduce a promising design strategy for harvesting energy from gas-solid interactions, offering a route to resilient and self-powered sensing systems.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials

NH2-PDMS-NH2 (Mn = ~5000, Gelest DMS-A21) was purchased from Gelest Inc. Copper(II) chloride (CuCl2), PTMG (Mn = ~ 1000), TEA (99%), the catalyst dibutyltin dilaurate (DBTDL; 95%), dimethyl sulfoxide (anhydrous, ≥99.9%), methanol (anhydrous, 99.8%), acetone (ACS reagent, ≥99.5%), DPG (97%), acetonitrile (anhydrous, 99.8%), and N,N′-dimethylformamide (DMF; anhydrous, 99.8%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Pte Ltd. IPDI (98%) and N,N′-dimethylacetamide (DMAc; anhydrous, 99.8%) were purchased from Alfa Aesar. (3,3-Difluorocyclobutylammonium)Cl ([DF-CBA]Cl) was purchased from ChemScene. Deuterated solvents for NMR analysis were purchased from Cambridge Isotope Laboratories Inc. PTMG and TEA were dried in vacuum at 80°C for 24 hours before use. The other chemicals were used as received.

Synthesis of PDMS-SUSP

SUSP was synthesized via a one-step precipitation polymerization, and the synthesis details were illustrated in fig. S1. Briefly, the polymerization reaction was conducted at ambient conditions without the use of stabilizers or surfactants. In a 1000-ml glass bottle, H2N-PDMS-NH2 (6 g) and IPDI (14 g) were separately dissolved in acetone (70 ml). The H2N-PDMS-NH2 solution was slowly added to the IPDI solution under vigorous agitation (600 rpm), followed by the addition of deionized water (acetone/water = 70/30, weight ratio). Subsequently, the polymerization proceeded for 3 hours at room temperature. Upon completion of the polymerization, the PDMS-SUSP particles were obtained by centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 20 min. After that, the particles were washed three times with acetonitrile and dried at 80°C under vacuum for 12 hours. The PDMS-SUSP particles then stirred with DMAc (20 ml) at 85°C for 12 hours, serving as the precursors for porous PSDU and PSDU-PK synthesis.

(DF-CBA)2CuCl4 synthesis and characterization

The synthesis procedure of (DF-CBA)2CuCl4 is similar to the previous method (18, 19). Initially, 16 mmol of [DF-CBA]Cl (powder, 99%) and 8 mmol of CuCl2 (powder, 99%) were dissolved in 30 ml of methanol (anhydrous, 99.8%). After a drying process at 50°C for 24 hours, green powder of (DF-CBA)2CuCl4 was obtained, which has been verified by XRD measurements (Bruker D8 Discover). The ferroelectric performance of perovskites was measured by commercial ferroelectric equipment (Precision LC, Radiant technologies).

Synthesis of porous PSDU-PKs

The synthesis route of the porous PDPU-PKs was demonstrated in fig. S3. Initially, the NCO-terminated IPDI-PTMG was prepared by mixing IPDI (4.439 g, 20 mmol) and PTMG-1000 (20 g, 10 mmol) in a 2:1 molar ratio (85°C, 2 hours, and N2 atmosphere). Then, 0.18 g of DBTDL catalyst was added into the mixture and stirred for additional 6 hours at 85°C. The weight fraction of NCO (wt % NCO) in the prepolymer was validated following ASTM D2572 standards. Subsequently, a predetermined quantity of TEA solution (0.184 g, 4 mmol in 5 mL DMAc) was added to the IPDI-PTMG mixture and stirred for 2 hours under room temperature. Next, the OH-terminated DPG (2.088 g, 9 mmol) was mixed with IPDI-PTMG-TEA precursor at 70°C for 4 hours to obtain DPG-PUP. Then, DPG-PUP was incorporated into the PDMS-SUSP solution with a molar ratio of 3:7 at 60°C for 2 hours to yield a cross-linked PSDU network. The resulting PSDU was then diluted with DMAc for subsequent characterization. To prepare the porous PSDU-PK composite, a forced drying process was adopted (9). Briefly, the prepared perovskite particles were added to the PSDU solution, and the composite was thoroughly mixed under vigorous stirring at 40°C for 1 hour to ensure the uniform distribution of perovskites. Then, the composite precursor was placed in a customized PTFE mold (3 cm × 3 cm with different thickness) for 20 min under ambient temperature. After that, the composite was placed in a hot plate for polymerization at 75°C for 4 hours under different air pressure conditions (pressure range: 2 to 6 mbar, relative humidity: <10%), followed by curing at 90°C for another 4 hours in the oven. Throughout the polymerization process, the gas pressure was meticulously adjusted within a mini glove box to achieve uniform void size and distribution. This control is crucially linked to the solidification process and the interaction between PSDU and perovskites, which can facilitate the condensation process and self-organization of various hydrogen bonds. Last, porous PSDU-PKs with different void size and distribution can be successfully synthesized, as illustrated in fig. S40.

Characterization of porous PSDU and PSDU-PKs

The chemical structure of porous PSDU and PSDU-PKs were confirmed by 1H NMR (400 MHz, Bruker DPX 400), 13C NMR (400 MHz, Bruker DPX 400), 29Si NMR (400 MHz, JEOL ECA400), and FTIR (FTIR-ATR, PerkinElmer, Frontier) spectrometers and XRD (XRD, Bruker D8 Discover). 1H NMR and 13C NMR analyses were conducted at ambient temperature, with tetramethylsilane serving as the internal reference and deuterated solvents serving as internal lock references. SAXS measurements were carried out using a SWAXS Xenocs Nano-inXider system (λ = 0.154 nm), and the corresponding data were collected using Fit2D software. TGA was also performed through the commercial equipment of TGA (Q500). Laboratory shear strength adhesion tests were conducted following the ASTM D1002 standards, using an MTS Criterion Model 43 test system (MTS Systems Corporation). The rheological characteristics of PSDU and PSDU-PKs were evaluated using a Physica MCR 501 rheometer (Anton Paar, USA). All assessments were performed at 25°C, using 1-Hz oscillation with an 8-mm probe (PP08-150, USA), sustained at a gap of 0.2 mm. Both PSDU and PSDU-PK composite were cut into rectangular shapes with a dimension of 20 mm by 10 mm by 2 mm for tensile stress-strain tests, following the ASTM D638-10 standards. The compressive measurements were conducted using an Instron 5567 testing system. The field-emission SEM (JEOL JSM-6340F) and a microscope (Nikon ECLIPSE TS100) were used to characterize the morphology and void structure of dense PSDU, porous PSDU, and PSDU-PKs. Finite element analysis simulation service was provided by Shenzhen Weihaosheng Technology Co. Ltd. The contact angle of PSDU-PKs was measured by an optical contact angle measurement system (DataPhysics OCA 15 Pro).

Electrical measurements

The output voltage, current, and transferred charge were measured by an oscilloscope (Tektronix MDO3024, impedance = 100 megohm), a low-noise current preamplifier (Stanford Research System, model SR570, impedance = 4 ohm), and the Keithley 6517B system. The applied pressure was recorded by a force transducer (SINOCERA CLYD-331). The corona poling method, combined with preheating and gradient poling processes, was used to achieve effective ferroelectric polarization. The porous PSDU-PK GS-TENG was preheated to 70°C to reduce internal defects and coercive field. A stainless-steel needle (diameter: 0.1 mm) was positioned 1 mm above the device as the top electrode for corona poling. An electric field of 25 kV cm−1 was initially applied and gradually increased to 100 kV cm−1 in 25 kV cm−1 increments, with each step maintained for 15 min. Last, a high electric field of 100 kV/cm was sustained for 2 hours to ensure effective polarization. A multifunctional stretching stage (Smart Memories, Singapore) was used to test the electric performance of the composite under different tensile strains. Surface potential measurements were performed using an electrostatic voltmeter (Trek, model 542A). All the electric tests were conducted in the ambient environment. The hand gesture recognition was realized by mounting the GS-TENG devices on the finger positions of the smart glove. The bottom Ag electrode pattern was fabricated using the screen printing technique. The electrode ink consisted of silver flakes (diameter: 10 μm) and PSDU solution (100 mg ml−1, dissolved in DMF solvent) in a 2:1 weight ratio.

DFT calculations

The geometric optimization of PSDU’s fragments and PSDU-PK system was done by R2SCAN-3C functional using ORCA 5.0.4 with the convergence criteria of 5.0 × 10−6 Ha energy, 1.0 × 10−4 Å displacement, and 2.0 × 10−3 Ha Å−1 gradients. After the optimization, the atoms-in-molecules analysis was conducted to calculate the individual hydrogen bond strength by using the electron density at the (3, −1) bond critical points of the PSDU-PSDU system and PSDU-PK provided by the Multiwfn software. The molecular orbitals, calculated during the optimization, were plotted using the Iboview software.

Machine learning algorithm

In this study, a CNN model is used for food identification purposes. Deep and sparse three-layer CNNs are implemented to effectively capture the variability inherent in small-scale datasets. Each convolutional layer within the three-layer CNN comprises a convolutional layer, a batch normalization layer, and an activation layer using hyperbolic tangent (Tanh). Through the utilization of stride convolutions with three convolution filters (1 by 3), the dimensions of the inputs are progressively reduced from 9997 by 6 to 64 by 1 across the layers. To address the challenge of parameter reduction in small-scale datasets, a global averaging pooling layer is used at the conclusion of the network in place of fully connected layers.
