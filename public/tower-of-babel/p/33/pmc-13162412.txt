Microbial decomposer diversity and metabolic function during the decomposition of brine shrimp carcasses in a saline lake
Authors: Lei Gao, Bao-Zhu Fang, Jian Yang, Zheng-Han Lian, Ying Chen, Osama Abdalla Abdelshafy Mohamad, Qing-Yu Xu, Yong-Hong Liu, Dildar Wu, Yang Yuan, Rashidin Abdugheni, Meng-Meng Li, Pandeng Wang, Maite Ortúzar, Xin-Yao Li, Jian-Rong Huang, Lan Liu, Hong-Chen Jiang, Wensheng Shu, Brian P Hedlund, Wen-Jun Li, Jian-Yu Jiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDecomposition of brine shrimp carcasses has a crucial role in carbon cycling of saline lakes, yet the microbial dynamics remain poorly understood.ResultsHere we integrated metagenomics, metatranscriptomics, culturomics, metabolomics, and microcosm experiments to investigate microbial community succession and function during brine shrimp (Artemia sp.) carcass decomposition in Barkol Lake, a hypersaline lake in China. A total of 149 metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) and 77 pure culture genomes were recovered across 33 phyla, with 72.12% genomes representing species-level novel lineages. Our results reveal diverse bacterial and archaeal taxa, including novel lineages from CG03, T1Sed10-126 and rare archaeal taxa (Asgardarchaeota, Thermoplasmatota, Nanoarchaeota, and Halobacteriota), involved in degradation of biomacromolecules—proteins, carbohydrates, lipids, and nucleic acids—via extracellular hydrolysis, nutrient transport, and intracellular catabolism. These taxa exhibit substrate preferences, rapidly responding to the breakdown of polysaccharides and proteins, followed by lipids and nucleic acids. Hydrolyzed oligomers are further oxidized by various microbes through fermentation, sulfate reduction, and methanogenesis via metabolic handoffs. Additionally, viral auxiliary metabolic genes (AMGs) further enhance microbial host functions, contributing to key ecological processes such as carbon cycling and stress response. A temporally structured microbial decomposer network (MDN) was observed, driving mineralization cascades from fermentation to sulfate reduction and methanogenesis.ConclusionsThis study reveals microbial metabolic handoffs and virus-mediated modulation as critical mechanisms for organic matter turnover, expanding the known diversity and function of decomposers in saline ecosystems. Our findings offer new insights into biogeochemical processes in saline lakes and highlight a synergistic microbial decomposer network involving bacteria, archaea, and viruses that collectively drive nutrient cycling during brine shrimp carcass decomposition. Introduction

Decomposition of animal carcasses is a fundamental microbial process with significant implications for global carbon cycling [1, 2]. Previous decomposition studies have focused primarily on terrestrial mammals or marine fish [2–5], yet the dynamics of microbial decomposer in extreme environments like saline lakes are poorly understood. Saline lakes, though often overlooked, are climate-sensitive emitters of CO2 comparable to national fossil fuel outputs, and must be integrated into global carbon cycle assessments and mitigation strategies [6–9]. Saline lakes, covering nearly one-fifth of Earth’s lake area, are high-salinity ecosystems [10] that contribute substantially to global carbon fluxes, emitting 0.11–0.15 Gt of CO2 annually [11]. This carbon release is largely driven by microbial mineralization of organic matter, derived from sources such as terrestrially dissolved organic matter (tDOM) [12], autochthonous microbial carbon fixation [13, 14], and zooplankton [15].

Among the zooplankton, brine shrimp (Artemia sp.) carcasses serve as a major carbon reservoir, supplying proteins, lipids, polysaccharides, and nucleic acids [16–23]. Brine shrimp are widely distributed zooplankton in saline lakes [24]. While laboratory studies have highlighted the roles of endogenous microbes, oxygen limitation, and mineral-organic interactions in brine shrimp decay [25, 26], these experiments largely overlooked the contributions of environmental microbial decomposers and the complex metabolic interactions. Notably, saline lakes harbor diverse candidate lineages and rare archaeal lineages, including members of the DPANN superphylum and rare taxa such as Pacearchaeota, Nanohaloarchaea, and Woesearchaeota [27–30], yet their ecological roles in organic matter decomposition remain scarcely explored. Viruses, as shown in deep-sea ecosystems where their decomposition fuels carbon and nutrient cycling [31], are abundant and active in hypersaline systems and increasingly recognized as modulators of host metabolism, drivers of cell lysis, and mediators of horizontal gene transfer [32, 33]. However, microbial decomposers involved in brine shrimp carcass decomposition, particularly the roles of candidate phyla, rare archaea, and viruses, remain poorly characterized in saline lakes. Addressing these gaps is essential to fully understand carcass decomposition processes in hypersaline ecosystems. Barkol Lake, located in northwestern China, sustains exceptionally high brine shrimp populations, with hundreds of tons of biomass harvested annually [34–36]. This unique setting provides an ideal natural system to investigate how complex microbial communities orchestrate the decomposition of animal biomass in hypersaline environments.

Here, we integrate metagenomics, metatranscriptomics, metabolomics, culturomics, and controlled microcosm experiments to track microbial succession during brine shrimp carcass decomposition in Barkol Lake. This multi-omics framework enables us to identify the core bacterial and archaeal decomposers, uncover the roles of rare archaeal lineages, and disentangle the ecological contributions of viruses. By doing so, we provide a comprehensive view of the microbial processes driving organic carbon mineralization in saline lakes, thereby filling critical gaps in our understanding of organic matter turnover in extreme but globally relevant ecosystems.

Materials and methods

Field sampling

Barkol Lake, a high-salinity lake located in Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region, China, is a saline lake with a water salinity of 65.89 g/L and a total salt content of 206.80 g/kg in the sediment (Table S1) [14, 37]. The sediment samples in this study were collected using aseptic sampling bags from Barkol Lake (approximately 0–20 cm below surface sediment, 92°48′11″E, 43°40′05″N) on July 29, 2021, when the maximum air temperature was 28 °C (Fig. S1). Sediment samples were kept at 4 °C for further laboratory microcosms. Subsequently, Barkol Lake water was collected into two 10 L polycarbonate bottles. All bottles were rinsed twice with 6 M HCl, followed by three rinses with Milli-Q water, sterilized, and dried at 60 °C before use to remove any contaminants that could interfere with the experiments as thoroughly as possible [38]. The water samples were also stored at 4 °C until further processing.

Microcosm experiments of brine shrimp carcasses

A laboratory microcosm experiment was conducted using brine shrimp carcasses and eggshells as the sole organic substrates to study microbial succession and catabolism during decomposition. For basal saltwater medium preparation, microbe-free saline lake water (approximately 1.6 L) was initially acquired through filtering in 0.22 μm Whatman Nucleopore membrane filters and dispensed into a 2-L borosilicate glass media bottle, which had been rinsed twice in 6 M HCl, washed three times in Milli-Q water, and dried at 60 °C. Then, the medium was autoclaved at 121 °C for 20 min to ensure sterility, and was allowed to cool before inoculation. For the preparation of inoculum, each microcosm received 160 g of microbe-containing sediment as inoculum. Before the microcosm experiments, lake water containing inoculum was pre-incubated at 28 °C in the dark for 7 days to deplete labile dissolved organic matter (DOM) [12].

Brine shrimp eggs from a commercial supplier (HO HAI AQUARIUM, China) were hatched under controlled laboratory conditions in seawater (30% salinity) at 28 °C with continuous light exposure; both the resulting larvae and eggshells were separately collected and autoclaved to ensure sterility before use as microcosm substrates. Four microcosms (labeled as BSd1-4) received 1.25 g of autoclaved freeze-dried brine shrimp carcasses each as the sole organic substrate. In saline lakes such as Barkol Lake, where hundreds of tons of brine shrimp and eggs are produced annually [34–36], eggshells also constitute a major fraction of the biomass and represent an important organic matter source alongside carcasses. Another four microcosms (labeled as BSe1-4) received 1.25 g of autoclaved freeze-dried brine shrimp eggshells each as the sole organic substrate. A final microcosm (labeled as BSCK) served as a control with no organic substrate addition. All bottles were covered with screw caps and then were incubated statically at 28 °C in the dark. During the incubation period, slurry samples were collected from BSd1-4, BSe1-4, and BSCK at the following time points: 0, 7, 14, 30, 45, and 60 days, for DNA and RNA extraction, as well as Fourier-transform ion cyclotron resonance mass spectrometry (FT-ICR-MS) analysis (Fig. S1 and Table S1).

Metabolomics detection and analysis based on FT-ICR-MS

The dissolved organic carbon (DOC) content of the filtered microcosm water samples was measured using a non-dispersive infrared absorption DOC analyzer following standard combustion oxidation methods [39]. The filtered microcosm water samples were initially filtered through a 0.1 mol/L hydrochloric acid-adjusted PES membrane (0.22 μm, Biosharp, Canada) to reach a pH of 2.0. Agilent Bond Elut PPL cartridge (1.0 g, 6 mL) was activated sequentially with 12 mL methanol (HPLC-grade) and 12 mL of 0.01 mol/L hydrochloric acid. Subsequently, a designated volume of the sample was gradually introduced to the PPL column at a flow rate of approximately 5 mL/min to concentrate target analytes (the sample volume was calculated based on its DOC concentration, accounting for a 50% loss during column flow, resulting in a final DOC concentration (approximately 100 mg C/L). Post-sample flow, an additional 18 mL of 0.01 mol/L hydrochloric acid was added to wash the column and remove salts. The column was then dried under a nitrogen stream, followed by elution with 12 mL of methanol. The eluate was collected and dried under nitrogen to complete methanol evaporation. The samples were stored at − 20 °C. FT-ICR-MS was used to characterize the carcass-derived nutrient DOM pool. Prior to FT-ICR-MS detection, samples were redissolved in a 1:1 methanol/water solution [40].

For FT-ICR-MS detection, we used 200 μL of prepared samples with a 15-Tesla FT-ICR-MS spectrometer (Bruker SolariX) equipped with negative-mode electrospray ionization. Key parameters included continuous injection mode, 120 μL/h injection speed, − 4.0 kV capillary entrance voltage, 0.08 s ion accumulation time, and a mass acquisition range of 100 to 1600 Da. Signal averaging over 300 times in the time domain enhanced the signal-to-noise ratio. Before and after sample detection, calibration utilized 10 mmol/L sodium formate and soluble organic matter to ensure a mass error of less than 1 ppm [41].

Molecular formula assignment and summary were performed using Formultitude (ver. 1.0.8846) [42] based on the following criteria: internal calibration with known CHO compounds in DOM, with criteria including a signal-to-noise ratio > 4, H/C ratios between 0.2 and 2.3, O/C ratios between 0.1 and 1.2, and preference for formulas with the fewest heteroatoms [43]. The analysis of chemodiversity and visualization was performed using MetaboDirect (ver. 0.3.4) with the default parameters [44].

DNA/RNA extraction and next-generation sequencing

Total DNA for sequencing from the filtered microcosm sediment samples was extracted using the FastDNA Spin Kit (MP Biomedicals). The integrity and concentration of the extracted DNA were checked by agarose gel electrophoresis and Invitrogen Qubit Fluorometer. The V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified using the targeted specific primers 515 F (5′-GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′) [45]. After quality control, all samples were sequenced using the HiSeq PE250 platform (Illumina, USA).

For metagenomic sequencing, DNA samples were prepared using a library preparation kit, and the insert size of the library was detected using Agilent 2100. Metagenomic sequencing was performed using the NovaSeq 6000 platform with PE150 (Illumina, USA) and generated approximately 30 Gbp (2 × 150 bp) data for each sample.

For metatranscriptomic sequencing, 100 mL microcosm samples were collected and harvested from the microcosm system through centrifugation (4000 × g, 15 min, 4 °C). Samples were lysed by using bead-beating methods, and total RNA was extracted using the HiPure Soil RNA Midi Kit (Guangzhou Magen Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). After quality checking, genomic DNA digestion, ribosomal RNA removal, cDNA synthesis, library construction, and metatranscriptomic sequencing were performed on each sample using the NovaSeq 6000 instrument with PE150 (Illumina, USA), yielding approximately 20 Gbp (2 × 150 bp) of data. All samples for different sequencing were sent to Beijing Biomarker Technologies Co., Ltd.

Analysis of 16S rRNA gene amplicon, metagenome, and metatranscriptome

The raw 16S rRNA gene amplicon reads were processed using the cutadapt plugin [46] in the QIIME2 platform (ver. 2024.2) to remove the primers. Then, the DADA2 plugin (ver. 1.26.0) [47] in the QIIME2 platform was used to merge, denoise, filter chimeras, dereplicate, and predict biological sequences of all reads to obtain feature table and representative sequences. Each amplicon sequence variant (ASV) was defined as a cluster of reads with 100% sequence similarity. Each ASV was taxonomically classified using the qiime feature-classifier plugin based on the SILVA database (Release 138.1) [48–50]. Microbial co-occurrence network analysis was conducted in two steps. First, pairwise associations among ASVs were inferred using SparCC (ver. 0.1.0–0) [51] from rarefied 16S rRNA gene abundance table. Only correlations with R ≥ 0.60 and P < 0.01 (based on 100 bootstrap replicates) were retained as significant. The resulting adjacency matrix was then imported into R (ver. 4.3.0), where the package “igraph” [52] was used to construct undirected, weighted graphs, remove isolated nodes, and compute network statistics (e.g., degree distribution, modularity with the fast-greedy algorithm). Finally, the networks were visualized and topological properties were explored using Gephi (ver. 0.10.0).

For assembled-based metagenomic analysis, the raw metagenomic reads were quality filtered, including eliminating adapters, deleting duplicated reads, and removing low-quality reads by using fastp (ver. 0.23.4) [53]. High-quality reads were de novo assembled into contigs using SPAdes (ver. 3.15.5) [54] with parameters –meta -k 21,33,55,77,99. Prodigal (ver. 2.6.3) [55] was used to predict the genes of contigs with the “-p anon” parameter. Subsequently, gene dereplication was carried out using MMseqs2 [56] with the following parameters: –min-seq-id 0.5, -c 0.8, and –cov-mode 1. By using the predicted gene sequences and DIAMOND (ver. 7.9; E value < 1e−10) [57], the NCBI non-redundant protein sequence database (NR) [58] and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) databases (Release 108.1) [59] were queried to annotate the taxonomy and functions at the contigs’ level.

Genome binning was performed by using MetaBAT2 (ver. 2.12.1) [60] with the contigs > 2500 bp. CheckM2 (ver. 1.0.2) [61] was used to analyze the completeness and contamination of the metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs). MAGs with estimated completeness < 80% and contamination > 5% were discarded from this study. dRep (ver. 3.4.2) can also “de-replicate” a genome set by identifying groups of highly similar genomes and choosing the best representative genome for further analysis. CoverM (ver. 0.7.0) was used for calculating read coverage and the relative abundance of MAGs, with parameters “ –min-read-aligned-length 50 –min-read-percent-identity 0.95 –min-read-aligned-percent 0.75 –proper-pairs-only -m tpm”. MAGs were taxonomically classified using GTDB-Tk (ver. 2.4.0) [62]. Protein-coding regions were predicted using Prodigal (ver. 2.6.3) [55] with the “-p normal” parameter. The functional annotation of MAGs performed by querying the predicted gene against the KEGG databases (Release 110.1) [59] using DIAMOND (ver. 0.7.9; E-values < 1e−10) [57]. We also employed the METABOLIC (ver. 4.0) [63] against MEROPS [64] and CAZymes [65] databases to further infer the metabolic function of MAGs.

Raw metatranscriptomic data was pre-processed as described for metagenomic data. The rRNA gene sequences from the high-quality reads were removed by SortMeRNA (ver. 4.3.7) [66] with parameters “–fastx –sam –aligned –other –paired_in –out2 -e 5 –de_novo_otu –otu_map”. Finally, filtered metatranscriptomic reads were mapped to genes by BBmap (ver. 38.85, http://sourceforge.net/projects/bbmap/) with a sequence similarity threshold of 95%.

Viral sequence extraction and analysis

ViWrap (ver. 1.3.0) [67] pipeline was utilized with default parameters, including the “vb-vs” approach (i.e. VIBRANT [68] (vb) and VirSorter2 [69] (vs)), to enhance confidence in virus identification from assembled contigs for rigorous viral characterization in metagenomes. Viral contigs were binned into metagenome-assembled genomes (vMAGs) using vRhyme [70] as part of the pipeline. vMAGs were classified and dereplicated as part of the pipeline using vContact [71] and dRep (ver. 3.4.2). The quality characterization of vMAGs was assessed using CheckV [72] as part of the pipeline. ViWrap [67] also provided a prediction of the lytic state for vMAGs, classifying them as lytic viruses, lysogenic viruses, integrated prophages, or undetermined. Viral hosts were predicted by implementing iPHoP [73] within ViWrap, leveraging both the default iPHoP database and custom MAGs from this study to associate viruses with their potential hosts. Viral auxiliary metabolic genes (AMGs) were extracted from the VIBRANT [68] output within the pipeline. DRAM-v (ver. 1.5.0) [74] annotation refined the identification of high-confidence AMGs by designating genes using default parameters, where auxiliary scores are below 4 and only the “M” flag is used, without “A”, “V”, or “T” AMG flags. Additionally, filtered metatranscriptomic reads were mapped to high-confidence AMGs using BBmap (ver.38.85, https://sourceforge.net/projects/bbmap/) with a sequence similarity threshold of 95%.

Enrichments verification with biomacromolecular analogs of brine shrimp carcasses and amplicon-based community analysis

This experiment was further designed to employ standardized biomacromolecular analogs, representing the principal components of brine shrimp carcasses, to elucidate microbial community responses. The decaying cells of brine shrimp bodies and eggshells contain various compounds, including proteins (37–71%, with amino acids), lipids (12–30%, with unsaturated fatty acids), carbohydrates (11–23%, with chitin), and ash (4–21%) [19, 20]. This prompts the selection of different types of analogs for further verified microcosms. The preparation of basal saltwater medium was consistent with the above-described method. Twenty-one bottles of basal medium were prepared, and each received 160 g of sediment inoculum (BS, 1:10 sediment to saline lake water ratio) as before. After a 7-day pre-incubation at 28 °C in the dark to deplete labile DOM, 1.25 g of individual substrate was autoclaved and added to each bottle. Substrates included carbohydrates—monosaccharides (BSa): galactose, ≥ 99.0% purity, Solarbio; disaccharides (BSb): lactose, ≥ 99.0% purity, Macklin; polysaccharides (BSc): chitin, practical grade, Sigma-Aldrich; amino acids (BSf): aminofusin, ≥ 80.0% purity, Shanghai Yuanye; protein (BSg): bovine serum albumin (BSA), ≥ 96.0% purity, Shanghai Yuanye; and lipid (BSh): phosphatidylcholine, ≥ 98.0% purity, Macklin. These substrates were selected as they represent the major biomacromolecules (proteins, lipids, and carbohydrates) found in brine shrimp. Three replicate bottles were prepared for each treatment. The last three bottles served as a control group (labeled as BSCK) and received no organic substrate. All treatments were sealed and incubated at 28 °C in the dark. Shaken slurry samples were collected throughout the incubation (0, 7, 14, 30 days) for DNA extraction and subsequent 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing analysis following the above-described pipeline (Fig. S1).

Isolation and functional verification of pure cultures based on culture-dependent methods

To isolate pure cultures for further screening specialized in decomposing specific compound classes, we employed a culture-dependent method using marine agar 2216E (BD Difco™). For the isolation of the original sediment sample, 1 g of sediment was added to 9 mL of sterile water, diluted to 10–3 in sterile water, and then spread onto plates for isolation. For the microcosms’ sample, 1 mL of microcosms’ sample was added to 9 mL of sterile water, diluted to 10–3 in sterile water, and then spread onto plates for isolation. Pure cultures were obtained by restreaking isolated colonies. Isolates were stored in a 20% glycerol solution [75] and identified by amplifying near-complete 16S rRNA gene using primers (27F: 5′-AGAGTTTGATCCTGGCTCAG-3′ and 1492R: 5′–TACGACTTAACCCCAATCGC–3′) [76] followed by sequencing at Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. 16S rRNA gene sequences were analyzed using EzBioCloud (Release 0230823) [77]. The screening of chitinase was conducted for all isolates following the previously described method [78]. The protease activity was assessed by cultivating all isolates on Skim Milk Agar medium, and after 7 days of incubation at 30 °C, positive results were indicated by the development of a clear zone surrounding the bacterial colonies [79]. Lipase activity was assayed by inoculating all isolates onto Tween80 medium, followed by 7 days of incubation at 30 °C, during which the development of a halo indicated a positive result [80].

Genome sequencing and analysis of pure cultures

Genomic DNA of pure cultures was extracted using the TIANamp Bacteria DNA Kit. Illumina short-read sequencing was performed by Guangdong Magigene Biotechnology Co. Ltd. The genome assembly of each strain was generated using Unicycler (ver. 0.5.0) [81]. Protein-coding regions were predicted using Prodigal (ver. 2.6.3) [55] with the “-p normal” parameter as well. Functional annotations were determined following the binning-based metagenomic analysis pipeline described above.

Construction of the phylogenomic tree

Maximum-likelihood phylogenetic trees were constructed for both Bacteria and Archaea using a multiple sequence alignment of 120 bacterial marker genes and 53 archaeal marker genes that were generated by GTDB-Tk (ver. 2.4.0) [62] for bacterial and archaeal phylogeny, respectively. Maximum-likelihood phylogenetic trees were constructed by using IQ-TREE (ver. 2.2.2.7) [82] with parameters (-alrt 1000 -bb 1000 AUTO).

Results

Microbial community dynamics during microcosms

The dynamics of microbial Shannon diversity in microcosms containing different organic substrates, brine shrimp carcasses (BSd) or eggshells (BSe), followed a consistent pattern across microcosms, initially declining before increasing (Fig. 1A and Table S2). Microbial communities in microcosms with different substrates exhibited convergent yet distinct temporal patterns (PERMANOVA on Bray–Curtis, pseudo-F = 0.94–31.20, P < 0.05; Fig. 1B–C, and Table S2), potentially due to the similar organic matter composition of brine shrimp carcasses and eggshells [19, 20], while remaining clearly distinct from the control group without substrate addition (labeled as BSCK). Community diversity analysis revealed that Pseudomonadota, Bacteroidota, Halanaerobiaeota, Bacillota, and Desulfobacterota were among the dominant bacterial groups, whereas Ca. Patescibacteria, Nanoarchaeota, and Chloroflexota, although less abundant, were consistently detected (Fig. 1C). The taxonomic identities of microbial hubs and connectors identified by co-occurrence networks shifted markedly across decomposition stages, hinting at a dynamic reorganization of community composition, interaction structures, and functional linkages within the decomposer microbiome (Fig. S2 and Table S3). These findings highlight the intricate dynamics and complexity of microbial interactions and community structures during the decomposition of brine shrimp organic matter, culminating in gradual stabilization over time.

Microbial decomposition preference for organic matter

The decreasing dissolved organic carbon (DOC) concentration in microcosms implies ongoing microbial degradation of the added substrates (Fig. S3). The ongoing microbial degradation of the added substrates is further supported by Fourier transform ion cyclotron resonance mass spectrometry (FT-ICR-MS) analysis, which reveals that the dissolved organic matter (DOM) exhibits high Gibbs free energy (GFE) values, indicative of thermodynamically unstable, high-energy molecular states (Fig. S4A). Such states are more susceptible to degradation via microbial or abiotic processes. Microbial decomposition of brine shrimp organic matter also altered the composition of DOM in saline lakes (Fig. S4B–G, and Table S4). The lower nominal oxidation state of carbon (NOSC) values for lipids, compared to carbohydrates and proteins, highlight their greater resistance to oxidative decomposition, suggesting that microbes can transform carbohydrates and proteins more readily and rapidly than lipids (Fig. 2A). Metagenomic and metatranscriptomic analyses revealed that genes and transcripts associated with protein and carbohydrate decomposition were more abundant than those associated with lipid decomposition (Fig. S5). These findings indicate that microbes preferentially decompose carbohydrates and proteins over lipids, consistent with the above NOSC properties and previous studies [83].

MAGs and genomes uncover diverse potential new microbial decomposers

A total of 19 archaeal and 130 bacterial medium and high-quality MAGs were recovered from microcosms with brine shrimp carcasses (Fig. 2B and Table S5). These MAGs were classified into 4 archaeal phyla (Asgardarchaeota, Halobacteriota, Nanoarchaeota, and Thermoplasmatota) and 29 bacterial phyla (such as Bacillota, Pseudomonadota, Bacteroidota) based on GTDB Release 220. Additionally, we also retrieved MAGs from two candidate bacterial phyla, CG03 and T1Sed10-126. Furthermore, 93.29% of MAGs were unclassified at the species level. Nearly all MAGs were detected in microcosms at all time points, exhibiting similar abundance patterns in different substrates (Fig. S6). We also isolated pure cultures from the original sample and microcosms and obtained 77 pure culture genomes, which were classified into four major phyla: Bacillota, Pseudomonadota, Bacteroidota, and Actinomycetota (Fig. 2B). Among the isolates, 24 (31.17%) were unclassified at the species level and may represent new species. This may indicate the involvement of numerous potentially novel microbial decomposers in microcosms. In brief, these MAGs and genomes represent diverse lineages, including dominant, rare, and uncultivated lineages.

Microbial contributions to carbohydrate degradation in brine shrimp carcasses

The fate of complex carbohydrates from decomposing brine shrimp carcasses remains poorly understood. Genes for glycoside hydrolases (GHs) and polysaccharide lyases (PLs) were found in many MAGs and genomes (Fig. S7). For the main structural component of brine shrimp (i.e., chitin), microbial taxa encoding actively transcribed putative chitinases (GH018, 020, 084, and E3.2.1.14) included MAGs and genomes of Armatimonadota (UBA5377), Asgardarchaeota (CR-4), Bacillota_A (Peptostreptococcales), Bacillota_F (Halanaerobiales), Bacteroidota (i.e., Balneolales), Chloroflexota (UBA1429), Fibrobacterota (Chitinivibrionales), Planctomycetota (Pirellulales), Pseudomonadota (i.e., Pseudomonadale), and Spirochaetota (JABMQR01). These taxa may play a pivotal role in chitin transformation (Fig. S8, Table S6, and Figs. 3 and 4). Moreover, two candidate bacterial phyla, CG03 (Y22_60dBSe2.bin.2 and Y23_30dBSe4.bin.20) and T1Sed10-126 (Y23_60dBSd3.bin.64), encode a diverse array of active transcribed genes responsible for the decomposition of peptidoglycan, xanthan, and starch, as well as multiple sugar transporters (Figs. S9–S10). Moreover, some rare archaeal taxa may participate in the above catabolism. For example, Asgardarchaeota, including Thorarchaeia (Y22_0dBSe2.bin.3) and Lokiarchaeia (Y23_0dBSd1.bin.87), possess actively transcribed genes associated with chitin, starch, and xanthan decomposition, while Halobacteriota (Halorubrum, including Y22_0dBSd3.bin.40 and Y23_30dBSd4.bin.49) and Nanoarchaeota (Woesearchaeales, including Y22_30dBSe2.bin.106 and Y23_60dBSd3.bin.17) harbor actively transcribed genes related to dextran and starch decomposition (Figs. S11–S13).

In complex microbial communities, some taxa exhibit deficiencies in specific exoenzyme classes while still encoding the corresponding oligomer transporters, suggesting that they may engage in opportunistic trophic interactions and trade-offs with decomposers that robustly produce exoenzymes [83, 84]. For example, Cyanobacteriota (Cyanobacteriales), Halobacteriota (Halobacteriales), and Thermotogota (Petrotogales) MAGs also encoded actively transcribed chitobiose transport system (ChiE/F/G, celB/chbC, and ugpB/A/E) (Fig. S8, Table S6, and Figs. 3 and 4). Subsequently, chitobiose could be decomposed into N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc) by some taxa (such as Armatimonadota, Bacillota_F, Bacteroidota) with nagZ gene actively expressed, then metabolized into fructose-6-phosphate (fru-6P) [85, 86], which ultimately entered glycolysis, the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, and energy-conserving pathways such as oxidative phosphorylation or fermentation, to provide energy for cell growth (Figs. S8, Figs. S14–S16, Table S6, and Fig. 4). Additionally, some microbial taxa, for example Actinomycetota, Armatimonadota, Bacillota_F, among others, also harbor actively transcribed multiple sugar transporter genes (ABC-MS/Msmx), PTS system components, glucose/mannose transporter genes (gtsA/B/C), and maltose/maltodextrin transporter genes (MalE/F/G), suggesting a role in decomposition of other monosaccharides and disaccharides as well (Table S6, Fig. S8, and Figs. 3 and 4).

Diverse microbial lineages driving protein decomposition in brine shrimp carcasses

The taxa potentially involved in protein decomposition were more diverse. Genes for peptidases were detected almost all MAGs and genomes (Fig. S17). Almost all taxa, except Fermentibacterota and Nanoarchaeota, encode actively transcribed extracellular peptidases (M20A-D/F/X or M24A/B/X) (Table S6, Fig. S18, and Figs. 3 and 4). Numerous taxa also possess actively transcribed intracellular peptidases (M14D/X, M29, M42, S15), mechanisms for the uptake of peptides or amino acids, aminotransferases, and amino acid cleavage and decomposition (Table S6, Figs. S18–S19, and Figs. 3 and 4). This crucial protein catabolism also involved several candidate bacterial phyla (CG03 and T1Sed10-126) and rare archaeal taxa (Asgardarchaeota, Thermoplasmatota, and Halobacteriota). The phylum CG03 (Y23_30dBSe4.bin.20) encodes transcriptionally active extracellular and intracellular peptidases, along with various peptide and amino acid transporter genes (Fig. S9). The phylum T1Sed10-126 MAG (Y23_60dBSd3.bin.64) also encode transcriptionally active extracellular peptidases, as well as various peptide and amino acid transporter genes (Fig. S10). Several archaeal lineages, including Asgardarchaeota (i.e., Thorarchaeia Y22_0dBSe2.bin.3 and Lokiarchaeia Y23_0dBSd1.bin.87), Thermoplasmatota (i.e., PWKY01 Y23_30dBSe4.bin.148), and Halobacteriota (i.e., Halorubrum Y22_0dBSd3.bin.40 and Y23_30dBSd4.bin.49, Natronomonas Y22_0dBSd3.bin.110, Natronocalculus Y22_60dBSe2.bin.207, Halodesulfurarchaeum Y23_60dBSd3.bin.2, Halanaeroarchaeum Y23_60dBSd3.bin.23, and Natrinema Y23_60dBSd3.bin.31), are characterized by transcriptionally active and diverse sets of peptidase, peptide, and amino acid transporter genes (Figs. S11–S13 and S20). These findings further supported the protein decomposition potential of the diverse microbial taxa.

Microbial metabolism underlying lipid decomposition in brine shrimp carcasses

Lipids can be catabolized by microorganisms, serving as sources of carbon, energy, and phosphorous for microbial metabolism [87–90]. In the microcosms, microbial taxa transcribing putative esterase/lipase genes (yvaK, ybfF, lipV, lip, K06999, or frsA) to decompose recalcitrant lipid components (such as glycolipids, phosphatidylglycerol) and membrane lipase genes (pldB, lpqC, or clsA_B) to transform phospholipids into fatty acids were predominantly assigned to Actinomycetota (i.e., Actinomycetales), Armatimonadota (UBA5377), Bacillota (i.e., Bacillales), Bacillota_F (i.e., Halanaerobiales), Bacteroidota (i.e., Bacteroidales), Ca. Patescibacteria (i.e., UBA9983_A), Campylobacterota (Campylobacterales), CG03 (SLGR01), Chloroflexota (Anaerolineales), Cyanobacteriota (Cyanobacteriales), Desulfobacterota (i.e., Desulfobacterales), Gemmatimonadota (Longimicrobiales), Ca. Muirbacteria (Muirbacteriales), Myxococcota (i.e., PHBI01), Halobacteriota (Halobacteriales), Planctomycetota (Pirellulales), Pseudomonadota (i.e., Pseudomonadales), Spirochaetota (i.e., JABMQR01), and T1Sed10.126 (Table S6, Fig. S21, and Figs. 3 and 4). Also, transcripts of phospholipid (mlaA–F), lipoprotein (lolC/D), and fatty acid (atoE, Ompp1, aas) transporters were mapped to MAGs affiliated with the candidate phyla CG03 (Y22_60dBSe2.bin.2 and Y23_30dBSe4.bin.20), T1Sed10-126 (Y23_60dBSd3.bin.64), and Halobacteriota (Y22_0dBSd3.bin.40), suggesting their active involvement in lipid transport (Table S6, Figs. S9–S10, S13, and Fig. 4). Furthermore, the candidate phyla CG03 and T1Sed10-126, along with the rare archaeal phyla Asgardarchaeota, Thermoplasmatota, Nanoarchaeota, and Halobacteriota, all transcribed β-oxidation genes to varying degrees (Table S6, and Figs. S11–S13, S21).

Expanded the microbial lineages involved in the decomposition of nucleic acid

Nucleic acid or nucleoside salvage could also serve as carbon, energy, or phosphorous sources for microorganisms [91–94]. Microbial taxa encoding actively transcribed extracellular nuclease genes (nuc or K07004) responsible for decomposing nucleic acids, such as DNA from brine shrimp, into nucleobases and nucleosides primarily include Acidobacteriota (Aminicenantales), Bacillota_A (i.e., Tissierellales), Bacillota_F (i.e., Halanaerobiales), Bacteroidota (i.e., Bacteroidales), Chloroflexota (Anaerolineales), Desulfobacterota (i.e., Desulfovibrionales), Desulfobacterota_F (Desulfuromonadales), Halobacteriota (i.e., Halobacteriales), Myxococcota (i.e., PHBI01), Planctomycetota, Pseudomonadota (Pirellulales), and Thermoplasmatota (PWKY01) (Table S6, Fig. S22, and Figs. 3 and 4). Interestingly, some candidate bacterial taxa, including CG03 (Y22_60dBSe2.bin.2 and Y23_30dBSe4.bin.20) and T1Sed10-126 (Y23_60dBSd3.bin.64), also transcribed nucleoside/base transporters despite lacking extracellular nuclease genes, suggesting a secondary role in nucleic acid degradation (Figs. S19–S10). Furthermore, some rare archaeal taxa, including Asgardarchaeota (Thorarchaeia (Y22_0dBSe2.bin.3) and Lokiarchaeia (Y23_0dBSd1.bin.87), Thermoplasmatota (PWKY01, including Y22_60dBSe2.bin.73 and Y23_30dBSe4.bin.148), Nanoarchaeota (Woesearchaeales, Y22_30dBSe2.bin.106), and Halobacteriota (including Natronomonas (Y22_0dBSd3.bin.110), Halorubrum (Y23_30dBSd4.bin.49), and Halanaeroarchaeum (Y23_60dBSd3.bin.23), show transcriptional activity of nucleoside phosphorylase, beta-ureidopropionase, dihydropyrimidinase, purine/pyrimidine deaminase, or other genes associated with nucleic acid decomposition or salvage (Figs. S11–S13, and S20).

Integrated enrichments and pure culture-based validation of biomacromolecular analog decomposition

To further support the functional roles of microbial lineages in brine shrimp carcass decomposition, we combined enrichments with biomacromolecular analogs and pure culture validations. Enrichments using monosaccharides (galactose, BSa), disaccharides (lactose, BSb), and chitin (BSc) as the sole organic substrate revealed a visible increase in the relative abundance of Pseudomonadota and Fibrobacterota (Fig. S23). Additionally, enrichments using aminofusin (BSf) and protein substrate (bovine serum albumin, BSg) as the sole organic substrates visibly improved the relative abundance of Pseudomonadota, Bacteroidota, Halanaerobiaeota, Bacillota, Desulfobacterota, Fibrobacterota, and Ca. Patescibacteria (Fig. S23). For the enrichments with phosphatidylcholine (BSh) as the substrate, the relative abundance of phyla Pseudomonadota, Halanaerobiaeota, Desulfobacterota, and Ca. Patescibacteria were increased (Fig. S23). As is well known, culture-dependent approaches have inherent limitations [95], and only a few bacterial decomposer taxa were successfully isolated, whereas many others revealed by MAGs and amplicon sequencing remain extremely difficult to cultivate [96]. In this study, culture-dependent recovery was also necessarily limited, yielding 77 isolates spanning 4 phyla, 5 classes, 14 orders, 22 families, and 31 genera, but covering only 12.1% of MAG-inferred phyla, 11.6% of classes, 12.1% of orders; thus, these isolated strains only partially supplemented the diversity of microbial degraders in saline lakes and can serve as strain-level evidence of degradation (Fig. S24A). Chitinase activity screening in pure cultures revealed positive results in Bacillus spp., including BLK94, BWD9A, BSD5, and BLK102-2, as well as in Providencia spp., such as BLK158 and BLK160-2, further corroborating the functional roles of the above key taxa (Fig. S24B and Table S7). Moreover, protease activity screening of pure cultures revealed positive results in several representatives from Bacillota (i.e., Bacillus sp. BLK3), Pseudomonadota (i.e., Idiomarina sp. BLK167), and Actinomycetota (i.e., Arthrobacter sp. BLK119) (Fig. S24B and Table S7). In addition, the lipase activity of pure cultures was mainly observed in the phyla Bacillota (Bacillus, Cytobacillus, Brevibacillus), Pseudomonadota (Idiomarina sp. BLK54), Actinomycetota (Brevibacterium sp. BWD14B), and Bacteroidota (Psychroflexus sp. BLK134) (Fig. S24B and Table S7). Together, these results provide more evidence that links community-level responses with isolate-level activities, thereby validating multi-omics predictions and underscoring the functional specificity of microbial lineages in brine shrimp carcass decomposition.

Potential roles of viruses in the decomposition of biomacromolecules

Additionally, we were also curious about the role of viruses in the degradation of biomolecules. A total of 1,385 viral metagenome-assembled genomes (vMAGs) were obtained, including 76 complete and 181 high-quality (Fig. 5A). Viral sequences comprised 125 lysogenic viruses, 331 lytic viruses, 26 integrated prophages, 98 lysogenic scaffolds, and 805 lytic scaffolds (Fig. 5B). The viruses consist of Caudoviricetes (the dominant taxa), Faserviricetes, Huolimaviricetes, Tectiliviricetes, Megaviricetes, and some unassigned taxa (Fig. 5C). Excluding the 74.51% of viral sequences without host assignments, the remainder were linked to Pseudomonadota, Bacteroidota, Campylobacterota, and Bacillota lineages, with only 9.68% assigned to MAGs or genomes recovered in this study (Fig. 5D). Such low linkage efficiency is a well-recognized challenge in viral ecology, likely reflecting the limited representation of reference host genomes and the rapid evolutionary divergence of viral sequences [97, 98]. Consistently, a recent study on lake salinization across the Qinghai-Tibetan Plateau reported comparably low host-assignment rates, with only 5.59% of vOTUs linked to host MAGs even when multiple prediction strategies were applied [29]. The identified viral AMGs, primarily associated with amino acid, cofactor/vitamin, energy, and nucleotide metabolism, as well as folding, sorting, and degradation of protein, align with the microcosms related to the decomposition of brine shrimp carcasses, suggesting that they may assist host microbes in the decomposition process (Fig. 5E).

Viral involvement in the decomposition of brine shrimp organic matter. A Virus sequence quality. B Virus sequence type. C Virus sequence taxonomy. D Virus sequence host prediction. E The function of potential AMGs in virus sequences

Specifically, lytic viruses, dominated by Caudoviricetes with sporadic Faserviricetes andHuolimaviricetes, infected hosts across Bacteroidota (Flavobacteriales), Bacillota (Bacillales, Halobacteroidales), Chloroflexota (Anaerolineales), Desulfobacterota (Desulfovibrionales), and Halobacteriota (Halobacteriales) (Table S8). Notably, several lytic genomes encoded AMGs such as nadM (NAD⁺ biosynthesis), purA/purF (purine nucleotide synthesis), and DNMTs (DNA cytosine-5-methyltransferases), which reprogram host metabolism by enhancing energy flux, expediting nucleotide synthesis, and modulating DNA methylation to enable rapid viral replication in hypersaline environments (Table S8). Lysogeny was pervasive yet taxonomically structured, dominated by Caudoviricetes with sporadic Faserviricetes, and distributed across Pseudomonadota (Pseudomonadales, Enterobacterales, Burkholderiales), Campylobacterota (Campylobacterales), Bacillota_F (Halanaerobiales), Bacteroidota (Bacteroidales), Planctomycetota (Sedimentisphaerales), and archaeal Halobacteriota (Halobacteriales) and Nanoarchaeota (Woesearchaeales) (Table S8). Lysogenic viruses encoded AMGs spanning redox regulation (cysH, moeB), carbon and amino-sugar metabolism (pgl, glmS, polysaccharide/LPS biosynthetic genes), and cofactor biosynthesis (MMAB, cobS), highlighting their potential to rewire host metabolic networks in hypersaline environments (Table S8). Moreover, metatranscriptomic evidence revealed that several key AMGs encoded by Caudoviricetes viruses were actively expressed, including cysH, ectC, rhnA-cobC, and manA (Table S9). The transcriptional activation of Caudoviricetes-encoded AMGs involved in sulfate reduction, organic matter degradation, stress response, and vitamin biosynthesis suggests that lysogenic viruses actively reshape host metabolic networks. By enhancing energy acquisition, substrate utilization, and stress tolerance, these viral AMGs likely promote host fitness under fluctuating hypersaline conditions. Moreover, the metabolic benefits conferred by viral AMGs may facilitate metabolic cross-feeding and niche expansion within microbial communities, thereby influencing community assembly and stability. Such virus-mediated enhancement of sulfur cycling, organic macromolecule decomposition, and cofactor synthesis underscores the integral role of lysogenic viruses in driving microbial metabolic connectivity and ecosystem-level biogeochemical processes.

Discussion

Actively diverse microbial decomposers orchestrate metabolic handoffs and temporal succession during substrate-preferential degradation of brine shrimp carcasses

Decomposition is a fundamental microbial function with significant implications for global carbon and nitrogen cycling [2]. Microbial communities are essential for the breakdown of brine shrimp carcasses, and identifying these decomposers is crucial for understanding the associated biogeochemical cycling and ecosystem functioning in saline lakes. In saline lakes such as Barkol Lake, where hundreds of tons of brine shrimp and eggs are produced annually [34–36], eggshells also constitute a major fraction of the biomass and represent an important organic matter source alongside carcasses. Given the evident structural differences between carcasses and eggshells, we aimed to determine whether distinct microbial lineages are recruited for their decomposition. However, our amplicon sequencing results showed no significant compositional differences between carcass- and eggshell-derived microcosms (PERMANOVA on Bray–Curtis, P > 0.05; Fig. 1B, C and Table S2). As previously reported, the shared biomacromolecular composition of carcasses and eggshells [19, 20] likely drives the recruitment of similar microbial decomposers.

Since most biomacromolecules are too large for direct cellular uptake or transport, they usually undergo extracellular decomposition by microbial hydrolases, a critical and rate-limiting step in organic matter mineralization [89, 99–101]. In this study, we identified abundant microbial decomposers from Bacillota_F, Bacteroidota, Planctomycetota, Pseudomonadota, and others during the degradation of brine shrimp carcasses and eggshells in saline lakes, which are known to possess a diverse array of actively transcribed extracellular enzymes, such as chitinases, esterases, lipases, peptidases, and nucleases (Figs. 3 and 4). Complementary to the MAGs analyses, assembly-based metagenomic and metatranscriptomic analyses with direct functional and taxonomic annotation at the contigs level also provide detailed insights into microbial taxa and their active gene transcription during the microcosms of brine shrimp carcasses, further validating the role of dominant microbes in carbohydrate, protein, lipid, and nucleic acid decomposition at the community level (Figs. S25–S29). Building upon previous findings that Halobacteriota (Haloferax volcanii) utilizes extracellular DNA in saline environments [102], our study reveals that a broader array of bacterial and archaeal taxa actively transcribed extracellular nuclease genes (Figs. 3 and 4), indicating their participation in nucleic acid degradation within saline lake ecosystems. Furthermore, some candidate lineages, such as CG03 and T1Sed10-126, expressed nucleoside/base transporter genes despite lacking extracellular nucleases, implying alternative strategies for nucleic acid utilization. These findings suggest that the capacity to exploit nucleic acids as nutrient sources may be more phylogenetically widespread than previously recognized, extending beyond the traditionally implicated phyla of Bacillota [103, 104], Bacteroidota [104–106], Pseudomonadota [104, 107], and Fusobacteriota [104]. Metabolic handoffs among microbial taxa lacking extracellular hydrolases facilitate the stepwise decomposition of biomacromolecules from brine shrimp carcasses (Fig. 4), reinforcing the ubiquity of metabolic exchanges in natural microbial communities [108]. The taxonomic divergence of microbial decomposers from those in soil [2, 3] and thermophilic environments [83] highlights the unique decomposer networks shaped by extremophiles in saline lake ecosystems (Table S5). Previous studies have primarily focused on the static composition of microbial communities or their ecological functions [109, 110], whereas this study reveals the temporal dynamics of microbial communities and their metabolic activities, showing that taxa at different stages play distinct roles in the decomposition of carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids, highlighting their alternating dominance in nutrient cycling (Fig. S30). Consistent with these community-level dynamics, multi-omics analyses revealed stronger genomic and transcriptional signatures for protein and carbohydrate degradation than for lipids, indicating that microbes preferentially target these energetically favorable substrates in agreement with NOSC-based thermodynamic expectations and previous studies [83].

Candidate bacterial and rare archaeal lineages broaden the functional and metabolic spectrum of microbial decomposers

Some crucial metabolic processes also involve several candidate bacterial phyla (CG03 and T1Sed10-126) and rare archaeal taxa (Asgardarchaeota, Nanoarchaeota, Thermoplasmatota, and Halobacteriota). Although research on CG03 and T1Sed10-126 is scarce, particularly regarding their metabolic roles [111, 112], our findings reveal transcriptionally active genes in these taxa that support carbohydrate (i.e., peptidoglycan, xanthan, starch) degradation, protein decomposition, nutrient transport (sugars, peptides, amino acids, lipids, nucleic acids), and β-oxidation pathways (Figs. S9–S10). Furthermore, comparative genomic analysis revealed that the aforementioned functions are widely conserved in the CG03 and T1Sed10-126 clades, and significantly expand the diversity and ability of these taxa (Figs. S31–S32). Our results also expand the knowledge of metabolic potential of Asgardarchaeota beyond protein decomposition (via extracellular/intracellular peptidases and peptide/amino acid transporters) and fatty acid degradation (β-oxidation) [113] to include polysaccharide breakdown, as they encode actively transcribed genes for carbohydrate decomposition (i.e., chitin, starch, and xanthan) (Fig. S11). Additionally, the actively transcribed RbcL (DPANN Form III-b) in Asgardarchaeota MAG Y23 0dBSd1.bin.87, containing key RuBisCO functional residues and showing high similarity to canonical archaeal versions [114, 115], combined with comparative genomic analysis and phylogenetic analysis further supports the potential carbon fixation activity of Asgardarchaeota (Lokiarchaeia) via the Calvin-Benson-Bassham (CBB) cycle (Fig. S33), with the exception of the previously reported Wood-Ljungdahl Pathway (WLP) [116–118]. Although previous studies have reported that Nanoarchaeota primarily adopt a symbiotic lifestyle [119, 120], our research provides evidence of metabolic flexibility, particularly in Woesearchaeales, including actively transcribed genes for polysaccharide (starch, peptidoglycan, and dextran) decomposition, peptide/amino acid transporters, beta-oxidation of fatty acids, and beta-ureidopropionase for nucleic acid decomposition (Fig. S12), further supporting potential metabolic flexibility and ecological versatility under specific conditions [121–123]. This study not only confirms that Thermoplasmatota (PWKY01) encodes actively transcribed extracellular peptidases, peptide/amino acid transporters, and beta-oxidation genes for fatty acids [124, 125], but also reveals the active transcription of intracellular peptidases, membrane lipases, and extracellular nucleases (Fig. S20). Moreover, Halobacteriota (Halobacteriales) also encodes actively transcribed genes for polysaccharide decomposition, sugar transporters, extracellular and intracellular peptidases, peptide/amino acid transporters, lipases, fatty acid transporters, beta-oxidation of fatty acids, extracellular nucleases, nucleobase transporters, and deaminase (Fig. S13), highlighting multifunctional metabolic capacity [126].

Previously overlooked virus-mediated contributions to carcass decomposition

Previous studies on carcass or corpse decomposition have largely overlooked the potential roles of viruses [2, 3, 127]. Lytic viruses contribute to organic matter degradation by inducing host cell lysis and releasing hydrolytic enzymes, as previously reported [128–132]. However, our findings suggest that certain lysogenic viruses (e.g., Caudoviricetes) may reprogram host metabolism through actively expressed AMGs associated with sulfate reduction (cysH), stress response (ectC), vitamin biosynthesis (rhnA-cobC), and nutrient cycling (manA), thereby facilitating organic matter degradation during brine shrimp carcass decomposition in saline lake ecosystems and contributing to broader ecological and biogeochemical processes (Tables S9). These interactions provide a foundation for future research on the roles of viral AMGs in regulating microbial communities, contributing to biogeochemical cycles, and shaping the ecosystem's biogeochemical landscape.

Cross-domain metabolic cascades shape microbial decomposer networks (MDN) in saline lake ecosystems

Metagenomic and metatranscriptomic analyses reveal that diverse fermentative lineages, including Bacillota, Bacteroidota, and Desulfobacteriota, express key genes (e.g., pta, ackA, pflD) to cleave biomacromolecules into acetate and formate, with Desulfobacteriota also producing H2 via hydAB (Fig. S34). Sulfate reducers (Desulfobacterota, Euryarchaeota, and Pseudomonadota) oxidize fermentation products, using sulfate for dissimilatory sulfate reduction (sat, aprAB, dsrAB) and CO₂ release (Fig. S34). Euryarchaeota (specifically Methanohalophilus) mediate methylotrophic methanogenesis via mcrA, completing the carbon cascade (Fig. S34). These metabolic processes have also been detected in previous studies of natural environments [133–135], suggesting their involvement in the subsequent degradation of biomacromolecules in saline lakes. Although anoxic microsites and high nitrate concentrations may promote dissimilatory nitrate reduction to ammonium (DNRA) by fermentative or sulfur-reducing bacteria [136], there is no evidence of DNRA in carcass decomposition hotspots [127]. This study, however, identified several potential DNRA taxa during the microcosms of brine shrimp carcass decomposition in saline lakes. These include the MAG Y23_14dBSd3.bin.101 from the JAAYDI01 genus of Planctomycetota, which encodes actively transcribed nitrate reductase (narH, RPKM = 22.3079) and nitrite reductase (nrfA, RPKM = 113.4238); the MAG Y23_30dBSe4.bin.24 from the JAIVHC01 genus of Desulfobacterota_F, which encodes actively transcribed nitrate reductase (napA, RPKM = 20,872.9247) and nitrite reductase (nrfA, RPKM = 6206.5985 and nrfH, RPKM = 10,146.346); and the MAGs Y23_14dBSe4.bin.87 and Y23_45dBSd4.bin.90 from the genus Halospina of Pseudomonadota, which encodes actively transcribed nitrate reductase (napA and napB) and nitrite reductase (nirB and nirD) (Table S6). Collectively, our findings show that the degradation of brine shrimp carcass organic matter in hypersaline lakes is not a linear process executed independently by any single group of bacteria or archaea, but rather the outcome of a highly interconnected microbial consortium. Notably, we also uncover an unexpected yet central role of viruses, which reshape host metabolism and community architecture to participate directly in a cascade of carbon-transformation pathways. These insights lead us to propose a conceptual microbial decomposer network (MDN), in which bacteria, archaea, and viruses function as an integrated cross-domain system that orchestrates the breakdown of biomacromolecules, fermentation, sulfate reduction, methanogenesis, and DNRA, ultimately driving the complete mineralization of organic matter (Fig. 6). While these laboratory microcosms provide controlled insights, future in situ studies will be necessary to account for environmental variables such as seasonal temperature fluctuations and salinity changes. Nevertheless, the MDN model established here not only applies to Barkol Lake but also offers a framework for understanding organic carbon cycling in other hypersaline systems, such as thalassohaline lakes or coastal salterns, where similar microbial consortia may operate.

The process and mechanism of the material cycle and energy flow driven by microbial decomposer network (MDN) in saline lakes

Conclusions

In summary, this study demonstrates that the microbial community structure undergoes complex dynamics during the decomposition of brine shrimp carcasses but stabilizes over time. The abundant extremophilic microbial decomposers were enriched, which can metabolize energy-rich biomacromolecules by metabolic handoffs between taxa. Our findings emphasize the habitat-specificity of decomposers and challenge the applicability of conserved decomposer networks observed in soils to extreme environments. These extremophilic microbial decomposers also exhibit substrate preference in decomposing biomacromolecules, swiftly responding to the breakdown of proteins and carbohydrates before lipids. This study also expands the potential microbial diversity involved in nucleic acid degradation. Hydrolyzed oligomers are further oxidized by various taxa through fermentation, coupled with sulfate reduction and methanogenesis, ultimately producing CO2, H2, H2S, or CH4. Viruses may also play an indispensable role in processes such as sulfate reduction, nutrient cycling, stress response, and vitamin synthesis through their AMGs. Certainly, our findings also underscore the critical role of MDN in regulating long-term carbon stabilization, material cycles, energy flow, and limiting biomass accumulation in saline lakes. This study uncovers previously overlooked ecological niches and reveals the extensive diversity of extremophilic microbial decomposers in saline lakes, advancing our understanding of these ecosystems and paving the way for future research on similar processes in other saline environments.
