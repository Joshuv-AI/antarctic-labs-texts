Unusual consequences of intravenous thrombolysis in a patient undergoing delayed aortic surgery for aortic dissection presenting as acute ischemic stroke: a case report

Background

Acute aortic dissection (AAD) is a cardiovascular emergency that typically presents with chest and/or back pain. AAD often involves critical conditions such as cardiac tamponade and organ or limb malperfusion, necessitating prompt diagnosis and management. However, patients with acute ischemic stroke (AIS) as the primary AAD presentation may inappropriately receive intravenous thrombolysis (IVT) [1–3] despite its contraindication [3] and uncertain efficacy in AAD [1]. Management in this situation, particularly regarding the timing of aortic surgery, remains unclear, with limited reports.

We present a case of delayed aortic surgery after IVT in a patient with AIS due to AAD. This report highlights the unusual consequences of IVT in the context of AAD and provides insights into decision-making in these complex scenarios.

Case presentation

An 87-year-old woman on medication for hypertension was reading at home. At 14:30, she suddenly lost strength in her left arm without any accompanying chest pain. She immediately called a taxi and visited a local emergency department. She was conscious and had a National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) of 5, owing to left upper limb paralysis and mild weakness in the left lower limb. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) at 15:20 revealed a cerebral infarction in the right frontal lobe (Fig. 1A) and a low signal in the proximal right vertebral artery (Fig. 2). She was deemed eligible for IVT, and alteplase 0.6 mg/kg (nationally approved dose in Japan) was intravenously administered, starting with a bolus injection at 15:40 followed by continuous infusion for 1 h. She weighed 46 kg, and the total dose of alteplase administered was 28 mg. However, the paralysis remained unchanged at the end of IVT (16:40). Subsequent MRI at 17:20 revealed expansion of the infarction in the right watershed area (Fig. 1B). Additionally, a small infarction was detected in the left cerebellar hemisphere (Fig. 1B). She was transferred to our hospital at 19:30 for further diagnosis and treatment.

On arrival, a discrepancy in blood pressure was noted between the upper extremities: 92/74 mmHg for the right side and 140/74 mmHg for the left side. Computed tomography (CT) aortogram confirmed a diagnosis of Stanford type A AAD with an entry in the ascending aorta (Fig. 3A). A wedge-shaped mural thrombus was observed in the false lumen of the ascending aorta (Fig. 3B, D). The dissection extended distally to the terminal aorta. The true lumen of the brachiocephalic artery was compressed by the false lumen (Fig. 3C). The left common carotid and left subclavian arteries, and other aortic branches were unaffected. Carotid artery ultrasonography revealed that the dissection extended to the right common carotid artery; however, there was no significant compression of the true lumen observed (Fig. 4). Duplex ultrasonography indicated preserved antegrade blood flow in the true lumen of the dissected right common carotid artery, whereas the right vertebral arterial flow was retrograde. Electrocardiography showed a sinus rhythm; the patient did not have a history of cardiac arrhythmia. Echocardiography demonstrated mild aortic regurgitation without evidence of intracardiac thrombus or significant pericardial effusion. Coagulation function test obtained 4 h after IVT indicated an elevated D-dimer level of 331 µg/mL (Table 1).

Although emergency aortic surgery was indicated, there was a substantial risk of excessive surgical bleeding. Furthermore, we were concerned about the risk of thrombolysis-related intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH), which could overlap with full-dose heparinization during aortic surgery. Therefore, we opted to delay the aortic surgery. Despite the absence of neurological deterioration and ICH, brain CT performed 24 h after IVT revealed new infarctions in the right occipital and left parietal lobes (Fig. 5). At that time, the plasma fibrinogen level was below the measurable limit (the lower limit of quantitation is 50 mg/dL). Consequently, we decided to commence aortic surgery on the morning of the third hospital day when the fibrinogen level recovered to 176 mg/dl, 38 h after IVT.

During surgery, cardiopulmonary bypass was initiated after the administration of unfractionated heparin at a dose of 300 IU/kg. The following inspections and procedures were conducted under hypothermic circulatory arrest at a rectal temperature of 28 ℃ with selective cerebral perfusion. A 3 cm-long intimal tear was observed transversely in the ascending aorta, accompanied by a gelatinous mural thrombus in the false lumen. The entry was resected, and the aortic arch was transected distal to the brachiocephalic artery. The ascending aorta and partial aortic arch were replaced with a vascular prosthesis and the brachiocephalic artery was reconstructed. After rewarming, cardiopulmonary bypass was discontinued. The cardiopulmonary bypass time was 218 min and cross-clamp time was 121 min. Heparinization was reversed by protamine administration. Although the intraoperative transfusion comprised 10 units of red blood cells, 12 units of fresh-frozen plasma, and 20 units of platelets, the surgery was concluded without difficulty in achieving hemostasis.

Postoperative bleeding was minimal, with drainage volumes of 90 ml/10 hours and 150 ml/10 hours from the substernal and pericardial tubes, respectively. Patient recovery proceeded without new neurological deficits. Restoration of the brachiocephalic artery reestablished the antegrade blood flow in the right vertebral artery. She was discharged home with an NIHSS of 2. Five years have passed since the surgery, the patient continues to live independently.

Discussion and conclusions

As emergency aortic surgery is usually unavoidable for patients with type A AAD, this case represents a rare instance in which delaying surgery could be considered. The specific conditions that permitted this delay were as follows: (1) hemodynamic stability with controlled blood pressure, (2) absence of severe chest and/or back pain, (3) lack of critical AAD-related manifestations such as cardiac tamponade, aortic regurgitation, and organ or limb malperfusion, and (4) maintained antegrade flow in the right common carotid artery. Expansion of the false lumen in the aortic arch branches without a reentry may obstruct the true lumen, occasionally leading to watershed infarction, as observed in this case. Conversely, a distal reentry in the aorta or affected branches may relieve the obstruction. In this case, this process likely played a role.

This was an uncommon case of new cerebral infarctions detected after IVT in the context of AAD. Given that the left common carotid and subclavian arteries were unaffected, the contralateral infarctions, specifically the left cerebellar infarction on MRI 40 min after IVT and the left parietal lobe infarctions on CT 24 h after IVT, were posited to be embolic in nature and occurred approximately during IVT administration. In a previous study [4], one-third of patients with AIS due to type A AAD did not exhibit involvement of the supra-aortic vessels, implying that an embolic mechanism also play a role in the pathogenesis of AIS due to type A AAD [4, 5]. A potential embolic source in type A AAD without involvement of the supra-aortic vessels is the transfer of fragments from a false lumen thrombus into the true lumen through an entry. Although the effects of the thrombolytic properties of alteplase on a false lumen thrombus remain unclear, IVT in the context of type A AAD with an entry usually located in the ascending aorta may potentially increase the risk of such aorta-to-artery embolism. Duplex ultrasonography and magnetic resonance angiography suggested that extension of the dissection was impeding antegrade blood flow in the right vertebral artery. The pathophysiology of the right occipital lobe infarction is intricate because it might also involve artery-to-artery embolism [4, 5] and reduced forward flow in the right vertebral artery.

Previous cases of IVT in patients with AAD have been predominantly reported by neurologists and emphasize the need for suspicion and exclusion of AAD within a time window [1]. To our knowledge, only two cases of delayed aortic surgery after total-dose alteplase administration have been reported in English language literature [6, 7]. In one patient, surgery conducted 13 h after IVT resulted in fatal bleeding [6]. The other patient underwent surgery 2 days after IVT and had a favorable outcome [7]. The timing of aortic surgery has not been discussed in relation to the IVT effects on the associated coagulopathy and course of cerebral infarction.

Following AAD onset, fibrinogen depletion and an increase in fibrin/fibrinogen degradation products (FDP) may occur because of consumptive coagulopathy associated with activated coagulation-fibrinolysis systems [8, 9]. These abnormalities in the fibrinogen and FDP levels may also result from early fibrinogen degradation after IVT [10–13]. Following IVT, decreased fibrinogen levels peak at 2 h, remain decreased for 24 h, and return to pre-IVT levels within 48 h [14, 15]. Nevertheless, severe hypofibrinogenemia, when defined as < 100 mg/dL, is an unusual occurrence in AAD or following IVT, with incidence rates of only 5.4% [8] and 1.7% [16], respectively. In this case, we unexpectedly encountered severe hypofibrinogenemia below the measurable limit (< 50 mg/dL), along with notably increased FDP and D-dimer levels. Fortunately, the fibrinogen levels recovered 38 h after IVT, which guided the surgery timing. However, when emergency surgery is inevitable, hypofibrinogenemia may become pronounced during cardiopulmonary bypass under hypothermic circulatory arrest [9, 17], potentially leading to excessive surgical bleeding. To the best of our knowledge, only one analogous case has been documented: a non-surgical case of type A AAD exhibited rapid and severe hypofibrinogenemia below the measurable limit following IVT, with recovery within 48 h [2]. Despite the scarcity of reports, it is plausible that IVT may intensify coagulopathy induced by AAD.

Symptomatic ICH is the most formidable IVT complication in patients with AIS. Radiographically, symptomatic ICH mainly corresponds to parenchymal hematoma [18], which is significantly associated with IVT and early fibrinogen degradation [11, 13]. Although symptomatic ICH after IVT is rare, occurring in 3.3–6.9% of patients with AIS [12, 19], its mortality rate reaches 50% [19]. Reported predictors of symptomatic ICH or parenchymal hematoma include low fibrinogen levels (< 200 mg/dL) [13] and high FDP levels (> 200 mg/L) [11] 2 h after IVT, as well as fibrinogen depletion (≥ 200 mg/dL decrease) 6 h after IVT [10]. More than 80% of symptomatic ICHs are diagnosed more than 2 h after IVT [19]. Most symptomatic ICHs occur within 24 h and overlap the duration of early fibrinogen degradation [12], whereas those developing after 36 h are considered unrelated to IVT [20]. Furthermore, the administration of high-molecular-weight heparin during the first 24 h after IVT is considered a factor of parenchymal hematoma [18]. Thus, delaying aortic surgery for approximately 36 h after IVT may avoid the coincidence of thrombolysis-related ICH with heparinization during aortic surgery. Nevertheless, ICH leads to poor prognosis in patients undergoing aortic surgery, regardless of the perioperative period in which it occurs.

Fibrinogen supplementation is a therapeutic option for thrombolysis- [16, 19] and surgery-related coagulopathies [9, 17]. However, the availability of cryoprecipitate is limited and preoperative use of fibrinogen concentrate is off-label in our country. Further research is needed to explore the beneficial agents for preventing thrombolysis-related ICH and reversing these coagulopathies.

This single case report, along with the lack of previous reports regarding the effects of IVT in the context of AAD on subsequent coagulopathy, cannot suggest a definite treatment strategy, particularly the timing of aortic surgery in similar cases. Patients with type A AAD usually require emergency surgery and AAD surgery itself poses risks of hypofibrinogenemia or cerebral embolism, potentially obscuring these adverse effects related to IVT. At least, this case underscores the importance of monitoring fibrinogen levels in patients who have received inappropriate IVT for AIS due to AAD.

In conclusion, we report a case of cerebral embolism and severe hypofibrinogenemia after inappropriate IVT in a patient with AAD presented as AIS. Delayed aortic surgery allowed for recovery from hypofibrinogenemia and was successfully performed without excessive bleeding or neurological complications. This case illustrates the complex challenges in managing AAD following inappropriate IVT, highlighting the importance of monitoring fibrinogen levels and paying attention to cerebral infarction in similar cases. For patients who have received IVT and develop hypofibrinogenemia, delaying aortic surgery until the recovery of fibrinogen levels with vigilance for ICH may be a viable option in selected cases. However, this approach must be weighed against the inherent risks of untreated AAD during the waiting period.
