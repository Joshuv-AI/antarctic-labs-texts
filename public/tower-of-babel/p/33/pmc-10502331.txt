Lipoma of the middle finger in an elderly male: A case report

Introduction

Lipomas, benign mesenchymal tumors arising from adipose tissue, constitute the most prevalent neoplasms in regions abundant with adipocytes [1,2]. Typically, these tumors remain asymptomatic, though they may sporadically elicit pain, and their size and location can lead to compression-related complications [3]. Lipomas exhibit a wide distribution in the body, with common occurrences observed in the upper back, neck, shoulder, abdomen, and hand. While hand lipomas are frequently encountered in the thenar and hypothenar aspects, their occurrence within the fingers is considered a rarity [4]. In this context, we present an exceptional case involving a 70-year-old male patient with a lipoma situated on the middle finger of his left hand. This case report has been reported in line with the SCARE criteria [5].

Presentation of case

A 70-year-old man presented with a swelling of the palmar aspect of his left middle finger at the level of the middle phalanx. The patient first noticed the swelling two years prior to seeking medical attention, and during this time, the mass exhibited gradual enlargement without the manifestation of any accompanying symptoms. Notably, the patient disclosed a history of self-inflicted puncture on the mass, driven by the belief that it would lead to the release of a collection. The self-inflicted injury caused pain which was the primary reason why the patient presented to seek medical attention despite the mass being present for two years. He did not have any other symptoms besides the cosmetic disfigurement and pain secondary to the self-inflicted injury. The patient's medical and surgical history were not significant.

Upon thorough physical examination, the soft, painless mass measured approximately 1 × 1.2 cm and demonstrated unrestricted mobility in all axes. The mass was situated between the proximal interphalangeal (PIP) joint, and the distal interphalangeal (DIP) joint in the intermediate phalanx. The overlying skin appeared unremarkable, and there were no signs of tenderness. Additionally, the patient exhibited preserved finger function and a full range of motion despite the presence of the mass.

Following a comprehensive clinical evaluation, surgical intervention was deemed appropriate for the excision of the mass. All the imperative discussion was made regarding the procedure and possible complications were discussed and patient consent was taken. Under the administration of digital block anesthesia, a well-placed skin incision was made just above the lipoma. Intraoperatively, the mass was observed to be well-circumscribed, encapsulated, and devoid of any adhesions or invasion into the surrounding tissues. Histopathological examination of the excised mass provided definitive confirmation of the diagnosis, identifying the presence of a lipoma composed of mature fat cells, devoid of any evidence of malignancy (Fig. 2). Subsequently, the patient underwent a follow-up period of two months. During this time, there were no indications of lipoma recurrence. Furthermore, the patient maintained intact sensation in the affected finger, and a full range of motion was sustained, signifying a successful therapeutic outcome. Throughout the postoperative period, no adverse events or unanticipated complications were reported.

Discussion

This case report highlights the clinical presentation, management, and outcome of a rare lipoma located on the middle finger of a 70-year-old male patient. Lipomas, benign tumors originating from adipocytes, are commonly found in adipose-rich regions but rarely occur in acral areas, making their presentation in the finger uncommon. The patient's lipoma remained asymptomatic, which is consistent with previous studies reporting that lipomas are typically painless and slow-growing. Surgical intervention was chosen for excision due to the patient's desire to alleviate the swelling and the potential for compression-related complications. The patient refrained from seeking medical attention for two years and then later resorted to treat the lump by himself which made him come to the hospital. The aim of us reporting this case to prevent such occurrences and increase the early diagnosis and management of finger lipomas despite their asymptomatic and benign nature.

The upper back, neck, shoulder, and abdomen are the most prevalent locations for lipomas [4]. Nevertheless, occurrences in the hand are quite rare [6], and when they do occur, they tend to be found in the thenar or hypothenar regions [7]. Lipomas are most commonly observed in individuals in their fifth and sixth decade of life [7], and they are more frequently seen in women [8]. The differential diagnosis for the presented case encompasses several potential conditions, including ganglion cysts, inclusion cysts, and giant cell tumors.

Current medical literature supports the notion that lipomas can occur in any finger and various regions within the finger. Previous studies have shed light on the varied manifestations of lipomas in the fingers, with their potential to develop in any finger and any part of the finger [2,4,9]. Most reported cases of finger lipomas were painless, while a subset of patients experienced variable degrees of pain, and in some instances, local paresthesia was observed in 18 % and 8 % of cases, respectively. Moreover, functional impairment was reported in 47 % of cases [2,4,6]. However, following surgical excision, no functional impairment or tumor recurrences were documented, indicating the efficacy of this therapeutic approach in resolving symptoms and preventing relapse [2,4,9].

Several reports advocate for surgical intervention in cases of pain or restricted motion attributed to lipomas [9,10]. Given the favorable rates of postoperative outcomes, including minimal complications and low recurrence rates, surgical resection may be considered more broadly, even in asymptomatic cases [9,10].

The etiology of lipomas remains uncertain, with various factors potentially contributing to their development, including genetic predisposition, hormonal influences, and mechanical factors. In the case presented, the asymptomatic and slow-growing nature of the lipoma suggested a benign nature, which was subsequently confirmed histopathologically. The rationale for surgical intervention in this case was two-fold: to alleviate the patient's discomfort and to prevent any future compression-related complications which were not present at the time of presentation to the hospital.

A significant strength of this case report lies in its rarity, as lipomas in the fingers are infrequently encountered. Finger lipomas still remain the rare presentation which are usually missed or might get diagnosed late as in our case given it's rarity and asymptomatic nature. Furthermore, to the best of our knowledge, finger lipoma is being reported for the first time from our country Nepal. Early diagnosis and management could have better outcomes. The comprehensive clinical evaluation, precise surgical technique, and histopathological confirmation enhance the robustness of the diagnosis. However, some limitations are present. The case lacks long-term follow-up data, which could have provided further insights into potential recurrence rates or late complications. Additionally, as this is a single-case report, generalizability to a broader population may be limited.

Conclusion

This case report underscores the significance of considering lipomas in the differential diagnosis of painless swellings, even in less common locations such as the fingers. Timely identification and appropriate surgical management can yield favorable outcomes with minimal postoperative complications, affording relief to the patient while averting potential compressive complications. This case contributes to the existing medical literature by presenting a rare occurrence of a lipoma in the finger. Surgical intervention successfully addressed the patient's concerns, and histopathological analysis confirmed the benign nature of the tumor. Long-term follow-up and additional case reports are warranted to further elucidate the clinical outcomes of similar cases and enhance our understanding of lipomas in atypical locations. Higher studies could be conducted to find out the possible etiologies linked to the occurrence of finger lipomas and find out better awareness and management options of treating finger lipomas to reduce their recurrence.
