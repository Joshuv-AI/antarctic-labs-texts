Clinical Status, Treatment, and Disease Burden of Generalised Myasthenia Gravis in the Asia–Pacific
Authors: Thomas Webb, Mami Kasahara-Kiritani, Keira Herr, DaeYoung Yu, Bolam Lee, Sophie Barlow, Shiva Lauretta Birija, Joe Conyers, Hannah Connolly, Gregor Gibson, Niall Hatchell, Ciara Ringland, Yoon Ho Hong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionGeneralised myasthenia gravis (gMG) is a rare autoimmune disorder presenting as variable skeletal muscle weakness and fatigue. Comorbidities are common and symptoms are unpredictable and fluctuating. Many patients rely on immunosuppressive treatments including corticosteroids in their disease management, despite adverse events (AEs) associated with extended or high-dose use. We aimed to investigate clinical characteristics, treatment patterns, and disease burden of patients diagnosed with gMG across the Asia–Pacific region.MethodsData were drawn from the Adelphi Real World gMG Disease Specific Programme™, a cross-sectional survey of neurologists and their patients with gMG in Australia, China, Japan, South Korea, Malaysia, and Taiwan, from July 2023 to January 2024. Neurologists reported clinical characteristics from diagnosis, current maintenance treatment initiation, and at survey completion.ResultsNeurologists (n = 173) reported data on 407 patients with gMG. At the time of survey, mean (standard deviation) patient age was 51.5 (15.3) years and 58.2% were female. Also at survey, 77.6%, 20.1%, and 2.2% of patients had a Myasthenia Gravis Foundation of America class of II, III, or IV, respectively. Since diagnosis, myasthenic crises (22.5%) or exacerbations (37.2%) were reported for most patients (52.7%). Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors (82.8%) and corticosteroids (69.5%) were most commonly prescribed gMG treatments at survey completion, and 65.1% of patients were not in pharmacological remission. AEs were experienced by 71.0% of those prescribed corticosteroids. Joint effects of corticosteroid dose and duration on the increased number of comorbidities and AEs was significant (p = 0.0319).ConclusionAcross the Asia–Pacific, most patients did not achieve pharmacological remission despite prescribed treatments, and experienced treatment-related AEs, indicating significant unmet clinical needs. It is suggested that corticosteroid overreliance in maintenance treatment could be associated with increases in comorbidities and AEs. Further research is needed to understand how novel treatments could impact clinical outcomes and reduce corticosteroid reliance for patients with gMG in the Asia–Pacific.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40120-025-00845-4.Keywords: Generalised myasthenia gravis, Unmet needs, Clinical burden, Treatment pattern, Asia–Pacific, Corticosteroid, Real-world study

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? Generalised myasthenia gravis (gMG) is a chronic, autoimmune disorder, which for many patients is characterised by variable skeletal muscle weakness and fatigue.There is a lack of real-world research on the clinical characteristics and disease burden of patients with gMG in the Asia–Pacific region. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the disease profiles of these patients across the Asia–Pacific region. What was learned from the study? From this large-scale study, patients with gMG across Asia–Pacific region were characterised, revealing indications of continued overreliance on corticosteroids, and the increase in both comorbidities and adverse events that results from this.Increased understanding of the gMG healthcare landscape in the Asia–Pacific region enables the discovery of unmet patient needs and the possibility for further treatment strategy development.

Introduction

Generalised myasthenia gravis (gMG) is a rare, chronic, autoimmune disorder affecting the neuromuscular junctions, which presents as variable and fatigable skeletal muscle weakness. Weakness can occur in areas of the head and neck, diaphragm, arms, and legs. Generalised MG is most frequently associated with autoantibodies against the acetylcholine receptor (AChR) or muscle-specific kinase (MuSK) [1, 2]. Studies from the overall Asia–Pacific (APAC) region are lacking, but recent studies from China and Taiwan have shown gMG incidences of 6.8 and 23.0 per million, respectively, which is within previously reported ranges of 4.1 to 30.0 from Europe [2–4].

Ocular muscle weakness commonly presents first, but within 2 years, up to 80% develop more generalised muscle weakness to other parts of the body [2]. For many patients, their experiences with gMG are unpredictable, with frequent symptom fluctuations and exacerbations [3, 5, 6]. When uncontrolled, patients with gMG are predicted to have a lifetime prevalence of life-threatening myasthenic crisis between 20% and 30%, requiring emergency department visits and hospitalisations [7].

Established treatment options for gMG comprise acetylcholinesterase inhibitors (AChEIs), corticosteroids, non-steroidal immunosuppressive therapy, thymectomy, and off-label anti-CD20 monoclonal antibodies. Short-term immunomodulatory treatments can be used in cases of severe myasthenic events (rescue therapy) with intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg) and/or plasma exchange (PLEX) [8, 9]. Recent developments have led to the approval of targeted biological therapies such as complement C5 inhibitors [10, 11] and FcRn inhibitors [12] for the treatment of gMG. However, while there have been approvals for these targeted treatments [13, 14], overall patient access varies across the APAC region. This variability in access is influenced by a range of factors, including disparities in regulatory approvals, and in national healthcare funding priorities [15].

Concerns surrounding adverse events (AEs), such as weight gain and hypertension, have been well reported in populations on long-term systemic corticosteroids [16], and for patients with gMG [17, 18], with indications that patients with MG have a greater number of comorbidities than the general population [19]. Additionally, comorbidities, such as hypertension and high cholesterol, may pose an issue to patients with gMG, as treatments for comorbidities can worsen disease condition [9], and patients with refractory MG have been shown to lack alternative treatment options [20].

Aims and Objectives

Despite the diversity in economic, social, cultural, and healthcare system across the APAC region, comprehensive multinational real-world research on the clinical characteristics, treatment patterns, and disease burden of patients with gMG remains significantly lacking. Data on the use of corticosteroids and their associated burden in this population are especially limited. The present study aims to investigate the disease profiles of patients diagnosed with gMG across the APAC region.

Methods

Study Design

This study was an analysis of secondary data derived from the Adelphi Real World gMG Disease Specific Programme™ (DSP), a large, real-world, cross-sectional survey, with elements of retrospective data collection, conducted in six regions from the APAC (Australia, China, Japan, South Korea, Malaysia, Taiwan) from July 2023 to January 2024. The DSP methodology has been previously described [21], validated [22], and demonstrated to be representative and consistent over time [23]. This approach is well established, with studies implemented globally across many different disease areas [24].

Neurologists across the APAC study region were recruited to participate in the DSP by local fieldwork agents, following completion of a short screening questionnaire. Neurologists were eligible to participate in the survey if they were personally responsible for the treatment decisions and management of at least one patient with a confirmed diagnosis of gMG. Neurologists were instructed to complete a patient record form for up to four consecutively consulting patients who visited them for routine care, completed at the time of their most recent consultation to mitigate against selection bias. Record forms were completed for patients who were 18 years or older, had a neurologist-confirmed diagnosis of gMG, and were not enrolled in a clinical trial at time of survey. Patients were not compensated for participation.

Neurologist-Reported Data

The neurologist-reported patient record form contained detailed questions on clinical and demographic characteristics including autoantibody status, Myasthenia Gravis Foundation of America (MGFA) classification, gMG symptoms, myasthenic events (exacerbation, and crisis), remission status, drug AEs, and treatment patterns. MGFA is a disease severity classification scheme, where class I–V designates the least to most severe MG [25]. Myasthenic exacerbation was defined as a weakening of some or all muscles throughout the body but where assistance was not required for breathing, and myasthenic crisis as a weakness and rapid fatigue of voluntarily controlled muscles which required intubation.

A gMG maintenance treatment was defined as a treatment prescribed for long-term use, rather than short-term rescue treatment. Treatment line was defined as the start, stop, or switch of any treatment excluding any treatment breaks.

Ethical Considerations

Neurologists provided informed consent during the screening questionnaire. Data were collected in such a way that patients and neurologists could not be identified directly, with data aggregated before being shared with the subscriber and for publication.

Data collection was undertaken in line with European Pharmaceutical Marketing Research Association (EphMRA) guidelines [26]. Exemption from ethics approval was sought and granted from Pearl IRB (IRB; study number is AG9525). Each survey was performed in full accordance with relevant legislation at the time of data collection, including the Helsinki Declaration of 1964 (and its later amendments), the US Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act 1996 [27], and Health Information Technology for Economic and Clinical Health Act legislation [28]. All authors from Johnson & Johnson had permission to access the Adelphi Real World gMG DSP dataset through the subscription purchased by Johnson & Johnson. Other authors include employees of Adelphi Real World, the dataset owner.

Data Analysis

This study included patients that, at the time of survey, were prescribed a maintenance treatment for their gMG and were classed as MGFA II–IV by their neurologist.

Two subgroup sets were used to make comparisons: remission status and corticosteroid prescription status at time of survey. The “not in remission” subgroup included patients with minimal manifestations (MM; defined in footnotes of Fig. 4) MM-1, MM-2, MM-3, and those not in remission. The “pharmacological remission” subgroup included patients defined as in pharmacological remission by their neurologist. The “corticosteroid” and “non-corticosteroid” subgroups comprised patients who were prescribed corticosteroids or not, respectively.

Reported adverse events for both the non-corticosteroid subgroup and corticosteroid subgroup. a Adverse events for patients who were receiving a corticosteroid at time of survey, b no adverse events experienced between countries and overall. a Other adverse events were altered mood, anorexia, muscle pain, insomnia, alopecia, fever, constipation, sleep deprivation/difficulty sleeping, diarrhoea, respiratory tract infection, lymphocytopenia, joint pain, rash, nausea, cardiac events, indigestion, blurred vision, photosensitivity, stomatitis, headache, fatigue, vomiting, dizziness, loss of appetite, and hyperhidrosis

Negative binomial regression analysis was performed to assess the association between corticosteroid dose and duration on the number of comorbidities and AEs for patients prescribed a corticosteroid treatment at the time of survey. This model was set up with main effects and interaction effect between both corticosteroid dose and duration and was tested using a Wald test. The model was also adjusted for age, sex, and body mass index (BMI). Via a two-sample t test, patients with weight gain were found to have a higher BMI (p = 0.0096). Therefore, a sensitivity analysis was performed on the regression group excluding patients with weight gain. Findings were similar between the full sample and sensitivity. Results were considered significant if p < 0.05.

Means and standard deviations (SD) were calculated for continuous variables, and frequency counts and percentages for categorical variables.

Analyses were conducted in UNICOM Intelligence Reporter version 7.5.1 (UNICOM Systems 2021) and Stata 18 (College Station, TX: StataCorp LLC. 2023). Missing data were not imputed; therefore, the base of patients for analysis could vary from variable to variable and was reported separately for each analysis.

Results

Neurologist Sample

During data collection, 173 neurologists (China, n = 50; Japan, n = 41; Australia, n = 25; Malaysia, n = 25; Taiwan, n = 25; South Korea, n = 7) reported data on 407 patients with gMG. Overall, participating neurologists saw a mean (SD) of 8.3 (11.0) patients with gMG in a typical month, with 62.3% (n = 254/407) in an academic hospital, 25.4% (n = 103/407) in a community hospital setting, and 10.3% (n = 42/407) of neurologists primarily practising in an office setting. Disaggregated data for male and female patients are provided in the supplementary materials.

Patient Demographic and Clinical Characteristics

The mean (SD) patient age was 51.5 (15.3) years, mean weight was 64.4 (14.0) kg, and 58.2% (n = 237/407) were female. Only Australia had a majority male cohort (54.7%, n = 29/53; Table 1). Overall, 33.9% (n = 138/407) of patients were from China, 21.9% (n = 89/407) from Japan, 13.3% (n = 54/407) from Malaysia, 13.0% (n = 53/407) from Australia, 11.8% (n = 48/407) from Taiwan, and 6.1% (n = 25/407) from South Korea.

In the overall cohort, 70.8% (n = 288/407) had been diagnosed with one or more comorbidities, ranging from 61.8% (n = 55/89) in Japan to 90.6% (n = 48/53) in Australia. Patient Hospitalisations and Myasthenic Exacerbations/Crises

Overall, at least one exacerbation of symptoms since gMG diagnosis was reported for 37.2% (n = 147/395) of patients, and at least one myasthenic crisis for 22.5% (n = 89/395) of patients. A myasthenic crises or exacerbations of symptoms since diagnosis was reported for 52.7% (n = 208/395) of patients and ranged from 68.0% (n = 17/25) in South Korea to 40.7% (n = 22/54) in Malaysia. In the previous 12 months to each patient’s record form completion, 50.8% (n = 67/132) and 27.8% (n = 22/79) of patients experienced an exacerbation or crisis, respectively (Table 2).

Patient Remission Status

There were 34.9% (n = 142/407) reported to be in pharmacological remission, ranging from 20.4% (n = 11/54) in Malaysia to 58.7% (n = 81/138) in China. Overall, patients in MM were reported as MM-1 (2.0%, n = 8/407), MM-2 (13.0%; n = 53/407) and MM-3 (33.7%, n = 137/407). While all patients in Malaysia were reported to be in a level of remission or MM, the other regions all had populations not in remission, which was reported as 16.5% (n = 67/407) of patients overall (Fig. 3).

Remission status at time of survey for the overall cohort and by APAC country. Remission definitions: complete stable remission (CSR): the patient has had no symptoms or signs of MG for at least 1 year and has received no therapy for MG during that time. There is no weakness of any muscle on careful examination by someone skilled in the evaluation of neuromuscular disease. Isolated weakness of eyelid closure is accepted. Pharmacological remission: the same criteria as for CSR except that the patient continues to take some form of therapy for MG. Patients taking cholinesterase inhibitors are excluded from this category because their use suggests the presence of weakness. Minimal manifestations (MM): the patient has no symptoms of functional limitations from MG but has some weakness on examination of some muscles. MM-1: the patient continues to receive some form of immunosuppression but no cholinesterase inhibitors or other symptomatic therapy. MM-2: the patient has received only low-dose cholinesterase inhibitors (< 120 mg pyridostigmine/day) for at least 1 year. MM-3: the patient has received cholinesterase inhibitors or other symptomatic therapy and some form of immunosuppression during the past year

Patients not in remission were receiving a mean (SD) of 2.6 (1.0) (n = 265) individual treatments in their current regimen, compared to 2.2 (1.0) (n = 142) for those in pharmacological remission. Mean (SD) prednisone/prednisolone dose of current treatment was 13.7 mg (13.0) (n = 177) for patients not in remission and 14.6 mg (14.9) (n = 74) for those in pharmacological remission (Table 3).

Overall, the most frequently reported class of drug treatment prescribed at survey was AChEIs (82.8%, n = 337/407), followed by corticosteroids (69.5%, n = 283/407), and non-steroidal immunosuppressants (58.0%, n = 236/407). The proportion of corticosteroid prescription ranged from 50.0% (n = 69/138) in China to 92.1% (n = 82/89) in Japan (Table 4).

The three most frequently reported maintenance treatments prescribed at survey were pyridostigmine (69.8%, n = 284/407), prednisone/prednisolone (61.7%, n = 251/407), and azathioprine (24.1%, n = 98/407). Intravenous immunoglobulins were reported to be used by 15.2% (n = 62/407) of the cohort, largely weighted by Japan (22.5%, n = 20/70) and Australia (49.1%, n = 26/50; Table 4).

Treatment Adverse Events

The most frequently reported AEs attributed to gMG treatment were weight gain (18.2%, n = 74/407), altered mood (16.2%, n = 66/407), and dizziness (15.7%, n = 64/407). In 68.3% (n = 278/407) of patients there were AEs reported (Supplementary Table S3).

AEs reported for the non-corticosteroid and corticosteroid subgroups are shown in Fig. 4a. For those receiving corticosteroids at the time of survey, 23.3% (n = 66/283), 15.2% (n = 43/283), and 11.7% (n = 33/283) were experiencing weight gain, hyperglycaemia, and increased blood pressure, respectively. For those not receiving corticosteroids at the time of survey, 6.5% (n = 8/124) were experiencing weight gain, and hyperglycaemia and increased blood pressure were both experienced by 1.6% (n = 2/124) of patients.

No AEs among those receiving corticosteroids were overall reported by 29.0% (n = 82/283) and ranged from 61.0% (n = 50/82) in Japan to 5.8% (n = 4/69) in China. For those not receiving corticosteroids, no AEs were overall reported for 37.9% (n = 47/124) and ranged from 66.7% (n = 4/6) in Taiwan to 29.0% (n = 20/69) in China (Fig. 4b).

Negative Binomial Regression Analysis

For both the full sample (n = 281) and sensitivity analyses (n = 204), the adjusted incidence rate ratios (IRR) of individual variables for the number of comorbidities and AEs are shown in Fig. 5. For the full analysis, the main effect of total daily corticosteroid dose gave an IRR (95% confidence interval (CI)) of 1.01 (1.00–1.02) [p = 0.076], and BMI gave an IRR (CI) of 1.04 (1.02–1.06) [p = 0.001]. For the sensitivity analysis, while confounding factors are held constant, the main effect of total daily corticosteroid dose was significant (IRR (CI) 1.02 (1.00–1.03), p = 0.031). Sex, age, and BMI were also significant covariates in the sensitivity, with being male (IRR (CI) 0.78 (0.63–0.97), p = 0.022), each year older (IRR (CI) 1.01 (1.00–1.02), p = 0.001), and each one unit of BMI (IRR (CI) 1.04 (1.01–1.07), p = 0.016) resulting in higher numbers of comorbidities and AEs.

Discussion

Data from this present study suggest unmet treatment needs in a subpopulation of patients in the APAC region. Despite maintenance treatment, commonly using corticosteroids, myasthenic events including exacerbation and crises were common. Additionally, regression analysis showed that for patients in our cohort, higher corticosteroid dosage was linked to a significantly higher number of current comorbidities and AEs.

Across our cohort, unmet treatment needs were found with around a fifth of patients MGFA class III or IV at the time of survey despite being prescribed maintenance treatment. There were also more than 15% of patients not achieving MM or better, which is in line with previous studies where this was reported between 10% and 20% [29]. In the 12 months prior to survey, roughly half of patients experienced myasthenic exacerbations and a quarter of patients experienced myasthenic crises; yet despite this, the time from patients most recent treatment initiation to survey was a mean of 2.5 years. Just over a third of patients were hospitalised in the previous 12 months, compared to 22.9% reported for a European cohort [30], with treating a complication of gMG the most common reason for patient hospitalisation, followed by adverse reactions to treatment. This data, along with only a third of patients reported to be in pharmacological remission, underscores the continued insufficiencies of current gMG maintenance treatment practice and the need for novel treatment options. However, the use of novel targeted therapies across this study cohort was mostly very low as a result of approval timelines and access limitations in the APAC region. The wider availability of novel targeted treatments would offer alternative options to patients that experience AEs [31] or inadequate response with their current treatment.

On the basis of the treatment insufficiencies seen in this present study, and the known impact of long-term systemic corticosteroid use on increased AEs [16], corticosteroid use in the cohort was investigated. The mean reported prednisone/prednisolone dose for patients not in remission and for patients in pharmacological remission suggests similar dosing levels between groups. Additionally, there was still high usage of corticosteroids in patients that received a third line of treatment. Together, this indicates that there is an overreliance on corticosteroids for patient maintenance treatment.

Furthermore, data from this present study suggest a possible association between corticosteroid use and increased rates of hypertension, weight gain, and hyperglycaemia. Overall, on the basis of the contour plots, the highest expected number of comorbidities and AEs are where we observe higher corticosteroid dose and lower duration on corticosteroid, while adjusted for confounders. This is an interesting finding as it conflicts with published literature that the longer the corticosteroid duration, the higher the number of AEs [17]. However, our regression sample had a low number of patients on a long-term corticosteroid regimen (for both high and low dosage) and information on AE severity was unavailable. Still, this highlights for many patients the need for effective therapies which can provide disease control with a steroid-sparing effect, and how further research is warranted into the impact of long-term corticosteroid exposure in patients with gMG.

It should be noted that in current Japanese treatment guidelines, the aim is for patients to achieve MM via reducing the dose of orally administered prednisolone to 5 mg/day, and has been associated with a favourable quality of life [32]. This may explain the seemingly higher rate of those in Japan both prescribed corticosteroids and experiencing no AEs with them, at time of survey.

Possibly in connection to the high corticosteroid use, most of the cohort had been diagnosed with at least one comorbidity, which can present challenges in the concurrent management of the comorbidity and gMG [9]. Hypertension was the most frequently reported comorbidity, consistent with reporting from both China and Taiwan [4, 18]. Beta-blocking agents, a commonly prescribed therapeutic treatment for hypertension, have been specifically shown to worsen gMG conditions [9]. Additionally, hypertension is a known AE of chronic steroid use in patients with gMG [8]. Further research into the clinical outcomes of patients with hypertension and other comorbidities compared to those without would be valuable information for physicians.

Strengths and Limitations

Minimal inclusion criteria governed the selection of the participating neurologists, who were recruited to provide a geographically diverse and pragmatic sample; participation was influenced by willingness to complete the survey. The cross-sectional design of this study prevents any conclusions about causal relationships; however, identification of significant associations was possible.

Secondly are limitations in relation to data availability and time of data collection. Patient refractory status was not surveyed, meaning comparisons to rates of refractory patients in other studies could not be made. This was also the case for the type of serological test used to determine autoantibody status. In the contour plot derived from our regression sample, very few patients were on corticosteroids long-term, meaning these results should be interpreted with caution. Lastly, Japan was the only place at the time of survey where the drugs efgartigimod and ravulizumab were approved at a national level for the treatment of gMG [13, 14], leading to the oversample.

Thirdly, as neither myasthenic exacerbation nor crisis is defined on an international level [9], definitions used for this study have been provided in the Methods. It should be noted that reappearance of minor symptoms was not excluded from the definition of exacerbation.

Fourthly, demographic differences may limit the generalisability of these results outside of the APAC region. While it appears cohorts in Europe and the USA had comparable gender proportions (tended to be majority female), they may be on average of a greater age (with patients in their late 50s) [30, 33–36].

Fifthly, while neurologists are asked to provide data for a consecutive series of patients to avoid selection bias, patients who visited their neurologist more frequently may have had greater disease severity than those who do not consult their neurologist as frequently.

Finally, as recall bias is a common limitation of surveys, the data for these analyses were collected at the time of each patient’s appointment, which is expected to reduce this likelihood. In addition, neurologists had access to patient medical records for data extraction.

Despite such limitations, real-world studies play an important part in highlighting areas of concern that are not addressed in clinical trials, with the data in this study representative of current clinical practice at the time the survey was conducted.

Conclusions

Across the APAC regions, despite receiving treatment, patients with gMG commonly experienced burdensome myasthenic exacerbations and crises, a notable proportion experienced treatment-related AEs, and most did not achieve pharmacological remission, indicating a significant unmet need. Corticosteroids were frequently prescribed to patients, with similar use levels seen across treatment lines, indicating corticosteroid overreliance, which is associated with increased rates of comorbidities and AEs. Further research is needed into the impact of chronic corticosteroids use on the clinical outcomes of patients with gMG. Additionally, studies into the real-world potential of novel targeted therapies for those who are currently an inadequate responder, drug-refractory, or experiencing burdensome treatment-related AEs. While this study’s results are from the APAC region, they may still be relevant in other regions with high corticosteroid use.
