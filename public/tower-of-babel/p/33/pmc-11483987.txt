Resistance gene Ty-1 restricts TYLCV infection in tomato by increasing RNA silencing
Authors: Xiaofang Ma, Yijun Zhou, Liming Wu, Peter Moffett
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
A major antiviral mechanism in plants is mediated by RNA silencing through the action of DICER-like (DCL) proteins, which cleave dsRNA into discrete small RNA fragments, and ARGONAUTE (AGO) proteins, which use the small RNAs to target single-stranded RNA. RNA silencing can also be amplified through the action of RNA-dependent RNA polymerases (RDRs), which use single stranded RNA to generate dsRNA that in turn is targeted by DCL proteins. As a counter-defense, plant viruses encode viral suppressors of RNA silencing (VSRs) that target different components in the RNA silencing pathway. The tomato Ty-1 gene confers resistance to the DNA virus tomato yellow leaf curl virus (TYLCV) and has been reported to encode an RDRγ protein. However, the molecular mechanisms by which Ty-1 controls TYLCV infection, including whether Ty-1 is involved in RNA silencing, are unknown. Here, by using a transient expression assay, we have confirmed that Ty-1 shows antiviral activity against TYLCV in Nicotiana benthamiana. Also, in transient expression-based silencing assays, Ty-1 augmented systemic transgene silencing in GFP transgenic N. benthamiana plants. Furthermore, co-expression of Ty-1 or other RDRγ proteins from N. benthamiana or Arabidopsis with various proteins resulted in lower protein expression. These results are consistent with a model wherein Ty-1-mediated resistance to TYLCV is due, at least in part, to an increase in RNA silencing activity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12985-024-02508-6.

Introduction

Tomato yellow leaf curl virus (TYLCV) is transmitted exclusively by whiteflies of the Bemisia tabaci (Gennadius) species (Hemiptera: Aleyrodidae), in a circulative and persistent manner. TYLCV is a monopartite, single-stranded circular DNA virus in the genus of Begomovirus and in the family of Geminiviridae [1, 2]. The genome of TYLCV is about 2.8 kb and encodes six open reading frames (ORFs), wherein two proteins (V1 and V2) are encoded by the sense strand and four proteins (C1-C4) are encoded by the anti-sense strand [3, 4]. The characteristic symptoms on tomato (Solanum lycopersicum) induced by TYLCV include stunting, yellowing, upward curling leaves, which can result in 100% yield loss when infections occur during early growth stages. TYLCV causes one of the most devastating emerging diseases of tomato worldwide, it is considered to be in the top ten important plant viruses [5]. Since it is difficult to control the transmission vector (B. tabaci) of TYLCV, breeding tomato cultivars that are resistant to TYLCV has been the main strategy for controlling this virus. To date, six TYLCV resistance loci from wild tomato have been introgressed into cultivated tomato, including Ty-1, Ty-3, Ty-4 and Ty-6 from Solanum chilense, Ty-2 from S. habrochaites and Ty-5 from cultivated tomato [6–12]. Ty-1 and Ty-3 are allelic and encode a RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RDR) possessing a conserved DFDGD motif, characteristic of RDRγ proteins [13].

Plants have developed diverse mechanisms to defend themselves against viral infections, including RNA silencing. RNA silencing is a sequence-specific mechanism that regulates the expression of endogenous genes as well as exogenous genetic elements, including viruses, transgenes, and transposable elements [14, 15]. As a defense against viruses in plants, RNA silencing relies on the recognition of viral double-stranded RNA by DICER-like (DCL) enzymes, which cleave the dsRNA into virus-derived small interfering RNAs (vsiRNA) of 21–24 nt in length. These vsiRNAs, also referred to as primary siRNAs, then bind to RNA-induced silencing complexes (RISC) complexes, which contain Argonaute (AGO) proteins that use the vsiRNAs as guides to target homologous single stranded RNAs or DNA. The latter results in post transcriptional gene silencing (PTGS) through the ribonucleolytic activity of AGO proteins on target RNAs. Alternatively transcriptional gene silencing (TGS) of DNA can be mediated by the recruitment of DNA methylation components by RISC complexes by a process known as RNA-directed DNA methylation (RdDM) [16, 17]. PTGS is a potent defense mechanism against RNA viruses [17], whereas TGS is thought to primarily target DNA viruses [16].

The major components in RNA silencing pathway includes DCLs, AGOs and RDRs [18]. Plant RNA silencing mechanisms have diversified to carry out multiple biological functions and different plant species encode different numbers of RNA silencing components. For example, tomato encodes seven DCLs, 15 AGOs and six RDRs [19, 20], whereas Arabidopsis thaliana encodes four DCLs, ten AGOs and six RDRs [14]. According to the sequence similarity of the six RDRs in Arabidopsis, they are divided into two classes: RDR1, RDR2 and RDR6 belong to RDRα class; RDR3, RDR4 and RDR5 belong to RDRγ class [13]. RDRα proteins are involved in transitive silencing, a process wherein RDRs use cellular transcripts or aberrant RNAs as templates to synthesize dsRNAs. The latter are then cleaved into secondary siRNAs by DCLs, so as to amplifying RNA silencing signal [21]. However, the function of RDRγ proteins and the role of Ty-1 in defending against TYLCV is unclear.

RDRs have been shown to be involved in vsiRNA generation in Nicotiana benthamiana and A thaliana, however, virus genome accumulation is not always increased in RDR mutants [22–24]. RDR1 has been reported to synthesize cucumber mosaic virus (CMV) and tobacco mosaic virus (TMV)-derived vsiRNAs. RDR6 plays a role in defending against CMV [23], whereas RDR1, RDR2 and RDR6 appear to play redundant roles in the synthesis of turnip mosaic virus (TuMV) and tobacco rattle virus (TRV)-vsiRNAs [25]. With respect to DNA viruses, RDR6 together with Suppressor of Gene Silencing 3 (SGS3) is involved in responses to cabbage leaf curl virus (CaLCuV) [26]. SGS3 is a dsRNA binding protein, which prefers to bind 5'-overhang dsRNA [27], and interacts with RDR6 to synthesize secondary siRNAs [26, 28, 29]. Given the strong pressure exerted by RNA silencing, plant viruses have evolved viral suppressors of RNA silencing (VSRs), which interfere with the RNA silencing machinery at multiple steps [30]. Several reports have demonstrated that the V2 protein of TYLCV functions as a VSR that can supress PTGS and TGS [31–33].

Despite the identification of the Ty-1 protein as an RDR, the mechanism by which it defends against TYLCV is not clear and a role for Ty-1 in RNA silencing has not been established. Here, by using a transient expression assay, we have confirmed that Ty-1 shows antiviral activity against TYLCV in N. benthamiana. Also, in transient expression-based silencing assays, Ty-1 augmented systemic transgene silencing in GFP transgenic N. benthamiana plants. Furthermore, co-expression of Ty-1 or other RDRγ proteins from N. benthamiana or Arabidopsis with TYLCV V2 or other transgenes resulted in a reduction in the expression multiple transiently expressed transgenes. These results suggest that Ty-1-mediated resistance to TYLCV involves a mechanism wherein it contributes to an augmentation in RNA silencing activity.

Materials and methods

Plant material and growth conditions

N. benthamiana wild type, GFP transgenic lines and tomato plants were grown from seed in soil (BM6, Berger) in a growth chamber at 25 °C and 60% relative humidity with 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod.

Plasmids construction

Full-length coding sequences of Arabidopsis DCP2, AtRDR3, AtRDR5 and N. benthamiana NbRDR5a and NbRDR5b were obtained by RT-PCR. Artificially synthesized full-length coding sequences of Ty-1 and ty-1 (Genscript, Nanjing, China) were used as templates for plasmid construction. To generate pEAQ-YFP-Ty-1/ty-1, digested inserts were cloned into the XmaI and XbaI sites of pEAQ-YFP. pBIN61-V2:HA construct was generated by amplifying the V2 ORF by RT-PCR from TYLCV-infected plants. Total RNA was extracted from 0.1 g tissue of N. benthamiana or Arabidopsis using Trizol reagent as per the manufacturer’s instructions (Invitrogen). RT was performed by using SuperScript II First-Strand Synthesis SuperMix Kit (Invitrogen). PCR amplification was conducted by using cloneAmp high-fidelity DNA polymerase (Takara). Primer sequences are listed in Additional file 1: Table S1. PCR fragments were either cloned by digestion with the appropriate restriction enzymes or directly using In-Fusion HD Cloning Kit as per the manufacturer’s instructions (Takara, Bio USA). All constructed vectors were verified by sequencing.

Binary vectors encoding mGFP5, P19, EV, and TYLCV have been previously described [34, 35].

Agrobacterium-mediated transient expression and virus inoculation

Agrobacterium tumefaciens-mediated transient expression assays in N. benthamiana plants were performed as previously described [36]. Briefly, Agrobacterium strain C58C1 carrying pCH32 virulence plasmid was transformed with binary vectors expressing TYLCV or the various proteins including, P19, mGFP5, YFP-tagged Ty-1 and ty-1, HA-tagged Ty-1, ty-1, AtRDR3, AtRDR5, NbRDR5a, NbRDR5b, and V2. Agrobacterium cultures were centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min and then resuspended in 10 mM MgCl2 to a final OD600 = 0.3 and 0.2 for protein expression and TYLCV infection, respectively. GFP was visualized by using a handheld UV lamp (BLAk, RAY, UVP).

Quantitative RT-PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from the indicated N. benthamiana leaf tissues by Trizol reagent as per the manufacturer’s instructions (Invitrogen). 1 μg total RNA was used in each 20 μL reaction using PrimeScript RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara, Dalian, China), followed by qPCR on a Bio-Rad CFX Connect Real-Time PCR system using TB Green Premix Ex Taq (Tli RNaseH Plus) (Takara, Dalian, China). The Nb-60 s ribosomal protein L23 gene was used as internal normalization standard. Primers used for Ty-1 and ty-1 were Ty-qpcr-F/Ty-qpcr-R. The primers used for Nb-60 s ribosomal protein L23 gene were L23-60 s-F/L23-60 s-R (Primers used in this study are listed in Additional file 1: Table S1).

SDS-PAGE and western blotting

Leaf tissues from N. benthamiana was ground in liquid nitrogen. Fifty mg of the ground powder was mixed with 50 ul 1.5 × sample buffer (18 uM Tris–HCl pH 6.8, 7.5% glycerol, 0.6% SDS, 0.3 mg/ml Bromophenol blue, 5mM DTT) and then incubated at 95 °C for 5 min. Protein samples from total leaf tissue extracts were separated by SDS-PAGE on 10.5% resolving gels and transferred onto a polyvinylidenedifluoride membrane (BioRad), followed by incubation with anti-HA/FLAG/GFP horseradish peroxidase (HRP) conjugated antibodies (Sigma-Aldrich; 1:3000 dilution) and proteins were revealed by western blotting luminol reagent (Santa Cruz Biotechnology).

Northern blotting

Total RNA was extracted from N. benthamiana plants using Trizol reagent as per the manufacturer’s instructions (Invitrogen). 15–20 μg of total RNA was separated on a 1.2% (w/v) formaldehyde agarose gel by electrophoresis, transferred to a positively charged nylon membrane (Roche) and cross-linked with UV light (Spectro linker, XL-1000 UV Crosslinker). Pre-hybridization, hybridization (Roche, DIG Easy Hyb) and washing (Roche, DIG Wash and Block Buffer Set) were performed as per the manufacturer's instructions. DNA probes were labeled with Dig-11-dUTP (Roche, PCR DIG Probe Synthesis Kit) according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Roche). A TYLCV-C4 probe were generated by PCR amplification using primers C4-F/C4-R. PCR products were revealed by CDP-Star ready-to-use (Roche). Signal intensities were quantified using Image J software.

Microscopy

Agrobacterium strain C58C1 carrying pCH32 virulence plasmid was transformed with binary vectors expressing PEAQ-YFP-Ty-1 or PEAQ-YFP-ty-1. Agrobacterium cultures were then infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves. Sixty hours post agroinfiltration (hpai), YFP signals were observed by microscopy. Confocal laser scanning microscopy was performed on a FV300 imaging system (Olympus). YFP was excited using a 488 nm argon laser and emission was detected using a 510–530 band-pass filter. Representative images are shown from experiments repeated at least three times.

Results

Transient over-expression of Ty-1 or ty-1 inhibits TYLCV accumulation in N. benthamiana

Alignment of the amino acid (aa) sequences of Ty-1 (resistant allele from tomato TY52) and ty-1 (the susceptible allele from tomato cv. Moneymaker) revealed only small differences between the two (97.84% aa identity). The most significant difference between Ty-1 and ty-1 is a four aa deletion in the N-terminal domain of the susceptible allele (Fig. 1A). TYLCV inoculation induced severe symptoms, including leaf curling and yellowing on ty-1 the expressing tomato line Moneymaker, but not on the Ty-1 expressing line TY52 (Fig. 1B). Previous reports have shown that the expression level of Ty-1 in resistant tomato lines is significantly higher compared to the susceptible ty-1 allele [13]. As such, we tested whether Ty-1 and ty-1 might have different anti-TYLCV abilities when overexpressed in a transient assay in N. benthamiana leaves, independent of the native promoter. Here, a functional approach was carried out, in which Ty-1 and ty-1 proteins fused to and HA epitope (35S:Ty-1/ty-1-HA) were transiently expressed together with TYLCV in N. benthamiana leaves (Fig. 1C). At 3 dpi, northern blotting of total RNAs from the infiltrated leaf patches showed that transient overexpression with either Ty-1 or ty-1 resulted in a lower accumulation of TYLCV, compared to expression with 35S-GUS as a control (Fig. 1D). Ty-1 and ty-1 increases systemic RNA silencing in GFP transgenic plants

To determine if Ty-1 or ty-1 affect the long distance spread of RNA silencing, we compared Ty-1, ty-1, empty vector (EV) and P19 for their ability to affect GFP silencing in systemic leaves of GFP-expressing transgenic N. benthamiana 16c plants. P19 encoded by the tomato bushy stunt virus (TBSV) is well known to function as a suppressor of gene silencing in plants as well as in animals [37, 38]. Agroinfiltration of GFP-expressing N. benthamiana 16c plants with an Agrobacterium strain harboring p35S-GFP (35S:GFP) results in silencing of the transgene in the infiltrated patch, which eventually spreads to the rest of the plant, except when certain VSRs are co-expressed [37, 38]. We performed this assay, by co-expressing 35S:GFP in 16c leaves together with 35S:EV, 35S:P19, 35S:Ty-1, or 35S:ty-1. As expected, at 13 dpi, the newly emerging leaves of a portion (7/28) of the plants infiltrated with EV plus 35S-GFP showed silencing of the GFP transgene, whereas those expressing P19 showed almost no systemic silencing (Fig. 3). In contrast, plant expressing 35S-GFP with 35S:Ty-1 or 35S:ty-1 showed clear patterns of systemic silencing in a higher portion of plants (Fig. 3A, lower panel). Both Ty-1 and ty-1 reduce the expression levels of transiently expressed transgenes in N. benthamiana

Previous studies have shown that resistant tomato plants carrying Ty-1 show low virus titers upon infection with TYLCV, concomitant with the production of relatively high levels of TYLCV-derived siRNAs, compared to susceptible tomato Moneymaker (MM) [39]. At the same time, it has been reported that the TYLCV VSR V2 can interact with, and likely inactivates, SGS3, therefore blocking the silencing pathway [40]. These results suggest that RdDM is important for defense against TYLCV and that Ty-1 may increase the plant’s ability to target TYLCV by this mechanism [39]. We therefore tested the effect of transiently overexpressing FLAG-tagged Ty-1 and ty-1 proteins together with TYLCV V2 fused with an HA tag in N. benthamiana leaves (Fig. 4A). At 3 dpi, total protein was extracted from the infiltrated leaf patches for immunoblotting analysis. Immunoblotting result showed that transient overexpression with Ty-1-flag or ty-1-flag resulted in a lower accumulation of V2-HA, compared to expression with empty alone as control (Fig. 4B). Although the effects of Ty-1 and ty-1 on V2 may be due to a direct effect, it is also possible that their RDR activity might affect transgene expression in general. Discussion

In our study, we showed that transient over-expression of Ty-1 with TYLCV in N. benthamiana resulted in lower TYLCV accumulation, which is consistent with previous results that stable Ty-1 transformants of N. benthamiana and tomato showed fewer symptoms and reduced viral titres upon infection, compared to wild-type plants [37]. Previous studies have shown that the Ty-1 gene is functional against geminiviruses but is found only in a small number of tomato cultivars. However, non-functional variants of Ty-1 appear to exist in all tomato cultivars. Here we sought to understand why some variants of the Ty-1 gene function whereas others do not. We thus cloned the ty-1 susceptible allele from tomato cv. Moneymaker. Interestingly, transiently expressing TYLCV with either Ty-1 or ty-1 in N. benthamiana resulted in decreased TYLCV accumulation (Fig. oneD). This result indicated that Ty-1 and ty-1 may both possess an intrinsic ability to restrict TYLCV accumulation when expressed at high levels (Fig. oneC). Consistent with this, we found the subcellular localization of Ty-1 and ty-1 showed no obvious differences (Fig. two). Two lines of evidence lead us to conclude that the Ty-1 and ty-1 alleles display different degrees of resistance to TYLCV in tomato because of their different expression levels in tomato. Firstly, the sequences of Ty-1 and ty-1 are highly similar (97.84% aa identity), although there were four amino acids insertion at the N terminal in the Ty-1 gene (Fig. oneB), however, a previous study have showed that the four amino acids insertion in Ty-1 is not exclusively linked to TYLCV resistance [41]. Second, regardless of the presence of the virus, the relative expression level of Ty-1 is significantly higher compared with the susceptible allele ty-1 [13, 41], which indicates that the anti-TYLCV function of Ty-1 is associated with its expression level. Our results showing that ty-1 has potential to function as an antiviral protein, are pertinent to efforts to engineer more effective versions of Ty-1 protein that can confer resistance to TYLCV in tomato and possibly to related viruses in other crop plants. Likewise, our demonstration that additional Ty-1 homologues from other species have the potential to function in RNA silencing (Fig. 5) open the possibility of using these proteins to generate virus-resistant plants.

Several results from previous studies indicate that the Ty-1 gene product is involved in RNA silencing. Firstly, TYLCV vsiRNA levels are higher in resistant tomato lines, wherein there was significantly elevated level of 22- and 24-nt size TYLCV-vsiRNAs in TYLCV-infected systemically leaves of Ty-1 resistant tomato plants compared to susceptible tomato Moneymaker (MM) [42–44] and increasing cytosine methylation of viral DNA [13]. Secondly, Ty-1 resistance against geminiviruses has been reported to be compromised by three VSRs, including the 2b protein of CMV, βC1 of ageratum yellow vein betasatellite (AYVB) and βC1 of tomato yellow leaf curl China betasatellite (TYLCCNB) [39, 42]. CMV-2b and TYLCCNB-βC1 inhibit transcriptional gene silencing (TGS) by different mechanisms [39, 42]. Consistent with these results, we show that both Ty-1 and ty-1 increase GFP silencing signal in systemic leaves of the infiltrated GFP-transgenic N. benthamiana 16c plants. Thus, we suggest that Ty-1 and ty-1 are involved in S-PTGS. However, whether Ty-1 or ty-1 enhances TGS leading to the methylation of the targeted DNA sequences through the 24-nts siRNAs remains to be demonstrated.

In RDR-mediated RNA silencing, the SGS3 protein interacts with RDR6, which is required by RDR6 to convert single-stranded RNA (from both endogenous and exogenous sources) to dsRNA to produce siRNAs [27–29]. V2 of TYLCV can directly interact with SGS3 and may compete with RDR6, leading to the inhibition of the RNA binding activity of SGS3 and reduced RNA silencing activity [40]. V2 encodes a multi-functional protein involved in virus movement, in the suppression of PTGS and methylation-mediated TGS [40, 45]. A previous study has reported that TYLCV V2 can be targeted by the cytoplasmic and nuclear degradation machineries, including proteases, ubiquitin 26S proteasome, and the autophagy pathway [46]. Previous study showed that RDRγ might function indirectly by promoting bidirectional transcription of the entire virus genome that might generate dsRNA substrates for all three DCLs, RDRγ might also convert ssRNA templates into dsRNA substrates preferentially processed by DCL2 [44]. Consistent with these results, in our study, V2 and DCP2 expression levels where reduced when co-expressed with Ty-1 or ty-1 or other RDRγ proteins, including Arabidopsis AtRDR3, AtRDR5 and N. benthamiana NbRDR5a and NbRDR5b in a transient assay (Fig. 4A-B, Fig. 5). These results suggest that Ty-1 mediated resistance against TYLCV could act by affecting the expression levels of V2 directly, possibly through inhibiting the V2/SGS3/RDR6 complex by competing with RDR6. Alternatively, it is likely that Ty-1 functions indirectly with its RDR activity viral RNAs through the generation of increased levels of siRNAs, which in turn target the viral genome. This would be consistent with the effects of Ty-1 on transient transgene expression as supported by our results that co-expression of Ty-1 and ty-1 resulted in lower levels of GFP as well as DCP2 (Fig. 4C-E).

TYLCV can avoid host defense mechanisms by sheltering in small/midsize aggregates, where viral proteins are less exposed to proteolysis [46]. It has been reported that TYLCV is detected in small aggregates in susceptible and resistant tomato lines at early stages of infection. Later in infection increased intermediate-sized aggregates emerge in resistance lines, whereas large aggregates appear to be characteristic in susceptible lines [45]. V2 was reported to appear as aggregates of increasing size during the viral infection progress [46], and V2 aggregates with SGS3 in pant cells [40]. According to these previous reports and our results in this study, we presumed that in the Ty-1 resistant tomato line Ty-1 expressed at high level, high expression of which directly increasing RNA silencing and then leading to the relatively reduced expression of V2, which is a VSR of TYLCV and the rest of V2 aggregates in intermediate-size.
