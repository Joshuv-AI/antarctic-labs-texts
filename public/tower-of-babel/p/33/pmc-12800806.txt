Surgical Delay Increases the Incidence of Ramp Lesions and Bucket-Handle Tears in Anterior Cruciate Ligament Injuries
Authors: Dong Hwi Kim, Do Kyung Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo investigate the correlation between surgical delay and the incidence of ramp lesions by performing meticulous arthroscopic evaluation of the posterior compartment in patients undergoing anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included patients who underwent primary ACL reconstruction between July 2015 and January 2023. To minimize underdiagnosis of ramp lesions, all patients received comprehensive arthroscopic evaluation of the posterior compartment via intercondylar notch view and posteromedial or posterolateral portals. Meniscal tears were classified on the basis of Cooper’s system, and radial and longitudinal tear components were analyzed separately. Patients were categorized into 3 groups according to time from injury to surgery: group 1 (<6 weeks), group 2 (6 weeks–6 months), and group 3 (>6 months). Incidence and tear patterns were compared among groups.ResultsA total of 201 patients were included in the study. The overall incidence of meniscal tears was lowest in group 2 (50%) compared with group 1 (73%; P = .02) and increased again in group 3 (75%; P = .04 vs group 2). Ramp lesions were significantly more common in group 3 than in group 2 (52.8% vs 23.1%; P = .02). Medial bucket-handle tears also increased significantly in group 3 (22.2%) compared to group 1 (2.9%) and group 2 (0%) (P < .01 and P = .02, respectively).ConclusionsA surgical delay of more than 6 months was associated with a higher incidence of medial ramp lesions, which can progress to bucket-handle tears in ACL-deficient knees. Early surgical intervention should be considered in ACL ruptures accompanied by rotational instability to prevent progression of medial meniscal pathology.Level of EvidenceLevel III, retrospective cohort study.

Longitudinal tears at the menisco-capsular junction of the medial meniscus, commonly referred to as ramp lesions, are associated with anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injuries.1,2 Recent studies have underscored the critical role of ramp lesions in contributing to both anterior and rotatory instability of the knee,1,3, 4, 5, 6 thereby garnering increased clinical and research interest. The proposed pathomechanism of ramp lesions is a contrecoup mechanism, in which a subluxated femur reduces during pivoting movements, leading to excessive stress at the menisco-capsular junction.1 Although the incidence of ramp lesions varies across studies, recent literature reported that these injuries may occur in up to 42% of patients with ACL ruptures.2,7, 8, 9, 10

Ramp lesions occur in the highly vascularized red-red zone of the meniscus, which theoretically confers a strong potential for self-healing.1 However, the altered biomechanics following an ACL rupture may result in repetitive pivoting movements, which can further hinder healing and lead to de novo ramp lesions or exacerbate pre-existing ones.8,11 In addition, the loss of ACL function leads to repetitive pathological pivoting movements, potentially contributing to the occurrence of ramp lesions, even in cases in which they were absent at the initial ACL injury.12

Previous studies have reported an increased incidence of medial meniscus tears in cases of delayed ACL reconstruction.8,13 The effect of surgical delay on the incidence of ramp lesions remains controversial, with some studies reporting no clear correlation, whereas others have demonstrated a higher incidence of ramp lesions in patients undergoing delayed ACL reconstruction.14, 15, 16 Moreover, the reported incidence of ramp lesions may vary depending on surgical timing and diagnostic methodologies. In particular, the sensitivity of MRI-based radiologic evaluation for detecting ramp lesions is estimated to be ∼71%17, potentially leading to underdiagnosis. The purpose of this study was to investigate the correlation between surgical delay and the incidence of ramp lesions by performing meticulous arthroscopic evaluation of the posterior compartment in patients undergoing ACL reconstruction. The hypothesis of this study was that surgical delay exceeding 6 months may increase the incidence of ramp lesions due to prolonged exposure to abnormal knee biomechanics.

Methods

Patient Selection

The inclusion criteria comprised patients who underwent primary ACL reconstruction performed by 2 experienced, fellowship-trained sports medicine surgeons (D.H.K. and D.K.L.) at 2 tertiary care institutions between July 2015 and January 2023. ACL reconstruction was indicated for patients diagnosed with ACL rupture on preoperative MRI who exhibited rotational instability—defined as a grade 1 or higher pivot-shift test—during physical examination under anesthesia prior to surgery. A total of 249 patients met these criteria and were initially included in the study. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB number: SCMC 2024-06-012).

The exclusion criteria for the study were as follows: (1) patients without arthroscopic images (n = 2), making retrospective analysis of meniscus injuries impossible; (2) patients with multiligament injuries or a fracture, in which the injury mechanism is different from the primary ACL ruptures (n = 37); and (3) patients with radiographic osteoarthritis classified as Kellgren–Lawrence grade II or higher (n = 4) or a history of ipsilateral knee joint surgery (n = 5), making it difficult to determine whether a meniscal tear occurred concomitantly with the ACL rupture.

After applying the exclusion criteria, 201 patients were enrolled in this retrospective cohort study (Fig 2). The demographic characteristics of the study population are presented in Table 1. Patients were divided into 3 groups based on surgical delay, which was defined as the time from initial trauma to ACL reconstruction. Patients were divided into 3 groups based on time from injury to ACL reconstruction: group 1 (<6 weeks), group 2 (6 weeks to 6 months), and group 3 (>6 months). The cut-offs were selected to reflect the acute postinjury period (<6 weeks), a subacute-to-intermediate phase (6 weeks–6 months), and a chronic phase (>6 months), consistent with clinical observations and previously published literature regarding the progression of secondary meniscal injury in ACL-deficient knees.13,20 In patients of group 2 and group 3 who underwent surgical treatment more than 6 weeks after injury, all cases exhibited grade I or higher rotational instability on pivot-shift testing at the initial outpatient visit. Surgical treatment was performed on the basis of rotational instability and was not intentionally delayed for study purposes. The reasons for surgical delay included (1) patient-related factors, such as scheduling conflicts or personal circumstances that prevented timely consultation or surgery; (2) cases in which initial symptoms improved after injury, but patients later presented to the hospital due to recurrent instability or knee pain during the follow-up period, resulting in delayed diagnosis and subsequent treatment; and (3) delayed referral from local clinics where the initial ACL injury was not accurately diagnosed.Fig. 2Flowchart.Table 1Demographic CharacteristicsGroup 1(n = 139)Group 2(n = 26)Group 3(n = 36)P ValueAge (year)30.5 ± 14.7(14-59 year)32.6 ± 16.1(15-57 year)30.4 ± 13.7(16-55 year).403∗GenderMale104 (74.8%)20 (76.9%)28 (77.8%).922†Female35 (25.2%)6 (23.1%)8 (22.2%)Side of injured kneeRight76 (54.7%)15 (57.8%)20 (55.6%).960†Left63 (45.3%)11 (42.2%)16 (44.4%)Duration from trauma to ACL reconstruction (days)16.4 ± 10(1-42 days)90.7 ± 35(49-171 days)863.5 ± 663.9(192-2540 days)<.001∗,1Injury MechanismNoncontact injury119 (85.6%)22 (84.6%)30 (83.3%).941†Contact injury or direct trauma20 (14.4%)4 (15.4%)6 (16.9%)Cause of injuryTwisting90 (64.7%)18 (69.2%)21 (58.3%).064†Slip down14 (10.1%)2 (7.7%)5 (13.9%)Jump and landing23 (16.6%)4 (15.4%)1 (2.8%)Miscellaneous12 (8.6%)2 (7.7%)9 (25%)‡Statistically significant (P < .05).∗ANOVA test.†Chi-Square test.

All surgical records and arthroscopic images were retrospectively reviewed by one of the authors (D.K.L.), who performed the procedures. The reviewer is a board-certified knee surgeon with more than 10 years of experience in arthroscopic surgery.

Diagnostic and Surgical Procedure

Prior to primary ACL reconstruction, the Lachman test and pivot shift test were performed on patients with suspected ACL tears based on MRI findings. Patients who exhibited grade I or higher on the anterior drawer test, Lachman test, or pivot shift test were diagnosed with ACL rupture or insufficiency and subsequently underwent primary ACL reconstruction.

In all patients undergoing ACL reconstruction, two surgeons performed a diagnostic arthroscopic examination via the anterolateral or anteromedial portal to assess for meniscal tears. One surgeon (D.H.K.) evaluated the posterior compartments using a 70° arthroscope passed through the intercondylar notch. The other surgeon (D.K.L.) used the same technique and additionally confirmed findings using a posteromedial or posterolateral portal as a secondary viewing portal. Because of the retrospective nature of the study, the specific portal used for the final diagnosis of each meniscal tear was not consistently recorded. If a meniscal tear was deemed unstable, meniscal repair was performed concurrently with ACL reconstruction.

Data Collection

A single surgeon (D.K.L.) retrospectively reviewed the surgical records of all patients. The type and location of meniscal tears were reconfirmed through arthroscopic image analysis. The meniscal tear locations were documented using Cooper’s classification system21 (Fig 3). Additionally, meniscus tears were further categorized into radial and longitudinal tear components for detailed analysis. Radial or oblique tears occurring within ∼1 cm of the meniscal root bony insertion were defined as root tears.22 The incidence of root tear was recorded as a subgroup of the radial tear component. Ramp lesions were defined as meniscocapsular injuries involving the posterior horn of the medial meniscus.1 Bucket-handle tear was defined as a longitudinal tear that continuously involves 2 adjacent zones, according to Cooper’s classification, and is considered unstable upon arthroscopic probing if the tear showed displacement into intercondylar notch or around the femoral condyle.23 The incidence of bucket-handle tears was recorded as a subgroup of the longitudinal tear component.Fig 3Cooper’s classification. Statistical Analysis

The χ2-test was performed to compare the incidence of meniscal tears among the groups. Statistical significance was defined as P < .05. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 20 (IBM). Significance was set at P < .05.

Results

Incidence of Meniscal Tears

Regardless of surgical delay, meniscal tears most commonly involved Cooper’s classification zone A and zone F across all groups (Fig 4). The incidence of meniscal tears was significantly lower in group 2 compared to group 1 (73% vs 50%; P = .02; Fig 5). However, in group 3, where surgical delay exceeded 6 months, the incidence of meniscal tears was significantly higher than in group 2 (50% vs 75%; P = .04; Fig 5). Discussion

In this study, delayed ACL reconstruction was significantly associated with a higher incidence of medial ramp lesions and bucket-handle tears, particularly in patients who underwent surgery more than 6 months after injury. Among patients with acute ACL injuries who underwent surgery within 6 weeks (group 1), the incidence of ramp lesions was observed to be as high as 42.4%, a rate nearly identical to that recently reported by Sonnery Cotet et al.10 In patients who underwent surgical treatment between 6 weeks and 6 months (group 2), the incidence was significantly lower compared to those treated within 6 weeks. This finding may be attributable to spontaneous healing initiated by bleeding and biological repair processes that occur shortly after the initial ACL injury. Sonnery-Cottet et al. previously reported that superficial ramp lesions, often concealed by synovial tissue, became more frequently detectable following minimal debridement using a shaver.10 These observations support the possibility that ramp lesions sustained at the time of ACL rupture may undergo partial or even complete healing over time, potentially accounting for the lower incidence observed in group 2. However, in patients who underwent surgery after 6 months (group 3), the incidence of ramp lesions was significantly higher than in those who had surgery between 6 weeks and 6 months (group 2). This suggests that repeated pivot shift motions in an ACL-deficient knee may lead to additional damage to the medial meniscus posterior horn. These findings indicate that ramp lesion may occur not only during the initial pivot shift motion at the time of injury but also secondarily due to repeated pathological pivot shift motions in untreated ACL-deficient patients.

Interestingly, our study found a statistically significant increase in the incidence of medial meniscus bucket-handle tears in patients who underwent surgery after 6 months (Group 3) compared to those who had surgery within 6 months (groups 1 and 2). Most of these tears exhibited an extension pattern from Cooper’s classification Zone A0 or A1 to Zone B0, B1, or B2. These findings suggest that if rotational instability or anterior translation following ACL injury is not appropriately addressed, repeated pivot shift motions may not only contribute to the development of ramp lesions but also cause their progression to involve the medial meniscus mid-body.24 This result underscores the importance of performing ACL reconstruction within 6 months to improve rotational instability and prevent secondary medial meniscus damage. This result is also consistent with the previous reports, indicating that bucket-handle tears are more common in the setting of chronic ACL deficiency.25

Previous studies have also demonstrated that surgical delay beyond 6 months increases the risk of medial meniscus tears in ACL rupture.13,26 Some research suggests that prolonged surgical delay may lead to meniscal degeneration, making meniscal repair unfeasible.20,27 Additionally, recent evidence highlights that ramp lesions contribute to both anterior and rotational instability.1,3, 4, 5, 6 When ramp lesions become irreparable, requiring meniscectomy, they may serve as critical factors for ACL failure and OA progression.28, 29, 30 Therefore, delayed surgery in ACL rupture accompanied by rotational instability should be carefully considered from in the context of OA prevention.20 Recent AAOS clinical practice guidelines also recommend early surgical intervention following anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) rupture, as additional meniscal or chondral damage may occur if surgery is delayed beyond 3 months.31 Recent study suggests that conservative treatment for ACL injuries may yield clinical outcomes comparable to surgical treatment.32 However, we believe that a uniform approach favoring conservative treatment may not be appropriate. As demonstrated in this study, delaying surgical intervention could lead to secondary meniscal damage and eventually increase the risk of OA progression.20 Therefore, applying conservative treatment to ACL rupture with rotational instability based solely on short-term outcomes may not be appropriate.

In the lateral meniscus, tears at the menisco-capsular junction occurred at a frequency nearly equivalent to that of ramp lesions in patients who underwent surgical treatment within 6 weeks (group 1). Additionally, although the incidence of lateral meniscus root tears was 12.9%, the incidence of longitudinal tears at the lateral menisco-capsular junction was notably higher at 40.3%. This finding differs from previous studies, which have reported that lateral meniscus root tears are the most common type of lateral meniscus injury.13,21,22,33 Moreover, there are limited data on longitudinal tears at the lateral menisco-capsular junction in existing literatures.13,21,22,33 The discrepancy between our findings and prior studies may be attributed to our surgical technique: in all patients, we used a 70° arthroscope passed through the intercondylar notch or viewed the posterolateral joint space via a posterolateral portal, enabling a more accurate assessment of this region. The mechanism of injury in this region can be understood through the pivoting injury mechanism. During the initial ACL injury, the pivoting motion induces posterior subluxation of the lateral femoral condyle over the lateral tibial plateau, which stretches and injures the menisco-capsular junction. Although this area has historically received little attention, recent biomechanical studies suggest that tears in this region may contribute to an increased pivot shift grade,5 highlighting the need for further clinical research. In our study, the incidence of these tears in patients who underwent surgery within 6 weeks (Group 1) was comparable to that of ramp lesions. However, in patients who underwent surgery between 6 weeks and 6 months (group 2), the incidence of these tears, like ramp lesions, significantly decreased. Interestingly, the incidence further declined in patients who underwent surgery after 6 months (group 3), showing a pattern distinct from that of ramp lesions. Given that chronic ACL deficiency involves repeated pivoting motions, the observed decrease in tear incidence over time is theoretically difficult to understand. However, our analysis suggests that these tears, due to their location in a hypervascular region, may have relatively high self-healing potential. Therefore, although longitudinal tears at the menisco-capsular junction of the lateral meniscus may resemble medial ramp lesions in morphology (Fig 1), our findings suggest that surgical delay does not appear to increase their incidence or lead to a higher occurrence of bucket-handle tears. This implies that such lesions in the lateral meniscus may follow a different clinical course than ramp lesions in the medial meniscus. Considering this, meniscal repair at the lateral menisco-capsular junction may be selectively considered, particularly when the tear does not demonstrate a highly unstable pattern. Nevertheless, there remains a possibility that the injured area may heal with residual laxity in the posterolateral joint space, which may not be detectable through arthroscopic examination. Currently, no standardized method exists to quantitatively assess posterolateral joint space laxity. Further research is needed to determine the clinical impact of these tears and their association with posterolateral joint space laxity and rotational instability.

One of the strengths of this study is that we assessed the incidence of meniscus tears at the menisco-capsular junction in both the posteromedial and posterolateral joint spaces in all patients, either by passing through the intercondylar notch or using a posterior portal as a viewing portal. This approach enabled us to identify a distinct pattern of meniscal tear incidence compared to previous studies on ACL injuries. Additionally, our findings indicate that surgical delay in untreated ACL deficiency with rotational instability may increase the incidence not only of medial ramp lesions but also of medial bucket-handle tears. Therefore, evidence for the justification of early intervention in ACL rupture could be confirmed. Furthermore, we confirmed that the most commonly observed meniscal tears in ACL ruptures occur at the posterior menisco-capsular junction in both the lateral and medial meniscus, with distinct trends based on surgical timing. Specifically, our results suggest that the medial meniscus is more vulnerable to secondary injuries (e.g., ramp lesions or bucket-handle tears) in the presence of residual rotational instability, whereas the lateral meniscus demonstrates a greater self-healing potential. These findings suggest that different meniscal repair strategies should be considered depending on the location of the meniscus tear. Therefore, a more aggressive repair approach may be warranted for medial meniscus tears, whereas a more conservative repair strategy may be appropriate for lateral meniscus tears unless they present with a highly unstable pattern.

Limitations

A limitation of this study is the relatively small sample size, which may have prevented some findings from reaching statistical significance. Larger-scale studies may be required to validate the statistical significance of these results. Another limitation of this study is its cross-sectional design, which precludes longitudinal observation of individual patients. Consequently, it remains unclear whether meniscal tears, including ramp or bucket-handle lesions, occurred at the time of the initial ACL injury or developed later due to chronic instability and abnormal knee biomechanics, resulting from ACL insufficiency. Lastly, as this study focused on the arthroscopic evaluation of meniscal tear incidence during anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction, postoperative outcomes such as patient-reported measures, satisfaction, and anterior or rotational instability were not analyzed. Accordingly, the impact of concomitant meniscal injuries on postoperative clinical outcomes remains undetermined.

Conclusions

A surgical delay of more than 6 months was associated with a higher incidence of medial ramp leions, which can progress to bucket-handle tears in ACL-deficient knees. These findings suggest that early surgical intervention should be considered in ACL ruptures accompanied by rotational instability to prevent progression of medial meniscal pathology.
