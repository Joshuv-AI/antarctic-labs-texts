Age-associated niche remodeling drives dental pulp progenitor dysfunction and fibrosis
Authors: Xiaoyi Yu, Tianmeng Sun, Mengyu Xu, Tianyuan Zhao, Huanyu Luo, Qing Zhong, Cangwei Liu, Zhengwen An
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDental pulp homeostasis and regeneration rely on specialized mesenchymal populations, yet aging promotes fibrotic remodeling and functional decline. Here, using the continuously growing mouse incisor as an experimentally tractable model, we integrate single-cell RNA sequencing, spatial transcriptomics, lineage tracing, and functional assays to investigate age-associated pulp remodeling. We identify a distinct Lypd1+ fibroblast progenitor population that is particularly vulnerable to age-associated dysfunction. Lineage tracing and trajectory analysis show that these cells originate from the Sfrp2hi stem cell pool, while functional studies of MACS-isolated human LYPD1+ cells provide supportive evidence for a related progenitor-like state. Notably, early aging preferentially affects Lypd1+ progenitors, rather than Sfrp2hi stem cells, promoting fibrotic conversion through aberrant upregulation of 11β-HSD2, which enhances aldosterone-mediated mineralocorticoid receptor (MR)-associated signals. Additionally, aging promotes the accumulation of Ccl4+ macrophages that establish a pro-inflammatory niche, where macrophage-derived PDGFB induces 11β-HSD2 via the P38 MAPK signaling. In vivo inhibition of the PDGF/P38/11β-HSD2 axis attenuates age-related pulp fibrosis. Together, our findings indicate that age-associated pulp fibrosis in this model is driven, at least in part, by niche-mediated metabolic reprogramming of progenitor cells rather than intrinsic stem cell exhaustion, highlighting corticosteroid metabolism as a potential therapeutic target for fibrotic degeneration in aging dental pulp. Subject terms: Stem cells, Immunology

Introduction

Tooth integrity and dental pulp tissue homeostasis rely on specialized mesenchymal stem cell populations and their lineage-restricted progenitors1–8. However, aging or chronic pathological stress9–11 progressively erodes the functional competence and regenerative capacity of these cells, leading to aberrant tissue remodeling and fibrotic degeneration12–14. Clinically, dental pulp fibrosis represents a prominent age-associated pathology, with approximately 25% of aged human pulps exhibiting progressive collagen accumulation. This fibrotic remodeling is characterized by marked reductions in cellularity, vascularization, and neural components15,16, which may compromise pulp defensive and regenerative capacity. In addition, pulp canal calcification associated with fibrotic remodeling can substantially increase the complexity and failure risk of endodontic treatment in elderly patients. Elucidating the molecular mechanisms that drive pulp cells from homeostatic to a fibrotic state is therefore essential for understanding pulp aging and for developing effective regenerative and antifibrotic therapeutic strategies.

Metabolic reprogramming is a crucial aspect of fibrosis development, where shifts in cellular metabolism directly influence the activation of immune cells and fibroblasts17. Systemic metabolic disturbances, such as obesity, type 2 diabetes, and metabolic syndrome, are also strongly linked to an increased risk of fibrosis in multiple organs18. These conditions are characterized by insulin resistance, altered adipokine signaling, and chronic inflammation, which further promote fibrotic processes through pathways mediated by TGF-β, PDGF, and other pro-fibrotic factors19. Elevated levels of insulin and inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α and IL-6, activate fibrogenic pathways in the liver, heart, and kidneys, enhancing ECM production and contributing to organ dysfunction20. The systemic metabolic dysregulation in these conditions highlights the need for a comprehensive understanding of fibrosis as both a local and systemic disorder.

Recent studies have highlighted mineralocorticoid receptor (MR) activation as a key driver of fibrosis, particularly in the context of chronic inflammation and metabolic dysregulation21,22. MR activation has been shown to exacerbate fibrosis in various tissues, including the heart, kidneys, and liver, by promoting the secretion of pro-fibrotic cytokines, such as TGF-β, and stimulating ECM production23–25. In fibrotic tissues, MR activation can enhance the transition of fibroblasts to myofibroblasts, the key cell type responsible for ECM synthesis, by upregulating collagen production and α-SMA expression26. Moreover, MR signaling contributes to the polarization of macrophages toward a pro-inflammatory (M1) phenotype, which further drives fibrogenesis through the release of inflammatory mediators27,28.

Targeting MR signaling, alongside metabolic reprogramming and immune modulation, offers potential for therapeutic intervention in fibrosis. In this study, we identify LYPD1⁺ fibroblasts as a progenitor population localized within the pulp stroma that is conserved across species and persists in both young and aged human teeth, yet is particularly vulnerable to age-associated dysfunction. By examining the immune microenvironment, metabolic reprogramming, and MR activation, we aim to uncover insights into the mechanisms driving fibrosis in aging dental pulp tissue. These findings could inform strategies to reverse fibrosis and mitigate degenerative changes, with potential applications in regenerative medicine and age-related tissue repair.

Results

Single-cell transcriptome profiling identified dental pulp cell heterogeneity and aging-related phenotypes

To assess age-related changes in dental pulp phenotypes, we harvested mandible tissues from mice across three developing stages: young (postnatal 5 days), homeostasis (7–8 weeks), and middle-aged (8–12 months), and performed a histological evaluation using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), Masson’s Trichrome, and Picrosirius Red (PSR) staining on sagittal tissue sections. We observed an accumulation of collagen-like deposition within the labial and lingual cervical loop regions in middle-aged specimens (Fig. 1A), which were previously characterized as specialized stem cell niches essential for maintaining tissue homeostasis and orchestrating regenerative responses1–4. This age-associated fibrotic transition was further observed in human pulps, characterized by an increase in collagen deposition (stained vivid blue) forming dense bundles, and a significant reduction in cellular density (stained red/purple) (Fig. 1B).

To investigate the molecular dynamics underlying dental pulp aging, we obtained single-cell suspensions from pulp tissues at three different age stages and performed single-cell RNA sequencing using the 10X Genomics platform. Sequencing reads in fastq format were processed with the CellRanger pipeline, initially capturing 37,680 cells. Following stringent quality control measures and duplicate removal using the Seurat package, a total of 28,887 high-quality cells were retained for further analysis. Utilizing uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP), we delineated 19 distinct cell clusters, each of which maintained a consistent representation across all age groups (Fig. 1C). These clusters were further categorized into five distinct cell types, endothelial cells (Plvap+, Pecam1+), epithelial cells (Krt5+, Krt14+, Epcam+), fibroblasts (Lum+, Col1a1+, Dcn+), immune cells (Ptprc+, Lyz2+, C1qb+), and glial cells (Plp1+, Sox10+), based on their gene expression profiles29 (Fig. 1D, E). Proportional analysis revealed a significant decrease in fibroblasts accompanied by a corresponding increase in immune cells with advanced age (Fig. 1F), suggesting that these cell types are particularly susceptible to age-associated changes.

Identification of Lypd1⁺ progenitors with age-associated susceptibility

Given that fibroblasts are the predominant cell type in dental pulp, we systematically profiled the entire fibroblast compartment and resolved 17 transcriptionally distinct clusters based on canonical markers and functional signatures1,3,4,30 (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). Both Monocle pseudo-time analysis and CytoTRACE trajectory inference positioned Sfrp2⁺ fibroblasts at the root of the differentiation hierarchy, consistent with their established stem cell identity and niche localization1,3,4,30,31. Lypd1⁺ fibroblasts, characterized by reduced Sfrp2 expression (Supplementary Fig. 3A), were located immediately downstream of stem cells and upstream of transit-amplifying cells (TACs) (Ki67+, Top2a+) and pre-odontoblasts (Notum+) (Fig. 2B, C), indicating an intermediate progenitor role. Although Lypd1⁺ and Sfrp2⁺ fibroblasts share largely overlapping transcriptional programs, the stress-protective gene Gpx332 was selectively enriched in Sfrp2⁺ cells (Supplementary Fig. 3B), suggesting preferential stress resilience within the stem cell compartment. Based on cell trajectory inference, functional gene signatures, and established anatomical subregions, we categorized dental pulp cells into three major compartments: the apical region, comprising stem cells, progenitors, TACs; the specialized region, including pre-odontoblasts and pericytes; and the distal pulp region, consisting of stress-responsive fibroblast lineage (Ddx17, Piezo2, Egr1, Fn1, Fosb, Isg15) and other mature pulp cell populations (Fmod, Tnc, S100a11, Rab3b, Gpha2, Postn) (Fig. 2D).

Differential gene expression (DEG) analysis across fibroblast clusters revealed that Lypd1+ fibroblasts exhibited the most extensive transcriptional remodeling, with the largest number of both upregulated and downregulated genes compared to other fibroblast clusters (Fig. 2E, Supplementary Fig. 3C), indicating that this cluster is particularly susceptible to age-associated perturbation. As Lypd1+ fibroblasts were suggested as downstream derivatives of Sfrp2+ fibroblasts, which have been previously defined as a stem cell population in the dental pulp30,31, we next investigated their lineage relationship and stem cell potential. Using the Sfrp2CreERT2; Rosa26mTmG lineage-tracing mouse line, we found that Sfrp2-derived GFP⁺ cells were initially confined to the cervical loop region at 2 days after tamoxifen induction. After 30 days of lineage tracing, these cells slowly differentiated into pulp cells and odontoblasts under homeostatic conditions (Supplementary Fig. 4A). In contrast, in an incisor clipping model, as previously reported3, Sfrp2-derived cells progressively expanded and populated LYPD1⁺ regions (Fig. 2F) 48 h post-clipping. These findings suggest that the Sfrp2-Lypd1 lineage preferentially supports injury-induced regeneration rather than homeostasis. This niche architecture supports the designation of LYPD1⁺ fibroblasts as intermediate progenitors that bridge stem cells and differentiated pulp lineages. To further confirm their progenitor functional potential in vitro, magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS) was used to isolate LYPD1+ cells from human dental pulp. After osteo/odontogenic induction, LYPD1⁺ progenitors showed only mild changes in early ALP activity, whereas Alizarin Red S staining demonstrated robust mineralized nodule formation after 28 days of induction (Fig. 2G). Real-time PCR analysis further showed significant upregulation of mineralization- and odontogenic-associated markers, including DMP1, OPN, and RUNX2, with upward but non-significant changes in DSPP and ALP expression (Supplementary Fig. 4B). These findings indicate that LYPD1⁺ cells possess osteo/odontogenic differentiation potential, with a stronger late-stage mineralization response than early ALP activity under the current induction conditions. In contrast, these cells exhibited limited adipogenic potential after 35 days of adipogenic induction (Supplementary Fig. 4C), supporting their preferential mineralization capacity. We also examined the LYPD1− fraction for comparison. Real-time PCR analysis showed that LYPD1− cells exhibited stronger induction of general osteogenic markers, including ALP, OPN, and RUNX2, after 7 days of induction (Supplementary Fig. 4D). In contrast, the LYPD1⁺ fraction showed more pronounced and specific upregulation of odontogenic markers, such as DMP1 and DSPP (Supplementary Fig. 4D). These results suggest that the LYPD1− fraction represents a heterogeneous population, likely containing SFRP2hi stem cells together with more mature pulp cells, and may therefore display broader mineralizing potential. By comparison, the LYPD1⁺ subpopulation appears to represent a more committed progenitor population with a specialized capacity for odontogenic lineage progression. We next examined the spatial relationship between LYPD1⁺ cells and the vasculature and innervation by co-immunostaining LYPD1 with endothelial (CD31) and neural (Neurofilament) markers. Blood vessels and nerve fibers were broadly distributed throughout the pulp, whereas LYPD1⁺ cells were spatially restricted to the apical region of the dental mesenchyme (Supplementary Fig. 4E–G). This distribution is consistent with previous studies identifying this region as a well-established stem/progenitor niche rather than perivascular or nerve-associated identities in the mouse incisor pulp1–8.

To assess if the similar cell subset is mouse incisor specific, we re-analyzed publicly available scRNA-sequencing datasets from human dental papilla and adult human molar pulp (GSE146123, GSE164157, GSE185222, Supplementary Table 1), complemented by tissue staining from human dental samples collected at our dental hospital. In the human apical papilla, LYPD1 was enriched in Cluster 3, which co-expressed mesenchymal and odontogenic-associated markers, including IBSP, SFRP4, POSTN, and NNMT. In adult human molar pulp, LYPD1 was mainly detected in Cluster 0, which co-expressed stromal markers such as BEX5, TMSB15A, and POSTN (Supplementary Fig. 4H). GO enrichment analysis further indicated that the LYPD1⁺ cluster in human dental papilla was associated with tissue development-related processes, consistent with the developmental nature of apical papilla. Whereas the LYPD1⁺ population in adult molar pulp was interpreted within a mature stromal maintenance context (Supplementary Fig. 4H). Immunostaining further confirmed LYPD1 expression in human dental pulp samples (Supplementary Fig. 4I), supporting the presence of LYPD1⁺ stromal/progenitor-like cells across developing and mature stages in human teeth.

To further examine Lypd1 expression in mouse molars, we integrated publicly available mouse molar scRNA-seq datasets spanning developmental and adult stages (GSE189381 and GSE146123, Supplementary Table 1). Lypd1 expression was transiently detected during late embryonic and early postnatal molar development, peaked around the early postnatal stage, and declined to not readily detectable levels in available adult mouse molar datasets (Supplementary Fig. 4J). This temporal pattern coincides with active crown morphogenesis and early pulp formation, suggesting that Lypd1⁺ fibroblast-like progenitors may be associated with molar development and early pulp establishment, although these dataset-based observations require further orthogonal validation.

Together, within the mouse incisor model, these findings indicate that early aging preferentially targets Lypd1⁺ progenitors downstream of the Sfrp2⁺ DPSC lineage, rather than the stem cells themselves.

Elevated 11β-HSD2/MR signaling is associated with age-related pulp fibrosis

We next performed DEG profiling of Lypd1+ fibroblasts and found an age-associated upregulation of Hsd11b2 (Fig. 3A). This finding was validated by qPCR (Fig. 3B) and IF staining of middle-aged dental pulp tissues (Fig. 3C). Hsd11b2 is predominantly expressed in aldosterone-sensitive tissues, including the kidney, colon, and specific glands such as salivary and sweat glands33,34. This gene encodes 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 2 (11β-HSD2), an enzyme that inactivates glucocorticoids by converting cortisol to cortisone (or corticosterone to 11-dehydrocorticosterone in rodents), thereby restricting glucocorticoid access to the MR and favoring aldosterone-dependent MR activation, which ultimately leads to tissue fibrosis35–38. To characterize aging-associated metabolic alteration, we performed targeted LC-MS/MS profiling of steroid hormone-related metabolites in dental pulp tissue from mice across different age groups. Metabolomic analysis revealed a significant upregulation of aldosterone synthesis and secretion pathways in the dental pulp of middle-aged mice compared to young and homeostasis groups (Fig. 3D). Furthermore, we observed a pronounced increase in the level of 11-Dehydrocorticosterone with aging (Fig. 3E), consistent with elevated 11β-HSD2-mediated glucocorticoid inactivation. Additionally, single-cell sequencing data revealed a marked upregulation of Nr3c2 (encoding MR) specifically in middle-aged Lypd1⁺ fibroblasts, which was further corroborated by IF showing pronounced nuclear translocation of the MR protein (Fig. 3F, G). Together, these findings indicate that aging dental pulp is associated with 11β-HSD2 upregulation, altered corticosterone metabolism, and increased MR-associated signaling. MR-associated signaling may contribute to the fibrotic remodeling observed within dental pulp, consistent with the established roles of MR signaling in other fibrotic conditions, including glomerulosclerosis and cardiac fibrosis21,37. Notably, pharmacological blockade of MR activation has been demonstrated to attenuate aldosterone-induced pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic response, underscoring the pivotal role of MR activation in the pathogenesis of tissue fibrosis23,24,26. However, because MR binds glucocorticoids and aldosterone with similar affinity, these data do not distinguish whether MR activation in aging pulp is primarily related to increased aldosterone, altered corticosterone/cortisol metabolism, or a combination of both. We therefore present the 11β-HSD2/MR axis as a supported model associated with age-related pulp fibrosis.

Ablation of 11β-HSD2 in Lypd1+ progenitors reverses senescence-associated fibrosis in vitro and in vivo

To investigate whether the upregulation of 11β-HSD2 is a causative factor in pulp progenitor fibrosis, middle-aged mice were treated with 18β-Glycyrrhetinic acid (GA), an 11β-HSD2 inhibitor39, via drinking water for 30 days. This intervention effectively diminished both mRNA and protein levels of 11β-HSD2, as confirmed by qPCR (Supplementary Fig. 5A) and immunofluorescence staining on incisor sections (Fig. 4A). Notably, in vivo inhibition of 11β-HSD2 appeared to reverse age-related pulp fibrosis, as evidenced by Masson’s Trichrome and PSR staining (Fig. 4B). Targeted metabolomic analyses of paired plasma and dental pulp tissues from middle-aged mice showed that GA treatment increased corticosterone levels within the dental pulp, supporting a model in which local inhibition of 11β-HSD2 alters pupal corticosteroid metabolism and modulates MR-associated pro-fibrotic signaling. In contrast, systemic plasma levels of corticosterone and aldosterone were reduced after GA treatment (Fig. 4C). These systemic changes may involve the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal/renin–angiotensin–aldosterone (HPA/RAAS) feedback responses, but may also reflect broader effects of GA on steroid metabolism, given that GA is not fully selective for 11β-HSD2.

To further corroborate the specific role of 11β-HSD2 in driving pulp fibrosis, we employed siRNA-mediated knockdown of HSD11B2 in D-galactose (D-Gal) induced cellular senescence models (Fig. 4D). 300 mM D-Gal was proven to recapitulate the age-associated upregulation of senescence markers (P21, P16), fibrosis markers (COL3A1, ACTA2), and SA-β-galactosidase-positive cells, whilst downregulation of proliferation marker (KI67) (Supplementary Fig. 5B, C) in both human gingival fibroblasts (HGF) and mouse L929 fibroblasts. Strikingly, specific siRNA-mediated knockdown of HSD11B2 in these cells significantly attenuated age-associated alterations induced by D-Gal and the expression level of MR (Nr3c2) (Fig. 4E, F, Supplementary Fig. 5D). To further substantiate this mechanism in vivo, we utilized siRNA sequence-2, which effectively targets mouse Hsd11b2 (Supplementary Fig. 5D). After methoxy and cholesterol modification, each of three middle-aged mice received 4.5 nmol of siRNA injection daily for 2 consecutive days into the sub-palatal rugae area near the maxillary incisor apices. Controls received modified control siRNA. Consistent with in vitro findings, genetic suppression of Hsd11b2 significantly reversed the established fibrotic phenotype in middle-aged mice pulps, reducing Col3a1 and Acta2 expression to levels comparable to homeostatic mice (Fig. 4G). These data provide in vivo evidence that 11β-HSD2 hyperactivation contributes to age-related dental pulp fibrosis.

Ccl4+ macrophage-derived signals engage predominantly with Lypd1+ progenitors via the PDGF pathway

Dynamic and reciprocal interactions within the stem cell niche are essential for the maintenance and fate determination of stem cells and progenitors, involving both resident and infiltrating immune cells, in maintaining tissue homeostasis, driving disease progression, and initiating fibrotic responses18,28,40. Our single-cell RNA sequencing data have revealed a notable increase in immune cell populations in middle-aged incisors compared with young and homeostatic states (Fig. 1F), hinting at a potential connection between immune presence and pulp aging. Sub-clustering of these immune cells identified 8 distinct subpopulations (Fig. 5A, B). Among these, cluster 0, characterized as Ccl4+ macrophages through gene expression profiling, emerged as the most enriched population and demonstrated substantial expansion with aging (Fig. 5C), which was confirmed by flow cytometry analysis (Fig. 5D, Supplementary Fig. 6A). To further investigate how immune cells may regulate Lypd1⁺ fibroblasts during aging, we performed CellChat analysis using our scRNA-seq datasets. The overall information flow analysis identified PDGF signaling as one of the most prominent communication pathways (Fig. 5E). Source-target analysis further indicated that this signal was primarily transmitted from Ccl4⁺ macrophages to Lypd1⁺ fibroblasts (Supplementary Fig. 6B). Consistently, interaction strength analysis revealed a particularly strong interaction between Ccl4⁺ macrophages and Lypd1⁺ fibroblasts (Fig. 5F). Ligand-receptor analysis further highlighted the PDGFB-PDGFRA axis as the major mediator of this communication (Fig. 5G).

Given that age-associated fibrosis is largely driven by chronic inflammation (inflammaging), we next assessed the inflammatory landscape at single-cell resolution using our scRNA-seq datasets. We found that key pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, including Il1b, Tnf, and Ccl2, were selectively enriched in the Ccl4⁺ macrophage subset (Supplementary Fig. 6C), the same population identified as the principal source of the PDGFB signaling. Importantly, expression of these inflammatory mediators increases progressively from the homeostatic to the middle-aged group (Supplementary Fig. 6C). These results suggest that the fibrotic changes in aging dental pulp may be associated with the development of a localized pro-inflammatory niche. The age-dependent accumulation of CCL4⁺ macrophages, therefore, appears to be a notable feature of this niche, providing a potential link between chronic inflammation and PDGFB-driven fibrotic remodeling, consistent with and extending our previous findings41. Spatial transcriptomics demonstrated an age-associated spatial reorganization of macrophages toward Lypd1⁺ progenitor niches. In homeostatic dental pulp, macrophages were predominantly localized distal to the cervical loops (dark green region). In contrast, in middle-aged pulp, Ccl4⁺ macrophages (blue region) relocalized toward the Lypd1⁺ progenitor niche positioned between the labial and lingual cervical loops (Fig. 5H), thereby establishing a localized paracrine signaling microenvironment. Multiplex immunohistochemistry further confirmed the close spatial association between CCL4⁺ macrophages and LYPD1⁺ progenitors in middle-aged pulp tissue (Fig. 5I), whereas this interaction was not observed under homeostatic conditions (Supplementary Fig. 6D).

To directly assess whether the PDGF signaling drives aging-associated induction of Hsd11b2, recombinant PDGF-BB was locally injected into the palatal rugae adjacent to the maxillary incisor apical region of young mice, thereby mimicking the PDGF-enriched niche observed during aging. Quantitative PCR analysis of dissected apical pulp tissue revealed a significant upregulation of Hsd11b2 following PDGF-BB treatment compared with PBS controls (Fig. 5J). Consistently, in vitro stimulation of MACS-isolated LYPD1⁺ dental pulp progenitors from healthy young human donors with recombinant PDGF-BB resulted in marked suppression of osteo/odontogenic differentiation markers, including ALP, DMP1, DSPP, OPN, and RUNX2 (Fig. 5K). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that aging-associated expansion and spatial repositioning of Ccl4⁺ macrophages establish a PDGF-enriched niche that directly targets Lypd1⁺ progenitors, induces Hsd11b2 expression, and functionally compromises their regenerative potential.

Targeting the PDGF/ P38 MAPK pathway mitigates aging-associated dental pulp fibrosis

The PDGF pathway is a critical signaling network triggered by the binding of PDGF to its receptor on the cell surface, activating an intracellular cascade that includes the P38 MAPK pathway42. The P38 MAPK pathway responds to stress signals and inflammatory cytokines, influencing gene expression and playing key roles in cell differentiation, apoptosis, and autophagy43. Previous studies have demonstrated that P38 signaling can transcriptionally regulate HSD11B2 expression, linking extracellular signaling cues to local glucocorticoid metabolism39,44. To determine whether the PDGF-P38 signaling is activated in dental pulp tissue, we analyzed P38 phosphorylation using immunohistochemistry (IHC) and IF staining. Both analyses revealed increased P38 phosphorylation in middle-aged pulp compared with young controls (Fig. 6A, B). Notably, phosphorylated P38 showed a spatial association with LYPD1 in the cervical loop area in middle-aged pulp tissue compared to their homeostasis counterparts, indicating activation of the P38 pathway is age-associated within pulp progenitor cells (Fig. 6B). To establish a causal link between the PDGF signal and P38 activation in LYPD1⁺ cells, MACS-sorted human LYPD1⁺ fibroblast progenitors were treated with recombinant PDGF-BB in vitro. Western blot analysis demonstrated an increase in P-P38 level following PDGF-BB stimulation, confirming that PDGF signaling directly activates the P38 pathway in LYPD1⁺ progenitors (Fig. 6C). Next, we investigated whether the P38 signaling contributes to aging-associated HSD11B2 upregulation in vivo. Pharmacological inhibition of P38 MAPK using SB203580 resulted in a pronounced reduction in P38 phosphorylation, accompanied by decreased activation of its SMAD3 phosphorylation in dental pulp tissues (Fig. 6D, E), suggesting attenuation of pathway activity. Importantly, qPCR analysis revealed that inhibition of P38 signaling significantly reduced Hsd11b2 expression in middle-aged pulp tissue (Fig. 6F), functionally linking P38 activity to the regulation of local glucocorticoid metabolism during aging. Consistent with these findings, elevated HSD11B2 expression was also observed in aged human dental pulp compared with young controls, as confirmed by qPCR analysis (Fig. 6G).

To better align the human analyses with our proposed mechanism, we examined the expression of key axis-related components in human dental scRNA-seq datasets. Our analysis showed that the LYPD1⁺ subcluster in adult human molar pulp exhibited higher co-expression of PDGFRA and NR3C2 compared with the LYPD1⁺ population in human dental papilla (Supplementary Fig. 7). This correlative evidence suggests that a PDGF-responsive and MR-associated molecular context may be more prominent in mature human dental pulp than in developmental-stage dental papilla.

Together, these results support a model in which the PDGF-P38-11β-HSD2-MR signaling axis contributes to progenitor dysfunction and fibrotic remodeling during dental pulp aging in the mouse incisor model. Human dental pulp datasets provide correlative evidence for a related PDGF-responsive and MR-associated molecular context, but further validation will be required to determine whether this pathway is functionally conserved in human pulp aging.

Discussion

Using single-cell RNA sequencing, we constructed a high-resolution atlas of cellular remodeling in aging dental pulp, and uncovered an association between progenitor dysfunction and fibrotic transformation. Notably, while collagen deposition increases with age, the total proportion of fibroblasts declines. This structural shift reflects both the replacement of cells by dense collagen and the functional decline of the fibroblast pool itself. Among fibroblast populations, Lypd1+ progenitors emerged as the most age-susceptible cluster. These cells contribute to dental mesenchymal differentiation and odontoblast polarization45, yet our data show that aging preferentially reprograms this progenitor compartment rather than the upstream Sfrp2⁺ stem cell pool.

A defining feature of aged Lypd1+ progenitors is upregulation of Hsd11b2, which converts active cortisol to cortisone (and corticosterone to 11-Dehydrocorticosterone in rodents). Although GA treatment reduced age-associated pulp fibrosis and altered local corticosteroid metabolism, its effects may not be interpreted as exclusively pulp-restricted. GA is not fully selective for 11β-HSD2 and may influence steroid metabolism in other 11β-HSD2-expressing tissues, such as the kidney, colon, and glands. Therefore, the observed reductions in circulating corticosterone and aldosterone may reflect broader systemic endocrine or metabolic effects, in addition to possible HPA/RAAS feedback responses. Thus, while our findings support the involvement of local corticosteroid metabolism and MR-associated signaling in pulp fibrosis, further studies using more selective or locally delivered approaches will be required to define the relative contributions of pulpal versus systemic GA effects.

Because glucocorticoid cortisol circulates at concentrations 100–1000 folds higher than aldosterone and binds MR with similar affinity34,46, MR is typically occupied by glucocorticoid cortisol at basal levels under homeostatic conditions36. As observed in other non-epithelial tissues33, MR activation in fibroblasts leads to their transformation into myofibroblasts and ECM deposition47,48. While our data support the involvement of the 11β-HSD2/MR signaling axis in age-associated pulp fibrosis, our study does not directly demonstrate MR ligand predominance or cell-type-specific activation of MR target genes in aging pulp. Future studies using cell-type-resolved MR target analysis, selective MR perturbation, and direct assessment of ligand occupancy will be required to define the precise hormonal drivers of MR activation in aged dental pulp. We further identified a macrophage-driven regulatory layer via PDGFB. The multifaceted role of PDGFB in dental pulp homeostasis and regeneration is well recognized. Previous studies demonstrated that PDGF-BB signaling via PDGFR-β is essential for maturation of DPSC-derived blood vessels by regulating mural cell recruitment and stabilization in a regenerative microenvironment49. Our findings suggest that in the context of aging, PDGFB preferentially targets LYPD1⁺ progenitors to promote metabolic and fibrotic reprogramming.

Although our in vitro experiments using MACS-purified human LYPD1⁺ cells support the functional relevance of this progenitor population, the in vivo molecular changes observed after treatment are based primarily on bulk dental pulp analyses, spatial validation, and single-cell transcriptomic inference, rather than direct biochemical measurements from the targeted mouse Lypd1⁺ progenitor population. Future studies using optimized isolation of viable Lypd1⁺ cells, lineage-restricted perturbation, or cell-type-specific genetic approaches will be important to confirm whether these molecular changes occur cell-intrinsically within Lypd1⁺ progenitors.

The differential detection of LYPD1/Lypd1 across human teeth, mouse molars, and mouse incisors may reflect biological differences in developmental stage, tooth type, species, and regenerative capacity, as well as technical factors such as tissue sampling, sequencing depth, and cell recovery. These observations suggest that LYPD1/Lypd1 may mark a context-dependent stromal/progenitor state associated with tissue development, repair, or remodeling, rather than a constitutive population uniformly maintained across all tooth types. Human molars retain limited repair potential into adulthood, whereas mouse molars are largely non-regenerative after development. Thus, LYPD1⁺ cells may persist in human molars, while Lypd1 expression is not readily detected in available adult mouse molar scRNA-seq datasets. However, this dataset-based observation should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of absence without further orthogonal validation.

In this context, the continuously growing mouse incisor provides a valuable experimental system, rather than a direct equivalent of human or molar teeth. Its anatomically defined and persistent apical stem/progenitor niche enables mechanistic investigation of how Lypd1⁺ progenitors, downstream of the Sfrp2⁺ DPSC lineage, respond to aging-associated microenvironmental changes. However, we recognize that the mouse incisor has unique growth properties and cervical loop niche architecture that differ substantially from mouse molars and human teeth. Accordingly, conclusions derived from this model should not be directly generalized to human or molar pulp fibrosis without careful validation. Nevertheless, given the differences in growth mode, niche architecture, and regenerative capacity between mouse incisors and human teeth, further studies using larger human cohorts and additional molar-specific functional models will be required to determine the extent to which this mechanism contributes to dental pulp fibrosis across tooth types and species.

Materials and methods

Animal work

C57BL/6 female mice were obtained from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd., Laboratory animal use license No. SYXK(JI)2023-0010. The mice were housed in the Animal Experimentation Center of the College of Basic Medical Sciences of Jilin University. All animal experiments were approved by the Laboratory Animal Ethics Committee of Basic Medical Sciences, Jilin University (Approval No. 2023491). The mice were humanely euthanized using an overdose of carbon dioxide, followed by confirmation of death through cervical dislocation. We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use.

Human pulp samples

Human pulp samples were obtained from teeth extracted for clinical reasons, with ethical approval by the Jilin University Dental Hospital Review Board (Approval No. SJDKQ2025003). All ethical regulations relevant to human research participants were followed. Samples were collected from patients younger than 20 years (young, 14 premolars and 1 incisor) and older than 60 years (old, 5 premolars and 2 incisors) (Supplementary Table 2). After extraction, the teeth were immediately preserved in ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline. The pulp was then extracted using the split-crown method. For subsequent analysis, pulp tissue was either processed for RNA extraction and qPCR experiments or fixed, dehydrated, and embedded for frozen sectioning procedures.

Single-cell RNA sequence using the 10x Genomics Chromium platform

Dental pulp cells were isolated from different age groups (n = 5 mice per group), and single-cell preparation was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions for the 10x Genomics Chromium platform. Approximately 10,000 cells were captured and barcoded as single-cell gel beads-in-emulsion (GEMs) before sequencing on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 system. Single-cell cDNA libraries were constructed using the Chromium Single-Cell 5’ Reagent Kits v2, following the manufacturer’s protocols.

Single-cell RNA sequence data analysis

Raw sequencing data from mouse dental pulp were demultiplexed and aligned to the mouse reference genome (GRCm38 (mm10)), and processed to generate the cell-gene expression matrix using Cell Ranger version 7.0.1 (10x Genomics). Subsequent data analysis was performed using the R package Seurat (v5.0.0). Low-quality cells were excluded based on cell counts, the number of genes detected, and the percentage of mitochondrial gene expression (percent.mt<15 & nCount_RNA >500 & nFeature_RNA >500 & log10geneperumi>0.8). Contaminating ambient and background were removed using the decontX package (v1.0.0), and potential doublets were excluded with the R package scDblFinder (v1.14.0). For each sample, gene expression data were normalized and scaled using the “SCTransform” function in Seurat, and batch effects were corrected using the “RunHarmony” function.

To characterize the mobilization and differentiation of subpopulations in fibroblasts, the R package Monocle2 was used to perform pseudo-time trajectory inference for both stem cells and their derived cells. Marker genes of each subpopulation of fibroblasts were used to calculate the pseudo-time.

To evaluate cell-cell interactions across relevant clusters, the R package CellChat (v2.1.2) was used based on expression levels of ligands and receptors from the database CellChatDB.mouse. The “computeCommunProbPathway” function was used to infer cell-cell communication at the signaling pathway level. The Communication probability of signaling pathways was calculated by summing up the probabilities of their associated ligand-receptor pairs, which represents the interaction strength.

Spatial transcriptomics and MIA analysis

Spatial transcriptomic profiling was performed on mandibular incisor dental pulp tissues from mice corresponding to the same groups used for single-cell RNA sequencing, using the 10x Genomics Visium platform. Spatial data were integrated with single-cell RNA sequencing datasets to infer cell-type localization. Multimodal Intersection Analysis (MIA) was applied to evaluate enrichment of specific cell populations within defined spatial regions. Enrichment significance was calculated based on the cumulative hypergeometric distribution. MIA scores from all spatial samples were combined and visualized as heatmaps.

Metabolomics analysis

Biological samples, including dental pulp tissue from mice aged at P5, 7 weeks old and 8 months (n = 6 mice per sample) were collected for steroid hormone metabolomics analysis (with a mass greater than 20 mg per sample). For targeted steroid metabolomics, a total of 20 steroid metabolites were screened. Metabolite separation was performed using ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography coupled to a high-resolution mass spectrometer. Raw data were processed for peak alignment, feature detection, deconvolution, and metabolite identification50. Processed data were normalized using internal standards and total ion intensity. Differential steroid hormone metabolites were identified using Student’s t-test (p < 0.05). The resulting P values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure to control the false discovery rate. Significant regulation was defined by the combined criteria of a raw P value < 0.05, an FDR-adjusted q value < 0.05, and an absolute log₂ fold change > 1. The Differential Abundance Score (DA Score) was calculated using the following formula: DA Score = (Number of upregulated differential metabolites in the pathway − Number of downregulated differential metabolites in the pathway)/Number of all metabolites annotated to the pathway.

Histological examination

H&E staining, Masson’s Trichrome staining, and Sirius Red staining were performed on paraffin-embedded 4-μm tissue sections (n = 3 mice per group). Mouse mandibles were fixed, decalcified in 10% EDTA solution, and paraffin-embedded. Sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series before specific staining.

For H&E staining, sections were incubated with hematoxylin, differentiated, counterstained with eosin, dehydrated, and mounted with neutral gum.

Masson’s Trichrome staining was performed using a modified kit (Solarbio, G1346). Sections were stained with Mordant, Celestite Blue, Mayer’s hematoxylin, and Acid Differentiation Solution, followed by Ponceau-Acid Fuchsin, Phosphomolybdic Acid, and Aniline Blue. Finally, sections were differentiated in 1% acetic acid, dehydrated, cleared, and mounted.

Sirius Red staining was conducted using a modified kit (Solarbio, G1473). Sections were stained with a 1:1 mixture of Iron Hematoxylin Solutions A and B for 8 min, followed by 20 min of Sirius Red staining, then rinsed, dehydrated, and sealed.

Immunofluorescence (IF) and immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining

Paraffin-embedded sections (4 μm, n = 3 mice per group) were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval using an EDTA solution (Proteintech, PR30002) via microwave heating. After cooling to room temperature and washing with PBS, sections were permeabilized and blocked with 5% BSA.

For IF staining, sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with appropriate fluorescent secondary antibodies. The primary antibodies used included: LYPD1 (Rabbit, 1:100, Signalway, C27827-AF488; Rabbit, 1:100, Proteintech, 11415-1-AP), CCL4 (Rabbit, 1:20, Invitrogen, 710391), NR3C2 (Rabbit, 1:100, Invitrogen, PA5-79761), 11β-HSD2 (Rabbit, 1:400, Abcam, ab203132), P-P38 (Rabbit, 1:300, Cell Signaling Technology, 9211s), NF-L (Rabbit, 1:400, Proteintech, 12998-1-AP), CD31 (Rabbit, 1:400, Cell Signaling Technology, 77699s), and CD68 (Rabbit, 1:400, Proteintech, 25747-1-AP). Secondary antibodies included Fluoro594 (Goat, 1:500, Boster, BA1142), FITC (Goat, 1:500, Proteintech, SA00003-2), and Alexa Fluor 647 (Goat, 1:300, Abcam, ab150079).

For IHC staining, sections were incubated with the primary antibody (P-P38, 1:300) overnight at 4 °C. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with 3% H2O2. Subsequently, sections were incubated with biotinylated secondary antibodies and horseradish peroxidase-labeled streptavidin. Color was developed using a DAB kit, followed by hematoxylin counterstaining, dehydration, clearing, and sealing with neutral gum.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted using RNAeasy™ Animal RNA Isolation Kit with Spin Column (Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. cDNA synthesis was performed using Hifair® Ⅲ 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix for qPCR (YESEN). Quantitative PCR was carried out using Hieff® qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (YESEN). Primer sequences and numerical data are provided in Supplementary Table 3 and Supplementary Data 1.

In Vivo inhibition of HSD11B2 and the P38 pathway

To inhibit HSD11B2 expression, female, above 9-month-old mice were maintained on a normal diet and administered 18β-Glycyrrhetinic acid (75 mg/kg/day) in drinking water (n = 3) or drinking water alone (n = 3) for 30 days. 18β-Glycyrrhetinic acid was purchased from MedChemExpress. (HY-N0180). To inhibit P38 MAPK, mice were injected intraperitoneally with SB203580 (5 mg/kg/day) for the specified duration. SB203580 was purchased from MedChemExpress. (HY-10256).

Lineage tracing and incisor clipping model

Lineage tracing was performed using Sfrp2CreERT2;Rosa26mTmG mice generated by crossing Sfrp2CreERT2 mice (from the Chinese Institute for Brain Research, Beijing) with Rosa26mTmG reporter mice (Jackson Laboratory, JAX 007676). At 7 weeks of age, mice received tamoxifen (75 mg/kg, Beyotime, ST1682) by intraperitoneal injection for 5 consecutive days to induce recombination. Dental pulp tissues were harvested at 2, 15, and 30 days after the final tamoxifen injection. The incisor clipping model was performed by removing one-third of the erupted portion of the lower left incisor in adult mice 5 days after tamoxifen administration, and tissues were harvested 48 h after clipping.

D-Galactose-induced cellular senescence model

To establish an in vitro senescence model, L929 mouse fibroblasts and human gingival fibroblasts (HGFs) were treated with D-galactose (D-Gal; MedChemExpress, HY-N0210). Both cells were exposed to 300 mM and 500 mM D-Gal for 48 h for experimental exploration of concentration. Cellular senescence was evaluated by assessing the expression of senescence-associated genes (P16, P21), fibrosis-related genes (COL3A1, ACTA2), and the proliferation marker KI67 using qPCR, and by senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) staining (Beyotime, C0602).

Small interfering RNA-mediated gene silencing in vitro and in vivo

HGF and L929 cells were seeded in 6-well plates 1 day prior to transfection and cultured to 60–70% confluence. The transfection reagent (KELBiotech, KC112-1.5) was diluted (5 μL in 200 μL serum-free DMEM) and incubated for 5 min at room temperature. In another sterile EP tube, 100 pmol siRNA (GenePharma) was diluted in 200 μL serum-free DMEM and incubated for 5 min. The diluted transfection reagent was then added dropwise to the siRNA solution and incubated for 20 min at room temperature to form complexes, prior to adding to the adherent cells. After 8 h incubation at 37 °C, the medium was replaced with complete culture medium. Cells were harvested 48 h post-transfection for RNA extraction and downstream analyses. The siRNA exhibiting the highest knockdown efficiency, verified by qPCR and Western blot, was used for subsequent in vitro and in vivo experiments.

For the in vivo experiment, three middle-aged mice were anesthetized with inhalation of isoflurane using 2.5% for induction and 1–2% for maintenance in oxygen before receiving injections of 4.5 nmol of methoxy and cholesterol-modified siRNA (GenePharma) into the sub-palatal rugae near the maxillary incisor apices once daily for 2 consecutive days. Controls received modified control siRNA, and pulp samples were collected at day 3.

Flow cytometry

Mouse incisor dental pulp tissues (n = 3 mice per group) were freshly dissected and dissociated into single-cell suspensions as described above. Zombie Violet dye (BioLegend, 423113) was used for live/dead discrimination. Fc receptor blocking reagent (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-092-575) was applied to reduce nonspecific antibody binding. For surface antigen staining, cells were incubated with fluorophore-conjugated primary antibodies. For intracellular staining, cells were fixed and permeabilized (BioLegend, 421403) prior to antibody incubation. Antibodies used for flow cytometry were CD45-PE (1:100, Invitrogen, MA5-17964), CD68-APC (1:100, Invitrogen, MA5-23616), CCL4 (Rabbit, 1:50, Invitrogen, 710391), and FITC-conjugated Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (SA00003-2, Proteintech, 1:300). Data acquisition was performed using a MACSQuant Analyzer 16 flow cytometer (Miltenyi Biotec), and data were analyzed using FlowJo software.

Magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS)

Second-passage human dental pulp cells were dissociated using 0.25% trypsin, counted, and incubated with Fc receptor blocking reagent (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-092-575) prior to primary and secondary antibodies incubation according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Antibodies used for MACS were LYPD1 (Rabbit, 1:100, Proteintech, 11415-1-AP) and secondary antibody 647 (Goat, 1:300, Abcam, ab150079). Cells were then incubated with Alexa Fluor 647 MicroBeads (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-091-395), washed, and subjected to magnetic separation using LS columns (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-042-102, 130-042-401).

In vitro differentiation induction assays

Magnetically labeled LYPD1+ cells were collected and cultured in osteo/odontogenic (HyCyte, DPHX-D101) or adipogenic (HyCyte, DPHX-D102R) induction medium. Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining was performed on day 7, Alizarin Red S (ARS) staining on day 28, and Oil Red O staining on day 35. For functional assays, cells were treated with or without recombinant human PDGF-BB (10 ng/mL; Proteintech, HZ-1308). Cells were harvested 48 h post-treatment for Western blot and qPCR analyses.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism, employing unpaired Student’s t-tests or one-way ANOVA (parametric) tests, followed by post hoc tests as appropriate. p values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. *, **, *** and **** indicate P < 0.05, P < 0.01, P < 0.001, and P < 0.0001, respectively.
