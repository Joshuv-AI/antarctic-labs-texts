Dental and Maxillofacial Trauma During Civil Suppression: A Clinical Perspective
Authors: Seyed Ali Mosaddad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dental and maxillofacial injuries represent an important but underrecognised component of trauma during episodes of violent crowd control. Blunt and projectile forces directed toward the head frequently result in tooth fractures, luxation and avulsion injuries, alveolar fractures, facial soft tissue trauma and temporomandibular joint involvement. Management is often complicated by delayed presentation, restricted access to care, overwhelmed facilities and communication blackouts. Using recent events in Iran as a contemporary clinical example, this commentary outlines injury mechanisms, clinical challenges in disrupted environments and practical considerations for dentists providing care under constrained conditions. Improved awareness and preparedness within the dental profession may help to reduce long-term functional and psychosocial morbidity in comparable settings worldwide.

Introduction

Episodes of civil unrest involving aggressive crowd control measures are frequently associated with traumatic injuries.1,2 While life-threatening systemic trauma often receives primary attention, injuries to dentoalveolar structures and the maxillofacial region contribute substantially to long-term morbidity.

The face is particularly vulnerable during crowd control events due to exposure and upper body targeting.2, 3, 4 Dental and facial injuries may compromise mastication, speech, aesthetics and psychological well-being.5 Similar injury patterns have been reported in multiple regions affected by civil violence.2,3,6,7

This commentary discusses mechanisms of injury, challenges in management during systemic disruption and the clinical role of dentists in such contexts.

Mechanisms and patterns of injury

Blunt trauma from batons, shields, kicks, physical restraint or falls commonly produces enamel-dentin fractures, crown-root fractures, luxation injuries and alveolar bone fractures.8,9 High-energy impacts may result in mandibular or midfacial fractures with disruption of occlusion.10,11

Penetrating injuries from low-velocity projectiles may produce avulsion, intrusion, comminuted alveolar fractures and contamination of soft tissue with foreign material.12, 13, 14, 15 Adjacent structures, including the orbit and nasal cavity, may be involved. Trauma to the temporomandibular joint can lead to capsular injury, internal derangement or post-traumatic dysfunction, particularly when treatment is delayed.16,17

Clinical challenges in disrupted settings

In contexts of civil suppression, management is often hindered by delayed presentation, limited diagnostic resources, restricted mobility and overwhelmed facilities.18, 19, 20 Communication blackouts may disrupt referral pathways and interdisciplinary consultation.21,22

For avulsion and severe luxation injuries, prognosis is time dependent.8,13 Delayed treatment increases the risk of pulp necrosis, infection, occlusal instability and impaired fracture healing, thereby increasing treatment complexity and reducing favourable outcomes.

Practical considerations for care

In constrained environments, management may follow damage control principles.23, 24, 25 Priorities include airway assessment, haemorrhage control and identification of unstable maxillofacial injuries.

When imaging or standard equipment is unavailable, provisional measures may include gentle repositioning of displaced teeth or alveolar segments, temporary stabilisation using available materials and wound decontamination to reduce infection risk.8,26,27 These measures aim to preserve tissue viability until definitive care becomes accessible.

In avulsion injuries, immediate replantation when feasible offers the best prognosis.13 If this cannot be performed safely, efforts should focus on limiting contamination and preserving alveolar bone for future rehabilitation.

Clear patient instruction regarding oral hygiene, soft diet, signs of infection and the importance of follow-up is essential when continuity of care is uncertain.

Accurate documentation, including written notes and clinical photography when feasible, supports continuity of care and future rehabilitation.28,29 Ethical principles—confidentiality, autonomy and non-discrimination—remain fundamental regardless of setting.30

Global relevance

Dental and maxillofacial trauma associated with civil suppression represents a broader global health concern. Comparable injury mechanisms and healthcare disruptions have been documented in protest settings, conflict environments and disaster contexts worldwide.

Incorporating training on trauma management in austere and disrupted environments into undergraduate curricula and continuing professional development may improve preparedness. Development of pragmatic, resource-adapted protocols warrants further investigation.

Conclusion

Dental and maxillofacial injuries are a significant yet underrecognised consequence of violent crowd control measures. Early recognition, pragmatic adaptation of trauma principles and professional preparedness are essential to reducing long-term functional and psychosocial morbidity. Dentists may serve an important frontline role in disrupted environments, particularly when access to conventional healthcare systems is limited.
