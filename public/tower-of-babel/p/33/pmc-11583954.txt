Causal Inference Over a Subpopulation: The Effect of Malaria Vaccine in Women During Pregnancy
Authors: Zonghui Hu, Dean Follmann
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPreventing malaria during pregnancy is of critical importance, yet there are no approved malaria vaccines for pregnant women due to lack of efficacy results within this population. Conducting a randomized trial in pregnant women throughout the entire duration of pregnancy is impractical. Instead, a randomized trial was conducted among women of childbearing potential (WOCBP), and some participants became pregnant during the 2‐year study. We explore a statistical method for estimating vaccine effect within the target subpopulation—women who can naturally become pregnant, namely, women who can become pregnant under a placebo condition—within the causal inference framework. Two vaccine effect estimators are employed to effectively utilize baseline characteristics and account for the fact that certain baseline characteristics were only available from pregnant participants. The first estimator considers all participants but can only utilize baseline variables collected from the entire participant pool. In contrast, the second estimator, which includes only pregnant participants, utilizes all available baseline information. Both estimators are evaluated numerically through simulation studies and applied to the WOCBP trial to assess vaccine effect against pregnancy malaria.Keywords: causal inference, randomized trial, subset selected post‐randomization, vaccine effect

Problem Description

Malaria is the second most common cause of infectious disease‐related death in the world. According to the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, nearly half of the world's population lives in areas where malaria is common. Women are more susceptible to malaria during pregnancy. Pregnant women infected with malaria usually have more severe symptoms and outcomes, with an increased risk of severe anemia and maternal death, and higher rates of miscarriage, premature delivery, and neonatal death [1, 2]. According to the World Health Organization, malaria accounts for over 10 000 maternal and 200 000 neonatal deaths per year. Preventing pregnancy malaria is an unmet need. Although there are effective vaccines to prevent malaria in children and adults, there are no approved vaccines for pregnancy malaria.

Before a malaria vaccine can be approved for use during pregnancy, it is essential to assess the safety and efficacy of vaccine in women throughout pregnancy, from conception to delivery. An ideal clinical trial would recruit women right at conception, randomize them to the study vaccine and placebo, and follow them throughout pregnancy for incidence of infection, see Figure 1a. If the vaccine demonstrates both safety and efficacy, it will qualify as a potential candidate vaccine for use in pregnant women upon conception to prevent pregnancy malaria. However, such a trial is not feasible since it is practically impossible to identify who will become pregnant during the designated study period and recruit them right before conception. Instead, a randomized trial was conducted over women of child‐bearing potential (WOCBP). Participants were not pregnant at randomization but planned to become pregnant. They were randomized to the study vaccine or placebo and stopped use of contraceptives during the 2‐year study. The study design is shown in Figure 1b. Within each arm, some participants became pregnant during the 2‐year follow up. These participants were followed from conception till delivery. More details about the trial are in Section 4. The primary objective of trial WOCBP was to evaluate the safety and efficacy of the vaccine among all participants throughout the 2 year following vaccination. In this work, we focus on the exploratory objective—vaccine efficacy in pregnant women during pregnancy.

The effectΔ can be considered as a principal causal effect in the sense that it is the causal effect of vaccine over a subset defined by the potential outcome of pregnancy. Principal causal effects are pursued when researcher are interested in the treatment effect over a subgroup, referred to as the principal stratum, defined by an intermediate outcome [5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. They are widely applied to noncompliance problems for the treatment effect over the potential compliers [10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15]. They are also applied to study vaccine effects in a subset selected post‐randomization [3, 4, 16, 17, 18, 19]. As the potential values of the intermediate variable in a subject cannot be simultaneously observed under both the treatment and the control, the principal stratum membership of an individual is not always ascertainable. Given this fundamental problem of causal inference, assumptions are necessary for the identification and estimation of the principal causal effects.

We assess the vaccine effect against pregnancy malaria under the principal causal effect framework. This work deviates from typical principal causal inferences in two aspects. First, the principal causal effect targets a specific principal stratum,𝒮lm in Table 1. However, the interest of this work is the effect over the naturally pregnant subpopulation𝒫0=𝒮10∪𝒮11. A principal stratum depends on the vaccine used in a trial. For two trials conducted on the same study population, the principal stratum𝒮lm can be different from one trial to the other, as a woman who can become pregnant under one vaccine may not become pregnant under the other vaccine. Nevertheless, the targeted subpopulation inΔ,𝒫0={i:Si(0)=1}, is a definitive subpopulation. It pertains solely to the study population and is indifferent to the vaccine used in a trial. This property enables a definitive interpretation ofΔ and facilities the comparison of various vaccines from trials conducted on the same population.

Second, in trial WOCBP, baseline variables inX were measured differently. Some baseline variables, such as age at randomization, were considered predictive of pregnancy and were measured in all participants. Others variables, like medical history data, were only collected retrospectively in participants who became pregnant during the study. To make effective use of baseline variable information, we employ two estimators forΔ. One estimator includes all participants but can use only the baseline variables measured in all participants. The other estimator involves only the pregnant participants but can include all measured baseline variables.

If variables inXP impact the infection outcome, includingXP improves the estimation efficacy ofΔ^(b). IfXS contains only part of the confounders and the other confounders are contained inXP, thenΔ^(b) is consistent butΔ^(a) is not. Overall, it is always beneficial to collect the baseline variables in all participants that impact pregnancy and infection. However this may be expensive or logistically challenging. The benefit ofΔ^(b) is that it is consistent and makes efficient use of resources, given these baseline variables are collected in the pregnant participants.

The above estimation is developed under the assumptions of monotonicity and conditional ignorability. Monotonicity is a commonly assumed condition in the identification of causal effects [4, 20, 21]. In trial WOCBP, the monotonicity assumption means that, if a woman would become pregnant under placebo, she would also become pregnant under the vaccine. It implies that vaccine does not reduce the likelihood of pregnancy. Compared to the two commonly assumed types of conditional independence in principal causal inferences, one being thatY(z) is independent of the principal stratum{S(0),S(1)} givenX [10, 22, 23] and the other being thatY(z) is independent ofX given the principal stratum [21, 24, 25], the conditional ignorability in Assumption 2 is relatively weak. All these assumptions are fundamentally untestable [15]. We examine the plausibility of monotonicity in trial WOCBP in Section 4 and explore the impact of possible violations to the conditional ignorability assumption below.

Conditional ignorability means that if a woman can become pregnant under vaccine, then conditional on her baseline characteristics, the expected mean outcome under vaccine is the same regardless of whether she can become pregnant or not under placebo. Extension

One complication in trial WOCBP is that, though all participants received study product about the same time, participants became pregnant at different times, see Figure 2. Timing of conception may affect the vaccine effect during pregnancy. First, in the region where the trial was conducted, malaria transmission season runs from August to February. Participants with different conception dates are subject to different level of malaria exposure during pregnancy. A participant with conception in August would have the first two trimesters falling in transmission season while a participant with conception in February has only the last trimester in transmission season. Second, the vaccine effect may wane over time. Those becoming pregnant shortly after vaccination may enjoy stronger protection from vaccine during pregnancy than those becoming pregnant late. Since all participants received study product around the same time, these timing effects are characterized by the conception date. We extend the method in Section 2.1 to explore the vaccine effect by stratifying the naturally pregnant according to the timing of conception.

Endpoint and Estimation

Vaccine effects are usually assessed on two endpoints, the binary endpoint of being infected or not and the time to first infection. LetTi be the number of days from conception to infection in subjecti. For those who are not infected during pregnancy,Ti is censored on the day of delivery which is denoted asCi. We consider two infection outcome endpoints. In the first,Y is the binary variableYi=I(Ti>Ci) andE{Y(1)−Y(0)|S(0)=k} is the vaccine effect on protecting against infection during pregnancy. In the second,Yi=min(Ti,τ) withτ=280 days the average length of human gestation andE{Y(1)−Y(0)|S(0)=k} is the vaccine effect on delaying infection during pregnancy.

For the estimation of vaccine effect, we need the pregnancy outcome modelp(S=k|Z=z,X) and the infection outcome modelE(Y|S=k,Z=z,X) forz=0,1,k=0,1 for binary pregnancy outcome andk=0,1,…,K for ordinal pregnancy outcome. The pregnancy outcome can be modeled via logistic regression if pregnancy outcome is binary or ordinal logistic regression if pregnancy outcome is ordinal. For the infection outcome, a logistic regression is fitted if the outcome is binary and an accelerated failure time model if the outcome is time to infection. To improve robustness, nonparametric regression such as the generalized additive model and machine learning methods such as the random survival forests can be employed [29, 30].

Application

In trial WOCBP, 288 women of childbearing potential, aged 18 to 38, were randomized to receive either placebo or vaccine. All received the assigned product in early August, 2019. In the trial‐conducted region, the malaria season spans 6 months, running from August to January. One hundred and sixty‐three participants became pregnant during the 2‐year study and were followed from conception to delivery. Age and the date of last contraceptive use were recorded in all participants at randomization. These two variables were believed to be predictive of pregnancy during the study. Additional baseline variables, such as the number of previous pregnancies and health markers, were retrospectively collected from participants who became pregnant during the study.

Conclusion

It is crucial to have an effective vaccine to prevent pregnancy malaria, yet assessing vaccine efficacy in pregnant women faces challenges. Conducting an ideal randomized trial exclusively over pregnant women throughout pregnancy is impractical. In this study, a randomized trial over women of childbearing potential was conducted, following participants who became pregnant during a 2‐year study from conception to delivery. We apply the concept of principal causal inference to infer the vaccine effect over the naturally pregnant subpopulation, which is the target of the ideal but unfeasible trial.

Two estimators for the vaccine effect are developed—one involving all participants and the other limited to pregnant participants. Due to the trial's data collection, where some baseline variables were obtained from all participants and others solely from pregnant participants, the second estimator ensures a more consistent and efficient estimation of the vaccine effect. Both estimators operate under the assumptions of monotonicity and conditional ignorability.

Results from both estimators consistently indicate a positive effect of the vaccine in preventing pregnancy malaria. In trial WOCBP, pregnancy outcome data aligns with the monotonicity assumption. If conditional ignorability fails, the vaccine effect estimates are likely conservative, tending to underestimate the true vaccine effect.
