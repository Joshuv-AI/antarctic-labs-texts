PbrMYB31 enhances abiotic stress tolerance by promoting suberin biosynthesis in Arabidopsis
Authors: Zixiao Wu, Xiexuan Wang, Jie Deng, Wei Wu, Jingtong Hu, Kaifeng Wang, Litao Cheng, Wei Heng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Plants, being immobile, are exposed to fluctuating environmental stresses and have therefore evolved sophisticated adaptive strategies. Transcription factors are central to these responses, integrating external signals with the regulation of stress-responsive genes. Among them, MYB proteins represent one of the largest and most intensively studied families, widely involved in secondary metabolite biosynthesis, including anthocyanins, suberin, and lignin. Despite the identification of many MYB members in abiotic stress responses, their precise regulatory mechanisms remain poorly understood. In this study, we characterized the PbrMYB31 gene from Pyrus bretschneideri, which encodes an R2R3-type MYB transcription factor. Overexpression of PbrMYB31 in transgenic Arabidopsis thaliana improved plant tolerance to salt and cold stress and enhanced ABA-mediated adaptive responses under abscisic acid (ABA) treatment. Mechanistically, PbrMYB31 was shown to directly bind to the promoter of the fatty acid metabolism-related gene CYP86A1, thereby promoting suberin biosynthesis. Consistent with these findings, PbrMYB31 enhances stress adaptation by reinforcing endodermal cell suberization through direct transcriptional activation of CYP86A1. Collectively, our results demonstrate that PbrMYB31 functions as a central regulator of the PbrMYB31– CYP86A1–suberin–ABA axis, thereby strengthening structural barriers and improving abiotic stress tolerance in plants, providing a mechanistic framework for understanding suberin-associated abiotic stress tolerance in plants.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08303-5.

Introduction

Plants, as sessile organisms, are constantly exposed to fluctuating environmental conditions such as salinity, drought, and low temperature, which severely restrict their growth and productivity [1, 2]. To survive under these stresses, plants have evolved complex adaptive strategies at morphological, physiological, and molecular levels. Among these, transcription factors (TFs) play central roles by regulating the expression of stress-responsive genes and coordinating multiple signaling networks. TFs function by binding to specific cis-regulatory elements in promoters, enabling dynamic transcriptional reprogramming that underlies stress adaptation and developmental plasticity [3]. Prominent TF families involved in these processes include AP2/ERF [4], MYB [5], bHLH [6], NAC [7], and WRKY [8], all of which have been implicated in the regulation of abiotic stress responses.

The MYB family represents one of the largest and most functionally diverse transcription factor families in plants [9]. Members of this family share several common structural and functional features: most MYB proteins possess a highly conserved N-terminal MYB DNA-binding domain (DBD), typically composed of one to four imperfect repeats (R), each of approximately 52 amino acids forming three \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}-helices, which enable their binding to specific cis-elements and transcriptional regulation [10]. Despite these shared characteristics, MYB proteins also exhibit remarkable divergence in structure, evolution, and biological function. Based on the number and organization of repeats, plant MYBs are divided into 1R-MYB, R2R3-MYB, 3R-MYB, and atypical multi-repeat types, among which the R2R3-MYB subfamily predominates in plants and has undergone extensive expansion. This diversification underlies functional specialization, leading different MYB subgroups to participate in distinct regulatory pathways, including secondary metabolism, hormone signaling, stress adaptation, and cell differentiation [10]. Moreover, individual MYB proteins may function either as transcriptional activators or repressors, reflecting their divergent C-terminal regulatory motifs. Increasing evidence highlights the involvement of R2R3-MYBs in abiotic stress tolerance. For example, SlMYB102 overexpression enhances salt and drought resistance in tomato [11], AtMYB61 regulates lignin deposition and stomatal movement in Arabidopsis thaliana [12], and AtMYB44 confers salinity tolerance by repressing type 2 C protein phosphatases (PP2Cs) [13]. These findings demonstrate that MYB transcription factors act as pivotal regulators integrating conserved structural frameworks with lineage- and function-specific diversification to coordinate developmental programs and environmental stress responses.

The R2R3-MYB subfamily, which predominates in plants, has undergone extensive expansion and functional diversification, allowing its members to regulate a broad spectrum of developmental and metabolic processes [9, 10]. Increasing evidence highlights the involvement of R2R3-MYBs in abiotic stress tolerance. For example, SlMYB102 overexpression enhances salt and drought resistance in tomato [11]. AtMYB61 regulates lignin deposition and stomatal movement in Arabidopsis thaliana [12], and AtMYB44 confers salinity tolerance by repressing type 2 C protein phosphatases (PP2Cs) [13]. These findings demonstrate that MYB TFs function as pivotal regulators integrating developmental and environmental cues to reprogram gene expression for stress adaptation.

Abscisic acid (ABA) is a key phytohormone that orchestrates plant responses to abiotic stresses, including drought, salinity, and cold. Beyond stress adaptation, ABA also regulates seed dormancy, germination, stomatal closure, and senescence through a highly coordinated signaling cascade involving perception, transduction, and transcriptional regulation [14]. Several MYB TFs have been identified as important components of ABA-mediated transcriptional networks. For instance, MYB33 and MYB101 positively regulate ABA responses during germination [15], whereas MYB7 acts as a negative regulator by repressing ABI5 expression [16]. Similarly, MYB52 and MYB2 enhance ABA sensitivity, further underscoring the importance of MYBs in integrating ABA signaling with environmental responses [17, 18]. These findings illustrate that MYB factors function as key transcriptional mediators integrating ABA signaling with stress-responsive gene expression.

Salt stress is a major abiotic factor that severely impairs plant growth, development, and crop yield worldwide. High soil salinity not only restricts water availability through osmotic stress but also causes ion toxicity, mainly due to excessive Na\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{+}$$\end{document} and Cl\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{-}$$\end{document} accumulation, which disrupts ion homeostasis and cellular metabolism [19]. Among the molecular regulators of salt tolerance, MYB transcription factors have been recognized as key contributors. They regulate the expression of genes associated with ion transport, osmoprotection, cuticle formation, and antioxidant defense. Functional studies in transgenic plants further support their importance: overexpression of AtMYB44 and AtMYB2 in transgenic Arabidopsis thaliana could increase the resistance of transgenic plants to salt stress [13, 20]. Conversely, overexpression of MYB74 results in hypersensitivity to NaCl during seed germination, highlighting functional diversity among MYB family members [21]. Crosstalk with abscisic acid (ABA) signaling further emphasizes their importance, under salt stress conditions, abscisic acid (ABA) can induce the accumulation of proline in plants and enhance the activity of related protective enzymes. For example, MYB12 enhances salt tolerance by activating ABA biosynthetic genes, whereas MYB44 and MYB20 promote tolerance by repressing ABA negative regulators such as PP2Cs [13, 22]. Additionally, MYB7 influences germination under salt stress by downregulating ABI5, a central ABA-responsive transcription factor [18]. These regulatory interactions underscore the central role of MYBs in coordinating hormone and stress signaling under saline conditions.

Low-temperature stress likewise threatens plant development by perturbing cellular metabolism and altering transcriptional networks. A rapid response to cold involves activation of C-repeat binding factors (CBFs), a family of transcription factors that regulate a broad range of cold-responsive (COR) genes and enhance freezing tolerance [23]. MYB transcription factors serve as critical regulators within this pathway. In Arabidopsis thaliana, AtMYB15 represses CBF expression, and myb15 mutants display improved freezing tolerance [24]. Conversely, MYB96 is activated under cold stress and promotes freezing tolerance by positively regulating CBF transcription [25]. Consistent with these roles, OsMYB4 has been reported to enhance cold and drought tolerance when overexpressed in Arabidopsis thaliana, tomato, and apple, underscoring the conserved contribution of MYB transcription factors to abiotic stress adaptation across species [26–28]. These contrasting functions highlight the complex roles of MYB proteins in fine-tuning cold responses via the CBF signaling cascade.

Suberin biosynthesis represents another critical defense mechanism that reinforces plant tolerance to environmental stress [29]. Suberin is a hydrophobic polymer deposited in cell walls, forming a protective barrier that minimizes water loss and ion leakage [30]. Under salinity and drought, increased suberin deposition in root endodermal tissues restricts toxic ion uptake and helps maintain cellular homeostasis [31], while low temperature can also induce suberization to reduce membrane damage and dehydration [32]. Among these, CYP86A1 plays an indispensable role in generating \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\omega$$\end{document}-hydroxylated fatty acids essential for the formation of functional suberin lamellae, and its disruption severely compromises root barrier integrity, resulting in enhanced water loss and ion imbalance under abiotic stress [33]. At the molecular level, ABA is a central inducer of suberin formation [34], directly upregulating the expression of biosynthetic genes such as CYP86A1, GPAT5, and FAR4 [35]. Conversely, ABA-deficient or signaling-impaired mutants exhibit reduced suberin deposition, confirming a causal link between ABA signaling and suberin accumulation. Long-chain fatty acids (LCFAs) and their derivatives constitute the major aliphatic monomers that confer hydrophobicity and barrier properties to suberin polymers. Importantly, stress-responsive MYB transcription factors, including AtMYB41, MdMYB93, and ShMYB78, directly activate LCFA-related biosynthetic genes, linking environmental and hormonal cues to suberin deposition [36–38]. These findings highlight the interplay between hormonal signaling, lipid metabolism, and structural reinforcement in plant stress adaptation.

Despite growing evidence for the critical roles of MYB transcription factors in stress regulation, their molecular functions in pear (Pyrus bretschneideri) remain poorly understood. In this study, we characterized PbrMYB31, an R2R3-type MYB transcription factor from pear, and demonstrated its involvement in abiotic stress tolerance. Overexpression of PbrMYB31 in Arabidopsis thaliana enhanced resistance to salt and cold stress and increased sensitivity to ABA. Mechanistically, PbrMYB31 directly binds to the promoter of CYP86A1, a key fatty acid metabolism gene, thereby activating suberin biosynthesis pathways. Consistent with this, histological and physiological analyses revealed enhanced suberin deposition and improved stress resilience in transgenic lines. Our findings suggest that PbrMYB31 integrates transcriptional regulation of lipid metabolism with structural reinforcement of the cell wall, contributing to enhanced abiotic stress tolerance. This study provides novel insights into the regulatory functions of pear MYB transcription factors and identifies PbrMYB31 as a promising candidate gene for genetic improvement of stress-tolerant crops.

RNA extraction and quantitative reverse transcription (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA and genomic DNA were extracted using the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB)-based method [39], and were quantified with a Nanodrop spectrophotometer (Thermo, USA). cDNA synthesis was performed using a one-step genomic DNA removal kit followed by a cDNA synthesis kit (both from Vazyme, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions and using 1 mg of total RNA as a template for first-strand cDNA synthesis. Relative expression levels of each gene were calculated using the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$2^{-\Delta \Delta CT}$$\end{document} method [40]. PbrGAPDH and AtActin were used as internal reference genes for pear and Arabidopsis, respectively. Each sample was analyzed in technical triplicate. Gene-specific primers were designed using CE Design v1.03 software (Supplementary Table S1).

Cloning and sequence analysis of the PbrMYB31 gene

The full-length coding sequence (CDS) of PbrMYB31 was amplified from cDNA of ‘Dangshansuli’ and ‘Dangshanjinsu’ using primers PbrMYB31-F/R (Supplementary Table S1) and Q5 High-Fidelity 2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document} Master Mix (NEB, USA). The PCR products were purified, cloned into pEasy vectors (TransGen, China), and sequenced from at least eight independent clones. Multiple sequence alignment of PbrMYB31 and MYB proteins from other plant species was performed using ClustalW software [41]. A phylogenetic tree was constructed using the Neighbor-Joining method in MEGA X with 1,000 bootstrap replicates [42]. The theoretical isoelectric point (pI) and molecular weight were predicted using the ExPASy (Expert Protein Analysis System) tool.

Arabidopsis transformation

The construct 35S::PbrMYB31-GFP was introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101 and transformed into Arabidopsis thaliana Col-0 plants using the floral dip method [43]. T1 transformants were screened on hygromycin-containing medium and confirmed by PCR analysis. Expression of PbrMYB31 in inflorescence stems of two-week-old T1 plants was examined by semi-quantitative RT-PCR (Supplementary Table S1). Three independent T3 homozygous lines (OE-1, OE-2, and OE-3) with high PbrMYB31 expression were selected for further experiments. Root length was measured in 7-day-old seedlings grown on 1/2 MS agar plates, followed by transplantation to soil in a greenhouse under a 16 h photoperiod. Leaf number, leaf area, and primary inflorescence height were measured at 20 days and 8 weeks after germination, respectively.

Cold treatment

Seven-day-old healthy seedlings of uniform size were then carefully transferred to fresh 1/2 MS agar plates and subjected to cold treatment. For cold stress, the seedlings were placed in a growth chamber maintained at 4 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^\circ$$\end{document}C with continuous light (16 h light/8 h dark) for 7 days. After 7 days of treatment, the survival of the seedlings was assessed. Seedlings were considered “surviving” if they exhibited visible signs of recovery, such as the emergence of new green leaves or apical bud regrowth. In contrast, seedlings that failed to develop green tissues or remained completely chlorotic and wilted were classified as “dead”. Survival rate was calculated as the percentage of surviving seedlings relative to the total number of seedlings per plate: Survival rate (%) = (Number of surviving seedlings/Total number of seedlings)\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}100.

Salt treatment

Surface-sterilized seeds of WT and PbrMYB31-overexpressing lines were germinated on 1/2 MS medium containing 1% (w/v) sucrose and 0.8% agar. After stratification at 4 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^\circ$$\end{document}C for 2 days, plates were incubated at 22 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^\circ$$\end{document}C under long-day conditions (16 h light/8 h dark) for 7 days. Based on preliminary results, 100 mM NaCl was used in subsequent assays. Seedlings were cultured vertically, and root growth and overall seedling morphology were recorded at 24, 48, and 72 h post-treatment. At least five seedlings per genotype and treatment were analyzed in each of three independent biological replicates.

Abscisic acid treatment

Wild-type and PbrMYB31-overexpressing Arabidopsis lines were grown as described above. Seven-day-old seedlings were transferred to 1/2 MS agar plates containing different ABA concentrations (10–200 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu$$\end{document}M) to determine an appropriate treatment concentration. Seedlings were cultured vertically and observed at 24, 48, and 72 h after treatment. Primary root length, leaf wilting, and chlorosis were recorded. Each treatment included at least five seedlings per genotype and was repeated three times. Plant morphology and stress symptoms were documented photographically.

Histochemical detection of suberization in Arabidopsis stems and roots

To investigate suberin deposition in stem tissues, the main inflorescence stems of two-month-old wild-type and PbrMYB31-overexpressing (OE) Arabidopsis thaliana plants were excised at the basal region and fixed in freshly prepared FAA solution (formalin : acetic acid : 70% ethanol = 1:1:18, v/v/v) at 4 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^\circ$$\end{document}C for one week. The fixed samples were then dehydrated through an ethanol series [85%, 95%, and 100% (v/v)], embedded in paraffin, and sectioned using a rotary microtome. To visualize cellular suberization, the sections were stained with toluidine blue O and safranin O-fast green, and examined using a Leica TCS SP2 spectral confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems, Germany). For histochemical detection of root suberin, 60-day-old roots were stained with freshly prepared FY 088 solution (0.01% w/v in lactic acid) at 70\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^\circ$$\end{document}C for 30 minutes. After thorough washing with water, suberin deposition was observed using a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM) equipped with a GFP filter set [44].

Determination of suberin content

Suberin analysis was carried out by gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS). Briefly, samples were depolymerized by methanolysis to release fatty acids and other monomers, which were then extracted with dichloromethane (DCM). After neutralization with sodium carbonate, the extracts were evaporated to dryness and derivatized with N,O-bis (trimethylsilyl) trifluoroacetamide (BSTFA) to improve volatility. The derivatized products were separated on a nonpolar GC column and detected by MS according to their mass-to-charge (m/z) ratios [45]. Identification and quantification of suberin monomers were based on comparison of retention times and mass spectra with authentic standards, and suberin content was calculated from the integrated peak areas.

Yeast one-hybrid assay

The promoter of PbrCYP86A1 was cloned into the pABAi vector, linearized with BstBI, and integrated into the yeast Y1HGold genome. The optimal concentration of aureobasidin A (AbA) for autoactivation suppression was determined on SD/-Ura medium. The PbrMYB31 coding sequence was inserted into the pGADT7 vector and transformed into the Y1H strain containing the promoter construct. Yeast transformants were screened on SD/-Leu medium supplemented with AbA to assess protein-DNA interaction (Supplementary Table S1).

Dual-luciferase reporter gene assay and in vivo imaging analysis

The effector plasmid PbrMYB31-pGreenII 62-SK (co-expressed with P19) and the reporter plasmid PbrCYP86A1pro-pGreenII 0800-LUC were introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3103. Bacterial cultures were co-infiltrated into Nicotiana benthamiana leaves. After 48 h, luminescence intensity was measured using a Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Luminescence was visualized with a low-light imaging system (Supplementary Table S1).

Molecular docking experiment analysis

First, the 3D structures of the protein complexes were predicted using AlphaFold3 [46]. Five parallel predictions were performed for each group, and the structure with the highest pLDDT score was selected for structural analysis in PyMOL. The analysis was conducted using PyMOL 2.5.1, with interactions measured using the Measurement plugin. Finally, annotations and rendering were performed in Adobe Illustrator 2019 CC.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using a Student’s t-test to assess significant differences between the two samples, with significance levels set at *P<0.05, **P<0.01, ***P<0.001. All data were obtained from at least three biological replicates, with error bars indicating the standard error.

Characterization of PbrMYB31 in Pyrus bretschneideri

To investigate the potential role of PbrMYB31 in abiotic stress responses in pear, the PbrMYB31 gene cDNA (Gene ID: 103958718) was cloned from Pyrus bretschneideri by RT-PCR (the reverse transcriptionpolymerase chain reaction) and RACE (random amplification of cDNA ends) methods. The full-length cDNA of PbrMYB31 is 1707 bp, with an open reading frame (ORF) of 1131 bp encoding 376 amino acids, and has a predicted molecular weight of 41,716.08 Da with an isoelectric point (pI) of 6.12. The sequence analysis showed that PbrMYB31 contained two Myb-like DNAbinding domains at the N-terminus (16–67, 80–130 aa) of the protein, which are typical features of the R2R3-MYB proteins (Fig. 1A). The R2 domain harbors three regularly spaced, conserved tryptophan (W) residues, whereas the R3 domain retains two conserved W residues, with the first position replaced by a hydrophobic amino acid, a typical feature of R3-MYB domains. Comparative analysis indicated that PbrMYB31 shares high sequence identity with MYB proteins from other species, including MdMYB306-like from apple (96.02%) and PpMYB306 from peach (79.63%). These conserved motifs and the high degree of sequence similarity to known MYB proteins suggest that PbrMYB31 is a typical member of the R2R3-MYB transcription factor family (Fig. 1B). Promoter cis-element analysis revealed that the PbrMYB31 promoter harbors a variety of regulatory elements associated with abiotic stress responses. Notably, multiple abscisic acid-responsive elements (ABRE, ABRE3a, and ABRE4) were distributed throughout the promoter region, suggesting that PbrMYB31 may participate in abiotic stress responses via an ABA-dependent signaling pathway. In addition, several MYB-binding sites (MBS, MYB, MYB-like sequence, and Myb) and MYC-binding sites (MYC and Myc) were identified, which are typically involved in transcriptional regulation under salt, drought, and cold stresses. Overexpression of PbrMYB31 enhances suberin biosynthesis in Arabidopsis thaliana

Suberin is a specialized hydrophobic cell wall polymer that plays a critical role in plant adaptation to abiotic stresses by restricting water loss, limiting ion permeability, and reinforcing cell wall integrity. To examine whether PbrMYB31 influences suberin formation, histological analysis was conducted on basal inflorescence stems of wild-type (WT) and PbrMYB31-overexpressing (PbrMYB31-OE) Arabidopsis plants using safranin-fast green staining. Suberin-stained regions (indicated by red arrows) were primarily localized in xylem tissues. Compared with WT plants, PbrMYB31-OE lines exhibited more extensive suberin deposition, characterized by a greater number of suberized cells and visibly thickened cell walls, indicating enhanced suberization (Fig. 5A). To further confirm suberin accumulation, mature stems were stained with Fluorol Yellow 088, a specific fluorescent dye for suberin detection. PbrMYB31-OE lines displayed stronger yellow-green fluorescence signals in suberin-enriched regions compared with WT plants, providing additional evidence for elevated suberin content (Fig. 5B). Discussion

Abiotic stresses such as drought, salinity, cold, and abscisic acid (ABA) signaling impose strong constraints on plant growth and productivity by perturbing cellular homeostasis—triggering osmotic imbalance, oxidative stress, and metabolic disruption that ultimately impair photosynthesis, nutrient acquisition, and cell expansion [47]. To cope with such adverse conditions, plants have evolved sophisticated regulatory networks that integrate stress perception with transcriptional reprogramming and hormone signaling. Among these regulatory components, MYB transcription factors, one of the largest families in plants, play pivotal roles in mediating responses to abiotic stresses [48]. The R2R3-MYB subgroup, in particular, represents the most abundant class, characterized by two conserved MYB repeats at the N-terminus and variable C-terminal domains containing transcriptional activation or repression motifs [49, 50]. These proteins are extensively involved in diverse biological processes, including hormone signaling, cell differentiation, secondary metabolism, and plant responses to abiotic and biotic stresses [51, 52]. For instance, in Arabidopsis thaliana, MYB60, MYB61, and MYB96 contribute to drought tolerance [53–55], whereas MYB109 has been identified as a negative regulator of stomatal closure under osmotic stress [56]. In this study, PbrMYB31 was identified as a typical R2R3-MYB transcription factor in pear (Pyrus bretschneideri). The presence of two conserved N-terminal MYB repeats with characteristic tryptophan residues, together with its close homology to the Rosaceae member MYB306, supports its conserved DNA-binding specificity and regulatory logic. Promoter analysis further revealed key cis-regulatory elements associated with abiotic stress responses. Notably, the enrichment of abscisic acid-responsive elements (ABREs) suggests that PbrMYB31 expression is regulated by ABA, a central phytohormone orchestrating plant adaptation to drought, salinity, and cold stress [57]. In addition, the presence of MYB-binding motifs implies potential autoregulatory or cross-regulatory interactions within the MYB network, while the coexistence of MYC-binding sites highlights possible crosstalk with MYC transcription factors. Such MYB-MYC modules are well recognized as core nodes in the activation of stress-responsive gene expression under drought and cold conditions [50]. Collectively, these findings suggest that PbrMYB31 functions as a key regulatory hub that integrates multiple environmental cues, particularly ABA-mediated signals, into transcriptional networks that enhance plant adaptation to abiotic stresses.

Low temperature is one of the most detrimental abiotic stresses for plants, as it disrupts membrane integrity, alters protein conformation, reduces enzymatic activity, and severely restricts growth and survival. Cold stress also impairs water uptake by increasing membrane rigidity and affects photosynthetic efficiency, thereby limiting energy supply. Thus, enhancing cold tolerance is critical for plant survival [58]. In our study, transgenic Arabidopsis lines overexpressing PbrMYB31 displayed higher survival rates compared with wild-type seedlings after exposure to 4 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^\circ$$\end{document}C treatment. Consistently, the expression level of PbrMYB31 was clear induced under cold conditions, suggesting that this transcription factor is strongly induced by low temperature and contributes to enhanced cold tolerance. Similarly, high salinity represents a major constraint on agricultural productivity worldwide, imposing both osmotic stress, which limits water availability, and ionic stress, which causes toxic ion accumulation, nutrient imbalance, and growth retardation. Salt stress particularly suppresses root elongation, thereby restricting water and nutrient acquisition. Under 100 mM NaCl treatment, PbrMYB31-overexpressing seedlings exhibited longer root growth and higher transcript abundance than wild-type, indicating that PbrMYB31 is associated with improved tolerance to salinity. By promoting root elongation, PbrMYB31 may facilitate improved water and nutrient uptake under saline conditions, providing a direct adaptive advantage in high-salt environments.

As a central stress hormone, abscisic acid (ABA) orchestrates plant responses to drought, salinity, and cold. In our experiments, ABA treatment significantly promoted root elongation and improved overall vigor in PbrMYB31 transgenic seedlings compared with WT plants. Meanwhile, PbrMYB31 transcript levels were consistently elevated following ABA exposure, indicating that this gene is responsive to ABA signaling. Consistently, the expression of ABA biosynthesis-related genes was markedly increased in PbrMYB31-overexpressing lines, accompanied by elevated endogenous ABA levels, suggesting that PbrMYB31 may enhance ABA-associated regulatory outputs under stress conditions. Taken together, our results indicate that PbrMYB31 contributes to improved tolerance to cold and salinity while simultaneously promoting ABA-mediated adaptive responses, rather than reducing ABA sensitivity. Therefore, PbrMYB31 likely acts as a regulatory hub integrating ABA signaling with cold- and salt-responsive pathways, ultimately strengthening plant resilience under multiple abiotic stresses.

Suberin deposition represents a crucial adaptive strategy that strengthens cell walls, restricts water loss, and limits ion flux, thereby enhancing plant tolerance to abiotic stresses [59]. In this study, elevated suberin accumulation in PbrMYB31-overexpressing lines, together with the direct activation of PbrCYP86A1, a key \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\omega$$\end{document}-hydroxylase required for fatty acid oxidation and the production of suberin monomers. Rather than functioning as a simple stress-responsive TF, PbrMYB31 integrates environmental signaling with cell wall remodeling, ultimately contributing to improved plant performance under cold, salt, and ABA-related stress. This functional pattern is consistent with previous reports that several R2R3-MYB transcription factors, such as AtMYB41, respond to ABA, drought, and salt stress while also influencing cell wall development [60], and that the salt stress-responsive OsMPS regulates both hormone signaling and cell wall synthesis in rice [61]. Importantly, PbrCYP86A1-dependent suberin accumulation is likely to play a functional role in enhancing stress resistance in PbrMYB31 overexpression lines. Under drought conditions, increased suberin deposition can reduce apoplastic permeability and water loss, helping to maintain tissue hydration. Under salinity stress, suberin is known to restrict excessive ion entry, thereby contributing to ionic homeostasis and preventing cellular injury. Although cold stress is not classically associated with suberin, strengthened barrier properties may indirectly stabilize cellular environments and mitigate cold-induced damage. Therefore, the elevated suberin levels observed in PbrMYB31 transgenic plants provide a plausible mechanistic explanation for their enhanced tolerance to ABA, salt, and cold treatments. Taken together, our findings support a working model in which PbrMYB31 enhances abiotic stress resistance not only by transcriptionally regulating stress-responsive genes, but also by promoting PbrCYP86A1-dependent suberin biosynthesis, thereby reinforcing cell wall integrity as a structural defense mechanism. Nevertheless, abiotic stress tolerance is orchestrated by complex and multilayered regulatory networks. It is therefore likely that PbrMYB31 regulates additional downstream targets and signaling pathways that were not explored in the present study.
