Subglacial water flow and ice dynamics during glacial lake outburst floods observed from space
Authors: Eyjólfur Magnússon, Vincent Drouin, Finnur Pálsson, Tómas Jóhannesson, Joaquín M. C. Belart, Jan Wuite, Valentyn Tolpekin, Krista Hannesdóttir, Etienne Berthier, Gunnar Sigurðsson, Bergur Einarsson, Benedikt G. Ófeigsson, Thomas Nagler, Magnús T. Gudmundsson, Thórdís Högnadóttir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Glacial lake outburst floods (jökulhlaups) are challenging to study because they often take place below hundreds of meters of ice, and the complex interplay between lifting of the glacier and melting of the flood path is not well understood. Here, we provide unique insights into the mechanics of jökulhlaups and how they affect three-dimensional glacier motion using satellite synthetic aperture radar (SAR) and field observations, acquired before, during and after two recent jökulhlaups (in 2021 and 2022) from the subglacial lake Grímsvötn in Iceland. The results indicate that the same event may alternate between channel flow and sheet flow and that ponding of water along the subglacial flood path can play a major role in jökulhlaup dynamics. This has important implications for physical jökulhlaup modeling and for better understanding the role of subglacial hydraulics on glacier dynamics, required for improved predictions of the future evolution of glaciers and ice sheets.

Introduction

Glacial lake outburst floods, often referred to by the Icelandic word “jökulhlaup”, include some of the largest documented floods on Earth1–3 and are among the most important geomorphological processes in watersheds downstream from glaciers. Jökulhlaups are expected to increase in distribution and frequency as glaciers retreat4. Understanding their underlying mechanisms is important due to the potential societal hazards downstream5 and for advancing knowledge of glacier dynamics. Studying jökulhlaups provides insight into the subglacial drainage system, under extreme water pressure, and discharge, and how the glacier dynamics respond to changes in basal hydraulic conditions. Nye’s classical theory of jökulhlaups assumes that flood water drains in an ice conduit at the glacier bed where the conduit size, regulating the discharge, is subject to two counteracting processes: melting of the conduit walls due to frictional heat expanding the conduit, and ice creep closing the conduit6–9. This theory has provided an explanation for the slow initiation and near-exponential rise in water discharge that is observed during many jökulhlaups, commonly referred to as “slowly-rising” jökulhlaups. Nye’s theory is, however, not applicable to many “rapidly-rising” jökulhlaups with a more abrupt, often approximately linear, rise in discharge8,10–12. Furthermore, the widespread impact of jökulhlaups on glacier motion13–18, observed for both rapidly- and slowly-rising jökulhlaups19, indicates more complex drainage mechanisms than simple conduit flow11,20. Subglacial drainage during jökulhlaups involves complex, widespread ice-velocity and surface-elevation disturbances due to changes in the basal hydraulic system12,19. Similar ice motion patterns have been observed in Greenland and Antarctica in drainage events that are not necessarily associated with outburst floods issuing from the ice margin21–24. This indicates that understanding of the dynamics of jökulhlaups may have wider implications for glacier dynamics in general.

To improve our understanding of jökulhlaups, we investigate here the dynamics of two recent floods from the subglacial lake Grímsvötn in SE-Iceland based on field observations and remote-sensing of the motion and elevation changes of the overlying glacier at their source as well as in their path. Using our new insights into the links between the subglacial water flow and glacier motion, we propose a conceptual model of Grímsvötn jökulhlaups that is in principle applicable to a wide range of rapidly- and slowly-rising jökulhlaups elsewhere.

Grímsvötn is located under the Vatnajökull ice cap (Fig. 1a). The floods from Grímsvötn have been the subject of efforts to explain the physics of jökulhlaups for decades6,25–28. The subglacial lake, covered by an up to 300 m thick ice shelf, is situated within a caldera of an active central volcano also named Grímsvötn. The lake collects meltwater produced by ~2 GW subglacial geothermal activity29 and volcanic eruptions, in addition to meltwater from the glacier surface. During most of the 20th century, water accumulated in the lake at rates of 1–3 km3 over the 4–6 years that elapsed between the semi-periodic outbursts29, with some exceptions when volcanic eruptions caused additional water accumulation and shorter interval between jökulhlaups. The jökulhlaups drain subglacially beneath the Skeiðarárjökull outlet glacier and into rivers issuing from the glacier margin ~50 km south of the lake. Satellite observations of disturbances in ice dynamics during jökulhlaups are rare. One of the first observations involved a jökulhlaup from Grímsvötn in March 1996. During this event interferometric synthetic aperture radar (InSAR) data from the ERS-1/ERS-2 tandem mission showed an up to threefold increase in ice velocity relative to normal winter values over an ~8-km wide area along the flood path19.

In November 1996, 3.2 km3 of meltwater had accumulated in the lake from an eruption in Gjálp subglacial volcano 10 km north of Grímsvötn30. This resulted in lifting of the ice damming the lake and a subsequent sudden catastrophic release of the accumulated water in a rapidly-rising jökulhlaup reaching a peak discharge of ~50,000 m3 s–1 at the glacier terminus in less than a day. Unfortunately, no usable velocity observations of the glacier were acquired during this event. Due to the resulting damage to the lake ice dam by the 8 °C flood water30 and further undermining by geothermal activity near the dam, the cycle of water accumulation and release of floods from Grímsvötn has been different since 1996, with floods typically occurring at 1–2-year intervals. Before the two jökulhlaups described in the present study, the largest floods occurred in 2004 and 201012 with a volume of ~0.6 km3 and peak discharge of ~3000 m3 s–1. The flood-discharge curve was still near-exponential (slowly-rising), but rose faster with a doubling time in discharge ~1/3 of that before 199612, reaching a peak discharge at the glacier front in 3 to 5 days after the first detection of flood water in the river.

A GNSS station operated in the lower part of the glacier (Fig. 1a), at a location where the satellite observations in March 1996 indicated subglacial ponding, recorded net uplift of 2.5 m during the rising outflow from Grímsvötn during the 2010 jökulhlaup. Furthermore, the inferred peak subglacial water storage was ~10-fold the estimated volume of ice that could have been melted by frictional heat during the flood12. Therefore, most of the subglacial storage during this flood was not created by positive feedback between water flow and conduit enlargement, as assumed in Nye’s model6 and some of its later developments7,9,28, but instead by hydraulic jacking of the glacier from the bed. The observed subglacial storage of flood water by large-scale lifting of the glacier from the bed exceeding a meter are also not consistent with more recent refinements of Nye’s theory, intended to explain the dynamics of rapidly-rising jökulhlaups, involving a coupled water-sheet–conduit model11 and a coupled linked-cavity model20 for the subglacial drainage. The water-sheet–conduit model is based on an expression for the sheet thickness as a function of the local subglacial water pressure, which leads to implausible subglacial water pressure for lifting ≳1 m. The linked-cavity model, on the other hand, assumes a “pseudo-steady description for the geometry of the cavities” that are formed by ice flow over bed-roughness elements with height on the order of 0.1 m in the simplified model considered20. This is unlikely to be an adequate model for subglacial water flow in large-scale lifting of the glacier from the bed over areas with dimensions of many kilometers. Additional observations of jökulhlaup dynamics, beyond the traditional discharge curve at the terminus, are essential to improve theoretical understanding of subglacial floods, in particular the interplay between hydraulic jacking, conduit formation by melting of ice and subglacial storage of flood water.

Results and discussion

The 2021 and 2022 Grímsvötn jökulhlaups

Two jökulhlaups drained from Grímsvötn between 15 November–10 December, 2021, and 4–22 October, 2022. The time frame of these events are defined from the start of significant lowering of the lake level (Fig. 2a) until the river at Gígjukvísl gauging station, near the glacier snout, reached its normal level (Figs. 3b and 4b). Despite different size of these events, reaching lake discharges of 3600 m3 s–1 in 2021 (Fig. 4a) but only ~600 m3 s–1 in 2022 (Fig. 3a), we found the sequence of events during the two jökulhlaups to be remarkably similar. The initiation of the jökulhlaups

For the 2021 flood, the initial lowering of the lake level was linear at rate of ~4 cm d–1, corresponding to a net outflow rate of ~8 m3 s–1. This rate of outflow is comparable to the typical filling rate of Grímsvötn between jökulhlaups (Supplementary Fig. 1). The linear lowering lasted for a week before switching to accelerating lake-level drop (Fig. 2a). In 2022, the initial lake-level lowering was also linear but slower (~1.5 cm d–1), probably due to higher inflow of surface melt water draining into the lake in early October than mid-November (as in 2021) and lasted only for 1.5 days before switching to accelerating rate of lowering. Besides these initial differences, lake discharge rates developed similarly for both jökulhlaups (Fig. 3a).

Propagation of the subglacial flood waves

In 2021, a sudden start of uplift and horizontal acceleration (Fig. 4d) was recorded at SKEM in the morning of 24 November indicating the arrival of a flood water, almost 3 days before the flood was detected in Gígjukvísl. During the first days of the jökulhlaup until 27 November, SKEM shows uplift of 0.6 m with more gradual uplift in the following days. In 2022, down-glacier propagation of uplift and acceleration in horizontal velocity was observed in 3 consecutive single-day InSAR images spanning 8–11 October indicating flood wave propagating ~7 km d–1 with up to 0.25 m d–1 uplift at the front (Fig. 5). The starting point of the flood wave is not directly observed from this InSAR record. We can however, state that it was initiated before the 8 October, either near the upper end of the flood related motion signal in the first InSAR image (8–9 October, Fig. 5a), at ~12 km distance from the ice dam, or farther upstream. In the night of 11 October 2022, the flood front was ~5 km wide and ~15 km upstream of the glacier margin (Fig. 5d). The first signs of flood water were detected in Gígjukvísl the following evening, indicating either that the flood wave accelerated in the last part of the path or that some subglacial flood water preceded the glacier surface velocity expression of the flood front seen in the InSAR images.

Propagation of subglacial flood waves has been reported for rapidly-rising jökulhlaups12, but not previously for slowly-rising jökulhlaups as described here. Reported propagation speed of flood waves associated with rapidly-rising jökulhlaups are much higher than obtained for the 2022 jökulhlaup (~7 km d–1). The flood wave associated with the large, rapidly-rising eruption-driven jökulhlaup from Grímsvötn in 1996, traveled at speed of ~120 km d–1 (refs. 10,11). Reported speed of flood waves related to rapidly-rising jökulhlaups from the Skaftá cauldrons in western Vatnajökull are between 15 and 50 km d–1 (ref. 12). The observed low speed of the flood wave in 2022 is similar to that of a flood wave caused by a supraglacial lake drainage cascade in Greenland23.

Subglacial storage of flood water

The difference between the cumulated discharge from Grímsvötn and the cumulated release of flood water in Gígjukvísl near the glacier front reveals vast storage of flood water in the flood path during the 2021 jökulhlaup (Fig. 4). This storage continued increasing after the passing of the flood wave down Skeiðarárjökull until peak flow from Grímsvötn when it reached a maximum of ~0.3 km3 late on 4 December (Fig. 4c). Less than 0.05 km3 of this volume was stored in lakes and river channels between the glacier margin and Gígjukvísl gauging station. In 2022 we could not directly calculate the flood path storage due to the lack of discharge measurements in Gígjukvísl. The development of the 2022 jökulhlaup in terms of outflow from Grímsvötn during rising discharge (Fig. 3a) and in terms of river-level in Gígjukvísl (Fig. 3b) was, however, very similar to the development in 2021. The subglacial storage controlled by discharge from Grímsvötn and outflow at the margin was, therefore, most likely comparable to what was observed during the 2021 jökulhlaup, while the lake drainage had not exceeded that in 2022. The similar timing of the ice dynamic response at SKEM (Figs. 3a, 4d and 5a) to flood-water input during the two jökulhlaups supports this further. We therefore consider the in-path water storage of ~0.1 km3 (Fig. 4c) on 1 December, 2021, when the lake drainage in 2021 reached the volume that was released from the lake in 2022, as an estimate of the 2022 peak storage.

The total volume of ice that could have melted by frictional heat during the entire 2021 and 2022 floods corresponds to only 0.04 km3 (cyan curve in Fig. 4c) and 0.007 km3, respectively. This implies that hydraulic jacking of the glacier from the bed opened up ~0.2 km3 of additional subglacial storage during the 2021 peak and almost half that volume in 2022. The uplift related to such water ponding and subsequent subsidence due to depletion of the stored water are clearly observed in the glacier motion during both jökulhlaups (Figs. 5–8). Substantial vertical motion can be seen over a 0.5–2 km wide area in the uppermost 17 km of the flood path (e.g., Fig. 8c), and over a 4–5 km wide area for most of the flood path farther downstream. Subglacial hydraulic jacking of approximately 0.2 and 0.1 km³ over the ~170 km² area of significant vertical motion corresponds to average uplifts of about 1.2 m in 2021 and 0.6 m in 2022, respectively, values that agree well with the uplift measured at SKEM in 2021 (Fig. 4d).

Conduits form near Grímsvötn lake

In the uppermost ~10 km of the flood path, where the glacier surface, bedrock, and consequently the piezometric surface of the hydraulic potential are relatively steep (Fig. 1f), neither uplift nor significant acceleration of horizontal velocity were detected during the two jökulhlaups. While no clear ice motion signal from the flood was detected in this area in 2022 (Fig. 5e, f) during rising discharge of 100–200 m3 s–1 from Grímsvötn (Fig. 3a), significant subsidence was observed (Fig. 6d) during rising discharge of 1000–2000 m3 s–1 in 2021 (Fig. 4a) as well as during the decline of both jökulhlaups (Fig. 8c–f). This observation is consistent with conduit flow dominating this part of the flood path, while hydraulic jacking associated with water ponding, plays a small or insignificant role. At relatively high discharge and relatively low water pressure, we expect creep closure of the conduit to become large enough to cause significant lowering of the glacier surface. The conduit could still expand since melting of the conduit walls by frictional heat would outpace creep closure. The total surface lowering of this area during the 2021 jökulhlaup, observed after the flood, is consistent with instantaneous release of frictional heat to melt ice (Fig. 8a, b).

Conduits form near the glacier margin

During the decline of the 2021 jökulhlaup we detect a 0.4–1 km wide and 5 km long surface lowering signal near the glacier margin (Fig. 8c). We interpret this feature as the closure of a basal ice conduit, resembling the outcome of analogue modeling of a flood beneath an ice stream, which produced conduit flow near the ice margin31. Conduits that form near the margin during rising discharge, connected with the over-deepened area of Skeiðarárjökull where a large proportion of the ponded flood water was stored (Fig. 8g), may be a controlling factor in the drainage into the river Gígjukvísl. The conduits allow for more rapidly increasing discharge in Gígjukvísl (Table 1, Fig. 4b) than for the Grímsvötn outflow (Table 1, Figs. 3a, 4a), while water pressure in the subglacial ponded areas remains high. This results in a variable time lag between peak discharge from Grímsvötn and in Gígjukvísl depending on the total flood volume, as observed for the 2021 (a few hours) and 2022 (~ 1 day) jökulhlaups.

Effect of jökulhlaups on ice dynamics

Downstream of the first ~10 km of the flood path, where the large-scale gradient of the piezometric surface is lower, up to 0.5 m d–1 uplift was observed during the late rise of the 2021 jökulhlaup (Fig. 6d). This was followed by subsidence exceeding 1 m d–1 shortly after peak discharge in Gígjukvísl was reached (Fig. 6e). The effect of this jökulhlaup is also clearly seen in the horizontal ice motion, which exceeded 2.5 m d–1 in some areas during the rising limb (Figs. 6a), 5–6 times faster than at the end of the jökulhlaup (see e.g., at ~30 km distance in Fig. 7a). The velocity at SKEM during the late rise was around 2.2 m d–1, approximately 4 times faster than prior to the jökulhlaup (Fig. 4d). The effect of the 2021 flood on horizontal motion was observed well beyond the possible flooded areas (Fig. 1a), including the fastest part of Skeiðarárjökull east of SKEM, which accelerated during the rise phase (Fig. 7e, Supplementary Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. Series 1). During the 2021 jökulhlaup, a wave-like pattern of elevation changes with ~0.05 m d–1 maxima, was also detected in this part of the glacier (Fig. 8d). This may be an indication of a more long-lasting disturbance of the jökulhlaup on Skeiðarárjökull’s ice dynamics since the same pattern is observed in the elevation change anomaly map that spans August 2021 to April 2022 (Fig. 8a). If the up to 6 m elevation changes in this area all took place after the jökulhlaup, this also corresponds to ~0.05 m d–1. In contrast to the glacier east of SKEM, the slower glacier west of SKEM did not seem to be affected by either jökulhlaup (Supplementary Fig. 2b, f and at distance <5 km on cross-section III to IV in Fig. 7e–h), even though both parts of the glacier join the inferred flood path below Skeiðarárjökull via subglacial valleys (Fig. 8g). The preexisting hydrological settings of the areas east and west of SKEM may, however, differ. The drainage catchment of the eastern area is much larger and sustains far more subglacial water discharge. Therefore, it might maintain a channelized drainage system32, even in December, while channels might be absent in wintertime beneath the western area, sealing it from the flooded area in terms of changes in subglacial water pressure.

Ice dynamics during flood decline and termination

Almost instantaneous slowdown of horizontal motion after peak discharge from Grímsvötn is observed during both jökulhlaups (Figs. 4d, 7 and Supplementary Fig. Series 4–5). In 2022, this slowdown occurred before peak discharge was reached at the glacier margin. A velocity field approximately spanning the time between peak discharge from Grímsvötn (~midnight 15 October) and in Gígjukvísl (~midnight 16 October) shows that horizontal motion over the entire flood path was much reduced compared with the motion during rising discharge from Grímsvötn (Fig. 7c and Supplementary Fig. Series 5). At the same time, subsidence of >0.1 m d–1 was taking place in the upper part of the flood path whereas the central part only subsided slightly (~0.05 m d–1 or less) and the lowermost part was still rising (Figs. 7d and 8e). The following day, large areas along the entire flood path were subsiding >0.15 m d–1 (Figs. 7d and 8f) and horizontal glacier motion decreased further (Fig. 7c). The horizontal speedup and subsidence gradually fade out with time after peak discharge was reached in Gígjukvísl for both jökulhlaups as illustrated in Fig. 7. Clear impact on ice motion was, however, still visible 5 days after the peak discharge from Grímsvötn during both jökulhlaups.

A conceptual model of jökulhlaups

In light of our observations at Grímsvötn and Skeiðarárjökull, we propose a new conceptual model of jökulhlaup development (Fig. 9). The model begins with initial leakage that breaches the lake dam and develops into an ice conduit (Fig. 9a), followed by the propagation of a subglacial flood wave to the glacier margin (Fig. 9b, c), ponding water that lifts the accelerating glacier from its bed and the formation of conduits near the glacier margin (Fig. 9d), and ultimately a drop in water pressure followed by the deceleration of the glacier and termination of the jökulhlaup (Fig. 9e, f). A bottleneck in the conduit flow (Fig. 9a at II) is formed early in the jökulhlaup, when the discharge and total amount of water drained from Grímsvötn are still <50 m3 s–1 and <0.006 km3, respectively, resulting in a transition from conduit flow to a propagating flood wave. The location where the flood wave is initiated is not well determined but Fig. 5a constrains it <12 km down-glacier from the lake dam. Near the higher bound, 12 km distance, the piezometric surface gradient becomes shallower (Fig. 1f). The formation of a bottleneck in the conduit flow resulting in a propagating flood wave is likely to have occurred in most, if not all the slowly-rising jökulhlaups after the Gjálp eruption in 1996. Prior jökulhlaups, which rose even more slowly12, may have developed an ice conduit much farther down-glacier without forming a bottleneck to discharge nor a flood wave, resulting in much less subglacial storage of flood water36. The satellite velocity observations acquired during the jökulhlaup in March 199619, do, however, indicate that the main phases of our conceptual model may to some degree apply to those jökulhlaups as well.

We base our interpretation of the jökulhlaup development (Fig. 9), largely on observations from time series of velocity fields extracted from ICEYE SAR images, including observations of water ponding in the flood path and changes in glacier motion, as well as the formation of conduits both in the uppermost part of the flood path and near the glacier margin. The vertical ice motion right after peak discharge in Gígjukvísl reflects the main areas of flood water storage during the jökulhlaup, showing a spatial distribution resembling a subaerial flood with the flood water mostly located in subglacial valleys, braiding around mounds in the bed (Fig. 8g and 9e). Two to four days later, depending on the size of the jökulhlaup and consequently the volume of subglacial water storage, water ponds interconnected with conduits are detected via the vertical ice motion as 0.5–2 km wide elongated areas of subsidence that gradually fade out over the course of about 1 week.

Our portrayal of two recent subglacial floods from Grímsvötn, based on 3D-velocity fields derived from spaceborne SAR images, satellite-derived elevation maps and various in-situ measurements uniquely acquired during the events, reveals a spatially variable and transient drainage system that transports jökulhlaup flood water, highlighting the limitation of classifying jökulhlaups based solely on the shape of their discharge curve. We found that some aspects of the jökulhlaups from Grímsvötn can be explained with classical models of slowly-rising jökulhlaups6–9, while other aspects indicate flood development generally attributed to rapidly-rising jökulhlaups10,11. Neither jökulhlaup type is applicable to all sections of the flood path throughout its duration, confirming that jökulhlaup type should be considered as a spectrum between these two endmembers rather than occurring as two distinct classes12. Our conceptual model provides a basic framework for integrating the elements of these two jökulhlaup endmembers into one physical jökulhlaup model. Our results improve the understanding of the links between subglacial water flow and glacier motion in general, which is needed to better predict the future development of glaciers on Earth37,38. Moreover, this study highlights the potential of using modern satellite data with high spatial and temporal resolution, such as ICEYE SAR images39, to explore these links further.

Methods

Monitoring lake levels at Grímsvötn using elevation data

Accurate monitoring of the vertical motion of the floating ice shelf above Grímsvötn allows real-time estimation of lake filling and draining between and during jökulhlaups when the ice shelf is floating. Thus, the hydrostatic level of Grímsvötn, as deduced from surface elevation measurements of the ~300 m thick floating ice shelf at GVTH (Fig. 1), has been monitored since the 1990s. These data have been essential for studies estimating subglacial water storage in the flood path of Grímsvötn jökulhlaups12,36. Elevation measurements at GVTH were initially carried out with 1–5 m uncertainty until May 2021 when a remotely connected GNSS station was deployed at GVTH allowing real-time monitoring of the lake level with <0.05 m uncertainty. In 2020, we deployed a second GNSS station on the ice shelf at GRVC as part of this study (Fig. 1a–c). In addition to an ArcticDEM40 digital elevation model (DEM) from July 2018, we prepared 9 DEMs for Grímsvötn and vicinity from Pléiades optical satellite images between October 2019 and October 2022 (see below), yielding a time series of surface elevation changes (Supplementary Fig. 1, Fig. 2b, c) and thus showing the development of lake volume and area (Fig. 1e). These DEMs, together with our new detailed bedrock topography (Fig. 1c) and ice-thickness map were used to establish an updated relationship between lake volume and area as functions of lake level (Fig. 1d).

Bedrock DEM

The data used for the construction of the bedrock DEM of Grímsvötn (Fig. 1c) are listed in Supplementary Fig. 4a. Most of the DEM is based on traced bed reflections from 2D-migrated low frequency (5 MHz) radio-echo sounding (RES) profiles acquired in 2009–2022, mainly from 2019 and 2021. The processing and tracing of the RES profiles was undertaken as in other recent studies that used the same digital survey system41,42. RES surveys were made over Grímsvötn and vicinity with analogue RES in 1987–200743 and were used in previous bedrock mapping of Grímsvötn29,44. These old profiles were only used where more recent digital RES profiles were lacking. The coverage of the new RES profiles is far better, and the old profiles have lower location accuracy, which would lead to substantial artifacts in interpolated bedrock maps at crossover points with other old profiles and, more importantly, new profiles. A small part of the area is mapped with traced bed reflections from 3D-migrated RES data acquired in 2021–2024, applying the same survey and processing methods as in a recent study41. Since this survey approach is most accurate, all bedrock reflections from 2D-migrated RES were omitted in the area with 3D-migrated RES data. The 20 × 20 m bedrock DEM of Grímsvötn and vicinity (Fig. 1e) was interpolated from all the data shown in Supplementary Fig. 4a, with kriging interpolation in Surfer 13 (© Golden Software, LCC) using the ISN93 Lambert geographical projection, EPSG 3057, and the ISH2004 vertical datum of the Natural Science Institute of Iceland (formerly the National Land Survey of Iceland). This applies also to other DEMs and raster data presented in this study.

Surface DEMs and elevation change

Most of the surface topography DEMs were deduced from optical stereo images acquired with the Pléiades satellites45. This includes 9 DEMs of Grímsvötn and vicinity (Supplementary Fig. 1) and 4 DEMs of various sections of Skeiðarárjökull at different times (Fig. 8a). A DEM of the middle part the jökulhlaup flood route (area III in Fig. 8a) was extracted from SPOT 7 optical stereo images46. The DEM processing was carried out using the Ames Stereo Pipeline47. A surface DEM from the ArcticDEM archive40, showing Grímsvötn in July 2018 (Supplementary Fig. 1a), was also used. The DEMs of the central and lower part of Skeiðarárjökull were co-registered by comparison with a lidar DEM48 in ice-free areas, first horizontally and then vertically with a bilinear correction49. The Pléiades DEMs of Grímsvötn were bilinearly corrected based on GNSS profiles generally acquired less than a month from the acquisition of the Pléiades stereo images (Supplementary Fig. 4b–g). The lake level at the time of Pléiades acquisitions, used in Fig. 1e, was extracted from these corrected DEMs. When extracting elevation changes related to filling or depletion of Grímsvötn (Supplementary Fig. 1), the horizontal shift between the DEMs, generally within the DEM cell size, was estimated using only the area outside the approximate lake area. Then, a vertical correction was estimated from the same area, compensating both for large-scale relative errors between the DEMs and for what can be considered as the normal seasonal cycle of elevation changes of the glacier surface. For Supplementary Fig. 1a, b, this simple bias correction was sufficient. The difference derived from the other Pléiades DEM pairs showed a clear stripe pattern from east to west related to jitter in the Pléiades satellite50. This jitter error in the DEM difference was estimated by filtering the difference outside the approximate lake area with a median filter 4100 m wide in easting and 300 m wide in northing directions, filling up the masked lake areas in the process. The filtered result was then subtracted from the original DEM difference (Supplementary Fig. 5a–d) to compensate for the stripe errors. The outlines of the floating glacier cover at the higher lake level and the volume change of the lake were then estimated from the corrected DEM difference (Fig. 1e). The same methodology was applied to extract elevation-change anomalies from the DEM differences in areas I and II on Skeiðarárjökull in Fig. 8a. Areas III and IV did not show clear signs of jitter errors. In these areas, large-scale seasonal elevation changes were derived by filtering the raw DEM difference within the glacier with a 4100×4100 m median filter. The filtered DEM difference was then subtracted from the original DEM difference to highlight local elevation-change anomalies due to the jökulhlaup.

When initially deployed in the 1990s, GVTH was located close to the center of the lake and the station subsided until the lake had drained almost completely. Processing of GNSS data

The processing of GNSS profiles from the RES-surveys in 2009–2024 as well as profiles for correction of the DEMs (Supplementary Fig. 4b–g) was carried out with the Trimble Business Center software, using the permanent GNSS station GFUM (see Fig. 1a–c, for location) as base station. The data from the GNSS stations GVTH, GRVC, and SKEM were processed with RTKlib51, again using GFUM as base station. Further information on the processing of GNSS data is given in the Supplementary Information.

ICEYE SAR images

Alerted of the jökulhlaup onset by real-time lake-level monitoring, we initiated dedicated acquisitions of ICEYE repeat ground-track SAR satellite images and acquired 24 strip-mode images during the jökulhlaups (Supplementary Fig. 2, Fig. 5 and Supplementary Fig. Series 1–3). The SAR images were provided by ICEYE as single look complex (SLC) files. The InSAR processing, including co-registration of SAR images, interferogram generation, topographic phase correction, as well as unwrapping and projection to map coordinates, was carried out in the Interferometric synthetic aperture radar Scientific Computing Environment, ISCE2, software52. The amplitude offset tracking was carried out with in-house software of ENVEO, using an iterative approach with template sizes of 64×64 pixels and a correlation threshold of 0.05. The topographic correction of the interferograms as well as projection of all SAR-derived data to map coordinates was carried out using the DEMs from autumn 2021, from SPOT 7 and Pléiades with the ÍslandsDEM53 outside the glacier margins (Fig. 1a and 8a). For Grímsvötn and the first few km of the flood path, the last Pléiades DEM (Supplementary Fig. 1) before each jökulhlaup was used for ICEYE data correction before peak outflow from Grímsvötn, whereas the first Pléiades DEM after each jökulhlaup was used for ICEYE data correction after peak outflow from Grímsvötn. To visualize where the glacier surface motion was affected by the jökulhlaups the phase signal due to “background” ice motion was subtracted using InSAR image acquired from the same track at the end of corresponding jökulhlaup as reference (Supplementary Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. Series 1–3). Further correction and filtering carried out after the InSAR and offset-tracking results had been projected to map coordinates (in 10 × 10 m resolution) were done with routines programmed in Matlab (®MathWorks) (see further description in the Supplementary Information). This included: masking of InSAR data with coherence <0.3; estimation of phase ramp (linear or in some cases second-order polynomial) probably mostly due to orbital errors; as well as revision and correction of the phase unwrapping, before calculating line-of-sight (LOS) motion from the corrected unwrapped InSAR images.

The motion in along-track (AT) and LOS directions derived from amplitude offset tracking was also further refined after projection to map coordinates. This included outlier removal for the derived AT and LOS motion separately, applying a 23 × 23-cell (230 × 230 m) median filter and masking out all values where either the number of obtained values within the filter window was less than 300 (~57% coverage) or the calculated daily motion differed by more than 0.2×pixel dimension of the SLC files (0.2 × 1.429 m and 0.2 × 0.749 m in the AT and LOS directions, respectively) from the median-filtered value. Next, the cleaned products were bias-corrected by subtracting the median value of the derived motion outside the glacier. For scenes combining results of both LOS motion from InSAR and offset tracking, the results were mosaicked, using offset-tracking results only where InSAR results were unavailable. Finally, a 15 × 15 (150 × 150 m) Gaussian filter was applied to subsample both the AT and LOS motion to 100 × 100 m cell size. Visual inspection revealed significant artificial stripes in the AT motion caused by fluctuations in ionospheric electron density54 for several image pairs from October 2022 (listed in Supplementary Table 1). The corresponding stripe pattern for these image pairs was estimated and corrected as explained in Supplementary Fig. 5e–h. The AT motion derived from SAR images from the ascending track in 2021 was particularly noisy and required further filtering as explained in the Supplementary Information. The Supplementary Information also explains the estimation of vertical motion due to compaction of firn to ice in the accumulation area of the glacier, which is compensated for in the LOS motion used in the 3D-velocity inversion described below.

The horizontal flow directions of Skeiðarárjökull are generally quite stable in the annual velocity fields extracted from Sentinel-1 SAR images in 2014–202058. The presumed flow direction, ω, was adopted from the annual velocity field in 2019–2020 (start of October to end of September) since it is free of data gaps and appears without significant artifacts. The difference between the average measured horizontal flow direction of the GNSS station SKEM (see Fig. 1a for location) during the 2021 jökulhlaup and the presumed value of ω at the same location is 2°, while the measured daily horizontal flow directions at SKEM differs by up to 5.5° from the presumed value of ω and span a flow-direction range of 8°. Equations 3–5 are sufficient to solve for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$v$$\end{document}v except for the horizontal flow direction parallel to the LOS direction (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\omega=\pm \varphi$$\end{document}ω=±φ). Furthermore, the derived 3D-velocity is very sensitive to errors in vAT and vLOS when the horizontal flow direction is close to the LOS direction. When inverting for the 3D ice motion, all areas where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\omega$$\end{document}ω was < 20° from LOS direction were therefore masked out. Fortunately, this mostly affects ice flow towards east or west, but since the flow of Skeiðarárjökull is mainly towards south, only a small proportion of the glacier was masked out due to this condition. To reduce the noise in the 3D-velocity field, the 3D-velocity inversion was carried out with energy functions corresponding to Eqs. 3–5 and with an additional energy function forcing the 2nd derivatives of vx, vy and vz to be close to zero. The definition of the energy functions is explained in the Supplementary Information. The 3D ice motion for each SAR image pair (Supplementary Fig. Series 4–5) was derived at the same resolution as the input vAT and vLOS (100 × 100 m cell size) by minimizing a weighted sum of the energy functions (see Supplementary Information). The minimization was carried out with Markov Random Field regularization, optimized with simulated annealing59,60. The results of the inversion, as well as the inputs vAT and vLOS, were validated with the measured motion of SKEM (Supplementary Fig. 6), with vAT and vLOS calculated from measured vx,vy and vz using Eqs. 3–4. The root-mean-square difference (RMSD) between the GNSS and inverted velocities from the descending track ICEYE data in 2021 is 0.13 m d–1 and 0.04 m d–1 for horizontal and vertical velocity, respectively (including the dashed lines in Supplementary Fig. 6). The corresponding values are 0.16 m d–1 and 0.08 m d–1, respectively, for ICEYE data from the ascending track in 2021. This probably gives an overestimate of the root mean square error (RMSE) for velocity fields, when vLOS is obtained from InSAR data of high coherence. In such cases, Supplementary Fig. 7 suggests 0.03–0.05 m d–1 and 0.005–0.02 m d–1 RMSE for horizontal and vertical velocity, respectively.
