Comments on: “Impact of RNF213 Polymorphism in Isolated Intracranial Arterial Steno-Occlusive Disease”
Authors: Umer Sajid, Maheen Sheraz, Mian Salman Zafar, Saqib Ali
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

We read with great interest the article titled “Impact of RNF213 Polymorphism in Isolated Intracranial Arterial Steno-Occlusive Disease” by Jeong et al.1 The study has provided important insights into the phenotypic implications of RNF213 mutations in isolated intracranial arterial steno-occlusive disease (ICAD) and their associations with stroke recurrence and moyamoya progression. However, several methodological considerations merit further attention.

First, the study’s relatively short and heterogeneous follow-up period represents a critical limitation. Although the mean follow-up was 2.47 years, the median duration was only 1 year, with an interquartile range of 0–4 years. This suggests that the surveillance period was insufficient for a large proportion of participants. Such a short duration limits the ability to capture late-onset stroke events and disease progression—especially in a condition with variable temporal trajectories like RNF213-related vasculopathy. Incorporating a minimum follow-up duration or using censoring-adjusted survival analyses would improve the temporal validity of recurrence risk estimates.2

Second, although the use of multivariate logistic regression is valuable, the model may be affected by residual confounding. Important clinical variables such as lesion severity, treatment adherence, and lifestyle factors were not accounted for. Additionally, the strong association between aspirin use and recurrent stroke may be explained by confounding by indication—where patients at higher risk are more likely to receive the medication. Methods such as propensity score matching or incorporating risk severity indices could better isolate the independent prognostic role of RNF213 mutations.3

Third, while the study included subgroup analyses stratified by stroke subtype, the small number of stroke events—particularly hemorrhagic strokes—limits the statistical power of these comparisons. For instance, only five hemorrhagic strokes occurred in the RNF213-positive group, weakening the reliability of inferential claims. Furthermore, multiple comparisons were made without statistical correction, increasing the risk of false-positive findings. Future studies should use power calculations to ensure adequate subgroup sample sizes and report confidence intervals to enhance transparency and interpretability.4

In summary, Jeong et al.1 present meaningful findings on the prognostic relevance of RNF213 polymorphisms in ICAD. Addressing limitations related to follow-up duration, confounding, and statistical power will be essential in future research to further clarify genotype–phenotype associations and guide clinical management.
