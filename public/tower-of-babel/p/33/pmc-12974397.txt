Exploring the Generalizability of Discharge Process Optimization: An Implementation Science Perspective
Authors: Daren Zhao
Published: 2026

I read with great interest the quality improvement study by Alharbi et al[1] on optimizing the patient discharge process. The authors’ integration of Lean methodology with the Re-Engineered Discharge (RED) toolkit has yielded remarkable results, including reduced discharge time, enhanced patient satisfaction, and lower readmission rates. The clinical and practical value of this work deserves recognition. However, after reading their study through the lens of implementation science, I began to reflect: how can this success be extended to other healthcare institutions?

Implementation science aims to systematically investigate the multidimensional factors that influence the adoption, implementation, and sustainment of evidence-based practice (EBP) and, based on this, to develop, test, and optimize corresponding implementation strategies to facilitate the integration of EBP into routine care.[2] A core concern of implementation science is precisely how to reliably spread effective interventions across different contexts. Building on this, I offer two constructive reflections to enhance the generalizability of such research.

First, by deepening the description of implementation strategies to enhance generalizability. Implementation strategies are defined as methods or techniques used to strengthen the adoption, implementation, sustainment, and scaling of innovations.[3,4] The core of implementation science lies not only in reporting the content of the intervention but also in clearly articulating the implementation strategies. This is a crucial foundation for determining whether an intervention can be successfully understood, adopted, and replicated in different settings. Although this study mentions “targeted strategies,” such as applying the single-minute exchange of die (SMED) tool, assigning discharge coordinators, and introducing the RED toolkit, it lacks detailed descriptions of the specific content, dose, implementing agents, and adaptive processes of these strategies. This not only makes it difficult for other institutions to accurately replicate the core operations of these strategies when drawing lessons, but also prevents practitioners from fully understanding the underlying mechanisms and contextual conditions that underpin their success. Consequently, it becomes challenging to judge whether the model is suitable for their own organizational environments and resource conditions.

Second, supplementing with a fidelity assessment solidifies the evidence. Implementation fidelity is defined as the extent to which the core components of an intervention are delivered as intended in actual practice.[5] In implementation science, implementation fidelity acts as a potential moderator of the relationship between interventions and their intended outcomes.[6] Therefore, measuring and reporting fidelity is a critical factor in determining and explaining intervention effectiveness. Although this study achieved positive outcomes, it did not systematically report the monitoring and evaluation of the intervention measures. Although the authors used process indicators, such as the proportion of patients discharged with an after-hospital care plan (AHCP) and the proportion of cardiology and nephrology patients contacted within 2 days after discharge, to assess the effectiveness of the implementation strategies, these indicators can only verify whether the strategies were carried out, not whether they were executed according to the intended standard. If true implementation fidelity measurement were applied, for example by reviewing the AHCP documents to check whether key items such as “medications,” “follow-up appointments,” and “pending tests” included verification marks or signatures indicating that they had been “explained by staff” and “confirmed by the patient,” it would provide clearer evidence that the observed effects indeed stem from the effective implementation of the intervention itself, rather than being influenced by concurrent factors or variations in execution.

In summary, if this study could be further refined from an implementation science perspective, specifically by detailing the “implementation strategies” that promoted behavioral change along with their concrete operationalization, and by systematically collecting evidence of “implementation fidelity” to verify whether the intervention was delivered as intended, it would significantly enhance the generalizability of this model.
