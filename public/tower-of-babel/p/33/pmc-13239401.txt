Real‐world use of lecanemab: patient pathway findings from a US multicenter study
Authors: Michael Rosenbloom, Courtney Adams, Brooke Allen, Brent Berry, Christian Camargo, Gregory Cooper, Samuel Giles, Cara Leahy, Marwan Sabbagh, Martin Sadowski, Curtis Schreiber, Paul E. Schulz, Jose Soria, David Weisman, Feride Frech, Daryl Rhys Jones
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTo ensure Alzheimer's disease‐modifying treatments can be initiated in diverse populations, efficient pathways to obtain timely diagnoses are required.MethodsThis interim sub‐analysis of a multicenter US study included cross‐sectional surveys and interviews with neurologists at 12 diverse sites to assess real‐world lecanemab use.ResultsAt survey completion, ∼1342 patients had received lecanemab. Most referrals originated from primary care. Amyloid pathology was confirmed primarily by positron emission tomography (58%) or cerebrospinal fluid (35%), with blood‐based biomarkers (BBMs) increasingly used to reduce diagnostic delays. All sites performed apolipoprotein E4 (APOE ε4) testing to inform risk/benefit decisions. Infusions usually started within 6 months of diagnosis. Delayed/incomplete referrals were identified as the most significant barrier in the current patient pathway.DiscussionThese findings demonstrate the feasibility of lecanemab integration in diverse clinical settings and highlight the importance of primary care physician engagement, optimization of referral pathways, and expanding BBM use in improving timely diagnosis, equitable access, and early treatment initiation.

BACKGROUND

Recent innovations in Alzheimer's disease (AD) diagnostics and treatments, most notably blood‐based biomarkers (BBMs) and disease‐modifying therapies, have led to a paradigm shift in the AD landscape. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 There is now an urgency to identify patients in the early symptomatic AD stages (mild cognitive impairment [MCI] due to AD or mild AD dementia), when anti‐amyloid therapies are indicated. 2 This requires accessible, affordable, and reliable diagnostic tools to confirm amyloid pathology, along with increased infusion and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) capacity for treatment administration and monitoring. 2 , 5 These requirements must also be scalable across diverse practice settings to ensure equitable access. 2 For diagnoses, high‐reliability BBMs are a promising, more accessible alternative to traditional methods, such as amyloid positron emission tomography (PET) or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) tests. 1 , 2 The Global CEO Initiative on Alzheimer's Disease (CEOi) established minimum acceptable performance thresholds for BBMs to ensure reliability in clinical settings. In a triage capacity, tests should have a sensitivity of ≥90% and specificity of ≥75% to 85% in secondary care (depending on follow‐up testing availability) and ≥85% in primary care. For confirmatory testing, sensitivity and specificity should be ∼90%. 1 These thresholds help clinicians select high‐reliability BBMs while preparing for broader adoption.

Lecanemab was the first anti‐amyloid therapy to receive traditional US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval for patients with early‐stage AD in July 2023. 5 However, over 2 years after approval, only a small proportion of eligible patients in the United States have initiated lecanemab treatment, 2 , 6 likely due to the evolution of the workflows and infrastructure required for its utilization. 2 This observation highlights an urgent need for evidence‐based learnings and practical, actionable insights relating to the integration of lecanemab into diverse practice settings.

Lecanemab in Early Alzheimer's Disease: A Comprehensive Multicenter, Real‐World, Retrospective Study (LEADER) aimed to describe lecanemab utilization patterns and implementation learnings from diverse US clinical settings. Gaining insights from AD‐treating physicians with real‐world experience of lecanemab and mapping operational workflows across diverse settings enable more widespread implementation of scalable, evidence‐based pathways aimed at achieving a timely diagnosis and equitable access to therapeutic intervention as lecanemab is established as the routine standard of care in early AD. Several physicians included in the study also had prior lecanemab clinical trial experience before transitioning to clinical standard of care use and, thus, can provide additional valuable insights into this transitional process.

METHODS

Study design

The LEADER study included a chart review, cross‐sectional health‐care provider (HCP) surveys, and HCP interviews. This manuscript reports interim findings (cutoff: November 2025) from the survey and interview components. Data collection for the chart review is ongoing and will be reported separately. The study received central ethical review and exemption from the Western Institution Review Board and Copernicus Group.

Site and HCP selection

Board‐certified, licensed, actively practicing US neurologists with ≥3 years’ experience managing patients with AD who had independently elected to prescribe lecanemab outside of study participation and had treated ≥5 patients who had completed ≥7 lecanemab infusions (aligning with the period in which most amyloid‐related imaging abnormalities [ARIAs] occur) 5 were recruited from sites varying in setting type (e.g., private, academic, and integrated healthcare systems) and location (i.e., urban, suburban, and rural). Three HCPs meeting these criteria served as an expert advisory committee involved in protocol and study instrument development. The target sample size for the full analysis was 15 HCPs.

Cross‐sectional HCP surveys and interviews

HCPs completed a 60‐min, cross‐sectional, semiquantitative survey via an electronic data capture system, which collected data on study site and HCP characteristics, standards of practice pertaining to lecanemab (e.g., eligibility and treatment monitoring), and characteristics of patients treated with lecanemab. HCPs then participated in a 90‐min, semi‐structured interview with trained interviewers, collecting standardized qualitative data on the lecanemab patient pathway and implementation learnings, including referral patterns, diagnosis, treatment administration, monitoring, the multidisciplinary team (MDT), and barriers to and facilitators of lecanemab implementation.

RESEARCH IN CONTEXT

Systematic review: The authors reviewed the literature using traditional (e.g., PubMed) sources. There is a paucity of evidence‐based guidance on lecanemab implementation in real‐world clinical practice. The limited available studies are appropriately cited. Interpretation: The findings of this interim sub‐analysis demonstrate the feasibility of lecanemab use across diverse clinical settings in the US (academic and community‐based practices in urban, suburban, and rural locations). However, they also highlighted several barriers to implementation and opportunities to optimize the current patient pathway. Future directions: Data collection is ongoing, and the retrospective case review component of the study will expand on these initial findings, providing insights into current lecanemab utilization patterns, including maintenance regimens, and real‐world efficacy and safety outcomes.

Endpoints and statistical analysis

The primary endpoint was real‐world utilization patterns of lecanemab, as summarized from survey data using descriptive statistics. The secondary endpoint was lecanemab implementation methods, barriers, and facilitators, identified from survey and interview data. De‐identified interview transcripts were coded using deductive content analysis, with predetermined codes derived from survey results, and inductive content analysis, whereby new codes were generated upon reviewing interview themes/concepts. Coding was conducted independently by two analysts using Delve qualitative data analysis software. 7 Resulting themes were used to identify lecanemab implementation methods, barriers, and facilitators.

Patient pathway insights

Referral process patterns

HCPs received a mean (SD) of 313.3 (176.2) new referrals for patients with AD per year. Mean patient volume was similar among urban, suburban, and rural settings (317.8 [187.3], 500 [N/A], and 200 [0.0], respectively). Of these patients, 231.6 (130.8) had early‐stage AD. Over half of referrals (mean [SD]: 58.8% [28.2%]) were made by physicians outside the health system; 28.5% (23.1%) of patients were referred by physicians within the health system and 12.8% (14.7%) by patient self‐referral. Interviews indicated that most referrals were from primary care physicians (PCPs), and the proportion of self‐referrals was increasing after lecanemab approval owing to increased awareness of anti‐amyloid therapies.

Barriers to and facilitators of timely and appropriate referrals from PCPs

Interviews identified several referral process barriers, as follows. (1) Patient disease stage mismatch: At referral, many patients have advanced beyond the disease stages eligible for lecanemab treatment (early AD). However, interviewees noted a recent shift toward early‐stage referrals, reflecting growing awareness. (2) Referral delays: Referrals can take up to 8 months, allowing disease progression to occur. (3) Incomplete referrals: Referrals often have missing information (e.g., initial cognitive tests and laboratory assessments); therefore, it can be challenging to determine which referrals to streamline/prioritize. Specialists must also conduct the required tests following referral, further delaying workup and, subsequently, treatment initiation. (4) Cultural denial and stigma: This can deter patient presentation and subsequent referral, particularly in underserved communities. (5) Insurance barriers: There are coverage limitations for tests and referrals, often requiring peer‐to‐peer advocacy or patient appeals.

Interviews identified several facilitators that may be implemented to improve time to and completeness of referrals. An ideal referral process was outlined (Figure 1). PCP education was identified as a crucial factor in achieving this, covering four key areas: urgency of timely detection, the process for a “complete referral,” use of MCI‐sensitive screening tools (e.g., Montreal Cognitive Assessment [MoCA]), and appropriate use of high‐reliability BBMs for triaging and prioritizing referrals.

Another identified facilitator was increased patient and care partner awareness of early AD symptoms. This would encompass culturally sensitive approaches for underserved communities, including the use of multilingual/multicultural staff, engagement with community leaders (e.g., clergy), and establishing a clinic presence in historically underserved areas.

The final identified facilitator was the use of brain health navigators. This new primary care nurse role has been introduced at three of the study sites as part of the Davos Alzheimer's Collaborative and provides support with initial workups and referrals, aiming to alleviate PCP burden and reduce delays.

Dementia specialist diagnostic process patterns

The average time from referral to diagnosis was 0 to 3 months at five (41.5%) sites, 4 to 6 months at four (33.3%) sites, 6 to 12 months at two (16.7%) sites, and 12 to 24 months at one (8.3%) site. The three sites citing the longest time from referral to diagnosis had the lowest patient volumes.

Initial assessments following referral are summarized in Table 2. These typically include medical history, blood work, cognitive tests (most commonly MoCA, used at all sites), and MRI (most commonly 1.5T or 3T with susceptibility‐weighted imaging). Interview analysis suggested that these initial assessments should ideally occur in primary care as part of a complete referral; however, they are usually conducted after a referral by specialists with advanced practice provider (APP) support.

At the time of data collection, amyloid pathology was most commonly confirmed using PET (mean [SD] 58.4% [35.3%]), followed by CSF (35.1% [30.4%]) and BBMs (10.3% [29.8%]), with a higher preference for CSF in community versus non‐community practices.

Neuropsychological referrals were used infrequently in complex cases only.

All surveyed HCPs performed APOE ε4 testing to inform risk/benefit treatment decisions, with half performing testing after amyloid confirmation and half at initial consultation. Of those, 91.7% provided genetic counseling for patients undergoing APOE ε4 testing (with/without genetic counselors), and all reported prescribing lecanemab to APOE ε4 homozygotes.

Barriers to and facilitators of timely diagnosis

Interviews identified several diagnostic process barriers, falling under five themes. (1) Patient‐related challenges: This can include comprehension issues, particularly surrounding biomarker results or denial of symptoms and/or diagnosis. (2) Sensitive communication of diagnosis: Delivering a diagnosis without causing distress or rejection is challenging. (3) Healthcare system challenges: The can include resource constraints and limited biomarker access, particularly in rural or financially constrained settings. (4) Technological/methodological challenges in biomarker interpretation: For example, there are concerns about inter‐rater variability in amyloid PET readings based on visual interpretation; however, this may be improving with more widespread adoption of computerized quantification software. (5) Cost and insurance: High cost and inconsistent insurance coverage for tests have been observed. In some centers, amyloid PET is financially unsustainable due to inadequate Medicare reimbursement. BBMs can also require out‐of‐pocket expenditures, potentially limiting access for low‐income patients.

Diagnostic facilitators identified in the interviews included clear, compassionate communication, especially when discussing complex biomarker results or genetic risk factors. BBMs were also identified as a key tool to reduce delays, as outlined in the following section.

BBM practice patterns

Most HCPs (58.3%) used high‐reliability BBMs as a triaging tool (i.e., with a positive result requiring follow‐up CSF or PET); 25% used high‐reliability BBMs as a confirmatory diagnostic tool (i.e., to confirm the presence of amyloid pathology without follow‐up CSF or PET) in cases with high clinical probability of AD. Most HCPs (75.0%) used an amyloid beta (Aβ)42/40 ratio BBM in their diagnostic approach, 50.0% used phosphorylated tau at threonine 217 (p‐tau217), 41.7% used the p‐tau217 ratio, and 8.35% used p‐tau181. Note that two BBM assays received FDA clearance during the course of the study: p‐tau217 received clearance as a BBM test to aid in the diagnosis of AD in May 2025 8 and p‐tau181 as a triaging BBM test in October 2025. 9 Blood was drawn locally and processed by third‐party labs, with turnaround times of 1 to 4 weeks.

Interviewees expressed optimism about expanding the use of high‐reliability BBMs, believing they could lead to enhanced diagnostic efficiency, accessibility, and accuracy. They reported that in their current capacity, high‐reliability BBMs reduce invasive testing and support early disease detection.

Barriers to and facilitators of BBM use

Interviews identified three key barriers to BBM use. (1) Insurance coverage inconsistencies had been observed by some HCPs. (2) Technical/methodological challenges, including the interpretation of BBM results (particularly indeterminate results when using the dual threshold approach, requiring further testing), lack of standardized interpretation protocols, and inconsistent assay quality across labs (most interviews were conducted prior to publication of the evidence‐based standardized sample‐handling protocol for AD BBMs from the Global Biomarker Standardization Consortium). 10 Additionally, there is a risk of misinterpretation if insufficiently reliable assays are used (i.e., those not meeting recommended specificity/sensitivity thresholds) 1 , 11 or if assays are used in the incorrect context (i.e., triage or confirmatory). (3) Lack of a standardized/clear pathway following a BBM result, but standardized pathways may help neurologists streamline referrals, ensuring patients with positive BBM results receive a prompt referral. This would reduce the risk of patients progressing beyond the eligibility criteria for anti‐amyloid treatment while awaiting clarification of their results.

Identified future facilitators of high‐reliability BBM implementation in routine clinical practice include improved insurance coverage, enhanced standardization to reduce indeterminate results, and standard protocols for approaching different BBM outcomes.

Lecanemab treatment administration and monitoring patterns

The mean (SD) number of providers prescribing lecanemab per site was 5.1 (3.1; range: 2 to 11). At the time of survey completion, the total number of patients treated with lecanemab after approval across all sites was ∼1342. The demographics of patients treated with lecanemab in clinical practice are summarized in Table 3. Across all sites, time from diagnosis to first infusion was ≤6 months: 0 to 2 months at seven (58.3%) sites, 2 to 4 months at two (16.7%) sites, and 4 to 6 months at three (25.0%) sites. No differentiating factors were identified from the surveys for the sites citing the longest timeframe (e.g., location, practice setting, patient travel time). Interview analysis identified limited resource and insurance barriers as potential contributory factors.

The mean (SD) number of infusions per week was 70.3 (99.1). Most sites did not have a dedicated infusion site (83.3%). Instead, infusions were most commonly performed at an outpatient/clinic facility (58.3%) or ambulatory infusion center (41.7%). Infusions were typically prepared by registered nurses (50.0%) or pharmacists (33.0%). Patients typically traveled 80.5 kilometers to receive infusions, as reported by 91.7% of sites.

Monitoring assessments are summarized in Table 2. Interviewees noted that MRI scans for safety monitoring were performed at baseline and between infusions, as detailed in the US prescribing information, and every 6 to 12 months thereafter. Two sites perform additional MRI monitoring for patients who are APOE ε4 homozygous. To facilitate MRI monitoring, sites have implemented dedicated MRI protocols and provided training for neuroradiologists on ARIA detection and microhemorrhage quantification. Assessments to monitor treatment response were typically performed every 3 to 6 months, often in line with insurance requirements. Along with neurological examinations and cognitive assessments, some sites also used biomarker tracking and digital tools. Sites utilized several methods for patient tracking, including spreadsheets and checklists, with some also exploring the use of nurse navigators to facilitate these processes.

As per the US prescribing information, following 18 months of bi‐weekly infusions, patients can transition to a maintenance dosing regimen of intravenous infusions every 4 weeks or weekly at‐home subcutaneous administration. 5 Interview analysis found that HCPs prefer a personalized, evidence‐based approach to maintenance therapy, considering amyloid clearance, disease progression, patient functioning, safety, patient/care partner preferences, and insurance coverage. Most sites assess transitioning to a maintenance regimen at 18 months, per label (some specialists consider transitioning before 18 months, at their discretion). The study HCPs would not recommend treatment continuation when progressing beyond mild AD dementia (e.g., National Institute on Aging and Alzheimer's Association numeric clinical staging stage > 4). 12

Barriers to and facilitators of lecanemab treatment administration and monitoring

Interviews identified barriers to lecanemab treatment administration and monitoring, grouped into seven themes: (1) patient‐related challenges, including adherence to infusion and MRI monitoring, care partner reliability, and expectation management; (2) logistical challenges, including infusion and MRI scheduling, organization of patient transport, and cross‐system patient tracking; (3) healthcare system and resource challenges, including limited staff and infusion facility capacity, and administrative burden; (4) clinical challenges, including complexity of treatment monitoring, difficulty in demonstrating effectiveness to patients/care partners, and complexity of treatment discontinuation decision‐making; (5) technological and methodological challenges, including limited availability of monitoring tools (e.g., adequate digital cognitive assessments) and MRI read variability; (6) safety challenges, including predicting, interpreting, and managing ARIA; and (7) insurance and reimbursement issues for required monitoring assessments.

Interviews identified several potential tools/strategies for facilitating treatment administration and monitoring. A shared decision‐making model was proposed to facilitate collaborative and informed treatment decisions (Figure 2). Other potential strategies included patient education to support adherence, the development of digital patient tracking systems, and the utilization of nurse navigators or care coordinators to facilitate cross‐system patient tracking.

Role of MDT

Interviews identified several key stakeholders who contributed to the diagnosis, treatment, and monitoring of patients receiving lecanemab. PCPs are the principal referral source and are ideally involved in conducting triaging tests and ordering laboratory/imaging assessments. Neurologists or other prescribing specialists are responsible for patient management throughout the pathway. APPs and geriatricians have a crucial role in patient management, supporting neurologists (or other prescribing specialists) in conducting diagnostic/monitoring assessments. Radiologists conduct diagnostic imaging assessments to rule out non‐AD causes of cognitive impairment, analyze PET scans for confirmation of amyloid pathology, and interpret MRI surveillance following lecanemab initiation. Other key support staff include infusion and pharmacy personnel, emergency room physicians, and social workers, along with anti‐amyloid therapy care coordinators, who facilitate patient communication and logistics. At some sites, genetic counselors were used to discuss genetic risks with patients and their families after APOE ε4 testing; however, this was not feasible for all sites, and most prescribing specialists were comfortable discussing genetic risk with their patients themselves.

DISCUSSION

This study found that, as the first anti‐amyloid therapy to receive traditional FDA approval for the treatment of patients with early AD, the introduction of lecanemab into the US healthcare system necessitated rapid and significant shifts in the AD patient pathway. These included the implementation of fast‐track referral pathways, amyloid confirmation and ARIA MRI monitoring protocols, and the widespread use of apolipoprotein (APOE) genotyping and BBM testing. This study also summarizes important considerations for the successful implementation of lecanemab into diverse academic and community‐based clinical centers (Table 4).

Treating physicians (neurologists and AD specialists) identified the referral process as a key area for optimization in the current patient pathway. Efficient and complete referrals are crucial for patients to receive a timely diagnosis of AD in the early stages of the disease, when lecanemab is indicated. PCP education and the implementation of standardized processes are crucial to streamline and optimize referral pathways. Although limited specialist access can also act as a barrier to timely diagnosis, it is believed that these facilitators could not only increase the timely referral of appropriate patients but also alleviate specialist burden by reducing inappropriate referrals and ensuring a thorough workup prior to referral. BBMs were also highlighted as an emerging tool to reduce diagnostic delays and HCP burden. In line with existing literature, 1 , 13 treating physicians expressed optimism about the expanding use of high‐reliability BBMs to enhance diagnostic efficiency and increase accessibility, particularly in socioeconomically disadvantaged populations, who are often disproportionately impacted by missed/delayed diagnoses. 14 , 15 The treating physicians emphasized the importance of selecting high‐reliability assays (i.e., those meeting the CEOi BBM workgroup and Alzheimer's Association recommendations) 1 , 11 in the correct capacity (i.e., as confirmatory or triage tools) and interpreting them with complete clinical context (i.e., to establish the pre‐test probability of amyloid pathology) to avoid misinterpretation. 1 , 11 With these key considerations, it is believed that high‐reliability assays used in a triaging capacity can predict the presence or absence of amyloid pathology as part of the initial diagnostic workup, before CSF or PET, potentially reducing the associated cost, burden, and invasiveness of these “traditional” diagnostics in patients without amyloid pathology. In a confirmatory capacity, BBMs may be used to confirm the presence of amyloid pathology without additional tests in patients with a high suspicion of AD based on clinical grounds. 1 , 13 However, further work is needed to demonstrate that these assays have adequate specificity for AD in both primary and specialty care as a confirmatory test. 11 In particular, it is important to consider that the presence of certain chronic conditions, including chronic kidney disease, obesity, and cardiovascular disease, can affect plasma levels of Aβ40, Aβ42, p‐tau181, and p‐tau217. 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 Additionally, some studies have reported differences in BBM levels by race and ethnicity. 21 , 22 , 23 Given this biological variability, the field is moving toward multi‐analyte analysis to potentially mitigate its impact. However, these variances are important considerations, particularly since existing BBM thresholds for amyloid positivity were primarily derived from populations that were not fully representative of the general AD population with respect to race, age, and comorbidities. Therefore, the generalizability and accuracy of current thresholds in the real world remain to be determined. 24

Introducing these facilitators may reduce systemic delays and referral gaps, thereby decreasing the number of patients who progress beyond eligibility for anti‐amyloid therapies, reducing time lost due to preventable progression, and ensuring optimal benefit of anti‐amyloid therapies by enabling initiation in the earliest symptomatic stages of the disease, where they have been shown to demonstrate the most efficacy. 25

Another key area for optimization was diagnosis. Variation was observed among the study sites regarding time from referral to diagnosis and from diagnosis to first infusion; however, no causal differentiating factors were identified to explain the variation in timing. This suggests that the determinants influencing the efficiency of diagnosis are likely complex and multifactorial. That being said, facilitators to timely diagnosis were identified from the interview analysis, including the widespread adoption of high‐reliability BBMs and clear patient communication.

In terms of lecanemab treatment administration and monitoring, a number of patient‐related, logistical, and system‐level challenges were identified that could be alleviated with approved maintenance dosing regimens (i.e., after 18 months of bi‐weekly infusions, patients can transition to a maintenance regimen of intravenous infusions every 4 weeks or weekly at‐home subcutaneous administration). 5 This could minimize time demands and alleviate quality‐of‐life burdens for patients and care partners. It should also be noted that as administration routes evolve (e.g., potential for subcutaneous administration at treatment initiation), so too will implementation and utilization practices. The intravenous maintenance dosing regimen was approved by the FDA shortly before data collection commenced (in January 2025), and the subcutaneous maintenance dosing regimen was approved during the study period (in August 2025). 5 Therefore, hypotheses regarding the impact of maintenance dosing require validation. These hypotheses will be further investigated. in an expansion of this ongoing study.

This study offers valuable insights into the use of lecanemab in real‐world clinical practice and identifies key areas for optimization. However, the cross‐sectional survey study design has several limitations, primarily susceptibility to sampling and recall bias and the potential for voluntary participation bias. A key strength of the study is the variation in practice settings and geographic location of the participating HCPs. However, as we sought to outline operational workflows from settings with established lecanemab pathways, most of the HCPs included in the study were early lecanemab adopters, with several having lecanemab clinical trial experience before transitioning to clinical standard of care. This, along with the small sample size, means the perspectives reported may not be fully representative of current clinical practice. Likewise, as all participating HCPs were prescribing specialists, it may be beneficial to obtain the perspectives of PCPs, given their key role in referrals. Lastly, BBMs and maintenance dosing practices evolved during the study period; therefore, survey and interview responses may have differed depending on when they were completed.

In summary, timely diagnosis and equitable access to lecanemab can be facilitated by several key factors, including appropriate use of high‐reliability BBMs in both a triage and confirmatory capacity, patient education, PCP training to increase referral process efficiency, leveraging MDT members, including APPs and care coordinators, digital patient tracking systems, and utilization of approved maintenance dosing regimens.
