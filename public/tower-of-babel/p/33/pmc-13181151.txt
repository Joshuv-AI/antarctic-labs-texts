Use of tandem‐affinity chromatography to incorporate nucleotide loading of small GTPases during their purification
Authors: Rofiat Oladimeji, Chigozie Odo, Ethan Lewis, Gracie V.L. Reid, Christian W. Johnson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA bottleneck for structure–function studies of closely related proteins is purification. Here, we built upon new affinity protein purification technology to develop a uniform and rapid tandem‐affinity purification protocol to generate significant quantities of pure GTPase proteins for use in structure–function studies. Our method relies on new and commercially available split‐intein based technology. We show that this technology can be used to speed up purification of small GTPases and at the same time facilitate nucleotide loading. Using K‐Ras4B and H‐Ras as case studies, we demonstrate that we can generate crystallizable grade protein, homogenously loaded with either GDP or the non‐hydrolysable GTP analogue GppNHp, in less than 36 h.

INTRODUCTION

Small GTPases are a superfamily of over 150 proteins that play a myriad of roles in the regulation of cell signaling, metabolism, motility, morphology, and intracellular trafficking (Cherfils & Zeghouf, 2013; Rojas et al., 2012). Generally, small GTPases can be viewed as regulatory nodes capable of switching between two guanine nucleotide‐bound states (Wittinghofer & Vetter, 2011). GDP or GTP binding influences GTPase conformation, which in turn alters the identity of GTPase binding partners to provide a “switch” mechanism for their regulation of biological output (Figure 1a). Thus, a defining factor in biochemical, biophysical, and structural studies of small GTPases is the acquisition of a relatively “pure” nucleotide‐bound state. However, small GTPases have high affinity for nucleotides and are rarely found in their apo‐state. For instance, previous studies of H‐Ras and K‐Ras report pM to high nM affinities for guanine nucleotides (Carta et al., 2026; Ford et al., 2009).

(b) The first two columns describe a general “tag”‐less purification scheme (Johnson et al., 2016) and a scheme incorporating a traditional His‐tag (Dharmaiah et al., 2019) for use in setting up crystal trays of the small GTPase K‐Ras. The third column shows an overview of the purification scheme described in the current article.

Methods for purifying small GTPases using recombinant techniques are numerous. Two example outlines of protein purification, one using a combination of tags (Dharmaiah et al., 2019), and one without (Johnson et al., 2019), are shown for the archetypical RAS sub‐family GTPase K‐Ras (Figure 1b, columns 1 and 2). However, while GTPase purification methods have improved in tandem with new approaches to protein purification, methods to homogenize nucleotide loading have not. Several non‐hydrolysable GTP analogues have been developed to “trap” the loaded protein in their active and GTP‐bound states (e.g., GTPγS, GppNHp, GppCH2p), and the standard for high‐efficiency loading of a particular nucleotide (e.g., GDP, GTP) involves either multiple rounds of saturating nucleotide along with a chemical mechanism to accelerate equilibration (e.g., EDTA), or the additional use of an enzyme, such as alkaline phosphatase, to break down the undesired nucleotide to GMP (Waybright & Stephen, 2024). Use of this “co‐enzyme” approach, first demonstrated by Scherer et al. in 1989 and shortly after described by John et al. (1990), is still standard for nucleotide loading in structural biology studies (John et al., 1990; Johnson et al., 2016; Scherer et al., 1989; Waybright & Stephen, 2024). However, this approach requires that the small GTPase in question be purified to near homogeneity before re‐contamination with commercially available, yet expensive, resin bound phosphatase and several rounds of buffer exchange (Johnson et al., 2019; Waybright & Stephen, 2024). Thus, nucleotide loading is an expensive and time‐consuming task that exposes the purified small GTPase to further contamination and denaturation.

Here, we discuss the use of a split‐intein purification system that relies on the sequential use of tags to couple protein purification and nucleotide exchange (Figure 1b, third column). Several intein systems are available for protein purification (e.g., iCapTag, IMPACT) (Clifford et al., 2024; Shi et al., 2013). Our approach makes use of a newly available Protein Select resin from Cytiva Life Sciences that couples a fast‐ and high‐affinity column binding step with a slow auto‐cleavage step, resulting in complete removal of N‐terminal tag sequences (Clifford et al., 2024). Protein Select resin is a split‐intein technology because the tagged protein contains a portion of the intein, while the resin contains the remainder. Re‐assembling of the functional intein immobilizes the tagged protein to the resin and facilitates separation from contaminants. After reassembling, the intein undergoes a conformational change to promote cleavage and release of the target protein (Clifford et al., 2024). By incorporating nucleotide loading after an initial immobilized metal affinity chromatography (IMAC) step, but before the final Cytiva Protein Select resin (CPS) step, we have generated pure and homogenously loaded Ras proteins. While lacking in overall yield as compared to non‐intein approaches, the speed, ease, and efficiency at which this method can be performed make it ideal for smaller academic labs.

RESULTS

Design of vector and validation of its functionality

We sought to create an Escherichia coli expression vector that could accomplish three tasks. First, we wanted the resulting protein of interest (POI) to have alternative routes for purification. Second, we wanted to decrease the necessity of expression optimization by adding a pro‐solubility element to the encoded POI. Third, we wanted to take advantage of CPS resin to remove these non‐native elements. Therefore, we designed a tandem 6xHis‐tag and Maltose Binding Protein tag (MBP‐tag) for selectivity and solubility, followed by the Cytiva protein select tag (CPS‐tag) at the N‐terminal end of the POI site. We chose truncated K‐Ras4B (residues 1–169) and truncated H‐Ras (residues 1–166) GTPases as the POI for the studies described here. We also included a Thrombin cleavage sequence as part of the linker between the MBP‐tag and CPS‐tags for use as a potential cleavage site if the efficacy of the CPS binding and proteolysis step was compromised by the internal CPS‐tag.

Before synthesis, we used AlphaFold3 to predict structures of our multi‐tagged K‐Ras4B (Supplementary Figure 1) (Abramson et al., 2024). As expected, the structures of the MBP‐tag and K‐Ras4B protein coding sequences were highly predictable, while the 6xHis‐tag and linker sequences showed high positional error. In contrast, the CPS‐tag showed reasonable position error with a consistent fold between predicted structures, indicating that internal placement of the Cc‐intein fragment, originally developed from Npu DnaE, would not impact its function (Figure 2a). Based on our Alphafold predictions, we utilized the design shown in Figure 2a for vector synthesis, and the overall features of this plasmid are shown in Figure 2b. The new vector was named 6HMCK, for 6xHis‐tag, MBP‐tag, CPS‐tag, K‐Ras4B consecutive sequences. The vector sequence is available in Supplementary Figure 2. To maximize the utility of our vector, we included NotI and EcoRI cloning sites inside the CPS‐tag and at the end of K‐Ras4B sequences, as well as a AflIII restriction sites (Supplementary Figure 2). We found that synthesis of DNA fragments containing the CPS‐tag with a silent substitution to remove the AflIII restriction site in the CPS sequence (i.e., 5′‐AGAACGTGTATG‐3′ to 5′‐AGAATGTTTATG‐3′) allowed for rapid screening and validation of clones via PCR and restriction digest. An example of cloning and validation by restriction digest is shown in Figure 2c.

Next, we tested the functionality of the 6HCMK vector for expression and production of K‐Ras4B. As shown in Figure 3a, transformation of the BL21 E. coli strain and subsequent induction by IPTG promoted overexpression of a protein product of the expected size. Enrichment and purification by IMAC, followed by application to the CPS column, and overnight incubation at 4°C, resulted in a pure protein product with the corresponding molecular weight of K‐Ras4B. As expected, we did not observe equivalently intense bands corresponding to the N‐terminal cleavage product, that is, the encoded “6HMC” tag protein. We explored different elution steps from the CPS column to determine where the cleavage product was eluting (Figure 3b). Consistent with Figure 3a, the eluent was pure for the K‐Ras4B band, whereas both the N‐terminal cleavage product and remaining un‐cleaved protein could only be detected in significant amounts during the column regeneration step. Thus, our tandem affinity and internal CPS‐tag strategy appeared sufficient to produce pure protein with minimal contamination from the auto‐cleavage reaction, or leakage of the uncleaved product from the CPS resin.

To ensure that our purified K‐Ras4B was folded and functional, we performed nucleotide exchange assays using fluorescent nucleotide (Figure 3c). In response to excess GTP, the fluorescence signal decayed exponentially, indicating that CPS purified K‐Ras4B was indeed functional. Furthermore, we found that the rate constant for nucleotide exchange calculated for the K‐Ras4B purified by our method was not significantly different from rate constants calculated from traditionally purified 6xHis‐K‐Ras4B or rate constants taken from the literature (Figure 3c). Further, when we subjected our protein to crystallization screening using the PEG/Ion HT kit from Hampton Reagents, we observed several crystals that formed only in the presence of GDP‐bound wildtype K‐Ras (Figure 3d). Thus, we conclude that our vector and purification approach is sufficient to produce pure and functional K‐Ras4B.

Incorporation of non‐hydrolysable nucleotide analogue loading into the purification scheme

Based on our success with the tandem affinity tag, we next tested whether we could use the final CPS step in our protocol as an alternative means for GppNHp loading. First, after the initial purification and enrichment of K‐Ras4B from IMAC, we performed buffer exchange into a solution containing ammonium sulfate, zinc chloride, DTT, soluble alkaline phosphatase, and GppNHp at 3‐ to 10‐fold molar excess compared to K‐Ras4B. We then tested whether it was necessary to remove the ammonium sulfate buffer prior to application to the CPS column. Figure 4a shows that there was no apparent difference in purity in response to the incorporation of nucleotide loading before the CPS step, nor was removal of the ammonium sulfate buffer necessary before loading of protein onto the column. While nucleotide pre‐loading did decrease the overall yield of cleaved K‐Ras4B (Figure 4b), the GppNHp loaded K‐Ras4B is folded and capable of binding to canonical effector proteins in a nucleotide dependent manner (Figure 4c). Thus, our procedure generates active GppNHp bound K‐Ras4B for use in biochemical and related functional assays.

(a) Representative samples of CPS after loading with GppNHp and removal of the nucleotide exchange buffer before the CPS step (lane 1), and loading with GppNHp without a buffer exchange step before CPS (lane 2). Each lane was loaded with 40 μg protein. (b) Comparison of yields after GppNHp loading per elution step. Overall decrease in yield as a result of GppNHp loading is ~40%. (c) Pulldown experiments using GDP or GppNHp loaded K‐Ras4B and GST‐tagged full‐length Rassf5. All lanes contained Rassf5 and each pulldown was performed in duplicate. (d) Crystallization of a control small GTPase, H‐Ras A59E, after pre‐loading with GppNHp using the above method. Crystals were formed under similar conditions as in Johnson et al. (2022) (PDB codes 7JII, 7JIH). An example of diffraction from these crystals is shown in Supplementary Figure 4.

Use of our purification scheme for proteins involved in biophysical or protein structure assays necessitates a high degree of homogeneity of nucleotide loading. To confirm that our nucleotide loading technique is comparable to other approaches, we used the GDP‐Glo assay to detect residual GDP bound to K‐Ras4B after exchange. This was done by first boiling the protein at 75°C to denature purified K‐Ras4B and release GDP into the solution to make it an available substrate for the GDP‐Glo assay. Assessment of released GDP from boiled K‐Ras4B was consistent with our standard GDP measurements up to a protein concentration of 3 μM (Supplementary Figure 3). Using this modified version of the GDP‐Glo assay, we tested several concentrations of GppNHp and alkaline phosphatase for nucleotide exchange, and found that in all cases detection of GppNHp loading was 99% (Supplementary Table 1). In all, our data support the efficacy of our approach to produce pure, untagged, and nucleotide loaded K‐Ras4B. To underscore this point, we have been able to reproduce crystallization of H‐Ras A59E bound to GppNHp (Figure 4d and Supplementary Figure 4).

DISCUSSION

Our goal was to create a facile and rapid protocol for the purification of homogenously loaded small GTPases bound to non‐hydrolysable nucleotide for structural biology applications. Here, we describe a protocol that facilitates the generation of ~1–2 mg of GppNHp loaded K‐Ras4B from 6 L of bacteria culture, pure enough for crystallization, in less than 2 days. Thus, our approach is reasonable for academic labs doing structural biology or biochemical studies requiring small to moderate amounts of pure protein. Further, we imagine this purification system will also be compatible with other systems that may require nucleotide or ligand pre‐loading. However, we note two caveats to our method. First, overall expression of protein from our plasmid was comparably lower than other expression vectors we are experienced with, particularly compared to untagged protein expression and its purification (Johnson et al., 2016, 2022, 2023). Attempts at longer induction times or alternative broths (e.g., terrific broth) were largely unsuccessful in improving yield (not shown). Considering the larger size of the expression construct this may not be surprising, but more extensive studies should be performed. The second caveat appeared to be heterogeneous binding of tagged K‐Ras to the CPS column. We consistently observed a portion of loaded protein that never underwent cleavage, regardless of incubation time. While we did not delve deeply into the underlying mechanism, it does impact overall yield. However, considering the accelerated nature of protein production, we believe these caveats do not outweigh the positives of the described protocol. For instance, we have circumvented the need for a separate nucleotide exchange procedure and for size‐exclusion chromatography. These two features free up investigator time and reduce FPLC usage to allow other experimental activities. Thus, using our protocol a single trainee should be capable of purifying three to four separate GTPases, inclusive of nucleotide loading, in a single week with relative ease.

METHODS AND MATERIALS

Plasmid design

Description of the plasmid(s) and their design are discussed in the main text. All plasmids and DNA sequences for cloning in the 6HCMK plasmids were synthesized by TWIST Biosciences. The vector sequence is available in Supplementary Figure 2.

Protein expression, cell lysis, and clarification

A starter culture of E. coli BL21 cells containing the 6HCMK plasmid was prepared overnight using 200 mL of LB media supplemented with ampicillin at 50 mg/L at 37°C with a shaking speed of 225 rpm. The following day, six baffled flasks, each containing 1 L of broth and ampicillin, were inoculated with the starter culture at a dilution ratio of 1:50 and allowed to grow until an optical density (OD) of 0.6–0.8. Once the target OD was achieved, the temperature was lowered to 30°C, and 1 mM of Isopropyl β‐D‐1‐thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) was added to induce protein expression for 4–5 h. Post‐induction, cells were collected via centrifugation at 4000 × g for 20 min at 4°C. Cell Pellets were stored at −80°C until use. For protein purification, cells were resuspended in Cell Resuspension Buffer (20 mM Tris, 100 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 5% glycerol, 20 μM GDP, 10 mM imidazole, 1 mM benzamidine, and 1 μg/mL leupeptin at pH 8.0). Cells were then lysed via sonication using a Fisherbrand Model 505 Sonic Dismembrator with a 1/2 in. diameter probe at 30–35% amplitude with 45 s ON and OFF cycles for 6 min. Lysates were then clarified by centrifugation at 11,000 rpm for 20 min at 4°C. Once clarified, all subsequent purification steps were conducted on an AKTA Pure FPLC system from Cytiva Life Sciences in deli fridge set to 4°C.

The GST‐RASSF5 construct used in this paper consists of the R777‐E261 plasmid cloned into the pDEST15 Destination vector. To produce the RASSF5 protein, 20 mL of LB media with 50 mg/L ampicillin was inoculated and shaken overnight at 37°C at 225 rpm. The next day, the starter culture was added to 1 L LB media and grown at 37°C at 225 rpm until reaching an OD of 0.6–0.8. Protein induction was induced with L‐arabinose to a final concentration of 2 g/L. These cells were then grown for a further 4 h at 30°C at 225 rpm. The cell pellet was resuspended in 40 mL RLB buffer (20 mM HEPES, 120 mM NaCl, 10% v/v glycerol; pH 7.50) and re‐centrifuged for 20 min at 4000 × g. Cell pellets were resuspended in 20 mL RLB buffer and sonicated at 23% amplitude for 5 min (45 s on/off). Cell lysates were then clarified by centrifugation for 20 min at 11,000 rpm. 1 mL aliquots of cell lysates were flash frozen and stored at −80°C.

Purification of GDP bound K‐Ras

Clarified lysate was applied at a flow rate of 2.5 mL/min using a 150 mL superloop to a 5 mL HiTrap TALON crude column prepared with CoCl2 via the manufacturer's instructions. Protein binding to the column was performed in Buffer A (20 mM Tris, 100 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 5% glycerol, and 20 μM GDP at pH 8.0). The column was washed with 15 column volumes (CV) of Buffer A after sample application. Elution was performed in 20%, 40%, 60%, and 80% gradient steps of Buffer B (20 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 5% glycerol, 20 μM GDP, and 300 mM imidazole), each comprised of 2CV, at a flow rate of 2.5 mL/min. Fractions of eluted protein were identified by SDS‐PAGE and then collected and concentrated before undergoing buffer exchange using a Sephadex™ G‐25 DNA grade gravity column and fresh Buffer A supplemented with 1 mM DTT. Fractions of 1–1.5 mL were collected by gravity, and protein was monitored using 5 μL of protein solution applied to 100 μL of Bradford reagent. During the buffer exchange process, the 5 mL HiTrap Protein Select (CPS) column was equilibrated in Buffer A with 1 mM DTT per the manufacturer's protocol. After buffer exchange, the protein was reapplied to the AKTA system in a 5–10 mL loop, and the column was washed at a flow rate of 4 mL/min for 15CV. After a 240‐min pause in the flow, protein was eluted over 3CV at 8 mL/min. Eluent was also collected after two more 240‐min pauses in the same way. Eluted protein was validated by SDS‐PAGE, pooled, and concentrated to greater than 10 mg/mL before being used or flash frozen in liquid nitrogen for storage at −80°C.

Pre‐column nucleotide loading

To load K‐Ras with GppNHp prior to CPS, the protein was concentrated after IMAC and buffer exchanged using the Sephadex™ G‐25 column protocol described above using nucleotide exchange buffer (32 mM Tris, 200 mM ammonium sulfate, 1 mM DTT, 0.1 mM ZnCl2). 150 mM GppNHp, dissolved in ultrapure nuclease‐free water, was added in an excess molar ratio to protein ranging from 3:1 to 10:1, along with 40–80 U of Calf Intestinal Alkaline Phosphatase (Invitrogen, 18009‐019). Protein was gently rotated (80 rpm) at 37°C for 60 min. Following nucleotide loading, concentrated MgCl2 was added to a final concentration of 20 mM MgCl2. The protein solution was allowed to incubate at 5 min at room temperature before loading onto the AKTA FPLC and application to the HiTrap Cytiva Protein Select column. Fractions of eluted protein were identified by SDS‐PAGE, collected and concentrated, and underwent another round of buffer exchange into stabilization buffer (20 mM HEPES, 50 mM NaCl, 20 mM MgCl2, 1 mM DTT at pH 7.5). Protein was concentrated to ~10 mg/mL before being used or flash freezing in liquid nitrogen for storage at −80°C.

Adaptation of GDP‐glo assay to determine nucleotide exchange of K‐Ras

To evaluate the efficiency of GppNHp loading relative to the remaining GDP in the protein, we conducted a GDP‐Glo assay using the Promega GDP‐Glo kit. In this experiment, we loaded the protein with 3× GppNHp and 40U of alkaline phosphatase; 3× GppNHp with 80U of alkaline phosphatase; 10× GppNHp with 40U of alkaline phosphatase; and 10× GppNHp with 80U of alkaline phosphatase before cleavage. After purification CPS, we performed a serial dilution of the protein ranging from 25 to 0.025 μM. Samples were boiled at 75°C for 5 min to denature protein and release any bound nucleotide. Boiled samples were then spun down at 16,000 × g for 5 min. The cleared supernatant was then assayed using kit instructions.

Binding of GppNHp‐loaded K‐Ras to RASSF5

Pulldown of recombinant and purified K‐Ras was performed essentially as in (Taylor et al., 2001) and modified in (Johnson et al., 2022). First, 250 μL of glutathione beads were washed six times with 200 μL RLB containing 1% IGEPAL, with centrifugation at 800 rpm for 2 min at 4°C in between washes. Concurrently, IGEPAL was added to 0.5% volume to thawed GST‐RASSF5 lysate. After washing the glutathione beads, 1 mL of GST‐RASSF5 lysate was added to the bead and rocked for 2 h at 4°C. After rocking, beads were washed six times with 600 μL RLB with 1% IGEPAL, with centrifugation at 800 rpm for 2 min at 4°C in between washes. Loaded beads were then resuspended in 50 μL RLB supplemented with 1% IGEPAL and 20 mM MgCl2. 40 μL of the resuspended bead set was then aliquoted per protein per replicate. To these bead aliquots, either K‐Ras4B (GDP) or K‐Ras4B (GppNHp) was added to reach a total amount of 5 μg protein per replicate. These bead sets were then brought to a volume of 400 μL using RLB with 1% IGEPAL and 20 mM MgCl2. These bead sets were then rocked for 1 h at 4°C. After rocking, the bead sets were washed four times using 200 μL RLB with 1% IGEPAL and 20 mM MgCl2 with centrifugation at 800 rpm for 2 min at 4°C between washes. The glutathione bead sets were run on SDS‐PAGE and transferred overnight onto a nitrocellulose membrane. The next day, the nitrocellulose membrane was treated with Ponceau S stain and bands were recorded using a Bio‐Rad ChemiDoc. Ponceau S stain was removed from the membrane via a 0.1% NaOH solution, and then blocked for 5 min using 20 mL of Every‐Blot Blocking Buffer from Bio‐Rad. Concurrently, RAS‐10 primary antibody (Millipore Sigma; Cat#.05‐516) was diluted to 200 μL, and then added directly to the blocking buffer at a final dilution of 1:1000. Primary blotting was allowed to proceed overnight at 4°C. The next day, the membrane was washed 5 times with 1× TBS (0.05% Tween‐20) for 5 min each. After removal of the primary antibody, the membrane was re‐blocked in 20 mL of Every‐Blot Blocking Buffer for 5 min, and then StarBright Blue 700 Goat Anti‐Mouse IgG secondary antibody was added to a final dilution of 1:2500. The membrane was rocked at room temperature for 1 h before being washed three more times with 1× TBS (0.05% Tween‐20) for 15 min each. The secondary blot was then imaged using a Bio‐Rad ChemiDoc.

Kinetics of Mant‐GDP exchange for GTP

His‐tagged wild type K‐Ras4B (6xHis‐K‐Ras4B) from (Johnson et al., 2022) was used as a control for these experiments and was purified without an AKTA using an Immobilized Metal Ion Affinity Pulldown approach. 1 mL of LB media supplemented with 50 mg/mL was inoculated and shaken overnight at 37°C and 225 rpm. The next day, the starter culture was added to 100 mL of fresh LB media and grown until the culture reached an OD of 0.6–0.8. Protein expression was induced with 1 mM of IPTG and grown for 4 h at 30°C and 225 rpm. Cells were centrifuged at 4°C 4000 rpm for 20 min and then stored at −80°C. The next day, the cell pellet was resuspended with lysis buffer (20 mM Tris, 100 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 5% glycerol, 20 μM GDP, 10 mM imidazole, 1 mM benzamidine and 1 μg/mL leupeptin at pH 7.5) and sonicated at 23% amplitude for 5 min (45 s on/off) using a Fisherbrand Model 505 Sonic Dismembrator with a 1/2 in. diameter probe. Clarification of the lysate was done by centrifugation at 11,000 rpm for 20 min at 4°C. The supernatant was collected and chilled on ice. 200 μL of HisPur™ resin was washed three times with lysis buffer by centrifugation at 800 rpm for 2 min. Resin was added to 10 mL of lysate and incubated at 4°C for 30 min. After incubation, the lysate‐resin mixture was spun down at 4°C at 800 rpm for 2 min. Supernatant was discarded, and resin washed four times with 5 mL Buffer A (20 mM Tris, 100 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 5% glycerol, 10 mM imidazole, and 20 μM GDP at pH 8.0) at 800 rpm for 2 min for each wash. The protein was eluted with Buffer B (20 mM Tris, 100 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 5% glycerol, 150 mM imidazole, and 20 μM GDP at pH 8.0). The eluent was then concentrated to 500 μL using a 3 kDa Amicon Ultra −15 mL concentrator at 4000 rpm for 40 min in 4°C. Concentrated protein was collected, flash frozen using liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C.

Both the wildtype 6xHis‐K‐Ras and tag‐free K‐Ras4B were preloaded with mant‐GDP using a slightly modified protocol from the NCI KRAS initiative (Waybright & Stephen, 2024). Briefly, 500 μL of each protein (50–70 nM) was added to an Amicon ultra‐0.5 mL concentrator and spun at 10,000 rpm for 5 min at 4°C. Proteins were then resuspended with 350 μL of exchange buffer (50 mM HEPES, 150 mM NaCl, 200 mM (NH4)2SO4, 25 mM EDTA, 1 mM DTT, pH 7.4) and spun down at 10,000 rpm for 5 min in 4°C. This step was repeated once. ~180 μL of each protein solution was transferred to a new Eppendorf tube. Empty concentrators were spun down again for 1 min at 1000 rpm, and concentrators were washed with 350 μL of fresh exchange buffer which was then transferred to the protein solution to give a final volume of 530 μL. A concentration of Mant‐GDP 10 times that of the protein was added to each solution and rocked at 4°C. After 3 h, another 10× mant‐GDP was added to each protein and rocked under the same condition for 1 h. MgCl2 was then added to a concentration of 18 mM to quench the activity of EDTA, and the nucleotide exchanged protein underwent buffer exchange into stabilization buffer (50 mM HEPES, 150 mM NaCl, 1.5 mM MgCl2, and 1 mM DTT at pH 7.4) using the Sephadex™ G‐25 DNA grade gravity column method described above.

We measured the release of mant‐GDP by exchange with free GTP for both the wildtype 6xHis‐K‐Ras4B and tag‐free K‐Ras. The nucleotide exchange assay was done using Greiner black non‐binding 96 well clear flat‐bottom plates and an Agilent Biotek Synergy H1 microplate reader heated to 37°C. Each experimental well contained 130 μL of reaction buffer (55 mM Tris, 55 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 5 mM DTT) and 100 nM of either 6xHis‐K‐Ras wildtype or tag‐free K‐Ras4B wildtype. The reference wells contained 130 μL of reaction buffer and 100 nM of free mant‐GDP. The plate was inserted into the microplate reader, and the microplate was set to dispense GTP to a final concentration of 1 μM per well with a 150 μL total reaction volume. Surrounding wells contained 150 μL of 1× PBS. Excitation and emission were 360 and 440 nm, respectively. GraphPad prism was used to analyze and fit the data using one phase dissociation protocol.
