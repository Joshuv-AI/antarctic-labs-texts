Introduction

Pediatric hepatic tumors pose intricate diagnostic challenges, marked by diverse clinical presentations and the limited communicative abilities of young patients. These tumors often manifest with nonspecific symptoms, including hepatomegaly and abdominal swelling, necessitating meticulous and systematic evaluation. Additionally, the elevation of serum alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) levels, commonly associated with hepatoblastoma, can also be observed in various other hepatic conditions, further complicating the diagnostic process [1]. While radiological imaging plays a pivotal role, it may not always seamlessly align with the underlying histopathological aspects.

Hepatoblastoma stands as the most prevalent malignant liver tumor in children, accounting for a significant majority of cases (79 %) [1]. It primarily affects infants and young children, with the median age at diagnosis being around 18 months. Several genetic syndromes and risk factors, such as Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome, familial adenomatous polyposis, and low birth weight, are associated with its development [1]. Clinical presentations typically encompass an asymptomatic abdominal mass, although hepatoblastoma can also manifest with abdominal pain, weight loss, anorexia, or jaundice. Serum AFP levels are typically elevated (>100 ng/mL) in hepatoblastoma, but exceptions exist with normal or mildly elevated AFP levels in some cases [2]. Imaging characteristics of hepatoblastoma depend on histological subtypes and the degree of differentiation. Generally, hepatoblastoma appears as a sizable, heterogeneous, hypervascular mass that may contain calcifications or necrosis. It can also extend its reach by invading portals or hepatic veins and potentially spreading to the lungs or lymph nodes [1,2].

In contrast, mesenchymal hamartoma represents a rare benign liver tumor originating from embryonic mesenchymal tissue. It predominantly affects infants and young children, with a male predominance noted [1,2]. Clinical presentation is often characterized by either an asymptomatic state or nonspecific symptoms like abdominal distension or pain. Mesenchymal hamartoma can give rise to complications such as rupture, hemorrhage, infection, compression of adjacent structures, or even malignant transformation [2]. Serum AFP levels typically fall within the normal range or exhibit mild elevation in mesenchymal hamartoma, although marked elevation can occur in specific cases due to entrapped hepatocytes or blood contamination [2]. Imaging findings in mesenchymal hamartoma tend to be nonspecific, varying depending on the proportion of cystic and solid components. A definitive diagnosis necessitates the histopathological examination of tissue samples, typically obtained through biopsy or surgery [1,2].

This case report resonates broadly within the realm of pediatric hepatic tumor diagnosis. It serves as a vivid reminder of the multifaceted nature of such cases, prompting clinicians to adopt an integrated, multidisciplinary approach in their quest for precise diagnoses and effective therapeutic strategies. Beyond unraveling the complexities of pediatric hepatic tumors, this report advocates for comprehensive and collaborative diagnostic methodologies that can significantly impact the trajectory of patient care and outcomes. Case presentation

A 1-year-old female child was admitted with abdominal distension and irratabiliy. Interestingly, there were no other reported symptoms, and her developmental milestones were age-appropriate. Clinical assessment revealed an active and playful child with a height of 70 cm and a weight of 6.5 kg. Physical examination revealed hepatomegaly, palpable 6 cm below the costal margin.

Discussion

The presented case emphasis the diagnostic complexities encountered in pediatric patients with hepatic tumors. In this instance, we encountered a 1-year-old female child who was admitted with abdominal distension and irritability, notably devoid of other reported symptoms. Remarkably, her developmental milestones were in accordance with her age, highlighting the subtlety of symptoms often observed in pediatric liver pathologies.

Upon clinical assessment, we observed an active and playful child with a height of 70 cm and a weight of 6.5 kg. Notably, physical examination revealed hepatomegaly, palpable 6 cm below the costal margin, serving as a significant clinical clue.

Initial laboratory investigations, as summarized in Table 1, raised our suspicions further. Subsequent contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT) imaging (Fig. 1) unveiled a distinctive, well-defined hypodense lesion within liver segments I, IV, V, and VII, embellished with punctate calcifications. Crucially, no vascular invasion was observed. Intriguingly, nodular areas were also identified in the left lower lobe's apical segment and the right lower lobe's posterior segment, adding to the diagnostic puzzle.

The notable elevation in serum alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) levels and the compelling CECT findings initially inclined us toward considering hepatoblastoma as the likely diagnosis. However, the initial ultrasound-guided biopsy (Fig. 2) yielded unexpected results, revealing scattered islands of abnormal hepatocytes, and haphazardly arranged bile duct structures embedded within the myxoid stroma—characteristics of mesenchymal hamartoma. This incongruity between biochemical and radiological features prompted the need for a second biopsy.

The histopathological evaluation of the second biopsy revealed features consistent with a mixed fetal type of hepatoblastoma, further confirmed by positive immunohistochemical staining for beta-catenin and glypican-3. This diagnostic journey was marked by the initial biopsy, likely sampling a site with predominant mesenchymal components, which contributed to the diagnostic dilemma we encountered.

Pediatric hepatic tumors present unique challenges in diagnosis and management due to their diverse histopathological characteristics and variable clinical manifestations [1,2]. These tumors can range from benign lesions like mesenchymal hamartomas to malignant entities such as hepatoblastoma or hepatocellular carcinoma [1]. Consequently, a meticulous diagnostic process is vital to guide appropriate therapeutic decisions.

Hepatoblastoma is a rare malignant liver tumor primarily affecting infants and young children, with a peak incidence in the first three years of life [1]. It is the most common malignant hepatic tumor in this age group [1]. Hepatoblastoma often arises from embryonic liver cells that fail to mature properly, leading to uncontrolled growth and the development of a tumor [2]. Diagnosing hepatoblastoma can be challenging due to its rarity and variable presentation [2,4]. It typically presents with nonspecific symptoms such as abdominal distension, palpable abdominal mass, irritability, and poor appetite [1,4]. In some cases, children may develop jaundice [2,4]. However, it's essential to note that, as in the presented case, hepatoblastoma can also be relatively asymptomatic, further complicating the diagnostic process [2].

In this case, the initial clinical presentation of abdominal distension and irritability might not immediately raise suspicion of a hepatic tumor. The absence of overt symptoms and the child's age-appropriate developmental milestones underscore the subtlety of symptoms that can be encountered in pediatric liver pathologies. Unlike adult patients, children may not express their discomfort clearly, making it crucial for healthcare providers to maintain a high index of suspicion and perform a thorough clinical evaluation in cases of unexplained abdominal signs.

Advanced imaging techniques, such as contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT), play a pivotal role in characterizing hepatic tumors in pediatric patients [4]. The CECT scan in this case revealed a distinct, well-defined hypodense lesion within multiple liver segments. The presence of punctate calcifications within the lesion was a significant imaging finding, providing insights into its composition. Importantly, the absence of vascular invasion indicated a confined lesion within the liver, suggesting a localized rather than a systemic process. Additionally, the identification of nodular areas in the lung lobes raised concerns about potential metastatic involvement, further emphasizing the need for an accurate diagnosis. Hepatoblastomas typically appear as well-defined, heterogeneous masses with variable degrees of enhancement on CECT and MRI.

Serum alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) levels serve as a valuable biomarker in the evaluation of pediatric hepatic tumors [4,5]. In this case, the markedly elevated AFP levels at 160,000 ng/dL raised concerns about potential malignancy. However, it is essential to note that while AFP levels are often elevated in hepatoblastoma, they can also be mildly elevated or even normal in other hepatic tumors like mesenchymal hamartoma [4,5]. The variability in AFP levels highlights the importance of combining clinical, radiological, and histopathological data to arrive at an accurate diagnosis.

Biopsy remains a cornerstone in the diagnosis of pediatric hepatic tumors. However, as demonstrated in this case, the interpretation of biopsy results can be challenging, and diagnostic discrepancies may arise. The initial ultrasound-guided biopsy indicated mesenchymal hamartoma, a benign liver tumor, in contrast to the clinical and radiological expectations of hepatoblastoma. This incongruity emphasizes the necessity of corroborative procedures, especially when clinical and radiological findings do not align with the initial biopsy results.

In the presented case, the first biopsy likely sampled a site within the tumor that had predominant mesenchymal components, contributing to the diagnostic dilemma. The second biopsy, which revealed features of a mixed fetal type of hepatoblastoma, ultimately provided clarity and guided the treatment plan. This experience highlights the importance of not solely relying on a single biopsy when there is a significant diagnostic discrepancy.

The management of hepatoblastoma involves a multimodal approach that considers various factors, including tumor stage, size, location, patient age, and overall health [[6], [7], [8]]. Treatment typically includes a combination of therapies. Surgical resection remains the mainstay of treatment, aiming for complete tumor removal whenever possible [9,10]. Surgical resection can cure most cases and reduce the risk of recurrence or malignant transformation [11,12]. As of 2023, there are no updated statistics on the incidence or prevalence of hamartoma or hepatoblastoma in the literature. However, based on the previous trends, it is likely that both tumors remain very rare and challenging to diagnose and treat. As of 2020, only 68 cases of adult hepatoblastoma had been reported in the literature [1,4,13]. The exact incidence and prevalence of hamartoma are unknown, but it is estimated that it accounts for approximately 10 % of pancreatic primary mesenchymal tumors [1]. As of 2012, only about 140 cases of splenic hamartoma had been reported in the literature [2,3,7]. The number of cases of other types of hamartoma is also very low. However, surgery may not always be feasible, particularly when tumors are large or involve vital structures. In such cases, alternative or adjuvant therapies come into play. These may include radiofrequency ablation (RFA), transarterial chemoembolization (TACE), or systemic chemotherapy [13,14]. The choice of therapy depends on individual patient factors and the specific characteristics of the tumor.

In the case presented here, the patient received seven cycles of platin and doxorubicin-based chemotherapy as part of the treatment plan for hepatoblastoma. This approach was chosen based on the tumor's characteristics and the need to reduce tumor size before surgical intervention. The decision to combine chemotherapy with surgery emphasis the importance of a multidisciplinary approach in the management of pediatric hepatic tumors, where different medical specialties collaborate to provide the best possible care.

This case serves as an alarming reminder of the complexities inherent in diagnosing children's hepatic tumors, particularly when clinical, biochemical, and radiographic data are inconsistent. It highlights the significance of confirming biopsies to establish an accurate diagnosis, as illustrated in our instance. It also emphasizes the need for a multidisciplinary approach to tailor treatment strategies to the specific diagnostic and clinical circumstances of the patient. As we go forward, this case motivates ongoing research to improve our understanding of pathophysiology, natural history, and therapeutic options for such difficult instances in juvenile hepatology.

Conclusion

This case report reveals the difficult nature of identifying pediatric hepatic malignancies, represented by a rare and complex instance of hepatoblastoma in a 1-year-old female child presenting with increased AFP levels and hepatomegaly. This example underlines the vital need for a complete and rigorous diagnostic approach involving clinical evaluation, laboratory data analysis, imaging tests, and histology. It highlights the need to evaluate hepatoblastoma within the range of differential diagnoses for pediatric hepatic malignancies, especially when AFP levels and imaging results demonstrate variability. Moreover, this instance emphasizes the crucial function of confirmation biopsies in establishing correct diagnoses and directing suitable therapy choices.
