Lower-limb prosthetic mechanisms: recent progress, control innovations and barriers to real-world adoption
Authors: Syed Riyas Ahamed, Sandip Saha, Awani Bhushan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lower-limb impairments caused by amputation, stroke or paralysis severely impact mobility and independence. Assistive technologies for these conditions include passive prostheses, powered limbs and robotic exoskeletons, each offering distinct advantages in simplicity, functionality and adaptability. Recent advancements have significantly enhanced these systems through innovations in biomechanics, intelligent control and materials engineering. Improvements in biomechanical modelling and neuromuscular interfaces such as electromyography (EMG) and pressure-based sensors have enhanced user intent recognition and facilitated more adaptive gait control. Intelligent control strategies, including machine learning algorithms, variable impedance control and phase-based coordination, enable responsive adaptation to different terrains and movement demands. In parallel, advancements in materials such as carbon fibre composites and 3D-printed polymers have enabled the creation of lightweight, robust and customisable components that enhance user comfort and device performance. These innovations demonstrate meaningful gains in gait symmetry, stability and metabolic efficiency. However, major challenges remain in developing intuitive and reliable control outside laboratory settings, achieving seamless adaptation to unpredictable terrain, ensuring long-term user comfort and fit and reducing weight and energy consumption to support full-day use. Limited real-world clinical validation and high cost further restrict widespread adoption. Continued progress will depend on integrated, human-centred co-design that combines efficient actuation, robust multimodal sensing, intelligent control and clinically meaningful evaluation. This review synthesises current research trends and highlights priority directions towards prosthetic systems that are more functional, accessible and capable of supporting confident everyday mobility.

Introduction

Mobility impairments resulting from lower-limb loss pose serious challenges to quality of life, limiting independence and increasing societal and caregiver burdens. Globally, the lower-limb loss rate is estimated to exceed one amputation every 30 s, primarily due to trauma, diabetes and vascular diseases. The demand for effective assistive solutions such as prosthetic limbs is massive, as they restore functional walking ability and reduce the physical and emotional burden of limb loss [1, 2]. Traditional prosthetic systems are mostly passive but have provided essential support for decades due to their simplicity, durability and affordability. However, they fall short in replicating the adaptive, energy-generating nature of the human leg. Many users report dissatisfaction due to poor comfort, a poor socket fit or a lack of functionality, leading to high rates of rejection or irregular use [3–6]. In contrast, the development of active and semi-active prosthetic systems, as well as powered exoskeletons, has introduced new opportunities to restore more natural gait patterns, reduce metabolic cost and enable greater autonomy in activities of daily living [7–9].

Recent advances in mechanical design, actuation systems, sensor technologies and control strategies have extended towards the advancement of next-generation lower-limb prostheses. Actuators now range from lightweight series elastic motor mechanisms to hydraulic/pneumatic designs that provide smoother, more human-like torque generation. At the same time, modern prostheses commonly integrate sensors such as inertial units, force sensors and surface EMG to estimate the intention of the user and adapt to changes in terrain or walking speed [10]. These innovations aim to close the gap between artificial and biological limb function by improving responsiveness, stability and comfort. However, the field's growth is insane. Yet there are challenges to address in the development of user-centred prosthetics. These devices should become lighter, more energy-efficient, easier to fit and more reliable during real-world use. Existing review articles often examine materials, actuation or control strategies separately, making it difficult to understand how these components work together in complete prosthetic systems. To address this gap, the present review provides a unified, cross-disciplinary overview of the technologies that shape modern lower-limb prostheses. The paper is organised as follows:

Sect. "Classification of Lower-Limb Prosthetic Devices" classifies the prosthetic devices and examines the user fit and ergonomics.

Sect. "Actuation Technologies" presents the different actuation technologies in prosthetic devices.

Sect. "Materials and Additive Manufacturing for Structural Components" addresses material choices and additive manufacturing processes that shape structural components of wearable systems.

Sect. "Control Strategies for Lower-Limb Devices" discusses hierarchical control strategies, sensor integration and feedback loops that inform device responsiveness and safety.

Sect. "Clinical Translation and User-Centric Design: Barriers to Real-World Design" synthesises the critical highlights of the interconnected challenges across actuation, control and clinical translation aimed at enhancing personalisation, performance and user satisfaction in lower-limb prosthetics.

Finally, Sect. "Outlook and Horizon: A Prioritised Path Forward" proposes a prioritised path forward for the future development of lower-limb prosthetic systems.

Classification of lower-limb prosthetic devices

Lower-limb prosthetic devices have undergone significant evolution over the past two decades. This provides diverse solutions to support or restore mobility for individuals with amputations, paralysis or neuromuscular disorders. These technologies span a broad spectrum from passive and spring-based prostheses to active systems that incorporate motors at the limb joints to more advanced hybrid devices that combine active with passive elements. Each category represents a distinct trade-off among complexity, energy consumption and functional benefits.

Passive mechanisms and spring-based designs

Passive devices rely on the mechanical properties of materials such as springs, dampers and compliant structures to deliver joint torque without the need for active power sources. These designs prioritise simplicity, affordability and robustness, especially for users in low-resource settings or those with limited mobility needs. They are engineered to replicate essential biomechanical functions such as shock absorption, energy storage and push-off dynamics.

Several designs exemplify innovation in this area. For example, Lenzi T. et al. [11] introduced a semi-active ankle prosthesis using an inverted linear cam-follower transmission to optimise torque while reducing height and weight. Though semi-active, the device functioned largely through passive mechanical transformation, making it ideal for users prioritising lightweight and low-energy systems. Similarly, Rice J. et al. [12] developed the CamWalk, a purely mechanical system that transforms vertical leg translation into rotational ankle push-off using cam mechanisms, mimicking natural ankle dynamics (Fig. 1a). The benefits of passive adjustments in clinical outcomes have been repeatedly demonstrated. Alimusaj M. et al. [13] showed that adjustable passive dorsiflexion improved stair ambulation by optimising knee kinematics, enabling more natural movement patterns in transtibial amputees. Safaeepour Z. et al. [14] developed a passive ankle–foot prosthesis replicating four distinct gait phases using viscoelastic elements, closely aligning with the natural moment–angle relationship of the ankle. Schlafly M. et al. [15] extended this principle through the Compliant and Articulating Prosthetic Ankle (CAPA) device, using a segmented structure with torsional springs to mimic anatomical foot and ankle joints, enhancing sagittal-plane dynamics.Fig. 1a Prototype of Camwalk [12]; b Prototype of ankle–foot prosthetic with step-specific equilibrium point [19]; c Passive dorsiflexing ankle prosthesis with a compliant low-profile foot [20]; d Assembly of keel, blade and foam [26]

Spring-based and energy-storing components have improved both the performance and adaptability. Brackx B. et al. [16] employed planetary gears and pawl-ratchet mechanisms to dynamically adjust spring equilibrium and enhance late-stance propulsion beyond conventional energy-storing-and-returning (ESAR) feet. Cherelle P. et al. [17] presented the Ankle Mimicking Prosthetic Foot 3, which utilises passive elastic actuation with two locking mechanisms to store energy at mid-stance and provide rigidity as needed, acting like a switchable energy-release system. Safaeepour Z. et al. [18] measured ankle joint quasi-stiffness and work during different gait phases and walking speeds in healthy individuals. It found that both stiffness and power output change significantly with gait phase and speed. Specifically, energy is absorbed during dorsiflexion and generated during powered plantarflexion. These findings emphasise the need for ankle prostheses to adapt stiffness and power dynamically to replicate natural ankle function. Passive solutions for different terrains have also emerged. Nickel E. et al. [19] integrated slope detection into a passive dorsi-plantarflexion mechanism, allowing the prosthetic ankle to adjust stiffness and orientation based on terrain and improved gait stability without power (Fig. 1b). Bartlett H. et al. [20] adopted a similar path of developing a dorsiflexion system employing prismatic-rotational coupling to enhance swing-phase foot clearance and reduce the fall risk. Comparative studies clarify the performance benefits of specific passive designs (Fig. 1c). Suprayogi D. et al. [21] and Thakre D. et al. [22] demonstrated superior gait speed and energy efficiency in single-axis and multi-axis prosthetic feet compared to standard SACH feet. This underscores the role of articulation and multidirectional compliance. Paradisi F. et al. [23] showed that low-activity users also benefit from multi-axial designs, gaining improvements in balance and mobility. Furthermore, advances in these devices have been driven by efforts to target populations with specific needs. For example, Mulder I. et al. [24] developed a compact prosthetic foot for Syme and Pirogoff amputees, achieving improved symmetry and user satisfaction. Meanwhile, Gholizadeh H. et al. [25] highlighted the role of passive suspension systems, such as the Iceross liner, showing that individualised fitting can enhance comfort and function despite the universal drawbacks of sweat accumulation. Falbriard M. et al. [26] developed a dynamic prosthetic foot using numerical simulation and modelling, tailored to the specific needs of the International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC) in low-resource settings, at a cost of below USD 100 (Fig. 1d). Bellmann M. et al. [27] demonstrated how microprocessor-controlled C-Leg can enhance swing-phase dynamics and stability on uneven terrain, despite not providing active power. Goktepe A. et al. [28] confirmed that passive system optimisation remains essential, as transfemoral users face the greatest energetic burdens during ambulation. Collectively, passive and spring-based systems are affordable and simple, but fundamentally limited by their inability to inject net positive energy. This restricts their functionality to steady-state, level-ground walking and prevents them from supporting high-demand activities such as stair ascent, running or rapid adaptation to uneven terrain. Their performance often lacks a precise mechanical tuning and can lead to compensatory gait patterns, increasing long-term joint strain on the intact limb.

Motorised multi-joint and hybrid systems

Active lower-limb prostheses have undergone significant development in recent years, aiming to restore natural movement and enhance user independence. These systems aim to replicate the complex dynamics of the human gait cycle through combinations of sensors, actuators and intelligent control strategies. Electromechanical actuators are most commonly used, which are often paired with variable impedance to better mimic joint behaviour. Advanced control strategies, including gait recognition and EMG-based intent detection, are also being adopted to enhance adaptability. While many of these devices show good performance, most remain in early stages with limited clinical testing, pointing to the need for further research and real-world evaluation [29]. This section reviews the progression of such systems across ankle, knee and integrated knee–ankle designs.

The ankle–foot complex plays a crucial role in push-off and stability during ambulation. Early powered ankle–foot prostheses (PAFP) focused primarily on reproducing the torque power of the ankle. Lathouwers E. et al. [30] evaluated the Talaris Demonstrator (TD), a mechanical ankle–foot prosthesis for gait coordination in individuals with lower-limb amputations. Using sagittal continuous relative phase (CRP) analysis during treadmill walking at varying speeds, the study found altered hip–knee and knee–ankle coordination patterns compared to able-bodied controls. While statistical differences were limited, visual assessment suggested the TD may offer biomechanical advantages over the user’s current prostheses. Feng Y. et al. [31] redesigned the PKU-RoboTPro-II ankle prosthesis by integrating a hybrid push-off and damping mechanism that facilitates both active propulsion and passive stability. The device achieved up to a 31% reduction in metabolic cost and supported approximately 10,000 strides on a 0.28 kg battery. Its terrain-adaptive modulation contributed to smoother transitions across varied surfaces and improved gait symmetry (Fig. 2a). In the same direction, Yu T. et al. [32] developed an electro-hydrostatically actuated (EHA) ankle prosthesis capable of dual-mode operation of active and passive. This design helps to remain functional even when the battery is depleted. But the system required manual recalibration for different walking speeds, which limits user convenience. Au S. et al. [33] introduced a powered ankle–foot prosthesis that delivered plantarflexion torque during late stance and reduced metabolic cost to 14%. However, its added mass compared to passive alternatives highlighted trade-offs between biomechanical performance and portability. Further exploration of the powered system was examined by Gabert L. et al. [34] on a polycentric ankle prosthesis equipped with sensors operating at 500 Hz for high-resolution feedback during stair and level-ground ambulation. Although actuator-assisted torque improved propulsion, the increased system weight reduced ankle mobility, illustrating the performance-agility trade-off in powered designs. Zhu J. et al. [35] presented a powered ankle with adaptive joints that closely mimicked natural ankle motion and improved walking symmetry. Their evaluations demonstrated improved ground reaction force profiles and joint kinematics compared to passive systems (Fig. 2b). Kim J. et al. [36] evaluated both objective metrics and user perceptions in powered prosthesis performance. Despite technological enhancements, the device did not significantly lower metabolic costs or increase walking speed across all users. The varying feedback of users underscored the importance of integrating subjective comfort and perceived benefits into prosthetic evaluation. Heremans F. et al. [37] incorporated regenerative energy recovery and introduced Efficient and Lightweight Spring Ankle (ELSA), a compact ankle–foot prosthesis which is particularly effective during ramp descent. ELSA delivered net positive mechanical energy, showcasing potential in compact hybrid energy systems. However, further development is needed to improve assistive torque and mechanical robustness (Fig. 2c). Esposito et al. [38] studied the BiOM-powered ankle–foot prosthesis, which supports active push-off and forward propulsion. It reduced metabolic cost on level terrain and varied performance on slope and different gait speeds. The major recurring issue in PAFP design is to balance mechanical complexity with energy efficiency and torque accuracy. To address energy efficiency, later designs increasingly adopted series elastic actuators (SEAs). These allowed real-time torque control while minimising actuator loads, as in Au S. et al. [43] and Zhu J. et al.’s PANTOE 1 system [44]. These systems not only generated plantarflexion during push-off but also provided joint compliance to improve shock absorption and gait smoothness. Expanding on this, Gao F et al. [45] implemented a parallel elastic actuator that showed 64% reduction in peak torque demand and also reduced the power consumption without compromising mechanical work output. While SEAs offered one path towards efficiency, others sought hybrid strategies by combining passive and active components. Naseri A et al. [46] proposed the hybrid-hydraulic ankle prosthesis (H2AP), integrating hydraulic damping, springs and a carbon footplate to recycle mechanical energy. By enhancing early-stance motion and reducing energy loss during push-off, this system bridged the gap between passive ESAR designs and fully powered devices. Similarly, Mazzarini A et al. [47] proposed a low-profile active module that reinforces energy storage during stance and precisely controls energy release at push-off, highlighting a growing interest in low power over full motorisation.

Further refinement of the mechanical structure appeared in the underactuated ankle–toe device of Gabert L et al. [48] where the toe energy was mechanically routed to support ankle propulsion to increase efficiency. These innovations contributed not only to energy reuse but also to improving anatomical alignment that makes devices more compact and socket-friendly. As hardware advanced, the need for equally capable control systems became apparent. For example, Shultz A et al. [49] demonstrated a prosthetic ankle controller that mimicked biomechanical torque profiles to improve mid-stance support, though it still lacked adaptability on uneven terrain. Internal model control (IMC) is applied by Suzuki R et al. [50] for more responsive torque generation by learning from the user’s gait. Similarly, Shepherd M et al. [51] introduced the variable-stiffness prosthetic ankle (VSPA) to improve the terrain adaptability by real-time stiffness modulation. This is the sign of convergence between hardware flexibility and intelligent control. Rad N et al. [52] proposed a modular 3-DOF cable-driven prosthesis capable of actively controlling dorsi/plantarflexion, toe movement and inversion/eversion. Its anatomically inspired design helps in complex multidirectional movement and advances the scope of real-world ambulation support. Rogers E et al. [53] developed a 2-DOF ankle–foot prosthesis using EMG-driven volitional control for both dorsiflexion and inversion. The compact, fast-responding system allowed natural motion ranges, advancing user-intent-driven control methods for active foot devices. A major barrier for the ankle–toe system is the inability to adapt to unstructured environments without extensive user-specific tuning. Furthermore, the increased mass of lightweight designs remains a critical usability concern due to the high metabolic cost.

The evolution of powered knee prostheses has steadily moved towards hybridised designs that combine active torque generation with passive energy efficiency. This approach aims to replicate natural gait while supporting functional versatility across different terrains and activities. A comparative gait analysis in transfemoral amputees demonstrated that using an active microprocessor-controlled knee (A-MPK) increased knee range of motion and improved gait symmetry compared to a passive MPK. The A-MPK reduced hip torque on the sound limb and promoted more balanced loading during stance. A four-week follow-up case showed improved user confidence over time, indicating that adaptation enhances the benefits of powered knee prostheses [54]. Li Z et al. [55] introduced a hydraulically actuated hybrid knee prosthesis capable of delivering up to 60 Nm torque and 60° of flexion. The device employed low-friction hybrid control and blended active and passive elements to enhance gait symmetry and enable stair ascent. Its compact profile and responsive behaviour improved usability in daily scenarios. Wang X et al. [56] developed a polycentric knee system featuring hybrid actuation and terrain-adaptive control. Mechanical linkages integrated with powered modules allowed for high torque output and increased toe clearance. The prosthesis facilitated smooth gait phase transitions, with clinical trials confirming its effectiveness in replicating functional gait across varied surfaces. Lenzi T et al. [57] engineered a 1.7 kg robotic knee that switches between active and passive modes. In active mode, it delivered up to 125 Nm torque, facilitating stair climbing. During level walking, the passive mode minimised energy expenditure. Chen W et al. [58] designed a transfemoral knee prosthesis powered by a series elastic actuator (SEA). The actuator allowed for precise torque delivery while maintaining compliance, resulting in smoother early-stance flexion. However, inconsistent flexion behaviour on uneven terrain pointed to the need for more robust control algorithms. Bartlett H et al. [59] proposed a swing-assist hybrid prosthesis combining passive hydraulic stance control with electromechanical swing-phase actuation. The system applied assist-as-needed torque during swing to support adaptive flexion and accommodate walking speed. This resulted in improved gait naturalness and reduced fall risk while retaining a compact and wearable form factor. Fu G et al. [39] addressed the noise generated by the implementation of a four-stage synchronous belt drive system. The knee joint’s noise level was reduced below 46 dBA, making it suitable for socially discreet usage. Coupled with high backdrivability and a multi-layered control architecture, the device offered responsive, low-noise gait adaptation (Fig. 2d). Tagliabue G et al. [60] introduced a powered knee prosthesis using variable-stiffness actuators (VSAs), which enabled dynamic modulation of joint impedance. ISO 10328-compliant load testing validated its ability to support users up to 100 kg. The system achieved a balance between lightweight construction and energy efficiency while adapting in real time to gait phase demands (Fig. 3a). Grabowski A et al. [61] provided biomechanical evidence supporting the use of powered knees over passive elastic alternatives. Their comparative study revealed that powered knees significantly lowered joint loading by up to 21%, specifically peak ground reaction forces and adduction moments. These reductions may decrease the risk of musculoskeletal strain in the contralateral limb over time. Culver S et al. [62] followed with a dual-mode prosthesis capable of toggling between energy-saving passive and power-assisted modes, illustrating the growing emphasis on multifunctionality. Recognising the need to support seamless task transitions (Fig. 3b). Similarly, Rouse E et al. [63] introduced the clutchable SEA (CSEA) Knee to combine compliance with selective energy delivery. The device achieved low energy consumption (1.8 J/stride), demonstrating that hybridisation can align with minimal power draw (Fig. 3c). Welker C et al. [64] advanced this principle with a phase-variable impedance control strategy, enabling dynamic tuning of joint behaviour during sit-to-stand transition. Their work emphasised smoother, more symmetric user movement. Hunt G et al. [66] introduced a holonomic-phase variable controller that could manage both rhythmic walking and adaptive tasks such as obstacle avoidance, reflecting a broader push towards context-aware prosthesis behaviour (Fig. 3d, 9a). The coordination between joints enabled more natural transitions, improved stability and reduced compensatory movements. Lawson B et al. [69] focused on simplifying control tuning for new users in powered knee–ankle systems. This intrinsic control method reduced parameter tuning time by 56% while enhancing stair gait transitions and multimodal adaptability, streamlining clinical deployment. Sullivan L et al. This enabled intention-driven, synchronised multi-joint actuation, creating a fully integrated neuro-mechanical prosthesis capable of responsive locomotion. Modularity, ergonomics and user-fit design

User-fit and ergonomics are the major factors for long-term prosthesis adoption. Poor socket fit and residual limb discomfort are commonly reported as primary reasons for device abandonment. Persistent challenges related to limb volume fluctuation, skin shear stress and suspension reliability often outweigh mechanical performance [3–6]. Recent work in composite and single-polymer socket materials [81] demonstrates improvements in strength-to-weight ratio and temperature regulation. At the other end, 3D-printed custom sockets [82] highlight the potential for faster fabrication and improved personalised fit. These developments indicate that ergonomic optimisation is as critical as actuation improvements for enabling daily usability. Lenzi T et al. [11] achieved a 50% reduction in prosthesis height and a 27% decrease in weight through a cam-follower transmission, emphasising modular compactness without sacrificing structural integrity. This type of design allows easier anatomical fit and gait pattern adjustment, supporting long-term comfort and wearability. Similarly, Heremans F et al. [37] incorporated a shoe-integrated architecture in ELSA, promoting unobtrusive daily use and social acceptance through compact ergonomic integration. Adaptability to individual gait strategies is another key theme. Alimusaj M et al. [13] stressed the importance of personalised dorsiflexion tuning, particularly for stair navigation, highlighting the need for prostheses that support fine-grained ergonomic adjustments. Complementing this, Bartlett H et al. [59] and Wang X et al. [56] implemented hybrid mechanisms and torque-adaptive profiles that automatically adjust assistance to terrain and gait phase, enhancing stability and safety without requiring manual input. User perception and psychological comfort were also highlighted as significant factors. Kim J et al. [36] found that even advanced control systems may fall short if they do not align with user comfort and expectations, reinforcing the need for modularity that accommodates lifestyle preferences. Prosthetic systems have increasingly adopted sensor-driven and user-centred control strategies to improve adaptability. Arifin N et al. [83] assessed postural steadiness in below-knee amputees using three types of prosthetic feet such as SACH, single axis and ESAR, under both eyes-opened and eyes-closed conditions. While foot type did not significantly affect overall stability, the absence of visual input notably reduced steadiness, particularly in the medial–lateral direction. These findings emphasise the importance of sensory integration and suggest that postural control in prosthesis users is more dependent on visual feedback than foot design alone. Chen W et al. [58] and Fu G et al. [39] utilised hybrid and multilayer control systems that respond dynamically to walking contexts, improving comfort and acceptance. Welker C et al. [64] further enhanced control by modulating impedance trajectories based on thigh-phase inputs, tailoring mechanical response to individual motion patterns. In terms of user interface and clinical usability, Simon A et al. [69] streamlined the fitting process through reduced-tuning control configurations. This approach facilitates easier customisation and scalability in clinical environments, promoting wider adoption. Hamzah A et al. [84] emphasised the need for globally accessible CAD/CAM fabrication methods, pointing out disparities in prosthetic availability and the importance of adaptable, scalable production technologies. Advances in sensing and control also contribute to ergonomic improvements. Fiumalbi T et al. [85] developed a modular sensory system that integrates pressure sensors and IMUs for accurate gait event detection and offers precise feedback for adaptive gait support. Hunt G et al. [86] introduced a shared neural controller capable of managing diverse tasks such as sitting and lunging, minimising user effort and fall risk through intuitive control. Meanwhile, Sobh K et al. [87] demonstrated how ergonomically optimised EMG sensor placement is crucial for reliable signal interpretation and control precision in myoelectric systems. Affordability and modularity converge in low-cost, yet functionally competent designs like Falbriard M et al.’s [26] dynamic foot for low-resource settings and Fagioli et al.’s [88] SynPro system using a differential underactuated mechanism for simplified actuation of knee and ankle (Fig. 4c). Carney M et al. [89] also contributed with a high-torque, lightweight actuator (MC-RFSEA) that supports compact design without compromising biomechanical fidelity (Fig. 4d). Studies consistently show that socket discomfort, poor fit and skin issues outweigh mechanical benefits for many users. Even advanced control systems can fail if they do not align with user comfort and perceptual expectations. Furthermore, reliance on visual feedback for balance [83] underscores that sensory integration and human factors are as critical as mechanical design. Global accessibility is also limited, as high-cost, custom-fabricated solutions remain inaccessible in low-resource regions [84]. The field must therefore prioritise co-design approaches that involve users early in the development process. This ensures that the technical advancements should also take concern about improvements in comfort, usability and daily quality of life.

Actuation technologies

Lower-limb prostheses rely on advanced actuation systems to match the complex biomechanics of human gait. A wide range of actuation approaches has been explored to meet the demands for torque, speed, compliance and adaptability across locomotor tasks. This section categorises key technologies such as electric, hydraulic, pneumatic and soft actuators, and series elastic and variable-stiffness actuators.

Electric actuation systems

Electric motors remain the most prevalent actuation method due to their precise control and high torque potential. Early work by Au S et al. [43] introduced a motor-driven prosthesis delivering biomimetic plantarflexion, significantly reducing user energy expenditure. Similarly, Gabert L et al. [34] developed a polycentric powered ankle with high-torque electric motors, improving ambulation but also increasing system weight, highlighting the classic trade-off between performance and bulk. Zhu J et al. [35] demonstrated the advanced electrically actuated joints capable of real-time kinematic adaptation and biomimetic push-off, while Zhu J et al. [44] deployed dual series elastic actuators (SEAs) in the PANTOE 1 system to enable compliant torque delivery in dynamic walking. Electric actuation combined with hybrid enhancements is becoming increasingly popular. Wang X et al. [56] and Lenzi T et al. [57] utilised electric motors coupled with passive linkages, achieving energy-efficient motion adaptable to terrain (Fig. 5a). Similarly, Sullivan L et al. [70] and Bhakta K et al. Hydraulic and electrohydraulic actuators

Hydraulic systems are valued for their high torque output and smooth actuation, particularly in high-flexion scenarios. Yu T et al. [32] applied electrohydrostatic actuators (EHA) in a novel ankle prosthesis offering both active and passive modes, supporting motion even in power-off conditions. Li Z et al. [55] integrated hydraulics into a knee prosthesis for stair climbing and dynamic flexion, while Naseri A et al. [46] demonstrated delayed energy release using hydraulics to boost late-stance propulsion and the low-damping knee prosthesis (LDKP) by Fu G. et al. [39] represented a new class of hydraulic–electric hybrids prioritising low acoustic noise and smooth transitions. Gao S et al. [73] integrated an electrohydraulic knee and motor-driven ankle, using hydraulic damping for stance and motor torque for swing. A finite-state controller enables adaptation across five terrain types, especially for uneven terrain. Wei Y et al. [91] developed an integrated, lightweight electrohydraulic ankle prosthesis. The system’s actuation closely mimics biological movement, focuses on effective energy utilisation, and reduces mass, particularly in the hydraulic valve block and skeletal structures.

Pneumatic and soft actuation

Pneumatic actuators have been explored for their compliance and low mechanical impedance. Lee J et al. [78] demonstrated a pneumatic–elastic hybrid using solenoid control to maximise torque and energy return, offering biologically accurate stance support. Recent work of Vallery H et al. [92] demonstrated that the low-power ERiK pneumatic transfemoral knee shows improved energy efficiency without an increase in mass. But this comes in minimal effort tasks of sitting or standing and not for the entire gait assistance. Soft actuation systems are popular for their human-compatible compliance. For example, Gao F et al.’s CLS-MRD [93], which integrates magnetic damping with motor regeneration and Endo K et al. [94] created a lightweight (1.20 kg) powered knee prosthesis using SEA with harmonic gearing (1:100) and a 70 W brushless motor. Tested on one amputee, the design yielded a high torque-to-weight ratio in a highly compact package.

Series elastic and variable-stiffness actuators (SEAs and VSAs)

Series elastic actuators offer enhanced compliance and improved energy efficiency. Chen W et al. [58] integrated an SEA in a transfemoral prosthesis to enhance torque modulation and walking endurance. Rouse E et al. [63] developed a clutchable SEA that supported biological joint dynamics while minimising energy consumption. Similarly, Samuel K et al. [43] combined unidirectional springs with force-controlled SEAs for rapid torque release and joint energy storage. Variable stiffness actuators (VSAs) offer an adaptive approach to compliance. Tagliabue G et al. [60] demonstrated torque delivery with dynamically tunable stiffness, improving energy use and responsiveness under high-load conditions. Shepherd M et al. [51] used a cam-based transmission to modulate stiffness in the VSPA foot, achieving task-specific joint tuning without continuous actuation.

Hybrid and multi-modal actuation approaches

Combining multiple actuation principles has emerged as a robust design strategy for achieving both biomechanical accuracy and energy efficiency. Feng et al. [31] fused electric motors with mechanical damping to enable efficient push-off and terrain adaptation, demonstrating the value of hybrid architectures. Dong D et al. [40] and Anderson A et al. [95] built an off-board actuated robotic ankle–foot prototype using a torsion spring-in-parallel structure. It achieved a peak plantarflexion torque of over 175 Nm and a control bandwidth of 6.1 Hz, serving as a platform for advanced biomechanical research (Fig. 5b). Bartlett H et al. [59] implemented a dual-mode system with passive hydraulic stance and electromechanical swing-phase actuation, enhancing gait stability through phase-dependent torque modulation (Fig. 5c). Managing torque and speed is central to prosthetic effectiveness. Mazzarini A et al. [47] proposed a low-power actuator integrated into ESAR designs, offering propulsion with minimal active input. Gao F et al. [45] used parallel elastic structures to reduce actuator demands while preserving joint work. Tran M et al. [96] used an actively variable transmission (AVT) to match biological joint demands on Utah Knee (Fig. 5d) and Culver S et al. [62] employed a two-speed transmission to optimise impedance across gait phases. Negi S et al. [97] employed a magnetorheological (MR) damper in a novel ankle–foot prosthesis prototype to adjust ankle resistance/damping dynamically. This shows a broader dynamic range and improved comfort and adaptability across different walking conditions.

Actuation paradigms: a critical trade-off between power, weight and efficiency

A wide range of actuation approaches has been explored to meet the demands for torque, speed, compliance and adaptability across locomotor tasks. The choice of actuation is foundational, necessitating a compromise between three competing goals: high output power, low device weight and operational energy efficiency. This challenge is often referred to as the ‘actuation trilemma’. This tension is evident across all powered prostheses and no current technology resolves these conflicting demands. Consequently, nearly every design reflects the strategic compromise that prioritises one corner of the triangle while accepting the other trade-offs.

Electric actuation: high control but weight and energy trade-offs

Many systems that rely on electrical [63] or series elastic actuation [58] offer safer human interaction and improved torque tracking (Fig. 6a). However, even the best among them has the limitation of high mass and bulkiness. Devices such as a polycentric powered ankle [34], reaction force-SEA actuator [89] and a lightweight knee platform [94] maintain high torque. Yet, these devices are still heavy compared to passive devices. The increase in power capacity must lead to a greater volume and heavier batteries. Most advanced devices with the combination of motors with passive linkages, as seen in the hybrid ankle [11] and the polycentric system aimed to improve the efficiency but fail to escape the mass constraints imposed by electric components. New functional approaches use biarticular actuation that shifts the focus towards structure to reproduce muscle activation [77]. These devices improve gait symmetry and result in biological patterns [80] (Fig. 6b). Hydraulic and electrohydraulic actuation: high power but heavy and complex

At the other end of the trade-off spectrum, the major strength of the hydraulic system is high power density. This makes it well suited for the heavy loading tasks such as stair ascent or knee bending. The hydraulic knee [55] and an electrohydraulic knee with a motor-driven ankle [73] demonstrate improved gait symmetry and terrain adaptability across five surface conditions while maintaining compact design and low energy consumption (Fig. 6c). User feedback reported better comfort compared to traditional hydraulic knees. It lacks durability, performance on complex uneven terrain and long-term reliability evaluation, underscoring the continuing need for large-scale field testing. However, all these positives come with drawbacks. The need for pumps, valve blocks, pressure vessels, accumulators and reinforced structural housings will significantly increase the mass of systems. Fluid systems also introduce complexity, mechanical damping losses and maintenance requirements, all of which hinder widespread clinical adoption. Studies achieving a reduction in the mass and size by optimised valve block, again lack validation on long-term durability and clinical performance. Hence, hydraulic systems excel in power and smooth motion but have the limitation of weight, complexity and energy efficiency.

Pneumatic and quasi-passive actuation: lightweight compliance but limited power

Pneumatic systems are naturally safer for human–robot interaction since they offer inherent compliance and low mechanical impedance. It also supports the smooth stance with the ability to store and return energy using pressurised chambers or elastic bladders. Despite the performance of delivering biologically inspired torque and energy return from the regulated pressure with minimal weight and comfort [78], the primary limitation of the pneumatic actuator is inconsistent output power (Fig. 7a). Though the recent ERiK pneumatic transfemoral knee supports knee flexion with low power consumption [92], they are only validated for sit/stand minimal efforts (Fig. 7b). Air compression is prone to delays and losses, making precise, high-torque push-off difficult. True soft robotic actuators for primary prosthetic use remain in the early research stage. Passive and semi-active actuation: lightweight and efficient but no net power

Passive and semi-active prostheses are valuable for their lightweight, high durability and efficiency. These systems rely on mechanical structures such as springs, cams, clutches, dampers or non-backdrivable transmissions, rather than electric power. Systems rely on non-backdrivable transmission [11] and a two-speed transmission [62] that states low power consumption with stable or natural gait behaviour. This modulates torque without continuous motor involvement. Passive ankle designs such as cam-based mechanisms [12] (Fig. 7c), push-off foot [16] (Fig. 7d), torsion spring foot [15] and a semi-active ESAR system [98] (Fig. 7e) were represented to show that mechanical structures alone can improve gait without power. Since these systems do not inject net positive energy, they cannot restore biological push-off or support demanding tasks such as stair ascent or running. Though they shine for ground-level walking, they diminish in variable or uneven environments.

Hybrid and variable-stiffness actuation: towards mechanically intelligent systems

Recent advancements focus on hybrid and variable-stiffness actuators that combine the strength of multiple actuators while minimising their individual weaknesses. Variable stiffness actuators in the MyKnee [60] and the VSPA foot [51] enable real-time stiffness adjustments without constant motor power. Hybrid systems strategically release the power when needed. This system integrates slope detection into a passive dorsi/plantarflexion mechanism [19], an active motor with a spring-damper structure [57], hydraulic with electromechanical [59], electric push-off with nonlinear damping [31], parallel elastic structures [45] and mechanical stiffness with neuromuscular signals [99]. This integration helps in the reduction of motor load, achieves better torque and improves efficiency, but also increases the complexity of control and integration. The clear trend that emerges in all of these developments is that strengthening one design objective typically compromises another. The actuation trilemma (Fig. 8) illustrates that prosthetic actuators must balance three competing objectives: high torque output, low device mass and energy efficiency. Current solutions can optimise two of these goals, but not all three simultaneously. Increasing torque typically requires larger motors or transmission architectures, which in turn increase device mass and battery demand. Conversely, prioritising lightweight structures reduces torque capacity and limits assistance during demanding tasks such as stair ascent or locomotion on uneven terrain. No currently available device is immune to this mechanical challenge. Materials and additive manufacturing for structural components

Common structural materials

Lower-limb prosthetic devices rely heavily on the appropriate selection of structural materials to ensure mechanical strength, durability, biocompatibility and user comfort. Traditionally, metals such as aluminium, titanium and nickel alloys are used for load-bearing elements due to their excellent mechanical properties. For instance, Bountourelis T et al. [100] utilised Nickel alloy 718 in a lower-limb prosthesis, where topology optimisation achieved a 67% weight reduction while maintaining a safety factor of 1.22, demonstrating the effectiveness of finite element analysis (FEA) in optimising metallic components (Fig. 9a). Composites, particularly carbon fibre-reinforced polymers, have emerged as promising alternatives due to their superior strength-to-weight ratio and fatigue resistance. Sehar B et al. [101] showed that increasing carbon fibre layers significantly improved tensile strength, with ten layers reaching 576.07 N/mm2, more than twice the strength of two-layer samples (Fig. 9b, c). Similarly, S Abbas M. et al. [102] found that a lamination configuration of 3 perlon + 2 carbon fibre + 3 perlon provided optimal mechanical strength and endurance with lower fabrication cost. In prosthetic joints, Amudhan K et al. While carbon fibre offers excellent strength-to-weight ratios, these advanced composites are expensive and limit accessibility in resource-constrained settings with specialised fabrication requirements [102]. Furthermore, customisable polymer-based interfaces frequently cause skin irritation, thermal discomfort and sweat accumulation [101], which are leading causes of device abandonment. The trade-off between structural rigidity and user comfort remains unresolved, as materials optimised for mechanical performance often compromise wearability and long-term usability.

Additive manufacturing techniques

The integration of additive manufacturing (AM) has revolutionised the fabrication of lower-limb prosthetics, enabling lightweight, customised and cost-effective designs. The primary objective of this approach is to implement AM principles, including topology optimisation, which reduces material usage while preserving structural performance. 3D printing has become a valuable tool in surgical applications such as orthopaedics and spinal procedures. Its use in producing anatomical models, surgical guides and custom implants has been shown to reduce surgical time and improve outcomes. However, higher imaging and production costs raise questions about overall cost-effectiveness, suggesting the need for further economic evaluation. These insights support the continued expansion of 3D printing in personalised medical device fabrication [107]. For example, Ozmen O et al. [82] achieved a 50.37% weight reduction and 32% faster fabrication using a topology-optimised 3D-printed below-knee prosthetic leg. The design demonstrated a safety factor of 4.14, validating its suitability for daily use. Similarly, Marinopoulos T et al. [108] applied material extrusion 3D printing with optimised print paths to create prosthetic sockets that complied with BS EN ISO 10328:2006 safety standards, showing mechanical advantages over traditional methods. AM also facilitates rapid prototyping and modular design, particularly beneficial for paediatric and rural healthcare scenarios. Additive manufacturing enables extraordinary customisation and faster prototyping, but it faces challenges in long-term durability, regulatory validation and cost-effectiveness. While 3D-printed sockets can meet safety standards under controlled conditions, their performance under real-world cyclic loading remains inadequately documented. Moreover, the costs of imaging and production equipment raise concerns about scalability and economic viability in clinical and low-resource environments. Without addressing these barriers, AM risks remaining a niche solution rather than a transformative prosthetic fabrication method.

Control strategies for lower-limb devices

High-level and low-level control

Control strategies in wearable robotics are commonly structured into two hierarchical layers: high-level and low-level control. Each layer contributes distinctly to enabling intuitive and responsive assistance during locomotion. High-level control is primarily concerned with interpreting user intent and detecting gait phases to generate appropriate movement goals. These strategies frequently leverage sensor fusion and pattern recognition techniques to decode biomechanical or neuromuscular signals. For instance, classifiers like k-nearest neighbours (k-NN) have been applied to transtibial prostheses for real-time gait event detection, providing robust phase classification based on wearable sensor input [12]. More advanced systems employ ML models for interpreting complex signals such as electromyography (EMG) and mechanomyography (MMG). Wang E et al. [109] developed a low-cost wearable system using a novel air-pressure PMMG thigh-ring sensor, IMU and FSR to improve lower-limb motion intent recognition. Using a multi-task fuzzy inference algorithm (GMT-TSK-FIS), the system achieved over 98% accuracy in recognising both gait phases and locomotion modes. Multimodal fusion and simultaneous recognition significantly outperformed traditional EMG-based or single-task methods, offering a robust and economical alternative for intelligent prosthetic control (Fig. 10a). Young A et al. [110] reduced the steady-state classification error from ~ 15% to ~ 1% by combining the EMG with mechanical sensors, which demonstrates the advantage of anticipatory control. Spanias J et al. [111] ensured the potential for personalised gait adaptation by an adaptive neural controller that maintained a 4.03% forward-prediction error across multiple days and terrain conditions. Best TK et al. [112] reduced the cognitive load by presenting a data-driven variable impedance controller for a powered knee–ankle prosthesis. This decreased the extensive manual tuning typically required for finite-state machine (FSM) impedance controllers. Also highlighted that the existing multimodal impedance controllers may require up to 140 tunable parameters and more than five hours of tuning. This approach integrates real-time gait phase estimation and model-based parameter selection and enables adaptive transitions between tasks without user-specific manual tuning.Fig. 10a Structure of the proposed motion intent recognition system [109], b Practising sit/stand in training with a powered prosthesis [64]

Low-level control executes the high-level commands by actuating the device joints with precision and stability. It governs joint torque, position or impedance, ensuring stability and immediate responsiveness during gait. Finite-state machine (FSM) and impedance control remain the most widely used methods, offering reliable transition timing and consistent power generation. For example, Shultz A et al. [42] developed a slope-adaptive controller to support transitions between walking and standing, enhancing user posture and gait kinematics. Bartlett H et al. [59] introduced a swing-assist prosthetic knee using combined passive and active elements to adapt torque assistance based on walking speed, reducing fall risk and promoting natural gait. Welker G et al. [64] employed a thigh-based phase variable to modulate impedance trajectories during sit/stand transitions, achieving improvements in movement symmetry and mechanical biomimicry (Fig. 10b). Rezazadeh S et al. [66] introduced a piecewise holonomic phase- variable controller capable of managing both rhythmic and non-rhythmic motions, enhancing multi-joint coordination and adaptability across terrains in transfemoral amputees (Fig. 11a). Sensor fusion, real-time adaptation and human-in-the-loop optimisation

Effective control in wearable robotics is heavily reliant on accurate and timely feedback from integrated sensors. Core sensor modalities include Inertial Measurement Units (IMUs) for tracking limb orientation and motion, Electromyography (EMG) sensors to interpret muscle activity and infer user intent and force sensors to measure ground reaction forces and joint torques. To ensure robust and precise control, multi-sensor fusion techniques are frequently employed, integrating data streams from these heterogeneous sources. Techniques such as Kalman filtering, dynamic Bayesian networks and deep learning-based fusion methods allow for more stable, real-time control responses under varying conditions. Surface EMG has emerged as a powerful tool for improving human–machine interaction. For example, Young A et al. [110] showed that using a dynamic Bayesian network (DBN) classifier reduced transitional intent recognition error from 18.4% to 12.2% and steady-state error from 3.8% to 1.0%, showcasing the potential of multimodal sensor fusion for accuracy enhancement. Advanced deep learning architectures also show promise (Fig. 11b). Posh R et al. [114] demonstrated a hybrid volitional controller that integrates FSM impedance control with neuromuscular input. This improved adaptability across tasks while reducing compensatory movement. Similarly, Angelidou C et al. [115] proposed a sensor-fusion-based ankle–foot prosthesis controller that achieved reliable multi-terrain prediction using integrated kinematic and EMG signals. This approach combined k-nearest neighbour (k-NN) and neural network (NN) classifiers, which improved the terrain transition prediction accuracy to 87.5% and reduced delays in surface transitions recognition. Additionally, introduced ASTRA-BF, a human-in-the-loop terrain adaptation controller for active prostheses to predict surface stiffness transitions by combining EMG and kinematic input. This enables anticipatory adaptation to unstable surfaces [116]. Keri M et al. [117] created a wireless IMU-based system capable of delivering timely vibrotactile feedback to above-knee amputees, helping simulate kinesthetic sensations with communication delays under 50 ms and high movement-tracking fidelity. Lastly, Crea S et al. [118] proposed a wearable system for lower-limb amputees that uses pressure-sensitive insoles and vibrating actuators to restore somatosensory cues. This system guides gait through discrete phase-based feedback, enabling better kinematic control and user adaptation (Fig. 11c). Prasanna C et al. [119] incorporated real-time gait data and personalised calibration parameters and presented a data-driven and personalised stance-symmetry controller for a robotic ankle–foot prosthesis to adjust actuator assistance based on the user’s natural loading pattern. This enhances weight-bearing symmetry during the stance phase for individuals with transtibial amputation. Together, these advancements highlight the critical role of multimodal sensing and sophisticated integration techniques in improving the intelligence, adaptability and user experience of wearable robotic systems.

Control strategies: the quest for intuitive and robust user interaction

Parallel to hardware constraints of actuation, prosthetic control systems face the control dilemma of balancing stability and robustness against adaptability and intuitive user interaction. Controllers that are reliable in predictable situations often fail during irregular movement and controllers that adapt well to different tasks tend to become fragile or cognitively demanding for the user. Phase-based control: improved timing and adaptation but still rhythm-dependent

Phase-based control drives continuous variable thigh [64] or shank movement, which estimates the user’s position in the gait cycle to offer a partial solution for adaptability (Fig. 13b). This is achieved without manual tuning [112] and only supports minimal transition tasks between sit–stand and stand–walk [42]. Meanwhile, the piecewise holonomic controller [66] demonstrated better handling of both rhythmic/non-rhythmic motions and a real-time IMU-based gait mode classifier supports independent adaptation during user movement change by onboard training [121]. Though these approaches offer better timing and smoother transitions, they still depend on periodic gait patterns. When the user walks with a random gait pattern in unpredictable environments, phase estimation becomes less reliable.

EMG-based and intent recognition control: natural interaction but fragility and cognitive load

Electromyography-based and volitional controls represent a better intuitive form of interaction by their behaviour of directly linking the prosthesis to the user’s muscle intent. Instead of detecting gait phases, these controllers infer the user’s movement intention directly from muscle activation. Many users prefer EMG-based control without complex calibration and for its movement closer to biological behaviour [122]. However, the cognitive load to adapt the movement strategy impacts the long-term daily use [113] (Fig. 13c). Recent works that use multi-sensor fusion of EMG and kinematic data to enhance the terrain transition prediction limit real-world applicability due to the sensitivity of the EMG, which depends on the electrode placement, socket pressure, sweat and environmental variability [115, 116]. Reviews by Ahkami B. et al. [123], Cimolato A et al. [124] and Fleming A et al. [125] highlighted the significant challenges in calibration time, robustness and signal quality. Compared to fully volitional control, shared neural control enables standing and squatting with reduced mental demand [86]. These findings show that while EMG enhances natural interaction, it requires solutions for reliability and demands a cognitive load [126].

Sensor fusion, multimodal recognition and hybrid volitional control

Toward the goal of improving the reliability, researchers employed multiple sensors and data-driven methods. Sensor fusion [109], machine learning models [127] and an EMG-driven intent recognition system [111] achieved better gait phase and intent recognition rate. Despite strong performance, increased errors during ramp transitions and the lack of extended real-world evaluation illustrate the persistent need for robustness beyond controlled indoor testing. The multimodal terrain prediction framework used EMG and kinematics to anticipate transitions between different surfaces, while neural networks combined with EMG and IMU [128] detected fatigue and adapted controller behaviour accordingly (Fig. 13d). Non-standard motions such as side-stepping are well performed by hybrid volitional control [114] that blends EMG and phase-based control while maintaining stability.

Beyond these strategies, the reinforcement learning-based approach of Wu R et al. [129] demonstrated real-time adaptation that improved stability and individualised gait behaviour. This ensures continuous self-tuning and context awareness but is limited by computational complexity and raises questions regarding safety, interpretability and long-term robustness outside laboratory settings. Taken together, these studies reveal a central tension that robust controllers lack adaptability, while adaptive controllers lack robustness. No single strategy currently achieves high naturalness, reliability and low cognitive load at the same time. Clinical translation and user-centric design: barriers to real-world design

Despite the vast improvement in technological progress in actuation and control, many systems fail to progress from the laboratory prototypes to the daily use product. Across the literature, the performance of the devices in controlled environments does not automatically result in the same for real-world settings. The major challenges start from the device weight, battery life, comfort, stability, cognitive effort and limited testing.

Device weight, bulk and metabolic burden

Many powered prosthetics are rejected for their drawback of being heavyweight. Although systems such as the hydraulic knee [55] and 2.7 kg powered knee [39] provide adequate torque, they are heavy to use over extended periods (Fig. 14a). This leads to disruptions in gait symmetry and compensatory movements [54], though it benefits mechanically [47] (Fig. 14b). Evaluating the prosthesis performance across the entire lower-limb shows that device mass can alter inter-joint coordination [132] and affect both stability, joint loading and limited ankle torque forces lead to compensatory hip loading [133]. Though the prosthesis was reported to be lightweight, it had a relatively higher mass compared to passive devices, which increases the metabolic and tuning burden [79].Fig. 14a Bulk hybrid knee [55]; b participant wearing Robotics Lab Transtibial Prosthesis [47]; c electrically controlled transmission knee prosthesis [62]; d Utah Bionic Knee [79]

Battery life and daily-use endurance

Battery life is another major concern for the powered prosthesis. Devices with high-power actuators often require large batteries for long battery life. This leads to an increase in weight, but it still fails to support full-day operation. Reviews by Windrich M et al. [29] and Voloshin A et al. [134], as well as clinical studies by Olaya-Mira N et al. [135], have identified that poor endurance is a key reason for users abandoning powered prostheses. To overcome this issue, the ballistic swing-phase actuator [62] offers high swing efficiency with minimal energy expenditure. But it is not suitable for stair climbing or incline walking since it does not provide stance-phase torque (Fig. 14c). In contrast, the Utah Bionic Leg [79] demonstrated high torque capability (1.67 Nm/kg) and showed a near-natural gait. This is enabled by a torque-sensitive actuator and an underactuated foot that regenerates energy (Fig. 14d). This device can perform for a long duration without recharging, offering a practical route to all-day powered mobility. Consequently, constraints emerged due to the lack of evaluation on uneven terrain, unclear contribution of individual joints to mobility and the absence of long-term metabolic and demographic outcome data.

Limited clinical evidence and short validation time

Universally, the lack of long-term and real-world validation is a limitation across most systems for clinical adoption. Many systems are tested only in small groups of users, indoors or for short sessions, for example, like ankle mimicking prosthetic (AMP) foot [17] and magnetorheological damper (MR)-damper foot [97] showed the mechanical benefits with the lack of testing in amputee participants, real-world terrains and long-term durability (Fig. 15a). As stated in the systematic review of Fagioli I et al. [136], the absence of long-term follow-up, outdoor testing and multi-population studies is one of the greatest obstacles to clinical trust and regulatory approval. For instance, in the study of Stafford N. et al. [131], the user’s preference for the myoelectric ankle does not always result in reliable gait enhancements (Fig. 15b). Kim J et al. [36] reported that improved biomechanical performance does not always translate to user satisfaction during long-term wear, stressing the need for extended evaluation. Heremans F. et al. [37] verified multi-terrain powered foot behaviour outside the laboratory, but durability and reliability over long-term daily use remain unreported (Fig. 15c).Fig. 15a The AMP-foot 3 prototype [17]; b testing setup with EMG on a small group [131]; c active mode actuation at the main events of the gait cycle [37]

Cognitive effort and ease of use

Despite advanced mechanical capabilities, fit, alignment and comfort remain the fundamental determinants of device acceptance. Reviews by Paterno L et al. [6], Safari R et al. [5] and Olaya-Mira N et al. [135] reported the frequently outweighing mechanical benefit factors such as socket discomfort, skin problems, limb volume changes and poor alignment. This problem extends to the lack of personalised assistance. Supported by studies of Afschrift M et al. [139] and Price M et al. [140], which show that users require specific torque profiles to prevent compensations and long-term strain. Karimi M et al. [141] demonstrated that adding simple haptic feedback can increase stability and embodiment, yet sensory augmentation remains uncommon in practical designs.

Cost, accessibility and real-world reliability

Another restriction for clinical adoption is cost and accessibility, a barrier for many patients to access. Complex hybrid or hydraulic systems are expensive to build and maintain. Studies in low-resource settings [142] show large mobility improvements even with simple prostheses, but powered systems remain inaccessible. Open-source efforts such as the bionic leg of Azocar A et al. [143] and the low-cost foot of Falbriard M et al. [26] offer promising alternatives but require clinical integration frameworks to achieve real-world impact. Overall, clinical translation challenges arise not from isolated design flaws but from systemic mismatches between engineering priorities and daily user requirements. The evidence strongly suggests that users prioritise reliability, comfort, safety and ease of use over laboratory-level peak performance. Closing the gap between technical innovation and real-world impact requires long-term testing, lightweight designs, energy-efficient actuation, intuitive control and a deeper focus on human experience.

To advance this field forward, a fundamental paradigm shift towards co-design is essential. This requires the integrated development of actuation, control, energy management, socket fit, clinical workflow and user comfort. Future prostheses must not only be strong or adaptive, but additionally be lightweight, intuitive, reliable and affordable for daily use. The core challenge is no longer demonstrating that a prosthesis can generate biological torque in a laboratory setting. The real challenge is building a system that can operate safely and comfortably in the unpredictable conditions of daily life, without excessive cognitive or maintenance burden. Only by adopting a systems-level, human-centred approach can the field break the long-standing cycle of trade-offs and bring advanced prosthetic technology into meaningful, widespread clinical use.

Outlook and horizon: a prioritised path forward

Although significant progress has been made in the development of lower-limb prostheses, widespread clinical adoption remains limited because long-standing problems continue to restrict real-world usability. Many promising devices demonstrate strong results in controlled laboratory settings, yet users still struggle with reliability, comfort and performance during everyday activities. The challenges identified across this review show that the barriers to success are deeply interconnected and cannot be solved by improving a single component in isolation.

The first path is resolving the persistent power-weight-efficiency trade-off. Many modern powered prostheses can generate biological-level torque and support tasks such as stair ascent and uneven terrain walking, but they do so with increased mass and battery demand. These penalties directly affect balance, socket comfort and daily use time. Lightweight systems, on the other hand, often lack sufficient push-off power or adaptability. Until this actuation trilemma is addressed, devices will continue to force users to choose between power and comfort rather than providing both. Future advances must integrate actuation, energy storage and structural design in a unified approach rather than optimising components separately.

A second priority concerns the control dilemma, where systems that are stable and predictable such as finite-state and impedance-based control, struggle in real-world irregular movement, while EMG-based and AI-assisted approaches that promise intuitive interaction remain fragile due to signal noise, fatigue, sweat-based drift, socket movement and increased cognitive effort. Reducing tuning burden and enabling automatic adaptation rather than expecting clinicians or users to manually adjust settings will be critical. Robust hybrid frameworks combining intent prediction, shared control and self-calibrating sensing are essential for moving beyond laboratory demonstrations.

The third is addressing the validation gap that separates research claims from actual user experience. Most systems are still tested indoors, for short periods, with small sample groups performing predictable tasks. Real-world challenges such as uneven surfaces, multitasking, weather, fatigue and long-term wear remain insufficiently evaluated. Multi-month clinical trials and standardised testing protocols are urgently needed to understand durability, user comfort, safety and mental workload. Without extensive evidence beyond step-count metrics, clinical confidence and regulatory approval remain limited.

Commercial advancements highlight both progress and ongoing shortcomings. Devices such as the Ottobock Genium X3 [144] or C-Leg 4 [145], Össur Proprio Foot [146] or Rheo Knee [147] and Blatchford Linx [148] or Elan [149] have successfully translated features like adaptive terrain response, microprocessor-based joint control and real-time sensor integration into market products (Fig. 17a–f). However, these systems still reflect the same fundamental constraints discussed above: high cost, tuning complexity, limited long-term evidence and accessibility challenges, particularly in low-resource settings. Taken together, these priorities show that the next major step in lower-limb prosthetics will not come from incremental improvements within isolated categories, but from system-level co-design that integrates actuation, control, sensing, socket comfort, user feedback, energy management and clinical workflow as a unified problem. Progress requires prostheses that are not only powerful or intelligent, but also lightweight, intuitive and reliable in unpredictable environments, allowing users to walk confidently and comfortably without excessive cognitive or maintenance burden. If the field shifts towards practical, user-centred innovation supported by long-term real-world evaluation, the next generation of prosthetic systems can move beyond technical demonstrations and into meaningful everyday life, restoring mobility that is comfortable, dependable and accessible.

Conclusion

Lower-limb prosthetic technology continues to advance, with notable progress in powered knee–ankle systems, multimodal sensing and lightweight structural materials. However, a consistent challenge remains that many devices demonstrate strong performance in controlled laboratory environments but struggle to deliver reliable, comfortable and intuitive function during daily real-world use. The persistent trade-offs between weight, power and energy efficiency in actuation, along with the fragility and tuning complexity of advanced control strategies, limit long-term adoption and everyday practicality. Clinical translation is further slowed by short-duration testing, small participant groups, limited outdoor evaluation and unmet needs related to comfort, cognitive load and accessibility.

Looking ahead, the most promising path forward lies in system-level, user-centred integration, rather than isolated improvements to hardware or control algorithms. The key priorities include developing lightweight and energy-efficient actuation, robust intent recognition and multimodal control that requires minimal cognitive effort and extensive long-term evaluations in real environments that reflect daily mobility challenges. Bridging these gaps will be essential for transforming current research prototypes into dependable prosthetic solutions that support confident, independent mobility for users in real-life contexts.
