Downregulation of mTORC1 Signaling is Associated with Increased Sensitivity to Protein Synthesis Perturbations in Multiple Myeloma
Authors: Didhiti Singha, Debopriya Choudhury, Meghna Mondal, Jahir Hossain, Brahmachari Vedeshachaitanya, Pushkar Malakar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Protein synthesis is critical for the survival and proliferation of multiple myeloma cells. mTORC1 signaling, through its downstream effectors S6K1 and S6, regulates ribosome biogenesis and protein homeostasis. However, the relative sensitivity of multiple myeloma cells to perturbations in protein synthesis compared with other cancer types remains unclear. In addition, the role of S6K1 and S6 signaling in the response to translational stress and the therapeutic potential of mTORC1 inhibition in multiple myeloma are not fully understood. Multiple myeloma and non-myeloma cancer cell lines were subjected to protein synthesis perturbations. Changes in mTORC1 signaling, including S6K1 and S6 activity, were analyzed. Cellular responses, including proliferation and apoptosis, were assessed. The mechanisms of protein synthesis were also evaluated under these conditions. The effects of the mTORC1 inhibitor Torin1 were examined across cell lines. Sensitivity to protein synthesis stress and mTORC1 inhibition was compared between multiple myeloma and other cancer types, including bortezomib-resistant cells. Multiple myeloma cell lines exhibited heightened sensitivity to protein synthesis perturbations, accompanied by marked suppression of S6K1 and S6 signaling and reduced global protein synthesis. This increased sensitivity resulted in decreased proliferation and increased apoptosis compared to non-myeloma cancer cell lines. Treatment with the mTORC1 inhibitor Torin1 produced a pronounced inhibitory effect in multiple myeloma cells. Notably, cell lines resistant to protein synthesis perturbations showed increased sensitivity upon mTORC1 inhibition. Furthermore, the bortezomib-resistant RPMI-8226 cell line was resensitized to bortezomib following Torin1 treatment. These findings establish a mechanistic link between mTORC1 signaling, protein synthesis stress, and multiple myeloma. Targeting mTORC1 signaling enhances sensitivity to translational stress and overcomes drug resistance, highlighting a potential therapeutic vulnerability in multiple myeloma.

Introduction

Protein synthesis plays a crucial role in multiple myeloma, a type of cancer that affects plasma cells, which are white blood cells responsible for producing antibodies. − The connection between protein synthesis and multiple myeloma lies in the fact that these cancerous plasma cells produce excessive amounts of abnormal proteins. − Understanding the mechanisms of protein synthesis in multiple myeloma is therefore important for developing targeted therapies that disrupt abnormal protein production and inhibit the growth of malignant plasma cells. −

However, the sensitivity of multiple myeloma to perturbations in protein synthesis has not been systematically compared with that of other cancers, particularly in the context of S6K1 and S6 signaling pathways that regulate protein synthesis. In addition, the relative sensitivity of multiple myeloma to perturbations in protein homeostasis induced by chemical agents such as MG132, bortezomib, and cycloheximide has not been explored. Investigating the molecular mechanisms underlying protein synthesis in multiple myeloma may uncover new therapeutic targets and improve treatment outcomes for patients with this challenging disease. −

mTORC1 (mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1) is a key regulator of protein synthesis in cells. It is a signaling complex that integrates environmental cues such as nutrient availability, energy status, and growth factor signals to regulate essential cellular processes, including protein synthesis. mTORC1 controls protein synthesis primarily through downstream effectors such as ribosomal protein S6 kinase 1 (S6K1) and eukaryotic initiation factor 4E-binding protein 1 (4E-BP1). , Upon activation, mTORC1 phosphorylates and activates S6K1, which subsequently phosphorylates ribosomal protein S6, thereby promoting ribosome biogenesis and protein translation. , In parallel, mTORC1 phosphorylates and inactivates 4E-BP1, resulting in the release of eIF4E, a critical translation initiation factor, and enhanced cap-dependent translation.

In multiple myeloma, aberrant activation of mTORC1 signaling is commonly observed and contributes to the dysregulated protein synthesis characteristic of the disease. Elevated mTORC1 activity promotes increased protein synthesis, supporting the growth and survival of malignant plasma cells. Increased ribosome biogenesis, a process essential for protein synthesis, has been shown to play a significant role in cellular growth and malignancy. ,,, Moreover, the oncoprotein MYC is known to regulate rDNA transcription and ribosome biogenesis in multiple cancers. − Importantly, S6K1 has been shown to control the rate of MYC-dependent rDNA transcription and ribosome biogenesis ,

Whether the heightened sensitivity of multiple myeloma to perturbations in protein synthesis is directly linked to mTORC1 signaling remains an open question. Furthermore, the molecular relationship between S6K1, S6-mediated translational control, and multiple myeloma has not been thoroughly investigated. Despite the central role of mTORC1 in protein synthesis and multiple myeloma pathophysiology, the therapeutic potential of mTORC1 inhibitors in multiple myelomaparticularly with respect to their effects on S6K1 and S6 signalingremains largely unexplored.

Materials and Methods

Cell Culture

RPMI-8226, U266B-1, HeLa, and HepG2 cells were obtained from the National Centre for Cell Sciences, Pune, India. RPMI 8226-BT cell line was obtained from Prof. Jacqueline Cloos of Amsterdam UMC. This cell line is resistant to 100 nM BTZ and was made by exposing RPMI 8226 cells to progressively higher concentrations of BTZ starting at 0.2 nM for about 15 months. RPMI-8226, U266B-1, and RPMI-8226-BTZR cells were grown in RPMI high glucose with 10% FCS, 0.1 mg/mL penicillin, 0.1 mg/mL streptomycin, and 2.5 μg/mL amphotericin-B in a CO2 incubator that was kept at 5% CO2. HeLa and HepG2 cells were grown in DMEM with 10% FCS, 0.1 mg/mL penicillin, 0.1 mg/mL streptomycin, and 2.5 μg/mL amphotericin-B in a CO2 incubator that was kept at 5% CO2.

Drug Sensitivity Data

Drug sensitivity data were obtained from the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) database (https://www.cancerrxgene.org/). − We used the GDSC2 data set, which includes pharmacological profiles for ∼969 cancer cell lines screened against ∼297 anticancer compounds using the CellTiter-Glo assay. Cells were treated with multiple drug concentrations (5–9 point dose–response curves) for 72 h. Dose–response curves were fitted using non-linear regression, and drug sensitivity was quantified as the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50). IC50 values were log-transformed (log IC50) prior to downstream analysis. For drug–cell line pairs where 50% inhibition was not achieved within the tested concentration range, IC50 values were estimated by curve extrapolation. For plotting graphs, we used geometric mean from the genomics of drug sensitivity in cancer (GDSC) database. For replicate measurements, central tendency was estimated using the geometric mean. Specifically, values were log-transformed, averaged, and back-transformed, which is equivalent to calculating the geometric mean in linear space. This approach is appropriate for drug response data, which typically follow a log–normal distribution and span multiple orders of magnitude.

Immunoblotting

The cells were lysed in Laemmli buffer (with BME) and were analyzed for the total protein concentration. 20 mg of total protein from the cell lysates were separated using SDS-PAGE and transferred it to a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane. Primary antibodies used were cleaved-caspase-3 (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology# 9661S), GAPDH (1:1000; Santa Cruz Biotechnology Technology # sc-32233), β-Actin (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology# 8457), phospho-S6 (1:1000; ThermoFisher Scientific # MA5 15140), total-S6 (1:1000; ThermoFisher Scientific # MA5 15164), phospho-4E-BP1 (1:1000; ThermoFisher Scientific # MA5-55314), 4E-BP1 (1:1000; ThermoFisher Scientific # MA5-51505), phospho-S6K1 (1:1000; Santa Cruz Biotechnology Technology # sc-8416), and total-S6K1 (1:1000; Santa Cruz Biotechnology Technology # sc-8418). The membrane used for phospho detection was subsequently stripped using a stripping buffer and reprobed for total. Secondary antibodies used were HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse (1:5000; Cell Signaling Technology) and goat anti-rabbit (1:5000; Cell Signaling Technology). We developed the blots using the SignalFire ECL Reagent (Cell Signaling Technology) in Chemi-Doc (Biorad).

Microscopy

The Zeiss primovert inverted cell culture microscope, equipped with a 10× magnification of the objective lens, was used for imaging cells treated with or without bortezomib, cycloheximide, azacytidine, torin1, and 0.1% serum and captured images with Mosaic 2.4 software (Zeiss). We used a 10× magnification lens to cover a larger area.

Trypan Blue Assay

Cells (0.3 × 106/well) were seeded in 6-well plates. After 24 h, the medium was changed to one that had or did not contain protein homeostasis perturbers such as bortezomib, cycloheximide, azacytidine, torin1, and others. The cells were then kept at 37 °C for another 24 h. Next, we carefully transferred each well’s medium to a 15 mL tube of cells and centrifuged to collect the cells. Next, we washed the cells with PBS. For HeLa and HepG2, 300 μL of trypsin was used to remove the cells from the plate and collect them in the same tube. Next, we centrifuged the cells in the tubes for 5 min at 1500 rpm (Remi −8C plus centrifuge with Rotar model R-81A), discarding the supernatant. The cells were washed with 2 mL of PBS, and the cell pellet was resuspended with 1000 μL of PBS. We mixed the cell suspension with 0.4% trypan blue in a 1:1 ratio and counted the live and dead cells using a hemocytometer. The cells that took up the dye and appeared blue were counted as dead cells.

Annexin V–FITC/Propidium Iodide Apoptosis Assay

Apoptosis was assessed using Annexin V–FITC and propidium iodide (PI) dual staining (Abcam Kit) followed by flow cytometry. Briefly, after the indicated treatments, both floating and adherent cells were collected. Adherent cells were detached using trypsin (without EDTA), combined with floating cells, and washed twice with cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Cells were resuspended in 1× Annexin V binding buffer at a concentration of approximately 1 × 106 cells/mL. For staining, 1 × 105 cells in 100 μL binding buffer were incubated with 5 μL Annexin V–FITC and 5 μL PI for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. Following incubation, 400 μL of binding buffer was added, and samples were analyzed immediately by flow cytometry (488 nm excitation). A minimum of 10,000 events per sample were acquired. Cell populations were distinguished as follows: viable (Annexin V–/PI–), early apoptotic (Annexin V+/PI–), late apoptotic (Annexin V+/PI+), and necrotic (Annexin V–/PI+). Data were analyzed using appropriate flow cytometry software.

Puromycin Incorporation Assay

Global protein synthesis was assessed using the surface sensing of translation (SUnSET) assay based on puromycin incorporation into nascent polypeptides. Briefly, cells were seeded at an appropriate density and allowed to reach ∼60–70% confluency prior to treatment. Following exposure to bortezomib (20 nM) for 20 h, puromycin (SRL, India) was added directly to the culture medium at a final concentration of 2 μg/mL and incubated for 30 min at 37 °C. After labeling, cells were washed twice with ice-cold PBS and lysed in 1× Laemmli buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Lysates were heated for 10 min at 90 °C and clarified by centrifugation at 12,000g for 15 min at 4 °C. Equal amounts of protein (20–30 μg) were resolved by SDS–PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes.

Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 30 min at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4 °C with an anti-puromycin monoclonal antibody (Abcam; 1:1000). After washing, membranes were incubated with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. Immunoreactive bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence. Puromycin incorporation was quantified by densitometric analysis of the total lane signal and normalized to the corresponding loading control (GAPDH). Reduced puromycin signal intensity was interpreted as decreased global protein synthesis.

Statistical Analysis

Error bars of all data represent SDs from the mean. P values were calculated using two-tailed type 2 student t tests. Statistical significance is shown as *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001.

Results

We began with the objective of identifying the molecular connection between mTORC1 signaling (S6K1 and S6 signaling) and sensitivity to protein synthesis perturbations in multiple myeloma compared with other cancers.

To compare the vulnerability of multiple myeloma cells to protein synthesis perturbations relative to other cancers, we selected bortezomib, MG132, and cycloheximide. Among these drugs, bortezomib is clinically used for the treatment of multiple myeloma Bortezomib (Velcade) in the treatment of multiple myeloma, whereas MG132 and cycloheximide were previously explored as potential therapeutic agents. Bortezomib is a proteasome inhibitor and can also disrupt protein homeostasis by inducing endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, thereby activating the unfolded protein response (UPR). Proteasome inhibition represents a key therapeutic strategy in multiple myeloma owing to the high protein synthesis burden of malignant plasma cells.

MG132 is a reversible peptide aldehyde proteasome inhibitor that is widely used as a research tool to study proteasome-dependent survival pathways in myeloma. Treatment with MG132 results in the accumulation of ubiquitinated proteins, leading to ER stress and activation of the unfolded protein response. This is accompanied by inhibition of NF-κB signaling, cell-cycle arrest, oxidative stress, and induction of apoptosis. Although MG132 is unsuitable for clinical use due to toxicity, it has been instrumental in elucidating mechanisms underlying proteasome inhibitor sensitivity and resistance in multiple myeloma.

Cycloheximide is a well-known inhibitor of protein synthesis in eukaryotic cells. It is a potent inhibitor of eukaryotic translational elongation and is widely used as an experimental tool to study protein synthesis and turnover. In multiple myeloma, where malignant plasma cells exhibit exceptionally high rates of immunoglobulin production, cycloheximide treatment rapidly suppresses global translation and depletes short-lived pro-survival proteins such as MCL-1 and MYC. This translational blockade results in cell-cycle arrest, induction of ER stress, and activation of apoptotic pathways. Although cycloheximide is unsuitable for clinical use because of its global toxicity, it has been instrumental in dissecting translation-dependent survival mechanisms and proteostasis regulation in multiple myeloma.

Multiple Myeloma Cells are More Sensitive to Protein Synthesis Perturbators, Particularly Bortezomib and MG132

To further investigate this sensitivity, we adopted a bioinformatics approach using the CancerRxGene (Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer) database, which contains detailed drug-response data across multiple cancer cell lines. − We first examined the response to bortezomib across various cancers and selected cervical squamous cell carcinoma and endocervical adenocarcinoma (CESC), liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LHC), and multiple myeloma (MM) based on the feasibility of performing downstream laboratory experiments. The database included data for 15 LHC, 14 CESC, and 17 MM cell lines (Figure ).

The box below the graph provides detailed information on bortezomib sensitivity in LHC. (B) Sensitivity of cervical squamous cell carcinoma and endocervical adenocarcinoma (CESC) cell lines to bortezomib. The box below the graph provides detailed information on bortezomib sensitivity in CESC. (C) Sensitivity of multiple myeloma (MM) cell lines to bortezomib. For bortezomib, the minimum screening concentration was 0.0000200 μM and the maximum screening concentration was 0.02 μM, representing a 1000-fold range. The minimum IC50 obtained for multiple myeloma cell lines was 0.000806 μM, compared with 0.00214 μM for CESC and 0.00383 μM for LHC cell lines (Figure A–C). Thus, the minimum IC50 for multiple myeloma was 2.65-fold lower than that for CESC and 4.75-fold lower than that for LHC. The maximum IC50 values were 0.00947 μM for multiple myeloma, 0.0323 μM for CESC, and 0.287 μM for LHC cell lines, corresponding to 3.4-fold and 30.3-fold lower values in multiple myeloma relative to CESC and LHC, respectively (Figure A–C). These results clearly demonstrate the increased sensitivity of multiple myeloma cell lines to bortezomib compared with hepatocellular and cervical cancers.

We next analyzed MG132 using the same database (Figure S1). The minimum screening concentration was 0.0156 μM and the maximum screening concentration was 4 μM, corresponding to a 256-fold range. The minimum IC50 for multiple myeloma cell lines was 0.0419 μM, compared with 0.127 μM for CESC and 0.156 μM for LHC cell lines (Figure S1A–C). Thus, the minimum IC50 was 3.03-fold and 3.72-fold lower in multiple myeloma than in CESC and LHC, respectively. The maximum IC50 values were 0.238 μM for multiple myeloma, 0.829 μM for CESC, and 2.95 μM for LHC (Figure S1A–C). These results further indicate the heightened sensitivity of multiple myeloma cell lines to MG132 compared with hepatocellular and cervical cancers.

In drug-screening experiments across multiple cell lines, the geometric mean is preferred over the arithmetic mean because drug-response metrics (e.g., IC50, EC50, AUC, GI50) typically span several orders of magnitude and follow a log–normal distribution. The geometric mean minimizes the disproportionate influence of extreme responders or highly resistant cell lines, providing a more robust estimate of central tendency. It better reflects the typical potency of a compound across heterogeneous biological systems and enables fairer comparisons between drugs. Consequently, the geometric mean is particularly valuable for summarizing overall drug efficacy in large-scale, multi-cell line screening studies. For Bortezomib, the geometric mean IC50 for multiple myeloma was 0.00372 μM, compared with 0.0116 μM for CESC and 0.0163 μM for LHC, indicating 3.2-fold and 4.4-fold greater sensitivity, respectively (Figure D). For MG132, the geometric mean IC50 for multiple myeloma was 0.126 μM, compared with 0.378 μM for CESC and 0.411 μM for LHC, reflecting 3-fold and 3.26-fold increased sensitivity, respectively (Figure D). These results further indicate the heightened sensitivity of multiple myeloma cell lines to Bortezomib and MG132 compared with hepatocellular and cervical cancers (Figures and S1).

Multiple Myeloma Cells are More Sensitive to Protein Homeostasis–Perturbing Treatments than HeLa and HepG2 Cells

After examining the bioinformatics data in greater detail, we assessed the vulnerability of HeLa (cervical squamous carcinoma), HepG2 (hepatocellular carcinoma), U266B1, and RPMI-8226 (multiple myeloma) cells by treating them for 24 h with or without bortezomib or cycloheximide (Figure ).

Cellular responses were first evaluated using microscopy and a trypan blue exclusion assay (Figure A,B). HeLa, HepG2, U266B1, and RPMI-8226 cells were treated with bortezomib or cycloheximide, followed by microscopic examination to assess changes in cellular morphology (Figure A). Cell viability was quantified by determining the percentage of live and dead cells using the trypan blue assay (Figure B).

HeLa cells exhibited modest morphological changes following treatment with bortezomib and cycloheximide, indicative of drug-induced stress (Figure A). Consistent with this observation, trypan blue staining revealed only a slight increase in cell death compared with control cells (Figure B). Similarly, HepG2 cells showed minimal morphological alterations and no significant change in viability following treatment with either bortezomib or cycloheximide, suggesting relative resistance to these agents (Figure A,B).

In contrast, U266B1 and RPMI-8226 cells displayed pronounced morphological changes after treatment with bortezomib or cycloheximide, characterized by a transition from large, circular, translucent cells to smaller, opaque cells (Figure A). Correspondingly, both U266B1 and RPMI-8226 cells exhibited a significantly higher percentage of dead cells compared with untreated controls (Figure B).

To determine whether cell death occurred through apoptosis, we assessed cleaved caspase-3 expression. U266B1 and RPMI-8226 cells showed a marked increase in cleaved caspase-3 levels following treatment with bortezomib (Figure C) or cycloheximide (Figure D), indicating apoptotic cell death. In contrast, HeLa and HepG2 cells did not exhibit detectable cleaved caspase-3 expression under the same treatment conditions, demonstrating a lack of apoptosis (Figure C,D).

Collectively, these results (Figure ) demonstrate that multiple myeloma cell lines, RPMI-8226 and U266B1, are significantly more sensitive to protein homeostasis–disrupting treatments such as bortezomib and cycloheximide than HeLa and HepG2 cells, which exhibit relative tolerance to these perturbations.

Increased Apoptosis in Multiple Myeloma Cell Lines Subjected to Treatment with Bortezomib

To evaluate the effect of bortezomib (BTZ) on cell death, Annexin V–FITC and Propidium Iodide (PI) staining was performed across multiple cell lines. Flow cytometric analysis revealed a pronounced induction of apoptosis in hematological cancer cell lines, whereas minimal effects were observed in solid tumor-derived cells.

In RPMI 8226 cells, BTZ treatment resulted in a marked shift from viable cells (Q3) to apoptotic populations. The viable fraction decreased substantially (Q3: 89.3% → 20.7%), accompanied by a strong increase in early apoptotic cells (Q4: 3.5% → 22.5%), and late apoptotic/necrotic cells (Q1/Q2 combined: 7.2% → 56.8%) (Figure A,B).

BTZ treatment resulted in a pronounced increase in apoptotic fractions in multiple myeloma cell lines (RPMI 8226 and U266B1), characterized by a reduction in viable cells and enrichment of Annexin V-positive populations. Similarly, U266B1 cells displayed enhanced apoptosis following BTZ exposure. The viable population declined (Q3:85.7% → 45.9%), while early apoptotic cells increased significantly (Q4:6.0% → 27.3%), along with a notable rise in late apoptotic cells (Q2:8.0% → 23.7%) (Figure C,D).

In contrast, solid tumor-derived cell lines demonstrated minimal sensitivity to BTZ. HeLa cells showed negligible alterations in viability (98.1% → 97.0%) with only marginal changes in apoptotic fractions (Figure E,F). Likewise, HepG2 cells maintained high viability following treatment (92.1% → 94.6%), with no meaningful induction of apoptosis (Figure G,H).

Collectively, these findings indicate that bortezomib robustly induces apoptosis in multiple myeloma cell lines while exerting limited cytotoxic effects in solid tumor cell models under the tested conditions.

Bortezomib-Induced Attenuation of Protein Synthesis Correlates with Drug Sensitivity

Puromycin incorporation assays were performed to evaluate the impact of bortezomib (BTZ) treatment on global protein synthesis across multiple cell lines (Figure ). Immunoblot analysis revealed a marked reduction in puromycin-labeled nascent polypeptides following BTZ exposure in the multiple myeloma cell lines RPMI-8226 and U266B1. In U266B1 cells, BTZ treatment resulted in a clear decrease in puromycin signal intensity, indicating suppression of protein synthesis (Figure A). A similar pattern was observed in RPMI-8226 WT cells, where BTZ-treated samples exhibited substantially reduced puromycin incorporation compared to untreated controls (Figure B left 2 lanes). Notably, this reduction was not observed in RPMI-8226 BT cells, a bortezomib-resistant cell line derived from RPMI-8226, suggesting that the decrease in protein synthesis is specifically associated with bortezomib sensitivity (Figure B right 2 lanes).

Bortezomib suppresses global protein synthesis as assessed by puromycin incorporation. Puromycin incorporation assays were performed to evaluate nascent protein synthesis following bortezomib (BTZ) treatment. Representative immunoblots showing puromycin-labeled polypeptides are presented for U266B1 cells (left) (A), RPMI-8226 WT and RPMI-8226 BT cells (middle) (B), and HeLa and HepG2 cells (right) (C), under untreated (−) and BTZ-treated (+) conditions. BTZ treatment resulted in a marked reduction in puromycin incorporation in RPMI-8226 and U266B1, indicating inhibition of global protein synthesis. GAPDH was used as a loading control to confirm equal protein loading. Representative blots from independent experiments are shown.

Consistent observations were made in solid tumor-derived cell lines. Both HeLa and HepG2 cells demonstrated little to no decrease in puromycin labeling upon BTZ treatment, indicating that the inhibitory effect of BTZ on protein synthesis is more pronounced in hematological malignancies, while exhibiting minimal or negligible effects in HeLa and HepG2 cells (Figure C).

Importantly, GAPDH levels remained comparable across all treatment groups, confirming equal protein loading and validating that the observed differences in puromycin signal reflect genuine alterations in translational activity (Figure ). To confirm equal protein loading, Ponceau S staining was performed. The same lysates used for the puromycin incorporation assay were first stained with Ponceau S and subsequently probed with an anti-puromycin antibody. While Ponceau S staining showed comparable total protein levels across samples, puromycin incorporation varied, indicating that the observed differences are not due to unequal protein loading but rather reflect alterations in protein synthesis (Figure S2).

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that BTZ treatment leads to robust attenuation of global protein synthesis in RPMI-8226 and U266B1 cells compared to HeLa and HepG2 cells. The use of the RPMI-8226-BTZ resistant cell line further confirms that this effect is specific to BTZ sensitivity.

Decreased mTORC1 Signaling is Associated with Increased Susceptibility to Bortezomib

To assess the status of mTORC1 signaling, RPMI-8226, U266B1, HeLa, and HepG2 cells were treated with bortezomib, and cellular lysates were collected at 0, 8, 16, and 24 h (Figure ). The lysates were probed for key components of the mTORC1 signaling pathway, including the phosphorylation status of S6K1 (p-S6K1), ribosomal protein S6 (p-S6), and eukaryotic translation initiation factor 4E-binding protein 1 (p-4E-BP1) along with their corresponding total protein levels (S6K1, S6, and 4E-BP1). To evaluate cellular susceptibility to bortezomib, cleaved caspase-3 was also measured at each time point (Figure ).

HeLa and HepG2 cells showed little to no reduction in the phosphorylation levels of S6K1 or S6 or 4E-BP1 relative to their total protein levels and did not exhibit detectable cleaved caspase-3 expression at any of the time points examined, indicating resistance to bortezomib-induced apoptosis (Figures A,C,D,F and S3C,D).

In contrast, U266B1 and RPMI-8226 cells displayed a marked reduction in p-S6 levels beginning at 8 h and persisting through 24 h following bortezomib treatment (Figure B–F). A reduction in p-S6K1 was observed only at the 24 h time point (Figure B–F). In addition, a reduction in p-4E-BP1 levels was observed at 16 and 24 h compared to 8 h following bortezomib treatment (Figure S3A,B). Notably, both U266B1 and RPMI-8226 cells exhibited increased cleaved caspase-3 expression as early as 8 h post-treatment (Figure B,E). These results indicate enhanced sensitivity of multiple myeloma cell lines to bortezomib, which correlates with decreased mTORC1 signaling, particularly at the level of ribosomal protein S6, along with S6K1, and 4E-BP1. This observation further suggests a link between ribosomal protein regulation and sensitivity to proteasome inhibition.

Decreased mTORC1 Signaling is Associated with Increased Susceptibility to Cycloheximide

Given the association between reduced mTORC1 signaling and bortezomib sensitivity, we next examined whether this relationship extends to other protein synthesis perturbations, specifically cycloheximide. HeLa, HepG2, and RPMI-8226 cells were treated with or without cycloheximide, and cellular lysates were analyzed to assess mTORC1 signaling and apoptotic response (Figure ).

(B) Quantification of p-S6K1 relative to S6K1 in cells treated with or without cycloheximide. (C) Quantification of p-S6 relative to S6 in cells treated with or without cycloheximide. (D–F) Analysis of phosphorylated S6K1 protein stability following cycloheximide treatment in HeLa, HepG2, and RPMI-8226 cells. Cells were treated with cycloheximide (CHX; 1 μg/mL) for the indicated time points. At each time point, cell lysates were prepared and probed for phosphorylated and total S6K1. GAPDH was used as a loading control. Error bars, SD (n = 3). A student t-test was used. *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001.

In HeLa and HepG2 cells, cycloheximide treatment caused little to no change in the phosphorylation status of S6K1 relative to total S6K1, while phosphorylation of ribosomal protein S6 was modestly increased compared with control cells (Figure A–C). Importantly, neither HeLa nor HepG2 cells exhibited cleaved caspase-3 expression, indicating limited sensitivity to cycloheximide (Figure A).

In contrast, RPMI-8226 cells showed a pronounced reduction in mTORC1 signaling, as evidenced by decreased phosphorylation of both S6K1 and S6 relative to their total protein levels, with a more substantial reduction observed for p-S6 than p-S6K1 (Figure A–C). Consistent with this decrease in mTORC1 activity, RPMI-8226 cells also displayed increased cleaved caspase-3 expression, indicating heightened sensitivity to cycloheximide-induced apoptosis (Figure A).

To further characterize the temporal dynamics of mTORC1 inhibition, we performed time-course experiments following cycloheximide treatment. Because protein stability plays a critical role in determining protein abundance, we focused on the phosphorylation status and total levels of S6K1 at multiple time points. Cycloheximide has been widely used to examine the role of ongoing protein synthesis in cell survival and proliferation.

In HeLa and HepG2 cells, the levels of phosphorylated S6K1 are comparable to total S6K1, suggesting that the mTORC1 pathway remains active during the 16 h period following cycloheximide treatment (Figure D,E). In contrast, in the RPMI-8226 multiple myeloma cell line, the level of phosphorylated S6K1 relative to total S6K1 decreases markedly as early as 4 h after cycloheximide treatment, indicating a higher sensitivity of the mTORC1 pathway in these cells (Figure F).

Together, these findings demonstrate that inhibition of protein synthesis preferentially disrupts S6K1 activation and expression in multiple myeloma cells, thereby linking mTORC1 signaling suppression to increased susceptibility to protein synthesis perturbations.

Serum Starvation Induces Apoptosis in the Multiple Myeloma Cell Line RPMI-8226 Compared with HeLa and HepG2 Cells

To further substantiate the role of reduced mTORC1 signaling in determining the sensitivity of myeloma cells, we employed serum starvation, a well-established approach known to suppress S6K1 and S6 signaling and regulate protein synthesis. − HeLa, HepG2, and RPMI-8226 cells were cultured in medium containing 0.1% serum for 24 h, and cellular responses were assessed by microscopic analysis and measurement of apoptosis via cleaved caspase-3 expression (Figure S4).

Microscopic examination revealed pronounced morphological changes in RPMI-8226 cells, characterized by a transition from large, translucent, circular cells to smaller, opaque cells under serum starvation conditions (Figure S4A, left panel). In contrast, HeLa and HepG2 cells displayed minimal morphological alterations following serum deprivation (Figure S4A, middle and right panels). Consistent with these observations, RPMI-8226 cells exhibited a marked increase in apoptosis, as indicated by elevated levels of cleaved caspase-3, compared with HeLa and HepG2 cells (Figure S4B).

Together, these findings demonstrate that multiple myeloma cells are highly sensitive to nutrient- and protein synthesis–limiting conditions, with suppression of S6K1 signaling playing a critical role in mediating this vulnerability.

Multiple Myeloma Cells are More Sensitive to mTORC1 Inhibitors

Given the strong association between mTORC1 signaling and sensitivity to protein synthesis perturbationssuch as bortezomib, MG132, cycloheximide, and serum deprivation (0.1%)we next evaluated the therapeutic potential of mTORC1 inhibitors in multiple myeloma. Because MG132, cycloheximide, and serum deprivation are unsuitable for clinical use due to their global toxicity, we focused on clinically relevant or advanced mTORC1 inhibitors, including rapamycin, Torin2, and AZD8055. To this end, we again analyzed drug sensitivity data from the CancerRxGene (Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer) database, comparing multiple myeloma with cervical squamous cell carcinoma and hepatocellular carcinoma. −

Rapamycin

For rapamycin, the minimum screening concentration was 0.000100 μM and the maximum was 0.100 μM, representing a 1000-fold range (Figure A–C). The minimum IC50 for multiple myeloma cell lines was 0.000588 μM, compared with 0.0109 μM for CESC and 0.0153 μM for LHC cell lines, corresponding to 18-fold and 26-fold greater sensitivity, respectively (Figure A–C). The maximum IC50 values were 0.642 μM for multiple myeloma, 1.83 μM for CESC, and 1.34 μM for LHC, indicating 2.9-fold and 2.1-fold lower values in multiple myeloma (Figure A–C). The geometric mean IC50 for multiple myeloma was 0.0221 μM, compared with 0.158 μM for CESC and 0.189 μM for LHC, reflecting 7.1-fold and 8.6-fold increased sensitivity, respectively (Figure A–C). These results indicate that multiple myeloma cell lines are highly sensitive to rapamycin compared with cervical squamous and hepatocellular carcinomas.

Torin2

For Torin2, drug sensitivity data were available for 15 multiple myeloma, 15 hepatocellular carcinoma, and 12 cervical squamous carcinoma cell lines (Figure S5). The minimum and maximum screening concentrations were 0.0781 μM and 20.0 μM, respectively (256-fold range). The minimum IC50 values were comparable across cancer types (0.0235 μM for multiple myeloma, 0.0250 μM for CESC, and 0.0201 μM for LHC). These findings demonstrate enhanced sensitivity of multiple myeloma cell lines to Torin2.

AZD8055

For AZD8055, data were available for 15 multiple myeloma, 13 hepatocellular carcinoma, and 13 cervical squamous carcinoma cell lines. The minimum and maximum screening concentrations were 0.00200 μM and 2.0 μM, respectively, representing a 1000-fold range (Figure S6A–C). The minimum IC50 for multiple myeloma was 0.0156 μM, compared with 0.253 μM for CESC and 0.127 μM for LHC, corresponding to 16-fold and 8-fold greater sensitivity, respectively. The maximum IC50 values were 9.62 μM for multiple myeloma, 31.2 μM for CESC, and 19.8 μM for LHC, indicating 3.2-fold and 2.1-fold lower values in multiple myeloma. The geometric mean IC50 for multiple myeloma was 0.294 μM, compared with 2.83 μM for CESC and 0.998 μM for LHC, reflecting 9.6-fold and 3.4-fold increased sensitivity, respectively (Figure S6A–C). These results further support the heightened sensitivity of multiple myeloma cell lines to AZD8055.

Multiple Myeloma Cell Lines are More Sensitive to Torin1 Compared with HeLa and HepG2 Cells

Given the significance of S6K1 activation in determining cellular fate under conditions of protein synthesis perturbation, we next evaluated the therapeutic potential of Torin1 in HeLa, HepG2, and RPMI-8226 cells (Figure ). The use of Torin2 in the bioinformatics data set was dictated by the availability of publicly accessible drug-sensitivity data, while Torin1 was employed for experimental validation because of its established use as a selective mTOR inhibitor in cellular studies. Torin1 is a potent and selective inhibitor of mTOR, a central regulator of cellular processes including protein synthesis, cell growth, and metabolism. Hyperactivation of mTOR, particularly mTORC1, promotes increased translation of proteins required for cancer cell proliferation, survival, and therapeutic resistance. , By inhibiting mTORC1, Torin1 reduces phosphorylation of downstream targets involved in protein synthesis, such as S6K1 and S6, thereby attenuating the synthesis of proteins necessary for cell growth and survival.

In U266B1 cells (Figure S7A), Torin1 treatment resulted in a gradual increase in cell death with increasing concentrations. Bortezomib treatment resulted in increased apoptosis in RPMI-8226 and U266B1 cells, as indicated by elevated levels of cleaved caspase-3 compared with untreated controls (Figure A). In contrast, the bortezomib-resistant RPMI-8226-BT cell line did not exhibit cleaved caspase-3 expression or reductions in either the phosphorylation status or total levels of S6K1 following bortezomib treatment, underscoring the importance of intact S6K1 signaling in mediating resistance to protein homeostasis perturbation (Figure A). Notably, RPMI-8226-BT cells displayed reduced proliferation and increased apoptosis, as evidenced by elevated cleaved caspase-3 expression, upon Torin1 treatment relative to control cells (Figure B,C). In addition, HepG2 cells, which are relatively insensitive to bortezomib treatment, became markedly more sensitive under 0.1% serum conditionsa condition known to suppress mTORC1 activitycompared to 10% serum. Under serum deprivation (0.1%), HepG2 cells exhibited reduced phosphorylation of S6K1 and S6, indicating diminished mTORC1 signaling (Figure D). Furthermore, Trypan blue exclusion assays revealed a higher relative dead/live cell ratio in 0.1% serum compared to 10% serum conditions (Figure E).

Taken together, these findings highlight the critical role of mTORC1 signaling in modulating cellular sensitivity to bortezomib.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate that multiple myeloma cells exhibit a heightened sensitivity to perturbations in protein homeostasis and translation, which is closely associated with the vulnerability of mTORC1 signaling, particularly at the level of S6K1 and ribosomal protein S6. By integrating large-scale pharmacogenomic analysis with experimental validation, our findings establish a mechanistic link between protein synthesis stress and selective cytotoxicity in multiple myeloma.

Analysis of drug response data revealed that multiple myeloma cell lines are significantly more sensitive to proteasome inhibitors such as bortezomib and MG132 compared with cervical and hepatocellular carcinoma models (Figure ). This observation is consistent with the known biology of malignant plasma cells, which sustain exceptionally high rates of immunoglobulin synthesis and are therefore heavily dependent on tightly regulated proteostasis networks. , Disruption of these networks, either through inhibition of protein degradation or translation, imposes a proteotoxic burden that exceeds the adaptive capacity of myeloma cells, ultimately triggering apoptosis.

Hyperactivation of mTORC1 in multiple myeloma promotes continuous anabolic activity, including high rates of protein synthesis, ribosome biogenesis, and nutrient consumption. While this supports rapid tumor growth, it also creates a state of “metabolic addiction” and chronic proteotoxic stress. − Myeloma cells already operate near the limit of their protein-folding and energy-producing capacity because they produce large amounts of immunoglobulins. , Under additional stress conditions such as nutrient deprivation, hypoxia, oxidative stress, or ER stress, these cells have limited adaptive reserve. Persistent mTORC1 signaling suppresses stress-response pathways such as autophagy and can worsen ATP depletion, unfolded protein accumulation, and ROS generation. − Consequently, despite being growth advantaged under nutrient-rich conditions, mTORC1-hyperactivated myeloma cells become more fragile or labile when exposed to stress. This phenomenon is often described as a trade-off between enhanced growth and reduced stress tolerance.

Our experimental data further support this model (Figure ). Both proteasome inhibition and direct blockade of translation resulted in robust apoptosis in multiple myeloma cell lines, whereas solid tumor-derived cell lines such as HeLa and HepG2 exhibited relative resistance under comparable conditions (Figure ). Notably, this differential sensitivity correlated with a pronounced reduction in global protein synthesis, as measured by puromycin incorporation, specifically in myeloma cells (Figure ). The absence of this effect in resistant or less sensitive cell linesincluding the bortezomib-resistant RPMI-8226 derivativesuggests that suppression of translation is not merely a consequence of cytotoxicity but a determinant of drug response.

A key finding of this study is the association between decreased mTORC1 signaling and increased susceptibility to both proteasome inhibition and translational blockade. In multiple myeloma cells, treatment with bortezomib or cycloheximide led to a marked reduction in phosphorylation of S6, and to a lesser extent S6K1, preceding or coinciding with the induction of apoptosis (Figures and ). In contrast, resistant cell lines maintained mTORC1 activity, as evidenced by sustained phosphorylation of these targets (Figures and ). These results suggest that the inability to preserve mTORC1 signaling under stress conditions may represent a critical vulnerability in multiple myeloma.

Interestingly, our data indicate that phosphorylation of ribosomal protein S6 is more dynamically regulated than S6K1 in response to these perturbations (Figures and ). In several conditions, S6 phosphorylation decreased earlier and more robustly than S6K1 phosphorylation, suggesting that S6 may serve as a more sensitive functional readout of mTORC1 activity under proteotoxic stress. This observation also raises the possibility of differential regulation of downstream mTORC1 targets, which may have implications for how translational control is selectively modulated during stress responses.

The link between mTORC1 suppression and apoptosis was further reinforced by experiments using cycloheximide and serum starvation. Both interventions, which directly or indirectly limit protein synthesis, resulted in rapid attenuation of mTORC1 signaling and induction of apoptosis selectively in multiple myeloma cells. These findings support a model in which continuous mTORC1-driven translation is essential for myeloma cell survival, likely through the maintenance of short-lived pro-survival proteins.

Importantly, pharmacological inhibition of mTORC1 using rapamycin, Torin2, AZD8055, and Torin1 demonstrated that multiple myeloma cells are intrinsically more sensitive to mTORC1-targeted therapies compared with solid tumor cell lines (Figure ). Among these, Torin1 exhibited strong pro-apoptotic effects, accompanied by suppression of S6 and S6K1 phosphorylation (Figures and ). Furthermore, Torin1 was able to induce apoptosis in the bortezomib-resistant RPMI-8226 cell line, highlighting the therapeutic potential of targeting mTORC1 signaling to overcome resistance to proteasome inhibitors (Figure ). The concordance between the Torin2-based drug sensitivity genomic analyses and the Torin1 experimental findings observed in our study suggests that the effects are primarily attributable to mTOR inhibition rather than to compound-specific activities.

Collectively, our findings support a model in which multiple myeloma cells exist in a state of “translational addiction,” characterized by a high dependence on sustained mTORC1 activity and protein synthesis. Perturbations that disrupt this balancewhether through proteasome inhibition, translational blockade, nutrient deprivation, or direct inhibition of mTORC1lead to rapid collapse of proteostasis and induction of apoptosis. In contrast, solid tumor cells appear to possess greater adaptive capacity to withstand such stresses, maintaining mTORC1 signaling and survival.

While the present study demonstrates that multiple myeloma cells exhibit greater sensitivity to protein synthesis perturbations than adherent solid tumor cell lines, the comparison was limited to these distinct cancer types. Future studies involving a broader panel of multiple myeloma cell lines, as well as comparisons among cell lines derived from the same cancer type, would help determine the extent to which the observed vulnerability is specific to multiple myeloma or reflects broader tumor-specific differences. In addition, our findings reveal an association between reduced mTORC1 activity and enhanced sensitivity to proteasomal perturbations. However, the mechanistic basis of this relationship remains to be fully established. Rescue experiments involving the expression of constitutively active mTORC1 or a rapamycin-resistant mTOR mutant in multiple myeloma cells, followed by assessment of their response to protein synthesis perturbations, would provide important evidence for a causal role of mTORC1 signaling in regulating this vulnerability. Such studies may further clarify the molecular determinants underlying the heightened sensitivity of multiple myeloma cells to disruptions in protein homeostasis.

In conclusion, our study identifies mTORC1 signaling as a critical determinant of sensitivity to protein homeostasis perturbation in multiple myeloma. The selective vulnerability of myeloma cells to disruptions in translation and proteostasis highlights potential therapeutic strategies aimed at targeting this axis. Combination approaches involving proteasome inhibitors and mTORC1 pathway inhibitors may offer a promising avenue for improving treatment outcomes, particularly in cases of drug resistance.
