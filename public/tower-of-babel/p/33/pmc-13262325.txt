Methylome profiling reveals context-dependent chemo-resistance mechanisms and enhances risk stratification in AML
Authors: Yi Chai, Keir Audrey Mendoza Bono, Nicole Xin-Ning Tang, Melissa G. Ooi, Wei-Ying Jen, Esther H. L. Chan, Pak Ling Lui, Chin Hin Ng, Jameelah S. Mohamed, Fangfang Song, Kevin P. White, Wee Joo Chng, Jianbiao Zhou

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute Myeloid Leukemia (AML) is the second most lethal hematologic malignancy, with approximately 30% of patients being refractory to first-line induction therapy. While current risk stratification systems, such as European LeukemiaNet (ELN), use cytogenetic and molecular markers to guide treatment decisions, their predictive accuracy remains suboptimal, particularly in forecasting treatment response.MethodsIn this study, we performed integrated methylome and transcriptome analysis of diagnostic samples from 146 AML patients, including methylome data on 25 patients, to investigate the molecular basis underlying the discrepancy between predicted and actual treatment outcomes. We utilized MSP-PCR, cell proliferation assays, and colony formation assays to evaluate the effects of DNA methylation on PTX4 expression and function in AML. A comprehensive machine-learning pipeline was implemented to develop a methylome-based classifier predicting primary refractory disease.ResultsUnsupervised analysis revealed that while genomic backgrounds strongly influence molecular profiles, treatment response patterns frequently diverge from predictions based on cytogenetic and mutational risk classifications. We identified PTX4 as a novel tumor suppressor gene (TSG) silenced by DNA hypermethylation in patients with adverse-risk AML. Within individual AML subtypes, comparison of refractory/relapsed (RR) cases versus those achieving complete remission (CR) uncovered distinct resistance mechanisms. Furthermore, analysis of hematopoietic developmental trajectories revealed that RR cases exhibit altered epigenetic programming, characterized by preferential methylation changes in regulatory regions, such as polycomb-repressed chromatin states. Based on these insights, we developed a methylome-based classifier (AUROC = 0.86) that addresses 32.2% misclassification rate observed with ELN criteria.ConclusionsThese results represent both context-dependent and unifying mechanism of treatment resistance that is independent of genetic background. These findings highlight the limitations of current genetic-based risk assessments and underscore the potential of incorporating epigenetic profiling, such as methylome analysis, to better understand more accurately, predict treatment response and guide therapeutic strategies in AML.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12935-026-04297-0.

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) represents a biologically and clinically heterogeneous malignancy characterized by the clonal expansion of undifferentiated myeloid precursors and subsequent impaired hematopoiesis [1]. Approximately 30% of patients remain refractory to first-line "7 + 3" induction therapy (7 days of cytarabine with first 3 days of an anthracycline) [2–4]. Current risk stratification systems, such as the European LeukemiaNet (ELN) classification, which rely predominantly on cytogenetic abnormalities and a limited panel of molecular markers to guide treatment decisions, frequently fail to accurately predict therapeutic response for a substantial proportion of patients [5, 6]. This limitation underscores the urgent need for more refined stratification approaches that capture the molecular complexity underlying treatment resistance in AML [7].

The pathophysiology of AML is significantly influenced by epigenetic dysregulation, particularly aberrant DNA methylation patterns, which interact with genetic alterations including mutations in epigenetic modifier genes (e.g., IDH1, IDH2, DNMT3A, TET2) and other recurrent mutations (e.g., CEBPA, NPM1, FLT3) [8–14]. Over the past decade, genome-wide methylation profiling has emerged as a powerful tool for AML classification and prognostication. Figueroa et al. [15] established that DNA methylation signatures could classify AML into clinically relevant subgroups and identify a CpG island methylator phenotype associated with poor prognosis. Vosberg et al. [16] demonstrated that epigenetic subgroups defined by methylation patterns, particularly those associated with mutations in DNMT3A, IDH1/2, TET2, and WT1, correlate with distinct clinical outcomes in adult AML. In pediatric AML, Krali et al. [17] showed that DNA methylation signatures can predict cytogenetic subtypes with 91% accuracy and identify prognostic subgroups within established categories. Their analysis revealed that the cytogenetically "normal karyotype" group split into two methylation-defined clusters with different mutational profiles and clinical outcomes, demonstrating intra-subtype heterogeneity not captured by conventional cytogenetic analysis. Also, different "epitypes" based on DNA methylation signatures are found to be associated with genetic aberrations and reflect blockage of differentiation at specific stages of myelopoiesis [18, 19]. More recently, Xu et al. [20] demonstrated that mutations in DNA methylation regulatory genes (DNMT3A, IDH1, IDH2, TET2) collectively serve as independent risk factors for relapse-free survival. Notably, their study revealed that hematopoietic stem cell transplantation can overcome the poor prognosis conferred by these mutations, highlighting the clinical actionability of methylation-based risk assessment.

Despite these advances in understanding the prognostic significance of DNA methylation in AML, a critical knowledge gap remains regarding its predictive value for treatment response. Previous studies have extensively characterized molecular markers associated with long-term outcomes, including cytogenetic abnormalities, recurrent gene mutations (such as NPM1, FLT3, CEBPA), and gene expression signatures [21]. DNA methylation studies have similarly focused primarily on disease subclassification and long-term survival. However, the relationship between pre-treatment DNA methylation profiles and response to first-line induction therapy remains incompletely characterized. This distinction is clinically crucial because approximately 30% of patients fail to achieve remission with standard "7 + 3" induction chemotherapy, and early treatment response fundamentally determines subsequent therapeutic decisions, including the timing and necessity of allogeneic stem cell transplantation. Moreover, current cytogenetic and mutation based ELN risk stratification frequently fails to predict treatment response within the same risk category [5, 6]. Systematic integration of baseline methylome data with established risk factors may illuminate mechanisms underlying this discordance and identify treatment-resistant patients at diagnosis.

In this study, we conducted integrated methylome and transcriptome analysis of diagnostic bone marrow samples from 146 local AML patients, including genome-wide methylome profiling on a subset of 25 patients. Building upon the established framework that DNA methylation patterns correlate with AML subtypes and long-term outcomes, we specifically investigated whether baseline methylation signatures could predict response to first-line "7 + 3" induction therapy. Our objective was to elucidate the molecular basis underlying the discrepancy between cytogenetic and molecular risk stratification and actual clinical outcomes following first induction therapy. In particular, we sought to determine whether methylation patterns offer additional predictive value beyond established risk factors for this critical early clinical endpoint.

Methods (also see supplementary methods)

Study cohort, RNA-seq and methylation profiling

Our study cohort comprised 146 patients with AML at the time of diagnosis, who subsequently underwent first standard induction therapy at National University Hospital, Singapore, between 2016 and 2022. Patients who were refractory to induction therapy or relapsed within 12 months of achieving complete remission (CR) were classified as refractory/relapsed (RR) cases. Among these, 25 patients, with 13 RR, 12 CR, balanced on age and sex were selected for methylome profiling to represent diverse cytogenetic and molecular subtypes. BM specimens were collected with informed consent. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of National Cancer Institute of Singapore. Clinical annotations were obtained from routine diagnostic workup at diagnosis. Cytogenetic abnormalities were identified by conventional G-banded karyotyping and fluorescence in situ hybridization. Recurrent molecular mutations including NPM1, FLT3-ITD, and CEBPA were detected by polymerase chain reaction and Sanger sequencing. Secondary AML with antecedent myeloproliferative disorder (AML with MPD) was defined by prior MPD diagnosis and disease progression documented in clinical records. Biphenotypic AML was classified by multiparameter flow cytometry according to WHO scoring criteria for mixed phenotype acute leukemia. Integrated WHO 2016 disease classification was assigned by synthesizing cytogenetic, molecular, immunophenotypic, and clinical findings. Patients were grouped according with/without these genetic alterations and disease categories with established clinical prognostic significance.

Methylation profiling was performed using the Illumina Infinium HumanMethylation EPIC BeadChip array, which interrogates over 850,000 cytosine and guanine (CpG) dinucleotides sites across the human genome. Whole transcriptome RNA sequencing was performed on all 146 samples using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Data processing, differentially methylated probe (DMP)/region (DMR) and differential gene expression analysis, Motif enrichment analysis, Myeloid development signature, and Feature selection and importance analysis are described in detail in Supplementary Methods. All authors had full access to the study data.

Methylation-specific polymerase chain reaction (MSP)

Primers distinguishing unmethylated and methylated alleles were designed with MethPrimer [22] to amplify PTX4 promoter region. More details were described in Supplementary Methods.

Demethylating agent 5-Azacytidine (5-Aza) treatment

Exponentially growing THP1 cells were seeded at a density of 1 million cells and treated with 0.5 μM and 1 μM 5-Aza for 96 h. Control cells were treated in parallel with dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). The medium with 5-Aza or DMSO was replaced every other day. Cells were harvested and DNA and mRNA were extracted, followed by MSP and real-time qRT-PCR analysis of methylation level and PTX4 mRNA expression, respectively. qRT-PCR and immunoblotting analysis are described in detail in Supplementary Methods.

Lentivirus infection and RNA-seq

GFP-tagged pLVX empty vector (EV), pLVX-PTX4-GFP vector were purchased from VectorBuilder (Chicago, IL, USA). The helper vector and lentivirus production and infection of THP1 cells were previously described [23]. The details of RNA-seq experiments and data analysis were provided in Supplementary Methods.

Cell proliferation assay and colony formation assay

Trypan Blue staining method was used to count the viable cell numbers at day 4, 6 and 8 post transduction of lentivirus. Colony formation assays were performed essentially as delineated [24], but with use of pLVX-GFP- or pLVX-PTX4-expressing THP1 cells.

Distinct methylation and expression patterns in AML subtypes

Unsupervised clustering of the top differentially methylated probes revealed distinct patterns aligned with specific cytogenetic and mutational subtypes, including NPM1, CEBPA, Philadelphia chromosome-positive (Ph +), myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS)-related changes, and complex karyotypes (Fig. 1A), demonstrating concordance with known biological differences. Notably, clustering patterns of molecular profiles showed limited discriminatory power between treatment response groups, highlighting the molecular complexity underlying treatment outcomes beyond conventional classifications (Fig. 1B). To explore this further, we performed dimensionality reduction on transcriptomic data using t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE). Mutations such as CEBPA showed relatively distinct clustering. To better understand markers such as FLT3-ITD, whose samples were more widespread and frequently harbored co-occurring mutations, we further subgrouped cases by mutation co-occurrence. However, transcriptomic profiles did not fully capture all clinically relevant genomic heterogeneity. For instance, CEBPA monoallelic and biallelic mutations did not show clear separation despite their different prognostic implications. Notably, two samples of secondary AML evolved from myeloproliferative neoplasms showed proximity. This suggests that disease evolution and cellular context contribute to transcriptomic identity beyond single mutation status. In contrast, grouping based on chemotherapy response showed considerably less distinct transcriptomic patterns (Fig. 1D). This suggests that resistance mechanisms may arise from subtler or context-dependent changes rather than global gene expression shifts. Cytogenetically defined cases (including trisomy 8, inv(16), monosomy, and complex karyotypes) (Supplementary Figure S1) displayed less pronounced global segregation compared to mutation-defined subgroups such as NPM1 and CEBPA.

Together, these findings suggest that ELN-based genetic and cytogenetic classifications explain only part of the molecular heterogeneity in AML. The discordance between treatment response and these conventional markers underscores the importance of dissecting subtype-specific resistance mechanisms.

Subtype-specific DNA methylation patterns correlate with clinical risk groups and reveal PTX4 as a novel tumor suppression gene

To directly assess the relationship between methylation patterns and clinical risk categories, we stratified patients according to the ELN22 criteria into favorable- and adverse-risk groups [5]. We identified differentially methylated probes (DMPs) and regions (DMRs) using FDR < 0.05 and |Δβ|> 0.2 for DMPs, and the Bumphunter algorithm (minimum 3 CpGs per region, maximum 1000 bp gap) for DMRs (see Sect. “Methods”). To understand the molecular basis underlying this risk-associated methylation pattern, we further investigated subtype-specific methylome features. We identified DMPs and DMRs within molecular subtypes (≥ 3 samples) compared to matched AML controls lacking the defining genetic alteration with balanced samples counts. We detected 11 DMRs for CEBPA mutation (n = 4), 9 for FLT3-ITD (n = 3), 66 for NPM1 mutation (n = 4), 6 for monosomic karyotype (n = 3), 101 for complex karyotypes (n = 4), and 5 in biphenotypic AML (n = 5). Examination of the genomic distribution of these DMRs revealed subtype-specific patterns (Fig. 2C). In the CpG context, most subtypes showed predominance in CpG islands, ranging from 60.6% in NPM1-mutated cases to 77.8% in FLT3-ITD. Across all subtypes, DMRs showed minimal enhancer localization. In the genomic context, AML with MDS displayed the highest promoter enrichment at 84.0%, followed by CEBPA-mutated at 81.8%, complex karyotype at 70.3%, and NPM1-mutated AML at 66.7%, consistent with direct transcriptional regulation of genes critical for myeloid differentiation and tumor suppression as the primary mechanism in these subtypes [15]. In contrast, FLT3-ITD exhibited a more balanced distribution with 44.4% in promoters and 55.6% in other genomic regions, suggesting mechanisms beyond canonical promoter-based transcriptional silencing. Examining the directionality of methylation changes across subtypes revealed associations with clinical risk categories (Fig. 2C). Adverse-risk subtypes including FLT3-ITD and complex karyotype exhibited net hypermethylation. In contrast, favorable-risk subtypes such as NPM1-mutated AML were primarily characterized by hypomethylation (Figs. 2D, Supplementary Figure S2).

To assess functional relevance of these methylation alterations, we identified genes whose promoters, enhancers, or gene bodies overlapped with DMRs in each subtype. Among those genes, we further identified a small set whose expression levels were associated with methylation changes when comparing each subtype to its corresponding wild-type samples (mean methylation difference >|0.2|, expression log2 fold change >|2.0|) (Supplementary Table S1). Favorable-risk subtypes exhibited distinct patterns of differential methylation affecting genes involved in immune regulation. For instance, NPM1-mutated samples showed higher expression of LTBR, which plays a critical role in lymphoid tissue development and immune response [25], and STXBP2, which is essential for vesicle trafficking and cytotoxic granule exocytosis by natural killer (NK) cells [26]. In CEBPA-mutated AML, LIME1, a signaling adaptor in T-cell receptor pathways [27], was upregulated (Fig. 2E), and RAB34, a promoter of AML proliferation, was downregulated [28]. Contrastively, in the adverse-risk AML (e.g., complex karyotype), TET2, a key enzyme involved in DNA demethylation, was hypermethylated. TET2 loss is known to induce enhancer hypermethylation, promoting leukemogenesis [29]. To further explore regulatory implications, we performed motif enrichment analysis of DMRs that distinguish favorable- from adverse-risk groups. This analysis revealed enrichment for hematopoietic transcription factors (TFs) in the DMRs distinguishing favorable- versus adverse-risk groups, including RUNX (found in 29.63% of target sequences) [30], GATA3 (present in 25.93% of target sequences) [31], and members of the CEBP family (Fig. 2F) [8]. Aberrant methylation of these TF binding sites in adverse-risk AML may impair TF binding and disrupt key gene regulatory programs governing hematopoietic differentiation and myeloid lineage commitment. Together, these results suggest that AML subtype-specific DNA methylation patterns correlate strongly with prognosis and may alter TF occupancy and subsequently gene expression, contributing to leukemic behavior and therapeutic response.

Building on these findings, we sought to identify genes whose methylation status may serve as therapeutic targets. We first prioritized DMRs overlapping with gene promoters from the comparison between adverse- and favorable-risk groups (Supplementary Table S2). Through visual inspection of methylation patterns across individual patients’ genomic tracks, we identified candidate genes exhibiting consistent differential methylation within each risk group. To further evaluate their prognostic significance, we interrogated the independent Beat-AML as validation cohort (n = 671) [32]. Among genes showing consistent promoter hypermethylation in adverse-risk patients identified from local cohort, PTX4 (Pentraxin 4) emerged as a significantly altered candidate (Fig. 3A), with markedly higher methylation in adverse-risk patients (p = 2.7e-6, t-test) and correspondingly lower expression (p = 0.002) (Fig. 3B). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis demonstrated that patients with lower PTX4 expression had significantly reduced overall survival (median OS: 14.0 vs 18.0 months, HR = 0.75, 95% CI [0.61–0.91], p = 4.3e-3) (Fig. 3C). To confirm regulation by DNA methylation, we assessed PTX4 promoter methylation via MSP and detected methylation in KG1, MOLM-14, and THP1, but not HL60 AML cells (Fig. 3D). Treatment of THP1 cells with 5-Aza (1 µM, 96 h) clearly showed reduced methylation level and increased PTX4 mRNA and protein levels compared to DMSO controls (Fig. 3E). These results demonstrate that methylation is the mechanism of reduced expression in AML cells. Notably, overexpression of PTX4 in THP1 cells revealed that PTX4 had a strong negative effect on leukemia cell proliferation (Fig. 3F). Similarly, we found that expression of PTX4 significantly inhibited the self-renewal capability of THP1 cells in colony formation assay (Fig. 3F). Overall, we have identified PTX4 as a target for DNA hypermethylation-mediated repression and demonstrated its tumor suppressive function in AML cells. To further explore PTX4’s mechanistic role, we performed RNA-seq on PTX4-overexpressing versus control THP1 cells (n = 3 per group). PTX4 overexpression significantly altered 102 genes (|log2FC|> 0.5, p < 0.05) (Fig. 3G, Supplementary Table S3). Downregulated genes included pro-inflammatory chemokines (CXCL8, CXCL2), the proliferation driver FOSB, and anti-apoptotic regulator BIRC3. Upregulated genes included T cell receptor component CD247, tumor suppressor BATF2, phosphatase subunit PPP2R2C, and differentiation factor PAX5. Pathway analysis revealed PTX4 suppressed oncogenic programs including NF-κB signaling, IL-17 signaling, Toll-like receptor pathways, and immunosuppressive granulocyte/neutrophil activation signatures (Fig. 3H). Conversely, PTX4 enhanced T cell receptor signaling and MAP kinase pathways (Fig. 3H). These results establish PTX4 act as a tumor suppressor that inhibits NF-κB survival signaling and pro-inflammatory networks while promoting T cell immunity and apoptosis.

These findings demonstrate that DNA methylation profiles across different AML subtypes not only correlate with established risk categories and functionally affect TF binding sites but also provide actionable insights. Notably, the identification of novel TSGs such as PTX4, which shows significant hypermethylation in adverse-risk AML, highlights potential candidates for further investigation using demethylating agents as complementary approaches to standard therapy.

Divergence between conventional risk classification and treatment response reveals complementary mechanisms of chemoresistance

PTX4 represents a specific example of promoter hypermethylation-mediated gene silencing in adverse-risk AML. However, the observation that patients within the same ELN risk category exhibit divergent treatment responses (Fig. 1B) suggests that resistance mechanisms extend beyond such events. To comprehensively characterize these resistance determinants, we next examined genome-wide methylation and transcriptomic differences between treatment-responsive and treatment-resistant cases within each AML subtype. As an initial assessment of how well conventional risk stratification captures treatment failure dynamics, we analyzed time to relapse among relapsed patients with available clinical follow-up (n = 64; 20 adverse-risk, 15 favorable-risk, 29 intermediate-risk). Favorable-risk patients showed a trend toward longer median time to relapse compared to adverse-risk patients (463 vs 255 days, p = 0.055, Wilcoxon test; log-rank p = 0.11; HR = 0.51, 95% CI: 0.25–1.01, p = 0.053, Cox regression), while intermediate-risk patients exhibited similar timing to adverse-risk disease (255 days, p = 0.799 vs adverse-risk; p = 0.206 vs favorable-risk) (Supplementary Figure S4). However, substantial heterogeneity existed for molecular subtypes within same risk groups. For instance, within adverse-risk disease, median time to relapse ranged from 250 days (AML with myelodysplasia-related changes, n = 12) to 314 days (complex karyotype, n = 5 and monosomic karyotype, n = 3). These findings demonstrate that while current ELN risk stratification systems partially account for relapse timing, they fail to fully capture the temporal heterogeneity of disease progression. Next, to uncover context-specific resistance mechanisms, we examined DMRs between RR and CR samples within each subtype. These DMRs were predominantly located in CpG islands, promoter regions, and non-enhancer elements across subtypes (Fig. 4A). Mapping subtype-specific DMRs to functional regions (promoter, enhancer, and gene bodies), revealed both unique and shared alterations (Supplementary Table S4). In monosomic karyotype AML, we identified differential methylation of GSTO2, a gene involved in xenobiotic metabolism [33], which may underlie poor chemotherapy response in this subgroup. HOXA5 was recurrently differentially methylated across subtypes, including normal karyotype and NPM1-mutated AML. Expression analysis also highlighted consistent differential expression of the MAPK signaling cascade [34] between RR and CR samples across multiple subtypes (Supplementary Table S5).

Subtype-specific pathway alterations were evident from transcriptomic data. Biphenotypic AML displayed changes in leukocyte mediated immunity (p-adj = 1.35e-19), reflecting its unique biological characteristics at the interface of myeloid and lymphoid differentiation. Complex karyotype AML showed altered expression of AKR1C2 and AKR1C1, aldo–keto reductase family enzymes that catalyze anthracycline detoxification [35, 36], providing a direct mechanistic link to doxorubicin resistance. In cases with unexpected clinical outcomes (i.e., where treatment response diverged from ELN classification), we observed distinct pathway enrichments. Among adverse-risk patients (n = 47), those achieving remission (n = 12) showed activation of NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity pathways (Fig. 4D), indicating enhanced anti-leukemic immune surveillance can overcome intrinsic genetic risk factors. Conversely, treatment-resistant patients within this adverse-risk category (n = 35) exhibited immunosuppressive features, dysregulated interferon-gamma response (NES = -1.34, padj = 0.024), and enhanced xenobiotic metabolism (NES = -2.10, padj = 1.65e-08) (Fig. 4F).

In favorable-risk AML (n = 43), mitochondrial metabolism differentiated RR (n = 17) from CR (n = 26) patients (Fig. 4E), with significant depletion of oxidative phosphorylation (NES = -2.62, padj = 5.05e-17) and reactive oxygen species (ROS) pathways (NES = -1.62, padj = 0.040) (Fig. 4G). These findings suggest that metabolic adaptation to hypoxia and reprogramming drive resistance even in low-risk patients.

We next categorized the DMPs into three mechanistically distinct groups based on their deviation from normal developmental patterns from HSPC to mature blood cells as aberrant hypermethylation, aberrant hypomethylation and failed hypomethylation (Fig. 5D). Subtype-specific patterns emerged, with marked hypermethylation in subtypes with RUNX1 point mutation, IDH1/2 mutations, t(8;21), monosomy, and MDS-related changes (Fig. 5E). Although RR samples generally showed increased hypermethylation compared with CR samples (Fig. 5D), this quantitative difference was less pronounced than the mutation or cytogenetic-subtype specific variations.

To dissect the mechanisms underlying RR vs CR changes, we examined chromatin state associated with aberrant DMPs in the context of myeloid lineage signatures (Fig. 5F). Utilizing the 15-state model defined by the Roadmap Epigenomics Project [37], we found RR-specific hypermethylation enriched in repressed polycomb (RepPC) and quiescent chromatin states (Fig. 5G). RepPC regions, silenced by polycomb repressive complexes (PRC1/PRC2) via H3K27me3, help preserve stem cell plasticity by reversibly repressing lineage-specific genes. In RR AML, this pattern of hypermethylation represents an "epigenetic switch" [38] phenomenon where the normally reversible polycomb-mediated repression is replaced by more permanent DNA methylation-based silencing, effectively "locking" cells in a stem-like state.

Conversely, hypermethylation was relatively depleted in active TSS and flanking regions, aligning with findings from Glass et al. [39], showing that active promoters and TSS regions are distinctly spared from the aberrant hypermethylation that affects other genomic compartments in AML. These data suggest a sophisticated machinery whereby RR AML preferentially stabilizes a stem-like state through hypermethylation of regions controlling differentiation and quiescence, while preserving active TSS regions from methylation changes to maintain leukemogenic gene expression.

DNA methylation patterns predict treatment resistance in AML

The convergent epigenetic signature seen in genetically diverse RR samples, characterized by strategic hypermethylation and developmental arrest, suggested its potential utility in predicting treatment outcomes. We therefore implemented a comprehensive machine-learning (ML) pipeline using Beat-AML methylation profiles as the discovery dataset and our local Singaporean cohort (n = 25; 13 RR, 12 CR) serving as an independent test set, with overall workflow demonstrated in Supplementary Figure S6. All samples were obtained at diagnosis. We evaluated the performance of multiple ML algorithms, including XGBoost, random forest, Support Vector Machines (SVM) with radial kernel, and elastic net regression. Hyperparameters were optimized through exhaustive grid search evaluating over 2,300 parameter combinations across all algorithms, using 100-fold bootstrap cross-validation. Feature sets ranging from 200 to 5,000 CpG probes were systematically evaluated, with probes selected from the top 20% most variable sites in the training set and ranked by differential methylation significance between CR and RR groups.

Our initial attempts to distinguish all RR from CR cases yielded modest discriminative power (AUROC of 0.63), likely reflecting the complex biological and temporal heterogeneity of relapse mechanisms that evolve during disease progression. However, restricting analysis to primary refractory disease yielded a robust methylation signature. The best-performing model (SVM with 2,000 features) achieved cross-validation AUROC of 0.88 ± 0.04 (Fig. 6A), with sensitivity of 75.3% and specificity of 85.2% aggregated from 100 bootstrap iterations (Supplementary Figure S7). Importantly, this model maintained strong predictive power in the independent test cohort (AUROC of 0.86; sensitivity = 1.00, specificity = 0.81) (Fig. 6B). The comparable performance between cross-validation and independent test set demonstrates robust generalization without overfitting. Feature importance was further assessed using a permutation-based approach to identify the most discriminative methylation markers. For each feature, importance scores were computed based on the decrease in model performance when the feature was randomly permuted while keeping others constant. The distribution of selected probes was heavily right-skewed, with a top contributor mapping to genes prominently associated with AML pathogenesis and treatment response (Fig. 6D). These genes included key hematopoietic TFs (MYB, MEIS2) [40, 41], leukemia stem cell marker (CD34) [42], and genes with functional roles in drug response and mitochondrial apoptotic pathways such as HIF1A [43]. These findings not only demonstrate the biological relevance of DNA methylation in distinguishing treatment-resistant from responsive AML, but also identify potential therapeutic targets that may influence treatment outcomes. In summary, these results establish that distinct DNA methylation patterns present at diagnosis can effectively predict primary refractory disease in AML, offering both prognostic value and mechanistic insights into the epigenetic determinants of treatment resistance.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate that DNA methylation in AML reveals context-dependent and fundamental epigenetic patterns of treatment resistance that transcend conventional genetic classifications. Through global methylome profiling of 25 Singaporean AML patients at diagnosis, nested within a cohort of 146 patients with transcriptomic data, we uncovered a critical discordance between established risk stratification systems and actual responses to first-line induction therapy.

Our analysis revealed distinct methylation patterns among AML subtypes associated with specific cytogenetic and mutational profiles (e.g., NPM1, CEBPA, Ph + , MDS-related changes, and complex karyotypes). Transcriptomic analysis revealed that mutation-defined subgroups (NPM1, CEBPA) exhibited more distinct clustering patterns compared to cytogenetically defined cases (trisomy 8, inv(16), monosomy, complex karyotypes) (Supplementary Figure S1). This may be because single gene mutations directly alter specific transcriptional programs through defined pathways, whereas chromosomal alterations exert polygenic effects with heterogeneous functional consequences. Despite these differences, both genetic and cytogenetic subgroups showed limited discriminatory power for predicting treatment response, emphasizing that resistance mechanisms extend beyond these conventional classification systems. When examining cases with unexpected treatment outcomes, we uncovered a dichotomy in resistance mechanisms based on genetic context. In favorable-risk patients experiencing unexpected resistance, molecular alterations manifested primarily as cell-autonomous adaptations, particularly mitochondrial metabolic reprogramming and depletion of ROS pathways. It is likely due to favorable-risk AMLs maintain relatively normal differentiation and apoptotic programs with lower genomic instability. Resistance likely requires active cellular adaptation resembling the cancer "persister" phenotype [44]. Conversely, adverse-risk AML patients achieving unexpected remission show enhanced immune surveillance, particularly NK cell and IFN-γ mediated cytotoxicity. This phenomenon may be related to their genomic instability, which presumably generates more neoantigens and inflammatory signals potentially creating pre-activated immune states amenable to therapeutic targeting [43, 45]. This dichotomy supports the "baseline context" theory, wherein the inherent genetic background fundamentally shapes and constrains possible resistance mechanisms [46, 47]. This framework explains why genetic classifications alone incompletely predict outcomes and suggests potential targeted therapeutic approaches: combination of chemotherapy with target inhibitors for resistant favorable-risk patients, and with immunomodulatory approaches for adverse-risk patients to enhance existing immune surveillance.

This study uncovered PTX4, a member of the pentraxin superfamily, as one of the TSGs in adverse-risk AML. Other pentraxin members, like PTX3, are involved in innate immunity and inflammation [48], however, PTX4 is less studied and its involvement in cancer has not been reported prior to our study. Further insights into the molecular mechanisms of PTX4 function in AML and other types of cancers will continue to be explored.

The complex relationship between genetic and epigenetic alterations in AML merits consideration. The traditional view that genetic mutations drive disease progression has been challenged by mounting evidence that epigenetic reprogramming may create a permissive state that precedes and facilitates the acquisition of genetic mutations [15, 49, 50]. Notably, RR samples exhibited a higher stemness states along the normal hematopoietic-to-myeloid trajectory based on methylome patterns. Chromatin state analysis of RR-specific DMPs revealed significant enrichment for aberrant hypermethylation in RepPC and quiescent chromatin states, with reduced methylation at active TSS regions. This methylation pattern silences differentiation-associated genes while preserving essential transcriptional programs, creating a stable, stem-like state that maintains leukemic functionality. Such epigenetic reprogramming likely contributes to resistance against induction therapy, which targets proliferating cells and depends on differentiation for cytotoxic efficacy. A methylation-based classifier predicting primary refractory disease (AUROC = 0.86) underscores the clinical relevance of these methylome-driven resistance mechanisms.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, our analysis lacks variant allele frequency (VAF) data, which precludes stratification of mutations into clonal versus subclonal events and assessment of homo/heterozygous states. VAF has demonstrated prognostic relevance in AML [51] and could refine our understanding of how clonal architecture intersects with methylation profiles during disease evolution. Second, comprehensive mutational profiling of certain epigenetic modifier genes such as IDH1, IDH2, and TET2 was yet unavailable in our local cohort, which could alter global methylation landscape, as exemplified by IDH1 mutation-induced global aberrant hypermethylation found in Beat AML. Future studies incorporating comprehensive epigenetic modifier mutation data alongside VAF would enable more precise dissection of mutation-specific methylation patterns and their respective contributions to treatment resistance. SVM with RBF kernel outperformed tree-based algorithms in our classifier, consistent with its established advantages for high-dimensional data in limited sample cohorts [52]. However, it is noteworthy that current publicly available methylation datasets with complete induction therapy response annotations for diagnostic AML samples are limited. Future validation would benefit from expanded multi-center cohorts as additional datasets become publicly available.

Overall, the methylome analysis provides complementary insights into conventional cytogenetic and mutation-based risk stratification systems [53, 54], revealing both context-dependent and shared epigenetic signatures underlying treatment resistance in AML. This dual contribution enhances our mechanistic understanding of where current classification systems like ELN fall short while simultaneously improving predictive accuracy. The development of a robust classifier that captures resistance determinants missed by genetic profiling alone (with initial misclassification rate of 32.2%) indicates the clinical utility of methylome-based biomarkers. Beyond prognostication, methylation patterns identify actionable therapeutic targets. The discovery of PTX4 as a hypermethylated tumor suppressor provides proof-of-concept that hypomethylating agents can restore silenced genes and inhibit leukemic growth. More broadly, patients exhibiting global hypermethylation with polycomb-repressed chromatin enrichment represent candidates for hypomethylating agent therapy, while the context-dependent resistance mechanisms uncovered within genetic subtypes suggest rational combination strategies targeting subtype-specific vulnerabilities. This work opens new avenues for therapeutic intervention, as integrating methylome profiling with conventional risk stratification systems may enable more precise, personalized therapeutic strategies for this heterogeneous disease.
