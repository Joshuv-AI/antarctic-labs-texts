Do We Really Need a Perfect Vasoactive Agent for Low-middle Income Countries in Pediatric Septic Shock?
Authors: Pradeep K Sharma
Published: 2025

Pediatric septic shock is a medical emergency with significant morbidity and mortality. Sepsis in children carries a high mortality and remains among the leading causes of childhood death globally.1 The mortality reported in literature varies from 10% in the developed world to 40–60% in low-middle-income countries (LMICs).1,2

The prompt recognition and timely interventions have the potential to improve outcomes significantly. Over the last decades, the understanding of pathophysiology, hemodynamic monitoring, and intervention strategies for septic shock has undergone significant changes. From earlier liberal and aggressive fluid resuscitation to conservative and restrictive fluid resuscitation, early vasopressor/inotropy initiation, use of bedside echocardiography, invasive arterial blood pressure monitoring, early antibiotics, and advanced supportive care have improved the overall survival of pediatric septic shock.3,4

Septic shock is a heterogeneous disease with multiple factors, i.e., infectious agent factors, host response factors, nutritional status, comorbidity, coinfections, immune response, and genetic factors, making the pathophysiology of septic shock complex. Shock is a dynamic process, where the difference in presentation not only varies between individuals but also across the time period in the same patient. The underlying pathology in septic shock is a mismatch between oxygen and substrate delivery and consumption, both being dysfunctional. A patient of shock may have absolute/relative hypovolemia leading to decreased preload, poor systolic/diastolic function, decreased systemic vascular resistance, increased or decreased cardiac output, or a combination of these adaptive/maladaptive responses to inciting agents. As the pathophysiology of shock is multifactorial and dynamic, it is difficult for a single vasoactive agent to address all these issues. Despite this, the search for a perfect vasoactive/inotropic agent is being studied across the world. This issue of the journal has published one such pilot study from a tertiary care center in India by Nataraja et al.5 The authors have conducted this pilot randomized controlled trial comparing epinephrine and norepinephrine as first-line vasoactive agents in pediatric septic shock to test the feasibility of a study protocol. They initially planned to conduct a non-inferiority trial comparing two commonly used vasoactive agents; however, due to logistical and operational issues, they carried out a feasibility trial. It is very important to have our own guidelines, evidenced by studies done locally, as highlighted by the FEAST trial.6 However, conducting a randomized trial in an Indian setting in a privately funded institute is very challenging. Inadequate research infrastructure, lack of uniform practices at various institutions, and difficulties in obtaining funding, and restrictions imposed by regulatory bodies make it extremely challenging. The regulatory bodies applying similar criteria as for a phase III new drug trial to judge a clinical trial protocol negatively impact the morale of a research team. The authors of this pilot trial are appreciated for their efforts in carrying out an important and relevant research study. The primary feasibility outcomes of this study were recruitment rates, time to randomization after screening, time to initiate study drug after randomization, and protocol violation rates. Authors reported median times from screening to randomization and randomization to intervention within the entire cohort were 10 minutes (IQR 5–10) and 12 minutes (IQR 8–15). These are excellent timeframes, and implementation of these timeframes in larger and multicenter trials may be difficult. They also studied exploratory secondary clinical outcomes including shock resolution at 6 and 12 hours of randomization, median time to shock resolution, survival free of vasoactive-inotrope support, and survival free of invasive ventilation. The study design and the power of the study were inadequate to detect a significant difference in clinical outcomes. Despite this, the results provide significant insight into the pathophysiology, research protocol used, clinical picture of pediatric septic shock, and paves a way for further clinical studies. There were many interesting observations from this cohort of septic shock patients. The proportion of hypotensive patients was very high (93%) in this cohort. Despite this, the mortality rate for the whole cohort (2/30, 6.6%) was very low. The study protocol not only differs from the standard guideline at many points but also in paradox to the pathophysiological state at some stage.3 The authors chose to use only 10–20 mL/kg of fluid boluses prior to randomization, and in fact, study population has received only 10 mL/kg of fluid bolus before addition of vasoactive agents. The addition of additional vasoactive agents—dobutamine/vasopressin at a study drug concentration of 0.1 µg/kg/min and escalating the study drug to a maximum concentration of 0.2 µg/kg/min only. The addition of dobutamine at 30 minutes of non-resolution of shock, with systolic dysfunction, especially to low-dose epinephrine group, may not be a sound strategy physiologically. Another significant observation was a high rate (46.7%) of predefined adverse events (tachycardia, hypotension, or a combination of both) in the epinephrine group, leading to drug discontinuation. The authors postulated that epinephrine, through its action on β-1 adrenergic receptors, resulting in decreased systemic vascular resistance, may be the reason of the development of these adverse effects. However, the rate of these adverse effects necessitating the drug stoppage is higher than previously reported.2,4 The possible reason could be insufficient preload optimization prior to starting the vasoactive agent, leading to aggravating the β-1 adrenergic effects of epinephrine. For the same reason, addition of dobutamine as per the study protocol may be potentially harmful, especially at centers with limited monitoring facilities.

Despite improvement in infrastructure and availability of advanced facilities, the sepsis mortality in India still remains high. One of the major reason for this is following the guideline without actually assessing the pathophysiological status of septic patients at the grassroot level. Earlier, patients received up to 120 mL/kg fluid boluses, but now patients are being referred on vasoactive agents with inadequate fluid resuscitation despite obvious dehydration. The majority of referred septic patients do not receive an antimicrobial agent at the outlying center. The mortality from septic shock may be reduced at the community level through implementing effectively already proven beneficial interventions than just finding a magic vasoactive agent. The current study proves this point effectively. The authors at their institute were able to deliver a bundle of therapies within a reasonable timeframe, along with advanced hemodynamic monitoring, leading to an excellent mortality rate in their whole cohort. It is important to spread the messages that are practical and feasible in our country.

We have a long way to go to achieve mortality reduction in pediatric sepsis in India. Spreading awareness and implementing already proven strategies at the peripheral level may help to significantly reduce pediatric sepsis mortality. Another aspect is to find ways to “ease of doing research” for the benefit of our own population, as it is difficult to extrapolate results of studies done in other countries to our population. Pediatric septic shock is a pathophysiologic dynamic disease. Hence, it may be more rewarding to identify a precise agent for the individual patient from the basket rather than finding a perfect vasoactive agent to improve clinical outcomes in pediatric septic shock.
