Dialysis Modality, Adequacy and the ESRD Quality Incentive Program Score
Authors: Osama El Shamy, Ankur D. Shah, Daniel Weiner
Published: 2026

To the Editor:

Small solute clearance currently is an influential metric in the End-Stage Renal Disease (ESRD) Quality Incentive Program (QIP). Studies from the 1980s observed that higher blood urea nitrogen values in hemodialysis patients were associated with higher morbidity and mortality and that higher urea reduction ratios were associated with lower mortality.1, 2, 3, 4 Accordingly, the 2015 Kidney Disease Outcomes Quality Initiative (KDOQI) guidelines commented that Kt/Vurea is the “most precise and tested measure of the dialyzer effect on patient survival.”5 In peritoneal dialysis (PD), the Canada–USA Peritoneal Dialysis Study Group (CANUSA) found that maintaining Kt/Vurea >2.1 was associated with a relative reduction in mortality, whereas the Adequacy of Peritoneal Dialysis in Mexico (ADEMEX) study found no difference in survival between the intervention (Kt/Vurea 2.27) and control (Kt/Vurea 1.8) groups.6,7

The ESRD QIP includes the Kt/V dialysis adequacy comprehensive clinical measure as a component of each facility’s total performance score, which is used to determine a facility’s QIP penalty.8 It examines the percentage of patients whose delivered dialysis dose meets the specified target thresholds: hemodialysis (HD) spKt/V ≥1.2, PD Kt/V ≥1.8 for children, and PD Kt/V ≥1.7 for adults. Through calendar year 2024, this measure combined HD and PD adequacy achievement, implicitly assuming that HD and PD adequacy are equivalent measures. We explore the impact of HD-only, both HD and PD, and PD-only facility status on facility-level QIP performance scores.

We analyzed data from 7,200 dialysis facilities using the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) Dialysis Facility and ESRD QIP-Dialysis Adequacy datasets for calendar year 2019 (payment year 2021). Facilities were categorized as hemodialysis only (HD only), peritoneal dialysis only (PD only), or both modalities (HD and PD). We compared 3 Kt/V measure outcomes across groups: comprehensive achievement measure rate (CAMR, 0-100), comprehensive improvement measure rate (CIMR, 0-100), and comprehensive measure score (0-10). Both unweighted and weighted analyses were conducted using Stata 18 (College Station, TX) to account for facility size differences. One-way analysis of variance was used to evaluate differences in adequacy measures between modality groups, with post hoc pairwise comparisons using Bonferroni correction.

This study was exempt because it was not considered human research by the George Washington University Institutional Review Board (NCR245932).

HD-only facilities (n = 3,364) achieved the highest performance scores across all measures (CAMR: 96.5 ± 4.1; CIMR: 95.8 ± 5.1; comprehensive score: 8.5 ± 2.2), followed by combined HD/PD facilities (n = 3,585), with PD-only facilities (n = 251) showing significantly lower performance (CAMR: 91.5 ± 9.3; CIMR: 84.8 ± 15.9; comprehensive score: 6.4 ± 3.3). Providing PD was associated with lower adequacy achievement scores, therefore potentially resulting in lower CMS reimbursement rates to facilities that provide PD. Maintaining QIP scores within the target range is essential for facilities to avoid up to a 2% payment reduction from CMS for their services. One key constituent of total performance score is the Kt/V dialysis adequacy comprehensive score. This could have disincentivized current HD units from providing PD and serves as a caution for providers contemplating having standalone PD units.

When an in-center HD patient’s monthly spKt/V does not meet the desired target, it is logistically simple to repeat laboratory tests until an adequate result is obtained given that patients are scheduled to come to the unit approximately 12 times a month. In contrast, PD adequacy typically is assessed every 3 to 4 months, not monthly. PD patients often come to the dialysis unit on a monthly basis (or as little as once every 3 months, if using telehealth), and the collection of dialysate and urine samples requires significant effort and planning on the part of patients. Moreover, prescription changes, such as increasing dialyzer size and increasing dialysis time, are far easier to implement in HD, whereas increasing treatment time and the number or volume of exchanges in a PD patient may have substantial lifestyle implications. Finally, the data supporting a specific threshold Kt/V in PD are limited, and for some patients receiving PD, achieving this threshold may not be feasible, incentivizing a transition to HD even if the patient is thriving to meet small molecule clearance targets. Additionally, in PD patients, the adequacy metric has greater weight given the absence of some metrics that are present in HD patients, including the In-Center Consumer Assessment of Healthcare Provider and Systems (ICH CAHPS) and vascular access scores.

Our analysis has several strengths, including the use of national data. However, it is limited by the lack of patient level data and inability to perform multivariable adjustments for patient and facility factors.

Notably, for calendar year 2025 (payment year 2027), CMS adopted a revised Kt/V dialysis adequacy measure topic, comprising 4 Kt/V measures: adult HD, adult PD, pediatric HD and pediatric PD.9 This revised measure disaggregates adequacy performance by modality, such that benchmarks and achievement thresholds are specific to modality before being reaggregated into an overall measure score. Given the emphasis on home dialysis provision in the Advancing American Kidney Health Initiative, this simple but important change has the potential to address the disincentive to providing home dialysis that has been present in the ESRD QIP until this time.
