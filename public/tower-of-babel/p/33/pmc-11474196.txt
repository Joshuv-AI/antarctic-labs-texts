Novel insights into the circadian modulation of lipid metabolism in chicken livers revealed by RNA sequencing and weighted gene co-expression network analysis
Authors: Panlin Wang, Fang Li, Yanyan Sun, Yunlei Li, Xiuyu Xie, Xue Du, Lu Liu, Yongshu Wu, Dan Song, Hui Xiong, Jilan Chen, Xiangchen Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The circadian clock is crucial for maintaining lipid metabolism homeostasis in mammals. Despite the economic importance of fat content in poultry, research on the regulatory effects and molecular mechanisms of the circadian clock on avian hepatic lipid metabolism has been limited. In this study, we observed significant diurnal variations (P<0.05) in triglyceride (TG), free fatty acids (FFA), fatty acid synthase (FAS), and total cholesterol (TC) levels in the chicken embryonic liver under 12-h light/12-h dark incubation conditions, with TG, FFA, and TC concentrations showing significant cosine rhythmic oscillations (P<0.05). However, such rhythmic variations were not observed under complete darkness incubation conditions. Using transcriptome sequencing technology, we identified 157 genes significantly upregulated at night and 313 genes significantly upregulated during the 12-h light/12-h dark cycle. These circadian differential genes are involved in processes and pathways such as lipid catabolic process regulation, meiotic cell cycle, circadian rhythm regulation, positive regulation of the MAPK cascade, and glycerolipid metabolism. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) revealed 3 modules—green, blue, and red—that significantly correlate with FFA, FAS, and TG, respectively. Genes within these modules were enriched in processes and pathways including the cell cycle, light stimulus response, circadian rhythm regulation, phosphorylation, positive regulation of the MAPK cascade, and lipid biosynthesis. Notably, we identified ten hub genes, including protein kinase C delta (PRKCD), polo like kinase 4 (PLK4), clock circadian regulator (CLOCK), steroid 5 alpha-reductase 3 (SRD5A3), BUB1 mitotic checkpoint serine/threonine kinase (BUB1B), shugoshin 1 (SGO1), NDC80 kinetochore complex component (NDC80), NIMA related kinase 2 (NEK2), minichromosome maintenance complex component 4 (MCM4), polo like kinase 1 (PLK1), potentially link circadian regulation with lipid metabolic homeostasis. These findings demonstrate the regulatory role of the circadian clock in chicken liver lipid metabolism homeostasis and provide a theoretical basis and molecular targets for optimizing the circadian clock to reduce excessive fat deposition in chickens, which is significant for the healthy development of the poultry industry.

INTRODUCTION

Through intensive breeding and nutritional interventions, the productive performance of chickens has seen rapid advancements. In particular, the growth rate of broiler chickens has increased by more than 300% (Wang et al., 2017). However, this remarkable progress has been accompanied by several negative consequences, including increased body fat deposition and a higher incidence of fatty liver disease, leading to significant economic issues that affect feed efficiency, meat quality, and egg production performance (Sarjeant and Stephens, 2012; Yadav et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024). Abdominal fat accumulation and fatty liver disease in chicken are closely related to hepatic lipid metabolism (Leveille et al., 1975). Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of liver lipid metabolism in chickens is vital for enhancing the economic efficiency of the poultry industry and improving animal health.

Since the 2017 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine was awarded to scientists for their research on the regulatory mechanisms of the circadian clock, the relationship between circadian rhythm disorders and increased obesity risk has garnered widespread attention (Schäbler et al., 2020; Villanueva et al., 2019). Studies have shown that individuals with circadian rhythm disorders have relatively higher body mass index and fat content (Chaput et al., 2023). Sleeping less than 6 to 7 h a day can lead to insulin resistance, reduced leptin levels, decreased glucose tolerance, and binge eating (Nedeltcheva and Scheer, 2014). Additionally, mutations in the BMAL1 gene are associated with impaired gluconeogenesis, hyperleptinemia, poor glucose tolerance, and dyslipidemia, and can regulate fat deposition in mice through the PPAR-γ/SLC1A5 pathway (Wang et al., 2022). Further research has revealed that the circadian clock plays a significant role in regulating lipid metabolism in the liver of mice. For example, transcriptome sequencing has identified 6,243 genes and noncoding RNAs in the mouse liver whose expression levels exhibit significant circadian rhythms. These rhythmic genes are significantly enriched in pathways related to lipid metabolism and circadian rhythms (Xin et al., 2021). Lipidomics analysis has also found that over 80 triglycerides and 8 diglycerides in the mouse liver show clear circadian rhythms (Dyar et al., 2018). These studies indicate that the circadian clock is crucial for maintaining the circadian rhythm homeostasis of lipid metabolism in the liver. However, its’ regulatory role and mechanisms in chicken liver lipid metabolism remain unclear.

Our previous research found that, compared to complete darkness, a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle during the incubation period helps chicken embryos establish circadian clock earlier, accelerating liver development and IGF1 secretion (Wang et al., 2021). Studies have shown that adipose tissue development during the embryonic and early postnatal periods determines the growth trajectory of chickens throughout their lives (Ailhaud et al., 1992; Guo et al., 2011). This underscores the necessity of researching how the circadian clock regulates liver lipid metabolism during the incubation period. Light is the primary zeitgeber for synchronizing the circadian clock with the external environment. To elucidate the molecular mechanisms of circadian rhythms in the liver, it is imperative to prioritize the investigation of the regulatory network during the light/dark cycle.

Genome-wide transcription and translation of protein-coding genes, such as CLOCK, BMAL1, PER, and CRY, play significant roles in the rhythmic oscillations of the mouse liver, mainly participating in regulating lipid metabolism, cholesterol metabolism, bile acid metabolism, alcohol metabolism, and xenobiotic detoxification (Panasiuk et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2014). However, since mouse are nocturnal animals and there are differences in liver de novo fatty acid synthesis among different species, studies on circadian transcriptional regulation in the mouse liver cannot fully reveal the regulatory mechanisms in the bird liver. Therefore, it is necessary to identify hub genes involved in the circadian clock's regulation of hepatic lipid metabolism homeostasis in chickens. Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) is a powerful method used to unveil key regulatory genes, functional modules, and critical nodes in specific biological processes (Langfelder and Horvath, 2008). Through this approach, genes like FABP1, ELOVL6, and ADIPOQ have been pinpointed as influencers of abdominal fat deposition in Wenchang chickens (Luo et al., 2022). Moreover, 3 modules and 6 key genes (including ACOX1, SCD, ALDH6A1, etc.) have been identified as players in regulating abdominal fat in Wannan chickens (Wei et al., 2024). Consequently, integrating transcriptomic techniques with WGCNA method holds promise for uncovering novel mechanisms by which the circadian clock regulates liver lipid metabolism in chickens.

In this study, we investigated the diurnal fluctuation patterns of acetyl-coA carboxylase (ACC), fatty acid synthase (FAS), triglyceride (TG), total cholesterol (TC), glucose (GLU), and free fatty acids (FFA) in the livers of chicken embryos incubated under a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle (12L:12D) as well as in complete darkness. Through examining the transcriptome of those livers under light-dark circadian time series, we discerned the circadian-specific regulation of pivotal modules and genes implicated in FAS, TG, and FFA mediated by the circadian clock. Our study demonstrates the critical role of the circadian clock in regulating hepatic lipid metabolism homeostasis in chickens, providing new insights into the mechanisms by which circadian rhythms govern lipid metabolism. This opens up new avenues for further exploration into circadian regulation of lipid metabolism.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animal Treatment and Sample Collection

To establish the circadian rhythm in the chicken embryos, fertilized eggs were obtained from Sanhuang chickens and incubated in white light-fitted incubators under a light:dark schedule of 12L:12D (lights on at 8:00, 450 lux). The incubation was maintained at a temperature of 37.8 ± 0.10°C, and a relative humidity around 60%. On the 18th day of incubation, 3 liver samples were collected every 4 h starting from 8:00 am, resulting in 6 time points named according to the zeitgeber time (ZT): ZT0 (lights on), ZT4, ZT8, ZT12, ZT16, and ZT20. Each liver sample was divided into 2 parts, with 1 part used for biochemical indicators related to lipid metabolism and Oil Red O staining, and the other part used for RNA-seq.

Biochemical Indexes Related to Lipid Metabolism Detection

To evaluate the impact of the light environment on lipid metabolism-related biochemical indicators, liver tissues were collected at the same time points as previously described, with liver tissues from chicken embryos incubated in complete darkness serving as the control group. The time series samples from the darkness group were labeled circadian time (CT) 0, CT4, CT8, CT12, CT16, and CT20. After defrosting, washing, and homogenizing the liver tissues in phosphate-buffered saline, the concentrations of ACC, FAS, TG, TC, GLU, and FFA were measured using commercial kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Protein levels were also determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China) for calibration.

Oil Red O Staining

To investigate the diurnal effects of 12L:12D and complete darkness incubation environments on hepatic lipid droplet deposition, liver samples from chicken embryos were collected at ZT/CT4 and ZT/CT16 and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. The samples were then embedded in OCT cryo-embedding compound, and sections with a thickness of 5 to 7 µm were prepared using a freezing microtome. The sections were stained with filtered Oil Red O solution according to the manufacturer's instructions. Finally, the number of lipid droplets and the average area of individual droplets in the liver were analyzed using Image Pro Plus 6.0 software.

RNA Extraction

Total RNA from the liver samples was extracted using the TRIzol Reagent kit (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China). The purity of the total RNA was assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham), while the concentration of total RNA was determined using the Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham). The integrity of the total RNA was evaluated by electrophoresis using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, CA).

RNA Library Construction and Transcriptome Sequencing

The qualified total RNA samples were sent to Shanghai LingEn Biotech Co., Ltd. After enrichment of polyadenylated eukaryotic mRNA using magnetic beads with Oligo (dT), the library construction was completed using the Illumina TruSeq RNA Sample Prep Kit method. Following quality inspection, the different libraries were pooled according to their effective concentrations and the desired target sequencing depth requirements, and then subjected to next-generation sequencing.

Data Quality Control

The raw data underwent quality assessment using FastQC, followed by trimming of adapter sequences and removal of low-quality bases (quality score < 20) from the 3′ end of reads using Trimmomatic software. Reads with an N ratio exceeding 10% were discarded, and sequences shorter than 75 bp after adapter removal and quality trimming were also filtered out to obtain high-quality sequencing data (Supplementary Table S1). The mRNA sequencing reads were aligned to the Galgal 6.0 genome using HISAT2 (http://ccb.jhu.edu/software/hisat2/index.shtml). The expression levels of mRNAs were calculated and normalized to the fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) values.

Analysis of Circadian Differentially Expressed Genes

Only genes with average FPKM values ≥ 0.1 in all the samples were screened for subsequent studies (Mele et al., 2015). For the liver samples collected at different times of a circadian day, 6 samples from time points ZT4 and ZT8 were selected as the daytime group, and 6 samples from time points ZT16 and ZT20 were selected as the nighttime group for circadian differential gene expression analysis. The analysis of circadian differentially expressed transcripts was performed using the DESeq2 R package (v4.2.2) (Love et al., 2014), defining genes with a false discovery rate (padj) ≤ 0.05 and |fold change| ≥ 1.5 as differentially expressed. The volcano plot was used to visualize the overall distribution of circadian differentially expressed genes.

Functional Enrichment Analysis

Gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopediaof Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis were performed using the online Metscape database (http://metascape.org) (Zhou et al., 2019). Gene enrichment analysis is a combination of GO and KEGG, using the clusterProfiler package and the Omicshare platform (https://www.omicshare.com/) for Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) analysis.

Weighted Gene Co-Expression Network Analysis

Filtering genes with FPKM > 1 and expressed in at least 3 samples, and selecting the top 40% genes based on median absolute deviation for WGCNA analysis. The data were processed using the WGCNA package (v1.72.1) in R Studio 4.1 software. Firstly, the Pearson correlation between genes was calculated to construct a gene co-expression correlation matrix. Subsequently, an appropriate soft threshold obeying the scale-free network was computed and selected to generate a weighted adjacency matrix. The module is partitioned based on the dynamic Tree Cut standard, with a minimum module capacity set to 30 and the shear height set to 0.3, using 1-TOM as the measurement value. The phenotypic data related to lipid metabolism and gene modules were quantified using Pearson correlation. To explore the potential biological functions of module genes, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed using the online Metscape database, and a corrected P-value < 0.05 was employed as the cut-off for defining statistical significance. Subsequently, we utilized the Cytoscape software (www.cytoscape.org) to construct a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network for genes within modules highly correlated with the phenotype, and clustered them based on gene function. Through assessing gene significance (GS) and module membership (MM) values, as well as the cosine distribution of gene expression, we have identified hub genes. The selected genes meet the criteria of GS > 0.5 and MM > 0.5, and their expression follows a cosine distribution. The determination of whether a gene meets the cosine distribution criteria is described in the Statistical analysis section.

Statistical Analysis

The differences in lipid metabolism-related biochemical indicators, as well as the number and average area of lipid droplets between day and night, were analyzed using a t-test with SAS software (SAS 9.2, SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC). The rhythmic patterns in the data of lipid metabolism-related biochemical indicators and hub gene expression were analyzed using the cosinor formula y = c +a cos [2 (t- ø)/24] in GraphPad Prism software. Here, c, a, and ø represent the mesor, amplitude, and phase of the cosine wave, respectively, while t denotes time in hours, R2 is the fitting degree. Statistically significant differences in gene rhythms were indicated by P < 0.05 and calculated using the website https://www.danielsoper.com/statcalc/calculator.aspx?id=15.

RESULTS

Diurnal Variations in Hepatic Lipid Metabolism

As shown in Figure 1, after 18 d of exposure to a 12:12-h light/dark cycle, the concentration of TC, GLU, FFA, ACC, FAS, and TG in the liver of chicken embryos were higher during the day and lower at night. Notably, the levels of TG, TC, FFA, and FAS exhibited significant day-night differences (P < 0.05) (Figure 1A). Cosinor analysis further confirmed that TG, TC, and FFA displayed significant circadian rhythms (P < 0.05) (Figure 1B). Moreover, TG was significantly positively correlated with TC (P < 0.05), while TC was highly significantly positively correlated with GLU (P < 0.01) (Table 1). In contrast, under complete darkness, no significant differences were observed in the concentrations of TG, FFA, TC, GLU, FAS, and ACC between day and night (P > 0.05) (Figure 2A). Cosinor analysis also indicated that these indicators did not exhibit significant rhythmic changes throughout the day-night cycle (P > 0.05) (Figure 2B). Furthermore, Oil red O staining results revealed that under a 12L:12D light/dark cycle, the number of lipid droplets at ZT4 during the day was significantly higher than at ZT16 during the night (P < 0.05), although the average lipid droplet area did not differ significantly (P > 0.05). Identification and Functional Annotation of Circadian Differentially Expressed Genes

Genes with an average FPKM ≥ 1 are considered expressed in chicken liver, totaling 13,770 genes. Gene expression in the liver varies among different times of day and night (Figure 4A). We found an average expression level correlation (Pearson r) of 0.96 for all biological replicates, with correlations between samples at different time points as high as 0.8 or more (Figure 4B). These results indicate that gene expression in the liver during day and night is highly conserved, and the specific expression of genes may be the genetic basis for regulating liver circadian physiological differences. The ZT0 and ZT12 time points represent the day-night transition, so we used liver samples from ZT4 and ZT8 as the daytime group, and samples from ZT16 and ZT20 as the nighttime group. We found that compared to daytime, 157 genes were significantly overexpressed at night, while 313 genes were significantly downregulated at night (P < 0.05, |fold change| > 1.5) (Supplementary Table S2). The differentially expressed genes (DEG) between daytime and nighttime are shown in the volcano plot (Figure 4C). GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis showed that those genes significantly upregulated at night were enriched in cellular response to cytokine stimulus, lipid biosynthetic process, PPAR signaling pathway, etc. (Figure 4D, Supplementary Table S3). Genes significantly downregulated at night were enriched in cell cycle and serotonin secretion and transport, etc. (Figure 4E, Supplementary Table S4). GSEA-GO analyses showed that all differentially expressed genes between day and night were significantly enriched in pathways such as regulation of lipid catabolic process, positive regulation of lipid kinase activity, regulation of circadian rhythm, response to light stimulus, positive regulation of the MAPK cascade, etc. (Figure 4F, Supplementary Table S5). Weighted Gene Co-Expression Network Construction and Module Detection

To investigate the key modules regulating lipid metabolism in chicken liver via the circadian clock, we constructed a weighted gene co-expression network. Based on a scale-free R2, the soft threshold value was set as 9 (Figure 5A). According to the dynamic tree cutting method, the minimum number of genes in each module was defined as 30, resulting in a total of 7 modules obtained, namely gray, blue, brown, yellow, green, red, black, pink, and turquoise (Figure 5B). We generated a heatmap depicting the expression relationship between modules, revealing that gene expression is relatively independent across modules (Figure 5C). Correlation analysis between module eigengenes and lipid metabolism-related phenotypes showed that the green module had a significant positive correlation with FFA (r = 0.56, P = 0.016), the blue module had a significant negative correlation with FAS (r = 0.56, P = 0.015), and the red module had a significant negative correlation with TG (r = −0.51, P = 0.029) (Figure 5D). Identifying Key Pathways and Regulators in Key Modules

We assessed the correlation between GS and MM within the 3 key modules. The blue module exhibited a correlation coefficient of 0.57 (P = 1.3e-30) (Figure 6A), the green module showed a coefficient of 0.53 (P = 1.3e-29) (Figure 6B), and the red module had a coefficient of 0.51 (P = 0.029) (Figure 6C). These findings underscore the vital role of gene regulation within these modules in relation to the corresponding phenotype. Functional annotation using GO and KEGG analyses revealed significant enrichment of the blue module in the cell cycle, phosphorylation, MAPK cascade, fatty acid biosynthesis, and lipid metabolism, etc. (Figure 6D, Supplementary Table S7). The green module was significantly enriched in the cell cycle, phosphorylation, lipoprotein biosynthesis etc. (Figure 6E, Supplementary Table S8). The red module showed significant enrichment in lipid biosynthesis process, MAPK cascade, negative regulation of phosphorylation, etc. (Figure 6F, Supplementary Table S9). Within the blue module network, the TOP2A gene demonstrated the highest connectivity, interacting directly with 43 other genes, with its node size significantly larger than others, suggesting that TOP2A is a key regulator of the FAS phenotype within this module (Figure 6G). In the green module network, the UBE2R2L and LOC431003 genes exhibited the highest connectivity, each connecting directly with 36 and 35 other genes, respectively, indicating they as key regulators of the FFA phenotype within this module (Figure 6H). Analysis of Protein Interaction Networks and Identification of Key Hub Genes

Using the STRING database, we constructed PPI networks for genes within the 3 key modules and clustered them based on their annotated functions (Figure 7). These findings reveal that the circadian clock regulates lipid metabolism diurnal homeostasis in chicken embryo liver by modulating circadian rhythm, response to light stimulus, cell cycle, lipid synthesis, phosphorylation, and positive regulation of the MAPK pathway. We identified genes with a GS greater than 0.5, MM greater than 0.5, and exhibiting diurnal expression patterns following a cosine rhythmic distribution as hub genes. Ten key hub genes were identified: PRKCD, PLK4, CLOCK, SRD5A3, BUB1B, SGO1, NDC80, NEK2, MCM4, and PLK1 (Supplementary S10). DISCUSSION

The circadian clock plays a crucial role in maintaining lipid metabolism homeostasis in mammalian livers (Frazier et al., 2023; Zhong et al., 2018). Currently, commercial egg incubation is conducted in almost complete darkness (Wang et al., 2020), while newly hatched chicks are exposed to nearly continuous light. Although light exposure decreases with age, this regimen neglects the circadian rhythm needs of the birds. Intensive light management aimed at enhancing production may exceed the chicken's circadian clock limits, leading to excessive fat accumulation, fatty liver disease and other metabolic diseases. Therefore, it is essential to investigate the regulatory role and mechanisms of the circadian clock in lipid metabolism in chicken livers. In our study, we observed significant diurnal variation in TG, TC, FFA, and FAS in chicken liver, with higher levels during the day and lower levels at night. Using RNA-seq and WGCNA, we systematically analyzed gene expression patterns associated with circadian regulation and lipid metabolism in chicken livers. This analysis revealed 3 key modules crucial for circadian control of hepatic lipid metabolism. Notably, we identified ten hub genes potentially bridging circadian regulation and lipid metabolic homeostasis. These findings highlight the regulatory role of the circadian clock in maintaining lipid metabolism homeostasis in the chicken liver and offer new insights into the underlying mechanisms, paving the way for novel approaches to alleviate lipid metabolic disorders in poultry.

In poultry, the liver serves as a primary site for de novo lipogenesis and regulates fat deposition (Yadav et al., 2024). Given the economic impact of poultry fat content, understanding the circadian clock's regulatory effects and molecular mechanisms on avian hepatic lipid metabolism is crucial. We selected the embryonic stage to investigate the circadian regulation mechanisms behind lipid metabolism homeostasis in the chicken liver for several key reasons. Firstly, it has been reported that circadian rhythms in chicks can be established during embryonic stage by exposing them to a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle, and this model has been successfully used to study human jet lag mechanisms (Kommedal et al., 2013). Our preliminary results also confirmed that under these conditions, core circadian clock genes in chicken embryos exhibit significant rhythmic changes over a 24-h cycle (Wang et al., 2021). Secondly, understanding the early mechanisms of fat deposition in chickens is crucial for developing early interventions that optimize developmental outcomes. Studying these mechanisms during the embryonic stage provides a foundational understanding for strategic early-stage interventions.

In this study, we observed that under 12L:12D cycles, lipid metabolism in the chicken embryo liver exhibited significant diurnal variations, whereas such phenomena were not observed under complete darkness. This highlights the crucial role of rhythmic light environments in regulating liver lipid metabolism. Specifically, in the 12L:12D light environment, significant day-night variations were found in the concentrations of TG, FFA, TC, and FAS, with TG, FFA, and TC displaying pronounced circadian rhythms. These findings underscore the key role of circadian rhythms in regulating lipid metabolism in the embryonic chicken liver. TG, a primary form of energy storage, exhibited higher concentrations during the day and lower concentrations at night. This pattern is consistent with studies in other species, which have shown that TG levels peak during the active period and decrease during the rest period (Adamovich et al., 2014; Wu et al., 2023). The observed variation in TG levels is likely associated with the need to store energy during periods of high activity and to mobilize energy reserves at night. FFA, key metabolic fuels, displayed diurnal fluctuations, with higher levels during the day and lower at night. This aligns with human and animal studies showing peak levels during active periods and lower levels during rest periods (Jackson et al., 2020; Plaizier et al., 2005). However, some studies have reported that FFA is also influenced by diet and metabolic state (Pendergrast et al., 2023; Shimizu et al., 2018), suggesting that external factors can impact their diurnal metabolic homeostasis. TC, an essential component for cell membrane synthesis and steroid hormone production, exhibits significant diurnal rhythms. Previous studies have suggested that daytime food intake and metabolic activity may promote cholesterol synthesis, while nighttime processes might enhance its breakdown and excretion (Lu et al., 2020), which aligns with our findings. Additionally, our study found significant positive correlations between TG and GLU with TC. This suggests that under circadian regulation, there may be a correlated regulatory relationship among glucose metabolism, energy storage, and cholesterol levels. FAS, a crucial enzyme in de novo fatty acid synthesis, exhibits higher expression during the day and lower at night, consistent with the diurnal trend of triglycerides. This aligns with studies showing that FAS and other lipid synthesis enzymes peak during active periods (Liu et al., 2024a). Overall, our findings provide new insights into the temporal regulation of hepatic lipid metabolism in chicks, consistent with previous studies but also highlighting the complexity of diurnal regulation in lipid metabolism. These results lay the groundwork for further research into the potential impacts of circadian rhythms on lipid metabolic disorders.

The mechanisms by which the circadian clock regulates the diurnal homeostasis of hepatic lipid metabolism are complex. Our study identified 157 genes with higher expression at night and 313 genes with higher expression during the day, significantly enriched in lipid metabolism pathways, as expected. WGCNA allows for in-depth mining of expression data and analysis of complex regulatory networks (Langfelder and Horvath, 2008). By focusing on the association between co-expression modules and traits of interest, we identified that the blue module is significantly associated with FAS, the green module with FFA, and the red module with TG. As anticipated, the genes within these modules were significantly enriched in the lipid synthesis process, demonstrating the accuracy of our module selection. Additionally, these genes overlapped with the enrichment results of diurnally differentially expressed genes in processes such as the cell cycle, response to light stimulus, circadian rhythm, phosphorylation, and regulation of MAPK cascades. Therefore, the genes involved in these biological processes are likely to play crucial roles in the circadian clock's regulation of lipid metabolism homeostasis.

Cell cycle plays a crucial role in regulating cell growth and division (Wu et al., 2024), and this process may be influenced by the circadian clock, thereby modulating cell proliferation and metabolic activity, which in turn affects lipid metabolism homeostasis. Our study identified 5 cell cycle pathway genes—SGO1, NDC80, MCM4, BUB1B, and PLK1—that may play important roles in the circadian regulation of liver lipid metabolism homeostasis. SGO1 is essential for accurate chromosome segregation during cell division. Haploinsufficiency of SGO1 can accelerate colorectal cancer progression primarily by activating lipid metabolism, Notch signaling, insulin signaling, and PPAR pathways (Rao et al., 2016). Dysregulation of SGO1 may alter genomic stability, leading to lipid metabolism disorders and disease progression. NDC80 is crucial for chromosome alignment and segregation during mitosis (Holmström et al., 2009). Previous studies have indicated that NDC80 is associated with metabolic processes that regulate human circadian rhythms (Polo et al., 2017). By influencing liver lipid metabolism, NDC80 can accelerate the progression of liver cancer or cirrhosis and is considered a biomarker for rehabilitation of hepatocellular carcinoma and cirrhosis (Jia et al., 2024; Su et al., 2021). It has also been identified as involved in the abnormal accumulation of lipids in mouse liver cells induced by chromate (Li et al., 2023). MCM4 is part of the DNA helicase complex essential for the initiation of DNA replication (Sun et al., 2024). Disruption of MCM4 rhythms has been linked to breast cancer and the regulation of the tumor immune microenvironment, and it may also affect lipid metabolism, contributing to atherosclerosis (Guo et al., 2024). BUB1B is a key player in the spindle assembly checkpoint, ensuring accurate chromosome segregation (Huang et al., 2023). The role of BUB1B in checkpoint control is related to metabolic homeostasis, and its disruption may lead to metabolic imbalance (Liu et al., 2024b). PLK1 is a serine/threonine-protein kinase involved in various stages of mitosis, including spindle formation and chromosome segregation. PLK1 has been shown to interact with metabolic pathways, affecting lipid metabolism and energy balance (Cheng et al., 2023). PLK1 can promote ABCA1 and ABCG1-dependent cholesterol efflux through the AMPK/PPARγ/LXRα pathway, thereby inhibiting macrophage lipid accumulation and alleviating atherosclerosis (Hu et al., 2022). In this study, we discovered for the first time that these 5 genes exhibit significant circadian rhythmic expression in normal chicken liver tissue, with higher expression during the day and lower expression at night. This finding reveals their potential role in circadian regulation and underscores their importance in lipid metabolism homeostasis. Given that circadian disruption is closely linked to cancer and various inflammatory diseases, understanding the circadian regulation mechanisms of these cell cycle-related genes and their relationship with lipid metabolism homeostasis provides new insights into their critical functions and enhances our understanding of the role of the circadian clock in disease development.

Our study also highlights several key genes and pathways that may be central mechanisms in the circadian regulation of hepatic lipid metabolism homeostasis. Specifically, these include the PRKCD gene in the response to light stimulus, the CLOCK gene regulating circadian rhythms, and the NEK2 and PLK4 genes involved in phosphorylation, as well as the SRD5A3 gene related to lipid biosynthesis process. A landmark article published in Cell in 2019 discovered that even if the circadian clock functions in all other tissues are abolished, the diurnal rhythm of metabolism in the liver can still directly sense and respond to light-dark cycles, maintaining normal operation. However, when the light-dark cycle is eliminated, leading to constant darkness or constant light, the liver's autonomous circadian clock function ceases as well (Koronowski et al., 2019). This indicates that the diurnal rhythm of liver metabolism is most directly and significantly influenced by the light environment. PRKCD is classified within the pathway that responds to light stimuli. Previous research has shown that the cleavage and activation of PKC-delta are crucial components of UV-induced apoptosis in human keratinocytes, and inactivation of PKC-delta can promote the survival of keratinocytes exposed to UV radiation (D'Costa and Denning 2005). This suggests that PRKCD plays a key role in responding to light stimuli, particularly UV radiation. Additionally, previous research has identified PRKCD as a target of escin for reducing obesity (Zhang et al., 2022). Elevated PRKCD gene expression in obese individuals has been associated with increased fasting blood glucose and circulating triglycerides, suggesting its potential as a target for metabolic syndrome treatment (Bezy et al., 2011). PRKCD regulates fat accumulation in liver cells by modulating IRS-1 serine phosphorylation and insulin-mediated signaling (Ghoshal et al., 2024). In our study, we observed significant diurnal variation in PRKCD expression in chicken liver, and through WGCNA analysis, we identified its potential role in lipid metabolism. This suggests PRKCD is a key gene linking light stimuli to the regulation of lipid metabolism. However, whether PRKCD responds to light stimuli in a similar manner in chicken embryonic liver and participates in regulating lipid metabolism requires further investigation. The CLOCK gene is a well-known regulator of circadian rhythms. Dysregulation of the CLOCK gene can lead to disruptions in circadian rhythms, which are associated with metabolic disorders and diseases. For instance, downregulation of CLOCK can impair endothelial cell autophagy and accelerate the formation of atherosclerotic plaques (Wang et al., 2023). Previous research has shown that the CLOCK gene interacts with the E-box enhancer elements in the promoter region of proline hydroxylase domain (PHD) proteins, affecting the interaction between HIF1α and the Cd36 promoter, thereby influencing fatty acid uptake (Pan et al., 2020). This suggests that CLOCK may be a core circadian gene essential for maintaining lipid metabolism homeostasis in the chicken embryonic liver. Phosphorylation events regulated by NEK2 and PLK4 are crucial for cell cycle progression and genomic stability (Jeon et al., 2024; Skinner et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2024). These kinases are involved in centrosome duplication and mitotic entry processes that are tightly regulated by the circadian clock (Jeon et al., 2024). The rhythmic expression pattern of NEK2 and PLK4 suggests their participation in coordinating cell cycle events with circadian rhythms, potentially impacting liver lipid metabolism. The SRD5A3 gene catalyzes the conversion of polyprenol to dolichol and is involved in the synthesis and metabolism of cholesterol and other sterols (Cantagrel et al., 2010). Our findings suggest that SRD5A3 play an important role in regulating the diurnal rhythm of lipid metabolism in the chicken embryonic liver, exhibiting a significant rhythmic expression pattern throughout the day. This indicates that SRD5A3 is a key effector gene in the circadian regulation of hepatic lipid metabolism homeostasis.

In summary, these findings underscore the circadian clock's pivotal role in maintaining chicken hepatic lipid metabolism homeostasis through diverse mechanisms encompassing light responsiveness, core circadian regulatory factors, phosphorylation events, and lipid biosynthesis. Crucially, pivotal genes like PRKCD, CLOCK, NEK2, etc. participate in these processes, offering novel insights into the molecular mechanisms governing circadian regulation of lipid metabolism in chicken liver. Given the critical roles of these hub genes, we propose that neglecting the circadian clock in poultry could lead to metabolic-related diseases and impact poultry health. To promote the health and sustainability of the poultry industry, we advocate for attention to maintaining circadian clock stability. Future research should validate the expression patterns and variations of these hub genes across different breeds, growth stages, and rearing conditions, and elucidate the specific mechanisms by which these hub genes interact with the circadian clock to regulate hepatic lipid metabolism homeostasis. The investigation in our study will establish theoretical foundations and molecular targets for adjusting light environments and optimizing the circadian rhythm to mitigate excessive fat deposition and circadian-related metabolic disorders in chickens. Such research carries significant implications for advancing the health and efficiency of the poultry industry.
