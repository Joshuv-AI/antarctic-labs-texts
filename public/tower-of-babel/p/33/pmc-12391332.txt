Subcutaneous interstitial pressure measurement during early septic shock: an exploratory study
Authors: Auguste Dargent, Isabelle Fournel, Marine Jacquier, Hugo Dumargne, Laurent Argaud, Jean-Pierre Quenot
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractFluid therapy is crucial in managing septic shock but may result in harmful fluid overload due to capillary leakage, causing interstitial fluid accumulation. Burns and endotoxemia models demonstrate that interstitium can reduce its hydrostatic pressure, increasing permeability, but this has not been clinically investigated in human sepsis. This study aimed to examine the changes in subcutaneous interstitial pressure (SIP) during sepsis. This prospective observational study involved adult patients admitted to the Intensive Care Unit (ICU) within 24 h, under sedation and mechanical ventilation, and who received < 50 ml/kg resuscitation. The septic shock group met the septic shock criteria, whereas the control group lacked sepsis or shock criteria (admission for non-traumatic coma). The SIP was measured using a subcutaneous transducer-tip probe. SIP was measured in 30 patients and was not significantly lower in septic shock: 1.69 ± 2.99 vs. 2.51 ± 2.39 mmHg in controls (p = 0.410). Among the 6 patients with a negative SIP, all but one were in the septic shock group, although the absolute difference was not significant (5 (33%) vs. 1 (7%), p = 0.169). Subcutaneous interstitial pressure was not significantly lower in septic shock patients, but sub-atmospheric SIP was more prevalent in this group, despite higher fluid balance. Further studies are required to clarify the role of interstitial pressure in capillary leakage during sepsis.Keywords: Septic shock, Sepsis, Fluid resuscitation, Interstitium, Interstitial pressureSubject terms: Cardiovascular biology, Infectious diseases, Bacterial infection, Infection, Inflammation

Introduction

Cardiocirculatory collapse of septic shock is induced by generalized vasodilation and capillary leakage, that is, leakage of fluid from the vascular compartment to the interstitium1,2. The short-lived efficacy of intravenous fluids may be a direct consequence of capillary leakage3,4. Thus, capillary leakage is paradoxically maintained and even aggravated by fluid replacement therapy, as it decreases colloid osmotic pressure and causes further alterations in the endothelial glycocalyx5. Fluid overload is associated with prolonged mechanical ventilation duration6 and decreased survival7 and has been identified as an independent risk factor for mortality during sepsis8,9.

The increase in transcapillary flow (transfer of fluid between the vascular and interstitial compartments) is governed by the so-called “Starling forces”: the pressure gradient (both hydrostatic and colloid osmotic) between the capillary lumen and the interstitium, and a coefficient of endothelial permeability10,11. The latter is classically considered to be responsible for the increase in capillary filtration during sepsis, where alterations in the capillary wall are well documented2. However, on the other side of the endothelial wall, the interstitium plays an important role in the capillary leakage. Recently, a study described for the first time in detail the architecture of this little-known tissue, which plays a critical role in all organs12. We recently reviewed the multiple roles that the interstitium may play in the pathophysiology of sepsis, interacting with capillary leak, edema, fluid overload, and organ dysfunction13. In this picture, a major contribution of the interstitium to shock may be its capacity to decrease its hydrostatic pressure.

This was first observed in burns, where the predicted permeability was much lower than that predicted by the filtration rate, implying that the pressure gradient was involved14. Lund et al. measured pore pressure in an experimental model of rat paw burns and found that it decreased from − 1 (basal) to -150 mmHg15 as measured after cardiac arrest (to stop the filtration flow and measure the full extent of pressure change). This finding was replicated in numerous studies16,17 and introduced the active suction-like role of the interstitial tissue in transcapillary filtration and edema formation during tissue inflammation. Subsequently, the same phenomenon was found in models of systemic inflammation, such as in an anaphylactic rat model18 with a -10 mmHg decrease in the Pi of the subcutaneous interstitial tissue, and in one model of endotoxemia19.

The mechanisms underlying this phenomenon have been well established. The expansion of hyaluronic gel in the extracellular matrix is restricted by the collagen fibrillar network, which interacts with interstitial cells (fibroblasts) via ß1 integrin receptors. Thus, fibroblasts can contract a collagen gel ex vivo up to 10% of its initial volume in 24 h20. Therefore, the decrease in interstitial pressure observed with inflammation is due to the relaxation of this cellular and fibrillar network, experimentally reproduced by anti-ß1-integrin antibodies21.

Quantitatively, the relative participation of decreased interstitial pressure and increased endothelial permeability in transcapillary flow during septic shock remains unclear. However, the revised Starling equation predicts that transcapillary flow is mainly driven by the filtration gradient (hydrostatic, “driving” pressure difference between intravascular and interstitial compartments)10.

The subcutaneous interstitium is quantitatively the main reservoir for capillary leakage22 because the skin is the largest organ in the body with the highest proportion of interstitial fluid15 and secondly, because the subcutaneous tissue has a large, very compliant interstitial space (i.e. it accepts a large volume with a small increase in pressure). Subcutaneous edema was specifically evaluated (using clinical scores and ultrasound) in an observational study that confirmed the association between subcutaneous edema and mortality in the ICU23. In addition, the pressure control mechanism described above, which leads to interstitial fluid suction, was mainly described in the subcutaneous interstitial tissue15,24.

The present study aimed at: assessing feasibility of subcutaneous interstitial pressure measurement using transducer-tipped probes; and exploring the changes in subcutaneous interstitial pressure during early septic shock in humans and its relationship with the extent of fluid overload. Thus, the objectives of this study were: (i) to compare the subcutaneous interstitial pressure between patients with and without septic shock, and (ii) to explore the association between SIP and other endpoints (central venous pressure (CVP), fluid balance, sequential organ failure assessment (SOFA), lactate, vital status at ICU discharge).

Patients and methods

Design

This was a prospective observational study that included patients admitted to intensive care for less than 24 h and mechanically ventilated. Patients were separated in two distinct groups: patients with septic shock in the early phase and patients without sepsis.

Ethical approval

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Central Ethics Committee (Ethics Committee Ile de France II, Paris, France, December 2018, Clinicaltrials.gov n° NCT03818269). All the participants and/or their relatives provided informed consent.

Sample size Estimation

The interstitial pressure range was expected to be between − 5 and − 1 mmHg in patients with septic shock and between − 2 and + 2 mmHg in controls. A study of healthy subjects with different hydration states found a standard deviation of 1 mmHg18. Considering a standard deviation of ± 2 mmHg, a hypothesized pressure difference of ≥ 2 mmHg between groups, an alpha risk of 5%, and a power of 90%, we estimated that 22 analyzable patients were required in each group.

Patient selection

All patients meeting the following criteria were eligible for both groups: age > 18 years, ICU admission for less than 24 h, ongoing invasive mechanical ventilation with orotracheal intubation, absence of clinically detectable edema (according to a validated score25), and affiliation to healthcare insurance.

In the septic shock arm, additional criteria included diagnosis of septic shock according to the definition of the Sepsis-3 consensus conference26 and total crystalloid resuscitation < 50 ml/kg.

In the control arm, additional criteria were absence of sepsis and shock from any cause (i.e. systolic arterial pressure > 100 mmHg, absence of vasopressors, diuresis > 0.5 ml/kg/h, normal serum lactate), and crystalloid resuscitation < 50 ml/kg over the previous 12 h.

The main exclusion criteria for both groups were clinical disseminated intravascular coagulation (purpura or bleeding), admission after resuscitated cardiac arrest, cardiogenic shock, acute pancreatitis, severe dehydration (clinical signs of dehydration and/or sodium level > 150 mmol/L), metformin poisoning, sepsis or septic shock for > 24 h, moribund or death appearing imminent (within 24 h), and legal protection.

Outcomes

Primary endpoint and interstitial pressure measurement

The primary endpoint was the measurement of subcutaneous interstitial pressure (SIP) as early as possible after ICU admission (D1). All measurements were performed by a single operator (AD).

Subcutaneous interstitial pressure was measured on the leg at the same location in all patients, 2–3 cm externally from the tibial crest and 5 cm below the anterior tibial tuberosity. An anaesthetic patch (Emlapatch) was applied before each measurement. SIP was measured using a sterile, single-use, dedicated transducer-tipped pressure probe (Micro-Cath ®, Millar Inc., USA) with a 3.5 French diameter. The pressure probe was inserted subcutaneously through a 30 mm, 16G venous catheter. The catheter was then removed from the skin (along the probe length), and the probe was finally removed by 1 cm to allow space around the probe and fixed using an adhesive transparent dressing. The probe was connected to a monitor (Powerlab®, AD Instruments, New Zealand), and the data were recorded using dedicated software (Labchart®, AD Instruments, New Zealand). The probe was zeroed in saline at room temperature prior to its introduction. SIP was measured after a stabilization phase of 20–30 min, averaged over 5 min. After probe removal, pressure readings were used to correct the measurement drift. In a subgroup of patients, the catheter was left for 24 h to measure temporal changes in the SIP.

Prospective reporting of bleeding and hematoma at the insertion site was planned (as a potential confounding factor for SIP leading to falsely increased measurements).

Ischemic interstitial pressure

Experimentally, the best evaluation of interstitial pressure is provided when transcapillary and lymphatic flows are stopped (e.g., after cardiac arrest in experimental studies). Thus, in a subset of patients, we evaluated the effect of interrupting blood flow on SIP using a tourniquet for a short period. A large blood pressure cuff was placed above the knee and rapidly inflated to 180–190 mmHg (or > 20 mmHg above the systolic blood pressure) for 5 min. The inflation and deflation times were reported in the pressure recording.

Secondary endpoints

1) CVP and perfusion pressure were measured simultaneously with SIP. When central venous access was not available, the CVP was approximated using inferior vena cava ultrasonography.

2) Daily and cumulated fluid balance (day 1 to 3).

Assessed daily by the difference in 24-hour fluid intake and diuresis (or losses by renal replacement therapy).

3) Edema, and stage of severity (day 1 to 3).

A validated edema score was used25 ranging from 0 (no edema) to 4 (severe edema). The maximum severity score observed during the study period was used.

4) SOFA at admission and day 3.

5) Lactate level at admission.

6) ICU mortality.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative variables were described in each group (septic shock/control) as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median [Q1-Q3] according to distribution, and compared using Student’s t-test or non-parametric test as appropriate. Qualitative variables were described in each group as n (%) and compared using Chi2 test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Subcutaneous interstitial pressure was described in each group (septic shock/control) with mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median [Q1-Q3] according to distribution and compared between groups using Student’s t-test or non-parametric tests, as appropriate, on the whole population. A sensitivity analysis was conducted after excluding patients with hematoma at the insertion site, as it could affect the measurement of SIP.

The correlation between subcutaneous interstitial pressure and quantitative outcomes was assessed using the Spearman correlation coefficient.

Results

Thirty-one patients were included in the study: 16 in the septic shock group and 15 in the control group. Inclusions were stopped earlier than planned because of recruitment difficulties. One patient in the septic shock group was enrolled after receiving more than 50 ml/kg of fluids. and was excluded from analyses, leaving 30 analyzable patients. Age, sex, medical history, and SAPS II at baseline did not significantly differ between the groups, whereas SOFA score was significantly higher in patients with septic shock (Table 1). The delay between ICU admission and SIP measurement was 15.2 ± 8.7 h and 12.7 ± 7.8 h in the septic shock and control groups, respectively (p = 0.414). The distribution of sepsis origins is shown in Table 1 (mostly pneumonia). Patients in the control arm were primarily admitted for non-traumatic coma, mostly due to acute poisoning (predominantly benzodiazepines) and acute cerebrovascular disease. Patients in the septic shock group had a higher fluid balance at the time of measurement (1940 [1350 ; 3150] vs. -95 [-500 ; 400], Table 1) and a lower mean arterial pressure (MAP); however, CVP was similar between the groups (Table 1). Albumin levels were significantly lower in patients with septic shock (Table 1).

Early subcutaneous interstitial pressure (SIP)

The SIP at baseline was measured in 30 patients. Figure 1 shows typical pressure tracing during the first hour after insertion. Usually, the pressure reading was erratic in the first few minutes but consistently stabilized over a half-hour period, which is why we waited for at least 30 min and a stable signal to average the pressure reading. SIP did not significantly differ between septic shock and control patients: 1.69 ± 2.99 versus 2.51 ± 2.39 mmHg, respectively (p = 0.410). Hemorrhage was present at the probe insertion site in three patients, all with septic shock and overt hematoma. After excluding these patients, the SIP in the septic shock group was 1.20 ± 3.13, which was not significantly different from the SIP in the control group (2.51 ± 2.39), p = 0.228 (Fig. 2). Six patients had a strictly negative SIP: 5 (33%) in the septic shock group and 1 (7%) in the control group (p = 0.169).

Ischemic interstitial pressure

Ischemic SIP was measured in a subset of 16 patients, and the effect of interrupting blood flow in the leg with a blood pressure cuff was inconsistent between patients. The interstitial pressure either increased or decreased during the ischemic period and rapidly returned to its original value afterward. The difference between SIP and ischemic SIP was − 0.08 ± 1.05 in septic shock patients versus − 0.32 ± 2.14 mmHg in control patients.

The median difference between CVP and SIP was not significantly different between septic shock and controls: 6.46 [4.20–8.86] versus 4.87 [3.70–8.78] mmHg, respectively (p = 0.528).

Evolution of interstitial pressure over 24 h

The pressure probe was left at the measurement site for 24 h in a subset of 12 patients: 9 patients with septic shock and 3 controls. There was no apparent correlation between the pressure difference (day-2 minus day-1) and the corresponding fluid balance (r = 0.175, p = 0.587).

SIP and other clinical endpoints

The description of management and clinical evolution according to the group is shown in Table 2. SIP was not correlated with SOFA score at D3 (r=-0.08, p = 0.698), lactate level at D1 (r=-0.160, p = 0.398), or day 1 and day 3 cumulative fluid balances (r=-0.08, p = 0.689 and r=-0.161, p = 0.394, respectively). Eight patients died in the ICU (four with septic shock and four controls). Vital status at ICU discharge was not significantly associated with SIP at baseline (2.12 [0-3.8] in ICU survivors versus 1.48 [1.08–4.47] in non-survivors, p = 0.707). In patients with septic shock, SIP at baseline was 0.42 [-1.86; 2.6] in ICU survivors, and 4.47 [2.14;6.12] in non-survivors.

Discussion

In this exploratory study, we did not find a significant difference in subcutaneous interstitial pressure between patients in the early phase of septic shock and control sedated patients without sepsis or shock.

Although SIP was not significantly lower in patients with septic shock, the absolute difference between the groups trended in the direction predicted by the research hypothesis (i.e., the average SIP was lower in patients with septic shock). The study was underpowered as we did not reach the planned sample size due to recruitment difficulties. Moreover, the observed difference between groups was lower than expected, and we estimated from the data observed in the present study that 172 patients in each group would be required to obtain an 80% power to show a statistically significant difference. Thus, our study failed to reproduce the results of experimental studies in which the lowering of SIP was measured after endotoxemia or anaphylaxis19,27 thereby questioning the involvement of this phenomenon in human sepsis. However, we observed that sub-atmospheric SIP was prevalent in septic shock patients, but almost never occurred in non-septic patients (Fig. 2). This finding supports the reality of sepsis-induced lowering of interstitial pressure in human sepsis.

One of the objectives of this study was to assess the feasibility of SIP measurement in patients using transducer-tipped probes. Unfortunately, accurate measurement of SIP is complex, with many confounding factors, especially in the clinical setting. The first and main factor is that the interstitium is not an isolated compartment; that is, it is irrigated by a permanent flow, with inflow from the vascular compartment (transcapillary flow) and outflow to the lymphatic vessels, which is a major and often overlooked determinant of edema formation28. Interstitial pressure itself is a major factor driving lymphatic flow29. Ideally, interstitial pressure should be interpreted along with interstitial flow (i.e., transcapillary flow and lymphatic flow), which is unfortunately impossible in the clinical setting. However, systemic lymphatic flow can be assessed non-invasively using a volume kinetic method30 which could bring very interesting insight to our results. To measure the full magnitude of the force developed by the extracellular matrix, circulation would ideally have to be stopped, as in experimental studies (-95 to -135 mmHg in burn edema31, because the measured pressure is dependent on the availability of fluid from the circulation.

In the clinical setting of our study, other factors may have hindered the observation of decreased SIP during sepsis. First, although SIP measurement was performed as soon as possible, the delay between the true onset of sepsis and ICU admission was several hours, even with the best possible care. During this time, interstitial pressure may have increased back to its initial level after fluid from the vascular compartment was drawn from the circulation. Furthermore, patients with septic shock received a large amount of fluid from the beginning of shock before SIP was measured. Thus, the excess of fluids received by septic shock patients compared to control patients may be a confounding factor, as it should have increased SIP, as demonstrated by the recent modeling of the pressure-volume relationship of the extracellular matrix32. Yet, we did not find a clear correlation between SIP and the amount of fluids received (or the elapsed time since hypotension) in septic patients, although many confounding factors exist…Applying a correction factor according to the level of hydration may have changed our results; however, we are not aware of an available method to correct the interstitial pressure. In addition, the hydration status of the subcutaneous tissue (i.e., extracellular water content) was not appropriately evaluated in our study. Finally, technical issues were encountered with the pressure probe. In particular, some level of bleeding was observed in some patients, resulting in high pressure readings and requiring new placement of the probe. In some cases, we suspected that covert bleeding might have been present, leading to overestimated results. Our technique using miniaturized transducer-tipped pressure sensors was previously described33 for interstitial pressure measurement in tumors but is subject to the bias of insertion trauma. The reference method remains the wick-in-needle34 or implanted capsule technique described by Guyton35 which is unfortunately not usable in modern clinical settings.

To circumvent the issue of transcapillary flow and reproduce experimental results, we tried to stop circulation locally by inflating a blood pressure cuff for a few minutes. Unfortunately, the results were inconsistent, and the pressure increased or decreased regardless of the patient group. We think this may be because progressively increasing pressure in the cuff occluded the veins first, which favored congestion in some patients. Thus, the overall pressure increase was explained by dilated venules bulging in the interstitium and/or increasing transcapillary flow induced by increased capillary pressure (and the exclusion of lymphatic drainage).

Our study had several strengths. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate interstitial pressure during sepsis. The prospective cohort design was appropriate, and major potential confounding factors (such as age and sex) were well balanced between the groups. Fluid balance was carefully recorded and reported in this study, as it has a major influence on interstitial hydration and interstitial fluid pressure.

However, our study had some limitations, and this exploratory study was underpowered to detect a statistically significant difference in the primary outcome. Moreover, the small sample size did not enable to study the effect of SIP on outcomes with adjustment for confounding factors. Second, the technique used for measurement has not been properly validated, although it is the only feasible bedside approach to date. Some of the variance in the results may have originated from insertion trauma with local inflammation and/or bleeding. Finally, a longer follow-up of pressure measurements would have been interesting, especially to describe its correlation with fluid balance.

Conclusion

In this study, interstitial pressure was measured for the first time in patients with early septic shock and compared with that in non-septic critically ill patients. Subcutaneous interstitial pressure was not significantly lower in patients with septic shock; however, we observed prevalent sub-atmospheric pressure in the septic shock group. Further studies are required to determine the role of interstitial pressure in increasing the capillary leakage during sepsis.
