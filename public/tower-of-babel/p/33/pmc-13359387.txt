Intravenous Dihydroxyacetone as Adjunctive Therapy in Aluminum Phosphide Poisoning: A Case Series
Authors: Hossein Niknahad, Reza Heidari, Leila Hosseini, Shayan Yazdani, Hanif Hashemnejad, Mohammadreza Rahimi Majd
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn three patients with aluminum phosphide poisoning, intravenous dihydroxyacetone (DHA) was added to standard supportive care. Improvement in metabolic acidosis and hemodynamic status was temporally associated with DHA administration. Controlled studies are needed to determine efficacy and safety.Keywords: acute toxicity, dihydroxyacetone, emergency medicine, pharmacotherapy, phosphine

Introduction

Aluminum phosphide (AlP) poisoning is associated with high mortality (up to ~85%) and remains a major clinical problem in many agricultural regions, including Iran, India, Pakistan, and Turkey [1, 2, 3, 4].

Mitochondrial toxicity is central to AlP poisoning. Phosphine inhibits cytochrome c oxidase in the electron transport chain, impairing ATP production, inducing oxidative stress, and causing cell death [4, 5, 6]. The early course of severe AlP poisoning is dominated by metabolic acidosis, cardiovascular collapse, and cellular energy failure [4, 7, 8]. In the absence of a specific antidote, adjunctive strategies that support mitochondrial function and ATP generation are of clinical interest.

Dihydroxyacetone (DHA) is a carbohydrate intermediate in cellular energy metabolism [9]. DHA has been reported to react with phosphine in vitro and to counteract mitochondrial respiratory blockade [9, 10, 11]. DHA is phosphorylated to dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP), entering glycolysis to generate ATP, and also supports NADH production to fuel oxidative phosphorylation [12]. Therefore, DHA has been explored as a metabolic adjunct in conditions associated with mitochondrial dysfunction [10, 11].

Independent studies have evaluated DHA in AlP poisoning. In a rat model, DHA prevented hemodynamic and ECG abnormalities and was associated with complete survival [13]. A clinical report described two severe AlP cases treated with oral DHA as an adjunct to supportive care, with subsequent improvement in metabolic and clinical parameters [14].

In the absence of an established parenteral DHA regimen, we used an institutional 5% (w/v) intravenous formulation as an investigational adjunct to standard supportive care in three patients with severe AlP poisoning. This report describes the temporal clinical and laboratory trends observed during hospitalization.

Methods

Three patients with AlP ingestion were managed at Imam Hassan Hospital (Bojnord, Iran). Diagnosis was clinical, based on reported ingestion and corroborating evidence (tablet remnants). Chemical confirmation was not performed. Data extracted from medical records included demographics, exposure history (tablet number, time from ingestion), serial vital signs, laboratory parameters, VBG analyses, and echocardiography when available. Clinical severity was defined as marked metabolic acidosis (pH < 7.2) and/or hemodynamic instability requiring vasopressor support. Primary outcome variables were serial VBG indices (pH, bicarbonate, PvCO2, PvO2) and hemodynamic trends.

All patients received the institution's routine supportive protocol for AlP poisoning (Figure 2). Intravenous DHA (5% w/v, 500 mL/bag) was added as an adjunctive therapy using an institutional formulation (Shiraz Serum Pharmaceutical Company, Iran) under local oversight [7]. There is no commercially available parenteral DHA product approved for this indication. The solution characteristics were pH 7, iso‐osmotic, and stable for 3 years at 4°C–8°C.

DHA was added as an adjunct when severe metabolic acidosis (pH < 7.2) and/or hemodynamic instability persisted despite initial supportive care. In the absence of established dosing guidelines, dosing was not weight‐based. The 5% concentration reflected the available institutional formulation. Administration (volume, duration, repetition) was individualized based on clinical response and is reported per case.

Serial VBG parameters (pH, HCO3 −, PvCO2, PvO2), hemodynamic status, and echocardiography (when available) were monitored (Table 1, Figure 1). Follow‐up findings are reported per case. Ethical approval was obtained. DHA was used on an emergency compassionate basis under institutional oversight (not a registered trial). Informed consent was obtained from patients or legal surrogates when feasible.

Case Presentation

Case 1

A 21‐year‐old man with AlP poisoning (the authenticity of the tablets was confirmed) was referred to Imam Hassan Hospital (Bojnord, Iran) about 2 h after ingesting two AlP pills. Serum biochemical analysis revealed elevated BUN (30 mg/dL; reference = 6–21 mg/dL), hypokalemia (3 mEq/L; reference = 3.5–5.5 mEq/L), high PT and INR (15.7 s and 1.25 respectively; reference = 11–13.5 s for PT and 0.8–1.1 for INR), and thrombocytopenia (platelet count = 85 × 109/l; reference = 150–400 × 109/l) (Table 1). At the time of the presentation, the heart rate was 100, the BP was 110/70, and the ECG was normal. However, the echocardiography revealed an EF = 15%–20% with severe LV and RV failure. The VBG analysis showed metabolic acidosis (pH = 7.25; reference = 7.35–7.45), low bicarbonate level (20.8 mEq/L; reference = 22–26 mEq/L), PvCO2 = 49 mmHg (reference = 41–51 mmHg), and very low PvO2 (18 mmHg, reference = 30–40 mmHg) (Figure 1). The routine hospital protocol for managing AlP poisoning was started (Figure 2). The patient also started receiving intravenous DHA (5% w/v, 500 mL per bag) approximately 3 h and 30 min after admission. The total DHA administered for this patient was fifteen 500‐mL bags (total volume = 7500 mL of 5% DHA). The first 500 mL bag was infused over 60 min, and subsequent bags were infused as 500 mL over 3 h, as documented in the medical record. The EF reached approximately 25% during patient treatment, and the signs of LV and RV failure disappeared. During the DHA infusion, the blood pH reached 7.26 at 4 h after the start of DHA infusion and 7.3 at 12 h after DHA infusion. The DHA infusion was continued for 24 h after its start, and the pH reached 7.33. Meanwhile, the bicarbonate level, PvCO2, and PvO2, as monitored indicators of clinical course, were 25 meq/L, 44 mmHg, and 46 mmHg, respectively, 24 h after starting DHA therapy for this patient. Moreover, complications such as thrombocytopenia, increased blood coagulation rate, and hypokalemia also began to normalize 24 h after patient admission, and the BUN level fell to 25 mg/dL (Table 1). When DHA administration was stopped, the patient's condition deteriorated; therefore, DHA therapy was continued on the second day (48 h after admission), and blood pH, bicarbonate level, PvCO2, and PvO2 reached 7.46, 28 meq/L, 44 mmHg, and 47 mmHg, respectively (Figure 1). Serial measurements showed improvement in acid–base status over time, with higher pH and bicarbonate values and increased PvO2 alongside lower PvCO2, as illustrated in Figure 1. After 72 h of patient admission, the EF was 40%, and the blood biochemistry and VBG became normal (Table 1 and Figure 1). Finally, the patient was discharged with normal cardiovascular signs, an ejection fraction (EF) of 50%, and normal blood biochemistry 5 days after admission. According to the available follow‐up notes, the patient had no cardiovascular events, electrolyte imbalances, or serum biomarkers of organ injury at subsequent visits.

Case 2

A 24‐year‐old woman was admitted to the emergency department of Imam Hassan Hospital (Bojnord, Iran) with abdominal pain, nausea, and vomiting. Cardiovascular symptoms included normal blood pressure (120/80 mmHg) and tachycardia (105 bpm). The ECG was normal, and the EF was 55%. Acidosis (pH = 7.2; Reference = 7.35–7.45), low bicarbonate level (HCO3 = 13.2; Reference = 22–26 meq/L), low PvO2 (PO2 = 25; Reference = 30–40 mmHg), and a PvCO2 = 49.47 (reference = 41–51 mmHg) were also detected in the VBG analysis of the patient (Figure 1 and Table 1). A medical history examination revealed that she had digested ¼ of an aluminum phosphide (AlP) tablet (a sample of the remaining tablet was evaluated and confirmed to be AlP). The patient was admitted about 2.5 h after AlP digestion. Along with the routine hospital protocols for managing AlP poisoning (Figure 2), intravenous DHA (5% w/v; 500 mL per bag) was started about 4 h after admission, as acidosis (pH = 7.25; Reference = 7.35–7.45) and bicarbonate levels (11.8 meq/L, Reference = 22–26 meq/L) persisted and the bicarbonate level had declined further despite initial supportive management. The patient received three 500‐mL bags of 5% DHA at approximately 2–3‐h intervals (total volume = 1500 mL). All parameters mentioned in Table 1 and Figure 1 were monitored during DHA therapy. 4 h after the first DHA administration, blood pH was 7.38, and bicarbonate level was 18 meq/L (Reference: 15–26 meq/L). About 12 h after the first DHA administration, blood parameters (Table 1), including pH (7.4) and bicarbonate levels (22 meq/L), were in the normal range. After 3 days of hospitalization, the patient was discharged with VBG parameters and key laboratory indices within reference ranges, as summarized in Figure 1 and Table 1. According to the available follow‐up notes, no cardiovascular events, electrolyte imbalances, or laboratory abnormalities suggestive of organ injury were documented at subsequent visits.

Case 3

A 19‐year‐old man was admitted to Imam Hassan Hospital (Bojnord, Iran). It was found that the patient ingested two AlP tablets. 2 h after AlP ingestion, the patient was admitted to the emergency department. On initial examination, BP was 100/80, HR was 95, and the ECG was normal. The echocardiography data also revealed an ejection fraction (EF) of 35%–40% at patient admission. Serum biochemical analysis showed elevated BUN (37 mg/dL; reference range = 6–21 mg/dL) without other significant changes (Table 1). Blood pH was 6.83 (Reference = 7.35–7.45), serum bicarbonate 15.5 meq/L (Reference = 22–26 meq/L), and the VBG showed PvCO2 = 21.9 (Reference range = 41–51 mmHg) and PvO2 = 39.8 (Reference range = 30–40 mmHg) (Figure 1). The routine protocols for AlP intoxication were started (Figure 2). On the second day (24 h after the start of routine AlP poisoning treatment), the patient had sinus tachycardia and an EF = 50%. The VBG analysis also revealed severe metabolic acidosis (pH = 6.7; reference = 7.35–7.45), PvO2 = 36 mmHg (reference = 30–40 mmHg), and PvCO2 = 36 mmHg (reference = 41–51 mmHg). Therefore, intravenous DHA (5% w/v; 500 mL per bag) was started about 19 h after admission. A total of five 500‐mL bags of 5% DHA were administered at approximately 2–3‐h intervals (total volume = 2500 mL). Key laboratory indices were followed during DHA infusion (Table 1), and the VBG findings were pH = 7.38 (reference = 7.35–7.45), PvO2 = 48 mmHg (reference = 30–40 mmHg), and PvCO2 = 33 mmHg (reference = 41–51 mmHg) after DHA administration (Figure 1). The blood biochemistry was normal on day three, and the VBG parameters came into the reference range (Table 1 and Figure 1). According to the available follow‐up notes, no cardiovascular or CNS events and no serum biomarkers of organ injury were documented at subsequent visits.

Discussion

In this case series, three AlP‐poisoned patients receiving intravenous DHA as an adjunct to supportive care showed temporal improvement in metabolic acidosis, VBG parameters, and hemodynamic status, with survival to discharge.

No specific antidote exists for AlP poisoning, and management remains predominantly supportive [15]. Several adjunctive strategies have been explored, including hyperinsulinemia‐euglycemia therapy (HIET) [16, 17] and ECMO for refractory shock [18, 19], but none are universally accepted. N‐acetylcysteine (NAC) has shown potential benefit in randomized trials but requires further confirmation [20]. Melatonin has been investigated mainly in experimental models with reported mitigation of oxidative injury [21]. Coenzyme Q10, alone or with NAC, has been studied as an antioxidant/mitochondrial support approach [22]. Other antioxidants (e.g., vitamin C) and bicarbonate‐based correction of acidosis are part of supportive care, but high‐quality comparative evidence remains limited [23].

DHA has been shown to support mitochondrial function and ATP production in experimental models [9, 10, 24]. Independent studies have reported that DHA mitigated hemodynamic abnormalities in a rat model of AlP poisoning [13] and was associated with clinical improvement in two oral DHA cases [14]. Our prior experience with intravenous DHA in AlP poisoning also suggested stabilization of hemodynamics and improvement in metabolic parameters [7]. The findings of the current study are consistent with these reports (Table 1; Figure 1).

The observed clinical course may be compatible with a metabolic/mitochondrial rationale. However, direct phosphine binding in humans has not been established, and DHA should be interpreted as an investigational adjunct, not a proven antidote. DHA infusion was associated with normalized pH, bicarbonate, and VBG parameters in our patients.

Conclusion

The use of DHA in these three cases provides preliminary clinical observations in AlP poisoning, particularly in patients with severe metabolic disturbances. Based on the proposed mitochondrial/metabolic rationale, DHA may warrant further evaluation as an adjunct to standard supportive care, but controlled clinical studies are needed to define efficacy, dosing strategies, and safety before it can be recommended beyond investigational use.

Limitations

This report has several limitations inherent to a small case series. The absence of a control group and the limited sample size do not allow causal inference regarding DHA. Chemical confirmation of phosphine exposure was not performed, and serum lactate was not routinely measured. Echocardiography and some laboratory parameters were not uniformly available across all time points, reflecting routine practice in a pragmatic emergency setting. Finally, because parenteral DHA is not part of routine management, broader conclusions regarding optimal dosing, safety, and effectiveness require larger controlled studies.
