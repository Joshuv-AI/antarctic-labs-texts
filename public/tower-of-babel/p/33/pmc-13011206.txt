NRF2 deficiency impairs proliferation and survival of chicken primordial germ cells via oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction and apoptosis
Authors: Ying-Jie Niu, Gaozhan Yuan, Wenjie Ren, Jun Wu, Guangzheng Liu, Mingyang Zou, Xinrui Wang, Yixin Diao, Nan Zhang, Guohui Li, Wei Han, Xiang-Shun Cui, Guohong Chen, Bichun Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In poultry, primordial germ cells (PGCs) play a crucial role in preserving and manipulating genetic resources for animal production. PGCs face challenges from oxidative stress during in vitro culture and manipulation processes. While the NRF2 pathway is known to centralize cellular homeostasis and stress responses in various cell types, its role in in vitro cultured PGCs remains poorly understood. This study investigates the impact of NRF2 inhibition on PGC proliferation, cellular basal characteristics, and potential underlying mechanisms. All experiments were performed with at least three biological replicates. The results showed that treatment with 24 μM ML385 significantly reduced PGC numbers after 3 d of culture compared to controls (2.83 ± 0.04 vs. 1.77 ± 0.06, × 105 cells), accompanied by a decrease in EdU-positive cells (28.85 ± 1.92% vs. 23.41 ± 1.49%) and a significant increase in apoptotic cells (3.50 ± 0.06% vs. 6.34 ± 0.13%). Transcriptomic sequencing analysis revealed 377 differentially expressed genes (234 up-regulated, 143 down-regulated) in ML385-treated PGCs, with significant enrichment of apoptosis-related pathways and downregulation of cell cycle pathways. NRF2 inhibition induced oxidative stress, with a 2.2-fold increase in reactive oxygen species levels and a 3.3-fold decrease in the GSH/GSSG ratio. Mitochondrial damage, including decreased mitochondrial number (27.86 ± 2.74 vs. 17.57 ± 1.55), vacuolization (9.54 ± 1.64% vs. 41.27 ± 11.18%) and hyperpolarization of mitochondrial membrane potential, was observed, along with increased markers of ferroptosis, including a 2.7-fold increase in MDA and a 2.4-fold increase in Fe²⁺ levels, and enhanced autophagy, as evidenced by increased LC3-II expression and upregulation of MAP1LC3B, ATG5, and BECN1. Notably, while in vivo migration efficiency of PGCs to recipient gonads was unaffected, the number of PGCs colonizing the gonads was significantly reduced (182.93 ± 23.48 vs. 120.66 ± 18.50) in the ML385 group. These findings demonstrate that NRF2 is essential for maintaining chicken PGC proliferation and survival under culture conditions, and its deficiency leads to oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, apoptosis, and increased susceptibility to ferroptosis.

Introduction

Primordial germ cells (PGCs) serve as the precursors for gametes and play a pivotal role in genetic information transmission (Glover and McGrew, 2012). Their development involves a series of intricate processes, including migration, proliferation, and differentiation, ultimately leading to the production of functional gametes in mature organisms (Nakamura et al., 2007). The precise coordination of these events is essential for ensuring accurate germ line transmission and maintaining species continuity.

In chickens, PGCs possess the unique ability to migrate with the bloodstream, facilitating convenient heterologous transplantation (Han et al., 2002; Nakamura et al., 2007). Recent advancements in understanding chicken PGC developmental mechanisms and in vitro culture systems have enabled long-term cultivation and extensive proliferation of PGCs (van de Lavoir et al., 2006; Whyte et al., 2015; Chen et al., 2018a; Xie et al., 2019; Ezaki et al., 2020), overcoming limitations associated with manipulating oocytes and early embryos in poultry. This technology holds significant promise in poultry breeding and genetic improvement, as chicken PGCs, with their ease of in vitro culture and germ line transmission capability, have become prime targets for genome editing strategies. With technologies like CRISPR-Cas9, precise genome modifications can introduce desired traits such as disease resistance (Idoko-Akoh et al., 2023), enhanced reproductive performance (Chen et al., 2023a), and superior meat quality into elite breeding lines, ensuring sustainable poultry production and breeding of superior varieties (Kim et al., 2023).

Moreover, ultra-low temperature preservation and in vitro culture of PGCs provide invaluable tools for conserving and propagating rare and endangered poultry species (Taylor et al., 2017; Woodcock et al., 2019; Lázár et al., 2021), safeguarding genetic diversity and preventing loss of valuable genetic resources. These breakthroughs owe much to in-depth research on PGC biology and germ line development, underlining the importance of understanding the molecular mechanisms controlling PGC development and function for both basic developmental biology and conservation and genetic editing of poultry.

In recent years, the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2) pathway has emerged as a central regulatory factor for cellular homeostasis and stress response across various cell types (Dodson et al., 2019a; Dai et al., 2020a). NRF2, a transcription factor, crucially coordinates cellular defense against oxidative stress and exogenous damage by regulating genes involved in antioxidant defense (Nguyen et al., 2009), detoxification (Kobayashi et al., 2006), and metabolic adaptation (Esteras et al., 2023). Activation of NRF2 signaling confers cellular protection and enhances cell survival under stress conditions, while inhibition or dysregulation can compromise cellular function (Motohashi and Yamamoto, 2004; Nguyen et al., 2009; Akino et al., 2019).

In the reproductive system, the protective role of NRF2 has been demonstrated across multiple species. In mice, NRF2 activation alleviates oxidative stress-induced follicular atresia and oocyte aging (Qiu and Yao, 2017; Wang et al., 2021), and protects male reproductive function under heat stress through the P38/NF-κB/Nrf2/HO-1 pathway (Yang et al., 2024b). In humans, antioxidant strategies that improve sperm cryosurvival, such as Edaravone supplementation, are associated with enhanced glutathione levels and reduced oxidative stress (Ghasemi et al., 2025), which are downstream effects consistent with NRF2 activation. These findings suggest that NRF2 plays a conserved role in protecting germ cells across mammals. Additionally, Nrf2 plays a significant role in stem cell development across multiple species, maintaining self-renewal, quiescence, and regenerative capacity while protecting against stress and aging (Dai et al., 2020b; Kahroba et al., 2021).

Despite extensive studies in mammalian systems, the role of NRF2 in avian germ cells, particularly in chicken PGCs, remains largely unexplored. Chicken PGCs possess unique biological characteristics: they migrate through the bloodstream to colonize the gonads and are highly susceptible to oxidative stress during in vitro culture, cryopreservation, and genome editing (Nakamura et al., 2007; Whyte et al., 2015; Ibrahim et al., 2024). Indeed, recent studies have documented that chicken PGCs encounter multiple environmental challenges, including oxidative stress during culture, cryopreservation-induced damage, and heat stress (Ibrahim et al., 2024; Niu et al., 2025; Gu et al., 2025). Given the established protective functions of NRF2 in mammalian germ cells and stem cells (Dai et al., 2020b; Ghasemi et al., 2025), and considering that chicken PGCs share characteristics with both germline and stem cells, the present study was designed to investigate the effects of NRF2 deficiency on PGC proliferation, survival, mitochondrial integrity, and migration, and to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms.

Our findings reveal that inhibiting NRF2 function resulted in decreased proliferation and increased apoptosis levels in chicken PGCs. Additionally, oxidative stress occurred within the cells, leading to mitochondrial damage and hyperpolarization. Moreover, abnormalities were observed in glutathione metabolism and ferroptosis markers within the cells. Furthermore, there was an upregulation in both mitophagy and cellular autophagy levels. Notably, NRF2 inhibition affected the number of PGCs in the gonads and caused aberrant expression of genes associated with migration and germ cells makers. These findings reveal that NRF2 plays a critical role in maintaining chicken PGC homeostasis by regulating oxidative stress responses, cell survival, proliferation, and mitochondrial integrity.

Materials and methods

Isolation and culture of PGCs

All experimental procedures were approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Yangzhou University (approval code: 202103273). PGCs were isolated from Rugao Yellow Chicken embryos at Hamburger and Hamilton (HH) stages 27–28 following established protocols (Whyte et al., 2015; Chen et al., 2018a). Gonads were dissected and rinsed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, P1020, Solarbio, Beijing, China) containing 0.1% bovine serum albumin (BSA, A8010, Solarbio, Beijing, China). Tissue dissociation was achieved by incubation in 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (25200056, Gibco, MA, USA) at 37°C for 1 min. After gentle pipetting, the cell suspension was centrifuged and the pellet re-suspended in FAcs culture medium. Cells were seeded into 48-well plates and maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂. The basal medium consisted of calcium-free DMEM diluted 3:1 with sterile water. Complete FAcs medium (containing FGF2, Activin A and chicken serum) was prepared by supplementing the basal medium with the following components at indicated final concentrations: 0.15 mM CaCl₂ (C7902, Sigma, MO, USA), 1 × B-27 supplement (17504044, Gibco, MA, USA), 2 mM GlutaMax (35050061, Gibco, MA, USA), 1 × non-essential amino acids (11140050, Gibco, MA, USA), 0.1 mM β-mercaptoethanol (31350010, Gibco, MA, USA), 1 mM sodium pyruvate (11360070, Sigma, MO, USA), 0.2% chicken serum (16110082, Gibco, MA, USA), 1 × nucleosides (E8-008-D, Sigma, MO, USA), 0.20% ovalbumin (A5503, Sigma, GER), 0.1 mg/mL sodium heparin (HY-17567A, MedChemExpress, NJ, USA), 25 ng/mL human Activin A (HY-P70311, MedChemExpress, NJ, USA), and 4 ng/mL human FGF2 (HY-P70600, MedChemExpress, NJ, USA).

Preparation and treatment of NRF2 inhibitor

ML385 (HY-100523, MedChemExpress, NJ, USA) was dissolved in DMSO to prepare a 12 mM stock solution. Based on preliminary dose-response experiments covering a range of concentrations informed by previous studies, ML385 was added to the FAcs medium at final concentrations of 6, 12, or 24 μM to evaluate its effects on chicken PGCs. Corresponding concentrations (0.05%, 0.1%, and 0.2% v/v) of DMSO were added to the FAcs medium of control groups to account for any potential effects of the solvents on the outcomes. The PGCs used in this study were derived from mixed-sex embryos (HH stage 27–28) without sex-specific selection, and thus the results represent an average response from both male and female PGCs. Following isolation, the cells were cultured for 74 d to establish stable lines and expand cell numbers prior to experimental use. The established PGC lines were seeded into 24-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well and treated with FAcs medium containing different concentrations of ML385. After 3 d of culture, cell morphology was observed, and cell numbers were quantified. Cell counting was performed using the Countess® II FL automated cell counter (Life Technologies, Bothell, WA, USA), and viable cells were distinguished by trypan blue staining prior to counting. The reported cell numbers represent counts of viable cells.

5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) proliferation assay

For all three biological replicates, cell proliferation was assessed using the EdU Cell Proliferation Assay Kit (C10310-1, RiboBio, Guangzhou, China) according to the manufacturer's protocol. The EdU stock solution was diluted 1:1000 in FAcs medium pre‑warmed to 37°C, yielding a final concentration of 50 μM. PGCs were incubated in this EdU‑supplemented medium for 2 h at 37°C. Following incubation, cells were collected by centrifugation, washed twice with PBS, and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min. After a brief treatment with 2 mg/mL glycine for 5 min, cells were permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X‑100 (T8200, Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 10 min at room temperature. Staining was performed by adding 100 μL of 1 × Apollo reaction solution to each sample and incubating for 10 min in the dark. Nuclear counterstaining was achieved with DAPI (C0065, Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 10 min, followed by three PBS washes. Samples were then mounted with cedar oleoresin and examined under a fluorescence microscope. For each sample, at least five random fields were captured, and the percentage of EdU‑positive cells was quantified using ImageJ software (version 1.53t, NIH, USA).

Apoptosis analysis by flow cytometry

Apoptosis was assessed using the Annexin V‑FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (40302ES50, Yeasen, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Harvested PGCs were washed twice with PBS and re‑suspended in 100 μL of 1 × Binding Buffer. Annexin V‑FITC (5 μL) and PI staining solution (10 μL) were added, and cells were incubated at room temperature in the dark for 15 min. After adding 400 μL of 1 × Binding Buffer, samples were kept on ice and immediately analyzed on a BD LSRFortessa™ flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA). Data acquisition was performed using BD FACSDiva™ software (version 8.0, BD Biosciences), and data analysis was performed using FlowJo software (version 10.8.1, BD Biosciences). For each sample, a minimum of 20,000 events were recorded. Debris and dead cells were excluded based on forward scatter (FSC) and side scatter (SSC) characteristics. For apoptosis analysis, quadrants were set based on single-stained controls (Annexin V-FITC only and PI only) to compensate for spectral overlap between FITC and PI. The percentage of early apoptotic (Annexin V⁺/PI⁻) and late apoptotic/necrotic (Annexin V⁺/PI⁺) cells was calculated accordingly.

RNA extraction, library construction and sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from 1 × 10⁶ PGCs using TRIzol reagent (15596018CN, Invitrogen, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA purity and concentration were assessed with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer, and integrity was verified on an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Libraries were prepared using the VAHTS Universal V10 RNA‑seq Library Prep Kit (NR606-01, Vazyme, Nanjing, China) following the manufacturer's guidelines. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform generating 150 bp paired‑end reads (OE Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China).

Bioinformatic analysis of RNA‑seq data

Raw reads were filtered with fastp (Chen et al., 2018b) to remove low‑quality reads. Clean reads were aligned to the chicken reference genome GRCg7b using HISAT2 (Kim et al., 2015). Gene expression levels were quantified as FPKM (Roberts et al., 2011), and raw read counts were obtained via HTSeq-count (Anders et al., 2015). Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed with R (v4.0.0).

For differential expression analysis, raw count matrices were processed with DESeq2 (Love et al., 2014) based on a negative binomial model. Genes were considered significantly differentially expressed if they satisfied both |log₂FC| > 1 and FDR-adjusted q < 0.05. Three independent biological replicates per group were used for all analyses. Hierarchical clustering of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was carried out in R to visualize expression patterns across samples. Enrichment analyses for Gene Ontology (GO) terms and KEGG pathways were performed using the hypergeometric distribution in R, with significance determined by adjusted p-values (Kanehisa et al., 2007; The Gene Ontology Consortium, 2019)

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was run using GSEA software (Mootha et al., 2003; Subramanian et al., 2005) with predefined gene sets (Supplementary Tables S1 and S2). Genes were ranked according to their differential expression between Ctrl-PGCs and ML385-PGCs, and the analysis determined whether each gene set was enriched at either end of the ranked list.

To assess the overall expression changes of apoptosis-related genes upon NRF2 inhibition, a set of previously reported apoptosis-related genes expressed in chicken PGCs (Rengaraj et al., 2022) was analyzed. FPKM values for each gene were extracted from the RNA-seq data. For heatmap visualization, FPKM values of selected genes were log2-transformed and normalized by row (gene) Z-score. Hierarchical clustering was performed using Euclidean distance as the distance measure and complete linkage as the clustering method. The heatmap was generated using the pheatmap package in R (version 4.0.0). To compare the global expression trend between Ctrl-PGCs and ML385-PGCs, a normalization procedure was applied as follows: for each gene, the average FPKM value across all samples (both Ctrl and ML385 groups) was calculated and set to 1. The relative expression level for each sample was then obtained by dividing the sample FPKM value by this gene-specific average. A paired t-test was performed on the normalized expression values of all genes to determine whether the overall expression of these genes was significantly altered between the two groups. The normalized data were visualized as a combined boxplot and scatter plot using GraphPad Prism version 9.0.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) measurement

Intracellular ROS levels were determined using the Reactive Oxygen Species Assay Kit (50101ES01, Yeasen, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. DCFH‑DA was diluted in FAcs medium to 10 μM. PGCs were incubated with the DCFH‑DA solution at 37°C for 30 min in the dark, with tube inversion every 10 min. After incubation, cells were washed twice with PBS and analyzed on a BD LSRFortessa™ flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) equipped with a 488 nm laser and 525 nm bandpass filter (FITC channel). For each sample, a minimum of 20,000 events were recorded, and debris was excluded based on FSC and SSC characteristics. Data analysis was performed using FlowJo software (version 10.8.1, BD Biosciences). The mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of the control group was calculated and set to 1, and the MFI values of all samples were normalized accordingly to allow for comparison across independent experiments. For the analysis of ROS-high and ROS-low PGC populations, fluorescence intensity histograms of Ctrl-PGCs and ML385-PGCs were overlaid. The intersection point of the two distribution curves was used as the threshold to define ROS-high and ROS-low cells. Cells with fluorescence intensity above this threshold were classified as ROS-high PGCs, while those below were classified as ROS-low PGCs. Subsequently, the percentages of ROS-high and ROS-low PGCs in each group were quantified by flow cytometry.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

PGCs were fixed overnight at 4°C in PBS containing 2.5% glutaraldehyde, then washed and post‑fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide for 40 min. After washing, cells were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (30–100%) and embedded in Epon 812 resin. Ultrathin sections (90–100 nm) were cut, stained with uranyl acetate for 7 min and Reynold’s lead citrate for 5 min. The sections were examined using a Hitachi HT7800 transmission electron microscope (Hitachi High-Technologies, Tokyo, Japan) operated at an accelerating voltage of 80 kV. Images were captured using a CMOS digital camera (EMSIS, Germany). For quantitative analysis, at least 10 randomly selected cells per group were analyzed. Mitochondria with intact cristae and continuous membranes were considered normal. Mitochondria were classified as vacuolated if they showed distinct electron‑lucent areas within the matrix, typically accompanied by cristae disruption or loss. The number of mitochondria per cell and the percentage of vacuolated mitochondria were counted from randomly selected TEM images.

Mitochondrial activity detection

Mitochondrial activity was assessed using the MitoTracker® Red CMXRos Kit (40741ES50, Yeasen, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Cells were incubated with 25 nM MitoTracker® Red CMXRos in pre‑warmed culture medium at 37°C for 15 min in the dark. Following staining, the cells were treated with 10 μl of an anti-fluorescence quenching agent (Solarbio), and the slides were mounted with cedar oleoresin as a mounting medium. Subsequently, the samples were observed using a Leica DMi8 inverted fluorescence microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany) at a total magnification of 200 × . To ensure comparability across experimental groups, all images were acquired under identical exposure settings and at the same microscope session. For quantitative analysis, at least 50 randomly selected cells per sample were analyzed. Fluorescence intensity was measured on a per-cell basis using ImageJ software (version 1.53t, NIH, USA). For each independent experiment (n = 3 biological replicates per group), the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of the control group was calculated and set to 1, and the MFI values of all samples were normalized accordingly. The normalized data were used for statistical analysis and graphical presentation.

Glutathione assay

Reduced (GSH) and oxidized (GSSG) glutathione levels were measured with the GSH and GSSG Assay Kit (S0053, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Approximately 1 × 10⁶ PGCs were collected, washed with PBS, and lysed in protein removal reagent M (three times the pellet volume) through two freeze‑thaw cycles. After centrifugation at 10,000 × g and 4°C for 10 min, supernatants were used for total glutathione determination. Total glutathione was measured by mixing 10 μL of sample or standard with 150 μL of detection working solution, incubating at 25°C for 5 min, then adding 50 μL of NADPH solution and measuring absorbance at 593 nm on a Tecan Spark® multimode microplate reader (Tecan Austria GmbH, Grödig, Austria). GSSG content was measured after removing GSH with the kit’s clearance reagents. All glutathione measurements were normalized to cell number. For each sample, the cell count was determined using a Countess® II FL automated cell counter (Life Technologies, Bothell, WA, USA) prior to lysis. The results are expressed as nmol per 10⁶ cells. Reduced GSH content was calculated as: GSH = Total Glutathione – 2 × GSSG.

Lipid peroxidation detection

Lipid peroxidation was evaluated by quantifying malondialdehyde (MDA) levels utilizing the Malondialdehyde Detection Kit (KGA7101, KeyGEN BioTECH, Nanjing, China) following the manufacturer's protocol. PGCs were harvested and adjusted to a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells per tube. Cells were lysed on ice for 20 min in 1.2 mL of ice‑cold RIPA buffer containing 1 mM PMSF, with vigorous shaking every 5 min. After centrifugation at 12,000 rpm and 4°C for 10 min, supernatants were collected for analysis. For MDA measurement, 0.2 mL of lysate or MDA standard was mixed with 1.5 mL each of reagent C and reagent D, followed by addition of 1 mL distilled water and thorough vortexing. The mixture was incubated in a boiling water bath for 50 min, rapidly cooled on ice, and centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C. A sample blank (lysis buffer without cells) was processed identically to correct for background absorbance. Absorbance of the supernatants was measured at 532 nm using a Tecan Spark® multimode microplate reader. MDA standards (0, 0.5, 1, 2, 5, 10, and 20 nmol per tube) were used to generate a standard curve. MDA concentrations were normalized to cell number and expressed as nmol per 10⁶ cells.

Ferrous ions (Fe2+) content detection

The concentration of Fe²⁺ was measured using the Cell Ferrous Iron Colorimetric Assay Kit (E-BC-K881-M, Elabscience, Wuhan, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. PGCs were harvested and resuspended at a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells per tube. Each tube received 0.2 mL of buffer solution, and cells were thoroughly lysed on ice for 10 min. After centrifugation at 15,000 × g for 10 min, the supernatants were collected for analysis. For measurement, 80 μL aliquots of sample were combined with 80 μL of chromogenic solution and incubated at 37°C for 10 min. Absorbance was read at 593 nm using a Tecan Spark® multimode microplate reader. As a negative control, 80 μL of control solution was substituted for the chromogenic solution. Iron standard solutions were prepared at concentrations of 0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 30, 40, and 50 μmol/L, and a standard curve was constructed to determine the Fe²⁺ content in the samples. Fe²⁺ levels were normalized to cell number and expressed as nmol per 10⁶ cells.

Western blotting

PGCs were collected and homogenized in 100 μL of RIPA buffer containing 1 mM PMSF. Vortexing was performed every 5 min during a 30‑min incubation on ice to achieve complete lysis. Protein content was quantified using a BCA protein assay kit (P0012, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Equal amounts of protein (30 μg per sample) were resolved on 12% SDS‑PAGE gels. After electrophoresis, proteins were transferred onto PVDF membranes using a wet transfer system at 100 V for 90 min at 4°C. The membranes were blocked with TBS containing 0.1% Tween 20 and 5% non-fat milk for 2 h at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation at 4°C with primary antibodies: rabbit polyclonal anti-NRF2 (1:2000, 16396-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), rabbit polyclonal anti-LC3B (1:1000, NB100-2220, Novus Biologicals, Littleton, CO, USA), and rabbit polyclonal anti-GAPDH (1:1000, 10494-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), which was used as the internal loading control for normalization. All primary antibodies used in this study have been validated for reactivity with chicken proteins. After washing, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:5,000, CW0103, CWBIO; Taizhou, China) secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using a CCD camera and UVISoft software (UVITEC Cambridge).

RNA isolation and reverse transcription

Total RNA was extracted from 1 × 10⁶ PGCs using TRIzol reagent (15596018CN, Invitrogen, Shanghai, China). Briefly, cells were homogenized in 1 mL TRIzol, incubated for 5 min at room temperature, mixed with chloroform, shaken vigorously for 15 s, and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C. The aqueous phase was collected, mixed with an equal volume of isopropanol, incubated for 10 min, and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C. The RNA pellet was washed with 75% ethanol, centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 5 min at 4°C, air-dried, and dissolved in 30 μL DNase/RNase-free water. RNA concentration was measured using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific™).

For cDNA synthesis, 1 μg of total RNA was treated with DNase I (Invitrogen) and reverse-transcribed using the HiScript® III RT SuperMix (+gDNA wiper) kit (R323-01, Vazyme, Nanjing, China). The reaction mixture containing RNA, 4 × gDNA wiper Mix, and RNase-free water was incubated at 42°C for 2 min. Then, 5 × HiScript III qRT SuperMix was added, and reverse transcription was performed at 37°C for 15 min followed by 85°C for 5 s.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

The qRT-PCR was conducted using the ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix kit (Q711-02, Vazyme, Nanjing, China) following the manufacturer's guidelines. All primers used in this study were validated for amplification efficiency (90–110%) using standard curves, and melting curve analysis was performed after each run to confirm amplification specificity. Each sample was run in technical triplicates, and the mean Ct values were used for quantification. The 20 μL reaction mixture contained 10 μL of 2 × Master Mix, 2 μL of cDNA, 0.4 μL each of forward and reverse primers, and nuclease‑free water. Thermal cycling conditions were: 95°C for 30 s; 40 cycles of 95°C for 10 s, 60°C for 30 s, 72°C for 15 s; and a final extension at 72°C for 5 min. GAPDH served as the internal control. Relative mRNA levels were calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001). Detection of PGC migration

Fertilized eggs were incubated at 37.5°C with 60% humidity for 55 h to obtain embryos at HH13–16. GFP-labeled PGCs were generated via lentiviral transduction using a CMV-GFP construct, followed by puromycin selection to establish a stable polyclonal line. Fluorescence intensity was verified microscopically prior to injection. All injections were performed using the same PGC line, with 30 embryos per group. Cell density was adjusted to 3000 cells/μL. At HH13–16, a small hole (∼1 cm) was made at the blunt end of each egg, and 1–2 μL of GFP-PGC suspension was injected into the dorsal aorta under a stereomicroscope. The opening was sealed with medical tape after applying 20 μL of penicillin/streptomycin. Eggs were returned to the incubator with the blunt end up. At HH32 (7.5 d), embryos were collected and examined under a stereomicroscope. Migration efficiency was quantified as the percentage of recipient embryos showing GFP-positive PGCs in the gonads relative to the total number of injected embryos that survived to the analysis stage. The migration of GFP-PGCs to the gonads was observed using a stereoscopic fluorescence microscope.

Statistical analysis

For all experiments, at least three independent biological replicates (n = 3 per group) were performed, with three technical replicates included for each biological replicate to ensure measurement accuracy. Representative images are shown in the figures. For functional assays involving two-group comparisons (EdU assay, apoptosis, ROS, GSH/GSSG, MDA, Fe²⁺, mitochondrial membrane potential, and migration efficiency), statistical analysis was carried out using the Student's t-test. For cell counting involving comparisons among multiple groups (0, 6, 12, and 24 μM ML385), one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's test for multiple comparisons. Before analysis, percentage data underwent arcsine transformation. All results are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical significance was defined at p < 0.05, and all statistical calculations for functional assays were performed using SPSS software version 19 (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Transcriptomic statistical details are provided in the RNA-seq analysis section.

Results

Inhibition of NRF2 leads to decreased proliferation and increased apoptosis in chicken PGCs

To investigate the role of NRF2 in chicken PGCs, different concentrations of ML385 (0, 6, 12, and 24 μM) were added to PGC cultures. After 3 d, a noticeable decrease in cell number was observed in the 24 μM group (Fig. 1A), and cell counting confirmed a significant reduction compared to the other three groups (Fig. 1B). To determine whether this decrease was due to impaired proliferation or increased apoptosis, we performed EdU incorporation and Annexin V/PI staining assays. EdU labeling revealed a significant reduction in proliferating cells in the ML385-treated group (Fig. 1C, D). Flow cytometry analysis showed significantly higher proportions of early apoptotic (Annexin V⁺/PI⁻) and late apoptotic (Annexin V⁺/PI⁺) cells in ML385-treated PGCs (Fig. 1E-G). Western blot analysis confirmed that ML385 treatment effectively reduced NRF2 protein levels (Fig. 1H, I). Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the role of NRF2 in chicken PGCs and its potential mechanisms. The results revealed that inhibiting NRF2 function led to decreased proliferation and increased apoptosis levels in PGCs. Furthermore, through transcriptomic analysis and experimental validation, we found that NRF2 inhibition resulted in elevated ROS levels, abnormal mitochondrial structure and function, disrupted glutathione metabolism, increased MDA and Fe²⁺ levels, enhanced autophagy, and a significant reduction in the number of PGCs colonizing the gonads. These findings demonstrate that NRF2 plays a crucial role in the in vitro culture of chicken PGCs, impacting cell proliferation, metabolism, intracellular homeostasis, and the maintenance of PGC characteristics.

In response to environmental factors such as oxidative stress, cells activate multiple regulatory networks to counteract oxidative damage (Tan and Suda, 2018). Within this network, NRF2 stands out as a core transcription factor that orchestrates antioxidant defense, regulates stem cell fate, and maintains cellular homeostasis (Kahroba et al., 2021; Kumar and Ramanarayanan, 2026). However, NRF2′s role in chicken PGCs remains unknown. To investigate its importance in the in vitro culture of PGCs, we inhibited NRF2 using ML385 and observed a significant decrease in PGC quantity, coupled with reduced proliferation and increased apoptosis, indicating a crucial role for NRF2 in maintaining PGC survival and proliferation, consistent with previous studies in other cell types (Fan et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2020). To systematically explore the mechanisms underlying these phenotypes, we performed transcriptomic sequencing analysis. Consistent with the observed phenotypes, GSEA confirmed that NRF2 inhibition led to the enrichment of gene sets associated with growth suppression and apoptotic programs, while pathways promoting cell cycle progression were significantly depleted. These results, in line with previous findings (Fan et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2023b), further support the critical role of NRF2 in regulating proliferation and apoptosis in PGCs. Having established the impact of NRF2 on PGC viability, we next explored the underlying cellular mechanisms.

Under normal circumstances, cells maintain a dynamic balance between the production and clearance of ROS. One important role of NRF2 in cells is to maintain the redox balance (Luchkova et al., 2024). In this study, transcriptomic data analysis revealed that inhibiting NRF2 activated pathways related to peroxisomes in chicken PGCs, indicating the disruption of the redox homeostasis. By measuring ROS levels in PGCs, we found that inhibiting NRF2 indeed led to an increase in cellular ROS levels, demonstrating the important role of NRF2 in maintaining cellular redox homeostasis in PGCs. Mitochondria, as the "ROS management center" in stem cells, have been widely confirmed to regulate the dynamic balance of stem cell maintenance, renewal, and differentiation by modulating ROS production(Tan and Suda, 2018). In this study, transcriptomic data analysis revealed that inhibiting NRF2 function in PGCs resulted in abnormalities in pathways related to mitochondrial autophagy, oxidative phosphorylation, the tricarboxylic acid cycle, mitochondrial structure, and function, indicating abnormal mitochondrial structure and function. The upregulation of mitochondrial autophagy may be aimed at clearing damaged mitochondria to alleviate further damage. We found a decrease in mitochondrial ridges and an increase in mitochondrial vacuolization in PGCs after NRF2 inhibition. Furthermore, the observed reduction in mitochondrial number after NRF2 inhibition provides additional evidence for enhanced mitophagy.

To further explore the molecular mechanisms underlying these mitochondrial changes, we analyzed the expression of key mitophagy regulators and mitochondrial dynamics genes. We found that BNIP3, a receptor mediating ubiquitin-independent mitophagy, was significantly upregulated in ML385-treated PGCs, while PARK2, a core component of the canonical PINK1-Parkin pathway, was significantly downregulated. This pathway switching suggests that upon NRF2 inhibition, chicken PGCs activate BNIP3-mediated non-canonical mitophagy as an adaptive response to clear damaged mitochondria under stress conditions (Yamashita et al., 2024). Regarding mitochondrial dynamics, we found that the fusion gene MFN1 was significantly upregulated, whereas MFN2 and the fission gene DNM1L showed no significant changes. The selective upregulation of MFN1 may represent a compensatory fusion response, reflecting a cellular attempt to dilute damaged mitochondrial components through enhanced fusion and thereby maintain the integrity of the mitochondrial network under oxidative stress. Although DRP1 transcript levels were unchanged, its activity is primarily regulated by post-translational modifications such as phosphorylation. Therefore, further experimental validation of mitochondrial quality control processes, including mitophagy, fusion, and fission, will be important to fully elucidate the mechanisms by which NRF2 regulates mitochondrial homeostasis in chicken PGCs. An intriguing finding of this study is the observation of mitochondrial membrane potential hyperpolarization following NRF2 inhibition, which appears to contrast with the established view that NRF2 deficiency typically leads to mitochondrial depolarization (Holmström et al., 2013). We interpret this phenomenon as a potential early compensatory stress response. Upon NRF2 inhibition, impaired antioxidant capacity results in initial ROS accumulation, which may transiently enhance proton pump activity to sustain mitochondrial membrane potential and ATP production in an attempt to cope with the stress (Pedrosa et al., 2010). Future studies employing time-lapse analysis of mitochondrial membrane potential dynamics and interventions with mitochondrial-targeted antioxidants would help to further elucidate the temporal sequence of these events. Furthermore, we propose that following NRF2 deficiency, oxidative stress and mitochondrial damage are more likely to form a positive feedback loop rather than a simple linear causality. NRF2 deficiency first leads to downregulation of antioxidant genes, compromising cellular antioxidant capacity. This results in elevated ROS levels, which attack mitochondria, leading to morphological and functional damage. Subsequently, damaged mitochondria become more efficient "ROS generators," further exacerbating oxidative stress and perpetuating the cycle (Fukui and Moraes, 2008).

Through transcriptomic sequencing data, we also found that inhibiting NRF2 caused a decrease in glutathione metabolism and an increase in ferroptosis in PGCs. This suggests that NRF2 may directly or indirectly participate in glutathione and ferroptosis metabolism processes in chicken PGCs. Previous studies have shown NRF2′s role in regulating ferroptosis (Dodson et al., 2019b; Anandhan et al., 2023). To further confirm this possibility, we examined glutathione metabolism and Fe2+ concentration and found a decrease in GSH levels and an increase in GSSG and Fe2+ levels in cells. Additionally, since ferroptosis is often the direct result of cellular lipid peroxidation(Dodson et al., 2019b), we also measured the effect of inhibiting NRF2 on lipid peroxidation in PGCs and found a significant increase. Therefore, we hypothesize that NRF2 inhibition in chicken PGCs increases ROS, leading to lipid peroxidation and creating a cellular environment conducive to ferroptosis. The decrease in GSH further reduces the cell's resistance to ferroptosis. We acknowledge that while our data present a compelling chain of evidence supporting this connection, definitive proof of causality would require rescue experiments using antioxidants such as N-acetylcysteine and ferroptosis inhibitors such as Ferrostatin-1, as well as immunofluorescence staining for lipid peroxidation markers. As this study represents the first characterization of NRF2 function in chicken PGCs, we focused on establishing these phenotypic associations; future studies employing such pharmacological interventions will be important to validate the direct role of ferroptosis in NRF2-deficient PGC death.

Moreover, transcriptomic data also revealed the activation of pathways related to cell autophagy and lysosomes upon inhibiting NRF2. Additionally, detection of the autophagy marker protein LC3 confirmed that inhibiting NRF2 indeed increased the level of autophagy in PGCs, possibly as a response to oxidative damage caused by ROS. The observation that LC3-II accumulation was accompanied by unchanged LC3-I levels may reflect a homeostatic mechanism in which LC3-I synthesis is upregulated to maintain the cellular pool of this essential autophagy protein under conditions of sustained autophagic activity. This interpretation is supported by the concurrent upregulation of ATG5 and BECN1 mRNA expression, suggesting transcriptional activation of the autophagy machinery. Alternatively, the possibility of impaired autophagic flux cannot be completely excluded and warrants future investigation using lysosomal inhibitors such as chloroquine.

Regarding PGC migration and development, studies have shown that chicken PGCs originate outside the gonads and migrate to the early gonads through a series of regulatory processes (Han et al., 2002; Nakamura et al., 2007). The in vivo migration assay revealed that migration efficiency, defined as the percentage of recipient embryos showing GFP-positive PGCs in the gonads, showed no significant difference between the control and ML385 groups. However, the absolute number of PGCs that successfully colonized the gonads was significantly reduced in the ML385 group. To analyze the causes underlying this phenomenon, we evaluated the transcriptomic data. Analysis revealed that inhibiting NRF2 led to the enrichment of genes related to cell migration in PGCs, and that migration-related gene expression showed that CXCR4 and ITGA4 were significantly upregulated. These findings indicate that the reduced number of GFP-labeled PGCs in the gonads of the ML385 group is more likely attributable to decreased proliferation and increased apoptosis (Fig. 1C-G), rather than impaired migratory capacity. A more detailed assessment of NRF2′s effect on PGC migratory capacity may require time-lapse imaging analysis in future studies.

In human embryonic stem cells (hESCs), NRF2 is highly abundant and directly binds to the upstream regions of pluripotency genes such as OCT4 and NANOG, thereby promoting their expression (Jang et al., 2014, 2016). Upon differentiation, NRF2 levels decline sharply, and its inhibition compromises self-renewal and impairs the establishment of pluripotency during reprogramming. In contrast, our study in chicken PGCs revealed that NRF2 inhibition did not significantly affect the expression of core pluripotency genes, including POUV (the avian homolog of OCT4) and NANOG. This divergence suggests that the regulatory role of NRF2 may differ substantially depending on cell type and species context. A possible explanation is that NRF2 in chicken PGCs may be more focused on cytoprotection, including maintaining redox balance, mitochondrial integrity, and cell survival, rather than directly regulating pluripotency gene expression. Germ cell markers are highly sensitive to oxidative stress, while the core pluripotency network may be more resilient to moderate stress levels. The underlying mechanisms warrant further investigation.

It is important to acknowledge that the use of pharmacological inhibitors such as ML385 carries the potential for off-target effects. ML385 has been validated as a specific NRF2 inhibitor across multiple species, including mammalian cells and, relevant to this study, avian cells (Yang et al., 2024a; Niu et al., 2025; Lu et al., 2025). In our study, Western blot confirmed reduced NRF2 protein levels in ML385-treated PGCs, alongside transcriptomic evidence of downregulated target genes (GPX1 and SOD2), providing functional evidence of NRF2 pathway inhibition. While the 24 μM concentration was selected based on its most pronounced effect on PGC viability in the dose-response assay, we did not examine NRF2 protein levels at lower concentrations (6 and 12 μM). Dose-dependent analysis of NRF2 protein levels would provide further evidence for ML385 specificity. Future studies using genetic rescue experiments, such as NRF2 overexpression, as well as investigations into the correlation between inhibitor concentration and phenotypic severity, will help to establish the causal relationship between NRF2 inhibition and the observed effects in chicken PGCs.

In conclusion, our study provides comprehensive insights into the role of NRF2 in chicken PGCs and its underlying mechanisms (Fig. 9). NRF2 plays a critical role in maintaining PGC proliferation, survival, and mitochondrial homeostasis during in vitro culture by activating antioxidant pathways. NRF2 deficiency leads to oxidative stress, mitochondrial damage, increased apoptosis, and elevated ferroptosis markers, ultimately reducing PGC numbers. Given that PGCs are frequently exposed to stressful conditions during procedures such as cryopreservation and genome editing, pharmacological activation of the NRF2 pathway may offer a promising strategy to enhance their endogenous antioxidant capacity and improve tolerance to these manipulations. Future studies should focus on optimizing the timing and dosage of NRF2 agonists in PGC culture systems to maximize cell survival without compromising germline competency, thereby supporting the application of PGCs in poultry genetic resource conservation and breeding programs.Fig. 9Schematic representation illustrating the impact of NRF2 deficiency on chicken PGCs.
