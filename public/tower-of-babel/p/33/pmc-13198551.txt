An oncostatin M receptor and chloride intracellular channel 1 crosstalk drives key oncogenic pathways in glioblastoma
Authors: Amir Hossein Mansourabadi, Dianbo Qu, Francesca Cianci, Jamie Snider, Kamaldeep Randhawa, Laura Raco, Max Kotlyar, Mohammad Al Ayach, Guido Rey, Shridhar Sanghvi, Mark Abovsky, Harpreet Singh, H. Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Oncostatin M receptor (OSMR) plays diverse roles in several human malignancies, including brain, breast, and pancreatic cancer. In glioblastoma (GB), OSMR orchestrates a feedforward signaling mechanism with the truncated active mutant of epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), the EGFRvIII, and signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) to drive GB progression. Beyond EGFRvIII, OSMR promotes brain tumor stem cell (BTSC) respiration and therapy resistance. The molecular mechanisms underlying OSMR’s multifaceted roles remain largely unclear. Here, we systematically mapped the OSMR interactome using Mammalian Membrane Two-Hybrid High-Throughput Screening (MaMTH-HTS). We identified OSMR-specific and OSMR/EGFRvIII-specific high-confidence candidate binding proteins, highlighting OSMR context-dependent functions. Among a subset of common interactors, we uncovered chloride intracellular channel 1 (CLIC1) as a critical regulator of OSMR-STAT3 signaling and the OSMR/EGFRvIII complex. CLIC1 physically associates with OSMR and EGFRvIII and facilitates EGFRvIII packaging into extracellular vesicles (EVs). Genetic deletion of CLIC1 disrupts the OSMR/EGFRvIII interaction, impairs STAT3 activation, reduces EGFRvIII EV content, and slows GB progression. Using whole-cell patch-clamp recordings and a monoclonal antibody that selectively targets transmembrane CLIC1 (tmCLIC1omab), we establish a distinct pharmacologically and biophysically tmCLIC1-mediated current in GB indispensable for sustaining EGFRvIII/STAT3 signaling. Importantly, we show that OSMR is required for maintaining CLIC1-mediated ionic balance at the plasma membrane (PM). Our study uncovers a bidirectional crosstalk between OSMR and tmCLIC1 in GB, essential for fueling its malignant growth.

Introduction

Glioblastoma (GB) is the most malignant primary brain tumor in adults, with no cure.1,2 The present standard of care includes maximal surgical removal of the tumors followed by treatment with Temozolomide (TMZ) and ionizing radiation (IR) therapy; however, despite these intense efforts, the median survival rate for GB patients remains less than 22 months following diagnosis. GB remains uniformly lethal in large part due to extensive intratumoral heterogeneity, and persistence of a dynamic population of stem-like cells, the brain tumor stem cells (BTSCs) that contribute to tumor state transitions, tumor resistance to therapy, and recurrence.3–11

Multiple genetic alterations have been identified in GB.12–16 The most common genetic alteration in GB is the amplification of epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) and its active mutant, EGFR variant III (EGFRvIII), historically assigned to the classical GB subtype.17 Approximately 50% of GB patients harbor amplification of EGFR, and more than half of these patients also carry the truncated active EGFRvIII.18,19 EGFRvIII is constitutively active and functions as a ligand-independent receptor, leading to sustained induction of oncogenic pathways.20 The mesenchymal GB subtype has also been described as one of the most malignant brain tumors. The aggressive nature of these subtypes is attributed in large part to the activity of signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3). STAT3 is a versatile transcription factor with multiple functions in the central nervous system (CNS).21,22 Phosphorylation of STAT3 at Tyrosine 705 (Y705) plays an important role in malignancy23 and endows the cells with the ability to enter an epithelial-to-mesenchymal (EMT)-like state, maintain stemness, and metastasize.20,24,25 While several receptors, kinases, and transcription factors are well-established drivers of GB, targeting intracellular signaling pathways has yielded limited clinical benefit. There is a critical need to identify novel targetable extracellular interactions that sustain oncogenic signaling and can be therapeutically exploited. More importantly, effective therapies will require identifying signaling nodes that integrate oncogenic, metabolic, and microenvironmental cues to sustain BTSCs and drive treatment resistance.

Oncostatin M receptor (OSMR) functions as a context-dependent central regulator of tumor cell state and therapy resistance. OSMR plays a crucial role in mediating oncogenic signal transduction initiated by its ligand, OSM.26,27 The OSM-OSMR axis induces the activation of STAT3 as well as key oncogenic pathways, including the phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)-AKT, c-Jun N-terminal kinases/mitogen-activated protein kinase (JNK/MAPK), and RAS/MAPK pathways.28–30 OSMR functions as a co-receptor for EGFRvIII in GB, inducing a positive feedback loop with STAT3.31 It also plays a crucial role in upregulating mitochondrial respiration and conferring resistance to ionizing radiation (IR) therapy.32 Additionally, OSMR is a key regulator of BTSC self-renewal32 and the immune microenvironment,33 suggesting that its diverse oncogenic functions are context-dependent. These findings have raised the question of how OSMR operates to confer its distinct functions depending on the cell’s genetic background.

In the present study, we employed Mammalian Membrane Two-Hybrid- High-Throughput Screening (MaMTH-HTS), a technology uniquely suited for identifying interacting partners of integral membrane proteins. This screening led to the identification of the chloride intracellular channel 1 (CLIC1) as a high-confidence candidate binding protein of OSMR, positioning it as a common interactor in different contexts. CLIC1 is highly expressed in various cancers,34–37 and is associated with poor prognosis in GB.38–40 CLIC1 exists as a soluble cytoplasmic protein; however, in cancer cells, it translocates to the plasma membrane where it functions as a chloride ion channel.41–43 Importantly, similar to OSMR,30 CLIC1 is also highly expressed in the mesenchymal GB subtype and is enriched in BTSCs.38,40 The transmembrane CLIC1 (tmCLIC1) is shown to maintain redox balance,44 and its knockdown (KD) elevates oxidative stress and disrupts redox homeostasis.

Our study reveals a previously unrecognized interaction between CLIC1 and OSMR. Analysis of publicly available patient datasets revealed that CLIC1 and OSMR were consistently upregulated in GB samples, and patients with high expression of CLIC1 and OSMR exhibited poor prognosis. Importantly, CLIC1 consistently displays significant upregulation with subtype progression. This analysis revealed that stratification of patients for CLIC1 and OSMR expression is an important predictor of survival. Mechanistically, we show that OSMR and tmCLIC1 interact physically and functionally to drive GB progression. Genetic deletion or pharmacological inhibition of tmCLIC1 impairs BTSC proliferation and downstream STAT3 signaling. Importantly, OSMR requires the membrane configuration of CLIC1 to effectively sustain its oncogenic signaling output. This crosstalk between a cytokine receptor and a metamorphic ion channel reveals a new layer of complexity in the GB membrane interactome. Our findings suggest that disrupting the OSMR/tmCLIC1 interaction provides a promising therapeutic avenue for GB treatment.

Results

Mapping OSMR interactome via mammalian membrane two-hybrid high-throughput screening (MaMTH-HTS)

To address the question of how OSMR networks with alternate molecules to confer its different functions, we performed MaMTH-HTS in HEK293T cells expressing an OSMR bait, in the presence or absence of co-expressed EGFRvIII, with a prey library of ~8000 open reading frames from the Human ORFeome V8.1 collection (Supplementary Fig. 1a–c).45 We identified 334 high-confidence candidate binding partners for OSMR (Fig. 1), following rigorous analyses and exclusion of MaMTH-HTS “frequent flyers,” which was established from repeated MaMTH-HTS results of 9 “individual bait” protein control experiments, as previously described.46–50 Among the identified candidates, 204 hits, including 59 PM proteins, were detected when OSMR was used as bait in the absence of EGFRvIII, and 160 candidate binding partners, including 40 PM proteins, were identified when OSMR was used as a bait in the presence of EGFRvIII. Importantly, our data revealed 30 candidate binding partners of OSMR that were commonly shared by both groups, 7 of which were PM proteins. The PM proteins category encompassed proteins that are integral to the membrane as well as proteins that are associated with the membrane. Gene Ontology (GO) term functional analysis of protein clusters in each group revealed molecules engaged in metabolic processes and signaling as top categories underscored in each group (Fig. 1). Furthermore, these analyses revealed that the GO term for immune system processes was highly enriched in the OSMR-unique interactome, whereas the regulation of biogenesis processes was among the top GO terms for the OSMR-EGFRvIII category. Analysis of OSMR common binding partners and the involvement of CLIC1

To unravel the mechanism by which OSMR operates to promote tumor growth across different GB subtypes, we focused on the common candidate binding partners (Supplementary Table 1), which encompassed different classes of proteins involved in metabolic processes, signaling, biogenesis, development, and immune system processes. To validate MaMTH data in the context of GB, we employed four different patient-derived BTSCs that naturally harbor EGFRvIII mutation (BTSC73 and BTSC147) or lack the mutation (BTSC12, BTSC30) (Supplementary Table 2). We conducted a counter-screen in which we employed siRNA (Supplementary Table 3) targeting each of the genes encoding these proteins, followed by cell viability assessment. Our results revealed that the knockdown (KD) of 12 genes demonstrated a substantial reduction in cell viability exceeding 50% across different BTSCs (Supplementary Fig. 1d). These included CLEC2B, CLIC1, CREB3, CSGALNACT2, DCN, EIF2C3, GC, GPBP1, H3C1, IMMP2L, NAT8, and ST3GAL4. Prior to follow-up investigation on the selected candidates, we applied additional screening criteria pertaining to establish roles of these proteins in BTSCs and GB, as well as their impact on the regulation of different hallmarks of cancer. These criteria led us to focus on CLIC1 as the top candidate.

We conducted an analysis of four publicly available patient datasets deposited in TCGA, Rembrandt, Gravendeel, Kamoun, and assessed the mRNA expression of CLIC1 and OSMR in GB tumor versus non-tumor samples. Both CLIC1 and OSMR were consistently upregulated in GB samples across TCGA, Rembrandt, and Gravendeel datasets (Fig. 2a, b). Importantly, CLIC1 and OSMR expression analysis across different GB subtypes revealed that CLIC1 displays consistent and significant upregulation with subtype progression, while OSMR was equally upregulated in both classical and mesenchymal subtypes relative to the proneural subtype (Fig. 2c, d). Next, we analyzed how upregulation of OSMR and CLIC1 impacts GB patient survival. Analysis of 4 independent datasets deposited at TCGA, Rembrandt, Gravendeel, and LeeY revealed that patients with high expression of CLIC1 or OSMR exhibited poor prognosis (Supplementary Fig. 2a–h). Importantly, patients stratified as CLIC1/OSMR high expressors had a significantly shorter lifespan compared to CLIC1/OSMR low expressors (Fig. 2e–h). CLIC1 regulation of BTSCs self-renewal and proliferation

To characterize the impact of CLIC1 knockdown on a panel of patient-derived BTSCs with varying oncogenic mutations, we next electroporated a pool of four siRNAs targeting CLIC1 into different BTSCs and assessed cell viability and stem cell frequency using PrestoBlue and extreme limiting dilution assay (ELDA),51 respectively. RT-qPCR and immunoblotting analyses showed efficient siRNA-mediated knockdown of CLIC1 by greater than 85% and a significant decline in CLIC1 protein expression level relative to BTSCs electroporated with a non-targeting RNAi (siCTL) (Fig. 3a–d, Supplementary Fig. 3a–j). Our data revealed a significant decrease in BTSC viability (Supplementary Fig. 3k, l), BTSC sphere size (Supplementary Fig. 3m, n), and stem cell frequency (Fig. 3e–h) upon knockdown of the CLIC1 gene. Next, we generated BTSCs in which we induced genetic deletion of CLIC1 using CRISPR-Cas9 in two different EGFRvIII-expressing BTSCs, BTSC73 and BTSC147 (Fig. 3i–l). Similar to our findings with the transient knockdown experiments, monoallelic (CRISPR-m) or biallelic (CRISPR-b) genetic deletion of CLIC1 induced a robust reduction in BTSC stem cell frequency (Fig. 3m, n), cell viability (Supplementary Fig. 3o, p), and sphere size (Supplementary Fig. 3q, r). We next conducted EdU assay and consistent with our results, deletion of CLIC1 resulted in a significant reduction in actively dividing cells (Fig. 3o, p, Supplementary Fig. 4). CLIC1 interacts with OSMR and EGFRvIII endogenously in BTSCs

MaMTH data revealed that CLIC1 is a high-confidence potential binding partner of OSMR. We first subjected different non-permeabilized BTSCs to immunostaining using an antibody to CLIC1 and observed the localization of CLIC1 at the cell periphery (Fig. 4a–c). To assess whether CLIC1 and OSMR colocalize in BTSCs, we conducted co-immunostaining using antibodies to OSMR and CLIC1. Our data revealed that CLIC1 colocalizes with OSMR in all BTSCs analyzed in our studies (Fig. 4d–g), raising the question of whether CLIC1 interacts with OSMR endogenously. We conducted a Proximity Ligation Assay (PLA) using OSMR and CLIC1 antibodies in different BTSCs, with primary antibodies omitted as a negative control. Our data showed an in situ interaction between CLIC1 and OSMR across all BTSC lines that were examined (Fig. 4h–l), with EGFRvIII-expressing BTSC73 and BTSC147 that harbor elevated OSMR expression, exhibiting stronger signal (Fig. 4h, i, l) compared to BTSC30 and BTSC12 lacking the EGFRvIII (Fig. 4j–l). To further confirm the specificity of the interaction signal, we subjected CLIC1 CRISPR BTSCs and their corresponding controls to PLA analysis. For each BTSC, two CRISPR lines, CRISPR-m and CRISPR-b, were included. Next, we subjected the BTSCs to co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) using an antibody targeting endogenous OSMR followed by immunoblotting with a CLIC1 antibody. Our data showed that OSMR interacts with CLIC1 endogenously in BTSCs (Fig. 4q, r).

Given that OSMR forms a co-receptor with EGFRvIII, we next asked whether CLIC1 is a component of the same complex with EGFRvIII. We conducted co-immunostaining analysis in different BTSCs and found co-localization of EGFRvIII with CLIC1 (Fig. 4s, t). Thus, we performed PLA experiments using antibodies against EGFRvIII and CLIC1 in control and CLIC1 CRISPR BTSCs. Our results showed an in situ interaction of CLIC1 with EGFRvIII in BTSCs (Fig. 4u, v).

The extracellular domain of CLIC1 interacts with OSMR/EGFRvIII

OSMR is found in complex with EGFRvIII to amplify receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) signaling.31 Given our results that CLIC1 interacts with both OSMR and EGFRvIII, we asked whether CLIC1 is required for the OSMR/EGFRvIII interaction. We subjected CLIC1 CRISPR and corresponding control BTSCs to PLA analysis using antibodies targeting OSMR and EGFRvIII. Strikingly, genetic deletion of CLIC1 significantly impaired the interaction of OSMR with EGFRvIII in each of BTSC147 and BTSC73 lines that harbor EGFRvIII mutation (Fig. 5a–e), suggesting that CLIC1 is required for the OSMR/EGFRvIII complex. In parallel, we conducted similar experiments in which we assessed the CLIC1-OSMR interaction in EGFRvIII knockdown BTSCs and the EGFRvIII-CLIC1 interaction in OSMR knockdown BTSCs. Given that CLIC1 functions as a membrane protein existing predominantly as a homodimer, we next modeled its interaction using the dimer of the CLIC1 extracellular domain (residues 1–24) with the extracellular domains of OSMR (residues 1–741) and EGFRvIII (residues 1–378) using AlphaFold-Multimer. The predicted structural model of this tetramer revealed that the CLIC1 dimer (purple and brown) interacts with OSMR (green) and EGFRvIII (orange) (Fig. 5f). Consistent with the prediction, our experiments using CLIC1(1–58 aa) and CLIC1(1–178 aa) as baits demonstrated that CLIC1(1–58 aa) interacts with OSMR or with the OSMR/EGFRvIII complex, indicating that the extracellular regions of these three proteins are sufficient to support complex formation (Fig. 5g, h). To further validate the interaction under native conditions, we performed native gel electrophoresis, which preserves intact protein complexes. The results revealed that CLIC1, OSMR, and EGFRvIII coexist within a ~720 kDa protein complex in EGFRvIII-expressing BTSCs (Fig. 5i). Furthermore, our data shows that the protein expression of each of the molecules in the complex is not altered by the presence of its binding partners, as revealed via immunoblotting analysis in BTSCs (Supplementary Fig. 5b–d), and IF staining on tumor tissues (Supplementary Fig. 5e). To investigate the regulatory relationship between EGFRvIII and STAT3 in controlling Clic1 expression, we used EgfrvIII-expressing Stat3loxp/loxp and Stat3−/−, as well as murine stem cell virus (MSCV)-control (non–EgfrvIII-expressing) Stat3loxp/loxp and Stat3−/− astrocytes. No differences in CLIC1 expression were observed among the groups, implicating that STAT3 does not regulate Clic1 expression (Supplementary Fig. 5f). Next, we asked if the complex formation is impacted by lipid rafts that bring CLIC1 and OSMR proteins together. We treated BTSCs with Methyl-β-cyclodextrin (MβCD) to disrupt lipid rafts, followed by PLA analysis. We observed a reduction in the interaction signal between CLIC1 and OSMR, suggesting that their interaction in part occurs within these lipid rafts on the PM (Supplementary Fig. 6).

OSMR-CLIC1 crosstalk maintains EGFRvIII/STAT3 signaling, and blockade of tmCLIC1 impairs the phosphorylation of EGFRvIII and STAT3

Our data raised the question of whether CLIC1 functionally regulates oncogenic OSMR-STAT3 and EGFRvIII signaling. CLIC1 is found as a soluble cytoplasmic protein as well as a transmembrane protein that forms an ion channel.42 We conducted cell fractionation experiments in different BTSC lines and found that CLIC1 is localized to both the membrane and cytoplasm (Supplementary Fig. 7); however, the majority of CLIC1 was found in the cytoplasm. Moreover, CLIC1 has been shown to be packaged into extracellular vesicles (EVs) to target neighboring cells, thereby promoting tumor growth.52 Interestingly, similar to CLIC1,38,52,53 EGFRvIII impacts various aspects of tumorigenesis not only in the cell of origin but also in neighboring cells, a process shown to be mediated via EVs.54 Thus, we first questioned whether CLIC1 is required for the horizontal propagation of EGFRvIII to neighboring cells. By extracting EVs from control (CTL) BTSCs and their corresponding CLIC1 CRISPR cells, we evaluated EGFRvIII protein expression levels in the cell lysates and EV fractions. Our analysis revealed that EGFRvIII levels were significantly reduced in CLIC1-deleted EVs, suggesting a role for CLIC1 in mediating the horizontal propagation of EGFRvIII to neighboring cells (Fig. 6a–c, Supplementary Fig. 8). Next, we set out to investigate the electrophysiological properties of tmCLIC1 in three distinct human BTSC lines, including BTSC73, BTSC147, and BTSC30. To assess basal tmCLIC1 levels, we used IAA94, a CLIC1 inhibitor. The tmCLIC1 current was measured as the IAA94-sensitive current, defined as the difference between the initial current and the current after drug perfusion. Next, we conducted an electrophysiological evaluation of tmCLIC1 levels at +40 mV on the membranes of CLIC1 CRISPR and control BTSCs. Our results revealed that the tmCLIC1 currents were significantly reduced in the CLIC1 CRISPR lines compared to the control BTSCs (Fig. 6d). To assess whether OSMR is required for the tmCLIC1-mediated ionic conductance, we induced knockdown of OSMR using short hairpin RNA (shOSMR) in different cell lines and subjected the OSMR KD and control scrambled cells (SCR) to patch clamp experiments to evaluate whether reduced OSMR expression levels alter Cl− currents. Cell currents were assessed both prior to (baseline) and following the addition of 100 μM of IAA94 to the bath solution (Fig. 6e–j). The corresponding current-voltage relationship was recorded in each group. Strikingly, we found that inhibition of OSMR significantly attenuated the tmCLIC1-associated Cl− current (Fig. 6i–k). Our data reveals a bidirectional OSMR-CLIC1 crosstalk in the regulation of CLIC1-mediated ionic conductance in BTSCs, leading to the question of whether tmCLIC1 impacts EGFRvIII/STAT3 signaling. To address this question, we generated and employed an anti-tmCLIC1 monoclonal antibody (tmCLIC1omab®) that specifically inhibits tmCLIC1 and its associated ionic currents (Fig. 6l–p, Supplementary Fig. 9a–d). We first evaluated the efficacy of tmCLIC1omab using an in vivo tumor model, in which BTSCs were xenografted into the flanks of SCID mice and treated with either tmCLIC1omab or an IgG control. Targeting tmCLIC1 with tmCLIC1omab significantly suppressed tumor growth in the antibody-treated group (Fig. 6q–s, Supplementary Fig. 9e). Next, we treated different glioma cells and BTSCs with the tmCLIC1omab or IgG control, followed by an analysis of EGFRvIII and STAT3 phosphorylation in response to the ligand OSM. Our data revealed that both the EGFR phosphorylation at tyrosine (Y) 1068 and STAT3 phosphorylation at Y705 were significantly attenuated upon inhibition of the CLIC1-mediated Cl− current by a tmCLIC1omab in EGFRvIII-expressing BTSC73 and BTSC147 cells. CLIC1 drives GB tumorigenesis and maintains EGFRvIII/EGFR phosphorylation in vivo

Our findings on the central role of CLIC1 in regulating the OSMR/EGFRvIII interaction and EGFRvIII and STAT3 phosphorylation led us next to examine whether CLIC1 contributes to GB tumorigenesis using BTSC patient-derived xenografts. We conducted intracranial tumor assay using BTSC73, BTSC147, and the corresponding CLIC1 CRISPR BTSCs. Our data revealed that the deletion of CLIC1 significantly impaired tumorigenesis and prolonged animal survival (Fig. 7a–h). We next subjected tumor sections obtained from the control mice, as well as the smaller tumors from the CLIC1 CRISPR group, to H&E staining (Fig. 7i, j) and immunostaining analysis using a phospho-Y1068-EGFR antibody that can detect both the phosphorylated wild-type (WT) EGFR and EGFRvIII (Fig. 7k). Strikingly, we found that the phosphorylation of EGFR/EGFRvIII was robustly attenuated in the group of mice xenografted with CLIC1 CRISPR BTSCs. Independently, we subjected different CLIC1 CRISPR and control BTSCs to immunoblotting experiments and analyzed the phosphorylation status using antibodies to STAT3-Y705 and EGFRvIII. Strikingly, similar to results obtained with tmCLIC1omab, we established that genetic deletion of CLIC1 significantly impaired the phosphorylation of STAT3 and EGFRvIII (Fig. 7l–s). Our data reveal that CLIC1 tightly maintains the phosphorylation of EGFRvIII and STAT3. In parallel, we conducted experiments in which we showed that the forced expression of CLIC1 in CLIC1 knockdown BTSCs restores STAT3 phosphorylation at Tyr705 (Supplementary Fig. 10b). Discussion

In this study, we comprehensively mapped the OSMR interactome and demonstrated that OSMR networks with various proteins depending on the cell’s genetic background. Furthermore, we establish a common set of interactions that are central to driving key oncogenic pathways independent of the cells’ genetic make-up. We report the discovery of tmCLIC1-OSMR crosstalk as a common oncogenic mechanism across different GB subtypes. We show that patients with high CLIC1- and high OSMR- expression exhibit significantly shorter lifespan. We establish that tmCLIC1 interacts endogenously with OSMR and EGFRvIII in BTSCs. Importantly, genetic deletion of CLIC1 impairs the OSMR/EGFRvIII interaction, positioning CLIC1 as a central mediator of this oncogenic complex. Structural modeling, binding assays, and native gel analyses revealed that the tmCLIC1 extracellular domain (residues 1–58 aa) is sufficient to mediate interaction with the extracellular domains of OSMR and EGFRvIII, forming a stable ~720 kDa protein complex in EGFRvIII-expressing BTSCs. We further demonstrate a bidirectional relationship for the tmCLIC1-OSMR interaction, which plays a pivotal role in regulating ion conductance. While tmCLIC1 generates a distinct chloride current at the PM, we found that OSMR is required for maintaining CLIC1-mediated ionic balance, as OSMR knockdown significantly reduced the chloride currents. Functionally, this complex is indispensable for sustaining key oncogenic pathways. Similar to our genetic model, pharmacological inhibition of tmCLIC1 using a monoclonal antibody resulted in robust attenuation of EGFRvIII (Y1068) and STAT3 (Y705) phosphorylation. This coordinated regulation between CLIC1-mediated ionic flux and RTK/STAT3 signaling acts as a critical driver of diverse oncogenic pathways and tumor growth.

CLIC1 is implicated in several malignancies, including lung cancer,34 pancreatic adenocarcinoma,35 epithelial ovarian cancer,36 and medulloblastoma.37 CLIC1 is abundantly expressed in BTSCs, and its elevated expression is associated with poor prognosis in GB.38,39 Previous studies report a role for CLIC1 in the regulation of cell viability, cell volume, mitosis, and proliferation.37 During the prophase to metaphase transition, cells undergo a significant volume decrease, reaching a minimum size at metaphase, referred to as pre-mitotic condensation.55,56 Pre-mitotic condensation requires the efflux of Cl−, which may be mediated by tmCLIC1. In further support, IAA94-mediated inhibition of tmCLIC1 prolongs the G1 phase, extending the overall cell cycle.57 Our findings establishing an OSMR-CLIC1 crosstalk in the regulation of BTSC self-renewal and proliferation, suggest that OSMR may be involved in modulating pre-mitotic condensation or G1 phase of cell cycle.

tmCLIC1 has been shown to play a prominent role in ROS production.41,58,59 Given tmCLIC1’s established function in maintaining redox balance,44 its knockdown likely elevates oxidative stress and disrupts redox homeostasis. The interplay between ROS and tmCLIC1-generated chloride currents, combined with evidence of the tmCLIC1/OSMR interaction, highlights the need to investigate CLIC1’s role in BTSC metabolism in the context of GB. Whether similar to OSMR,32 tmCLIC1 regulates oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) to maintain BTSCs and buffer ROS in the mitochondria remains to be investigated.

CLIC1 is implicated in tumor microenvironment modulation, as it is positively associated with stromal activation, EMT, and angiogenesis.60 Chloride channels, including CLIC1, are implicated in innate and adaptive immunity, specifically in phagocyte-mediated responses. CLIC1 is required for phagosomal acidification and antigen processing, which are crucial for CD4⁺ T cell activation.60–62 Furthermore, CLIC1-mediated regulation of macrophage-driven inflammation and ROS production may facilitate the establishment of a pro-tumorigenic niche conducive to growth and metastasis. CLIC1 also contributes to innate immunity by activating the NLRP3 inflammasome.63 Aberrant NLRP3 inflammasome activity is implicated in the progression of several cancers, including glioma.64,65 Additionally, tmCLIC1 modulates Cl− currents in activated microglia, particularly in response to β-amyloid protein (Aβ) stimulation.59 Interestingly, OSM secreted by immune cells typically binds to its receptor on tumor cells in a paracrine fashion to direct oncogenic signaling. These findings suggest that the OSMR-CLIC1 crosstalk may influence immune surveillance, an area that requires future investigation.

Loss of CLIC1 may shift cells toward a more differentiated phenotype. Previous research demonstrated that differentiation of BTSCs with serum leads to loss of EGFRvIII expression and decreased tumorigenic potential.66 Interestingly, CLIC1 expression was shown to be reduced upon induction of differentiation.67 These results suggest that both EGFRvIII and CLIC1 are important in maintaining stemness or suppressing differentiation.68 Further studies are required to determine if the CLIC1-EGFRvIII interaction establishes a network that suppresses BTSC differentiation.

Another interesting finding from our study was that CLIC1 regulates the horizontal transfer of EGFRvIII via EVs. We observed a striking attenuation of EGFRvIII in EVs derived from CLIC1 CRISPR cells, suggesting that CLIC1-mediated packaging is a key mechanism by which GB cells influence the tumor microenvironment. This aligns with evidence that EGFRvIII transfer via EVs drives glioma progression,54 and that tmCLIC1 vesicle transfer specifically promotes glioma stem cell proliferation.52 Beyond GB, EV-encapsulated CLIC1 has been shown to confer vincristine resistance in gastric cancer,69 suggesting that tmCLIC1 may represent a broader adaptive mechanism for drug resistance. Consequently, targeting these chloride currents with repurposed drugs offers a promising therapeutic strategy.67,70

Our findings have established that tmCLIC1 interaction with OSMR and EGFRvIII is essential for sustaining oncogenic signaling. Using a monoclonal CLIC1 antibody that can specifically target tmCLIC1, we establish the significance of tmCLIC1; however, the functional contribution of cytoplasmic CLIC1 and its involvement in mitochondrial and metabolic pathways remain to be investigated in future studies.

In conclusion, we establish tmCLIC1 as a central player in GB pathogenesis, orchestrating cytokine networks and enhancing RTK signaling. Its interaction with OSMR and EGFRvIII is critical for sustaining BTSC self-renewal and GB tumorigenesis. These results highlight tmCLIC1 as a promising therapeutic target, suggesting that its inhibition, in combination with standard GB treatments, may benefit GB patients. Importantly, our data suggest that inhibiting the interaction of CLIC1 and OSMR in GB using small inhibitory peptides could serve as a promising therapeutic avenue for GB.

Methods

Brain tumor stem cells (BTSCs) and generation of transgenic cell lines

The patient-derived BTSC lines, BTSC73, BTSC147, BTSC12, and BTSC30, were generously provided by Dr. Samuel Weiss at the University of Calgary. They were generated in accordance with the University regulation from excess or discarded tissue collected during surgery from adult patients following informed consent from patients. BTSCs were characterized for major mutations (Supplementary Table 2) and were cultured and maintained as described in the Supplementary Materials and Methods section. Transgenic CRISPR BTSCs were generated, using methods previously described.71 To design the gRNA, Off-Spotter software, version 0.2.2 (https://cm.jefferson.edu/Off-Spotter/), was used. Two guide RNA strands (forward and reverse complement) were generated to target exons 5–9 of the CLIC1 gene. To generate the construct, the Golden Gate Assembly Cloning strategy72 was used, in which gRNA1 and gRNA2 were cloned into pL-CRISPR.EFS.GFP (Addgene plasmid, Cat. #57818) and pL-CRISPR.EFS.tRFP (Addgene plasmid, Cat. #57819) plasmids, respectively. Plasmids were sequenced (Genome Quebec), verified, and electroporated [1300 volts by the AMAXA nucleofector 2b device (Lonza, Cat. #AAB1001) to deliver 3 µg of each plasmid construct (gRNA1-GFP and gRNA2-RFP) into ~2 million BTSC147 or BTSC73]. Electroporated cells were cultured in a T-75 low attachment flask at 37 °C in a 5% CO2. 48 h after electroporation, BTSC spheres were dissociated into a single-cell suspension using Accumax dissociation solution (Innovative Cell Technologies, Cat. #AM105) and subjected to Fluorescent-activated cell sorting (FACS) analysis using the BD FACSAriaTM Fusion (BD Biosciences) to sort for double positive GFP and RFP cells. Sorted cells were plated at a density of 1 cell/well into two 96-well plates containing 100 μl of BTSC media. Wells were monitored every 2 days to assess sphere formation, and clonal samples were collected from multiple positive clones and were subject to genomic DNA isolation. Isolated DNA was analyzed by PCR using internal primers and external primers to the gRNA-guided Cas9 cut site, designed using Primer3Plus software, version 3.3.0 (https://www.primer3plus.com/), to determine monoallelic, biallelic, or non-deletion control clones. Monoallelic deletion clones in both BTSC73 and BTSC147 cell lines were determined by PCR, in which the presence of one internal and one external band on a 2% Agarose gel was observed, while a biallelic deletion was determined by a single external band. Knockout or knockdown of CLIC1 was validated using RT-qPCR and Western blotting (WB) to assess gene and protein expression. Since there were no clones identified that presented no cuts in the CLIC1 gene following incubation with the CRISPR-Cas9 construct, which could have been used as a control for Cas9, the chosen control for experiments using the CRISPR-Cas9-CLIC1 cells was that of the parental BTSC73 or BTSC147 line. The gRNA and primer sequences used in this experiment are listed in Supplementary Table 4. Finally, small interfering RNA (siRNA) was used to generate a transient knockdown in the patient-derived BTSCs. BTSCs were processed into a single-cell suspension. ON TARGET-plus SMART pool human CLIC1 siRNA (Dharmacon, Cat. #L-009530-00-0005) at a concentration of 100 nM, and ON TARGET-plus non-targeting pool (Dharmacon, Cat. #D-001810-10-05) were employed, as described in the Supplementary Materials and Methods section.

Mammalian membrane two-hybrid high-throughput screening (MaMTH-HTS)

To identify binding partners of OSMR in the presence and absence of EGFRvIII, the Mammalian Membrane Two-Hybrid (MaMTH) High Throughput Screen (HTS) technology was used as described.46,73 Briefly, a plasmid expressing the OSMR “Bait” protein with a C- terminally fused MaMTH-HTS Bait tag (Cub-GAL4TF-P2A-tagBFP) alone or alongside a plasmid expressing EGFRvIII (fused with 3xFLAG at its N-terminus) was transfected into a pooled “Prey” library of HEK293T MaMTH-HTS reporter cell lines. The library of reporter cell lines stably expressed members of the Human ORFeome V8.1 collection (~8000 open reading frames, ORFs) fused to the MaMTH-HTS Prey tag (Nub) at their N-terminus and P2A-mCherry at their C-terminus and contained a chromosomally integrated GFP reporter under the control of the GAL4 transcription factor (GAL4TF) promoter. Transfections were performed using X-tremeGene™ 9 Transfection Reagent (Roche, XTG9-RO) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. In order to induce Bait (Supplementary Fig. 1) and Prey expression, cells were grown for 2–3 days in the presence of 0.5 µg/ml Tetracycline in the following conditions: 37 °C, 5% CO2 in DMEM containing 10% FBS and 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin media. Cells were then harvested by trypsinization and resuspended at a concentration of 1–2 × 106 cells/ml in Basic Sorting Buffer (1X phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), 5 mM EDTA, 25 mM HEPES pH 7.0, 1% BSA) and subjected to FACS using BD FACSMelody (BD Biosciences). Cells were sequentially selected according to the following: tag-BFP fluorescence indicating “Bait” expression, mCherry fluorescence indicating “Prey” expression, and GFP fluorescence indicating Bait-Prey interaction. Cells were collected for analysis in DMEM containing 25% FBS, and centrifuged pellets were processed in Phire Tissue Direct Dilution Buffer (ThermoFisher Scientific). Amplification of ORFs was performed using Phire Tissue Direct PCR Master Mix (ThermoFisher Scientific), and products were purified using the QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen). Purified PCR products were subjected to Nextera XT library preparation and deep sequencing using the Illumina HiSeq 2500 system (150 bp single read). Sequencing data were processed, and hits were identified using custom software developed using R programming language and integrated Bowtie2 alignment tool, version 2.5.2.74

Counter-screen assay

BTSCs were transfected with siRNA using Lipofectamine™ RNAiMAX in a 96-well plate. Briefly, 1 pmol of siRNA and 0.1 µl of RNAiMax were diluted in 5 µl of Opti-MEM and incubated separately for 5 min. The solutions were then gently combined and incubated at room temperature (RT) for 15 min to form transfection complexes. A total of 1000 BTSCs per well were seeded in 80 µl of NeuroCult medium, followed by the addition of 10 µl of the siRNA-RNAiMax complex with gentle shaking. After incubation at 37 °C for 5 h, 90 µl of NeuroCult medium was added to each well, and the cells were further incubated at 37 °C for 48 h. Transfected cells were then subjected to a PrestoBlue assay to assess cell viability.

Duolink proximity ligation assay (PLA)

The Proximity Ligation Assay was conducted following the manufacturer’s protocol using the Duolink In Situ Red Starter Kit (Sigma, Cat. #DUO92101) as optimized by our group,75 and described in the Supplementary Materials and Methods section.

Extreme limiting dilution assay (ELDA)

ELDA was performed as previously described,76 with detailed methods presented in the Supplementary Materials and Methods section.

5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) assay

Cell proliferation in BTSC73, BTSC147, and their corresponding CLIC1 CRISPR KD cell lines was assessed using the Click-iT™ Plus EdU Alexa Fluor™ 488 Flow Cytometry Assay Kit (Invitrogen, Cat. #C10632). According to the manufacturer’s protocol, cells were incubated with 10 μM EdU for 2 h at 37 °C to label newly synthesized DNA. Following this step, cells were fixed, permeabilized, and washed twice with the permeabilization and wash buffer provided in the kit. The incorporated EdU was then detected with the Alexa Fluor 488 azide via a copper-catalyzed click reaction. Following two final washes, samples were analyzed using a Cytek Aurora Flow Cytometry System (5) Lasers/ (64) Fluorescence Channels/ (67) Detector (Cytek Biosciences, Cat. #62352, USA). Data were analyzed using FlowJo™ v10 Software.

In vitro binding assay

HEK293T cells were seeded in 6-well plates at approximately 60–70% confluency one day prior to transfection. Cells were transfected using PEI with plasmids encoding Myc-tagged truncated CLIC1 constructs, CLIC1(1–58) and CLIC1(1–178), together with expression vectors for OSMR and/or EGFRvIII. The total amount of DNA per well was normalized using an empty vector to ensure equal transfection conditions across samples. 24 h post-transfection, cells were washed twice with ice-cold PBS and lysed on ice in lysis buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5% NP-40, 1 mM EDTA) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails. Lysates were incubated on ice for 20 min and clarified by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatants were collected for immunoprecipitation (IP). Specifically, 500 µg of total cell lysate was incubated with 1 µg of anti-Myc antibody overnight at 4 °C with gentle rotation to IP Myc-tagged CLIC1 and associated proteins. The following day, Protein G Dynabeads were added and incubated for an additional 2 h at 4 °C. Beads were then washed three times with lysis buffer. IPed proteins were eluted by boiling the beads in 2X SDS sample buffer for 5 min and subjected to SDS-PAGE, followed by WB. Proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes, which were blocked in 5% BSA in TBST (20 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% Tween-20, pH 7.4) for 1 h at RT. Membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies targeting Myc (for CLIC1), OSMR, and EGFRvIII. Following incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h at RT, proteins were visualized using ECL. A fraction of the total lysate (5% of the total protein used for IP) was analyzed in parallel to confirm expression of transfected constructs (input control).

Stereotaxic injections and bioluminescent imaging

Stereotaxic surgeries were conducted by injecting ~3 × 105 luciferase-expressing CLIC1 CRISPR BTSCs and control BTSCs into the right striata (0.8 mm lateral to the bregma, 1 mm dorsal, and 2.5 mm deep from the pial surface). Prior to injection, BTSCs were dissociated into single-cell suspensions in serum-free, antibiotic-free medium and tested for luciferase activity using an IVIS imaging system. Kaplan–Meier survival plots were generated using mice at the point of reaching the end stage (major body weight loss, dehydration, hunched back, piloerection, and lethargy). Median survival was calculated using a log-rank test with GraphPad Prism, following previously described methods.77 Briefly, the Kaplan–Meier approach estimated the probability of survival at each time point. Before any time elapses, all mice were considered “at risk”; however, no deaths occurred. Therefore, the probability of survival was defined as 1. Subsequently, information about the next elapsed time (day 7) included information about how many participants experienced the event of interest (death). From this information, a survival probability was calculated using the formula: St+1 = St*((Nt+1-Dt+1)/Nt+1), where St represents survival probability, Nt represents number at risk, and Dt represents number of events. The survival probability for each subsequent time point is calculated, and a stair-step survival curve is plotted. For tumor volume assessment, the mice received intraperitoneal injections of 200 µl of 15 mg/ml D-luciferin (ThermoFisher Scientific, Cat. #88292), underwent anesthesia via isoflurane inhalation, and were then subjected to weekly bioluminescence imaging using a CCD camera (IVIS, Xenogen). Subsequent collection and analysis of all bioluminescent data were performed utilizing Living Image 2.0 software (PerkinElmer, MA, USA). Briefly, after initialization of the IVIS Spectrum system and acquiring an image, the images were analyzed using “ROI tools” in the tool palette, selecting the area of interest. The IVIS imager captures pixels with specific photon intensity values, with brighter areas corresponding to higher photon detection, indicative of tumor induction.

Generation and characterization of the anti-tmCLIC1 monoclonal antibody, tmCLIC1omab

We designed and generated the tmCLIC1omab® antibody (License No. 11665M) to specifically target the NH₂-terminal region of the CLIC1 protein. Polyclonal antibodies were initially generated through a 138-day immunization protocol in which mice were immunized with a synthetic peptide corresponding to the NH₂-terminus of CLIC1 (NH₂-EQPQVELFVKAGSDGAKIGNC-COOH), conjugated to ovalbumin (OVA) and dissolved in a buffer composed of 0.1 M glycine (pH 3.0) and 1 M Tris-HCl (pH 8.0). Antibody specificity was confirmed by the supplier using an ELISA assay. Hybridoma cells producing antibodies were sorted as single cells to isolate monoclonal clones. A total of 44 distinct monoclonal antibodies, derived from all four immunized mice, were sent to our laboratory for further screening. Among these, we selected the clone showing the highest specificity and sensitivity. Sequencing of the selected clone revealed strong binding affinity to the QVELF motif of the immunizing peptide, a sequence conserved across several species and uniquely expressed by tmCLIC1, but not by other members of the CLIC protein family. Multiple experiments were conducted to assess the specificity and functional effectiveness of tmCLIC1omab in inhibiting tmCLIC1 activity (Fig. 6l–n, Supplementary Fig. 9a, b). We also demonstrated that the antibody retains its effect on primary human glioblastoma cells even after short incubation periods (Fig. 6o), and that it is efficiently internalized into cells, as shown using the pHrodo™ dye assay (Supplementary Fig. 9c, d). Additionally, we evaluated the in vivo tolerability of tmCLIC1omab in a mouse model of human colorectal cancer. Mice received biweekly injections of the antibody for 3 weeks. Body weight monitoring showed no significant difference between tmCLIC1omab-treated mice and those receiving an isotype control, indicating that the antibody is well tolerated in vivo (Fig. 6p) (The expanded version can be found in the Supplementary Materials and Methods section).

Electrophysiology and patch-clamp recordings

Patch electrodes (BB150F-8P with filaments, Science Products), with a diameter of 1.5 mm, were pulled from hard borosilicate glass on a Brown-Flaming P-87 puller (Sutter Instrument, Novato, CA) and fire polished to a tip diameter of 1–1.5 µm and an electrical resistance of 3–4 MΩ. Cells were voltage-clamped using an Axopatch 200B amplifier (Axon Instruments) in whole-cell configuration, in which, after formation of a giga-seal, the membrane patch is disrupted, providing a direct low-resistance access to the cell interior, allowing recordings from ion channels of the whole cell. The voltage step protocol used to isolate current/voltage (I/V) relationships consisted of 800 ms pulses from −60 mV to +60 mV (20 mV voltage steps). The holding potential was set according to the resting potential of the single cell (between −40 and −80 mV). tmCLIC1-mediated chloride currents were isolated from other ionic currents by perfusing 100 μM IAA94 dissolved in the bath solution and by mathematical subtraction of the residual current from the control. The solutions used were as follows: bath solution (mM): 125 NaCl, 5.5 KCl, 24 HEPES, 1 MgCl2, 0.5 CaCl2, 5 D-Glucose, 10 NaOH; pH 7.4; Pipette solution (mM): 135 KCl, 10 HEPES, 10 NaCl; pH 7.4. Analysis was performed using Clampfit 10.2 (Molecular Devices) and OriginPro 9.1. tmCLIC1-mediated current (IAA94-sensitive current) was measured by analytical subtraction of residual ionic current after addition of inhibitor from total current (ITOT) of the cell at each membrane potential tested. I/V relationship was constructed by plotting the averaged current density of the last 100 ms of the pulse against the corresponding membrane potential. Current density (pA/pF) was calculated as the ratio between the ionic current (pA) and cell capacitance (pF). Statistical analyses were performed comparing the slopes (proportional to channel conductance) of the I/V curves of the different groups.

Extracellular vesicle isolation and validation

To sequentially isolate EVs, the cell supernatant was centrifuged at 200 × g for 10 min, 300 × g for 10 min, 2000 × g for 10 min, 10,000 × g for 30 min, and 100,000 × g for 2 h, respectively (Optima XPN-100 ultracentrifuge, Beckman Coulter, USA). A beige/white pellet of EVs and a clear supernatant were evident post-centrifugation. After removing the supernatant and resuspending the small EV pellet in 1 ml of 1X PBS, the resulting pellet was then washed and centrifuged at 49,000 rpm for 30 min (Optima MAX 130 K Refrigerated Benchtop Ultracentrifuge, Beckman Coulter, USA). Isolated EVs were then visualized, measured, counted, and characterized in the range of 10–2000 nm using a Particle Size Analyzer (Particle Metrix ZetaView® Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis).78,79 We used size-mode analysis with ZetaView software (version 8.02.28) following calibration with polystyrene beads (105 and 500 nm). Samples were analyzed at a minimum of nine camera positions with 2-s video length at 21 °C.

Bioinformatic analysis

Novel interactions from the MaMTH screen were integrated with the known interactions from the Integrated Interaction Database IID ver. 2021-05.80 and further annotated with Gene Ontology Biological Process and Cellular Component terms (release 2022-11-03), and a subset of pathways from the pathDIP database ver. 4.81 Resulting network was built, annotated, and analyzed using NAViGaTOR ver. 3.0.19.82

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis between conditions was performed using a Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA in which the means of n = 3 replicates were compared between two conditions to assess significance. Analysis was undertaken using GraphPad Prism 7. Data are presented as mean ± SEM. P values less than 0.05 were considered significant and were marked with an asterisk as follows: *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001. Precise P values are provided in the figure legends.
