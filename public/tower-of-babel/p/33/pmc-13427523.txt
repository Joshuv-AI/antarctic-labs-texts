Thrombosed and calcified epididymal-vasal vascular structures mimicking a paratesticular mass: A case report
Authors: Ali Büyük, Mehmet Nuri Durgun, Ozan Can Çeküç, Serdar Alparslan Bostan, Yasin Pehlivan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThrombosed scrotal venous structures are rare causes of scrotal pain and may mimic paratesticular tumors. We report a 53-year-old man with painful rosary-like nodularity along the right vas deferens after prior hydrocelectomy. Doppler ultrasonography showed preserved testicular perfusion and a thrombosed epididymal appearance. Surgical exploration revealed nodular changes extending from the vas deferens to the epididymis. Testis-sparing epididymectomy with excision of the involved vasal segment was performed. Histopathology showed enlarged epididymal structures with dilated thrombosed vascular channels and gross calcified foci. Pain resolved completely after surgery without testicular atrophy.Keywords: Epididymis, Vas deferens, Scrotal pain, Paratesticular mass, Vascular thrombosis, Calcification, Case report

Introduction

Scrotal pain is a common urological presentation with a broad differential diagnosis that includes inflammatory, ischemic, traumatic, vascular, and neoplastic conditions. Although testicular torsion and epididymo-orchitis are usually prioritized in the acute setting, thrombosis of scrotal venous structures is an uncommon but important mimic. Published reports have most often involved the pampiniform plexus, thrombosed varicocele veins, or the spermatic/testicular vein.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 These lesions may present with acute pain, persistent discomfort, swelling, or a firm cord-like abnormality and can be confused with epididymo-orchitis, incarcerated inguinal hernia, torsion-related pathology, or paratesticular tumors.

Chronically thrombosed venous structures may calcify, producing phlebolith-like nodules. Phleboliths within varicocele veins have been described as palpable spermatic cord masses.8 However, a painful rosary-like nodular process extending along the vas deferens and epididymal region, with histopathological evidence of dilated thrombosed vascular channels and grossly evident calcified foci, appears exceptionally rare. We present a testis-sparing surgical case that broadens the differential diagnosis of painful extra-testicular nodular lesions.

Case presentation

A 53-year-old man presented with right scrotal pain. His history was notable for right hydrocelectomy performed 5 years earlier. Routine biochemical evaluation was unremarkable. On genital examination, the right testis was clinically preserved, whereas multiple firm nodules were palpated along the expected course of the right vas deferens. The lesions were arranged in a beaded, rosary-like configuration rather than as a solitary circumscribed mass, raising concern for an atypical paratesticular process involving the vasal-epididymal axis.

Scrotal color Doppler ultrasonography demonstrated preserved testicular perfusion, and no abnormality was reported in the testicular parenchyma. The right epididymal region was described as having a thrombosed appearance. These findings supported an extra-testicular thrombotic process localized to the right epididymal region, without evidence of intratesticular parenchymal involvement or impaired testicular perfusion. Because the patient had persistent pain, an unusual palpable nodular pattern, and indeterminate extra-testicular imaging findings, surgical exploration was performed.

At exploration, the right testis appeared grossly normal. The vas deferens was carefully separated from the surrounding spermatic cord structures. Nodular and irregular changes were identified along the vasal segment and appeared to continue toward the epididymis. As the abnormal process involved the epididymal region and was not confined to a discrete isolated vasal nodule, right epididymectomy with excision of the involved vasal segment was undertaken while preserving the testis.

Gross pathological examination documented irregular, tortuous gray-white tissue fragments, the largest measuring approximately 12 cm in length. Multiple calcified foci were identified macroscopically within the larger specimen fragment (Fig. 1). Histopathological evaluation demonstrated enlarged epididymal structures with dilated and thrombosed vascular channels. No malignancy was reported. At 1-month postoperative follow-up, the patient's scrotal pain had completely resolved. The right testis remained clinically normal in size, and there was no evidence of postoperative testicular atrophy.

Discussion

This case is clinically notable because several uncommon findings converged: painful presentation, preserved testicular perfusion, multiple palpable rosary-like nodules tracking along the vas deferens, Doppler-described thrombosed epididymal involvement, intraoperative continuity of the nodular process toward the epididymis, and histopathological evidence of dilated thrombosed vascular structures with gross calcified foci. Together, these findings support an unusual benign paratesticular vascular tumor-mimicker rather than a conventional cystic, inflammatory, or neoplastic epididymal lesion.

Thrombosis of paratesticular venous structures is rare and can be diagnostically challenging. Reported cases of pampiniform plexus, varicocele-related, and spermatic vein thrombosis often overlap clinically with more common causes of acute scrotum or with solid extra-testicular masses.1-7 Color Doppler ultrasonography is the key first-line diagnostic modality. When the thrombosed segment is clearly visualized, venous dilatation, reduced or absent intraluminal flow, and noncompressibility may support the diagnosis. Nevertheless, uncertainty may persist when the abnormality is described in the epididymal or paratesticular region rather than in a recognizable venous plexus, or when examination reveals an atypical nodular configuration. In such settings, exploration may be justified to exclude surgically relevant pathology and to treat persistent symptoms.

The macroscopic calcified foci in the present specimen are also relevant. Calcification may develop in chronically thrombosed vascular structures, producing phlebolith-like change. Lazarou et al. reported phleboliths in varicocele veins presenting as spermatic cord masses, illustrating that calcified venous lesions can imitate solid scrotal pathology.8 Our case is related conceptually but anatomically distinct: the palpable nodules followed the vas deferens and continued toward the epididymis, while histopathology demonstrated enlarged epididymal structures containing dilated thrombosed vascular channels. Because the available pathology report did not specifically classify the calcified foci microscopically, phlebolith-like change should be considered a cautious interpretation rather than a definitive diagnosis.

The likelihood of spontaneous pain resolution in this specific setting is unknown. Unlike isolated acute thrombosis of the pampiniform plexus, spermatic vein, or thrombosed varicocele, the present case involved a chronic-appearing, calcified, rosary-like epididymal-vasal process that clinically mimicked a paratesticular mass. The available literature on related scrotal venous thromboses consists mainly of case reports and small reviews, and standardized treatment recommendations are lacking. Several reports describe symptom resolution with conservative measures, including analgesia, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, scrotal support, observation, and selective anticoagulation.1,2,5,7 Therefore, surgical excision should not be considered mandatory when the diagnosis is secure, testicular perfusion is preserved, there is no concern for malignancy or other urgent pathology, and symptoms are improving. However, persistent or chronic pain has also been reported despite sonographic thrombus resolution.6 Exploration remains appropriate when pain is persistent, the palpable abnormality is atypical or mass-like, imaging findings are indeterminate, or alternative pathology cannot be confidently excluded. In our patient, the combination of persistent pain, beaded nodularity along the vas deferens, epididymal involvement, gross calcified foci, and indeterminate extra-testicular imaging findings justified testis-sparing diagnostic and therapeutic excision. The complete resolution of pain at 1-month follow-up supports the clinical relevance of the excised lesion, but does not establish that all similar lesions require surgical treatment.

The differential diagnosis of nodularity along the vas deferens and epididymis is broad.9 Vasitis nodosa is a benign reactive proliferation of the vas deferens, classically associated with prior vasectomy or obstructive injury, and can present as irregular nodular thickening.10,11 Chronic granulomatous inflammation, sperm granuloma, inflammatory pseudotumor, adenomatoid tumor, leiomyoma, and other paratesticular tumor-like lesions may also produce extra-testicular nodules.12 In the present case, however, the histopathological conclusion favored a non-neoplastic thrombotic vascular process, with no evidence of malignancy or proliferative vascular tumor.

The postoperative outcome supports the clinical relevance of the treated lesion. The patient's pain resolved completely within 1 month, and the preserved testis remained normal without atrophy. This suggests that, in selected symptomatic patients with an indeterminate painful paratesticular lesion involving the epididymal-vasal structures, testis-sparing excision can provide both diagnostic clarification and symptom relief. Awareness of this entity may help urologists interpret atypical physical examination findings when Doppler imaging suggests an extra-testicular thrombotic process.

Conclusion

Painful rosary-like nodularity along the vas deferens may rarely reflect a thrombosed and calcified vascular process involving the epididymal-vasal region. This case clinically mimicked a nodular paratesticular mass, was interpreted sonographically as a thrombosed epididymal abnormality, and was characterized histopathologically by dilated thrombosed vascular channels within enlarged epididymal tissue, with grossly evident calcified foci. Recognition of this unusual benign tumor-mimicker may broaden the differential diagnosis of painful extra-testicular nodules and support testis-preserving management when exploration is warranted. Although related paratesticular venous thromboses may sometimes be managed conservatively, the natural history of calcified epididymal-vasal thrombotic vascular lesions remains uncertain. Testis-sparing excision may be considered when symptoms persist or when atypical mass-like findings create diagnostic uncertainty.
