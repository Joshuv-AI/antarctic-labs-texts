Relative risk and risk factors of all-cause, natural-cause and external-cause mortality after psychiatric hospitalisation: a population-based electronic health-record study in Hong Kong
Authors: Joe Kwun Nam Chan, Simon Sai Yu Lui, Leo Long Tin Choo, Natalie Wing Tung Chu, Anna Wing Yan Chung, Corine Sau Man Wong, Hon Cheong So, Pak Chung Sham, Marco Solmi, Christoph U. Correll, Wing Chung Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundDischarge from psychiatric hospitalisation is associated with increased mortality risk, but risk estimates across fine-grained post-discharge intervals and identified risk factors are lacking.MethodsWe conducted a population-based study utilising a medical health record database of public healthcare services in Hong Kong. We identified all individuals aged ≥12 years who had been discharged from psychiatric inpatient units between January-01-2006 and December-31-2021. Individuals without history of psychiatric hospitalisation attending primary care clinics during the same period served as comparison group (i.e., primary-care clinics [PCC] cohort). Survival analyses estimated cumulative incidences and hazard ratios (HRs) for all-cause, natural-cause and external-cause mortality across discrete intervals from ≤1 month to >10 years post-discharge. Risk factors for excess mortality 1-year and 10-year post-discharge were evaluated.FindingsThe discharged cohort comprised 114,395 patients with 975,229 controls. Within the first-year post-discharge, within the deceased patient group, natural causes and suicide-related mortality accounted for 65.8% and 18.8% of deaths in the discharged cohort, and for 87.8% and 1.6% of deaths in the PCC cohort. The relative proportion of all deaths due to suicide in the discharged cohort decreased from 18.8% at 1-year to 11.7% at 10-year, while the relative proportion of all deaths from natural causes increased from 65.8% at 1-year to 73.3% at 10-year. Respiratory diseases (year 1 = 30.6%, year 10 = 35.0%) and cardiovascular diseases (year 1 = 10.7%, year 10 = 11.0%) were consistently leading causes of natural deaths. Relative all-cause mortality risk peaked within 1-month post-discharge (HR = 10.31 [95% confidence interval (CI) = 9.36–11.36]), remaining high at 1-year (HR = 3.40 [95% CI = 3.17–3.65]) and 10-year (HR = 2.61 [95% CI = 2.52–2.69]). External-cause mortality, particularly suicide-related mortality, showed extremely high risk during 1-month (HR = 78.54 [95% CI = 52.64–117.18]), and remained high at 1-year (HR = 42.40 [95% CI = 30.02–59.87]) and 10-year (HR = 10.29 [95% CI = 8.75–12.10]). Depression diagnosis at discharge conferred the highest short-term suicide risk, while substance/alcohol use disorders showed the highest long-term any-cause mortality risk. Risk factors for premature mortality within 1-year and 10-year post-discharge included male sex, old age, not receiving social/disability allowance, greater physical morbidity burden, self-harm history, and poor discharge psychiatric status. Ethnicity data were not available in the database.InterpretationThe immediate post-discharge phase is a critical window for targeted and diagnosis-specific intervention, including psychiatric/social/financial support. Future research should prioritise the development of robust risk prediction models that incorporate key risk factors identified to facilitate personalised care and improves post-discharge outcomes for high-risk individuals.Funding10.13039/501100005847Health and Medical Research Fund.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

The transition from inpatient psychiatric care to community living is recognised as a critical and vulnerable period for individuals with mental disorders. Recent research has focused on quantifying the risks of all-cause mortality and suicide-related mortality following psychiatric discharge, as well as understanding how these risks evolve over time. We conducted a PubMed search for English articles from inception to October 30, 2025, using the terms (post-discharge OR discharge OR inpatient OR hospitalisation) AND (mortality OR suicide OR death). The most recent meta-analysis, published in 2019 and comprising 71 studies, found that suicide was the leading cause of death in the immediate post-discharge period, while cardiovascular disease became predominant over the medium- and long-term. Another recent meta-analysis from 2025, synthesising data from 83 studies, identified previous self-harm, suicidal ideation and depressive symptoms as the strongest risk factors for post-discharge suicide-related mortality. However, studies were largely limited by methodological constraints, including focussing solely on discharge from the first hospitalisation, adult populations, suicide-related outcomes alone, employing broad post-discharge time intervals that obscure more nuanced temporal patterns, and offering limited exploration of risk factors relevant to post-discharge mortality. Moreover, the overwhelming majority of studies originate from Western countries and have limited applicability of their findings to non-Western settings, where healthcare systems, sociocultural contexts, and population health indices may differ substantially. To date, there has been no comprehensive study examining both suicide-related and natural-cause mortality across detailed follow-up intervals, nor a thorough assessment of relevant risk factors, in Asian populations. This gap underscores the need for research that addresses these limitations and enhances the understanding of post-discharge mortality risk in diverse global contexts.

Added value of this study

To the best of our knowledge, this is among the first comprehensive studies in Asia, and one of the few worldwide, to examine the cumulative incidence and relative risks of all-cause, natural-cause, and external-cause mortality, including specific causes such as accidental death and suicide, at fine-grained time intervals following discharge from psychiatric hospitalisation. Data from the territory-wide electronic medical record database of public healthcare services in Hong Kong were utilised. The analysis included 114,395 individuals discharged from psychiatric hospitalisation in 2006–2021, compared to 975,229 patients attending primary care clinics but without records of psychiatric hospitalisation in the same period. Our findings revealed that all-cause mortality risk was highest within the first month post-discharge (hazard ratio [HR] = 10.31 [95% confidence interval (CI) = 9.36–11.36]), but remained significantly elevated at one year (HR = 3.40 [95% CI = 3.17–3.65]) and even at ten years (HR = 2.61 [95% CI = 2.52–2.69]). The risk of external-cause mortality, particularly due to suicide, was extremely high in the first month, with the HR exceeding 70-fold, and declining over time but staying markedly elevated at one and ten years (HRs >10). Importantly, >60% of post-discharge deaths, including those in the first month, were attributable to physical diseases, most notably respiratory and cardiovascular diseases. Discharge diagnoses of depression were associated with the highest short-term risk of suicide-related mortality, while substance and alcohol use disorders conferred the greatest long-term risk of all-cause mortality. Significant risk factors for premature mortality within both one- and ten-year follow-up included male sex, adulthood, absence of social or disability allowance, higher physical morbidity burden, history of self-harm, and poor psychiatric status at discharge.

Implications of all the available evidence

Overall, current evidence underscores the immediate post-discharge period as a uniquely vulnerable phase in the recovery journey, warranting focused and proactive interventions. The evidence aligns with NICE guidelines, which advocate for structured support and timely follow-up, ideally within the first week after hospital discharge. It is also noted that there is substantial excess mortality from physical illnesses in individuals discharged from psychiatric hospitalisation, especially in the long term, which highlights the importance of ensuring that healthcare providers address both mental and physical health challenges following psychiatric hospitalisation. Psychiatric admission presents a critical opportunity for comprehensive medical assessment and initiation of preventive measures targeting physical health. Furthermore, the observed variation in mortality risk according to discharge diagnosis emphasises the necessity of tailored, diagnosis-specific interventions during both the short- and long-term post-discharge periods. Future research should prioritise the development of robust risk prediction models that incorporate key risk factors identified in this and other studies, enabling high-quality personalised care, including enhanced social and financial support, to improve outcomes for individuals at highest post-discharge mortality risk.

Introduction

The transition from inpatient psychiatric care to community settings represents a particularly critical and vulnerable period. Evidence from population-based studies demonstrated substantially increased risk of suicide-related mortality shortly after discharge.1,2 Moreover, a recent meta-analysis revealed that, over a two-year follow-up period, the cumulative mortality from natural causes, especially cardiovascular diseases, exceeded deaths by suicide.3 These findings highlighted the complexity and evolving nature of post-discharge risks. Understanding of how such post-discharge risks would change over time and their associated risk factors are crucial for optimising mental healthcare strategies and resource allocation to better support discharged patients.

Despite a growing body of research on post-discharge risks of mortality among individuals with psychiatric hospitalisations, several critical methodological limitations persist in the literature from both the Western and Asian regions. First, many studies only focused on patients’ first psychiatric admission,2,4, 5, 6, 7 thereby overlooking patients having recurrent hospitalisations who may be at even greater mortality risk. In fact, previous studies indicated that suicide risk increased with the number of psychiatric admissions.8,9 Second, the majority of studies were limited to adult populations,5,6,10,11 but did not consider post-discharge risks among children and adolescents. Therefore, how the risk profiles may differ across age groups, particularly among younger patients, remains unclear. Third, while suicide has been the primary outcome of interest in the extant literature, there is a relative paucity of research addressing natural-cause mortality following psychiatric discharge.1,4,6,8, 9, 10, 11 Fourth, prior studies often reported outcomes at broad time intervals, such as three months, one year, or three years post-discharge,4, 5, 6, 7,12,13 without capturing more granular temporal patterns, such as the first month after discharge and over 10 years, which might obscure short-time peaks and long-term trends, precluding more precise identification of critical periods for intervention. Fifth, there has been limited investigation into the risk factors associated with mortality, especially due to suicide, in the immediate post-discharge period.1,2,4,5,9,12 These shortcomings constitute significant knowledge gaps hindering development of targeted interventions and effective preventive strategies for individuals transitioning from inpatient to community care. Furthermore, the majority of evidence to date has been derived from Western countries,11,12 undermining the generalisability of findings to non-Western regions, which could vary widely in healthcare systems, sociocultural environments, and population health indices. To our knowledge, no comprehensive study has been conducted to date to examine both suicide-related and natural-cause mortality across fine-grained follow-up intervals, and to investigate the relevant risk factors.

To address the aforementioned research gaps, we conducted this large, population-based study to systematically evaluate the cumulative and relative risk of all-cause, natural-cause (i.e., any deaths owing to physical diseases) and external-cause mortality (i.e., any deaths owing to causes other than physical diseases) for discrete and more fine-grained post-discharge periods (ranging from <1 month to >10 years) in a prevalent sample of individuals discharged from psychiatric hospital care in Hong Kong, a metropolitan city located at the south-eastern tip of China with a population of approximately 7.5 million people, utilising data retrieved from a territory-wide medical-record database of public healthcare services. A comprehensive array of data, encompassing demographic variables, physical and psychiatric morbidities, history of self-harm, and hospitalisation characteristics, was included to identify potential risk factors for post-discharge mortality.

Methods

Data source

Data of patients were retrieved from the Clinical Data Analysis and Reporting System (CDARS), a territory-wide electronic health record (EHR) database developed by the Hospital Authority (HA) which is a statutory body delivering government-subsidised, universal health coverage to all HK residents (approximately 92% being Chinese) by managing all public hospitals, specialist and general outpatient clinics in HK. CDARS is an integrated, longitudinal EHR system capturing clinical data across all healthcare settings of HA facilities since 1995. Clinical data are collected and entered into the computerised clinical management system by treating clinicians and other healthcare professionals, and are transferred to CDARS for audit and research purposes. CDARS generates unique, anonymised patient identifiers to protect patient privacy and to link all medical records. This database has been used previously to generate high-quality population-based studies on various physical diseases and mental disorders.14,15

Study population, inclusion and exclusion criteria

We identified all individuals aged ≥12 years who had been discharged from psychiatric inpatient units January-01-2006 to 31 December-31-2021 (i.e., the discharged cohort). The analysis was therefore based on a population-based cohort, and a formal a priori sample size calculation was not applicable. Younger children aged below 12 years were excluded as psychiatric inpatient admission among children younger than 12 years were uncommon and often managed in paediatric rather than psychiatric units. For patients who had psychiatric hospitalisations both before and during the study period, we defined their index episode as the first hospitalisation during the study period (i.e., not first-ever but a recurrent psychiatric hospitalisation). The discharge date served as the start of follow-up. For each discharged patient, we recorded the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) 10 psychiatric diagnoses at discharge, including (1) substance and alcohol use disorders (SAUD; F10–F19), (2) schizophrenia-spectrum disorders (SSD; F20–F29), (3) bipolar disorder (BD; F30, F31, F34.0), (4) depressive disorders (F32, F33, F34.1), (5) neurotic, stress-related, and somatoform disorders (NSSD; F40–F48), and (6) other psychiatric disorders. As patients could have >1 psychiatric diagnosis at discharge, they could be included in multiple diagnostic groups in subsequent analyses. As a proxy for the general population, one million individuals were randomly selected from a pool of people who had ever attended the primary-care clinics (PCC) of the public healthcare system. Individuals from the PCC sample who had no record of psychiatric hospitalisation before or during the study period constituted the comparison group for the relative risk of adverse outcomes (i.e., PCC cohort). For the comparison group, the date of first PCC attendance was treated as the start of follow-up. All participants were followed from their respective start dates until death or December-31-2021, whichever came first. Members of our authorship team involved in the planning, data analysis, writing and interpretation of this study have clinical perspectives in mental disorders, but we did not consult individuals diagnosed with mental disorders or discharged from psychiatric hospitalisation for this work.

Study outcomes and covariates

The outcomes under investigation included all-cause and cause-specific mortality. Causes of death were classified according to ICD-10 codes and divided into natural (A00–R99) and external causes (V01–Y98). External causes were further categorised into accidents (V01–X59, Y85–Y86) and suicide (X60–X84, Y10–Y34, Y87.0, Y87.2).

Utilising the ample clinical information captured in the EHR database, we selected a comprehensive set of covariates at baseline, including demographic variables, physical and psychiatric morbidities, history of self-harm, and index hospitalisation characteristics. Demographic variables comprised age, sex, calendar year at index admission, receipt of social/disability allowance (as proxy for socioeconomic status), and catchment area of public healthcare receipt. Physical morbidity was evaluated using the Charlson Comorbidity index (CCI), as well as other relevant chronic physical diseases including hypertension and dyslipidaemia. Psychiatric morbidities included SAUD, SSD, BD, depressive disorders, NSSD, and other psychiatric disorders. For the discharged cohort, additional hospitalisation characteristics were obtained, including Health of the Nation Outcome Scales (HoNOS) scores at admission and discharge,16 history of psychiatric hospitalisation, length of index inpatient stay, and legal status of the index admission (voluntary/informal vs. compulsory). Appendix pp.2 and 3 shows the ICD9-CM and ICD10 codes for physical and psychiatric diagnoses, respectively. Since all covariates were complete, no imputation or case-wise exclusion due to missingness was required.

Statistical analysis

Cumulative incidences of each adverse outcome at 1-month, 3-month, 1-year and 10-year timepoints were estimated for both cohorts using the Kaplan–Meier survival analysis method, which accounts for censoring. To assess the risk of adverse outcomes following discharge from psychiatric hospitals, Cox-proportional hazards regression models were employed, adjusting for covariates that differed significantly between the two cohorts. Risks were evaluated separately within the discrete follow-up intervals, i.e., ≤1 month, >1–3 months, >3–6 months, >6 months–1 year, >1–5 years, >5–10 years and >10 years. For each interval, only patients who survived to the start of the interval were included, with their follow-up censored at the end of the respective interval. Regarding the cause-specific mortality, the Fine and Grey sub-distribution hazard model was additionally performed as a post-hoc sensitivity analysis to account for the competing risk of death causes.

Associations of the adverse outcomes with specific mental disorder diagnoses at discharge (SAUD, SSD, BD, depressive disorders and NSSD), as well as prior admission status before the index discharge (first hospitalisation vs. a history of previous admissions), were examined by repeating cumulative incidence estimation and Cox-regression analyses in these subgroups. The PCC cohort served as the reference group for the Cox models. Additional multivariable Cox-regression models were conducted within the discharged cohort to identify risk factors for excess mortality (all-cause, natural-cause and external-cause mortality, and suicide) at 1-year and 10-year post-discharge, including demographic variables, physical morbidities, history of self-harm and characteristics of index hospitalisation. Bonferroni correction for multiple comparison was also applied on the risk factor analysis. Subgroup analyses were also performed to explore relative risks of adverse outcomes following discharge vs. the PCC cohort, stratified by sex (female vs. male), age at admission (≤17 years, 18–34 years, 35–44 years, 45–64 years vs. ≥65 years), receipt of social/disability allowance (yes vs. no), CCI score (0, 1–2 vs. ≥3), and history of self-harm (yes vs. no). Hazard ratios (HRs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported for all relative risk estimates. Statistical analyses were performed using R (versions 4.1.2), and two-sided p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Ethics statement

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Hong Kong/Hospital Authority HKWest Cluster (UW23-540). Since this study was conducted on data from an EHR database with strictly anonymised patient records, the requirement for informed consent was waived. Currently, the study design and analysis were largely in line with the study protocol, except that individuals with mental disorders in the PCC sample were retained and the Fine and Grey model was newly added as a post-hoc sensitivity analysis in response to reviewers’ suggestions.

Role of the funding source

The study was supported by the Health and Medical Research Fund. The funders had no involvement in study design, data collection, data analyses, data interpretation, or the writing of the report.

Results

The study included 114,395 patients in the discharged cohort (51,710 men and 62,685 women) and 975,229 patients in the PCC cohort (441,523 men and 533,706 women). The two cohorts differed significantly regarding age, sex, calendar year of index admission, social/disability allowance receipt, psychiatric diagnoses, history of self-harm, physical morbidities and catchment areas for healthcare services, and such differences were adjusted for in the subsequent Cox models. Among discharged patients, the most common diagnoses were SSD (35.2%), depressive disorders (14.6%) and NSSD (13.6%). Discussion

To our knowledge, this study is among the first few studies worldwide to comprehensively assess the cumulative incidence and relative risks of all-cause, natural-cause and external-cause mortality, as well as specific external causes including accidental death and suicide-related mortality at fine-grained time intervals following discharge from psychiatric hospitalisation. We also investigated risk factors associated with increased mortality risk at 1-year and 10-year after psychiatric discharge. Results indicated that within the first-year post-discharge, approximately 3.4% of patients died from any causes, compared to 0.9% in a primary care cohort. Strikingly, suicide accounted for 18.8% of all deaths in the discharged group. Consistent with previous research,17 we found that the relative risk of all-cause mortality, especially suicide-related mortality, was highest within the first-month post-discharge (HR for suicide exceeding 100-fold), compared to individuals without a history of psychiatric hospitalisation. This elevated risk for suicide-related mortality remained pronounced (HR > 20) throughout the first year after discharge, gradually declining thereafter. The estimates for mortality risk shortly after discharge (e.g., three-month post-discharge) were in fact heterogeneous in prior registry studies,2,5,12 ranging from 5-fold to 23-fold for all-cause mortality, and ranging from 50-fold to >100-fold for suicide, and the risk estimates in our result fall within these ranges. The markedly elevated risk of suicide-related mortality immediately following discharge may be attributable to the re-emergence of pre-existing social stressors and new challenges associated with transitioning out of hospital care.18,19 These findings highlighted the importance of recognising the immediate post-discharge period as a critical and vulnerable phase in the recovery process, necessitating targeted interventions. Our results also echoed the NICE guidelines,20 which recommend arranging support and early follow-up appointments, particularly within the first week, after discharge. Effective strategies to support this transition among discharged patients may include comprehensive pre-discharge and post-discharge psychoeducation, formal needs-based assessments, and enhanced communication among inpatient teams, outpatient care providers, patients and families.20 In this context, providing social/disability allowance may also mitigate against mortality risk, including suicide-related mortality, especially during the first-year post-discharge as indicated by our findings.

Despite the excessively heightened risk of suicide-related mortality relative to outpatient cohorts following discharge from psychiatric hospitalisation, our findings revealed that the majority of the absolute post-discharge deaths (>50%), including those occurring within the first month, were due to physical diseases, most notably respiratory and cardiovascular diseases. This trend was particularly driven by the high cumulative incidence of physical diseases among discharged patients aged ≥65, which likely reflected the higher rates of physical comorbidities in that group. Our results aligned with a UK population-based study, which also reported that most deaths within three months post-discharge among older adults were from natural causes.5 These findings highlight the need for holistic post-discharge care in older adults, encompassing thorough physical health assessments and treatments, appropriate adjustments to living arrangements, and strengthened social support.21, 22, 23 Consistent with a previous meta-analysis,3 we also observed that, over longer-term follow-up, the proportion of deaths attributable to natural causes surpassed that of suicide even among younger discharged patients. Factors contributing to such excess natural-cause mortality may include side effects of psychotropic medications (although the net effect of antipsychotics and antidepressants seems to be protective against early death),24,25 self-neglect, unhealthy lifestyle, diagnostic overshadowing, and, especially, the greater prevalence and insufficiently monitored and under-treated physical comorbidity.21,26, 27, 28 Therefore, healthcare providers should address psychiatric inpatients’ long-term physical health needs and modify their lifestyle after psychiatric hospitalisation. Importantly, psychiatric admission can be a highly valuable window for comprehensive medical evaluation, including cardiovascular risk assessment, and for implementing preventive physical health measures.

All major psychiatric diagnoses, including SAUD, SSD, BD, depressive disorders and NSSD, were associated with significantly increased mortality risk during follow-up. Notably, patients with depressive disorders experienced the highest cumulative incidence of all-cause mortality, natural-cause mortality and suicide-related mortality within the first 12 months post-discharge. In contrast, patients with SAUD had the greatest cumulative incidence of accidental death during this period. Over a 10-year follow-up, the excess mortality risk of any causes remained most pronounced for patients with SAUD, consistent with a prior meta-analysis indicating the largest life expectancy gap for patients with SAUD.29 An earlier Danish case–control study revealed substantially elevated suicide-related mortality risk (relative risk >200) within one-week post-discharge for patients with mood disorders.9 Recent Danish cohort studies showed the highest immediate post-discharge suicide-related mortality rates among patients with depressive and anxiety/stress disorders,1 while SAUD conferred the highest absolute risk for self-harm (including non-fatal self-harm and completed suicide) at both one-year and 10-year follow-ups after discharge.4 A UK cohort study found comparable risk of readmission following a suicide attempt among patients with depressive disorders, anxiety disorders and SAUD, all exceeding the risk observed in SSD.8 In a large US cohort study, the greatest suicide-related mortality risk within 90 days of discharge was observed among patients with depressive disorders and BD, followed by patients with SSD and SAUD.6 A recent Taiwanese cohort study further demonstrated that SAUD was associated with the highest risk of external-cause mortality (mainly due to suicide) over five years post-discharge, compared to other major psychiatric diagnoses.12 Taken together, these findings suggested that a discharge diagnosis of depressive disorders appeared to confer a particularly high short-term risk of suicide-related mortality, whereas SAUD was associated with a sustained, long-term elevated suicide-related mortality risk. These findings underscored the importance of targeted, diagnosis-specific screenings and interventions during both the immediate and extended periods following psychiatric hospitalisation. For instance, in the high-risk first-month post-discharge, patients with depressive disorders might benefit from an intensive suicide prevention package comprising structured safety planning before discharge and weekly proactive follow-up contacts focussing on mood and suicidal ideation, whereas patients with SSD might receive an intervention emphasising relapse prevention and adherence support, such as early provision of long-acting injectable antipsychotics where appropriate, weekly community visits, and systematic monitoring of psychotic symptoms and early warning signs.

Moreover, we examined the risk factors contributing to the substantially elevated mortality risk within 1-year and 10-year period following discharge from psychiatric hospitalisation. Consistent with prior research on 1-year post-discharge mortality risk,5 we found that men had a higher cumulative incidence of both external-cause and natural-cause mortality than women at both 1 year and 10 years post-discharge. However, our data also revealed that discharged women experienced a greater relative risk increase for external-cause mortality, particularly suicide-related mortality, compared to women in primary care cohort. Within the discharged cohort, adolescents and younger adults exhibited a lower absolute risk of premature mortality compared to the elderly. Nevertheless, risks of discharged adolescents and younger adults remained substantially higher than their same-age counterparts in the primary care cohort. We also observed that individuals who did not receive social/disability allowance exhibited an increased mortality risk, with the relative risk for external-cause mortality and suicide appearing more pronounced than for natural-cause mortality. Such findings may be related to higher self-stigma among discharged patients with higher socioeconomic status, possibly leading to delays in diagnosis and treatment, and increased suicide risk.30 Social/disability allowance could also reduce financial hardship and promote access to basic needs (e.g., healthcare, support networks), thereby improving the overall physical and mental well-being. It is also plausible that individuals who have greater illness severity may struggle to navigate the social welfare system, resulting in reduced receipt of benefits among some of those at the highest risk. Finally, a higher CCI score was associated with an increased risk of mortality, likely reflecting the impact of a greater burden of physical comorbidity, such as reduced quality of life, chronic pain, loss of autonomy, and hopelessness,31 on both physical and mental health status.

Within the discharged cohort, we also found that a history of self-harm, and higher HoNOS scores at discharge were independently associated with an increased risk of premature mortality at both 1 year and 10 years post-discharge. Additionally, prior psychiatric hospitalisation was linked to an increased risk of all-cause mortality during the long-term interval following discharge. This finding may suggest that individuals with greater illness severity were especially vulnerable to physical health disparities and suicide-related mortality after discharge, underscoring the need for more intensive clinical monitoring and enhanced safety planning for this subgroup of high-risk patients. Previous research similarly found that a history of self-harm and a greater number of psychiatric hospitalisations were associated with elevated suicide-related mortality risk.9,32 Specifically, the consistent risk factors for different causes of death and follow-up periods (i.e., short- and long-term intervals) may imply the presence of shared underlying vulnerabilities that persist over time, emphasising the need for comprehensive, sustained, and multifaceted post-discharge interventions to effectively reduce premature mortality among individuals discharged from psychiatric hospitalisation. Future research should also prioritise the development and validation of risk prediction models that incorporate the key risk factors identified in this study (e.g., psychiatric comorbidities, allowance status, self-harm history), leveraging artificial intelligence or machine learning algorithms, which have shown promise in enhancing the accuracy of mortality risk prediction.33 Studies further investigating the synergistic effect of these risk factors in post-discharge mortality risk via more advanced modelling approaches might also represent a promising avenue for improving mortality risk prediction. Such approaches could better translate our and other findings into personalised clinical decision-making and more effective post-discharge care.

Several limitations warrant consideration in interpreting the study results. First, lifestyle variables such as physical activity, dietary patterns and smoking, as well as other important factors that may influence mortality risk, such as social support and loneliness, were not adequately recorded in the medical database, and therefore could not be included as potential covariates or risk factors. These unmeasured confounders may, however, partly account for the observed mortality differences between the discharged group and PCC cohort. Second, since the current study design that compared the discharged group with the PCC cohort did not allow us to fully disentangle the casual effect of hospitalisation from the effects of underling psychiatric disorders and their comorbidities. Nonetheless, the pronounced early peak in mortality risk immediately after discharge suggests that discharge-related factors are likely to contribute substantially to this high-risk period over stable characteristics such as diagnosis and chronic comorbidity. Third, since children aged below 12 years were excluded from this analysis, the findings may not be generalisable to younger children. Fourth, the PCC cohort was used as a comparison group to approximate the general population. However, it is possible that PCC users may be more likely to seek help or better engaged with health services, which could be associated with earlier detection and management of physical and mental illness, and potentially lower mortality. This might tend to make the PCC cohort healthier than the underlying population and could bias the HR away from the null. On the other hand, PCC users may include a higher proportion of people with chronic physical diseases compared with individuals who rarely or never seek medical care, with might increase mortality within the PCC cohort and bias the HR towards the null. Since these mechanisms operate in opposite directions, the net impact on our estimates is uncertain. Fifth, psychiatric diagnoses were analysed in non-mutually exclusive groups. The mortality risk estimate for a given diagnostic category may be confounded by comorbid conditions. That said, psychiatric comorbidity is highly prevalent among individuals with mental disorders, especially in inpatient settings. The current approach may provide a more clinically realistic picture of risk. In line with prior research,2,6,11 the estimates are best understood as reflecting risk associated with having that diagnosis within the context of typical real-world comorbidity patterns, rather than as “pure” diagnosis-specific effects. Sixth, the extracted dataset did not include information on the total number, timing, or detailed sequence of psychiatric hospitalisations either before or during the observation period, nor did it contain separate records for each admission that would have allowed us to construct an admission-level dataset. As a result, we were unable to apply more sophisticated analytic approaches, such as hierarchical or multilevel models clustering hospitalisations within patients (to account for within-person correlation and admission-level variation), redefining the index admission as a randomly selected or last hospitalisation episode (to assess the robustness of results and better capture clinical status closer to death), or treating hospitalisation as a time-varying exposure (to more effectively capture changing risk over time). In the current study, we defined the first psychiatric hospitalisation within the observation period as the index admission. This choice, however, provided a longer potential follow-up period and therefore enabled a comprehensive examination of long-term mortality patterns. Seventh, since patient data were obtained from medical records database of public healthcare services managed by the HA, individuals receiving private psychiatric care were not included. However, HA is the predominant provider of psychiatric services in HK, and thus the risk of selection bias of hospitalised patients should be minimised. Eighth, although mortality reasons were drawn from the regional deaths registry from the Immigration Department, misattribution, especially missed suicide-related mortality, cannot be ruled out. Nineth, ethnicity data were not available in the database, precluding us from investigating the potential effect of ethnicity on the post-discharge mortality pattern. That said, HK residents are predominantly Chinese (92%), and thus the effect of ethnicity on the results should be minimal. Lastly, since the indications and thresholds for psychiatric hospitalisation differ across countries, this post-discharge mortality risk profile should be interpreted with caution and may not be directly-generalisable to other healthcare settings.

In conclusion, risks of all-cause, natural-cause and external-cause mortality are substantially elevated in the first year after psychiatric discharge, with suicide-related mortality risk peaking in the first month and natural causes accounting for most of the absolute increase in deaths over the short- and long-term follow-up. Discharge diagnoses of depressive disorders and SAUD were associated with especially high short-term and sustained mortality risks, respectively. The immediate post-discharge period represents a time of high vulnerability, particularly among males, the elderly, patients with higher physical morbidity, history of self-harm, worse psychiatric status at discharge, and those not receiving social/disability allowances. The increased mortality risk among discharged patients who are not having social/disability allowance indicates that social and financial factors are important modifying issues that can be addressed. Targeted, diagnosis-specific post-discharge interventions and better risk prediction models should be used to promote personalised clinical decision-making and effective post-discharge care, particularly for high-risk discharged psychiatric patients.
