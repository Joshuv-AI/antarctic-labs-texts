Triglyceride-Rich Lipoprotein Metabolism and Cardiovascular Risk in Diabetes: Bedside to Bench to Bedside
Authors: Henry N. Ginsberg, Karin E. Bornfeldt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Type 1 diabetes and type 2 diabetes are associated with an increased risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), manifested as myocardial infarction, ischemic stroke, and peripheral artery disease. The increased ASCVD risk in diabetes cannot be fully explained by traditional risk factors. This review highlights the dysregulation of TRLs (triglyceride-rich lipoproteins; VLDL [very low-density lipoprotein] and chylomicrons), and their remnants, as contributors to ASCVD risk in diabetes by examining 6 typical clinical cases integrated with mechanistic data obtained in preclinical models. Type 2 diabetes is often associated with insulin resistance and increased hepatic secretion of VLDL particles, which are enlarged by an increased lipid load, increased intestinal secretion of chylomicrons, and reduced hepatic clearance of both VLDL and chylomicron remnants. The increased levels of TRLs in turn contribute to the predominance of smaller, denser LDL (low-density lipoprotein) particles and smaller cholesterol-depleted HDL (high-density lipoprotein) particles compared with people without diabetes. When type 2 diabetes occurs in individuals with genetic variants causing altered function of proteins stimulating TRL clearance (LPL, APOC2, APOA5, GPIHBP1, LMF1, or LDLR) or suppressing TRL clearance (APOC3, ANGPTL3, ANGPTL4, ANGPTL8), the result is a compounded accumulation of TRLs and their remnants in plasma and an associated increased ASCVD risk. VLDL secretion is usually unaltered in individuals with well-controlled type 1 diabetes, but ASCVD risk is increased when insulin resistance and defects in TRL clearance are also present. Mechanistically, TRLs enhance ASCVD risk at least in part by exacerbating local vascular inflammation caused by the accumulation of free cholesterol and triglyceride lipolysis products. Because cardiovascular outcome trials targeting triglyceride levels to date have been mostly futile, the field is now focused on trials targeting proteins involved in TRL clearance. Carefully determining inclusion and exclusion criteria for such trials will be critical for advancing our understanding of how to better prevent ASCVD in individuals living with diabetes.

The prevalence of diabetes is increasing worldwide, with ≈12% of the US population now having diabetes and many more being at risk of transitioning from prediabetes to frank diabetes. The most common forms of diabetes are type 2 diabetes (T2D), which is often preceded by a state of prediabetes or metabolic syndrome, and type 1 diabetes (T1D), a less prevalent autoimmune form of diabetes. All forms of diabetes are due to defective insulin secretion or insulin resistance (IR) in target tissues, or both, leading to reduced ability of insulin to stimulate glucose uptake into target tissues and to suppress lipolysis. Although prediabetes and diabetes are often thought of solely as conditions of dysfunctional blood glucose regulation, insulin also has important actions on lipid metabolism,1 which contribute to the well-known lipid abnormalities associated with diabetes.

Both T1D and T2D increase the risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD).2,3 In a large meta-analysis, diabetes doubled the likelihood of having an ASCVD event, after adjusting for age, sex, smoking status, body mass index, and systolic blood pressure.2 Moreover, individuals with long-duration T1D, despite lacking many traditional ASCVD risk factors, show a very high burden of coronary artery calcium, a measure of atherosclerosis.4 Because of the growing population now living with diabetes, including children and youth, it is critical to identify diabetes-associated mediators of ASCVD risk so that cardiovascular events can be prevented.

An emerging risk factor for increased ASCVD risk in individuals with diabetes is dysfunctional metabolism of TRLs (triglyceride-rich lipoproteins) and their remnants. In this review, we highlight 6 clinical model cases of diabetes associated with dysfunctional TRL metabolism and increased ASCVD risk, as well as supporting mechanistic data from preclinical models. Important take-home messages are that the effects of diabetes on TRL metabolism can be compounded by an individual’s genetic profile, and that underlying IR, which may be independently inherited, can occur both in individuals with T1D and T2D, again compounding dyslipidemia and ASCVD risk. We also review therapies used to correct the dyslipidemia of diabetes, focusing particularly on the mechanisms whereby these agents may target TRLs.

Definition of TRLs and TRL Subpopulations

TRLs are produced by the intestine, which releases large triglyceride-rich chylomicron particles into the blood after a meal containing dietary fat, and by the liver, which produces VLDL (very low-density lipoprotein) for transport of hepatic triglyceride to adipose tissue and cardiac and skeletal muscle.5 In humans, the structural protein of VLDL is APOB100, synthesized in the liver, whereas chylomicrons carry APOB48, a truncated form of APOB100, synthesized in the small intestine. Hydrolysis of the triglyceride cargo of TRLs by several different lipases, particularly LPL (lipoprotein lipase), but also HL (hepatic lipase) and possibly EL (endothelial lipase), produces smaller remnant lipoprotein particles, which are depleted of triglycerides relative to cholesterol. Importantly, lipolysis contributes to the large range of TRL sizes in circulation, ranging from ≈500 to 70 nm in diameter for lipolyzed chylomicrons, 80 to 30 nm for VLDL, and 25 nm for the smallest TRL remnants.5,6 Remnants derived from chylomicrons and VLDL can be distinguished in humans by the presence of APOB48 or APOB100, respectively.

TRL remnants are either cleared from circulation by the liver or, in the case of VLDL remnants, a fraction is converted to LDL (low-density lipoprotein). The main receptor responsible for clearing APOB100–containing lipoproteins is the LDLR (LDL receptor), which binds APOB100 present on VLDL remnants (also called intermediate-density lipoproteins) and LDL. The LDLR-lipoprotein complex is then internalized for degradation of the lipoprotein components in lysosomes, whereas the LDLR is recycled back to the hepatocyte plasma membrane.7 In contrast, APOB48 present on chylomicron remnants does not bind to the LDLR. These lipoproteins are cleared instead by the liver via interactions of the abundant APOE on these particles with the LDLR, the LRP1 (LDLR-related protein 1), and syndecan 1.8

Characteristic Phenotype of Dyslipidemia in T2D

The typical phenotype of dyslipidemia in individuals with T2D is characterized by elevations in plasma triglyceride levels compared with levels seen in nondiabetic individuals. Elevated levels of triglycerides result from increases in the particle number and size of VLDL, with a predominance of larger, more buoyant VLDL1 particles.9 An additional common characteristic of T2D dyslipidemia is a shift in the composition and size of LDL, so that small, dense, cholesteryl ester (CE)–poor LDL particles predominate.10 Thus, for any plasma level of LDL cholesterol (LDL-C), there are more LDL particles in individuals with T2D than in those without diabetes. LDL in T2D is not only CE-depleted but also enriched in triglycerides, and the abnormal composition, plus the small size of LDL in individuals with T2D, can reduce the affinity of the particles for the LDLR. Overall, T2D is associated with increased plasma levels of APOB100, the defining protein of the atherogenic lipoproteins that include VLDL, VLDL remnants, and LDL.

In contrast to the elevated plasma concentrations of APOB100 lipoproteins, T2D is almost always associated with reduced levels of HDL (high-density lipoprotein) cholesterol (HDL-C) due to fewer, smaller HDL particles that are relatively CE-depleted.10 This is particularly true when hypertriglyceridemia is also present, as is usually the case in individuals with T2D. Total APOB100 levels are highly correlated with non–HDL-C levels (the sum of cholesterol in VLDL, VLDL remnants, and LDL particles), although they can be discordant in patients with dyslipidemia.11 In contrast, plasma levels of APOA1, which is the predominant structural protein on HDL particles, are reduced in dyslipidemic individuals even in the absence of hyperglycemia due to the action of CETP (CE transfer protein) and lipases discussed below.10 However, levels of triglyceride increase and levels of HDL-C decrease further in association with worsening hyperglycemia. T2D seems to exert a greater negative impact on several cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk factors in women, including triglycerides, HDL-C levels, and LDL particle size.12,13

Although T2D is considered a dual metabolic disorder arising from both IR and either relative or absolute insulin deficiency,14 the major driving force of dyslipidemia in individuals with T2D is IR. Importantly, evidence from the Insulin Resistance Atherosclerosis Study and other clinical studies suggests that the relationship between IR and dyslipidemia extends across major ethnic groups, including African Americans and Hispanic Americans.15 However, the impact of both insulin deficiency and hyperglycemia can be significant at several key metabolic steps, leading to heterogeneity in the phenotype. Further variability derives from differences in genetic background among individuals with T2D, particularly when there are differences in major genes affecting lipid and lipoprotein metabolism.

Why Can Patients With T2D Have Markedly Different Lipid Profiles?

We will describe 4 clinical model cases to address this question. We have not addressed starting or modifying treatments for each case, as this would be beyond the focus of this review.

Case 1A

A 50-year-old woman presents with a body mass index of 30 kg/m2, a waist circumference of 32 inches, blood pressure of 130/85 mm Hg, and a fasting glucose of 140 mg/dL (Figure 1A). Her total plasma cholesterol is 205 mg/dL, triglyceride 200 mg/dL, HDL-C 45 mg/dL, and LDL-C 120 mg/dL. Although her LDL-C is slightly lower than average for her age and sex, her non–HDL-C is significantly increased at 160 mg/dL (the goal for non–HDL-C in an individual with increased CVD risk is <100 mg/dL) and her plasma APOB level is high at 110 mg/dL (an APOB plasma concentration <80 mg/dL is considered as a goal for individuals with increased CVD risk, such as someone with diabetes). Furthermore, her LDL particle size is smaller than normal, and there are more CE-poor, denser LDL than normal. Her triglyceride 3 hours after an unhealthy breakfast of eggs, bacon, a muffin with butter and jam, and a glass of whole milk, is 400 mg/dL (healthy individuals without diabetes would have triglyceride levels about 200 mg/dL 4 hours after this breakfast). Finally, her fasting plasma insulin is high at 20 μUnits/mL, indicative of IR. She has an HbA1c (hemoglobin A1c) of 6.7%, which meets the definition of diabetes. Her plasma Lp(a) (lipoprotein[a]), a CVD risk factor, is normal, but her coronary artery calcium score, which is a standardized way of assessing calcified plaque in the coronary arteries, is 250, placing her above the 75th% for women of her age and indicative of high long-term risk for ASCVD.16

This woman’s dyslipidemia is driven by abnormal regulation of lipoprotein metabolism in both the fasting and the postprandial state, with increased levels of VLDL and chylomicrons. Figure 1A depicts the increased secretion of VLDL, defined by the necessary structural protein, APOB100, in the fasting state with either a normal state of insulin sensitivity or IR and T2D. The figure also shows the removal of VLDL-triglyceride by LPL-mediated lipolysis and the production of VLDL remnants. Importantly, VLDL remnants can be metabolized further by either HL-mediated lipolysis, hepatic uptake, or the generation of LDL via further lipolysis by HL. The involvement of several proteins in the LPL-mediated lipolysis of triglyceride and the uptake of remnants by the liver is described in detail further in this section.

The level of hepatic triglyceride is determined by uptake of plasma free fatty acids (FFA) coming from adipose tissue, uptake of VLDL remnant–triglyceride and chylomicron remnant–triglyceride, and hepatic de novo lipogenesis of FFA from glucose.17 In T2D and the presence of IR and even mildly elevated insulin levels, all 3 sources of hepatic triglyceride are increased.6,18,19 In this woman with hyperglycemia, there will also be induction of a transcription factor, CHREBP (carbohydrate-responsive element–binding protein), which activates genes involved in de novo lipogenesis.20

It was long believed that intestinal assembly and secretion of APOB48–containing chylomicrons in the postprandial state were not affected by IR/T2D; we now know that this was incorrect.21 In T2D, secretion of chylomicrons from the small intestine after consumption of a fatty meal is increased compared with healthy individuals. The elevated levels of chylomicrons compete with VLDL for LPL-mediated lipolysis in the capillary beds of adipose tissue and muscle, leading to increased levels of both APOB100–containing and APOB48–containing remnants,22 which are atherogenic. Furthermore, this competition reduces the ability of LPL to completely remove triglyceride from either lipoprotein, leading to more triglyceride in VLDL and chylomicron remnants that are taken up by the liver, adding to the pool that stimulates the assembly and secretion of VLDL (Figure 1A).

The metabolic pathways that determine the reduced levels of HDL-C and increased concentration of small-dense LDL are in part mediated by CETP. This protein mediates the bimolecular exchange of CE from the cores of HDL and LDL for triglyceride from the cores of VLDL and chylomicrons. Importantly, the absolute rates of exchange are determined significantly by the levels of VLDL and chylomicrons: higher concentrations of these TRLs result in greater exchange, and therefore, depletion of core CE from both HDL and LDL. Enrichment of HDL with triglyceride leads to lipolysis by LPL and HL, producing smaller HDL particles and dissociation of some of the surface APOA1. Free APOA1 in plasma is filtered by the kidney glomeruli and taken up by proximal tubule cells, where it is degraded.23 Thus, higher triglyceride levels lead to lower concentrations of both HDL-C and HDL–APOA1 and smaller HDL particles. The same bimolecular exchange occurs between VLDL or chylomicrons and LDL and is followed by lipolysis of triglyceride in the core of LDL. Importantly, APOB100 does not dissociate from the smaller LDLs that are generated, causing a shift of LDL to smaller and denser CE-depleted particles.

Although the central defect of mild to moderate hypertriglyceridemia of T2D is increased secretion of VLDL, lipolysis of VLDL and chylomicron-triglyceride, as noted above, is also important, and when LPL-mediated lipolysis is defective, hypertriglyceridemia becomes a dual-defect disorder. In addition, because lipolysis is a saturable process, VLDL and chylomicrons compete for LPL, and if the enzyme is not fully functional, the hypertriglyceridemia is driven by both lipoproteins.26,27

For many years, LPL-mediated lipolysis was thought to be regulated only by a required activator, APOC2 and an inhibitor, APOC3.28,29 Although APOC2 appears to be present in excess of what is necessary for normal LPL activity, that is, the concentration of APOC2 is not closely related to plasma triglyceride concentrations, levels of APOC3 in plasma do correlate strongly with plasma triglyceride levels. This relationship is due both to increased secretion of APOC3 from the liver in individuals with IR/T2D and the sequestering of APOC3 on VLDL in circulation.30

In the last few decades, however, the complexity of LPL-mediated lipolysis has become much clearer, with the identification of several additional proteins that facilitate (APOA5, LMF1 [lipase maturation factor 1], GPIHBP1 [glycosylphosphatidylinositol-anchored-HDL-binding protein 1]) or inhibit (ANGPTL [angiopoietin-like] 3, ANGPTL4, ANGPTL8) LPL-mediated lipolysis. Small to modest effect variants in the genes for these proteins and for others that have small effects on plasma triglyceride levels can, on the background of T2D, lead to polygenic hypertriglyceridemia with levels in the range of case 1B (Figure 1B).31 Complete loss-of-function (LOF) of the genes for LPL, APOC2, APOA5, GPIHBP1, or LMF1, or combinations of heterozygous LOF of any 2 of these genes (double heterozygosity) can lead to plasma levels of triglyceride present in case 1C (Figure 2A).32 Homozygosity for LOF variants in those 5 genes is extremely rare; from 1 in hundreds of thousands for LPL to 1 in more than 1 million people for the other genes. On the other hand, a small proportion of individuals who have heterozygous LOF of any one of those genes, with LPL being the most common, together with a severe polygenic background for high triglyceride levels, can have a phenotype that is very similar to complete LOF.33

A critical concept is that for fasting plasma triglyceride levels up to about 400 to 500 mg/dL, increasing rates of VLDL secretion are the major driving forces; however, for triglyceride levels of 500 mg/dL or more, secretion rates do not increase further (nor do they decrease), but defective lipolysis and associated defective hepatic clearance of these larger TRL particles have an increasingly important impact.5 The defective lipolysis has been attributed in part to decreased LPL activity in individuals with diabetes and hypertriglyceridemia,34 but controversies in this area remain because of LPL’s complex regulation and differential tissue expression.29 Lipolysis generates remnants of TRLs that can be removed from the circulation by the liver via interaction of APOE on the surface of TRLs with several hepatic receptors. Carriers of the APOE2 genotype (<10% of people) have a reduced interaction of remnants with liver receptors, and this can play a role in their hypertriglyceridemia.

Case 1D

This woman presents with the characteristics of case 1A, except her total plasma cholesterol is 280 mg/dL, triglyceride 200 mg/dL, HDL-C 45 mg/dL, and LDL-C is 195 mg/dL (Figure 2B). Her LDL-C is about the 90th% for her age and sex, her non–HDL-C is markedly increased at 235 mg/dL, and her plasma APOB level is very high at 150 mg/dL. Her Lp(a) is normal, but her coronary artery calcium score is at the 90th% for her age.

The pathophysiology of the TD2 dyslipidemia does not explain why case 1A has an LDL-C of 120 mg/dL, and case 1D has an LDL of 195 mg/dL. LDL-C in any individual is determined by several physiological pathways. First is the rate of VLDL secretion and the generation of LDL particles by lipolysis of VLDL; higher rates of VLDL secretion can, but not always, result in greater numbers of LDL particles. Second is hepatic uptake of VLDL remnants; increased hepatic uptake of remnants will mean less generation of LDL particles for any VLDL secretion rate. Third, as described above, LDL composition and size will depend on the CETP-mediated exchange of LDL-CE for VLDL-triglyceride or chylomicron-triglyceride; greater rates of exchange will generate CE-depleted LDL, with lower LDL-C than expected for any number of LDL particles. However, the major determinant of the level of LDL-C and the number of circulating LDL particles is the number of functional LDLRs, which in turn is determined by the activity of the LDLR gene (LDLR).

In cases 1A and 1D, differences in the activity of their LDLR pathways are the basis for their different LDL-C levels (Figure 2B). With an LDL-C of 195 mg/dL, it is possible that case D has monogenic familial hypercholesterolemia, with heterozygous LOF of the LDLR gene, a gain of function of the proprotein convertase subtilisin kexin 9 (PCSK9) gene, or a mutation in the APOB gene that causes defective binding of APOB100 to the LDLR. LDLR mutations account for more than 90% of familial hypercholesterolemia cases. She could also have many more common LDL-C–raising gene-variants than case 1A, variants that have small to moderate effects on pathways such as cholesterol absorption, cholesterol synthesis, bile acid metabolism, and LDLR number.35 Differences in dietary intake of cholesterol and saturated fat could also contribute to the differences in LDL-C levels. Importantly, it should not be forgotten that the heterozygous monogenic familial hypercholesterolemia is present in about 1 of 150 to 300 individuals and can be inherited independently of whatever polygenic background causes the T2D dyslipidemia. However, if case 1D had monogenic heterozygous familial hypercholesterolemia, she would very likely have a significantly higher LDL-C, usually in the range of 225 to 500 mg/dL. Studies have shown that as T2D and hyperglycemia worsen, the efficiency of clearance of LDL from circulation is reduced.36 Importantly, whatever the mechanism, increased LDL-C levels above the more typical normal concentrations present in individuals with high triglyceride, low HDL-C dyslipidemia, will result in greater ASCVD risk.

Dyslipidemia in T1D

In approaching the question of dyslipidemia in patients with T1D, it is helpful to start with a conceptual framework that divides individuals into those with and without IR. This allows one to differentiate the effects of pure insulin deficiency on lipoprotein metabolism from the combined effects of insulin deficiency and IR. In individuals with T1D and good glucose control, elevated TRL levels occur when there is IR due to underlying genetic or environmental factors.37 TRLs are also increased when glucose control is poor, which is associated with IR due in part to increased FFAs or counterregulatory hormones.38 Importantly, individuals with T1D who are at high risk for ASCVD are those who also have IR and features of metabolic syndrome.39 In addition to quantitative differences in TRLs in T1D, compositional changes in VLDL rather than altered VLDL secretion and clearance rates might be present.40–44 The origins of altered VLDL composition are likely to result from a combination of reduced lipolysis and increased CETP activity.

When Do Individuals With T1D Have Abnormal TRL Metabolism?

We will describe 2 clinical model cases to address this question.

Case 2A

A 20-year-old man with a history of T1D since the age of 10 years, when he presented in diabetic ketoacidosis after several weeks of weight loss, increased thirst, and increased hunger. Initial workup identified insulin- and glutamic acid decarboxylase 65 autoantibodies. He now has a body mass index of 23 kg/m2, a waist circumference of 28 inches, blood pressure of 120/75 mm Hg, and a fasting glucose of about 110 mg/dL, receiving insulin. His diabetes treatment includes a combination of multiple injections of long- and short-acting insulin, which he self-administers based on blood glucose levels. He is an example of classical T1D, the autoimmune disease that leads to loss of insulin-producing β cells. However, he regularly neglects controlling his diabetes when in social settings, especially when out drinking alcohol with friends. This leads to frequent episodes of hypoglycemia and rebound hyperglycemia, with fasting glucose levels ranging from 60 to 220 mg/dL and an HbA1c of 9.5%, and associated IR with triglyceride levels rising to 250 mg/dL (Figure 3A). Concomitant plasma lipids during these periods of poor glycemic control include a total plasma cholesterol of 255 mg/dL, HDL-C 35 mg/dL, and LDL-C 170 mg/dL. APOB is 110 mg/dL, Lp(a) is normal, and he does not have any of the characteristics of the metabolic syndrome.

Case 2B

This man initially has the same history as case 2A. His diabetes treatment includes rapid-acting insulin via a pump (both basal and pulses) that he manages based on his plasma glucose levels provided by a continuous glucose monitoring device. He is 5′6″ and weighs 160 lbs with a body mass index of 25.8 kg/m2 and a waist circumference of 30 inches. His plasma lipids are: total cholesterol 140 mg/dL, triglyceride 125 mg/dL, LDL-C 75 mg/dL, and HDL-C 40 mg/dL, and non–HDL-C of 100 mg/dL. His APOB is 70 mg/dL. His HbA1c is 6.6%, and his blood pressure is 120/75. However, this man has a genetic predisposition for IR, and when he moves to go to college in a different state, he gains 30 lbs, and his body mass index increases to 32.5 kg/m2 with a waist circumference of 34 inches, during his first semester. This leads to penetrance of underlying IR. At the end of his first semester, his blood pressure is 140/85 mm Hg, and HbA1c is 7.0% on insulin. His total plasma cholesterol is 215 mg/dL, triglyceride 250 mg/dL, LDL-C 130 mg/dL, HDL-C 35 mg/dL, and non–HDL-C 180 mg/dL. His APOB is 110 mg/dL, and Lp(a) is normal (Figure 3B).

These 2 men differ in the causes of IR. Case 2A depicts the typical lipid profile in an individual with T1D. In such individuals, when their glucose control is poor, VLDL-triglyceride secretion is elevated, and at the same time, the fractional clearance of triglyceride falls, leading to moderate hypertriglyceridemia, but secretion rates fall to normal or low/normal levels with tight glucose control.45 HDL-C levels are generally moderately increased, and LDL-C is normal or moderately reduced in individuals with T1D when glycemia is controlled.

IR in poorly controlled T1D has been attributed to several factors, including glucose toxicity, low-grade inflammation, peripheral hyperinsulinemia, increased FFA, and hyperglucagonemia.38,46 When poorly controlled, elevated levels of TRLs in T1D can be due both to an increase in VLDL secretion secondary to relative IR and to a slower clearance of TRLs.37,47 Small studies have shown slower TRL clearance in poorly controlled individuals with T1D,48,49 and glucose infusion reduced the plasma fractional clearance rate of VLDL-triglyceride by ≈50% in lean women, supporting elevated glucose levels as playing a role.50 Other studies in teens have found no difference in TRL fractional clearance rates.51 Moreover, ketoacidosis results in reduced LPL activity and therefore reduced hydrolysis of TRL-triglycerides.37

In contrast, case 2B has inherited a polygenic predisposition to IR that, in the college environment, results in dyslipidemia, hypertension, and central obesity. Thus, case 2B, who has a significant insulin deficiency, is unable to compensate for his IR. This results in increased VLDL secretion and concomitant decreased fractional clearance of triglycerides. HDL-C is reduced, and LDL-C is moderately elevated with small CE-depleted LDL particles.

Like case 2A, case 2B is also at risk for developing severe hypertriglyceridemia when his insulin secretory capacity falls further in times of stress, leading to severe hyperglycemia and even ketoacidosis. In the latter instance, significant reductions in LPL-mediated lipolysis of VLDL-triglyceride, rather than a further increase in secretion of that lipoprotein, is the major driver of severe hypertriglyceridemia. When T1D with ketoacidosis is associated with severe hypertriglyceridemia, partial genetic loss of function of LPL or one of its activators should be considered.

What Do Mouse Models Tell Us About Mechanisms of Dysfunctional TRL Metabolism Associated With IR and Diabetes?

Mouse Models of Dysfunctional TRL Metabolism Associated With IR and T2D

The 2 main mouse models of atherosclerosis were developed based on the understanding of the role of the LDLR and APOE in atherogenic lipoprotein metabolism.52 The LDLR-deficient model (Ldlr−/−) and APOE-deficient (Apoe−/−) model have been extensively used for atherosclerosis studies.53 When made insulin resistant, for example, by a diet rich in sucrose, these mice exhibit increased dyslipidemia due to accumulation primarily of TRLs.54 The mechanism involves increased hepatic de novo triglyceride synthesis fueled by the sucrose and FFAs due to IR and increased adipose tissue lipolysis, leading to increased secretion of VLDL, similar to the human case 1A. Moreover, when plasma TRLs are high, competition between TRLs, remnants, and LDL for hepatic uptake results in reduced TRL remnant and LDL clearance rates, which further contributes to the dyslipidemia. Like in humans, when plasma triglycerides are high, defective lipolysis and associated defective hepatic clearance of these larger lipoprotein particles have an increasingly important impact.5 This competition for clearance is exaggerated both in the Ldlr−/− model and the Apoe−/− model because of the lack of LDLR-mediated hepatic uptake and APOE-mediated hepatic uptake, respectively. Thus, when LDLR is deleted, TRL remnants and LDL compete for clearance through alternative mechanisms, including LRP1 and syndecan 1. LRP1 binds APOE, which is present on TRLs and TRL remnants but is present at very low levels on LDL. In the mouse, syndecan 1 is the main HSPG (heparan sulfate proteoglycan) mediating hepatic TRL remnant clearance, but not LDL or HDL clearance.55

In contrast to humans, in which APOB-lipoproteins derived from the liver (VLDL, VLDL remnants, and LDL) contain APOB100, and APOB-lipoproteins generated by the intestine (chylomicrons and chylomicron remnants) carry APOB48 as the structural protein, the origin of APOB-lipoproteins in the mouse is more difficult to discern because mice express the Apob mRNA editing enzyme APOBEC1 (apolipoprotein B mRNA editing enzyme catalytic subunit 1)56 in the liver and thus generate APOB48–carrying VLDL, VLDL remnants, and LDL.57,58

Although IR is easily introduced in mice by diets or mimicked by deletion of the insulin receptor in different cell types, there are only a few models of TRL dyslipidemia and frank T2D. One of those is the leptin-deficient ob/ob mouse model. This model mimics severe obesity, hyperglycemia, and severe IR, and when LDLRs are silenced, it also mimics the elevated TRL lipid levels associated with T2D in humans.59 Like in humans with T2D and high TRLs, the elevated TRL levels are likely due in large part to suppressed hepatic TRL remnant clearance, especially because these mice are LDLR deficient.

Deletion of insulin receptors specifically in the liver results in secretion of APOB100–containing and APOB48–containing lipoproteins that are relatively enriched in cholesterol over triglycerides,60 which could contribute to the elevated VLDL-C levels in severely insulin-resistant mice (Figure 4). In addition, in the hepatic insulin receptor–deficient model, clearance of LDL is reduced because of a marked downregulation of LDLRs.60

Insulin resistance increases VLDL (very low-density lipoprotein) secretion and hinders hepatic TRL (triglyceride-rich lipoprotein) remnant clearance through multiple mechanisms. Insulin signaling has a multitude of direct effects in hepatocytes, and insulin receptor deficiency suppresses cholesterol biosynthesis, perhaps due to an increase in endoplasmic reticulum cholesterol content, suppresses SREBP-1c (sterol regulatory element–binding protein-1c) activity, and reduces APOB degradation via the proteasome and other mechanisms, leading to increased secretion of cholesterol-enriched, relatively triglyceride (TG)–poor VLDL. Insulin resistance (IR) or insulin deficiency also hampers remnant uptake by reducing LDLR (low-density lipoprotein receptor) levels, suppressing plasma membrane translocation of LRP1 (LDL receptor–related protein 1), and altering HSPGs (heparan sulfate proteoglycans). Another component of the effects of IR on hepatocytes is mediated by increased adipose tissue lipolysis, which leads to an increase in free fatty acid (FFA) uptake into hepatocytes and subsequent enhanced VLDL secretion. Moreover, increased remnants in circulation result in increased lipids delivered to the liver by those particles, further contributing to VLDL secretion and arterial lipid accumulation. Finally, hyperphagia and increased secretion of chylomicrons from the intestine should not be underestimated as a contributor to TRL dyslipidemia in ob/ob mice. Both the ob/ob mouse in which LRLR has been downregulated and the hepatic insulin receptor–deficient model develop accelerated atherosclerosis.59,60 One caveat is that mice do not normally express CETP,60–62 and therefore do not typically mimic all aspects of diabetic dyslipidemia discussed above, such as the presence of small, dense LDL and low HDL-C.

Do Mouse Models of T2D Support Causal Roles for Apolipoproteins Involved in TRL Metabolism?

The different proteins involved in dyslipidemia in human cases 1B and 1C have been studied extensively in mice, but few mechanistic studies have addressed the compounding effects of alterations in these proteins with IR and T2D. The role of APOA5, which enhances LPL activity, was studied in a fructose-fed insulin-resistant Apoa5−/− mouse model, in which lack of APOA5 increased plasma triglyceride levels more than in chow-fed insulin-sensitive controls,63 consistent with the compounding effects of IR and loss of APOA5 on TRL metabolism. APOC3 has been evaluated as a causal factor in atherosclerosis in the ob/ob model of T2D treated with an LDLR antisense oligonucleotide. APOC3 silencing using an antisense oligonucleotide reduced plasma triglyceride levels and VLDL-C levels, without affecting LDL-C or HDL-C.59 This effect of APOC3 silencing was likely mediated by an increased hepatic uptake of the high levels of TRL remnants through LRP1,64 perhaps in combination with increased LPL activity.65 APOC3 silencing also suppressed atherosclerosis,59 demonstrating that APOC3 plays a causal role in the accelerated atherosclerosis in this model of T2D.

Additional mouse models of T2D that better mimic IR/T2D in humans are needed to provide further mechanistic insights into how TRLs and their remnants promote atherosclerosis associated with T2D.

Mouse Models of T1D Associated With Dysfunctional TRL Metabolism

Mouse models of T1D are based on genetic LOF of insulin or ablation of the insulin-producing β cells by toxins or immune mechanisms. All models show dyslipidemia when combined with mouse models of impaired APOB-lipoprotein clearance, and atherosclerosis. The Akita mouse, which carries a mutation in the Ins2 gene resulting in incorrect insulin folding and β-cell toxicity, when crossed with Ldlr−/− mice, exhibits markedly elevated levels of triglycerides and VLDL-C, associated with increased atherosclerosis.66 A similar phenotype has been demonstrated in an Ldlr−/− model of autoimmune T-cell–mediated destruction of β cells, especially when these mice were fed cholesterol-rich diets.67 The atherosclerosis analyses showed that the more cholesterol that was provided in the diet, the more VLDL-C accumulated in plasma from diabetic mice. Atherosclerosis increased in parallel. Moreover, in Apoe−/− models of T1D induced by the β-cell toxin streptozotocin, dyslipidemia was much worse in diabetic mice than in nondiabetic Apoe−/− controls.68 Because mouse models of T1D typically are severely diabetic, even when treated with baseline insulin,67 they are hyperphagic. An increased food intake can therefore contribute to elevated TRLs in these models.

In mouse models of T1D, liver triglyceride levels are typically not elevated,69 and the liver does not produce appreciably more VLDL; instead, the main reason for the elevated plasma TRLs is defective TRL clearance mechanisms.70,71 An important concept relevant to both T1D and T2D mouse models deficient in LDLR or APOE is that suppressed TRL clearance is a more important mechanism behind the elevated plasma TRLs than increased TRL secretion. Severely elevated levels of TRLs cause competition for available hepatic uptake mechanisms, which elevates plasma levels of TRLs and their remnants. Reduced HSPGs or HSPG sulfation is another possible culprit of the reduced hepatic TRL clearance,71,72 reduced hepatic plasma membrane localization of LRP1 yet another69 (Figure 4). IR associated with poorly controlled T1D38,73,74 could also contribute to the slower TRL clearance.

How Does Dysfunctional TRL Metabolism Promote Atherosclerosis in Mouse Models of T1D?

Recent research has focused on the role of endothelial receptors involved in TRL uptake into the artery wall. These studies have demonstrated that SRB1 (scavenger receptor class B type 1) mediates endothelial transcytosis of LDL and TRLs.75,76 Another receptor, ACVRL1 (activin A receptor–like type 1, also known as ALK1 [activin-like kinase receptor 1]), preferentially mediates endothelial uptake of the APOB100–containing LDL and VLDL77,78 (Figure 5). In a mouse model of T1D, competition for SRB1-mediated endothelial transcytosis between TRLs and HDL was implicated in the increased atherosclerosis progression and arterial accumulation of APOB.79 This mechanism could be especially important in diabetes, because of the elevated levels of circulating TRLs combined with a shift towards smaller HDL particles, which are less effective at competing with APOB-lipoproteins for endothelial uptake via SRB1 than large HDL particles. It is not known, however, how diabetes affects the endothelial SRB1 and ALK1 transcytosis mechanisms.

Mouse models of LPL-deficiency have been utilized to investigate the atherogenicity of chylomicrons. These studies have revealed that chylomicrons internalized by endothelial cells via SRB1 undergo lysosomal hydrolysis, resulting in abluminal release of chylomicron-derived phospholipids in extracellular vesicles.80 The extracellular vesicles enhance both macrophage and endothelial inflammatory activation, perhaps because they carry inflammatory microRNAs.80 The exact mechanisms whereby chylomicrons promote vascular inflammation and atherosclerosis progression81 and whether those mechanisms are exacerbated by diabetes need further study.

Of the proteins regulating TRL production and clearance described in Figure 1, APOC3 is the most studied in mouse models of T1D. This is in part because elevated plasma levels of APOC3 predict incident ASCVD in individuals with T1D.82–84 Moreover, silencing APOC3 in a mouse model of T1D effectively reduces TRL particle levels, TRL-triglyceride, TRL cholesterol, and atherosclerosis without affecting blood glucose,82,85 suggesting that APOC3 is in a causal pathway of diabetes-associated atherosclerosis independent of hyperglycemia. Hepatic APOC3 silencing likely relieves the backup of TRL particles in circulation to be cleared in diabetic mice.85

How does hepatic APOC3 silencing mediate its protective effects in the artery wall in diabetes? One hypothesis is that APOC3 may become liberated from lipoprotein particles once they have entered the artery wall. Delipidated APOC3 can induce inflammatory activation of monocytes via an alternative inflammasome pathway.86 However, the likelihood of inflammation induced by free APOC3 in lesions of atherosclerosis is uncertain.87 Nevertheless, recent studies suggest that silencing hepatic APOC3 prevents the arterial inflammatory activation in a T1D mouse model.85 Rather than reducing a local proinflammatory effect of APOC3 itself, hepatic APOC3 silencing lowers TRL cholesterol and protects from the accumulation of free cholesterol in the lesioned artery.85 Because free cholesterol can induce inflammasome activation in vascular cells, it is possible that inflammasome activation in T1D mouse models is due to free cholesterol accumulation.66,88,89 Hepatic ANGPTL3 silencing in diabetic mice, on the other hand, leads to reduced levels of triglycerides both in plasma and in the artery wall, without a marked effect on plasma cholesterol levels.85

Together, these studies in T1D mouse models highlight impaired clearance of TRL cholesterol, perhaps together with increased endothelial transcytosis of TRLs, as mechanisms behind TRL dyslipidemia and increased atherosclerosis, and support a beneficial role for APOC3 silencing in diabetes-associated atherosclerosis.

Are TRLs or Their Remnant Particles More Atherogenic Than LDL Particles?

The prevailing consensus has long been that compared with TRLs, LDL drives most of the ASCVD risk.5 This is because of the effective prevention of ASCVD by LDL-lowering therapies (see below) and because LDL particles are 3× to 10× more abundant than TRLs and their remnants in circulation, and in addition have a longer plasma residence time (2.5–3.5 days for LDL versus 4–13 hours for chylomicrons and VLDL in subjects with raised triglycerides).5

However, TRLs and their remnants are atherogenic, as evidenced by the very high risk for CVD in people with dysbetalipoproteinemia, who have high levels of TRL remnants in the circulation90 and genetic studies, such as the association of low plasma APOC3 with reduced ASCVD risk.91,92 In fact, atherogenicity on a per-lipoprotein particle basis has been suggested to be higher for TRLs/remnants than for LDL. In a recent article, Bjornson et al93 used Mendelian randomization analysis of UK Biobank data to conclude that TRL/remnants are almost 4× more atherogenic than LDL on a per-particle basis. Moreover, although TRL/remnants exhibited causal relationships with markers of systemic inflammation (C-reactive protein, white blood cell count, neutrophil count, and glycoprotein acetyls), these associations did not explain their greater atherogenicity.

Another relevant question is whether smaller remnants are more atherogenic than larger remnants, VLDL, or chylomicrons. Smaller remnants have been considered more atherogenic because larger TRLs were long believed to be unable to efficiently enter the artery wall.94 As discussed above, recent research demonstrates that chylomicrons cause endothelial inflammation and worsen atherosclerosis.95 This might be (1) because of their larger size, these lipoproteins are more likely to interact with endothelial SRB1, (2) because they are metabolized in endothelial cells, causing inflammatory lipolysis products and extracellular vesicles to be released abluminally,80 or (3) because larger particles carry more cholesterol (per particle) into the artery wall than smaller particles (Figure 5).

Thus, the clinical relevance of LDL versus TRL/remnant particle concentrations is more complicated than just comparing particle numbers. Although LDL particles predominate in fasting and postprandial states, to gauge the relative atherogenicity of TRL and remnant particles one must consider plasma residence time, cholesterol load, rates of penetration and retention in the arterial intima, susceptibility to modification, and rates of uptake by lesional cells.5 In addition, further studies are needed on TRL subpopulations and their compositions and functions.

Overall, it is likely that all APOB-containing lipoproteins contribute at various extents to atherogenesis and ASCVD risk, perhaps with a relatively higher contribution of TRL and TRL remnants in conditions in which these particles are elevated, such as IR and diabetes.

Treatment of Dyslipidemia in Diabetes

Statins

Statins lower LDL-C through inhibition of HMG-CoA reductase (3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase), the rate-limiting enzyme in cholesterol biosynthesis, leading to upregulation of hepatic LDLRs and an increased LDL fractional clearance rate. Statin treatment can also reduce hepatic assembly and secretion of APOB-containing lipoproteins in people with IR and T2D.96 Some statins may reduce VLDL-triglyceride secretion.97 The reduction in VLDL-triglyceride secretion is evidenced by consistent reductions in plasma triglyceride levels, with increasing efficacy as baseline triglyceride levels increased.98 The molecular mechanisms for statin-induced reduced assembly and secretion of VLDL are not completely clear, although inhibition of hepatic cholesterol synthesis may be important.99

Major guidelines for cardiovascular risk reduction in people with diabetes have highlighted the use of statins, with or without other LDL-C–lowering medications in almost all patients.100–102 A meta-analysis of 18 686 patients with T2D in 14 randomized controlled trials, statin monotherapy resulted in a 9% reduction in all-cause mortality and a 21% reduction in the incidence of major cardiovascular incidents per mmol/L of LDL-C lowered.103 Similar results were obtained in a primary prevention cohort with T2D.104 Use of statins is associated with a modest increase in incident T2D, but the increased CVD risk associated with diabetes is far outweighed by the direct benefit of lowering LDL in people with and without diabetes.105

Ezetimibe

Ezetimibe works by inhibiting the absorption of cholesterol, whether dietary or biliary derived, from the small intestine via inhibition of the NPC1L1 (Niemann-Pick C1-like 1) transporter.106 This reduces cholesterol absorption and transport to the liver on chylomicrons, resulting in upregulation of both cholesterol synthesis and LDLRs, with the net response being reduced levels of LDL-C. People with T1D have plasma biomarkers indicative of reduced cholesterol synthesis and increased cholesterol absorption, which, based on mouse studies, results from loss of insulin’s physiological suppression of the transcription factor FOXO1 (forkhead box O1).107,108 Importantly, ezetimibe reduced cholesterol absorption in a study of individuals with T1D and was more effective at lowering LDL than a statin.108 Ezetimibe reduces LDL-C by 15% to 25%, with some individuals having larger reductions, possibly related to higher baseline rates of cholesterol absorption. Ezetimibe has minimal fasting triglyceride-lowering effects.

The IMPROVE-IT trial demonstrated a significant reduction of 7% in ASCVD events associated with additional LDL-C lowering with simvastatin plus ezetimibe compared with simvastatin alone109 in participants after acute coronary syndrome. Importantly, participants with diabetes had a 15% reduction in ASCVD compared with essentially no reduction in events in participants without diabetes.109

PCSK9 Inhibitors

Serum PCSK9 binds to LDLRs and targets them to the lysosome, along with LDL, instead of allowing the receptor to recycle to the cell surface. This results in fewer hepatic LDLRs, increasing serum LDL-C concentrations.110 Therapeutics that reduce PCSK9 levels or activity lead to 50% to 70% reductions in LDL-C with each drug. The 2 monoclonal antibodies, alirocumab111 and evolocumab112 reduced CVD events by 15% in high-risk subjects already treated with statins113,114 in Odyssey and Fourier, respectively. Approximately one-third of the subjects in those studies had either T1D or T2D, and both alirocumab and evolocumab reduced CVD to similar degrees in those participants as in participants without diabetes.113–115 CVD outcome trials are underway with a small interfering RNA, called inclisiran, that targets PCSK9 (HPS-4/TIMI 65/ORION-4), an orally administered macrocyclic peptide (enlicitide decanoate) that binds to PCSK9, inhibiting its interaction with the LDLR (CORELReef Outcomes), and Lerodalcibep, an adnectin-albumin fusion protein that inhibits PCSK9 activity. AZD0780, a small molecule that blocks the trafficking of the LDLR to the lysosome, is also in clinical trials.

In Odyssey and Fourier, triglyceride levels were reduced by about 16%. In a meta-analysis of inclisiran trials, plasma triglyceride levels were reduced by 12%.116 In smaller studies targeting subjects without diabetes and those with diabetes or dyslipidemia, fasting triglyceride levels fell between 10% to 25%, depending on baseline levels.117,118 Postprandial studies, with a mixed meal or a high-fat liquid drink, have, in general, shown no significant effects on postprandial triglyceride or APOB48 levels when the postprandial responses are adjusted for treatment-induced changes in baseline concentrations,119 although there have been exceptions.120 However, this is not to suggest that lower absolute postprandial concentrations of triglyceride or TRL components, such as APOB48, would not be beneficial, even if they result from lower fasting concentrations of each analyte.

Fibrates

Fibrates have been used to lower elevated levels of triglycerides and raise HDL-C levels for the past 5 decades. In several CVD outcome trials, fasting plasma triglyceride levels were reduced by about 25%. Fibrates are also effective at lowering both triglyceride and APOB48 levels after oral fat loads, with the degree of triglyceride lowering correlated with fasting triglyceride levels.121 The mechanisms underlying the triglyceride-lowering effects of fibrates have been studied extensively, with both decreased VLDL-triglyceride and APOB100 secretion and increased LPL-mediated lipolysis of VLDL-triglyceride demonstrated to be significant.122–124 The basis for increased LPL-mediated lipolysis in vivo in humans on a fibrate has not been completely defined, although fibrates may lower APOC3 secretion, which is increased in people with hypertriglyceridemia.125

Three early cardiovascular outcome trials with fibrate monotherapy, the Helsinki Heart Study126 and the VA-HIT trials,127 with very different cohorts, were positive for ASCVD event reductions. In contrast, the Bezafibrate Infarction Prevention Trial,128 the Field Trial,129 and the ACCORD Lipid Trial130 were all negative for reduction of ASCVD, with the last 2 studying individuals with diabetes. However, in a post hoc subgroup analysis in FIELD, and a prespecified analysis in ACCORD, subjects with HDL-C<40 mg/dL and triglyceride >200 mg/dL in FIELD and HDL-C <34 mg/dL and triglyceride >204 mg/dL in ACCORD, had reductions of 27% and 29% in primary ASCVD outcomes in the groups receiving fenofibrate. Based on these subgroup analyses, and the development of pemafibrate, a SPPARM (selective PPARα [proliferator-activated receptor α] modulator) with a different transcriptional profile,131 the PROMINENT study was designed to determine the effects of this drug on ASCVD outcomes in 10 000 participants with triglyceride levels >200 mg/dL and HDL-C<40 mg/dL.132 The trial had a median follow-up of 3.4 years, with 572 CVD end points in the pemafibrate group and 560 CVD end points in the placebo group (hazard ratio, 1.03). No clear cause for this negative outcome has been identified to date. One possibility is that pemafibrate’s modified transcriptional profile may have resulted in loss of activation of genes that were critical for the benefits seen in the dyslipidemic subgroups in FIELD and ACCORD. Alternatively, this SPPARM may not have repressed genes that would have detrimental effects on ASCVD.131

Recent Therapeutic Advances for Preventing TRL Dyslipidemia by Targeting APOC3 and ANGPTLs in People With T2D

Based on a 2 decades of remarkable advances in our understanding of the molecular regulation of LPL-mediated lipolysis, new therapeutic agents, particularly inhibitors of APOC3, ANGPTL3, and ANGPTL4, have been developed133 and, in some cases, received FDA approval for patients with severe hypertriglyceridemia or severe hypercholesterolemia. Landmark studies have demonstrated that a monoclonal antibody to ANGPTL3 can significantly reduce LDL-C in patients with homozygous familial hypercholesterolemia134 and an antisense oligonucleotide and small interfering RNA targeting APOC3 can markedly reduce plasma triglyceride levels as well as episodes of pancreatitis in patients with familial chylomicronemia syndrome and severe hypertriglyceridemia.135–137

An important and timely question is whether APOC3 or ANGPTL3 could be targets for ASCVD prevention in diabetes. On the one hand, ANGPTL3 inhibition may be more beneficial than APOC3 inhibition because it would result in reductions in both triglyceride levels secondary to increased LPL activity and LDL-C lowering through a mechanism that is independent of LDLR and involves EL and HSPGs.138,139 Loss of ANGPTL3 also hampers VLDL secretion.140,141 A monoclonal antibody that targets the combination of ANGPTL3 and ANGPTL8 and a monoclonal antibody to ANGPTL4 are also in early clinical trials.142,143 Little is known about whether ANGPTL3 inhibition would be beneficial in the setting of diabetes. A recent study on 3 individuals with very rare LOF mutations in ANGPTL3 included 1 individual with T2D and 1 with suspected diabetes.141 In this study, diabetes did not seem to affect the TRL-lowering effects of ANGPTL3 LOF, but the number of ANGPTL3-deficient subjects was too low to draw conclusions. In a mouse model of T1D, hepatic ANGPTL3 silencing resulted in marked triglyceride-lowering and modest LDL particle lowering, with no effect on the number of TRL particles in diabetic mice, in which TRLs were higher mainly because of defective TRL clearance.85

Hepatic APOC3 silencing, on the other hand, reduced both TRL and LDL particle concentrations in this mouse model,85 and importantly, reduced TRL levels in patients with homozygous loss of function of LPL.135,137 The latter results support increased TRL uptake by the liver when APOC3 levels are reduced. The reduced LDL particle concentrations in the Ldlr−/− mouse model could be explained by reduced competition for LDLR-independent hepatic clearance pathways when TRLs are lowered. Moreover, in the mouse model of T1D, APOC3 silencing prevented diabetes-stimulated accumulation of free cholesterol in the aorta, while ANGPTL3 silencing lowered aortic triglyceride levels.85 Because free cholesterol is believed to promote inflammation and atherosclerosis, this mouse study suggests that APOC3 might be a better target than ANGPTL3 in the setting of diabetes. Ultimately, this question cannot be answered without cardiovascular outcome trials for these targets in participants with diabetes.

In this review, we have elected to not cover the new classes of antidiabetic sodium glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors, glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists, or the omega-3 fatty acid eicosapentaenoic acid, although they have all demonstrated the ability to reduce ASCVD events in people with diabetes. These medications and their mechanisms of action, which extend beyond lipid-lowering, have been covered extensively in other review articles.144,145 It should be noted that another omega-3 fatty acid, docosahexaenoic acid, alone or in combination with eicosapentaenoic acid, has not been shown to reduce ASCVD events.

Perspective and Future Directions

Several important milestones have been reached in our understanding of the role of dysfunctional TRL metabolism in ASCVD in diabetes in recent years. These include the findings that (1) APOC3 is a risk factor for incident ASCVD in individuals with T1D or T2D, implicating dysfunctional TRL/remnant metabolism in the increased ASCVD risk in diabetes; (2) that TRLs/remnants are more atherogenic than LDL on a per-particle basis, which suggests, although LDL particles are much more prevalent in circulation, that TRLs/remnants contribute to ASCVD risk in states where these particles are elevated, such as in IR and diabetes; and (3) despite some positive results on triglyceride lowering by fibrates, that a highly selective PPARα fibrate modulator, did not prevent CVD in participants with T2D, despite marked TRL and remnant-cholesterol lowering.

These findings have led to several important areas of research: first, how to accurately quantify and measure composition and function of size-defined TRLs and remnants; second, whether targeting hepatic removal of TRLs and remnants would be a more effective strategy for CVD prevention in diabetes than stimulating LPL and preventing VLDL production; third, the mechanisms whereby APOC3 and ANGPTL3 work; and fourth, whether lowering APOC3 levels combined with LDL-lowering would provide additional ASCVD risk reduction compared with only LDL lowering in individuals with diabetes. The next several years should provide answers to these questions.
