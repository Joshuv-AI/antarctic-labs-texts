Cost‐utility of sirolimus in the treatment of vascular malformations
Authors: Grace Xiaoying Li, Deshan Frank Sebaratnam
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSirolimus is being increasingly employed to manage specific vascular anomalies. We performed an exploratory cost‐utility analysis to evaluate sirolimus as a treatment for vascular malformations from the Australian healthcare system perspective. Over a one‐year time horizon, sirolimus treatment was associated with an increased expenditure of AU$2832.80 and a gain of 0.08 quality‐adjusted life years (QALYs) when compared to supportive care, resulting in an incremental cost‐effectiveness ratio of AU$35,410/QALY. By most metrics, sirolimus would be considered a cost‐effective treatment for vascular malformations.Keywords: cost‐utility analysis, cost‐effectiveness analysis, sirolimus, vascular anomalies, vascular malformations

INTRODUCTION

Vascular anomalies comprise a heterogeneous group of disorders characterised by disordered growth and development of vascular structures. 1 For decades, therapeutic options were limited, but as our understanding of the genetics underpinning vascular anomalies has evolved, targeted therapies have revolutionised the management of many of these diseases.

There is a growing body of evidence for the mTOR inhibitor sirolimus as an effective treatment for many low‐flow vascular malformations and specific vascular tumours, such as tufted angioma and Kaposiform hemangioendothelioma. 2 , 3 However, it remains ‘off‐label’ and as these patients are usually managed in tertiary hospital settings, special access from hospital pharmacies is typically required.

Healthcare resources will always be finite, and increasingly policy makers are employing economic evaluations to guide allocation of healthcare resources. 4 The maximum acceptable incremental cost‐effectiveness ratio (ICER), also known as the ‘willingness‐to‐pay’ (WTP) threshold, is the maximum value that a healthcare system will pay to gain one health unit, such as a quality‐adjusted life year (QALY). Although no official Australian value has been disclosed, most of the Pharmaceutical Benefits Advisory Committee's past recommendations are within the range of 15,000–75,000 Australian dollars (AU$)/QALY, with AU$50,000 often utilised as a guide. 5 This exploratory decision analysis aims to evaluate the cost‐effectiveness of sirolimus for vascular malformations from an Australian healthcare system perspective.

METHOD

The cost‐utility decision analysis modelled a simulated population of Australian patients aged ≥18 years with slow flow vascular malformations requiring treatment. Additional patient characteristics have been described previously. 6 In the model, patients were either initiated on sirolimus 1 mg daily or received supportive care. The costs and utilities associated with each arm were accrued over a one‐year time cycle. The ICER was calculated from the difference in cost between the two interventions divided by their difference in effectiveness based on QALYs gained. The analysis employed a decision‐tree model developed with TreeAge Pro Software (Version 2024 R1.1) and is presented in accordance with the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards reporting guideline.

Health state utility values were derived from a phase IIB open‐label clinical study of patients with vascular anomalies 6 and costing data were sourced from hospital pharmacy data and publicly available information on the Medicare Benefits Schedule 7 (Table S1). Only direct costs were included, as per convention of cost‐utility analyses adopting a healthcare sector perspective. 4 Costs were reported in August 2024 AU$. Discounting was not applied given the short time horizon.

A one‐way sensitivity analysis was performed, where each efficacy and cost parameter was altered independently to assess their impact on the results. The minimum and maximum inputs for the cost of sirolimus were based on dosing regimens of 0.5 mg and 2 mg, respectively. All other inputs were varied within their known minimum and maximum values, and a standard ±25% adjustment was utilised where data was lacking (Table S2).

In the probabilistic sensitivity analysis, beta and gamma distributions were assigned for the utility and cost input parameters, respectively (Table S2). A standard error of 0.25 was applied in the absence of standard deviations. Using Monte Carlo simulation, input values were sampled from the distributions across 10,000 iterations to explore the effects of combined uncertainty on the results.

RESULTS

Over a one‐year time horizon, sirolimus was associated with increased expenditure of AU$2832.80 and a gain of 0.08 QALYs, yielding an ICER of AU$35,410/QALY (Table S3). When a standard AU$50,000/QALY WTP threshold was applied, sirolimus was cost‐effective compared to supportive care for the treatment of vascular malformations. The one‐way sensitivity analysis indicated that the ICER was sensitive to both utility input parameters and the cost of sirolimus medication (Figure 1). Varying the daily dose from 1 mg in the base case to 0.5 mg (minimum value) and 2 mg (maximum value) resulted in ICER values of AU$21,996.25 and AU$62,237.50, respectively.

Tornado diagram of the one‐way sensitivity analysis. The most influential input parameters were the utilities of sirolimus and supportive care and the cost of sirolimus medication. AU$, Australian Dollar; ICER, incremental cost‐effectiveness ratio; QALY, quality‐adjusted life year.

The probabilistic sensitivity analyses indicated that relative to the AU$50,000/QALY threshold, base‐case sirolimus was more cost‐effective compared to supportive care in 55.1% of the 10,000 iterations of the Monte Carlo simulation. When cost‐effectiveness was assessed across the willingness‐to‐pay threshold range of AU$0–100,000/QALY, sirolimus was considered cost‐effective in most iterations above a threshold of approximately AU$34,000/QALY (Figure 2).

DISCUSSION

This is the first economic evaluation of sirolimus as a treatment for vascular malformations in Australia. Our cost‐utility analysis demonstrated an ICER of AU$35,410/QALY, which would be widely regarded as a cost‐effective intervention. As the first of its kind to use cost input parameters specific to the Australian healthcare system, the results support subsidisation of sirolimus.

Our base‐case results were similar to those of a Dutch cost‐effectiveness study from which utility inputs were derived. 6 In the study, 6 months of sirolimus treatment reduced total healthcare costs by €426.84 (~2024AU$690). Although an ICER was not reported, the study identified a >95% certainty that sirolimus would be cost‐effective at a WTP threshold of €50,000/QALY gained.

There are several limitations to this study. The cost of sirolimus used was calculated based on a dosing regimen of 1 mg daily; however, dosing regimens and monitoring protocols vary widely between clinicians. 6 This was accounted for in our sensitivity analysis, which demonstrated that varying the daily dose from 1 mg to between 0.5 mg and 2 mg had a significant impact on the ICER. Interestingly, dose doesn't correlate with therapeutic response for many vascular anomalies, and our model is plainly an oversimplification of a complex disease process. Notably, our results indicate that a lower dose of 0.5 mg daily enhances the cost‐effectiveness of sirolimus further, suggesting that it may be a more economically favourable alternative. Conversely, increasing the dose to 2 mg daily resulted in an ICER that exceeded the assumed WTP threshold.

The setting of our model was an Australian public hospital, with expenses relating to sirolimus sourced from hospital pharmacy data. However, costs would vary depending on the healthcare setting, for example if a patient was to be treated in a private practice. Whilst the Dutch study included the costs incurred by interventions (i.e. sclerotherapy), clinician visits and indirect costs such as productivity losses, our costing inputs only accounted for direct costs. Therefore, we did not include potential cost savings from reduced medical appointments nor risk reduction of complications. As such, our model was narrow in scope, and it likely underestimates the cost‐effectiveness of sirolimus treatment.

Our input parameters were based on data available for adult patients. However, in paediatric populations, there may be a potentially greater utility gain as fewer medical interventions and increased productivity can contribute to improved long‐term quality of life. 6 Furthermore, decreased need for intervention is likely to offer greater cost‐saving in paediatrics as procedures often require general anaesthesia and admission as day procedures. 8 There is difficulty ascertaining whether the cost‐utility outcomes may be more favourable in paediatric populations due to the paucity of specific and applicable utility data points.

The aforementioned considerations were beyond the scope of this preliminary analysis, however, as more evidence is available on the efficacy and safety profile of sirolimus, more robust analytical modelling will be possible.

CONCLUSION

Our preliminary results suggest that sirolimus shows promise as a cost‐effective therapeutic option for vascular malformations in an Australian healthcare setting.
