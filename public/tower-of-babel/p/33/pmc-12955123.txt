Management of a chronic non-healing viper bite ulcer in a patient with alcoholic liver cirrhosis: A Case Report
Authors: Maitri J Parmar, Rutu D Shah, Kalapi B Patel, Shivenarain N Gupta, Manish V Patel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSnake bite complication often lead to tissue necrosis, cellulitis, non-healing ulcers and systemic disturbance such as coagulopathy and ascites, making management highly challenging. A 50-year-old male presented with chronic non-healing ulcer on the right limb that persisted for 58 days despite conventional treatment and was associated with severe pain, foul odour and deep tissue involvement including subcutaneous tissue and facia, bone and muscle were not exposed. The ulcer measured 11.5 cm in length, 6.2 cm in width and 5 mm in depth with necrotic fascia involvement. The patient also had decompensated liver cirrhosis, characteristic by ascites (abdominal girth 95 cm), pitting oedema, oliguria, urinary dribbling, low serum albumin (3.4 g/dL) and elevated total bilirubin (2.7 mg/dL). Therefore, the overall prognosis remains poor owing to advanced hepatic decompensation with associated hepatorenal involvement and necrotic fascio-muscular changes, indicating multi-organ dysfunction and markedly reduced physiological reserve. Ayurveda management was initiated with vardhamana pippali followed by virechana, along with supportive therapies including vrana prakshalana, vrana ropana and jalaukavacharana. Management based on vrana and yakritodara chikitsa improved ulcer healing, liver function and ascites, leading to a better clinical outcome despite a poor prognosis. Over two months, there was progressive ulcer healing with a reduction in ulcer size to 4.5 cm in length, 2 cm in width and 2mm in depth, complete resolution of oedema, 29 cm decrease in abdominal girth, improved liver function (serum albumin – 4.1 g/dL, total bilirubin 0.8 mg/dL) and relief in urinary complaints. Subsequently, complete healing was achieved after 10 months, with no recurrence of the ulcer and ascites observed during the follow-up period. The Child-Pugh score improved from Grade C to Grade A. This case highlights the potential role of Ayurveda interventions in chronic non-healing ulcers with systemic complications.Keywords: Viper bite ulcer, Non-healing ulcer, Ayueveda vrana chikitsa, Decompensated liver cirrhosis, Alcoholic liver cirrhosis, Chronic complications, Viper snake bite, Ayurvedic treatment of non-healing ulcer

Introduction

Viper bites prevalent in South India often result in systemic envenomation leading to coagulation dysfunction, thrombocytopenia, neurotoxicity, liver impairment and tissue necrosis [1]. Liver cirrhosis represents the advanced stage of chronic hepatic fibrosis and is characterised by the formation of regenerative nodules resulting from abnormal fibrogenesis in response to persistent hepatic injury. In decompensated cirrhosis complications such as ascites, coagulopathy, thrombocytopenia, portal hypertension and impaired immune functions indicate severe functional compromise of the organ. Among the etiological factors, prolonged and excessive alcohol intake remains a predominant cause in many regions [2]. The patient had pre-existing decompensated alcoholic liver cirrhosis with ascites, indicating baseline hepatic dysfunction. Even before the snake bite, the patient's liver was already incapable of maintaining normal coagulation, adequate platelet counts and effective detoxification mechanisms thereby increasing susceptibility to systemic infections and delaying ulcer repair. Management of such a case is highly challenging and requires an integrated therapeutic approach that addresses chronic hepatic decompensation, systemic envenomation effects, and local tissue repair simultaneously.

The impact of snake bite on human life can be understood by the fact that Acharya vagbhata named the whole branch of toxicology “damstrachikitsa”. Ayurveda texts describe dusta vrana (∼chronic ulcers) symptoms ati shool (∼intense pain), ati srava (∼excessive discharge), putipuya (∼excessive Pus formation), ati daha (∼severe burning sensation), ati kleda (∼excessive moisture), ati krishna (∼dark appearance), ati mamsa vrddhi (∼excessive granulation tissue), durgandha (∼foul smell), rakta mamsa pradurbhava (∼exposure of deeper tissue), dirgha kala anupakrama (∼long-standing nature and non-healing). The irregular shape of the ulcer and tridosas involvement, makes them difficult to heal [3]. Compensated liver cirrhosis with ascites can be considered in Ayurveda under the broad spectrum of udararoga. Among tridosa the prakupita vata gets accumulated in udara between tvaka (∼skin) and mamsa (∼muscle tissue) leading to sotha (∼swelling) this is termed as udararoga. Vata is one of the prime causative factors in the manifestation of udararoga. Along with the aggravated vata, manda agni causes udararoga. [4].

The treatment is planned according to Ayurvedic principles, focusing on the correction of underlying dosa–dushya samoorchana and enhancement of agni and dhatu poshana. The treatment aimed at promoting ulcer healing, reducing ascites, and restoring hepatic function. A combination of Ayurvedic medicines was used, including vardhamana pippali, punarnavadi kvatha, shveta parpati, kaishora guggulu, bhumyamalaki churna, sarpunkha churna, bhringaraja churna, kakamachi churna, bhallataka churna, and agnitundi vati. These formulations were selected for their shothahara (anti-inflammatory), deepana-pachana (digestive and metabolic enhancing) and mutrala (diuretic) properties. The line of management was thus designed to address both the root cause and clinical manifestations in a holistic manner as per Ayurveda.

Patient information

A 50-year-old male with a history of chronic alcohol consumption for the past 23 years, consuming about 250-300 ml of country liquor daily, having a poor lifestyle and residing in a rural area and exhibited a vata-pitta prakruti with hina agnibala and hina rogibala. He was diagnosed with decompensated liver cirrhosis with ascites two years prior, for which he received conventional allopathic management such as diuretics. Despite treatment, symptoms such as progressive abdominal distension, abdominal tightness, oliguria, loss of appetite, generalised weakness and pedal oedema persisted indicating progressing hepatic dysfunction. Fifty-eight days before the Ayurveda consultation, the patient sustained a snake bite over the right ankle joint. He was administered anti-snake venom at the time of the bite; however, he subsequently developed cellulitis with neurotoxic manifestations, including worsening dribbling of urine. The bite site gradually progressed into a chronic non-healing ulcer with recurrent suppuration, so (cefuroxime) antibiotic therapy was initiated. Despite this, the ulcer still did not show adequate healing, indicating a poor response to the conventional medication. The ulcer was characterised by excessive pain, profuse purulent discharge, severe burning sensation, blackish discolouration, foul odour and exposure of deeper tissue suggestive of a long-standing, infected, non-healing ulcer. The pre-existing liver impairment further compromised the healing process with laboratory tests showing deterioration in complete blood count and liver function. During this period, ascites and pedal oedema worsened due to aggravated hepatic decompensation. Considering the chronicity and complexity of the disease, the patient was advised hospitalisation along with long-term Ayurveda management aiming at systemic support, improvement of hepatic function and facilitation of local ulcer healing.

Clinical findings

The patient initially presented with progressive abdominal distension and lower limb swelling with a chronic non-healing ulcer on the right ankle region following a snake bite. He had continuous intense pain lasting throughout the day and dribbling of urine. On inspection, the ulcer showed irregular margins, hyperpigmented, indurated, and inflamed surrounding skin with exposed underlying fascia and necrotic tissue. The surrounding area was erythematous and oedematous, indicating chronic infection and poor healing response. On palpation, the ulcer edges were firm, base tender, and discharge purulent with foul odour, suggestive of an infected non-healing ulcer. Local tenderness and elevated temperature were present around the non-healing ulcer, indicating active inflammation in the surrounding tissues. The ulcer measured approximately 11.5 cm in length, 6.2 cm in width and 5mm in depth. The slough appeared yellowish-white, mixed with pus and necrotic debris, and there was continuous oozing from the ulcer surface. These findings indicated active infection and delayed healing of the chronic non-healing ulcer.

Simultaneously, he exhibited systemic symptoms, the abdomen appeared distended with visible fullness and bulging of flanks, the umbilicus was everted, fluid thrill and shifting dullness was present, confirming gross ascites. Abdominal girth was 95 cm (abdominal girth was measured at the point of the umbilicus.) and other symptoms such as abdominal heaviness, anorexia and significant weight loss of 15 kg (from 70 to 55 kg) were present. The patient showed signs of pitting oedema (3-4mm in depth) in the lower limbs, pallor and icterus suggestive of underlying anaemia and elevated bilirubin level. Urine output was reduced to approximately 200 ml/day, and no organomegaly was palpable due to the presence of ascitic fluid. He also reported peripheral neuropathy symptoms including numbness and weakness in both hands and legs.

Blood Pressure: 110/70 mmHg, Pulse: 64 bpm, SpO2: 98%

Respiratory examination: chest movements - reduced bilaterally in lower zones, respiratory rate – 20/min, in percussion-dullness over bilateral lower lung field, in auscultation – breath sound decreased in bilateral zones. The patient did not complain of dyspnoea at rest but experienced mild breathlessness on exertion corresponding to Grade-1dyspnea (MRC scale).

Cardiovascular examination: No abnormality was detected.

Central nervous system examination: The patient was conscious, alert and oriented, mild weakness and numbness in the limbs suggested peripheral neuritis.

The Bates-Jensen Wound Assessment Tool (BWAT) score was 44, indicating a severe wound with poor healing status (Table 1).

Therapeutic intervention

The patient was managed using a combination of shodhana (External therapy) and shamana (Internal therapy) therapies to address the root pathology and local ulcer management. For yakrit dushti and jalodara, virechana karma was selected as pitta-shodhana and rakta shodhana chikitsa to improve agni, reduce pitta–kapha dushti, and manage ascites. After shodhana, punarnavadi kvatha, kaishora guggulu, bhumyamalaki, etc.) were continued to support liver function and reduce fluid accumulation. For a non-healing ulcer, Local jalaukavacharana was performed (on 5/9/24, after virechana karma) to relieve inflammation and promote wound healing. Vrana shodhana and ropana dravya (panchavalkala kvatha and katupila taila) were used locally. Thus, the treatment was planned integratively — first by correcting systemic dosa imbalance (through shodhana) and then by local management of ulcer (through jalaukavacharana and vrana-ropana measures).

At the time of hospitalisation, the patient was started on finely powdered dried fruit of pippali (Piper longum), administered orally following a modified vardhamana krama (∼gradually dose escalation) for 13 days. The initial dose was 1 g twice daily with lukewarm camel milk before meals, the dose was gradually increased by 1 g per day up to 5 g, maintained at 5 g for 5 days and then tapered back to 1 g. Camel milk is described as the best pathya in the management of Udara Roga in classical Ayurvedic texts. During the course of treatment, the patient was restricted to fluid and food except milk, as per the classical approach. Hence, any oral medicine administered during the treatment was given along with camel milk. Along with this punarnavadi kvatha - decoction of punarnava (Boerhevia diffusa), daruharidra (Berberis aristata), musta (Cyperus rotundus), haridra (Curcuma longa), nimba (Azadirachta indica), guduci (Tinospora cordifolia), sunthi (Zingiber officinale), and katuki (Picrorhiza kurroa) [5]– 40ml twice daily and shveta parpati (shuryakshara 16 parts + sphatika 2 parts + navasadara 1 part) 500mg twice daily were administered [6]. Local ulcer management includes vrana prakshalana with panchavalkal kvatha and ropana with katupila taila.

On the morning of the 14th day, virechana karma (∼therapeutic purgation) was performed once, using katuki (4 gms) + trivrit (4 gms) with lukewarm camel milk, virechana, highly effective in treating conditions involving pitta and kapha imbalance, was followed by one day of rest after virechana karma. [7] subsequently, oral medications were initiated i.e. kaishor guggulu (Commiphora wightii) 2tab thrice daily (each tablet of 500 mg), agnitundi vati 2tab twice daily (each tablet of 250 mg) bhringaraja churna (Eclipta alba) 3 gm twice daily, kakmachi churna (Solanum nigrum) 3 gm twice daily, sarpunkha churna (Tephrosia purpurea) 3 gm twice daily, bhumyamalaki churna (Phyllanthus amarus) 3 gm twice daily, shuddha bhallataka churna (Semacarpus anacardium) 50 mg twice daily, katuki churna (Picrorhiza kurroa) 3 gm twice daily was administration to facilitate regular elimination of malas. All this medicine was given with lukewarm camel milk. Jalaukaavcharana was performed once during the treatment period.

Dietary intake – At the time of hospitalisation, the patient was on lukewarm camel milk. During the initial two weeks, the patient consumed six bottles of Amul camel milk daily, after two weeks, the intake was increased to ten bottles per day (each bottle containing – 200 ml). After discharge, the diet was gradually modified. Initially, the patient was advised lukewarm camel milk and masoor (∼Red lentil) soup. After two months, the diet was advanced to include dal and rice. Subsequently, after another two months, the patient was able to tolerate a regular diet consisting of sabji (vegetable), dal (moong dal), and rice.

This confirms the achievement of samyaka virechana. [8] Notable clinical improvements were seen including a reduction of 29 cm in abdominal girth from 95 cm to 66cm (Fig. 1). Resolution of ascites is evidenced by the absence of fluid thrill and shifting dullness. Discussion

At the onset of the IPD admission, the patient was started with a modified vardhamana krama (modified) for the first 13 days. Vardhamana Pippali is also mentioned as a rasayana (∼rejuvenating) drug in Ayurveda and it is an immunomodulator and hepatoprotective [9] It was also indicated in udararoga particularly plihodara. As per Charaka Samhita (chikitsa sthana), the therapeutic principles for plihodara, are extended to yakritodara, suggesting similarity in their pathological and treatment approach. Based on these properties, pippali was selected as a primary intervention [10]. The primary ingredient punarnava (Borhevia diffusa) itself has a sothahara and mutral action (∼anti-inflammatory and anti-oedematous) [9]. The punarnavadi kvatha is well documented for its diuretic, anti-inflammatory, anti-arthritic, anti-spasmodic and antibacterial properties [11]. Shveta Parpati indicated in disorders of mutravaha strotas and udakavah strotas [12], The formulation also aids in correcting deranged apana vata and thereby improves normal urinary flow. It has mild diuretic and alkalinising effects, helping to eliminate excess fluid and maintain electrolyte balance.

Virechana procedure is highly effective in treating conditions involving pitta and kapha imbalance [13], such as decompensated liver cirrhosis with ascites and dusta vrana. Virechana facilitates detoxification by eliminating morbid dosas and Malas from the pakvashaya as well as from the whole body which are the main etio-pathological factors of jalodara. Katuki (Picrorhiza kurroa) contains picrosides with strong antioxidant and hepatoprotective activity, preventing liver cell injury.

Kaishora guggulu [14] works in vatarakta, kustha roga as the main ingredient has rasayan, tridosamak, purgative, antipyretic, ulcer healing, analgesic and anti-inflammatory properties they help reduce systemic inflammation and support liver detoxification [15]. It's rakta dushti and sotha-hara properties demonstrate pharmacodynamic effects that are congruent with the pathological feature of a chronic snake bite ulcer. Bhumyamalaki has hepatoprotective, analgesic, anti-inflammatory and anti-bacterial properties. The protein isolate of Phyllanthus amarus indicates hepatoprotective effect against acetaminophen-induced toxicity [16]. Sarpunkha (Tephrosia purpurea) is known as plihasatru [17] it has anti-inflammatory, diuretic, anthelmintic and splenomegaly modulating properties. Sarpunkha promotes bile secretion improves digestion and reduces portal hypertension. It also aids in detoxifying blood which is beneficial in non-healing ulcers. Bhringaraj (Eclipta alba) is mentioned as the best tonic for the liver in classical texts. It is used in hepatitis and spleen enlargement. Kakmachi (Eclipta alba) is anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, hepatoprotective, antimicrobial, diuretic, and immunomodulator. For a non-healing ulcer its anti-inflammatory and anti-microbial effects, reduce local infection and promote granulation tissue formation. Bhallataka churna (Semacarpus anacardium) has rasayana and vishaghna properties supporting hepatic regeneration and is beneficial in neutralising toxic metabolites and promoting tissue healing. Properly purified Bhallataka exhibits immunomodulatory, antioxidant and anti-inflammatory action that aid in hepatic regeneration and tissue healing. Several studies have demonstrated its protective effects against toxin-induced liver injury when used in standardised doses after purification. The formulation chosen is a low, titrated dose and closely monitored, ensuring there are no adverse hepatic reactions during the course of therapy. Agnitundi vati has a deepana, pachana and vishaghna properties [18], in this sthavara visa (∼plant/mineral poison) is considered opposite in potency to jangama visa (∼animal poison) and vatsanabha serves as an antidote against snake-venom related toxicity. It; s vishaghna property supportive action in counteracting neurotoxic effects and promoting systemic detoxification. Ustra dugdha (∼camel milk) is indicated for the management of oedema, abdominal swelling, skin disorders and poisoning.

Ulcer represents a dushta vrana, where vitiated pitta and rakta dosa cause chronic inflammation, slough formation, and impaired granulation tissue development. The therapeutic approach was planned based on samprapti vighatana, addressing both systemic dosa imbalance and local dushti. After virechana karma to purify pitta-rakta, jalaukavacharana was administered locally to remove dushta rakta, improve circulation, and reduce inflammatory congestion around the ulcer margins. The procedure also aligns with modern understanding of leech therapy, which facilitates microcirculation, anti-inflammatory enzyme action, and decongestion, thus accelerating healing in chronic ulcers. Following leech application, panchavalkala kvatha was used for vrana shodhana and katupula taila for ropana. Promoting epithelization and reducing pain, discharge, and slough. Gradual healing with healthy granulation tissue and ulcer contraction was observed during follow-up.

Jalaukavacharana is a type of raktamokshana that counters vitiated rakta and pitta. Raktamokshana aimed at removing vitiated blood to reduce inflammation, pacify pitta and rakta dosas and restore normal circulation. It is performed through invasive (siravyadha, prachchanna) or non-invasive methods (jalaukavacharana). The inborn qualities of jalauka i.e. sheeta (∼cold) and madhura (∼sweet) are opposite to pitta dosa and these qualities might help in pacifying vitiated pitta. [19] Panchvalkal kvatha has vrana shodhaka along with vrana ropaka properties of the sixty upakramas of vrana management described in the Sushrut Samhita [20]. Panchvalkal kvatha is believed to act by raktapittagna, kapha shamaka. Multiple actions attributable to its kashaya (astringent) rasa, tikta (bitter) uparasa, ruksha (dry) in property that helps to remove slough and give a proper atmosphere to restart of regeneration of damaged tissue cells. These gunas are effective on vitiated kapha and normalise it. It helps to eliminate symptoms like srava, kandu, dourgandhya, etc. It's also helpful to provide relief from pain and enhance healing. Kvatha has also been proven to have anti-microbial properties in earlier research works [21]. Securinega leucopyrus is known as katupila in Sri Lanka, Humari in India, and Spinous fluggea in English. Chemical constituents (Pharmacognostical study) of katupila have crystals of calcium oxalate, tannins, and oil globules. These constituents are responsible for ulcer healing [22]. Katupila taila is an anubhuta yoga (clinically practised formulation) of P. D. Patel Ayurveda Hospital, Nadiad. It is not mentioned as a classical Taila in the Samhitas; however, the drug katupila (Securinega leucopyrus) is described in Ayurvedic literature as vranya (ulcer-healing) in property.

At our institute, katupila taila is prepared using tila taila (Sesame oil) as the base. The whole panchanga (root, stem, leaf, flower, and fruit) of katupila is taken, cleaned, and processed with tila taila following the classical taila paka vidhi under sneha kalpana principles described in Ayurvedic pharmacopeial standards. The formulation is prepared and procured from Sundar Pharmacy, the GMP-certified pharmacy unit of P. D. Patel Ayurveda Hospital, ensuring authenticity, standardisation and quality of the taila used for clinical application.

The initial reduction in abdominal girth from 95 cm to 76 cm within 30 days was due to the active reduction of ascitic fluid as the treatment progressed. Subsequently, when the fluid accumulation resolved, the abdominal contour returned to near normal size (66 cm). After that stage only, minor variation (66 cm to 65 cm) was noted during follow-ups which represents normal abdominal tone and not pathological distention. Even though platelet levels remained static, the clear reduction in serum creatinine marked a positive shift in systemic function. The present case report is limited by its single-patient observation, Further studies on a larger sample size are required to substantiate these findings. All allopathic medications were discontinued at the time of admission.

Conclusion

The present study concludes that Ayurveda interventions are helpful in patients with chronic non-healing viper bite ulcers with alcoholic liver cirrhosis as they improve the patient's quality of life and expectancy. Depending on the involvement of dosha and dushya, appropriate Ayurvedic treatment should be selected. However, in such chronic disease conditions prolonged hospitalisation and long-term follow-up are required for sustained results. Patient should be advised to follow the lifestyle modification, diet and prescribed regimens. The patient was regularly monitored for 10 months during the follow-up period, showing sustained improvement without any recurrence of symptoms.
