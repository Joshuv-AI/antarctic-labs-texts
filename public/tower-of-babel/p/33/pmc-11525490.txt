A Battery Thermal Management System Integrating Immersion Preheating and Immersion Cooling
Authors: Yunjun Luo, Dequan Zhou, Zhiyang Zou, Chunxue Bi, Xiaoxia Yang, Xinxi Li, Xiaoqing Yang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe battery thermal management system (BTMS) depending upon immersion fluid has received huge attention. However, rare reports have been focused on integrating the preheating and cooling functions on the immersion BTMS. Herein, we design a BTMS integrating immersion cooling and immersion preheating for all climates and investigate the impact of key factors on the preheating/cooling performance. For the preheating mode, adopting an inlet flow rate of 2 L·min–1, an inlet temperature of 15 °C, and a preheating time of 600 s is optimal by considering both heating performance and energy efficiency under a low ambient temperature (Ta) of −20 °C. The corresponding minimum temperature, average temperature, maximum temperature difference (ΔTmax), and rate of temperature rise could achieve 11.81 °C, 14.1 °C, 3.18, and 3.18 °C·min–1, respectively. For the cooling mode, the immersion BTMS could effectively control the ΔTmax and maximum temperature of the battery module below 3.08 and 28.48 °C, and 3.21 and 28.5 °C at a high-rate discharge of 3 C under a normal Ta of 25 °C and a high Ta of 40 °C, respectively.

Introduction

With the shortage of fossil fuels and serious pollution of the environment, various countries in the world have introduced a series of measures in various fields to reduce their dependence on fossil fuels. Electric vehicles (EVs) have attracted a great deal of interest in recent years due to their advantages of energy efficiency and low pollution. At present, lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) are mainly utilized in EVs, because they possess advantages of long cycle life and high energy density.1,2 Unfortunately, studies have shown that LIBs are very sensitive to the operating temperature, and their best operating temperature is 15–40 °C with a temperature difference (ΔT) less than 5 °C.3,4 During the charging–discharging process, LIBs generate a lot of heat, and the severe temperature rise affects their performances seriously.5−7 Likewise, an overly low operating temperature also greatly reduces the capacity of LIBs,7 which is due to the significant reductions in the dielectric conductivity, charge transfer kinetics, and solid-state diffusion of lithium ions in LIBs.8−10 Many studies have pointed out that to ensure the normal start-up of the battery pack, the module temperature needs to be preheated to at least 10 °C.11−13 In this context, it is particularly important to design a battery thermal management system (BTMS) for ensuring suitable operating temperatures of the power battery modules/packs.

At present, the most common cooling methods mainly consist of air cooling, indirect liquid cooling (LC), direct LC (immersion cooling (IC)), and phase change cooling. Among them, air cooling is based on the direct heat exchange between the incoming air and the batteries.14 In spite of its simple structure and low cost, the cooling efficiency and temperature-uniform ability are too low to meet the requirements of large battery modules. Phase change cooling utilizes the large latent heat of phase change materials (PCMs) to absorb the generated heat of the battery module.15,16 However, the bottleneck of phase change cooling still remains at the low thermal conductivity, easy flammability, and leakage risk of PCMs.17,18 Indirect LC is now the mainstream thermal management technology adopted in commercial EVs.19,20 It usually adopts LC components with a flowing coolant inside to take away the generated heat from the cells. In recent years,21,22 IC, in which the cells are directly immersed in nonconductive fluids, has successfully received huge attention.23 It is considered as a potential technology to substitute the indirect LC by virtue of its higher cooling ability and simpler structure compared to the indirect LC.24 Wang et al.25 designed a new type of IC-based BTMS for LIB modules by taking No. 10 transformer oil as the immersion fluid. Their results showed that a suitable working time below 40 °C of the battery module could be extended greatly from 735 to 1378 s by increasing the immersion depth. In addition, increasing the flow rate of the immersion fluid could enhance the heat transfer capacity by forming more turbulence, thus reducing the maximum temperature (Tmax) and maximum ΔT (ΔTmax) of the module. Jithin and Rajesh26 compared IC with air cooling and found that IC could provide higher cooling rates. When deionized water is adopted as the immersion fluid and the flow rate is set to 0.05 kg·s–1, the temperature increase could be controlled at 2.2 °C.

On the other hand, traditional preheating technology is mainly divided into internal heating and external heating. Internal heating consists of directly heating the cells through internal resistance or chemical reactions inside the cells.27 Obviously, internal heating possesses the advantage of high efficiency, but previous investigations have proved that it will inevitably accelerate the aging of the battery.28,29 Thus, the current mainstream preheating technology is based on external heating, which refers to preheating the cells by heat conduction or convective heat transfer outside the cells.30 Lei et al.31 developed a preheating technology by installing a metal film on the battery surface. The results suggested that the heating rate reached 2.33 °C·min–1, and the discharge capacity could be restored after 15 min of preheating. Wang et al.32 designed a heating container to preheat the battery module through air convection, and the results showed that the battery was heated from −15 to 0 °C in 21 min. Wang et al.33,34 designed an immersion preheating (IP) structure and studied the influence of the key factors on its preheating performance. They found that the battery pack could be preheated from −28 to 25 °C with a heating rate of 4.18 °C·min–1 within 11.0 min.

It could be seen that the immersion BTMS demonstrates considerable convenience in realizing the cooling and preheating functions simultaneously. In spite of the aforementioned research on IC and IP, to our knowledge, no research has been focused on developing BTMS integrating IC and IP simultaneously. As we know, in practical application, a complete BTMS must possess dual functions consisting of cooling under high temperature conditions and preheating under low temperature conditions. Therefore, this work proposes an integrated BTMS (IBTMS) suitable for all climates and investigates the impact of key factors on the thermal management performance of the system via numerical simulation. Under high temperature conditions, the flowing immersion fluid in the IBTMS takes away the heat from the battery module in time. Under low temperature conditions, the battery module can be preheated to a suitable operating temperature in a short time by adjusting the heating parameters of the flowing immersion fluid.

Mesh Independence Validation and Boundary Conditions

In the model of this study, the inlet flow rate and temperature were set as constant values. The inlet was set as velocity inlet, and the outlet was set as pressure outlet. The immersion fluid and the battery were set as fluid–structure coupling, and the heat transfer mode was the forced convection heat transfer. The natural convection heat transfer coefficient between the acrylic box and the outer space was set to be 5 W·(m·K)−1. Under low-temperature conditions, the immersion fluid in the model flowed through the battery modules and transferred the heat to the cells. When the inlet velocity was set from 0.5 to 1.5 L·min–1, the calculated Reynolds number (Re = ρvd·μ–1) ranged from 674.2 to 2022, and thus we adopted the laminar flow model (y+ = 5). Then, the realizable k–ε model was adopted for the inlet velocity of ≥2 L·min–1 (Re ≥ 2300), while the enhanced wall treatment was selected (y+ = 1). Results and Discussion

According to the Newtonian cooling principle, with the increase of the flow rate of the hot/cold immersion fluid, the preheating and cooling performances of the battery module would be improved, respectively. However, an increase in the power consumption of the external circulation system is inevitable. In this work, three indexes, i.e., Tmin, ΔTmax, and Taver, were adopted to appraise the thermal management performance of this IBTMS. For the preheating mode, Ta was set as −20 °C, and the inlet temperature of the immersion fluid in the container was 15 °C. Figures 5 and 6 present the Tmin, ΔTmax, and Taver and temperature contours (TCs) of the modules after preheating for 600 s with different flow rates, respectively.

TC of the module with various inlet flow rates.

Apparently, the Tmin and Taver of the battery module increase and ΔTmax decreases with increasing the flow rate from 0.5 to 3 L·min–1. The corresponding RTRs are 1.83, 2.67, 3.02, 3.18, 3.26, and 3.3 °C·min–1 at the flow rates of 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 2.5, and 3 L·min–1, respectively. At the same time, it is found that when the flow rates are 0.5 and 1 L·min–1, the ΔTmax of the battery module is over 5 °C (Figure 5a). This is because an overslow flow rate leads to a low heat capacity of the immersion fluid. As shown in the TC (Figure 5), the slow flow rate results in a low preheating efficiency at the outlet of the battery module, causing a large ΔT. When the flow rates increase to 1.5, 2, 2.5, and 3 L·min–1, the ΔTmax reduce remarkably to 4.79, 3.18, 2.41, and 1.95 °C, respectively, which can meet the thermal safety requirements of LIB modules. The Taver of the battery module after preheating for 600 s are 13.56, 14.10, 14.37, and 14.52 °C with the flow rates of 1.5, 2, 2.5, and 3 L·min–1, respectively, and the resultant IRs are 10.2, 4, 1.9, and 1.1%, respectively. These results indicate that although the increment in flow rate would further increase the heating efficiency of the battery module, the improvement extent remarkably decreases and tends to be steady at flow rates higher than 2 L·min–1. Taking account of the indexes of Tmin, ΔTmax, Taver, and the calculated IR, the flow rate of 2 L·min–1 is considered as the optimal value and thus selected in the following research.

Another factor affecting the preheating effect is the inlet temperature. Therefore, the preheating performance of the immersion fluid flowing into the battery module with various inlet temperatures was explored. The environment temperature and initial temperature of the module were also set to −20 °C, and the inlet flow rate of the immersion fluid was set as 2 L·min–1. Figures 7 and 8 show the TCs, Tmin, ΔTmax, Taver, and IR of the module with various inlet temperatures, respectively. It is found that as the inlet temperature increases, the temperature of the battery module after preheating for 600 s increases obviously (Figure 8a). The Taver are 9.23, 11.17, 14.1, 17.2, and 19.0 °C with the inlet temperatures of 10, 12.5, 15, 17.5, and 20 °C, respectively (Figure 8b), and the corresponding RTRs are 2.73, 2.91, 3.18, 3.45, and 3.64 °C·min–1, respectively. Moreover, with all the inlet temperatures, the ΔTmax is always lower than 5 °C. As calculated, the IRs of each increase of the inlet temperature are 21.1, 26.2, 20.7, and 11.4%, respectively. Considering the preheating performance, the IR value, and the increased power consumption at higher inlet temperatures, the inlet temperature of 15 °C is considered optimal for raising the comprehensive performance of the battery module.

The preheating time also affects the preheating effect of the battery module. An overlong preheating time not only leads to unnecessary energy consumption but also increases the waiting time of the passengers. An overshort preheating time may give rise to an insufficient start-up temperature. This means that a suitable preheating time is also another key parameter for the preheating function of the IBTMS. Thus, the battery modules were then preheated for 300, 450, 600, 750, and 900 s under the inlet flow rate and temperature of 2 L·min–1 and 15 °C, respectively. Figures 9, 10, and 11 show the Tmin, ΔTmax, and TCs of the module under various Ta of −20 and 0 °C with different preheating times, respectively. It can be seen that with increasing the preheating time, Tmin and Taver of the battery module increase continuously first and turn to a relatively steady state at around 600 s (Figure 9). For the ΔT, it first increases, because the initial high temperature of the fluid leads to an uneven temperature distribution. After ∼70 s, ΔT starts to decrease gradually and reaches a relatively steady state after 600 s as well (Figure 9). On the other hand, when Ta is 0 °C, the calculated RTRs with the preheating times of 300, 450, 600, 750, and 900 s are 1.88, 1.62, 1.36, 1.12, and 0.95 °C·min–1, respectively. When Ta decreases to −20 °C, the RTRs with the preheating times of 300, 450, 600, 750, and 900 s are 4.39, 3.82, 3.18, 2.62, and 2.21 °C·min–1, respectively. By taking account of the heating efficiency, ΔT, and calculated RTR, it can be concluded that the most suitable preheating time in this IBTMS is 600 s.

In addition to the preheating performance, the cooling function of such an all-climate IBTMS is also very important. Under harsh working environments, controlling the temperature increase and ΔT within suitable ranges during the charge–discharge processes is critical to ensuring the safety of the battery module. Therefore, we subsequently evaluate the cooling performance of this all-climate IBTMS under different operating conditions.

Figure 12 presents the TCs of the battery module at the terminal of the discharge process at 1, 2, and 3 C. We can see that as the discharge rate is increased, the temperature of the module increases due to the more rapid heat generation rate under higher discharge rates. As a result, the calculated Tmax shown in Figure 13a demonstrates an increasing trend with increasing discharge rate. The Tmax increases from 26.99 °C at 1 C discharge to 28.14 and 28.48 °C at 2 and 3 C discharge, respectively. At the same time, the ΔTmax of the entire module also obviously increases with an increase in the discharge rate. The ΔTmax are 1.83, 2.85, and 3.08 °C at 1, 2, and 3 C discharges, respectively. The increase of the ΔTmax could be explained as follows: as evidenced in the contours (Figure 12), at high discharge rates, the accumulation phenomenon of heat is more severe at the corner cell due to the relatively low flow rate in the corner of the module.

TCs of the modules after charging and discharging at different rates under different Ta values.

Tmax and ΔTmax of the modules after charging and discharging at different rates under various Ta of (a) 25 and (b) 40 °C.

Under the extreme harsh working environment, as the Ta increases to 40 °C, similar trends are observed on the TC, Tmax and ΔTmax (Figure 13a,b). When the discharge rates increase from 1 to 2 and 3 C, the Tmax increases from 26.99 to 28.25 and 28.48 °C, while the ΔTmax increases from 1.83 to 2.96 and 3.21 °C, respectively. Under all of the above working environments, the Tmax is controlled below 29 °C, and the ΔTmax is controlled below 3.5 °C. It can be concluded that this all-climate IBTMS simultaneously demonstrates excellent preheating performance and cooling performance under various working conditions.

Overall, the above results clearly demonstrate the superior performance of this IBTMS in both preheating and cooling. Such dual-functional BTMS is beneficial to the high-integration pursuit in practical applications of EVs. Future work should be focused on the experimental validation of the feasibility and reliability of this IBTMS toward large-scale battery modules. This is because the current immersion BTMSs still face a series of challenges when applied to real-world EVs, especially in terms of weight, cost, and immersion fluids.

First, the immersion BTMSs require sufficient immersion fluids surrounding the cell units. In order to prevent leakage of these immersion fluids, various accessories are required and the container needs to be strong enough and anticorrosive, which undoubtedly raises the total weight. Therefore, lightweight but high-strength materials can be used to manufacture the containers, such as carbon fiber composite materials. Second, due to the lack of engineering investigations about immersion BTMSs, the cost of immersion fluids and related containers is generally high, which greatly affects the promotion of practical applications. In addition, excellent electrical insulation performance is required by immersion fluids to prevent internal short circuits in the battery modules. However, the current immersion fluids still possess high thermal properties and viscosity, which undoubtedly affect the thermal management performance and power consumption. Therefore, developing immersion working fluids with excellent thermal properties and low viscosity is another major challenge in immersion BTMSs.

Conclusions

An IBTMS for all climates is designed based on the IC and IP technologies. It simultaneously processes excellent preheating performance in low temperature conditions and cooling performance in high temperature conditions. Main conclusions are obtained as follows:

1.With increasing the inlet flow rate and temperature, the preheating efficiency increases. Nevertheless, the improving trend gradually turns easing when the flow rate is higher than 2 L·min–1.

With increasing the inlet flow rate and temperature, the preheating efficiency increases. Nevertheless, the improving trend gradually turns easing when the flow rate is higher than 2 L·min–1.

2.The optimal heating performance is obtained at the flow rate of 2 L·min–1 with the inlet temperature of 15 °C. The corresponding Tmin, Taver, and ΔTmax are 11.81, 14.1, and 3.18 °C, respectively, and the most suitable preheating time is 600 s for this IBTMS under low temperature environments of 0 and −20 °C.

We believe that this IBTMS coupling immersion preheating and immersion cooling is very promising for the all-climate thermal management of EVs in midlatitude regions, which simultaneously face the low and high temperature environments.
