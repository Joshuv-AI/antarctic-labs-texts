Overlap of severe hypokalaemia and Guillain-Barré syndrome following Salmonella infection: a rare double etiology of acute lower limb paralysis – case report
Authors: Adam Kaicer, Timothée Klopfenstein, Souheil Zayet, Beate Hagenkötter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This case highlights hypokalaemia and Guillain–Barré syndrome as rare and serious complications of Salmonella infection.A 25-year-old woman was admitted to the emergency department with neuropathic pain, paraesthesia, paraparesis and circulatory shock associated with salmonella infection and severe hypokalaemia. Despite prompt and adequate correction of hypokalaemia, the neuropathic pain and paresis persisted, raising concern for other differential diagnosis, such as Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS). Therefore, further neurological workups were performed to evaluate this condition. After intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) treatment, the patient finally showed significant improvement on day 12 of admission.The rare association of GBS and hypokalaemia following Salmonella infection represents a clinical and diagnostic challenge. Clinicians should remain alert to the potential overlap between electrolyte imbalances and neuromuscular disorders.

Introduction

Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS) following infection with Campylobacter spp. is a rare but known complication. Dimachkie and Barohn (2013) reported that Campylobacter jejuni is the most common infection preceding axonal GBS (up to 33%) [1]. GBS following salmonella infection is a rarity: Kapoor et al. [2] published a case report of GBS after typhoid fever and Khan et al. [3] a case of GBS associated with Salmonella paratyphi. To our knowledge, GBS following infection with non-typhoidal Salmonella has only been described in two studies, occurring in 0% to 0.03% of cases [4]. Co-infection with Campylobacter jejuni and non-typhoid salmonella has been described in the literature [5] and should be ruled out.

Progressive weakness of the arms and legs, reduced or absent tendon reflexes in the affected limbs, and progression of symptoms over a period of no more than four weeks are the required features for the diagnosis of Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS) [6]. Nerve conduction studies that detect polyneuropathy further support the diagnosis [6]. Severe hypokalaemia can lead to rapidly progressive weakness within minutes to several hours, with the limbs often being more severely affected than the trunk, with predominantly proximal weakness [7]. GBS-like EMG findings have been described in severe hypokalaemia [8], suggesting that hypokalaemia can cause neurogenic symptoms in addition to myopathic manifestations. The overlap between severe hypokalaemia and GBS poses a challenge [9]. These two conditions require different treatments. Our case report addresses the diagnostic and therapeutic management of this complex clinical scenario.

Patient information

The 25-year-old woman with a history of amoxicillin allergy and a suicide attempt in 2018, with no signs of underlying psychiatric disorder and stable mental health since then without long-term medication, developed vomiting and severe watery diarrhea on 8 January 2025 during her holiday in the Dominican Republic, as did other travelers. This was reported by the patient but could not be further investigated medically. After her return on 17 January 2025, her symptoms worsened with vomiting, fever and ascending paraesthesia and dysaesthesia of the lower extremities. She developed severe arterial hypotension and neuropathic pain of intensity 10/10 and was admitted to the emergency room on 19 January 2025 due to severe dehydration with circulatory shock.

Clinical findings

On admission in the emergency room, she was conscious but in hypovolemic shock (systolic blood pressure at 50 mmHg), tachycardia, hyperventilation, requiring intravenous fluid administration and opioid analgesia (morphine).

The lower limbs were cold and cyanotic with preserved distal pulses. Neurological examination revealed a paraparesis of the lower limbs (2/5 strength on the Medical Research Council Scale MRC), asymmetric limb reflexes with reduced reflexes on the left. The upper limb function was normal. No involvement of the cranial nerves was detected, no objective sensory deficits were found, and there was no sensory level. Diagnostic assessment, therapeutic intervention, outcome

Laboratory tests revealed severe hypokalemia at 2.4 mmol/l (normal range: 3.5–5), respiratory alkalosis, hyperlactatemia and high inflammation markers with a C-reactive protein value of 148 mg/l (normal range < 5) on 20 January and a white blood cell count of 13 G/l. Renal function was normal. Liver enzymes were partially elevated, with ALAT normal, ASAT 48 U/l (normal range 7–40) and GGT 84 U/l (normal range < 38). Magnesium levels were normal and creatine kinase (CK) was elevated at 233 U/l (normal range 35–145). A stool culture identified Salmonella enterica Enteritidis; antimicrobial and sensitivity testing revealed a wild-type strain that was sensitive to all β-lactams. Testing for Campylobacter jejuni was negative as well as peripheral blood cultures and malaria polymerase chain reaction. Magnetic resonance imaging of the brain and spine revealed no abnormalities. Analysis of the cerebrospinal fluid showed a normal cell count of 2 cells/mm3, a normal protein content of 0.3 g/l and a normal glucose content of 3.3 mmol/l.

Neurophysiological examination three days after admission (after correction of hypokalaemia) showed incomplete bilateral motor conduction block of the common peroneal nerve and almost complete bilateral conduction block of the tibial nerve with reduced motor velocities (standard value of motor nerve conduction velocity of the lower extremities > 40 m/s) (Table 1). The distal motor latencies of the upper and lower extremities and the distal amplitudes were within the normal range. The sensory conduction velocity of both sural nerves was slowed (standard value of sensitive nerve conduction velocity of the lower extremities > 42 m/s) (Table 1). F-waves were not assessed. No temporal dispersion was observed. Electromyography of the tibialis anterior muscle showed spontaneous activity in the form of fibrillation potentials. This finding cannot be attributed at the early stage of GBS (no neurographic evidence of axonal motor nerve lesion) and furthermore EMG typically demonstrates denervation patterns after two weeks in severe axonal subtype cases.

Initial therapeutic treatment included fluid replacement, intravenous and oral potassium supplementation of 4 g i.v. and 2400 mg orally, empirical antibiotics (cefotaxime and metronidazole), multimodal analgesia and supportive treatment of gastrointestinal symptoms. Potassium level monitoring showed a normal value of 3.9 mmol/l 6 hours after the initial analysis and normal further values (J1 3.7 mmol/and 3.5 mmol/l, J2 4.0 mmol/l, J3 3.8 mmol/l, J4 4.4 mmol/l, J8 4.4 mmol/l). Follow-up CRP showed regression of inflammation

Clinical improvement occurred immediately with resolution of cyanosis and stabilisation of circulation following initial therapeutic shock management in the intensive care unit. Partial resolution of lower extremity paralysis followed within eight hours of admission (MRC score improved from 2/5 to 3/5).

The clinical presentation, including recent history of infection, paraesthesia, dysaesthesia, neuropathic pain, and persistent incomplete paraparesis (MRC score 3/5), together with electrodiagnosis of polyneuropathy, led to the diagnosis of GBS. On the third day, IVIG therapy (0.4 g/kg/day for 5 days) was initiated. The symptoms continued to improve, with a reduction in pain, paresthesia and motor weakness. The patient was discharged on 31 January 2025 in a fully autonomous state. A slight unilateral weakness in left foot lift (MRC 3/5) was treated with an orthosis and neurological rehabilitation was not necessary. Due to logistical constraints, no follow-up examination could be performed as the patient had moved away.

Discussion

Although classic presentations often clearly indicate GBS, the syndrome encompasses a broad spectrum of clinical, laboratory and neurophysiological findings [10]. According to the diagnostic criteria of the European Academy of Neurology/Peripheral Nerve Society [6], the recent history of infection, the relative symmetry of the symptoms, the presence of pain, the paraesthesia occurring two days prior to admission, the progressive deterioration and the polyneuropathy on electrodiagnosis support the diagnosis of GBS. The normal cerebrospinal fluid protein level does not exclude this diagnosis [6].

The initially severe gastrointestinal symptoms including severe vomiting indicated both GBS and hypokalaemia with hypokalaemia-related neurological deficits. Severe hypokalaemia could be a differential diagnosis that mimics GBS [10, 11]. Nayak [7] described weakness that developed rapidly within minutes to several hours, with the limbs being more severely affected than the trunk and the weakness occurring predominantly proximally. In cases characterised by pure motor deficits, periodic paralysis should be considered [7]. Mohamed et al. [11] recommend systematic testing for hypokalaemia in all cases of acute paralysis with early intensive potassium supplementation. The subsequent clinical response can help distinguish hypokalaemia from true GBS.

Comi et al. [12] have described potassium depletion myopathic changes with pathological features of myogenic damage (phagocytosis of degenerated muscle fibres and muscle fibre regeneration). But all cases had markedly increased serum creatine kinase (CPK) levels and in our case the CPK was only slightly elevated. Krause et al. [8] described fibrillations and positive sharp waves in the tibialis anterior muscle in severe hypokalaemia (baseline potassium level 1.6 mmol/l, examination on the second day after admission following the start of therapy). An initially myogenic pattern of hypokalaemia was also described by Hashimoto et al. in 1980 [13], which later developed into neurogenic abnormalities. Whether the spontaneous activity observed in our case constitutes an expression of hypokalaemic myopathy remains uncertain and requires further investigation. Spontaneous activity in the muscle without neurographic evidence of axonal lesion is unlikely and cannot be explained in the early phase of GBS.

Although hypokalaemia primarily affects muscle membrane function, a study involving 10 patients with hypokalaemic periodic paralysis showed a reduction in the amplitude of sensory action potentials during paralytic episodes [14]. However, to our knowledge, no cases of prolonged sensory conduction velocity have been reported to date; this was observed in our case and supports the hypothesis of concomitant GBS. It is unlikely that the observed conduction block of the tibial nerves is due to submaximal electrical stimulation. The patient had a slim build with minimal subcutaneous tissue at the stimulation site, a condition that generally facilitates effective current delivery and sufficient nerve depolarisation. Repeated stimulation with increasing current intensity failed to restore normal compound muscle action potential, which also argues against insufficient stimulation as the underlying cause. These findings support the interpretation that the conduction blocks reflect a true neurophysiological abnormality rather than a technical artefact related to the stimulation parameters. Polyneuropathic features with early neurogenic blockades and reduced motor and sensory nerve conduction velocity are consistent with GBS. In our case, the early symptoms of paraesthesia and neuropathic pain following gastroenteritis, the incomplete resolution of paraparesis (after potassium correction) with polyneuropathic findings in electrodiagnostics, and the response to immunotherapy support the diagnosis of GBS.

Although potassium supplementation led to incomplete improvement of paraparesis, significant clinical recovery occurred after IVIG administration, with almost complete resolution of symptoms after one month, supporting a dual diagnosis of hypokalaemia and Guillain-Barré syndrome.

Conclusion

This case report illustrates the complex clinical presentation of a patient with severe hypokalaemia and Guillain-Barré syndrome, highlighting the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges as well as the rare association of GBS following salmonella infection. Clinicians should be aware of the potential overlap between electrolyte disturbances and neuromuscular disorders. Early correction of hypokalaemia and careful monitoring of the clinical course can prevent misdiagnosis and enable early diagnosis of overlapping GBS with timely and appropriate therapy.
