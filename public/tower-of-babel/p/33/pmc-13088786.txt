Background

Contraceptive continuation is a key indicator of the effectiveness and quality of family planning programs, reflecting women’s ability to consistently and correctly use contraceptives to prevent unintended pregnancies and achieve their reproductive goals [1]. The decision to continue contraceptive use is influenced by multiple factors, including characteristics of the chosen method, potential side effects, user satisfaction, support from partners, fertility intentions, access to services, and prevailing social norms [2, 3]. Understanding the factors that affect contraceptive continuation can help design and implement interventions that address the needs and preferences of different groups of women and improve their reproductive health outcomes [3].

However, contraceptive continuation remains challenging in many settings, especially in sub-Saharan Africa, where the unmet need for family planning is high, and the prevalence rate is low [4]. According to the latest estimates, only 25% of women aged 15–49 in sub-Saharan Africa use any modern method of contraception, and 22% have an unmet need for family planning [5]. Moreover, about one-third of women who use modern methods discontinue within 12 months of initiation [6]. Contraceptive discontinuation can result in unwanted pregnancies, unsafe abortions, maternal morbidity and mortality, and reduced child survival and well-being [2, 3].

Previous studies have examined the patterns and determinants of contraceptive continuation and discontinuation in sub-Saharan Africa using data from Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) [2, 3, 7]. These studies have found that contraceptive continuation and discontinuation vary by region, country, method type, age group, parity, marital status, education level, occupation, residence, religion, fertility preferences, and other factors [1, 6, 7]. However, most of these studies have used conventional statistical methods that may not capture the complex interactions and nonlinear relationships among variables. Machine learning is a domain of artificial intelligence that uses algorithms and data to learn from patterns and make predictions [8]. Machine learning techniques have been highly applied to various health research fields, such as diagnosis, prognosis, treatment, epidemiology, and health policy [8, 9]. Machine learning techniques offer several advantages over conventional statistical methods, such as handling large and complex data sets, detecting hidden patterns and associations among variables, generating accurate and robust predictions, and providing interpretable results [10, 11]. Machine learning models can be interpreted even when they use a black box model using SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations), which is based on game theory [12]. SHAP assigns a value to each feature (variable) that represents its contribution to the prediction for a given observation.

Hence, our present study aimed to implement a machine learning model to predict a continuation of contraception among women aged 15–49 in a West African country, using data from the last four Demographic and Health Surveys. The study also used SHAP to identify and rank the most influential features for the prediction.

Data and method

Study framework

We used the IPUMS-DHS (Integrated Public Use Microdata Series) platform to analyse the data from the DHS surveys conducted in West African countries (Benin, Burkina Faso, Côte d’Ivoire, Ghana, Guinea, Liberia, Mali, Niger, Nigeria, Senegal), where the data were available for our study topic. The IPUMS-DHS platform harmonised the variables related to the health and well-being of women, children, men, births, and all randomly selected household members from the DHS surveys of 32 African and 9 Asian countries. This platform enabled us to use the database more efficiently and consistently across different countries and periods.

Study design and data sources

A secondary analysis was conducted on cross-sectional data from the demographic and health survey conducted in West African countries to predict contraceptive continuation among women aged 15–49 in low- and middle-income countries since The 1980s. The data extraction and analysis guidelines were obtained from the DHS program website [13], which provided information on the surveys’ objectives, organisation, sampling plan, and data analysis methods.

Study population

The study included all women aged 15 to 49 residing in regular households during the last four Demographic and Health Surveys conducted in West African countries. Since the surveys were conducted at different periods in each country, the earliest survey in the study dates back to 1980, and we considered the four most recent surveys for each country. The initial database extraction revealed approximately 448,844 women from the targeted countries, based on the four most recent demographic and health surveys, comprising over 66 variables of interest. The attached table presents the country-specific data collected from 1992 to 2018, from which we selected the four most recent surveys for each country with available data.

Response variable

Our study focused on the dependent variable, contraceptive continuation, operationalized using the DHS reproductive calendar. Continuation was defined as maintaining the same contraceptive method for at least 12 consecutive months following initiation, while discontinuation was defined as stopping the process before 12 months or switching to another method. In the case of a method switch, the previous method was classified as discontinued at the time of the switch. Women whose contraceptive episodes extended beyond the observation period of the calendar were treated as right-censored at the end of the timeline. Following DHS guidelines [14], we measured the time (in months) between the initiation and termination of each contraceptive episode. Machine learning algorithm

We initially employed the lazypredict library (version 0.2.12) [19] to rapidly generate and compare a set of baseline models with minimal coding or parameter tuning, including logistic regression, random forest, support vector classifiers, and gradient boosting methods. This benchmarking step allowed us to identify LightGBM as the best-performing algorithm. LightGBM is a gradient-boosting framework based on decision trees that is particularly efficient for large-scale datasets. It incorporates several technical advantages, such as leaf-wise tree growth, histogram-based feature discretization, support for categorical features, and early stopping, which together reduce loss, memory usage, and overfitting while improving speed and accuracy. To account for the hierarchical nature of our data (women nested within countries), we implemented two modeling strategies: (i) a country-based model, where a separate LightGBM model was trained and evaluated for each country, and (ii) a multilevel LightGBM model, which included country-level variation as a random effect while simultaneously controlling for individual-level predictors. Both models were optimized through hyperparameter tuning to enhance predictive performance and computational efficiency.

Model training and evaluation

After defining the modeling approaches, we developed a rigorous evaluation framework to ensure reliable results. The dataset was randomly partitioned into training (70%) and testing (30%) subsets. Stratification was applied by both country and survey wave to maintain heterogeneity in contraceptive patterns across time and geography. In addition, a 5-fold cross-validation procedure was applied within the training set. This strategy optimized hyperparameters and provided robust internal validation, mitigating the risk of overfitting. Model performance was evaluated using multiple complementary metrics. These included accuracy (overall proportion of correct predictions), precision (proportion of predicted positive cases that were correct), recall (proportion of actual positive cases correctly predicted), F1-score (harmonic mean of precision and recall), and the area under the ROC curve (AUC), which measures overall discriminative ability. To further illustrate classification performance, we generated a confusion matrix, which tabulates correct and incorrect predictions across continuation and discontinuation categories. This matrix provides a direct view of model strengths and weaknesses in handling each class.

Results

Data preprocessing

The data preprocessing stage included multiple steps to ensure accuracy and comparability across surveys and countries. First, extracted datasets underwent extensive cleaning, which involved checking consistency across variables, harmonizing coding schemes, and verifying alignment with DHS metadata. Quality checks were performed to confirm the completeness and plausibility of values.

Handling of missing data was an important step, given the complexity of DHS datasets. For continuous variables, missing values were imputed using the mean; for categorical variables, missing values were imputed using the most frequent category (mode). Sensitivity analyses demonstrated that substituting median values for continuous variables produced no significant difference in model performance. Observations with more than 20% missing values across key predictors were excluded from the analysis to maintain data integrity. Categorical variables were label-encoded to convert them into numerical representations suitable for machine learning algorithms. Continuous variables were standardized to ensure comparable scales across features. Outliers were identified and treated as missing, then imputed following the same strategy, thereby reducing the risk of bias from extreme values.

These preprocessing procedures ensured that the final analytical dataset was consistent, harmonized across multiple DHS survey waves, and ready for downstream modeling.

Descriptive analysis

Out of the total extracted data (448,884), we excluded patients who had never used a contraceptive method (384,815) and those who had used sterilization methods (1,472). The final sample size consisted of 61,510 participants, of whom 35,271 were not discontinued from their contraception method, while 26,239 had discontinued, resulting in a discontinuation rate of 42.7% (Fig. 2).

The analysis revealed significant differences in discontinuation rates across different contraceptive methods (Table 1). For example, abstinence had a low usage rate of 1.7% and a lower discontinuation rate of 1.4%, while astrology had a shallow usage rate of 0.3% and a higher discontinuation rate of 0.4%. Among modern methods, injections were the most popular, with a usage rate of 20.7%, but they also had a relatively high discontinuation rate of 24.2%. Likewise, lactational amenorrhea (LAM), a method related to breastfeeding, had a moderate usage rate of 3.9% but a higher discontinuation rate of 6.3%. In contrast, methods such as diaphragm, foam/jelly, and herbs/plants had negligible usage and discontinuation rates of less than 0.1%. The analysis also showed that folkloric methods had a higher discontinuation rate (7.5%) than their usage rate (6.8%), while modern methods had a lower discontinuation rate (74.1%) than their usage rate (71.8%). Traditional methods had a similar usage and discontinuation rate of around 20%.

Contraceptive methods used and their association with discontinuation

The analysis indicated that all features, except Total children ever born, were significantly associated with contraceptive discontinuation (Table 2). For instance, age was significantly different between the two groups, with younger women being more likely to discontinue contraceptives (mean age 28.0) than older women (mean age 31.0). Likewise, the number of children under 5 in the household was associated with discontinuation, as women who discontinued had a higher average number of children (mean 1.9) than those who did not (mean 1.3). Other factors that were significant in the analysis include the woman’s current marital or union status, desire for more children, household wealth index, total years of education, husband’s desire for children, the reason for last discontinuation, religion, and various sociodemographic factors. These findings suggest that multiple factors influence contraceptive discontinuation and underscore the importance of considering them when addressing this issue.

Hyperparameter tuning

Optuna was used to tune the Model’s hyperparameters, which are the parameters that determine the Model’s complexity and performance. The optimal hyperparameter values were found by searching within a specified range. For instance, colsample_bytree is the hyperparameter that indicates the percentage of features used for each tree. The optimal value for this hyperparameter is 0.80, which implies that nearly 80% of the features are used. Another hyperparameter is learning_rate, which controls the learning speed of the Model from each boosting iteration and the influence of each tree on the final prediction. The optimal value for this hyperparameter is 0.09. The max_depth parameter sets the maximum number of layers in each tree, and its optimal value is 10. The min_child_samples parameter specifies the minimum number of samples needed in a leaf node, and its optimal value is 120. The num_leaves parameter limits each tree’s maximum number of leaf nodes, and its optimal value is 99. Additionally, the reg_alpha and reg_lambda parameters serve as regularization terms, which shrink the Model’s complexity and prevent overfitting. Their optimal values (Table 3) are 3.71e-08 and 1.79e-07, respectively. The subsample parameter defines the proportion of training data used for each boosting iteration, and its optimal value is 0.47. Nearly half (50%) of the data is used (Table 4).

Country-based model

The country-based Model (Fig. 7) indicates that Nigeria has the highest accuracy (0.80), followed by Benin (0.79) and Niger (0.76). Liberia has the lowest accuracy (0.73), followed by Guinea (0.74) and Mali (0.75). The Model tends to predict no contraceptive discontinuation (class 0) better than contraceptive discontinuation (class 1), as the average precision, recall, and F1-score are higher for class 0 across all countries. However, exceptions exist, such as Niger, where the Model has a higher precision, recall, and F1 score for class 1. These variations may reflect the differences in the size and distribution of the data, the prevalence and characteristics of contraceptive use, and the socioeconomic and cultural context of each country.

Multilevel LGBM modelling

The confusion matrix of the multilevel LightGBM model (Fig. 8) shows that the algorithm correctly classified 8,670 cases of no discontinuation and 5,043 cases of discontinuation. However, it misclassified 1,903 women who continued contraceptive use as discontinuers and 2,837 discontinuers as continuers. These results highlight the Model’s stronger performance in identifying continuation compared to discontinuation, with false negatives remaining a notable challenge.

Fig. 8Confusion matrix for the LightGBM multilevel model

Confusion matrix for the LightGBM multilevel model

The classification report of the multilevel LightGBM model (Fig. 9) indicates solid predictive performance. For the “No discontinuation” class, the Model achieved a precision of 0.74 and a recall of 0.82, showing a strong ability to identify women who continued contraceptive use correctly. For the “Discontinuation” class, precision was 0.72 and recall was 0.64, suggesting more modest success in detecting true cases of discontinuation. The corresponding F1-scores were 0.78 and 0.68, respectively, reflecting a reasonable balance between precision and recall. Overall, the Model reached an accuracy of 0.74, with a macro-average F1-score of 0.73 and a weighted F1-score of 0.74, confirming consistent and reliable performance across both outcomes despite class imbalance.

Fig. 9Metric of the LGBM multilevel model

Metric of the LGBM multilevel model

The Model’s performance improved after tuning the hyperparameters. The Model achieved an accuracy of 74% and an AUC of 80% (Fig. 10). These metrics show the Model’s ability to make meaningful predictions and outperform random guessing. Random guessing would have an accuracy of about 50% and an AUC of around 0.5, indicating no predictive power. Therefore, our Model’s accuracy and AUC values show its effectiveness in predicting contraceptive continuation among women aged 15–49.

Model explanation

Using SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) to analyze the data, we found that the following features had the most impact on the probability of contraceptive method continuation: the number of children under 5 in the household, age, desire for more children, current family planning (FP) use by method type, total children ever born, relationship structure in the household, visiting a health facility in the past 12 months, country, and husband’s desire for children (Fig. 11). These features are crucial to consider in the predictive Model and other variables to understand the factors affecting contraceptive behaviour among women in the target age group.

Fig. 11Selected feature importance from the final model

Selected feature importance from the final model

The analysis of feature impacts (Fig. 12) reveals that the number of children under 5 in the household, age, and desire for more children have high values and negative impacts, meaning that women with these characteristics are more likely to stop using contraceptive methods. Conversely, current family planning (FP) use by method type and total children ever born have moderate to high values and positive impacts, implying that these factors encourage continued contraceptive use. The relationship structure in the household, visits to health facilities in the past 12 months, and the husband’s desire for children also have positive impacts, but their values differ. The country has a low value and a negative impact, indicating that it has a relatively minor influence on discontinuation. These features should be considered in conjunction with others, as the combined impact of the remaining features, although individually low in value, can still significantly influence contraceptive discontinuation.

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate and predict the continuation of contraception among women aged 15–49 in a West African country, using data from the most recent four Demographic and Health Surveys and a machine learning model. The study also used SHAP analysis to identify and rank the most influential features for the prediction. The machine learning model’s performance was evaluated based on metrics such as accuracy and AUC. The results from the analysis of 61,510 participants provide valuable insights into the use and discontinuation of different contraceptive methods and revealed differences between the discontinuation rates and the use rates for various categories of contraceptive methods. Folkloric methods had a higher discontinuation rate (7.5%) than their use rate (6.8%), indicating that these traditional methods may need to be more reliable and suitable for long-term use. Conversely, modern methods had a lower discontinuation rate (74.1%) than their use rate (71.8%), suggesting that individuals who choose modern methods may be more likely to continue using them once initiated. Traditional methods had similar use and discontinuation rates of around 20%, indicating that while they are widely used, they may also have challenges or limitations that lead to discontinuation. The study also identified several factors associated with contraceptive discontinuation, excluding the Total children ever born. These findings emphasize the multifactorial nature of contraceptive discontinuation and the need to consider these factors when addressing this issue.

Age was identified as a significant factor, with younger women (mean age 28.0) being more prone to contraceptive discontinuation compared to older women (mean age 31.0). This finding aligns with previous research, which highlights higher discontinuation rates among younger women [1, 4]. Furthermore, the number of children under 5 in the household was found to be associated with discontinuation. Women who discontinued contraceptives had a higher average number of children (mean 1.9) compared to those who did not discontinue (mean 1.3). Similar findings have been reported in previous studies, illustrating the influence of parity on contraceptive use [22–24].

The analysis also revealed the significance of other factors, such as the woman’s current marital or union status, desire for more children, household wealth index, total years of education, husband’s desire for children, the reason for last discontinuation, religion, and various sociodemographic factors. These findings align with a growing body of research highlighting the complex interplay of social, economic, and cultural factors in contraceptive decision-making [2, 6, 25].

In addition, our study illustrates that contraceptive discontinuation rates vary across different countries and years. These results are consistent with previous studies that reported regional variations in contraceptive discontinuation rates in sub-Saharan Africa. According to Polis et al. [26], contraceptive failure rates were lower in East Africa than in West Africa for most methods. Similarly, Barden-O’Fallon et al. [27] found that the probability of pregnancy after discontinuation of contraception was higher in East Africa than in francophone West Africa. These regional differences may reflect variations in contraceptive method mix, quality of care, fertility preferences, social norms, and other factors that influence contraceptive behaviour. The machine learning model implemented in this study performed well, achieving an average accuracy of 0.74 and an average AUC of 0.82 across all countries. These findings indicate that the Model can differentiate between the two classes (0 and 1) and accurately predict contraceptive continuation outcomes in most cases.

The SHAP analysis revealed several crucial features for predicting contraceptive continuation among women aged 15–49 in the West African country. These features included the number of children under 5 in the household, age, desire for more children, current FP method type, total children ever born, relationship structure in the household, visiting a health facility within the past 12 months, country, and husband’s desire for children. These features had the highest SHAP values across all countries, showing their significant influence on the prediction of contraceptive continuation. The SHAP values also explained how each feature affected predictions for individual or grouped observations.

These findings have important implications for sub-Saharan African family planning programs and services. The study underscores the need to overcome the main barriers and challenges women face in accessing and using contraceptive methods, especially in countries with low contraceptive use and high unmet need [5]. The study also highlights the utility of offering women a variety of contraceptive choices that suit their preferences and needs, as well as ensuring the delivery of high-quality and continuous contraceptive services [3, 6]. Previous research has also emphasized the role of engaging men and partners in decision-making and communication about family planning and promoting positive attitudes and norms within communities [7, 28]. Moreover, this study shows the potential of machine learning techniques in enhancing the analysis and prediction of contraceptive behaviour using large and complex datasets [8, 9, 29]. The interpretable results produced by the SHAP analysis can help healthcare professionals understand the factors that affect contraceptive continuation and enable them to design interventions accordingly [30].

However, it is essential to recognize the limitations of this study, mainly related to data quality and availability. The cross-sectional and retrospective nature of the DHS data might lead to recall bias and limit causal inference. Also, the data did not include some relevant variables, such as quality of care, method satisfaction, partner communication, social norms, and service availability. Moreover, data aggregation at the country level might mask regional or local differences in contraceptive behaviour. Future research should utilize longitudinal data and incorporate additional variables that capture the multidimensional aspects of contraceptive behavior. Furthermore, additional studies are required to validate and compare various machine learning models and feature selection methods for predicting contraceptive continuation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study highlighted significant variations in contraceptive discontinuation rates and identified regional disparities in contraceptive behavior. The findings underscored the potential of machine learning techniques in analyzing complex datasets and predicting contraceptive behavior. Nevertheless, the study acknowledged certain limitations regarding data quality, causal inference, and variable availability. To address these limitations and further validate prediction models, future research should be conducted. Overall, this study contributes to our understanding of contraceptive behavior and lays the foundation for future investigations in this field.
