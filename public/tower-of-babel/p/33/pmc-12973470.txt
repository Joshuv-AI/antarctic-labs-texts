Human papillomavirus awareness and vaccination willingness among male community residents in Beijing based on the Health Belief Model
Authors: Yan Xing, Rujing Shi, Yulian Li, Run Cai, Tianwei Ma, Xinhai Yuan, Li Liu, Ziyun Jiang, Wenjing Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTFollowing the January 2025 approval of the first male HPV vaccine in the Chinese mainland, we investigated vaccination willingness and its influencing factors among community-dwelling men. We conducted a cross-sectional study in Beijing, recruiting 480 community-based male residents and 399 men who have sex with men (MSM). Participants completed a questionnaire assessing HPV knowledge and the Health Belief Model (HBM) constructs. Multivariable logistic regression was performed to identify determinants of willingness. We found a high level of vaccination willingness, which was notably higher among MSM (90.73%) than the community-recruited male population (87.29%). Critically, willingness surpassed awareness, indicating significant latent demand despite low overall HPV knowledge. HBM analysis showed that perceived benefits promoted willingness in both groups, while self-efficacy of prevention paradoxically acted as an inhibitor. MSM reported higher perceived susceptibility and benefits, but lower severity, compared to community-recruited men. Determinants diverged significantly: income and sexual behavior were unique drivers for community-recruited men, whereas HPV knowledge and STI self-testing drove willingness in MSM. This study confirms high post-approval vaccination willingness but highlights distinct influencing factors. Findings suggest the need for differentiated strategies: universal approaches should highlight vaccine benefits to convert latent demand, while targeted education for MSM must focus on improving knowledge accuracy.KEYWORDS: human papillomavirus, male, vaccination, China, knowledge, community

Human papillomavirus (HPV) is one of the most common sexually transmitted agents worldwide.1 It is estimated that the cumulative lifetime probability of acquiring HPV exceeds 80% in sexually active individuals.2 Since HPV infection serves as a key etiological factor of cervical cancer, it is regarded as a significant women’s health concern3; yet in this context, the male population has often been overlooked.4 In men, high-risk HPV types can lead to penile, anal, and head and neck cancers, while low-risk types may cause genital warts, resulting in both physical changes and psychological distress.1,5 A recent study highlighted the high prevalence of HPV in men, with one in three men has been infected with at least one type of genital HPV.6 Recent global surveillance indicates that the incidence of head and neck cancer in males is nearly four times that of females, while male anal cancer rates have demonstrated an increasing trend across most regions, with annual increases as high as 7.8%.7 Due to specific sexual behavior patterns, men who have sex with men (MSM) are particularly at higher risk of HPV infection.8

HPV vaccines have proven significantly high efficacy in preventing HPV infection and related diseases.9 Currently, the Chinese mainland is progressively rolling out HPV vaccination subsidy policies for eligible females. Moreover, in January 2025, China’s National Medical Products Administration (NMPA) approved the first HPV vaccine for use in males aged 9–26. Research indicates that HPV vaccination confers economic benefits related to men’s health.10,11 Furthermore, improving HPV-related health outcomes among men can also have substantial beneficial effects on women’s HPV-related health.12

Since the approval of HPV vaccination for men, relatively few studies have focused on men’s willingness to undergo vaccination. Xu et al. conducted a survey among male college students in Jinan13; however, there remains limited understanding of HPV awareness and vaccination willingness among general community-based men and MSM populations. The Health Belief Model (HBM), which includes perceived susceptibility, perceived severity, perceived benefits, perceived barriers, and self-efficacy,14 provides a useful framework for explaining health-related decision-making behaviors. This model is particularly relevant for understanding the willingness to receive the HPV vaccine.15

The objective of this study is to investigate the awareness of HPV and the willingness to receive HPV vaccination among men in communities and MSM in Beijing, as well as to identify factors influencing their vaccination willingness. The findings aim to provide evidence to future efforts to advocate HPV vaccination among male populations.

Method

Study population

This cross-sectional study was conducted in Haidian District in Beijing. To align with community-level public health practices, we stratified the study population into two groups based on recruitment channels: community-recruited men (representing residents from local community grids) and MSM (representing high-risk groups from specific networks). All participants provided informed consent, and the study obtained approval from the Ethics Committee of Beijing Shangdi Hospital (SDEC-KT-2025-18-S01). For community-recruited men, a convenience sampling approach was adopted in five communities randomly selected from a total of 29 communities. Eligible individuals were invited to complete an electronic questionnaire. For the MSM population, initial recruitment was carried out through convenience sampling at MSM-focused community-based organizations or the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) voluntary counseling and testing (VCT) clinic affiliated with Haidian center for disease control and prevention (CDC). Following this, a snowball sampling strategy was implemented, whereby enrolled participants were encouraged to refer other eligible individuals, thus aiding in the expansion of this hard-to-reach group. All participants were restricted to ages 18 to 45 y. Although the current approved age range for HPV vaccination in males in the Chinese mainland is up to 26 y, the upper age limit of 45 was chosen to align with the eligible age for females, allowing for potential future expansion. Questionnaire design

Demographic characteristics

This section collected data on age, occupation, monthly average expenditure, education, and family history of tumors.

Awareness and knowledge of HPV and HPV vaccine

Awareness was assessed by asking participants “Have you heard of HPV?” Those who responded “Yes” proceeded to a knowledge test comprising 20 true-or-false items, as shown in Supplementary Table S1. Each correct answer was assigned one point. The total score, which served as the knowledge score, had a maximum of 20.

HBM scale

Based on a literature review, a scale was developed according to HBM to quantify participants’ health beliefs regarding HPV prevention. The scale consisted of 17 items (Supplementary Table S2), covering five dimensions: perceived severity (4 items), perceived susceptibility (3 items), self-efficacy (2 items), perceived barriers (3 items), and perceived benefits (5 items). Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale. The score for each dimension was calculated as the mean of its respective items. The scale demonstrated good internal consistency, with Cronbach’s α for each dimension ranging from 0.802 to 0.868, and good validity (KMO = .815, Bartlett’s test p < .05).

Sexual behavior

Information on relationship status, sexual frequency, condom use, number of sexual partners in the past 6 months, frequency of STI testing, and history of STI diagnosis was collected. The STIs specified included HIV, gonorrhea, syphilis, genital herpes, genital warts, and nongonococcal urethritis.

Statistic analysis

Descriptive analysis

Categorical demographic variables were presented as frequencies and percentages (%). Age was dichotomized based on the cutoff of 26 y, which is the approved vaccination age for males in the Chinese mainland. Chi-square tests were employed to compare the demographic characteristics between the community-recruited male and MSM groups.

Comparisons on HPV knowledge and health belief

Differences in knowledge scores and health belief dimensions between the community-recruited males and MSM were assessed using linear regression analyses. These models controlled for potential confounding effects from key demographic and clinical variables, namely age, monthly expenditure, education, and family history of tumors.

Influencing factors of HPV vaccination willingness

To determine the independent predictors of HPV vaccination willingness, a multivariable logistic regression model was used. The complete variable list is presented in Supplementary Table S3.

Analytical parameters

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.3). A two-sided p-value of less than 0.05 was defined as the threshold for statistical significance.

Result

Demographic characteristics

This study enrolled 879 men from local communities, comprising 480 men from the community and 399 MSM. The demographic characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Overall, 31.39% of the participants were aged ≤26 y, aligning with the recommended age group for HPV immunization. Furthermore, 13.39% were employed in medical-related fields, 58.82% reported an average monthly expenditure exceeding 5000 yuan, 72.35% held a bachelor’s degree or higher, 21.78% had a family history of tumor. When comparing the two groups, MSM participants were significantly older (χ2 = 122.990, p < .001) and more likely to report a family history of tumors (χ2 = 10.765, p = .001) than community-recruited males. Moreover, the MSM group had lower educational attainment (χ2 = 48.967, p < .001) and were less frequently employed in medical-related occupations (χ2 = 71.128, p < .001).

Awareness and knowledge of HPV

The majority of the participants reported having heard of HPV (84.61%). Notably, the HPV awareness rate was significantly higher among MSM (90.68%) compared to community-recruited males (79.58%) (β = 1.141, p < .001). Overall HPV knowledge score was low, with the average score of (9.44 ± 4.70) out of a maximum possible score of 20.00. Consistent with the awareness finding, the MSM group demonstrated significantly better overall knowledge (10.08 ± 3.91) than community-recruited males (8.91 ± 5.22) (β = 1.474, p < .001).

Furthermore, significant differences were observed between the two groups on several specific knowledge items, which are detailed in Supplementary Table S4. The result revealed several critical knowledge gaps (correct rate <60%). Community-recruited males performed poorly on ‘The HPV vaccine can prevent other STIs, such as HIV/AIDS’. MSM performed poorly on ‘Most people who are infected with HPV will have noticeable symptoms’, ‘A person’s chance of getting HPV increases with the number of sexual partners’, ‘Using condoms is not an effective way to prevent HPV infection’. In addition, all participants performed poorly on ‘Almost all sexually active men or women will be infected with HPV at some point.

Health belief of HPV prevention

A 5-point Likert scale questionnaire was employed to assess HPV-related health beliefs. Overall, perceived benefits received high scores (4.24 ± 0.67), whereas other dimensions were rated at a moderate level, as shown in Table 2. MSM reported higher perceived susceptibility (β = 0.511, p < .001) and perceived benefits (β = 0.125, p = .014) than community-recruited males, while presented lower perceived severity (β = −0.575, p < .001).

Willingness to HPV vaccination among males

The willingness to receive HPV vaccination was notably high in this study, with 88.85% of all participants reporting acceptance. MSM exhibited a slightly higher acceptance rate (90.73%) compared to community-recruited males (87.29%) (β = 0.511, p = .024). Furthermore, 84.46% of the participants expressed willingness to recommend HPV vaccination to people around them though no significant differences were found between MSM and community-recruited males (β = 0.170, p = .420). Interestingly, the proportion of participants willing to receive the HPV vaccine was higher than the proportion of those who had previously heard of HPV.

Influencing factors of HPV vaccination willingness

Multivariable logistic regression was conducted to identify factors associated with HPV vaccination willingness in each of the two groups. Self-efficacy and perceived benefits were influencing factors in both groups, with perceived benefits acted as a promoter (community-recruited males: β = 2.089, OR = 8.080, p < .001; MSM: β = 1.219, OR = 3.383, p < .001) and self-efficacy acted as an inhibitor (community-recruited males: β = –0.918, OR = 0.399, p = .023; MSM: β = −0.482, OR = 0.618, p = .019). Several factors were uniquely associated with vaccination willingness in community-recruited males, including income, sexual frequency, and condom use. In contrast, knowledge score and STI testing frequency were unique positive factors among MSM. Detailed information can be found in Table 3.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first community-based study to investigate HPV willingness among men residing outside of the school setting in the Chinese mainland since the first approval of HPV vaccine for male. We recruited 879 male participants from Haidian District of Beijing, comprising 480 men from the community-recruited population and 399 MSM.

The overall vaccination willingness rate was high at 88.85%. Men from the community reported a willingness rate of 87.29%, which reflects the community’s general attitude and is significantly higher than the 50.81% reported in a systematic review of Western Pacific countries.17 The willingness rate of MSM was 90.73%, slightly higher than that of the community-recruited male population. Though in community-recruited population, vaccination willingness rates show high variability, MSM consistently reported a higher willingness to be vaccinted.18,19

It is important to note that our participants were recruited from Beijing’s Haidian District, a major technology and economic hub characterized by a highly educated, younger population with access to superior healthcare resources. Furthermore, the MSM participants were primarily recruited through a public welfare organization, suggesting they may be more health-conscious. Given the significant regional and socioeconomic disparities across China, the willingness rates observed in our study are likely higher than those of the general Chinese male population. Nevertheless, this study provides valuable insights into the HPV vaccination willingness among community-dwelling men in China.

Regarding awareness and knowledge, 84.61% of all participants had heard of HPV, with the awareness rate reaching 90.68% among MSM. This level of awareness is higher than that reported in other studies.20 However, knowledge scores were relatively low, suggesting that while information dissemination has increased name recognition of HPV, it has not yet translated into systematic knowledge.21 A significant knowledge gap was observed across the entire cohort regarding the statement, “Almost all sexually active men or women will be infected with HPV at some point,” indicating a prevalent underestimation of HPV infection risk. Men from the community-recruited population also held misconceptions, such as believing the HPV vaccine offers protection beyond HPV. Conversely, MSM appeared to underestimate their vulnerability by overlooking asymptomatic infections, downplaying the risks of multiple sexual partners, and misjudging the effectiveness of condoms in preventing HPV. Interestingly, we also found that the willingness to receive the HPV vaccine was higher than the general awareness of HPV, as previous study mentioned.22 This suggests a latent demand for the vaccine among men, which could be activated through targeted health education.

Regarding health beliefs, community-recruited men reported low perceived susceptibility but high perceived severity, whereas MSM exhibited the opposite pattern. This divergence may stem from distinct cognitive frameworks regarding HPV. MSM are generally more aware of sexual health risks due to long-term HIV prevention efforts. However, as their HPV knowledge often centers on genital warts—considered a manageable nuisance23—they may overlook the virus’s long-term oncogenic risks. In contrast, men from the general community are often entirely unfamiliar with HPV. For this group, the initial introduction of the concept can trigger a pronounced fear response, leading to higher perceived severity once the potential health threats are disclosed. Self-efficacy for preventing HPV was relatively high, and perceived barriers, such as cost, were low, which may be attributed to the high education and income levels of our sample. Encouragingly, the perceived benefits of vaccination were high across all participants, particularly among MSM.

The influencing factors of HPV vaccination willingness were different between the two groups. Perceived benefits emerged as a shared promoter of willingness, while self-efficacy functioned as a shared inhibitor. This finding aligns with prior literature, where perceived benefits were consistently and strongly associated with greater HPV vaccination willingness among men.13,24,25 A key and counterintuitive finding of our study is the negative association between self-efficacy and vaccination willingness. We propose this can be explained by a phenomenon we term the ‘health illusion’: our measure of self-efficacy captured participants’ confidence in preventing HPV in general, not specifically through vaccination. Consequently, individuals with high self-efficacy may believe alternative protective measures are sufficient, thereby diminishing their perceived need for a vaccine. In the community-recruited male population, income and specific sexual behaviors (sexual frequency and condom use) were identified as unique determinants. Given that the HPV vaccine is self-paid in this setting, the influence of income suggests a clear economic barrier. Furthermore, the correlation with sexual safety indicators implies that vaccination is currently perceived as a discretionary measure, primarily pursued by those with higher health consciousness. Conversely, among MSM, unique factors included HPV knowledge and STI self-test intention. Although this group demonstrated higher general HPV awareness, specific misconceptions might negatively affect the vaccination willingness. For example, as our results show, a significant portion of MSM underestimated the risk of asymptomatic transmission and the limitations of condoms in preventing HPV, which could lead to a lower perceived necessity for vaccination. The link to STI self-test intention indicates a proactive, health-seeking orientation related to sexual health, suggesting these men are already engaged with preventative behaviors. The study revealed two distinct psychological profiles of risk perception: community-recruited men, who perceive HPV-related disease as severe but not personally relevant, require external support; whereas, MSM, who perceive high susceptibility but low severity, require the activation of internal drivers.

Consequently, public health campaigns aimed at increasing HPV vaccination willingness among men should adopt tailored strategies specific to these divergent target populations. A universal strategy involves raising awareness about the risks associated with HPV infection and emphasizing the comprehensive benefits of vaccination. Campaigns should frame vaccination not only as a benefit to men’s personal health but also as a protective measure for their partners and future generations.25,26 Indeed, research suggests that framing HPV vaccination as a moral or ethical responsibility may promote vaccination in China.27 For high-risk populations, specifically, despite existing high awareness, filling the specific knowledge gaps remains paramount. In this context, healthcare providers are critically positioned to disseminate accurate information and provide proactive recommendations.28

This study has several strengths. Firstly, the recruitment of participants from community-dwelling settings distinguishes this work from most previous studies focused on school students. Given the likely future decentralization of HPV vaccination services to community health centers, our findings provide evidence to community-based HPV prevention and health education strategies. Secondly, the broad inclusion of men up to 45 y of age offers valuable preliminary data that may inform future policy decisions regarding the potential expansion of the HPV vaccine’s applicable age range. Thirdly, by systematically investigating the hard-to-reach MSM population and developing distinct promotional strategies for the community-based and high-risk groups, this research addresses a significant gap in targeted public health interventions.

Despite its strengths, this study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes any causal inference between the identified factors and HPV vaccination willingness. Second, selection bias is inevitable. As noted, all participants were recruited from Haidian District, a developed urban area with relatively high education and income levels. Consequently, the participants may possess greater health awareness than the general population, which limits the generalizability of our findings to rural areas or regions with different socioeconomic profiles. Additionally, a robust analysis of occupational influence was not feasible due to the limited sample size of MSM in medical-related fields, although this factor may be significant.29 Lastly, HPV vaccination willingness is a complex, multi-faceted construct involving personal, cultural, and economic considerations. As a quantitative study, it may not capture the full nuance of this decision-making process; thus, complementary qualitative research would be beneficial to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the underlying motivations and barriers.

Conclusions

This study investigated HPV vaccination willingness among male residents in communities of Haidian District, Beijing, including both the community-based population and MSM. High willingness was observed in both groups, exceeding 90% among MSM, which was higher than the awareness of HPV. Although awareness of HPV was generally high, knowledge about the virus and its vaccine remained inadequate. Within the HBM framework, perceived susceptibility was relatively low in both groups. Compared to the community-recruited population, MSM reported higher perceived susceptibility and perceived benefits, but lower perceived severity. Perceived benefits were a strong promoter of vaccination willingness in both groups. In contrast, self-efficacy in HPV prevention acted as a potential inhibitor, likely due to a ‘health illusion’ where confidence in non-vaccine protective measures reduced the perceived need for vaccination. Unique determinants of vaccination willingness included income and specific sexual behaviors (such as sexual frequency and condom use) in the community-recruited population, and HPV knowledge and intention to self-test for STIs among MSM. Targeted strategies are recommended to enhance HPV vaccination uptake. General measures should focus on raising awareness about HPV and underscoring the benefits of vaccination. For MSM, who already perceive their susceptibility as high, interventions should focus on addressing specific knowledge gaps.
